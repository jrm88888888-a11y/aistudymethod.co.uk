Mission 1

You dream mostly in one stage of sleep. What are the others for?

Make a guess: You dream mostly in one stage of sleep. What are the others for?

Sleep and dreaming · the sleep cycle

Why do we need sleep?

Sleep is an active state with three big benefits:

  • Healthy brain: sleep clears waste from the brain and strengthens the pathways that store new memories.
  • Physical repair: in deep sleep the body repairs tissue, builds bone and muscle and supports the immune system.
  • Emotional stability: sleep, especially REM, helps you process emotions. After poor sleep people are more irritable and less able to cope.
Sleep and dreaming · the sleep cycle

Four stages, light to deep

StageWhat happens
Stage 1Light sleep as you drift off; muscles relax; easily woken
Stage 2Heart rate and temperature fall; brief bursts of activity called sleep spindles
Stage 3Deeper sleep; slow delta waves appear
Stage 4Deepest sleep; mostly delta waves; very hard to wake
  • These are non-REM (NREM) stages. With REM sleep they make the five stages of one complete sleep cycle.
  • Some sleep scientists now merge stages 3 and 4 into one stage. In the exam, use stages 1 to 4 plus REM.
Sleep and dreaming · the sleep cycle

REM sleep

  • REM stands for rapid eye movement: the eyes dart about under closed lids.
  • Brain activity is fast and close to waking levels.
  • Breathing and heart rate become quicker and less regular.
  • The arm and leg muscles are temporarily paralysed, so you can't act out dreams.
  • Dreaming happens mostly in REM. Some dreaming also occurs in NREM sleep, but it is usually less vivid.
Sleep and dreaming · the sleep cycle

The sleep cycle

  • One sleep cycle: stages 1, 2, 3, 4, back up through 3 and 2, then REM.
  • A cycle lasts about 90 minutes, and a night usually has 4 to 5 cycles.
  • Early cycles contain the most deep sleep (stages 3 and 4).
  • Later cycles contain less deep sleep and longer REM periods; the last one can last up to an hour.

Mission check

?You dream mostly in one stage of sleep. What are the others for?

Dreaming happens mostly in REM, but the other four stages matter too. Stages 1 and 2 take you down into stages 3 and 4, the deepest sleep, when the body repairs tissue and builds bone and muscle and the brain clears waste and strengthens memories. You repeat this cycle about every 90 minutes.

Sleep and dreaming · body clocks

Bodily rhythms

  • Your body runs on cycles called biological rhythms.
  • A circadian rhythm repeats about every 24 hours. The sleep–wake cycle is one: you feel sleepy at night and alert by day.
  • Body temperature also follows a circadian rhythm, lowest in the early morning.
Sleep and dreaming · body clocks

The clock inside you

  • An endogenous pacemaker is a body clock that keeps rhythms going without outside cues.
  • The main endogenous pacemaker is the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) in the hypothalamus. It receives light information from the eyes.
  • As it gets dark, the brain signals the pineal gland to release the hormone melatonin, which makes you sleepy.
  • Melatonin levels are high at night and low in the morning.
Sleep and dreaming · body clocks

Cues from outside

  • An exogenous zeitgeber ("time-giver") is an environmental cue that resets the body clock daily.
  • Light is the most important. Light reaching the eyes stops melatonin release and shifts the clock.
  • Others: meal times, alarm clocks, social routines.
  • Jet lag and shift work: the clock is out of step with these cues.
  • With no zeitgebers, rhythms continue but drift from 24 hours.

Mission 3

Lying awake at 3 a.m.: is it the body, the bedroom or the brain?

Make a guess: Lying awake at 3 a.m.: is it the body, the bedroom or the brain?

Sleep and dreaming · sleep disorders

What is insomnia?

  • Insomnia means regularly having problems sleeping.
  • Symptoms: finding it hard to fall asleep, waking in the night and lying awake, waking too early, and still feeling tired, irritable or unable to concentrate during the day.
  • Sleep onset insomnia: difficulty getting to sleep. Sleep maintenance insomnia: difficulty staying asleep.
Sleep and dreaming · sleep disorders

Why do people lie awake?

  • Stress and anxiety activate the sympathetic nervous system: heart and breathing speed up and the mind races, so the body is too aroused to sleep.
  • The bedroom: noise, light, heat, an uncomfortable bed.
  • Lifestyle: late caffeine, a large meal, late gaming, jet lag, shift work.
  • Health: pain or illness.
  • Evaluation: poor sleep also raises stress, so cause and effect are hard to separate.
Sleep and dreaming · sleep disorders

Damage to the hypothalamus

  • The hypothalamus contains the SCN, the body clock. After SCN damage, people can sleep erratically through day and night, because their sleep–wake cycle no longer follows light and dark.
  • It also contains the cells that make hypocretin, which keeps us awake. Damage there (such as injury or a tumour) can cause extreme daytime sleepiness.
  • One small brain area can disrupt sleep.
Sleep and dreaming · sleep disorders

Managing insomnia

  • Relaxation techniques (deep breathing, muscle relaxation, meditation) activate the parasympathetic nervous system, slowing heart rate and breathing so sleep comes more easily.
  • Sleep hygiene improves the physical environment: blackout blinds, a quiet, cool, tidy room, devices out of sight, the clock turned away.
  • This describes what psychologists study, not personal advice. Long-lasting sleep problems are for a GP to assess.

Mission check

It can be any of the three, and they often combine. Stress arouses the body through the sympathetic nervous system, a noisy or bright bedroom makes sleep harder, and caffeine, screens, pain or illness all play a part.

Mission 4

Freud thought every dream is a disguised wish. Disguised from whom?

Make a guess: Freud thought every dream is a disguised wish. Disguised from whom?

Sleep and dreaming · Freud's theory

The unconscious mind

  • Freud said most of the mind is unconscious: thoughts, memories and desires you are not aware of.
  • Repression pushes disturbing or unacceptable wishes out of the conscious mind into the unconscious.
  • Repressed wishes don't disappear. They push to be expressed.
  • Freud called dreams "the royal road to the unconscious".
Sleep and dreaming · Freud's theory

Wish fulfilment: two layers of a dream

  • Wish fulfilment: Freud claimed every dream fulfils a wish, often a repressed one.
  • In sleep the mind's guard relaxes, so wishes can surface.
  • A raw wish would shock the dreamer awake, so it is disguised. Freud called dreams the guardians of sleep.
  • Manifest content: the dream as you remember it, its story, people and places.
  • Latent content: the hidden meaning underneath, the real wish.
Sleep and dreaming · Freud's theory

Dreamwork

Dreamwork turns the latent content into the manifest content:

  • Condensation: several ideas are squeezed into one image.
  • Displacement: a feeling is moved onto a less important person or object.
  • Symbolism: the wish appears as a symbol.
  • Secondary elaboration: the mind stitches the pieces into a story.

In dream analysis, the therapist works backwards from manifest to latent content.

Key study Sleep and dreaming · Freud's theory

Freud (1918) the Wolfman

Aim
To use dream analysis to find the childhood cause of a patient's mental health problems.
Method
Case study of one wealthy young Russian man, treated with psychoanalysis from 1910 to 1914. Freud analysed a dream he recalled from early childhood.
Results
Manifest content: his bedroom window opened, and six or seven white wolves were sitting in a walnut tree outside. Terrified of being eaten, he woke screaming.
Conclusion
The latent content was a repressed memory of seeing his parents having sex as a very young child; the wolves stood for his father.
Strength
Very detailed data from about four years of analysis.
Weakness
The interpretation is subjective; the patient later called it improbable.
Quick check
?Maya dreams she misses a train that leaves without her. Her analyst says the dream really shows a hidden wish to leave home. In Freud's terms, the hidden wish is the dream's…
Sleep and dreaming · Freud's theory

The problem of subjectivity

  • Subjectivity means a judgement is shaped by a person's own views, feelings or expectations.
  • Only the analyst decides what the latent content is. Two analysts could find two different wishes in the same dream.
  • Freud expected to find childhood sexual wishes, and he found them. The Wolfman disagreed with Freud's interpretation.
  • An objective method would give the same result whoever applied it.

Mission check

?Freud thought every dream is a disguised wish. Disguised from whom?

From you. Freud said the wish is repressed because your conscious mind would find it disturbing, so dreamwork disguises the latent content as a harmless-looking manifest story. That way the wish is fulfilled without waking you: the dream guards your sleep.

Mission 5

Brain busy, body paralysed: why does REM sleep turn into a story?

Make a guess: Brain busy, body paralysed: why does REM sleep turn into a story?

Sleep and dreaming · activation-synthesis

The brain in REM sleep

  • In REM sleep brain activity is close to waking levels, the eyes move rapidly and most vivid dreams happen.
  • Hobson and McCarley (1977) proposed the activation-synthesis theory: dreams are a by-product of brain activity in REM sleep.
  • Dreams have no hidden message. They come from the brain trying to make sense of its own activity.
Sleep and dreaming · activation-synthesis

Activation

In REM sleep three things happen at once:

  • Random activation: neurons in the pons, in the brain stem, fire randomly, sending signals up to the higher brain.
  • Sensory blockade: sensory signals are blocked.
  • Movement inhibition: signals to the muscles are blocked, so the body is paralysed and can't act out the dream.
  • The limbic system is also activated, giving dreams strong emotions such as fear.
Sleep and dreaming · activation-synthesis

Synthesis

  • The cerebral cortex receives the random signals and tries to make sense of them.
  • It synthesises them, combining them with stored memories into a story: the dream.
  • Because the input is random, dreams are often bizarre: sudden scene changes, impossible events, odd mixtures of people.
  • Any meaning is added by the cortex as it builds the story, not hidden in advance.
Key study Sleep and dreaming · activation-synthesis

Williams et al. (1992)

Aim
To test activation-synthesis by comparing how bizarre dreams are with waking fantasies.
Method
Natural experiment: 12 psychology students at Harvard (10 women, 2 men) kept home journals. 60 dream and 60 fantasy reports were split into sentences and scored for bizarreness by three judges who didn't know which were dreams.
Results
Bizarreness was about twice as common in dreams as in fantasies, most of all plot discontinuity (sudden story changes).
Conclusion
Dreams are more bizarre than waking thought, supporting the idea that the cortex makes sense of random REM activity.
Strength
Blind judges and a set scoring system made scoring more objective.
Weakness
Home reports may have been forgotten or edited, and 12 students is a small sample.
Quick check
?Leo dreams that a dog is chasing him, but his legs won't move however hard he tries. Which part of the theory best explains why he can't move?
Sleep and dreaming · activation-synthesis

Reductionism and holism

  • Reductionism explains behaviour by breaking it into its simplest parts. Activation-synthesis reduces dreams to neurons firing in the pons and cortex.
  • Strength: those parts can be measured with EEG and brain scans, so the theory is testable.
  • Weakness: it ignores meaning, emotions and the dreamer's life, which may explain why dreams reflect real worries.
  • Holism looks at the whole person: brain activity plus experiences and feelings.

Mission check

?Brain busy, body paralysed: why does REM sleep turn into a story?

In REM sleep, neurons in the brain stem fire randomly while the senses are blocked and the muscles are paralysed. The cerebral cortex tries to make sense of this random activity by combining it with stored memories, and the story it builds is the dream, often bizarre because its input was random.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Sleep keeps the brain healthy, repairs the body and supports emotional stability.
  • A complete sleep cycle has five stages: stages 1 to 4 (light to deep) and REM, when most dreaming occurs.
  • A cycle lasts about 90 minutes and repeats 4 to 5 times a night, with more deep sleep early and more REM later.
  • The sleep–wake cycle is a circadian rhythm of about 24 hours, kept going by endogenous pacemakers such as the SCN in the hypothalamus.
  • The pineal gland releases melatonin as it gets dark, making you sleepy; levels are high at night and low in the morning.
  • Exogenous zeitgebers, above all light, reset the endogenous clock each day.
  • Insomnia means regularly having trouble getting to sleep or staying asleep, with tiredness during the day.
  • Stress (through the sympathetic nervous system), the bedroom, lifestyle and health can all cause it, often together.
  • Application: relaxation calms the nervous system and sleep hygiene improves the bedroom; damage to the hypothalamus can disrupt the body clock and wakefulness.
  • Freud: dreams fulfil repressed wishes from the unconscious, disguised by dreamwork so they don't wake you.
  • Manifest content is the remembered story; latent content is the hidden wish that dream analysis tries to uncover.
  • Freud (1918) interpreted the Wolfman's wolf dream as a repressed childhood memory; the main criticism is subjectivity.
  • Activation-synthesis (Hobson and McCarley, 1977): in REM sleep, random activation from the brain stem, sensory blockade and movement inhibition happen together.
  • The cortex synthesises the random signals with memories into a story, which is why dreams are often bizarre.
  • The pons and limbic system drive activation; Williams et al. (1992) found dreams about twice as bizarre as fantasies; the theory is reductionist.
