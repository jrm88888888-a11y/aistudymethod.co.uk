Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Make a guess: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Mission 1

Enzymes are catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst.
  • A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
  • It is not used up: it leaves the reaction unchanged, so one molecule catalyses the same reaction again and again.
  • The reaction can happen without the enzyme, but at body temperature it would be far too slow.
  • Benefit: reactions in cells run fast enough to sustain life.
  • Say: "speeds up a reaction" — not: "makes a reaction possible".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how an enzyme acts as a catalyst in a cell?
Mission 1

Metabolism: a web of reactions

  • Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism — a network of linked pathways.
  • Almost every step is catalysed by its own enzyme. So an organism needs thousands of different enzymes.
  • Control the enzyme → control the step. That is how cells regulate metabolism.
Mission 1

Lowering the energy barrier

  • To react, bonds in the substrate must first be broken. That needs energy: the activation energy.
  • An enzyme gives the reaction a route with a lower activation energy.
  • At the same temperature, more substrate molecules now have enough energy to react. So the rate rises.
  • The energy of the substrate and of the products is unchanged. So is the energy released overall.
  • The enzyme supplies no energy.
  • Say: "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" — not: "the enzyme provides the activation energy".
Mission 1

  • Activation energy = peak − substrate level. Not the peak value read from the axis.

Mission 1

The active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: one or more polypeptide chains folded into a compact 3D shape (tertiary structure).
  • The active site is a small pocket or groove where the substrate binds.
  • Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions between R groups fix the shape of the active site.
  • The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site; its charged groups are attracted to opposite charges there.
  • Substrate + active site → enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme free again.
  • Say: "complementary shape" — not: "the same shape".
Mission 1

Specificity

  • Each enzyme catalyses one reaction, or a few very similar ones: specificity.
  • Reason: only a molecule whose shape and charges suit the active site can bind.
  • Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Amylase hydrolyses starch but not cellulose.
  • The glucose units are linked differently (α-glucose in starch, β-glucose in cellulose), so cellulose does not fit amylase's active site.
  • Size alone does not decide: many small molecules do not bind.
  • Say: "the active site is complementary to one substrate" — not: "any molecule small enough fits".
Quick check
?Lipase catalyses the hydrolysis of triglycerides but has no effect on starch. What is the reason?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical bacterium, enzyme E catalyses the conversion of compound A to compound B. The conversion of A to B releases energy. A mutation stops the bacterium from making enzyme E. No other enzyme in the bacterium acts on compound A. Which prediction about compound B in the mutant bacterium is best supported?

An enzyme changes the route, not the destination: a lower barrier, the same start and finish, and the enzyme itself unchanged.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Make a guess: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Mission 2

Meeting by chance

  • Molecules in solution are in constant, random motion.
  • A reaction can only happen when a substrate collides with the active site, the right way round.
  • Enzymes do not seek, pull or grab their substrates.
  • More collisions per second → more enzyme–substrate complexes → faster rate (used again in Mission 3).
  • Say: "substrate and active site collide by random motion" — not: "the enzyme attracts its substrate".
Mission 2

Model 1: lock and key

  • Proposed by Emil Fischer, 1894.
  • The active site is a rigid shape. Only a substrate of exactly complementary shape fits, like one key in one lock.
  • Strength: explains specificity.
  • Limits: a rigid site does not explain how bound substrate is strained into reacting; and later evidence showed many enzymes change shape when they bind.
Mission 2

Model 2: induced fit

  • Proposed by Daniel Koshland, 1958.
  • The active site is flexible. As the substrate binds, the active site changes shape and moulds closely around it.
  • Both the enzyme and the substrate change shape as they bind.
  • The tight fit strains bonds in the substrate, which lowers the activation energy.
  • Enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme returns to its original shape.
  • Say: "the active site changes shape as the substrate binds" — not: "the active site is a rigid exact match".
Quick check
?According to the induced-fit model, what happens when a substrate binds to the active site of an enzyme?
Mission 2

Inside or outside the cell?

  • Intracellular enzymes act inside the cell that made them.
  • e.g. catalase breaks down toxic hydrogen peroxide inside cells.
  • Extracellular enzymes are made inside cells, secreted, and act outside them.
  • e.g. amylase (in saliva and pancreatic juice) digests starch; trypsin (from the pancreas) digests protein in the small intestine.
  • Say: "extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted" — not: "made outside cells".
Quick check
?Trypsin is made by cells in the pancreas and digests proteins in the small intestine. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Hexokinase is the enzyme that adds a phosphate group to glucose. X-ray structures show that the two halves of the enzyme are further apart when no glucose is bound. When glucose binds, the two halves close around it. What does this evidence show?

Not a rigid lock: the enzyme and its substrate adjust to each other, and that close, strained fit is part of what speeds the reaction.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does warming an enzyme speed it up, until suddenly it hardly works at all?

Make a guess: Why does warming an enzyme speed it up, until suddenly it hardly works at all?

Mission 3

Measuring the rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time.
  • Measure product appearing (e.g. oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearing (e.g. starch, with iodine).
  • A product–time curve is steep at first, then flattens as substrate is used up.
  • Initial rate: the gradient at time zero, before the substrate runs down. The fairest way to compare conditions.
  • Timing a fixed end point (a colour change, a disc rising)? Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
Mission 3

Worked example: initial rate from a tangent

Illustrative data: oxygen released by catalase, plotted against time.

1. Draw a straight line touching the curve at t = 0: the tangent.

2. Pick two points far apart on the tangent: (0 s, 0) and (40 s, 36).

3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 36 ÷ 40.

  • Initial rate = 0.90 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Same method at any point on a curve: tangent there, then gradient.
  • Fixed end point: a disc took 25 s to rise. Rate = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was recorded: 0 s, 0 cm³; 20 s, 12 cm³; 40 s, 20 cm³; 60 s, 24 cm³ (illustrative data). What is the best estimate of the initial rate of reaction from these data?
Mission 3

Temperature

  • Warmer → molecules have more kinetic energy → they move faster.
  • More frequent collisions between substrate and active sites, and more with enough energy → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second → faster rate.
  • Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure.
  • The active site changes shape; the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.
  • Denaturation is usually permanent: cooling does not restore the shape.
  • In the cold, enzymes are slow, not denatured.
  • Optima differ: many human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; DNA polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium ≈ 75 °C.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Mission 3

Q10, the temperature coefficient

  • Q10 = R2 ÷ R1: the rate at (T + 10 °C) divided by the rate at T.
  • Illustrative: rate 0.42 at 20 °C; 0.86 at 30 °C. Q10 = 0.86 ÷ 0.42 = 2.0. No units.
  • Below the optimum, Q10 for an enzyme-controlled reaction is often about 2: the rate roughly doubles per 10 °C.
  • Above the optimum: rate 1.50 at 40 °C; 0.45 at 50 °C. Q10 = 0.30. Below 1: denaturation.
  • Plants, most animals and microorganisms have body temperatures that follow their surroundings. Warmer → faster enzyme reactions, growth and development, up to the optimum; beyond it, slower. So climate change alters their activity and life cycles.
Quick check
?A student measured the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at six temperatures. The rates, in arbitrary units, were: 10 °C, 2; 20 °C, 4; 30 °C, 8; 40 °C, 15; 50 °C, 9; 60 °C, 1 (illustrative data). Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission 3

pH

  • pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺).
  • H⁺ ions change the charges on R groups (e.g. –COO⁻ + H⁺ → –COOH).
  • Ionic and hydrogen bonds change → the active site's shape and charges change → substrate binds less well.
  • Far from the optimum the enzyme is denatured.
  • Optima differ: pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.
  • Say: "pH changes the charges on R groups" — not: "pH slows the molecules down".
Quick check
?Pepsin has an optimum pH of about 2. When it passes with food into the small intestine, where the pH is about 8, its activity falls almost to zero. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

Substrate and enzyme concentration

  • More substrate: more collisions with active sites → faster rate.
  • At high substrate concentration, every active site is occupied almost all the time: the enzyme is saturated. The rate levels off; enzyme concentration now limits it.
  • More enzyme: more active sites → rate rises in proportion, as long as substrate is in excess.
  • A rate–concentration plateau (saturation) is not the same as a product–time curve flattening (substrate used up).
  • Say: "the active sites are saturated" — not: "the enzyme is used up".
Quick check
?At high substrate concentrations, the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction levels off even when more substrate is added. Why?
Mission 3Practical

Rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction

  • CP4: enzyme and substrate concentration on initial rates. CP12: temperature on initial rate, to include Q10.
  • Method (catalase and hydrogen peroxide): put substrate and enzyme in separate tubes in a water bath to reach the set temperature.
  • Mix; collect oxygen in a gas syringe; read the volume every 10 s.
  • Repeat at five or more values of the independent variable, three times each.
  • Plot volume against time; tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate.
  • Control: temperature (water bath), pH (buffer), volumes and concentrations not being tested. Control tube: boiled enzyme.
Mission check

?An enzyme from a cold-water fish has an optimum temperature of about 15 °C. A sample was kept at 45 °C for 10 minutes and then cooled back to 15 °C. Its activity at 15 °C was only 5% of that of an unheated sample tested at the same time (illustrative data). Which explanation is correct?

Heat speeds enzymes by speeding collisions, until it shakes apart the bonds that shape the active site. Then the rate falls, and usually stays down.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 4

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
Quick check
?A cut in the skin heals as new skin cells are produced by cell division. Why must DNA replication take place before each of these divisions?
Mission 4

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand in condensation reactions, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug inhibits helicase but has no effect on DNA polymerase. What will happen to DNA replication in a cell treated with the drug?
Mission 4

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?During replication, a section of a template strand with the base sequence TACGGA is copied. Which statement describes the new strand built on this section?
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Make a guess: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Mission 5

Three possible models

  • Conservative: the parent molecule stays whole; the copy is all new.
  • Semi-conservative: each new molecule has one parent strand + one new strand.
  • Dispersive: old and new DNA are mixed in pieces along every strand.
  • In 1958, Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment that gave each model a different prediction.
Mission 5

The experiment

  • Bases contain nitrogen. ¹⁵N is a heavy isotope; ¹⁴N is the normal, light one.
  • E. coli were grown for many generations on ¹⁵N: all their DNA became heavy.
  • They were moved to a ¹⁴N medium. Every new strand made after that is light.
  • Samples were taken after each generation (one round of replication).
  • DNA was extracted and spun in a caesium chloride density gradient.
  • DNA settles where its density matches the solution: heavier DNA settles lower.
Mission 5

Read the data

Generations in ¹⁴NBands seen
0one band: heavy
1one band: intermediate
2two bands, equal amounts: intermediate and light
  • Intermediate (hybrid) DNA = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand.
  • Light DNA = two ¹⁴N strands.
  • Read each row as a test. Which models survive it?
Quick check
?Bacteria were grown for many generations with only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), so all their DNA was heavy. They were then moved to a medium with only light nitrogen (¹⁴N). After one round of replication, all the DNA had a density midway between heavy and light. After a second round, half the DNA was of this intermediate density and half was light. What do these results show about replication?
Mission 5

Which model fails, and when

  • Conservative predicts generation 1: two bands, heavy and light. Seen: one intermediate band. Refuted at generation 1.
  • Dispersive predicts generation 1: one intermediate band. That fits.
  • But dispersive predicts generation 2: still one band, lighter than intermediate. Seen: two bands. Refuted at generation 2.
  • Semi-conservative predicts both results. It is supported.
  • New data supported the accepted theory and refuted the competing theories.
  • Say: "the results support the semi-conservative model" — not: "the results prove it".
Mission 5

Worked example: after n generations

Start: one ¹⁵N molecule. Grow it in ¹⁴N.

  • Molecules after n generations = 2ⁿ.
  • The two original ¹⁵N strands never break up. Each ends up in one hybrid molecule.
  • So there are always exactly 2 hybrid molecules; all the rest are light.

Quick check
?A double-stranded DNA molecule in which both strands contain only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) is replicated three times in a medium that supplies only light nitrogen (¹⁴N), producing 8 DNA molecules. How many of the 8 DNA molecules contain any ¹⁵N?
Mission 5

Judging the evidence

  • Each model made a different, testable prediction before the results were known.
  • The bands were sharp and clearly separated.
  • Pure ¹⁵N and pure ¹⁴N DNA gave reference positions for heavy and light.
  • Generation 2 was essential: generation 1 alone could not rule out dispersive.
  • The method and results were published in full (1958), so others could check and repeat them.
  • A limit: it was done in one bacterium. Other studies were needed to extend it to eukaryotes.
Quick check
?In the Meselson–Stahl experiment, which result after one generation in ¹⁴N would have supported the conservative model?
Mission check

?The experiment is run in reverse. Bacteria are grown for many generations on light nitrogen (¹⁴N), then moved to a medium containing only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N). Replication is semi-conservative. What will the DNA show after two generations on ¹⁵N?

Change the labels and the logic still works. Each old strand survives intact, paired with a new partner, in every generation.

Mission 6

What is a gene mutation?

  • A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Many arise as errors during DNA replication that are not corrected.
  • The changed DNA is transcribed into changed mRNA. That may change the polypeptide.
  • Larger changes, to whole chromosomes, are chromosome mutations.
Mission 6

Three ways to change a sequence

  • (1) Substitution: one base replaced by another. Same length.
  • (2) Deletion: a base removed. One shorter.
  • (3) Insertion: a base added. One longer.
  • Compare the sequences base by base, and count their lengths.
Quick check
?Part of the base sequence of a gene is GAT TCA GGC ATT before a mutation and GAT TCG GCA TT after it. Which type of gene mutation has occurred?
Mission 6

A substitution: three possible outcomes

  • A substitution changes one codon only. The reading frame is untouched.
  • Outcome 1: the new codon codes for the same amino acid. No change. The code is degenerate.
  • Outcome 2: the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid changes.
  • Outcome 3: the new codon is a stop codon. The polypeptide is cut short.
  • You may also see: silent (1), missense (2), nonsense (3).
Mission 6

Worked example: four mutations, one gene

  • Code table: AUG Met · GCA, GCU Ala · UGG Trp · AAG Lys · GAG Glu · CUU Leu · CAU His · GGA Gly · AGC Ser · UGA stop.
  • Normal mRNA: AUG GCA UGG AAG CUU → Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu.
  • (a) GCA → GCU: Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu. No change.
  • (b) AAG → GAG: Met–Ala–Trp–Glu–Leu. One amino acid changed.
  • (c) UGG → UGA: Met–Ala–stop. Two amino acids only.
  • (d) Delete the G of codon 2: AUG CAU GGA AGC UU… → Met–His–Gly–Ser… Every codon after the deletion changes.
Quick check
?In mRNA the codons GCU, GCC, GCA and GCG all code for alanine. A base substitution in a gene changes the codon GCU in its mRNA to GCC. What is the effect on the polypeptide?
Mission 6

Deletions and insertions: the frame shifts

  • The ribosome reads in threes from the start codon. There are no gaps or commas.
  • Delete one base and every later base moves up one place: a frameshift.
  • Inserting one base does the same.
  • Every downstream codon is regrouped. Codons before the mutation are unchanged.
  • A new stop codon usually appears early. The polypeptide almost always stops working.
  • Three bases added or removed: the frame is kept. One amino acid is gained or lost (a neighbour may change too).
  • Say: "frameshift: all downstream triplets change" — not: "one base, one amino acid".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, strain A has a deletion of one nucleotide in codon 10 of a gene that has 300 codons. Strain B has a deletion of the three adjacent nucleotides that make up codon 10 of the same gene. Which prediction about the polypeptides translated from the two genes is best supported?
Mission 6

A real point mutation: sickle cell

  • Gene: β-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin.
  • DNA coding strand: GAG → GTG. mRNA: GAG → GUG.
  • Amino acid 6: glutamic acid (charged) → valine (non-polar).
  • At low oxygen, these haemoglobin molecules stick together into long fibres.
  • Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, rigid, and block capillaries.
  • One base out of hundreds. Two copies of this allele: sickle cell anaemia.
  • Cystic fibrosis is a contrast: it can result from any one of many different mutations in one gene.
Quick check
?In the allele that causes sickle cell anaemia, the mRNA codon for amino acid 6 of the beta chain of haemoglobin is GUG instead of GAG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG codes for valine. What is the effect of this mutation on the protein?
Mission 6

Harmful, neutral or beneficial?

  • Harmful: the protein no longer works, or works worse.
  • Neutral: no detectable effect on the organism. Silent mutations are usually neutral.
  • Beneficial: the new protein gives an advantage.
  • The environment decides: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic helps only where that antibiotic is present.
  • Say: "silent: same amino acid sequence" — not: "silent: no effect on the phenotype". That is neutral.
Mission check

?A gene codes for a polypeptide of 400 amino acids. A single base substitution changes the fifth codon of its mRNA from UAC to UAA. In the code table, UAC codes for tyrosine and UAA is a stop codon. What is the most likely effect?

One letter can do nothing, change one amino acid, or stop the whole chain. It depends on which codon it creates.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts: they lower the activation energy, leave the energy released unchanged, and are not used up.
  • The active site, held in shape by the whole tertiary structure, is complementary in shape and charge to one substrate: hence the enzyme–substrate complex and specificity.

Mission 2

  • Substrate and active site meet by random collisions; in induced fit the active site changes shape as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.
  • Intracellular enzymes act inside cells; extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted to act outside.

Mission 3

  • Temperature and substrate concentration raise the rate by increasing collisions, until denaturation (temperature) or saturation (substrate) takes over; pH acts on the charges that hold the active site's shape.
  • Compare initial rates, found from the tangent at time zero.
  • Q10 = R2 ÷ R1, about 2 below the optimum.

Mission 4

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.

Mission 5

  • Meselson and Stahl (1958): heavy (¹⁵N) DNA, then growth on ¹⁴N; one intermediate band after 1 generation, intermediate + light after 2.
  • Generation 1 refutes conservative; generation 2 refutes dispersive; semi-conservative predicts both.

Mission 6

  • Substitutions change one codon: same amino acid (degenerate code), one different amino acid, or an early stop. Deletions and insertions not in multiples of three shift the frame and change every downstream codon.
