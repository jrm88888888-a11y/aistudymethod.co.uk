Make a guess: Why do countries buy from abroad things they could make perfectly well at home?

Mission 1

Why countries trade

Benefits of international trade:

  • Increased competition: domestic firms face rivals, so they cut costs and innovate.
  • Lower prices for consumers and for firms buying inputs.
  • Greater choice: goods a country cannot make at all, and more varieties of those it can.
  • Acquisition of resources: oil, metals, foods a country lacks.
  • More foreign exchange earnings from exports.
  • Access to larger markets, so firms gain economies of scale.
  • More efficient resource allocation and production: resources shift into what each country does relatively well, so less is given up to get each good.
Mission 1

The costs of trade

  • Workers in import-competing industries can lose jobs: structural unemployment if skills do not transfer.
  • Dependence: on imports of food, energy or key parts; exposed to supply shocks.
  • Infant industries may struggle against established foreign firms.
  • Gains are often unequal: some groups gain a lot, others lose.
  • Environmental costs of transport and of production moved abroad.

Mission 1

Absolute advantage

  • Absolute advantage: producing more of a good than another country with the same resources.
  • Two countries have the same quantity of resources. Output if all of them go to one good (illustrative data):
Food (tonnes)Cloth (rolls)
Country A600300
Country B240240
  • A can make more food and more cloth: A has absolute advantage in both.
  • Careful with input tables (hours or workers per unit): there, fewer inputs per unit means absolute advantage.
  • So should A make everything itself? Not if it wants the most it can get.
Mission 1

Comparative advantage

  • Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
  • Output table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = output of Y ÷ output of X.
Opportunity cost of…1 tonne of food1 roll of cloth
Country A300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 cloth600 ÷ 300 = 2 food
Country B240 ÷ 240 = 1 cloth240 ÷ 240 = 1 food
  • Food is cheaper (in cloth given up) in A: A has comparative advantage in food.
  • Cloth is cheaper (in food given up) in B: B has comparative advantage in cloth.
  • With different opportunity costs, each country has a comparative advantage in one good.
  • Input table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = input per unit of X ÷ input per unit of Y.
  • Same idea for people: a surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still leaves the typing to the receptionist. Each hour typing costs the surgeon an hour of surgery.
  • Say: "lower opportunity cost" — not: "can make more", which is absolute advantage.

Mission 1

Terms of trade that benefit both

  • Terms of trade here: how many units of one good exchange for one unit of the other.
  • A cloth roll costs A 2 food to make at home and costs B 1 food.
  • So any rate between 1 and 2 food per cloth benefits both:
    • A pays less than 2 food for cloth; B gets more than 1 food for each roll it gives up.
  • Example at 1.5 food per cloth: A sends 150 food to B for 100 cloth.

Mission 1

Where does comparative advantage come from?

  • Factor endowments (Heckscher–Ohlin): a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively.
    • Plenty of labour → labour-intensive goods (garments, assembly).
    • Plenty of capital and skills → capital-intensive goods (aircraft, chemicals).
  • Climate and natural resources: land, minerals, sunshine, rainfall.
  • Technology and human capital: education, research, know-how.
  • History and scale: early starters gain experience and economies of scale.
  • Comparative advantage is dynamic: investment in skills and capital can change it over time.
Mission 1

Assumptions and limitations

The model assumes:

  • Constant opportunity costs (linear PPCs). In reality costs usually rise as a country specialises, so full specialisation is unlikely.
  • No transport costs and no trade barriers. Both can wipe out the gain.
  • Factors move freely between industries inside a country. In reality workers and machines are not easily switched.
  • Perfect knowledge, full employment, and only two countries and two goods.
  • Terms of trade fall between the two opportunity costs; where they settle decides who gains most.
  • Gains are totals: some groups inside each country lose.
Mission 1

Evaluate: how useful is comparative advantage?

It depends on…

  • Transport costs and barriers. Low costs make the gains real; high tariffs or freight cancel them.
  • Who gains. Total output rises, but workers in shrinking industries may lose unless they retrain or are compensated.
  • Risk of over-specialisation. Real case: ready-made garments earned over 80% of Bangladesh's export revenue in 2022–23. Strong comparative advantage, but heavy reliance on one industry.
  • Time. Today's comparative advantage may not be tomorrow's; some countries protect infant industries to build a new one.
  • What it misses. Much trade is between similar rich countries in similar goods (cars for cars), driven by economies of scale and variety.

Mission 2

Why trade patterns change

  • Pattern of trade: what a country exports and imports, and with whom.
  • It changes because of:
    • Comparative advantage shifts: skills, technology and investment change relative costs.
    • Emerging economies grow: new low-cost producers and fast-growing markets (China, India, Vietnam).
    • Trading blocs and bilateral agreements: trade grows inside a bloc; leaving one raises barriers.
    • Exchange rates: a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.
    • Income growth abroad: richer buyers want more services and high-value goods.
    • Transport and communication costs: lower costs make distant partners worthwhile.

Mission 2

The terms of trade

  • Terms of trade: the rate at which a country's exports exchange for its imports.
  • Measured as an index:
  • Terms of trade = (index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100
  • Base year = 100.
  • Index rises: an improvement (favourable movement): each unit of exports buys more imports.
  • Index falls: a deterioration (unfavourable movement): each unit of exports buys fewer imports.
  • Say: "the terms of trade improved" — not: "the trade balance improved". They are different measures.
Mission 2

Worked example: calculating the index

Illustrative data:

YearExport price indexImport price indexTerms of trade

Year 2115104115 ÷ 104 × 100 = 110.6
Year 3112120112 ÷ 120 × 100 = 93.3
  • Year 2: export prices rose faster than import prices: an improvement.
  • Year 3: import prices overtook export prices: below 100, a deterioration on the base year.
  • From percentage changes: export prices +5%, import prices +12%.
  • Terms of trade = 105 ÷ 112 × 100 = 93.75: a deterioration of 6.25%.
  • Check: export prices always go on top.

Mission 2

What changes the terms of trade

Short run:

  • Exchange rate: an appreciation raises export prices in foreign currency and lowers import prices at home: the terms of trade improve. A depreciation worsens them.
  • Relative inflation: faster inflation at home raises export prices relative to import prices.
  • World commodity prices: changes in world demand or supply for oil, metals, food.

Long run:

  • Relative productivity: faster productivity growth cuts costs and export prices.
  • Incomes: as world incomes rise, demand for food and raw materials (low YED) grows more slowly than for manufactures and services.
  • Competition: low-cost producers in emerging economies push down world prices of many manufactures.

Real data: terms of trade index (2015 = 100):

201520202022
Australia (exports coal, gas, iron ore)100131.2181.0
Japan (imports most of its energy)100106.579.3
  • 2022: world energy and commodity prices surged. Exporters of them gained; importers lost.
  • By 2022 each unit of Australia's exports bought 81% more imports than in 2015. Each unit of Japan's exports bought about 21% fewer.

Mission 2

Impact: it depends on why

An improvement in the terms of trade:

  • Demand-led (world demand for the exports rose): higher export revenue, incomes and jobs; the current account improves; each export buys more imports, so living standards rise.
  • Cost-led (appreciation, high domestic inflation): exports lose price competitiveness. If demand is price elastic: revenue falls, jobs go, the current account worsens.

A deterioration:

  • Imports cost more: imported inflation and lower real incomes.
  • If caused by a depreciation, exports become cheaper abroad: export volumes rise and the current account can improve.
  • Whether it does depends on the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports: the Marshall–Lerner condition.
  • Primary exporters face a long-run risk: the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis says their terms of trade tend to fall against manufactures. Evidence is debated.
Quick check
?Country K's currency appreciates by 10%. Its export prices in foreign currency rise, and its import prices in its own currency fall. Demand for its exports is price elastic. Which outcome is most likely?
Mission 2

Evaluate: is an improvement good news?

It depends on…

  • Why it happened. Rising world demand for exports usually helps; higher domestic costs or an appreciation may hurt.
  • Price elasticities of demand for exports and imports.
  • Time. Trade volumes respond slowly, so short-run and long-run effects differ.
  • Openness. Where trade is a large share of GDP, the effects are larger.
  • What is imported. Dearer energy and food raise firms' costs and hit poorer households hardest.
  • Real case: Japan's index fell from 106.5 to 79.3 between 2020 and 2022: each unit of exports bought about 26% fewer imports.
Mission check

?Country C exports coffee (illustrative data). In the base year its export and import price indices are both 100. A year later its export price index is 90, its import price index is 108, and the volume of its coffee exports has risen by 30%. Which statement is correct?

The terms of trade compare prices, not earnings: a deterioration can come with higher export revenue, and an improvement with lower.

Mission 3

From no trade to free trade

  • Start with domestic demand and domestic supply only (no trade). Price settles at 7, quantity 50 (illustrative data).
  • Now open the border. A small country is a price taker: it can buy or sell any amount at the world price (Pw).
  • World price 4, below the domestic price:
    • consumers buy more: 80
    • domestic firms produce less: 20
    • the gap, 80 − 20 = 60, is imports.
  • Say: "domestic supply" — not just "supply": with trade, total supply at Pw is domestic output plus imports.
Quick check
?A country that did not trade in rice opens its market. The world price of rice is below its domestic no-trade price. Which outcome follows?
Mission 3

The protectionist toolbox

Protectionism: government measures that shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

  • Tariff: a tax on imports (per unit, or a percentage of value).
  • Quota: a legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported.
  • Subsidy: a payment to domestic producers, so they can compete with imports or sell more abroad (export subsidy).
  • Embargo: a ban on trade in a good, or with a country.
  • Administrative barriers (non-tariff barriers, "red tape"): standards, regulations, licences and slow customs checks that make importing harder.
Mission 3

The tariff diagram

Follow the diagram step by step (illustrative data):

1. Free trade at Pw = 4: domestic output 20, consumption 80, imports 60.

2. A tariff of 2 per unit: the price in the home market rises to Pw + tariff = 6.

3. Domestic output extends to 40; consumption contracts to 60; imports fall to 20.

4. Government revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff: the rectangle.

5. Two triangles: the deadweight (welfare) loss.

  • Producer side: high-cost domestic firms replace cheaper imports (wasted resources).
  • Consumer side: buyers who valued the good above the world price no longer buy it.
  • Say: "domestic supply extends" — not: "supply increases": the curve does not shift.
Mission 3

Worked example: counting a tariff's effects

Tariff of 2 per unit, Pw 4 → 6 (illustrative data):

Mission 3

Quotas

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, free-trade imports 60.

  • A quota of 20 units: once 20 units have come in, no more imports.
  • Supply in the home market = domestic supply + 20: draw S + quota, 20 units right of domestic supply, starting at Pw.
  • Price rises until demand meets S + quota: price 6, consumption 60, domestic output 40.
  • Same price and quantities as the tariff of 2, with one key difference:
    • the gap (6 − 4) × 20 = 40 is not government revenue.
    • It goes to whoever holds the import licences (domestic importers or foreign exporters): the quota rent.
    • If foreign exporters hold the licences, the 40 leaves the country: an extra loss to the home economy.
  • If demand grows, a quota lets the price rise further; a tariff lets imports grow at the same price.
Quick check
?A government replaces a tariff on imported shoes with an import quota. The quota is set so that the domestic price of shoes stays exactly the same. What changes?
Mission 3

Export subsidies

  • Export subsidy: a payment per unit sold abroad.
  • Exporters now earn the world price plus the subsidy on each export, so they will not sell at home for less.
  • Foreign rivals face cheaper competition, and their governments may retaliate.
  • Real case: WTO members agreed in Nairobi (2015) to eliminate agricultural export subsidies.
Mission 3

Embargoes, red tape and other barriers

  • Embargo: a total ban. Real case: in August 2014 Russia banned many food imports from the EU, USA and others.
  • Administrative barriers: product standards (safety, labelling, health) and regulations that imports must meet.
    • Real case: the EU bans beef from cattle given growth hormones, which shuts out much US and Canadian beef. The WTO ruled against the ban in 1998; the EU kept it.
  • Red tape: deliberately slow paperwork, checks and licences at the border.
  • Standards can protect health, safety and the environment. They become protectionism when they are designed to block imports rather than reduce real risks.
Quick check
?Country X requires all imported toys to be tested in its own laboratories, which takes six months. Domestic toys are tested within a week. There is no limit on numbers and no tax. Which tool of protection is this?
Mission 3

Real case: washing machines, 2018

  • In 2018 the USA put safeguard tariffs on imported large washing machines.
  • The tariff rate was higher once imports passed a set number of units each year.
  • Researchers (Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, 2020) found:
    • washer prices rose by nearly 12%
    • dryers, which were not taxed, rose by about the same amount: dryers are complements, often bought as a pair, and sellers raised both prices
  • Domestic brands raised their prices too. Counting washers and dryers together, buyers in the USA paid more than the tariff itself.
  • Lesson: a tariff's cost can spread into related markets.
Mission 3

Evaluate: how much harm does a tariff do?

It depends on…

  • Elasticities. The more price-elastic domestic demand and supply, the more imports fall and the larger the welfare-loss triangles.
  • Country size. A large importer can push the world price down, so foreign producers bear part of the tariff; a small country cannot.
  • Retaliation. Partners may answer with tariffs on this country's exports.
  • Time. Protected firms may invest and catch up, or grow complacent.
  • The tool. Quotas fix quantity, so rising demand pushes prices up; tariffs raise revenue; subsidies avoid higher prices but cost taxpayers.
  • Administration and evasion. Tariffs invite smuggling and lobbying for exemptions.
Mission check

?A small country puts a tariff on imported steel. It cannot affect the world price of steel. Which statement is correct?

In a small country, home buyers bear a tariff through higher prices; home firms and the government gain less than buyers lose.

Mission 4

Free trade: winners and losers

Free trade raises total output and consumption (comparative advantage). But:

  • Winners: consumers (lower prices, more choice), exporters, firms buying cheaper imported inputs.
  • Losers: firms and workers in import-competing industries.
  • Gains are spread thinly over millions of buyers; losses are concentrated in particular towns and industries.
  • Losers are easier to organise, so they lobby for protection.
Mission 4

For protection: new and strategic industries

  • Infant (sunrise) industry: a new domestic industry has high average costs, because it is small and inexperienced.
    • Established foreign firms produce at scale, at lower average cost.
    • Temporary protection lets the infant grow, gain economies of scale and learn, until it can compete at the world price.
    • Then the protection should be removed.
  • National security / strategic industries: food, energy, steel, defence equipment. Relying on imports is risky if trade is cut off by war or sanctions.
  • Say: "infant industry" — not "small firm": the argument is about costs falling as the industry grows.
Mission 4

For protection: unfair trade and standards

  • Dumping: selling exports below cost, or below the price charged at home, often supported by state subsidies.
    • Aim: win market share abroad and drive out rivals.
    • Anti-dumping duties are allowed under WTO rules, if dumping and injury are shown.
  • Unfair competition: rivals backed by state subsidies, or by very weak labour or environmental rules.
  • Health and safety: block unsafe food, medicines or toys.
  • Environmental standards: stop goods made in ways that cause serious pollution or harm wildlife.
  • These arguments are strongest when the risk is real; they are weakest when standards are a disguise for protecting home firms.
Mission 4

For protection: jobs, the balance of payments and revenue

  • Protecting jobs: slow the decline of sunset (declining) industries, giving workers time to retrain and limiting structural unemployment.
  • Balance of payments: tariffs switch spending from imports to home goods, reducing a current account deficit (at least for a time).
  • Government revenue: tariffs are easy to collect at the border. They matter most in low-income countries with weak income-tax systems.
Quick check
?A government notices that a foreign producer is selling tyres in its market at a price lower than the producer charges in its own country, and below its cost of production. Domestic tyre makers are losing sales. Which argument for protection fits best?
Mission 4

Against protection

  • Misallocation of resources: resources stay in industries where the country has no comparative advantage.
  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers. These hit poorer households hard, as they spend more of their income on goods.
  • Increased costs for firms that use imported inputs (steel for car makers, parts for electronics).
  • Inefficiency: protected firms lack the incentive to cut costs or innovate.
  • Retaliation: other countries answer with their own tariffs: a trade war that hurts exporters.
  • Reduced export competitiveness: dearer inputs and retaliation make exports harder to sell.
  • Protection can also break the trade rules a country has agreed to, inviting disputes.
Mission 4

Real case: steel tariffs and retaliation, 2018

  • In March 2018 the USA put tariffs of 25% on steel and 10% on aluminium imports, using a national security argument.
  • The EU, Canada, Mexico, China and others retaliated.
    • The EU's duties (June 2018) hit US whiskey, motorcycles and jeans: goods from industries unrelated to steel.
  • US steelmakers gained protection; US firms that use steel faced higher input costs.
  • The case shows three arguments at once: security (for), retaliation (against) and higher costs for users (against).
Quick check
?Country Z puts a tariff on imported steel to protect its steelworks. Z's car makers, which export most of their cars, buy steel as a key input. What is the most likely effect on Z's car industry?
Mission 4

Evaluate: free trade or protection?

It depends on…

  • The real problem. For security, health or pollution, a targeted tool (regulation, a subsidy, a tax on pollution) usually does less harm than a tariff on all imports.
  • Whether protection is temporary. Infant industries need a clear end date; many never "grow up", and firms lobby to keep protection.
  • Retaliation and size. A small country gains little leverage; a trade war can cost exporters more than protection saves.
  • Time. Protection can save jobs in the short run; the long-run costs of inefficiency build up.
  • Stage of development. Arguments for diversification and revenue are stronger for low-income economies.
Quick check
?A country's textile industry is declining because of cheaper imports, and its workers face long-term unemployment. The government's aim is to help those workers. Which policy targets this aim most directly?
Mission check

?A developing country puts a tariff on imported solar panels to help its new solar-panel industry grow. Which condition makes this infant-industry argument strongest?

The infant-industry case holds only if protection is temporary and the industry's costs really fall as it grows.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources. Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost.
  • Specialising by comparative advantage and trading at terms between the two opportunity costs lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.
  • The model rests on strong assumptions; the gains are real but unequally shared.

Mission 2

  • Trade patterns change with comparative advantage, emerging economies, trading blocs, exchange rates, incomes and transport costs.
  • Terms of trade = (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100; a rise is an improvement.
  • Whether an improvement is good news depends on its cause and on price elasticities.

Mission 3

  • Free trade at a world price below the domestic price: consumption rises, domestic output falls, imports fill the gap.
  • A tariff or quota raises the domestic price: home producers gain, consumers lose more, and there is a deadweight loss. Tariff revenue goes to the government; quota rent goes to licence holders.
  • Subsidies, embargoes, administrative barriers and (for some) currency manipulation are other tools, each with its own winners and losers.

Mission 4

  • Free trade raises total income, but its losses are concentrated, so pressure for protection is strong.
  • Arguments for protection: infant industries, security, dumping and unfair competition, health, safety and environmental standards, jobs, the balance of payments, revenue and (for low-income economies) diversification.
  • Arguments against: misallocation, higher prices, less choice, higher costs, inefficiency, retaliation, weaker exports. The case for protection depends on the problem, the tool, time and retaliation.
