Mission 1

When are you responsible?

  • Moral responsibility: being the right person to praise or blame for an act.
  • Two classic conditions:
  • Free will: the act came from you, not from force. (Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.1: an act done under compulsion or through ignorance is not fully voluntary.)
  • Knowing right from wrong: you could understand what you were doing and that it was wrong.
  • The law uses both: the M'Naghten Rules (1843) excuse someone who did not know the nature of the act, or did not know it was wrong.
  • The hard question: if every act has a cause, is any act really free?
Mission 1

Causes and three answers

  • Principle of causality: every event has a cause that makes it happen. Human choices are events.
  • Determinism: every event, including every choice, follows necessarily from prior causes.
  • Hard determinism: determinism is true, so free will is an illusion and no one is morally responsible in the full sense.
  • Soft determinism (compatibilism): determinism is true, but an act is still free if it flows from your own wants, not from force.
  • Libertarianism: some choices are not fixed by prior causes, so we are free and responsible (see Mission 2).
  • Incompatibilists (hard determinists and libertarians) agree that freedom and determinism cannot both be true; they disagree about which one is.
Mission 1Read the source

Locke's locked room

John Locke (1632–1704), An Essay concerning Human Understanding (1690), Book II, chapter XXI, section 10:

> "suppose a man be carried, whilst fast asleep, into a room where is a person he longs to see and speak with; and be there locked fast in, beyond his power to get out: he awakes, and is glad to find himself in so desirable company, which he stays willingly in"

> "I ask, is not this stay voluntary? I think nobody will doubt it: and yet, being locked fast in, it is evident he is not at liberty not to stay, he has not freedom to be gone."

  • The man feels he chooses to stay. But staying was the only possible outcome.
  • The Eduqas reading: our sense of free choice may be an illusion: we "choose" what was going to happen anyway.
  • Locke's own conclusion is narrower: liberty belongs to a person who has the power to act or not act; asking whether the will is free is, he says, "altogether improper" (II.xxi.14).

Q1 Why is the man's stay voluntary but not free?

Show answer

He wants to stay, so it is willing; but he has no power to leave.

Q2 How could a hard determinist use the example?

Show answer

Every choice may be like the man's: felt as free, though no other outcome was open.

Mission 1

Biological determinism

  • Biological (genetic) determinism: human behaviour is controlled by an individual's genes.
  • Evidence used: twin and adoption studies find that traits such as aggression and impulsivity are partly heritable.
  • MAOA gene: a Dutch family study (Brunner and colleagues, 1993) linked a rare MAOA mutation to impulsive aggression in several men of one family.
  • Courts have heard such evidence: in a 2009 Tennessee murder trial (Bradley Waldroup), MAOA evidence was presented and the jury returned a lesser verdict.
  • Critics: "linked to" is not "controlled by"; most people with these variants never become violent.
Mission 1

Pavlov: conditioned behaviour

  • Ivan Pavlov (1849–1936), Russian physiologist (Nobel Prize 1904, for work on digestion); Conditioned Reflexes (lectures, English translation 1927).
  • Classical conditioning: a dog salivates at food (an unconditioned response). A neutral stimulus (a metronome or a bell) is paired with food again and again.
  • In time the dog salivates at the sound alone: a conditioned response.
  • Psychological determinists extend this to humans: much behaviour is learned association, shaped by environment, not freely chosen.
  • J. B. Watson's "Little Albert" study (1920) conditioned fear in an infant: used to argue that emotions and habits can be conditioned.
  • Critics: humans reason, reflect and can resist conditioning; therapy can undo it.

Mission 1

Hobbes: internal and external causes

  • Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), Leviathan (1651), chapter 21; and Of Liberty and Necessity (1654).
  • Every act has a cause, back in a chain to God, "the first of all causes".
  • Liberty is "the absence of opposition", that is, of external impediments of motion.
  • An act from internal causes (your own desires and will) is free; an act blocked or forced by external causes (chains, walls, force) is not.

> "'Liberty' and 'necessity' are consistent, as in the water that hath not only 'liberty,' but a 'necessity' of descending by the channel"

  • So Hobbes is a soft determinist: necessity and freedom are both true of the same act.
Mission 1

Ayer: caused v forced

  • A. J. Ayer (1910–1989), "Freedom and Necessity" (1946; in Philosophical Essays, 1954).
  • The opposite of free is not caused but constrained (paraphrase).
  • Caused acts (free): I choose tea because I like it; the liking has causes, but nothing compels me.
  • Forced acts (not free): someone at gunpoint; a kleptomaniac who would steal whatever he decided; someone under another person's habitual domination.
  • Test (paraphrase): would the person have acted differently if they had chosen differently? If yes, the act is free.
  • So responsibility survives determinism: we blame free (caused) acts, not constrained ones.

Mission 1

If determinism is true: what follows?

  • The worth of right and wrong: hard determinists say moral words still describe acts as helpful or harmful, but not as deserving praise or blame.
  • Blaming moral agents: retributive blame looks unfair; blame and punishment may still be useful to change behaviour (deterrence, reform), which a determinist expects to work.
  • Normative ethics (utilitarianism, Kant, natural law): on hard determinism, theories cannot be freely chosen and followed; they may still act as causes of better behaviour.
  • Soft determinists keep all three: praise and blame fit acts that flow from a person's own desires.
  • Case: in 1924 the lawyer Clarence Darrow pleaded for the lives of Leopold and Loeb, two young murderers, partly by arguing that their acts were the product of heredity and upbringing. They were sentenced to life imprisonment, not death.
Mission 1Evaluate

Hard or soft determinism?

  • Claim: Soft determinism gives a better account of moral responsibility than hard determinism.
  • For: It fits ordinary practice: courts excuse constraint (duress, insanity), not every caused act.
  • For: It keeps determinism, which fits science (genes, conditioning, causality).
  • Against: Hard determinists reply that the soft view only moves the problem back: my desires are caused too; I did not choose them.
  • Against: Libertarians say "could have done otherwise if I had chosen" is not real freedom if I could not have chosen otherwise.
  • Against (soft determinism's critics): William James (1884) called it a "quagmire of evasion".
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view fit the evidence of science? Does it keep blame fair? Does it fit our experience of choosing?
  • Spec issue: "strengths and weaknesses of Hard and/or Soft Determinism".

Mission 2

Libertarianism

  • Libertarianism (in ethics; not the political view): humans have free will, and at least some choices are not fixed by prior causes.
  • It is incompatibilist: if determinism were true, we would not be free. Libertarians deny determinism holds for free choices.
  • Could have done otherwise: at the moment of choice, more than one future was really open.
  • The agent (the self) causes the choice; the choice is not simply the output of earlier events.
  • Evidence offered: our constant experience of deliberating and choosing; our practice of praise, blame and regret.
  • Consequence: people are fully responsible for their free acts.
Mission 2

Sartre: condemned to be free

  • Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980), French existentialist; Being and Nothingness (1943); Existentialism is a Humanism (lecture 1945, published 1946).
  • Existence precedes essence: there is no fixed human nature, and (Sartre the atheist argues) no God who designed one.
  • So we make ourselves by our choices; we cannot escape choosing. Even refusing to choose is a choice.
  • Humans are "condemned to be free" (Existentialism is a Humanism). In Being and Nothingness: we are not free to stop being free (paraphrase).
  • Eduqas wording: "man is not free not to be free".
  • Freedom brings total responsibility and anguish: we cannot blame God, nature, upbringing or "human nature".
  • Bad faith: pretending a role or label fixes what we are, to escape our freedom.
Mission 2Read the source

The café waiter

Sartre, Being and Nothingness (1943), Part One, chapter 2, "Bad Faith" (paraphrase):

  • A café waiter moves a little too precisely and too eagerly; he bends forward a little too politely; he carries his tray like a tightrope-walker.
  • He is playing at being a waiter, as if "waiter" were his fixed nature, like an inkwell is an inkwell.
  • This is bad faith (mauvaise foi): pretending we are a fixed thing, so that our role, not our freedom, decides what we do.
  • But he is not a waiter in the way a table is a table. He could walk out tomorrow.

Q1 What is the waiter pretending?

Show answer

That his role is his fixed nature, so he has no real choice.

Q2 Why does Sartre call this "bad faith"?

Show answer

It is self-deception: denying the freedom he cannot lose.

Mission 2

Sirigu: the brain and intention

  • Angela Sirigu, neuroscientist (CNRS, Lyon). Key study: Desmurget, Sirigu and colleagues, Science (2009).
  • Patients having brain surgery were stimulated in two areas while awake:
  • Parietal cortex → they felt a conscious wish to move (a hand, a foot); at stronger stimulation they believed they had moved, though they had not.
  • Premotor cortex → their limbs moved, but they denied having moved.
  • Reading used for libertarianism: the brain has a dedicated region for conscious intention, separate from mere movement; willing is a real process, not an illusion.
  • Contrast: Benjamin Libet (1983) found brain activity ("readiness potential") before people were aware of deciding; often used for determinism.
  • Caution: Sirigu's work locates intention in the brain; whether that shows the choice is free is disputed.
Mission 2

Rogers: becoming a person

  • Carl Rogers (1902–1987), American psychologist; On Becoming a Person (1961).
  • Humanistic psychology: a reaction against psychoanalysis and behaviourism (conditioning, as in Pavlov).
  • Every person has an actualising tendency: a drive to grow towards self-actualisation, becoming fully oneself.
  • Conditions of worth (love given only if we behave) distort us; unconditional positive regard, empathy and genuineness free us to grow.
  • The fully functioning person is open to experience and experiences himself or herself as free to choose (paraphrase).
  • Psychology here supports freedom: we are not only products of conditioning. Rogers debated the behaviourist B. F. Skinner on this in 1956.

Mission 2

Three views side by side

  • Hard determinism: all choices caused → no free will → no full moral responsibility.
  • Soft determinism (compatibilism): all choices caused → free when uncoerced → responsible for uncoerced acts.
  • Libertarianism: some choices not determined → free will real → fully responsible.
  • The deep question: does responsibility need could have chosen otherwise in the same situation (libertarian), or only acting on my own wants (compatibilist)?
Mission 2

Reward and punishment

  • Four aims of punishment: retribution (deserved), deterrence, reform, protection of the public.
  • Libertarian: offenders freely chose, so retribution is just; reward is deserved too.
  • Hard determinist: no one deserves punishment; deterrence, reform and protection can still be justified because punishment causes better behaviour.
  • Compatibilist: punish free (uncoerced) acts; excuse constraint: duress, insanity, compulsion.
  • The law mixes views: diminished responsibility (Homicide Act 1957, s.2) reduces murder to manslaughter where an abnormality of mental functioning substantially impaired the person.
  • Rewards follow the same logic: praise is deserved for libertarians, useful for hard determinists.

Mission 2

If we are free: what follows for morality?

  • The worth of right and wrong: moral words keep full force: acts are truly right or wrong, and agents truly praiseworthy or blameworthy.
  • Blaming moral agents: blame is deserved; so is moral credit for resisting temptation.
  • Normative ethics is useful: a free agent needs a method (Kant, utilitarianism, natural law, situation ethics) to choose well.
  • Sartre adds a twist: no theory can choose for you; picking a theory is itself a free choice you answer for.
  • Rogers: freedom grows with self-understanding, so responsibility can be developed, not just assumed.
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which free moral agents should follow a normative ethic".
Mission 2

Free will and religious belief

  • God and evil: the free will defence (Augustine; Plantinga): moral evil comes from creatures' free misuse of freedom, not from God.
  • Omnipotence: many theists say God freely limits his own power so that creatures can choose; critics ask whether this is still all-powerful.
  • Omnibenevolence: on this view love must be freely given, so a loving God gives freedom even at the risk of evil.
  • Prayer: petitionary prayer makes sense if the future is open; but can God answer "change his heart" without overriding someone's freedom?
  • Miracles: if God intervenes in the world, do free agents' choices remain free? Many believers say God acts through free choices, not against them.
Mission 2Evaluate

How free are we?

  • Claim: Humans have genuine free choice.
  • For: We experience deliberating between open options (Sartre); moral life of praise and regret assumes it.
  • For: Sirigu: conscious intention has its own brain basis.
  • For: Rogers: people can grow beyond conditioning.
  • Against: Feeling free may be an illusion (Locke's room).
  • Against: Libet's readiness potential: brain activity can begin before we are aware of deciding.
  • Against: Genes, environment and conditioning strongly shape behaviour (see Mission 1).
  • Against: "Uncaused" choices might be random, and randomness is not control.
  • Judgement criteria: Is introspection reliable evidence? Can science settle a philosophical question? What does responsibility actually need?

Mission 3

What predestination means

  • Predestination: the Christian teaching that God, before creation, determined the eternal destiny of human beings (salvation or loss).
  • It is religious determinism: the cause is God's will, not nature, genes or conditioning.
  • Key text:

> "For whom he foreknew, he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son … Whom he predestined, those he also called. Whom he called, those he also justified. Whom he justified, those he also glorified." (Romans 8:29–30, WEBBE)

  • Most Christian traditions accept that God predestines in some sense. They disagree about how: by choosing unconditionally (Augustine, Calvin) or by foreknowing who will freely believe (Arminius).
Mission 3

Augustine: humanity after the Fall

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), North African bishop; Confessions (c. 397–400), City of God (413–426), anti-Pelagian writings (412–430).
  • Original sin: Adam's sin passed to all his descendants:

> "as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned." (Romans 5:12, WEBBE)

  • Concupiscence: disordered desire, above all sexual desire no longer ruled by reason; through it sin is passed on.
  • Humanity is a massa peccati, "a lump of sin" (To Simplicianus I.2, 396): all justly deserve condemnation.
Mission 3

Augustine: free choice without freedom

  • Liberum arbitrium (free choice): human nature is still essentially "free"; we choose what we want.
  • Libertas (true liberty): the power to choose the good, lost to our sinful nature at the Fall.
  • Augustine's stages (based on On Rebuke and Grace 33; the three-stage formula is a later summary): Adam was able not to sin; fallen humans are not able not to sin; the saved in heaven will be not able to sin.
  • So only grace can restore libertas. Grace is unearned: God gives it to the Elect (also called the Saints), saved through Christ's atonement.
  • The rest are left, justly, in the mass of sin. God's choice does not depend on foreseen merit.
  • Augustine's prayer (Confessions X.29, Pilkington 1876): "Give what Thou commandest, and command what Thou wilt."

Mission 3Read the source

Calvin's definition

John Calvin (1509–1564), French Reformer in Geneva, Institutes of the Christian Religion (final edition 1559), Book III, chapter 21, section 5 (Beveridge translation, 1845):

> "By predestination we mean the eternal decree of God, by which he determined with himself whatever he wished to happen with regard to every man. All are not created on equal terms, but some are preordained to eternal life, others to eternal damnation"

Q1 What two groups does Calvin name?

Show answer

Those preordained to eternal life (the * Elect ) and those to damnation (the Reprobates *).

Q2 Where does the decision lie: with God or with the person?

Show answer

Wholly with God's eternal decree.

  • This is double predestination: God decrees both salvation and damnation. Calvin himself called it a "dreadful decree" (decretum horribile, III.23.7), but held it just.
Mission 3

Calvin's doctrine of election

  • The absolute power of God: nothing happens outside God's will and providence.
  • The corrupted nature of humans (often called total depravity): every part of human nature is corrupted by sin; no one can turn to God unaided.
  • Unconditional election: God's choice of the Elect rests on his will alone, not on foreseen faith or works.
  • Limited atonement: Christ died to save the Elect only, not all people; for them his death is fully effective.
  • Irresistible grace: when God calls the Elect, his grace cannot finally be refused.
  • Perseverance of the elect: the truly Elect cannot finally fall away.
  • These became the five points of the Synod of Dort (1618–19), against the Arminians; "TULIP" is a much later memory aid.

Mission 3

Predestination and moral responsibility

  • Worth of right and wrong: God's law still defines right and wrong; for Calvin it also guides the Elect in how to live.
  • Blaming moral agents: both Augustine and Calvin hold that sinners sin willingly: by necessity, but not by compulsion (Calvin, Institutes II.3.5, following Bernard of Clairvaux). So blame is just.
  • This is close to soft determinism (Hobbes): caused, yet voluntary.
  • Critics: if God decreed both the sin and the sinner's nature, blaming the sinner looks unfair.
  • Normative ethics: moral effort seems pointless if destiny is fixed. Calvinists reply that good works are the fruit and sign of election, not its cause.
Mission 3

Predestination and religious belief

  • God and evil: if God decrees the Fall and reprobation, is God the author of evil? Calvin denies it: humans sin by their own will, and God's purpose is just though hidden.
  • Omnipotence: strengthened: nothing escapes God's will.
  • Omnibenevolence: questioned: why not elect everyone? Critics cite 1 Timothy 2:4 (God "desires all people to be saved"). Calvin: all deserve condemnation; saving any is mercy.
  • Prayer: Calvin (Institutes III.20): prayer does not inform or change God; God commands it and uses it as a means of what he has decreed.
  • Miracles: God's providence governs every event; Calvin saw biblical miracles as signs confirming the gospel.
Mission 3Diversity

Christian responses

  • Catholic: the Council of Orange (529) affirmed grace but condemned predestination to evil; Trent (1547) taught that free will is weakened but not destroyed and cooperates with grace.
  • Orthodox: synergy: salvation is God's grace and human response working together; Orthodox theology generally does not accept inherited guilt in Augustine's sense.
  • Reformed (Calvinist): double predestination, e.g. the Westminster Confession (1646), chapter 3.
  • Lutheran: election to salvation, but no decree of reprobation (Formula of Concord, 1577).
  • Methodist and other Arminian traditions: election conditional on faith (see Mission 4).
  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Church Dogmatics II/2 (1942): Christ is both the Elect and the Rejected one, so election is God's "yes" to humanity in Christ.

Mission 3Evaluate

Should religious believers accept predestination?

  • Claim: Religious believers should accept predestination.
  • For: Scripture speaks of it (Romans 8:29–30; Ephesians 1:4–5).
  • For: Salvation is a gift: "by grace you have been saved through faith … not of works" (Ephesians 2:8–9, WEBBE); predestination keeps grace unearned and gives the Elect assurance.
  • For: It fits God's omnipotence and omniscience.
  • Against: Reprobation seems unjust and unloving; it clashes with 1 Timothy 2:4.
  • Against: It seems to make God the author of evil, and blame unfair.
  • Against: It may weaken moral effort, prayer and mission (Calvinists deny this).
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to scripture as a whole? Coherence with God's justice and love? Effect on Christian living?
  • Spec issues: "whether religious believers should accept predestination"; "the extent to which predestination influences our understanding of God".
Mission 3Worked answer

Linking teaching, source and development

Task (AO1): Explain Calvin's teaching on the Elect and the Reprobates.

  • Teaching: Calvin taught that by an eternal decree God chose some for eternal life (the Elect) and others for damnation (the Reprobates).
  • Source: Institutes III.21.5: "some are preordained to eternal life, others to eternal damnation".
  • Development: Because human nature is wholly corrupt, no one can choose God unaided; election is unconditional, grace irresistible, and the Elect persevere.
  • Link: This explains why Calvin denied that human merit plays any part, and why his followers at Dort rejected Arminius.
  • Why it works: teaching → exact source → technical terms → connection to the wider doctrine.

Mission 4

Pelagius and the quarrel

  • Pelagius (c. 354–c. 418), a monk from Britain who taught in Rome around 380–410.
  • He was shocked by moral laziness among Roman Christians, who excused sin as human weakness.
  • He objected to Augustine's prayer, "Give what Thou commandest, and command what Thou wilt" (Confessions X.29): it seemed to make goodness wait for God, not for human effort.
  • Key work: Letter to Demetrias (c. 413), written to a young Roman noblewoman who had vowed herself to God.
  • Augustine wrote against him for nearly twenty years (412–430).
Mission 4

Pelagius' teaching

  • Original sin: Adam's sin harmed humanity by bad example, not by passing on guilt or a corrupted nature. Babies are born innocent.
  • Humanity maturing in God's image: humans were created good, and grow towards God by using their freedom well.
  • Accepting the responsibility of free will: freedom to choose good or evil is God's gift and belongs to human nature; no one can blame nature for sin.
  • Free will used to follow God's laws: God's commands show what we are able to do.
  • Grace in salvation: grace is the gift of nature and free will itself, God's law, Christ's teaching and example, and forgiveness of past sins in baptism. It helps us do good more easily, but is not needed to make good choices possible (paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

Commanded to be perfect

> "Therefore you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect." (Matthew 5:48, WEBBE)

Pelagius, Letter to Demetrias 2 and 16 (paraphrase):

  • Before teaching how to live a holy life, he first shows the power of human nature, so that no one thinks the commands too hard.
  • God, who is just and good, would not command what humans cannot do; to say so is to blame God, not ourselves.

Q1 How would Pelagius use Matthew 5:48?

Show answer

God commands perfection, so humans must be able to pursue it by their own free will.

Q2 What modern principle is this close to?

Show answer

Kant's "ought implies can": a duty assumes we are able to do it.

Mission 4

Condemned, and why it matters

  • Council of Carthage (418): condemned the teaching that grace only makes good acts easier, and that infants need no forgiveness. Pope Zosimus confirmed it.
  • Council of Ephesus (431): condemned Pelagius' follower Caelestius.
  • Council of Orange (529): rejected also the milder view (later called semi-Pelagianism) that humans can make the first move towards God.
  • So "Pelagian" became a charge in later debates: Calvinists used it against Arminians (unfairly, Arminians say).
  • Some modern writers have argued that Pelagius was more orthodox than his opponents reported; much of his work survives only in quotations by his critics.
Mission 4

Arminius: against Calvin's decree

  • Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609), Dutch Reformed theologian; studied in Geneva under Calvin's successor Theodore Beza; professor at Leiden from 1603; Declaration of Sentiments (1608).
  • Denial of the Calvinist view of predestination: an unconditional decree of reprobation makes God the author of sin and contradicts his love and justice.
  • Effect of original sin on free will: unlike Pelagius, Arminius held that sin leaves the fallen will unable, by itself, to choose any true good or turn to God (paraphrase, Public Disputations XI).
  • So Arminius accepts the Fall's damage. His question is how grace works.
Mission 4

Prevenient grace and conditional election

  • Prevenient grace ("grace that comes before"): the Holy Spirit goes before every human response, freeing the will so that a person can respond to God.
  • The possibility of rejecting God's grace: this grace is resistible; people can refuse it.
  • Election conditional on faith: God elects to salvation those he foresees will believe in Christ and persevere.
  • So the Elect are believers: election is real, but it follows God's foreknowledge of faith, not a decree regardless of it.
  • After his death his followers (the Remonstrants, 1610) set out five articles; the Synod of Dort (1618–19) rejected them. John Wesley (1703–1791) later spread Arminian teaching through Methodism.

Four Christian thinkers compared

  • Original sin: Augustine and Calvin: inherited guilt and corruption · Arminius: inherited corruption; the will cannot turn to God unaided · Pelagius: no inherited guilt; bad example.
  • Free will after the Fall: Augustine: free choice, no liberty for good · Calvin: will in bondage to sin · Arminius: freed by prevenient grace · Pelagius: intact.
  • Grace: Augustine and Calvin: given to the Elect, effective · Arminius: offered to all, resistible · Pelagius: nature, law, example and forgiveness; makes good easier.
  • Election: Augustine and Calvin: unconditional · Arminius: conditional on foreseen faith · Pelagius: depends on one's choices.
  • Today: Calvinist and Arminian views both live in Protestant churches; Catholic and Orthodox teaching hold grace and free cooperation together, and all condemn Pelagianism.
Mission 4

Free will and moral responsibility

  • The worth of right and wrong: Pelagius gives moral words full force: we can obey, so failure is truly our fault. Arminius keeps real responsibility, because grace makes response possible.
  • Blaming moral agents: for Pelagius, there is no excuse for sin; for Arminius, those who reject grace are rightly blamed for their refusal.
  • Normative ethics: a free agent needs moral guidance; Pelagius' letters are full of practical rules for holy living.
  • Critics: Pelagius makes morality harsh (no excuse for weakness) and may lead to pride; Augustine said it empties the cross of Christ.
  • Prayer: Augustine objected that if nature and free will are enough to do good, there is no point in praying for God's help against temptation; for Arminius, prayer for grace still matters, because the will needs grace to respond.

Mission 4Evaluate

Can free will be reconciled with predestination?

  • Claim: Religious belief in free will can be reconciled with belief in predestination.
  • For: Arminius: God predestines those he foresees will freely believe ("whom he foreknew", Romans 8:29).
  • For: Boethius (c. 477–524), The Consolation of Philosophy V: God sees all time in an eternal present; knowing a choice does not cause it.
  • For: Catholic and Orthodox traditions hold grace and free cooperation together without contradiction.
  • Against: If God infallibly foreknows a choice, could it have been otherwise? Foreknowledge may still fix the future.
  • Against: Calvinists: conditional election makes humans, not God, decide salvation.
  • Against: Pelagius' full freedom leaves little room for predestination or the need for grace.
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to scripture? Coherence with God's omniscience? Does it keep salvation a gift and responsibility real?
  • Spec issues: "how convincing are religious views on free will"; "the degree to which free will makes the use of prayer irrelevant".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Responsibility needs free will and knowing right from wrong; determinism says every choice is caused.
  • Hard determinism (Locke's room as read by Eduqas; genes; Pavlov's conditioning): free will is an illusion. Soft determinism (Hobbes: internal v external causes; Ayer: caused v forced): caused acts can still be free.

Mission 2

  • Libertarianism: some choices are not determined; we could have done otherwise and are fully responsible.
  • Sartre (condemned to be free; the waiter's bad faith), Sirigu (parietal cortex and conscious intention), Rogers (self-actualisation).
  • Retribution needs desert (libertarian); deterrence, reform and protection fit any view.
  • Free will shapes how believers see evil, prayer and miracles.

Mission 3

  • Augustine: original sin and concupiscence make humanity a massa peccati; liberum arbitrium remains, libertas is lost; grace saves the Elect.
  • Calvin: God's eternal decree elects some and reprobates others; unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace, perseverance of the elect.
  • Predestination raises questions about blame, God and evil, God's love, prayer and miracles; Christian traditions answer differently.

Mission 4

  • Pelagius: no inherited guilt; humans mature in God's image by using free will to follow God's laws; grace (nature, law, Christ's example, forgiveness) makes good easier. Condemned 418, 431.
  • Arminius: sin leaves the will unable; prevenient grace (the Holy Spirit) frees it; grace can be rejected; election is conditional on foreseen faith.
  • Free will strengthens responsibility and God's goodness, but raises questions about grace, prayer and how it fits with predestination.
