Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do Hindus honour a warrior goddess for nine nights, then tie a thread on a brother's wrist?

Make a guess: Why do Hindus honour a warrior goddess for nine nights, then tie a thread on a brother's wrist?

Mission 1

Three festivals, one idea

  • Raksha Bandhan: the full moon of Shravana (July or August). Raksha = protection; bandhan = tie.
  • Navaratri ("nine nights", September or October) honours the warrior goddess Durga: divine power that protects.
  • Shared idea: a sacred thread as a bond of protection, backed by stories of the gods.
Mission 1

Raksha Bandhan customs

  • A sister ties a rakhi (decorated thread) on her brother's wrist, puts a mark (tilak) on his forehead and offers sweets.
  • She prays for his well-being; he promises to protect her and gives a gift.
  • Cousins and close friends become "rakhi brothers and sisters": the bond is chosen, not only by birth.
  • Priests may tie a protective thread on worshippers' wrists, with a mantra.
  • In parts of South India, Brahmin men renew their sacred thread on the same full moon.
  • Today some tie rakhis on sisters, soldiers, neighbours or even trees, to stress protection for all.
Mission 1Read the source

Two Raksha Bandhan myths

  • King Bali and Lakshmi (linked by Eduqas with the Vishnu Purana): the generous demon king Bali gives Vishnu (as the dwarf Vamana) three steps of land. Vishnu covers the universe, then, pleased with Bali's devotion, agrees to stay and guard his kingdom. Lakshmi misses her husband. On the full moon she ties a thread on Bali's wrist as a sister. When he offers a gift, she asks for Vishnu to come home, and Bali agrees. (paraphrase)
  • Indra and the demon (Bhavishya Purana): Indra, king of the gods, is losing a war against the demons. His wife Indrani (Sachi), advised by the gods' teacher Brihaspati, ties a thread blessed with mantras on his wrist. Protected, Indra wins. (paraphrase)

Q1 In the Bali story, who ties the thread, and on whom?

Show answer

Lakshmi, on Bali.

Q2 What does the thread do in the Indra story?

Show answer

It protects Indra in battle; it is tied by his wife, not a sister.

Quick check
?Which detail comes from the myth of King Bali and Lakshmi, not the myth of Indra?
Mission 1Diversity

Different Hindus, different emphases

  • Raksha Bandhan is family-centred; some Hindus stress its religious stories, others mainly the sibling bond. Some see it as celebrating a protective role for brothers; others note that sisters pray for and protect brothers too.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Festivals are the most important part of being a Hindu today."

  • For: festivals pass on stories and values to children; they gather families and communities; in Britain they keep identity strong.
  • Against: daily puja, dharma and good actions matter every day, not only at festivals.
  • Against: for those seeking moksha through knowledge or meditation, inner practice matters more than outward celebration.
  • Another view: festivals are where belief becomes action: they make everyday dharma visible.
  • Judge it by: What does a Hindu need most to live by dharma? Does it differ for a child, a householder and a sannyasin? Do festivals change behaviour after they end?
Mission check

?At a Hindu wedding in Leicester, the groom's mother ties a thread on her son's wrist before he leaves home, asking God to keep him safe. Which Raksha Bandhan story or custom does this action echo most closely?

A raksha is a tie of protection. A sister, wife, mother or priest may tie it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why would millions of Hindus travel to bathe in one cold river, when God is believed to be everywhere?

Make a guess: Why would millions of Hindus travel to bathe in one cold river, when God is believed to be everywhere?

Mission 2

A tirtha: a crossing place

  • Tirtha means a ford: a shallow place to cross a river.
  • A holy place is a crossing between this world and the divine, and between samsara and moksha.
  • Tirtha-yatra = a journey to a tirtha: pilgrimage.
  • Tirthas include rivers, mountains, temples, and places linked with the lives of deities or saints.
  • History: the Mahabharata already lists tirthas; the Puranas praise each place's power; tradition says the teacher Shankara (8th century) founded monasteries at four sacred points of India.
  • Pilgrimage is not compulsory for Hindus. It is a choice, often made after a vow or at a turning point in life.
Mission 2

Why go, and what happens there?

  • Darshan: to see, and be seen by, the deity in a holy place.
  • Purification: bathing in holy water to wash away bad karma.
  • Vows: thanks for an answered prayer, or a request for help.
  • Ancestors: rites (shraddha) for the dead; scattering ashes.
  • Moksha: some places are believed to bring liberation.
  • Practices: bathing, circling a holy place clockwise (parikrama), puja and arti, offerings, giving to the poor, fasting, singing bhajans, meeting holy people.
Mission 2

Ganga, the river goddess

  • Many Hindus honour the Ganges (Ganga) as a goddess: Ganga Ma, Mother Ganga.
  • Story: King Bhagiratha prays for Ganga to come down from heaven to purify his ancestors' ashes. Shiva catches her in his hair so her fall does not shatter the earth.
  • Bathing in her water is believed to wash away sins and bad karma.
  • Pilgrims take Gangajal (Ganges water) home in sealed pots, for puja, for the dying and for rites.
  • Many hope their ashes will be placed in the Ganges.
Mission 2

Varanasi (Kashi)

  • On the Ganges in North India; also called Kashi, "city of light", and Banaras. Shiva's city.
  • Ghats: stone steps down to the river. At dawn pilgrims bathe, pray and offer flowers and lamps.
  • Many believe that to die in Kashi, or be cremated there, brings moksha: Shiva whispers a liberating mantra to the dying.
  • Cremation at Manikarnika Ghat goes on day and night.
  • Each evening a great Ganga arti with lamps is offered at Dashashwamedh Ghat.

Mission 2

Vrindavan

  • A town on the Yamuna in the Braj region, where Krishna is believed to have spent his youth with the cowherds and Radha.
  • A Vaishnava centre: thousands of temples and shrines (e.g. Banke Bihari; ISKCON's Krishna-Balaram Mandir).
  • Pilgrims walk the parikrama around the town and around Govardhan Hill, which Krishna is said to have lifted to shelter the villagers.
  • Kirtan, Holi and Janmashtami are celebrated here with great devotion; pilgrims greet each other "Radhe Radhe".
  • Purpose: bhakti: to love Krishna where he lived, and to be among his devotees.
Mission 2Diversity

Vaishnava and Shaiva sacred sites

  • Vaishnava sites (Vishnu and his avatars): Vrindavan and Mathura (Krishna); Ayodhya (Rama); Puri (Jagannatha); Tirupati; Badrinath.
  • Shaiva sites (Shiva): Varanasi; the 12 jyotirlingas (e.g. Kedarnath, Somnath); Mount Kailash, Shiva's home.
  • Vaishnava pilgrimage often stresses bhakti: darshan, kirtan, the stories of the avatar's life.
  • Shaiva pilgrimage often stresses renunciation and liberation: sadhus, austerity, moksha in Kashi.
  • Shared: both honour the Ganges, said to flow from Vishnu's foot and through Shiva's hair; both crowd the Kumbh Mela.
  • Many pilgrims visit both kinds of site: one God, many forms.
Quick check
?A pilgrim walks barefoot around a sacred hill, singing the names of Krishna and Radha, then joins evening kirtan. Where is she most likely to be?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Pilgrimage is not important for Hindus today."

  • For: pilgrimage is not compulsory; God is believed to be everywhere, including in the heart and the home shrine.
  • For: some texts and teachers say the true tirthas are inner: truth, forgiveness, self-control. The Arya Samaj (a Hindu reform movement founded in 1875) rejects the idea that bathing removes sin.
  • For: cost, crowds, distance from Britain, and pollution of rivers.
  • Against: darshan in holy places is believed to be especially powerful; bathing, rites for ancestors and vows matter to millions.
  • Against: pilgrimage builds faith and community; British Hindus often visit sites when in India, or go to Bhaktivedanta Manor or Neasden.
  • Judge it by: Is the outer journey a help to the inner one, or a replacement for it? Does importance depend on the person's path (bhakti, jnana)? Is "not compulsory" the same as "not important"?
Mission check

?A Hindu grandmother in Leicester cannot travel to India. She keeps sealed Ganges water at her home shrine, sprinkles it during puja, and asks that her ashes be placed in the Ganges. Which belief best explains her choices?

A tirtha is a crossing. For many Hindus, Ganga makes the crossing possible wherever her water reaches.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some Hindus care for old cows that give no milk, and clean rivers they call mothers?

Make a guess: Why do some Hindus care for old cows that give no milk, and clean rivers they call mothers?

Mission 3

Why care for nature?

  • Brahman in all: many Hindus believe the divine is present in everything.
  • "All this, whatsoever exists in the universe, should be covered by the Lord." (Isha Upanishad 1, Paramananda 1919)
  • Atman in every living being: animals have souls too, moving through samsara.
  • Ahimsa (non-harm): avoid harming any living being; many Hindus are vegetarian.
  • Karma: harm to nature brings consequences.
  • Bhumi Devi: many honour the Earth as a goddess and mother; some touch the floor in apology before stepping on it each morning.
Mission 3

Rivers and sacred places

  • Rivers are honoured as goddesses: Ganga, Yamuna, Narmada, Godavari.
  • Sacred places include groves, hills (e.g. Govardhan), and plants such as tulsi (holy basil).
  • Problem: many sacred rivers are badly polluted by sewage, factory waste and plastic.
  • Action: clean-up drives on the ghats; campaigns to stop waste and offerings going into rivers; tree planting along banks; India's national programme to clean the Ganges (Namami Gange, 2014).
  • Chipko movement (1973): villagers in the Himalayan foothills hugged trees to stop them being felled.
  • It recalled the Bishnoi community, who in 1730 gave their lives to protect khejri trees.
Quick check
?Which Hindu belief most directly explains why many Hindus avoid harming animals?
Mission 3

Why the cow?

  • Many Hindus call the cow gau mata, "mother cow": she gives milk, as a mother does, and asks little back.
  • Ghee for lamps and havan; dung for fuel and plaster; oxen for ploughing.
  • Kamadhenu: the wish-fulfilling cow of the gods.
  • Krishna grew up as a cowherd: Gopala, "protector of cows".
  • So most Hindus will not eat beef, and many see cow killing as a grave wrong.
  • Not all Hindus agree on practice: not all are vegetarian, and some communities in India have traditionally eaten beef. In India, laws on cow slaughter differ by state, and the issue can be politically divisive.
Mission 3

Goshalas: homes for cows

  • Your spec spells it Goshallas.
  • A goshala is a shelter where cows and bulls are cared for for life, including old, sick and male animals that farms would send for slaughter.
  • Many are run by temples or charities; many Hindus give money to feed and house the cows.
  • Bhaktivedanta Manor (ISKCON, Hertfordshire): cows are never slaughtered; oxen plough the land; milk is used for offerings and food.
  • Significance: ahimsa in action; gratitude; a model of farming without killing.
  • Some Hindus go further and avoid dairy from ordinary farms, where male calves are often killed.
Mission 3

Vrindavan: caring for Krishna's forest

  • Vrindavan, in the Braj region, is where Krishna is believed to have grown up among forests and cowherds.
  • Its forests had been cut down and the Yamuna badly polluted.
  • The Vrindavan Conservation Project (from 1991, funded at first by WWF) planted trees along the parikrama path, restored sacred groves and taught pilgrims and schools to care for them.
  • A UK charity, Friends of Vrindavan, supported tree planting, waste clearance and cow care.
  • Religious motive: Vrindavan's forests are part of Krishna's sacred land, so restoring them is worship.
  • Pilgrims who walk the parikrama see the work: devotion and ecology together.
Quick check
?A temple in Britain spends money keeping a bull and three old cows that give no milk. Which reason would most Hindus involved give?
Mission 3Diversity

Different Hindu understandings

  • The world as sacred: if Brahman is in all things, harming nature harms the divine. This drives most Hindu projects.
  • The world as maya: some stress escape from the world (moksha) over caring for it; critics say this can make people less engaged.
  • Ritual and ecology: offerings, ashes and painted murtis put into rivers can add pollution; many now use clay murtis, collect flowers for compost, or scatter ashes with care.
  • Cow first, or all creatures? Some stress cow protection above all; others say ahimsa applies equally to every creature and to the whole Earth.
  • How far to go: vegetarian, vegan, or neither; some Hindus question modern dairy farming.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Hindu teachings require Hindus to protect the environment."

  • For: Brahman in all things; ahimsa; Earth as mother; rivers as goddesses; the cow as a model of gentle care.
  • For: karma: harm to nature brings consequences for the one who harms.
  • Against: many teachings stress liberation from the world (moksha) more than repairing it.
  • Against: ancient texts were not written about pollution or climate change; modern "ecology" readings are new interpretations.
  • Another view: dharma changes with the age; protecting nature is today's dharma.
  • Judge it by: Does "the divine in all" create a duty, or only an attitude? Can old texts guide new problems? What do Hindu practices show?
Mission check

?A Hindu youth group in Leicester spends a day collecting plastic from a riverbank and then holds a short puja by the water. Which statement best explains how most of them would see the day?

For many Hindus, caring for a cow or a river is not a hobby beside faith: it is faith in action.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does the Bhagavad Gita call someone who takes without giving back a thief?

Make a guess: Why does the Bhagavad Gita call someone who takes without giving back a thief?

Mission 4

Dana and sewa

  • Dana = giving: money, food, clothes, time or knowledge.
  • Sewa (seva) = selfless service: work for others with no reward.
  • Both are part of dharma, especially for householders (grihastha), who earn and so can give.
  • Both are karma yoga: action done as an offering, not for praise.
  • The Gita calls the best gift one given gladly, "when he who takes / Can render nothing back", at the right place and time (Bhagavad Gita 17.20, Arnold 1885).
  • Giving to gain status or with a grudge is a lower kind of giving.
Mission 4Read the source

The thief who gives nothing back

> "Spake Prajapati-- / In the beginning, when all men were made, / And, with mankind, the sacrifice-- 'Do this! / Work! sacrifice! Increase and multiply / With sacrifice! ... But if one eats / Fruits of the earth, rendering to kindly Heaven / No gift of toil, that thief steals from his world.'" (Bhagavad Gita 3.10–12, Arnold 1885)

  • Prajapati, the creator, made people together with yajna (sacrifice, offering).
  • Life is a cycle of giving: the gods give rain and food; humans give back in offering and work.
  • To take without giving back breaks the cycle: such a person is a thief.

Q1 What did the creator make together with people?

Show answer

Sacrifice, yajna.

Q2 Who is called a thief?

Show answer

Someone who enjoys what they are given but gives nothing back.

Quick check
?According to Bhagavad Gita 3.10–12, why should people give back?
Mission 4

ISKCON Food for Life

  • Started in 1974 in Mayapur, India, by followers of A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada, founder of ISKCON (the Hare Krishna movement).
  • Tradition says he saw village children fighting with dogs over scraps of food, and told his followers that no one near a temple should go hungry.
  • It serves free vegetarian meals that are first offered to Krishna: prasadam ("mercy").
  • Food is given to anyone, with no conditions: homeless people, students, disaster victims, schoolchildren.
  • In Britain, Food for Life volunteers serve free hot meals in London and other cities.
  • Why: feeding the body and, ISKCON believes, the soul; ahimsa (vegetarian food); service to Krishna by serving others.
Quick check
?Why does ISKCON Food for Life serve only vegetarian food that has first been offered to Krishna?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Sewa is better than giving money."

  • For: sewa costs the self (time, effort, pride); it is karma yoga in its purest form.
  • For: serving in person builds relationships and humility; volunteers who serve free meals (such as ISKCON Food for Life) meet those they feed.
  • Against: money can do more at a distance: disaster relief needs funds; the Gita praises the right gift at the right time.
  • Against: not everyone can serve in person (age, health, work); dana is open to all.
  • Another view: both are needed; what matters is the attitude: done without wanting reward.
  • Judge it by: Which helps the person in need more? Which changes the giver more? Does the motive matter more than the result?
Mission check

?A Hindu student volunteers every Saturday at a food bank but asks the organisers not to mention her in their newsletter. Which Hindu teaching does her choice show most clearly?

For many Hindus, everything is received, so service is not extra: it is giving back.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Raksha Bandhan: a thread of protection; Bali and Lakshmi (Vishnu Purana); Indra and Indrani (Bhavishya Purana).

Mission 2

  • A tirtha is a "crossing place"; pilgrimage (tirtha-yatra) is for darshan, purification, vows, ancestors and moksha, and is not compulsory.
  • The Ganges is Mother Ganga; Varanasi is Shiva's city, where many hope to die and be cremated.
  • Vrindavan: Krishna's Braj, bhakti and parikrama.

Mission 3

  • Brahman in all, atman in every creature, ahimsa and karma lead many Hindus to care for nature: rivers, sacred places, trees and animals.
  • The cow (gau mata) is protected: goshalas care for cows for life.
  • Vrindavan: restoring Krishna's forests and the Yamuna.

Mission 4

  • Dana (giving) and sewa (selfless service) are part of dharma and karma yoga; Gita 3.10–12: one who takes without giving back is a thief.
  • ISKCON Food for Life: free vegetarian prasadam for anyone, since 1974.
