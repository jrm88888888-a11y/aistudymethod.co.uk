Mission 1

Mission 1: Every day your body makes enough ammonia to poison itself. Why doesn't it?

Make a guess: Every day your body makes enough ammonia to poison itself. Why doesn't it?

Mission 1

Excretion: clearing metabolic waste

  • Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism from the body.
  • Carbon dioxide (from respiration): excess forms carbonic acid → H⁺ ions lower blood pH (respiratory acidosis). It also binds haemoglobin, reducing oxygen transport. Excreted by the lungs.
  • Nitrogenous waste: surplus amino acids cannot be stored. Their amino groups become ammonia, then urea in the liver. The kidneys excrete urea in urine.
  • Wastes left to build up disturb enzymes and pH: metabolism and homeostasis fail.
Mission 1

The liver's blood supply

  • Hepatic artery: oxygenated blood from the aorta.
  • Hepatic portal vein: blood from the gut, rich in absorbed nutrients (and any toxins). About three-quarters of the liver's blood arrives this way.
  • Hepatic vein: blood leaves the liver for the vena cava.
  • Bile duct: carries bile, made by liver cells, to the gall bladder and small intestine.
  • Say: "hepatic portal vein brings blood from the gut" — not: "the hepatic vein brings blood from the gut".
Mission 1

Inside a lobule

  • The liver is made of lobules, roughly hexagonal in section.
  • Rows of liver cells, hepatocytes, radiate from a central (intralobular) vein.
  • At the corners: interlobular branches of the hepatic artery, hepatic portal vein and bile duct.
  • Blood from artery and portal vein mixes in sinusoids, flows past the hepatocytes, and drains into the central vein → hepatic vein.
  • Kupffer cells line the sinusoids: macrophages that break down old red blood cells.
  • Bile flows the other way, in bile canaliculi, to the bile duct.

Mission 1

Storage and detoxification

  • Glycogen: hepatocytes store glucose as glycogen granules, and release it when blood glucose falls.
  • Detoxification: liver enzymes convert toxins into harmless or more easily excreted substances.
  • Hydrogen peroxide → water + oxygen, by catalase.
  • Ethanol → ethanal (alcohol dehydrogenase) → ethanoate (ethanal dehydrogenase). Ethanoate → acetyl coenzyme A → respiration.
  • Both ethanol steps reduce NAD.
  • Heavy drinking: NAD runs short, so less is available to oxidise fatty acids. Fat builds up in hepatocytes: fatty liver.
Quick check
?Why can heavy, regular drinking of alcohol lead to fatty liver?
Mission 1

From amino acid to urea

  • Surplus amino acids cannot be stored.
  • 1: Deamination: amino acid + oxygen → keto acid + ammonia. The keto acid can be respired, or converted to glucose or fat.
  • 2: Ornithine cycle (outline): ammonia and carbon dioxide join ornithine → citrulline → arginine → urea released, and ornithine re-formed.
  • Overall: 2NH3 + CO2 → CO(NH2)2 + H2O.
  • 3: Urea dissolves in plasma, travels to the kidneys and is excreted in urine.
  • Why bother? Ammonia is highly toxic and very soluble; urea is far less toxic. Making urea costs ATP.
Quick check
?What happens to a surplus amino acid in a hepatocyte?
Mission check

?In severe liver failure, the concentration of ammonia in the blood rises and the concentration of urea falls. Which explanation is correct?

The ornithine cycle is the liver's way of turning a poison into a manageable waste. Lose the liver and the poison stays.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Make a guess: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Mission 2

Two jobs

  • Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism, such as urea (made in the liver from excess amino acids).
  • Osmoregulation: controlling the water potential of the blood.
  • The kidney does both, but they are separate jobs.
  • Faeces are not excreted: undigested food never entered metabolism. Removing it is egestion.
  • Say: "excretion removes metabolic waste" — not: "excretion removes anything the body doesn't need".
Mission 2

The kidney, outside and in

  • Blood in: renal artery. Blood out: renal vein.
  • Outer cortex. Inner medulla, divided into cone-shaped pyramids.
  • Urine drains into the pelvis, then down the ureter to the bladder, and out through the urethra.
  • Each kidney holds about a million nephrons: the working units.
Mission 2

One nephron

  • Bowman's capsule cups a knot of capillaries, the glomerulus. Both sit in the cortex.
  • Then: proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) → loop of Henle (dips into the medulla) → distal convoluted tubule (DCT) → collecting duct.
  • Blood: afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → capillaries around the tubules.
  • Fluid entering the capsule is glomerular filtrate. What reaches the end of the collecting duct is urine.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes osmoregulation from excretion?
Mission 2

Ultrafiltration

  • The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole → high hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus.
  • This pressure forces fluid out through a three-layer filter: capillary endothelium (with pores), basement membrane, and podocytes in the capsule wall.
  • The filter sorts by size. Through: water, glucose, amino acids, urea, ions.
  • Stay in the blood: blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins (too large).
  • So filtrate = plasma minus its proteins. An adult makes about 180 dm³ a day.
  • Say: "filtered by size, under pressure" — not: "the glomerulus picks out the waste".
Quick check
?Which statement about ultrafiltration in the glomerulus is correct?
Mission 2

Selective reabsorption in the PCT

  • 1: Na⁺–K⁺ pumps in the cell membrane facing the blood use ATP to pump Na⁺ out of the cell. Na⁺ inside stays low.
  • 2: Na⁺ diffuses into the cell from the filtrate through co-transporter proteins, carrying glucose (or amino acids) with it, against glucose's own gradient.
  • 3: Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • Solutes moving into the blood lower its water potential, so water follows by osmosis.
  • In the PCT: all glucose and amino acids, and about two-thirds of the water and Na⁺, are reabsorbed.
  • Say: "glucose is co-transported with sodium ions" — not: "glucose diffuses back into the blood".
Quick check
?A drug stops the sodium–potassium pumps in the cells lining the proximal convoluted tubule. What happens to the reabsorption of glucose from the filtrate?
Mission 2

PCT cells: built for reabsorption

  • Microvilli (a brush border) on the side facing the filtrate.
  • Basal invaginations (infoldings) on the side facing the blood.
  • Both add membrane without adding volume → high surface area to volume ratio and room for many co-transporters and pumps.
  • Many mitochondria: ATP for the Na⁺–K⁺ pumps.
  • Close to capillaries: short diffusion distance to the blood.

Mission check

?Fluid was sampled from Bowman's capsule and from the end of the proximal convoluted tubule of a healthy kidney. Glucose was 5 mmol dm⁻³ in the capsule and undetectable at the end of the tubule. Urea was 5 mmol dm⁻³ in the capsule and higher at the end of the tubule. Which explanation is correct?

Filter everything small, then take back what is useful. That is how 180 dm³ of filtrate a day becomes about 1.5 dm³ of urine.

Make a guess: The kidney's filter lets sugar straight through but holds back proteins. What is it built from?

Mission 3

Three layers, one filter

  • 1: Capillary endothelium: thin, flattened (squamous) cells with pores (fenestrations). Holds back blood cells.
  • 2: Basement membrane: a mesh of collagen and glycoproteins. The effective filter: holds back plasma proteins.
  • 3: Podocytes: capsule-wall cells whose foot processes wrap the capillaries, leaving narrow filtration slits.
  • Path: blood → pores → basement membrane → slits → capsule space.
  • Say: "the basement membrane acts as the filter" — not: "the capillary pores filter out proteins".
Mission 3

Linings along the nephron

  • Bowman's capsule: inner wall of podocytes; outer wall of squamous epithelium.
  • PCT: cuboidal epithelium with microvilli, many mitochondria and basal invaginations.
  • Descending limb of the loop: thin walls of flat (squamous) cells, permeable to water.
  • Ascending limb: impermeable to water.
  • DCT and collecting duct: cuboidal epithelium.
Mission 3

Practical: dissecting a kidney

  • OCR PAG2: dissection.
  • Gloves and eye protection; scalpel on a board, cutting away from you.
  • Outside: find the renal artery (thick wall), renal vein (thin wall), ureter (muscular tube, no blood).
  • Cut a longitudinal section, in one plane, from the outer edge to the ureter.
  • Find cortex (outer, darker), medulla with striped pyramids, pelvis.
  • Draw: sharp pencil, clear single lines, no shading, ruled label lines, title, scale.
  • Calculation: magnification = drawing size ÷ real size.
Mission 3

Practical: nephrons under the microscope

  • OCR PAG1: examine and draw stained sections of nephrons.
  • Cortex, low power: round glomeruli in their capsules, among cut tubules.
  • PCT in cross-section: cuboidal cells, fuzzy brush border, narrow lumen.
  • DCT: no brush border, clearer lumen. Medulla: loops and collecting ducts in parallel.
  • Electron micrographs: podocyte foot processes and slits; microvilli and mitochondria in PCT cells.
  • Worked example: a glomerulus is 40 mm across on a ×200 micrograph.

1. Actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

2. 40 mm ÷ 200 = 0.2 mm.

3. 0.2 mm × 1000 = 200 µm.

Quick check
?On an electron micrograph at ×5000, one microvillus of a PCT cell is 5 mm long. What is its actual length?
Mission 3

Urine as a test sample

  • Small molecules in blood are filtered into urine, so urine can show what is in the blood.
  • Pregnancy test: detects hCG, a hormone made by the embryo (later the placenta).
  • 1: Urine soaks along the strip. hCG binds mobile monoclonal antibodies carrying a coloured bead.
  • 2: Test line: immobilised antibodies bind the hCG–antibody–bead complex. A line appears.
  • 3: Control line: immobilised antibodies bind free mobile antibodies. A line here shows the test worked.
  • Anabolic steroids and other drugs: urine screened, then confirmed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry, which separates and identifies each compound.
Quick check
?A pregnancy test shows a control line but no test line. What does this show?
Mission check

?In a kidney disease, the basement membrane and the podocyte slits of many glomeruli are damaged. Blood pressure and the tubules are normal. Which change in the urine is expected?

Three layers, each holding back something different. Break the basement membrane and proteins leak into urine.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Make a guess: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Mission 4

Keeping blood water in balance

  • Osmoregulation: control of the water potential of the blood.
  • Sweating, salty food, not drinking: the blood is short of water.
  • Drinking a lot: the blood has surplus water.
  • The kidney responds by changing how much water it reabsorbs: a small volume of concentrated urine, or a large volume of dilute urine.
  • The loop of Henle makes concentrated urine possible. ADH controls how much water is kept.
Mission 4

The loop of Henle builds a salty medulla

  • 1: Ascending limb: impermeable to water. It actively transports Na⁺ (with Cl⁻) out of the filtrate into the medulla tissue.
  • This keeps the water potential of the medulla low.
  • 2: Descending limb: permeable to water. Water leaves by osmosis, so the filtrate is most concentrated at the tip of the loop.
  • 3: The collecting duct runs down through this salty medulla, so water can be drawn out of it by osmosis.
  • Two limbs side by side, flowing opposite ways: a countercurrent multiplier.
  • Longer loops → saltier deep medulla → more concentrated urine possible.
  • Say: "the ascending limb pumps sodium ions out" — not: "the loop pumps water out".
Quick check
?How does the loop of Henle make it possible for the kidney to produce concentrated urine?
Mission 4

The adjustable part: DCT and collecting duct

  • Filtrate leaving the loop is dilute.
  • In the DCT and collecting duct, water can leave by osmosis into the salty medulla, then into the blood.
  • How much leaves depends on how permeable their walls are to water.
  • That permeability is set by ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
Mission 4

ADH: the control loop

  • 1: Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a fall in blood water potential.
  • 2: ADH is made in the hypothalamus, then stored in and released from the posterior pituitary into the blood. More ADH is released.
  • 3: ADH binds receptors on collecting duct cells. Vesicles containing aquaporins (water channels) fuse with the membrane facing the filtrate.
  • 4: More water leaves the duct by osmosis → small volume of concentrated urine. As blood water is restored, less ADH is released: negative feedback.
  • Surplus water: less ADH → aquaporins taken back into vesicles → large volume of dilute urine.
  • Say: "ADH makes the duct walls more permeable to water" — not: "ADH pumps water back into the blood".
Quick check
?A person has drunk very little water for a day and the water potential of their blood has fallen. Which sequence of events follows?

Mission 4

Kidney failure

  • Causes include diabetes, high blood pressure and infection.
  • Glomerular filtration rate (GFR): volume of filtrate formed per minute. Estimated from blood creatinine (eGFR).
  • eGFR of 90 cm³ min⁻¹ or more is normal; below 15 is kidney failure.
  • Low GFR: urea and creatinine build up in the blood.
  • Electrolyte balance fails: high K⁺ can disturb heart rhythm; retained Na⁺ and water cause oedema and high blood pressure.
  • Protein or blood in urine: a sign of filter damage.
Mission 4

Treatments

  • Haemodialysis: blood flows through a dialyser past a partially permeable membrane. Dialysis fluid flows the other way (countercurrent).
  • Dialysis fluid: normal glucose and ion concentrations, no urea. Urea and surplus ions diffuse out; glucose is kept. Usually 3 sessions a week, about 4 hours each.
  • Peritoneal dialysis: dialysis fluid fills the abdomen; the peritoneum is the membrane. Drained after some hours; done at home.
  • Transplant: a matched donor kidney. Frees the patient from dialysis, but needs lifelong immunosuppressant drugs to prevent rejection; donors are scarce.
Quick check
?Why does the dialysis fluid used in haemodialysis contain glucose at the same concentration as normal blood plasma?
Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, the collecting duct cells cannot respond to ADH, although the hypothalamus and pituitary work normally. Which prediction is correct?

The loop builds the gradient; ADH decides how much water uses it. Break the response and the feedback loop keeps shouting.

Make a guess: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Mission 5

One clone, one target

  • An antibody binds an antigen with a complementary shape.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: identical antibodies made by one clone of cells.
  • So every molecule binds the same antigen, and nothing else.
  • Made in the laboratory, in large amounts.
  • They can carry a dye, an enzyme or a drug to one target.
Mission 5

The pregnancy test

  • After implantation, the embryo, then the placenta, secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin). Some reaches the urine.
  • (1) Urine soaks along the strip. Mobile monoclonal antibodies, complementary to hCG and carrying coloured particles, bind any hCG.
  • (2) Test line (T): fixed antibodies to hCG catch the hCG–antibody complexes. A coloured line forms.
  • (3) Control line (C): fixed antibodies that bind the mobile antibodies themselves. A line forms whether or not hCG is present.
  • C and T: pregnant. C only: not pregnant. No C line: the test has failed.
  • Say: "antibodies complementary to hCG" — not: "antibodies that detect pregnancy".
Quick check
?A home pregnancy test strip contains monoclonal antibodies. Which statement explains how the test indicates pregnancy?
Mission 5

Tests for drugs and steroids

  • Urine carries breakdown products of drugs and hormones: easy to sample.
  • Drug tests: strips carry monoclonal antibodies complementary to a drug or its breakdown product, e.g. from cannabis.
  • Anabolic steroids (banned in sport: they build muscle): monoclonal antibody tests exist for some steroids and their breakdown products.
  • Antibody tests screen many samples quickly and cheaply.
  • Positives are confirmed by gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS), which identifies the exact molecule.
Mission check

?A rapid test for virus X has monoclonal antibodies complementary to protein N of virus X, on mobile coloured particles and fixed at the test line. The control line works as in a pregnancy test. A person infected with virus Y, whose protein N has a different shape, uses the test correctly. What result is expected?

One clone, one shape, one target. That is how a strip finds a single hormone in a sample full of other molecules.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Excretion removes metabolic wastes: CO2 by the lungs; nitrogen as urea, made in the liver and excreted by the kidneys.
  • In lobules, blood from the hepatic artery and hepatic portal vein mixes in sinusoids and drains to the central vein; hepatocytes store glycogen, detoxify ethanol and hydrogen peroxide, and make urea.

Mission 2

  • Ultrafiltration: blood pressure forces water and small solutes through the glomerular filter; cells and plasma proteins stay behind.
  • Selective reabsorption in the PCT: Na⁺ gradient drives glucose co-transport; water follows by osmosis; microvilli, infoldings and mitochondria make it fast.

Mission 3

  • The filter: fenestrated endothelium (holds back cells), basement membrane (the effective filter for proteins), podocyte slits.
  • Dissect in one plane and draw with clear lines; actual size = image size ÷ magnification.

Mission 4

  • The ascending limb actively transports Na⁺ out, making the medulla salty; the collecting duct loses water to it by osmosis when ADH has inserted aquaporins.
  • Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus → ADH from the posterior pituitary → aquaporins → more water reabsorbed; negative feedback switches it off.
  • Kidney failure lowers GFR and upsets electrolytes; dialysis or a transplant replaces kidney function.

Mission 5

  • Monoclonal antibodies are identical and bind one complementary antigen, so they can find, report or deliver to a single target.
