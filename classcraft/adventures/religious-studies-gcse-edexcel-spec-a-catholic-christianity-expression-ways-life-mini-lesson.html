Mission 1: Why is the font by the door and the altar at the far end of a Catholic church?

Make a guess: Why is the font by the door and the altar at the far end of a Catholic church?

Mission 1Read the source

Worship in spirit and truth

"God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." (John 4:24, WEBBE)

  • The Catechism starts here: worship is not tied to one place; the whole earth is holy, and Christians are the "living stones" of God's house (CCC 1179, 1 Peter 2:5; paraphrase).
  • Yet where they are free to, Catholics build churches. A church is more than a meeting hall: it is a visible sign of the Church living in that place, God dwelling with his people (CCC 1180, paraphrase).
  • It is a house of prayer where the Eucharist is celebrated and kept, and it should be beautiful and fit for prayer (CCC 1181, paraphrase).

Q1 If God can be worshipped anywhere, why build a church at all?

Show answer

It gathers the community, houses the Eucharist, and is a sign of God among his people.

Q2 What does "living stones" suggest the real Church is?

Show answer

The people, not only the building.

Mission 1

A building shaped like the faith

  • Many churches have a cruciform (cross-shaped) plan: the building itself recalls Christ's death.
  • The nave (from the Latin for "ship") holds the people: the Church as a ship carrying believers to God.
  • The sanctuary, raised and at the head of the church, holds the altar and ambo.
  • Walking from the door to the altar pictures the Christian life: entering by baptism, fed by the Eucharist, travelling towards heaven.
  • The church as the Father's house that believers journey towards: CCC 1186 (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Facing east

  • Traditionally a church is oriented: the altar is at the east end.
  • The sun rises in the east, a sign of Christ's resurrection and of his promised return; the prophet speaks of the "sun of righteousness" rising "with healing in its wings" (Malachi 4:2).
  • So the whole assembly faces one way, towards the coming Christ, in reflection and hope.
  • Not every church can face east (the site may not allow it); St Peter's in Rome has its entrance at the east.
  • Orientation still shapes worship: everything leads the eye to the altar.
Mission 1

Common and divergent styles

  • Common to all: an altar, an ambo, a tabernacle, a font, a crucifix, a chair for the priest.
  • Gothic and Gothic Revival (e.g. St Chad's Cathedral, Birmingham, by A. W. N. Pugin, 1841): height, pointed arches and stained glass lift the eye and mind to heaven: God's transcendence.
  • Byzantine style (e.g. Westminster Cathedral, opened 1903): domes and mosaics: heaven brought close.
  • Modern, "in the round" (e.g. Liverpool Metropolitan Cathedral, 1967): the altar in the centre with people gathered around it.
  • After the Second Vatican Council (1962–65), Catholic teaching asks that churches help the people take a full and active part in the liturgy (Sacrosanctum Concilium 14, 124, paraphrase).
  • Decoration (statues, Stations of the Cross, windows) teaches the faith and supports prayer.

Mission 1

The altar

  • The altar is the centre of the church. The church is built around it.
  • It is both an altar of sacrifice and the table of the Lord (CCC 1182, paraphrase).
  • Sacrifice: at Mass, Catholics believe Christ's one sacrifice on the cross is made present, so the altar stands for the cross itself.
  • Meal: Jesus "took bread … saying, 'This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me.'" (Luke 22:19)
  • So the altar expresses redemption: Christ's death and resurrection bring salvation, and believers share in it.
  • A fixed altar is usually of stone (Christ the "cornerstone", Ephesians 2:20), often with relics of saints placed beneath it.
Mission 1

The lectern (ambo)

  • The ambo (CCEA's term; AQA and Edexcel say lectern) is the fixed reading desk from which the Word of God is proclaimed.
  • The Catechism says the dignity of God's Word needs a fitting place, where everyone's attention is easily drawn (CCC 1184, paraphrase).
  • Used for the readings, the psalm and usually the homily.
  • Redemption: the readings tell the story of salvation, from creation to Christ. "So faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." (Romans 10:17)
  • Catholics speak of two tables: the table of the Word (ambo) and the table of the Eucharist (altar). Both feed the people.
Mission 1

The tabernacle

  • The tabernacle is a locked, richly made box where the consecrated hosts are reserved (kept) after Mass.
  • Catholics believe Christ is really present in the consecrated bread (the Real Presence).
  • A sanctuary lamp (often red) burns beside it: Christ is here.
  • Catholics genuflect (bend the knee) towards it, and come to pray before it.
  • The Catechism asks that it stand in a most worthy place, so that its dignity and position help people adore the Lord present there (CCC 1183, paraphrase).
  • Redemption: the risen Christ stays with his people; Holy Communion is taken from it to the sick and dying.
Mission 1

The crucifix

  • A crucifix is a cross bearing the figure of the crucified Christ.
  • Catholic liturgical rules ask for one on or near the altar at Mass.
  • It shows the cost of redemption: "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23).
  • It joins the altar and the cross: what happened on Calvary is what the Mass makes present.
  • It invites personal reflection on Christ's love and on carrying one's own cross.
Mission 1

The font, by the door

  • The font holds the water for baptism. In many churches it stands near the entrance; some have a baptistery (CCC 1185).
  • Its place says: you enter the Church through baptism.
  • Baptism unites a person with Christ's death and resurrection: "buried … with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised … so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)
  • Many fonts are eight-sided: Christ rose on the "eighth day", the start of the new creation.
  • Holy water stoups at the door let Catholics bless themselves and remember their baptism as they come in.

Mission 1Diversity

Table or altar? Cross, crucifix or risen Christ?

  • Altar: stresses the sacrifice of the Mass. Table: stresses the shared meal. Many modern Catholic altars look like a table: both meanings at once. (Many Protestant churches have a communion table, stressing the shared meal.)
  • Crucifix: Christ's suffering and self-giving love.
  • Plain (empty) cross: Christ's victory; the cross is empty because he is risen. Common in Protestant churches; found in Catholic churches too.
  • "Risen Christ": a figure of Christ risen or reigning, often robed, on or before the cross: resurrection and new life.
  • For the individual: each artefact invites a different prayer (sorrow, thanks, hope). For the community: together they tell the whole Paschal Mystery: death and resurrection.
Mission 1

Sacred objects

  • Sacred vessels: chalice (wine), paten and ciborium (hosts), monstrance (for adoration), pyx (to carry Communion to the sick). Made of worthy materials and kept only for worship.
  • Sarcophagi: early Christian stone coffins carved with Bible scenes, e.g. the "Passion sarcophagus" (4th century, Museo Pio Cristiano, Vatican): an empty cross crowned with Christ's monogram: death defeated.
  • Hunger cloths: large cloths that once veiled the altar in Lent; since 1976 the German Catholic charity Misereor has commissioned new ones from artists around the world, linking Lent with justice for the poor.
  • CCC 1161 (paraphrase): holy images of Christ, Mary, angels and saints may be displayed in churches, on vessels and vestments, in homes and streets, so that people remember and imitate those shown.
  • More on sarcophagi and hunger cloths.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Catholics do not need a special building to worship God."

  • For: God is worshipped "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24); the whole earth is holy (CCC 1179). Mass can be said in a field, hospital or prison. The Church is the people, "living stones" (1 Peter 2:5).
  • Against: the sacraments need an altar and a font; the tabernacle keeps Christ present for prayer and for the sick. A church is a visible sign of God among his people (CCC 1180). Beauty, orientation and layout teach redemption and lift the mind to God.
  • Another view: the building is not essential, but it is valuable: worship is possible anywhere, and still richer in a place made for it.
  • Judge it by: Is worship mainly personal or communal? What does a building do that a field cannot? Does persecution (Christians without churches) show what is essential?

Mission 2

Christian sarcophagi in Rome

  • A sarcophagus is a stone coffin, often carved. Wealthy Romans used them; so did wealthy early Christians.
  • Christians buried their dead rather than cremating them: the body would be raised (1 Thessalonians 4:14).
  • Many lie in the catacombs, underground burial places outside Rome.
  • Their carvings use Bible scenes of rescue and new life: Jonah coming out of the fish, the raising of Lazarus, Daniel among the lions, the Good Shepherd.
  • After Christianity became legal (313), carvings grew bolder: Christ in triumph, the Chi-Rho (☧, the first two Greek letters of Christos).
  • Many are now in museums; for Catholics they remain sacred witnesses to the faith of the first Christians.
Mission 2

The Sarcophagus with Scenes of the Passion

  • Made in Rome, c. 350, from the catacomb of Domitilla; now in the Museo Pio Christiano, Vatican Museums.
  • Five niches divided by columns, read left to right:
  • 1. Simon of Cyrene carries the cross for Jesus.
  • 2. The crowning: a soldier places a crown on Jesus; the crown of thorns appears as a jewelled crown of honour: suffering shown as glory.
  • 3. Centre: an empty cross topped by the Chi-Rho in a laurel wreath (the Roman sign of victory), held by an eagle; two doves; below, two Roman soldiers, one asleep, one looking up: the guards at the tomb (Matthew 28:4). This is the Anastasis (Resurrection).
  • 4–5. Jesus before Pilate; Pilate washes his hands.
  • No crucified body, no scene of death: Jesus is calm and dignified throughout.
  • Meaning for eternal life: the Passion leads straight to victory over death; the person buried here hoped to share that victory.

Mission 2

Sacred vessels

  • Chalice: cup for the Precious Blood. Paten: plate for the host.
  • Ciborium: covered bowl holding consecrated hosts, kept in the tabernacle.
  • Pyx: small case to carry Holy Communion to the sick and dying (Viaticum).
  • Monstrance: displays the host for Eucharistic adoration.
  • Made of worthy materials and handled with reverence, because Catholics believe they hold the Real Presence of Christ, the "bread of life" that gives eternal life (John 6:35, 54).
  • CCC 1161: images of Christ, Mary, the angels and the saints all point to Christ and may be shown in churches, on sacred vessels and vestments, and in homes (paraphrase, citing the Second Council of Nicaea, 787). The Catechism adds that images express the same Gospel that Scripture tells in words (CCC 1160).
Mission 2

Hunger cloths

  • Hunger cloths (German Hungertuch, "hunger cloth"; also Lenten veils): large cloths hung in churches during Lent.
  • Medieval origin: from around the year 1000 they hid the altar or sanctuary, a "fast for the eyes" as part of Lenten penance; removed for Easter.
  • Many were painted with scenes of the Bible, from creation to the Last Judgement (e.g. the Great Zittau Lenten Veil, 1472).
  • Revived since 1976 by the German Catholic development agency MISEREOR, which commissions new hunger cloths, often from artists in the Global South.
  • Belief expressed: penance and longing for Easter; and solidarity with the hungry: fasting linked with justice (Matthew 25:35).
  • Used today in churches, schools and homes, often with prayer resources.
Mission 2Diversity

Same objects, different uses

  • Catholic and Orthodox: vessels richly made; reverence for the reserved Sacrament (Catholic) and for icons (Orthodox, who also use ornate chalices and a spoon for Communion).
  • Many Protestants: plainer vessels, sometimes individual cups; bread and wine as a memorial or a spiritual presence, so less focus on the vessels themselves.
  • Images: some Protestant traditions avoid religious images in worship, following the second commandment (Exodus 20:4); Catholics follow Nicaea II (CCC 1161).
  • Settings: a sarcophagus in a museum is studied as art and history; for Catholics it is also a witness to faith. A hunger cloth in a school hall is a teaching tool; in church, part of worship.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Objects and images teach faith better than words."

  • Reasons for: a candle or carving speaks to everyone, including those who cannot read (much of the Christian world in history); symbols engage the senses and memory.
  • The Passion sarcophagus still proclaims the Resurrection about 1,650 years after it was carved.
  • CCC 1160: images proclaim the same Gospel that Scripture tells in words.
  • Reasons against: objects can be misunderstood or treated superstitiously; some Protestants argue faith comes by hearing the Word (Romans 10:17) and warn against images (Exodus 20:4); words can explain why, not just that.
  • Symbols need explaining: a visitor may not know why a cloth hides the altar in Lent.
  • Another view: Catholics see word and image as partners.
  • Hunger cloths come with prayer resources for churches, schools and homes.
  • Judge it by: Who is the audience? What happens when no one explains the symbol? Do objects add something words cannot?

Mission 3

Why paint the faith?

  • Christian painting expresses belief in pictures: for those who make it and those who look.
  • The artist may paint as an act of prayer and offer skill to God.
  • The observer may learn, pray, or be moved to awe.
  • Teaching: Pope Gregory the Great (c. 600) said pictures let those who cannot read "read" the faith on the walls (paraphrase).
  • Devotion: an image focuses prayer.
  • Beauty: Catholics believe beauty reflects God.
  • A fresco is painted on wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall.
Mission 3Read the source

Catechism 2502–2503

Catechism of the Catholic Church (paraphrase):

  • 2502: Sacred art is true and beautiful when it fits its purpose: to bring to mind and glorify, in faith and adoration, the mystery of God, made visible in Christ, and also Mary, the angels and the saints. It draws people to adoration, prayer and love of God.
  • 2503: Bishops should encourage sacred art, old and new, and remove from worship whatever does not fit the truth of faith and true beauty.

Q1 According to 2502, what should genuine sacred art lead people to do?

Show answer

Adore, pray, and love God.

Q2 According to 2503, who is responsible for the art used in worship?

Show answer

The bishops.

Mission 3

The Sistine Chapel ceiling

  • Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564): Italian sculptor, painter and poet.
  • Pope Julius II asked him to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, the Pope's chapel in the Vatican (1508–1512).
  • Down the centre: nine scenes from Genesis, from God separating light from darkness to the story of Noah.
  • The Creation of Adam (c. 1511–12) is one of the central scenes.
  • The chapel is still used: cardinals elect a new pope there.
Mission 3

Reading the Creation of Adam

  • Left: Adam lies on bare earth, strong and beautiful, but limp: his hand hangs, waiting.
  • Right: God, shown as a powerful elderly man, sweeps through the sky, carried by angels in a billowing red cloak.
  • Centre: their fingers almost touch. The gap is the moment just before life passes from God to Adam.
  • Adam's pose roughly mirrors God's: humans are made in God's likeness.
  • Under God's arm, a young woman looks towards Adam: often read as Eve, not yet created; some read her as Wisdom or Mary.
  • Some modern viewers see the red cloak as shaped like a human brain: perhaps God gives a mind as well as life (one interpretation).

Mission 3Evaluate

"How far does the Creation of Adam express Catholic beliefs?"

  • Expresses them well: God as active, powerful creator (omnipotent); God comes from heaven to earth (transcendent, beyond the created world, yet personal); life as God's gift; Adam's likeness to God (imago Dei, "the image of God") and the beauty of the human body (dignity); Eve already in God's plan.
  • Falls short: Catholic teaching is that God is spirit (John 4:24), neither man nor woman; the old man is a symbol (cf. "Ancient of Days", Daniel 7:9), and some Christians think God should not be pictured at all. Genesis 2:7 has breath, not a touch. The painting shows the body, not reason, free will or the soul, which Catholics see as central to God's image.
  • Judge it by: Can a picture ever show an invisible God? Does a symbol have to be literal to be true? Which Catholic beliefs about humans matter most?
Mission 3

The Last Judgement

  • Michelangelo, 1536–1541, on the altar wall of the Sistine Chapel, for Popes Clement VII and Paul III.
  • Centre top: Christ as judge, arm raised; Mary beside him.
  • Around: saints and martyrs, many holding the instrument of their death (e.g. St Bartholomew with his skin; St Lawrence with a gridiron).
  • Below centre: angels blow trumpets and hold books: a small book of the saved, a large book of the damned.
  • Left: the dead rise from graves, some pulled up to heaven.
  • Right: the damned are dragged down; at the bottom, a ferryman and a judge from Dante's Inferno receive them.
Mission 3

Themes of the Last Judgement

  • Christ the judge: "When the Son of Man comes in his glory… Before him all the nations will be gathered" (Matthew 25:31–32).
  • Resurrection of the body: "the trumpet will sound and the dead will be raised" (1 Corinthians 15:52): bodies, not just souls.
  • Heaven and hell: Catholics believe each person's choices have eternal results.
  • Mercy and intercession: Mary and the saints are close to Christ.
  • Hope and warning: the saved are lifted up by others; the priest saying Mass beneath it is reminded of what is at stake.
  • Controversy: many figures were nude; after the Council of Trent, Daniele da Volterra painted cloths over some (1565). Some saw nudity as fitting (we rise as God made us); others as unfitting for a chapel.

Mission 3Diversity

Paintings in different places

  • Catholic churches: altarpieces, frescoes, Stations of the Cross paintings: for teaching, devotion and beauty (CCC 2502).
  • Orthodox churches: painted icons, used for prayer in a special way (a later mission).
  • Protestant churches: many keep worship plain, for fear of distracting from the Word or of idolatry (Exodus 20:4); some Anglican churches display paintings, e.g. William Holman Hunt's The Light of the World, Christ knocking at a door (Revelation 3:20), in St Paul's Cathedral.
  • Other settings: homes (a picture for prayer), schools, galleries and museums. In a gallery one visitor sees history, another prays.
  • The artist's meaning and the observer's meaning can differ.

Mission 4

Why Catholics make statues

  • Catholic churches and homes have statues: of Jesus, Mary, the saints and angels.
  • The Incarnation: God became human in Jesus, so God has been seen, touched and heard. Catholics believe this makes images of Christ possible.
  • CCC 2501 (paraphrase): made in God's image, humans express the truth of their relationship with God through the beauty of their art; art is a uniquely human gift.
  • A statue is three-dimensional: it stands in the same space as the believer, as if present.
  • Purposes: to remember, to teach, to inspire, and to focus prayer.
Mission 4Read the source

The Word became flesh

"The Word became flesh and lived amongst us. We saw his glory" (John 1:14)

"He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." (Colossians 1:15)

Q1 According to John 1:14, what could people now do that they could not do before?

Show answer

See God's glory in a human life.

Q2 How do Catholics use Colossians 1:15 to defend statues of Jesus?

Show answer

If Christ is the visible image of the invisible God, making an image of Christ shows what God has already shown.

Mission 4

Veneration, not worship

  • Catholics worship God alone (adoration).
  • They venerate (honour) Mary and the saints: a statue is a reminder, not a god.
  • The Second Council of Nicaea (787) and the Catechism (CCC 2132) teach that honour given to an image passes to the person it shows.
  • So lighting a candle before a statue of St Anthony is asking him to pray with you, not praying to stone.

Mission 4

Statues in a Catholic church

  • Crucifix: Christ on the cross, above or near the altar: his sacrifice, renewed in the Mass.
  • Mary: often a side altar or shrine, with candles and flowers.
  • Saints, especially the church's patron; Sacred Heart of Jesus: his love for all people.
  • Stations of the Cross: 14 carved or painted scenes around the walls.
  • Christmas crib: figures of the Nativity scene.
  • How they are used: kneeling, lighting candles, prayer, processions (e.g. crowning a statue of Mary in May), funerals and feasts.
Mission 4

Michelangelo's Pietà

  • Pietà (Italian: "pity", "compassion"); Michelangelo, carved 1498–1499 from one block of Carrara marble, aged about 24.
  • Now in St Peter's Basilica, Rome; the only work he signed (on the sash across Mary's chest).
  • Mary holds the dead body of Jesus across her lap after the crucifixion.
  • Details: a pyramid shape: stable and calm; Mary looks young and serene; her eyes are lowered; her left hand is open, as if offering or accepting; Christ's wounds are small; his body is at peace.
  • The scene is not described in the Gospels: it is a devotional image of Mary's sorrow.
  • Catholics read her calm as trust in God and her open hand as offering her son to God, in hope of the resurrection: so the statue gives hope in suffering.

Mission 4

Christ the Redeemer: a statue in the open

  • A 30-metre statue of Jesus above Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, completed 1931 (Catholic; designed by Paul Landowski).
  • His arms are stretched wide: welcome for everyone, and the shape of the cross.
  • It shows the risen Christ, not the dying one: Christ as Redeemer, saviour of the city and the world.
  • Used for: Masses and blessings at its base; pilgrimage; a national symbol, seen by tourists of every faith and none.
  • Shows how a statue can express belief (the artist) and be received in many ways (the observers).
Mission 4Diversity

Different Christian views on statues

  • Catholic: statues welcomed; veneration, not worship (CCC 2132).
  • Anglican: varied. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) condemned the "Worshipping and Adoration, as well of Images as of Reliques" (Article XXII). Since the 19th century many Anglo-Catholic churches have statues of Mary and the saints (e.g. the Anglican shrine at Walsingham); evangelical Anglican churches are usually plain, with an empty cross.
  • Orthodox: honour icons, but rarely use free-standing statues.
  • Reformed (Calvinist) and many other Protestants: no images of Jesus in worship. The Westminster Larger Catechism (1648) lists "the making any representation of God, of all or of any of the three persons" among sins against the second commandment (Exodus 20:4).
  • Many Protestants still accept nativity scenes, statues in museums, or art for teaching outside worship.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Statues of Jesus help faith more than they put it at risk."

  • Reasons for: the Incarnation made God visible (John 1:14; Colossians 1:15); statues teach, comfort and focus prayer; the Pietà shows how art can give hope in suffering; honour passes to the person shown (Nicaea II).
  • Reasons against: the commandment against images (Exodus 20:4–5; part of the first commandment for Catholics, the second for most Protestants); a statue can become an object of superstition; no one knows what Jesus looked like, so a statue may fix a false image; money spent on art could serve the poor.
  • Judge it by: Does the Incarnation change what that commandment forbids? Is the risk in the statue or in how people use it? Do statues help everyone, or only those taught how to use them?
Mission check

For Catholics a statue is a reminder that God became visible in Christ: it is honoured for the person it shows, and worship is kept for God alone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A church is a sign of God dwelling with his people and a house of prayer; its layout traces the journey from baptism (font) to Eucharist (altar).
  • Altar (sacrifice and meal), ambo/lectern (Word), tabernacle (Real Presence), crucifix (cost of redemption) and font (new birth) each express redemption.
  • Contrasting artefacts (table/altar, crucifix, plain cross, risen Christ) stress different sides of the Paschal Mystery.

Mission 2

  • The Passion sarcophagus (c. 350) shows Christ's Passion as victory: an empty cross crowned with the Chi-Rho in a victor's wreath.
  • Sacred vessels and hunger cloths express belief in the Real Presence, penance and solidarity (CCC 1161).

Mission 3

  • Michelangelo's Creation of Adam (Sistine Chapel, c. 1511–12): God as creator; life as God's gift; humans in God's image (Genesis 1:27); how far it fits Catholic belief is debated.
  • CCC 2502–2503: sacred art leads to prayer and adoration; paintings are used differently in Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant churches and beyond.

Mission 4

  • Catholics use statues because of the Incarnation (God made visible in Christ): images remind, teach and focus prayer; they are venerated, not worshipped.
  • Sources for the Incarnation argument: John 1:14; Colossians 1:15.
  • Michelangelo's Pietà (1498–99, St Peter's) shows Mary holding the dead Christ: real suffering, trust in God's goodness, hope of resurrection.
  • Views differ: Catholic welcome; Anglican range; Orthodox prefer icons; many Protestants reject images of Jesus in worship.
