Russia in Revolution 1894-1924

Where this sits in Edexcel. The revolutionary period 1894-1924 is studied at Edexcel as the opening span of Paper 3, Option 38.1: The making of modern Russia, 1855-1991 - a 'themes in breadth with aspects in depth' paper. Paper 3 Section A assesses your handling of a primary source (AO2); the historians' debate below is what you deploy in the essays.

In thirty years Russia went from the accession of the last Tsar to a one-party Communist state. This lesson follows that collapse and seizure of power: late Tsarism, the 1905 revolution, the twin revolutions of 1917, and the Bolshevik consolidation down to the death of Lenin.

1894-1905 - autocracy under strain

The last Tsar and the 1905 revolution

Nicholas II came to the throne in 1894, determined to preserve the autocracy he had inherited. But rapid, state-driven industrialisation (under finance minister Sergei Witte) created an urban working class with grievances, while the peasantry hungered for land.

  • Defeat in the Russo-Japanese War (1904-05) shattered the regime's prestige.
  • On 22 January 1905 (9 January, Old Style) - Bloody Sunday - troops fired on a peaceful workers' procession in St Petersburg, triggering the 1905 Revolution: strikes, mutinies and the first workers' soviets.
  • To survive, Nicholas issued the October Manifesto (1905), promising civil liberties and an elected Duma. But the Fundamental Laws (1906) reaffirmed his autocratic power, and he dissolved Dumas that displeased him.

Historian's verdict to weigh: was 1905 a 'dress rehearsal' (as Lenin claimed) that revealed the regime's fragility, or proof that the autocracy could still recover when it chose to compromise?

1906-1917 - reform, war and collapse

Stolypin, the war, and February 1917

After 1905 the regime tried both repression and reform:

  • Pyotr Stolypin, Prime Minister from 1906, combined harsh repression (the gallows became known as 'Stolypin's necktie') with agrarian reform meant to create a class of prosperous, loyal peasants - a 'wager on the strong'. He was assassinated in 1911, and the reforms were unfinished.
  • The First World War (from 1914) broke the regime. Military defeats, food shortages and inflation drained support. Nicholas's decision to take personal command of the army in 1915 tied him to every failure and left government in the hands of the unpopular Empress Alexandra and Rasputin.
  • The February Revolution (1917) began with strikes and bread queues in Petrograd. The garrison mutinied, and on 15 March 1917 (2 March, Old Style) Nicholas II abdicated - ending three centuries of Romanov rule.

1917 - the October Revolution

The Bolsheviks seize power

The Provisional Government's fatal error was to continue the war. Into the vacuum stepped Lenin, who returned in April 1917 and, in his April Theses, demanded 'peace, land and bread' and 'all power to the soviets'.

  • After the failed July Days and the Kornilov affair (August 1917) - in which the Bolsheviks were armed to defend Petrograd - support for the soviets surged.
  • On 7 November 1917 (25 October, Old Style), Bolshevik Red Guards and soldiers, coordinated by Trotsky's Military Revolutionary Committee, seized key points in Petrograd and stormed the Winter Palace. The October Revolution put the Bolsheviks in power.
  • The new government issued the Decrees on Peace and Land, but when the Constituent Assembly (January 1918) returned a non-Bolshevik majority, Lenin dissolved it by force.

1918-1924 - consolidation

Civil war, terror and the New Economic Policy

Power had to be held against enormous odds:

  • The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) bought peace with Germany at the cost of vast territory - buying time but enraging opponents.
  • In the Civil War (1918-21) the Bolshevik 'Reds', with Trotsky's Red Army, defeated the divided 'Whites'. The economy was militarised under War Communism - grain requisitioning, nationalisation - and the Cheka waged the Red Terror.
  • War Communism produced famine and revolt. The Kronstadt mutiny (March 1921) by once-loyal sailors was the final warning. Lenin retreated to the New Economic Policy (NEP, 1921), which restored limited private trade and a market in grain while the state kept the 'commanding heights'.
  • The USSR was formally created in 1922. Lenin, weakened by strokes, died on 21 January 1924, leaving a one-party state and an unresolved succession.

Interpretations - the historians' debate

Coup from above, or revolution from below?

The historiography of 1917 is one of the sharpest in modern history, and it powers your Paper 3 essays:

Richard Pipes, in The Russian Revolution (1990), argued that October was a coup by a small, disciplined, ideologically-driven minority imposed from above, with little genuine popular support - the product of Russia's long authoritarian tradition and of Lenin's will. Against this 'liberal' reading, the revisionist Sheila Fitzpatrick and other social historians studied the revolution 'from below', finding real workers' and soldiers' grievances and genuine popular participation that the Bolsheviks rode rather than manufactured. Orlando Figes (A People's Tragedy, 1996) framed it as a vast popular tragedy in which ordinary people were both participants and victims, while older Soviet historiography presented an inevitable proletarian revolution.

Exam skill: when a source or essay leans one way, test it against evidence - the size of the Bolshevik party, the July Days, the Kornilov affair, the fate of the Constituent Assembly - and judge how far the claim holds.

Exam technique - Paper 3

How Paper 3 is assessed

Edexcel Paper 3 (Option 38.1): 2 hours 15 minutes, 60 marks, 30% of the A-level. It is 'themes in breadth with aspects in depth'.

  • Section A (AO2): analyse and evaluate a primary source - assess its value for an enquiry, using provenance, content and context.
  • Section B (AO1): an essay on a depth aspect of the period.
  • Section C (AO1): essays on the breadth themes across 1855-1991.

Note: Paper 3 Section A rewards evaluating a source's value (AO2), not spotting 'bias'. The historians' debate above strengthens your Section B and C essays.
