Mission 1

Knowledge as a duty

  • Hadith: seeking knowledge ('ilm) is an obligation on every Muslim (paraphrase; Sunan Ibn Majah, no. 224).
  • "O my Lord! advance me in knowledge." (Qur'an 20:114, Yusuf Ali)
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Revival of the Religious Sciences, Book of Knowledge: medicine and arithmetic are fard kifaya, a communal duty: someone in every community must master them, or all are at fault.
  • Khalifa (2:30): humans are trustees of the earth; understanding creation helps them care for it.
Mission 1

Worship needed science

  • Qibla: facing Makkah from Spain or India needs spherical trigonometry.
  • Prayer times: shift with the sun and season; mosque timekeepers (muwaqqit) built tables and instruments.
  • Calendar: a lunar calendar needs prediction of the new crescent (Ramadan, Hajj).
  • Inheritance: the Qur'an's shares (4:11–12) need careful arithmetic; al-Khwarizmi's algebra book includes inheritance problems.
  • So religious practice gave scholars real problems to solve.

Mission 1

The translation movement

  • Under the Abbasid caliphs (from 750), Baghdad became a centre of learning.
  • Greek, Persian, Syriac and Sanskrit works on medicine, mathematics, astronomy and philosophy were translated into Arabic (8th–10th centuries).
  • The House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma), associated with caliph al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833), linked libraries and translators.
  • Translators included Christians such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873), translator of Galen; Jews and Sabians also took part.
  • Paper, learned from China, spread from Samarkand to Baghdad in the late 8th century and made books cheaper.
  • Scholars checked, criticised and extended the texts: Arabic became the international language of science.
Mission 1

Mathematics

  • Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–c. 850), The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing (c. 820): systematic solving of equations. "Al-jabr" in the title gave us algebra; his name gave algorithm.
  • He also explained the Indian decimal numerals with zero; Europe later learned them in Arabic form (hence "Arabic numerals").
  • Trigonometry: sine and tangent tables developed for astronomy and the qibla (e.g. al-Battani, d. 929).
  • 'Umar al-Khayyam (1048–1131): geometric solutions of cubic equations.
Mission 1

Medicine

  • Hadith: God has not sent down a disease without sending down its cure (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Medicine, no. 5678). Seeking cures is part of trusting God.
  • Hospitals (bimaristan) in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo treated patients free, with wards and teaching.
  • Al-Razi (c. 865–925): clinical case notes; first clear account distinguishing smallpox from measles.
  • Ibn Sina (980–1037), The Canon of Medicine (c. 1025): a systematic medical encyclopaedia, used in European universities for about five centuries.
  • Al-Zahrawi (c. 936–1013): surgery, with drawings of instruments.
  • Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288): described the passage of blood through the lungs (pulmonary circulation).
Mission 1

Astronomy and optics

  • Observatories: Maragha (founded 1259) and Samarkand (1420s, under Ulugh Beg).
  • Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274) and Ibn al-Shatir (1304–1375), timekeeper of the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, built models of planetary motion that appear in mathematically similar form in Copernicus. How they reached him is still debated.
  • Al-Biruni (973–c. 1050): measured the radius of the Earth.
  • Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), Book of Optics: sight happens when light enters the eye; he tested claims with controlled experiments, so he is often called a pioneer of experimental method.

Mission 1

Philosophy and its challenge: al-Ghazali

  • Falsafa (philosophy in the Greek tradition): al-Kindi (c. 801–873), al-Farabi (c. 870–950) and Ibn Sina tried to unite Aristotle with Islam.
  • Challenge: Aristotle taught an eternal world and necessary causes. Does that leave room for a Creator who acts freely?
  • Al-Ghazali, The Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095): on three points the philosophers fall into unbelief: the eternity of the world; God knowing only universals; denial of bodily resurrection. Seventeen other points are innovation.
  • He accepted mathematics and logic and criticised Muslims who rejected proven results out of zeal.
  • Causation: fire does not burn cotton by its own power; God creates the burning when they meet. Nature's regularity is God's custom ('ada), which God may break (miracles).
  • Debate: did this slow science, or free it from Aristotle's fixed causes? (See the later screen.)
  • Al-Ghazali's own turn to Sufism.
Mission 1

Did Muslim science decline after al-Ghazali?

  • The old "decline" story (e.g. Ernest Renan, 1883): orthodoxy, led by al-Ghazali, killed science after the 11th century.
  • Historians now: major work came after al-Ghazali: Maragha (1259), Ibn al-Nafis, Ibn al-Shatir, Samarkand (1420s). George Saliba, Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance (2007).
  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), Science and Civilization in Islam (1968): Islamic science saw nature as a unity reflecting Tawhid; it studied the world as sacred, unlike later secular science (paraphrase).
  • Later slowdowns had many causes: the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258), shifts in trade and wealth, late adoption of printing.

Mission 1Evaluate

Was the medieval achievement driven by Islam?

  • Claim: the medieval Muslim contribution to science shows that Islam itself encourages science.
  • For: the Qur'an calls nature "signs" and commands reflection; worship needed astronomy and mathematics; hadith on knowledge and cures.
  • For: many scientists were also religious scholars (Ibn al-Shatir a mosque timekeeper; Ibn al-Nafis wrote on law and hadith).
  • Against: much built on Greek, Indian and Persian learning; Christians, Jews and Sabians took part; courts, not mosques, paid for much of it.
  • Against: some scholars distrusted philosophy; science later slowed in the same societies.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "Islam" its texts, its scholars or its civilisation? Does motivation matter, or only results? What else explains the later slowdown?

Mission 2

Two thinkers, one question

  • Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111), from Tus in Persia: jurist, theologian, later Sufi. Head teacher at the Nizamiyya college in Baghdad from 1091.
  • Ibn Rushd (Averroes, 1126–1198), from Córdoba in al-Andalus: judge (qadi), doctor and the great commentator on Aristotle.
  • The question: Greek philosophy (falsafa) had become part of Islamic learning. Should Muslims adopt it, and how far?
  • Al-Ghazali: use its tools, reject its errors, and look beyond reason to experience of God.
  • Ibn Rushd: philosophy, rightly done, is commanded by the law, and cannot contradict revelation.
Mission 2

Three points of unbelief

  • In The Incoherence of the Philosophers (1095) al-Ghazali listed twenty errors: three are unbelief (kufr), seventeen are innovation.
  • 1. The world is eternal: it denies that God created it in time.
  • 2. God knows only universals, not particular events: it denies God's knowledge of each person and act.
  • 3. Only souls, not bodies, are resurrected: it denies the Qur'an's bodily resurrection.
  • He repeats these three in the Munqidh as the points where philosophers must be judged unbelievers.

Mission 2

Ibn Rushd: the law commands philosophy

  • The Decisive Treatise (Fasl al-Maqal, c. 1180): a legal ruling on philosophy.
  • The Qur'an commands reflection on creation: "So learn a lesson, O ye who have eyes!" (Qur'an 59:2, Pickthall); "Have they not considered the dominion of the heavens and the earth" (Qur'an 7:185, Pickthall).
  • Reflection at its best is demonstration (burhan), the method of philosophy. So philosophy is obligatory or recommended for those able to do it.
  • Learning from non-Muslims (the Greeks) is like using a knife for a sacrifice: what matters is whether the tool is sound, not who made it.
  • Truth does not contradict truth: where scripture seems to clash with demonstration, scripture is to be interpreted (ta'wil).

Both men used reason and loved the Qur'an: they differed on whether demonstration or experience is the summit of knowing God.

Mission 3

Modern science meets Islam

  • Modern European science reached Muslim lands in the 19th century, often with colonial power (Napoleon in Egypt, 1798; British India).
  • It stressed evidence and reason, natural laws, deep time and evolution.
  • Muslim reformers welcomed it as their own heritage returning:
  • Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898), founder of the Aligarh college (1875): the Word of God (Qur'an) and the Work of God (nature) cannot contradict.
  • Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/9–1897): answering Ernest Renan (1883), he argued Muslims must master modern science.
  • Others feared science came bundled with materialism and Western dominance.
Mission 3Read the source

Creation in the Qur'an

> "Have not those who disbelieve known that the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them, and we made every living thing of water?" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall)

> "Then turned He to the heaven when it was smoke" (Qur'an 41:11, Pickthall)

> "We have built the heaven with might, and We it is Who make the vast extent (thereof)." (Qur'an 51:47, Pickthall)

  • Creation took "six Days" (7:54), but "a Day with Allah is as a thousand years of what ye reckon" (Qur'an 22:47, Pickthall): many Muslims read the days as ages.

Mission 3

Big Bang, Steady State, oscillating universe

  • Big Bang: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a hot, dense state and is expanding.
  • Many Muslims welcome it: a beginning fits God as Originator (2:117); 21:30 and 41:11 seem to describe a single mass and "smoke".
  • Steady State (Hoyle, Bondi and Gold, 1948): an eternal universe with no beginning, matter created continually. Most Muslims see a universe without a beginning as clashing with creation, though some link continual creation to God's constant sustaining (50:15 "a new creation").
  • Oscillating universe: endless cycles of expansion and collapse. Some cite "As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it" (21:104); but endless cycles with no first beginning would clash with creation.
  • Some Muslims warn against tying the Qur'an to any theory, since theories change.

Mission 3Diversity

Responses to Darwin

  • Muslims have debated Darwin since the 1870s. Three broad positions today:
  • Rejection (creationism): species, and above all humans, were created directly; evolution is seen as materialist. Best-known: "Harun Yahya" (pen name of Adnan Oktar), Atlas of Creation (2006).
  • Evolution, except humans: plants and animals evolved, but Adam was created directly from clay (15:28; 3:59). Sometimes called "Adamic exceptionalism".
  • Full acceptance: evolution is God's way of creating, as rain is His way of growing crops; "He created you by (divers) stages" (Qur'an 71:14, Pickthall). E.g. the astrophysicist Nidhal Guessoum, Islam's Quantum Question (2011).
  • Common ground: all three agree that God is Creator and that humans have a purpose as khalifa (2:30). They differ on how God created.
  • Al-Afghani criticised "materialist" Darwinism (Refutation of the Materialists, 1881) while urging science; the debate is about worldview as much as biology.

Mission 3

Maurice Bucaille

  • Maurice Bucaille (1920–1998), French physician, The Bible, the Qur'an and Science (1976).
  • His claims (paraphrase): the Qur'an contains no statement that contradicts established modern science, while the Bible does (e.g. Genesis' chronology).
  • It contains statements no 7th-century person could have known: embryo stages (23:12–14), life from water (21:30), the heavens as smoke (41:11), widening of the heaven (51:47), a barrier between two seas (55:19–20).
  • So the Qur'an must be from God.
  • His book was hugely popular; it helped inspire a genre of "scientific miracles of the Qur'an" (i'jaz 'ilmi).
Mission 3

Critics of Bucaille; Nasr's view

  • Reading in: modern meanings are read back into Arabic words ('alaqa, musi'un) that earlier commentators understood differently.
  • Changing science: if faith is tied to today's theory, what happens when the theory changes?
  • Selective: verses that seem not to fit are passed over.
  • Purpose: the Qur'an calls itself guidance (2:2), not a science textbook (e.g. Ziauddin Sardar's critique of "Bucaillism").
  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), e.g. Man and Nature (1968): Bucaille-style arguments let modern science judge the Qur'an. Modern Western science has desacralised nature, treating it as matter without meaning, which helped cause the environmental crisis. Muslims should build science on a sacred view of nature rooted in Tawhid (paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Science in Islam today

  • Science is neutral; adopt it: follow evidence; the Qur'an gives values, science gives facts (in the line of Sayyid Ahmad Khan; many Muslim scientists).
  • Scientific miracles: modern discoveries confirm the Qur'an (Bucaille; popular preaching).
  • Islamise knowledge: rebuild the sciences on Islamic principles (Isma'il al-Faruqi, Islamization of Knowledge, 1982).
  • Sacred science (Nasr): recover a traditional, metaphysical science of nature; critique modern science's worldview, not its facts.
  • Reject parts: accept technology but reject theories seen as contradicting scripture (e.g. human evolution).

Mission 3Evaluate

Is Islam compatible with modern science?

  • Claim: Islam and modern science are fully compatible.
  • For: the Qur'an commands study of creation (3:190); a beginning fits the Big Bang; many Muslim scientists see no conflict; a rich history (see Mission 1).
  • For: God works through causes; evolution or expansion can be God's method.
  • Against: a literal Adam created from clay clashes with common descent; Steady State-type eternity clashes with creation.
  • Against: modern science's naturalism (only natural causes count) can clash with belief in God's constant action and miracles.
  • Judgement criteria: Compatible with the Qur'an, or with a reading of it? With scientific results, or with naturalism as a worldview? Does reading verses as predictions help or harm faith?

Mission 4

Jihad means striving

  • Jihad: from j-h-d, "to strive, to make an effort". Not the word for "holy war".
  • "And those who strive in Our (cause),- We will certainly guide them to our Paths" (Qur'an 29:69, Yusuf Ali).
  • Forms of jihad: of the self (nafs); of the tongue and pen (speaking truth, teaching); of wealth (giving); and armed struggle.
  • "Strive in His cause as ye ought to strive" (Qur'an 22:78, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tradition: greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar): struggle with the self; lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar): armed struggle.
Mission 4

Greater jihad: the nafs

  • Nafs: the self, the ego with its desires.
  • Three states named from the Qur'an: the soul inciting to evil (12:53); the self-reproaching soul, conscience (75:2); the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Greater jihad: lifelong effort to move the self towards peace with God: prayer, fasting, honesty, controlling anger and greed.
  • The Prophet is reported to have said that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for God (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • A popular report calls returning from battle a return "from the lesser jihad to the greater" (al-Bayhaqi; paraphrase; many scholars grade it weak).
  • S. H. Nasr: inner jihad comes first; outward action is right only when it flows from a self in submission (paraphrase).

Mission 4

Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time

  • Makkah (610–622): persecution, but no fighting; patience and preaching.
  • 622: the hijrah to Madinah. Permission to fight comes because the Muslims "are wronged" (22:39).
  • Battles: Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627).
  • 628: Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace accepted on hard terms.
  • 630: Makkah taken almost without fighting; a general amnesty.
  • Jurists made lesser jihad a communal duty of the ummah (fard kifaya), led by legitimate authority; it becomes an individual duty if the land is invaded.

Mission 4

Conditions of lesser jihad

  • Just cause: defence, or ending oppression of the weak (4:75), never forcing faith: "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a recognised leader, not by individuals.
  • Right intention: for God and justice, not gain or revenge.
  • Last resort and proportion: "do not transgress limits" (2:190).
  • Protect non-combatants: the Prophet forbade killing women and children (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase); Abu Bakr told his commanders not to kill the old or children, cut fruit trees or destroy buildings (Malik, Muwatta; paraphrase).
  • Stop when the enemy seeks peace (8:61); treat prisoners humanely.

Mission 4Diversity

Defensive, expansionist, suspended

  • Classical Sunni jurists (c. 8th–12th centuries) divided the world into dar al-Islam and dar al-harb and allowed wars of expansion led by the caliph, alongside truces.
  • Many modern Sunni scholars read the Qur'an as permitting defensive war only; expansion reflected the empires of its time.
  • Twelver Shi'a: most jurists hold that offensive jihad needs the presence of the infallible Imam, so it is suspended during the Occultation; defensive jihad remains.
  • Sufi teachers stress the inner jihad above all.
  • Ahmadiyya Muslims teach that armed jihad for religion is not permitted today; jihad of the pen is the duty of the age.
Mission 4

Modern warfare and WMD

  • Nuclear, chemical and biological weapons cannot tell soldiers from civilians: they break non-combatant protection.
  • They destroy crops, animals and land for generations: compare the condemnation of one who would "destroy crops and cattle" (2:205).
  • So most scholars hold their use haram; some allow possession only as a deterrent.
  • Iran's Supreme Leader is reported to have issued a fatwa forbidding nuclear weapons; Pakistan holds them for deterrence.
  • Other problems today: who is the "legitimate authority" when Muslims live in many nation-states? Drones and remote war? Wars between Muslim states?
Mission 4

Terrorism is not jihad

  • Terrorism: violence against civilians to spread fear for political goals.
  • Mainstream scholars across Sunni and Shi'a Islam reject calling it jihad: no legitimate authority, civilians targeted, suicide forbidden (4:29).
  • "If any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people" (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Examples: the Amman Message (2004–05); Tahir-ul-Qadri's Fatwa on Terrorism and Suicide Bombings (2010); scholars' open letter to the leader of ISIS (2014).
  • Groups that use the word for terror are a small minority, condemned by most Muslim bodies; many of their victims are Muslims.
Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

Is lesser jihad still relevant?

  • Claim: The teachings on lesser jihad have no relevance today.
  • For: modern weapons make the conditions almost impossible to meet.
  • For: the misuse of the word by terrorists harms Muslims; some argue the inner jihad should be stressed instead.
  • Against: the conditions are a just-war ethic that can limit modern war: protect civilians, accept peace, use proportion.
  • Against: Muslims still face invasion and oppression; the right of defence (22:39) still matters.
  • Judge it by: Are the principles separable from the 7th-century setting? Who has authority now? Does stressing the greater jihad answer the misuse?

Mission 5

Two scholars, two questions

  • Majid Khadduri (1909–2007), Iraqi-born scholar of Islamic and international law, Johns Hopkins University, Washington. War and Peace in the Law of Islam (1955); extract: chapters 5 "The Doctrine of Jihad" and 6 "Types of Jihad".
  • His question: what did classical Islamic law teach about war, and why?
  • Muhammad Sa'id Ramadan al-Buti (1929–2013), Syrian Sunni scholar and preacher, dean of shari'a at Damascus University. Jihad in Islam: How to Understand and Practice It (Arabic 1993; English 1995); extract: chapter 2.
  • His question: what does Islam require of Muslims about jihad, and how is it being misused?
  • Edexcel anthology: paraphrase only; cite the scholar and the extract.
Mission 5

Khadduri: the classical doctrine

  • Khadduri describes jihad as Islam's doctrine of the just war (bellum justum): war is lawful only for a religious and just purpose.
  • Classical jurists divided the world into dar al-Islam (the territory of Islam) and dar al-harb (the territory of war).
  • In legal theory, the two were in a permanent state of war, though not of constant fighting; truces could suspend it.
  • Jihad was a collective duty (fard kifaya) of the community, directed by the imam (head of state).
  • He reads the doctrine as a product of an expanding empire, later modified as Muslim states lived alongside others.
Mission 5

Khadduri: ways and types

  • Jurists said jihad could be fulfilled in four ways: by the heart (fighting the devil and one's own evil), the tongue and the hand (enjoining good, forbidding wrong), and the sword (fighting).
  • So the inner struggle is part of the doctrine, but his focus is the legal, outward kind.
  • Types of jihad in the law books: against unbelievers outside the state; against apostates; against rebels (baghi); against highway robbers and those who spread disorder.
  • Each type had its own rules about who may be fought, captives and property.

Mission 5

al-Buti: jihad begins with the word

  • al-Buti: jihad is all effort to raise God's word, and its first form was da'wa (calling people to God) in the Makkan years, before any fighting.
  • He points to the Makkan command to strive against unbelievers "with the (Qur'an)" (25:52): a jihad of argument, not arms.
  • That jihad of the word never stops; armed jihad is one form, under conditions.
  • He wrote against two mistakes: critics who say Islam was spread by the sword, and militants who reduce jihad to fighting.
Mission 5

al-Buti: why fight at all?

  • The cause of fighting, he argues, is aggression against Muslims (hiraba), not unbelief (kufr).
  • So peaceful non-Muslims must not be fought: God does not forbid kindness and justice to those who do not fight Muslims (60:8).
  • "No compulsion in religion" (2:256): faith cannot be imposed by war.
  • Armed jihad must be declared by legitimate authority; groups that fight on their own authority bring disorder (fitna), not jihad.
  • His context: Syria's conflicts between the state and armed Islamist groups.

Two kinds of striving

Mission 5

The nafs in both writers

  • 4D-5.1b: subjection of the nafs: the constant struggle to live in submission (islam) to Allah.
  • Khadduri: the jihad "of the heart" against the devil and the self is the first of the jurists' four ways.
  • al-Buti: the jihad of the self and of da'wa are the ground of all others; without them, fighting becomes pride or politics.
  • S. H. Nasr (b. 1933): the inner, greater jihad is primary; outer action is right only when it reflects inner balance and submission (paraphrase).
  • Source: "Truly he succeeds that purifies it" (Qur'an 91:9, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 5Diversity

Comparing the two

  • Method: Khadduri, a historian of law describing classical texts · al-Buti, a jurist-preacher saying what Muslims must do.
  • Cause of war: Khadduri's jurists, extending dar al-Islam · al-Buti, repelling aggression.
  • Key verses: classical weight on 9:5 and 9:29 · al-Buti stresses 25:52, 60:8, 2:256 and reads 9:5 in context.
  • Authority: both: the imam or ruler, not individuals.
  • Inner jihad: Khadduri, one of four ways · al-Buti, the foundation.
  • Purpose of writing: Khadduri, explaining Islamic law to a Western readership in the 1950s · al-Buti, answering militants and critics in the 1990s.
Mission 5Evaluate

Whose account is closer to Islam?

  • Claim: al-Buti's account of jihad is truer to Islam than the classical doctrine Khadduri describes.
  • For al-Buti: it rests on the Qur'an's own limits (2:190, 2:256, 60:8) and the Makkan priority of da'wa.
  • For al-Buti: it fits how most Muslims today live as citizens of nation-states.
  • For Khadduri's picture: it reports what the great jurists actually wrote for centuries; al-Buti may read modern values back into the texts.
  • For Khadduri's picture: a historian's description is not a verdict on Islam; the doctrine itself was a just-war theory with limits.
  • Judge it by: Should Islam be judged by its texts, its jurists or its history? Does a modern reading betray or recover the sources? Are they even answering the same question?
Mission 5Worked answer

Clarify and compare

Task: Compare al-Buti's and Khadduri's views of lesser jihad.

  • Clarify Khadduri: classical law saw jihad as a just war between dar al-Islam and dar al-harb, a collective duty led by the imam (extract, ch. 5).
  • Clarify al-Buti: jihad begins in da'wa; armed jihad answers aggression, not unbelief, under legitimate authority (extract, ch. 2).
  • Compare: both require authority and limits; they differ on the cause of war and on whether the classical doctrine binds today.
  • Source: 60:8 supports al-Buti; the classical weight on 9:29 lies behind the doctrine Khadduri describes.
  • Skill tip: paraphrase the extract, name it, then link it to a Qur'anic passage.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Qur'an calls creation "signs" and urges reflection (3:190, 88:17); hadith make seeking knowledge a duty; al-Ghazali made medicine and arithmetic communal duties.
  • Medieval Muslim scholars translated and extended Greek, Persian and Indian learning: algebra (al-Khwarizmi), medicine (al-Razi, Ibn Sina, Ibn al-Nafis), astronomy and optics (al-Tusi, Ibn al-Shatir, Ibn al-Haytham).
  • Historians now reject the idea that science stopped after al-Ghazali.

Mission 2

  • Al-Ghazali finds unbelief in three of the philosophers' claims: an eternal world, God knowing only universals, and resurrection of souls only.
  • Ibn Rushd: the law commands philosophy; truth cannot contradict truth.

Mission 3

  • Modern science arrived with colonial power; reformers (Sayyid Ahmad Khan, al-Afghani) welcomed it as compatible with Islam.
  • Big Bang: many welcome a beginning (21:30, 41:11, 51:47); most Muslims see Steady State and endless cycles as clashing with creation.
  • Darwin: rejection, evolution with a human exception, full acceptance.
  • Bucaille: the Qur'an anticipated science; critics: reading in, changing theories.
  • Nasr: modern science desacralises nature.

Mission 4

  • Jihad = striving: greater (against the nafs) and lesser (armed, under conditions).
  • Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time was a response to persecution and attack, limited by the Qur'an (22:39, 2:190, 8:61) and the Prophet's rules.
  • Most scholars hold WMD and terrorism break the conditions; whether lesser jihad is relevant today is debated.

Mission 5

  • Khadduri (1955): jihad as the classical just-war doctrine: dar al-Islam/dar al-harb, collective duty, four ways (heart, tongue, hand, sword), types of jihad.
  • al-Buti (1993): jihad begins with da'wa; fighting answers aggression, not unbelief, only under legitimate authority.
  • Both root outer jihad in the struggle with the nafs; Nasr makes the inner jihad primary.
