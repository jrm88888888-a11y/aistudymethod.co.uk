Unit 2 · The mix

The 7Ps of the marketing mix

The marketing mix is the set of controllable variables the business uses to position its product. The traditional 4Ps extend to 7Ps for services:

  • Product — features, quality, design, branding, packaging.
  • Price — the strategy and tactics used to set price.
  • Place — distribution channels: direct, retailer, wholesaler, e-commerce, multichannel.
  • Promotion — advertising, sales promotion, PR, personal selling, digital and social media.
  • People — staff skills and service; critical where staff are the product.
  • Process — how the service is delivered: queueing, booking, ordering, returns.
  • Physical evidence — the tangible cues of an intangible service: the store, uniforms, packaging, website.

Integration is the mark-scoring point: the Ps must be consistent. A premium price with cut-price packaging, sold in a discount shed, destroys the positioning. Judge the mix as a whole.

Unit 2 · Product

The product life cycle and extension strategies

The product life cycle plots sales over time: development → introduction → growth → maturity → decline.

Extension strategies delay decline: new packaging, new flavours/variants, a new target market, price cuts, added features, more promotion.

Link to finance: a launch consumes cash (heavy promotion, low sales). Mature products fund the launches. That is exactly the logic behind the Boston matrix.

Unit 2 · Portfolio

The Boston matrix

The Boston (BCG) matrix analyses a portfolio of products on two axes: relative market share and market growth.

High market shareLow market share
High growthStar — invest heavily; cash-neutral at bestQuestion mark / problem child — invest or divest?
Low growthCash cow — milk it; funds the starsDog — divest, or keep only if it supports the range

A healthy portfolio is balanced: cash cows generate the cash that funds stars and selected question marks, so there is a pipeline of tomorrow's cash cows.

Unit 2 · Price

Pricing strategies

  • Cost-plus (mark-up) pricing — add a percentage mark-up to unit cost. Simple, guarantees a margin; ignores competitors and demand.
  • Penetration pricing — launch low to win share fast; relies on price-elastic demand and later price rises.
  • Price skimming — launch high to recoup R&D from early adopters, then fall (new tech).
  • Competitive / price-matching — set price by reference to rivals; common in oligopoly.
  • Predatory pricing — price below cost to drive rivals out. Illegal in the UK under competition law.
  • Price discrimination — different prices to different groups for the same product (peak rail fares, student tickets).
  • Psychological pricing — £9.99 rather than £10.
  • Dynamic pricing — algorithmic, real-time pricing by demand (airlines, ride-hailing).
cost-plus price = unit cost × (1 + mark-up %)

Unit 2 · Elasticity

Price elasticity of demand (PED)

PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in pricePED is negative — we usually quote the magnitude and ignore the sign
  • |PED| > 1 — price elastic: demand is sensitive. Cutting price raises total revenue; raising price cuts it.
  • |PED| < 1 — price inelastic: demand is insensitive. Raising price raises total revenue.
  • |PED| = 1 — unitary: revenue unchanged.

Demand is more inelastic when there are few substitutes, the brand is strong, the product is a necessity or addictive, and the price is a small share of income.

Worked example

Price rises from £20 to £22 → % change in price = (2 ÷ 20) × 100 = +10%

Demand falls from 40,000 to 34,000 → % change in quantity = (−6,000 ÷ 40,000) × 100 = −15%

PED = −15 ÷ 10 = −1.5 → elastic, so the price rise will cut total revenue.

Unit 2 · Elasticity

Income elasticity of demand (YED)

YED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in real income
  • YED positive → normal good. If YED > 1 it is a luxury (demand rises faster than income: restaurant meals, new cars).
  • YED negative → inferior good: demand falls as incomes rise (value-range food, bus travel).

Why it matters: a business selling luxuries is highly exposed to recession. Knowing YED helps a firm forecast demand across the economic cycle and balance its product portfolio.

Unit 2 · Forecasting

Sales forecasting — moving averages

A time series of sales contains a trend, seasonal variation, cyclical movements and random shocks. A moving average smooths out the noise so the trend is visible.

3-period moving average = (period 1 + period 2 + period 3) ÷ 3the answer is centred on the middle period
Worked example

Sales (£000): Jan 240 · Feb 260 · Mar 286

3-month moving average centred on February = (240 + 260 + 286) ÷ 3 = 786 ÷ 3 = 262

Extrapolation projects the past trend forward. It is quick and cheap, but assumes the future behaves like the past — dangerous after a shock, a new entrant or a change in tastes.

Unit 2 · Performance

Judging marketing performance

Marketing spend must be justified. Common measures:

  • Sales volume and value growth against target.
  • Market share — the sharpest test, because it strips out market-wide growth.
  • Customer acquisition cost and customer retention rate (retaining is far cheaper than acquiring).
  • Conversion rate and click-through rate for digital campaigns.
  • Brand awareness and customer satisfaction / NPS scores.

Evaluation: a rise in sales does not prove the campaign worked — the whole market may have grown, or a rival may have withdrawn. Compare against market share and the counterfactual.

Unit 2 · Performance

Measuring the customer experience

Alongside sales and market share, H436 expects you to interpret customer-facing measures:

  • Complaints — the number and type; a falling complaint rate suggests improving quality, but silence can also mean customers have simply left.
  • Customer feedback and reviews — public, powerful and hard to control; a single viral complaint can outweigh a year of advertising.
  • Net promoter score (NPS) — the percentage of promoters minus the percentage of detractors: how likely customers are to recommend you. It links directly to the advocacy stage of the customer journey.
