Mission 1

Mission 1: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Make a guess: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Mission 1

Households and firms

  • The simplest model has two groups:
    • Households: own the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and buy goods and services.
    • Firms: hire factors and produce goods and services.
  • Two flows go round in opposite directions:
    • Real flow: factor services → firms; goods and services → households.
    • Money flow: factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) → households; spending → firms.
  • Assumptions of this simple model: no saving, no government, no trade. Households spend all their income.
Mission 1

Income = output = expenditure

  • Illustrative data. In one year, firms produce final goods worth 500 billion.
  • Households buy them: expenditure = 500 billion.
  • Firms pay out what they receive:
    • wages 320 + rent 40 + interest 30 = 390 billion
    • the rest is profit: 500 − 390 = 110 billion, income to the firms' owners.
  • Income = 390 + 110 = 500 billion.
  • Because profit is whatever is left, total income must equal the value of output.
  • And every unit spent is someone's income. So:
    • national output = national income = national expenditure
  • Three ways to measure national income: output (value added), income (factor incomes), expenditure (spending on final output).
  • In real data, the three measures differ a little, because of measurement errors (a "statistical discrepancy").

Mission 1

Withdrawals and injections

  • Leakages (also called withdrawals): income not passed on as spending on domestic output.
    • Saving (S), Taxation (T), Imports (M).
  • Injections: spending on domestic output that does not come from households' consumption.
    • Investment (I) by firms, Government spending (G), Exports (X).
  • Withdrawals shrink the flow; injections enlarge it.
  • Say: "investment is spending by firms on capital goods" — not: buying shares, which is saving.
  • Say: "government spending (G) on goods and services" — not: transfer payments, which only move income between households.
Mission 1

The five sectors

  • Households: supply factors; spend, save, pay tax, buy imports.
  • Firms: produce; pay factor incomes; invest.
  • Government: collects taxes (T); buys goods and services (G); pays transfers.
  • Banks and the financial sector: take households' savings; lend to firms for investment.
  • Foreign sector: buys exports (X); sells imports (M).
  • Every sector depends on the others: one group's spending is another's income.
Quick check
?Which of these is an injection into the circular flow of Canada?
Mission 1

Equilibrium

  • National income is in equilibrium when total withdrawals (W) = total injections (J):
    • S + T + M = I + G + X
  • Injections > withdrawals: more is entering the flow than leaving → national income rises.
  • Withdrawals > injections: more is leaving than entering → national income falls.
  • As income changes, withdrawals change too (more income → more saving, tax, imports), until W = J again.
  • The pairs need not match: S can be above I, as long as the totals are equal.

  • Illustrative data, billions:

Mission 1

Case: spring 2020

  • April 2020, United States, during COVID-19 lockdowns (first estimates):
    • Household spending fell 13.6% in one month.
    • Households saved 33% of disposable income (12.7% in March).
  • More saving = a bigger withdrawal. Less spending = less income for firms and their workers.
  • Personal income actually rose 10.5%, mainly from government benefit payments: a reminder that transfers move income but are not G.
  • Many countries saw the same pattern: saving up, spending and output down.

Income and output are the same flow seen from two sides. Profit is the balancing item, so total income always equals the value of output.

Mission 2: If a country's output is worth more money this year, has it really produced more?

Make a guess: If a country's output is worth more money this year, has it really produced more?

Mission 2

What GDP counts

  • Gross domestic product (GDP): the total value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a period (usually a year or a quarter).
  • Final goods: bought by the end user. Intermediate goods (steel for a car, flour for a baker) are not added separately: their value is already in the final price.
  • Within a country: a foreign-owned factory's output counts where the factory is.
  • Not counted:
    • second-hand sales (the car was counted when it was new)
    • transfer payments (pensions, unemployment benefits): money moves, nothing is produced
    • buying shares or bonds: a change of ownership, not production
  • Say: "final goods and services" — not: "everything that is sold".
Quick check
?Which of these is counted in a country's GDP for this year?
Mission 2

Three measures, one total

  • The circular flow: every unit spent on output becomes someone's income.
  • So national output, income and expenditure measure the same flow:
    • Output method: add the value added by every industry.
    • Income method: add factor incomes: wages, rent, interest, profit.
    • Expenditure method: add spending on final output: C + I + G + (X − M).
  • In practice the data come from different surveys, so the totals differ slightly: a statistical discrepancy.
  • Say: "national output = national income = national expenditure" — not: three different sizes of economy.
Mission 2

Worked example: value added

  • Illustrative data. One loaf's journey (assume the farmer buys no inputs):
StageSells forBuys inputs forValue added
Farmer: wheat40040
Miller: flour704030
Baker: bread1207050
  • Value added = sales − cost of bought-in inputs.
  • Sum of value added = 40 + 30 + 50 = 120 = the price of the final loaf.
  • Adding every sale = 40 + 70 + 120 = 230: the wheat and flour are counted twice. That is double counting.
Mission 2

Nominal and real

  • Nominal GDP: output valued at current prices. It rises if output rises or if prices rise.
  • Real GDP: output valued at constant prices. Only changes in the quantity of goods and services move it.
  • Real GDP strips out inflation, so it is the measure of economic growth.
  • Rough rule: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation (exact method next).
  • Say: "real GDP" when you mean output — not: "GDP went up, so output went up".
Mission 2

Worked example: the GDP deflator

  • The GDP deflator is a price index for everything in GDP. Base year = 100.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator.
  • Illustrative data (billions):
Nominal GDPDeflatorReal GDP (Year 1 prices)
Year 1800100800
Year 2880105838.1
  • Year 2 real GDP = 880 × 100 ÷ 105 = 838.1.
  • Nominal growth = (880 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 10%.
  • Real growth = (838.1 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 4.76%: about half the headline rise was higher prices.
Mission 2Graph lab

Money up, output up?

Illustrative data. The money flow around the economy = price level × volume of output.

  • Takeaway: nominal GDP can rise with no extra output at all; only real GDP tracks the quantity produced.
Mission 2

Per person, per worker, per year

  • Real GDP per capita = real GDP ÷ population. A bigger total can hide a smaller share per person.
  • Illustrative data, from the deflator example: Year 1, 800 billion ÷ 40 million people = 20,000 per person. Year 2, 838.1 billion ÷ 41 million = 20,441: up only 2.2%.
  • Rate of economic growth = (real GDP this year − real GDP last year) ÷ real GDP last year × 100.
  • Always use real figures, and divide by the earlier year.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's real GDP was 500 billion last year and 520 billion this year. What is its rate of economic growth?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A country's nominal GDP rose from 2,000 billion to 2,120 billion. Over the same year, its GDP deflator rose from 100 to 108. What happened to real GDP?

A bigger money figure is not more output. Remove the rise in prices first.

Mission 3

Aggregate demand

  • Aggregate demand (AD): total planned spending on a country's goods and services at each price level, in a given time period.
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Who spends: households (C), firms (I), government (G), the rest of the world (X), minus spending that goes abroad (M).
  • Axes: "Price level" (vertical) and "Real national output (Y)" or "Real GDP" (horizontal).
  • The AD curve slopes down: at a lower price level, more real output is demanded.
  • Say: "price level" — not: "price", on a macro diagram.
Mission 3

The four components

  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Investment (I): firms' spending on capital goods: machines, buildings, equipment. (Plus new housing.)
  • Government spending (G): on goods and services, e.g. teachers' pay, roads. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not in G: they are spent later as C.
  • Net exports (X − M): foreigners' spending on our exports minus our spending on imports.
  • Saving is income not spent. Investment is spending on new capital. Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • C is by far the largest component. Real data: consumer spending was 67.5% of US GDP in 2023. It is also well over half of GDP in the UK.
  • Investment is smaller but swings far more over the business cycle.
Quick check
?Which of these is counted as investment (I) in aggregate demand?
Mission 3

Why the AD curve slopes down

  • AD is not a market demand curve. When the price level rises, there are no cheaper home-produced goods to switch to.
  • Instead, a higher price level cuts real output demanded through three effects:
  • Wealth (real balance) effect: money and savings buy less, so households feel poorer → C falls.
  • Interest rate effect: buying the same goods needs more money → demand for money rises → interest rates rise → borrowing costs more → C and I fall.
  • International trade effect: home goods become dearer relative to foreign goods → X falls, M rises.
  • A lower price level works the other way: real output demanded rises.
Mission 3

Movement along AD or shift of AD?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD.
  • Price level up: a contraction of real output demanded. Price level down: an extension.
  • A change in anything else that changes C, I, G or X − M → a shift of AD.
  • More spending at every price level: AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2). Less: AD shifts left.
  • The three effects of the previous screen explain the slope. They are never reasons for a shift.
  • Say: "a rise in the price level causes a contraction along AD" — not: "a rise in the price level shifts AD left".
Quick check
?A rise in world oil prices pushes up the general price level in a country. Nothing else that affects spending changes. How should this be shown on the country's AD diagram?
Mission 3

What shifts C and I

Consumption (C) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Disposable income rises (e.g. income taxes are cut).
  • Interest rates fall: borrowing is cheaper, saving earns less.
  • Wealth rises (house or share prices go up).
  • Consumer confidence improves.
  • Household debt is lower, or credit is easier to get.
  • People expect prices to rise, so they buy now.

Investment (I) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Interest rates fall: projects cost less to finance.
  • Business confidence improves: firms expect more sales and profit.
  • New technology makes new equipment worth buying.
  • Business taxes (e.g. on profits) are cut.
  • Corporate debt is lower, so firms can borrow more easily.

The reverse of each shifts AD left.

Mission 3

What shifts G and net exports

  • G: set by government priorities and fiscal policy, e.g. more spending on health or roads → AD right.
  • Exports (X) rise when:
    • Incomes abroad rise (trading partners grow; the world economy booms).
    • The currency depreciates: exports become cheaper abroad.
    • Other countries cut trade barriers (e.g. remove tariffs).
    • Non-price factors improve: quality, design, reliability, reputation.
  • Imports (M) rise, cutting X − M, when:
    • Real income at home rises: people buy more of everything, including imports.
    • The currency appreciates: imports become cheaper at home.
    • The country removes its own tariffs or quotas.
  • Say: "a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home" — not the other way round.
Mission 3Graph lab

Movement or shift?

  • Takeaway: the price level moves the economy along AD; a change in the interest rate set by the central bank shifts AD.
Quick check
?A country's main export markets fall into recession. What is the most likely effect on the country's aggregate demand?
Mission 3Evaluate

How far will AD shift?

It depends on …

  • Which component changes: C is the largest part of spending in most economies (often well over half), so a small % change in C moves AD a lot. In very open economies, X − M matters more.
  • Confidence and debt: a cut in interest rates may barely lift spending if households and firms are pessimistic or heavily in debt.
  • Time lags: changes in interest rates or taxes take months to affect spending.
  • Offsetting changes: higher G paid for by higher taxes cuts C; extra spending may leak into imports.
  • Judge: the direction of a shift is usually clear; its size is uncertain. What it does to output and prices also depends on aggregate supply.
Mission check

?The general price level in a country falls by 3% (illustrative data). Nothing else that affects spending changes. What happens to real output (real GDP) demanded, and why?

AD slopes down because of what the price level does to real wealth, interest rates and trade, not because buyers swap one home good for another.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What makes a whole country suddenly stop spending, even when pay has not changed?

Make a guess: What makes a whole country suddenly stop spending, even when pay has not changed?

Mission 4

Spend it or save it

  • Disposable income (Yd): income after direct taxes, plus state benefits (transfers).
  • Households do two things with it: consume or save.
  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Saving (S): disposable income not spent. Yd = C + S.
  • C is the largest part of AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Saving is a withdrawal (leakage) from the circular flow.
Mission 4

Average and marginal propensities

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) = ΔC ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income spent.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS) = ΔS ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income saved.
  • Every extra unit of disposable income is spent or saved, so MPC + MPS = 1.
  • Average propensity to consume (APC) = C ÷ Yd. The share of all income spent.
  • Average propensity to save (APS) = S ÷ Yd. APC + APS = 1.
  • Say: "MPC is the change in consumption over the change in income" — not: "consumption over income". That is an average, not a marginal, share.
Mission 4

Worked example: propensities from data

Illustrative data (one household, per month).

  • MPC: ΔC ÷ ΔYd = 80 ÷ 100 = 0.8. MPS: ΔS ÷ ΔYd = 20 ÷ 100 = 0.2. Check: 0.8 + 0.2 = 1.
  • APC at 400: 360 ÷ 400 = 0.9. APC at 500: 440 ÷ 500 = 0.88.
  • APS at 500: 60 ÷ 500 = 0.12. Check: 0.88 + 0.12 = 1.
  • Here APC (0.88) > MPC (0.8), so APC falls as income rises.

Mission 4

The consumption function

  • Consumption function: C = a + bYd. Illustrative: C = 40 + 0.75Yd.
  • Autonomous consumption (a = 40): spent even at zero income, from past saving or borrowing.
  • Induced consumption (bYd): rises with income. The slope b is the MPC (0.75).
  • Step 2: the 45° line, where C = Yd. The function crosses it at Yd = 160: the break-even income, where S = 0.
  • Below 160, C > Yd: dissaving. Above 160, the gap below the 45° line is saving.
  • A change in Yd is a movement along the function. Any other influence shifts it.

Change the slope

  • Takeaway: the MPC sets the slope; autonomous consumption sets the intercept.
Mission 4

What else moves spending

  • Interest rates: a rise makes borrowing dearer (cars, furniture, mortgages) and saving more rewarding. C usually falls.
  • Confidence and expectations: fear of job loss or lower future income → more precautionary saving.
  • Wealth effects: rising house or share prices make owners feel richer and able to borrow → C rises.
  • Taxes and benefits: change disposable income.
  • Household debt and credit: heavily indebted households, or banks that stop lending, cut C.
  • Step 2: C rises with the price level unchanged → AD shifts right, AD1 → AD2.
  • Say: "a rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".
Mission 4

Real world: the 2020 saving surge

  • In spring 2020, lockdowns closed shops and jobs felt at risk.
  • UK: the household saving ratio rose from 9.6% (January to March) to a record 29.1% (April to June 2020).
  • US: the personal saving rate hit 33% in April 2020, up from 12.7% in March.
  • These are the first official estimates (ONS, BEA). Later revisions moved them a little; the surge stayed.
  • Two reasons:
  • Forced saving: little to spend on.
  • Precautionary saving: fear about jobs and health.
  • Most incomes were protected by furlough and support schemes, yet C fell sharply.
Quick check
?House prices fall sharply across a country. Incomes and interest rates do not change. What is the most likely effect on consumption?
Mission 4

Saving is not investment

  • Saving: income not spent by households. A withdrawal.
  • Investment: spending by firms on new capital goods (machines, buildings). An injection.
  • Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • Saving and investment are done by different people, for different reasons.
  • Banks may lend savings to firms, but planned saving need not equal planned investment.
  • Say: "investment is spending on capital goods" — not: "investing in shares".
Mission 4Evaluate

Will a cut in interest rates boost spending?

It depends on:

  • Confidence: worried households may still save, whatever the rate.
  • Borrowers or savers? Borrowers gain; savers lose interest income and may spend less.
  • Debt and credit: if banks will not lend, cheaper rates help little.
  • Time lags: fixed-rate loans and contracts delay the effect.
  • Size of the cut: a small cut may change little.
Quick check
?In economics, which of these counts as investment?
Mission check

?Share prices rise strongly over a year (illustrative). Households' disposable incomes and interest rates do not change, and consumer confidence improves. What happens?

Spending depends on income, but also on wealth, confidence, interest rates and credit. Any of them can move C even when pay is unchanged.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Spending by one sector is income to another, so national output = national income = national expenditure.
  • Withdrawals (S, T, M) leave the flow; injections (I, G, X) enter it. Equilibrium: S + T + M = I + G + X.

Mission 2

  • GDP: the value of final goods and services produced within a country in a period.
  • Output, income and expenditure measure the same flow; value added avoids double counting.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP × 100 ÷ deflator. Only real GDP measures growth in output.
  • Growth rate = change in real GDP ÷ earlier year × 100; per capita = ÷ population.

Mission 3

  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M): planned spending on real output at each price level. Consumption is the largest component.
  • A change in the price level moves the economy along AD (wealth, interest rate and trade or exchange rate effects); a change in anything else that alters C, I, G or X − M shifts it.
  • The size of a shift depends on the component, confidence, time lags and offsetting changes.

Mission 4

  • Yd = C + S. Consumption is the largest part of AD.
  • Interest rates, confidence, wealth, taxes and credit move C; a change in C shifts AD.
  • MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd; MPC + MPS = 1. In C = a + bYd, b is the MPC.
