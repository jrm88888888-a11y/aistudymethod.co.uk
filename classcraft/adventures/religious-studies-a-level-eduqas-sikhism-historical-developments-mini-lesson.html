Mission 1

What "Khalistan" means

  • Khalistan: "land of the Khalsa" (the pure): a proposed independent Sikh state in Punjab.
  • Background memory: the Khalsa's 18th-century struggle and the Sikh kingdom founded by Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1799–1849; he died in 1839), ended by British conquest.
  • Many gurdwaras recite "Raj karega Khalsa" ("the Khalsa shall rule") after Ardas. Sikhs read it differently: as political sovereignty, or as the rule of righteousness.
  • The demand for a separate state became prominent only in the 1970s–80s.
  • Sikhs hold many views on it. This mission explains the history; it takes no side.
Mission 1

1919: the Vaisakhi massacre

  • 13 April 1919, Jallianwala Bagh, Amritsar, near the Harmandir Sahib.
  • A crowd of Sikhs, Hindus and Muslims, many in town for Vaisakhi, gathers in a walled garden; some are protesting against arrests under British emergency laws.
  • Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer orders troops to fire without warning. Hundreds are killed (official British figure 379; Indian estimates far higher).
  • Afterwards the British-backed manager of the Harmandir Sahib honours Dyer.
  • Impact: Sikh trust in British rule collapses; it fuels the gurdwara reform movement and Sikh nationalism.
Mission 1

1947: Punjab divided

  • At independence, British India is partitioned into India and Pakistan; Punjab is split in two.
  • Sikh holy places are left in Pakistan, including Nankana Sahib, Guru Nanak's birthplace, and Lahore, where Guru Arjan died.
  • Millions migrate across the new border; there is terrible violence on all sides.
  • Sikh leaders (e.g. Master Tara Singh) had pressed for Sikh safeguards; in the end Sikhs joined India, trusting promises of a fair place there.
  • Many Sikhs later felt those promises were broken: this sense of betrayal shapes later demands.

Mission 1

From Punjabi Suba to the Anandpur Sahib Resolution

  • 1950s–60s: the Punjabi Suba campaign for a Punjabi-speaking state, led by the Akali Dal.
  • 1966: Punjab is reorganised; the new state has a Sikh majority. Disputes remain over the shared capital Chandigarh and river waters.
  • 1973: the Anandpur Sahib Resolution asks for much greater autonomy for Punjab within India; it is read by some as federalism, by others as a step to separation.
  • Early 1980s: Akali protests (called the Dharam Yudh Morcha, 1982) and rising violence in Punjab.
  • Jarnail Singh Bhindranwale (1947–1984), a preacher of strict Khalsa discipline, becomes a central figure; in 1983 he and armed followers move into the Akal Takht. Whether he sought Khalistan is debated.
Mission 1

1984: Operation Blue Star

  • June 1984: the Indian army attacks the Harmandir Sahib complex in Amritsar to remove Bhindranwale and armed militants.
  • It falls on the anniversary of Guru Arjan's martyrdom: many pilgrims are inside.
  • The Akal Takht is badly damaged; the Sikh Reference Library burns; hundreds die, including many pilgrims (figures are disputed). Bhindranwale is killed.
  • Indian government view: the action was needed to end an armed occupation and violence in Punjab.
  • Many Sikhs' view: an assault on their holiest shrine; a wound to the whole community.
  • Spec spelling: Operation Bluestar.
Mission 1

After Blue Star

  • 31 October 1984: Prime Minister Indira Gandhi is assassinated by two Sikh bodyguards.
  • November 1984: mobs kill thousands of Sikhs in Delhi and other cities, while police largely fail to act; many Sikhs call it a genocide; justice was slow, with some convictions decades later.
  • 1984 to the mid-1990s: armed insurgency in Punjab for Khalistan. Civilians are killed by militants; many young Sikhs are killed or "disappeared" by security forces.
  • 1985: Canada-based militants bomb Air India Flight 182, killing 329 people.
  • Result: Khalistan becomes a demand for some; 1984 becomes a defining memory for almost all Sikhs.
Mission 1

Persecution and Sikh identity

  • Sikh memory joins 1984 to a longer story: Guru Arjan (1606), Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675), the 18th-century martyrs, 1919, 1947.
  • Persecution strengthens a sense of a distinct qaum (nation/people), not just a religion.
  • It gives the Khalistan aspiration emotional force: "we can only be safe in our own state".
  • It also shapes identity without politics: the turban and 5Ks worn with new pride by many diaspora Sikhs after 1984.
Mission 1Diversity

Sikh views on self-rule

  • Independence: some Sikhs, especially in parts of the diaspora, campaign for a sovereign Khalistan, citing 1984 and "Raj karega Khalsa".
  • Autonomy within India: mainstream Akali politics has sought more powers for Punjab, not separation.
  • Justice, not a state: many Sikhs want accountability for 1984 and human-rights abuses, but reject separatism.
  • Religious objection: some argue Sikhism's message is universal; tying it to a territory narrows Guru Nanak's vision.
  • Everyday priorities: many Sikhs in Punjab focus on farming, jobs, drugs and education.
  • Governments differ too: India treats several pro-Khalistan groups as terrorist organisations; the issue still strains India's relations with Canada, the UK and the USA.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Persecution has shaped Sikhism more than anything else."

  • For: the martyrdoms led to miri-piri (1606) and the Khalsa (1699); 18th-century persecution forged the Khalsa's identity.
  • For: 1919, 1947 and 1984 shaped modern Sikh politics, memory and diaspora identity.
  • Against: the core of Sikhism (nam, sewa, equality, the Guru Granth Sahib) comes from Guru Nanak's teaching before persecution.
  • Against: Punjabi language and culture, migration and the diaspora also shape Sikh identity (see Mission 6).
  • Against: many Sikhs' daily faith centres on the gurdwara and sewa, not historical grievance.
  • Judgement criteria: shaping beliefs vs shaping institutions; Sikhism "as a whole" vs Sikh politics; Punjab vs the diaspora.
  • Related spec issue: the relative importance of Khalistan, Punjabi language and culture within Sikhism as a whole.

Mission 2

Guru Nanak: why call her inferior?

  • In Guru Nanak's Punjab, women were often seen as ritually impure and spiritually lesser; widows could face sati (burning on a husband's pyre); purdah (veiling) was common among elites.
  • Guru Nanak, Asa di Var, Guru Granth Sahib 473 (paraphrase): from woman we are born and conceived; to woman we are engaged and married; woman is our friend and the source of future generations. Why call her inferior, when from her even kings are born?
  • He adds that without woman there would be no one at all; only the One True Lord is beyond needing a mother.
  • Your spec also lists Adi Granth 62 for Guru Nanak's teaching.
  • Spiritual basis: the same divine jot (light) is in every person.
Mission 2

Guru Amar Das: reform in practice

  • Women teachers: Sikh tradition says he set up preaching districts (manjis and piris) and appointed women as well as men to lead them.
  • Against sati (Guru Granth Sahib 787, paraphrase): true satis are not those who burn on the pyre, but those who live with virtue and contentment, and those who die of the pain of separation.
  • Against purdah: women came into the sangat unveiled; the Rahit Maryada later forbade Sikh women to veil their faces.
  • Widows could remarry.
  • Langar: women and men cooked, served and ate together (Mata Khivi before him).
Mission 2

Guru Gobind Singh and the Khalsa

  • Women as well as men take amrit and join the Khalsa; women take the name Kaur ("princess"), men Singh: a name that removes caste surnames for both.
  • Tradition says the Guru's wife (Mata Jito in most accounts, Mata Sahib Kaur in others) added sugar to the first amrit; Mata Sahib Kaur is honoured as "mother of the Khalsa".
  • Mai Bhago led forty Sikhs back into battle at Khidrana (Muktsar), 1705.
  • Mata Sundari guided the Panth for decades after Guru Gobind Singh's death (1708), issuing letters (hukamnamas) to the sangats.
  • Your spec: "example of Guru Gobind Singh".
Mission 2

Equality in Sikh codes and practice

  • Marriage: monogamous and a religious ceremony (Anand Karaj); the same four laavan for bride and groom.
  • Widows: a widow may remarry; she may be head of the family.
  • Education and worship: equal rights to learn, read the Guru Granth Sahib, sing kirtan and lead worship.
  • Religious roles: women may be granthis, ragis, members of the Panj Piare (in principle) and gurdwara committee members.
  • Dress: the same 5Ks for amritdhari women and men; some women wear a turban (dastaar) or a small under-turban (keski).

Mission 2

Teaching and practice

  • Gaps many Sikhs themselves point out:
  • Very few women in the Panj Piare or as leaders of major Sikh bodies (though Bibi Jagir Kaur became the first woman president of the SGPC in 1999).
  • Women do not perform kirtan inside the sanctum of Harmandir Sahib: an SGPC decision in 2005 to allow it has not been put into practice.
  • Dowry and preference for sons persist in parts of Punjabi culture, against the Gurus' teaching.
  • Many Sikhs explain the gaps as Punjabi culture, not Sikh teaching; others say institutions themselves need reform.
Mission 2Diversity

Equal, but with different roles?

  • Complementary view: men and women are spiritually equal and equally able to reach mukti, but often have different roles (e.g. in family and household).
  • Egalitarian view: equal souls should mean equal roles everywhere, including leadership and the Panj Piare.
  • Conservative practice: some gurdwaras and families keep traditional roles; some Sikh groups (e.g. the Akhand Kirtani Jatha) expect women to wear the keski, to which others object as unnecessary.
  • Your spec: "recognition of men and women as equal, but with different roles".
Mission 2

Three Sikh women scholars

  • Dr Mohinder Kaur Gill: stresses the Gurus' reforms: the Gurus gave women a dignity and equality new in their society; later inequality comes from culture, not the Gurus.
  • Dr Gurnam Kaur: stresses theology: the same divine light in all; the soul is beyond gender, so spiritual equality is built into Sikh teaching.
  • Kanwaljit Kaur Singh: stresses practice and the diaspora: Sikh women are equal in teaching, but often disadvantaged in families and gurdwaras; women must claim their roles.
  • All three: equality belongs to Sikh teaching itself; they differ on why practice falls short and what should change.
Mission 2Read the source

The soul-bride

  • The Guru Granth Sahib often speaks in a woman's voice: every soul, male or female, is a bride longing for the divine Husband-Lord (e.g. the laavan, Guru Granth Sahib 773–774).
  • (Paraphrase of a common theme.) The bride must give up ego (haumai) and adorn herself with virtues to meet her Lord.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Feminism has always been part of Sikhism."

  • For: GGS 473; Guru Amar Das's women teachers and his stand against sati and purdah; women in the Khalsa (Kaur); Mai Bhago and Mata Sundari; the same divine light in all.
  • Against: "feminism" is a modern movement and concept; no women Gurus and no hymns by women in the Guru Granth Sahib; much imagery is of a male Lord; practice has often lagged.
  • Middle view: the Gurus taught spiritual equality and attacked specific injustices; whether that is "feminism" depends on definition.
  • Judge it by: what counts as feminism (equal dignity, equal roles, challenging patriarchy)? Teaching or practice? Which period?
  • Linked issue: "whether men and women are equal in Sikhism": equal in teaching? in roles? in practice?

Mission 3

Diversity is a fact; pluralism is a response

  • Religious diversity: the plain fact that many religions (and many groups within each) exist side by side.
  • Pluralism is a response to that fact. The word is used in two ways:
  • Social pluralism: welcoming and protecting many faiths in one society.
  • Philosophical (religious) pluralism: the view that different religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate truth.
  • A person can welcome diversity in society without holding that all religions are equally true.
  • Sikhism was born in Punjab between Hindu and Muslim communities, so these questions were there from the start.
Mission 3

Testimony from many traditions: the bhagat bani

  • The Guru Granth Sahib (GGS) contains the hymns of six Gurus and of bhagats (saints) from other traditions: the bhagat bani.
  • Bhagats from the Hindu bhakti and sant traditions (e.g. Kabir, Ravidas, Namdev, Ramanand) and a Muslim Sufi, Sheikh Farid; several were of low caste.
  • Guru Arjan included them in 1604 because they agreed with the Gurus: one formless God, the Name, against caste and empty ritual.
  • Their hymns are sung with the same reverence as the Gurus'.
  • So the Sikh scripture itself is a meeting place: Sikhs bow daily before words written by people who were not Sikhs.
Mission 3Read the source

What the Guru Granth Sahib says about other faiths

  • Kabir (Prabhati, GGS 1349, paraphrase): first God created light; from that one light the whole creation came. So who is good and who is bad?
  • Kabir (Prabhati, GGS 1350, paraphrase): do not say that the Vedas and the books of the Semitic faiths are false. False is the one who does not reflect on them.
  • Guru Arjan (Ramkali, GGS 885, paraphrase): some call on Ram, some on Khuda; some serve Gosain, some Allah. The one who understands the Lord's will knows the Lord's secret.
  • Guru Arjan (Bhairo, GGS 1136, paraphrase): I keep neither the Hindu fast nor the Muslim Ramadan; I am neither Hindu nor Muslim. Body and breath belong to the One, called both Allah and Ram.

Q1 What do these passages agree on about God?

Show answer

There is one God of all people, called by many names.

Q2 Does GGS 1136 say that the other religions are worthless?

Show answer

No: it rejects their outward rituals as necessary for the speaker, while naming God with their names. It marks a distinct path, not contempt.

Mission 3

The pattern of the dialogues

  • Respect for persons: Nanak ate, travelled and debated with Hindus, Muslims and yogis as equals.
  • Criticism of hypocrisy in every tradition, including his own birth tradition.
  • Reinterpretation: he took each group's own symbols (thread, mosque, yogi's coat) and gave them an inner, ethical meaning.
  • One God, one message: the call to the Name was addressed to all.
  • Three examples (tradition and GGS, paraphrase): Nanak, born into a Hindu family, refused the sacred thread as a boy and asked for a thread of compassion and truth instead (GGS 471); to Muslims he said, make mercy your mosque and honest living your Qur'an (GGS 140–141); to the yogis, who left family life, he said live like the lotus, untouched by the water around it (GGS 938).
Mission 3

History: conflict as well as friendship

  • Sikh attitudes to other faiths were also shaped by persecution under Mughal rule:
  • 1606: Guru Arjan executed in Lahore under the emperor Jahangir.
  • 1675: Guru Tegh Bahadur executed in Delhi under Aurangzeb. Sikh tradition: he died defending the Kashmiri Pandits' (Hindu priests') freedom to practise their own faith.
  • Guru Gobind Singh fought both Mughal forces and Hindu hill rajas.
  • Yet friendships ran across these lines: the Muslim Sufi Pir Buddhu Shah fought for Guru Gobind Singh (tradition); Sikh tradition says the Sufi Mian Mir laid the foundation stone of the Harmandir in Amritsar.
  • So the history gives Sikhs both a memory of conflict and a model of defending other people's religious freedom.

Mission 3Evaluate

Do Guru Nanak's dialogues show that all religions are equally valid?

  • Claim: Nanak's dialogues show that, for Sikhs, all religions are equally valid paths to God.
  • For: He taught one God of all, called by many names (GGS 885); "there is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" (janamsakhi tradition).
  • For: The scripture includes Hindu and Muslim saints, whose words Sikhs revere as Gurbani alongside the Gurus'.
  • Against: He criticised core practices of each group (the thread, ritual prayer, renunciation) and offered the Name as the true way.
  • Against: GGS 1136 (paraphrase) declares the speaker neither Hindu nor Muslim: a distinct path, which suggests inclusivism (truth found elsewhere, fulfilled in the Guru's way) more than pluralism.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "valid" mean God can be found there, or that every practice is equally good? Do the dialogues criticise people or only hypocrisy? Which reading fits the whole scripture?
  • Issue in your spec: "the extent to which Sikhism is an exclusivist or inclusivist tradition" (continued in Mission 4).

Mission 4

Three answers to "Is my religion the only true one?"

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true; salvation or liberation comes only through it.
  • Inclusivism: one religion has the fullest truth, but others contain real truth, and their followers can be saved or liberated through it, often without knowing.
  • Pluralism: many religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate reality.
  • Recap: diversity is the fact of many religions; pluralism is one response to it (see Mission 3).
  • Your spec: "the conflict between exclusivism and inclusivism".
Mission 4

Where Sikh teaching sits

  • The Mool Mantar begins Ik Onkar: there is one Creator of all, not a Sikh God.
  • Spelling on your papers: Mul Mantra.
  • God is beyond any one name; people call God Ram, Allah, Khuda (GGS 885).
  • Liberation comes by God's grace (nadar) to those who remember the Name and live truthfully. No birth, caste or label earns it.
  • So many Sikhs hold that enlightenment can be reached through any theistic tradition lived with true devotion.
  • But the Guru's word is the clearest guide; Sikhs follow it, and the Rahit Maryada says a Sikh owes allegiance to no other religion.
  • Many scholars therefore call the Sikh view inclusivist; some Sikhs describe it as pluralist.
  • Every Ardas ends by asking for sarbat da bhala: the welfare of all.
Mission 4Diversity

Different Sikh responses to religious freedom and pluralism

  • Open engagement: gurdwaras and langar are open to all; Sikhs serve in interfaith councils; Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom (1675) is cited as a duty to defend everyone's freedom of religion.
  • Distinct identity: the Singh Sabha movement (from 1873) worked to separate Sikh practice from Hindu custom. Kahn Singh Nabha's book Ham Hindu Nahin ("We are not Hindus", 1898) argued that Sikhs are a separate faith.
  • Many Sikhs still object that the Indian Constitution (Article 25) groups Sikhs with Hindus for some legal purposes.
  • Defending visible faith in secular states: in the UK, Mandla v Dowell Lee (House of Lords, 1983) held that Sikhs are an ethnic group protected from discrimination (a school had refused a boy in a turban).
  • These responses are not opposites: many Sikhs are both open to others and firm about their own identity.
Mission 4

No longer a missionary religion?

  • Guru Nanak travelled widely and taught all who would listen; the early Panth grew fast.
  • Today Sikhs welcome converts but do not, on the whole, run organised campaigns to convert others.
  • Reasons Sikhs give: God's grace, not a label, liberates; other faiths can lead to God; the Gurus forbade forced conversion.
  • But Sikhs do teach their faith (parchar): e.g. the Sikh Missionary Society UK publishes free literature, mainly for Sikhs and enquirers.
  • Issue in your spec: "the extent to which Sikhism can be regarded as a non-missionary religion".

Mission 4

Interfaith relations today

  • Langar: the free kitchen serves anyone; many interfaith events in the UK and beyond are hosted in gurdwaras.
  • Shared service: Sikh relief groups help people of every faith in disasters.
  • The Kartarpur Corridor (opened November 2019, for Guru Nanak's 550th birth anniversary) lets Indian pilgrims visit Kartarpur in Pakistan, where Nanak spent his last years.
  • Barriers: memories of persecution; disputes over identity; hate attacks on Sikhs mistaken for other groups (e.g. after 11 September 2001).
  • Your spec: "Sikh attitudes to interfaith dialogue".
Mission 4

Khalsa and non-Khalsa Sikhs

  • Amritdhari: initiated into the Khalsa; keeps the 5Ks and the Rahit.
  • Keshdhari: keeps uncut hair but has not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: follows the Gurus but cuts hair and has not taken amrit.
  • The Rahit Maryada defines a Sikh as one who believes in one God, the ten Gurus, the Guru Granth Sahib and the Gurus' teaching, believes in the necessity of amrit, and owes allegiance to no other religion (paraphrase).
  • Khalsa identity marks clear boundaries: a visible, distinct faith.
  • Many non-Khalsa Sikhs, and some Hindu Punjabi and Sindhi families who revere Guru Nanak, live with looser boundaries: attending both gurdwara and mandir.
  • So Sikhs differ over how sharp the line between Sikh and non-Sikh should be.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is Sikhism an exclusivist or an inclusivist tradition?

  • Claim: Sikhism is an exclusivist tradition.
  • For: the Rahit Maryada requires allegiance to no other religion; the Khalsa marks a distinct, visible identity; the Singh Sabha insisted "we are not Hindus".
  • For: the Gurus rejected many practices of other faiths (see Mission 3).
  • Against: liberation by grace is open to all; God is named Ram and Allah; Hindu and Muslim saints are in the scripture.
  • Against: Sikhs do not seek converts and defend others' religious freedom.
  • Judgement criteria: Does exclusivism mean a distinct identity or a claim that only Sikhs are liberated? Which counts more: the Rahit's boundaries or the scripture's openness? Do Khalsa and non-Khalsa Sikhs answer differently?
  • Issue in your spec, verbatim: "The extent to which Sikhism is an exclusivist or inclusivist tradition".

Mission 5

Creation in the Guru Granth Sahib

  • Before creation (Guru Nanak, Maru Solhe, Guru Granth Sahib 1035, paraphrase): for countless ages there was only darkness (dhundhukar); no earth, no sky; only the infinite hukam (divine order, will).
  • Creation by one command (Japji 16, Guru Granth Sahib 3, paraphrase): with one word, God made the whole expanse, and from it flowed countless rivers of life.
  • Countless worlds (Japji 22, Guru Granth Sahib 5, paraphrase): there are worlds beneath worlds and hundreds of thousands of skies above; searchers grow weary trying to count them.
  • God remains: Waheguru is Karta Purakh (Creator) in the Mul Mantra, and sustains creation continuously.
Mission 5Read the source

When did it begin?

Guru Nanak, Japji 21, Guru Granth Sahib 4 (paraphrase):

  • What was the time, the moment, the date, the season, the month, when creation came to be?
  • The pandits (Hindu scholars) do not know, or it would be written in their texts; the qazis (Muslim judges) do not know, or it would be in the Qur'an. The yogis do not know.
  • Only the Creator, who made the world, knows.

Q1 Which religious authorities does Guru Nanak say do not know the date?

Show answer

Pandits, qazis and yogis.

Q2 Why might Sikhs say this leaves room for scientific cosmology?

Show answer

The scripture fixes no date or method, so the age of the universe is open to inquiry.

Mission 5

Big Bang and continuous creation

  • Big Bang: the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Many Sikhs see parallels: a "before" with no matter (GGS 1035); creation from a single command (Japji 16); an immense, expanding cosmos (Japji 22).
  • Continuous creation: God did not create once and withdraw. Creation unfolds under hukam and God sustains it at every moment.
  • Your spec also names Guru Granth Sahib 1163 for Sikh openness to scientific accounts of creation.
  • Caution: parallels are interpretations; the Gurus wrote devotional poetry, not science.
Mission 5

Evolution and 8.4 million life forms

  • Sikh scripture speaks of the soul passing through 8.4 million (84 lakh) life forms before human birth.
  • Many Sikhs read this as compatible with evolution: many forms of life; humans arriving late; the human birth as a precious opportunity.
  • No literal Adam and Eve and no fixed six days: so the conflicts some Christians and Muslims face over Genesis or the Qur'an's creation accounts do not arise in the same way.
  • The rebirth of the soul is a religious claim that science neither confirms nor tests: it concerns meaning and the soul, not biology.

Mission 5

Challenges science still raises

  • Miracle stories in the janamsakhis (e.g. Guru Nanak stopping a boulder with his hand at Panja Sahib): many Sikhs read them as symbolic; Sikh teaching itself warns against seeking miracles.
  • Rebirth and karma: cannot be shown scientifically; scientific naturalism may treat the self as only the brain.
  • Bioethics: IVF, organ donation, end of life.
  • Rationalist Sikh writers (e.g. D. S. Chahal) argue Guru Nanak's teaching is scientific in spirit; critics say this reads modern ideas into the text.
Mission 5

What is secularisation?

  • Secularisation: religion losing influence over society, institutions and individuals' lives.
  • Secularism: a political principle: the state is neutral between religions.
  • In Britain: falling attendance; religion seen as private; some young Sikhs cutting hair, not reading Gurmukhi, or attending only for weddings and festivals.
  • But also: revival among some young Sikhs after 1984; youth camps; online katha; new visibility of the turban.
  • Sikh values often line up with secular ones: equality, freedom, democracy.
Mission 5

Sikh responses to secularisation

  • Social obligations and reform: sewa, langar, Sarbat da bhala (the good of all): faith shown in action others can see and value.
  • Duties as a citizen: Kirat Karo (honest work), obeying just laws, military and public service.
  • Freedom of religion: Guru Tegh Bahadur (martyred 1675) is remembered for defending Hindus' right to worship: freedom for others, not just for Sikhs.
  • Equality: rejection of caste; equality of women and men (see Mission 2); langar open to all.
  • Democracy: the Panj Piare decide collectively; the gurmatta (resolution) of the sangat; elected gurdwara committees.
Mission 5Diversity

Is Sikhism compatible with secular democracy?

  • Yes: Sikh values support equality, freedom of conscience and collective decision-making; Sikhs serve in Parliament and armed forces; India's constitution and Britain's law protect Sikh practice.
  • Tension: miri-piri means religion is not merely private; the Akal Takht has spiritual and temporal authority; some Sikhs seek a Sikh homeland (Khalistan).
  • Middle view: Sikhs accept a neutral state that protects all religions (like Guru Tegh Bahadur's defence of others' freedom) but reject a secularism that pushes religion out of public life.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Sikh responses to science and secularisation are effective."

  • For (science): no date or method of creation to defend; openness to Big Bang and evolution; Sikhs prominent in medicine and science.
  • Against (science): "parallels" can be selective reading; rebirth and miracle stories remain untestable; rationalist readings may change the tradition.
  • For (secularisation): Sikh values fit equality and freedom; visible service (langar, disaster relief) earns public respect; legal campaigns succeeded.
  • Against (secularisation): declining Punjabi and observance among some young Sikhs; tension between miri-piri and a privatised secularism.
  • Judge it by: effective at what: keeping young Sikhs, persuading outsiders, or keeping teaching intact? Evidence from Britain vs Punjab?
Mission check

?A science teacher says: "Religions must choose between their creation stories and the Big Bang." Which reply would many Sikhs give, based on their scripture?

Sikh scripture leaves the when and how of creation open; the bigger challenge of modern life is secularisation, which Sikhs meet with service, citizenship and visible identity.

Mission 6

Before mass migration

  • Maharaja Duleep Singh, last ruler of the Sikh empire, was exiled to Britain after 1849; he lived at Elveden, Suffolk.
  • Sikh soldiers fought for Britain in both world wars; small numbers of Sikh pedlars and students lived in Britain between the wars.
  • 1911: the first British gurdwara opened in Putney, London; from 1913 it met in Shepherd's Bush.
  • Numbers stayed very small until after 1945.
Mission 6

From Punjab: post-war labour

  • 1950s–60s: Britain needed workers; Partition (1947) had disrupted Punjab.
  • Most came from the Doaba region (Jalandhar, Hoshiarpur), many from farming (often Jat) families.
  • Men usually came first, worked in foundries, textile mills, factories, transport and at Heathrow, and sent money home.
  • Chain migration: villagers followed relatives and neighbours to the same towns.
  • After the Commonwealth Immigrants Act (1962), wives and children joined them: temporary workers became settled families.
  • Some men cut their hair and stopped wearing turbans to find work.
Mission 6

From East Africa: "twice migrants"

  • From the 1890s Sikhs, many of them skilled Ramgarhia artisans, went to Kenya and Uganda to build the railway and stayed.
  • 1960s: "Africanisation" policies after independence pressed Asians to leave; 1972: Idi Amin expelled Asians from Uganda.
  • They came as whole families, often English-speaking, with business and professional experience and established religious institutions.
  • Many had kept the turban and other religious practices more strictly than earlier Punjabi migrants.
  • The anthropologist Parminder Bhachu called them "twice migrants" (1985): India to Africa, then Africa to Britain.
Mission 6

Where Sikhs live

  • 2021 Census (England and Wales): about 524,000 people identified as Sikh, about 0.9% of the population.
  • Concentrated in a few areas: West London (Southall, Hounslow), East London (Ilford), the West Midlands (Birmingham, Wolverhampton, Smethwick, Coventry), Leicester, Leeds, Gravesend, Slough.
  • Clustering followed jobs and chain migration; it allowed large gurdwaras, shops and Punjabi schools.
  • Far fewer Sikhs live in Scotland, Wales, Northern Ireland and rural England, where there may be no local gurdwara.

Mission 6

Perceived difficulties

  • Racism and discrimination in jobs, housing and the street.
  • The turban: Sikh bus workers won the right to wear it at work after long campaigns: Manchester, 1959–66 (G. S. Sagar); Wolverhampton, 1967–69 (Tarsem Singh Sandhu).
  • Mandla v Dowell Lee (House of Lords, 1983): a school refused a boy who wore a turban; the Lords ruled that Sikhs are an ethnic group protected by the Race Relations Act 1976.
  • Language: many first-generation migrants spoke little English; their children grew up speaking more English than Punjabi.
  • No Sikh structures: no gurdwaras, no Punjabi teachers, few granthis at first.
Mission 6

Adopting Sunday

  • In Punjab there is no fixed weekly holy day: Sikhs visit the gurdwara daily or when they can.
  • In Britain, shifts and the working week left the weekend free, so the main diwan moved to Sunday, followed by langar.
  • Gurdwaras also hold daily prayers (morning and evening), but Sunday became the day of the full sangat.
  • Some see this as practical adaptation; others note it echoes the Christian pattern of the host society.
Mission 6

The gurdwara as a social centre

  • First gurdwaras met in houses and halls; later, converted churches and warehouses; then large purpose-built buildings (e.g. Gurdwara Sri Guru Singh Sabha, Southall; Guru Nanak Gurdwara, Smethwick).
  • Beyond worship: langar, weddings (Anand Karaj), funerals, Punjabi classes, kirtan and gatka (martial art) teaching, youth clubs, day care for elderly people, advice on immigration, housing and welfare.
  • A place to meet people from the same village or caste; a centre for campaigns (turban cases) and politics.
  • Gurdwara committees are elected; elections can be fiercely contested.
Mission 6Diversity

One community or many?

  • Caste: some gurdwaras are run by caste-based groups (e.g. Ramgarhia, Bhatra), despite Sikh teaching against caste. Some Ravidassia communities have separate places of worship (bhawans).
  • Observance: amritdhari, keshdhari and sahajdhari Sikhs share gurdwaras but differ in practice.
  • Generation: British-born Sikhs, many fluent in English, often ask why things are done, not only how.
  • Politics: 1984 (Operation Blue Star and the anti-Sikh violence) sharpened religious identity; many young British Sikhs took amrit or kept their hair.
Mission 6

Traditional values and popular culture

  • Alcohol, tobacco and drugs: the Rahit Maryada forbids them to all Sikhs (tobacco is a grave breach for amritdhari Sikhs); widespread in popular culture.
  • Hair: pressure to cut hair to fit in; some young men later return to the turban.
  • Marriage and family: arranged or assisted marriage, family honour (izzat) and caste expectations meet ideals of romantic choice; mixed marriages (see Mission 7).
  • Gender: daughters and sons may be treated differently, against Sikh teaching on equality (see Mission 2).
  • Responses: Sikh youth camps, university Sikh societies, Sikh media; Punjabi music (bhangra) as a hybrid British-Punjabi culture.
Quick check
?Why did the main weekly gathering in most British gurdwaras come to be held on Sundays?
Mission 6Evaluate

"The distribution of Sikhs in Britain has hampered the community's development."

  • For: clustering in a few cities left Sikhs in other areas without gurdwaras, Punjabi teaching or a sangat. Concentration may slow integration and keep caste-based divisions alive. National organisation came late.
  • Against: clustering enabled large gurdwaras, Punjabi schools, shops, media and political influence (e.g. local councillors, MPs elected in areas with large Sikh communities). It helped campaigns such as the turban cases succeed.
  • Linked issue: can Sikhs assimilate into a secular society? Some say visible identity (turban, 5Ks) resists assimilation; others distinguish integration (full participation, keeping identity) from assimilation (losing it) and argue Sikhs have integrated.
  • Judge it by: what counts as "development" (numbers, institutions, faith passed on)? Do clustered and dispersed Sikhs differ in observance?
Mission check

?A Sikh family moves from Smethwick to a Cornish town with no gurdwara. Which loss best shows how the gurdwara developed in Britain?

For British Sikhs the gurdwara became far more than a place of worship: it was how a migrant community built itself.

Mission 7

Assimilate or integrate?

  • Diaspora: a community living outside its homeland (for Sikhs, outside Punjab).
  • Assimilation: a minority becomes like the majority, losing distinctive practices (e.g. cutting hair, dropping Punjabi).
  • Integration: full participation in society while keeping a distinct identity.
  • Secular society: the state does not favour one religion; religion is often seen as private.
  • Sikh identity is visible (turban, uncut hair, kara, kirpan): it cannot simply be kept private.
Mission 7

The panj kakke in UK law

  • Kesh and turban: Motor-Cycle Crash Helmets (Religious Exemption) Act 1976; Employment Act 1989 (turban-wearing Sikhs exempt from hard hats on construction sites).
  • Race and religion: Mandla v Dowell Lee (1983): Sikhs are an ethnic group; the Equality Act 2010 now protects both race and religion.
  • Kirpan: carrying a blade in public is an offence, but the Criminal Justice Act 1988 gives a defence for carrying it for religious reasons. Schools, courts and airports may set their own rules.
  • Kara: in 2008 the High Court ruled that a Welsh school had discriminated against Sarika Watkins-Singh by banning her kara.
  • Pattern: Sikhs won exemptions mostly case by case, through campaigns and courts.
Mission 7

Why the kirpan: miri and piri

  • Piri: spiritual authority. Miri: temporal (worldly) authority. Guru Hargobind wore two swords to show both.
  • Teg and deg: sword and cooking pot. The Khalsa protects the oppressed (teg) and feeds the hungry (deg).
  • The kirpan expresses this duty to defend the weak: Sikhs stress it is a religious article, not a weapon of aggression.
  • Minority challenge: the law, schools and employers must balance this religious duty with public safety.
  • Sikh liberationist thinking applies the same principle today: defending the poor and oppressed, meeting material and spiritual needs.
Quick check
?How does the law in England and Wales treat a Sikh who carries a kirpan in a public place?
Mission 7Evaluate

"Sikh identity cannot survive in a secular diaspora without compromise."

  • For: many first-generation men cut hair for work; Punjabi is declining among the young; mixed marriages are increasing; some gurdwaras use English and chairs.
  • Against: legal campaigns protected the 5Ks; many young Sikhs have adopted the turban and taken amrit; secular law protects religious freedom; miri-piri gives Sikhs a basis for public engagement rather than withdrawal.
  • Distinguish: compromise of essentials (amrit, Guru Granth Sahib, 5Ks for the Khalsa) vs adaptation of customs (Sunday diwan, English katha).
  • Judge it by: who defines the essentials (Akal Takht, Rehat Maryada, local sangat)? Does adaptation weaken or renew identity?
Mission check

?A Sikh nurse is told by a hospital that she must remove her kara for hand hygiene. Which response best fits the way British Sikhs have usually handled such conflicts?

Sikh identity in the diaspora is negotiated: keeping the essentials, adapting the customs, and using the law to protect both.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Khalistan ("land of the Khalsa") is a proposed independent Sikh state; the demand grew mainly in the 1970s–80s.
  • Key moments: 1919 Vaisakhi massacre at Jallianwala Bagh, 1947 Partition of Punjab, 1984 Operation Bluestar and its aftermath; persecution shapes Sikh identity and aspiration.
  • Sikh views differ: independence, autonomy within India, justice without separation, and a universal, non-territorial reading of the faith.

Mission 2

  • Guru Nanak (GGS 473), Guru Amar Das (women teachers; against sati and purdah) and Guru Gobind Singh (Kaur, women in the Khalsa) taught women's equal dignity.
  • Codes give equal rights in marriage, education, worship and dress; Gill, Gurnam Kaur and Kanwaljit Kaur Singh agree equality is Sikh teaching but stress different reasons for gaps.
  • Sikhs differ: equal with different roles, or equal roles everywhere; practice often lags.

Mission 3

  • Diversity is the fact of many religions; pluralism is a response to it, either social (welcome) or philosophical (equally valid routes).
  • The bhagat bani puts Hindu and Muslim saints' testimony at the heart of Sikh worship; Nanak met Hindus, Muslims and yogis as equals but challenged empty ritual in each.
  • Persecution (1606, 1675) and friendship (Mardana, Mian Mir, Pir Buddhu Shah) both shaped Sikh attitudes to other faiths.

Mission 4

  • Exclusivism, inclusivism and pluralism are three answers to other religions' truth claims; many scholars call mainstream Sikh teaching inclusivist.
  • Sikhs respond to pluralism in different ways: open engagement, a firm distinct identity (Singh Sabha), and defence of religious freedom; Sikhism welcomes but does not actively seek converts.
  • Khalsa and non-Khalsa Sikhs differ over how sharp the boundary with other faiths should be.

Mission 5

  • The Guru Granth Sahib speaks of darkness before creation, creation by one command and countless worlds, and says only the Creator knows when it began.
  • Many Sikhs see no conflict with the Big Bang, continuous creation or evolution (8.4 million life forms); some challenges remain.
  • Responses to secularisation: service, citizenship, freedom and equality, democracy; tension remains with a privatised secularism (miri-piri).

Mission 6

  • Sikhs came from Punjab (1950s–60s labour) and East Africa (1960s–70s families); about 524,000 in England and Wales (2021), clustered in a few areas.
  • Difficulties: racism, turban cases, Mandla v Dowell Lee (1983), language; adaptations included the Sunday diwan.
  • Gurdwaras became social centres; traditional values meet popular culture, and the community is internally diverse.

Mission 7

  • UK law protects the 5Ks largely case by case (helmets, hard hats, Mandla 1983, kirpan defence, kara case 2008).
  • The kirpan expresses miri-piri and the duty to protect: a religious duty balanced with public safety.
