Mission 1Topics 6.1, 6.5

Mission 1: Race a hoop and a solid cylinder down a slope. Which wins, and does their mass matter?

Make a guess: Race a hoop and a solid cylinder down a slope. Which wins, and does their mass matter?

Mission 1

Rotational kinetic energy

  • Each bit of a spinning object moves at v = rω, so has ½mr²ω².
  • Add them up: Σ½mr²ω² = ½(Σmr²)ω².
  • K = ½Iω²
Quick check
?A uniform grinding wheel spins at constant angular velocity on a fixed axle, so its center of mass stays at rest. A student claims: "The velocities of points on opposite sides of the wheel are equal in size and opposite in direction, so they cancel, and the wheel has no kinetic energy." Which response to the student's claim is correct?

A spinning wheel on a fixed axle has kinetic energy even though its center of mass is at rest. Kinetic energy is a scalar: the same spinning either way.

Quick check
?The diagram shows a wheel rolling without slipping along level ground; the arrow shows the velocity v of its center C. Points P and B are on the rim, as shown. Which statement describes the velocities of P and B relative to the ground at the instant shown?
Mission 1

Rolling without slipping

  • The contact point is momentarily at rest on the ground.
  • So the center moves exactly one circumference per turn:
  • vcm = rω, acm = rα, Δx = rΔθ
  • Top of the wheel: 2vcm. Bottom: 0.
  • Static friction acts at a point that is not sliding: in the ideal case it does no work and dissipates no energy.
Mission 1

Total kinetic energy

  • A rolling body moves and spins:
  • total K = ½Mvcm² + ½Icmω²
  • Example: solid cylinder, M = 2.0 kg, I = ½MR², rolling at 3.0 m/s.
  • Translational: ½ × 2.0 × 3.0² = 9.0 J.
  • Rotational: ½(½MR²)(v/R)² = ¼Mv² = 4.5 J.
  • Total 13.5 J. R cancels.
Mission 1

Worked example: down a slope

  • Solid cylinder (I = ½MR²) rolls from rest down a slope, dropping h = 0.80 m.
  • Energy: Mgh = ½Mv² + ½(½MR²)(v/R)² = ¾Mv²
  • v = √(4gh/3). M and R cancel.
  • v = √(4 × 10 × 0.80 ÷ 3) = 3.3 m/s
  • A block sliding without friction: √(2gh) = 4.0 m/s. The cylinder is slower: some energy goes into spin.
Mission 1

The race

  • Write I = kMR². Then v = √(2gh/(1 + k)).
  • Hoop: k = 1. Half its kinetic energy is rotational.
  • Solid cylinder: k = ½. One third rotational.
  • Solid sphere: k = 2/5. Two sevenths rotational.
  • Smaller k → less energy locked in spin → faster.
  • Mass and radius cancel. Only the shape (k) matters.
Quick check
?A thin-walled hollow ball (I = (2/3)MR²) is released from rest and rolls without slipping down a ramp. Its center of mass descends a vertical height of 1.2 m. Use g = 10 m/s². What is the speed of its center of mass at the bottom?
Mission check

?A solid ball (I = (2/5)MR²) and a thin-walled hollow ball (I = (2/3)MR²) have the same mass M and the same radius R. Both roll without slipping along a level floor at the same speed. Which ball must have more energy removed to bring it to rest?

The bigger the share of energy locked in spin, the slower an object rolls down a slope. The cylinder (k = ½) beats the hoop (k = 1), whatever their masses.

Mission 2Topics 6.2, 6.5

Mission 2: Why do a landing plane's tires smoke at touchdown, but not once they are rolling?

Make a guess: Why do a landing plane's tires smoke at touchdown, but not once they are rolling?

Quick check
?An electric motor keeps a flywheel turning at constant angular velocity. The motor exerts a torque on the flywheel in the sense of its rotation, and friction in the axle exerts an opposing torque of equal magnitude. A student claims: "The flywheel's kinetic energy does not change, so the motor's torque does no work on the flywheel." Which reasoning correctly evaluates the student's claim?
Mission 2

Work done by a torque

  • A force F tangent to the circle at radius r: as the object turns through Δθ, its point of application moves an arc length rΔθ.
  • W = F × rΔθ = τΔθ (constant torque).
  • Torque in the same sense as the rotation: W > 0, energy in.
  • Torque opposing the rotation (brakes, axle friction): W < 0, energy out.
  • No rotation, no work, however large the torque.
Mission 2

Area under a torque–angle graph

  • Work = area under the τ–θ graph (θ in radians).
  • Example: τ rises steadily from 0 to 3.0 N·m over the first 2.0 rad, then stays at 3.0 N·m to 4.0 rad.
  • Area = ½ × 2.0 × 3.0 + 2.0 × 3.0 = 3.0 + 6.0 = 9.0 J.
  • Flywheel, I = 0.50 kg·m², from rest, no friction: 9.0 = ½ × 0.50 × ω².
  • ω = √(2 × 9.0 ÷ 0.50) = 6.0 rad/s.
Quick check
?The graph shows the torque τ exerted on a wheel by a motor as a function of the wheel's angular position θ. How much work does the motor's torque do on the wheel as the wheel turns from θ = 0 to θ = 4.0 rad?
Mission 2

Rolling or slipping?

  • Rolling without slipping: vcm = rω. Contact point at rest. Static friction does no work: no energy dissipated (ideal case).
  • Slipping: vcm ≠ rω. The surfaces slide over each other.
  • Kinetic friction acts at a sliding point: it dissipates energy (thermal energy, tire smoke, worn rubber).
  • While slipping, vcm and ω change separately; you cannot get one from the other.
Quick check
?A bowling ball is launched along a level lane so that at first it slides without rotating. The graph shows the speed vcm of its center of mass and the product rω of its radius and angular speed as functions of time t. Which claim is supported by the graph?
Mission 2

The skid that ends in rolling

  • A bowling ball is launched sliding, not spinning: vcm = v0, ω = 0.
  • Kinetic friction acts backward at the contact point.
  • It slows the center and exerts a torque that spins the ball up.
  • When vcm = rω, sliding stops. The ball rolls; on level ground friction is then zero.
Mission 2

Back to the runway

  • At touchdown the wheels are not turning (ω = 0), but the plane moves fast.
  • The tires slide on the runway: kinetic friction dissipates energy → hot rubber, smoke.
  • The same friction exerts a torque that spins the wheels up.
  • Once vcm = rω, the tires roll: no sliding, no dissipation, no smoke.
Mission check

?A hoop is spun about its axle and then set down upright on a rough, level floor, with its center at rest. At first the hoop skids, spinning in place; then it moves off and soon rolls without slipping. What happens to the hoop's total kinetic energy while it skids?

Sliding contact + kinetic friction = energy dissipated. Rolling contact = no sliding = no dissipation. That is why the smoke stops once the wheels roll.

Mission 3Topics 6.3, 6.4

Mission 3: A spinning skater pulls in her arms and whirls faster. Where does the extra spin come from?

Make a guess: A spinning skater pulls in her arms and whirls faster. Where does the extra spin come from?

Mission 3

Angular momentum

  • Angular momentum: L = Iω for a rigid object rotating about a fixed axis.
  • I = rotational inertia about that axis.
  • Unit: kg·m²/s (the same as N m s).
  • Same ω, more mass far from the axis → larger I → larger L.
  • The rotational partner of linear momentum p = mv.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A bicycle wheel: I = 0.12 kg·m², spinning at ω = 25 rad/s.
  • L = Iω = 0.12 × 25 = 3.0 kg·m²/s.
  • ω must be in rad/s. Convert from rev per second or per minute first (1 rev = 2π rad).
Quick check
?A thin hoop (I = MR²) and a uniform solid disk (I = ½MR²) have the same mass M and the same radius R. The hoop rotates about its central axis at angular speed ω; the disk rotates about its central axis at 2ω. How do their angular momenta compare?
Mission 3

Angular impulse

  • A net torque acting for a time changes L.
  • Angular impulse = τΔt = ΔL, for a constant (or average) torque.
  • ΔL = L − L0: final minus initial. Choose a positive sense (e.g. counterclockwise) and keep signs.
  • Rearranged: τ = ΔL/Δt. With I constant: τ = IΔω/Δt = Iα.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A wheel, I = 0.50 kg·m², spins counterclockwise at 12 rad/s. A brake exerts a clockwise torque of 1.5 N·m for 2.0 s.
  • Counterclockwise positive. L0 = 0.50 × 12 = +6.0 kg·m²/s.
  • Angular impulse = τΔt = (−1.5)(2.0) = −3.0 N·m·s.
  • L = L0 + ΔL = 6.0 − 3.0 = +3.0 kg·m²/s → ω = 3.0 ÷ 0.50 = 6.0 rad/s, still turning the same way.
Quick check
?Counterclockwise is taken as positive. A motorized turntable with rotational inertia I is rotating counterclockwise with angular speed ω0. The motor then brings it to rotate clockwise with angular speed 3ω0. Using this sign convention for both the initial and final angular momenta, which expression gives the change in the turntable's angular momentum?
Mission 3

Conservation of angular momentum

  • Net external torque zero → the system's total L stays constant.
  • Torques that parts of a system exert on each other come in equal and opposite pairs (Newton's third law). They cancel: internal torques cannot change the total L.
  • So: I1ω1 = I2ω2 for one object that changes shape.
Mission 3

Changing shape

  • Skater, arms out: I1 = 3.6 kg·m², ω1 = 2.0 rad/s.
  • Arms in: I2 = 1.2 kg·m². Ice and air exert negligible torque about her vertical axis.
  • ω2 = I1ω1/I2 = (3.6 × 2.0) ÷ 1.2 = 6.0 rad/s.
  • L is unchanged. I falls, so ω rises. Nothing creates angular momentum.
  • Rotational kinetic energy rises: 7.2 J → 21.6 J. Her muscles do work pulling her arms in.
Mission 3

Coupled pairs

  • A turntable (I = 0.030 kg·m²) spins freely at 4.0 rad/s. A disk (I = 0.010 kg·m²), not spinning, drops onto it on the same axis.
  • Friction between them is internal. No external torque about the axis.
  • Before: 0.030 × 4.0 + 0 = 0.12 kg·m²/s. After: (0.030 + 0.010)ω.
  • ω = 0.12 ÷ 0.040 = 3.0 rad/s.
  • Kinetic energy is not conserved here: friction while they slip transfers some to internal energy.
Quick check
?Disk 1, with rotational inertia 0.40 kg·m², rotates counterclockwise at 5.0 rad/s on a vertical axle. Disk 2, with rotational inertia 0.20 kg·m², rotates clockwise at 4.0 rad/s on the same axle, just above disk 1. Friction at the axle is negligible. Disk 2 drops onto disk 1, and the two disks soon rotate together. What is the angular speed of the two disks when they rotate together?
Mission check

?A playground merry-go-round with a rotational inertia of 240 kg·m² about its axis turns freely at 1.5 rad/s. A child of mass 40 kg, moving straight toward the axis, steps onto the edge, 1.5 m from the axis. Treat the child as a point mass. What is the new angular speed?

No external torque about the axis, so Iω is constant. The skater is the same story: pulling mass inward cuts I, so ω must rise. The "extra spin" is only extra ω; the angular momentum never changed.

Mission 4Topics 6.3, 6.4

Mission 4: A brake stops a spinning flywheel. Where does its angular momentum go?

Make a guess: A brake stops a spinning flywheel. Where does its angular momentum go?

Mission 4

Angular impulse from a graph

  • Pick a positive sense, e.g. counterclockwise positive.
  • Angular impulse has the same sense as the torque, whatever way the object is turning.
  • Area under a τ–t graph = angular impulse (N·m·s).
  • Area under a net external τ–t graph = ΔL of the object.
  • Area below the t-axis counts as negative.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Wheel: I = 2.5 kg·m², ω0 = 2.0 rad/s counterclockwise, so L0 = +5.0 kg·m²/s.
  • Net counterclockwise torque: rises steadily from 0 to 8.0 N·m in 1.0 s, then stays at 8.0 N·m for 2.0 s.
  • Area = triangle + rectangle = ½(1.0)(8.0) + (2.0)(8.0) = 4.0 + 16 = 20 N·m·s.
  • L = 5.0 + 20 = 25 kg·m²/s → ω = 25 ÷ 2.5 = 10 rad/s.
Quick check
?Counterclockwise is taken as positive. The graph shows the net external torque τ exerted on a wheel as a function of time t. At t = 0 the wheel's angular momentum is +2.0 kg·m²/s. What is the wheel's angular momentum at t = 3.0 s?
Mission 4

Torque from an L–t graph

  • τnet = ΔL/Δt: net torque = slope of the L–t graph.
  • Flat line: net torque zero, even if L is large. A wheel spinning steadily needs no net torque.
Quick check
?Counterclockwise is taken as positive. The graph shows the angular momentum L of a wheel as a function of time t. A student claims that the net torque exerted on the wheel is zero at t = 2 s. Which statement correctly evaluates the claim?
Mission 4

Choose the system

  • Student on a free stool spins up a bicycle wheel held above her head.
  • Her torque on the wheel and the wheel's torque on her: a third-law pair. Equal and opposite angular impulses.
  • System = student + stool + wheel: those torques are internal. Total L stays zero: wheel one way, student the other.
  • System = wheel alone: her torque is external, so the wheel's L changes.
  • Same physics; the choice of system decides whether L is constant.
Quick check
?A student stands at rest at the edge of a turntable that is also at rest and can rotate with negligible friction about a fixed vertical axle. The student starts to walk counterclockwise around the edge, and the turntable starts to rotate clockwise. For which system is the total angular momentum about the axle constant while the student starts to walk?
Quick check
?A flywheel rotates on an axle in a machine. For part of the time shown, a brake pad fixed to the machine's frame, which is bolted to the floor, presses against the flywheel. The graph shows the angular momentum L of the flywheel about its axle as a function of time t. Taking the flywheel alone as the system, which claim about its angular momentum is correct?
Mission 4

Never destroyed, only transferred

  • Angular momentum is conserved in all interactions. If a system's L changes, the same amount appears somewhere else.
  • Nonzero net external torque → L is transferred between the system and its environment.
  • Flywheel braked by a pad fixed to a frame bolted to the floor: the flywheel's L passes through pad, frame and floor to Earth.
  • Earth's huge rotational inertia means its change in ω is far too small to detect.
  • Friction does not destroy angular momentum. (It does transfer kinetic energy to internal energy: a separate quantity.)
Mission check

?An astronaut floats inside a space station, touching nothing and holding nothing, and rotates slowly about an axis through her center of mass. Air resistance is negligible. Which of the following could change her angular momentum about that axis?

Only an interaction with something outside the system can change its angular momentum, and then the same amount goes the other way. Her push on the handrail gives the station an equal and opposite angular impulse; the braked flywheel's angular momentum goes through pad, frame and floor to Earth.

Mission 5Topics 6.3, 6.4, 6.6

Mission 5: A puck slides past a post in a straight line. Does it have angular momentum about the post?

Make a guess: A puck slides past a post in a straight line. Does it have angular momentum about the post?

Mission 5

Angular momentum of a point object

  • About a chosen point P:
  • L = rmv sin θ: r = distance from P to the object; θ = angle between r and v.
  • r sin θ = r⊥, the perpendicular distance from P to the line of motion. So L = mv r⊥.
  • Moving in a straight line at constant velocity: r⊥ is fixed → L about P is constant, even though r and θ keep changing.
Mission 5

It depends on the point

  • P on the line of motion: θ = 0 or 180° → L = 0 about P.
  • P on opposite sides of the path: opposite senses (one clockwise, one counterclockwise).
  • Farther from the path: larger r⊥, larger L.
  • Always state "about which point". A circle is not needed for angular momentum.
Quick check
?The diagram shows, viewed from above, a puck of mass m sliding with constant velocity v along a straight line on a horizontal frictionless surface, and two fixed points, A and B. Which statement about the puck's angular momentum at the instant shown is correct?
Mission 5

Worked example; circles agree

  • Puck: m = 0.20 kg, v = 3.0 m/s; its path passes 0.40 m from a post.
  • L = mv r⊥ = 0.20 × 3.0 × 0.40 = 0.24 kg·m²/s about the post, at every point of the path.
  • Point object in a circle of radius r: θ = 90°, so L = mvr. Also I = mr² and ω = v/r, so Iω = mvr. The two descriptions agree.
  • Total L = sum of the parts, each about the same axis, with signs for sense.
Mission 5

Collisions with rotation

  • Merry-go-round at rest: I = 240 kg·m², radius 2.0 m. A 30 kg child runs at 4.0 m/s along a tangent and jumps on at the rim.
  • The axle's force passes through the axis: no torque about the axle → L conserved about it.
  • Before: L = mvR = 30 × 4.0 × 2.0 = 240 kg·m²/s (child only).
  • After: Itotal = 240 + 30 × 2.0² = 360 kg·m² → ω = 240 ÷ 360 = 0.67 rad/s.
  • Not conserved: linear momentum (the axle pushes) and kinetic energy (240 J → 80 J).
Quick check
?A lump of clay moving horizontally in a straight line strikes and sticks to the outer edge of a door that is initially at rest. The door can rotate about vertical hinges with negligible friction. The collision decreases the kinetic energy of the clay–door system. Which statement about the angular momentum of the clay–door system about the hinge axis is correct?
Mission 5

Orbits

  • Gravity on a satellite points at the planet's center: no torque about the center → the satellite's L is constant.
  • Only gravity acts → the system's mechanical energy is constant.
  • Circular orbit: r and v fixed → K, Ug, E and L all constant.
  • Elliptical orbit: E and L constant; K and Ug trade. Closer → Ug more negative, K larger, faster.
  • At the closest and farthest points, v ⟂ r, so mv1r1 = mv2r2.
  • Example (Earth): 8.7 × 10³ m/s at 6.8 × 10⁶ m from the center → at 1.2 × 10⁷ m: v = 8.7 × 10³ × 6.8 ÷ 12 = 4.9 × 10³ m/s.
Quick check
?The diagram shows the elliptical orbit of a satellite around a planet whose motion is negligible, with the satellite's distances from the planet's center at its closest point P and its farthest point A, and its speed at P. At P and at A the satellite's velocity is perpendicular to the line from the planet's center. What is the satellite's speed at A?
Mission check

?Car 1, of mass m, drives at constant speed v along a straight road that passes a lamp post at a perpendicular distance d. Car 2, of mass 2m, drives at constant speed v/2 along a straight road that passes the same post at a perpendicular distance 3d. What is the ratio L2/L1 of the magnitudes of the cars' angular momenta about the post?

Yes. A puck in straight-line motion has L = mv r⊥ about any point off its path, constant all along the path; zero only about points on the line itself. A satellite's angular momentum about the planet is the same idea.

Mission 6Topic 6.6

Mission 6: How fast must a probe leave a planet so that it never comes back?

Make a guess: How fast must a probe leave a planet so that it never comes back?

Mission 6

Escape speed

  • To escape, an object must reach infinity, where potential energy is zero, with kinetic energy ≥ 0.
  • So it needs total energy ≥ 0. The minimum launch speed gives total energy exactly 0.
  • K + Ug = 0: ½mv² − GMm/r = 0 → vesc = √(2GM/r).
  • The object's own mass cancels. It holds at any distance r from the center, not only at the surface.
  • Earth's surface: √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ ÷ 6.37 × 10⁶) = 11.2 km/s (ignoring air resistance and Earth's rotation).
Quick check
?A probe is launched straight up from the surface of a planet with exactly the escape speed. The planet has no atmosphere, its motion is negligible, and the only force exerted on the probe after launch is the planet's gravitational force. Which statement describes the probe's motion after launch?
Quick check
?A 500 kg probe is launched from the surface of a moon of mass 7.3 × 10²² kg and radius 1.7 × 10⁶ m. The moon has no atmosphere and its motion is negligible. Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N·m²/kg². What is the escape speed from the moon's surface?
Mission check

?A satellite moves in a circular orbit of radius r around a planet with speed v0. The planet's motion is negligible. Which expression gives the escape speed at the distance r from the planet's center, in terms of v0?

Escape means K + Ug = 0: ½mv² = GMm/r, so vesc = √(2GM/r). From Earth's surface that is about 11 km/s, whatever the mass launched.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Krot = ½Iω²; total K = ½Mvcm² + ½Icmω².
  • Rolling without slipping: vcm = rω; ideal static friction does no work. Down a slope, smaller I/(MR²) wins; mass and radius cancel.

Mission 2

  • W = τΔθ, or the area under a τ–θ graph; a torque opposing the rotation takes energy out.
  • Slipping: kinetic friction dissipates energy and vcm ≠ rω; rolling without slipping: no dissipation.

Mission 3

  • L = Iω. A net torque changes it: ΔL = τΔt (angular impulse).
  • No external torque → L constant. Smaller I → larger ω (skaters, divers, coupled disks).

Mission 4

  • Area under τ–t = angular impulse = ΔL; slope of L–t = net torque. Angular impulse has the torque's sense.
  • Angular momentum is never destroyed: internal torques cancel; external torques transfer L to or from the environment.

Mission 5

  • Point object: L = rmv sin θ = mv r⊥ about a chosen point; zero about points on its line of motion.
  • A force through the axis exerts no torque: L about that axis is conserved in sticking collisions and in orbits (mv1r1 = mv2r2 at the closest and farthest points).

Mission 6

  • Escape: K + Ug = 0, so vesc = √(2GM/r), independent of the launched mass.
