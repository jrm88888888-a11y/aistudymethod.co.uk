Mission 1

Mission 1: In earthquake-prone California, most homeowners' policies do not cover earthquakes. Why not?

Make a guess: In earthquake-prone California, most homeowners' policies do not cover earthquakes. Why not?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.5.1 nature, forms and potential impacts of natural hazards (geophysical, atmospheric, hydrological)
  • 3.1.5.1 hazard perception and its economic and cultural determinants
  • 3.1.5.1 human responses (fatalism, prediction, adjustment/adaptation, mitigation, management, risk sharing) and their relationship to incidence, intensity, magnitude, distribution and level of development
Mission 1

What is a natural hazard?

  • A natural hazard is a natural event that threatens people, property or the environment.
  • It becomes a disaster when it overwhelms a community's ability to cope.
  • No people exposed = no hazard: risk needs both the event and vulnerable people.
Mission 1

Three forms of hazard

  • Geophysical: from Earth's internal processes: earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, tsunamis, many mass movements.
  • Atmospheric: from weather and climate: tropical storms, tornadoes, droughts, heatwaves.
  • Hydrological: from water on and near the surface: river and coastal floods.
  • Geophysical events were only 9% of recorded disaster events in 2000–2019, but can be very deadly.
  • Impacts differ by wealth: low-income countries had 23% of disaster deaths with under 10% of world population; high-income countries had 67% of economic losses (US$1.99 trillion).
Quick check

Mission 1

Hazard perception

  • Hazard perception: how people see a hazard's risk. It can differ from the measured risk.
  • Economic determinants: wealth buys protection, insurance and the choice to live somewhere safer, and can breed confidence that technology will cope; poverty limits choices and puts daily needs ahead of rare risks.
  • Cultural determinants: past experience, religious or other beliefs (some see hazards as fate), education, trust in authorities, age and gender roles.
  • Perception shapes what people do, so it shapes the response.
Mission 1

Perception in practice

  • Volcán de Fuego, Guatemala: residents "clearly remember and identify" only eruptions "that required a community-wide response".
  • Communities on its west and east flanks differed in direct experience of past eruptions and in communication with the authorities.
  • California: in 2025 only 15.24% of homeowners' policies included earthquake cover.
  • Same hazard, different perceptions: experience, trust and cost all shape them.
Quick check
?Which is an economic determinant of hazard perception?
Mission 1

Responses 1: accept, forecast, adapt

  • Fatalism: accept the hazard as fate or God's will; little action, perhaps relief afterwards.
  • Prediction: forecast and warn. Earthquake early warning: fast P waves trip sensors and send alerts, giving "seconds to minutes".
  • Adjustment/adaptation: change how people live with the hazard. California's fault-zone law: no building for human occupancy over an active fault, generally 50 feet away.
Mission 1

Responses 2: reduce, organise, share

  • Mitigation: reduce the impact. Hong Kong's slope safety system cut the landslide risk from man-made slopes by 75%.
  • Management: organised, planned action by authorities before, during and after an event (the Hazard Management Cycle).
  • Risk sharing: spread the cost, mainly through insurance. New Zealand homeowners with private insurance get state natural-hazards cover up to NZ$300,000 (from Oct 2022), paid for by levies.
Quick check
?A city bans new homes within 50 feet of an active fault. Which response is this?
Mission 1

What shapes the response?

  • Incidence (frequency): frequent hazards build experience and routines; rare ones are easy to ignore.
  • Intensity and magnitude: bigger events overwhelm adaptation; they need management and relief.
  • Distribution: where hazards are concentrated (plate boundaries, cyclone belts, floodplains), adaptation and zoning pay off.
  • Level of development: wealthier places can afford prediction, engineering and insurance; poorer places rely more on coping, relief and sometimes fatalism.
  • But wealth is not enough: perception matters (California's low uptake of earthquake cover).
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): link each response to these five factors, with a named example.
Mission check

?A riverside town has small floods every few years. Its one huge flood was 60 years ago. Many residents refuse flood insurance, saying "the big one won't come again in our lifetime". What best explains their choice?

Perception is shaped by experience, beliefs, trust and money. In California, as in the flood town, a rare but large hazard is easy to underrate, so people under-use responses such as insurance (risk sharing).

Mission 2: Why do some disasters leave a community better prepared, and others leave it worse off for years?

Mission 2

Why model responses?

  • A disaster unfolds over time: before, during, after.
  • Models simplify that sequence so events can be compared.
  • AQA names two: the Park model (how quality of life falls and recovers) and the Hazard Management Cycle (the repeating stages of managing risk).
Mission 2

The Park model

  • Graph of quality of life (or normal activity) against time.
  • Disruption: a sharp fall at impact.
  • Relief (hours to days): search, rescue, emergency aid.
  • Rehabilitation (weeks to months): temporary shelter, services restored.
  • Reconstruction (months to years): rebuilding. The line may end above the old level (if lessons are built) or below it.
  • The curve's depth and slope depend on the hazard's magnitude and on preparation and development.
Quick check
?On a Park model graph, a curve falls very deeply and climbs back slowly, ending below its starting level. What does this most likely show?
Mission 2

The Hazard Management Cycle

  • Mitigation: reduce the risk before events (zoning, barriers, building design).
  • Preparedness: monitoring, warnings, plans, drills (links to prediction).
  • Response: evacuation, rescue, relief.
  • Recovery: repair, rebuild, compensate (links to risk sharing: insurance).
  • Recovery feeds back into mitigation, so the cycle repeats. Management is the whole cycle run by authorities.
Mission 2

Grindavík, Iceland, 2023–24

  • Preparedness: radar and GPS tracked ground uplift as magma built up beneath the area.
  • Response: on 10 November 2023 magma moved under the town; the town (3,700 people) was evacuated that day; half the town dropped more than a metre.
  • Mitigation: earth-and-rock barriers built from November 2023 diverted lava; in January 2024 a fissure opened outside the barrier and lava burned three homes.
  • Recovery: state natural-catastrophe insurance (NTÍ) covers buildings damaged by eruptions; under a 2024 law the state offered to buy homes, with 546 applications by 27 March 2024.
Mission 2

Grindavík on the models

  • Cycle: every stage operated: monitoring → early evacuation → barriers → insurance and buy-out.
  • Park: a sharp fall for residents (homes lost or abandoned), then fast relief and money to rebuild lives elsewhere.
  • So the town itself may not recover, but most households' quality of life can: recovery by relocation, not reconstruction in place.
  • High development paid for monitoring, engineering and risk sharing.
Quick check
?Which Grindavík action belongs to the recovery stage of the Hazard Management Cycle?
Mission 2

Freetown, Sierra Leone, 2017

  • 1,040 mm of rain fell from 1 July to 14 August 2017.
  • At 6:50 a.m. on 14 August a slope in the Regent area collapsed into a fast debris flow.
  • About 6,000 people were affected; 1,141 were declared dead or missing; 349 buildings were destroyed in Regent.
  • Before the event: "Nearly all the poor in the Western Area live in informal housing"; the World Bank assessment describes "highly fragmented governance".
Mission 2

Freetown on the models

  • Cycle: the mitigation and preparedness stages were weakest: informal housing on steep slopes, unclear planning control, no warning.
  • Park: no warning and a fast debris flow → a deep, sudden fall; relief and rebuilding then depended on fragmented local governance.
  • Compare: Grindavík's slow lava and wealth gave time and money; Freetown's fast flow and poverty gave neither.
  • Response depends on magnitude, speed of onset, incidence and development, all at once.
Quick check
?Why would Freetown's Park model curve fall more steeply than Grindavík's?
Mission check

?Two towns suffer the same river flood. Town X had flood warnings, defences and widespread insurance; Town Y had none. How do their Park model curves most likely differ?

The hazard sets the challenge; what a community does around the Hazard Management Cycle (and can afford) sets the shape of its Park curve. Strong mitigation, preparedness and insurance mean a shallower fall and faster, sometimes better, recovery.

Mission 3

Earth's structure

  • Crust: oceanic about 5 km thick; continental about 30 km on average, up to 100 km under large mountain ranges.
  • Mantle: about 2,900 km thick.
  • Core: a liquid outer core about 2,200 km thick and a solid inner core about 1,250 km thick.
  • Lithosphere (crust plus rigid upper mantle): at least 80 km thick over much of Earth; it rides on the asthenosphere: hot mantle rock that is solid but can flow slowly.
Mission 3

Internal energy

  • Earth's heat comes from two main sources: radioactive decay and residual heat from Earth's formation.
  • Heat moves slowly towards the surface.
  • "Cold, denser material convects downward and hotter, lighter material rises because of gravity": mantle convection.
Quick check
?Which layer forms the tectonic plates?
Mission 3

Sea-floor spreading

  • In 1961 scientists began to propose that mid-ocean ridges are weak zones where the ocean floor is splitting.
  • Harry Hess suggested new oceanic crust "continuously spread away from the ridges in a conveyor belt-like motion".
  • Prediction: rocks should be young at the ridge crest and older further away. That is what was found.
  • Spreading rates: the Mid-Atlantic Ridge averages about 2.5 cm a year; the East Pacific Rise more than 15 cm a year.
Mission 3

Evidence: magnetic stripes

  • As lava cools at a ridge, magnetic minerals record the direction of Earth's magnetic field: palaeomagnetism.
  • Mapping in the 1950s found alternating stripes of magnetically different rock on either side of the mid-ocean ridge.
  • The pattern showed that the field "has flip-flopped many times": reversals.
  • Matching stripes on both sides, the same width at the same distance, fit crust spreading out from the centre.
Quick check
?Why do the magnetic stripes on one side of a ridge mirror those on the other side?
Mission 3

Convection currents

  • The older view: convecting mantle drags the plates along by friction at their base.
  • Convection still matters: it carries heat up, and cold slabs sinking into the mantle are part of it.
  • But it struggles to explain why plates attached to sinking slabs move fastest.
Mission 3

Ridge push and gravitational sliding

  • New lithosphere at a ridge is hot and buoyant, so the ridge stands high above the ocean floor.
  • Ridge push: gravity makes the lithosphere slide away "downhill" from the elevated ridge. The intrusion of magma at the ridge adds a further force.
  • Gravitational sliding is AQA's term for this gravity-driven slide of plates down the slopes away from the ridge.
Mission 3

Slab pull

  • At a subduction zone, old oceanic lithosphere is cold and dense, so it sinks into the mantle.
  • Slab pull: the sinking slab drags the rest of the plate behind it.
  • USGS's 1996 booklet called slab pull "the driving force of plate tectonics".
  • Evidence: plates attached to subducting slabs, such as the Pacific, Australian and Nazca plates, move the fastest.
Quick check
?Why does new lithosphere slide away from a mid-ocean ridge?
Mission 3

Magma plumes and hotspots

  • A mantle plume is a column of exceptionally hot material; where it reaches the base of a plate, it feeds a hotspot.
  • Hawaii formed more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary.
  • As the Pacific Plate moves over the hotspot, older islands are carried away: the Big Island's oldest exposed rocks are under 0.7 million years old; Kauai's about 5.5 million.
  • Some hotspots sit under spreading ridges, such as Iceland.
Quick check
?How can a hotspot island chain show which way a plate is moving?
Mission 3

Weighing the mechanisms

  • USGS: "none of the proposed mechanisms can explain all the facets of plate movement", and the forces are too deep to test directly.
  • The plume model is also debated: some studies suggest hotspots are "neither deep phenomena nor 'fixed'".
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): say which force has the strongest evidence (slab pull: fastest plates), then show how heat, convection and gravity work together.
Mission check

?The East Pacific Rise spreads more than 15 cm a year, more than six times as fast as the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The Pacific and Nazca plates on either side are edged by subduction zones. What best explains the difference?

The energy is internal heat (radioactive decay and residual heat) driving convection; gravity turns it into motion through slab pull and ridge push (gravitational sliding). Plates with sinking slabs move fastest.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 4

Three kinds of margin

  • Constructive (divergent): plates move apart at spreading centres.
  • Destructive (convergent): plates move together; one may sink (subduction), or two continents collide.
  • Conservative (transform): plates slide horizontally past each other.
  • What moves them: heat drives mantle convection; gravity adds ridge push at ridges and slab pull where cold slabs sink.
Mission 4

Constructive: ocean ridges

  • Magma rises where plates part and builds new ocean floor along an ocean ridge; the Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year on average.
  • Iceland sits on that ridge; rifting at Krafla opened about 7 m between 1975 and 1984.
  • Seismicity: frequent, mostly shallow earthquakes. Vulcanicity: runny basaltic lava from fissures, building the ridge.
Mission 4

Constructive on land: rift valleys

  • Where continental crust stretches, blocks drop between faults to form a rift valley.
  • A new spreading centre may be developing along the East African Rift.
  • Spreading has already torn Arabia from Africa, opening the Red Sea.
Quick check
?Which pair of landforms both form at constructive margins?
Mission 4

Destructive: ocean meets continent

  • The oceanic Nazca Plate is being subducted under the South American Plate; the overriding plate is lifted, building the Andes.
  • Subduction creates trenches, the deepest parts of the ocean floor.
  • Vulcanicity: this convergence "sustains many of the Earth's active volcanoes". Water released by the sinking slab helps the mantle above it melt, feeding viscous, explosive eruptions.
  • Seismicity: strong, destructive earthquakes; some are very deep, such as one at 636 km under Bolivia.
Mission 4

Destructive: ocean meets ocean

  • One oceanic plate sinks beneath the other, forming a trench and a curved chain of volcanoes: an island arc.
  • Examples: the Marianas (Pacific Plate under the Philippine Plate) and the Aleutian Islands.
  • The Challenger Deep, at the southern end of the Mariana Trench, is about 10,935 m deep.
Mission 4

Destructive: continents collide

  • When two continents meet, "neither is subducted because the continental rocks are relatively light".
  • India's collision with Asia, about 50 million years ago, crumpled the crust into the Himalaya; most of the growth was in the last 10 million years.
  • These are young fold mountains: strong shallow earthquakes, but little or no volcanic activity.
Quick check
?A chain of volcanic islands curves around a deep trench in the western Pacific. What kind of margin is it?
Mission 4

Conservative margins

  • Plates slide horizontally past one another; most transform faults are on the ocean floor, offsetting ridges.
  • The San Andreas fault zone, California, is about 1,300 km long; the Pacific Plate has slid past the North American Plate for 10 million years, at about 5 cm a year on average.
  • Seismicity: shallow earthquakes. Vulcanicity: little or none, because no crust is made or destroyed.
Quick check
?If the Pacific Plate has slid past the North American Plate at an average of 5 cm a year for 10 million years, how far has it moved?
Mission 4

The global pattern

  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes occur around the Pacific, where oceanic plates subduct; about 17% in the Alpide belt.
  • There are about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, not counting those along spreading ridges on the ocean floor; many ring the Pacific.
  • So the biggest hazards cluster at destructive margins.
Mission 4

Linking process, landform and example

  • Each margin: movement → process (seismicity, vulcanicity) → landform → named example.
  • Constructive: ridge push → shallow quakes, basaltic fissures → ocean ridge, rift valley → Mid-Atlantic Ridge, East Africa.
  • Destructive (subduction): slab pull → deep quakes, explosive volcanoes → trench, island arc → Andes, Marianas.
  • Destructive (collision): continents converge, neither sinks → strong shallow quakes, few volcanoes → young fold mountains → Himalaya.
  • Conservative: shear → shallow quakes, no volcanoes → fault zone → San Andreas.
  • Exam move (AQA "Explain"): one margin, one process, one landform, one located example per paragraph.
Mission check

?A plate margin has a deep trench offshore, earthquakes that get deeper inland, and a chain of explosive volcanoes on land. What kind of margin is it, and why are there volcanoes?

Where plates meet, the movement decides the hazards and landforms: plates parting build ridges and rift valleys; a sinking slab makes trenches, deep earthquakes and explosive volcanoes; colliding continents raise fold mountains; plates sliding past give shallow earthquakes and almost no volcanoes.

Mission 5

What makes an eruption explosive

  • Magma composition, viscosity and gas content largely decide whether an eruption is explosive.
  • Silica has the greatest effect on viscosity; most igneous rocks hold about 45–78% silica by weight.
  • Low-silica basalt is runny: at Kīlauea, lava flow fronts move up to 10 km/h. Gas escapes easily, so eruptions are effusive.
  • Silica-rich magma is sticky and traps gas, so pressure builds until it erupts explosively.
Mission 5

Eruption style follows the setting

  • Constructive (divergent) margins: basalt from the mantle; effusive fissure eruptions. Reykjanes, Iceland, is where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level.
  • Destructive (convergent) margins: the sinking plate feeds more silica-rich, gas-rich magma; explosive stratovolcanoes. Fuego sits where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate.
  • Hot spots: plumes away from margins. Hawaii is more than 3,200 km from the nearest plate boundary; its eruptions are effusive.
Mission 5

Measuring eruption size: the VEI

  • The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) runs from 0 (non-explosive); 8 is the top of the usual scale, though USGS calls it open-ended.
  • Each step is about 10 times the volume of ash, except VEI 1. Mount St Helens (1980) was VEI 5: about 1 km³ of ash.
  • Most Hawaiian eruptions rate VEI 0 or 1.

?A new eruption produces a column of ash many kilometres high from a steep cone above a subduction zone. Which magma best explains it?
Mission 5

Primary hazards: lava and pyroclastic flows

  • Lava flows are usually slow enough to walk away from, so they mostly destroy property: at Grindavík in January 2024, lava burned three homes.
  • Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot rock fragments and gas, typically faster than 80 km/h and 200–700 °C. They destroy nearly everything in their path.
  • AQA calls glowing pyroclastic flows nuées ardentes ("glowing clouds"). At Mont Pelée (Martinique) in 1902, one demolished St Pierre, "killing nearly 30,000 inhabitants".
  • Their speed means evacuation before they start is the only reliable protection.
Mission 5

Primary hazards: tephra, ash and gases

  • Tephra: any rock fragments blasted into the air, from fine ash to large bombs.
  • Ash is "hard, does not dissolve in water, is extremely abrasive and mildly corrosive". It can collapse roofs, and wet ash can weigh twice as much as dry. Jet engines have failed after flying through thin ash.
  • Gases: water vapour is the most abundant; carbon dioxide and sulfur dioxide (SO₂) matter most.
  • SO₂ "can cause acid rain and air pollution downwind". CO₂ is dense and collects in hollows: three ski patrol members died at Mammoth Mountain, California, after falling into a CO₂-filled snow depression.
Quick check
?Why does ash fall kill more people when heavy rain falls at the same time?
Mission 5

Secondary hazard: lahars

  • Lahars (volcanic mudflows) are slurries of debris and water that look like wet concrete.
  • Water comes from melting snow and ice, heavy rain, or crater lakes and rivers.
  • They often grow to more than 10 times their starting size, and can exceed 200 km/h on steep slopes.
  • They follow river valleys far from the volcano, and outlast the eruption: rain after Fuego's 2018 eruption produced many lahars.
Mission 5

Distribution, frequency and predictability

  • Distribution: about 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, many around the Pacific "Ring of Fire", plus spreading ridges and hot spots.
  • Frequency: about 500 of the 1,350 have erupted in historical time. In the Cascades (USA), eruptions averaged about 2 per century over 4,000 years.
  • Magnitude: small eruptions are common; each VEI step up is rarer.
  • Predictability: rising magma deforms the ground. At Svartsengi, GPS showed 35 cm of uplift by mid-December 2023. Forecasts at Pinatubo in 1991 saved at least 5,000 lives.
  • Regularity: low. Eruptions cluster and pause; few volcanoes erupt at fixed intervals.
Mission 5

Armero, 1985

  • Nevado del Ruiz (Colombia) is a 5,389 m stratovolcano topped by glaciers.
  • On 13 November 1985 an explosive eruption melted ice. Lahars raced down river valleys and reached Armero, 45 km away, about two hours later.
  • Within minutes about 23,000 people, most of the town, were killed.
  • Why no escape: scientists lacked the equipment and government support to monitor the volcano, the government was focused on political unrest, and residents had been told they were safe. USGS: two hours was "plenty of time" to evacuate.
  • Impacts: the lahars were a secondary hazard. The deaths at Armero came from them, not from primary hazards near the vent.
Mission check

?A town of 20,000 lies in a river valley 50 km from an ice-capped stratovolcano. No lava could reach it. How could an eruption still kill most of its people hours later?

At Armero, melted ice made lahars that followed the valleys 45 km to the town in about two hours. Weak monitoring and governance meant no warning reached people who had time to climb to higher ground.

Mission 6

Magnitude: how big the earthquake is

  • Magnitude measures the energy released at the source; it does not change with distance.
  • The Richter scale (1935) is now used mainly for small, local earthquakes. USGS uses the moment magnitude scale (Mw) for the rest.
  • Each whole number up means 10 times the measured amplitude and about 32 times the energy.
  • Agencies can differ: Nepal's seismological centre gave the 2015 earthquake 7.6; USGS gives Mw 7.8. Name your source.
Mission 6

Intensity: what people feel

  • Intensity is the effect of an earthquake at a place. The US uses the Modified Mercalli Intensity (MMI) scale, in Roman numerals.
  • It has no mathematical basis: it is "an arbitrary ranking based on observed effects".
  • Low numbers describe how people feel the shaking; engineers help assign VIII and above.
  • One earthquake has one magnitude but many intensities, usually falling with distance.
Quick check
?Using about 32 times the energy for each whole step, how many times as much energy does an Mw 7.8 release as an Mw 5.8?
Mission 6

Volcanic magnitude: the VEI

  • The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) runs from 0; each step is about 10 times the ash volume (except VEI 1).
  • VEI 5, such as Mount St Helens in 1980, is about 1 km³ of ash; most Hawaiian eruptions are VEI 0–1.
  • A VEI 8 eruption puts out more than 1,000 km³. Pinatubo in 1991 ejected more than 5 km³.
  • The VEI measures explosivity only: a huge effusive lava eruption can score low.
Mission 6

Magnitude and frequency

  • Small events are far more frequent than large ones. Averages from USGS counts, 2000–2021: about 1,613 earthquakes of M5–5.9 a year, 137 of M6–6.9, 14 of M7–7.9 and 1.2 of M8 or more.
  • Over the long term USGS expects about 15 M7 and one M8+ a year.
  • Volcanoes follow the same pattern: each VEI step is rarer.
  • Distribution shapes frequency: about 81% of the largest earthquakes strike the circum-Pacific belt; volcanoes cluster on the Ring of Fire.
Mission 6

Regular or random?

  • Parkfield, on the San Andreas Fault, had magnitude-6 earthquakes in 1857, 1881, 1901, 1922, 1934 and 1966. Gaps: 12–32 years, average about 22.
  • In 1985 USGS forecast another "within the next several years". It came on 28 September 2004 (Mw 6.0), 38 years after 1966.
  • So even Parkfield, with an unusually regular record, is only roughly regular: the timing of the next earthquake is close to random.
  • Volcanoes are no more regular: in the Cascades, eruptions averaged about 2 per century, in clusters.

Mission 6

Predictability

  • Earthquakes: USGS: "Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major earthquake". Where is predictable (plate margins); when is not.
  • Instead, hazard maps give probabilities: the shaking with a 10% or 2% chance of being exceeded in 50 years.
  • Volcanoes give warning signs. At Svartsengi, GPS showed 35 cm of uplift by mid-December 2023; forecasts at Pinatubo saved at least 5,000 lives.
  • Tsunamis: the source earthquake is unpredictable, but the wave's arrival can be forecast once it forms.

Mission 6

Rare events cause most deaths

  • In USGS's list for 2000–2019, two years made up about 65% of all earthquake deaths: 2004 (298,101) and 2010 (226,050), out of 808,717.
  • The 2010 total rests on Haiti's disputed toll: officially 316,000, but other estimates are "as low as fewer than 100,000".
  • Haiti was Mw 7.0, in the M7–7.9 class that averages about 14 a year: rare in impact, not in magnitude.
  • UNDRR: risk models tuned to the largest events often miss "smaller, often recurrent and in sum equally damaging events".
Mission check

?A city sits beside a volcano that has small eruptions every few years and has had two very large ones in the last 2,000 years. Which plan is best?

Magnitude and frequency are inversely related, and a few high-impact events cause most deaths. Planners weigh probability against impact: design standards for likely events, warnings and evacuation for rare extremes.

Mission 7

Two plate settings

  • Fuego, Guatemala: a stratovolcano in the Central American arc, where the Cocos Plate subducts beneath the Caribbean Plate (destructive margin).
  • Reykjanes Peninsula, Iceland: where the Mid-Atlantic Ridge rises above sea level; a rift zone (constructive margin).

Mission 7

Fuego, 3 June 2018: the event

  • The first pyroclastic flows came at 10:00. Activity peaked at about 13:10, with a column 16–19 km high.
  • At 15:05 a large pulse began; at 15:09 it hit the Las Lajas bridge and overflowed onto the RN-14 road towards San Miguel Los Lotes.
  • About 15.1 million m³ of pyroclastic flow material was deposited in Las Lajas, more than double the average since 1999.

Mission 7

  • Social: about two-thirds of the north-western part of San Miguel Los Lotes suffered "total inundation, destruction, and burial". An estimated 5,000 people lost their homes.
  • The death toll is uncertain: official counts of the missing are 332 (Naismith et al., 2020) and 430 (Charbonnier et al., 2023); survivors' estimates are far higher.
  • Economic: the flows overran the RN-14 road at the Las Lajas bridge.
  • Environmental: valleys filled with deposits, which rain then remobilised as lahars.

  • Political: a 2024 law (Act 16/2024) let the state company Þórkatla buy residents' homes; by 27 March 2024 it had 546 applications.

Mission 8: After a 2004 earthquake off Sumatra, people died in 10 countries around the Indian Ocean. How?

Mission 8

Focus, epicentre and depth

  • The focus (hypocentre) is where rupture starts underground; the epicentre is the point on the surface above it.
  • Depth classes: shallow 0–70 km, intermediate 70–300 km, deep 300–700 km.
  • Shallow-focus earthquakes occur at every type of margin. Deep-focus ones occur only inside slabs sinking at subduction zones.
  • Distribution: about 81% of the largest earthquakes strike the circum-Pacific belt and about 17% the Alpide belt.
Mission 8

P, S and L waves

  • Rupture on a fault fractures the crust and releases energy as seismic waves.
  • P waves (primary): compressional, fastest, 5–7 km/s in typical crust.
  • S waves (secondary): shear waves, about 60% of P-wave speed; they cannot travel through liquids.
  • L waves (surface: Love and Rayleigh): slowest, but with the largest ground motions; they "cause buildings to fail".
Quick check
?Take P waves at 6.0 km/s and S waves at 3.5 km/s in crust (within the ranges earlier in this mission). The S wave arrives 20 s after the P wave. How far away is the earthquake?
Mission 8

Ground shaking and displacement

  • Ground shaking is the main primary hazard: surface waves cause buildings to fail and fall.
  • AQA calls these shockwaves.
  • Ground displacement: the fault itself moves. Kaikōura (New Zealand), 2016: the rupture "unzipped" about 180 km in nearly 2 minutes, displacing the ground by up to 12 m.
  • More than 20 faults ruptured, a world record; parts of the coast rose by up to 6.5 m.
Mission 8

Liquefaction

  • Liquefaction: loosely packed, saturated sediments lose strength in strong shaking.
  • Buildings sink or tilt, and buried pipes can float up.
  • Christchurch (New Zealand): in March 2012 the government rezoned 251 more properties red, citing "widespread liquefaction".
Mission 8

Landslides

  • Shaking triggers landslides on steep slopes: at least 25,000 in Nepal in 2015; over 10,000 at Kaikōura in 2016.
  • Avalanches: in Nepal, an avalanche mostly of snow, then rockfalls, destroyed Langtang village; another killed 20 people at Everest Base Camp.

Mission 8

How a tsunami forms

  • At a subduction zone the plates lock, then the fault slips in a great earthquake.
  • The sea bed is thrust up (or drops) and lifts the whole water column above it: a tsunami.
  • In deep water a tsunami travels at 800–1,000 km/h with a wavelength of 500–1,000 km; near shore it slows to 30–50 km/h, and its height and steepness grow.
Mission 8

Tsunami impacts

  • 2004, Indian Ocean (Mw 9.1, Sumatra–Andaman): the tsunami killed over 230,000 people. The USGS records deaths in 10 countries.
  • 2011, Tōhoku (Japan): the tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died. Inundation was much wider than hazard maps predicted.
  • Near the source there is little time: at Kaikōura the first wave arrived within 10 minutes, with a 6.9 m run-up at Goose Bay.

In 2004 the sea bed moved along the Sumatra–Andaman subduction zone and lifted the whole water column. The tsunami crossed the Indian Ocean at deep-water speeds of 800–1,000 km/h and killed people in 10 countries.

Mission 9

Nepal, 25 April 2015: the event

  • Mw 7.8 (USGS) on Saturday 25 April at 11:56 local time. The epicentre was near Barpak, Gorkha district, about 76 km north-west of Kathmandu.
  • Thrust faulting where India converges with Eurasia at about 45 mm a year; the focus was about 8 km deep.
  • The rupture spread east, towards Kathmandu. Aftershocks included an M7.3 on 12 May.
  • Secondary hazards: at least 25,000 landslides; an avalanche killed 20 people at Everest Base Camp.
Mission 9

Nepal: social impacts

  • Over 8,790 deaths and 22,300 injuries by June 2015; the lives of about 8 million people, almost a third of the population, were affected.
  • Over half a million houses destroyed; nearly 7,000 schools completely or significantly damaged.
  • More women and girls died than men and boys, partly because of gendered roles that kept women indoors.
  • Data: USGS lists at least 8,669 killed and 384 missing, including the 12 May aftershock; EM-DAT gives 8,969. Counts differ by date and source.
Mission 9

Nepal: economic, environmental and political impacts

  • Economic: damage and losses of about US$7 billion, about one-third of GDP (2013–14). At least 700,000 more people were expected to fall into poverty.
  • Environmental: weakened slopes left the region more prone to landslides and floods in the monsoon.
  • Political: the government made an official request for international help "within hours", and later ran a national housing reconstruction programme.
  • Primary or secondary? Deaths and collapse from shaking are primary impacts; landslide damage and the poverty that followed are secondary.
Mission 9

Nepal: why vulnerability varied

  • Between communities: "poorer rural areas" were hit hardest; the six lowest-HDI districts with high losses per person included Sindhupalchowk, Gorkha and Dolakha.
  • Buildings: many houses "did not have any seismic-resistant features".
  • Isolation: road damage further isolated rural areas.
  • Timing: a Saturday, so schools were closed; a night-time quake would "certainly" have killed more.
Mission 9

Nepal: responses, short and long term

  • Short term: 134 international search-and-rescue teams from 34 countries; 4,236 helicopter flights; 7,558 people rescued by air and 4,689 by land.
  • Neighbours, especially young people, dug others out of the rubble. Transit shelters opened in Kathmandu, but tarpaulins ran short.
  • Long term: the housing programme built over 700,000 earthquake-resilient houses. Grants of NPR 300,000 came in three parts, paid only after engineers' inspections.

Mission 9

Kaikōura, 14 November 2016: the event

  • Mw 7.8 at 12:02 a.m. on Monday 14 November; focus about 15 km deep.
  • Oblique-reverse faulting on the Pacific–Australia plate boundary, which moves about 40 mm a year.
  • More than 20 faults ruptured over about 180 km, a world record.
  • Secondary hazards: over 10,000 landslides, more than 200 damming rivers; coastal uplift up to 6.5 m; a tsunami with a 6.9 m run-up at Goose Bay.
Mission 9

Kaikōura: impacts and responses

  • Social: two deaths, one from a heart attack and one when a homestead collapsed. Landslides cut road and rail, stranding many visitors.
  • Response: road access to Kaikōura was restored before Christmas 2016; rail reopened in September 2017 and State Highway 1 in December 2017.
  • Economic: about 40,000 claims went to the state Earthquake Commission.
  • Environmental: raised sea bed exposed along the coast (uplift up to 6.5 m).
Mission 9

Comparing Nepal and Kaikōura

  • Hazard: similar. Both Mw 7.8 and shallow, with thousands of landslides.
  • Buildings: many Nepali houses had no seismic-resistant features; New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads.
  • Exposure: Nepal's rupture spread towards the crowded Kathmandu Valley.
  • Recovery: New Zealand homeowners had state hazard insurance; Nepal relied on government grants and donor-funded programmes.

The hazard was similar. Vulnerability and exposure were not: in Nepal over half a million houses were destroyed, many with no seismic-resistant features, and the poorest rural districts lost most. Event frequency: no upward trend

  • USGS: catalogues list more earthquakes "not because there are more earthquakes, but because there are more seismic instruments".
  • Year-to-year swings are normal: 2010 had 23 major earthquakes, 1989 only 6 (USGS FAQ; the USGS yearly table behind the chart counts 24 for 2010). Over the long term, about 16 a year.
Mission 10

Exposure is rising

  • People living in seismic areas rose from about 1.41 billion (1975) to 2.72 billion (2015).
  • People within 100 km of the 220 most dangerous volcanoes rose from about 227 million to 414 million, but stayed about 5.5% of world population: the rise follows population growth.
  • World population is projected to grow from 8.2 billion (2024) to about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s (UN projection).
  • The JRC: "With a growing population and urbanizing area, also exposure is expected to increase".

Mission 10

Hi-tech monitoring and warning

  • GPS: Hawaii's stations fix their positions daily, usually to better than a centimetre, revealing magma moving underground.
  • Earthquake early warning detects the fast P waves and alerts people "seconds to minutes" before stronger shaking. Japan's system, built for US$600 million after Kobe (1995), stopped trains: none derailed in 2011.
  • Phones: Android phones now send alerts in 98 countries; in user feedback, 36% received the alert before shaking began.
  • Limit: warning, not prediction.
Mission 10

Four kinds of response

  • Prevention: stopping the hazard or keeping people out of it (land-use zoning).
  • Mitigation: reducing its effects (building design, lava barriers).
  • Preparedness: plans, drills, warnings, so people act fast.
  • Adaptation: changing how people live with the risk (insurance, retreat from the riskiest land).
  • Short-term responses rescue and relieve; long-term responses rebuild and reduce future risk.
Mission 10

Education and community preparedness

  • Drills: "Drop, Cover, and Hold On". In 2015 more than 40 million people in 60 countries registered for ShakeOut drills.
  • Education: a review of eleven studies found "consistent and robust evidence" that formal education reduces vulnerability.
  • Communities act first: in Nepal in 2015, neighbours, especially young people, dug others out of the rubble.
Mission 10

Land-use zoning and managed retreat

  • California's Alquist-Priolo Act (after 1971 San Fernando): buildings for people may not be placed over an active fault, and must generally be set back 15 m (50 feet).
  • Limit: it covers only surface rupture; a separate Act maps liquefaction and landslides.
  • Managed retreat: in Christchurch the owners of almost 5,500 properties accepted government offers; the 602-hectare red zone is now a river-corridor regeneration area.
Mission 10

Insurance: spreading the loss

  • New Zealand: homeowners with private insurance also get state natural-hazard cover, generally up to NZ$300,000 plus GST.
  • Iceland: NTÍ covers volcanic, earthquake and landslide damage to buildings.
  • California: only 15.24% of homeowners' policies included earthquake cover in 2025.

Mission 10

Future strategies and perspectives

  • Technology: phone-based alerts reach places without seismometer networks, though warning times can be short.
  • Governments: zoning and buy-outs protect many people but uproot communities (Christchurch, Grindavík).
  • Households: insurance, retrofitting and drills depend on income and knowledge.
  • Exam move: weigh who pays, who benefits, and over what time scale.
Mission check

?A city on an active fault has had no more large earthquakes this century than last. Yet its expected losses from the next large earthquake are now far higher. What best explains this?

Event frequency is steady, but exposure is rising: 93% more people lived in seismic areas in 2015 than in 1975, and the world's population is projected to reach about 10.3 billion. Monitoring, preparedness, zoning and insurance decide whether more exposure means more deaths.

Mission 11

The hazard management cycle

  • Mitigation: reduce the hazard's effects or people's vulnerability before it strikes.
  • Preparedness: warnings, plans, drills and stockpiles.
  • Response: rescue and relief in the hours and days after.
  • Recovery: rehabilitation (weeks to months: shelter, water, services), then reconstruction (years).
  • How a place recovers sets its vulnerability for the next event, so the cycle repeats.
Mission 11

Park's model

  • Park's model (Park, 1991) plots quality of life against time: normal → a sharp fall at impact → relief → rehabilitation → reconstruction.
  • A well-prepared place falls less and recovers faster; reconstruction can end above the old level (built back better) or below it.
  • Limitation: it is a generalised curve, not a measurement, and recovery is uneven between groups.
Mission 11

Modify the event: land-use zoning and defences

  • Land-use zoning: California's Alquist-Priolo Act bars buildings for people from active fault traces, with a set-back of 15 m (50 feet). After 2011, Christchurch's 602-hectare red zone became a river corridor.
  • Engineering defences: in 2011 Japan's tsunami overtopped seawalls of mainly 5–10 m; more than 15,000 people died.
  • Japan now plans for two levels: defences for frequent tsunamis, and evacuation-centred measures for the largest. Walls of about 12 m are planned; critics argue relocating shrinking communities may be more efficient.
Mission 11

Modify the event: hazard-resistant design

  • Braced frames and shear walls resist sideways forces; base isolation bearings change how a building moves and add damping.
  • Codes matter: in Mashiki (Kumamoto, Japan, 2016) wooden houses built before 1981 collapsed far more often than newer ones, so retrofitting older buildings is a priority.
  • New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquake loads.
Mission 11

Modify the event: lava diversion

  • Earth barriers at Fagradalsfjall (2021) delayed lava by up to 16 days.
  • At Heimaey (1973), seawater sprayed on a lava flow cooled and slowed it, saving the harbour.
  • Limit: at Grindavík on 14 January 2024 a fissure opened outside the barrier, and its lava burned three homes.
  • Diversion works for slow lava, not for pyroclastic flows or lahars.

  • Volunteer mapping: over 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, used by Nepal's army and the Red Cross.
  • Phones: anonymised data from 12 million phones showed an estimated 390,000 more people than normal leaving the Kathmandu Valley.

Mission 11

Evaluating management

  • Mitigation can pay: a US study found federal mitigation grants "save $6 per $1 spent", and adopting model building codes $11.
  • Pre-event measures can be exceeded by rarer events (Tōhoku's walls).
  • Rescue saves lives in hours; reconstruction decides how vulnerable people are next time.
  • Scale: local (barriers, slope drains), national (codes, insurance), global (volunteer mapping, phone alerts).
Mission check

?New Zealand kept Kaikōura's deaths to two, and Nepal's inspected rebuilding produced over 700,000 safer homes. Which judgement do these support?

Hong Kong's slope stabilisation cut the risk from man-made slopes by about 75% (2013), and US studies find mitigation repays its cost several times over. Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hazards are geophysical, atmospheric or hydrological; impacts depend on exposure and wealth (low-income countries: 23% of deaths, under 10% of people).
  • Perception has economic and cultural determinants; responses (fatalism, prediction, adaptation, mitigation, management, risk sharing) depend on incidence, magnitude, distribution and development.

Mission 2

  • Park model: quality of life over time (disruption → relief → rehabilitation → reconstruction); the Hazard Management Cycle: mitigation → preparedness → response → recovery.
  • Grindavík (2023–24): every stage funded, recovery by relocation; Freetown (2017): weak mitigation and preparedness, a deep, sudden fall. Development, magnitude and speed of onset shape the response.

Mission 3

  • Structure: crust, mantle, core; rigid lithosphere over a weak asthenosphere. Heat from radioactive decay and residual heat drives convection.
  • Sea-floor spreading (supported by palaeomagnetic stripes); ridge push/gravitational sliding and slab pull; plumes create hotspots whose island chains record plate motion.

Mission 4

  • Constructive: ocean ridges and rift valleys; shallow quakes; basaltic fissure eruptions.
  • Destructive: trenches, island arcs, volcanoes and young fold mountains; deep and strong quakes. Conservative: shallow quakes, no volcanoes.

Mission 5

  • Silica and gas decide eruption style; setting decides the magma: effusive at constructive margins and hot spots, explosive at destructive margins.
  • Primary hazards (lava, pyroclastic flows, tephra and ash, gases) strike near the vent; secondary hazards such as lahars can kill far away and later.

Mission 6

  • Magnitude (Richter, Mw; VEI) measures the event; intensity (Modified Mercalli) measures its effects at a place.
  • Small events are frequent, large ones rare; timing is close to random, so we forecast probabilities rather than predict dates.

Mission 7

  • Plate setting sets the hazard: explosive pyroclastic flows at Fuego, effusive lava at Reykjanes.
  • Evacuation practice, communication and capacity explain who survived; insurance and buy-outs shape recovery.

Mission 8

  • Shallow-focus earthquakes occur at all margins; deep-focus ones occur only inside slabs sinking at subduction zones.
  • P, S and L waves bring shaking; secondary hazards include liquefaction, landslides.
  • Subduction earthquakes lift the water column to make tsunamis, which give distant coasts hours of warning but nearby coasts minutes.

Mission 9

  • Nepal 2015 (developing) and Kaikōura 2016 (developed): same magnitude, very different impacts, because of buildings, poverty, exposure and timing.
  • Short-term responses rescue; long-term responses rebuild, and decide future vulnerability.

Mission 10

  • Large earthquakes are not more frequent; recorded disasters and losses rise with exposure and reporting.
  • Prediction is not possible for earthquakes; forecasts, monitoring, preparedness, zoning and insurance reduce losses.

Mission 11

  • The management cycle runs mitigation → preparedness → response → recovery; Park's model shows how preparation and wealth shape the fall and recovery.
  • Modify the event (zoning, design, defences, diversion), the vulnerability (warnings, preparedness) and the loss (aid, insurance, community action).
