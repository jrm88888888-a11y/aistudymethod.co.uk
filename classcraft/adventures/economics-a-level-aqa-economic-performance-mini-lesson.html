Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 1

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Also called the economic cycle or trade cycle: the same thing.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 1

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 1

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Negative output gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Positive output gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
Mission 1

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a negative output gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is a positive output gap.
  • On a PPC, a negative gap is a point inside the curve: resources are unemployed.
  • A positive gap is output beyond its sustainable level. Resources are over-used (overtime, machines run without maintenance).
Mission 1

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A negative output gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.

Mission 1

Why gaps are hard to measure

  • Actual output is measured; potential output is not. It has to be estimated.
  • Estimates use past trends, business surveys of spare capacity, and unemployment data.
  • Different institutions get different answers, and revise them later.
  • GDP itself is revised. UK example: the fall in real GDP in the 2008–09 recession was estimated at 5.8% in earlier data, then revised to 6.3% (OBR, 2012).
  • So policymakers may not know in real time whether the gap is positive or negative.
Mission 1

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Why instability builds up:
    • credit and debt: easy borrowing fuels spending; when lending stops, spending falls
    • asset bubbles and speculation: house or share prices rise far above underlying value, then crash
    • animal spirits and herding: confidence spreads; people copy each other's optimism or panic
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Mission 1

Smoothing the cycle

  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and spending that happen without any new decision.
    • Recession: incomes fall → tax revenue falls; unemployment rises → benefit spending rises. AD falls by less.
    • Boom: tax revenue rises; benefit spending falls. AD rises by less.
  • Policy conflicts: boosting AD to close a negative gap can push up inflation and imports if the gap is smaller than thought.
  • Supply-side policies that raise potential output can ease the conflict, but they take years.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1Evaluate

Should governments try to close every output gap?

It depends on:

  • Measurement: potential output is estimated, so the size and even the sign of the gap may be wrong.
  • Time lags: policy can take a year or more to work, so it may arrive when the gap has closed.
  • The cause: a demand shock suits demand-side policy; after a supply shock, boosting AD can add to inflation.
  • Size and length: a small, short gap may close on its own; a deep recession can leave lasting damage.
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If people in finance are paid to judge risk, how can a whole market get it so badly wrong?

Make a guess: If people in finance are paid to judge risk, how can a whole market get it so badly wrong?

Mission 2

Finance and the real economy

  • The real economy: the production of goods and services, jobs and incomes.
  • The financial sector serves it: it moves savings to borrowers, runs payments, and shares out risk.
  • It works well when prices of assets and loans reflect real risks and returns.
  • When finance misprices risk, money flows to the wrong uses, and a crash can cut lending, spending and jobs.
  • So failures in financial markets matter far beyond the banks.
Mission 2

Asymmetric information

  • Asymmetric information: one side of a deal knows more than the other.
  • Borrowers know more about their ability to repay than lenders.
  • Sellers of financial products know more than buyers: complex terms, hidden fees.
  • Adverse selection (before the deal): high interest rates attract the riskiest borrowers.
  • Moral hazard (after the deal): someone protected from risk takes more of it.
  • UK example: payment protection insurance (PPI) was mis-sold with loans for years; banks paid about 38 billion pounds in compensation by 2020.
  • 2000s US: lenders who sold mortgages on to investors had little reason to check borrowers; buyers of the bundles could not see the risk.
Quick check
?A lender sells each mortgage it makes to investors within weeks, earning a fee every time. The investors cannot check each borrower. What market failure does this create?
Mission 2

Externalities: risk that others pay for

  • When a bank takes risks, it weighs its own costs and gains (MPC, MPB).
  • A crash also hurts third parties: firms that lose credit, workers who lose jobs, taxpayers who fund rescues.
  • So the marginal social cost of risky lending is above the private cost: MSC > MPC.
  • The market lends at Q1 (MPB = MPC). The social optimum is Q* (MSB = MSC).
  • Too much risky lending: the shaded triangle is the welfare loss.
Mission 2

Speculation and bubbles

  • Speculation: buying an asset to sell it later at a higher price, not to use it.
  • A bubble: an asset's price rises far above the value its future income can justify.
  • Rising prices draw in buyers who expect more rises; demand shifts right; prices rise again.
  • When expectations turn, everyone tries to sell; the price crashes.
  • Real-world: the US technology share index (the Nasdaq Composite) peaked in March 2000, then fell by about 78% by October 2002.
  • 2000s: a US housing bubble, fed by easy credit, burst from 2006 and set off the 2007–08 crisis.
  • Say: "a rise in expected future prices shifts demand to the right" — not: "higher prices increase demand".

Mission 2

Herding and animal spirits

  • Herding: people copy what others are doing, assuming the crowd knows something.
  • For a fund manager, being wrong with everyone else is safer for their career than being wrong alone.
  • Animal spirits (Keynes, 1936): waves of optimism and fear drive decisions, not careful calculation.
  • Long booms make people forget risk: borrowing and debt build up while things look safe.
  • So credit, debt, speculation and herding can all turn a boom into instability.
Mission 2

When finance fails, the real economy pays

  • Credit: banks with losses cut lending; firms cancel investment.
  • Debt: households with big debts cut spending when house prices fall.
  • Wealth effect: falling asset prices make people feel poorer, so consumption falls.
  • Confidence: fear spreads, and firms and households wait.
  • AD falls, output and jobs fall: a financial crash becomes a recession.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can regulators stop bubbles?

  • For acting: lean against booms with macroprudential tools. UK example: from 2014 the FPC limited the share of new mortgages at 4.5 times income or more to 15%.
  • For acting: better information rules and fines reduce mis-selling and rigging.
  • Against: bubbles are hard to spot in time; rising prices may reflect real changes.
  • Against: rules can push risk to less regulated lenders; and popping a bubble can cause the crash it aims to avoid.
  • It depends on: how quickly a bubble can be recognised; how much of it is funded by borrowing; and whether rules cover all lenders, not just banks.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: the price of a rare collectible digital token rises by 300% in a year. Nothing about how it can be used has changed. Most buyers say they bought it because they expect to sell it at a higher price. Which explanation and risk fit best?

When buyers buy because they expect prices to rise, expectations can drive a market far from real value, and then into a crash.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 3

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
  • Seasonal unemployment: jobs that disappear at certain times of year (farm harvests, beach resorts, ski resorts, winter construction).
  • Seasonal unemployment is predictable and recurs every year.
Mission 3

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
Mission 3

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • Also called demand-deficient unemployment.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand).
  • Real national output falls below full-employment output: a negative output gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS, Labour Force Survey).
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 3

Wages stuck above equilibrium

  • If wages are held above the market-clearing level, more people want to work than firms want to hire.
  • The excess supply of labour is unemployment.
  • This is real-wage unemployment (also called classical unemployment).
  • Causes: a minimum wage set above equilibrium; trade union bargaining; wages that do not fall when demand for labour falls.
  • Debate: whether minimum wages cost jobs in practice depends on the level set and the market. Studies disagree; the evidence is in an earlier lesson.
  • Say: "an excess supply of labour at the minimum wage" — not: "a shortage of workers".

Mission 3

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • It is the unemployment that remains when the labour market is in equilibrium: frictional, structural and seasonal.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
  • Voluntary unemployment: people who choose not to take a job at the going wage (e.g. still searching for a better offer). Involuntary: willing to work at the going wage but unable to find a job (e.g. cyclical).
Mission 3

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Seasonal 0.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5 = 5.5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5.5 = 1.5 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "1.5 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "1.5% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

The costs of unemployment

  • Personal: lost income; skills fade; stress and worse mental and physical health.
  • Social: links with crime and family strain; whole communities decline when a big employer closes; inequality rises.
  • Economic:
    • lost output that can never be recovered: the economy is inside its PPC
    • lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so a larger budget deficit
    • long spells reduce skills, so potential output may fall too
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?A deep recession keeps unemployment high for three years. Which effect could reduce the economy's potential output, not just its actual output?
Mission 3Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: short frictional spells can improve job matches; long spells cut skills and can raise the natural rate.
  • Who and where: losses concentrated in one region or group cause deeper social costs than the same rise spread thinly.
  • Support and policy response: benefits, retraining and quick action on demand reduce the lasting damage.
  • What else is happening: rising unemployment with falling inflation leaves room for demand-side policy; with high inflation, the choice is harder.
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Make a guess: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Mission 4

Two routes to inflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive.
  • In the AD/AS model, the price level rises in two ways:
    • AD shifts right: spending pulls prices up → demand-pull inflation
    • SRAS shifts left: costs push prices up → cost-push inflation
  • One shift gives a one-off rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs the shift to keep happening.
  • Say: "inflation is a rising price level" — not: "inflation means prices are high".
Mission 4

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • What can push AD right: lower interest rates, easy credit, rising house prices (wealth), high confidence, an export boom, more government spending, tax cuts.
  • With spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises real output.
  • Near full capacity, firms cannot expand much, so extra AD mostly raises the price level.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve: the steeper the section AD moves along, the more of the rise is in prices.
Quick check
?Interest rates are cut and house prices climb, so households borrow and spend more. The economy is already close to full capacity. Which best describes what happens in the short run?
Mission 4

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push: firms' costs rise, so they supply less at each price level. SRAS shifts left.
  • Sources of higher costs:
    • wages rising faster than productivity
    • energy and raw materials (commodity prices)
    • imported inputs (a weaker currency or dearer world prices)
    • indirect taxes on inputs or goods
  • Result: the price level rises and real output falls. Unemployment rises: stagflation.
  • Real world: wholesale energy prices jumped in 2021–22. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022; energy and food were the largest contributors.
  • Say: "costs shift SRAS left" — not: "costs shift AD".

Mission 4

Imported inflation and commodity prices

  • Imported inflation: higher prices of imports feed through to home prices.
  • Two triggers:
    • world prices rise (oil, gas, metals, food: commodity prices)
    • the home currency depreciates, so each import costs more in home currency
  • Imported inputs raise firms' costs: a cost-push shift of SRAS. Imported consumer goods raise the CPI directly.
  • Real world: sterling fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose from 0.5% (June 2016) to 3.1% (November 2017).
  • Say: "a depreciation makes imports dearer" — not: "a weaker currency makes imports cheaper".
Quick check
?Country X's currency is the crown. A firm there imports a part priced at 50 euros. The exchange rate falls from 1 crown = 1.25 euros to 1 crown = 1.00 euro. By how much does the part's price in crowns change?
Mission 4

Expectations and the wage-price spiral

  • Inflation expectations: what workers and firms think inflation will be.
  • If workers expect prices to rise 5%, they bargain for pay rises of at least 5% to protect real wages.
  • Firms expecting higher costs raise prices in advance.
  • Wage-price spiral: higher prices → higher wage claims → higher costs → higher prices → …
  • Expectations can make inflation self-fulfilling and slow to fall.
  • That is why central banks try to keep expectations anchored to their inflation target.
  • Real world: in the UK in the 1970s, oil price rises fed into large wage claims. UK RPI inflation peaked at about 27% in August 1975.
Mission 4

Money and inflation: the quantity theory

  • Equation of exchange: MV = PQ
    • M = money supply; V = velocity (times each unit of money is spent a year)
    • P = price level; Q = real output
  • MV = total spending. PQ = nominal value of output. It is true by definition.
  • Quantity theory (monetarists) adds two assumptions:
    • V is stable
    • Q is set by supply-side factors, not by M
  • So, in the long run, faster money growth → inflation. "Inflation is caused by too much money."
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's central bank printed money to pay for government spending. Official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • Keynesian reply: V can change a lot, and with spare capacity more money can raise Q, not just P.
Mission 4

Worked example: money growth in numbers

  • Illustrative data. M = 200 billion, V = 5, Q = 1,000 billion units.
  • P = MV ÷ Q = 200 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 1.00.
  • M rises 10% to 220. V and Q unchanged.
  • New P = 220 × 5 ÷ 1,000 = 1.10: the price level rises 10%, the same as M.
  • Now suppose Q also grows 3% to 1,030.
  • New P = 1,100 ÷ 1,030 = 1.068: inflation about 6.8%.
  • Rule of thumb: inflation ≈ money growth − real output growth (if V is stable). 10 − 3 = 7%.
Quick check
?In a monetarist model, velocity is constant. Over a year the money supply grows by 8% and real output grows by 3%. Roughly what is the inflation rate?
Mission 4

Causes of deflation

  • Deflation also has a demand side and a supply side.
  • AD falls (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession): the price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • SRAS rises (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology): the price level falls, real output rises.
  • So falling prices are not always bad news. The cause matters.
  • Say: "disinflation: inflation falls from 4% to 2%" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 4Evaluate

Which cause, and does it matter?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity. The closer to full capacity, the more a rise in AD shows up as inflation.
  • One-off or ongoing. A single jump in oil prices raises the price level once. It becomes lasting inflation only if wages, expectations or money growth follow.
  • Velocity and the time scale. Monetarists see money as the long-run cause; Keynesians stress that V and Q can change.
  • The policy response. Higher interest rates curb demand-pull well. Against cost-push, they cut inflation only by deepening the fall in output.
  • Mixed causes. Real episodes often combine both. 2021–22 saw energy shocks and strong post-pandemic demand.
Mission check

Mission 5

Mission 5: If a country printed twice as much money, would everything really cost twice as much?

Make a guess: If a country printed twice as much money, would everything really cost twice as much?

Mission 5

The equation of exchange

  • MV = PQ
  • M = the money supply.
  • V = the velocity of circulation: how many times, on average, each unit of money is spent in a year.
  • P = the average price level.
  • Q = real output: the quantity of goods and services produced.
  • Left side: money × times spent = total spending. Right side: prices × output = nominal GDP.
  • The two sides are equal by definition: an identity, not yet a theory.
Mission 5Worked example

Finding velocity

Illustrative data:

  • Money supply M = 500 billion. Nominal GDP = 2,000 billion.
  • V = nominal GDP ÷ M = 2,000 ÷ 500 = 4. Each unit of money is spent 4 times a year.
  • Real output (at base-year prices) = 1,600 billion.
  • P = nominal GDP ÷ real output = 2,000 ÷ 1,600 = 1.25 (prices are 25% above the base year).
  • Check: M × V = 500 × 4 = 2,000 = P × output = 1.25 × 1,600. ✔
  • Any one of the four can be found from the other three: M = P × output ÷ V = 1.25 × 1,600 ÷ 4 = 500; output = M × V ÷ P = 500 × 4 ÷ 1.25 = 1,600.
Mission 5

From identity to theory

  • The quantity theory of money adds three assumptions:
  • (1) V is stable: it depends on slow-changing habits and payment technology.
  • (2) Real output is set by real factors (labour, capital, technology), not by money, at least in the long run.
  • (3) Causation runs from M to P: money changes first.
  • So if M rises and V and output are fixed, P must rise in proportion.
  • Double M → double P.
  • Say: "the quantity theory says money growth causes inflation" — not: "MV = PQ proves it". The equation is always true; the theory is the assumptions.
Mission 5Worked example

In growth rates

  • In growth rates: % change in M + % change in V ≈ % change in P + % change in real output.
  • Illustrative data: money grows 10%, V is constant, real output grows 3%.
  • Inflation ≈ 10 + 0 − 3 = 7%.
  • (Exact: 1.10 ÷ 1.03 − 1 = 6.8%; the approximation is close for small changes.)
  • Rule of thumb: lasting inflation ≈ money growth − output growth, if V is stable.

Mission 5

Money neutrality and monetarism

  • Money neutrality: in the long run, money changes nominal variables (prices, wages) but not real ones (output, jobs).
  • Milton Friedman: "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon."
  • Monetarists: to control inflation, control the growth of the money supply; keep it close to the growth of real output.
  • Expectations: if people expect fast money growth to bring inflation, they ask for higher wages and set higher prices now, so inflation arrives faster.
  • UK: from 1980 the government set targets for money supply growth (the Medium Term Financial Strategy). The targets were often missed and were dropped in the mid-1980s.
Mission 5

Real case: Zimbabwe

  • 2000s: Zimbabwe's output fell sharply, while its central bank created money to pay for government spending.
  • M soared while real output shrank, so P rose explosively.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In January 2009 a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note was issued.
  • People spent money as fast as they could before it lost value: V rose too, pushing prices up even faster.
  • In 2009 the Zimbabwe dollar was abandoned; people used US dollars and other currencies.

Mission 5Evaluate

Does more money always mean inflation?

  • V is not stable: US M2 velocity fell from about 2.2 in 1997 to about 1.1 in 2020.
  • UK quantitative easing: the Bank of England bought 895 billion pounds of assets (2009–2021). CPI inflation averaged a little over 2% a year in the 2010s: much of the new money was held, not spent.
  • Spare capacity (Keynesian view): below full employment, more spending can raise output, not just prices.
  • Causation can run the other way: banks create money when people want to borrow and spend, so rising P and output can drive M.
  • But: in 2021–22, after rapid money growth and an energy price shock, UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% (October 2022). How much was due to money is still debated.
  • It depends on: whether V is stable, how much spare capacity there is, the time period, and whether new money is spent.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: over five years, an economy's money supply rises by 50%. Real output rises by 5% and the price level hardly changes. According to the quantity equation, what must have happened to V?
Mission check

?A country's central bank doubles the money supply. In the same period, people start holding on to money twice as long before spending it, so V halves. Real output does not change. What happens to the price level?

More money raises prices only if it is spent: what matters is M × V compared with real output.

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (a common benchmark: over 50% a month). Money loses its value fast.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".

Mission 6

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Even fully expected inflation still has some costs.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 6

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.

Mission 6

The costs of inflation

  • Menu costs: time and money spent changing prices (price lists, labels, websites).
  • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around to protect its value.
  • Uncertainty: high, variable inflation makes future costs and revenue hard to predict, so firms invest less. Growth can suffer.
  • Savings discouraged: if interest rates lag behind inflation, saving loses real value.
  • International competitiveness: if inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports get dearer and imports relatively cheaper (unless the exchange rate falls to offset it). The current account can worsen.
  • Inefficient resource allocation: when all prices rise fast, changes in relative prices are hard to spot, so price signals work less well.
  • Fiscal drag: if tax thresholds stay fixed while pay rises with prices, more income is taxed at higher rates.
  • Most costs grow with the rate of inflation and with how unpredictable it is.
Mission 6

Hyperinflation: when money stops working

  • At very high inflation, money fails at its jobs:
    • store of value: savings in cash are wiped out
    • medium of exchange: sellers refuse it and people turn to barter or foreign currency
    • unit of account: prices change too fast to compare
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed foreign currencies such as the US dollar and the South African rand, and people stopped using its own currency.
  • Hyperinflations usually end only with a new currency or a new monetary regime (money growth).
Mission 6

Deflation: good news or bad?

  • Benign ("good") deflation: SRAS shifts right (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Malign ("bad") deflation: AD shifts left (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession).
    • Price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Most worries about deflation are about the malign kind.

Mission 6

The costs of deflation

  • Deferred consumption: if prices are expected to fall, households wait to buy. AD falls further.
  • Real value of debt rises: debts are fixed in money, but prices and incomes fall. Borrowers cut spending; some go bankrupt.
  • Unemployment: firms' revenue falls, but money wages are sticky downwards, so real wages rise and firms cut jobs (cyclical unemployment).
  • Policy ineffectiveness: interest rates cannot go far below zero, so real interest rates (≈ nominal − inflation) stay high when prices fall.
  • Deflationary spiral: falling prices → spending delayed → AD falls → output and jobs fall → prices fall further → …
  • Real world: US consumer prices fell by about a quarter from 1929 to 1933.
  • Real world: Japan's consumer prices fell in most years from 1999 to 2012, with interest rates close to zero.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm owes 100,000 on a loan with no interest. Over a year, the price level falls by 10%, and so do the prices the firm charges. What happens to the real burden of the debt?
Mission 6Evaluate

How much does inflation hurt?

It depends on:

  • The rate and its stability. Low, steady inflation costs little; high, volatile inflation costs a lot.
  • Whether it is expected. Expected inflation is built into wages and interest rates; surprises redistribute.
  • Indexation. Pensions, benefits and tax thresholds linked to prices protect people on fixed incomes.
  • Inflation abroad. What matters for competitiveness is inflation relative to trading partners, and the exchange rate.
  • Low inflation is not zero inflation. Many central banks target about 2%: a small positive rate lets real wages adjust without money pay cuts and leaves room above zero for interest rates.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A family takes a one-year loan from a bank at a fixed 4% interest rate. Both sides expect 3% inflation. Instead, prices fall by 1% over the year. Who gains from the surprise?

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Mission 7

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 7

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 7

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1950s–60s: UK data fitted the curve well. Many thought governments could choose a point on it.
  • 1970s: UK inflation averaged about 24% in 1975 (RPI), while unemployment also rose.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outwards.
  • Two reasons: oil-price supply shocks, and workers and firms coming to expect high inflation.
Mission 7

Expectations shift the short-run curve

  • Milton Friedman (1968) and Edmund Phelps (1967): the trade-off is only temporary.
  • Adaptive expectations: people expect inflation to be what it was recently.
  • (1) Point A: unemployment at its natural rate, inflation 2%, expected inflation 2%.
  • (2) AD rises. Workers' money wages rise, but prices rise faster than they expected. Real wages fall, firms hire: move along SRPC1 to B (lower unemployment, higher inflation).
  • (3) Workers now expect the higher inflation and bargain for higher money wages. Real wages recover, firms stop the extra hiring: unemployment returns to the natural rate at C. SRPC1 → SRPC2: each SRPC is drawn for one expected inflation rate.
  • Result: same unemployment, higher inflation.
Mission 7

The long-run Phillips curve

  • Join points A and C: the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • In the long run there is no trade-off: pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation.
  • The natural rate of unemployment: unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium. It is frictional plus structural unemployment.
  • NAIRU: the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. Below it, inflation keeps rising. It is often treated as the same as the natural rate.
  • Vertical LRPC = vertical LRAS: the classical (neo-classical) view.
Mission 7

Worked example: chasing low unemployment

  • Illustrative data. Natural rate = 5%. Expected inflation starts at 2%.
  • Short-run rule: inflation = expected inflation + (5 − unemployment rate).
  • Year 1: government holds unemployment at 3%. Inflation = 2 + 2 = 4%.
  • Year 2: people now expect 4%. Inflation = 4 + 2 = 6%.
  • Year 3: expect 6%. Inflation = 6 + 2 = 8%.
  • Each year the SRPC shifts up. Holding unemployment below the NAIRU makes inflation accelerate.
  • Stop pushing: unemployment returns to 5% with inflation stuck at 8%.
  • Bringing inflation back down needs unemployment above 5% for a time: at 7%, inflation = 8 − 2 = 6%.

Mission 7

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favourable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
  • A change in expected inflation shifts the SRPC.
  • A change in the natural rate shifts the LRPC. Better skills matching, job search help or more flexible labour markets move it left. Long spells of unemployment that erode skills (hysteresis) can move it right.
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?
Mission 7Evaluate

How useful is the Phillips curve?

It depends on:

  • Time horizon: a short-run trade-off may exist.
  • In the long run, if expectations adapt, it disappears.
  • Supply shocks: they shift the SRPC, so past data may not predict the future.
  • Measurement: the NAIRU cannot be observed. It is estimated, and it changes over time.
  • Credible inflation targets: if people trust the central bank, expectations stay near target. The curve can look flat.
  • UK evidence: in 2019 unemployment was 3.8%, yet CPI inflation was only 1.8%.
  • Global factors (cheap imports, energy prices) also drive inflation, not just domestic unemployment.
Mission check

?A sudden rise in world energy prices hits an economy. Over a year, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which best describes this on a Phillips curve diagram?

A lower-unemployment, higher-inflation deal exists only along one short-run curve. Supply shocks can shift that curve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.
  • Credit, bubbles and herding make them bigger.

Mission 2

  • Financial markets fail through asymmetric information, externalities (crashes hurt third parties), moral hazard, and speculation and bubbles.
  • Expectations, herding and animal spirits can shift demand for assets, so prices overshoot and then crash.
  • Failures in finance spread to the real economy through credit, debt, wealth and confidence.

Mission 3

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Seasonal: jobs that vanish at certain times of year.
  • Real-wage: wages held above equilibrium.
  • Costs are personal, social and economic; lost output is never recovered.

Mission 4

  • Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity; prices and real output rise together.
  • Cost-push: higher costs shift SRAS left; prices rise while output falls.
  • Expectations, wage claims and import prices can keep inflation going.
  • Monetarists: MV = PQ, so with stable V, lasting inflation comes from money growing faster than output.

Mission 5

  • The equation of exchange is an identity: money × velocity = price level × real output.
  • The quantity theory assumes stable V and fixed real output, so money growth causes proportional inflation; money is neutral in the long run.
  • Evidence is mixed: V can change a lot, and spare capacity and the direction of causation matter.

Mission 6

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
  • Inflation also brings menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty and weaker competitiveness; malign deflation can spiral into falling output and jobs.

Mission 7

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
  • With adaptive expectations, the SRPC shifts up whenever unemployment is held below the natural rate. The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU).
