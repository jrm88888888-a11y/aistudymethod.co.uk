Mission 1: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Make a guess: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Mission 1

Amino acids

  • An α-amino acid (2-amino acid): RCH(NH2)COOH.
  • Both groups sit on one carbon, the α-carbon. R is the side chain.
  • Glycine: R = H, H2NCH2COOH. Alanine: R = CH3, H2NCH(CH3)COOH.
  • About 20 amino acids build proteins. They differ only in R.
Mission 1

Two groups, two jobs

  • –COOH is an acid group. –NH2 is a base group, like ammonia.
  • With alkali: H2NCH2COOH + NaOH → H2NCH2COO⁻Na⁺ + H2O.
  • With acid: H2NCH2COOH + HCl → ⁺H3NCH2COOH Cl⁻.
  • Each group reacts with its opposite: the molecule is amphoteric.
Quick check
?Glycine, H2NCH2COOH, is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid at room temperature. Which species of glycine is present?
Mission 1

The zwitterion

  • In the solid and in water, the –COOH passes H⁺ to the –NH2 of the same molecule.
  • Result: ⁺H3NCH(R)COO⁻, a zwitterion (dipolar ion): one ion with a + and a − end. Overall neutral.
  • Zwitterions attract each other strongly: an ionic lattice.
Mission 1

pH sets the form

  • In acid: the –COO⁻ end gains H⁺ → ⁺H3NCH(R)COOH (a cation).
  • In alkali: the –NH3⁺ end loses H⁺ → H2NCH(R)COO⁻ (an anion).
  • In between: mainly the zwitterion.
  • Draw all three for alanine: ⁺H3NCH(CH3)COOH · ⁺H3NCH(CH3)COO⁻ · H2NCH(CH3)COO⁻.
Quick check
?Alanine is added to excess sodium hydroxide solution. Which species of alanine is present?
Mission 1

The peptide link

  • –COOH of one amino acid + –NH2 of another → peptide link, –CONH–, + H2O.
  • This is condensation, the same amide link as in polyamides.
  • Glycine + alanine gives two dipeptides: Gly-Ala, H2NCH2CONHCH(CH3)COOH, and Ala-Gly, H2NCH(CH3)CONHCH2COOH.
  • Many amino acids → a polypeptide. One or more polypeptides → a protein.
  • Draw tripeptides the same way: three residues, two peptide links, two H2O lost.
Mission 1

Hydrolysis and TLC

  • Hydrolysis: heat the peptide with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl for several hours. Water breaks each peptide link.
  • The amino acids come back (as cations, ⁺H3N–, in the acid).
  • Separate them by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).
  • Amino acids are colourless. Locate spots by spraying with ninhydrin (purple spots) or by UV light.
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front, both from the baseline.
  • Compare Rf with known amino acids run in the same solvent.
Quick check
?On a TLC plate the baseline is drawn 1.0 cm above the bottom edge. After running, the solvent front is 8.5 cm above the bottom edge and an amino acid spot is 3.7 cm above the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of the amino acid?
Mission 1

Three levels of protein structure

  • Primary: the sequence of amino acids, joined by peptide links (covalent).
  • Secondary: the chain coils into an α-helix or folds into a β-pleated sheet.
  • Both are held by hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O of peptide links.
  • Tertiary: the whole chain folds into a 3-D shape, held by interactions between R groups:
  • disulfide (S–S) bonds between two cysteines (covalent); hydrogen bonds; ionic attractions (–COO⁻ and –NH3⁺); London forces.
  • In a diagram: sequence → primary; regular helix or sheet → secondary; overall fold → tertiary.
Quick check
?Which interaction holds the turns of an α-helix in place?
Mission check

?The dipeptide Gly-Ala, H2NCH2CONHCH(CH3)COOH, is dissolved in excess cold dilute hydrochloric acid. No hydrolysis happens. Which species is present?

A peptide still has an acid end and a base end, so it reacts with acids and bases just like a single amino acid.

Mission 2: Why can an enzyme grab one molecule but not that molecule's mirror image?

Make a guess: Why can an enzyme grab one molecule but not that molecule's mirror image?

Mission 2

Enzymes: protein catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a catalyst: it speeds up a reaction and is not used up.
  • Its folded chain leaves a small pocket: the active site.
  • The reacting molecule, the substrate, binds in the active site: an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Bound, the substrate is held in a good orientation and reacts by a path with a lower activation energy.
  • The products leave; the enzyme is free to bind another substrate.
Quick check
?Which statement explains how an enzyme speeds up a reaction?
Mission 2

Why a mirror image doesn't fit

  • A chiral substrate has two enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • The active site is 3-D. It has several binding points, made by groups on the amino acids.
  • One enantiomer can line up with all the binding points at once.
  • Its mirror image can match at most two at a time: it binds weakly or not at all.
  • So the active site is stereospecific: it binds only one enantiomer.
Mission 2

Drugs that block the active site

  • An inhibitor binds in the active site and blocks the substrate. The reaction slows or stops.
  • Many drugs are enzyme inhibitors, e.g. drugs that block enzymes a virus needs.
  • The drug must fit the stereospecific active site, so usually only one enantiomer works.
  • Computers model the 3-D shape of the active site and test which drug shapes fit and bind, before anything is made.
Quick check
?A drug molecule is chiral. One enantiomer strongly inhibits an enzyme. The other enantiomer has almost no effect. Which explanation is best?
Mission 2

DNA: a chain of nucleotides

  • A nucleotide = phosphate + 2-deoxyribose (a pentose sugar) + one base.
  • Four bases: adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), thymine (T). Structures are in the Data Booklet.
  • In a strand, the phosphate of one nucleotide is covalently bonded to the sugar of the next.
  • Result: a sugar–phosphate–sugar–phosphate backbone, with a base on each sugar.
Mission 2

The double helix

  • DNA has two complementary strands coiled into a double helix.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G with C (3).
  • The N–H and C=O / N groups only line up in those pairs: shape decides the partner.
  • So one strand fixes the order of bases on the other: the strands are complementary.
Quick check
?Why does guanine pair with cytosine in DNA, and not with thymine?
Mission 2

Cisplatin meets DNA

  • Cisplatin: square planar Pt(NH3)2Cl2, the two Cl⁻ at 90°.
  • In the cell, the Cl⁻ ligands are replaced: ligand replacement.
  • Pt forms a bond to a nitrogen atom on guanine, then to a second guanine next to it on the same strand.
  • The DNA kinks. It can't be unzipped and copied: DNA replication stops, and the cell can't divide.
  • Shape matters: in transplatin the leaving groups are 180° apart, so it can't grip two neighbouring guanines; it is far less effective.
Mission 2

Benefits and harms

  • Benefit: cancer cells divide rapidly, so they are hit hard. Cisplatin treats several cancers very successfully.
  • Harm: it also binds DNA in healthy cells that divide quickly: hair follicles, gut lining, blood-forming cells. Hair loss, nausea.
  • It can also damage the kidneys and hearing.
  • Doctors limit the dose and frequency. Society weighs the benefits against the adverse effects.
Quick check
?Patients treated with cisplatin often lose hair and feel sick. Which explanation is best?
Mission check

?Lactic acid, CH3CH(OH)COOH, is chiral. An enzyme that oxidises lactic acid is added to a racemic (50 : 50) mixture of its enantiomers. The reaction stops when half of the lactic acid has reacted, although the enzyme is still active. Which explanation is best?

The leftover lactic acid is the mirror image. It can't line up with the active site, just as on the card.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An amino acid has an acid group (–COOH) and a base group (–NH2), so it reacts with both acids and bases.
  • It exists as a zwitterion, ⁺H3NCH(R)COO⁻; acid gives the cation, alkali the anion.
  • Amino acids join by peptide links (condensation); hydrolysis reverses it.

Mission 2

  • Binding needs an exact 3-D fit: an active site is stereospecific, so the mirror image doesn't fit.
  • DNA strands pair A–T and G–C by hydrogen bonds.
  • Cisplatin's two cis Cl⁻ are replaced by N on neighbouring guanines, stopping DNA replication.
