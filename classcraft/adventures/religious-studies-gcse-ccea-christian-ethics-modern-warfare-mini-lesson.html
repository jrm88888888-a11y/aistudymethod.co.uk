Mission 1

Mission 1: When a war is fought in God's name, is religion really the cause?

Make a guess: When a war is fought in God's name, is religion really the cause?

Mission 1

Conflict and war

  • Conflict: a serious disagreement or struggle between people or groups. It may or may not become violent.
  • War: armed conflict between countries, or between groups within a country (civil war).
  • Wars almost never have one cause. Historians look for several causes working together.
  • Religions teach that war is a great evil, even when they accept it may sometimes be necessary (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Greed, self-defence, retaliation

  • Greed: wanting more land, wealth, power or resources. "Where do wars and fightings amongst you come from? Don't they come from your pleasures that war in your members?" (James 4:1, WEBBE)
  • "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil." (1 Timothy 6:10, WEBBE)
  • Self-defence: protecting a country or its allies from attack. Most religions and international law (UN Charter, Article 51) accept it.
  • Retaliation: striking back after an attack, often for revenge. It can start a cycle of violence that is hard to break.
  • Religions generally reject greed and revenge as reasons for war; many accept self-defence.
Mission 1

Politics, resources, history, culture

  • Politics: struggles for power; clashing ideologies; leaders seeking support at home.
  • Resources: oil, water, farmland, minerals.
  • History: old grievances, past wars, borders drawn by empires.
  • Culture and identity: ethnic, national or language groups who feel threatened or excluded.
  • Religion: beliefs, holy places, or religious identity can be part of a conflict.
  • Usually these overlap: religion often marks who belongs to a side, even when the dispute is about land or power.
Quick check
?After a border guard is killed, Country X shells a village in Country Y "to teach them a lesson". Country Y then bombs a city in Country X. Which reason for war is shown most clearly?
Mission 1

Holy war

  • Holy war: a war fought for religious reasons, believed to be commanded or approved by God.
  • The Crusades (1095–1291): popes called Christians to recapture Jerusalem and the Holy Land; Muslim, Jewish and Christian civilians suffered.
  • Some modern extremist groups claim religious reasons for violence; most believers of those faiths reject their claims.
  • In Muslim teaching, lesser jihad is struggle in defence of the faith under strict conditions, not a war to force conversion: "There is no compulsion in religion" (Surah 2:256, Pickthall).
Mission 1

Is religion to blame?

  • The case against religion (many atheists and Humanists): religion divides people into "us" and "them"; it can make violence feel sacred; leaders use it to win support. Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006), argues religion makes such divisions worse (paraphrase).
  • Religious replies: most wars have mainly political, economic or ethnic causes; the most destructive wars of the 20th century were not fought for religion; extremists misuse religion against its own teaching.
  • Religion also works for peace: religious leaders and groups mediate and help victims.
  • Christians differ on how to read "a time for war, and a time for peace" (Ecclesiastes 3:8, WEBBE): as realism about a fallen world, or as a description, not a command.
  • Humanists also note that non-religious ideologies (nationalism, fascism, communism) have caused terrible wars.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Religion is the main cause of war."

  • Reasons for: holy wars such as the Crusades; extremist violence in religion's name; religious identity can harden divisions; sacred places are fought over.
  • Reasons against: most wars have political, economic or ethnic causes; non-religious ideologies caused the deadliest wars; religion is often used as a label for sides formed for other reasons; religious teachings limit war.
  • Another view: religion can be both a cause of conflict and a powerful force for peace.
  • Judge it by: Would the war happen without religion? Is religion the cause, or the badge of a side? Do the teachings support the war, or are they misused?
Mission check

?Two neighbouring countries dispute a dam that controls water for millions of farmers. Talks fail. One president calls the coming war "a sacred duty". Most people in both countries follow the same religion. Which analysis is most accurate?

A war fought "in God's name" may really be about land, water or power. Ask what would happen without religion, and whether religion is the cause or the badge.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can a war ever be fought justly, and who decides the rules?

Make a guess: Can a war ever be fought justly, and who decides the rules?

Mission 2

Where the idea came from

  • Just war theory: a set of conditions that, Christians in this tradition hold, must all be met for a war to be morally right, and limits on how it is fought.
  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430) asked how Christians, taught to love enemies, could serve as soldiers.
  • His answer: war may be a sad necessity to restore peace and stop wrongdoing, never for revenge or gain.
  • In City of God (XIX, 12) he writes that "peace is the end sought for by war".
  • St Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) set out three conditions in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 40, a. 1).
  • Later thinkers (e.g. Francisco de Vitoria, c. 1483–1546; Hugo Grotius, 1583–1645) added more, including rules for conduct in war.
Mission 2Read the source

Aquinas' three conditions

Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40, a. 1 (English Dominican translation):

> "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged."

  • Second: "a just cause": those attacked deserve it because of some wrong they have done.
  • Third: "a rightful intention": to advance good or avoid evil, not for cruelty or power.
  • Aquinas quotes Augustine: "we go to war that we may have peace."

Q1 Why can a private person not start a just war?

Show answer

Only the ruler responsible for the common good has authority.

Q2 Could a war with authority and a just cause still be unjust?

Show answer

Yes: a wicked intention, such as revenge or greed, makes it unlawful.

Mission 2

The criteria today

Going to war (jus ad bellum):

  • Just cause: e.g. self-defence against attack, or protecting the innocent.
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a lawful government, not a private group.
  • Right intention: to restore peace and justice, not to gain land, wealth or revenge.
  • Last resort: every peaceful means (talks, sanctions) has been tried.
  • Reasonable chance of success: no pointless loss of life.
  • Proportionality: the good achieved must outweigh the harm caused.

Fighting the war (jus in bello):

  • Discrimination: civilians must never be deliberately targeted.
  • Proportional force: no more force than needed; prisoners and the wounded treated humanely.

?Before going to war, a government spends a year on talks, mediation and sanctions, and only then sends troops. Which just war condition is it trying to meet?
Mission 2

Christian sources and views

  • "Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities, for there is no authority except from God" (Romans 13:1). The ruler "doesn't bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4).
  • "For everything there is a season … a time for war, and a time for peace." (Ecclesiastes 3:1, 8; verse 3 adds "a time to kill, and a time to heal")
  • Catholic and Anglican teaching, and many other Protestants, accept just war as a last resort.
  • Quakers and some Mennonites reject it: Jesus taught non-violence (pacifism in Mission 5).
  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics, 1966): do the most loving (agape) thing in each case. War might sometimes be the most loving choice, to protect the innocent; it could also rule out a war that meets the criteria but causes great suffering.
Quick check
?Which Bible passage do Christians who support just war most often use to show that lawful rulers may use force?
Mission 2Evaluate

"A just war is impossible today."

  • Reasons for: modern weapons (bombs on cities, nuclear weapons) cannot spare civilians, so discrimination and proportionality fail. Pope Francis (Fratelli Tutti, 2020, §258) argues it is now very hard to apply the old just war criteria (paraphrase). Pacifists say war is never the most loving way.
  • Reasons against: a country that is invaded still has a just cause to defend itself; the UN Charter (1945, Article 51) recognises this. Intervention may stop genocide. Precision weapons can reduce civilian deaths.
  • Another view: the theory still matters because it is used to judge and limit wars, even when no war fully meets it.
  • Judge it by: Can any modern war meet every condition? Is doing nothing ever worse? Who decides whether the conditions are met?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why many Christians accept the Just War theory.

  • Reason 1: Lawful governments have God-given authority to protect people. Source: the ruler "doesn't bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4). Development: so a government may use force to defend its citizens, as long as the war is a last resort.
  • Reason 2: War may be needed to restore peace and protect the innocent. Development: Augustine taught that peace is the aim of a just war; the strict conditions stop war becoming revenge or greed.
Mission check

?A country's border towns are shelled by its neighbour. After months of failed talks, its parliament votes to defend them. Its generals then propose bombing the neighbour's capital, home to millions, to end the war quickly. Which just war condition does the generals' plan most clearly break?

A war can begin justly and still be fought unjustly; the theory judges both.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can owning a weapon you must never use keep the world safe?

Make a guess: Can owning a weapon you must never use keep the world safe?

Mission 3

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD: weapons that can kill huge numbers of people and cause great damage, without distinguishing soldiers from civilians.
  • Nuclear: blast, heat and radiation. Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945) are the only wartime uses.
  • Chemical: poison gases (e.g. nerve agents). Biological: deadly diseases spread deliberately.
  • Effects last for generations: radiation sickness, cancers, poisoned land and water.
  • International law: Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1993); Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968); Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017), which nuclear-armed states, including the UK, have not joined.
Mission 3

Nuclear deterrence

  • Deterrence: owning nuclear weapons so that no enemy dares attack, for fear of devastating retaliation.
  • "Mutually assured destruction": if both sides would be destroyed, neither strikes first.
  • The UK keeps a nuclear deterrent (Trident submarines).
  • For: no nuclear war between major powers since 1945; protects a nation against blackmail.
  • Against: accidents or misjudgement could cause catastrophe; enormous cost; encourages other states to seek weapons; terrorists cannot be deterred; it rests on a threat to kill civilians.
  • Unilateral disarmament: giving weapons up alone. Multilateral: all sides disarming together by agreement.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes nuclear deterrence?
Mission 3

Catholic teaching

  • Second Vatican Council, Gaudium et Spes 80 (1965): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities and their people is "a crime against God and man himself" (also CCC 2314).
  • The arms race is not a safe way to keep peace (Gaudium et Spes 81).
  • John Paul II (1982): deterrence "may still be judged morally acceptable", but only as a step towards progressive disarmament.
  • Pope Francis (2017): the threat to use nuclear weapons, and even possessing them, is to be firmly condemned. At Hiroshima (2019) he called their use and possession immoral.
  • The Holy See was among the first states to ratify the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017).
  • Different Catholic views: some Catholics still accept deterrence for now, until all sides disarm together; others (e.g. Pax Christi) campaign for disarmament now.
Mission 3

Other Christian views

  • Many Anglicans and other Protestants have accepted deterrence as a temporary necessity while working for multilateral disarmament.
  • Quakers, Methodists and Christian CND oppose nuclear weapons outright: they cannot meet the just war rule of discrimination.
  • "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52).
  • Genesis 1–2: humans are stewards of creation; WMD can poison the earth for generations.
Mission 3

Utilitarian and non-religious arguments

  • Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, 1748–1832; John Stuart Mill, 1806–1873): actions are right "in proportion as they tend to promote happiness" (Mill, Utilitarianism, 1863).
  • Argument for acquiring WMD: if deterrence prevents a world war, owning them produces the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • Some argued the Hiroshima bomb saved more lives than it took by ending the war sooner; historians dispute this.
  • Utilitarian argument against: even a small risk of nuclear war risks suffering on an unimaginable scale; the money could relieve poverty.
  • Humanists and atheists judge by consequences and human rights; most support multilateral disarmament; some accept deterrence as the lesser evil for now.
  • Religious responses: some believers accept the deterrence argument; others say deliberately threatening civilians is wrong whatever the outcome.
Quick check
?Which argument would a utilitarian most likely give for a country keeping nuclear weapons?
Mission 3

Conventional, technological and nuclear warfare

  • Conventional warfare: armies, ships and aircraft with ordinary weapons; just war rules can be applied to it.
  • Technological warfare: drones and "surgical" strikes.
  • For: fewer of your own soldiers die; targets can be chosen precisely.
  • Against: civilians still die; killing from far away may feel easier, making war more likely; who is responsible when machines choose targets?
  • Pope Francis (G7, 2024) called for a ban on lethal autonomous weapons.
  • Nuclear ("apocalyptic") warfare: it could end civilisation; most religious traditions see it as impossible to justify.
Quick check
?A Christian argues that drone strikes are less wrong than sending in ground troops. Which reason is she most likely to give?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Nuclear deterrence is morally justified."

  • Reasons for: no nuclear war between great powers since 1945; a nation has a duty to protect its people; utilitarian benefit if war is prevented; John Paul II (1982) accepted it as a temporary step.
  • Reasons against: it rests on a willingness to kill millions of civilians; one accident could be catastrophic; Francis (2017) condemned even possession; Qur'an 5:32 and the First Precept treat each life as precious; vast cost.
  • Another view: acceptable only temporarily, with real steps towards multilateral disarmament.
  • Judge it by: Is threatening an act as wrong as doing it? Has deterrence actually kept the peace? What happens if one side disarms alone?
Mission check

?A country builds a cyber-weapon that could shut down an enemy's hospitals, water and power for months, harming millions. Its leaders say they will never use it: owning it will stop attacks. What would Pope Francis's teaching on nuclear weapons most likely lead him to say?

Deterrence asks whether threatening a terrible act can be right if it prevents that act; believers and non-believers divide over the answer.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When the fighting stops, who pays the price of war, and who helps them?

Make a guess: When the fighting stops, who pays the price of war, and who helps them?

Mission 4

The human cost

  • Civilians (people not fighting) are killed, injured and bereaved; homes, schools and hospitals are destroyed.
  • Hidden wounds: trauma, grief and post-traumatic stress, for civilians and soldiers.
  • After the war: landmines and unexploded bombs kill and maim for decades.
  • Families are separated; disease and hunger follow when water, power and farms are destroyed.
  • Soldiers too: death, disability and mental injury; many return to families who must care for them.
Mission 4

Refugees and child soldiers

  • Refugee: someone forced to flee their country because of war or persecution (1951 Refugee Convention). Internally displaced people flee but stay inside their own country.
  • Refugees lose home, work, school and community, and often wait years in camps.
  • The UN refugee agency (UNHCR, 1950) protects and supports them.
  • Child soldiers: children under 18 used by armed forces or groups as fighters, porters, spies or cooks.
  • An international agreement (Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the Child, 2000) sets 18 as the minimum age for taking direct part in fighting.
  • Harm: lost childhood and education, trauma, and difficulty returning home, where they may be feared or rejected.
Mission 4

Economic and environmental cost

  • Economic: money spent on weapons is not spent on schools, hospitals or aid; destroyed roads, factories and farms take decades to rebuild.
  • US President Eisenhower (1953): "Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed."
  • Environmental: poisoned land and water (e.g. the defoliant Agent Orange in Vietnam), burning oil wells (Kuwait, 1991), destroyed forests and farmland, landmines that make fields unusable.
  • Even ancient rules limited this: do not cut down an enemy's fruit trees (Deuteronomy 20:19).

"I was a stranger"

"for I was hungry and you gave me food to eat. I was thirsty and you gave me drink. I was a stranger and you took me in." (Matthew 25:35)

"because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)

  • "The stranger who lives as a foreigner with you shall be to you as the native-born amongst you, and you shall love him as yourself; for you lived as foreigners in the land of Egypt." (Leviticus 19:34)
  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37) helps a wounded enemy without asking who he is.

Q1 Who do Christians believe they are really helping when they help a refugee?

Show answer

Christ himself: "you did it to me".

Q2 What reason does Leviticus give for loving the stranger?

Show answer

The Israelites were once foreigners in Egypt.

Mission 4

Christian teaching on victims of war

  • Christians believe every person, including the enemy, the refugee and the former child soldier, has dignity as made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Christian responses: prayer, emergency aid, hosting refugees, campaigning for peace and fair treatment, rebuilding communities.
  • Diversity: Christians agree that refugees must be helped; they differ on how many a country should take and how quickly.
  • Non-religious views: Humanists support refugees on the basis of human rights and shared humanity; many work through secular agencies such as the Red Cross.
Mission 4

Religious organisations helping victims

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, 1962), part of the worldwide Caritas network: emergency food, water and shelter in war zones; long-term development; local peacebuilding and trauma support.
  • Why: Matthew 25:35–40; Catholic social teaching on dignity and the common good.
  • Christian Aid (1945): founded by British and Irish churches to help refugees after the Second World War; still works in conflict zones.
  • Jesuit Refugee Service (1980): accompanies, serves and speaks up for refugees, including education in camps.
Quick check
?A Catholic charity gives emergency shelter and counselling to families fleeing a war, without asking their religion. Which teaching most directly explains why it helps everyone equally?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Religious groups should focus on helping the victims of war, not on trying to stop wars."

  • Reasons for: helping victims is concrete and urgent (Matthew 25); religious groups have little power over governments; taking sides in politics may risk their neutrality and access.
  • Reasons against: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); preventing war prevents victims; Romero, King and Sant'Egidio show religious groups can change events; aid without justice treats symptoms, not causes.
  • Another view: the two belong together: CAFOD and Caritas give aid and also work for peacebuilding.
  • Judge it by: Which saves more lives in the long run? Does campaigning against war cost an agency its access to victims? What do the religion's sources command?
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two religious beliefs about helping the victims of war. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching.

  • Belief 1: Christians should care for the stranger and the suffering. Source: "I was a stranger and you took me in" (Matthew 25:35). Development: Christians believe that helping a refugee is helping Christ, so many support CAFOD and Christian Aid.
  • Belief 2: Christians believe every person has equal dignity as made in God's image (Genesis 1:27). Development: so help is given to victims on all sides, including former child soldiers, who need rehabilitation as well as justice.
Mission check

?A former child soldier returns to his village. Some villagers want him driven out. A church-run programme offers him schooling, counselling and a public ceremony of reconciliation with the families he harmed. Which Christian belief most directly underlies the programme?

The costs of war last long after the fighting; religious responses aim to heal people as well as rebuild places.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Is refusing to fight brave or naive? Why do some believers never use violence?

Make a guess: Is refusing to fight brave or naive? Why do some believers never use violence?

Mission 5

What pacifism is

  • Pacifism: the belief that war and violence are wrong; disputes should be settled peacefully.
  • Absolute pacifism: violence is never right, not even in self-defence or to protect others.
  • Conditional (relative) pacifism: violence is almost always wrong, but may be the lesser evil in extreme cases; or opposition to some kinds of war (e.g. nuclear war).
  • Conscientious objector: someone who refuses to fight on grounds of conscience. Many have served in non-combat roles such as ambulance work.
  • Passive resistance / non-violent direct action: opposing injustice without violence: marches, boycotts, sit-ins, refusing to obey unjust laws.
  • Pacifism is not doing nothing: pacifists actively work for peace.
Mission 5Read the source

The Sermon on the Mount

"Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (Matthew 5:9)

"You have heard that it was said, 'An eye for an eye, and a tooth for a tooth.' But I tell you, don't resist him who is evil; but whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:38–39)

  • "love your enemies … pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you" (Matthew 5:44)
  • At his arrest Jesus tells Peter: "all those who take the sword will die by the sword." (Matthew 26:52)

Q1 What does Jesus replace "an eye for an eye" with?

Show answer

Not returning violence: turning the other cheek.

Q2 How might a conditional pacifist read Matthew 5:39?

Show answer

As a rule for personal revenge, not as forbidding a government to protect the innocent.

Mission 5

Quakers and the peace churches

  • The Religious Society of Friends (Quakers), founded in 17th-century England, holds a Peace Testimony.
  • Their declaration to King Charles II (1660): "We utterly deny all outward wars and strife and fightings with outward weapons, for any end or under any pretence whatsoever."
  • Reason: "that of God" is in every person, so no one may be killed.
  • Many Quakers were conscientious objectors in the World Wars; some served in the Friends Ambulance Unit.
  • Other historic peace churches: Mennonites, Church of the Brethren.
Quick check
?Gemma says: "War is nearly always wrong, but I would support it to stop a genocide." Which kind of pacifism, if any, does she hold?
Mission 5

Catholic teaching and the popes

  • The Catholic Church is not absolutely pacifist: states may use force in legitimate defence under strict conditions (CCC 2308–2309).
  • But it honours those who renounce violence, as long as others' rights are not harmed (CCC 2306, paraphrase), and supports conscientious objectors (CCC 2311).
  • A developing theme in papal teaching:
  • John XXIII, Pacem in Terris (1963): in the atomic age, war can hardly be a way to restore justice (paraphrase).
  • Paul VI at the United Nations (1965): "No more war, war never again!"
  • Francis, World Day of Peace message (2017): "Nonviolence: A Style of Politics for Peace". Fratelli Tutti (2020) repeats "Never again war!"
  • Some Catholics (e.g. members of Pax Christi) now ask the Church to move further towards non-violence; others defend the just war tradition.
Mission 5

Non-violent resistance in action

  • Mohandas (Mahatma) Gandhi (1869–1948): satyagraha ("truth-force"): resisting injustice by non-violence and willingness to suffer. The Salt March (1930) helped win Indian independence (1947).
  • Martin Luther King Jr (1929–1968), Baptist minister: led the Montgomery bus boycott (1955–56) and the civil rights movement. Inspired by the Sermon on the Mount and by Gandhi.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of passive resistance?
Mission 5

Criticisms and non-religious views

  • Criticism: pacifism may let aggressors win. Could Hitler have been stopped without war?
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), a German Lutheran pastor drawn to pacifism, joined resistance circles plotting against Hitler, believing it was his duty; he was executed in 1945.
  • Criticism: there is a duty to protect the weak, and doing nothing can also cause death.
  • Responses: non-violence has worked (India 1947; US civil rights; the largely peaceful revolutions of 1989); violence breeds more violence.
  • Non-religious views: Humanists differ. Many are conditional pacifists who judge each war by its consequences and human rights. Bertrand Russell (1872–1970) was imprisoned in 1918 for anti-war writing and became first president of CND (1958).
Mission 5Evaluate

"Religious believers should always be pacifists."

  • Reasons for: Jesus taught "turn … the other also" and "love your enemies"; Quakers, many Buddhists and Gandhi show non-violence can work; modern war kills mainly civilians.
  • Reasons against: most Christians accept just war as a last resort (CCC 2309); Islam, Judaism, Sikhism and Hinduism allow defensive war; failing to protect the innocent may be the greater evil (Bonhoeffer).
  • Another view: conditional pacifism: work for peace always, but accept force as a last resort.
  • Judge it by: Is non-violence a rule or a goal? Is a refusal to protect others itself a choice to let harm happen? Has non-violence succeeded against brutal regimes?
Mission 5Worked answer

Two contrasting beliefs

Question: Explain two contrasting beliefs in contemporary British society about pacifism.

  • Belief 1: Most Quakers are absolute pacifists: all war is wrong. Development: "that of God" is in every person, and Jesus taught "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44), so no one should be killed.
  • Belief 2: Most Christians (e.g. Catholics, Anglicans) are not pacifists: war can be justified as a last resort. Development: governments have a duty to protect citizens; the Catechism sets strict conditions for legitimate defence (CCC 2309).
Mission check

?During a war, a young nurse says she would never use violence, even to defend herself. She volunteers to drive ambulances on the front line, helping the wounded of both sides. Which best describes her position?

Pacifists differ on whether violence is ever allowed, but most agree that peace has to be actively built.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Wars usually have several causes: greed, self-defence, retaliation, politics, resources, history, culture and sometimes religion.
  • Holy war is war believed commanded by God (e.g. the Crusades); most religious believers today reject it, and their teachings limit war.
  • Critics say religion divides and sanctifies violence; believers reply that most wars are political or economic and that religion is often misused.

Mission 2

  • Just war theory (Augustine, Aquinas) sets conditions for going to war (just cause, authority, intention, last resort, success, proportionality) and rules for fighting (discrimination, proportional force).

Mission 3

  • WMD (nuclear, chemical, biological) kill indiscriminately; deterrence keeps them so they are never used.
  • Catholic teaching condemns indiscriminate destruction (Gaudium et Spes 80); popes have moved from tolerating deterrence (1982) to condemning possession (2017).
  • Utilitarians weigh consequences; most religious traditions reject using WMD, but believers differ about deterrence.

Mission 4

  • War's costs are human (civilians, refugees, child soldiers, trauma), economic and environmental, and they outlast the fighting.
  • Christians respond from Matthew 25 and human dignity, through organisations such as CAFOD, Christian Aid and the Jesuit Refugee Service.

Mission 5

  • Absolute pacifists reject all violence; conditional pacifists accept it as a last resort; passive resistance opposes injustice without violence.
  • Quakers, many Buddhists and Gandhi are pacifists; most Christians, Muslims, Jews, Sikhs and Hindus accept defensive war, while valuing peace.
  • The Catholic Church is not pacifist, but recent popes have moved strongly towards non-violence ("War never again!").
