A Thrush at the End of the Century

3 missions and a finale. A bleak winter poem, a thrush that sings anyway, and how to compare two poems. Guess, learn, then check.

Mission 1: What has died in this poem?

Make a guess: What has died in this poem?

Mission 1

Meet the poem

I leant upon a coppice gate When Frost was spectre-gray, And Winter's dregs made desolate The weakening eye of day. The tangled bine-stems scored the sky Like strings of broken lyres, And all mankind that haunted nigh Had sought their household fires. The land's sharp features seemed to be The Century's corpse outleant, His crypt the cloudy canopy, The wind his death-lament. The ancient pulse of germ and birth Was shrunken hard and dry, And every spirit upon earth Seemed fervourless as I. At once a voice arose among The bleak twigs overhead In a full-hearted evensong Of joy illimited; An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small, In blast-beruffled plume, Had chosen thus to fling his soul Upon the growing gloom. So little cause for carolings Of such ecstatic sound Was written on terrestrial things Afar or nigh around, That I could think there trembled through His happy good-night air Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew And I was unaware. 31st December 1900.
  • Thomas Hardy, "The Darkling Thrush": 32 lines, 4 stanzas, one winter evening at a gate.
  • Stanzas 1–2: a frozen landscape. Stanzas 3–4: a bird sings.
  • Darkling is an old word meaning in the dark.
  • Treat it as unseen: no notes, just the words on the page.
Mission 1

Stanza 1: the frost is a ghost

I leant upon a coppice gate When Frost was spectre-gray, And Winter's dregs made desolate The weakening eye of day. The tangled bine-stems scored the sky Like strings of broken lyres, And all mankind that haunted nigh Had sought their household fires.
  • "When Frost was spectre-gray": a spectre is a ghost. Frost gets a ghost's colour.
  • "Winter's dregs": what is left at the bottom of a cup. The season's bitter remains.
  • "bine-stems … / Like strings of broken lyres": a simile. Dead climbing stems look like snapped harp strings.
  • Everyone else has gone in to "their household fires". The speaker is outside, alone.
Quick check
?"The tangled bine-stems scored the sky / Like strings of broken lyres." What does this simile most suggest?
Mission 1

Stanza 2: the land is a corpse

The land's sharp features seemed to be The Century's corpse outleant, His crypt the cloudy canopy, The wind his death-lament. The ancient pulse of germ and birth Was shrunken hard and dry, And every spirit upon earth Seemed fervourless as I.
  • "The land's sharp features seemed to be / The Century's corpse outleant": the landscape is a dead body, stretched out.
  • Its crypt (tomb) is "the cloudy canopy": the sky. Its "death-lament" is the wind: a funeral song.
  • One extended metaphor: land = corpse, sky = tomb, wind = mourning. Every detail points at death.
  • Notice "seemed to be". The speaker admits this is how it looked to him.
Mission 1

Whose corpse? Read the date

  • The Collected Poems (1930) date it 31 December 1900: the 19th century's last day, counting 1801–1900. Earlier printings say "December 1900".
  • It was first printed on 29 December 1900 in The Graphic, under the title "By the Century's Deathbed".
  • So the corpse is the century: a hundred years ending. Nobody in the poem has died.
  • Exam tip: if an unseen poem comes with a date or a changed title, use it.
Quick check
?"And every spirit upon earth / Seemed fervourless as I." What does this line do?
Mission check

?What has died in this poem?

No one, literally: the land is pictured as the dead century.

Mission 2: Why does the thrush sing?

Make a guess: Why does the thrush sing?

Mission 2

The turn

At once a voice arose among The bleak twigs overhead In a full-hearted evensong Of joy illimited; An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small, In blast-beruffled plume, Had chosen thus to fling his soul Upon the growing gloom.
  • Line 17: "At once a voice arose among / The bleak twigs overhead."
  • Two stanzas of death, then a sudden sound. This is the poem's turn (volta).
  • The scene has not changed: still "bleak twigs", still "the growing gloom". Only the sound is new.
Mission 2

Not a pretty bird

  • "An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small, / In blast-beruffled plume": old, thin, wind-battered.
  • Its song: "a full-hearted evensong / Of joy illimited". Joy without limit. (Evensong: evening song; also an evening church service.)
  • It has "chosen thus to fling his soul / Upon the growing gloom": a reckless, almost defiant verb.
  • Weak body, huge song. The contrast is the point.
Quick check
?Why might the speaker describe the thrush as "aged", "frail, gaunt, and small"?
Mission 2

The speaker will not claim the hope

So little cause for carolings Of such ecstatic sound Was written on terrestrial things Afar or nigh around, That I could think there trembled through His happy good-night air Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew And I was unaware.
  • "So little cause for carolings … / Was written on terrestrial things": nothing on earth explains the joy.
  • "That I could think there trembled through … / Some blessed Hope": could think, not knew.
  • "whereof he knew / And I was unaware": the bird may know a hope; the speaker does not share it.
  • A possibility, not a promise. Say "the speaker", not "Hardy": the voice in a poem is made, like the rest of it.
Quick check
?"Some blessed Hope, whereof he knew / And I was unaware." Which reading is best supported by the words?
Mission 2

A steady shape for an unsteady feeling

  • Each stanza rhymes ABABCDCD: gate / gray / desolate / day, then sky / lyres / nigh / fires.
  • Lines alternate four beats and three: "I LEANT | upON | a COP|pice GATE / When FROST | was SPEC|tre-GRAY".
  • That is the shape of many hymns and ballads: regular, singable.
  • One reading: the tidy form keeps ticking while the content describes a death. Form and content pull against each other.
Quick check
?In stanza 3, "small" is rhymed with "soul". What is this?
Mission check

?Why does the thrush sing?

The poem never says: the speaker only guesses at a Hope he can't feel.

Mission 3: How do you compare two winter poems?

Make a guess: How do you compare two winter poems?

Mission 3

A second frost: Coleridge, 1798

The Frost performs its secret ministry, Unhelped by any wind. The owlet's cry Came loud—and hark, again! loud as before. The inmates of my cottage, all at rest, Have left me to that solitude, which suits Abstruser musings: save that at my side My cradled infant slumbers peacefully.
  • Coleridge, "Frost at Midnight" (February 1798): "The Frost performs its secret ministry, / Unhelped by any wind."
  • Ministry: service, quiet work. This frost is a careful worker, not a ghost.
  • The speaker is indoors by a low fire: "My cradled infant slumbers peacefully." Calm, not alone.
  • Coleridge wrote in 1798, inside what some historians call the Little Ice Age.
Mission 3

How Coleridge ends

Therefore all seasons shall be sweet to thee, Whether the summer clothe the general earth With greenness, or the redbreast sit and sing Betwixt the tufts of snow on the bare branch Of mossy apple-tree, while the nigh thatch Smokes in the sun-thaw; whether the eave-drops fall Heard only in the trances of the blast, Or if the secret ministry of frost Shall hang them up in silent icicles, Quietly shining to the quiet Moon.
  • The ending imagines the child's future winters: "the redbreast sit and sing / Betwixt the tufts of snow on the bare branch".
  • Last lines: "the secret ministry of frost / Shall hang them up in silent icicles, / Quietly shining to the quiet Moon."
  • No rhyme scheme: the poem is blank verse, unrhymed lines of five beats.
  • A bird on a bare winter branch in both poems. Coleridge's belongs to a promise; Hardy's to a puzzle.
Quick check
?Both poems give frost a character. What is the main difference?
Mission 3

A four-step comparison

  • 1 Shared subject. Both are winter-night poems that give frost a character.
  • 2 Different attitudes. Hardy's speaker: outside, drained, uncertain. Coleridge's: indoors, calm, hopeful for his child.
  • 3 How. Language ("spectre" against "ministry"); structure (Hardy's turn at line 17; Coleridge's drift from the fire to the future).
  • 4 Your judgement. Which attitude does the imagery earn? Say so, and quote.
Mission 3

What the mark scheme rewards

  • AQA's comparison question (27.2, 8 marks) tests AO2 only: "the language, form and structure used by a writer to create meanings and effects".
  • Top level: "Critical, insightful comparison of poems" and "Analytical comparison of the effects of writers' methods to create meanings".
  • Bottom level: "Identification of method(s)". Naming a simile earns little. Say what it does.
  • Compare methods and effects, poem against poem, in every paragraph.
Quick check
?Which sentence would score highest on an AQA-style comparison of the poets' methods?
Mission check

?How do you compare two winter poems?

Shared subject, different attitudes, how the methods make them, then your judgement.

Finale

Two birds on bare branches

  • Hardy: an "aged thrush" on "bleak twigs", singing as a century ends.
  • Coleridge: a "redbreast" on "the bare branch / Of mossy apple-tree", in a future the speaker promises his child.
  • Same picture, opposite moods. In an unseen exam you build meaning from details like these.
  • "Darkling" has a history with birds: Milton's bird "Sings darkling"; Keats, to a nightingale: "Darkling I listen". Hardy's title uses the same word.
Quick check
?In AQA Section C, the first unseen question (24 marks) is marked for AO1 and AO2 only. Which of these would earn nothing by itself?
Recap

The big ideas

  • Imagery builds mood: spectre, dregs, broken lyres, corpse, crypt, death-lament.
  • The date and first title tell you whose corpse it is: the century's.
  • The turn at line 17 brings a sound, not a change of scene. The speaker reports a Hope he does not share.
  • Regular ABABCDCD stanzas and a steady beat carry bleak content: form against content.
  • Compare: shared subject → different attitudes → methods and effects → your judgement.
  • Examiners reward effects of methods, compared, not technique-spotting.
