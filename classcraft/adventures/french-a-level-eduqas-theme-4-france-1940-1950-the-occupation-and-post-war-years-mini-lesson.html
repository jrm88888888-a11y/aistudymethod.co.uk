Mission 1

Defeat and division, 1940

  • 10 May 1940: German offensive in the West. 14 June: German troops enter Paris.
  • 17 June: Marshal Philippe Pétain, the new head of government, announces he is seeking an armistice.
  • 22 June: armistice signed at Rethondes. France is divided:
  • la zone occupée: the north and the Atlantic coast, about three fifths of the country, under German military command;
  • la zone libre (or non occupée): the south, governed from Vichy;
  • la ligne de démarcation: the guarded line between them;
  • Alsace and Moselle annexed in practice by Germany; a small Italian zone in the south-east.
  • France pays the occupation costs: at first 400 million francs a day.
  • About 1.8 million French soldiers are prisoners of war.
  • 11 November 1942: after the Allied landings in North Africa, Germany occupies the southern zone too.
Mission 1

Daily life under the Occupation

  • Rationing from autumn 1940: les tickets de rationnement, long queues, le marché noir (black market), le système D (getting by).
  • Curfew (le couvre-feu), censorship and propaganda; Paris runs on German time.
  • 1942 la Relève: one prisoner of war freed for every three volunteer workers sent to Germany.
  • 16 February 1943: the STO (Service du travail obligatoire): young men must work in Germany. About 600,000 French workers go; many others hide (see Mission 3).
  • Watch out: l'Occupation (capital: the period 1940–44) · l'occupant – the occupier · un collaborateur also means "a colleague" today; un collabo is the hostile slang of the period.

Mission 1

Collaboration

  • 24 October 1940: Pétain meets Hitler at Montoire. 30 October, on the radio: « … j'entre aujourd'hui dans la voie de la collaboration. »
  • State collaboration: Vichy hopes for a better place in a German-dominated Europe. It keeps its own police, prefects and laws, and puts them to work for the occupier's demands.
  • Collaborationism (le collaborationnisme): groups in Paris wanting a Nazi-style France and a German victory, e.g. Jacques Doriot's PPF and Marcel Déat's RNP.
  • 1941: the LVF: French volunteers fighting in German uniform on the Eastern Front.
  • 1943: the Milice (Joseph Darnand): a French paramilitary force that hunts résistants and Jews.
  • Economic collaboration: some firms work for the German war economy. Renault was nationalised in 1945 as a sanction.
Mission 1

Antisemitic laws

  • 3 October 1940: Vichy's first statut des Juifs. Jews are excluded from the civil service, the army, teaching, the press and cinema. Germany had not demanded it.
  • 4 October 1940: prefects may intern foreign Jews in camps (e.g. Gurs, Rivesaltes).
  • 7 October 1940: Algerian Jews lose French citizenship (the 1870 Crémieux decree is repealed).
  • March 1941: the Commissariat général aux questions juives.
  • 2 June 1941: second statut: stricter definition, census of Jews in the southern zone, quotas in professions.
  • L'aryanisation: Jewish businesses are taken over by non-Jewish administrators.
  • 7 June 1942: German order: in the occupied zone, Jews aged 6 and over must wear a yellow star.

Mission 1

The Vel' d'Hiv round-up and the deportations

  • 27 March 1942: the first convoy of Jews leaves France for Auschwitz.
  • July 1942: Vichy's police chief, René Bousquet, agrees that French police will carry out the arrests, starting with foreign and stateless Jews.
  • Why? Historians point to Vichy's wish to keep its police under French command in both zones, and to its own antisemitic policy.
  • 16–17 July 1942: in Paris, French police arrest 13,152 Jews, including 4,115 children. Families with children (about 8,000 people) are held for days at the Vélodrome d'Hiver (a cycling stadium) in terrible conditions; the other adults are sent straight to Drancy.
  • The families are moved to camps at Pithiviers and Beaune-la-Rolande. About 3,000 young children are separated from their parents and deported in August 1942. None returned.
  • August 1942: Vichy hands over foreign Jews from the southern zone too.
  • 6 April 1944: at Izieu, 44 Jewish children and their 7 adult carers are arrested on Klaus Barbie's orders.
  • In total 75,721 Jews were deported from France, including more than 10,000 children. 2,566 survived.
Mission 1

Rescue and survival

  • About three quarters of the Jews living in France in 1940 survived (Serge Klarsfeld's estimates).
  • Many were helped by ordinary people who hid, fed or guided them.
  • Summer 1942: some Catholic and Protestant leaders protest publicly, e.g. Archbishop Jules Saliège of Toulouse.
  • Le Chambon-sur-Lignon (Haute-Loire): a Protestant village and plateau that sheltered hundreds of Jews, many of them children.
  • Organisations such as the OSE (Œuvre de secours aux enfants) hide children under false names.
  • Les Justes parmi les Nations: Yad Vashem's title for non-Jews who saved Jews at risk to themselves. More than 4,000 French people have been recognised.
Mission 1

Memory and justice: argue both sides

  • For decades the role of the French state was little discussed (historian Henry Rousso called this "the Vichy syndrome").
  • Trials: Klaus Barbie (1987, life sentence); Paul Touvier (Milice, 1994, life); Maurice Papon (Bordeaux prefecture official, 1998, 10 years for complicity in crimes against humanity).
  • 16 July 1995: President Jacques Chirac recognises the French state's responsibility in the deportation of Jews.
  • Debate at the Papon trial: should someone be tried more than 50 years later?
  • Pour: Les crimes contre l'humanité sont imprescriptibles : la justice n'a pas de date limite. – Crimes against humanity have no time limit.
  • Contre (arguments the defence made): Juger un homme âgé si longtemps après les faits, c'est juger une autre époque. – Trying an old man so long afterwards means judging another era.

Mission 2

10 July 1940: the end of the Third Republic

  • Deputies and senators meet in Vichy, a spa town in the southern zone.
  • They vote full powers to Marshal Pétain's government to write a new constitution: 569 for, 80 against, 20 abstentions.
  • Some members were absent: communist deputies had been stripped of their seats in 1940, and others had sailed to North Africa.
  • 11 July: Pétain makes himself chef de l'État français. Parliament is adjourned and never meets again under Vichy.
  • La République française disappears from official documents: the regime calls itself l'État français.
  • No new constitution was ever promulgated.
  • Les quatre-vingts ("the Eighty") who voted against are honoured today.
  • Watch out: les pleins pouvoirs – full powers (plural; plein agrees). Le régime de Vichy is named after the town; les vichystes = its supporters.
Mission 2

Who was Pétain?

  • Born 1856; le vainqueur de Verdun (1916); Marshal of France in 1918.
  • 1939: ambassador to Franco's Spain.
  • 16 June 1940: aged 84, he becomes head of government and asks for an armistice.
  • On the radio he blames the defeat on the years before the war: « l'esprit de jouissance l'a emporté sur l'esprit de sacrifice » (20 June 1940).
  • Cult of the Marshal: portraits in schools and town halls, posters, the song Maréchal, nous voilà !, oaths of loyalty from officials, large crowds on his tours.
  • Key ministers: Pierre Laval (1940, then head of government 1942–44) and Admiral François Darlan (1941–42).

Mission 2

Travail, Famille, Patrie

  • Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité is replaced by Travail, Famille, Patrie (Work, Family, Fatherland) on coins and official documents.
  • La Révolution nationale: Vichy's programme to remake France:
  • order, hierarchy and authority instead of elections and parties;
  • rejection of the individualism of 1789 and of the parliamentary Republic, which it blamed for the defeat;
  • the Catholic Church's support welcomed; the peasant and the family idealised.
  • Vichy propaganda blamed an « anti-France »: Jews, Freemasons, communists and foreigners.
  • Key point: historians show these policies were Vichy's own choices. Germany did not require them.
Mission 2

The policies

  • Travail: national union confederations dissolved (November 1940). The Charte du travail (October 1941) bans strikes and groups employers and workers in "corporations".
  • Famille: divorce banned in the first three years of marriage (1941). Mother's Day made official (1941). Married women discouraged from public-sector jobs (1940). Abortion made a crime against the State (1942); a woman was guillotined for performing abortions in 1943.
  • Patrie / youth: the Chantiers de la jeunesse (1940), months of compulsory outdoor service for young men of 20 in the southern zone. Teacher-training colleges, seen as republican, closed (1940).
  • Retour à la terre: « la terre, elle, ne ment pas » (Pétain, 25 June 1940). Rural life and regional folklore idealised.

Mission 2

Exclusion and repression

  • Denaturalisation (law of July 1940): naturalisations since 1927 reviewed; about 15,000 people lose French nationality, many of them Jews.
  • Antisemitic laws: statuts des Juifs of 1940 and 1941 (see Mission 1).
  • Freemasons: lodges dissolved (August 1940); members' names published; banned from public posts.
  • Communists: their party had been banned in 1939; Vichy creates special courts (sections spéciales, 1941) against them.
  • Internment camps in the southern zone (e.g. Gurs, Rivesaltes, Le Vernet) for foreigners, Jews and political opponents.
  • The Riom trial (1942): Léon Blum, Édouard Daladier and others are tried for the defeat. They turn the trial against Vichy, and it is suspended.

Mission 2

From support to rejection, 1940–1944

  • 1940: many French people saw Pétain as a protector after the defeat. Historians stress that support for the man was broader than support for his policies.
  • April 1942: Laval returns as head of government. 22 June 1942, on the radio: « Je souhaite la victoire de l'Allemagne ». He launches the Relève.
  • November 1942: Germany occupies the southern zone; the French fleet is scuttled at Toulon. Vichy's claim to independence is empty.
  • 1943: the STO and the Milice turn many against the regime; thousands join the maquis (see Mission 3).
  • August 1944: the Germans take Pétain to Sigmaringen in Germany.
  • 1945: Pétain is tried by the Haute Cour de justice and sentenced to death. De Gaulle commutes the sentence to life imprisonment; Pétain dies on the Île d'Yeu in 1951. Laval is executed in October 1945.
Mission 2

Judging Vichy: argue both sides

  • After 1945 Pétain's defenders described him as le bouclier (the shield) protecting the French, while de Gaulle was l'épée (the sword).
  • Since the American historian Robert Paxton (La France de Vichy, 1973), historians have shown that Vichy offered collaboration and pursued its own programme, including the antisemitic laws.
  • Arguments made in Vichy's defence:
  • La France avait subi une défaite totale ; ses marges de manœuvre étaient très limitées. – France had suffered total defeat; its room for manoeuvre was very limited.
  • Arguments of historians and critics:
  • Le régime a souvent devancé les exigences allemandes. – The regime often acted before Germany asked (devancer = to anticipate).
  • La Révolution nationale n'était pas imposée par l'occupant : c'était un choix. – The National Revolution was not imposed by the occupier; it was a choice.
  • Your turn: "Pétain's defenders claimed that he had protected the French."

Mission 3

18 June 1940: Free France

  • 17 June 1940: Pétain announces he is seeking an armistice.
  • 18 June: General Charles de Gaulle, in London, speaks on the BBC. He says the war is not lost and calls on French soldiers and workers in Britain to join him.
  • Few heard it live; it became the founding text of the Resistance: l'appel du 18 juin.
  • La France libre (Free France): small at first; Vichy sentences de Gaulle to death in his absence (August 1940).
  • Colonies rally: Chad first (August 1940, governor Félix Éboué), then French Equatorial Africa.
  • The Forces françaises libres fight with the Allies, e.g. Bir Hakeim (Libya, 1942).
  • rejoindre – to join · un appel – an appeal, a call · la France libre (London) ≠ la zone libre (Vichy's southern zone).
Mission 3

Resistance inside France

  • Les mouvements: groups that publish papers, recruit, then arm:
  • southern zone: Combat (Henri Frenay, Berty Albrecht), Libération-Sud (Emmanuel d'Astier de La Vigerie, Lucie and Raymond Aubrac), Franc-Tireur;
  • northern zone: Libération-Nord, OCM, Défense de la France.
  • Les réseaux: networks linked to London for intelligence, escape lines and sabotage, e.g. Alliance.
  • Communists: after Germany invades the USSR (June 1941), the Communist Party commits to armed struggle (FTP, Francs-tireurs et partisans).
  • Actions: underground press, false papers, hiding Jews and Allied airmen, intelligence, sabotage of railways.
  • Le maquis: from 1943, young men refusing the STO hide in forests and mountains (e.g. Vercors, Glières).
  • Risks: torture, deportation, execution; hostages shot in reprisal (e.g. Châteaubriant, October 1941: 27 hostages, including Guy Môquet, aged 17).
  • Watch out: in 1941 the communists named one movement Front national. It has no link with the party founded in 1972.

Mission 3

Jean Moulin: uniting the Resistance

  • 1940: prefect of Eure-et-Loir. On 17 June he refuses to sign a German document falsely blaming African soldiers of the French army (tirailleurs sénégalais) for atrocities, and tries to take his life rather than sign. Vichy dismisses him (November 1940).
  • October 1941: he reaches London and meets de Gaulle.
  • 1 January 1942: he is parachuted into Provence as de Gaulle's representative. Code names: Rex, then Max.
  • January 1943: the three main southern movements merge into the MUR (Mouvements unis de la Résistance).
  • 27 May 1943: first meeting of the Conseil national de la Résistance (CNR) in Paris: movements, political parties and trade unions, united behind de Gaulle.
  • 21 June 1943: arrested at Caluire, near Lyon; tortured under Klaus Barbie; dies in July 1943 in German custody.
  • 1964: his ashes are moved to the Panthéon.
Mission 3

The CNR programme (15 March 1944)

  • A plan for France after the Liberation, circulated secretly in 1944 under the title Les Jours heureux:
  • punish the collaborators;
  • restore democracy, universal suffrage and freedom of the press;
  • a complete social security plan;
  • return major means of production, energy, banks and insurance to the nation (nationalisations);
  • a pension so that older workers can end their days in dignity.
  • Much of it was applied in 1944–46 (see Mission 4): la Sécurité sociale (1945); nationalisation of coal, electricity, main banks and Renault.
  • Today politicians across the spectrum refer to the CNR.

Mission 3

Women of the Resistance

  • Women were vital as couriers (agents de liaison), in hiding people, printing and distributing papers, and gathering intelligence. Some led networks.
  • Berty Albrecht: co-founder of Combat; arrested in 1943, she died in Fresnes prison.
  • Lucie Aubrac (Libération-Sud): in October 1943 she organised an armed attack in Lyon that freed her husband Raymond.
  • Marie-Madeleine Fourcade: head of the Alliance intelligence network.
  • Danielle Casanova: communist activist; died in Auschwitz in 1943.
  • Germaine Tillion and Geneviève de Gaulle-Anthonioz: deported to Ravensbrück; entered the Panthéon in 2015.
  • Joséphine Baker: intelligence work for Free France; entered the Panthéon in 2021.
  • Recognition came late: only 6 of the 1,038 Compagnons de la Libération were women.
  • 21 April 1944: an ordinance of de Gaulle's Free French committee in Algiers (the CFLN) gives women the vote (first used in April 1945).
Mission 3

Foreigners in the Resistance: the Affiche rouge

  • Many résistants were foreigners or immigrants: Spanish Republicans, Italian antifascists, Jewish refugees from Central Europe, Armenians.
  • In Paris, the FTP-MOI (Main-d'œuvre immigrée): an armed group led by Missak Manouchian, an Armenian poet who had survived the 1915 genocide.
  • Arrested in November 1943; 22 men are shot at Mont-Valérien on 21 February 1944. Olga Bancic, the only woman, is executed in Germany in May 1944.
  • German propaganda puts up l'Affiche rouge, a red poster presenting them as foreign "criminals".
  • 21 February 2024: Missak and Mélinée Manouchian enter the Panthéon.
Mission 3

How many resisted? Argue both sides

  • After 1944, official memory stressed a France that had liberated itself. The historian Henry Rousso calls this view le résistancialisme.
  • Le Chagrin et la Pitié (Marcel Ophuls, 1969): a documentary showing attentisme (waiting to see) and collaboration in one town; it was not shown on French television until 1981.
  • Historians today: active résistants were a small minority, growing in 1943–44; many more gave occasional help (hiding, feeding, keeping silent).
  • Pour — the Resistance mattered (arguments historians and veterans make):
  • Les renseignements et les sabotages ont aidé les Alliés lors du débarquement de 1944. – Intelligence and sabotage helped the Allies at the 1944 landings.
  • La Résistance a permis à la France de retrouver sa place parmi les vainqueurs. – The Resistance let France regain its place among the victors.
  • Contre — its role is often overstated (arguments others make):
  • Sur le plan militaire, la Libération a d'abord été l'œuvre des armées alliées. – Militarily, the Liberation was mainly the work of the Allied armies.
  • Le mythe d'une France résistante a longtemps caché la collaboration. – The myth of a resisting France long hid collaboration.
  • Your turn: "Without the Resistance, France would not have had the same place among the victors."

Mission 4

Liberation, 1944–1945

  • 6 June 1944: Allied landings in Normandy (le Débarquement).
  • 15 August 1944: landings in Provence; the French army there includes many soldiers recruited in North and sub-Saharan Africa.
  • 25 August 1944: Paris is liberated by Leclerc's 2nd Armoured Division and the Resistance (FFI), after an uprising that began on 19 August.
  • Charles de Gaulle heads the Gouvernement provisoire de la République française (GPRF), the provisional government.
  • 8 May 1945: Germany surrenders. Prisoners of war, forced labourers and deportees return; many deportees are received at the Hôtel Lutetia in Paris.
  • About 75,700 Jews were deported from France; only about 2,500 came back.
  • la Libération · le Débarquement · un(e) déporté(e) · le retour des prisonniers
Mission 4

Judging collaboration: l'épuration

  • L'épuration = the purge of people accused of collaboration.
  • Without trial (l'épuration sauvage / extrajudiciaire), 1944: historians estimate 9,000–10,000 people killed.
  • About 20,000 women accused of relations with Germans had their heads shaved in public (les femmes tondues).
  • By the courts (l'épuration légale): about 6,800 death sentences, many in the accused's absence; fewer than 800 carried out.
  • A new offence, l'indignité nationale, punished with la dégradation nationale (loss of civic rights).
  • Pétain: sentenced to death on 15 August 1945; de Gaulle commuted it to life imprisonment. He died in 1951, still in detention on the Île d'Yeu.
  • Pierre Laval, Vichy's head of government: executed on 15 October 1945.
  • Amnesty laws in 1951 and 1953 released most of those still in prison.

Mission 4

Women get the vote

  • 21 April 1944: an ordinance of de Gaulle's committee in Algiers gives women the right to vote and to stand for election.
  • 29 April 1945: women vote for the first time, in municipal elections.
  • October 1945: 33 women are elected to the Constituent Assembly.
  • One reason given at the time: women's role in the Resistance (see Mission 3).
  • 1946: the new Constitution's preamble guarantees women equal rights with men in all areas.
  • Watch out: le droit de vote – the right to vote; l'éligibilité – the right to stand. Une électrice – a woman voter.

Mission 4

A new social model

  • March 1944: the Conseil national de la Résistance (CNR) adopts a programme for after the war: social security, nationalisations, economic democracy.
  • October 1945: ordinances create the Sécurité sociale (health, old age, family benefits), designed by Pierre Laroque and put in place by Ambroise Croizat, Minister of Labour.
  • Nationalisations:
  • Renault (January 1945), as a sanction for the firm's work for Germany;
  • the Banque de France and the four largest deposit banks (December 1945);
  • EDF and GDF, electricity and gas (April 1946).
  • 1945: comités d'entreprise (works councils); the École nationale d'administration (ENA) to train senior civil servants.
  • nationaliser · la protection sociale · les allocations familiales

Mission 4

Rebuilding the country

  • Towns such as Le Havre, Brest, Saint-Malo and Caen were largely destroyed; bridges, railways and ports were cut.
  • 1944: a Ministry of Reconstruction and Urban Planning (MRU).
  • Le Havre is rebuilt to plans by the architect Auguste Perret (concrete, grid layout); UNESCO World Heritage Site since 2005.
  • 1946: Jean Monnet heads a new planning office; its first modernisation plan, the Plan Monnet (adopted January 1947), targets coal, steel, electricity and transport.
  • 1948–1952: the American Marshall Plan; France is one of its main recipients.
  • Daily life stays hard: rationing continues until 1949; housing is scarce.
  • From 1946: the baby-boom, over 800,000 births a year.
  • la reconstruction · le rationnement · la pénurie – shortage · la natalité – birth rate
Mission 4

A new Republic, and what lasts today

  • January 1946: de Gaulle resigns as head of the provisional government.
  • May 1946: voters reject a first draft constitution; 13 October 1946: a second draft is approved by referendum → the Fourth Republic.
  • 19 March 1946: Guadeloupe, Martinique, French Guiana and Réunion become French départements (law defended by Aimé Césaire).
  • Still in force today:
  • the 1946 preamble is part of the present Constitution: right to strike, trade union rights, equality of women and men, right to health protection;
  • the Sécurité sociale.
  • Memory: after the war, many stressed a France united in Resistance. Historian Henry Rousso calls this view le résistancialisme.
  • 16 July 1995: President Jacques Chirac recognises the French State's responsibility in the Vel' d'Hiv round-up (see Mission 1).
Mission 4

Argue both sides

  • Was the épuration justice?
  • Arguments for: Il fallait juger les collaborateurs pour rétablir l'État de droit. – Collaborators had to be tried to restore the rule of law.
  • Arguments against: Les exécutions sommaires ont parfois relevé de la vengeance plutôt que de la justice. – Summary executions were sometimes revenge rather than justice.
  • Was the 1951–53 amnesty right?
  • For: Le pays avait besoin de se réconcilier pour se reconstruire. – The country needed to reconcile in order to rebuild.
  • Against: L'amnistie a permis à certains coupables d'échapper à leur responsabilité. – The amnesty let some guilty people escape responsibility.
  • Linkers: D'un côté … de l'autre … · Certes …, mais … · Il n'en reste pas moins que … – The fact remains that …

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • 1940: armistice, two zones, Vichy; occupation costs, prisoners, rationing, STO (1943).
  • Collaboration of the State (Montoire, police, laws) and collaborationism (PPF, RNP, LVF, Milice).
  • Antisemitism: Vichy's statuts (1940, 1941); Vel' d'Hiv (16–17 July 1942: 13,152 arrested by French police); 75,721 deported, 2,566 survived; about three quarters of Jews in France survived; 1995 recognition.

Mission 2

  • 10 July 1940: full powers (569–80–20); 11 July: l'État français, Pétain head of state; the Third Republic ends in practice.
  • National Revolution: Travail (Charte du travail), Famille (divorce, Mother's Day), Patrie (youth camps, cult of the Marshal); exclusion of Jews, Freemasons, communists and many naturalised French people.
  • Support fell from 1942 (Laval, southern zone occupied, STO); Pétain tried 1945. Paxton showed Vichy chose its policies.

Mission 3

  • 18 June 1940: de Gaulle's appeal and Free France; inside France, movements, networks, communists (FTP) and, from 1943, the maquis.
  • Jean Moulin unified the Resistance: MUR (January 1943), CNR (27 May 1943); CNR programme (March 1944) shaped post-war France.
  • Women (Albrecht, Aubrac, Fourcade, Tillion) and foreigners (Manouchian) were central; active résistants were a minority, and their memory was long simplified.

Mission 4

  • 1944 Liberation → GPRF (de Gaulle) → épuration (summary and legal) → 1951–53 amnesties.
  • 1944–46: women's vote, Sécurité sociale, nationalisations, Fourth Republic, 1946 preamble (still in force).
  • Rebuilding: Le Havre (Perret), Plan Monnet, Marshall Plan, rationing until 1949, baby-boom.
