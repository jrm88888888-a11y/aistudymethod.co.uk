Mission 1

Mission 1: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Make a guess: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Mission 1

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level. The inflation rate is its % change, usually over 12 months.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Real data: UK CPI inflation was 11.1% in October 2022 and 2.0% in May 2024. That fall was disinflation: prices kept rising.
  • Say: "inflation fell, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "inflation fell, so prices fell".
Mission 1

Index numbers and the base year

  • An index number shows a value relative to a base year, which is set to 100.
  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a price is 2.00 in the base year, 2.50 a year later, 2.60 the year after.
  • Index: 100 → 125 → 130.
  • Change from the base year: read it straight off: 125 means 25% higher.
  • Change between two later years: % change, not points. 125 → 130 is a 5-point rise but (130 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 4%.
  • Index numbers have no units, so series in different units or currencies can be compared.
  • Say: "the index rose by 5 points, a 4% rise" — not: "the index rose by 5%".
Quick check
?A country's consumer price index (base year 2015 = 100) was 125 in 2023 and 130 in 2024 (illustrative data). What was the inflation rate in 2024?
Mission 1

Measuring inflation with a basket

  • Statisticians pick a basket of goods and services that typical households buy.
  • They record the prices of the basket's items every month, in many shops and places.
  • A household spending survey shows how much households spend on each item. These shares become the weights.
  • Big spending shares (e.g. housing, food) get big weights: their price changes move the index more.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of this basket over time.
  • The CPI is the most common measure of inflation around the world; each country's statistics office runs its own basket.
Mission 1

Worked example: the cost of a fixed basket

  • Illustrative data. A basket of three items; the quantities stay fixed. Year 1 is the base year.

  • CPI: Y1 = 100; Y2 = 270 ÷ 250 × 100 = 108; Y3 = 283.5 ÷ 250 × 100 = 113.4.

  • Inflation Y3: (113.4 − 108) ÷ 108 × 100 = 5%.

Mission 1

Real versus nominal

  • Nominal (money) value: measured in the prices of the time (current prices).
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation, measured in the prices of one year (constant prices).
  • Real value = nominal value × (price index in base year ÷ price index in current year).
  • Illustrative data: weekly pay rises from 400 to 520 while the CPI rises from 100 to 135.
  • Real pay now, in base-year prices: 520 × 100 ÷ 135 = 385.19.
  • Nominal pay is up 30%. Real pay is down 3.7%: prices rose faster than pay.
  • Shortcut: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. It works well only for small changes (here: 30 − 35 = −5, against an exact −3.7).
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when nominal pay rose.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 540. Over the same period, the consumer price index rises from 120 to 132. What happened to the worker's real pay?
Mission 1

Limits of the CPI

  • Substitution bias: when one item's price jumps, people switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket misses this, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living.
  • New products: new goods enter the basket only after a delay.
  • Quality changes: a dearer phone may also be a much better phone. Not all of the price rise is inflation.
  • Not your basket: the CPI tracks an average household. Students, pensioners and renters spend differently, so face different inflation rates.
  • Weights lag behind: they come from past spending surveys and are updated only from time to time (often yearly).
  • Real data: in 1996 the US Boskin Commission estimated that the CPI overstated the rise in the cost of living by about 1.1 percentage points a year; about 0.4 points of this was substitution bias.
  • Say: "the CPI measures changes in the price of a basket" — not: "the CPI measures each person's cost of living".
Quick check
?The price of beef rises by 30% while the price of chicken does not change. Many households buy less beef and more chicken. The consumer price index uses a fixed basket bought in the base year. What is the most likely effect on the measured inflation rate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In one year, food prices rise by 10% and all other prices stay the same. Household X spends 40% of its budget on food. Household Y spends 10% on food. In the country's consumer price index, food has a weight of 20%. Which set of inflation rates is correct?

A price index is only as good as its weights. Each household has its own inflation rate, and the CPI is the average one.

Mission 2

Two routes to inflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level (see Mission 1).
  • In the AD/AS model, the price level rises in two ways:
    • AD shifts right: spending pulls prices up → demand-pull inflation
    • SRAS shifts left: costs push prices up → cost-push inflation
  • One shift gives a one-off rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs the shift to keep happening.
  • Say: "inflation is a rising price level" — not: "inflation means prices are high".
Mission 2

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • What can push AD right: lower interest rates, easy credit, rising house prices (wealth), high confidence, an export boom, more government spending, tax cuts.
  • With spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises real output.
  • Near full capacity, firms cannot expand much, so extra AD mostly raises the price level.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve: the steeper the section AD moves along, the more of the rise is in prices.
Quick check
?Interest rates are cut and house prices climb, so households borrow and spend more. The economy is already close to full capacity. Which best describes what happens in the short run?
Mission 2

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push: firms' costs rise, so they supply less at each price level. SRAS shifts left.
  • Sources of higher costs:
    • wages rising faster than productivity
    • energy and raw materials (commodity prices)
    • imported inputs (a weaker currency or dearer world prices)
    • indirect taxes on inputs or goods
  • Result: the price level rises and real output falls. Unemployment rises: stagflation.
  • Real world: wholesale energy prices jumped in 2021–22. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022; energy and food were the largest contributors.
  • Euro area inflation reached 10.6% in the same month.
  • Say: "costs shift SRAS left" — not: "costs shift AD".

Mission 2

Imported inflation and commodity prices

  • Imported inflation: higher prices of imports feed through to home prices.
  • Two triggers:
    • world prices rise (oil, gas, metals, food: commodity prices)
    • the home currency depreciates, so each import costs more in home currency
  • Imported inputs raise firms' costs: a cost-push shift of SRAS. Imported consumer goods raise the CPI directly.
  • Real world: sterling fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose from 0.5% (June 2016) to 3.1% (November 2017).
  • Internal value of money: what it buys at home. Inflation lowers it.
  • External value of money: what it buys abroad, shown by the exchange rate. A fall in external value can cause imported inflation.
  • Say: "a depreciation makes imports dearer" — not: "a weaker currency makes imports cheaper".
Quick check
?Country X's currency is the crown. A firm there imports a part priced at 50 euros. The exchange rate falls from 1 crown = 1.25 euros to 1 crown = 1.00 euro. By how much does the part's price in crowns change?
Mission 2

Expectations and the wage-price spiral

  • Inflation expectations: what workers and firms think inflation will be.
  • If workers expect prices to rise 5%, they bargain for pay rises of at least 5% to protect real wages.
  • Firms expecting higher costs raise prices in advance.
  • Wage-price spiral: higher prices → higher wage claims → higher costs → higher prices → …
  • Expectations can make inflation self-fulfilling and slow to fall.
  • That is why central banks try to keep expectations anchored to their inflation target.
  • Real world: in the UK in the 1970s, oil price rises fed into large wage claims. UK RPI inflation peaked at about 27% in August 1975.
Mission 2Evaluate

Which cause, and does it matter?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity. The closer to full capacity, the more a rise in AD shows up as inflation.
  • One-off or ongoing. A single jump in oil prices raises the price level once. It becomes lasting inflation only if wages, expectations or money growth follow.
  • The policy response. Higher interest rates curb demand-pull well. Against cost-push, they cut inflation only by deepening the fall in output.
  • Mixed causes. Real episodes often combine both. 2021–22 saw energy shocks and strong post-pandemic demand.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Over a year, an economy's price level rises 6% and real output grows 4%, taking it above its potential level. Unemployment falls to a record low. Firms report full order books and staff shortages. World energy and raw material prices are unchanged. What is the most likely main cause of the inflation?

Check what happened to real output and jobs: they tell you whether prices were pulled or pushed.

Mission 3

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (a common benchmark: over 50% a month). Money loses its value fast.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 3

Worked example: reading the inflation rate

  • Illustrative data. Consumer price index (CPI) at the end of each year:
Year1234
CPI250260265.2262.5
  • Inflation rate = (new CPI − old CPI) ÷ old CPI × 100.
  • Year 2: (260 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = 4.0%. Inflation.
  • Year 3: (265.2 − 260) ÷ 260 × 100 = 2.0%. The rate fell but prices still rose: disinflation.
  • Year 4: (262.5 − 265.2) ÷ 265.2 × 100 = −1.0%. Prices fell: deflation.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's annual inflation rate was 3% in 2020, 5% in 2021, 2% in 2022 and −1% in 2023. In which year did the country have disinflation?
Mission 3

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Even fully expected inflation still has some costs.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 3

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.
Quick check

Mission 3

The costs of inflation

  • Menu costs: time and money spent changing prices (price lists, labels, websites).
  • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around to protect its value.
  • Uncertainty: high, variable inflation makes future costs and revenue hard to predict, so firms invest less. Growth can suffer.
  • Savings discouraged: if interest rates lag behind inflation, saving loses real value.
  • International competitiveness: if inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports get dearer and imports relatively cheaper (unless the exchange rate falls to offset it). The current account can worsen.
  • Inefficient resource allocation: when all prices rise fast, changes in relative prices are hard to spot, so price signals work less well.
  • Most costs grow with the rate of inflation and with how unpredictable it is.
Mission 3

Hyperinflation: when money stops working

  • At very high inflation, money fails at its jobs:
    • store of value: savings in cash are wiped out
    • medium of exchange: sellers refuse it and people turn to barter or foreign currency
    • unit of account: prices change too fast to compare
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed foreign currencies such as the US dollar and the South African rand, and people stopped using its own currency.
  • Hyperinflations usually end only with a new currency or a new monetary regime (money growth).
Mission 3

Deflation: good news or bad?

  • Benign ("good") deflation: SRAS shifts right (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Malign ("bad") deflation: AD shifts left (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession).
    • Price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Most worries about deflation are about the malign kind.

Mission 3

The costs of deflation

  • Deferred consumption: if prices are expected to fall, households wait to buy. AD falls further.
  • Real value of debt rises: debts are fixed in money, but prices and incomes fall. Borrowers cut spending; some go bankrupt.
  • Unemployment: firms' revenue falls, but money wages are sticky downwards, so real wages rise and firms cut jobs (cyclical unemployment).
  • Policy ineffectiveness: interest rates cannot go far below zero, so real interest rates (≈ nominal − inflation) stay high when prices fall.
  • Deflationary spiral: falling prices → spending delayed → AD falls → output and jobs fall → prices fall further → …
  • Real world: US consumer prices fell by about a quarter from 1929 to 1933.
  • Real world: Japan's consumer prices fell in most years from 1999 to 2012, with interest rates close to zero.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm owes 100,000 on a loan with no interest. Over a year, the price level falls by 10%, and so do the prices the firm charges. What happens to the real burden of the debt?
Mission 3Evaluate

How much does inflation hurt?

It depends on:

  • The rate and its stability. Low, steady inflation costs little; high, volatile inflation costs a lot.
  • Whether it is expected. Expected inflation is built into wages and interest rates; surprises redistribute.
  • Indexation. Pensions, benefits and tax thresholds linked to prices protect people on fixed incomes.
  • Inflation abroad. What matters for competitiveness is inflation relative to trading partners, and the exchange rate.
  • Low inflation is not zero inflation. Many central banks target about 2%: a small positive rate lets real wages adjust without money pay cuts and leaves room above zero for interest rates.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A family takes a one-year loan from a bank at a fixed 4% interest rate. Both sides expect 3% inflation. Instead, prices fall by 1% over the year. Who gains from the surprise?

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise; deflation is a falling price level.
  • A price index compares the cost of a weighted basket with its cost in a base year (= 100). Inflation is the % change in the index, not the change in points.
  • Real values remove inflation: real = nominal × (base-year index ÷ current index). The CPI has limits, including substitution bias, quality change and its average-household basket.

Mission 2

  • Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity; prices and real output rise together.
  • Cost-push: higher costs shift SRAS left; prices rise while output falls.
  • Expectations, wage claims and import prices can keep inflation going.

Mission 3

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
  • Inflation also brings menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty and weaker competitiveness; malign deflation can spiral into falling output and jobs.
