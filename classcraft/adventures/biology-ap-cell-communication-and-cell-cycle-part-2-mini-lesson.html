Mission 1

Mission 1: Identical twins start life with the same genes. Why do they become more different as they age?

Make a guess: Identical twins start life with the same genes. Why do they become more different as they age?

Mission 1

The environment changes the tags

  • Diet, stress, chemicals and pollution can add or remove epigenetic tags.
  • Changed tags → changed gene expression → changed phenotype. The base sequence stays the same.
  • The changes are reversible: the same modifications can be removed again.
  • Say: "the environment altered gene expression" — not: "the environment mutated the genes".

Mission check

?Female honeybee larvae in a hive are genetically alike. Larvae fed only on royal jelly develop into fertile queens; larvae switched to a diet of worker jelly develop into smaller, sterile workers. Which explanation is consistent with the understanding of gene expression?

Twins, bees, polluted air: the same genome can be read in different ways, and the environment helps decide which.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Make a guess: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Mission 2

A steady inside

  • Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment within limits, whatever happens outside.
  • Internal environment = blood and tissue fluid around the cells.
  • Regulated variables include core temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and the water potential of the blood.
  • Each variable fluctuates around a set point. It is never perfectly constant.
  • AP: the system returns to its target set point after each disturbance.
Mission 2

The control loop

  • Stimulus: a change in a variable.
  • Receptor detects it.
  • Coordinator (e.g. hypothalamus, pancreas) compares it with the set point.
  • Effector (a muscle or a gland) makes the response.
  • Say: "receptors detect; effectors respond" — never swap them.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the feedback mechanisms that maintain an organism's internal environment?
Mission 2

Negative feedback

  • Negative feedback: the response reverses the change.
  • Variable rises above the set point → response lowers it.
  • Variable falls below the set point → response raises it.
  • So it works from both sides, and the variable oscillates around the set point.
  • The response starts only after a change, so small swings are normal.
  • AP: negative feedback reduces the initial stimulus. It works at molecular, cellular and organismal levels.
  • Say: "negative feedback opposes the change" — not: "negative feedback lowers the level".
Quick check
?In a person whose pancreas does not release insulin, which prediction about blood glucose concentration after a meal containing carbohydrate is best supported?
Mission 2

Positive feedback

  • Positive feedback: the response increases the original change.
  • The loop runs until an end point removes the stimulus.
  • It cannot hold a set point, so it is not used to keep variables steady. That is why homeostasis uses negative feedback.
  • Childbirth: the baby stretches the cervix → oxytocin → stronger uterine contractions → more stretching … until birth.
  • Lactation: suckling → oxytocin → milk release → more suckling, while suckling continues.
  • Fruit ripening: ethylene triggers ripening; ripening fruit makes more ethylene.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "positive feedback is the helpful kind".
Quick check
?When an infant suckles, nerve signals from the mother's nipple cause her to release the hormone oxytocin, which causes milk to be released from the mammary glands. The flow of milk stimulates the infant to keep suckling. Which statement best describes the release of milk in this feedback loop?
Mission check

?In liver cells of a hypothetical mammal, an amino acid is made from a starting molecule by a pathway of four enzyme-catalyzed steps. The amino acid, the end product, binds to an allosteric site on the enzyme that catalyzes the first step and inhibits it. Which statement best explains how this keeps the concentration of the amino acid near a steady level?

The level rises, the response pushes it back down, and it falls, so it wobbles near a steady value: negative feedback at the molecular level.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Make a guess: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Mission 3

One cell becomes two

  • A parent cell (often called the mother cell) divides into two daughter cells.
  • The parent does not survive as a third cell: its contents are shared out.
  • First the nucleus divides: mitosis.
  • Then the cytoplasm divides: cytokinesis.
  • Not every cell keeps dividing: most mature nerve cells never do.
Mission 3

What mitosis is for

  • Growth: more cells.
  • Repair and replacement: cells lost by wounds or wear.
  • Asexual reproduction: the offspring are genetically identical to the parent.
  • Each daughter cell gets the same chromosome number and the same genes: genetic constancy.
  • Say: "growth needs cell division to add cells" — not: "organs grow just by their cells getting bigger".
Mission 3

Copy first: two chromatids

  • Before a cell divides, every DNA molecule is replicated.
  • This happens in S phase of interphase.
  • Each chromosome is then two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • They stay joined until anaphase.
  • One chromatid = one DNA molecule. Two joined chromatids = one chromosome.
  • Say: "replication gives each chromosome two sister chromatids" — not: "replication doubles the chromosome number".
Quick check
?A cell of an organism has 8 chromosomes before its DNA is replicated. How many chromosomes and how many DNA molecules are in its nucleus at metaphase of mitosis?
Mission 3

The cell cycle

  • Interphase (G1 → S → G2), then mitosis, then cytokinesis.
  • G1: the cell grows, makes proteins, duplicates organelles.
  • S: DNA is replicated.
  • G2: more growth and protein synthesis; the cell prepares to divide.
  • G2: ATP is produced in large quantities; centrosomes replicate.
  • Interphase takes most of the cycle. It is not a resting stage.
  • A cell can leave the cycle and enter G0, a non-dividing state. It can stay for days, weeks or years, and some cells re-enter the cycle when signaled.
Quick check
?Which statement describes a cell in G2 of the cell cycle?
Mission 3

Mitosis in four stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense (shorter, thicker, visible). The nuclear envelope breaks down. The spindle forms.
  • Centrosomes move to opposite poles.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Spindle fibers from both poles attach at each centromere.
  • Anaphase: centromeres divide. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles; the spindle fibers shorten.
  • Telophase: chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. A nuclear envelope forms round each set. The spindle breaks down.
  • Say: "sister chromatids separate" — not: "homologous chromosomes separate".
Quick check
?A body cell has 4 chromosomes. It is in anaphase of mitosis. What is moving toward each pole of the cell?
Mission 3

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
  • It usually begins during telophase.
  • Animal cell: the membrane is pulled in at the equator, forming a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two.
  • Plant cell: the rigid wall cannot be pinched. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse into a cell plate.
  • The cell plate becomes new membranes and a new wall. It grows outward until it joins the old wall.
  • Say: "spindle microtubules move chromosomes" — not: "the spindle pinches the cell in two".
Quick check
?A dividing cell is examined after its two new nuclei have formed. Many small vesicles are seen gathering and fusing in a line across the equator of the cell, midway between the nuclei. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission check

?A population of a hypothetical freshwater plant reproduces only by fragmentation: pieces break off a parent plant and grow into new plants by mitosis. Which claim about the new plants is best supported?

Copy every chromosome, then pull the copies apart: each new cell gets a full, identical set. Four million times a second, or once in a broken-off fragment.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Make a guess: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Mission 4

Read the stage from the chromosomes

  • Interphase: intact nucleus; no separate chromosomes (DNA uncoiled). Most cells look like this.
  • Cells divide out of step, so the commonest stage is the one that lasts longest.
  • Prophase: thread-like chromosomes, scattered; nuclear envelope disappearing.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes in a single line across the equator.
  • Anaphase: two groups of V-shaped chromatids moving apart.
  • Telophase: two clusters at the poles; nuclear envelopes re-forming. In plants, a cell plate between them.
  • Why visible? Condensed chromosomes are short and thick, so they take up stain densely.
  • Say: "intact nucleus, no visible chromosomes = interphase" — not: "= prophase".
Quick check
?Two cells are seen in a stained micrograph of a root tip. Cell 1 has an intact nucleus in which no separate chromosomes can be distinguished. Cell 2 has no nuclear envelope, and its condensed chromosomes lie in a single line across the middle of the cell. Which identification is correct?
Quick check
?In a stained root-tip squash, one cell shows two groups of V-shaped chromatids, their points facing opposite ends of the cell. Which explains this appearance?
Mission 4

Counting cells measures time

  • Cells in a tissue divide out of step. So the fraction of cells in a stage ≈ the fraction of the cycle spent in it.
  • Time in a stage = (cells in that stage ÷ total cells) × length of one cycle.
  • Most cells are in interphase: it is the longest stage.
  • Say: "divide by the total number of cells" — not: "by the cells not in that stage".

  • Fraction in mitosis = 60 ÷ 500 = 0.12 (12%).

  • Prophase: 30 ÷ 500 × 20 h = 1.2 h, the longest stage of mitosis here.
  • Assumes cells are out of step, and every stage was identified correctly.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Make a guess: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Mission 5

Division on demand

  • Cells divide only when signals and internal checks allow it.
  • Genes that control the cycle make proteins that push it on or hold it back.
  • If control fails, a cell divides without restraint. Its descendants pile up into a tumor.
  • Uncontrolled division can lead to cancer. Or a damaged cell can be removed by programmed death.
Mission 5

Gates in the cycle

  • A checkpoint holds the cycle until conditions for the next stage are met.
  • G1 checkpoint: is the cell large enough? Are nutrients and signals to divide present? Is the DNA undamaged?
  • G2 checkpoint: is all the DNA replicated, and undamaged?
  • Spindle checkpoint (in metaphase): is every chromosome attached to the spindle? Only then does anaphase start.
  • Problem found → the cycle stops for repair. Damage that cannot be repaired → the cell may die by apoptosis.
  • Say: "a checkpoint holds the cell until conditions are met" — not: "a checkpoint destroys any faulty cell at once".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the role of checkpoints in the cell cycle?
Mission 5

Cyclins: the timer

  • Cyclins are proteins whose concentration rises and falls in every cycle.
  • Different cyclins peak at different points in the cycle.
  • To pass a checkpoint, a specific cyclin must reach a threshold concentration.
  • After that checkpoint, the cyclin is broken down. Its level falls, so the cycle can repeat.
  • Each cyclin binds a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK). A CDK is active only while bound to its cyclin.
  • CDK concentration stays fairly constant; CDK activity rises and falls with the cyclin.
  • An active cyclin–CDK complex phosphorylates target proteins, which moves the cell past the checkpoint.
  • Say: "rising cyclin lets the cell pass the checkpoint" — not: "cyclin is a brake that must fall first".

Mission 5

When control fails

  • A mutation can disable a gene that controls the cycle.
  • A damaged cell then keeps dividing. Each division copies the damage to its descendants.
  • More mutations build up in that cell line.
  • Usually several mutations in one cell line are needed before a tumor forms.
  • With working controls, a cell with damage it cannot repair dies by apoptosis instead.
  • Say: "cancer cells divide without the normal controls" — not: "cancer is just fast division".
Quick check
?The risk of developing most cancers rises steeply with age. Which explanation fits how tumors form?
Mission 5

Apoptosis: death that shapes the body

  • Apoptosis is programmed cell death.
  • Enzymes break down the cell's contents. The cell shrinks and breaks into small membrane-bound pieces.
  • Phagocytes engulf the pieces. Neighboring cells are not harmed.
  • A disrupted cell cycle can lead to cancer, or to apoptosis of the faulty cell.
Quick check
?A cell's DNA is severely damaged, and the damage cannot be repaired. The proteins that control the cell's checkpoints function normally. Which prediction about this cell is best supported?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical mammal, protein Q halts the cell cycle at the G1 checkpoint when a cell's DNA is damaged and triggers apoptosis if the damage cannot be repaired. A mutation in a skin cell inactivates protein Q. Which statement best explains how this mutation can lead to cancer?

Every checkpoint is a brake. Lose one and a damaged cell keeps going; lose several and a tumor can start.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The environment can change epigenetic tags, so genetically identical cells or organisms can express different genes; the base sequence is unchanged.

Mission 2

  • Homeostasis keeps internal variables within limits around a set point; negative feedback reverses a change from either side, so small oscillations are normal.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point; it drives processes such as birth or ripening, not steady states.

Mission 3

  • Every chromosome is replicated first (two sister chromatids); mitosis then sends one chromatid of each chromosome to each pole, so both daughter cells get an identical, complete set. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm (cleavage furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).
  • The cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) → cytokinesis.

Mission 4

  • Stages are identified from the chromosomes: none visible (interphase), scattered (prophase), in a line (metaphase), V-shaped groups moving apart (anaphase), clusters at the poles (telophase).
  • Cells divide out of step, so the fraction of cells in a stage gives the fraction of the cycle spent in it.

Mission 5

  • Cell division is controlled by genes whose proteins push the cycle on or hold it back; when mutations disable these controls, a cell line divides without restraint and can form a tumor.
  • Checkpoints (G1, G2, spindle) hold the cycle until conditions are met; irreparable damage can trigger apoptosis.
  • A specific cyclin must reach a threshold to pass each checkpoint, then falls.
  • Cyclins activate CDKs, which phosphorylate target proteins.
