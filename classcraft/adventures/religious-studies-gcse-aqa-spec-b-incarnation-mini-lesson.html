Mission 1

Why symbols?

  • A symbol is a sign that stands for something more than itself.
  • Christian symbols pack a belief into a simple image: who Jesus is, what he did, what Christians hope for.
  • They teach (even those who cannot read), identify Christians and their buildings, and focus prayer.
  • They appear on church walls and windows, vestments, altar cloths, jewellery, gravestones and car stickers.
  • The most important Christian symbol is the cross.
  • The grid also shows the fish, the Greek letters Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega, and the dove.
Mission 1

Cross and crucifix

  • Cross: reminds Christians that Jesus died on a cross to save people from sin. "The word of the cross is… to us who are being saved… the power of God." (1 Corinthians 1:18)
  • Plain (empty) cross: Jesus is no longer on it: he is risen. Common in Protestant churches; also used by Catholics.
  • Crucifix: a cross with the figure of Christ: his suffering and sacrifice out of love. Usual in Catholic churches, and in some Anglican and Lutheran ones; Orthodox use painted crosses.
  • Uses: on or above the altar; carried in processions; worn on a chain; held by the dying; the sign of the cross made over the body in prayer.
  • "If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me." (Matthew 16:24)
Mission 1

Contrasting artefacts

  • Plain cross: stresses the resurrection: death defeated.
  • Crucifix: stresses Christ's sacrifice, made present in the Mass.
  • "Risen Christ" (Christ shown alive on or before the cross, robed and crowned, arms open): stresses victory, resurrection and Christ as King.
  • Altar: a place of sacrifice: Catholics believe the Mass makes present Christ's one sacrifice.
  • Table: a meal shared, as at the Last Supper: "you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26). Catholics teach the altar is both: altar of sacrifice and table of the Lord.
  • A church's choice shows which aspect of the one mystery it puts first, for the individual and the community.

Mission 1

The fish (Ichthus)

  • Ichthus (ΙΧΘΥΣ) is Greek for "fish".
  • Its letters begin the words Iesous Christos Theou Uios Soter: "Jesus Christ, God's Son, Saviour": a short creed.
  • Fish recall the Gospels: Jesus calls fishermen to be "fishers for men" (Mark 1:17) and feeds thousands with loaves and fish.
  • Used by early Christians in catacombs and on tombs; tradition says it was a secret sign in times of persecution.
  • Today: car badges, jewellery, logos: a public sign of faith.
  • Relevance: a whole statement of belief in one simple outline.
Mission 1

Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega

  • Chi-Rho (☧): the Greek letters Χ (ch) and Ρ (r), the first two letters of Christos (Christ, "anointed one").
  • Tradition links it with the emperor Constantine, who put Christ's sign on his soldiers' shields before winning the battle of the Milvian Bridge (AD 312).
  • Seen on vestments, altar cloths, tombs and church doors: Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah.
  • Alpha (Α) and Omega (Ω): the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End." (Revelation 22:13)
  • Meaning: Christ is eternal, there before creation and at the end of time.
  • At the Easter Vigil the priest marks Α, Ω and the year on the Paschal candle: all time belongs to the risen Christ.

Mission 1Diversity

Symbols used in different ways

  • Catholic and Orthodox: rich use: crucifixes, painted crosses, Chi-Rho and Α–Ω on vestments, the Paschal candle, mosaics and icons.
  • Anglican and Lutheran: wide range: from crucifixes and vestments to plain crosses.
  • Many Protestant and evangelical churches: plain cross, simple communion table; symbols kept few so that the Word is central.
  • Quakers: no symbols in meeting houses.
  • Other settings: jewellery, car badges, school logos, gravestones, flags; some Christians worry a cross worn as fashion loses its meaning.
Mission check

?A hospital prayer room is shared by Christians of every denomination. The chaplains choose one plain wooden cross with no figure on it. Which is the most likely reason?

Every Christian symbol is a compressed belief: the cross, plain or with Christ's figure, says the same thing with a different emphasis.

Mission 2

Why Catholics make statues

  • Catholic churches and homes have statues: of Jesus, Mary, the saints and angels.
  • The Incarnation: God became human in Jesus, so God has been seen, touched and heard. Catholics believe this makes images of Christ possible.
  • A statue is three-dimensional: it stands in the same space as the believer, as if present.
  • Purposes: to remember, to teach, to inspire, and to focus prayer.
  • So the Incarnation shapes Catholic art: images are welcomed, because the invisible God became visible in Christ.

The Word became flesh

"The Word became flesh and lived amongst us. We saw his glory" (John 1:14)

"He is the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation." (Colossians 1:15)

Q1 According to John 1:14, what could people now do that they could not do before?

Show answer

See God's glory in a human life.

Q2 How do Catholics use Colossians 1:15 to defend statues of Jesus?

Show answer

If Christ is the visible image of the invisible God, making an image of Christ shows what God has already shown.

Mission 2

Veneration, not worship

  • Catholics worship God alone (adoration).
  • They venerate (honour) Mary and the saints: a statue is a reminder, not a god.
  • The Second Council of Nicaea (787) and the Catechism (CCC 2132) teach that honour given to an image passes to the person it shows.
  • So lighting a candle before a statue of St Anthony is asking him to pray with you, not praying to stone.

Mission 2

Statues in a Catholic church

  • Crucifix: Christ on the cross, above or near the altar: his sacrifice, renewed in the Mass.
  • Mary: often a side altar or shrine, with candles and flowers.
  • Saints, especially the church's patron; Sacred Heart of Jesus: his love for all people.
  • Stations of the Cross: 14 carved or painted scenes around the walls.
  • Christmas crib: figures of the Nativity scene.
  • How they are used: kneeling, lighting candles, prayer, processions (e.g. crowning a statue of Mary in May), funerals and feasts.
Mission 2

Michelangelo's Pietà

  • Pietà (Italian: "pity", "compassion"); Michelangelo, carved 1498–1499 from one block of Carrara marble, aged about 24.
  • Now in St Peter's Basilica, Rome; the only work he signed (on the sash across Mary's chest).
  • Mary holds the dead body of Jesus across her lap after the crucifixion.
  • Details: a pyramid shape: stable and calm; Mary looks young and serene; her eyes are lowered; her left hand is open, as if offering or accepting; Christ's wounds are small; his body is at peace.
  • The scene is not described in the Gospels: it is a devotional image of Mary's sorrow.
Mission 2

The Pietà: God's goodness and human suffering

  • Simeon told Mary: "a sword will pierce through your own soul" (Luke 2:35). The Pietà shows that sword.
  • Suffering is real: a mother holds her dead son. Catholics do not pretend suffering is good.
  • Suffering with meaning: Catholics believe Christ's death was a gift of love for the world; Mary's open hand offers him back to God.
  • God's goodness is not cancelled by suffering: the calm faces point to trust and to the resurrection to come.
  • Catholics say Christ shares human suffering, and believers can unite their sufferings with his (Pope John Paul II, Salvifici Doloris, 1984, paraphrase).
  • So the Pietà comforts the grieving: God is present in sorrow, and death is not the end.

Mission 2

Christ the Redeemer: a statue in the open

  • A 30-metre statue of Jesus above Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, completed 1931 (Catholic; designed by Paul Landowski).
  • His arms are stretched wide: welcome for everyone, and the shape of the cross.
  • It shows the risen Christ, not the dying one: Christ as Redeemer, saviour of the city and the world.
  • Used for: Masses and blessings at its base; pilgrimage; a national symbol, seen by tourists of every faith and none.
  • Shows how a statue can express belief (the artist) and be received in many ways (the observers).
Mission 2Diversity

Different Christian views on statues

  • Catholic: statues welcomed; veneration, not worship (CCC 2132).
  • Anglican: varied. The Thirty-Nine Articles (1571) condemned the "Worshipping and Adoration, as well of Images as of Reliques" (Article XXII). Since the 19th century many Anglo-Catholic churches have statues of Mary and the saints (e.g. the Anglican shrine at Walsingham); evangelical Anglican churches are usually plain, with an empty cross.
  • Orthodox: honour icons, but rarely use free-standing statues.
  • Reformed (Calvinist) and many other Protestants: no images of Jesus in worship. The Westminster Larger Catechism (1648) lists "the making any representation of God, of all or of any of the three persons" among sins against the second commandment (Exodus 20:4).
  • Many Protestants still accept nativity scenes, statues in museums, or art for teaching outside worship.
Quick check
?Which statement about statues in Anglican churches is most accurate?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Statues of Jesus help faith more than they put it at risk."

  • Reasons for: the Incarnation made God visible (John 1:14; Colossians 1:15); statues teach, comfort and focus prayer; the Pietà shows how art can give hope in suffering; honour passes to the person shown (Nicaea II).
  • Reasons against: the commandment against images (Exodus 20:4–5; part of the first commandment for Catholics, the second for most Protestants); a statue can become an object of superstition; no one knows what Jesus looked like, so a statue may fix a false image; money spent on art could serve the poor.
  • Judge it by: Does the Incarnation change what that commandment forbids? Is the risk in the statue or in how people use it? Do statues help everyone, or only those taught how to use them?

For Catholics a statue is a reminder that God became visible in Christ: it is honoured for the person it shows, and worship is kept for God alone.

Mission 3

What "Incarnation" means

  • Incarnation comes from Latin in carne: "in flesh".
  • Christians believe God the Son, the second Person of the Trinity, became a human being: Jesus of Nazareth.
  • Jesus is fully God and fully human: not half and half, not God in disguise, not a man who became God.
  • One person, two natures (divine and human): agreed at the Council of Chalcedon (451).
  • The Nicene Creed: he "was incarnate by the Holy Ghost of the Virgin Mary, and was made man".

Mission 3

The divine Word

  • Word translates the Greek Logos: reason, message, God's self-expression.
  • In Genesis 1, God creates by speaking ("God said…"). John says that Word is a Person, present at creation.
  • So Jesus is not a messenger who brings God's word: he is God's Word in human life.
  • Christians believe that to see what God is like, they look at Jesus.

The annunciation (Luke 1)

  • The angel Gabriel greets Mary: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28)
  • "He will be great and will be called the Son of the Most High. The Lord God will give him the throne of his father David." (Luke 1:32)
  • Mary asks how, as a virgin. "The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you. Therefore also the holy one who is born from you will be called the Son of God." (Luke 1:35)
  • Matthew tells it from Joseph's side: the child is "Immanuel", "God with us" (Matthew 1:23).

Q1 Which two titles does Gabriel give the child?

Show answer

Son of the Most High / Son of God; heir to David's throne.

Q2 What does "Immanuel" tell Christians about Jesus?

Show answer

God himself is present with his people.

Mission 3

The virgin birth

  • Christians believe Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit, without a human father, and born of the Virgin Mary (Apostles' Creed).
  • It shows Jesus is from God (divine) and born of a woman (human).
  • Matthew and Luke both tell it; Christians see it as God's initiative, not human achievement.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians honour Mary as Theotokos, "God-bearer" (Council of Ephesus, 431).
  • Most Christians accept the virgin birth as a historical event; some liberal Protestants read it as a symbol of Jesus' unique link with God.

Mission 3Diversity

Why it matters, and how Christians differ

  • For all mainstream Christians: God is not distant; Jesus shows what God is like; God understands human life from the inside.
  • Catholic: Mary as Mother of God; the Angelus prayer recalls the Incarnation three times a day; Christmas Midnight Mass.
  • Orthodox: God became human so humans can share God's life (theosis). Athanasius: "He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54).
  • Protestant: often stress the cross more; the Incarnation makes the saving death possible.
  • Impact: worship at Christmas; respect for human bodies and lives; serving the poor, in whom Jesus is met (Matthew 25:40).
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Incarnation is the most important Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: without it, Jesus' death is the death of a good man, not God's saving act. It shows God's love (John 3:16). Only God become human can reveal God fully (John 1:18).
  • Reasons against: Paul says faith depends on the resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:14). Many Protestants see the cross as the centre. The Trinity is the foundation all else rests on.
  • Another view: the beliefs depend on each other: Incarnation, cross and resurrection form one story.
  • Judge it by: Which belief do the others depend on? What do Christians celebrate most (Christmas or Easter)? Which makes most difference to daily life?
Mission 3Worked answer

Influence on Christians today

Question: Explain two ways belief in the Incarnation influences Christians today.

  • Way 1: Christians celebrate it at Christmas. Development: worship centres on God coming to Earth as a baby, "the Word became flesh" (John 1:14), so many attend Midnight Mass or carol services.
  • Way 2: Christians follow Jesus' example. Development: if God showed himself in a human life, then Jesus' way of loving and serving is how God wants people to live.
  • 4-mark tip: two ways, each one developed.

Mission 4

Incarnate Son, divine Word

  • Incarnation (Latin in carne, "in flesh"): Catholics believe God the Son became a human being in Jesus, without ceasing to be God.
  • Fully God and fully human: one Person with two natures, divine and human (Council of Chalcedon, 451).
  • Incarnate Son: Jesus is the eternal Son of the Father, conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of Mary.
  • Divine Word: the Logos, God's self-expression, through whom all things were made, "became flesh" (John 1:14).
  • Significance: God is not distant; God can be seen, heard and touched in Jesus.
  • Because God became visible in Jesus, Catholics make and honour images of him, such as crucifixes and statues (veneration, not worship).
  • Because the Son truly suffered as a human, Catholics believe God knows suffering from the inside.

Mission 4

Son of Man, Son of God (Mark)

  • "He began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again." (Mark 8:31)
  • Son of Man: Jesus' usual name for himself. Here it stresses his humanity: he will suffer, be rejected and die. It also echoes the glorious figure "like a son of man" in Daniel 7:13.
  • At his trial: "'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?' Jesus said, 'I am. You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky.'" (Mark 14:61–62)
  • Son of God: Catholics read this as his divine identity. The high priest hears blasphemy.
  • Together: Mark shows Jesus as fully human and fully God.

Mission 4

Irenaeus: "fully alive"

  • St Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–c. 202), bishop; Against Heresies (Adversus Haereses, c. 180), Book 4, 20.7:
  • "For the glory of God is a living man; and the life of man consists in beholding God." (1885 translation; often given as "the glory of God is a human being fully alive")
  • Context: he opposed Gnostics, who taught that the body and the material world are bad.
  • The Incarnation proves the opposite: God took a body, so human bodies and human life matter.
  • Fully alive = flourishing in body, mind and spirit, and above all in relationship with God ("beholding God").
  • Significance: holiness is not escape from being human but becoming fully human; Catholics value education, health care and justice as part of God's glory.
Mission 4

Dei Verbum 4 and Verbum Domini 12

  • Dei Verbum ("The Word of God"), Second Vatican Council, 1965, §4 (paraphrase):
  • God spoke through the prophets, then through his Son (Hebrews 1:1–2). Jesus, the Word made flesh, completes revelation by his whole life: words and deeds, signs, above all his death, resurrection and the sending of the Spirit.
  • No new public revelation is expected before Christ returns in glory.
  • Verbum Domini ("The Word of the Lord"), Benedict XVI, 2010, §12 (paraphrase):
  • The Word is not only heard: "now the word has a face", Jesus of Nazareth. The eternal Word made himself small enough to lie in a manger.
  • Similar: both see Jesus as the fullness of God's revelation; both build on John 1.
  • Different: DV stresses that revelation is complete in Christ's words and deeds; VD stresses the Word as a person to meet: visible, humble, close.

Mission 4

The Incarnation and images

  • The Old Testament forbids images of God (Exodus 20:4): no one has seen God.
  • But in Jesus the invisible God became visible: "He is the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15).
  • St John of Damascus (c. 675–749), On the Divine Images: he does not worship matter, but the Creator who became matter for our sake (paraphrase).
  • Second Council of Nicaea (787): images of Christ, Mary and the saints may be venerated (honoured); the honour passes to the person shown. Worship is for God alone.
  • Catholic practice: crucifixes, statues, Stations of the Cross, stained glass.
  • Other views: the Orthodox prefer flat icons to statues; many Reformed Protestants keep churches plain, fearing idolatry.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Because of the Incarnation, Christians should use images of Jesus in worship."

  • Reasons for: God made himself visible in Jesus (Colossians 1:15); Nicaea II (787) approved veneration; images teach and help prayer; Catholics and Orthodox have used them for centuries.
  • Reasons against: Exodus 20:4–5 forbids images; risk of treating the object as holy in itself; "God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth" (John 4:24); images can mislead (Jesus is often shown as European).
  • Another view: symbols (cross, fish, Chi-Rho) honour Christ without a picture of his face.
  • Judge it by: Is veneration different from worship? Does the image help or distract from prayer? Does it fit scripture?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The cross is the central Christian symbol: plain (risen Christ) or crucifix (sacrifice); altar, table and different crosses emphasise different aspects of belief.
  • Chi-Rho = Christ; Alpha–Omega = Christ eternal.

Mission 2

  • Catholics use statues because of the Incarnation (God made visible in Christ): images remind, teach and focus prayer; they are venerated, not worshipped.
  • Sources for the Incarnation argument: John 1:14; Colossians 1:15.
  • Michelangelo's Pietà (1498–99, St Peter's) shows Mary holding the dead Christ: real suffering, trust in God's goodness, hope of resurrection.
  • Views differ: Catholic welcome; Anglican range; Orthodox prefer icons; many Protestants reject images of Jesus in worship.

Mission 3

  • Christians believe in the Incarnation: God the Son became human in Jesus, fully God and fully human (John 1:14).
  • Jesus is the divine Word (Logos), through whom all things were made.
  • He was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary (Luke 1:35).

Mission 4

  • Jesus is the incarnate Son and divine Word: fully God and fully human, one Person (John 1).
  • Irenaeus: God's glory is a human being fully alive; DV 4 and VD 12 see Christ as the fullness of revelation; images of Christ are allowed because God became visible.
