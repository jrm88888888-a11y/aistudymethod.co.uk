Coursework

This mini-lesson is a skills workshop: it shows you exactly how to plan and write a top-band coursework essay for Edexcel International GCSE English Literature (4ET1) — from choosing a focused title to redrafting against the mark scheme.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (choose the strongest thesis, fix a weak paragraph, pick the best-embedded quote) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The component

What the coursework is

Coursework is Component 3 (4ET1/03) — an alternative to the exam Component 2, worth 40% of the qualification. It is set and marked by your teacher and then moderated by Pearson.

  • Two assignments, 30 marks each (60 total): A — a modern drama text; B — a literary heritage text.
  • Each is one essay responding to a teacher-devised task on a text you have studied.
  • You have real freedom: within the approved texts, you and your teacher shape a task that plays to your ideas.

Marks split: each essay is marked 15 for AO1 and 15 for AO2. So argument + evidence and analysis of methods matter equally. (Always confirm set texts, word-length and deadlines with your teacher and the current spec.)

Step 1 · the title

Choose a focused, arguable title

A strong coursework title is narrow enough to explore in depth and arguable — it invites a view, not a summary.

  • Name a method or idea: how a writer presents a character, uses a setting, or develops a theme.
  • Use verbs that demand analysis: "explore how…", "how does the writer present…".
  • Keep it tight: one character, one relationship or one theme beats "everything in the play".
Weak vs focused

❌ "Write about the main character." — too broad, invites retelling.

✅ "Explore how the writer presents the change in the protagonist's power across the play." — focused and arguable.

Step 2 · the argument

Build a thesis (your line of argument)

A thesis is a clear, arguable answer to your title that your whole essay defends. It turns an essay from a list of points into a sustained argument — the heart of a top AO1 mark.

Thesis = a claim + how the writer achieves ita debatable answer to the title, previewing the methods you'll analyse
Generic model thesis

"The writer presents the protagonist's power as fragile rather than absolute: through shifting dialogue, stage directions and structural reversals, apparent authority is repeatedly undercut, inviting the audience to question it."

Test your thesis: could a sensible reader disagree? If not, it's a summary, not an argument.

Step 3 · the plan

Map the essay before you write

Plan a clear structure: an intro that states your thesis, three or four analytical paragraphs each led by a topic sentence, and a conclusion that pulls the argument together.

Step 4 · the paragraph

Write PEA / PEEL paragraphs

Each analytical paragraph follows a shape that keeps you analysing, not retelling:

Golden rule: spend most of the paragraph on analysis. Name the method (word choice, imagery, structure, stage direction) and explain its effect.

Model paragraph

A PEA paragraph in action

Here is a short, generic model (invented text, no copyrighted quotation) showing the shape:

Model (methods analysed, not plot retold)

P: The writer first presents the character's power as performance rather than substance.

E: When another says the line "you decide, of course", the deference is only surface.

A: The adverb "of course" flatters, yet the caesura and hesitant stage direction that follow expose the authority as staged — the audience senses control that must be constantly reasserted, which unsettles our trust in it.

L: This fragility set up early is repaid by the structural reversal in the final act, confirming the thesis.

Notice: the quotation is short and embedded in the sentence, and the analysis names methods (adverb, caesura, structure) and their effect on the audience.

Step 5 · evidence

Embed quotations, keep them short

Top essays weave brief quotations into their own sentences and then zoom in on a single word or device — not drop a long chunk and move on.

  • Short — a phrase or a word beats a whole speech.
  • Embedded — the quotation flows inside your grammar.
  • Analysed — every quote is followed by "the effect of this is…".
Embedded vs dropped

❌ The writer says: "the pale, cold light spread over the room." This shows it is sad.

✅ The writer's image of a "cold light" drains warmth from the scene, its adjective foreshadowing the emotional distance to come.

Step 6 · AO2

Analyse methods, not just plot

AO2 is the difference between a middle and a top essay. Always ask: what did the writer choose to do, and why does it work on the reader?

  • Language — word choice, imagery, semantic fields, sound.
  • Form — genre features: dramatic devices, verse vs prose, dialogue.
  • Structure — openings, shifts, repetition, endings, foreshadowing.

Misconception: naming a device ("this is a metaphor") is not analysis. Analysis explains the effect: the metaphor makes the reader feel…. Feature-spotting stays low-band.

Strong or weak analysis?

Step 7 · context

Weave context, don't bolt it on

Context (ideas, values or conventions of the writer's time) belongs inside your analysis, lifting AO1. A whole paragraph of history lowers your mark.

Bolted-on vs woven

❌ "The play was written in a certain period. Society was different then. Anyway, back to the character…"

✅ "By making obedience a virtue, the writer taps a contemporary anxiety about authority — so the character's quiet defiance would have felt genuinely dangerous to the first audience."

One sentence of woven context that sharpens your argument beats a page of background.

Step 8 · conclusion

A conclusion that resolves

A strong conclusion does not just repeat the intro. It resolves the argument and shows how the parts add up.

  • Return to your thesis and confirm how the analysis has proved it.
  • Draw the methods together — what overall effect has the writer built?
  • End on your informed personal view — this is prime AO1 territory.

Avoid: "In conclusion, as I said…". Say something your reader now understands because of your argument.

Step 9 · redraft

Redraft against the mark scheme

Coursework's superpower is the redraft. Mark your own first draft against the criteria and lift it band by band before you submit.

  • Does every paragraph advance the thesis? (AO1)
  • Have I analysed a method + its effect for each quote? (AO2)
  • Are quotations short, embedded and accurate?
  • Is context woven, not bolted on?
  • Have I cut retelling and vague words ("nice", "interesting")?

Misconception: "the first draft is basically final." Redrafting is where AO2 marks are won — treat feedback as a checklist, not criticism.

Step 10 · integrity

Quote accurately, avoid plagiarism

Coursework must be your own work. Moderators expect honest referencing:

  • Quote accurately — copy wording exactly and use quotation marks.
  • Never copy from the internet, a guide or a friend and pass it off as yours.
  • If you draw on a critic's idea, acknowledge it and make the point your own.
  • Your teacher authenticates that the essay is genuinely yours before it is moderated.

Plagiarism can void your marks. The safest route is simple: your own argument, your own words, short accurate quotations from the text.

Recap

The coursework checklist

Title: focused & arguable — "explore how the writer presents…"

Thesis: one debatable claim your whole essay defends (AO1)

Structure: intro → topic-sentence PEA paragraphs → resolving conclusion

PEA: Point · Evidence (short, embedded) · Analysis of method + effect (AO2)

Context: woven into the argument, never bolted on

Redraft: mark against AO1 & AO2; cut retelling

Integrity: accurate quotes, your own words, no plagiarism
