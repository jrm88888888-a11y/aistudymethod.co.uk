Paper 2: Modern Drama & Literary Heritage

This is a skills mini-lesson. Your set texts vary by school, so we teach the exam technique that works for any play or heritage text: how to analyse dramatic methods, handle older language, embed quotations, weave context, and plan a timed essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (MCQs and short written checks) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The paper at a glance

How Paper 2 is built

Paper 2 (code 4ET1/02) lasts 1 hour 30 minutes and is worth 60 marks — 40% of the whole IGCSE. You answer two essays: one from each section.

  • Section A — Modern Drama (30 marks): open book. You may take a clean copy of your play in. Usually an extract is printed and you write an essay linking it to the whole play.
  • Section B — Literary Heritage Texts (30 marks): closed book. No text allowed — you must have quotations memorised. A whole-text essay on a Shakespeare play or a 19th-century novel.

Split the time: roughly 45 minutes each. Both sections carry equal marks, so never let one essay eat the other's time.

Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark maps to an Assessment Objective (AO). Know which ones apply so you spend your words where they score:

Key move: in Section A, keep asking "what method, what effect?" In Section B, keep asking "how does the context shape this moment?"

Analysing drama

Drama is built from methods

A play is written to be performed. So don't just retell the story — analyse the tools the playwright uses to shape an audience's response:

  • Stage directions — lighting, movement, silence. They tell actors and readers how a moment should feel.
  • Dramatic irony — the audience knows something a character doesn't, building unease or humour.
  • Dialogue — interruptions, pauses, questions and repetition reveal power and emotion.
  • Structure — where an act ends, a climax lands, or a secret is exposed.
Method → effect

Naming the effect

Analysis is method + effect, not just spotting the method. Compare these two generic responses to the same moment in a modern play:

Weak (plot-telling): "The character doesn't know the truth, but we do, so it's dramatic irony."
Stronger (method + effect)

"By allowing the audience to know more than the character on stage, the playwright creates dramatic irony that makes us dread the confession to come. The gap between what is said and what we know forces us to watch the character's confidence as painfully misplaced — sharpening the tension of the whole scene."

The upgrade: the second version names the method, then explains the effect on the audience — that is what AO2 rewards.

Paragraph shape

Build a P.E.A. paragraph

A reliable analytical paragraph makes a Point, gives Evidence, then Analyses the method and effect. In Section B (closed book) your evidence must be from memory.

Embedding a quote (generic example)

Clunky: The character is angry. The quote is: "how dare you". This shows anger.

Embedded: The character's clipped demand — "how dare you" — turns the exchange into an interrogation, and the brevity signals barely-controlled fury.

Rule of thumb: keep quotations short (a word or phrase) and fold them inside your own sentence. A whole memorised speech copied out earns nothing on its own.

Section A technique

Extract → whole-text essay

Many Section A questions print an extract and ask you to analyse it and link it to the rest of the play. The best answers zoom in on the extract, then zoom out to the whole text.

Balance it: don't spend the whole essay glued to the extract, and don't ignore the extract to write everything you know. The question wants both, connected by one clear argument.

Analysing heritage texts

Older texts, older worlds

Literary heritage texts (a Shakespeare play, or a 19th-century novel) were written for audiences with very different beliefs. To analyse them well you read the language and the world behind it:

  • Older language — unfamiliar words, inverted word order, imagery. Slow down and work out the literal meaning first, then the effect.
  • Form & conventions — a Shakespeare play uses soliloquy, blank verse and dramatic convention; a Victorian novel uses a narrator, chapters and description.
  • Context (AO4) — Jacobean beliefs about kingship and fate; Victorian attitudes to class, money, women or reputation.
Handling older language (generic)

If a line inverts the word order — "never a man so true" for "a man never so true" — note that the inversion foregrounds a word for emphasis, which is itself a method to analyse.

Weaving context (AO4)

Context serves the analysis

AO4 rewards context — but only when it illuminates the text. Bolted-on history dumps score little. The trick is to make context do work inside your analysis.

Weak (bolted on): "In Victorian times, women had few rights. Anyway, in this chapter…" — the fact just sits there.
Stronger (context woven in)

"Because a woman's reputation was, in this period, treated as her whole worth, the writer makes the character's public shaming feel catastrophic — the language of ruin is not exaggeration to its first readers but social reality."

The upgrade: the context (reputation = worth) explains why the writer's choices land as they do. Context should always answer "so what for the meaning?"

Exam craft

Planning the timed essay

With ~45 minutes per essay, a 2–3 minute plan pays for itself. It stops you drifting into plot-retelling and keeps every paragraph pointed at the question.

Answer the question, not the topic. If the question says "how does the writer present X", every paragraph must analyse a method — not just tell the story of X.

Your turn

Turn a topic into an argument

2Question: "How does the writer present power?" Write ONE topic-sentence that states an argument about power and signals a method (use the word "power").
Hint: don't just say "there is power in the play" — make a claim about HOW power works, and hint at the method you'll analyse.
Common traps

Three traps to avoid

  • Retelling the plot. Examiners already know the story. Marks come from analysing methods and effects, not narrating events.
  • Context as decoration. A history paragraph that never touches the text wastes words. Context must explain why a choice creates meaning.
  • Long quotations. A copied-out speech isn't analysis. Use short, embedded quotations and zoom into a word or phrase.

Section B reminder: it's closed book. Memorise a bank of short, flexible quotations you can deploy for several possible themes.

Exam-day checklist

Before you hand it in

  • ✅ Did I answer both sections and split the time evenly?
  • ✅ Does every paragraph analyse a method + effect (AO2)?
  • ✅ Are my quotations short and embedded (AO1)?
  • ✅ In Section B, does context explain meaning, not just sit there (AO4)?
  • ✅ Do my intro and conclusion both point straight at the question?

Written accuracy matters too: clear, controlled writing helps the examiner follow your argument — leave a minute to check spelling and punctuation.

Recap

The skills to carry in

Structure: Sec A Modern Drama (open, AO1+AO2) · Sec B Heritage (closed, AO1+AO4) · 45 min each

Drama: analyse stage directions, dramatic irony, dialogue, structure, tension, staging

Heritage: decode older language, use form & conventions, weave context (AO4)

Paragraphs: P.E.A. — Point, embedded Evidence, Analysis of method + effect

Section A: zoom into the extract, then out to the whole play

Golden rule: analyse methods, not plot; short embedded quotes; context serves meaning
