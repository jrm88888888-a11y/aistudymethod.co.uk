Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a Sikh place of worship have a kitchen, a throne and a flag you can see from far away?

Make a guess: Why does a Sikh place of worship have a kitchen, a throne and a flag you can see from far away?

Mission 1

The door of the Guru

  • Gurdwara means "door (or gateway) of the Guru".
  • Any building where the Guru Granth Sahib is installed with honour is a gurdwara.
  • Guru Nanak's community at Kartarpur met in a dharamsal (place of faith); later such places came to be called gurdwaras.
  • Three purposes: to house the Guru; to gather the sangat (community); to serve anyone who comes.
  • Through this "door", Sikhs believe they come into the Guru's presence and closer to God.
Mission 1

Outside: flag and doors

  • Nishan Sahib: a tall flagpole wrapped in cloth, with a triangular saffron (or blue) flag bearing the Khanda symbol.
  • It tells travellers: here is a gurdwara, with shelter, food and welcome for all.
  • The flag and its cloth are renewed every year at Vaisakhi.
  • Four doors: the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar has doors on all four sides.
  • Meaning: people from every direction, of every caste and every faith, are welcome.
  • Most local gurdwaras keep the idea by opening their doors to all, even if the building has one entrance.
  • On entering: shoes off, head covered, hands washed; no tobacco or alcohol.
Mission 1

The prayer hall

  • The main hall is the diwan hall (court); the Guru Granth Sahib is its sovereign.
  • Takht: the raised throne on which the Guru Granth Sahib rests, higher than everyone in the room.
  • Palki: the ornate canopied structure, like a royal carriage, around the takht.
  • Manji sahib: the small stand on which the scripture lies, with cushions and cloths (rumalas).
  • Chanani: the canopy above it, a sign of honour.
  • Chaur: the whisk waved over the scripture by the reader or a sewadar, as over a monarch.
  • Everyone sits on the floor at the same level: no seats of honour. Often men and women sit on different sides, by custom.
  • No statues and no pictures to bow to: Sikhs bow (matha tekna) only to the Guru Granth Sahib, and may leave an offering.
Mission 1

Sach khand and the langar hall

  • Sach khand ("realm of truth"): the room where the Guru Granth Sahib is laid to rest each night (sukhasan) and brought out at dawn (prakash).
  • Its name links to the highest stage of the soul's journey to God.
  • Langar hall: a kitchen and dining hall where a free meal is served to anyone.
  • Volunteers cook, serve and wash up. People sit in rows on the floor (pangat), side by side.

Mission 1

What happens there: the gurdwara's role

  • Worship: morning and evening prayers; kirtan (hymns sung with harmonium and tabla); katha (talks); Ardas (standing prayer); the day's hukamnama.
  • Karah prashad: a sweet offering (flour, sugar, ghee) given to everyone equally at the end.
  • Ceremonies: naming, Amrit initiation, weddings (Anand Karaj), funeral prayers, Akhand Paths.
  • Learning: Punjabi and Gurmukhi classes, kirtan lessons, Sikh history, gatka (martial art).
  • Community: langar every day, help for elderly and new arrivals, food banks, shelter in emergencies.
  • In Britain many gurdwaras hold the main gathering on Sunday, when most people are free; daily prayers continue all week.
  • Role: the gurdwara is where Sikhs meet the Guru and the sangat, and practise sewa.

Mission 1Diversity

How important is the gurdwara today?

  • Essential: the Guru Granth Sahib is present there, and Sikhs need the sangat to grow spiritually. Many visit daily.
  • Helpful, not essential: God is everywhere; nam japna and Nitnem happen at home and at work. Some Sikhs visit mainly on Sundays and festivals.
  • Keep features as they are: e.g. langar eaten sitting on the floor, as a sign of equality; services in Punjabi.
  • Adapt features: e.g. chairs for elderly or disabled people; English translations on screens for young Sikhs and visitors.
  • Sikhs disagree on how far adaptation should go; all keep the Guru Granth Sahib at the centre.

Mission 2

What nam japna means

  • Nam: God's Name, meaning God's nature and presence in everything, not just a word.
  • Japna: to repeat, recite and meditate on.
  • Nam japna (meditating on God's Name) is the first of the three things Guru Nanak taught every Sikh to do:
  • Nam japna (remember God), kirat karni (earn an honest living), vand chhakna (share with others).
  • Your spec writes Nam Japna; it is also called nam simran (remembering the Name).
Mission 2

Ways of nam japna

  • Repeating "Waheguru" ("Wonderful Lord"), the name most Sikhs use for God, silently or aloud, often in rhythm with the breath.
  • Some Sikhs use a mala (string of prayer beads) to keep focus; many do not.
  • Reciting and singing Gurbani: the hymns of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Amrit vela: the early hours before dawn, which Sikhs see as the best time.
  • In the gurdwara: the sangat sings kirtan and chants "Waheguru" together in simran sessions.
  • In daily life: remembering God while working, travelling or cooking, not only at set times.
Mission 2

Why it matters

  • Sikhs believe the mind is pulled away from God by haumai (ego) and the five thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride).
  • Nam japna turns the mind back to God, making a person gurmukh (God-centred) rather than manmukh (self-centred).
  • It brings inner peace and the courage to face hardship (chardi kala, rising spirits).
  • Sikhs believe it prepares the soul for mukti (liberation), which comes by God's grace (nadar).
  • It is not magic: the aim is a changed heart, shown in honest work and sharing.

Mission 2Read the source

Guru Granth Sahib 305

Guru Granth Sahib 305 (Guru Ram Das, paraphrase): one who calls himself a Sikh of the True Guru should rise early and meditate on the Lord's Name. He should make the effort in the early hours, bathe in the pool of nectar, and repeat the Name as the Guru teaches. Through the day he sings the Guru's hymns, and remembers God with every breath and every bite of food.

Mission 2

Prayer at home: Nitnem

  • Nitnem: the daily set prayers, read from a gutka (prayer book), after bathing, with head covered.
  • Morning (amrit vela): Japji Sahib (Guru Nanak), Jaap Sahib and Tav-Prasad Savaiye (Guru Gobind Singh). Many add Chaupai Sahib and Anand Sahib.
  • Evening: Rehras Sahib.
  • Night, before sleep: Kirtan Sohila.
  • Some families also gather to read, sing kirtan, or take a hukamnama if the Guru Granth Sahib is kept at home.
  • Role: it shapes the day around God, from waking to sleeping, and teaches children the prayers.
Mission 2

Different prayers, different purposes

  • Nitnem: praise and remembrance at fixed times every day.
  • Ardas: a standing prayer of petition and thanks, said at the end of worship and before or after any important task (a journey, an exam, a meal).
  • Simran: personal, silent remembrance of God's Name at any time.
  • Optional banis: e.g. Sukhmani Sahib (Guru Arjan), read for peace of mind.
  • Why have different prayers? Each meets a need: to praise, to ask, to thank, to stay calm, to remember God through the whole day.
Mission 2

Kirtan and Ardas in the gurdwara

  • Kirtan: singing Gurbani to music (harmonium, tabla), led by ragis. Guru Nanak sang his hymns with Bhai Mardana.
  • Sikhs believe singing and hearing Gurbani is itself nam japna: the mind is absorbed in the Guru's word.
  • Ardas: the sangat stands facing the Guru Granth Sahib, hands joined.
  • It remembers God, the Ten Gurus, the Guru Granth Sahib, and Sikhs who gave their lives for their faith.
  • It ends with a prayer for all: "by your will, may all be blessed" (sarbat da bhala).
  • The day's hukamnama follows, and karah prashad is shared.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Nam japna is the most important Sikh practice."

  • Reasons for: it is the first of Guru Nanak's three teachings; GGS 305 makes remembering God the mark of a true Sikh; it overcomes haumai and leads towards mukti.
  • Reasons against: Guru Nanak taught three duties together: nam japna without honest work and sharing would be empty. Sewa and langar express faith in action. For Khalsa Sikhs, keeping Amrit and the Five Ks is also central.
  • Another view: the practices are one: honest work and sewa done while remembering God are nam japna in daily life.
  • Judge it by: Can inner devotion and outward action be separated? What did the Gurus say makes someone a Sikh?
Mission check

?A Sikh nurse cannot get to the gurdwara during a run of night shifts. Between patients she silently repeats "Waheguru", and reads Kirtan Sohila on her break before sleeping. Which Sikh teaching does her practice show best?

Nam japna is not confined to a building or an hour: Sikhs aim to remember God with every breath.

Mission 3

Welcoming a baby

  • Sikhs see a child as a gift from God, born by God's will (hukam).
  • In many families, soon after birth, the Mool Mantar (the opening words of the Guru Granth Sahib) or "Waheguru" (Wonderful Lord) is whispered in the baby's ear: the first words the child hears are about God.
  • Some families give a drop of sweetened water (amrit), stirred with a kirpan, or of honey.
  • The family visits the gurdwara for the naming once the mother is well. There is no fixed day.
  • The Sikh code rejects the idea that birth makes a home "impure" for a set number of days.
Mission 3

Naam Karan, step by step

  • Naam Karan: "naming ceremony", in the gurdwara with the sangat (congregation).
  • The family brings karah prashad (sweet blessed food) and often a rumala (cloth) to cover the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Hymns of joy and thanks are sung.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib is opened at random and a hymn is read: the hukamnama, the Guru's "order" or guidance for that moment.
  • The first letter of the first word of the hymn is announced. A name is chosen beginning with that letter.
  • The name is announced and the sangat agrees, often with the shout "Jo bole so nihal, Sat Sri Akal".
  • Singh is added for a boy, Kaur for a girl.
  • Closing: six verses of Anand Sahib, Ardas (prayer) giving thanks, karah prashad shared; often langar.
Mission 3Read the source

The Sikh code of conduct

The Sikh Rahit Maryada (the Sikh code of conduct, approved by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee in 1945), on birth and naming (paraphrase):

  • Go to the gurdwara when the mother is able to move about and bathe; the number of days does not matter.
  • Take the hukam by opening the Guru Granth Sahib. The granthi proposes a name beginning with the first letter of the hymn; it is announced once the sangat accepts it.
  • Add Singh to a boy's name and Kaur to a girl's.
  • Do not hold that birth causes ritual pollution: birth and death happen by God's will.

Mission 3

What the ceremony means

  • Guided by the Guru: the name comes from the Guru's word, so from the start the child's life is placed under the Guru's guidance.
  • Thanks to God: the child is God's gift, not something the family made alone.
  • Belonging: the sangat welcomes the child into the Sikh community.
  • Equality: the same ceremony for girls and boys; most Sikh names (e.g. Harpreet, Jaspreet) are used for both.
  • No superstition: no horoscopes, no lucky days, no "impure" period.
  • Many families celebrate with sweets and gifts for relatives and friends.
Mission 3

Singh and Kaur: where they come from

  • At Vaisakhi 1699, Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa (the community of initiated Sikhs).
  • He gave the men the name Singh ("lion") and the women Kaur (usually translated "princess").
  • In India, family surnames often showed caste. Using Singh or Kaur instead hides caste: all are equal members of one family, the Guru's.
  • Lion: courage to stand for truth and protect the weak.
  • Princess: dignity and royalty for every woman, whatever her birth.
  • A Kaur keeps her name for life; she does not have to take her husband's name.
Quick check
?Why did Guru Gobind Singh give Sikhs the names Singh and Kaur?
Mission 3Diversity

Singh, Kaur and naming today

  • Most Sikhs receive Singh or Kaur at the naming ceremony.
  • Use varies in Britain: some use Singh or Kaur as their surname; many add a family name (e.g. Sandhu, Gill), with Singh or Kaur as a middle name.
  • Some Sikhs argue that family names bring caste back in, against the Guru's aim. Others say a family name is practical for official forms and shows family heritage, not caste pride.
  • Some Sikh women in Britain take a husband's surname; others keep Kaur, as the Rahit Maryada expects.
  • Khalsa (amritdhari) families tend to follow the Rahit Maryada closely: hukam letter, no superstition.
  • Some non-Khalsa families add Punjabi customs alongside, such as celebrating a first Lohri (winter festival) for a new baby; many now hold it for daughters as well as sons.
  • At Amrit Sanskar (a later mission), anyone without Singh or Kaur takes the name.
Mission check

A Sikh name joins guidance from the Guru's word with the equality of the Guru's family.

Mission 4

Marriage in Sikh teaching

  • Sikhs value the householder life (grihasth): working, raising a family, serving others and remembering God at the same time.
  • Almost all the Gurus married; Sikhism has no celibate priesthood.
  • Sikhs see marriage as a spiritual union, not only a contract: two people helping each other towards God.
  • It also pictures the soul's longing for union with God (the soul as bride, God as the Beloved).
  • Husband and wife are equal partners.
Mission 4

Before the ceremony

  • Many marriages are arranged or "introduced" by families, with the couple's consent; many couples also meet independently.
  • The Rahit Maryada says both partners should be Sikhs; caste, horoscopes and dowry play no part.
  • Milni: the two families meet and embrace at the gurdwara, often exchanging gifts: two families joining.
  • The wedding is called Anand Karaj: "ceremony of bliss". It usually takes place in the morning, in the gurdwara, before the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • In India, Sikh weddings have been recognised in law since the Anand Marriage Act (1909).
Mission 4

The Lavan: four rounds

  • The bride's father places one end of a scarf (palla) in the groom's hand and the other in the bride's.
  • The Lavan, four verses by Guru Ram Das, are read from the Guru Granth Sahib one at a time.
  • After each verse is read, it is sung while the couple walk clockwise round the Guru Granth Sahib, the groom leading.
  • 1st round: do your duties as a householder, guided by the Guru's word.
  • 2nd round: meet God with love and awe; let go of pride.
  • 3rd round: love for God grows in the company of the holy.
  • 4th round: peace and union with God.
  • The couple are married by walking round the Guru, not by a priest's declaration. Then: Anand Sahib, Ardas, a hukamnama, karah prashad and langar.
Quick check
?At an Anand Karaj, what is the moment that makes the couple married?
Mission 4Read the source

One light in two bodies

  • Guru Granth Sahib 788 (Guru Amar Das, paraphrase): they are not really husband and wife who merely live together; they alone are husband and wife who have one light in two bodies.
  • The Lavan, Guru Granth Sahib 773–774 (Guru Ram Das, paraphrase): in the fourth round, the mind is at peace, and the soul has found the Lord.

Q1 According to GGS 788, what makes a true marriage?

Show answer

Spiritual unity: "one light in two bodies", not only living together.

Q2 What do the Lavan suggest marriage is ultimately about?

Show answer

The soul's journey to union with God.

Mission 4Diversity

Sikh marriage today

  • Mixed-faith marriage: the Rahit Maryada says both partners should be Sikh. Many gurdwaras will hold an Anand Karaj only if both are Sikh; some have been more flexible, which has caused protests.
  • In Britain: many couples also have a separate civil ceremony, unless the gurdwara is registered to hold legal weddings.
  • Arranged or love marriage: some families prefer arrangement for stability and family support; many young Sikhs choose their own partner and seek family blessing.
  • Remarriage: the Gurus spoke against the burning of widows; widowed Sikhs may remarry with an Anand Karaj.
  • Divorce: discouraged, as marriage is a spiritual bond; civil divorce is accepted by many Sikhs today, though attitudes vary.
Mission 4

Beliefs about death

  • Sikhs believe death happens by God's will (hukam), so it is not to be feared.
  • Sikhs believe the body dies but the soul (atma) does not.
  • They believe karma shapes what follows: rebirth into another life, or mukti (liberation): union with God, ending the cycle of rebirth.
  • So grief is real, but loud wailing is discouraged; Sikhs remember God and accept God's will.
  • At a death, those present say "Waheguru".
Mission 4

Funeral rites

  • The body is washed and dressed in clean clothes; an amritdhari Sikh is dressed in the Five Ks.
  • Family and friends recite hymns; often the body is taken home or to the gurdwara before the funeral.
  • Cremation is the usual practice. At the cremation, Kirtan Sohila (the night prayer) is recited and Ardas offered.
  • The ashes are placed in flowing water, a sign that the body returns to nature.
  • No monuments or gravestones: the Rahit Maryada forbids them; the person lives on with God, not in a stone.
  • Over the following days, a complete reading of the Guru Granth Sahib (sahaj path) is held; it ends with the bhog ceremony, Ardas and langar.

Mission 4Diversity

Death rites today

  • Ashes: some families take them to Kiratpur Sahib in Punjab, a traditional place; others use rivers in Britain where councils allow it.
  • Cremation: open-air pyres in India; in Britain, usually a crematorium.
  • Reading: some families hold a sahaj path (over several days); others an akhand path (48 hours, continuous).
  • Customs: some families keep Punjabi customs such as loud mourning or rituals for the dead, which the Rahit Maryada discourages; others follow the code strictly.
  • If cremation is impossible, the Rahit Maryada allows another method, such as placing the body in a river or the sea.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Rites of passage matter more to the family than to the individual."

  • Reasons for: families arrange and pay for weddings and funerals; the milni joins two families; funerals and the bhog support the grieving; ceremonies pass on identity to children.
  • Reasons against: each rite centres on one soul before God: the Lavan are about the soul's union with God; at death the soul faces God by its own karma; the Gurus taught that each person must remember God for themselves.
  • Another view: the sangat, not only the family, is the real community at every rite.
  • Judge it by: Who is each ceremony addressed to: God, the person, or the family? Can the two be separated in Sikh thinking?
Mission check

For Sikhs, rites of passage point beyond the ceremony to God: marriage as one light in two bodies, death as the soul's return.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gurdwara = door of the Guru: Nishan Sahib and the open-door idea outside; takht, palki, chanani, chaur and rumalas inside; sach khand and langar hall.

Mission 2

  • Nam japna (meditating on God's Name) is the first of Guru Nanak's three duties; repeating "Waheguru", Gurbani and kirtan turn the mind from ego to God.
  • Prayer at home: Nitnem (morning, evening, night), Ardas, simran; GGS 305 calls a Sikh to rise early and remember God all day.

Mission 3

  • Naam Karan: in the gurdwara, the Guru Granth Sahib is opened at random; the first letter of the hukamnama guides the name.
  • Singh ("lion") and Kaur ("princess"), given since 1699, show equality and reject caste.
  • How Sikhs use Singh, Kaur and family names today varies, and is debated.

Mission 4

  • Anand Karaj: in the morning, before the Guru Granth Sahib; four rounds (Lavan) by Guru Ram Das make the marriage.
  • Sikhs see marriage as a spiritual union of equals ("one light in two bodies", GGS 788).
  • At death: Sikhs accept God's will; cremation, Kirtan Sohila, ashes in flowing water, no monuments, sahaj path; practice varies between families and countries.
