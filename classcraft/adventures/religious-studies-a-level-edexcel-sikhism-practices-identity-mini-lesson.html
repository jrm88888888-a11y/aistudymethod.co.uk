Mission 1

Why Sikhs felt a need for reform

  • 1849: the British annex Punjab after the fall of Ranjit Singh's kingdom.
  • Many Sikhs share customs with Hindu neighbours: Brahmin priests at weddings, images of Hindu deities kept in the precincts of the Harmandir Sahib, caste practices.
  • Gurdwaras are run by hereditary custodians (mahants), some corrupt, backed by the British.
  • Pressure from outside: Christian missions (Punjab's last Maharaja, Duleep Singh, became a Christian in 1853) and the Hindu reform movement Arya Samaj (founded 1875), which criticised the Gurus and sought to bring Sikhs back into Hinduism.
  • Many Sikh leaders fear the community may dissolve.
Mission 1

The Singh Sabha movements

  • 1873: the first Singh Sabha ("Sikh society") is founded at Amritsar; 1879: the Lahore Singh Sabha.
  • Two wings:
  • Sanatan ("traditional") Sikhs, strong in Amritsar: Sikhism as part of the wider Hindu world; respect for old customs and hereditary leaders.
  • Tat Khalsa ("true Khalsa"), strong in Lahore: Sikhism as a distinct religion, based on the Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib; reject idols, Brahmin rites and caste.
  • The Tat Khalsa wing wins: schools (Khalsa College, Amritsar, 1892), Punjabi newspapers, tracts in Gurmukhi.
  • Kahn Singh Nabha, Ham Hindu Nahin ("We are not Hindus", 1898), sums up the Tat Khalsa case.

Mission 1

The Anand Marriage Act 1909

  • Many Sikh weddings followed the Hindu rite of circling a sacred fire, with a Brahmin priest.
  • Reformers revived the Anand Karaj ("blissful union"): the couple walk four times around the Guru Granth Sahib while the four laavan (Guru Ram Das' wedding hymn) are sung.
  • Some families objected; the legal validity of Anand marriages was questioned.
  • 1909: the Anand Marriage Act, passed by the British-run Imperial Legislative Council, recognises the Sikh rite in law.
  • Impact: a distinctive Sikh rite becomes official; a visible boundary from Hindu practice.
Mission 1

Gurdwara reform, 1920–1925

  • After 1919 (Jallianwala Bagh), Sikhs campaign to take gurdwaras back from the mahants.
  • 1920: the SGPC (Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee) and the Akali Dal are founded.
  • 1921: at Nankana Sahib, a mahant's armed men kill many Akali reformers; non-violent protests (morchas) follow, e.g. Guru ka Bagh (1922).
  • Thousands are arrested; the reformers stay largely non-violent.
  • 1925: the Sikh Gurdwaras Act places historic gurdwaras in Punjab under an elected SGPC.
  • Impact: reformist (Tat Khalsa) practice now runs the main gurdwaras.
Mission 1

The Rahit Namas

  • Rahit: the Sikh code of conduct; namas: manuals. So Rahit Namas are 18th- and early 19th-century codes of Khalsa discipline.
  • Examples: the Tankhah-nama (attributed to Bhai Nand Lal), and rahit namas associated with Bhai Chaupa Singh, Bhai Desa Singh and Bhai Prahlad Singh.
  • They record the 5Ks, daily prayers, the ban on tobacco and other rules, often attributed to Guru Gobind Singh.
  • Problems: they differ from one another; some contain rules that reflect their own era (e.g. conflict with Mughals) or Brahmin influence.
  • Your spec spells it Rehat; Rahit is also used.
Mission 1

The Sikh Rehat Maryada

  • SGPC committee work on one agreed code begins in 1931; approved 1945; published 1950.
  • Drafted after consulting scholars and the main Sikh institutions, drawing on the Guru Granth Sahib, the Gurus' example and the Rahit Namas.
  • Two parts:
  • Personal life: nam simran and daily banis (prayers), the gurdwara, ceremonies (birth, Anand Karaj, death), honest living, sewa.
  • Panthic (community) life: amrit initiation, the 5Ks, the four kurahits (cardinal prohibitions: cutting hair; eating ritually slaughtered meat; sexual relations outside marriage; tobacco), and tankhah (penance).
Mission 1Read the source

Who is a Sikh?

Sikh Rehat Maryada, Article I (paraphrase): a Sikh is any person who believes in one Immortal Being; the ten Gurus, from Guru Nanak to Guru Gobind Singh; the Guru Granth Sahib; the words and teachings of the ten Gurus; and the initiation (amrit) given by the tenth Guru; and who owes allegiance to no other religion.

Q1 Does this definition require every Sikh to have taken amrit?

Show answer

No: it requires belief in the amrit given by the tenth Guru; amrit is the ideal, taken when ready.

Q2 Why does "no other religion" matter for the Singh Sabha's aims?

Show answer

It draws the boundary between Sikh and Hindu that the reformers sought.

Mission 1

Oberoi vs the Institute of Sikh Studies

  • Harjot Oberoi, The Construction of Religious Boundaries (1994).
  • Oberoi (paraphrase): before the reformers, Sikh identity was fluid and plural: many Sikhs honoured Hindu deities, Sufi shrines and village saints, and were not Khalsa. He calls this the Sanatan Sikh tradition. The Tat Khalsa turned one variant, the Khalsa, into the only "true" Sikhism, drawing hard boundaries.
  • Institute of Sikh Studies (Chandigarh) and allied scholars (e.g. the essays in Invasion of Religious Boundaries, eds J. S. Mann, S. S. Sodhi and G. S. Gill) reply (paraphrase): a distinct Sikh identity comes from the Gurus themselves: Nanak's rejection of Hindu and Muslim ritual, the Khalsa of 1699, the Rahit Namas. The Singh Sabha restored an identity that had decayed; it did not invent it. They also challenged Oberoi's sources and method.
  • Spec: assess the impact of the Rehat Maryada, the 1909 Act and gurdwara reform "in making official a particular variant" of Sikhism.
Mission 1

Impact: uniformity and its limits

  • Uniformity worldwide: the same daily banis, amrit ceremony, Anand Karaj and gurdwara order from Amritsar to Vancouver.
  • Identity: clear definitions of Sikh and non-Sikh; the 5Ks and kurahits as shared markers.
  • Self-discipline: a daily rule of life; tankhah allows wrongs to be confessed and put right.
  • Gurdwara practice: how the Guru Granth Sahib is installed, read and closed; Ardas; langar.
  • Limits: some groups keep their own maryada (e.g. the Damdami Taksal, Nihangs, the Akhand Kirtani Jatha), and the Namdharis follow a living Guru; many sahajdhari Sikhs are not fully covered by its panthic rules (see Mission 2).
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Rehat Maryada is an effective guide to Sikh living."

  • For: clear and practical; agreed by the main Sikh representative body; gives every Sikh a shared rule of life.
  • For: grounded in the Guru Granth Sahib and the Gurus' example; it removed contradictions found in the Rahit Namas.
  • Against: it reflects one variant (Tat Khalsa) and sidelines other Sikh ways of life (as some historians argue).
  • Against: it is not accepted in full by every group, and many Sikhs do not keep all of it.
  • Against: it says little on many modern issues (e.g. bioethics, online worship), so interpretation is still needed.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to the Gurus; acceptance across the Panth; usefulness in daily life today.
  • Apply the same criteria to the 1909 Act and gurdwara reform: restoration (Institute of Sikh Studies) or construction (Oberoi)?

Mission 2

Vaisakhi 1699: the Khalsa

  • Vaisakhi 1699, Anandpur: Guru Gobind Singh asks the gathered Sikhs who will give their head for the faith.
  • Five men, from different castes and regions, step forward one by one: the Panj Piare ("Five Beloved Ones").
  • The Guru initiates them with amrit ("nectar") and founds the Khalsa ("the pure"): a community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Then the Guru asks the Five to initiate him: Guru and disciple become equals.
  • (Story and its sources in an earlier lesson.)
Mission 2

The amrit ceremony

  • Also called amrit sanskar or khande di pahul.
  • Held in private before the Guru Granth Sahib; five amritdhari Sikhs take the place of the Panj Piare.
  • Water and sugar (patashe) are stirred in an iron bowl with a khanda (double-edged sword) while five banis (prayers) are recited.
  • Each initiate drinks amrit five times; it is sprinkled five times on the eyes and five times on the hair; all drink from the same bowl, a sign that caste is abolished.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited; initiates, who come wearing the 5Ks, are taught the Rehat (code of conduct).
  • Open to men and women of any caste or background who are ready to keep the discipline.
Mission 2

Singh, Kaur and the discipline

  • Men take the name Singh ("lion"); women take Kaur ("princess").
  • These replace caste surnames: all Khalsa Sikhs are one family.
  • Initiates vow to avoid the four kurahits (cardinal prohibitions): cutting hair; eating ritually slaughtered (kutha) meat; sexual relations outside marriage; tobacco.
  • Daily discipline: the set banis morning and evening, nam simran, honest work, sharing, sewa.
Mission 2

The 5Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, kept covered (men wear a turban, many women too): accepting God's will and the natural form God gave.
  • Kangha: a small wooden comb: cleanliness and order, in body and mind.
  • Kara: a steel bangle on the wrist: one God without beginning or end; a reminder to do only good with one's hands.
  • Kachera: cotton undershorts: modesty, self-control, readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: a sword: defending justice and the weak.
  • Together: an outward uniform of the Khalsa, and reminders of an inner discipline.

Mission 2

Four kinds of Sikh

  • Amritdhari: has taken amrit; keeps the 5Ks and the Rehat.
  • Keshdhari: keeps uncut hair (and usually a turban) but has not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: follows the Gurus' teaching but cuts their hair and has not taken amrit. Often explained as "slow adopter" (on the way, gradually); some read sahaj as "inner peace".
  • Patit: an apostate: an amritdhari who breaks a kurahit, especially cutting hair, without seeking forgiveness. Some use the word more widely for any Sikh who cuts their hair.
  • Many Sikhs are keshdhari or sahajdhari all their lives; most Sikhs worldwide have not taken amrit.
  • Your spec spells it sehajdhari.
Mission 2Read the source

Who may take amrit?

Sikh Rehat Maryada, on the amrit ceremony (paraphrase): any man or woman, of any country, religion or caste, who embraces the Sikh faith and promises to live by its principles may receive amrit. They should be of an age to understand, and should come with hair uncut and the 5Ks, ready to keep the Rehat.

Q1 Which barriers does this remove?

Show answer

Gender, nationality, religion of birth and caste.

Q2 Why must initiates be old enough to understand?

Show answer

Amrit is a lifelong commitment chosen freely, not a ceremony done to a child.

Mission 2Diversity

How important are initiation and the 5Ks?

  • Khalsa view (Rehat Maryada; many amritdhari Sikhs): the Guru commanded the Khalsa form; every Sikh should aim to take amrit; the 5Ks are the Guru's gift, not optional dress.
  • Sahajdhari view: Guru Nanak and the first nine Gurus taught inner devotion; the Guru Granth Sahib does not demand outward forms; a Sikh is one who follows the Guru's word.
  • Keshdhari view: uncut hair is essential to Sikh identity, but amrit should be taken only when one is spiritually ready.
  • Within the Khalsa: some groups (e.g. the Akhand Kirtani Jatha) count the keski (small turban) as the K instead of kesh; Nihangs wear many more weapons.
  • Law and politics: sahajdharis lost their vote in SGPC elections by a 2003 notification; a court struck it down in 2011; Parliament removed the vote by law in 2016.
  • Diaspora: some young Sikhs take amrit to affirm identity; others feel pressure to cut hair at school or work.
Mission 2

Breaking the vows: tankhah and return

  • An amritdhari who breaks a kurahit becomes patit in Rehat Maryada terms.
  • The way back: appear before five amritdhari Sikhs in the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib, confess, and accept a tankhah (penance: e.g. extra prayers, sewa).
  • Then the person may take amrit again.
  • So "patit" is not a permanent label: Sikh discipline aims at return, not exclusion.
  • Pastoral debate: some Sikhs think the word is used too harshly of people who simply cut their hair.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Only amritdhari Sikhs are true Sikhs."

  • For: the Rehat Maryada says every Sikh should take amrit; Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa as the Sikh ideal.
  • For: the 5Ks are a visible commitment; without amrit, the Khalsa discipline is incomplete.
  • Against: the Rehat Maryada's own definition of a Sikh (see Mission 1) requires belief in amrit, not having taken it.
  • Against: Sikhs existed for over 200 years before 1699; the Gurus taught inner devotion first.
  • Against: most Sikhs have not taken amrit; excluding them would split the Panth.
  • Judgement criteria: what the Gurus required vs what they recommended; inner faith vs outward form; the authority of the Rehat Maryada.

Mission 3

Nam and simran

  • Nam ("Name"): not just a word for God, but God's presence and nature running through all creation.
  • Simran: remembrance: keeping God constantly in mind.
  • Nam simran: remembering God through the Name; nam japna is its active form: repeating the Name.
  • Your spec spells it nam simaran.
  • Aim: to turn the self from haumai (ego) to God, moving from manmukh (self-centred) to gurmukh (God-centred).
  • Nam is also a controlling force: the divine order (hukam) that sustains everything.
Mission 3

Names of God

  • Waheguru: "Wonderful Lord / Wonderful Enlightener", the name most used by Sikhs.
  • Satnam: "whose Name is Truth" (from the Mool Mantar).
  • Akal Purakh: "Timeless Being"; Nirankar: "the Formless"; Kartar: "Creator".
  • Gurbani also uses Hindu and Muslim names: Hari, Ram, Gobind, Allah, Rahim: God is beyond any one tradition.
  • Guru Arjan: the tongue recites names drawn from God's deeds; Satnam is God's primal Name (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1083; paraphrase).
  • Each name shows an attribute; none captures God fully: God is nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (known through qualities).
Mission 3

Mantras

  • Gurmantar: Waheguru, given to the initiate at the amrit ceremony (see Mission 2); repeated silently or aloud.
  • Mool Mantar ("root mantra"): the opening of the Guru Granth Sahib, from Ik Onkar to Gur Prasad: God is one, Truth is the Name, Creator, without fear or hate, timeless, unborn, self-existent, known by the Guru's grace (paraphrase).
  • Mantra here means a sacred formula that focuses the mind; it is not a spell that controls God.
  • Some Sikhs use a mala (rosary) to keep count; others think counting distracts from love.
  • Many link repetition with the breath: Wahe on the in-breath, Guru on the out-breath.

Mission 3

Practices of nam simran

  • Amrit vela: the "ambrosial hours" before dawn: Japji tells Sikhs to meditate on the True Name then (Japji, pauri 4, Ang 2; paraphrase).
  • Nam japna: personal repetition of Waheguru or the Mool Mantar.
  • Nitnem: the daily banis (morning, evening, night prayers).
  • Kirtan: singing hymns from the Guru Granth Sahib, alone or in the sangat: collective nam simran.
  • Akhand Path: a continuous reading of the whole scripture over about 48 hours.
  • Guru Arjan's Sukhmani ("Jewel of Peace") is a long hymn in praise of simran.
Mission 3Read the source

Japji, pauri 20

Guru Nanak (paraphrase): when hands, feet and body are dirty, water washes them; when clothes are soiled, soap cleans them; but when the mind is stained by wrongdoing, only the love of the Name can cleanse it (Japji, pauri 20, Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4).

Q1 What can the Name do that water and soap cannot?

Show answer

Cleanse the mind of evil thoughts and wrongdoing.

Q2 Why does the verse say love of the Name, not just repetition?

Show answer

The cleansing comes from devotion and attention, not from sound alone.

Mission 3

From repetition to experience

  • Gurbani describes stages of deepening:
  • Repetition with the tongue → with the mind → ajapa jaap, "unrecited repetition": the Name repeats itself, without effort.
  • Vismad: awe and wonder at God's greatness.
  • Sahaj: calm, natural poise; the mind at peace in God.
  • Anahad shabad: the "unstruck sound": a divine music heard within. Gurbani speaks of the panch shabad, the "five sounds" (pictured as five instruments), resounding in the attuned soul (e.g. Anand Sahib, Ang 917).
  • All by grace (nadar): effort prepares; God gives.

Mission 3Diversity

Different perspectives on practice

  • Mainstream practice: Nitnem, kirtan in the gurdwara and personal japna; the Rehat Maryada lists nam simran among the Sikh's daily duties.
  • Akhand Kirtani Jatha: long night-time kirtan (rainsabai) and intense, often audible repetition of the Gurmantar.
  • Sant-led groups (e.g. Nanaksar): long silent meditation and continuous recitation; a living sant may guide.
  • Sikh Dharma / 3HO (mostly Western converts, from 1969): combines nam with Kundalini yoga; many Sikhs question whether this fits the Gurus' rejection of yogic techniques.
  • Critique of ritualism: Gurbani warns that saying God's name with the tongue, without love in the heart, does not reach God (e.g. Ang 491; paraphrase). Some Sikhs therefore distrust counting with a mala or mechanical chanting.
  • Social view: nam simran is not withdrawal: it must go with kirat karni and sewa.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Naam Japo is evidence of a personal mystical union with God."

  • For: Gurbani describes ajapa jaap, sahaj, anahad shabad and Sach khand: union with the Formless.
  • For: many Sikhs report a felt sense of God's presence through simran, in line with descriptions of mystical experience.
  • Against: union depends on grace, not technique: repeating the Name does not guarantee union.
  • Against: inner experiences are private and cannot be checked; they may have psychological explanations.
  • Against: for many Sikhs, simran is ethical remembrance (living as if God is always present) more than mystical experience.
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as evidence; how Gurbani describes union; whether the effects on conduct (humility, sewa) are a better test than feelings.
  • Spec: different perspectives on the importance of nam simaran compared with other practices, e.g. sewa and the Khalsa discipline.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Under British rule, the Singh Sabha (Tat Khalsa) defined Sikhism as distinct: the Anand Marriage Act 1909, gurdwara reform (SGPC, 1925 Act) and the Rehat Maryada.
  • The Rahit Namas are varied 18th-century codes; the Rehat Maryada (Rahit Maryada; 1931–1950) sifts them into one standard: definition of a Sikh, personal and panthic life.
  • Oberoi: one variant was made official; Institute of Sikh Studies: the Gurus' identity was restored.

Mission 2

  • 1699: Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa; amrit (khande di pahul) initiates men and women of any caste; names Singh and Kaur; four kurahits.
  • The 5Ks: kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan: an outward uniform and inner discipline.
  • Amritdhari, keshdhari, sahajdhari (sehajdhari) and patit Sikhs hold different views on how essential initiation and the 5Ks are.

Mission 3

  • Nam is God's presence and nature; nam simran is loving remembrance; nam japna (Naam Japo) repeats the Name.
  • Mantras: the Gurmantar Waheguru and the Mool Mantar; practices include amrit vela, Nitnem, kirtan and Akhand Path.
  • Repetition deepens to ajapa jaap, sahaj and the "unstruck sound" (panch shabad); Sikhs differ on methods and on whether simran is mystical union or ethical remembrance.
