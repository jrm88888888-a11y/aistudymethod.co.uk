Mission 1

Mission 1: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Make a guess: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Mission 1

What a mitzvah is

  • A mitzvah (plural mitzvot) is a commandment from God in the Torah.
  • The rabbis counted 613 in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative (Talmud Makkot 23b).
  • They cover worship, food, Shabbat, family life, business, courts, care for the poor and for animals.
  • At 13 (boys) or 12 (girls, in Orthodox practice) a young Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah: "son/daughter of the commandment", now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is seen as a response of love to God and a way to make ordinary life holy.
Mission 1

Ways to group the mitzvot

  • Positive (mitzvot aseh, "do"): e.g. honour parents, keep Shabbat, give tzedakah.
  • Negative (mitzvot lo ta'aseh, "do not"): e.g. do not steal, do not eat pork.
  • Between people and God (bein adam la-Makom): prayer, Shabbat, kashrut, festivals.
  • Between people (bein adam la-chavero): honesty, charity, kindness, respect for parents.
Mission 1Read the source

Reminders to keep the mitzvot

> "And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them" (Numbers 15:39)

  • This is the command of tzitzit: fringes on the corners of a garment, worn on the tallit and by many Orthodox men every day.
  • "Therefore shall ye lay up these My words in your heart and in your soul; and ye shall bind them for a sign upon your hand" (Deuteronomy 11:18): tefillin.
  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house" (Deuteronomy 11:20): the mezuzah.
  • Both passages are part of the Shema, said twice daily.

Mission 1

Free will: "choose life"

  • "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Judaism teaches that God gave every person free will (bechirah): the power to choose good or evil.
  • Free will and the mitzvot fit together: a command only makes sense if you can obey or disobey it.
  • Choice makes reward and punishment fair, and makes keeping a mitzvah a real act of love.
  • Even after doing wrong, a person can choose teshuvah (return, repentance).
Mission 1

Two inclinations

  • The rabbis taught that every person has a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (bad inclination).
  • "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21); but God tells Cain: "sin coucheth at the door … but thou mayest rule over it" (Genesis 4:7).
  • The yetzer ha-ra is not a devil: it is selfish desire. Used rightly, it drives people to build, marry and work (Genesis Rabbah 9:7, paraphrase).
  • Sin (chet, "missing the mark") happens when the yetzer ha-ra rules. The Torah is its remedy (Talmud Kiddushin 30b, paraphrase).
  • A teaching links bar/bat mitzvah age with the yetzer ha-tov growing strong enough to guide choices.
Mission 1Read the source

Maimonides, Sefer Madda

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) wrote the Mishneh Torah (completed c. 1180), a code of all Jewish law. Book 1 is Sefer Madda, "the Book of Knowledge".
  • It begins: the foundation of all is to know that God exists (Laws of the Foundations of the Torah 1:1, paraphrase). Every mitzvah grows from that.
  • Laws of Repentance 5:1–4 (paraphrase): freedom is given to every person. Anyone can become as righteous as Moses or as wicked as Jeroboam. No one forces us; God does not decree whether we will be good or bad.
  • If God decided our actions, the Torah's commands, and reward and punishment, would make no sense.

Mission 1Diversity

Which mitzvot matter most?

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God; both kinds are equally binding. You may not choose between them.
  • Yet they differ in one way: Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but not for sins against people until you have made peace with them (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • Reform and Liberal: the ethical mitzvot are central; ritual mitzvot are valued and chosen by informed choice.
  • The Talmud shows prophets summing up the 613: David in 11 principles, Micah in 3, Habakkuk in 1: "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4) (Makkot 24a).
  • Hillel: not doing to others what is hateful to you is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

"Mitzvot between people matter more than mitzvot between people and God."

  • For: Yoma 8:9: wrongs against people need their forgiveness. Hillel calls not harming others "the whole Torah". Reform Judaism puts ethics first. Prophets condemn worship without justice.
  • Against: Orthodox teaching: all 613 are God's will, equally binding. The Ten Commandments start with duties to God. Ritual mitzvot (Shabbat, kashrut) keep Jewish identity alive.
  • Another view: the two are linked: treating people well is serving God, since every person is made in God's image.
  • Judge it by: Orthodox and Reform views; what the sources stress; whether the two kinds can really be separated.

Mission 2

Why life is holy

  • Sanctity of life: Jews believe human life is holy (sacred) because it comes from God and belongs to God.
  • Every person is made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27), so every life has equal and infinite worth.
  • Genesis tells how God breathes life into the first human (Genesis 2:7). Jews see life as a gift, held on trust.
  • Murder is forbidden "for in the image of God made He man" (Genesis 9:6).
  • God alone gives and takes life: "I kill, and I make alive" (Deuteronomy 32:39).
  • The Mishnah: whoever destroys one life is as if they destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if they saved a whole world (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).

Life is God's

> "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • "Thou shalt not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • "neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16): not only "do not kill", but act to save.

Mission 2

Pikuach nefesh: saving a life

  • Pikuach nefesh: "watching over a soul". Saving a human life overrides almost every other mitzvah, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur.
  • The source: "Ye shall therefore keep My statutes … which if a man do, he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5).
  • The Talmud reads this: you should live by the mitzvot, not die because of them (Talmud Yoma 85b, paraphrase).
  • So breaking Shabbat to save a life is not a sin: it is itself a mitzvah.
  • Three exceptions: a Jew must not commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations (e.g. adultery, incest), even to save their own life (Talmud Sanhedrin 74a, paraphrase).
Mission 2Read the source

Talmud Yoma 83–85

All paraphrased from the Talmud tractate Yoma ("The Day", about Yom Kippur):

  • 83a (Mishnah): a seriously ill person may be fed on Yom Kippur; any doubt about danger to life overrides Shabbat.
  • 84b: if a child falls into the sea, spread a net and pull them out on Shabbat; if a child is locked in a room, break the door.
  • 84b: do it at once, and do not hand the task to a non-Jew or a child: the one who acts quickly is praiseworthy.
  • 85a: if a building collapses on Shabbat, dig out the rubble even if you are unsure anyone is alive.
  • 85b: "live by them": profaning one Shabbat lets a person keep many Shabbats.

Show answer

Mission 2

Pikuach nefesh in practice

  • Shabbat: phone an ambulance, drive to hospital, switch on lights for a doctor.
  • Yom Kippur: a person whose health is at risk must eat and drink; fasting would be wrong.
  • Kashrut: non-kosher medicine may be taken when it is needed for health.
  • Community: in the UK, Hatzola volunteer ambulance teams serve Jewish neighbourhoods, working on Shabbat and festivals.
  • Social: blood donation, medical careers and (for many) organ donation are seen as mitzvot.

Mission 2Diversity

Applying pikuach nefesh today

  • Agreed by all groups: saving life overrides Shabbat, fasting and food laws.
  • Relieving suffering: does it cover pain or illness that is not life-threatening? Orthodox law relaxes some Shabbat rules for the sick but not all; many rabbis now treat serious mental illness as pikuach nefesh.
  • Organ donation: living donation is widely praised. For donation after death, Orthodox authorities differ, mainly over how death is defined. Reform and Liberal Judaism encourage it.
  • End of life: all mainstream groups reject actively killing a patient. Many Orthodox authorities allow stopping treatment that only prolongs dying. Some Reform and Liberal Jews support assisted dying for the terminally ill.
  • Abortion: all permit it, and Orthodox law requires it, when the mother's life is in danger (Mishnah Oholot 7:6); views on other reasons differ.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Saving a life should always come before religious laws."

  • For: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5; Yoma 85b). Saving one life is like saving a world (Sanhedrin 4:5). Even doubt about danger overrides Shabbat.
  • Against: the three exceptions (Sanhedrin 74a): some laws may not be broken even to save life; many Jews were martyred rather than worship idols.
  • Against: "always" is too broad: the danger must be real, and many rabbis say pikuach nefesh does not cover every kind of suffering.
  • Judge it by: the Talmud's own limits; Orthodox and Reform views; whether saving life is itself a religious law.
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why pikuach nefesh is important for Jews. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: human life is holy. Source: "God created man in His own image" (Genesis 1:27). Development: so saving any person matters more than keeping almost any other mitzvah.
  • Reason 2: the Torah is meant to give life. Source: "he shall live by them" (Leviticus 18:5), which the Talmud reads as "not die by them" (Yoma 85b). Development: so a Jew who breaks Shabbat to save a life is praised, not blamed.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A synagogue has no statues and no pictures of God. So what fills it, and why does it face Jerusalem?

Make a guess: A synagogue has no statues and no pictures of God. So what fills it, and why does it face Jerusalem?

Mission 3

A house of meeting

  • Synagogue is from a Greek word meaning "to bring together". Many Jews say shul (Yiddish, "school").
  • Hebrew names show its three roles:
  • Beit knesset: house of assembly (community).
  • Beit tefillah: house of prayer.
  • Beit midrash: house of study.
Mission 3

The Ark

  • The aron hakodesh (holy Ark) is a cabinet on the wall facing Jerusalem (in Britain, roughly east).
  • It holds the Sefer Torah scrolls: the holiest objects in the synagogue.
  • A curtain (parochet) often hangs in front, recalling the curtain before the Holy of Holies in the Temple.
  • The congregation stands when the Ark is opened.
  • Above it there are often two tablets with the first words of the Ten Commandments.
  • Everyone prays facing the Ark, and so facing Jerusalem, where the Temple stood.
Mission 3

The eternal light and the menorah

  • Ner tamid ("eternal light"): a lamp above the Ark that never goes out.
  • It recalls the lamp that burned "continually" in the Tabernacle and Temple (Exodus 27:20).
  • It symbolises God's constant presence, and that the Torah's light is never put out.
  • Menorah: the seven-branched lampstand of the Temple; many synagogues display one.
  • Many avoid an exact copy of the Temple menorah, out of respect for the Temple.
Mission 3

The bimah and the Torah reading

  • Bimah: a raised platform with a reading desk, from which the Torah is read.
  • Raised, so that all can hear and see; the Torah is honoured above the people.
  • Orthodox: the bimah usually stands in the centre, with seats around it.
  • Reform and Liberal: often at the front, joined to the Ark, so the room faces one way like a hall.

Mission 3

No images

  • There are no statues, no pictures of God and usually no pictures of people in a synagogue.
  • "Thou shalt not make unto thee a graven image, nor any manner of likeness" (Exodus 20:4)
  • Jews believe God has no body and cannot be pictured; worship goes to God alone.
  • Decoration uses Hebrew words, patterns, plants, and symbols such as the twelve tribes, the menorah or the tablets.
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox, Reform and Liberal synagogues

  • Seating: Orthodox: men and women sit separately, divided by a screen (mechitzah) or with women in a gallery. Reform and Liberal: mixed family seating.
  • Bimah: Orthodox: centre. Reform and Liberal: front.
  • Who leads: Orthodox: men. Reform and Liberal: men and women.
  • Minyan: Orthodox: ten men aged 13 or over for full public prayer. Reform and Liberal: men and women count; many hold services without ten.
  • Music: some Reform and Liberal synagogues have an organ or choir; Orthodox Shabbat singing is unaccompanied.
  • Reasons given: Orthodox Jews follow halakhah and tradition (and stress focus in prayer); Reform and Liberal Jews stress equality.

Mission 3

More than a place of worship

  • Worship: daily, Shabbat and festival services.
  • Study: adult classes; religion school (cheder) for children; Bar and Bat Mitzvah lessons; a library.
  • Community: a hall for kiddush after services, weddings, celebrations, youth clubs and lunch clubs for older people.
  • Care: support for the sick, the bereaved and those in need; collecting for charities (tzedakah).
Mission 3Evaluate

"A synagogue is more important as a community centre than as a place of worship."

  • For: one Hebrew name is "house of assembly"; many members come mainly for events, classes and life-cycle celebrations; care for the needy is itself a mitzvah.
  • Against: the Ark and Torah scrolls make it holy; daily prayer with a minyan is its central purpose; community halls exist elsewhere.
  • Another view: in Judaism the three roles cannot be separated: praying, studying and caring are all serving God.
  • Different traditions: Orthodox communities often stress daily prayer; Reform and Liberal communities often stress education, social action and inclusion. Both do all three.
  • Judge it by: What makes a building a synagogue? Why do people actually come? Could prayer happen without the building?

Mission 4

Praying as a community

  • Jews may pray anywhere, but communal prayer has special value: the community stands together before God.
  • Minyan: the ten adults needed for full public prayer.
  • Some prayers are said only with a minyan: the Kaddish, the Barechu call to prayer, the Kedushah, the public Torah reading, and the repeated Amidah.
  • Orthodox: ten men aged 13 (Bar Mitzvah age) or over.
  • Reform and Liberal: men and women count; many congregations pray together even without ten.
Mission 4

Three services a day

  • Shacharit: morning. Minchah: afternoon. Ma'ariv (Arvit): evening.
  • The prayers come from the siddur (prayer book), in a fixed order.
  • The Talmud gives two reasons for three times (Berakhot 26b):
  • They match the daily offerings in the Temple (morning and afternoon, with the evening service later added).
  • Abraham, Isaac and Jacob each began one of them.
  • Musaf ("additional"): an extra service on Shabbat and festivals, recalling the extra Temple offering of those days.
Mission 4

A Shabbat morning service

  • 1. Opening blessings and psalms: praise to warm up the heart.
  • 2. The Shema and its blessings: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • 3. The Amidah: said silently, then repeated aloud by the leader.
  • 4. The Torah service: the scroll is taken from the Ark and carried round; members are called up (aliyah) as the weekly portion is read; then the haftarah from the Prophets.
  • On ordinary weekdays the Torah is read only at the Monday and Thursday morning services.
  • 5. Sermon by the rabbi, and prayers for the community, the country (in Britain, the royal family) and Israel.
  • 6. Musaf (Orthodox), then closing prayers such as Aleinu, the mourners' Kaddish and a hymn.
  • Afterwards: kiddush, a blessing over wine and food together in the hall.

Mission 4

The Amidah

  • Amidah means "standing": the central prayer of every service. Also called the Shemoneh Esreh ("eighteen") and, by the rabbis, simply the Tefillah ("the prayer").
  • How: standing, feet together, facing Jerusalem; three steps forward at the start; bowing at set points; said silently, lips moving.
  • Why stand? Like a servant before a king; and the rabbis link feet together to the angels Ezekiel saw.
  • Three parts:
  • Praise (3 blessings): God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; God's power; God's holiness.
  • Requests (13 on weekdays): knowledge, forgiveness, healing, a good year, the return of exiles, justice, the rebuilding of Jerusalem, the Messiah…
  • Thanks (3 blessings): accept our prayer; we give thanks; grant peace.
  • On Shabbat the 13 requests are replaced by one blessing for the holiness of Shabbat: no weekday requests on the day of rest.

  • The prophet Hosea calls on the people to "render for bullocks the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:2): words of prayer in place of animal sacrifices.

Mission 4

Hebrew in worship

  • Hebrew is called the holy language: the language of the Torah and of the prayers since ancient times.
  • Praying in Hebrew links Jews across the world and across history: the same words in London, New York and Jerusalem.
  • The Kaddish is in Aramaic, the everyday language of many Jews in the time of the Talmud.
  • Orthodox services are almost all in Hebrew; many prayer books print a translation alongside.
  • Reform and Liberal services use much more English, so everyone understands; key prayers (the Shema, parts of the Amidah) stay in Hebrew.
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Reform services

  • Length: Orthodox Shabbat morning services are long (often around three hours), with Musaf. Reform services are usually shorter.
  • Language: mainly Hebrew (Orthodox) or Hebrew and English (Reform).
  • Who leads and reads: men only (Orthodox); men and women (Reform).
  • Seating and music: separate, unaccompanied (Orthodox); mixed, sometimes with choir or instruments (Reform).
  • The words: Reform prayer books have changed some prayers: e.g. adding the matriarchs Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah to the first blessing, and leaving out prayers to restore Temple sacrifices.
  • Shared: the Shema, the Amidah, the Torah reading, the Kaddish, and facing the Ark.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Jewish services should be in the language people speak every day."

  • For: prayer needs kavanah (inner intention), which is hard without understanding; the Mishnah allows the Shema to be said in any language (Sotah 7:1); English helps newcomers and children feel included.
  • Against: Hebrew unites Jews worldwide and links them to the Torah and their ancestors; translations lose meaning; learning Hebrew prayers is itself part of Jewish education.
  • Different traditions: Orthodox Jews keep Hebrew; Reform and Liberal Jews mix Hebrew and English.
  • Another view: use both: Hebrew for key prayers, with a translation printed beside.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for: understanding, unity or tradition? Can one service serve all of these? Who is the service mainly for?

Mission 5

Prayer from waking to sleeping

  • For Jews, prayer is not only for the synagogue: the home and the day are full of it.
  • Three times a day: morning, afternoon and evening prayers can be said at home or at work if not in the synagogue.
  • Jews face Jerusalem when they pray, as Daniel did: "his windows were open in his upper chamber toward Jerusalem" (Daniel 6:10).
  • The words come from the siddur (prayer book); many Jews own one for home use.
  • Orthodox men are bound to the fixed times; Orthodox women are not bound in the same way, though many pray daily.
Mission 5

Modeh Ani: the first words of the day

  • Said on waking, still in bed:
  • "I give thanks before You, living and eternal King, for You have returned my soul to me with compassion; great is Your faithfulness." (Modeh Ani, traditional prayer, own translation)
  • Sleep is seen as like a small death; each morning is a gift.
  • The last words echo "great is Thy faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:23).
  • It is short and simple, so it is often the first prayer children learn.
Mission 5

The Shema

  • The Shema is the Jewish declaration of faith: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three passages: Deuteronomy 6:4–9 (love God, teach the words), Deuteronomy 11:13–21 (keep the commandments), Numbers 15:37–41 (fringes, remember the mitzvot).
  • When: morning and evening, "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up" (Deuteronomy 6:7); again at bedtime.
  • How: the first line is said aloud with the right hand covering the eyes, to concentrate.
  • It is taught to young children first, and traditionally said as a Jew's last words.
Mission 5

The mezuzah

  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house, and upon thy gates." (Deuteronomy 6:9)
  • Mezuzah (Hebrew "doorpost"): a small parchment scroll, handwritten by a sofer, with Deuteronomy 6:4–9 and 11:13–21.
  • It is rolled into a case and fixed to the right-hand doorpost, in the upper part, often slanting.
  • On the doorways of the rooms in a Jewish home (not bathrooms); many Jews touch it and kiss their fingers on passing.
  • It reminds Jews that God is present in the home, and that the Torah's words go with them in and out.
  • The scroll is checked by a scribe from time to time, to make sure no letter has faded.

Mission 5

Many ways to pray

  • Set prayers from the siddur, shared by all Jews; spontaneous prayer in one's own words, at any time.
  • Blessings (berakhot): short thanks before eating, on seeing lightning, on hearing good news.
  • Grace after meals (Birkat Hamazon): after a meal with bread, from "thou shalt eat and be satisfied, and bless the LORD thy God" (Deuteronomy 8:10).
  • Teaching children: parents teach Modeh Ani, the Shema at bedtime, and blessings over food.
  • Torah study at home is itself a form of worship.

Mission 5Diversity

Different approaches to home and private prayer

  • Orthodox: fixed prayers at fixed times, mainly in Hebrew; the home is run by halakhah (kashrut, Shabbat, mezuzah on every door).
  • Reform and Liberal: prayer is valued but times and words are more personal; English is common; many keep a mezuzah and say the Shema while choosing other practices.
  • Hasidic teaching stresses kavanah (heartfelt intention) and some encourage daily spontaneous prayer alone.
  • Shared: the Shema, the mezuzah, blessings over food and teaching children.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Private prayer matters more than prayer in the synagogue."

  • For: God hears every prayer, anywhere (Psalm 55:17); the home is where faith is taught and lived; personal prayer can be more honest.
  • Against: some key prayers (Kaddish, the repeated Amidah, the Torah reading) need a minyan; praying together builds the community; the synagogue's set times keep prayer regular.
  • Different traditions: Orthodox men are expected to pray with a minyan when possible; Reform Jews often stress personal meaning.
  • Another view: they support each other: the same Shema is said in both, and each gives the other strength.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for: relationship with God, or community? Which is harder to keep up alone? Do different Jews need different things?

Mission 6

A day made holy

  • Shabbat (Sabbath, "rest") is the seventh day: from sunset on Friday to nightfall on Saturday, about 25 hours.
  • Origin: God finished creation in six days and rested on the seventh (Genesis 2:1–3).
  • So Shabbat is holy time, not a holy place: it comes to every Jew, wherever they are.
  • Two themes: menuchah (rest) and oneg (delight): Isaiah calls on Jews to "call the sabbath a delight" (Isaiah 58:13).
  • It is the only holy day named in the Ten Commandments.
  • Significance: a weekly reminder that God is Creator, that people are free, and that life is more than work.
Mission 6Read the source

Remember and observe

"Remember the sabbath day, to keep it holy." (Exodus 20:8)

"Observe the sabbath day, to keep it holy, as the LORD thy God commanded thee." (Deuteronomy 5:12)

  • Deuteronomy adds a second reason: remember that you were slaves in Egypt and God brought you out (5:15, paraphrase). Shabbat celebrates freedom.
  • Jews light two candles on Friday night, often explained as one for "remember" and one for "observe".

Mission 6

What counts as "work"?

  • The Torah forbids work (melachah) but gives few examples, such as lighting a fire (Exodus 35:3).
  • The Mishnah lists 39 kinds of creative work, taken from the work of building the Tabernacle: e.g. sowing, baking, writing, building, lighting and putting out fire, carrying between private and public places.
  • Tiring effort is not the point: carrying a pen outside can be work, while moving furniture indoors may not be.
  • Today many Orthodox authorities apply these to electricity, driving and using phones.
  • So Orthodox Jews prepare: food cooked beforehand and kept warm, lights on timers, an eruv (a boundary) that allows carrying within a neighbourhood.
  • Saving life overrides every Shabbat law (pikuach nefesh).

Mission 6

Getting ready

  • Shabbat is prepared for all week: "remember" it in advance.
  • Friday: cooking all the food, cleaning the house, setting the table with the best cloth, bathing and dressing in good clothes.
  • Challah: two plaited loaves, recalling the double portion of manna given on the sixth day in the wilderness (Exodus 16:22).
  • Many families give tzedakah (charity) before Shabbat begins.
  • Lights are set, and anything that would break Shabbat is finished before sunset.
  • Significance: preparing turns an ordinary day into a holy one; the whole family takes part.
Mission 6

Friday night at home

  • Candles: at least two, lit by a woman of the house (or anyone, if no woman is present) before sunset; she circles her hands, covers her eyes and says a blessing. Shabbat has begun.
  • Blessing the children, and songs welcoming Shabbat and the angels of peace (Shalom Aleichem).
  • Kiddush ("making holy"): a blessing over wine in a Kiddush cup, with Genesis 2:1–3, declaring the day holy.
  • Washing hands, then the blessing over the two challah loaves, uncovered, sliced and dipped in salt.
  • A festive meal, with songs (zemirot), Torah talk and Grace after meals.
  • Significance: family time, gratitude and joy; the home becomes a sanctuary and the table an altar.
Mission 6

Shabbat in the synagogue

  • Friday evening: Kabbalat Shabbat ("welcoming Shabbat"): psalms and the song Lecha Dodi, greeting Shabbat as a bride or queen; then the evening service.
  • Saturday morning: the main service: the Shema, the Amidah for Shabbat, and the reading of the weekly Torah portion from the scroll.
  • Then the haftarah, prayers and a sermon; in Orthodox synagogues an additional service (Musaf).
  • Afterwards, a kiddush in the hall: wine, food and conversation.
  • Afternoon: a short service with the first part of next week's portion; a third meal.
  • Significance: the community meets, learns and celebrates together each week.
Mission 6

Havdalah: saying goodbye

  • Havdalah ("separation") ends Shabbat on Saturday night, when three stars can be seen.
  • Wine: a full cup, overflowing, for a good week ahead.
  • Spices (besamim) in a spice box: their scent comforts the soul as Shabbat leaves.
  • Plaited candle with several wicks: the first fire of the new week; people look at the light on their fingernails.
  • A blessing praises God who separates holy and ordinary, light and darkness, Shabbat and the six working days.
  • The candle is put out in the wine; everyone wishes Shavua tov ("a good week").

Mission 6Diversity

Keeping Shabbat today

  • Orthodox: Shabbat kept in full according to halakhah: no driving, cooking, writing, money or switching electricity; walking to the synagogue.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhah binding, with some modern rulings (e.g. in the US, driving to synagogue allowed by some).
  • Reform and Liberal: keep the spirit of rest and delight; families decide which practices make the day holy for them: e.g. driving to synagogue, using a phone to speak to family, but not going to work or shopping.
  • Many Jews who do not keep every law still light candles and share a Friday-night meal.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Shabbat rules are too hard to keep in the modern world."

  • For: many jobs, sports fixtures and exams fall on Saturdays; modern life runs on electricity and phones; in winter Shabbat starts very early on Fridays.
  • Against: many Jews around the world keep it fully; timers, eruvs and Shabbat lifts help; a break from screens is valued by many non-Jews too.
  • Different views: Orthodox Jews keep the laws as given, trusting their purpose; Reform and Liberal Jews adapt them to keep the spirit of rest.
  • Another view: the difficulty is the point: Shabbat sets Jews apart and shows the week does not own them.
  • Judge it by: Is a rule "too hard" if many people keep it? What is the purpose of Shabbat? Does adapting it keep or lose that purpose?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Torah contains 613 mitzvot: positive and negative; between people and God, and between people.
  • Jews believe God gave every person free will (Deuteronomy 30:19); the yetzer ha-tov and yetzer ha-ra pull both ways, and the Torah guides the choice.
  • Maimonides (Sefer Madda): anyone can be as righteous as Moses; commands would be pointless without freedom.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all mitzvot equally binding; Reform Jews put ethical mitzvot first.

Mission 2

  • Jews believe human life is holy because each person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and life belongs to God.
  • Pikuach nefesh: saving a life overrides almost all mitzvot, including Shabbat and Yom Kippur (Leviticus 18:5; Talmud Yoma 83–85), except idolatry, murder and forbidden relations.
  • Jews agree on the principle but differ on applications: relieving suffering, organ donation, end of life.

Mission 3

  • A synagogue is a house of prayer, study and assembly; its key features are the aron hakodesh, Sefer Torah, ner tamid and bimah, with no images.
  • Orthodox synagogues have separate seating and a central bimah; Reform and Liberal synagogues have mixed seating and a front bimah.

Mission 4

  • Jews pray three times a day (Shacharit, Minchah, Ma'ariv), echoing the Temple offerings; a minyan of ten is needed for full public prayer.
  • The Amidah, the standing prayer, has praise, requests and thanks, and is said silently facing Jerusalem.
  • Orthodox services are longer and mainly in Hebrew with men leading; Reform services use more English and include women leading.

Mission 5

  • Jews pray three times a day, at home or in the synagogue, facing Jerusalem; Modeh Ani begins the day and the Shema is said morning and night.
  • The mezuzah holds the first two Shema passages; Grace after meals, blessings and teaching children make the home a place of worship.

Mission 6

  • Shabbat runs from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall, remembering creation (Genesis 2:1–3) and freedom; it is holy time for rest and delight.
  • Friday night: candles, Kiddush, challah and a family meal; the synagogue welcomes Shabbat, reads the weekly Torah portion, and Havdalah ends it.
  • Orthodox Jews avoid the 39 kinds of creative work, now applied to electricity and driving; Reform and Liberal Jews keep the spirit and choose practices.
