Mission 1

Mission 1: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Make a guess: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Mission 1

Where is the receptor?

  • A hormone acts only by binding a receptor with a complementary shape.
  • Polar hormones (proteins, peptides, adrenaline) cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer.
  • So their receptors are on the cell-surface membrane. The hormone stays outside.
Mission 1

The second messenger model

  • Adrenaline is the first messenger. It binds a receptor on the surface of a liver cell.
  • This activates the enzyme adenylate cyclase.
  • The enzyme converts ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • cAMP activates protein kinase, which phosphorylates enzymes, switching on glycogen → glucose.
  • Glucagon acts on liver cells by the same model.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next: the signal is amplified.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events after adrenaline binds to its receptor on a liver cell?
Mission check

?Inside liver cells, the enzyme phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to an inactive product. Caffeine inhibits phosphodiesterase. Liver cells are given caffeine and then a low dose of adrenaline. Compared with cells given adrenaline alone, what is predicted?

The second messenger is the amplifier: anything that keeps cAMP around turns the volume up.

Mission 2

Mission 2: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Make a guess: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Mission 2

Glucose in, glucose out

  • Cells need a steady supply of glucose as respiratory substrate. The brain depends on it most.
  • Too much glucose lowers the blood's water potential; too little starves cells of substrate.
  • Raises blood glucose: digesting and absorbing carbohydrate; the liver releasing glucose from glycogen or making new glucose.
  • Lowers blood glucose: respiration (e.g. exercise); cells storing glucose as glycogen or fat.
  • Control: two pancreatic hormones, insulin and glucagon, by negative feedback.
Mission 2

The pancreas: two tissues

  • Most of the pancreas is exocrine: clusters of cells (acini) secrete digestive enzymes into a duct.
  • Scattered among them: the islets of Langerhans, the endocrine tissue.
  • β (beta) cells secrete insulin. α (alpha) cells secrete glucagon.
  • Islet cells secrete straight into capillaries: no duct.
  • The islet cells are both the receptors and the source of the hormones: no signal from the brain is needed.
Quick check
?Blood glucose concentration rises after a meal. What happens next?
Mission 2

Three liver words

  • Glycogenesis: glucose → glycogen. ("genesis" = making)
  • Glycogenolysis: glycogen → glucose. ("lysis" = splitting)
  • Gluconeogenesis: new glucose from non-carbohydrates: glycerol and amino acids.
  • All three happen in the liver. Muscle also stores glycogen, but for its own use.
  • Say: "glycogen is the store; glucagon is the hormone" — never mix them.
Mission 2

Glucose too high: insulin

  • β cells detect the rise and secrete insulin into the blood.
  • Insulin binds receptors on target cells: liver, muscle and adipose (fat) cells.
  • In muscle and adipose cells, more glucose transporter channel proteins are put into the cell-surface membrane, so they take up more glucose.
  • Insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fat.
  • Cells respire more glucose.
  • Blood glucose falls → β cells secrete less insulin: negative feedback.
  • Say: "insulin makes cells take up glucose" — not: "insulin breaks down glucose".
Mission 2

Glucose too low: glucagon

  • α cells detect the fall and secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon binds receptors on liver cells.
  • It activates enzymes that convert glycogen → glucose.
  • It also activates gluconeogenesis: glucose from glycerol and amino acids.
  • Glucose leaves the liver cells; blood glucose rises → α cells secrete less glucagon.
  • Glucagon acts through the second messenger model: adenylate cyclase → cAMP → protein kinase (see Mission 1).
  • Adrenaline binds receptors on liver cells too, activating glycogen → glucose by the same model, before exercise or danger.
Quick check
?Which option correctly pairs a hormone with its effect on target cells?
Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, liver cells cannot break down glycogen. Their glucagon receptors work normally. Which prediction for a person with this condition after an overnight fast is best supported?

Feedback needs every link: sensor, hormone, receptor and effector. Break the effector and the hormone shouts into silence.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Make a guess: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Mission 3

Two types of diabetes

  • Diabetes mellitus: blood glucose stays too high because the insulin loop fails.
  • Type 1: the immune system destroys the β cells (autoimmune). Little or no insulin. Often starts in childhood; onset is usually quick.
  • Type 2: insulin is made, but target cells respond less to it: insulin resistance. Later, β cells may secrete less. Usually develops slowly in adults.
  • Type 2 risk factors: obesity (especially around the abdomen), physical inactivity, a high-sugar, energy-dense diet, increasing age, family history.
  • Type 1 is not caused by diet or lifestyle.
  • AQA writes type I and type II.
  • Say: "in type 2, target cells respond less to insulin" — not: "type 2 is a milder type 1".
Quick check
?A lean 12-year-old develops very high blood glucose within a few weeks; antibodies against pancreatic islet cells are found in the blood and blood insulin is very low. A 55-year-old with obesity has had slowly rising fasting blood glucose for years, with a high blood insulin concentration. Which interpretation is best supported?
Mission 3

Treatment and prevention

  • Type 1: insulin by injection or pump, matched to carbohydrate eaten and exercise. Regular blood glucose monitoring.
  • Type 2: first diet (less sugar and refined carbohydrate), exercise and weight loss: these improve insulin sensitivity.
  • Then drugs, e.g. metformin (the liver releases less glucose; cells respond better). Insulin is added later if needed.
  • Prevention of type 2: healthy body mass, regular activity, a balanced diet. Type 1 cannot be prevented this way.
  • AQA: "control by insulin and/or manipulation of the diet".

Mission 3

Who should act?

  • Health advisers: WHO says keep free sugars under 10% of energy, ideally under 5%; UK advice (SACN, 2015) is no more than 5%.
  • They back sugar taxes (UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy, 2018), clearer labels and less marketing to children.
  • Food industry: has reformulated some products to cut sugar.
  • It argues that weight gain reflects total energy and inactivity, not one ingredient, and prefers voluntary action and consumer choice. It notes costs to jobs and prices.
  • To evaluate: Who funded the evidence? Is it peer-reviewed? Does it show cause or only correlation? How large is the sample?

Mission 3Practical

Glucose in urine (Required practical 11)

  • When blood glucose is very high, the kidneys cannot reabsorb it all: glucose appears in urine.
  • Make a dilution series of glucose of known concentrations, plus distilled water (0).
  • Add the same excess volume of Benedict's reagent to each; heat in a water bath at the same temperature for the same time.
  • Remove the precipitate; measure the absorbance of the remaining blue solution with a colorimeter, zeroed with distilled water.
  • Plot a calibration curve (absorbance against glucose concentration). Read off the concentration of the unknown "urine" sample.
  • Key control: everything except glucose concentration kept the same (full method in an earlier lesson).
Mission check

?In a strain of mice, the liver, muscle and adipose cells have no working insulin receptors. Their β cells are normal. Which prediction after a meal is best supported?

High glucose with high insulin means the message is sent but not received: the signature of insulin resistance.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Make a guess: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Mission 4

Two jobs

  • Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism, such as urea (made in the liver from excess amino acids).
  • Osmoregulation: controlling the water potential of the blood.
  • The kidney does both, but they are separate jobs.
  • Faeces are not excreted: undigested food never entered metabolism. Removing it is egestion.
  • Say: "excretion removes metabolic waste" — not: "excretion removes anything the body doesn't need".
Mission 4

The kidney, outside and in

  • Blood in: renal artery. Blood out: renal vein.
  • Outer cortex. Inner medulla, divided into cone-shaped pyramids.
  • Urine drains into the pelvis, then down the ureter to the bladder, and out through the urethra.
  • Each kidney holds about a million nephrons: the working units.
Mission 4

One nephron

  • Bowman's capsule cups a knot of capillaries, the glomerulus. Both sit in the cortex.
  • Then: proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) → loop of Henle (dips into the medulla) → distal convoluted tubule (DCT) → collecting duct.
  • Blood: afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → capillaries around the tubules.
  • Fluid entering the capsule is glomerular filtrate. What reaches the end of the collecting duct is urine.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes osmoregulation from excretion?
Mission 4

Ultrafiltration

  • The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole → high hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus.
  • This pressure forces fluid out through a three-layer filter: capillary endothelium (with pores), basement membrane, and podocytes in the capsule wall.
  • The filter sorts by size. Through: water, glucose, amino acids, urea, ions.
  • Stay in the blood: blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins (too large).
  • So filtrate = plasma minus its proteins. An adult makes about 180 dm³ a day.
  • Say: "filtered by size, under pressure" — not: "the glomerulus picks out the waste".
Quick check
?Which statement about ultrafiltration in the glomerulus is correct?
Mission 4

Selective reabsorption in the PCT

  • 1: Na⁺–K⁺ pumps in the cell membrane facing the blood use ATP to pump Na⁺ out of the cell. Na⁺ inside stays low.
  • 2: Na⁺ diffuses into the cell from the filtrate through co-transporter proteins, carrying glucose (or amino acids) with it, against glucose's own gradient.
  • 3: Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • Solutes moving into the blood lower its water potential, so water follows by osmosis.
  • In the PCT: all glucose and amino acids, and about two-thirds of the water and Na⁺, are reabsorbed.
  • Say: "glucose is co-transported with sodium ions" — not: "glucose diffuses back into the blood".
Quick check
?A drug stops the sodium–potassium pumps in the cells lining the proximal convoluted tubule. What happens to the reabsorption of glucose from the filtrate?
Mission 4

PCT cells: built for reabsorption

  • Microvilli (a brush border) on the side facing the filtrate.
  • Basal invaginations (infoldings) on the side facing the blood.
  • Both add membrane without adding volume → high surface area to volume ratio and room for many co-transporters and pumps.
  • Many mitochondria: ATP for the Na⁺–K⁺ pumps.
  • Close to capillaries: short diffusion distance to the blood.

Mission check

?Fluid was sampled from Bowman's capsule and from the end of the proximal convoluted tubule of a healthy kidney. Glucose was 5 mmol dm⁻³ in the capsule and undetectable at the end of the tubule. Urea was 5 mmol dm⁻³ in the capsule and higher at the end of the tubule. Which explanation is correct?

Filter everything small, then take back what is useful. That is how 180 dm³ of filtrate a day becomes about 1.5 dm³ of urine.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Make a guess: A kangaroo rat can live its whole life without drinking. How do its kidneys hold on to water?

Mission 5

Keeping blood water in balance

  • Osmoregulation: control of the water potential of the blood.
  • Sweating, salty food, not drinking: the blood is short of water.
  • Drinking a lot: the blood has surplus water.
  • The kidney responds by changing how much water it reabsorbs: a small volume of concentrated urine, or a large volume of dilute urine.
  • The loop of Henle makes concentrated urine possible. ADH controls how much water is kept.
Mission 5

The loop of Henle builds a salty medulla

  • 1: Ascending limb: impermeable to water. It actively transports Na⁺ (with Cl⁻) out of the filtrate into the medulla tissue.
  • This keeps the water potential of the medulla low.
  • 2: Descending limb: permeable to water. Water leaves by osmosis, so the filtrate is most concentrated at the tip of the loop.
  • 3: The collecting duct runs down through this salty medulla, so water can be drawn out of it by osmosis.
  • Two limbs side by side, flowing opposite ways: a countercurrent multiplier.
  • Longer loops → saltier deep medulla → more concentrated urine possible.
  • Say: "the ascending limb pumps sodium ions out" — not: "the loop pumps water out".
Quick check
?How does the loop of Henle make it possible for the kidney to produce concentrated urine?
Mission 5

The adjustable part: DCT and collecting duct

  • Filtrate leaving the loop is dilute.
  • In the DCT and collecting duct, water can leave by osmosis into the salty medulla, then into the blood.
  • How much leaves depends on how permeable their walls are to water.
  • That permeability is set by ADH (antidiuretic hormone).
Mission 5

ADH: the control loop

  • 1: Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus detect a fall in blood water potential.
  • 2: ADH is made in the hypothalamus, then stored in and released from the posterior pituitary into the blood. More ADH is released.
  • 3: ADH binds receptors on collecting duct cells. Vesicles containing aquaporins (water channels) fuse with the membrane facing the filtrate.
  • 4: More water leaves the duct by osmosis → small volume of concentrated urine. As blood water is restored, less ADH is released: negative feedback.
  • Surplus water: less ADH → aquaporins taken back into vesicles → large volume of dilute urine.
  • Say: "ADH makes the duct walls more permeable to water" — not: "ADH pumps water back into the blood".

Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, the collecting duct cells cannot respond to ADH, although the hypothalamus and pituitary work normally. Which prediction is correct?

The loop builds the gradient; ADH decides how much water uses it. Break the response and the feedback loop keeps shouting.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Polar hormones bind cell-surface receptors and stay outside the cell.
  • Adrenaline's message is relayed and amplified inside by cAMP and protein kinase.

Mission 2

  • High glucose → β cells → insulin → cells take up glucose and make glycogen; low glucose → α cells → glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen. Both are negative feedback.
  • Liver: glycogenesis, glycogenolysis, gluconeogenesis.

Mission 3

  • Type 1: autoimmune loss of β cells, no insulin, treated with insulin. Type 2: target cells respond less to insulin, treated first by diet, exercise and weight loss.
  • World diabetes prevalence nearly doubled from 1980 to 2014 (WHO); a rise in a risk factor alongside is a correlation, not proof.

Mission 4

  • Ultrafiltration: blood pressure forces water and small solutes through the glomerular filter; cells and plasma proteins stay behind.
  • Selective reabsorption in the PCT: Na⁺ gradient drives glucose co-transport; water follows by osmosis; microvilli, infoldings and mitochondria make it fast.

Mission 5

  • The ascending limb actively transports Na⁺ out, making the medulla salty; the collecting duct loses water to it by osmosis when ADH has inserted aquaporins.
  • Osmoreceptors in the hypothalamus → ADH from the posterior pituitary → aquaporins → more water reabsorbed; negative feedback switches it off.
