Mission 1

Mission 1: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Make a guess: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Mission 1

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA). Its position on a chromosome is its locus.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene, at the same locus.
  • Body cells are diploid: two copies of each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has, e.g. Tt.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable traits. It results from the genotype and the environment.
  • Gametes are haploid: one allele of each gene. Fertilisation makes a diploid zygote with one allele from each parent.
  • Say: "two alleles of the gene for height" — not: "a gene for tall and a gene for short".
Mission 1

Dominant and recessive

  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present. Capital letter: T.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when homozygous (tt). Lower-case letter: t.
  • So TT and Tt plants are equally tall.
  • Why? T codes for a functional protein; one copy makes enough of it.
  • t usually codes for a non-functional or absent product. It is still there, and still passed on.
  • Say: "the recessive allele has no effect in the heterozygote" — not: "the dominant allele switches it off".
Quick check
?In peas the allele T for tall stems is dominant to the allele t for dwarf stems, and TT and Tt plants are equally tall. What is the reason that the heterozygote is not shorter than the homozygous dominant plant?
Mission 1

The monohybrid cross

  • Show every step in a fully labelled genetic diagram: parental phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes, phenotypes, ratio.
  • P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf). Gametes: all T; all t. F1: all Tt, all tall.
  • F2: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes T and t gametes in equal numbers.
  • A Punnett square gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
  • The two alleles separate into different gametes. That is the law of segregation.
  • This is Mendel's first law: the law of segregation of factors.
  • The hook: both tall parents were Tt. The short plant is the ¼ that got t from each.

Mission 1

A recessive disorder in a family

  • Some human disorders are caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene.
  • Heterozygotes are unaffected carriers. Two carriers can have an affected child.
  • Cystic fibrosis: caused by recessive alleles of the CFTR gene.
  • Carrier × carrier: each child has a ¼ chance of being affected.
  • Each fertilisation is independent. Earlier children do not change the odds.
Quick check
?Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene. Two parents who are both heterozygous for this gene have three children, all of whom have cystic fibrosis. Which statement correctly describes the probability that their fourth child will have cystic fibrosis?
Mission 1

The test cross

  • A tall plant could be TT or Tt. You cannot tell by looking.
  • Test cross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt) plant.
  • tt passes on only t, which masks nothing. So each offspring shows the allele it got from the tall plant.
  • All tall offspring: the plant is probably TT.
  • Any dwarf offspring: the plant must be Tt. Expect about 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
Quick check
?To find out whether a purple-flowered pea plant is homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp), a breeder crosses it with a white-flowered plant (pp). Why is a white-flowered plant used for this test cross?
Mission check

?In mice, the allele B for black fur is dominant to the allele b for brown fur. A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse. Of the 9 offspring, 5 are black and 4 are brown. Which conclusion is correct?

Like the short pea plant in the hook, the brown pups reveal a recessive allele that was there all along, hidden in a heterozygote.

Mission 2: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Make a guess: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Mission 2

Genes plus surroundings

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: its observable traits.
  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment.
  • Same genotype, different surroundings → different phenotypes. That's the cuttings.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles of a gene. Heterozygous: two different alleles.
Quick check
?Identical twins have the same genotype. Two identical twins were raised apart, one in a sunny country on a good diet and the other in a cloudy country on a poor diet. Which of these traits are expected to be the same in both twins as adults: ABO blood group, adult height and skin colour?
Mission 2

Where variation comes from

  • Genetic sources: mutation (the primary source of new alleles), then meiosis and random fertilisation (new combinations).
  • Environmental sources: in animals, diet; in plants, light and minerals.
  • So a population varies widely: many allele combinations × many environments.
Quick check
?Seeds of one genetically uniform variety of bean were sown in two trays. Tray X was kept in darkness; tray Y in light. After two weeks, the seedlings in X had long, thin, pale stems and small yellow leaves. The seedlings in Y were short and green. Which statement is correct?

Mission 2

Many genes, one smooth curve

  • Polygenic inheritance: many genes, at different loci, affect one trait.
  • Their effects are additive: each contributing allele adds a little.
  • Worked example: three genes (A/a, B/b, C/c). Each capital allele adds one unit of height.
  • Cross AaBbCc × AaBbCc. Offspring carry 0–6 capital alleles.
  • Step 1: one gene alone → 3 classes, 1 : 2 : 1.
  • Step 2: two genes → 5 classes, 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1.
  • Step 3: three genes → 7 classes, 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1 (out of 64).
  • Step 4: add the environment. Each class spreads out; the gaps fill in → a smooth bell curve.
  • More genes → more classes → closer to continuous.
Quick check
?Human skin colour varies continuously across a population, whereas ABO blood group falls into four separate classes. Which explanation of this difference is correct?
Mission check

?Two true-breeding varieties of a crop plant differ in seed mass. Crossing them gives F1 plants with seeds of intermediate mass, all very similar. The F1 are interbred. Grown in the same field as the F1, the F2 show a wide, bell-shaped spread of seed mass, with very few at either parental extreme. Which explanation fits best?

Many genes plus the environment: that is why genetically identical cuttings still differ, and why a whole population varies even more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Make a guess: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Mission 3

Two genes on different chromosomes

  • A plant is RrYy. R and Y are on different chromosomes.
  • In meiosis I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the other pair.
  • So R is just as likely to go with Y as with y.
  • Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry in equal numbers.
  • This is Mendel's second law: the law of independent assortment.
  • Each gamete still gets one allele of each gene (segregation).
  • Genes on the same chromosome are linked: their alleles tend to pass into gametes together, so they do not assort independently. Crossing over in prophase I can sometimes separate them.
Mission 3

Worked example: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Peas: R round > r wrinkled; Y yellow > y green. F1 RrYy × RrYy.

  • Each parent makes 4 gamete types: a 4 × 4 Punnett square, 16 cells.
  • Group the cells by phenotype: 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.
  • Short cut: each gene gives 3 : 1. P(round and yellow) = ¾ × ¾ = 9/16.
  • 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is a phenotype ratio. The 16 cells hold 9 different genotypes.
  • Mendel's real F2: 315 : 108 : 101 : 32. Close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (tested in a later lesson).
Mission 3

The dihybrid test cross: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

  • Cross the double heterozygote with a double homozygous recessive: RrYy × rryy.
  • rryy gives only ry gametes. So each offspring shows the gamete from the heterozygote.
  • Four gamete types, equally likely → 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
  • Round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green.
Quick check
?Two pea plants heterozygous for two unlinked genes (RrYy) are crossed. Why is the expected phenotypic ratio among their offspring 9:3:3:1?
Mission check

?In guinea pigs, black fur (B) is dominant to white (b), and rough coat (R) is dominant to smooth (r). The genes are on different chromosomes. A BbRr guinea pig is crossed with a Bbrr guinea pig. What fraction of the offspring are expected to be black and smooth?

Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, so you can multiply their separate probabilities.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Make a guess: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Mission 4

Sex chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 22 pairs of autosomes + one pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Female: XX. Male: XY.
  • Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries X or Y. So the sperm decides the sex of the zygote.
  • The X carries far more genes than the much smaller Y.
  • A gene on a sex chromosome is sex-linked. Most are on the X: X-linked.
  • A male has one copy of each X-linked gene. A female has two.
Mission 4

Writing an X-linked cross

  • Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting), Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y carries no allele.
  • Haemophilia: a recessive X-linked allele; a clotting factor is missing, so blood clots slowly.
  • Carrier mother × unaffected father: XᴴXʰ × XᴴY.
  • Gametes: Xᴴ or Xʰ · Xᴴ or Y.
  • Offspring: XᴴXᴴ, XᴴXʰ (carrier), XᴴY, XʰY (affected).
  • Daughters: none affected, ½ carriers. Sons: ½ affected.
  • Label the diagram fully: phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, offspring, sexes.
Quick check
?A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia (XᴴXʰ) has children with a man who does not have haemophilia (XᴴY). Their first child is a boy. What is the probability that he has haemophilia?
Mission 4

Why sons are affected more often

  • A male's one X comes from his mother. One recessive allele is enough: XʰY is affected.
  • A female needs two: XʰXʰ. That needs an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
  • An affected father passes his X to every daughter (all carriers) and to no son.
  • So X-linked recessive traits never pass father → son.
  • Say: "a son inherits his X chromosome from his mother" — not: "haemophilia passes from father to son".
Mission 4

Dominant sex-linked characters

  • Some X-linked alleles are dominant. Example: X-linked hypophosphataemia (a form of rickets).
  • One copy is enough, in either sex.
  • Affected father (XᴬY) × unaffected mother (XᵃXᵃ): all daughters affected, no sons.
  • Affected heterozygous mother (XᴬXᵃ): each child, son or daughter, has a ½ chance.
  • Clue: affected fathers have only affected daughters.
Mission 4

Reading a pedigree

  • Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected. Generations I, II, III; individuals numbered left to right.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is recessive; both parents carry it.
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → the allele is dominant.
  • Recessive allele + affected daughter of an unaffected father → not X-linked, so autosomal.
  • A father passes his X to no son. If a son can only have got the allele from his father, the gene is not X-linked.
Quick check
?A man and a woman who are both unaffected by a genetic disorder have three children: an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected daughter later has four children with an unaffected man, and all four are unaffected. What can be deduced about the disorder?
Mission 4Read the data

Queen Victoria's descendants

Selected members of Queen Victoria's family. Shaded = had haemophilia; dot = carrier (inferred from her children).

  • I: Victoria (carrier) × Albert (unaffected).
  • II: Edward VII (unaffected), Alice (carrier), Leopold (affected), Beatrice (carrier).
  • III: Alice's son Friedrich (affected) and daughter Alexandra (carrier); Leopold's son Charles Edward (unaffected) and daughter Alice (carrier); Beatrice's son Leopold (affected).
  • IV: Alexandra's son Alexei (affected); Leopold's daughter Alice's son Rupert (affected).

  • DNA from the Russian royal remains (2009) found the mutation: an X-linked clotting-factor gene, haemophilia B.
Quick check
?From the earlier screen: Prince Leopold had haemophilia; his wife did not carry the allele. Which statement about their two children, Charles Edward and Alice, is correct?
Mission 4

Predicting from a pedigree

  • Step 1: dominant or recessive? Look for unaffected parents with an affected child.
  • Step 2: autosomal or X-linked? Check affected daughters' fathers and affected fathers' sons.
  • Step 3: write the genotypes you know (affected recessives; carriers revealed by children).
  • Step 4: use a cross to give a probability for the next child.
  • Keep "sex-linked" for genes on sex chromosomes. Genes linked on the same autosome are a different idea (see Mission 3).
Mission check

?In birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W: males are ZZ and females are ZW. In a hypothetical species of bird, a feather trait is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on the Z chromosome; the W chromosome carries no allele of this gene. In a population in which this allele is present, which sex is expected to show the trait more often, and why?

It is not about being male. It is about having only one copy of the chromosome that carries the gene.

Mission 5: How can one gene decide whether another gene gets any say at all?

Make a guess: How can one gene decide whether another gene gets any say at all?

Mission 5

Genes that interact

  • Many traits depend on more than one gene.
  • Epistasis: the alleles of one gene affect, or mask, the expression of a different gene at another locus.
  • CCEA calls this gene interaction, which includes epistasis.
  • Often the two genes code for enzymes in one pathway: precursor → (enzyme 1) → intermediate → (enzyme 2) → pigment.
  • No working enzyme 1? No pigment, whatever the alleles of gene 2.
  • Say: "dominance" for alleles of one gene; "epistasis" for two genes.
Mission 5

Recessive epistasis: Labrador coats

  • Gene B: B black pigment > b brown (chocolate) pigment.
  • Gene E: E lets dark pigment into the hair. ee: no dark pigment in the hair, so the coat is yellow.
  • ee masks gene B. Because two recessive alleles do the masking, this is recessive epistasis.
  • BE_ → black · bbE_ → chocolate · Bee or bbee → yellow.
  • (_ = either allele.)
Mission 5

Worked cross: 9 : 3 : 4

  • BbEe × BbEe. Genes on different chromosomes.
  • 16-cell Punnett square, as for any dihybrid cross.
  • BE_ = 9 black. bbE_ = 3 chocolate. Bee = 3 and bbee = 1: both yellow.
  • So 9 black : 3 chocolate : 4 yellow.
  • Clue: 3 phenotypes, and the ratio adds to 16 with a 4 class.
  • The hook: two black BbEe dogs can have a yellow (ee) puppy.
Quick check
?A black Labrador with genotype BbEe is mated with a yellow Labrador with genotype bbee. What ratio of puppies is expected?
Mission 5

Dominant epistasis: squash fruit

  • Gene W: a dominant allele W blocks pigment: fruit is white, whatever gene Y says.
  • Gene Y (works only in ww plants): Y yellow > y green.
  • W_ _ _ → white · wwY_ → yellow · wwyy → green.
  • WwYy × WwYy → 12 white : 3 yellow : 1 green.
  • One dominant allele does the masking, so this is dominant epistasis.
  • Another dominant-epistasis ratio: 13 : 3, when the double recessive looks the same as the masked plants.
Mission 5Read the data

Which ratio?

  • One sixteenth of 192 = 12.

  • So about 12 : 3 : 1: dominant epistasis.
  • Whether the gap from exactly 12 : 3 : 1 is just chance is a χ² question.
Quick check
?For the 192 plants in the earlier screen, how many yellow-fruited plants are expected if the ratio is exactly 12 : 3 : 1?
Mission 5

Spotting the ratio

  • Two genes, F2 from double heterozygotes, but fewer than four phenotypes → interaction.
  • 9 : 3 : 4 → recessive epistasis (one homozygous recessive masks the other gene).
  • 12 : 3 : 1 → dominant epistasis.
  • 13 : 3 → dominant epistasis, with the double recessive in the larger class.
  • 9 : 7 → complementary genes: a dominant allele of both genes is needed (sweet pea flower colour).
  • 15 : 1 → duplicate genes: a dominant allele of either gene is enough.
  • Every one is 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 with classes merged. Totals are still 16.
Mission check

?In chickens, the dominant allele I of one gene stops any feather pigment forming. At a second gene, C allows pigment and cc gives white feathers. Only iiC_ birds are coloured. A white IiCc bird is crossed with a white iicc bird. What ratio of chicks is expected?

Gene I decides whether gene C gets any say: in I_ chicks, C's alleles are never seen.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Alleles are forms of one gene; genotype + environment → phenotype; one dominant allele is enough for the dominant phenotype.
  • Heterozygote × heterozygote → 3 : 1; a test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals a hidden recessive allele.

Mission 2

  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment; genetically identical organisms still differ.
  • Continuous variation comes from many genes with additive effects plus the environment; discontinuous variation from one or a few genes.

Mission 3

  • Unlinked genes assort independently: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 from two double heterozygotes, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 from a test cross.

Mission 4

  • X-linked recessive alleles show in any male who carries one (one X), but in females only when homozygous; sons get their X from their mother.
  • Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; then an affected daughter with an unaffected father → autosomal.

Mission 5

  • Epistasis: one gene masks the expression of another gene; recessive epistasis gives 9 : 3 : 4, dominant epistasis 12 : 3 : 1 or 13 : 3.
  • Each epistatic ratio is 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 with classes merged, so the total is still 16.
