Mission 1

"Music helps you revise." How could you test that?

Make a guess: "Music helps you revise." How could you test that?

Research methods · hypotheses and variables

From a claim to a prediction

  • An aim says what a study will investigate, e.g. whether music affects memory.
  • A hypothesis is a precise, testable prediction.
  • The alternative hypothesis predicts a difference or relationship: "Students recall more words in silence than with music."
  • The null hypothesis predicts no difference or relationship: "There will be no difference in recall with music and in silence."
Research methods · hypotheses and variables

What you change and what you measure

  • The independent variable (IV) is what the researcher changes (manipulates) to create the conditions.
  • The dependent variable (DV) is what the researcher measures.
  • Operationalise each variable: define it precisely so it can be measured.
Music studyOperationalised
IVMusic playing or silence while learning a word list
DVNumber of words recalled out of 20 in two minutes
Research methods · hypotheses and variables

What you keep the same

  • An extraneous variable is any other variable that could affect the DV.
  • If it isn't controlled, you can't tell whether the IV caused the change in the DV.
  • Situational variables come from the setting: noise, time of day, the word list.
  • Participant variables come from the people: age, memory ability, tiredness.
  • Control them by keeping them the same for every participant, e.g. the same room, list and time limit.

Mission check

?"Music helps you revise." How could you test that?

Turn the claim into a testable hypothesis. Make music the IV (music or silence), operationalise the DV (e.g. words recalled out of 20), and keep extraneous variables such as the room, word list and time limit the same, so any difference in recall can be put down to the music.

Mission 2

Lab or real classroom: which experiment's result should you trust?

Make a guess: Lab or real classroom: which experiment's result should you trust?

Research methods · types of experiment

The laboratory experiment

  • The researcher manipulates the IV and measures the DV in a controlled, artificial setting.
  • Participants know they are taking part.
  • Strengths: extraneous variables are tightly controlled, so cause and effect can be shown. Standardised procedures make it easy to replicate.
  • Weaknesses: artificial tasks may not reflect real life (low ecological validity). Participants may guess the aim and change their behaviour (demand characteristics).
Research methods · types of experiment

Field and natural experiments

TypeWho controls the IV?Setting
LaboratoryThe researcher manipulates itArtificial and controlled
FieldThe researcher manipulates itThe participants' natural, everyday setting
NaturalIt varies naturally; the researcher doesn't change itUsually real life
  • In a field experiment, participants often don't know they are in a study.
  • In a natural experiment, the researcher compares groups that already differ, e.g. pupils at schools that already start the day at different times.
Research methods · types of experiment

Trading control for realism

  • Reliability means consistency: repeat the study and get similar results.
  • Validity means the study measures what it intends and applies beyond the study.
  • Field: higher ecological validity and fewer demand characteristics, but less control and harder to replicate. Participants often can't give consent.
  • Natural: can study IVs that would be unethical or impossible to manipulate, but participants aren't randomly allocated, so participant variables may explain results.

Mission check

?Lab or real classroom: which experiment's result should you trust?

Trust each for different things. The lab's tight control makes it more likely that the IV caused the result, and it is easy to replicate, but its artificial setting lowers ecological validity. The classroom field experiment is closer to real life, but extraneous variables such as noise could explain its result.

Mission 3

Same people in both conditions, or different people: which is better?

Make a guess: Same people in both conditions, or different people: which is better?

Research methods · experimental designs

Different people in each condition

  • In an independent measures design, each participant takes part in only one condition.
  • Strengths: no order effects, and participants are less likely to guess the aim.
  • Weaknesses: participant variables: the groups may differ (e.g. one has better memories), and that could cause the result. More participants are needed.
  • Control: random allocation to conditions (e.g. drawing names from a hat) spreads participant variables across the groups.
Research methods · experimental designs

The same people in every condition

  • In a repeated measures design, each participant takes part in every condition.
  • Strengths: no participant variables between conditions, and fewer participants are needed.
  • Weaknesses: order effects: a practice effect (better second time) or a fatigue effect (worse through tiredness or boredom). Participants may guess the aim (demand characteristics).
  • Control: counterbalancing: half do condition A then B, half do B then A, so order effects balance out.
Research methods · experimental designs

Matched pairs

  • In a matched pairs design, participants are paired on a variable that could affect the DV, e.g. a memory test score or age.
  • One member of each pair does each condition.
  • Strengths: reduces participant variables, and there are no order effects.
  • Weaknesses: matching takes time and is never perfect. If one person drops out, the pair is lost.
Research methods · experimental designs

Keeping the procedure fair

  • Standardised procedures: every participant gets the same tasks, materials, timings and setting, and standardised instructions (the same words).
  • Randomisation: chance decides things like the order of word lists, so the researcher's choices can't bias the results.
  • Single-blind: participants don't know their condition, reducing demand characteristics.
  • Double-blind: neither participants nor the researcher testing them knows, so expectations can't bias results.

?Same people in both conditions, or different people: which is better?

Each choice brings its own problem. Different people risk participant variables, reduced by random allocation. The same people risk order effects and demand characteristics, reduced by counterbalancing. Matched pairs avoids order effects and reduces participant variables, but matching takes time.

Mission 4

You can't test every UK teenager. Are 20 from your class enough?

Make a guess: You can't test every UK teenager. Are 20 from your class enough?

Research methods · sampling

Population and sample

  • The target population is the whole group a researcher wants to find out about, e.g. UK 13 to 16-year-olds.
  • The sample is the group who actually take part.
  • A representative sample has the same mix of characteristics as the target population.
  • Then the findings can be generalised: applied to the whole target population.
Research methods · sampling

Random and opportunity sampling

MethodHow it worksStrengthWeakness
RandomEvery member of the target population has an equal chance of being picked, e.g. a computer picks names from a full listThe researcher can't bias who is chosenNeeds a full list; can still be unrepresentative by chance; people chosen may refuse
OpportunityUse people who are available and willing at the timeQuick and easyUnlikely to be representative; the researcher may pick people who look approachable
  • Pulling names from a hat is random only if every member's name is in the hat.
Research methods · sampling

Stratified sampling

  • Stratified: identify the subgroups (strata) in the population, e.g. year groups, then randomly pick from each in the same proportions as the population.
  • Highly representative of the subgroups, but slow, and it needs detailed information about the population.
Research methods · sampling

People who put themselves forward

  • In volunteer sampling, people choose to take part, e.g. by replying to an advert, poster or online post.
  • Strength: easy, and participants are willing. An advert can reach many people.
  • Weakness: people who volunteer tend to share traits, such as being more helpful or more interested in the topic, so the sample may be unrepresentative (volunteer bias).

?You can't test every UK teenager. Are 20 from your class enough?

Twenty classmates are an opportunity sample of the same age, school and area, so they probably don't represent all UK teenagers and the findings may not generalise. A fairer sample gives everyone in the target population an equal chance of being picked (random sampling), or matches the population's subgroups (stratified sampling).

Mission 5

How do you study a crowd, a childhood, or what people really think?

Make a guess: How do you study a crowd, a childhood, or what people really think?

Research methods · non-experimental methods

Questionnaires

  • A questionnaire is a set of written questions, on paper or online.
  • It can reach a large sample quickly and cheaply, and everyone gets the same questions.
Question typeExampleData it gives
Closed"Do you revise with music? Yes / No"Quantitative (numbers): easy to analyse, less detail
Open"Describe how you feel before an exam."Qualitative (words): rich detail, harder to analyse
Research methods · non-experimental methods

Interviews

  • An interview asks questions face to face, or by phone or video.
  • Structured: the same questions in the same order for everyone. Easy to compare and repeat, but no follow-up.
  • Unstructured: like a conversation; the interviewer follows up answers. Rich detail, but hard to compare.
  • Semi-structured: set questions, plus follow-up questions where useful.
  • Interviewees and questionnaire respondents may give socially desirable answers: ones that make them look good.
Research methods · non-experimental methods

Observations

  • In an observation, researchers watch and record behaviour as it happens.
  • Categories of behaviour are decided in advance, e.g. "pushes", "shares a toy", and tallied each time they occur.
  • Strength: shows what people actually do, not what they say. Weakness: shows what happens, not why.
Research methods · non-experimental methods

Case studies

  • A case study is an in-depth study of one person or a small group, often over a long time.
  • It combines methods (interviews, observations, records) and mainly gives qualitative data from a small sample.
  • Strengths: rich detail; can study rare cases, e.g. an unusual brain injury.
  • Weaknesses: findings may not generalise; hard to replicate; relies on memory and the researcher's interpretation.

Mission 6

More ice creams sold, more swimmers rescued. Is ice cream to blame?

Make a guess: More ice creams sold, more swimmers rescued. Is ice cream to blame?

Research methods · correlation

Two things that change together

  • A correlation is an association between two variables.
  • Both variables are measured using quantitative data. Neither is manipulated, so there is no IV or DV.
  • A scatter diagram shows a correlation: one variable on each axis and one dot for each person or case.
Research methods · correlation

Positive, negative or zero

  • Positive correlation: as one variable increases, so does the other, e.g. if more hours of revision went with higher test scores.
  • Negative correlation: as one variable increases, the other decreases, e.g. if more screen time went with less sleep.
  • Zero correlation: no relationship, e.g. shoe size and test score.
  • The closer the dots lie to a straight line, the stronger the correlation.
Research methods · correlation

Linked, but not proved to cause

  • A correlation can't show that one variable causes the other.
  • A third variable may cause both, or the cause may run the other way.
  • Strengths: can study links that would be unethical or impossible to manipulate; quick, often using existing data; suggests ideas to test in experiments.
  • Weaknesses: no cause and effect; an unmeasured third variable may explain the link.

Mission check

?More ice creams sold, more swimmers rescued. Is ice cream to blame?

Not on this evidence. It is a positive correlation, and correlation doesn't show causation. A third variable, hot sunny weather, could make people buy more ice cream and also swim more, so more swimmers need rescuing.

Mission 7

Psychologists have lied to participants. When is that allowed?

Make a guess: Psychologists have lied to participants. When is that allowed?

Research methods · ethics

The rules psychologists follow

  • An ethical issue arises when a study could harm participants or ignore their rights.
  • UK psychologists follow the British Psychological Society (BPS) Code of Ethics and Conduct.
  • Its four principles are respect, competence, responsibility and integrity.
  • Researchers must weigh what a study could teach us against any cost to participants, and protect participants first.
Research methods · ethics

Consent and deception

  • Informed consent: participants agree to take part after being told what the study involves. Under-16s also need consent from a parent or guardian.
  • Deception: misleading participants or hiding the true aim.
  • Deception is acceptable only if telling the aim would spoil the results, it won't cause distress, and participants are debriefed afterwards.
Research methods · ethics

Harm, withdrawal and confidentiality

  • Protection of participants: participants should leave in the same state as they arrived, facing no more physical or psychological risk than in everyday life.
  • Right to withdraw: participants can leave at any time and ask for their data to be removed.
  • Confidentiality: personal information stays private; participants get numbers, not names, so they can't be identified.
Research methods · ethics

Dealing with ethical issues

  • Debriefing: at the end, explain the true aim, reveal any deception, check participants are OK and remind them they can withdraw their data.
Ethical issueWays of dealing with it
Informed consentA consent form explaining the study; parent or guardian consent for under-16s
DeceptionUse only when necessary; a full debrief at the end
Protecting participants from harmStop if anyone becomes distressed; debrief and offer support
Right to withdrawTell participants at the start and remind them during the study
ConfidentialityUse numbers instead of names; store data securely
Research methods · ethics

Ethics in classic studies

  • Real studies show why the guidelines matter.
  • In Piliavin's subway study, passengers on a New York subway train didn't know they were in a study, so they gave no informed consent and weren't debriefed.
  • In Haney, Banks and Zimbardo (1973), some "prisoners" became so distressed they were released early: participants weren't protected.

Mission check

?Psychologists have lied to participants. When is that allowed?

Only when the study couldn't work if participants knew the aim, and the deception won't cause distress. Afterwards participants must be debriefed: told the true aim, checked on, and reminded they can withdraw their data. These rules come from the BPS Code of Ethics and Conduct.

Mission 8

Two classes have the same average mark. What can the average hide?

Make a guess: Two classes have the same average mark. What can the average hide?

Research methods · data handling

Kinds of data

TypeWhat it isStrength
QuantitativeNumbers, e.g. scores, times, talliesEasy to analyse, compare and show on graphs
QualitativeWords or descriptions, e.g. interview answersRich detail about thoughts and feelings
PrimaryCollected first-hand by the researcher for this studyFits the study's aim exactly
SecondaryCollected by someone else, e.g. school records, earlier studiesQuick and cheap to use
  • Quantitative data can be summarised with averages, measures of spread and charts.
Research methods · data handling

Averages and spread

  • Mean: add the scores and divide by how many there are. Uses every score, but extreme scores distort it.
  • Median: the middle score when they are in order (with two middle scores, take halfway between). Not affected by extreme scores.
  • Mode: the most common score.
  • Range: highest minus lowest, a measure of dispersion (spread).

milliseconds
Calculate

words
Calculate
?Class A scored 6, 6, 6, 6, 6, 6 out of 12, and class B scored 2, 10, 3, 9, 2, 10. Both means are 6. What is the range of class B's marks?
marks
Research methods · data handling

Charts and the normal distribution

ChartUse it for
Frequency table (tally)Counting how often each score or category occurs
Bar chartSeparate categories, e.g. the mean in each condition; bars don't touch
HistogramContinuous scores in equal intervals, with frequency on the y-axis; bars touch
Scatter diagramA correlation between two variables
  • A normal distribution is a symmetrical, bell-shaped curve. The mean, median and mode are equal, at the centre. Most scores are near the middle; few are at the extremes.
Calculate
?A response time of 0.0062 seconds is written in standard form as 6.2 × 10ⁿ. What is n?
Quick check
?A researcher studying stress uses the number of days each pupil was absent last year, taken from school records. What kind of data is this?
Mission check

?Two classes have the same average mark. What can the average hide?

The average hides the spread. Class A's marks are all 6 (range 0), while class B's run from 2 to 10 (range 8). That is why researchers report a measure of dispersion, such as the range, alongside the mean.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A hypothesis is a testable prediction: the alternative predicts a difference or relationship, the null predicts none.
  • The IV is changed and the DV is measured; both must be operationalised. Extraneous variables, situational or participant, must be controlled.
  • Lab and field experiments both manipulate an IV; a natural experiment uses an IV that varies naturally.
  • Labs give control and replicability (reliability); field and natural experiments give higher ecological validity but less control.
  • Choose the type that suits the question: a lab for cause and effect, the field for real behaviour, a natural experiment when the IV can't ethically be manipulated.
  • Independent measures: different people in each condition, so watch for participant variables (use random allocation).
  • Repeated measures: the same people in every condition, so watch for order effects and demand characteristics (use counterbalancing).
  • Matched pairs reduces participant variables; standardised procedures, randomisation and single- or double-blind techniques control extraneous variables.
  • A sample should represent the target population so the findings can be generalised.
  • Random, opportunity, stratified and volunteer sampling each trade convenience against representativeness.
  • Questionnaires and interviews ask; observations watch; case studies go deep into one person or small group.
  • Closed questions give quantitative data and open questions give qualitative data; answers may be socially desirable.
  • Interviews can be structured, semi-structured or unstructured; case studies give rich detail from small samples.
  • A correlation measures two variables and shows whether they are positively, negatively or not related, usually on a scatter diagram.
  • Correlations can study links that can't be manipulated, but they can't show cause and effect: a third variable may explain the link.
  • Psychologists follow the BPS Code of Ethics and Conduct.
  • The key issues are informed consent, deception, protection of participants, the right to withdraw and confidentiality; each has a way of dealing with it, such as debriefing.
  • Data can be quantitative or qualitative, primary or secondary.
  • Mean, median and mode describe the centre; the range describes the spread.
  • A normal distribution is symmetrical and bell-shaped, with mean, median and mode equal at the centre.
