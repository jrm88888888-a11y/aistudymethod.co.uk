Mission 1

Mission 1: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 1

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • Products of photosynthesis (glucose, O2) are the raw materials of aerobic respiration; its products (CO2, H2O) are the raw materials of photosynthesis.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 1

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 1

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organised into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission 1

Built for the job

  • Huge thylakoid membrane area: room for many photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Small volume inside each thylakoid: protons pumped in build a steep gradient fast.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
  • DNA and ribosomes: the chloroplast makes some of its own proteins.
  • Starch grains: sugar stored where it is made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Make a guess: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Mission 2

One leaf, several pigments

  • Chloroplasts hold several photosynthetic pigments:
  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green.
  • Carotenoids: carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment. The others are accessory pigments.
  • Each pigment absorbs a different set of wavelengths, so together they use more of the spectrum.
Mission 2

Absorbing light

  • A pigment absorbs a photon only if its energy matches the gap to an excited state.
  • Then an electron is raised to a higher energy level. This energy is later turned into chemical energy (see Mission 4).
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue (about 430–450 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm) strongly.
  • It absorbs little green (about 500–600 nm): green is reflected or transmitted, so leaves look green.
  • Absorption spectrum: absorbance against wavelength (nm), for one pigment.
  • Say: "leaves look green because green light is reflected" — not: "chlorophyll absorbs green light".
Mission 2

Light harvesting

  • Pigments are grouped in light-harvesting systems (antenna complexes) in the thylakoid membrane.
  • Absorbed energy passes from pigment to pigment, inwards.
  • It reaches one reaction centre chlorophyll a.
  • Light-harvesting system + reaction centre = a photosystem.
Quick check
?The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a has peaks at about 430 nm (blue) and 660 nm (red) but a trough at about 550 nm (green). Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 2

Practical: separating the pigments

  • PAG6: thin layer chromatography (TLC) of photosynthetic pigments.
  • Grind leaf with sand and solvent (e.g. propanone). Spot the extract on a pencil origin line; dry; repeat to concentrate.
  • Stand in solvent below the origin, in a sealed container. Remove before the front reaches the top; mark the front at once.
  • Pigments separate by solubility in the solvent versus attraction to the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance from origin to spot centre ÷ distance from origin to solvent front.
  • Identify by Rf and colour against reference values for the same solvent and stationary phase.
Mission 2

Worked example: Rf

Illustrative data. Distances from the origin line: solvent front 82 mm.

1. Orange spot: 77 mm. Rf = 77 ÷ 82 = 0.94.

2. Blue-green spot: 49 mm. Rf = 49 ÷ 82 = 0.60.

3. Yellow-green spot: 33 mm. Rf = 33 ÷ 82 = 0.40.

  • Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
  • Measure from the origin, not the paper edge; to the centre of the spot.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Make a guess: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Mission 3

Why every cell respires

  • Respiration releases energy from carbon compounds and transfers it to ATP.
  • It runs in every living cell, day and night: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria.
  • Autotrophs respire too. Photosynthesis makes their sugar; respiration releases its energy.
  • Photosynthesis makes glucose and oxygen: the raw materials of respiration. Respiration makes carbon dioxide and water: the raw materials of photosynthesis.
  • ATP powers active transport, synthesis (e.g. proteins from amino acids), movement and cell division.
  • Main substrates: glucose and fatty acids. Many other carbon compounds can be used.
  • Not the same as gas exchange. Breathing moves gases; respiration is a chemical process inside cells.
  • Say: "respiration releases energy" — not: "respiration produces energy".
Quick check
?Which organisms release energy by the oxidation of carbon compounds in cell respiration?
Mission 3

Small steps, not one flash

  • Overall (aerobic): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
  • Burning releases the energy all at once, as heat and light.
  • Respiration releases the same total energy, but in many small steps.
  • Each step is catalysed by its own specific enzyme, inside the cell.
  • At some steps, part of the energy is captured in ATP. The rest is released as heat.
  • Say: "energy is transferred to ATP" — not: "glucose is turned into energy".
Mission 3

Two routes from glucose

  • First stage, glycolysis: glucose → 2 pyruvate, in the cytoplasm. No oxygen needed.
  • With oxygen: pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is oxidised completely to carbon dioxide and water. Far more ATP.
  • The mitochondrial stages: link reaction and Krebs cycle (in the matrix), then oxidative phosphorylation (on the inner membrane).
  • Without oxygen: pyruvate stays in the cytoplasm. In human muscle it becomes lactate.
  • In yeast it becomes ethanol and carbon dioxide.
Mission 3

Glycolysis in four steps

  • (1) Phosphorylation: 2 ATP add two phosphate groups to glucose. The sugar becomes more reactive.
  • The product is hexose bisphosphate.
  • (2) Lysis: the 6-carbon sugar splits into two 3-carbon triose phosphate molecules.
  • (3) Oxidation: hydrogen is removed from each triose phosphate. NAD accepts it and becomes reduced NAD.
  • (4) ATP formation: each 3-carbon molecule becomes pyruvate, forming 2 ATP.
  • Net per glucose: 4 ATP made − 2 used = 2 ATP, plus 2 reduced NAD and 2 pyruvate.
  • Each step has its own enzyme. All in the cytoplasm; no oxygen used.
  • Say: "phosphorylating glucose uses ATP" — not: "it makes ATP".
Mission 3

NAD carries hydrogen

  • Oxidation = loss of electrons. Reduction = gain of electrons.
  • Removing hydrogen (with its electron) is dehydrogenation. It oxidises the substrate, with no oxygen involved.
  • NAD accepts the hydrogen, so NAD is reduced. Both happen together: a redox reaction.
  • With oxygen, the hydrogen (its electrons) from reduced NAD is passed into the mitochondria, where it is used to make far more ATP.
  • NAD, FAD and coenzyme A are coenzymes: non-protein molecules that enzymes need. FAD and coenzyme A appear in a later lesson.
  • Say: "NAD is reduced when it gains hydrogen" — not: "NAD is oxidised when it gains hydrogen".

Mission check

Mission 4

Mission 4: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Make a guess: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Mission 4

Photosystems catch light

  • Thylakoid membranes hold two photosystems: photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI).
  • Each is an array of chlorophyll and accessory pigments, with a special chlorophyll a at the reaction centre.
  • Absorbed energy is passed to the reaction centre.
  • There an electron is raised to a higher energy level and emitted to an electron acceptor.
  • Say: "light excites an electron, which the chlorophyll loses" — not: "chlorophyll makes electrons".
Mission 4

The electron path: PSII → PSI → NADP

1. Light excites PSII. Its electron passes to an electron transport chain (ETC).

2. Carrier to carrier: each step is a redox reaction that releases energy.

3. The electron reaches PSI and is excited again by light.

4. It passes to NADP in the stroma.

  • This one-way flow is non-cyclic photophosphorylation. (Cyclic.)
Mission 4

Photolysis refills PSII

  • PSII has lost electrons. It must get them back.
  • Water is split: 2H2O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O2. This is photolysis.
  • Electrons replace those lost from PSII.
  • Protons (H⁺) stay inside the thylakoid: they add to the proton gradient.
  • Oxygen is a by-product (waste): it diffuses out of the leaf.
  • Say: "splitting water supplies electrons" — not: "splitting water supplies the energy". The energy comes from light.
Quick check
?Which statement about the photolysis of water in photosystem II is correct?
Mission 4

Reducing NADP

  • At the end of the chain, PSI passes two electrons to NADP, on the stroma side.
  • NADP also takes a hydrogen ion from the stroma: NADP is reduced to reduced NADP.
  • Reduced NADP and ATP carry the energy to the light-independent stage (see Mission 6).
Mission 4

Chemiosmosis makes ATP

  • Energy released along the ETC pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space.
  • Photolysis adds more H⁺ inside; NADP reduction removes H⁺ outside.
  • Result: high [H⁺] inside, low outside: an electrochemical gradient.
  • H⁺ flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase. This drives ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, released into the stroma.
  • ATP made this way: photophosphorylation. The mechanism: chemiosmosis.
  • Say: "ATP synthase is driven by H⁺ flowing down their gradient" — not: "the carriers make ATP".
Quick check

Quick checkRead the data

Mission check

?A chemical is added that takes electrons from photosystem I in place of NADP. The chloroplasts are illuminated with a supply of water, ADP, phosphate and NADP. Which prediction is correct?

Electrons from water normally end on NADP; on the way, their energy builds the proton gradient that makes ATP. Swap the final acceptor, and only the reduced NADP is lost.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why would a chloroplast send its electrons round in a loop that makes no oxygen at all?

Make a guess: Why would a chloroplast send its electrons round in a loop that makes no oxygen at all?

Mission 5

PSII and PSI: an array each

  • Both photosystems sit in the thylakoid membranes.
  • Each: hundreds of pigment molecules (chlorophyll a, b, carotenoids) around one reaction centre.
  • Reaction centres absorb best at 680 nm in PSII (P680) and 700 nm in PSI (P700).
  • PSII: linked to water splitting; passes electrons to the ETC.
  • PSI: passes electrons to NADP, or back to the ETC.
  • Why an array? A lone chlorophyll absorbs photons rarely, over a narrow range.
  • The array absorbs many wavelengths and funnels the energy to one reaction centre, which is excited many times a second.
Quick check
?Why is a structured array of many different pigment molecules, rather than a single chlorophyll molecule, needed to carry out the first step of photosynthesis?
Mission 5

Cyclic photophosphorylation

1. Light excites PSI. Its electron passes to the ETC.

2. The electron flows along the carriers, pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid space.

3. It returns to PSI: a closed loop.

  • H⁺ flow through ATP synthase: ATP is made by chemiosmosis.
  • PSI only. No water split, no O2, no reduced NADP.
  • Say: "cyclic flow uses PSI only and makes only ATP" — not: "cyclic flow also makes reduced NADP and O2".

Quick check
?A herbicide blocks the flow of electrons out of photosystem II. Chloroplasts treated with it are kept in bright light with a supply of ADP, phosphate and NADP. Which process can continue?
Mission 5

Evidence: ATP in the dark

  • Chemiosmotic theory (Mitchell, 1961): a proton gradient drives ATP synthase.
  • Test (Jagendorf and Uribe, 1966): thylakoids soaked in the dark at pH 4, so the inside became pH 4.
  • Then moved, still in the dark, to pH 8 with ADP and Pᵢ: ATP formed briefly.
  • No pH difference: no ATP.
  • So the gradient alone, without light or electron flow, can drive ATP synthesis.

Mission 6

Mission 6

The light-independent stage

  • Happens in the stroma.
  • Uses ATP and reduced NADP from the light-dependent stage.
  • Builds CO2 into sugar in a cycle: the Calvin cycle.
  • Three steps: fixation, reduction, regeneration.
  • It needs no light directly, but stops soon after the light does.
  • Say: "light-independent reactions" — not: "dark reactions that happen at night".
Quick check
?A student is drawing a model of how the two stages of photosynthesis in a chloroplast are connected. Which set of arrows between the light-dependent stage and the Calvin cycle should the model show?
Mission 6

Fixation: rubisco captures CO2

  • CO2 combines with ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP, 5C).
  • Enzyme: rubisco.
  • The 6C product is unstable: it splits at once into two glycerate 3-phosphate (GP, 3C).
  • No ATP is used in this step.
  • The enzyme is written RuBisCO (ribulose bisphosphate carboxylase-oxygenase).
Mission 6

Reduction: GP to triose phosphate

  • Each GP is converted to triose phosphate (TP, 3C).
  • ATP supplies energy (and phosphate).
  • Reduced NADP supplies hydrogen: it reduces GP.
  • ADP, Pᵢ and NADP go back to the thylakoids.
  • Say: "reduced NADP reduces GP" — not: "rubisco reduces CO2".
Quick check
?What is required to convert glycerate 3-phosphate into triose phosphate in the Calvin cycle?
Mission 6

Regeneration: keeping the cycle turning

  • Most TP is used to regenerate RuBP, using ATP (no reduced NADP).
  • 5 TP (15 C) → 3 RuBP (15 C).
  • Worked count for one glucose (6 C):

1. Fix 6 CO2 → 12 GP → 12 TP (36 C).

2. 10 TP (30 C) → 6 RuBP (30 C): the cycle is ready again.

3. 2 TP (6 C) leave → one glucose.

  • So five-sixths of TP is recycled; one-sixth is the product.
  • Cost per glucose: 18 ATP (12 for reduction, 6 for regeneration) and 12 reduced NADP.
Quick check
?In a Calvin cycle that produces one molecule of glucose, how many molecules of triose phosphate are made in total, and how many of them are used to regenerate RuBP?
Mission 6

What TP becomes

  • Carbohydrates: glucose → sucrose (transport), starch (store), cellulose (cell walls).
  • Lipids: glycerol and fatty acids.
  • Amino acids: carbon skeletons from the cycle, plus nitrogen from nitrate or ammonium ions taken up by roots.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Make a guess: Brighter lamps stop making a greenhouse crop grow any faster. What should the grower change instead?

Mission 7

The limiting factor

  • Rate of photosynthesis depends on several factors at once: light intensity, CO2 concentration, temperature.
  • The one in shortest supply sets the rate: the limiting factor.
  • Raise the limiting factor → rate rises.
  • Raise any other factor → no change.
  • Light: powers the light-dependent stage. CO2: substrate for rubisco. Temperature: speed of enzyme-catalysed steps.
Mission 7

Reading the curves

1. Rate rises as light rises: light is limiting.

2. The curve flattens: light is no longer limiting. Another factor is.

3. A curve at higher CO2 reaches a higher plateau: CO2 was limiting the first plateau.

  • On the rising part, curves at different CO2 overlap: there, only light matters.
  • Say: "the plateau shows another factor is limiting" — not: "the plant has reached its maximum rate".
Quick check
?Pondweed submerged in water at 25 °C with the carbon dioxide concentration of ordinary air was exposed to increasing light intensity. The rate of photosynthesis rose at first and then levelled off, staying constant however much the light intensity was increased. Which change is most likely to raise the rate at this plateau?
Mission 7

Temperature and water

  • Light-independent reactions are enzyme-catalysed: warmer → faster, up to an optimum.
  • Above the optimum: enzymes such as rubisco denature; rate falls.
  • Low temperature can limit the rate even in bright light and high CO2.
  • Water stress: guard cells lose turgor, stomata close → CO2 uptake falls → CO2 limits the rate.
  • Effects on GP, RuBP and TP levels: see Mission 6.
  • Say: "water stress limits photosynthesis by closing stomata" — not: "water is short as a reactant".
Mission 7

Practical: measuring the rate

  • Practical investigation into factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis.
  • Pondweed (e.g. Elodea or Cabomba) in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (CO2 source).
  • Measure O2: volume collected in a photosynthometer capillary per minute (better than counting bubbles).
  • Vary light: lamp distance; intensity falls about as 1 ÷ d². Use an LED or a water heat shield.
  • Vary CO2: hydrogencarbonate concentration. Vary temperature: water bath.
  • Let the plant acclimatise at each setting; repeat; keep other factors constant.
  • Alternatives: algal beads in hydrogencarbonate indicator; floating leaf discs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 2

  • Chromatography separates leaf pigments; identify each by Rf (origin to spot ÷ origin to front) and colour, against references for the same solvent.
  • Pigments absorb only wavelengths whose energy matches an electron jump: mainly blue and red. Accessory pigments widen the range used.

Mission 3

  • Respiration releases the energy in carbon compounds in many small, enzyme-catalysed steps; part is captured in ATP, the rest is heat.
  • Glycolysis: phosphorylation (2 ATP used), lysis to two triose phosphate, oxidation (NAD reduced), ATP formation: net 2 ATP, 2 reduced NAD, 2 pyruvate.

Mission 4

  • Light excites electrons in PSII and PSI; they pass along an electron transport chain to NADP. Water splitting replaces PSII's electrons and releases O2.
  • Energy from the chain pumps protons into the thylakoid space; their flow back through ATP synthase makes ATP (chemiosmosis). Products: ATP and reduced NADP.

Mission 5

  • Non-cyclic flow (PSII → PSI → NADP) makes ATP, reduced NADP and O2. Cyclic flow (PSI → carriers → PSI) makes only ATP, topping up the ATP the Calvin cycle needs.
  • A proton gradient drives ATP synthase, even in the dark (Jagendorf and Uribe, 1966).

Mission 6

  • Rubisco fixes CO2 with RuBP → 2 GP; ATP and reduced NADP reduce GP → TP; five-sixths of TP regenerates RuBP (using ATP); one-sixth makes sugars, lipids and amino acids.
  • Light off: GP rises, RuBP falls. CO2 down: RuBP rises, GP falls.

Mission 7

  • The rate is set by the factor in shortest supply (light, CO2 or temperature). A plateau means another factor now limits.
