Mission 1: Why can't one wheat farmer charge even slightly more than the market price?

Make a guess: Why can't one wheat farmer charge even slightly more than the market price?

Mission 1

The assumptions

  • Perfect competition assumes:
    • many buyers and sellers, each too small to affect the price
    • identical (homogeneous) products: one seller's output is a perfect substitute for another's
    • perfect information: everyone knows every price
    • free entry and exit: no barriers
  • Also assumed: firms aim to maximise profit; factors of production are mobile.
  • Real markets come close, but never fully: wheat and other crops, some currency markets.
  • It is a model: few real markets meet every assumption. It is used as a benchmark for comparing other structures.
Mission 1

A price taker

  • Two diagrams:
    • Industry: market D and S set the price, Pe.
    • Firm: sells any amount it wants at Pe. Its demand curve is horizontal at Pe.
  • Charge above Pe? Buyers switch to identical products: the firm sells nothing.
  • Charge below Pe? Pointless: it can already sell all it wants at Pe.
  • So the firm is a price taker: every extra unit sells for Pe.
  • P = AR = MR = D, a horizontal line.
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve is perfectly elastic" — not: "market demand is perfectly elastic". Market demand slopes down as normal.
Quick check

Mission 1

Case: the world wheat price

  • Wheat (US hard red winter), world price, annual averages:
    • 2021: about 315 dollars per tonne
    • 2022: about 430 dollars per tonne
    • 2023: about 340 dollars per tonne
  • 2022 was the year Russia invaded Ukraine; both are major wheat exporters.
  • No single farmer caused these swings, and none could resist them.
  • Each farmer's only choice: how much to grow and sell at the world price.
Mission 1

How much to produce?

  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR.
  • For a price taker, MR = P. So produce where MC = P, on the rising part of MC.
  • Why:
    • MC below P: the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost → produce it.
    • MC above P: the unit adds more to cost than to revenue → don't produce it.
Mission 1

Profit, normal profit or loss

  • Find output where MC = MR. Then compare AR (= P) with AC at that output.
  • P > AC: supernormal profit = (P − AC) × Q, a rectangle.
  • P = AC: normal profit (break-even): just enough to keep the firm in this industry.
  • P < AC: loss = (AC − P) × Q.
  • Say: "supernormal (or abnormal) profit" — not: "a big profit". Normal profit is already counted in AC as a cost.

Mission 1

Shut down or carry on?

  • In the short run, fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Shut down → loss = fixed cost.
  • Carry on → loss = TC − TR.
  • Rule: carry on if TR ≥ TVC, i.e. P ≥ AVC. Revenue covers variable costs and pays part of the fixed cost.
  • Shut down if P < AVC at every output.
  • Shutdown price = minimum AVC.
  • The firm from an earlier screen: shutdown price = minimum AVC = 10.
Mission 1Graph lab

Same firm. Fixed cost 360. Shutdown loss = 360.

  • Takeaway: produce as long as P ≥ AVC. At 15: TR 300 > TVC 11 × 20 = 220, so the extra 80 pays part of the fixed cost and the loss is 280 < 360: carry on. Below 10, shutting down loses less.

Mission 1

The firm's supply curve

  • At each price, the firm produces where P = MC.
  • So its MC curve shows how much it supplies at each price.
  • Below minimum AVC it supplies nothing.
  • Firm's short-run supply curve = its MC curve above minimum AVC.
  • Industry supply = the firms' supply curves added sideways (horizontally).
  • So under perfect competition, the supply curve is the MC curve.
Mission check

?A firm sells standard cardboard boxes in a perfectly competitive market. The market price is 15 per box. At its current output, the marginal cost of its last box is 12. What should it do to maximise profit?

A price taker cannot choose its price. Its only lever is output: produce up to where MC equals the market price.

Mission 2

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP
  • Price up → quantity supplied up. So PES is normally positive.
  • A bigger number = a stronger response.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases" (that is a shift).
Mission 2

  • The dividing line is 1.
Mission 2

Straight-line supply curves

  • A straight supply line through the origin: PES = 1 at every point, whatever its slope.
  • A straight line that starts on the price axis: PES > 1 (elastic) all along it.
  • A straight line that starts on the quantity axis: PES < 1 (inelastic) all along it.
  • So slope alone does not tell you PES. Check where the line meets the axes.
  • Say: "relatively elastic supply" for a line cutting the price axis — not: "elastic because it is flat".
Quick check
?Three straight-line supply curves are drawn on one diagram (illustrative). S1 starts on the price axis above the origin. S2 passes through the origin and is the steepest of the three. S3 starts on the quantity axis to the right of the origin. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A furniture maker supplies 400 tables a month at a price of £20.
  • The price rises to £25. Quantity supplied rises to 450.
  • %ΔQs = (450 − 400) ÷ 400 × 100 = +12.5%.
  • %ΔP = (25 − 20) ÷ 20 × 100 = +25%.
  • PES = 12.5 ÷ 25 = 0.5 → inelastic.
  • Working backwards (illustrative data): PES = 1.6 and price rises 5% → %ΔQs = 1.6 × 5 = +8%.
Quick check
?A firm supplies 1,200 units a week. The market price rises by 8%, and the firm's PES is 2.5. How many units a week will it now supply?
Mission 2

What makes supply elastic?

  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply is.
  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise fast.
  • Stocks and storage: goods held in stock, or that can be stored, can be released quickly.
  • Mobility of factors: workers, machines and land that can switch from other uses → more elastic.
Mission 2

Short run vs long run

  • Momentary (very short) run: output is fixed. Supply is vertical (PES = 0). E.g. fish already landed today.
  • Short run: firms use spare capacity, overtime and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build capacity, and new firms enter. Supply becomes more elastic.
  • The same rise in demand → in the short run mostly a price rise; in the long run more of a quantity rise.
Mission 2Graph lab

  • Takeaway: the less elastic supply is, the more a rise in demand shows up as a higher price rather than more output.

Mission 2

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in the 2023–24 season cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa futures in New York passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to bear beans. In the short run, PES is very low.
  • Long-run response: growers elsewhere (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand production.
Mission 2Evaluate

How useful is PES for decisions?

  • Firms: knowing PES shows whether they can profit from a demand surge. Keeping stocks and spare capacity raises PES.
  • Government: the more inelastic supply is, the larger the share of a tax producers bear. A subsidy to raise output works only if supply can respond.
  • Policy example: if housing supply is inelastic, measures that raise demand mostly push up prices. Measures that make supply more elastic (e.g. faster planning) raise quantity.

It depends on …

  • Time horizon: short-run and long-run PES can be very different.
  • Spare capacity in the economy: near full capacity, supply is less elastic than in a recession.
  • Accuracy: PES estimates come from past data. Costs, technology and regulation change.
Mission check

?Demand for electric-bike batteries rises sharply (illustrative data). One month later, the price is 20% higher and quantity supplied is 5% higher than before. Two years later, compared with the original position, the price is 5% higher and quantity supplied is 30% higher. Which statement is correct?

The same product can have inelastic supply this month and elastic supply in two years: time is the key determinant.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Make a guess: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Mission 3

Equilibrium: where the curves cross

  • Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • At that price the market clears: every unit offered is bought, every buyer willing to pay it gets one.
  • No pressure for the price to change, unless something else changes.
  • Label it Pe (equilibrium price) and Qe (equilibrium quantity).
  • In this model: many buyers and many sellers, none big enough to set the price alone (a competitive market).
Mission 3

Price too high: excess supply

  • At a price above Pe, quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
  • This is excess supply, or a surplus.
  • Size of the surplus = Qs − Qd at that price.
  • Signs: unsold stock piling up, discounts, "clearance" sales.
  • Only Qd is actually sold: firms cannot sell more than buyers want.
  • Market forces: sellers cut the price to shift their stock.
Mission 3

Price too low: excess demand

  • At a price below Pe, quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
  • This is excess demand, or a shortage.
  • Size of the shortage = Qd − Qs at that price.
  • Signs: queues, waiting lists, "sold out", resale above the original price.
  • Only Qs is actually bought: buyers cannot buy what is not supplied.
  • Market forces: buyers compete, so the price is bid up.
  • A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Scarcity is always present; a shortage exists only when the price is below equilibrium.

Mission 3

Market forces: movements, not shifts

  • Surplus → price falls → quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls → until Qd = Qs.
  • Shortage → price rises → quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises → until Qd = Qs.
  • These are movements along the curves. Neither curve shifts: the price is on the axis, so it cannot shift its own curve.
  • Say: "the price falls, causing an extension of demand and a contraction of supply" — not: "demand rises and supply falls".

Mission 3Evaluate

Does a market always clear?

  • The model predicts the price moves to Pe. How fast, and whether it gets there, depends on:
  • Price flexibility: some prices are sticky. Firms with printed price lists, long contracts, or worries about customer goodwill (e.g. concert and sports tickets) may leave a shortage.
  • Information: buyers and sellers must notice the surplus or shortage before they react.
  • Time lags: farmers cannot grow more until the next harvest, so a shortage can last a season.
  • Intervention: a legal maximum or minimum price stops the market clearing.
  • And the target moves: if a curve shifts, the market chases a new equilibrium (see Mission 4).
Mission check

?A new games console launches at a price of 300, below its equilibrium price of 400 (illustrative data). At 300, quantity demanded is 9 million and quantity supplied is 5 million. At 400, both are 7 million. The firm is free to raise the price. As the price rises from 300 to 400, what happens to the quantity actually bought and sold?

In a shortage the short side, supply, limits sales, so a rising price can raise the quantity traded until Qd = Qs.

Mission 4

A shift starts a chain

  • Something other than the good's own price changes. One curve shifts.
  • Example: incomes rise and the good is normal. Demand shifts right, D1 → D2.
  • At the old price P1 there is now excess demand (a shortage).
  • The price is bid up. Suppliers respond by moving along S.
  • New equilibrium: higher price P2, higher quantity Q2.
  • Say: "a rise in demand causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".

  • Supply shifts: price and quantity move opposite ways.
  • Always ask first: which curve does the event shift? Usually only one.
  • A supply shift right means more is offered at every price, not a move "down" the graph.

Mission 4

How far? It depends on elasticity

  • Same leftward shift of supply, two demand curves:
  • Price inelastic demand (drawn steeper through the starting point): large price rise, small fall in quantity.
  • Price elastic demand (drawn flatter through the same point): small price rise, large fall in quantity.
  • Slope is only a drawing guide here: elasticity is about percentage changes.
  • The same logic applies to supply: inelastic supply turns a demand shift mainly into a price change.
  • So markets with inelastic demand and supply have volatile prices. Example: farm commodities, where a harvest cannot be changed until next season.
  • Elasticity in full: (demand) (supply).
Mission 4Case

Cocoa, 2024

  • Most of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa.
  • Late 2024: hot weather with less rain threatened crops in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Another global supply deficit was expected.
  • Supply shifted left. Chocolate makers have few substitutes for cocoa beans.
  • Result: cocoa futures in London rose 66% in three months: from US$6,896 per tonne (1 Oct 2024) to US$11,441 (31 Dec 2024).
  • On the diagram: S1 → S2, price P1 → P2 up sharply, quantity Q1 → Q2 down.
Mission 4

Real markets: housing and energy

  • Housing: demand rises with population, incomes and cheaper or easier mortgages.
  • Supply is price inelastic: land, planning rules and years of building. So rising demand mostly raises prices, not the number of homes.
  • Energy: in 2022, Russia cut pipeline gas deliveries to Europe. Supply shifted left.
  • Demand for gas is inelastic in the short run (heating, power stations), so European wholesale gas prices hit record highs.
  • Over time, higher prices brought more liquefied gas by ship (extension of supply) and lower use (contraction of demand).
  • Commodities: The earlier screens.
Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

How far can we trust the model?

  • The model predicts the direction of change well. How useful it is depends on:
  • Ceteris paribus: real markets face many changes at once, so outcomes are often double shifts with uncertain results.
  • Competition: it assumes many buyers and sellers who take the price. Firms with market power set prices.
  • Information and rationality: it assumes buyers and sellers know prices and respond to them. Habits and limited information slow responses.
  • Speed of adjustment: prices can be sticky, or overshoot, so markets can spend time out of equilibrium.
  • We never see the curves: data show only prices and quantities traded, so we must work out which curve shifted from other evidence.
Mission check

?Over one year, the price of a good rose sharply, but the quantity bought and sold stayed the same. The demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up. Which change could explain this?

Make a guess: The price of fuel jumps. What happens to sales of big cars that use a lot of fuel?

Mission 5

Product markets and factor markets

  • Product markets: goods and services. Households buy, firms sell.
  • Factor markets: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Firms buy, households sell.
  • The two are linked: firms hire factors because they want to make products.
  • So a change in a product market spreads to the factor markets that serve it, and back again through incomes.
Mission 5

Joint demand: goods used together

  • Joint demand: goods bought together, such as games consoles and games, or printers and ink. Also called complements.
  • Step 1: price of printers falls → extension of demand for printers.
  • Step 2: more printers in use → demand for ink shifts right.
  • Step 3: ink price and quantity rise.
  • The price of a complement is a shift factor of demand.
  • Say: "demand for ink increases (shifts)" — not: "the demand for printers shifts" (a change in a good's own price is a movement).
Mission 5

Competitive demand: goods that replace each other

  • Competitive demand: goods that are substitutes, such as coffee and tea, or train and coach travel.
  • Price of coffee rises → contraction of demand for coffee → demand for tea shifts right.
  • The closer the substitutes, the bigger the shift.
Quick check
?The price of printers falls sharply. What happens in the market for printer ink?
Mission 5

Composite demand: one good, many uses

  • Composite demand: a good wanted for several different uses.
  • Milk → drinking milk, cheese, butter, cream.
  • If demand for cheese rises, more milk goes to cheese makers.
  • Less milk is left for butter: the supply of butter shifts left and its price rises.
  • Other examples: steel (cars, buildings, cans); land (homes, farms, shops).
  • Say: "composite demand: one good, many uses" — not: "joint demand" (goods used together).
Mission 5

Derived demand

  • Derived demand: demand for something because of what it helps to produce.
  • Demand for factors is derived from demand for products.
  • More homes wanted → more demand for bricklayers, bricks and building land.
  • So a rise in demand in a product market shifts demand in a factor market.
  • Labour demand is derived demand: the basis of wage theory.
Quick check
?More people want to eat in restaurants, so the demand for chefs rises. What kind of demand for chefs is this?
Mission 5

Joint supply: by-products

  • Joint supply: making one good automatically makes another.
  • Cattle → beef and leather. Crude oil refining → petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
  • Step 1: demand for beef rises → more cattle are reared and slaughtered.
  • Step 2: more hides → supply of leather shifts right.
  • Step 3: leather price falls, quantity rises.
  • The two goods move in opposite price directions here.
Mission 5

Case: carbon dioxide, 2021

  • In the UK, food-grade CO₂ is a by-product of making fertiliser from natural gas.
  • In 2021, gas prices nearly quadrupled. In September, the fertiliser firm CF Industries halted its two UK plants.
  • Those plants made about 60% of the UK's food-grade CO₂.
  • CO₂ supply fell sharply. Poultry processors (which use it to stun birds) and drinks makers (for the fizz) warned of disruption. The government told food firms to expect CO₂ prices to rise about 400%.
  • The government agreed short-term financial support to restart one plant, to give the market time to adjust.
  • A gas market shock cut the supply of CO₂ and raised its price, in a market that seems to have nothing to do with gas.
Quick check
?Petrol and diesel are both produced when crude oil is refined. Demand for petrol falls a lot, and refiners process less crude oil. What happens in the market for diesel?

Mission 5

Knock-on effects

  • One change can ripple through several markets. Illustrative chain:
  • More people buy electric cars.
  • Joint demand: demand for home chargers rises.
  • Derived demand: demand for electricians who fit them rises; their wages rise.
  • Competitive demand: demand for petrol cars falls.
  • Composite demand: more lithium for car batteries leaves less for other batteries.
  • How big is each knock-on? It depends on how closely the goods are linked (cross elasticity, an earlier lesson) and on the elasticity of supply in each market.
Mission check

?Crushing sunflower seeds produces sunflower oil and sunflower meal, which is sold as animal feed. Demand for sunflower oil rises sharply. What is the most likely effect in the market for sunflower meal?

When goods come from the same process, a boom in one makes the other more plentiful.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: P = AR = MR = D, a horizontal line.
  • It produces where MC = MR = P. Compare P with AC: supernormal profit, normal profit or loss.
  • In the short run it keeps producing while P ≥ AVC; its supply curve is MC above minimum AVC.

Mission 2

  • PES = %ΔQs ÷ %ΔP: below 1 is inelastic, above 1 is elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, mobile factors and, above all, more time.

Mission 3

  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd), the price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs), the price rises. Both adjust by movements along the curves.

Mission 4

  • A shift creates excess demand or supply at the old price; the price then changes and the market moves along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Demand shifts move price and quantity the same way; supply shifts move them opposite ways. Two shifts at once leave one result uncertain.
  • The less elastic the curves, the more the price moves.

Mission 5

  • Joint demand (complements) move together; competitive demand (substitutes) move in opposite directions.
  • Joint supply: more of one product means more of its by-product.
  • Derived demand links product markets to factor markets.
