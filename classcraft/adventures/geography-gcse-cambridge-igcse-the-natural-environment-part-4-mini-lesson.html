Mission 1

Inside the Earth

  • Crust: thin, solid, rigid rock. About 5 km thick under oceans; about 30 km on average under continents.
  • Mantle: hot, dense rock, about 2,900 km thick. It is solid, but over millions of years it can flow very slowly.
  • Outer core: liquid, about 2,200 km thick.
  • Inner core: solid, about 1,250 km thick.
Mission 1

Plates

  • The crust and the top of the mantle form a rigid layer: the lithosphere, at least 80 km thick over much of Earth.
  • The lithosphere is broken into tectonic plates. They move slowly over a weaker, mobile layer of the mantle, the asthenosphere.
  • Main plates: Pacific, North American, South American, Eurasian, African, Indo-Australian, Antarctic, and the smaller Nazca plate.
  • Plate tectonics theory: the moving plates explain where earthquakes, volcanoes and mountains are found.
Quick check
?Which layer of the Earth is liquid?
Mission 1

Heat and convection currents

  • Heat inside Earth comes from radioactive decay and heat left over from Earth's formation.
  • Hot, less dense mantle rock rises; cooler, denser rock sinks. This slow loop is a convection current.
  • Where currents spread out under the plates, they may help move plates apart; where they sink, plates move together. Scientists think gravity at the plates' edges also matters a lot.
  • Convection takes millions of years: plates move only centimetres a year.
Mission 1

Ridge push and slab pull

  • Newer research adds two forces driven by gravity.
  • Ridge push: new plate at a mid-ocean ridge is hot and stands high, so it slides downhill away from the ridge.
  • Slab pull: at a subduction zone, the cold, dense edge of an oceanic plate sinks and drags the rest of the plate behind it.
  • Plates attached to sinking slabs, such as the Pacific and Nazca plates, move fastest. So many scientists think slab pull is the strongest force.
  • No single mechanism explains all plate movement.
Quick check
?Plates with a sinking edge at a subduction zone move fastest. Which force does this support?
Mission 1

Constructive margins

  • Plates move apart (divergence). Magma rises from the mantle to fill the gap and forms new crust.
  • Under the ocean this builds a mid-ocean ridge. The Mid-Atlantic Ridge spreads about 2.5 cm a year.
  • On land, stretching crust cracks and sinks into a rift valley, as in the East African Rift.
  • Earthquakes: shallow and usually small. Volcanoes: runny lava and fairly gentle eruptions. Iceland sits on the Mid-Atlantic Ridge.
  • Your specification also calls this a divergent boundary.
Mission 1

Destructive margins

  • Plates move together (convergence). The denser oceanic plate sinks under the lighter continental plate: subduction.
  • Where it bends down, an ocean trench forms: the deepest parts of the ocean floor.
  • Example: the Nazca Plate sinks under South America, pushing up the Andes.
  • Friction as the plate sinks causes powerful earthquakes. Water released from the sinking plate makes the mantle above it melt; this magma rises to form explosive composite volcanoes.
Mission 1

Collision margins

  • Two continental plates move together. Continental rock is light, so neither plate sinks.
  • The crust crumples and folds upwards: fold mountains.
  • Example: India collided with Asia about 50 million years ago, building the Himalayas.
  • Earthquakes: frequent and can be large. Volcanoes: few or none, because no plate sinks into the mantle, so no magma forms.
Mission 1

Conservative margins

  • Plates slide past each other. Crust is neither made nor destroyed.
  • The plates lock together. Stress builds up, then is released suddenly as an earthquake.
  • Example: the San Andreas Fault, California: the Pacific Plate moves past the North American Plate at about 5 cm a year.
  • Landform: a fault line. No volcanoes: no magma is produced.
  • Cambridge also calls this a transform boundary.
Quick check
?An oceanic plate sinks beneath a continental plate. Which landform forms where it starts to sink?
Mission 1

Where earthquakes and volcanoes are

  • They form narrow belts along plate margins, not an even spread.
  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes happen around the Pacific (the Ring of Fire), and about 17% in a belt from Java through the Himalaya to the Mediterranean.
  • About 1,350 potentially active volcanoes, many around the Pacific Ring of Fire.
  • Exceptions: hotspots such as Hawaii, far from any margin.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes where earthquakes and volcanoes occur?
Mission check

?The Nazca Plate moves east and sinks beneath South America. What moves it?

The same forces widen the Atlantic. Internal heat drives convection currents; gravity adds slab pull (sinking edges) and ridge push (sliding off ridges).

Mission 2

Why earthquakes happen

  • Plates move a few centimetres a year. Where they meet, they lock together.
  • Stress builds up in the rock, then is released suddenly as the rock breaks along a fault.
  • The energy spreads out from the break as seismic waves: the shaking we feel.
Mission 2

Earthquakes at each type of margin

  • Constructive: plates pull apart. Earthquakes are shallow and mostly small; Iceland's have reached at least magnitude 6.9.
  • Destructive: one plate sinks. Where the plates lock near the surface, friction causes the largest earthquakes. Deeper earthquakes happen inside the sinking plate, down to about 700 km.
  • Collision: continents crush together. Shallow, sometimes very large: Nepal 2015, magnitude 7.8, about 8 km deep.
  • Conservative: plates slide past each other. Shallow earthquakes, such as on the San Andreas Fault.
Mission 2

Where earthquakes happen

  • Earthquakes form narrow belts along plate margins.
  • About 81% of the world's largest earthquakes happen round the Pacific, the Ring of Fire.
  • About 17% happen in a belt from Java through the Himalaya to the Mediterranean.
  • Few happen in the middle of plates.
Mission 2

Focus and epicentre

  • Focus (hypocentre): the point underground where the rock first breaks.
  • Epicentre: the point on the surface directly above the focus.
  • Shaking is usually strongest near the epicentre and weakens with distance from it.
Quick check
?What is the epicentre of an earthquake?
Mission 2

Shallow or deep focus?

  • Shallow-focus earthquakes: 0–70 km deep. Deep-focus: 300–700 km deep.
  • Every earthquake deeper than 70 km happens inside a plate sinking into the mantle. So deep-focus earthquakes occur only at destructive margins.
  • Constructive, collision and conservative margins have shallow earthquakes.
  • Deep-focus earthquakes cause less damage at the surface than shallow ones of the same magnitude: the waves weaken on their long journey up.
  • Example: the Sea of Okhotsk earthquake on 24 May 2013 (magnitude 8.3) started about 600 km down. It was felt all over Asia, as far away as Moscow, and in Tokyo.
Mission 2

Seismic waves

  • P waves (primary): the fastest, so they arrive first. They push and pull the rock.
  • S waves (secondary): slower; they shake the rock from side to side and cannot pass through liquids.
  • Surface waves: the slowest, but they often cause the largest ground motion and make buildings fail.
Quick check
?At which type of plate margin can an earthquake start 500 km below the surface?
Mission 2

Liquefaction

  • Liquefaction: loose, waterlogged sediment loses its strength when it is shaken.
  • The ground behaves like a liquid. Buildings sink or tilt; buried pipes can float up.
  • Christchurch, New Zealand: in March 2012 the government red-zoned 251 more homes because there had been "widespread liquefaction".
Mission 2

Tsunamis

  • An earthquake under the sea moves the seabed up or down. The water above is pushed up and spreads out as a tsunami.
  • Indian Ocean, 2004 (magnitude 9.1): the tsunami killed over 230,000 people.
  • Kaikōura, New Zealand, 2016: the first wave arrived within 10 minutes, reaching 6.9 m up the shore at Goose Bay.
Mission 2

Impacts of earthquakes

  • Primary: caused by the shaking itself, such as collapsing buildings. Secondary: what follows, such as landslides, tsunamis and liquefaction.
  • Nepal 2015: over 8,790 deaths and over half a million houses destroyed; at least 25,000 landslides.
  • Economic: damage and losses of about US$7 billion, about one-third of Nepal's GDP.
  • Group impacts as social (people), economic (money, jobs) and environmental (land, rivers).
Quick check
?Which ground is most at risk of liquefaction in an earthquake?
Mission check

?In 1994 a magnitude 8.2 earthquake started about 630 km below Bolivia, inside the Nazca Plate. Why would it do less damage at the surface than a magnitude 8.2 earthquake 20 km down?

The Sea of Okhotsk earthquake (2013, magnitude 8.3) started about 600 km down, inside the sinking Pacific Plate. It was felt across Asia, but its deep focus meant the waves were much weaker by the time they reached the surface.

Mission 3

Runny or sticky magma

  • Viscosity = how much magma resists flowing.
  • Silica affects viscosity most. Most volcanic rocks are about 45–78% silica.
  • Low-silica (runny) lava flows easily: at Kīlauea, Hawaii, flow fronts can move up to 10 km/h.
  • High-silica (sticky) magma traps gas, so eruptions tend to be explosive.
Mission 3

Shield volcanoes

  • Built almost entirely from runny lava flows that spread a long way.
  • Shape: a broad cone with gentle slopes, like a warrior's shield.
  • Eruptions are mostly gentle (effusive): lava pours out rather than exploding.
  • Mauna Loa, Hawaii, is the largest: about 4,170 m (13,677 ft) above sea level.
  • Where: constructive margins (Iceland) and hotspots (Hawaii), where runny, low-silica magma rises from the mantle.
Mission 3

Composite volcanoes

  • Steep-sided cones built from alternating layers of lava and ash.
  • They can rise up to about 2,400 m (8,000 ft) above their base.
  • Sticky magma makes many eruptions explosive.
  • Where: destructive margins. Water released from the sinking oceanic plate makes the mantle above it melt; the magma that rises through the crust is sticky and gas-rich.
  • Examples: Nevado del Ruiz (Colombia) and Fuego (Guatemala).
  • Cambridge calls this a strato-volcano (composite cone).
Quick check
?A volcano has broad, gentle slopes built from thousands of thin lava flows. Which type is it?
Mission 3

Cinder cones

  • The simplest type of volcano.
  • Blobs of gas-rich lava are blown out of a single vent, fall back as cinders and pile up round it.
  • Parícutin, Mexico, began growing in a farm field in 1943. In 9 years it built a cone about 370 m (1,200 ft) high.
  • Cinder cones are small and usually short-lived.
Mission 3

Parts of a volcano

  • Magma chamber: a store of molten rock (magma) deep in the crust.
  • Vent: the opening that magma escapes through.
  • Crater: the funnel-shaped hollow at the top, left by eruptions.
  • Secondary cone: a smaller cone on the volcano's side, fed by a branch of the vent.
  • Once magma reaches the surface it is called lava.
Mission 3

Active, dormant or extinct?

  • Active: erupting now, or has erupted recently and is expected to again.
  • Dormant: has not erupted for a long time, but could erupt again.
  • Extinct: not expected ever to erupt again.
  • Careful: the Smithsonian's Global Volcanism Program notes that quiet gaps between big eruptions can last thousands of years, so "dormant" and "extinct" are judgements, not certainties.
Quick check
?A small cone has grown on the side of a large volcano, fed by a branch of the main pipe. What is it?
Mission 3

Hazards: pyroclastic flows and lava flows

  • Pyroclastic flows: avalanches of hot rock fragments and gas. They usually travel at more than 80 km/h, at 200–700 °C.
  • They knock down, burn or bury almost everything in their path.
  • Lava flows: runny lava can move up to 10 km/h. Sticky lava moves far more slowly. Lava flows mainly destroy buildings, roads and farmland.
Mission 3

Hazards: tephra, ash and gases

  • Tephra: rock fragments blown into the air, from fine ash to large volcanic bombs.
  • Ash is hard and abrasive. Its weight can collapse roofs; wet ash can weigh twice as much.
  • Ash clouds can make jet engines fail.
  • Toxic gases: most volcanic gas is water vapour, but sulfur dioxide causes acid rain downwind, and carbon dioxide can collect in hollows and kill.
Mission 3

Hazards: lahars

  • A lahar is a volcanic mudflow: ash and rock mixed with water, like wet concrete.
  • The water comes from melting snow and ice, heavy rain, or lakes and rivers.
  • Lahars follow river valleys. They can grow to more than 10 times their first size and exceed 200 km/h on steep slopes.
Quick check
?What makes a lahar different from a lava flow?
Mission 3

Why speed, size, frequency and spread matter

  • Speed: pyroclastic flows are so fast that leaving before they start is the only reliable protection.
  • Size: a bigger eruption throws out more ash and affects a wider area.
  • Frequency: volcanoes that erupt often are watched closely; long-quiet volcanoes can catch people unprepared.
  • Spread: ash can drift far downwind; lahars can travel tens of kilometres along valleys.
Mission 3

Armero, 1985

  • Nevado del Ruiz, Colombia: a composite volcano 5,389 m high, topped by glaciers.
  • On 13 November 1985 an eruption melted ice. Lahars raced down the river valleys.
  • Armero, 45 km away and built on old lahar deposits, was hit about two hours after the eruption. About 23,000 people died.
  • Scientists lacked equipment to monitor the volcano, and residents had been told they were safe for the night.
Mission check

?Volcano A is a broad shield of runny lava in a warm climate. Volcano B is a steep, ice-capped composite volcano. A town lies 40 km down a river valley from each. Which town faces the greater danger, and why?

At Armero, an explosive eruption of an ice-capped composite volcano melted ice. Lahars, a secondary hazard set off by the eruption, followed the valleys 45 km to the town in about two hours.

Mission 4

What is a natural hazard?

  • A natural hazard is a natural event, such as an earthquake, volcanic eruption, tropical storm or flood, that threatens people or could cause damage, destruction and death.
  • An event in an empty place is not a hazard to anyone. It becomes a disaster when it seriously harms people.
Mission 4

Hazard, exposure, vulnerability, risk

  • Hazard: the natural process that could cause harm.
  • Exposure: the people and buildings in harm's way.
  • Vulnerability: conditions that make people more likely to be harmed.
  • Risk: the chance of loss of life, injury or damage. It depends on the hazard, exposure, vulnerability and the capacity to cope.
Quick check
?Two towns of the same size face the same earthquake hazard. In Town X, more families live in unreinforced brick houses. What is different about Town X?
Mission 4

Economic factors

  • Money pays for strong buildings, monitoring and warnings.
  • 2000–2019, all disasters: low-income countries had 23% of disaster deaths but under 10% of the world's population.
  • High-income countries had 67% of the economic losses: US$1.99 trillion.
  • Richer places tend to lose more money; poorer places lose more lives.
Mission 4

Social factors: education and gender

  • Education: people who know the warning signs act sooner. A review of eleven studies found that formal education reduces vulnerability.
  • Gender: in Kuala Cangkoy (Aceh), four females died for every male. In Cuddalore, India, 391 women died and 146 men.
  • Oxfam's reasons: many women stayed behind to look for their children; more men than women can swim or climb trees.
  • In Aceh the wave struck on a Sunday morning, when women were at home near the shore and men were often away.
Quick check
?In Cuddalore, 391 women and 146 men died. What percentage of these deaths were female?
Mission 4

Impacts of earthquakes and eruptions

  • Health: Nepal 2015: over 8,790 deaths and 22,300 injuries. Armero 1985 (eruption): about 23,000 deaths.
  • Infrastructure: Nepal: over half a million houses destroyed and nearly 7,000 schools badly damaged. Ash can collapse roofs and stop jet engines.
  • Economy: Nepal's losses were about US$7 billion, about one-third of its GDP.
  • Homes and environment: at Fuego, Guatemala, 2018, an estimated 5,000 people lost their homes.
  • Positive impacts of volcanoes: fertile soils, geothermal energy, tourism.
  • Sort impacts as social, economic and environmental, and as positive or negative.
Quick check
?An ash cloud closes an airport for a week and flights are cancelled. Which kind of impact is this mainly?
Mission check

?In Nepal's 2015 earthquake, more women and girls died than men and boys. What best explains this?

The wave did not choose. Exposure (where people were) and social vulnerability (what they were able to do) decided who died, in Aceh in 2004 and in Nepal in 2015. Risk depends on people as well as the hazard.

Mission 5

Magnitude or intensity?

  • Magnitude: the size of the earthquake at its source. One number, the same wherever you measure it.
  • Intensity: how strongly the ground shakes at a place. It changes from place to place and usually falls with distance from the epicentre.
  • Magnitude is measured with seismometers; intensity comes from what people feel and see.
Mission 5

The Richter scale

  • Charles Richter introduced earthquake magnitude in 1935.
  • Each whole number up = 10 times the measured shaking on a seismogram.
  • The scale is logarithmic: a magnitude 6 is not "a bit bigger" than a 5.
  • Today the Richter scale is used mainly for small, local earthquakes.
Mission 5

The moment magnitude scale

  • Moment magnitude (Mw) is based on the physical size of the break: how big an area of the fault slipped, and how far.
  • It is more accurate for most earthquakes, so agencies such as the USGS use it.
  • Each whole number up = about 32 times more energy released.
Quick check
?Using about 32 times the energy for each whole step, how many times more energy does a magnitude 8 earthquake release than a magnitude 5?
Mission 5

The Mercalli scale

  • The Modified Mercalli scale measures intensity, in Roman numerals, from imperceptible shaking to catastrophic destruction.
  • It has no mathematical basis: it ranks observed effects.
  • Low numbers describe what people feel; high numbers describe damage to buildings.
  • Example: the 2013 Sea of Okhotsk earthquake (magnitude 8.3) was felt at intensity V at Severo-Kurilsk, Russia, and at II in Tokyo.
Quick check
?A newspaper wants to report how strongly the ground shook in one town, from residents' reports and damage. Which scale fits?
Mission 5

The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI)

  • The VEI measures how big an explosive eruption is, from 0 (non-explosive) to 8.
  • From VEI 2 up, each step is about 10 times more ash.
  • Most Hawaiian eruptions: VEI 0 or 1. Mount St Helens, USA, 1980: VEI 5, about 1 km³ of ash.
  • VEI 8 is a "supereruption": more than 1,000 km³.
Quick check
?About how many times more ash does a VEI 5 eruption produce than a VEI 3 eruption?
Mission 5

Big ones are rare

  • Averages of USGS counts, 2000–2021: about 1,613 earthquakes of magnitude 5–5.9 a year, 137 of 6–6.9, 14 of 7–7.9 and 1.2 of 8 or more.
  • Over the long term the USGS expects about one magnitude 8 or larger each year.
  • The bigger the earthquake, the less often it happens.
Mission 5

Using the scales to plan

  • A magnitude 8 releases about 32,000 times the energy of a magnitude 5 and shakes a far wider area.
  • But magnitude 5s happen about 1,600 times a year. A shallow one under a town of weak buildings can still do harm: intensity, not magnitude, decides the damage at a place.
  • Earthquakes cannot be predicted. Instead, USGS hazard maps show the shaking that has a 10% or a 2% chance of being exceeded in 50 years, and building rules are set from them.
Mission check

?A town lies near a volcano that has small eruptions (VEI 1–2) every few years and has had rare large ones (VEI 5). Which should planners prepare for?

For earthquakes too, magnitude and frequency go together: about 1,600 magnitude 5s and about one magnitude 8 a year. Planners use the scales to weigh how often against how much harm, and design for the shaking (intensity) each place is likely to feel.

Mission 6

Why people stay

  • About 800 million people live within 100 km of an active volcano.
  • Fertile soil: volcanic ash weathers into rich farmland.
  • Energy: about 90% of the energy for home heating in Iceland comes from geothermal sources.
  • Jobs: tourism, mining and quarrying.
  • Home: family, friends and work are there; moving costs money that many people do not have.
  • Rare events: big eruptions and earthquakes may not happen in a lifetime, and the risk can be reduced.
Quick check
?A family grows coffee on the rich soil of a volcano's lower slopes. Which reason for staying does this show?
Mission 6

Monitoring

  • Monitoring means watching for signs of change.
  • Volcanoes: GPS stations measure the ground's position every day, usually to better than 1 cm. Rising ground shows magma moving in. At Svartsengi, Iceland, the ground rose 35 cm between November and mid-December 2023.
  • Seismometers pick up the small earthquakes made by rising magma and show where they are.
  • Gas measurements can show fresh magma near the surface.
  • Tsunamis: DART stations, a pressure sensor on the seafloor linked to a surface buoy, detect a passing tsunami early and relay the data by satellite to warning centres.
Mission 6

Prediction

  • Volcanoes can sometimes be forecast. In 1991, scientists' warnings before Mount Pinatubo (Philippines) erupted saved at least 5,000 lives.
  • Where monitoring is missing, warnings fail: at Armero, Colombia, in 1985, scientists lacked the equipment to monitor the volcano.
  • Earthquakes cannot be predicted: no scientist has ever predicted a major earthquake.
Mission 6

Early warning technology

  • Earthquake early warning detects the fast, weaker P waves and sends alerts seconds to minutes before the strong shaking arrives.
  • Japan built a system after the 1995 Kobe earthquake. In 2011, no trains derailed.
  • Android phones now act as a network: alerts have been sent in 98 countries.
  • It is a warning, not a prediction: the earthquake has already started.
Quick check
?Why is an earthquake early-warning alert not a prediction?
Mission 6

Protection: buildings that resist shaking

  • Cross-bracing (steel braces) and shear walls stiffen a building against sideways shaking.
  • Base isolation: rubber or sliding bearings between the building and its foundations, so the ground moves more than the building.
  • In Mashiki, Japan, in 2016, wooden houses built before 1981 collapsed far more often than newer ones, so retrofitting older buildings matters.
  • New Zealand's Building Code requires buildings to withstand earthquakes.
Mission 6

Protection: sea walls and lava barriers

  • Tsunami walls: Japan, 2011: the tsunami overtopped sea walls mostly 5–10 m high, and more than 15,000 people died.
  • Japan now plans walls for frequent tsunamis, and evacuation for the largest ones.
  • Lava barriers: earth-and-rock walls diverted lava from Grindavík, Iceland. But in January 2024 a crack opened outside the barrier and lava burned three homes.
Quick check
?What does base isolation do?
Mission 6

Planning

  • Hazard mapping: maps show where shaking, lava, lahars or tsunamis are likely. Risk assessment combines them with where people live.
  • Land-use planning: California bans homes and offices over active faults; they must usually be set back 15 m (50 ft).
  • Emergency plans and drills: in 2015, more than 40 million people in 60 countries registered for "ShakeOut" drills: Drop, Cover and Hold On.
  • Evacuation: Japan's plans for the largest tsunamis centre on evacuation and hazard maps.
Mission 6

Responses after the event

  • Primary (immediate) responses: rescue, emergency aid, shelter, water, food and medical care. Nepal, 2015: 134 international rescue teams; transit shelters in Kathmandu, though tarpaulins ran short.
  • Secondary (longer-term) responses: rebuilding homes, roads and services. Nepal built over 700,000 earthquake-resistant houses, with grants paid only after engineers' checks.
Quick check
?Engineers inspect rebuilt homes before the next part of a grant is paid. Which kind of response is this?
Mission 6

Is it worth it? An evaluation

  • For: a US study found that adopting model building codes saves about $11 for every $1 spent, and mitigation grants about $6.
  • Limits: defences can be beaten by rare, extreme events (Japan's walls, 2011). Early warning gives only seconds to minutes. Earthquakes cannot be predicted.
  • Cost: strong buildings and monitoring cost money, so poorer countries can do less; low-income countries suffer the most deaths per disaster.
  • Exam move: weigh each method's cost, who it protects, and what it cannot do.
Mission check

?Many cities, such as Tokyo and San Francisco, stand in earthquake zones. Which answer best explains why millions of people stay?

People stay near volcanoes and faults because of benefits (soil, energy, jobs, home) and because the risk can be cut by monitoring, prediction (for volcanoes), protection and planning. None of these removes the risk completely.

Make a guess: Nepal 2015 and Kaikōura 2016 were both magnitude 7.8. One killed more than 8,790 people; the other killed 2. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 4.4.3 causes and impacts of an earthquake in a named country
  • 4.4.3 responses
  • 4.4.3 strategies and techniques to manage impacts there
Mission 7

Two earthquakes, two levels of wealth

  • In 2015, income per person (GNI per capita) was US$870 in Nepal and US$40,660 in New Zealand.
  • That is about 47 times as much in New Zealand.
Mission 7

Nepal, 25 April 2015: cause

  • Magnitude 7.8, on Saturday 25 April at 11:56 local time.
  • Plates: the Indian Plate moves north into the Eurasian Plate at about 45 mm a year, pushing up the Himalaya.
  • The rock broke along a thrust fault about 8 km deep: a shallow focus.
  • Epicentre near Barpak, Gorkha district, about 76 km north-west of Kathmandu. The break spread east, towards Kathmandu.
Mission 7

Nepal: primary effects

  • Over 8,790 deaths and 22,300 injuries by June 2015.
  • Over half a million houses destroyed.
  • Nearly 7,000 schools completely or badly damaged.
  • The lives of about 8 million people, almost a third of Nepal's population, were affected.
Mission 7

Nepal: secondary effects

  • At least 25,000 landslides on steep Himalayan slopes; an avalanche killed 20 people at Everest Base Camp.
  • Road damage cut off rural villages.
  • Damaged slopes became more likely to slide or flood in the monsoon.
  • Economic: about US$7 billion of damage and losses, about one-third of Nepal's GDP. At least 700,000 more people expected to fall into poverty.
Quick check
?Which of these was a secondary effect of the Nepal earthquake?
Mission 7

Nepal: immediate responses

  • The government asked for international help within hours.
  • Search and rescue: 134 international teams from 34 countries. Helicopters made 4,236 flights; 7,558 people were rescued by air and 4,689 by land.
  • Shelter: transit shelters were set up in Kathmandu, but there were not enough tarpaulins.
  • Maps: over 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, used by Nepal's army and the Red Cross.
Mission 7

Nepal: long-term responses

  • Over 700,000 earthquake-resistant houses were built under the government's programme.
  • Families got grants of NPR 300,000 (about US$3,000) in three parts. Later parts were paid only after engineers inspected the building.
  • Masons and engineers were trained. More than 18,000 vulnerable people who had not finished rebuilding were found and given extra help.
Mission 7

Kaikōura, New Zealand, 14 November 2016

  • Magnitude 7.8 at 12:02 a.m. on Monday 14 November; focus about 15 km deep.
  • Plates: the Pacific and Australian plates, moving about 40 mm a year relative to each other. More than 20 faults broke, a world record.
  • Effects: 2 deaths. Over 10,000 landslides; the coast rose by up to 6.5 m; a tsunami ran 6.9 m up the shore at Goose Bay.
  • Landslides cut road and rail links, stranding many visitors in Kaikōura.
Mission 7

Kaikōura: responses

  • Immediate: road access to Kaikōura was restored before Christmas 2016.
  • Long-term: the railway reopened in September 2017 and the main coast road (State Highway 1) in December 2017.
  • About 40,000 insurance claims went to the state Earthquake Commission, which pays for natural-hazard damage.

  • Who was exposed: Nepal's break spread towards the crowded Kathmandu Valley; poorer rural areas were hit hardest.
  • Recovery: New Zealand homeowners had state hazard insurance.
Mission 7

Preparation and management: an evaluation

  • Nepal before 2015: no national search-and-rescue system, though trained army and police teams carried out effective rescues. Many homes were built with no earthquake-resistant features.
  • Nepal after: inspected rebuilding makes the next earthquake less deadly, but it took years and depended on grants.
  • New Zealand: a building code and state insurance through private policies were in place before the earthquake.
  • A judgement to argue: the evidence earlier in this mission suggests that preparation before the event (building rules) mattered more for lives than any response after it.

Mission check

?Nepal wants fewer deaths when the next large earthquake strikes. Which action targets the main cause of the 2015 death toll?

The hazard was similar (magnitude 7.8, shallow). Vulnerability was not: Nepal's poorer, rural communities lived in houses with no earthquake-resistant features, while New Zealand builds to a seismic code and insures homes. Wealth shaped both the effects and the responses.

Mission 8

Mission 8: On Fuego, in 2018, a golf resort evacuated safely. A village two kilometres further on was buried. Why?

Make a guess: On Fuego, in 2018, a golf resort evacuated safely. A village two kilometres further on was buried. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 4.4.4 causes and impacts of an eruption of a named volcano
  • 4.4.4 responses to the eruption
  • 4.4.4 strategies and techniques to manage eruption impacts there
Mission 8

Fuego: where and why

  • Fuego is a steep composite volcano (stratovolcano) above the city of Antigua, Guatemala. Its summit is 3,763 m high.
  • Cause: the Cocos Plate sinks beneath the Caribbean Plate (a destructive margin). Water from the sinking plate makes the mantle above it melt; the magma that rises is sticky and gas-rich.
  • Fuego has had at least 60 summit eruptions in about 450 years, and has been "vigorously erupting" since January 2002.
Mission 8

3 June 2018: the eruption

  • 10:00: the first pyroclastic flows.
  • About 13:10: the eruption peaked, with an ash column 16–19 km high.
  • 15:05: a large pulse of flows raced down the Las Lajas ravine. At 15:09 it hit the bridge and spilled out onto the road towards San Miguel Los Lotes.
Quick check
?What mainly buried San Miguel Los Lotes on 3 June 2018?
Mission 8

Shorter-term impacts

  • About two-thirds of the north-west part of San Miguel Los Lotes was completely buried and destroyed.
  • An estimated 5,000 people lost their homes.
  • The death toll is uncertain. Official counts of the missing were 332 (Naismith et al., 2020) and 430 (Charbonnier et al., 2023). Survivors' estimates are far higher.
Mission 8

Longer-term impacts

  • In the rainy weeks that followed, rain on the new deposits produced many lahars down the valleys.
  • Thousands of families had to start again elsewhere: their homes were buried.
  • One of the two volcano observatories, at Sangre de Cristo, was evacuated and its observer moved.
Quick check
?Two research papers give 332 and 430 people officially missing at Fuego, and survivors' estimates are far higher. What should an exam answer do?
Mission 8

Responses on the day

  • Scientists gave updates on the volcano's activity continuously from 06:30.
  • The authorities "persistently tried to remove people from high-risk areas".
  • But the system relied on self-evacuation: residents were expected to notice danger and leave by themselves, then be taken from a safe point to temporary shelters.
Mission 8

Why the resort and the village differed

  • La Reunión, a private golf resort, evacuated successfully. Los Lotes, about 2 km further south, suffered heavy loss of life.
  • Researchers found La Reunión had a culture of preventative self-evacuation: leaving early, before danger was certain.
  • Many residents believed wealth decided who survived. The researchers call this "debatable".
  • Communities around Fuego differed in their experience of past eruptions and their communication with the monitoring and civil-protection agencies.
Quick check
?Residents near Fuego clearly remembered only the eruptions that had forced a whole-community response. What does this show?
Mission 8

Managing eruptions at Fuego

  • Monitoring: Guatemala's volcano agency (INSIVUMEH) runs a network of instruments from a central office, plus observatories on the volcano.
  • Warnings: the civil-protection agency (CONRED) issues alerts and organises evacuation.
  • Local coordinators (COLREDes) link villages to the agencies. Researchers recommend supporting them: working radios, good batteries and ongoing training.
  • Evaluation: pyroclastic flows are too fast to escape once they start, so leaving early is the only reliable protection.
Mission check

?Fuego erupted strongly again in November 2018. Which response would do most to stop a repeat of June's deaths?

Fast pyroclastic flows spilled out of the Las Lajas ravine. La Reunión had a habit of leaving early; Los Lotes depended on residents leaving by themselves. Early, organised evacuation is the only protection against flows this fast.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Heat from inside Earth drives convection in the mantle; gravity adds slab pull and ridge push. Together they move the plates.
  • Plates move apart (constructive) or together (destructive, collision).
  • Plates also slide past each other at conservative margins.
  • Each margin has its own landforms, earthquakes and volcanoes.

Mission 2

  • Earthquakes happen where locked plates suddenly slip, in narrow belts along plate margins.
  • The focus is underground; the epicentre is above it. Shallow-focus earthquakes (0–70 km) do the most damage; deep-focus ones happen only in sinking plates at destructive margins.

Mission 3

  • Runny, low-silica lava builds gentle shield volcanoes; sticky, gas-rich magma builds steep, explosive composite volcanoes.
  • Hazards differ in speed and spread: pyroclastic flows are fast near the volcano; lahars follow valleys far from it.

Mission 4

  • A natural hazard threatens people; risk depends on the hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity to cope.
  • Economic and social (education, gender) factors make some people and countries more vulnerable than others.

Mission 5

  • Magnitude (Richter, moment magnitude) measures an earthquake's size; intensity (Mercalli) measures shaking at a place; the VEI measures explosive eruptions.
  • Each step is a multiple (about 32 times the energy for magnitude; about 10 times the ash for the VEI), and bigger events are rarer.

Mission 6

  • People live with tectonic hazards for economic and social reasons, and because events are rare.
  • Monitoring, prediction, protection (building design, defences) and planning (maps, zoning, drills) reduce risk; primary and secondary responses deal with the event and the rebuild.

Mission 7

  • Primary effects come from the shaking; secondary effects follow (landslides, tsunami, poverty).
  • Immediate responses save lives (rescue, shelter); long-term responses rebuild. Wealth and building standards explain why two magnitude 7.8 earthquakes had such different outcomes.

Mission 8

  • Fuego is a composite volcano on a destructive margin (Cocos under Caribbean). On 3 June 2018, fast pyroclastic flows buried part of San Miguel Los Lotes; about 5,000 people lost their homes.
  • Responses relied on self-evacuation. Early, organised evacuation and clear communication reduce deaths. Supporting local coordinators (COLREDes) helps too.
