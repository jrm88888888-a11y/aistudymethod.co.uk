Mission 1

Mission 1: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Make a guess: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Mission 1

Diffusion

  • Particles move randomly, all the time.
  • More particles cross from the crowded side than from the other: net movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • This is movement down a concentration gradient.
  • It is passive: no energy from ATP.
  • At equal concentrations, particles still cross both ways. Net movement is zero.
  • Say: "net movement stops" — not: "the molecules stop moving".
Mission 1

Straight through the bilayer

  • Simple diffusion: particles pass between the phospholipids.
  • Only small non-polar molecules do this readily: O2, CO2.
  • Permeability of the bilayer depends only on size and polarity or charge. It is not selective in any other way.

Mission 1

Channel proteins

  • Facilitated diffusion: diffusion through a protein. Still passive; still down the gradient.
  • A channel protein spans the membrane with a water-filled, hydrophilic pore. Ions pass through it, avoiding the hydrophobic core.
  • Selective: the pore's width and the charges lining it let only certain ions through. A K⁺ channel passes K⁺ but not the smaller Na⁺.
  • Many are gated: open or closed. No ions pass when closed.
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts of water cross quickly.
  • Say: "a channel is specific" — not: "a channel is a hole any small particle can use".
Quick check
?A potassium channel in a cell membrane is open. Which statement describes the movement of ions through it?
Mission 1

Gated channels in neurons

  • Neurotransmitter-gated: the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor. Acetylcholine binds on the outside; the channel opens; mainly Na⁺ diffuses in.
  • Voltage-gated: Na⁺ and K⁺ channels in neurons open when the membrane potential changes (depolarisation).
  • Either way, ions diffuse down their gradients: no ATP.
  • Acetylcholine does not pass through the channel. Na⁺ does.
Quick check
?Acetylcholine released at a synapse binds to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors on the postsynaptic membrane. What happens as a result?
Mission 1

Which route?

  • O2, CO2 → between the phospholipids.
  • Water → between the phospholipids, slowly.
  • Water → aquaporins, fast.
  • Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) → channel proteins.
  • The bilayer sorts only by size and charge. Proteins make a membrane selective: each lets through particular substances.

Two routes, two rules: the bilayer lets through what is small and non-polar; proteins let through specific substances, ions included.

Mission 2

Osmosis

  • Osmosis: the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.
  • The membrane lets water through but not the solute.
  • Water molecules cross randomly both ways; more cross towards the more concentrated solution.
  • Water moves from the lower solute concentration to the higher solute concentration.
  • Compared with another solution, one is hypotonic (lower solute concentration), hypertonic (higher) or isotonic (equal).
  • Say: "lower solute concentration" — not: "higher water concentration".
Mission 2

Water potential

  • Water potential (ψ): the tendency of water to move out of a solution.
  • It is the potential energy of water per unit volume, measured relative to pure water at atmospheric pressure and 20 °C.
  • Pure water has ψ = 0: the highest value.
  • Solutes lower ψ. Solutions have negative ψ; more solute, more negative.
  • Units: kPa.
  • Water moves from higher (less negative) ψ to lower (more negative) ψ.
  • Say: "−300 kPa is higher than −900 kPa" — not: "−900 kPa is the bigger water potential".
Quick check
?Two adjacent plant cells have water potentials of −300 kPa (cell A) and −900 kPa (cell B). Which statement describes the net movement of water between them?
Mission 2

Cells without a wall

  • Outside more dilute than the cytoplasm: water enters. The cell swells and can burst: lysis.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves. The cell shrinks and its surface puckers: crenation.
  • These are hypotonic and hypertonic surroundings.
  • Equal concentrations: water still crosses both ways, with no net movement.
  • This is a dynamic equilibrium.
  • Freshwater single-celled organisms, such as Paramecium, gain water all the time. A contractile vacuole expels it.
  • Multicellular animals keep their tissue fluid isotonic with their cells.
Mission 2

Cells with a wall

  • Outside more dilute: water enters. The cell contents push on the wall: turgor pressure. The cell is turgid.
  • The wall resists, so the cell does not burst.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves; the contents shrink; the membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis.
  • The wall is freely permeable: the gap fills with the outside solution.
  • No turgor pressure: the cell is flaccid.
  • Say: "the wall is freely permeable; the membrane is partially permeable" — not: "the wall keeps water out".
Quick check
?A strip of onion epidermis is mounted in a concentrated sucrose solution and viewed under a microscope. Which statement describes what happens to its cells?
Mission 2

Aquaporins

  • Water crosses the bilayer, but slowly.
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts cross quickly.
  • Evidence: Preston, Agre and colleagues (1992) made frog egg cells produce an aquaporin. In dilute solution these eggs swelled fast and burst; ordinary eggs barely swelled.
  • Cells with many aquaporins: kidney collecting-duct cells, root cells.
Mission 2

Isotonic fluids in medicine

  • A common drip fluid has about the same solute concentration as blood plasma: 0.9% sodium chloride.
  • It is isotonic with plasma.
  • Pure water would enter red blood cells by osmosis. They would swell and burst.
  • A concentrated solution would draw water out of them. They would shrink.
  • Organs for transplant are stored in an isotonic solution, so their cells neither swell nor shrink.
Quick check
?A kidney removed for transplantation is bathed in a solution while it is stored. Which type of solution is used, and why?
Mission check

?Jam contains so much sugar that most bacteria landing on it cannot grow. Which statement explains this?

Water follows solute, whether it is entering red blood cells from pure water or leaving bacteria for the jam.

In water potential terms: the jam's ψ is far lower than the cytoplasm's.

Mission 3

Two parts of water potential

  • ψw = ψs + ψp
  • Solute potential: the effect of dissolved solutes. Zero for pure water; negative when solutes are present.
  • Pressure potential: the effect of pressure. In a plant cell, the wall pressing back on the contents makes it positive.
  • In xylem, where sap is under tension, pressure potential is negative.
  • A plasmolysed cell: pressure potential = 0, so water potential = solute potential.

Mission 3Practical

Water potential of plant tissue

  • Measure changes in length and mass; deduce the isotonic solute concentration.
  • Make a dilution series of sucrose from a stock solution: water and 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8.
  • Concentrations in mol dm⁻³.
  • Cut equal cylinders with a cork borer; blot; weigh each (or measure its length).
  • Leave in each solution for the same time at the same temperature. Blot the same way; reweigh.
  • % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. This allows for different starting masses.
  • Repeat at each concentration; use the mean.

Mission 3

How reliable is each mean?

  • Repeat each concentration, e.g. five cylinders.
  • Standard deviation (SD): the spread of individual results around the mean.
  • Standard error (SE) = SD ÷ √n: how precisely the mean is known. More repeats, smaller SE.
  • Example: SD = 1.0%, n = 5: SE = 1.0 ÷ √5 = 0.45%.
  • Show SE as error bars on the graph of means.
  • Formulae need not be memorised; a calculator or spreadsheet does them.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose. Their percentage changes in mass had a mean of −3.0%, a standard deviation of 1.0% and a standard error of 0.45%. What does the standard error indicate?
Mission 3

Explaining tissue changes

  • Hypotonic bath (higher ψw than the cells): water enters. The wall resists, so ψp rises. In pure water, uptake stops when ψs + ψp = 0. Tissue gains mass.
  • Hypertonic bath (lower ψw): water leaves. ψp falls to zero as the protoplast pulls from the wall. Then ψw = ψs, which falls as the sap concentrates. Tissue loses mass.
  • Isotonic: the cells' ψw equals the bath's. No net change.
Quick check
?Plant tissue is bathed in a sucrose solution with a water potential of −2500 kPa. The cells initially have a solute potential of −900 kPa and a pressure potential of +600 kPa. Which statement explains what happens?
Mission check

?Potato cylinders were left uncovered on a bench for two hours, so they lost some water by evaporation. They were then used in a mass-change experiment in sucrose solutions. The zero point marks the solution that matches the cells; change the cells and the zero point moves.

In water potential terms: the dried tissue has a lower (more negative) ψ.

Mission 4

Active transport

  • Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient: from low to high concentration.
  • It needs energy from ATP, made in respiration.
  • It needs a membrane protein that binds the particle.
  • The proteins are pump proteins.
  • Each carrier binds only specific particles: active transport is selective.
  • Say: "active transport moves particles against their gradient" — not: "active transport is faster diffusion".
Mission 4

How a carrier pumps

  • (1) The particle binds to a specific site on the carrier, on one side of the membrane.
  • (2) ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pᵢ. The phosphate attaches to the carrier, which changes shape.
  • (3) The particle is released on the other side. The phosphate leaves; the carrier returns to its first shape.
  • No ATP, no shape change, no transport.
Quick check
?Root cells absorb nitrate ions from soil water in which the nitrate concentration is much lower than in the cell. When the roots are deprived of oxygen, nitrate uptake falls sharply. What explains these observations?
Mission 4

How do you know it is active?

  • Sign 1: the substance builds up above the outside concentration.
  • Sign 2: uptake falls sharply when the cell's ATP supply falls: no oxygen, or a respiratory inhibitor.
  • Diffusion is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. That is the test.
  • Ions leak back down their gradient. So a gradient needs a continuing energy supply, not a one-off.

Mission 4

The sodium–potassium pump

  • One carrier, two ions, both moved against their gradients.
  • Each cycle, one ATP: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in.
  • An exchange transporter: one ion out for another in.
  • Result: Na⁺ is low inside the cell; K⁺ is high.
  • One more positive charge leaves than enters, so the inside stays negative: the pump helps generate the membrane potential.
  • The pump keeps up the Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients that the rest of the potential depends on.
  • Say: "3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in" — not: "Na⁺ in, K⁺ out", nor "one for one".
Quick check
?A neuron is treated with a drug that inhibits sodium–potassium pumps. Over the following hours its resting membrane potential declines steadily towards zero. Why does inhibiting the pump have this effect?
Mission 4

Co-transport: glucose rides in with sodium

  • (1) Na⁺/K⁺ pumps on the blood side of each epithelial cell move Na⁺ out into the blood. Na⁺ inside the cell stays low.
  • (2) In the membrane facing the lumen, a co-transporter protein binds Na⁺ and glucose together. Na⁺ moves in down its gradient and carries glucose in against its gradient.
  • (3) Glucose builds up in the cell, then leaves into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • The protein is a sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter. This is indirect active transport: ATP is used by the pump, not by the cotransporter.
  • Cells of the proximal convoluted tubule in the nephron reabsorb glucose from the filtrate the same way.
  • Say: "the co-transporter uses the Na⁺ gradient" — not: "the co-transporter hydrolyses ATP".
Quick check
?Epithelial cells from the small intestine were treated with a substance that inhibits sodium–potassium pumps. Uptake of glucose from the lumen by the cells fell sharply, although the sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters (co-transporter proteins) themselves were not affected by the substance. What explains this result?
Mission 4

Bulk transport: vesicles

  • Large molecules and particles cannot cross the bilayer. They move in vesicles.
  • Endocytosis (in): the membrane folds inwards around the material and pinches off a vesicle.
  • Phagocytosis: solid particles, e.g. a phagocyte engulfing a bacterium. Pinocytosis: droplets of liquid.
  • Exocytosis (out): a vesicle moves to the membrane, fuses with it and releases its contents outside. E.g. digestive enzymes, hormones, neurotransmitters.
  • Both need ATP: to reshape membrane and move vesicles.
  • Both depend on membrane fluidity: bilayers bend, pinch off, fuse and re-seal.
  • Clathrin builds a lattice on the cytoplasmic side, bending the membrane into a pit that pinches off. The coat is then shed.
  • In exocytosis the vesicle membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.
  • Say: "endocytosis and exocytosis need ATP" — not: "they are passive because no pump is involved".
Quick check
?What is the role of clathrin in the formation of a vesicle?
Mission check

?Red blood cells hold K⁺ at a much higher concentration than the plasma around them. In donated blood stored at 4 °C, the cells slowly lose K⁺. When the cells are warmed to 37 °C with glucose, they take K⁺ back up to the original high level. Which explanation fits these observations?

A gradient against the odds is never free. Stop the energy supply and it runs down; restore it and the pumps rebuild it.

Mission 5

The secretory pathway

  • Ribosomes on the rough ER make the protein. It passes into the space inside the RER.
  • The protein is folded, then carried in transport vesicles that bud off the RER.
  • The vesicles fuse with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein.
  • Secretory vesicles bud off the Golgi and move to the cell surface.
  • They fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing the protein outside: exocytosis.
  • Proteins for secretion are made by ribosomes bound to the RER. Proteins that stay in the cytoplasm are made by free ribosomes.

Mission 5

Finishing the protein

  • Many polypeptides do not work until they are modified.
  • Carbohydrate chains are added to many proteins, making glycoproteins. This starts in the RER and is finished in the Golgi.
  • The Golgi sorts proteins into vesicles by destination, e.g. secretion or the plasma membrane.
  • Say: "the Golgi modifies and packages proteins" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".
Mission 5

From pre-proinsulin to insulin

  • The insulin gene codes for one polypeptide: pre-proinsulin.
  • Its signal sequence directs it into the RER. There the signal sequence is cut off: proinsulin.
  • Proinsulin folds, and disulfide bonds form.
  • In the Golgi and secretory vesicles, a central section is cut out.
  • What remains is insulin: two chains, A and B, held by disulfide bonds. Now it can function.
Quick check
?Insulin consists of two polypeptide chains, A and B, held together by disulfide bonds, yet it is coded for by a single gene. How is functional insulin produced?
Mission 5

Vesicles: bud, travel, fuse

  • A vesicle buds from one membrane and fuses with another. Membranes are fluid, so they can bend, pinch off and fuse.
  • Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. Its contents leave; its membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.
  • Endocytosis: the plasma membrane folds in around material and pinches off as a vesicle.
  • Both use energy from ATP.
  • Clathrin proteins form a lattice on the cytoplasmic side of the membrane, bending it into a pit that pinches off as a vesicle.
  • Examples: exocytosis of neurotransmitter at a synapse; endocytosis of a bacterium by a phagocyte.
Quick check
?Which property of membranes allows a vesicle to bud off one membrane and later fuse with another?
Mission 5

Why build compartments?

  • Membranes keep enzymes and their substrates together in a small volume, so reactions run faster.
  • Membranes keep incompatible processes apart: hydrolytic enzymes stay inside lysosomes.
  • Each compartment can keep its own conditions: the inside of a lysosome is acidic (about pH 5).
  • Proteins made for export stay inside membranes until they leave the cell.
  • Lysosomes fuse with phagocytic vacuoles: engulfed bacteria are digested without harming the cell.
Quick check
?A phagocyte engulfs a bacterium into a phagocytic vacuole, and lysosomes then fuse with the vacuole. What is the advantage of digesting the bacterium inside this compartment rather than in the cytoplasm?
Mission 5

The nucleus: a compartment for mRNA

  • Transcription happens in the nucleus; translation happens on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So mRNA can be modified before it ever meets a ribosome.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus: mRNA may meet ribosomes immediately, even while it is still being made.
Quick check
?In a eukaryotic cell, newly transcribed RNA is modified in the nucleus before it is translated. How does the separation of the nucleus from the cytoplasm make this possible?
Mission check

?Plasma cells make antibodies, which are proteins they secrete into the blood. At which stage, if any, is a newly made antibody molecule free in the cell's cytoplasm?

Like the pancreas's enzymes, an exported protein is never loose in the cytoplasm. Membranes surround it from the RER until exocytosis.

Mission 6

An action potential

  • A nerve impulse is an action potential: a brief reversal of the membrane potential, lasting about 1–2 ms.
  • Resting: −70 mV.
  • Depolarisation: a stimulus raises the potential to threshold (about −55 mV), then it shoots up to about +30 to +40 mV.
  • Repolarisation: it falls back towards −70 mV.
  • Hyperpolarisation: a brief dip below −70 mV, then back to rest.
Mission 6

Ions and gates

  • A stimulus opens some Na⁺ channels: Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation.
  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open. More Na⁺ in → more depolarisation → more channels open: positive feedback.
  • At the peak, Na⁺ channels close. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open: K⁺ diffuses out → repolarisation.
  • K⁺ channels are slow to close → too much K⁺ leaves → hyperpolarisation.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump and leak channels then hold the resting potential.
  • Say: "K⁺ diffusing out repolarises the membrane" — not: "the pump repolarises it".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events produces depolarisation and then repolarisation during an action potential?
Mission 6

All or nothing

  • Below threshold: no action potential.
  • At or above threshold: a full-size action potential, the same size every time.
  • This is the all-or-nothing principle.
  • So a stronger stimulus cannot make a bigger action potential.
  • It makes more action potentials per second: a higher frequency.
Quick check
?Two oscilloscope traces (illustrative data) were recorded from the same sensory neurone. With a weak stimulus there were 5 action potentials in 100 ms; with a strong stimulus there were 20 action potentials in 100 ms. In both traces every action potential peaked at +30 mV. What do the traces show?
Mission 6

Local currents carry it along

  • Na⁺ that entered at the active region diffuses sideways inside the axon.
  • Outside, Na⁺ moves towards the active region.
  • The next region is depolarised to threshold → its own voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → a new action potential.
  • The region behind is refractory (it cannot fire again yet), so the impulse moves forwards only.
  • The ions only move a short way: the impulse travels, not the ions.
Mission 6

Myelin speeds things up

  • Myelinated fibres conduct much faster than non-myelinated fibres of similar diameter.
  • Myelin insulates: no ions cross the membrane under it.
  • Ion channels and pumps are clustered at the nodes of Ranvier.
  • So action potentials occur only at the nodes. Local currents spread to the next node.
  • The impulse jumps node to node: saltatory conduction. Fewer regions to depolarise → faster.
  • Say: "myelin insulates" — not: "myelin conducts the impulse".
Mission 6

What sets the speed?

  • Myelination: the biggest effect.
  • Axon diameter: wider axon → less resistance to current flow inside it → faster.
  • Squid giant axon: non-myelinated but about 0.5 mm wide. Fast enough for its escape response.
  • Small non-myelinated fibres are slow: often about 1 m/s or less.

Mission 6

Reading an oscilloscope trace

  • x-axis: time (ms). y-axis: membrane potential (mV).
  • Flat at −70 mV: resting. Steep rise: depolarisation. Fall: repolarisation. Dip: hyperpolarisation.
  • Worked example: 6 action potentials in 30 ms. Frequency?
  • Step 1: 30 ms = 0.030 s.
  • Step 2: 6 ÷ 0.030 = 200 impulses per second.
  • Count spikes; divide by time in seconds.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Simple diffusion: small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids, down their gradient; ions and polar molecules are kept out by the hydrophobic core.
  • Facilitated diffusion: specific channel or carrier proteins carry them across, still passively and down the gradient.

Mission 2

  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane into the more concentrated solution; cells without walls burst or shrink, cells with walls become turgid or plasmolysed.
  • Water moves from higher to lower water potential; pure water has ψ = 0 and solutes make ψ negative.

Mission 3

  • Water potential = solute potential + pressure potential; solute potential is zero or negative, pressure potential in a turgid cell is positive.
  • Tissue in a sucrose series: the concentration giving zero change in mass matches the tissue.

Mission 4

  • Active transport uses carrier (pump) proteins and ATP to move specific particles against their concentration gradient; it stops when the ATP supply stops.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump keeps Na⁺ low inside ileum cells; Na⁺ then flows in through a co-transporter, carrying glucose in against its gradient.
  • Endocytosis and exocytosis move large substances in vesicles and need energy.

Mission 5

  • Secreted proteins: made on RER → transport vesicles → Golgi (modified, packaged) → secretory vesicles → exocytosis.
  • Compartments concentrate enzymes and keep incompatible processes apart; exported or digestive enzymes stay behind membranes.
  • The nuclear envelope separates transcription from translation, so mRNA can be modified first.

Mission 6

  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (depolarisation); then K⁺ channels open (repolarisation). Action potentials are all-or-nothing; strength is coded by frequency.
  • Myelinated and wider fibres conduct faster.
  • Myelin restricts action potentials to the nodes (saltatory conduction).
