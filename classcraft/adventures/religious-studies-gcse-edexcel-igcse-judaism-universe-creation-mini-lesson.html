Mission 1: Jews say "the LORD is One" twice every day. What does that one word tell them about God?

Make a guess: Jews say "the LORD is One" twice every day. What does that one word tell them about God?

Mission 1

One

  • Judaism is monotheistic: there is one God, and nothing else is divine.
  • God is a unity: not divided into parts or persons, and with no partner or rival.
  • The central statement is the Shema: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • The first of the Ten Commandments in Jewish counting: "I am the LORD thy God" (Exodus 20:2).
  • Maimonides (1138–1204) made God's unity the second of his Thirteen Principles of Faith (Commentary on the Mishnah, c. 1168).
  • Your spec calls God the Almighty.
Mission 1Read the source

The Shema

> "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE. And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." (Deuteronomy 6:4–5)

  • The passage continues: keep these words on your heart; teach them to your children; speak of them at home and on the way; bind them on your hand and between your eyes (tefillin); write them on your doorposts (mezuzah) (Deuteronomy 6:6–9, paraphrase).
  • Jews recite the Shema morning and evening, and ideally as their last words.

Q1 What response to the one God does verse 5 ask for?

Show answer

Love with the whole self: heart, soul and might.

Q2 Name two ways verses 6–9 keep God's oneness in daily life.

Show answer

Teaching children; tefillin; mezuzah on the doorpost.

Mission 1

Creator

  • "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)
  • Most Jews believe God created from nothing, freely and with purpose; the world is good.
  • God rested on the seventh day (Genesis 2:2–3): the origin of Shabbat.
  • Genesis 2:15 gives humans the task of looking after God's world: "to dress it and to keep it".
  • Importance today: blessings (berakhot) before food thank the Creator; Shabbat remembers creation each week.
Mission 1

Humans in creation

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Genesis 2: "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life" (Genesis 2:7). God plants a garden, places the man there, gives him a command and makes a partner for him: a Creator who is close and personal.
  • "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15): humans are stewards of God's world.
  • Humans are made last, as the goal of creation: the only creatures in God's image, responsible for the rest.
  • Tikkun olam ("repairing the world"): Jews share God's work by improving the world through justice, charity and care for the environment.

Mission 1

Law-Giver

  • Jews believe God gave the Torah to Moses at Mount Sinai: teaching and law for how to live.
  • The rabbis counted 613 mitzvot (commandments) in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative.
  • The law is part of the covenant: Jews believe God freed Israel and gave the law; Israel agreed to keep it.
  • Importance today: kashrut, Shabbat, prayer, charity and ethics are ways of obeying the Law-Giver. Keeping mitzvot is a response of love, not only duty.
Mission 1

Judge: just, loving and merciful

  • In Jewish belief:
  • Judge: God judges people's actions with perfect justice. Abraham asks: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25)
  • Merciful and loving: "The LORD, the LORD, God, merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6). This begins the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on festivals.
  • A rabbinic teaching: God created the world with both justice and mercy; with justice alone the world could not survive, with mercy alone wrong would run free (Genesis Rabbah 12:15, paraphrase).
  • God is holy: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3).
  • Justice and mercy together: wrong matters, and God forgives those who return.

Mission 1

Beyond everything, close to everyone

  • In Jewish belief:
  • Omnipotent (all-powerful): God creates by word and frees Israel from Egypt.
  • Omniscient (all-knowing) and all-good.
  • Omnipresent (everywhere): "If I ascend up into heaven, Thou art there" (Psalm 139:8).
  • Transcendent (beyond the universe) and immanent (present in it), concerned with humanity and intervening in history, as in the Exodus.
Mission 1

Evil and suffering

  • The problem: if God is all-powerful, all-knowing and all-good, why is there evil and suffering?
  • Free will: "I have set before thee life and death … therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19). Humans have a yetzer ha-tov and yetzer ha-ra (good and bad inclinations): much evil comes from human choice.
  • A test or discipline: suffering can strengthen faith and character.
  • Mystery: God's ways are higher than ours (Isaiah 55:9); humans cannot see the whole picture.
  • The Shoah (Holocaust) in the Jewish community: Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992, Faith after the Holocaust, 1973) spoke of God "hiding the face" to leave humans free; Emil Fackenheim (1916–2003) said Jews must not give Hitler a victory by abandoning Judaism; Richard Rubenstein (1924–2021, After Auschwitz, 1966) rejected belief in a God who acts in history (all paraphrased).
  • Response in action: comfort the suffering, give tzedakah, work for tikkun olam.
Mission 1Read the source

Job and Jonah

  • Job: a righteous man loses his children, wealth and health. At first he says: "the LORD gave, and the LORD hath taken away; blessed be the name of the LORD." (Job 1:21)
  • His friends say he must have sinned; the book rejects this. God answers from a storm: "Where wast thou when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4) Message: suffering is not always punishment; God's wisdom is beyond human understanding.
  • Jonah: he flees God's call, but cannot escape God (omnipresence). When Nineveh repents, God spares it: "should not I have pity on Nineveh, that great city" (Jonah 4:11).
  • Message: God is merciful to all peoples, and repentance changes judgement.

Q2 Which two attributes of God does the Book of Jonah show?

Show answer

Mission 2: Jews pray for "the world to come", yet say this life matters most. What do they believe follows death?

Make a guess: Jews pray for "the world to come", yet say this life matters most. What do they believe follows death?

Mission 2

Body and soul

  • "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)
  • In Jewish teaching, a human is one being: a body from the earth and a soul (neshamah) breathed by God.
  • The morning prayers thank God for a soul that is pure when given (Talmud Berakhot 60b, paraphrase).
  • The body is not a prison: it is God's gift, used to keep the mitzvot. So the dead are buried with care and respect.
  • A Talmud parable: a blind man and a lame man steal fruit together; the owner judges them together. So God judges body and soul together (Talmud Sanhedrin 91a–b, paraphrase).
  • Your spec stresses the unity of body and soul.
Mission 2

A faith centred on this life

  • The Torah says almost nothing about life after death. Its rewards are long life, family and the land.
  • Sheol, in the earliest books, is the shadowy place of all the dead, good and bad alike: "in the nether-world who will give Thee thanks?" (Psalm 6:5)
  • Later books and the rabbis developed fuller ideas: resurrection, judgement, a world to come.
  • The rabbis still put this life first: this world is like a hallway before the banquet hall; prepare in the hallway (Pirkei Avot 4:16, paraphrase).
  • One hour of repentance and good deeds in this world is better than all the life of the world to come (Pirkei Avot 4:17, paraphrase).
Mission 2Read the source

Ecclesiastes 12

> "And the dust returneth to the earth as it was, and the spirit returneth unto God who gave it." (Ecclesiastes 12:7)

> "The end of the matter, all having been heard: fear God, and keep His commandments; for this is the whole man. For God shall bring every work into the judgment concerning every hidden thing, whether it be good or whether it be evil." (Ecclesiastes 12:13–14)

  • The chapter opens: "Remember then thy Creator in the days of thy youth" (Ecclesiastes 12:1).
  • Readings: many Jews see verse 7 as the soul surviving and returning to God, and verses 13–14 as judgement.
  • Others note the book's doubts: "Who knoweth the spirit of man whether it goeth upward" (Ecclesiastes 3:21). For them, the message is: live well now.

Mission 2

Gan Eden, Gehinnom and Olam Ha-Ba

  • Gan Eden ("Garden of Eden"): in rabbinic teaching, where the souls of the righteous enjoy closeness to God.
  • Gehinnom: named after a valley outside Jerusalem. For most souls it is a place of cleansing, lasting at most twelve months (Mishnah Eduyot 2:10, paraphrase), not eternal hell.
  • So mourners say Kaddish for eleven months: they do not suggest a parent needed the full twelve.
  • Olam Ha-Ba ("the world to come"): the soul's life after death, or the renewed world after the resurrection. Rabbis disagree which.
  • All Israel have a share in the world to come (Mishnah Sanhedrin 10:1, paraphrase), and so do the righteous of all nations (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase).
Mission 2

Resurrection and judgement

> "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2)

  • Resurrection (techiyat ha-metim): Jewish tradition teaches that at the end of days, in the Messianic Age, God will raise the dead, body and soul reunited.
  • The Amidah, prayed daily, praises God who "revives the dead". Maimonides made it his thirteenth principle.
  • Judgement in three stages: each year at Rosh Hashanah; at death, when the soul is judged; and at the end of days.
  • God judges with justice and mercy: by deeds and by heart, not by being Jewish or not.

Mission 2Diversity

Orthodox and Reform beliefs

  • Orthodox: the soul is immortal; bodily resurrection in the Messianic Age; judgement after death and at the end of days; Gan Eden and Gehinnom.
  • Because of resurrection and respect for the body, Orthodox Jews bury the dead quickly and do not cremate.
  • Reform: classical Reform (Pittsburgh, 1885) kept the immortality of the soul but rejected bodily resurrection and Gehenna and Eden as places of reward and punishment.
  • Many Reform prayer books replaced "who revives the dead" with "who gives life to all"; some recent ones offer both.
  • Many Reform Jews speak of living on through deeds, memory and children; some believe in spiritual resurrection; many stay agnostic. Cremation is permitted.
  • Both stress this life: what happens next is God's concern; ours is how we live now.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Belief in life after death is not important for Jews."

  • For: the Torah barely mentions it; Avot 4:17 puts good deeds in this world first; many Reform Jews are agnostic about it.
  • Against: Maimonides made resurrection the thirteenth principle; the Amidah praises God who revives the dead every day; Orthodox burial practices rest on it.
  • Against: belief in judgement gives a reason to live justly, and hope gives comfort in mourning (Kaddish, shiva).
  • Judge it by: Orthodox and Reform views; what Jews pray daily; whether "this life first" means "the next life does not matter".

Jews hold that the soul returns to God and, for many, the dead will rise; but the focus stays on living this life well.

Mission 3: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Make a guess: Judaism gives 613 commandments. If God commands so much, are Jews still free to choose?

Mission 3

What a mitzvah is

  • A mitzvah (plural mitzvot) is a commandment from God in the Torah.
  • The rabbis counted 613 in the Torah: 248 positive and 365 negative (Talmud Makkot 23b).
  • They cover worship, food, Shabbat, family life, business, courts, care for the poor and for animals.
  • At 13 (boys) or 12 (girls, in Orthodox practice) a young Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah: "son/daughter of the commandment", now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is seen as a response of love to God and a way to make ordinary life holy.
Mission 3

Ways to group the mitzvot

  • Positive (mitzvot aseh, "do"): e.g. honour parents, keep Shabbat, give tzedakah.
  • Negative (mitzvot lo ta'aseh, "do not"): e.g. do not steal, do not eat pork.
  • Between people and God (bein adam la-Makom): prayer, Shabbat, kashrut, festivals.
  • Between people (bein adam la-chavero): honesty, charity, kindness, respect for parents.
Mission 3Read the source

Reminders to keep the mitzvot

> "And it shall be unto you for a fringe, that ye may look upon it, and remember all the commandments of the LORD, and do them" (Numbers 15:39)

  • This is the command of tzitzit: fringes on the corners of a garment, worn on the tallit and by many Orthodox men every day.
  • "Therefore shall ye lay up these My words in your heart and in your soul; and ye shall bind them for a sign upon your hand" (Deuteronomy 11:18): tefillin.
  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house" (Deuteronomy 11:20): the mezuzah.
  • Both passages are part of the Shema, said twice daily.

Mission 3

Free will: "choose life"

  • "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Judaism teaches that God gave every person free will (bechirah): the power to choose good or evil.
  • Free will and the mitzvot fit together: a command only makes sense if you can obey or disobey it.
  • Choice makes reward and punishment fair, and makes keeping a mitzvah a real act of love.
  • Even after doing wrong, a person can choose teshuvah (return, repentance).
  • Rabbi Akiva: everything is foreseen by God, yet freedom of choice is given (Pirkei Avot 3:15, paraphrase).
Mission 3

Two inclinations

  • The rabbis taught that every person has a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (bad inclination).
  • "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21); but God tells Cain: "sin coucheth at the door … but thou mayest rule over it" (Genesis 4:7).
  • The yetzer ha-ra is not a devil: it is selfish desire. Used rightly, it drives people to build, marry and work (Genesis Rabbah 9:7, paraphrase).
  • Sin (chet, "missing the mark") happens when the yetzer ha-ra rules. The Torah is its remedy (Talmud Kiddushin 30b, paraphrase).
  • A teaching links bar/bat mitzvah age with the yetzer ha-tov growing strong enough to guide choices.
  • Your spec spells them Yetzer hatov and Yetzer hara; selfishness, greed and ignorance come from letting the yetzer hara lead.
Mission 3Read the source

Maimonides, Sefer Madda

  • Maimonides (1138–1204) wrote the Mishneh Torah (completed c. 1180), a code of all Jewish law. Book 1 is Sefer Madda, "the Book of Knowledge".
  • It begins: the foundation of all is to know that God exists (Laws of the Foundations of the Torah 1:1, paraphrase). Every mitzvah grows from that.
  • Laws of Repentance 5:1–4 (paraphrase): freedom is given to every person. Anyone can become as righteous as Moses or as wicked as Jeroboam. No one forces us; God does not decree whether we will be good or bad.
  • If God decided our actions, the Torah's commands, and reward and punishment, would make no sense.
  • Your spec calls God the Almighty.

Mission 3

Providence and the limits of freedom

  • Divine providence (hashgachah): the belief that God knows, cares for and guides the world and each life.
  • Job confesses: "I know that Thou canst do every thing, and that no purpose can be withholden from Thee." (Job 42:2)
  • Jonah: he flees God's call, yet a storm and a great fish bring him back. Providence guides; Jonah still chooses how to respond.
  • Limits of freedom: we do not choose our birth, family, health or what happens to us. Everything is in God's hands except whether we revere God (Talmud Berakhot 33b, paraphrase): our moral choices remain ours.
  • Jews hold both together: God's providence and human responsibility.

Mission 3

Salvation through keeping the law

  • Judaism does not teach original sin; humans are born pure and need no rescue from it.
  • "Salvation" means living in right relationship with God in this life, through the covenant, and a share in the world to come (see Mission 2).
  • The individual: keeping mitzvot, and teshuvah, prayer and charity when one fails.
  • The community: all Israel are responsible for one another (Talmud Shevuot 39a, paraphrase). Many prayers need a minyan (ten adults); Yom Kippur confession is said as "we".
  • Redemption is also communal: the people, and in the end the whole world.
  • The righteous of all nations also have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Which mitzvot matter most?

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God; both kinds are equally binding. You may not choose between them.
  • Yet they differ in one way: Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but not for sins against people until you have made peace with them (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • Reform and Liberal: the ethical mitzvot are central; ritual mitzvot are valued and chosen by informed choice.
  • The Talmud shows prophets summing up the 613: David in 11 principles, Micah in 3, Habakkuk in 1: "the righteous shall live by his faith" (Habakkuk 2:4) (Makkot 24a).
  • Hillel: not doing to others what is hateful to you is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary (Talmud Shabbat 31a, paraphrase).
Mission check

In Jewish teaching, 613 commands do not cancel freedom: they assume it. Every mitzvah is a choice, made by a person with two inclinations.

Mission 4

Justice (tzedek)

  • "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20). The word is doubled: justice matters intensely.
  • God "doth execute justice for the fatherless and widow, and loveth the stranger" (Deuteronomy 10:18). Jews are to do the same.
  • "only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God" (Micah 6:8).
  • Justice means fairness in courts and business, honest weights, paying workers on time, and protecting the weak.
  • The prophets condemn worship without justice: "let justice well up as waters" (Amos 5:24).
  • A judge who judges truly becomes God's partner in creation (Talmud Shabbat 10a, paraphrase).
Mission 4

Tzedakah: righteous giving

  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek (justice). It is a duty, not a favour: the poor have a right to help.
  • Behind it: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1). Jews take this to mean wealth is held on trust from God.
  • "thou shalt surely open thy hand unto him" (Deuteronomy 15:8).
  • Many Jews give about 10% of income (ma'aser). Even a person who receives tzedakah is expected to give a little.
  • Maimonides' ladder (Laws of Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14, paraphrase): eight levels, from giving grudgingly at the bottom, through giving anonymously, up to the highest: helping a person become self-supporting with a loan, a partnership or a job.

Mission 4

Gemilut hasadim: acts of loving-kindness

  • Gemilut hasadim: practical kindness, done with time and effort, not only money.
  • "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18), and "thou shalt love him as thyself" of the stranger (Leviticus 19:34).
  • Examples: visiting the sick (bikur cholim), welcoming guests (hachnasat orchim), comforting mourners, helping at a funeral.
  • The rabbis said it is greater than tzedakah in three ways: it can be done with your body as well as money; for rich and poor; for the living and the dead (Talmud Sukkah 49b, paraphrase).
  • The world stands on three things: Torah, worship, and acts of loving-kindness (Pirkei Avot 1:2, paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

Kindness in the Torah

> "And when ye reap the harvest of your land, thou shalt not wholly reap the corner of thy field, neither shalt thou gather the gleaning of thy harvest." (Leviticus 19:9)

  • The next verse adds: "thou shalt leave them for the poor and for the stranger" (Leviticus 19:10).
  • "Thou shalt not take vengeance, nor bear any grudge … but thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:18)

Mission 4

Tikkun olam: healing the world

  • Tikkun olam means "repairing (or healing) the world".
  • Origins: the Mishnah uses it for laws made "for the good order of the world" (Mishnah Gittin 4:2–3); the Aleinu prayer hopes "to perfect the world under the kingdom of the Almighty".
  • Kabbalah: Isaac Luria (1534–1572) taught that sparks of holiness were scattered at creation; each mitzvah helps gather them.
  • Today: working for justice, fighting poverty, welcoming refugees, protecting the environment.
  • Many Jews see humans as partners with God: the world is good but left for people to help complete.
  • Jews believe humans are the goal of creation, made last and in God's image (Genesis 1:27), with responsibility for all other creatures.
  • Orthodox Jews tend to see tikkun olam as coming through all the mitzvot; Reform and Liberal Jews stress social action.
Mission 4

Stewards of God's world

  • Genesis tells how God placed the first human in the garden "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15): humans are stewards, not owners.
  • Bal tashchit ("do not destroy"): even in war, "thou shalt not destroy the trees thereof" (Deuteronomy 20:19). The rabbis extend it to all needless waste.
  • A midrash: God shows Adam the trees of Eden and warns him not to spoil or destroy the world, because no one will come after him to repair it (Kohelet Rabbah 7:13, paraphrase).
  • Honi saw an old man planting a carob tree that would fruit only after 70 years. The man said his ancestors had planted for him, so he plants for his children (Talmud Ta'anit 23a, paraphrase).
  • Tu Bishvat, the new year for trees: many Jews plant trees and hold eco-seders.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jews believe in one God (Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4): Creator (Genesis 1–2), Law-Giver (Torah, Exodus 20) and Judge, both just and merciful (Genesis 18:25; Exodus 34:6).
  • God is omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, eternal, transcendent and immanent, and has no body.
  • Jewish responses to suffering include free will, testing and mystery (Job, Jonah).
  • After the Shoah, thinkers such as Berkovits and Fackenheim responded in different ways.

Mission 2

  • In Jewish teaching, body and soul form one person (Genesis 2:7); at death "the spirit returneth unto God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7).
  • Ideas developed from Sheol to Gan Eden, Gehinnom (mostly temporary) and Olam Ha-Ba; resurrection and judgement (Daniel 12:2).
  • Orthodox Jews believe in bodily resurrection; many Reform Jews in the immortality of the soul only; all put this life first.

Mission 3

  • The Torah contains 613 mitzvot: positive and negative; between people and God, and between people.
  • Jews believe God gave every person free will (Deuteronomy 30:19); the yetzer ha-tov and yetzer ha-ra pull both ways, and the Torah guides the choice.
  • Maimonides (Sefer Madda): anyone can be as righteous as Moses; commands would be pointless without freedom.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all mitzvot equally binding; Reform Jews put ethical mitzvot first.

Mission 4

  • Justice (tzedek) is central: "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20).
  • Tzedakah is righteous giving, a duty; gemilut hasadim is practical kindness; tikkun olam is repairing the world, which many Jews see as partnership with God.
  • Jews see humans as stewards of God's world (Genesis 2:15; bal tashchit).
