Crime & Punishment c1000–present

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole Edexcel Crime and punishment in Britain, c1000–present thematic study — from medieval tithings and trial by ordeal, through the early modern Bloody Code and witch-hunts, to modern policing and the abolition of the death penalty — plus the Whitechapel c1870–c1900 historic environment: crime, policing and the inner city.

Medieval · c1000–1500

Catching and trying criminals

In Anglo-Saxon and Norman England there was no police force, so communities policed themselves:

  • The tithing — men over 12 were grouped in tens and made responsible for each other's behaviour.
  • The hue and cry — if a crime was witnessed, everyone had to chase and catch the criminal.
  • Trial by ordeal — where guilt was unclear, God was asked to judge (e.g. ordeal by hot iron or water). It was abolished by the Church in 1215.
  • Wergild — Anglo-Saxon fines paid to victims to make up for a crime.

After 1066 the Normans added their own laws — the Forest Laws (William I made huge areas royal hunting forests), the Murdrum fine on a village if a Norman was murdered, and trial by combat.

Medieval · the Church & punishment

The Church and medieval punishment

The medieval Church had a big role in law:

  • Sanctuary — a criminal who reached certain churches could claim protection.
  • Benefit of clergy — churchmen could be tried in more lenient Church courts.
  • The Church also ran trial by ordeal (until 1215).

Punishments aimed at deterrence and retribution: fines, corporal punishment (whipping, the stocks and pillory) and capital punishment (execution) for the most serious crimes.

The stocks and pillory shamed offenders in public — a way of humiliating them and warning others not to break the law.

Early modern · c1500–1700

New crimes and fears

Between c1500 and 1700 big social and religious changes created new crimes:

  • Vagabondage / vagrancy — unemployed people wandering to find work were feared and treated as criminals.
  • Heresy and treason — as England changed religion under the Tudors, holding the "wrong" beliefs became a crime.
  • Witchcraft — the Witchcraft Acts made it a serious crime; Matthew Hopkins, the self-styled "Witchfinder General", led witch-hunts in 1645–47.

Fear, religious upheaval and poverty drove these panics. Witch-hunting peaked during the chaos of the English Civil War in the 1640s.

c1700–1900 · the Bloody Code

The Bloody Code and transportation

By the 1700s the law relied heavily on fear to deter crime:

  • The Bloody Code — by around 1800 there were over 200 crimes punishable by death, even minor theft. Public executions were meant to be a deterrent.
  • Transportation — convicts were shipped overseas: first to North America, then to Australia from 1787 (ending around 1868).

Reform · c1800–1900

Reform: prisons and police

In the 1800s attitudes shifted from pure deterrence towards reform:

  • The Bloody Code was abolished and public executions ended in 1868.
  • Prison reformers like John Howard and Elizabeth Fry pushed for cleaner, more humane prisons, leading to the separate and silent systems.
  • Robert Peel, as Home Secretary, passed the Metropolitan Police Act 1829, creating London's Metropolitan Police (nicknamed "Peelers" or "Bobbies"). He also reduced the number of capital crimes.

Robert Peel is a key individual: he set up the first professional, uniformed police force in 1829 — the beginning of modern policing in Britain.

Modern · c1900–present

Crime and punishment today

The modern period brought major changes:

  • The death penalty for murder was suspended in 1965 and abolished in 1969.
  • New crimes appeared — cyber crime, terrorism and driving offences.
  • Conscientious objectors (people who refused to fight in WW1 and WW2) were treated as criminals by some, but attitudes slowly changed.
  • Policing became more specialised, using forensics and neighbourhood policing, and punishment increasingly aimed at rehabilitation.

A key modern shift is the move away from harsh deterrence towards trying to rehabilitate offenders so they do not reoffend.

Historic environment · Whitechapel c1870–1900

The East End of London

Whitechapel, in London's East End, was one of the poorest, most overcrowded areas of Victorian Britain:

  • Overcrowded slums (rookeries), doss houses (cheap lodgings) and workhouses for the destitute.
  • Deep poverty, unemployment, drink and crime.
  • Immigration — many Irish, and Jewish refugees fleeing persecution (pogroms) in Eastern Europe — which sometimes caused tension.

These conditions — poverty, overcrowding and dark, maze-like streets — made Whitechapel very difficult to police.

Whitechapel · policing

Policing Whitechapel

Whitechapel was policed by the Metropolitan Police (H Division), with the neighbouring City of London policed by the separate City of London Police.

  • The main officer was the beat constable, who patrolled a set route on foot.
  • Problems: poor street lighting, narrow alleyways and courts, extreme poverty, and widespread distrust of the police.

Whitechapel · 1888

The Whitechapel murders (1888)

In 1888 a series of brutal murders — the Whitechapel murders, blamed on an unknown killer nicknamed "Jack the Ripper" — shocked London. The killer was never caught.

The investigation was hampered by the limited methods of the time:

  • No fingerprinting was yet used by the police, and forensic science was in its infancy.
  • Detectives relied on house-to-house enquiries, early photography and witness statements.
  • The press sensationalised the case, spreading fear and sometimes hindering the police.

The failure to catch the killer exposed the weaknesses of Victorian policing and investigation — limited forensic methods and the difficult conditions of Whitechapel.

Whitechapel · sources

Sources for studying Whitechapel

The Whitechapel enquiry uses a range of contemporary sources — and each has strengths and limits:

  • The census — records who lived in an area, their jobs and origins.
  • Police and coroners' records — details of investigations and inquests.
  • Newspapers — vivid but often sensational and unreliable.
  • Charles Booth's poverty maps — colour-coded maps of London showing how poor or well-off each street was.

Charles Booth surveyed London and produced maps grading streets by wealth — a key source showing just how poor much of Whitechapel was.

Exam skill

Change and continuity

This is a thematic study, so the exam rewards spotting change, continuity and the reasons behind them across the whole period:

  • Change: from community self-policing (tithing, hue and cry) to a professional police force (1829); from the Bloody Code to rehabilitation.
  • Continuity: for centuries the main aims of punishment were deterrence and retribution; crime was often blamed on the poor.
  • Factors driving change: the role of government and individuals (e.g. Peel, Fry), attitudes in society, science and technology.

A strong answer explains not just what changed but why — for example, why professional policing appeared in the growing, crowded cities of the 1800s.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Medieval c1000–1500: tithing, hue and cry, trial by ordeal (ended 1215), wergild; Norman Forest Laws, Murdrum fine, trial by combat; Church — sanctuary, benefit of clergy; stocks & pillory.

Early modern c1500–1700: vagabondage, heresy, treason, witchcraft (Matthew Hopkins 1645–47); Bloody Code emerging; transportation begins.

c1700–1900: Bloody Code (200+ capital crimes) then decline; transportation to Australia (1787–c1868); public executions end 1868; prison reform (Howard, Fry); Peel's Metropolitan Police 1829.

Modern c1900–present: death penalty abolished for murder (1965 suspended, 1969); new crimes; conscientious objectors; forensics; rehabilitation.
