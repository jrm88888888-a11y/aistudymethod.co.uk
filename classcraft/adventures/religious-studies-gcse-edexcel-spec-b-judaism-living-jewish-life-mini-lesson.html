Mission 1: Why do Jews stand still, feet together, for one silent prayer three times a day?

Make a guess: Why do Jews stand still, feet together, for one silent prayer three times a day?

Mission 1

Praying as a community

  • Jews may pray anywhere, but communal prayer has special value: the community stands together before God.
  • Minyan: the ten adults needed for full public prayer.
  • Some prayers are said only with a minyan: the Kaddish, the Barechu call to prayer, the Kedushah, the public Torah reading, and the repeated Amidah.
  • Orthodox: ten men aged 13 (Bar Mitzvah age) or over.
  • Reform and Liberal: men and women count; many congregations pray together even without ten.
  • Psalm 116 promises to give thanks "in the presence of all His people": public worship.
Mission 1

Three services a day

  • Shacharit: morning. Minchah: afternoon. Ma'ariv (Arvit): evening.
  • The prayers come from the siddur (prayer book), in a fixed order.
  • The Talmud gives two reasons for three times (Berakhot 26b):
  • They match the daily offerings in the Temple (morning and afternoon, with the evening service later added).
  • Abraham, Isaac and Jacob each began one of them.
  • Musaf ("additional"): an extra service on Shabbat and festivals, recalling the extra Temple offering of those days.
Mission 1

A Shabbat morning service

  • 1. Opening blessings and psalms: praise to warm up the heart.
  • 2. The Shema and its blessings: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • 3. The Amidah: said silently, then repeated aloud by the leader.
  • 4. The Torah service: the scroll is taken from the Ark and carried round; members are called up (aliyah) as the weekly portion is read; then the haftarah from the Prophets.
  • On ordinary weekdays the Torah is read only at the Monday and Thursday morning services.
  • 5. Sermon by the rabbi, and prayers for the community, the country (in Britain, the royal family) and Israel.
  • 6. Musaf (Orthodox), then closing prayers such as Aleinu, the mourners' Kaddish and a hymn.
  • Afterwards: kiddush, a blessing over wine and food together in the hall.

Mission 1

The Amidah

  • Amidah means "standing": the central prayer of every service. Also called the Shemoneh Esreh ("eighteen") and, by the rabbis, simply the Tefillah ("the prayer").
  • How: standing, feet together, facing Jerusalem; three steps forward at the start; bowing at set points; said silently, lips moving.
  • Why stand? Like a servant before a king; and the rabbis link feet together to the angels Ezekiel saw.
  • Three parts:
  • Praise (3 blessings): God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob; God's power; God's holiness.
  • Requests (13 on weekdays): knowledge, forgiveness, healing, a good year, the return of exiles, justice, the rebuilding of Jerusalem, the Messiah…
  • Thanks (3 blessings): accept our prayer; we give thanks; grant peace.
  • On Shabbat the 13 requests are replaced by one blessing for the holiness of Shabbat: no weekday requests on the day of rest.
Mission 1Read the source

Thanks in public

"How can I repay unto the LORD all His bountiful dealings toward me? I will lift up the cup of salvation, and call upon the name of the LORD. My vows will I pay unto the LORD, yea, in the presence of all His people." (Psalm 116:12–14)

  • The psalm ends with thanks paid "in the courts of the LORD'S house, in the midst of thee, O Jerusalem" (Psalm 116:19).
  • Psalm 116 is part of the Hallel (Psalms 113–118), sung in the synagogue on festivals and new moons.

Mission 1

Hebrew in worship

  • Hebrew is called the holy language: the language of the Torah and of the prayers since ancient times.
  • Praying in Hebrew links Jews across the world and across history: the same words in London, New York and Jerusalem.
  • The Kaddish is in Aramaic, the everyday language of many Jews in the time of the Talmud.
  • Orthodox services are almost all in Hebrew; many prayer books print a translation alongside.
  • Reform and Liberal services use much more English, so everyone understands; key prayers (the Shema, parts of the Amidah) stay in Hebrew.
Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox and Reform services

  • Length: Orthodox Shabbat morning services are long (often around three hours), with Musaf. Reform services are usually shorter.
  • Language: mainly Hebrew (Orthodox) or Hebrew and English (Reform).
  • Who leads and reads: men only (Orthodox); men and women (Reform).
  • Seating and music: separate, unaccompanied (Orthodox); mixed, sometimes with choir or instruments (Reform).
  • The words: Reform prayer books have changed some prayers: e.g. adding the matriarchs Sarah, Rebecca, Rachel and Leah to the first blessing, and leaving out prayers to restore Temple sacrifices.
  • Shared: the Shema, the Amidah, the Torah reading, the Kaddish, and facing the Ark.
Mission 1Diversity

Compared with Christian worship

  • Similar: both use set prayers (the Amidah; the Lord's Prayer), read scripture in a cycle, sing psalms, and gather weekly (Shabbat; Sunday).
  • The Shema and the creeds: both declare belief together; Jesus quoted the Shema as the first commandment (Mark 12:29).
  • Different: the high point of most Christian worship is the Eucharist (Holy Communion); the synagogue has no sacrament or altar. The Torah reading is the high point of the Shabbat morning service.
  • Different: Jews pray the Amidah three times a day; most Christians have no fixed daily times (Catholic and Anglican clergy and religious do pray the Liturgy of the Hours).
  • Different: Orthodox Jews need a minyan for full public prayer; Christian worship has no fixed number.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Jewish services should be in the language people speak every day."

  • For: prayer needs kavanah (inner intention), which is hard without understanding; the Mishnah allows the Shema to be said in any language (Sotah 7:1); English helps newcomers and children feel included.
  • Against: Hebrew unites Jews worldwide and links them to the Torah and their ancestors; translations lose meaning; learning Hebrew prayers is itself part of Jewish education.
  • Different traditions: Orthodox Jews keep Hebrew; Reform and Liberal Jews mix Hebrew and English.
  • Another view: use both: Hebrew for key prayers, with a translation printed beside.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for: understanding, unity or tradition? Can one service serve all of these? Who is the service mainly for?

Mission 2

Three parts, one book

  • The Jewish Bible is the Tenakh: a name made from the first letters of its three parts, T-N-K.
  • Torah ("teaching", "law"): the five books from Genesis to Deuteronomy. The most holy part.
  • Nevi'im ("Prophets"): history books (Joshua to Kings) and the prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, the Twelve).
  • Ketuvim ("Writings"): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Daniel, Chronicles, and the five scrolls (e.g. Esther, Ruth).
  • Jews count 24 books: the same books as the Protestant Old Testament, counted and ordered differently (Catholic and Orthodox Bibles add further books).
  • Your spec calls the Tenakh the written law.
Mission 2

Sefer Torah and Chumash

  • Sefer Torah: the Torah handwritten in Hebrew on parchment by a trained scribe (sofer), with a quill and special ink.
  • Every letter must be perfect; one wrong or missing letter makes the scroll unfit until it is corrected.
  • The scroll is kept in the Ark and read in the synagogue; in most synagogues the reading cycle finishes every year at Simchat Torah.
  • Chumash (from the Hebrew for "five"): a printed book of the five books of the Torah, often with commentary.
  • The Chumash is used to follow the reading and to study at home; the public Torah reading is always from the scroll, never from a Chumash.
Mission 2

Treated with respect

  • The scroll is "dressed" in a cover (mantle) and often a breastplate and crown.
  • Readers follow the text with a yad (pointer) so that hands do not touch the parchment.
  • The congregation stands when the Ark is opened and when the scroll is carried round.
  • People touch the scroll with the fringe of their tallit or a prayer book, then kiss it.
  • If a scroll is dropped, the custom in many communities is that those present fast.
  • A scroll too worn to repair is not thrown away: it is buried, like a person.

Mission 2

The oral Torah: Mishnah and Talmud

  • Jews believe the written Torah needs explaining: e.g. "no work on Shabbat", but what counts as work?
  • Orthodox Jews believe God gave Moses an oral Torah at Sinai with the written one, handed down teacher to pupil.
  • After the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE) and scattered the people, rabbis feared it would be lost.
  • Mishnah: the oral law written down in Hebrew by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi, about 200 CE, in six sections (orders).
  • Gemara: centuries of later rabbis' discussion of the Mishnah, mostly in Aramaic.
  • Talmud = Mishnah + Gemara. The Babylonian Talmud (about 500 CE) is the main one studied; there is also a Jerusalem Talmud (about 400 CE).
  • Your spec calls the Talmud the oral law.
Mission 2

From Talmud to daily law

  • Halakhah ("the way to walk"): Jewish law, covering prayer, food, Shabbat, business and family life.
  • Codes gather the Talmud's rulings in order, so people can find the answer:
  • Responsa: written answers by expert rabbis to questions people send them. They still appear today.
  • Example: may a lift, a phone or an electric light be used on Shabbat? Responsa apply old principles to new things.
  • So the chain runs: Torah → Mishnah → Talmud → Codes and Responsa → a Jew's choice today.
Mission 2Read the source

Pirkei Avot 2

Pirkei Avot ("Sayings of the Fathers") is a tractate of the Mishnah full of moral teaching. From chapter 2 (paraphrase; verse numbers vary by edition):

  • Your spec spells it Perkei Avot.
  • Hillel: do not cut yourself off from the community; do not say "I will study when I have time", because you may never have time (2:4).
  • The more Torah, the more life (2:7).
  • Rabbi Yose: let all your actions be for the sake of Heaven (2:12).
  • Rabbi Tarfon: you are not required to finish the work, but you are not free to give up on it (2:16).

Mission 2

The Tenakh and Talmud in daily life

  • Synagogue: the Torah is read on Shabbat, Monday and Thursday mornings and festivals.
  • After the Torah reading on Shabbat and festivals comes the haftarah, a passage from the Nevi'im.
  • Ketuvim in worship: Psalms fill the daily prayers; five scrolls are read at festivals (e.g. Esther at Purim).
  • Study: Torah study is itself a mitzvah. Many Jews learn a page of Talmud a day (Daf Yomi) or study in pairs (chavruta).
  • Home: parents teach children; the mezuzah on the door holds Torah verses; meals and Shabbat follow halakhah.
  • Decisions: when a hard choice arises (medicine, business), Jews may ask a rabbi, who uses the Talmud and later rabbinic rulings.

Mission 2Diversity

How binding are the Torah and Talmud?

  • Orthodox (including Haredi): the whole Torah, written and oral, comes from God (Torah from heaven). Halakhah is binding and unchanging in its principles.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhah is binding, but it has developed over history and can still develop.
  • Reform: the Torah is inspired by God but written by people over time; ethical mitzvot are central; individuals make informed choices about ritual laws.
  • Liberal: like Reform, stressing the individual conscience and the prophets' call for justice.
  • All study the Tenakh. The Talmud's place differs most: central in Orthodox life, a valued guide in Reform and Liberal life.
Mission 2Evaluate

"For Jews today, the Talmud is as important as the Torah."

  • For: the Torah cannot be kept without it (what is "work" on Shabbat?). Orthodox Jews believe the oral Torah was also given at Sinai. Daily practice (prayer, kashrut, Shabbat) follows the Talmud's rulings.
  • Against: only the Torah is in the Ark and read in worship; it is the source the Talmud itself explains. Reform and Liberal Jews see the Talmud as human interpretation from a particular time. The Talmud records arguments, not only answers.
  • Another view: for many Jews they are one Torah: written and oral, each incomplete without the other.
  • Judge it by: What do Jews actually use when making daily decisions? What do they treat as most holy? Does "important" mean authority or use?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the Talmud is important for Jews today.

  • Reason 1: it explains how to keep the Torah's commandments. Development: e.g. the Torah forbids work on Shabbat; the Mishnah lists 39 kinds of work, so a Jew knows what to avoid.
  • Reason 2: it encourages lifelong study. Source: Hillel: do not wait for spare time to study (Pirkei Avot 2:4, paraphrase). Development: many Jews study Talmud daily, so learning shapes their whole week.
  • 5-mark (c): two developed reasons plus a source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 3: Why can't a kosher café serve a cheeseburger, even when the beef and the cheese are both kosher?

Make a guess: Why can't a kosher café serve a cheeseburger, even when the beef and the cheese are both kosher?

Mission 3

Kosher means "fit"

  • Kashrut: the Jewish food laws. Food that meets them is kosher ("fit", "proper").
  • Food that does not is treifah: literally "torn", from the Torah's ban on meat "torn of beasts in the field" (Exodus 22:31). It now means any forbidden food.
  • The source: the Torah (Leviticus 11; Deuteronomy 14), explained in the Talmud.
  • Many food laws are chukim: laws given without a stated reason. Jews keep them because God commanded them.
  • The reason the Torah does give: holiness. "Ye shall therefore be holy, for I am holy." (Leviticus 11:45, JPS 1917)
  • Pirkei Avot 2:1 urges care with a "light" mitzvah as with a "weighty" one, since only God knows the reward of each (paraphrase): a reason to keep even small food rules.
Mission 3Read the source

Hoofs, cud, fins and scales

> "And every beast that parteth the hoof, and hath the hoof wholly cloven in two, and cheweth the cud, among the beasts, that ye may eat." (Deuteronomy 14:6, JPS 1917)

> "These ye may eat of all that are in the waters: whatsoever hath fins and scales may ye eat;" (Deuteronomy 14:9, JPS 1917)

  • Your set passage is Deuteronomy 14:3–10: it opens "Thou shalt not eat any abominable thing" (14:3) and names the pig, camel and hare as unclean (14:7–8).

Q1 What two signs make a land animal kosher?

Show answer

Q2 Why is a pig not kosher, though it has split hoofs?

Show answer

Mission 3

Which creatures?

  • Mammals: both signs needed. Kosher: cattle, sheep, goats, deer. Not kosher: pig, camel, hare, horse.
  • Fish: fins and scales. Kosher: salmon, cod, tuna, herring. Not kosher: shellfish (prawns, lobster), eels, sharks.
  • Birds: the Torah lists forbidden birds, mostly birds of prey. Jews eat only birds with a long tradition of being kosher: chicken, turkey, duck, goose.
  • Insects: almost all forbidden. Lev 11:22 allows some locusts; only a few communities (e.g. some Yemenite Jews) keep a tradition of which ones.
  • Fruit and vegetables: all kosher; but leafy vegetables are checked for tiny insects.
  • Products of a creature follow it: eggs of kosher birds and milk of kosher animals are kosher; pork gelatine is not.
Mission 3

Shechitah: how meat is made kosher

  • Shechitah: slaughter by a shochet, a trained, observant Jew licensed by a beth din.
  • A razor-sharp, perfectly smooth knife; one swift cut across the throat. Jewish authorities hold that blood pressure to the brain falls at once, so the animal quickly loses consciousness.
  • The animal is inspected; certain defects make it treifah. Lungs with no defects are glatt ("smooth").
  • No blood: "ye shall eat no manner of blood, whether it be of fowl or of beast" (Leviticus 7:26, JPS 1917). Meat is soaked and salted to draw it out.
  • Certain fats and the sciatic nerve are removed (Genesis 32:32), so some cuts are rarely sold as kosher in Britain.
  • An animal that dies by itself is not kosher (Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • Debate: UK law lets Jews and Muslims slaughter without pre-stunning. Some animal-welfare groups want this ended; Jewish bodies argue shechitah is humane and that a ban would restrict religious freedom.

Mission 3

Milk and meat; parev

  • Three times the Torah says: "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk." (Deuteronomy 14:21; Exodus 23:19; 34:26, JPS 1917)
  • The Talmud explains: never cook, eat, or benefit from meat and milk together.
  • Poultry counts as meat; so a chicken sandwich with butter is not kosher.
  • Jews wait between meat and dairy: commonly six hours; some communities three, or one. After dairy, a much shorter wait or none.
  • Parev (or pareve): neither meat nor dairy. Fish, eggs, fruit, vegetables, grains: eaten with either.
  • Many Jews also avoid cooking fish and meat together, by custom.
  • So a cheeseburger is forbidden even when the beef and the cheese are each kosher.
Mission 3

The kosher kitchen

  • Two sets of pans, plates, cutlery and utensils: one for meat, one for dairy; often colour-coded (e.g. red for meat, blue for dairy).
  • Separate sinks or washing-up bowls, tea towels, and work surfaces or boards; some homes have two ovens or dishwashers.
  • Parev foods may be prepared with either set.
  • Packaged foods carry a hechsher: a mark from a kosher authority (in Britain e.g. KLBD, the London Beth Din's kosher division, or the Manchester Beth Din).
  • A mashgiach (supervisor) checks kosher restaurants, caterers and factories.
  • At Pesach, many families use a third set again, kept free of leaven.

Mission 3Diversity

Different ways of keeping kosher

  • Haredi (strictly Orthodox): extra stringencies, e.g. only glatt meat; milk produced under Jewish supervision; some foods cooked only with a Jew's involvement.
  • Modern Orthodox / United Synagogue: kashrut as binding law, at home and outside; eat out only where supervised.
  • Masorti: kashrut as binding, with some different rulings.
  • Reform: an informed personal choice. Many keep some rules (no pork or shellfish; no meat with milk at home); some keep fully.
  • Liberal: the ritual laws are not binding; ethical eating, e.g. vegetarianism or eco-kashrut, may matter more.
  • Many Jews who are not observant still avoid pork as a sign of identity.
Mission 3

Keeping kosher in Britain

  • Benefits: daily obedience to God; every meal becomes a reminder of holiness; a strong sense of Jewish identity; family and community meals; self-discipline; respect for animal life.
  • Challenges: kosher shops and restaurants are mostly in cities with large Jewish communities (e.g. north London, Manchester, Leeds, Gateshead); kosher meat costs more; school dinners, work events, university and travel need planning.
  • Help available: hechsher lists, kosher sections in some supermarkets, kosher school meals in Jewish schools, online delivery.
  • Pressure points: campaigns to ban slaughter without stunning; eating with non-Jewish friends.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The food laws are still important for Jews today."

  • For: they are mitzvot in the Torah, given by God, so they do not expire; they make daily life holy (Leviticus 11:45); they keep Jewish identity strong in a non-Jewish society; they teach self-control.
  • Against (some Jews): Reform and Liberal Jews see many ritual laws as belonging to an earlier time; ethics, such as how animals and workers are treated, may matter more than which animal is eaten.
  • Against (practical): cost and access make full observance hard for some British Jews.
  • Another view: a law can keep its importance even when its reason is not stated: obedience itself is the point (chukim).
  • Judge it by: Is the Torah's command binding for all time? Does the law still build holiness and identity? Is a personal-choice approach still "keeping" the law?

Mission 4

Prayer from waking to sleeping

  • For Jews, prayer is not only for the synagogue: the home and the day are full of it.
  • Three times a day: morning, afternoon and evening prayers can be said at home or at work if not in the synagogue.
  • The psalmist prays "Evening, and morning, and at noon" (Psalm 55:17).
  • Jews face Jerusalem when they pray, as Daniel did: "his windows were open in his upper chamber toward Jerusalem" (Daniel 6:10).
  • The words come from the siddur (prayer book); many Jews own one for home use.
  • Orthodox men are bound to the fixed times; Orthodox women are not bound in the same way, though many pray daily.
Mission 4

Modeh Ani: the first words of the day

  • Said on waking, still in bed:
  • "I give thanks before You, living and eternal King, for You have returned my soul to me with compassion; great is Your faithfulness." (Modeh Ani, traditional prayer, own translation)
  • Sleep is seen as like a small death; each morning is a gift.
  • The last words echo "great is Thy faithfulness" (Lamentations 3:23).
  • It is short and simple, so it is often the first prayer children learn.
Mission 4

The Shema

  • The Shema is the Jewish declaration of faith: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three passages: Deuteronomy 6:4–9 (love God, teach the words), Deuteronomy 11:13–21 (keep the commandments), Numbers 15:37–41 (fringes, remember the mitzvot).
  • When: morning and evening, "when thou liest down, and when thou risest up" (Deuteronomy 6:7); again at bedtime.
  • How: the first line is said aloud with the right hand covering the eyes, to concentrate.
  • It is taught to young children first, and traditionally said as a Jew's last words.
Mission 4

The mezuzah

  • "And thou shalt write them upon the door-posts of thy house, and upon thy gates." (Deuteronomy 6:9)
  • Mezuzah (Hebrew "doorpost"): a small parchment scroll, handwritten by a sofer, with Deuteronomy 6:4–9 and 11:13–21.
  • It is rolled into a case and fixed to the right-hand doorpost, in the upper part, often slanting.
  • On the doorways of the rooms in a Jewish home (not bathrooms); many Jews touch it and kiss their fingers on passing.
  • It reminds Jews that God is present in the home, and that the Torah's words go with them in and out.
  • The scroll is checked by a scribe from time to time, to make sure no letter has faded.

Mission 4

Many ways to pray

  • Set prayers from the siddur, shared by all Jews; spontaneous prayer in one's own words, at any time.
  • Blessings (berakhot): short thanks before eating, on seeing lightning, on hearing good news.
  • Grace after meals (Birkat Hamazon): after a meal with bread, from "thou shalt eat and be satisfied, and bless the LORD thy God" (Deuteronomy 8:10).
  • Teaching children: parents teach Modeh Ani, the Shema at bedtime, and blessings over food.
  • Torah study at home is itself a form of worship.
  • Different forms matter: set words unite the community; one's own words express real need, as in Psalm 55.

Mission 4Diversity

Different approaches to home and private prayer

  • Orthodox: fixed prayers at fixed times, mainly in Hebrew; the home is run by halakhah (kashrut, Shabbat, mezuzah on every door).
  • Reform and Liberal: prayer is valued but times and words are more personal; English is common; many keep a mezuzah and say the Shema while choosing other practices.
  • Hasidic teaching stresses kavanah (heartfelt intention) and some encourage daily spontaneous prayer alone.
  • Shared: the Shema, the mezuzah, blessings over food and teaching children.
  • Compared with Christianity: Christians also pray at home and in their own words, and the Lord's Prayer is a shared set prayer; but most have no fixed three-times-daily duty and nothing like the mezuzah on every door (though some homes have a crucifix or holy water by the door).
Mission 4Evaluate

"Private prayer matters more than prayer in the synagogue."

  • For: God hears every prayer, anywhere (Psalm 55:17); the home is where faith is taught and lived; personal prayer can be more honest.
  • Against: some key prayers (Kaddish, the repeated Amidah, the Torah reading) need a minyan; praying together builds the community; the synagogue's set times keep prayer regular.
  • Different traditions: Orthodox men are expected to pray with a minyan when possible; Reform Jews often stress personal meaning.
  • Another view: they support each other: the same Shema is said in both, and each gives the other strength.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for: relationship with God, or community? Which is harder to keep up alone? Do different Jews need different things?
Mission check

Mission 5: What do the Jewish ceremonies for a newborn baby and for a 13-year-old actually change?

Make a guess: What do the Jewish ceremonies for a newborn baby and for a 13-year-old actually change?

Mission 5

Brit Milah: the covenant in the body

  • A rite of passage is a ceremony that marks a move from one stage of life to the next: birth, coming of age, marriage, death.
  • Brit Milah means "covenant of circumcision": the removal of the foreskin of a Jewish baby boy.
  • It goes back to God's covenant with Abraham: "every male among you shall be circumcised" (Genesis 17:10).
  • It takes place on the eighth day after birth (Genesis 17:12; Leviticus 12:3), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur.
  • If the baby is unwell it is delayed: protecting life comes first.
  • Meaning: the boy enters the covenant; it is a sign of Jewish identity carried for life.
  • It is a mitzvah for the father, who appoints a trained mohel to carry it out for him.
Mission 5

The ceremony

  • Held at home or in the synagogue, usually in the morning, with a minyan where possible.
  • The baby is carried in by a couple chosen as godparents; guests say "Blessed is he who comes".
  • He is placed briefly on the Chair of Elijah, the prophet believed to be present at every brit.
  • The sandek (often a grandfather) holds the baby on his knees: a great honour.
  • The mohel performs the circumcision and says a blessing; the father says a blessing for the mitzvah of bringing his son "into the covenant of Abraham our father".
  • Everyone answers: may he grow into Torah, marriage and good deeds.
  • The baby's Hebrew name is announced; a festive meal (seudat mitzvah) follows.
  • Significance for the community: a new member is welcomed into the people of the covenant.
Mission 5Read the source

Isaac's birth and the mother's purification

"And Abraham circumcised his son Isaac when he was eight days old, as God had commanded him." (Genesis 21:4)

"And Sarah said: 'God hath made laughter for me; every one that heareth will laugh on account of me.'" (Genesis 21:6)

  • Genesis 21:1–8 also tells of Isaac's naming (21:3) and a great feast when he was weaned (21:8): a child's arrival is a time of joy and celebration.
  • Genesis 17 is the covenant itself: Abram renamed Abraham, the promise of land and descendants, and circumcision as its sign (17:9–14).
  • Leviticus 12: after giving birth the mother is ritually impure for a set time, then brings an offering; verse 3 commands circumcision on the eighth day (paraphrase).
  • Interpretations of Leviticus 12: Orthodox women still keep the purity laws, ending with immersion in a mikveh, though offerings ended with the Temple. Reform Jews read it as historical; some mark the mother's recovery with a blessing of thanks.

Mission 5

Welcoming a baby girl

  • There is no circumcision for girls: Jewish girls are part of the covenant from birth.
  • Orthodox (Ashkenazi): the father is called up to the Torah in the synagogue soon after the birth; a prayer is said for mother and baby, and the girl's Hebrew name is announced; then a kiddush.
  • Sephardi Jews hold a zeved habat ("gift of a daughter") with blessings and verses from the Song of Songs.
  • Simchat bat ("joy of a daughter"): a newer home or synagogue celebration, now held in many communities, Orthodox and Progressive.
  • Reform and Liberal: both parents stand on the bimah for a baby blessing, for girls and boys alike; some hold a covenant ceremony for girls.
  • Significance: the community welcomes the girl, and she receives the name she will be called up by all her life.
Mission 5

Bar Mitzvah: son of the commandment

  • Bar Mitzvah means "son of the commandment". At 13, a boy becomes an adult in Jewish law.
  • This happens automatically on his 13th birthday (by the Jewish calendar), whether or not there is a ceremony.
  • New responsibilities: keeping all the mitzvot; fasting on Yom Kippur; wearing tefillin; being counted in a minyan; answering for his own actions.
  • Pirkei Avot teaches: at five for Scripture, at ten for Mishnah, at thirteen for the commandments (5:21, paraphrase).
  • In Orthodox communities the father says a blessing that he is no longer answerable for his son's deeds.
Mission 5

Preparing and the service

  • Lessons for a year or more: reading Hebrew, chanting the Torah portion to its traditional tune, learning the prayers and their meaning.
  • Many also take on a mitzvah project: volunteering or raising money for charity.
  • The service: on the Shabbat after his 13th birthday he is called up to the Torah (aliyah) for the first time, and reads from the scroll and often the haftarah.
  • He may give a short talk (d'var Torah) on the portion; the rabbi blesses and addresses him.
  • In some communities sweets are thrown, wishing him a sweet life; a kiddush or party follows.
  • Afterwards: what matters is the life that follows: daily prayer, tefillin, study and keeping the mitzvot. Some Progressive communities add a later ceremony at 15 or 16.

Mission 5

Bat Mitzvah and Bat Chayil

  • Bat Mitzvah: "daughter of the commandment". In Orthodox law a girl comes of age at 12.
  • Reform and Liberal: girls celebrate at 12 or 13 exactly as boys do: reading from the Torah, leading prayers, giving a talk. The first public Bat Mitzvah in the US was in New York in 1922.
  • Orthodox: girls do not read from the Torah in a mixed service. Many give a talk, hold a celebration, or read in a women's service.
  • Bat Chayil ("daughter of valour"), from Proverbs 31: "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10)
  • It is a group ceremony in some British Orthodox synagogues, often on a Sunday: girls who have studied together present projects and read prayers and psalms.
  • Some Orthodox rabbis welcome these ceremonies; others prefer no formal ceremony for girls, seeing it as a recent change.
Mission 5Diversity

Orthodox and Reform compared

  • Brit Milah: kept by Orthodox, Reform and Liberal Jews alike. A small number of Jews, and some outside critics, question infant circumcision; the main movements uphold it as a central mitzvah.
  • Naming girls: Orthodox: the father's aliyah and a prayer; Reform: both parents on the bimah.
  • Bar Mitzvah: 13 in every movement.
  • Bat Mitzvah: Orthodox: 12, no Torah reading in a mixed service, sometimes Bat Chayil. Reform and Liberal: 12 or 13, the same service as boys.
  • Why: Orthodox Jews follow halakhah on men's and women's roles in public worship; Reform and Liberal Jews stress equality of men and women.

Mission 5Evaluate

"The responsibilities matter more than the ceremony."

  • For: in Jewish law a boy is Bar Mitzvah at 13 and a girl Bat Mitzvah at 12 with or without a ceremony; the aim is a life of mitzvot; lavish parties can overshadow the meaning.
  • Against: the ceremony motivates years of study; it welcomes the young person publicly into the community; the father's blessing and the first aliyah are meaningful acts in themselves.
  • Different views: Orthodox Jews stress the new legal duties; Reform and Liberal Jews stress personal commitment and equality, and some add a later ceremony when commitment is more mature.
  • Another view: the ceremony and the responsibilities belong together: a public promise makes a private duty real.
  • Judge it by: What changes at 13 if there is no ceremony? What do young Jews themselves remember? Does the community need the public event?

Mission 6

Marriage: making something holy

  • Jews see marriage as a mitzvah (commandment) and the normal path for adult life.
  • The Hebrew name for the ceremony is kiddushin, from kadosh, holy: two people are set apart for each other.
  • Reasons in the Torah: companionship ("It is not good that the man should be alone", Genesis 2:18) and children (Genesis 1:28).
  • The home a couple builds is a mikdash me'at, a "small sanctuary": Shabbat, festivals and teaching children happen there.
  • Once there were two separate stages: kiddushin or erusin (betrothal) and, about a year later, nissuin (the wedding itself). Since the Middle Ages both happen in one ceremony.
Mission 6Read the source

One flesh

> "Therefore shall a man leave his father and his mother, and shall cleave unto his wife, and they shall be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24, JPS 1917)

  • Jews read this as the Torah's picture of marriage: a new unit, bound closer than to parents.
  • "Cleave" means hold fast: marriage is meant to be loyal and lasting.
  • "One flesh" covers body, home and life together; sexual love within marriage is seen as good and holy.

Mission 6

Before the chuppah

  • Many couples fast on the wedding day until the ceremony: a personal day of atonement and a fresh start.
  • The ketubah (marriage contract, in Aramaic) is signed by two witnesses.
  • It sets out the husband's duties to his wife: to provide food, clothing and marital love, and a payment for her if they divorce or he dies.
  • It protected women when they had few legal rights. The bride keeps it; many are beautifully decorated.
  • Bedeken (veiling): the groom lowers the veil over the bride's face, recalling Rebecca veiling herself when she met Isaac (Genesis 24:65).
Mission 6

Under the chuppah

  • The chuppah is a canopy held up on four poles, open at the sides: the couple's new home, open to guests like Abraham's tent.
  • In many Orthodox weddings the bride circles the groom (often seven times).
  • Betrothal: a blessing over wine; both drink. The groom places a plain ring on the bride's finger and says: "Behold, you are consecrated to me with this ring, according to the law of Moses and Israel."
  • The ketubah is read aloud.
  • Seven blessings (sheva brachot) over a second cup: God as Creator, joy of the couple, hope for Jerusalem.
  • The groom breaks a glass underfoot: even at the happiest moment, Jews remember the destroyed Temple (Psalm 137:5–6). Guests shout "Mazel tov!"
  • Yichud: the couple spend a few minutes alone together.

Mission 6Diversity

Orthodox and Reform weddings

  • Both: chuppah, wine, ring, ketubah, seven blessings, breaking the glass.
  • Orthodox: only the groom gives a ring and speaks the betrothal words; the ketubah sets out the husband's duties; both partners must be Jewish.
  • Reform and Liberal: often a double-ring ceremony; both partners speak; an egalitarian ketubah with promises from each.
  • Many Reform and Liberal rabbis bless same-sex marriages; Orthodox Judaism does not.
  • Some Reform and Liberal rabbis take part in weddings where one partner is not Jewish; Orthodox rabbis do not.
  • Your spec asks for divergent Orthodox and Reform understandings: keep the shared core and the differences side by side.
Mission 6

From death to burial

  • At death, the eyes are closed, a candle is lit near the head ("The spirit of man is the lamp of the LORD", Proverbs 20:27, JPS 1917), and the body is never left alone.
  • Onen: a close relative between the death and the burial; the period is called aninut. An onen is freed from many mitzvot, such as daily prayer, to arrange the funeral. Comforting waits until after burial.
  • The Chevra Kaddisha ("holy society"): volunteers who wash the body (taharah) and dress it in plain white shrouds. Rich and poor are buried alike.
  • Their work is called true kindness: the dead can never repay it.
  • Keriah (tearing of clothes): mourners tear a garment and say "Blessed is the true Judge."
  • Why tear? It lets grief show, as Jacob "rent his garments" for Joseph (Genesis 37:34, JPS 1917); the blessing accepts God's judgement even in loss.
  • Burial soon, ideally within a day (based on Deuteronomy 21:23): a plain coffin, psalms, a eulogy; mourners help fill the grave with earth.
  • Orthodox Jews require burial ("for dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return", Genesis 3:19, JPS 1917); Reform and Liberal Jews also permit cremation, and some keep a shorter shiva.
Mission 6

The Kaddish

  • The Kaddish is a prayer in Aramaic said by mourners at the graveside and in services.
  • It opens: "Magnified and sanctified be his great name in the world which he hath created according to his will" (Singer's Authorised Daily Prayer Book, 1890).
  • It never mentions death. The mourner praises God at the moment of loss.
  • It needs a minyan (ten adults; in Orthodox practice, ten men), so mourners are drawn back into the community.
  • For a parent, many say it daily for eleven months, then on each anniversary.
  • Many Jews also believe saying Kaddish brings merit to the soul of the one who has died.
Mission 6

Mourning in stages

  • Shiva ("seven"): seven days from the burial. Mourners stay home on low chairs; mirrors are covered; no work, shaving or leather shoes; a candle burns; visitors bring food and form a minyan.
  • Sheloshim ("thirty"): to day 30. Mourners return to work but avoid parties and music.
  • Avelut (mourning): for a parent, it lasts twelve months. Many set the gravestone during this year.
  • Yahrzeit (anniversary): on the Hebrew date of the death each year: a 24-hour candle, Kaddish, often tzedakah (charity) or a visit to the grave.
  • The shape is gradual: grief is given room, then the mourner is led back into daily life.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Jewish mourning rituals are more for the living than for the dead."

  • Claim: the rituals mainly help mourners, not the person who has died.
  • For: shiva surrounds mourners with community and food; the stages lead them back to normal life step by step; Kaddish lets a grieving person affirm faith in God.
  • Against: the Chevra Kaddisha, taharah and never leaving the body alone show respect for the dead (kevod ha-met); quick burial honours the body; many Jews believe Kaddish benefits the soul.
  • Another view: Jewish law names two separate duties, honouring the dead and comforting mourners (nichum avelim); the rituals serve both.
  • Different Jews: Orthodox Jews stress the full laws for both; Reform Jews often explain the rituals mainly through their help to mourners.
  • Judge it by: What does each ritual say it is for? Would it make sense if there were no mourners? How do Orthodox and Reform Jews explain it?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jews pray three times a day (Shacharit, Minchah, Ma'ariv), echoing the Temple offerings; a minyan of ten is needed for full public prayer.
  • The Amidah, the standing prayer, has praise, requests and thanks, and is said silently facing Jerusalem.
  • Orthodox services are longer and mainly in Hebrew with men leading; Reform services use more English and include women leading.

Mission 2

  • Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah is handwritten as a Sefer Torah, printed as a Chumash, and treated with great respect.
  • The oral Torah was written down as the Mishnah (about 200 CE) and the Talmud (about 500 CE); halakhah guides daily life.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the written and oral Torah as given by God and binding; Reform and Liberal Jews see them as inspired guidance for informed choice.

Mission 3

  • Kosher = fit; treifah = forbidden. Land animals need split hoofs and cud-chewing; fish need fins and scales (Deuteronomy 14; Leviticus 11).
  • Meat comes from shechitah with the blood removed; meat and milk are never mixed; parev foods go with either; a kosher kitchen keeps two sets.
  • Orthodox Jews see kashrut as binding; Reform Jews as informed choice; in Britain it brings identity and holiness but also cost and planning.

Mission 4

  • Jews pray three times a day, at home or in the synagogue, facing Jerusalem; Modeh Ani begins the day and the Shema is said morning and night.
  • The mezuzah holds the first two Shema passages; Grace after meals, blessings and teaching children make the home a place of worship.

Mission 5

  • Brit Milah on the eighth day continues God's covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17); the mohel performs it, the sandek holds the baby, and the child is named.
  • Girls are named in the synagogue or at a simchat bat; Bar Mitzvah (13) and Bat Mitzvah (12 or 13) mark adulthood in Jewish law.
  • Orthodox girls may have a Bat Chayil and do not read from the Torah in mixed services; Reform and Liberal ceremonies are the same for girls and boys.

Mission 6

  • A Jewish wedding: ketubah, chuppah, wine, ring (kiddushin), seven blessings, breaking the glass; Genesis 2:24 pictures marriage as "one flesh".
  • Mourning moves in stages: aninut and burial, shiva, sheloshim, the year (avelut), then yahrzeit each year; the Chevra Kaddisha prepares the body and Kaddish praises God.
