Mission 1

Mission 1: Rainforest at the equator, desert a little further north. Why do the world's big ecosystems come in bands?

Make a guess: Rainforest at the equator, desert a little further north. Why do the world's big ecosystems come in bands?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.1 distribution and characteristics of large-scale ecosystems
  • 3.1 role of climate and local factors (soils, altitude) in distribution
Mission 1

What is a biome?

  • A biome is "a community of plants and animals living together in a certain kind of climate". Biomes are large-scale ecosystems.
  • Climate (temperature and rainfall) does the most to decide which biome grows where.
Mission 1

The world map of biomes

  • Biomes form rough bands across the map, by latitude.
  • Near the equator: tropical forest. Either side: tropical grassland, then hot desert.
  • Further from the equator: temperate grassland and temperate forest, then boreal forest, then tundra and polar ice.
Mission 1

Why the bands? Rising and sinking air

  • The equator gets more direct sunlight; the poles get less.
  • Near the equator warm air rises in the ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone), "forming thunderstorms": heavy rain all year feeds rainforest.
  • The air sinks over the subtropics (about 30°), "creating high-pressure regions over … deserts like the Sahara". Sinking air warms and dries, so clouds cannot form.
  • Near the poles there is "little or no solar energy in the winter", and the sun is at a low angle: cold biomes.
Quick check
?Why are many of the world's hot deserts found around 30° north and south?
Mission 1

Tropical rainforest

  • Where: between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (e.g. Amazon, Congo).
  • Climate: hot all year (at Manaus, Brazil, every month averages 27–28 °C); 2,000–10,000 mm of rain a year.
  • Plants: giant trees up to 75 m form the canopy; vines, ferns and palms below.
  • Soil: leaf litter rots fast in the heat and wet, "sending nutrients back into the soil".
Mission 1

Hot deserts

  • Where: around 30° N and S under sinking air (e.g. Sahara).
  • Rain: about 250 mm a year; most get under 300 mm.
  • Temperature: days average 38 °C; nights fall to about −3.9 °C, because dry air lets heat escape.
  • Plants: cacti store water in their stems; some plants lie dormant until rain comes.
Mission 1

Grasslands: tropical and temperate

  • Rain: about 500–900 mm a year: too little for dense forest.
  • Tropical grassland (savanna): warm all year with a wet and a dry season; between rainforest and desert.
  • Temperate grassland: cold winters, warm summers: prairies (North America), pampas (South America), steppes (Central Eurasia), veldt (South Africa).
  • Plants: grasses die back to their roots each year.
Mission 1

Temperate and boreal forests

  • Temperate deciduous forest (eastern USA, Europe, China, Japan): 750–1,500 mm of rain; average 10 °C; four seasons; leaves fall in winter.
  • Boreal (coniferous) forest: about 50–60° N; winters very cold, summers around 10 °C; 300–900 mm of rain.
  • Conifers are evergreen, with needles that survive cold and dry conditions.
Mission 1

Tundra and polar regions

  • Tundra: south of the Arctic ice caps, across North America, Europe and Siberia.
  • Below 0 °C for 6 to 10 months; only 150–250 mm of rain a year.
  • Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) and a short growing season mean almost no trees: mosses, lichens, grasses and shrubs.
  • Polar regions: little or no winter sun; some areas are so dry they are polar deserts.
Quick check
?A climate graph shows: total rain 200 mm a year; monthly temperatures below 0 °C for eight months; warmest month about 6 °C. Which biome is it?
Mission 1

Local factors: altitude

  • Temperature falls with height, so biomes change up a mountain, like travelling towards the poles.
  • Kilimanjaro (5,895 m): lower slopes → montane forest → heath and moorland → alpine desert → summit.
  • Alpine tundra grows on very high mountains far from the poles.
Mission 1

Local factors: soils

  • Soil supplies water and nutrients, so it can change the plants inside a biome.
  • In rainforest, nutrients are held in the plants; leaf litter rots fast and is reused at once. Clear the forest and the soil is soon poor.
  • Badly drained or thin soils hold fewer trees than the climate alone would allow.
Mission check

?Imagine the belt of sinking air moved to about 25° north and south instead of 30°. Where would the hot deserts most likely have been?

Biomes form bands because air rises near the equator (rain, rainforest) and sinks around 30° (dry, deserts like the Sahara).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Make a guess: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.2 the biosphere provides food, medicine, building materials and fuel, but is increasingly exploited commercially for energy, water and minerals
Mission 2

Goods and services

  • The biosphere is the living layer of the Earth: plants, animals and the soils and waters they live in.
  • Ecosystem services are "the benefits people obtain from ecosystems".
  • Goods: things we take — food, water, timber, fuel, medicine.
  • Services: things ecosystems do for us — protect coasts, regulate climate, clean water, attract tourists.
Mission 2

The goods

  • Food: crops, fish, wild fruit and game.
  • Medicine: "Many drugs are now being developed from compounds discovered in coral reef animals and plants".
  • Building materials: timber, bamboo, thatch.
  • Fuel: wood and charcoal. In 2024 only 22% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had clean cooking, against 75% worldwide; most of the rest cook with fuels such as wood.
Quick check
?Which of these is a good, not a service, from an ecosystem?
Mission 2

The services

  • Natural protection: mangroves "stabilize the coastline, reducing erosion from storm surges, currents, waves, and tides".
  • Climate regulation: about half the CO₂ people emit each year stays in the air; "the rest is absorbed by Earth's land ecosystems and oceans".
  • Tourism: Australia's Great Barrier Reef is reported to contribute $9 billion a year to the economy (a study quoted by the park authority).
  • Health: medicines from reefs and forests, clean water and clean air.
Mission 2

From local use to commercial exploitation

  • Local people have always used ecosystems for subsistence: food, fuel and shelter for their own families.
  • Now ecosystems are exploited commercially: cleared or mined on a large scale to sell goods in global markets.
  • Example (minerals): Indonesia's share of world nickel mine production rose from about 30% in 2019 to about 67% in 2025 (calculated from USGS figures). Nickel is used in electric-vehicle batteries.
Mission 2

Food: mechanised farming

  • Mechanised farming: machines and large fields replace hand labour, so huge areas can be farmed by few people.
  • In South America the area of soybeans "more than doubled from 26.4 Mha to 55.1 Mha" between 2000 and 2019.
  • "Across the continent, 9% of forest loss was converted to soybean by 2016", nearly half of it in Brazil's Cerrado savanna.
  • Modification: natural forest and grassland replaced by one crop (a monoculture).
Mission 2

Food: commercial fishing

  • Large boats with big nets catch fish to sell worldwide.
  • In FAO's latest count, wild catch was 92 million tonnes; fish farming (aquaculture) produced 103 million tonnes.
  • In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were "biologically sustainable".
  • Modification: fewer large fish, changed food webs, damage to the seabed from some gear.
Quick check
?In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were biologically sustainable. What share were not?
Mission 2

Energy: deforestation and mining

  • Deforestation: forests are cleared for fuelwood, charcoal, farms and mines.
  • World forest area fell from about 4.34 billion hectares in 1990 to 4.14 billion in 2025: a net loss of about 203 million hectares, or 4.7% (calculated from FAO data).
  • Mining for energy: coal and uranium are dug from open pits or deep mines. Open pits remove the whole ecosystem above the rock.
  • Minerals such as nickel, used in electric-vehicle batteries, are mined the same way.
Mission 2

Water: reservoirs and transfer schemes

  • A reservoir is an artificial lake made by building a dam across a valley. The valley behind it is flooded.
  • A water transfer scheme moves water from a wet area to where it is needed, through rivers, pipes or tunnels.
  • Kielder Water, Northumberland (1982), holds about 200 billion litres.
  • Its water can be released into the Tyne, Derwent and Tees to supply homes and industry in north-east England: a reservoir and a transfer scheme.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a water transfer scheme?
Mission 2

Using goods can cost services

  • Between 2000 and 2020, the biggest single cause of mangrove loss worldwide was aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds): 27% of losses.
  • The pond owner gains a good (shrimp to sell). The coast loses a service (protection from waves).
  • The people who gain are often not the people who lose.
Mission check

?A government plans to clear a mangrove forest on a low coast to build shrimp ponds for export. Which outcome is most likely?

Ecosystems give goods that can be sold (shrimp, timber) and services that are usually free (protection, climate regulation). Clearing for goods is why ecosystems keep being cut down: the losses fall on people who are not paid for them.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are the UK's heather moors and peat bogs found mostly in the north and west?

Make a guess: Why are the UK's heather moors and peat bogs found mostly in the north and west?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.3 distribution and characteristics of UK terrestrial ecosystems (moorland, heath, woodland, wetland)
  • 3.3 importance of marine ecosystems to the UK as a resource and how human activities degrade them
Mission 3

Four land ecosystems

  • Moorland: open upland covered by heather, grasses and bog, often on peat.
  • Heath: low shrubs (heather, gorse) on dry, sandy or acidic soils; upland or lowland.
  • Woodland: land dominated by trees: broadleaf (oak, ash, birch) or conifer.
  • Wetland: land that is waterlogged for much of the year: bogs, fens, marshes, wet grassland.
Mission 3

Moorland and blanket bog

  • Where: blanket bogs are found "in the north and west of the UK, extending from Devon in the south to Shetland in the north".
  • Why there: they form where there is "high rainfall and a low level of evapotranspiration", so peat builds up over whole hillsides.
  • Plants: heather, cotton-grass, bog-mosses (Sphagnum).
  • Use: many heather moors are grazed by sheep or managed as grouse moors.
Mission 3

Heathland

  • Soils: freely draining, acidic, low in nutrients.
  • Plants: heather, gorse, bilberry, bell heather.
  • Where: throughout the UK; most extensive in the uplands, more patchy in the lowlands of south and central England.
  • Made by people: "Nearly all dry heath is semi-natural, being derived from woodland through a long history of grazing and burning".
  • Lowland heath has declined through tree planting and farming improvement. Example: Ashdown Forest, Sussex; Dartmoor is upland heath.
Quick check
?Why would most UK heathland slowly turn back into woodland if grazing and burning stopped?
Mission 3

Woodland

  • Woodland covers 13.6% of UK land; Europe as a whole has 47% forest cover.
  • Scotland has 1.5 million of the UK's 3.3 million hectares, 46% (our calculation); 71% of it is conifer.
  • England's woodland is 77% broadleaf.
  • Western oak woods grow in the wet north and west, rich in mosses and ferns; they are "much more thinly distributed in drier eastern regions".
Mission 3

Wetland

  • Upland wetland: blanket bog and wet heath on shallow peat; wet heath is "typical … in the north and west of the UK".
  • Lowland wetland: the Somerset Levels and Moors, over 60,000 hectares, very flat and one of the lowest areas in the UK.
  • Much of the Levels was drained for farming; water levels are still managed for "communities, farming and nature".
Mission 3

What controls the pattern

  • Rainfall: over 4,000 mm a year in the western Scottish mountains; about 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England. Wet + cool = waterlogged, acidic soils → peat, bog and moor.
  • Altitude: higher land is colder and wetter.
  • Geology and soils: acidic, poor soils → heath; richer, drier soils → broadleaf woods or farmland.
  • People: grazing, burning, draining and tree planting decide much of what we see.
Quick check
?A hillside in western Scotland is cool, gets over 2,000 mm of rain a year and has little evaporation. Which ecosystem is most likely to develop on it?
Mission 3

The sea as a resource

  • Food: in 2024 UK vessels landed 745,000 tonnes of fish worth £1.16 billion.
  • Wildlife: UK coasts and islands give nesting sites for around seven million seabirds.
  • Energy: e.g. Gwynt y Môr, a 576 MW offshore wind farm of 160 turbines off North Wales.
  • The UK is still a net importer of fish: it imported 633,000 tonnes and exported 380,000 tonnes in 2024.
Mission 3

How people degrade UK seas

  • Overfishing: in 2015 only 53% of quota fish stocks were fished at sustainable levels (below maximum sustainable yield). North Sea seabed fish are "recovering from over exploitation".
  • Seabed damage: most seabed habitats are not in good condition; bottom-towed fishing gear is one pressure being restricted.
  • Litter: not yet in good condition; beach litter in the Greater North Sea has "slightly increased".
  • Sewage: in 2024, England's storm overflows (into rivers and coastal waters) spilled an average of 31.8 times each.
  • Seabirds: most populations are not in good condition, likely from climate change and human activity.
Quick check
?In 2015, 53% of the assessed UK quota fish stocks were fished at sustainable levels. What does this tell you?
Mission check

?Dartmoor, in Devon, is the southernmost blanket bog in Europe. It is upland, rising to 621 m, and gets up to about double the rain of the Devon coast. Why can blanket bog grow this far south?

Moors and bogs are mostly in the north and west because that is where rain is highest and evaporation lowest, so peat forms. High, wet ground further south, like Dartmoor, grows bog too; heaths are kept open by grazing and burning.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Rainforest soils are surprisingly poor. So how do they grow such huge, rich forests?

Make a guess: Rainforest soils are surprisingly poor. So how do they grow such huge, rich forests?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.4 biotic and abiotic characteristics (climate, soils, water, plants, animals, humans)
  • 3.4 interdependence and the nutrient cycle (Gersmehl model)
  • 3.4 why biodiversity is very high; plant (layers, buttress roots, drip tips) and animal adaptations
Mission 4

Where are tropical rainforests?

  • Between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, mostly close to the equator.
  • The largest areas: the Amazon (South America), the Congo Basin (Africa) and South-East Asia.
Mission 4

The climate: hot and wet all year

  • Manaus, Brazil (1970–2000):
    • every month 27–28 °C: an annual range of only 1 °C
    • 2,323 mm of rain a year; even the driest month (August) has 68 mm
  • Rainforests typically get 2,000 mm or more of rain a year.
Mission 4

Why: the equatorial climate

  • Latitude: near the equator, the sun is overhead or nearly overhead all year, so heating is strong every month.
  • Hot air rises, creating low pressure; the trade winds meet and rise in the ITCZ, forming thunderstorms.
  • Convectional rainfall: the sun heats the ground → warm, moist air rises → cools → condenses → towering clouds → afternoon storms.
Mission 4

A day in an equatorial climate

  • Singapore, 1° north (1991–2020):
    • monthly mean temperature 26.8–28.6 °C
    • 2,113 mm of rain a year, on 171 days
  • Storms come mostly in the afternoon, when solar heating is strongest; clouds tower 9–12 km high.
Quick check
?Singapore's warmest month averages 28.6 °C and its coolest 26.8 °C. What is its annual temperature range?
Mission 4

Layers of the forest

  • Emergents: the tallest trees, standing above the rest.
  • Canopy: a dense roof of leaves that blocks most sunlight from reaching the ground. Woody vines climb trees to reach the light.
  • Under canopy: smaller trees, vines, ferns and palms in dim light.
  • Shrub layer and forest floor: dark, with few plants, covered in leaf litter.
Mission 4

Soils: latosols

  • Latosols are deep: warm, wet conditions weather the rock far down.
  • They are red, from iron oxides.
  • They are poor in nutrients: heavy rain washes nutrients down and out (leaching).
  • Only a thin top layer is rich, where leaf litter rots.
Mission 4

The nutrient cycle (Gersmehl model)

  • Nutrients are held in three stores: biomass (living things), litter (dead leaves) and soil.
  • Flows link them: leaf fall, decomposition, uptake by roots, plus leaching and runoff (out) and rain and weathered rock (in).
  • In a rainforest, the biomass store is largest. Litter rots fast in warm, wet conditions, and roots take the nutrients straight back up.
  • So the litter and soil stores are small.
Quick check
?In the Gersmehl model of a tropical rainforest, which store holds the most nutrients?
Mission 4

The water cycle

  • The canopy intercepts rain; much evaporates back into the air.
  • Trees take up water and release it through their leaves (transpiration).
  • This moist air makes more rain: air that has passed over forest produces at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • So the forest helps make its own rain: a regional-scale process.
Mission 4

Plant adaptations

  • Drip tips: pointed leaf tips shed heavy rain fast, so fungi and algae cannot grow on wet leaves.
  • Buttress roots: huge ridges at the base support tall trees whose roots spread near the surface, where the nutrients are.
  • Lianas: woody vines climb trees to reach sunlight.
  • Epiphytes: plants growing on branches high up, to reach the light.
Mission 4

Animal adaptations: the sloth

  • Long, curved claws (8–10 cm) for hanging in the canopy.
  • Algae grow in grooves in its hair, turning it green: camouflage among leaves.
  • A metabolic rate only 40–45% of that expected for its size: it saves energy on a low-energy leaf diet.
  • Other adaptations to know: strong limbs (climbing), modified wings and beaks (flying between trees, reaching fruit), camouflage.
Quick check
?Which adaptation helps a rainforest plant cope with very heavy rain?
Mission 4

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: the parts of an ecosystem depend on each other; change one and the others change.
  • Abiotic parts are non-living (climate, soil, water); biotic parts are living (plants, animals, people).
  • Climate → plants: heat and rain all year let plants grow all year.
  • Plants → soil: leaf fall feeds the thin fertile layer; roots hold the soil.
  • Plants → water → climate: transpiration makes more rain.
  • Animals ↔ plants: animals eat fruit and spread seeds; plants give food and shelter.
  • People: forest peoples depend on it for food and materials; clearing breaks the links.
Mission 4

Biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of living things in an area.
  • About 60% of all vascular plant species are found in tropical forests.
  • Forests are home to 80% of known amphibian species, 75% of birds and 68% of mammals.
  • Amazonia alone has roughly 16,000 tree species.
  • Why so high: heat, light and rain all year allow constant growth; the layers create many different habitats.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes biodiversity?
Mission check

?Farmers clear a patch of rainforest and burn the trees. Crops grow well for two or three years, then fail. What best explains this?

In the Gersmehl model the rainforest's nutrients sit mainly in biomass; litter rots fast and is taken straight back up. Clear the trees and heavy rain leaches the thin, poor soil, so crops soon fail.

Mission 5: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Make a guess: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.5 goods and services from rainforests (foodstuffs, medicines, timber, recreation)
  • 3.5 political and economic factors (governance, commodity value, ecotourism) behind sustainable management in a named region
Mission 5

What rainforests give people: goods

  • Timber: hardwoods for building and furniture.
  • Foodstuffs: nuts, fruit and game. In one Guatemalan forest village, families still hunt forest animals for food.
  • Medicines: quinine, from the bark of the Andean "fever tree" (Cinchona), was for centuries the main treatment for malaria.
  • These are goods: things people take from the forest and use or sell.
Mission 5

What rainforests give people and the planet: services

  • Recreation: tourists pay to see wildlife and ruins in the forest, e.g. Tikal's Maya temples in Guatemala.
  • Biodiversity: the Amazon alone holds roughly 16,000 tree species.
  • Carbon store: Amazon trees store "more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth".
  • Rain: over much of the tropics, air that has crossed forest gives at least twice as much rain as air that has crossed little vegetation.
  • These are services: benefits the forest gives while it is still standing.
Quick check
?Which of these is a service a standing rainforest provides, rather than a good taken from it?
Mission 5

What makes management sustainable?

  • Sustainable management meets people's needs today without destroying the forest for the future.
  • Test any plan with three questions:
  • Environment: does forest cover and wildlife stay?
  • People: do local people earn a living from it?
  • Long term: will it still work in 20 years, with rules that are enforced?
Mission 5

The case: the Maya Biosphere Reserve, Guatemala

  • Petén, northern Guatemala: the largest expanse of continuous rainforest in Central America.
  • Guatemala's protected areas cover 32% of its land, yet only Nicaragua has lost forest faster in Latin America and the Caribbean since 2000.
  • The reserve is shared out in zones, each with different rules.
  • Guatemala has a medium HDI (0.662, 2023): an emerging country in Edexcel's terms.
Mission 5

Zoning

  • Core zone: strictly protected; no logging. Its national parks include Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul.
  • Multiple-use zone: forest used under rules. Rights to timber and other forest products were given to 12 community groups and 2 timber companies.
  • Buffer zone: along the southern edge; farming and settlement, with some clearing allowed "under certain circumstances".
  • UNESCO's model: core = strict protection; buffer = activities compatible with conservation; transition = where communities live and work.
Mission 5

Local scale: Uaxactún's community forest

  • A village of about 800 people that manages 835 km² of forest in the multiple-use zone.
  • Selective logging since 2000; certified by the FSC since 2001.
  • Residents agreed hunting rules: no hunting at waterholes, no selling meat to outsiders.
  • Result: camera-trap surveys estimated 25 species of large ground animals in Uaxactún's forest and 24 in the strictly protected Mirador–Río Azul area next door.
  • But heavily hunted species, such as the great curassow, were scarcer near the village.
Mission 5

Why it works: political and economic factors

  • Governance: the state gave communities legal rights to use the forest through concession agreements. People who own the benefits have a reason to protect the forest.
  • Commodity value: FSC certification lets standing forest earn money from timber; elsewhere, high prices for farm commodities drive clearing (agriculture caused 90–99% of tropical deforestation, 2011–15).
  • Ecotourism: Maya ruins in the eastern parks are "essential for the tourism industry".
Mission 5

Does it work?

  • Success: Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul national parks stayed over 99% stable forest, 2000–2017.
  • Mixed: some concessions manage forests well; others "have had limited success or failed completely".
  • Failure: the buffer zone lost 1.4% of its forest a year; by 2017 only 17% was undisturbed forest.
  • Pressure on the core: Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared despite its protection.
Quick check
?Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared, while Mirador–Río Azul National Park, in the far north, stayed almost untouched. Both are in the same strictly protected core zone. What does this suggest?
Mission check

?A government wants to cut deforestation in a rainforest region where many villagers depend on the forest for income. Which plan is most likely to be sustainable?

In the Maya Biosphere Reserve, a certified community forest at Uaxactún kept as many large animal species as the strictly protected forest next door. Where rules were not enforced, as in the buffer zone, the forest kept shrinking.

Mission 6

Mission 6: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Make a guess: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.5 how climate change threatens rainforest structure, functioning and biodiversity
  • 3.5 economic and social causes of deforestation: agriculture, resource extraction, population pressure
Mission 6

The Amazon rainforest

  • The largest tropical rainforest on Earth. Most of it is in Brazil; it also covers parts of eight other South American countries and territories.
  • About 16,000 tree species.
  • Amazon trees store more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth.
  • This mission uses the Brazilian Amazon as the case study.
Mission 6

Changing rates of deforestation

  • From 2004 to 2012 the rate of clearing in the Brazilian Amazon fell by 84%.
  • 2012: 4,571 km² cleared, the lowest on record until 2026.
  • The rate then rose again: 5,731 km² in August 2024–July 2025.
  • August 2025–July 2026: an estimated 3,335 km², the lowest since records began in 1988.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: By what percentage did clearing fall from 2024–25 (5,731 km²) to 2025–26 (3,335 km²)?
Mission 6

Cause: farming

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–15 was driven by agriculture.
  • In the Amazon, most cleared land first becomes cattle pasture (commercial farming).
  • Soy then spreads, mostly onto pastures first cleared for cattle.
  • Much soy is exported: in 2025 China imported 82.3 million tonnes of soybeans from Brazil, 74% of its soybean imports.
Mission 6

Cause: logging

  • Selective logging takes valuable trees and leaves the rest standing.
  • 1999–2002, in Brazil's top five timber states, 12,075–19,823 km² were logged each year: 60–123% of the area deforested.
  • Logging roads open up the forest to farmers and settlers.
Mission 6

Cause: roads, mining and energy

  • Roads: nearly 95% of deforestation happened within 5.5 km of a road or 1 km of a river. Over 190,000 km of the roads are unofficial.
  • Mining: small-scale gold mining (garimpo) in Brazil grew from about 218 km² (1985) to about 2,627 km² (2022). More than 91% is in the Amazon, and at least 77% of 2022 sites showed signs of illegality.
  • Energy (HEP): the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River flooded land and forced people in Altamira to move.
Mission 6

Cause: settlement and population growth

  • New roads bring settlers looking for land.
  • Growing towns and villages need land for homes, food and fuel.
  • This is population pressure: a social cause. Farming and resource extraction are economic causes.
Quick check
?Which cause most often opens the forest up for the others?
Mission 6

Impact: rainfall and regional climate

  • Trees pump water vapour into the air (transpiration), which falls again as rain downwind.
  • Over more than 60% of tropical land, air that has passed over forest gives at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Replacing forest with monoculture crops or pasture means less moisture is recycled.
  • If clearing trends continued, Amazon rainfall could fall by 12% (wet season) and 21% (dry season) by 2050.
Quick check
?Which impact of clearing works at a regional scale, across much of the Amazon basin?
Mission 6

Climate change threatens the forest

  • 2023: the river at the port of Manaus fell to 12.70 m, its lowest since records began in 1902.
  • In one lake, water reached 41 °C. In the central Amazon, 209 river dolphins died.
  • Drought and heat kill trees and dry the forest, so fires spread: this changes the forest's structure and functioning.
  • By 2050, 10–47% of Amazon forests could face combined stresses that may tip them into a different ecosystem.
Mission check

?In September–October 2015, forest and peatland fires across maritime south-east Asia, mostly in Indonesia, released more CO₂ per day than all the fossil fuels burned in the EU. Which conclusion best fits?

In the Amazon, farming (cattle, then soy) is the main cause, helped by roads, logging, mining and HEP. The impacts reach far beyond Brazil: rainfall.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why do Britain's oak and beech trees drop all their leaves every autumn?

Make a guess: Why do Britain's oak and beech trees drop all their leaves every autumn?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.6 abiotic and biotic characteristics of deciduous woodland (climate, soil, water, plants, animals, humans)
  • 3.6 interdependence of deciduous woodland components and the nutrient cycle (Gersmehl model)
  • 3.6 why deciduous woodland biodiversity is moderate; plant (leaf, winter water conservation) and animal (migration, hibernation, food storage) adaptations
Mission 7

Where deciduous woodland grows

  • Temperate deciduous woodland grows in the mid-latitudes: western and central Europe, the eastern USA and eastern Asia.
  • Its trees are deciduous: they drop their leaves in autumn and grow new ones in spring.
  • In the UK, broadleaved woodland covers 1.72 million hectares, about 52% of all woodland.
  • In England, broadleaves make up about 77% of woodland.
  • Example used in this mission and the next: the New Forest, Hampshire.
Mission 7

Climate: four seasons, no dry season

Hurn (on the edge of the New Forest), 1991–2020 averages:

  • Coldest months: January and February, mean 5.3 °C. Warmest: July, mean 17.2 °C.
  • Rain: about 878 mm a year, falling in every month. Wettest October–January (about 100 mm a month).
  • About 56 air frosts a year, mostly November to March.
  • So: mild, wet winters with frosts; warm summers; long summer days and short winter days.
Mission 7

Abiotic: soil and water

  • Soil: brown earth, deep and fairly fertile.
  • Each autumn, fallen leaves form a litter layer. Earthworms and fungi break it down and mix it into the soil.
  • Water: rain all year keeps soils moist; little water is lost in winter when trees are bare.
  • Climate, soil and water are the abiotic (non-living) parts.
Mission 7

Biotic: plants, animals, people

  • Layers: canopy (oak, beech), under-canopy (holly), shrub layer, field layer (bluebells, bracken), ground layer (mosses).
  • In the New Forest, "ancient oaks, beech and holly support an extraordinary range of wildlife, from insects and fungi to birds and mammals".
  • Animals: deer, badgers, squirrels, woodland birds, insects.
  • People: the New Forest has been shaped by people for centuries, from a royal hunting forest in about 1079 to free-roaming ponies, cattle and pigs today.
Quick check
?Which is an abiotic feature of deciduous woodland?
Mission 7

The Gersmehl nutrient cycle

  • The Gersmehl model shows nutrients stored and moving in an ecosystem.
  • Three stores: biomass (living things), litter (dead leaves and wood), soil.
  • Flows: leaf fall (biomass → litter); decomposition (litter → soil); uptake by roots (soil → biomass).
  • Inputs: weathering of rock, and nutrients dissolved in rain. Outputs: leaching and run-off.
  • Deciduous woodland: large biomass and soil stores; leaf fall each autumn means a big yearly flow into the litter.
Mission 7

Everything is linked

  • Climate → trees: warm, wet summers let trees grow fast; cold winters make them drop leaves.
  • Trees → soil: leaf litter feeds the soil each year.
  • Soil → trees: fertile soil supplies nutrients for new leaves in spring.
  • Trees → animals: acorns and nuts feed squirrels, jays and deer; trees give nest sites.
  • Animals and people → plants: grazing animals "physically shape the lawns and heathland of the Forest".
Quick check
?In the Gersmehl model, what happens when leaves fall in autumn?
Mission 7

Why biodiversity is moderate

  • More species than coniferous forest or tundra: mild climate, fertile soils and several layers give many habitats.
  • Fewer species than tropical rainforest: cold winters and short winter days mean plants grow for only part of the year.
  • Fewer layers and less energy than the rainforest, so fewer niches.
  • So biodiversity is moderate: between the rainforest and the cold biomes.
Mission 7

How plants adapt

  • Leaf fall: in winter, frozen or cold soil makes water hard to take up, and short days give little light. Broad leaves would lose more water than they could replace, so trees drop them.
  • Winter water conservation: bare branches lose very little water; buds are protected by tough scales.
  • Broad, thin leaves in summer catch as much light as possible.
  • Early flowering: bluebells flower in spring before the canopy closes, while light still reaches the floor.
  • Thick bark protects trunks from cold.
Mission 7

How animals adapt

  • Migration: some birds leave in autumn for warmer places; others arrive for the winter. Thousands of Brent geese overwinter on the New Forest coast from "their Siberian breeding grounds".
  • Hibernation: hedgehogs and dormice sleep through winter, when food is scarce.
  • Food storage: squirrels and jays bury acorns and nuts in autumn to eat later.
  • All three help animals survive the season when plant food is scarce.
Quick check
?A grey squirrel buries acorns in October. Which adaptation is this?
Mission check

?Sugar maple trees in the north-eastern USA also drop their leaves every autumn. Which reason is best?

Deciduous trees drop their leaves because, in cold winters with short days, broad leaves would lose more water than the roots can take up from cold or frozen soil. The fallen leaves then feed the soil through the nutrient cycle.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Can a forest that millions of people visit every year be used and protected at the same time?

Make a guess: Can a forest that millions of people visit every year be used and protected at the same time?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 3.7 goods and services from deciduous woodlands: timber, fuel, conservation, recreation
  • 3.7 how climate change threatens deciduous woodland structure, function and biodiversity
  • 3.7 economic and social causes of deciduous deforestation: urbanisation, population growth, timber extraction, agricultural change
  • 3.7 approaches to sustainable use and management of deciduous woodland in a named region
Mission 8

What woodlands give us

Goods (things we take) and services (benefits we get):

  • Timber: furniture, building, tools. New Forest wood has been "hewn into" furniture, instruments, toys and bowls; it once supplied ship-building.
  • Fuel: firewood and charcoal. Since 1217, commoners have had the right of estover, collecting firewood.
  • Conservation: habitats for rare wildlife; trees capture carbon.
  • Recreation: walking, cycling, riding. The New Forest has "over 15 million day-visits" a year and more than 100 miles of waymarked cycle routes.
Quick check
?Which is a service rather than a good from a deciduous woodland?
Mission 8

Climate change: a threat

  • New Forest records: hottest summer in 2022, wettest winter 2023/24, one of the driest and hottest spring–summers in 2025.
  • Expected: "warmer, wetter winters and hotter, drier summers", more intense and frequent storms, and "longer periods of higher fire risk".
  • Structure: storms and drought can kill or topple old trees, opening gaps in the canopy.
  • Function: hot, dry summers stress trees, slowing growth and the nutrient cycle; wildfire burns the litter store.
  • Biodiversity: species suited to cooler, wetter conditions may decline; new pests and diseases arrive. 19 serious new tree pests and diseases reached the UK between 1990 and 2021.
Quick check
?Hot, dry summers kill old beech trees, which fall and leave gaps in the canopy. Which part of the woodland does this change describe?
Mission 8

Why deciduous woodland is lost

  • Agricultural change: over centuries, woods were cleared for fields. Today woodland covers only 14% of UK land, and 10% of England.
  • Timber extraction: wood for ships, building and fuel; forest law later "served mainly to protect the timber in royal forests".
  • Urbanisation and population growth: new homes, roads and business parks. "More than 1,000 irreplaceable ancient woods have been threatened by development over the last 10 years".
  • New Forest today: "thousands of homes being built around its boundaries", and a port terminal is proposed at Dibden Bay.
Quick check
?A town grows by 5,000 people, and a housing estate is planned next to an ancient wood. Which cause of woodland loss is this?
Mission 8

The New Forest: who manages it?

  • A National Park: its National Park Authority was set up in 2005.
  • Forestry England manages 47% of the National Park, including much of the ancient woodland.
  • The Verderers' Court, a body dating from forest law, still meets in Lyndhurst.
  • Commoners turn out ponies, cattle, donkeys and pigs to graze the open forest.
Mission 8

Sustainable approaches

  • Commoning: free-roaming animals graze the open forest. Grazing "physically shape[s] the lawns and heathland" and helps biodiversity.
  • Visitor management: the New Forest Code; parking charges since April 2026, which help "fund vital work to look after this very special landscape".
  • Planning control: 900+ planning applications checked each year to protect the Park.
  • Restoring nature: land equal to 350 football pitches restored; £1m+ in grants to landowners and farmers for climate and nature.
  • Climate action: nature-based solutions that store more carbon.
Quick check
?Why does the New Forest charge visitors to park?
Mission 8

Does it work?

  • Successes: ancient woodland and heath survive beside a busy part of southern England; commoning still shapes the landscape.
  • Pressures: just over 50% of the Park's Sites of Special Scientific Interest are in "favourable condition".
  • Commoning at risk: farming schemes suit it poorly, and cattle numbers have fallen "due to reduced support payments and rising costs".
  • Visitors: 75% of the 15 million day-visits come from about 300,000 local people.
  • Housing: average house prices are about 15 times average earnings.
Mission 8

Exam move: evaluate management

  • Name the approach (commoning, parking charges, planning control).
  • Say what it protects: conservation, recreation, timber and fuel, people's livelihoods.
  • Say who gains and who pays.
  • Judge: does it keep the woodland for future generations while people use it today? Use a figure from the named region.
Mission check

?Epping Forest, an ancient wood near London, has many visitors and housing growth around it. Which plan best fits sustainable management?

Yes, a heavily visited woodland can be used and protected, if use is managed (paths, parking charges, planning rules) and the people who use and live by it share the benefits and costs. The New Forest shows it is possible, but pressures from climate change, housing and the cost of commoning remain.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Biomes are large-scale ecosystems; their distribution follows climate: rainforest at the equator, deserts at about 30°, temperate and boreal forest further out, tundra and ice near the poles.
  • Altitude and soils alter biomes locally.

Mission 2

  • Goods (food, medicine, building materials, fuel, water) are taken; services (protection, climate regulation, tourism, health) are provided.
  • Commercial exploitation modifies ecosystems: mechanised farming and fishing (food), deforestation and mining (energy, minerals), reservoirs and transfers (water).

Mission 3

  • Moorland and blanket bog: north and west, high rainfall, peat, heather, cotton-grass. Heath: acidic, poor soils, made and kept by grazing and burning. Woodland: 13.6% of UK land; conifer in Scotland, broadleaf in England. Wetland: bogs in the uplands, the Somerset Levels in the lowlands.
  • UK seas give food (£1.16 billion of fish in 2024), energy and wildlife, but are degraded by overfishing, seabed damage, litter, sewage and climate change.

Mission 4

  • Characteristics: between the Tropics; hot (27–28 °C) and wet (over 2,000 mm) all year from overhead sun, low pressure and convectional rain; layers from emergents to forest floor; deep, red, leached latosols.
  • How it works: nutrients stored mainly in biomass and recycled fast; forests make their own rain; everything is interdependent.
  • Plants and animals are adapted (drip tips, buttress roots, sloths).
  • Biodiversity is the highest on land.

Mission 5

  • Rainforests give goods (timber, food, medicines) and services (recreation, carbon, rain, biodiversity); management must keep both.
  • Zoning (strict core, managed multiple-use, buffer) works where people benefit and rules are enforced: Uaxactún kept its wildlife; the buffer zone lost most of its forest.

Mission 6

  • Amazon clearing fell sharply after 2004, rose again after 2012, and in 2025–26 reached its lowest estimate since 1988.
  • Causes: farming (cattle, soy), logging, roads, mining, HEP, settlement.
  • Impacts: less rainfall as less moisture is recycled; drought, heat and fire threaten the forest's structure and functioning.

Mission 7

  • Temperate deciduous woodland: mid-latitudes; four seasons; rain all year; fertile brown earth soils.
  • Gersmehl model: biomass, litter and soil stores; leaf fall, decomposition and uptake link them.
  • Biodiversity is moderate: more than cold biomes, fewer than the rainforest.
  • Plants drop leaves and flower early; animals migrate, hibernate or store food.

Mission 8

  • Deciduous woodlands give goods (timber, fuel) and services (conservation, recreation, carbon storage).
  • Climate change threatens structure (canopy loss), function (growth, nutrient cycling) and biodiversity (species loss, new pests).
  • Causes of loss: agricultural change, timber extraction, urbanisation and population growth.
  • New Forest: commoning, visitor management, planning control and nature restoration, but with pressures still building.
