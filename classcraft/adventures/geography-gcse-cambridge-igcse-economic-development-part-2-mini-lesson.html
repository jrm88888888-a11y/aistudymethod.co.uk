Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do fewer than one in a hundred UK workers farm, while most Ethiopian workers do?

Make a guess: Why do fewer than one in a hundred UK workers farm, while most Ethiopian workers do?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.1.1 classifying industries: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary
  • 9.1.2 changing employment structure of LICs, MICs and HICs
Mission 1

Four sectors of work

  • Primary: taking raw materials from the land or sea: farming, fishing, mining, forestry.
  • Secondary: making things: manufacturing, construction.
  • Tertiary: providing services: shops, health care, teaching, transport, tourism.
  • Quaternary: knowledge and information: research and development, IT, consultancy.
  • Employment structure: the share of workers in each sector.
Quick check
?A scientist designs a new vaccine in a research laboratory. Which sector is this?
Mission 1

Three countries, three structures (2023)

Share of all workers:

  • UK (high income): agriculture 0.9%, industry 15.9%, services 83.2%.
  • India (lower-middle income): agriculture 43.5%, industry 25.0%, services 31.5%.
  • Ethiopia (low income): agriculture 62.2%, industry 6.4%, services 31.5%.
  • Income groups from the World Bank.
  • These are an HIC (high-income country), a MIC (middle-income country) and a LIC (low-income country).
  • The data group work into three sectors: quaternary jobs are counted inside services.
Mission 1

Structures change over time

1991 → 2023, share of workers:

  • UK: industry 30.8% → 15.9%; services 66.8% → 83.2%.
  • India: agriculture 63.1% → 43.5%; industry 14.6% → 25.0%.
  • Ethiopia: agriculture 77.2% → 62.2%; services 16.2% → 31.5%.
  • Pattern: as countries develop, workers move out of farming, then from industry into services.
Quick check
?India's share of workers in agriculture fell from 63.1% in 1991 to 43.5% in 2023. By how much did it fall?
Mission 1

The Clark-Fisher model

  • A model of how employment structure changes as a country develops.
  • Stage 1 (pre-industrial): most people work in the primary sector.
  • Stage 2 (industrial): secondary jobs rise as factories grow; primary jobs fall.
  • Stage 3 (post-industrial): tertiary (and quaternary) jobs dominate; secondary jobs fall.
Mission 1

The UK through the model

  • Primary share of workers: 14.3% in 1920 → 1.3% in 2016.
  • Tertiary share rose "from 1966 onwards" to 83.6% in 2016.
  • Manufacturing "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • So the UK is in stage 3; Ethiopia is still mostly in stage 1; India is in between.
Mission 1

Why jobs shift (1)

  • Mechanisation: machines do farm and factory work, so fewer workers are needed for the same output.
  • Availability of raw materials: when a resource runs out or stops being used, its jobs go. UK coal fell from about 40% of electricity in 2012 to 0%.
Mission 1

Why jobs shift (2)

  • Globalisation: firms move factory work to where costs are lower. Apple's manufacturing is done by "outsourcing partners located primarily in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam". Factory jobs grow there, while design and services stay in the HIC.
  • Demographic change: an ageing population needs more care and health workers (tertiary); a growing young population needs more schools and jobs.
  • Government policy: governments can close or support industries. The UK has moved to ban new coal mining; other governments offer support to attract factories.
  • Changing rules and technology also change who works where: the ONS points to "changes in technology, industrial structure, legislation and social change".
Quick check
?Combine harvesters let one farm worker do the work of many. Which cause of change is this, and which sector loses jobs?
Mission 1

The model does not fit every country

  • In Ethiopia, 1991–2023: agriculture fell by 15.0 points, services rose by 15.3, industry barely changed (6.7% → 6.4%).
  • So many workers moved straight from farming to services (shops, transport, small trades), skipping a big industrial stage.
  • The model is based on the history of HICs such as the UK; today's LICs face global competition for factory work.
Mission check

?China's employment structure: 1991: agriculture 59.6%, industry 21.4%, services 18.9%. 2023: agriculture 22.8%, industry 31.4%, services 45.8%. Which statement fits best?

As countries develop, mechanisation cuts farm jobs, factories grow (helped by globalisation and government policy), and later services and knowledge jobs take over. The UK went through this long ago, so under 1% of its workers farm; Ethiopia is early in the process, and is moving into services without a large industrial stage.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are some factories by the sea, some beside motorways and some next door to universities?

Make a guess: Why are some factories by the sea, some beside motorways and some next door to universities?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.1.3 factors influencing the location of different types of industries: land, labour, raw materials, energy, transport, markets, political policies, technology, communications, containerisation
Mission 2

Why location matters

  • A firm locates where it can keep costs low and reach its customers.
  • Different industries need different things, so the best site differs.
  • The factors to know: land, labour, raw materials, energy, transport, markets, political policies, technology, communications, containerisation.
Mission 2

Land, raw materials and energy

  • Land: big factories need flat, cheap land with room to grow.
  • Raw materials: if they are bulky and heavy and lose weight in processing (e.g. iron ore into steel), it pays to be near the source or a port where they arrive.
  • Energy: early factories clustered on coalfields; heavy industry still needs cheap, reliable power.
Mission 2

Labour, markets, transport and policy

  • Labour: enough workers with the right skills, at a cost the firm can pay.
  • Markets: near customers, especially if products are bulky or perishable.
  • Transport: motorways, railways, ports and airports.
  • Political policies: grants, tax breaks and planning rules can pull a firm to a place.
  • Example of labour needs: Toyota's UK plants (announced in 1989; cars at Burnaston, Derbyshire, engines at Deeside, north Wales) employ about 3,000 people. A plant that size needs a large local workforce.
Quick check
?A company turns heavy iron ore into steel, and the steel weighs much less than the ore. Where should it locate?
Mission 2

Containerisation

  • Containerisation: goods packed in standard steel boxes that move from ship to train to lorry without unpacking.
  • World container port traffic rose from 547 million to 913 million TEU (twenty-foot container units) between 2010 and 2024.
  • Research finds containerisation was a driver of 20th-century economic globalisation.
  • Effect on location: parts and products can come from far away cheaply, so factories need not sit beside raw materials or markets. Deep-water container ports attract warehouses and distribution centres.
  • Example: London Gateway opened a fourth berth; London's container traffic rose 38% in 2025.
Quick check
?How has containerisation changed where factories can locate?
Mission 2

Technology and communications

  • High-tech and research firms need skilled graduates, fast communications and a pleasant environment.
  • They cluster near universities.
  • Cambridge Science Park: in 1970 Trinity College turned farmland outside Cambridge into the UK's first science park.
  • It now has more than 7,000 members in software, life sciences, engineering and biotech.
Mission 2

Factors differ by sector

  • Primary (farming, mining, fishing): must go where the resource is: good soils and climate, the ore body, the fishing grounds.
  • Secondary (manufacturing): raw materials, energy, labour, transport, markets.
  • Tertiary (services, shops): near customers and easy to reach: town centres, retail parks by main roads.
  • Quaternary (research, IT): universities, skilled staff, communications.
Mission 2

Factors change: Port Talbot

  • The steelworks at Port Talbot, south Wales, was "the UK's largest single industrial carbon emitter".
  • The UK government offered up to £500 million towards a £1.25 billion plan to replace its blast furnaces with an electric arc furnace.
  • Construction began on 14 July 2025; the project is expected to cut the site's carbon emissions by about 90% and is said to secure 5,000 jobs.
  • Lesson: energy and policy (grants, climate targets) now shape where and how steel is made.
Mission check

?A firm makes bottled drinks. Its main ingredient, water, can be found almost anywhere, but full bottles are heavy and are sold in every town. Where is it most likely to locate?

Each industry weighs the factors differently. Bulky products follow their markets; heavy industry follows raw materials, energy and ports; high-tech firms follow universities, skills and communications; and factors change over time with containerisation, technology and policy (e.g. Port Talbot's new furnace).

Make a guess: The Netherlands tops the KOF Globalisation Index. The USA, the world's largest economy at market exchange rates, ranks 28th. How can that be?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.2.1 what is meant by globalisation
  • 9.2.2 key features of globalisation
  • 9.2.3 impacts of globalisation on trade, transport, culture, communications and technology
Mission 3

What is globalisation?

  • Globalisation: the growing connections and interdependence between places, through flows of:
    • goods and services (trade)
    • money (investment, loans, aid)
    • people (migrants, tourists, students)
    • ideas and information (internet, media, culture).
  • Places become linked so that events in one place affect others far away.
Mission 3

Measuring it: the KOF index

  • The KOF Globalisation Index (ETH Zurich) scores 195 countries using 42 variables.
  • Three dimensions: economic (trade, investment), social (people, information, culture) and political (embassies, treaties, international organisations).
  • 2025 ranking: 1st Netherlands, 2nd Switzerland, 3rd Belgium, 4th UK. Singapore 17th, USA 28th, China 82nd, India 94th.
Quick check
?The KOF index counts the number of McDonald's restaurants per person in each country. Which dimension of globalisation does this measure?
Mission 3

Why size matters

  • Most KOF variables are ratios: trade is measured as exports and imports "(% of GDP)".
  • A small, open economy, such as the Netherlands, trades a lot compared with its size.
  • The USA trades a huge amount in total, but its "large internal markets reduce the relative share of international flows".
  • So the index measures how connected a country is, not how big it is.
Mission 3

Key features of globalisation

  • Global trade in goods and services keeps growing.
  • Transnational corporations (TNCs) produce and sell in many countries; foreign direct investment reached $1.6 trillion in 2025.
  • Fast, cheap transport and instant communications.
  • Migration and tourism move people; global brands and media spread culture.
  • International organisations set rules, e.g. for trade.
Mission 3

Impacts on trade

  • World goods exports: $3.46 trillion (1990) → $24.62 trillion (2024).
  • World service exports: $0.88 trillion → $8.88 trillion.
  • + Cheaper goods, more choice, export jobs.
  • − Risk for countries that depend on a few exports: 95 of 142 developing countries were commodity-dependent (2019–21).
Mission 3

Impacts on transport

  • Air passengers: 310 million (1970) → 4,456 million (2019).
  • Container port traffic: 547 million TEU (2010) → 913 million TEU (2024). (TEU: one 20-foot container.)
  • + Goods and people move faster and more cheaply: places feel closer.
  • − More fuel burned and more emissions; busy hubs face congestion.
Quick check
?Container port traffic rose from 547.1 million TEU (2010) to 913.3 million TEU (2024). What was the percentage increase?
Mission 3

Impacts on communications and technology

  • Internet users: 15.6% of the world's people (2005) → 73.6% (2025).
  • ICT services rose from 5.3% of world service exports (2000) to 14.8% (2024).
  • + Firms run global supply chains online; services such as call centres and software move across borders.
  • − A digital divide: in 2025 about a quarter of people were still offline.
Mission 3

Impacts on culture

  • Global brands, films, music, food and sport spread worldwide.
  • + More choice and cultural exchange.
  • − Local languages, foods and traditions may be squeezed out: cultural homogenisation.
  • Many places adapt global ideas to local tastes rather than simply copying them.
Quick check
?A call centre for a UK bank opens in another country, linked by internet cables. Which impact of globalisation is this mainly?
Mission check

?Belgium ranks 3rd on the KOF index; China, a far bigger economy, ranks 82nd. Which is the best explanation?

KOF mostly measures flows relative to size (% of GDP). The Netherlands' trade is huge for its size; the USA's is huge in total but small compared with its vast home market. Globalisation is about connection and interdependence, not size.

Make a guess: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.2.4 role and global organisation of TNCs

Mission 4

Transnational corporations

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) is a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Its headquarters is in its home country; a country where it sets up is a host country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business abroad and runs it.
  • Outsourcing: a company pays another firm to do part of its work, such as making its products.
Mission 4

How TNCs organise themselves

  • Headquarters, design and research usually stay in the home country: Apple is based in Cupertino, California.
  • Making the product can happen anywhere. Apple's manufacturing is mostly done by outsourcing partners in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
Mission 4

Why locate in many countries?

  • Lower costs: Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs".
  • Near customers: in the year to March 2026 Toyota made 73.9% of the vehicles it sold outside Japan in factories outside Japan. Cars are big and heavy to ship.
  • Local supplies and money: buying parts locally matches local costs to local sales.
  • Avoiding trade barriers: Toyota notes a "significant increase" in US tariffs, including on cars, announced in 2025; making cars inside a market avoids them.
  • Government incentives such as tax breaks also attract TNCs.
Quick check
?Which is an example of FDI rather than outsourcing?
Mission 4

Apple in India: outsourcing

  • India is one of the main countries where Apple's outsourcing partners make its products.
  • The partner firms own and run the factories and employ the workers; Apple designs the products and sells them worldwide.
  • Apple says outsourcing lowers costs but "reduce[s] the Company's direct control over production".
  • Because partners work in six countries, production can shift between them.
Mission 4

Toyota in India: FDI

  • Toyota Kirloskar Motor has made cars in India since December 1999.
  • Models include the Innova, Fortuner and Urban Cruiser Hyryder, some as hybrids.
  • A Toyota group company, Toyota Kirloskar Auto Parts, has made axles and transmissions in India since 2002.
  • Toyota invests in its own factories and in nearby suppliers: its Just in Time system needs parts to arrive as they are needed.
Mission 4

FDI into India

  • Foreign direct investment into India (World Bank):
    • 2000: $3.6 billion · 2020: $64.4 billion (peak) · 2024: $27.1 billion · 2025: $40.8 billion
  • FDI grew hugely after 2000, but it rises and falls from year to year.
Mission 4

Advantages for the host country

  • Jobs and wages in factories, and in partner and supplier firms.
  • Skills and technology: workers learn new methods (for example Toyota's production system).
  • Multiplier effect: suppliers (such as parts makers) and local services grow too.
  • Exports and taxes: goods made in India are sold abroad, and firms pay tax.
Mission 4

Disadvantages for the host country

  • Profits flow back to the home country.
  • Top jobs (design, research, management) often stay at headquarters.
  • Footloose: contracts and investment can move. FDI into India fell by about 58% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Competition: local firms may struggle against large TNCs.
  • Working conditions and the environment depend on how well laws are enforced.
Quick check
?Why might outsourcing jobs be less secure for a host country than FDI factories?
Mission check

?Company P makes heavy washing machines. Company Q designs small wireless earbuds. Which strategies are they most likely to use?

Apple keeps design and services and outsources assembly to partners in Asia, including India: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, with its own factories in India since 1999, building cars near customers. Both bring jobs and skills to host countries, but profits and top jobs mostly stay at home, and contracts and investment can move.

Mission 5: A South Korean phone giant built huge factories in Vietnam. Who gains, and who carries the risk?

Make a guess: A South Korean phone giant built huge factories in Vietnam. Who gains, and who carries the risk?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.2.6 detailed example (named country or area): impacts of globalisation
  • 9.2.6 impacts of a named TNC located in that country or area
Mission 5

The named country: Vietnam

  • South-East Asia; upper-middle income.
  • Globalisation: countries becoming more connected through trade, investment, technology and people.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) into Vietnam: US$0.18 billion in 1990, US$22.0 billion in 2025.
Mission 5

Globalisation: trade and jobs

  • Exports grew from 36% of GDP in 1990 to 98% in 2025.
  • Jobs moved from farms to factories (ILO estimates):
    • agriculture: 64% of workers (2000) → 26% (2024)
    • industry: 12% → 34%
Mission 5

Globalisation: people's lives

  • GDP per person: US$404 (2000) → US$4,717 (2024), in current dollars.
  • People living on under $3 a day: 43.3% (2002) → 1.6% (2022).
  • Many things changed together (farming reforms, education, investment), so not all of this is due to globalisation.
Quick check
?Which statistic most directly measures how connected Vietnam's economy is to the rest of the world?
Mission 5

The named TNC: Samsung in Vietnam

  • Samsung Electronics: a South Korean transnational corporation (TNC).
  • In Vietnam:
    • Bắc Ninh (SEV): mobile devices, components, laptops, network equipment
    • Thái Nguyên (SEVT): mobile devices and components
    • Ho Chi Minh City (SEHC): consumer electronics
    • Hanoi: a research and development centre
Mission 5

Phones and Vietnam's exports

  • Telephones and parts made up 2.9% of Vietnam's exports in 2010, 21.2% in 2022 and 14.8% in 2023 (calculated from UN Comtrade).
  • Samsung's Bắc Ninh and Thái Nguyên factories both make mobile devices.
  • So one industry, led by foreign companies, became a big part of the economy.
Quick check
?Vietnam's exports of phones and parts fell from US$78.5 billion (2022) to US$52.1 billion (2023). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 5

Positive impacts of Samsung

  • Jobs and exports: factories in Bắc Ninh, Thái Nguyên and Ho Chi Minh City.
  • Local suppliers: since 2015, Samsung and Vietnam's Ministry of Industry and Trade have advised nearly 380 Vietnamese firms and trained over 400 improvement consultants.
  • Skills: a research and development centre in Hanoi.
  • Environment: Samsung reports 100% renewable electricity at its Vietnam sites, through certificates and solar deals: a rooftop solar deal at Bắc Ninh (2025) and a 28 MW solar plant under construction at its Ho Chi Minh City site.
Mission 5

Negative impacts and risks

  • Dependence: phone exports fell by about a third from 2022 to 2023. When world demand falls, Vietnam's exports and jobs fall too.
  • Profits go to the parent company and its shareholders abroad.
  • Control: decisions on where to invest are made at headquarters; a TNC can move production to another country.
  • Environment: two Vietnam sites are among Samsung's five highest-priority sites for nature-related risk, including water availability, tropical-storm exposure and pollution levels.
Quick check
?Which is a negative impact for Vietnam of relying on a TNC like Samsung?
Mission 5

The multiplier effect

  • A TNC factory creates jobs → workers spend wages → local shops and services grow → more taxes for schools and roads.
  • The effect is bigger if local firms supply parts. Samsung's supplier programme aims at this.
  • Samsung's view: as Vietnamese firms' skills rise, more can join global supply chains. This is the company's own claim.
Mission 5

Weighing up the impacts

  • Economic: exports, jobs, supplier training (+); dependence on one industry, profits leaving the country (−).
  • Social: higher incomes and skills (+); jobs at risk if production moves (−).
  • Environmental: renewable electricity (+); water and pollution risks at sites (−).
  • Exam move: for a named example, quote the place, the TNC and at least one figure for each impact.
Quick check
?Which policy would most help Vietnam keep more of the benefit from TNCs like Samsung?
Mission check

?In another country, one foreign TNC's factories make a third of all exports. World demand for its product then falls sharply. Who in that country is most exposed?

Vietnam gained investment, exports and jobs as it globalised: FDI rose from US$0.18 billion (1990) to US$22.0 billion (2025). But phone exports fell by about a third from 2022 to 2023: gains from a TNC come with dependence.

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.3.1 factors that have led to the growth of tourism as an industry
Mission 6

Tourism: a global industry

  • About 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals were recorded in 2025, almost 60 million more than in 2024.
  • The industry body WTTC estimates that travel and tourism made up 9.8% of the world economy in 2025 and supported 366 million jobs, about 1 in 9 jobs.
  • WTTC represents tourism businesses, and its figures include indirect effects, so treat them as an upper-end estimate.
Mission 6

More people can afford to travel

  • Rising incomes: more people have disposable income left after essentials.
  • About 3.2 billion people were in the global middle class in 2016 (a Brookings estimate), with growth expected to be "heavily concentrated in Asia".
  • More leisure time: paid holidays. China gave employees a legal right to paid annual leave from 1 January 2008: 5 to 15 days a year, depending on years worked.
  • In 2025, UK residents made an estimated 99.9 million visits abroad.
Mission 6

Travel has become easier

  • Transport: jet aircraft made long-haul trips fast; more routes and low-cost airlines made flying cheaper.
  • Technology: booking online. Booking Holdings' platforms recorded 1.2 billion room nights booked in 2025.
  • Marketing and media: films, TV and social media show places to millions of people.
  • Government support: airports, visas made easier, and national tourism campaigns.
Quick check
?Which change most directly gives more people the money to take holidays?
Mission 6

What attracts tourists?

  • Primary attractions were not made for tourists: climate, landscapes, wildlife, culture and heritage.
  • Secondary attractions were built for tourists: hotels, theme parks, transport, events and the marketing that sells them.
  • Most destinations need both: something to see, and a way to reach it and stay there.
Mission 6

Iceland's growth

  • Foreign visitors to Iceland (by air and sea):
    • 1950: 4,400 · 1980: 65,900 · 2000: 302,900 · 2010: 488,600
    • 2018: 2,342,200 · 2020: 489,900 · 2025: 2,297,121
  • Iceland is a high-income country (HIC), and its main attractions are rural and natural.
Quick check
?In 2025 Iceland had 2,297,121 foreign visitors and 394,324 residents on 1 January 2026. About how many foreign visitors were there for each resident?
Mission 6

Why Iceland grew

  • Primary attractions: volcanoes, glaciers, waterfalls, hot springs and the northern lights.
  • Publicity and marketing: after the Eyjafjallajökull eruption of April 2010, the "Inspired by Iceland" campaign asked Icelanders to share their stories on social media.
  • Its advertising agencies claimed it brought an extra £165 million to the economy. That claim comes from an advertising-awards case study, not an independent check.
  • Access: flights from source markets made the landscape reachable. Visitors rose almost fivefold from 2010 to 2018.
Mission 6

Growth can reverse

  • Iceland's visitors fell 14% in 2019 and 76% in 2020.
  • Tourism depends on air links, the economies of source countries and global events such as a pandemic.
  • Exam move: for "explain the growth of tourism", link a demand factor (income, paid holidays) to a supply factor (cheap flights, online booking, marketing), with a named place and dated figures.
Mission check

?A remote mountain valley with spectacular waterfalls had few visitors for decades. Then an airline opened direct flights from two large cities, and a popular film was shot there. Visitor numbers rose tenfold. Which explanation fits best?

Like Iceland, the valley had a primary attraction. Human factors made it reachable and known. Iceland's visitors grew almost five times from 2010 to 2018, to about 5.8 per resident in 2025; the data show the rise, not exactly how much each factor caused.

Mission 7

Mass tourism and carrying capacity

  • Mass tourism: very large numbers of tourists visiting the same places, often on package holidays.
  • Carrying capacity: the most visitors a place can take before unacceptable damage occurs.
  • Damage can be environmental (coral, wildlife, litter), cultural (residents pushed out, traditions sold as shows) or felt as overcrowding.
Mission 7

Impacts of mass tourism

  • Economic +: jobs and income. The industry body WTTC estimates tourism supported 366 million jobs worldwide in 2025. Economic −: low-paid seasonal work; profits leak abroad; rising prices for residents.
  • Cultural +: money to restore heritage; interest in local crafts. Cultural −: in Venice, UNESCO reports homes replaced by tourist accommodation.
  • Environmental +: entry fees can fund protected areas. Environmental −: Maya Bay's coral was damaged by thousands of daily visitors.
Quick check
?In a historic city, flats are turned into holiday lets and long-term residents move away. What kind of impact is this?
Mission 7

Maya Bay, Thailand: a tropical coast

  • A bay on the uninhabited island of Phi Phi Leh, in a national park in Krabi province.
  • It became world-famous after the 1999 film The Beach. At its peak in 2017, almost 5,000 people a day visited.
  • It closed in June 2018, "when it was determined the area's coral and other marine life needed time to recover" from the damage caused by thousands of daily tourists.
  • Thailand is upper-middle income.
Mission 7

Managing Maya Bay

  • Closure: announced for four months from 1 June 2018, then extended; a plan to reopen on 1 January 2022 came after about three and a half years.
  • A new jetty on the far side of the island: boats must land there, not in the bay. Experts advised this was the only way for the coral to keep recovering.
  • Controlled daily numbers, a marked swimming area, and coral and beach trees replanted.
  • Dispersal: Thailand's tourism authority promoted Krabi's other islands while the bay was shut.
Mission 7

Venice, Italy: an urban lagoon

  • UNESCO: "The exceptionally high tourism pressure on the city of Venice has resulted in a partial functional transformation".
  • About 53,000 residents lived in the historic centre in 2018; on average 66,800 visitors and tourists were there each day (year not stated).
  • That is about 1.26 visitors per resident every day. Day-trippers alone: almost 22 million a year.
  • Italy is high income.
Mission 7

Managing Venice

  • Cruise ships: UNESCO's World Heritage Committee urged Italy in 2014 to ban large ships from the lagoon. In 2021 the Italian government banned large cruise ships from the Venice Lagoon.
  • Access fee: on set dates in 2026, day visitors to the historic city had to pay a fee unless exempt. The 2026 trial ended; from 27 July the fee no longer applied.
  • Both aim to cut pressure on the lagoon and the city without stopping tourism.
Quick check
?Which measure most directly protected Maya Bay's coral after it reopened?
Mission 7

The Butler model (1980)

  • Richard Butler's model traces a resort through stages: exploration (a few visitors) → involvement (locals offer services) → development (outside investment, rapid growth) → consolidation (growth slows) → stagnation (peak numbers; capacity reached).
  • Then decline (falling numbers and quality) or rejuvenation (new attractions or reinvestment).
  • Visitor numbers are drawn on the y-axis, time on the x-axis: an S-shaped curve with branches at the end.
Mission 7

Butler: strengths and limitations

  • Strengths: simple; fits many seaside resorts; warns planners that growth past carrying capacity leads to decline; shows management can bring rejuvenation.
  • Limitations: real places rarely follow one smooth curve; shocks such as a pandemic break the pattern; stages are hard to spot at the time; it ignores places managed from the start.
  • Maya Bay shows management can interrupt the cycle: the site was closed, then reopened with limits on numbers.
Quick check
?A resort's visitor numbers have peaked. Its beaches are crowded, its hotels are ageing and it is no longer fashionable. Which Butler stage fits best?
Mission 7

Who can make tourism more sustainable?

  • Sustainable tourism takes "full account of its current and future economic, social and environmental impacts", meeting the needs of visitors, industry, environment and host communities (UN Tourism).
  • Individuals: follow site rules, keep off coral, visit out of season, use locally owned businesses.
  • Organisations: Thailand's national parks department and tourism authority (Maya Bay); UNESCO pressing Italy over cruise ships (Venice).
  • Governments: Italy's ban on large cruise ships (2021) and Venice's access fee (city council); Thailand closing the bay and limiting daily numbers.
Quick check
?Which of these is an action by individuals to make tourism more sustainable?
Mission 7

Evaluating strategies at different scales

  • Site (local): closure and a jetty let Maya Bay's coral and land ecosystems recover, but income from the site stopped while it was shut.
  • City: Venice's access fee raises money and may deter some day-trippers, but it was a trial and can exclude poorer visitors.
  • National: Italy's cruise-ship ban protects the lagoon, but moves ships and their spending elsewhere.
  • Global: UNESCO and UN Tourism set principles; they rely on governments to act.
  • Exam move: evaluate each strategy by who gains, who loses, and whether it protects the place long term.
Mission check

?A national park caps a famous mountain trail at a fixed number of walkers a day, even though many more want to come and would pay. Why would it turn paying visitors away?

Maya Bay passed its carrying capacity: almost 5,000 visitors a day in 2017 damaged its coral. Closing it, then reopening with a jetty and controlled daily numbers, let it recover. Venice faces the same pressure in a city, managed by a cruise-ship ban and an access fee.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Make a guess: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.3.3 benefits and problems caused by tourism, at a range of scales
Mission 8

A growing global industry

  • About 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals were recorded in 2025, almost 60 million more than in 2024.
  • The industry body WTTC estimates travel and tourism was 9.8% of the world economy in 2025 and supported 366 million jobs, about 1 in 9 jobs.
  • WTTC speaks for tourism businesses and counts indirect effects, so its totals are at the high end.
Mission 8

Benefits of tourism

  • Economy: jobs, foreign currency, taxes for the government, and new airports and roads.
  • Multiplier effect: wages and spending circulate locally, so each tourist's money supports more local jobs.
  • People and environment: money for services and conservation. In Rwanda, from 2017, 10% of national park tourism revenue was to be shared with communities next to the parks.
Mission 8

Problems of tourism

  • Leakage: money that leaves the destination: profits to foreign owners, imported food and fuel, commission to foreign booking sites.
  • Jobs: often seasonal, low-paid, and taken by migrant workers.
  • Environment: crowds damage fragile places. Thailand closed Maya Bay in 2018 so its coral could recover from damage caused by thousands of daily visitors.
  • People: crowding, rising prices, and culture turned into a product to sell.
Quick check
?Which is an example of leakage?
Mission 8

Case study: the Maldives

  • An upper-middle-income country of small islands in the Indian Ocean.
  • 2,246,516 tourist arrivals in 2025; tourism was 20.3% of GDP. The World Bank calls it "the largest economic sector".
  • 179 resorts, 16 hotels, 973 guest houses and 168 safari boats in 2025.
Mission 8

Enclave tourism

  • Enclave tourism: tourists stay inside a self-contained resort and spend almost all their money there.
  • Example: a resort on its own island, where guests sleep, eat, shop and take trips without leaving the resort.
  • At the end of 2022, resorts employed 55,874 people: 59% foreign workers and 41% Maldivian. Women were 11% of resort workers; Maldivian women 2%.
  • Consequences: high leakage, few links to local businesses, and few jobs for local people, especially women.
Mission 8

Over-dependence on tourism

  • When one industry dominates, a shock to tourism hits the whole economy.
  • Tourist arrivals in the Maldives: 1,702,887 in 2019; 555,494 in 2020.
  • The World Bank warns of "potential external shocks to tourism".
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, by what percentage did tourist arrivals in the Maldives fall from 2019 to 2020?
Mission 8

Benefits and problems at different scales

  • Global: 366 million jobs (WTTC's estimate), but heavy reliance on flights and on distant source markets.
  • National: Maldives tourism is 20.3% of GDP, but arrivals fell 67.4% in 2020.
  • Local: resort islands employ many foreign workers; guest-house islands keep more spending locally.
  • Exam move: for "at a range of scales", name the scale for each benefit or problem.
Quick check
?Which judgement about tourism in the Maldives is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?A luxury resort on a private island in a lower-income country is owned by a foreign hotel chain. It imports most of its food, recruits most staff from abroad, and its guests rarely leave the resort. Who gains most from each dollar a guest spends?

Several groups gain, unequally. In the Maldives, tourism is about a fifth of GDP, but 59% of resort jobs in 2022 went to foreign workers and only 2% to Maldivian women. Enclave tourism and leakage limit local gains; over-dependence adds risk.

Mission 9: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Make a guess: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 9.3.5 reasons for the growth of tourism in a named country
  • 9.3.5 benefits and problems of tourism there
  • 9.3.5 strategies to manage tourism sustainably there
Mission 9

Rwanda: a low-income country

  • The World Bank classes Rwanda as a low-income country. Income per person (GNI per capita) was US$1,150 in 2025.
  • Its mountain gorillas live in Volcanoes National Park, part of the Virunga Massif, shared with DR Congo and Uganda.
Mission 9

Why tourism grew

  • International arrivals rose from 667,000 in 2010 to 1,634,000 in 2019, about 2.5 times (our calculation).
  • Rare wildlife: mountain gorillas live in only a few protected forests.
  • Marketing: "Visit Rwanda" became Arsenal football club's first sleeve sponsor in 2018.
  • Conferences: business meetings and events earned US$94.7 million in 2025.
  • By 2018, the national parks were "attracting a record number of tourists".
Mission 9

Sustainable tourism and ecotourism

  • Sustainable tourism takes full account of its economic, social and environmental impacts, now and in the future, for visitors, businesses, the environment and host communities.
  • Ecotourism: "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education".
  • One of its principles: provide direct money for conservation.
  • Not every nature holiday is ecotourism.
Quick check
?Which trip best fits the definition of ecotourism?
Mission 9

High value, low volume

  • On 6 May 2017 the Rwanda Development Board (RDB) raised the gorilla permit from US$750 to US$1,500.
  • RDB called this "high-value, low-volume": fewer visitors, each paying more.
  • Visits are limited: at most 8 visitors per group, and one hour with the gorillas.
  • Visitors who also spent 3 days in Rwanda's other national parks got a 30% discount.
Mission 9

Sharing the money

  • Since 2005, a revenue-sharing scheme has given part of park income to communities living next to the parks.
  • In 2017 the communities' share rose from 5% to 10% of park tourism revenue.
  • Since 2005 it has funded nearly 1,300 projects worth more than 23 billion Rwandan francs: schools, health centres and water supplies.
Quick check
?Communities receive 10% of park tourism revenue. How much of one US$1,500 permit is that?
Mission 9

Protecting the environment

  • Mountain gorillas in the Virunga Massif rose from as few as 242 in 1981 to 480 in 2010 and 604 in 2016.
  • The world total was about 1,063 in the 2019 census results.
  • Tourism pays for protection, and gives local people jobs as rangers, wardens and porters, so they gain from the forest standing.
  • Careful: these counts come before the 2017 price rise. They show recovery under long-term protection, not the effect of the new price.
Mission 9

Closing the development gap?

  • Income: tourism earned US$685 million in 2025. In 2019 it brought in about 28% of Rwanda's export earnings.
  • Jobs: porters, rangers, wardens, guides, hotel and transport staff.
  • Multiplier effect: workers spend their wages locally, so shops and farms gain, creating more jobs.
  • Infrastructure and services: revenue sharing has built schools and health facilities.
  • The gap: Rwanda's income per person rose from US$580 (2010) to US$1,150 (2025); the world average rose from about US$9,410 to US$14,240. The world figure fell from about 16 times Rwanda's to about 12 times (our calculation), but the gap in dollars grew.
Quick check
?World income per person was about 16 times Rwanda's in 2010 and about 12 times in 2025, but the gap in dollars grew. Which conclusion fits?
Mission 9

Problems and trade-offs

  • Access: a foreign visitor's permit (US$1,500) costs more than Rwanda's average income per person (US$1,150). Rwandan and East African citizens pay a promotional US$200.
  • Wildlife: diseases caught from people remain a threat to gorillas.
  • Land: the park is being expanded; the first phase affects 547 households, who are being moved to a new village.
  • Dependence: tourism fell from about 28% of export earnings in 2019 to about 11% in 2020. A sector that depends on travel is easily hit by global shocks.
Mission check

?A national park in another low-income country plans to double its entry fee. Which plan is most likely to make its tourism more sustainable?

Rwanda chose high value, low volume: a US$1,500 permit, small groups and 10% of park revenue shared with neighbouring communities. Gorillas were already recovering under protection. Tourism earned US$685 million in 2025. The trade-offs: access, disease risk, relocation and dependence on travel.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sectors: primary (extract), secondary (make), tertiary (serve), quaternary (knowledge).
  • HICs (UK) have most workers in services; LICs (Ethiopia) in farming; MICs (India) in between.
  • Clark-Fisher: primary → secondary → tertiary as countries develop.

Mission 2

  • Location factors: land, labour, raw materials, energy, transport, markets, policy, technology, communications, containerisation; the most important depends on the sector.
  • Factors change: containerisation and electricity free many firms from raw materials and coalfields; grants and climate policy reshape heavy industry.

Mission 3

  • Globalisation: growing connections and interdependence through flows of goods, money, people and ideas.
  • Indices such as KOF measure it with economic, social and political variables, mostly relative to size.
  • It has changed trade, transport, communications, technology and culture, with gains and costs.

Mission 4

  • TNCs keep headquarters and design at home and spread production abroad, by outsourcing (Apple) or FDI (Toyota).
  • For India: jobs, skills, exports and suppliers; but profits leave, top jobs stay away, and FDI rises and falls.

Mission 5

  • Vietnam (globalisation): FDI US$0.18 bn → 22.0 bn (1990–2025); exports 36% → 98% of GDP; farm jobs 64% → 26%; extreme poverty 43.3% → 1.6% (2002–2022).
  • Samsung (TNC): factories in Bắc Ninh, Thái Nguyên and Ho Chi Minh City; positives (jobs, exports, supplier training, renewable power) and negatives (dependence: phone exports −34% in 2023; profits abroad; site water and pollution risks).

Mission 6

  • Tourism grew because more people have disposable income and paid holidays, and because cheaper, faster transport, online booking and marketing made places easier to reach and choose.
  • Iceland: a natural (primary) attraction, made accessible and promoted; visitors 488,600 (2010) → 2,297,121 (2025).

Mission 7

  • Mass tourism brings jobs and income but can damage environments and push out residents: positive and negative economic, cultural and environmental impacts.
  • Maya Bay (Thailand, upper-middle income; tropical coast and coral): closure, jetty, daily limits. Venice (Italy, high income; urban lagoon): cruise-ship ban, access fee.
  • Butler's model: exploration → involvement → development → consolidation → stagnation → decline or rejuvenation; useful, but real places and managed sites do not follow one curve.

Mission 8

  • Tourism brings jobs, foreign currency, taxes and infrastructure (benefits), but also leakage, low-paid seasonal work, environmental damage and crowding (problems).
  • Maldives: 20.3% of GDP; 59% of resort workers foreign in 2022; arrivals fell 67.4% in 2020.

Mission 9

  • Ecotourism protects nature, benefits local people and educates visitors.
  • Tourism brings income, jobs and services to an LIC, helping narrow the development gap in relative terms; access, land and dependence are the trade-offs.
