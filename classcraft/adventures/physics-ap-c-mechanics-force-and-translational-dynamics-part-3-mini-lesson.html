Mission 1

Mission 1: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Make a guess: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Mission 1

Period, frequency, speed

  • Period T: time for one full revolution (s).
  • Frequency f: revolutions per second (Hz). f = 1/T.
  • One revolution = one circumference, 2πr. Constant speed: v = 2πr/T.
Quick check
?A runner jogs at a constant speed around a circular track, completing each lap in time T. She then jogs around a second circular track with 4 times the radius of the first, at twice her original speed. By what factor is the time for one lap multiplied?
Mission 1

Steady speed, changing velocity

  • Velocity is a vector. Going around a circle, its direction keeps changing.
  • Changing velocity = acceleration, even at constant speed.
  • Draw v1 and v2 at two nearby points. The change, Δv = v2 − v1, points toward the center.
  • So the acceleration points to the center: centripetal acceleration.
  • It needs a net force toward the center.
Mission 1

Centripetal acceleration

  • ac = v²/r, toward the center. Using v = 2πr/T: ac = 4π²r/T².
  • Example: a child on a spinning playground ride, 1.5 m from the center, one revolution every 4.0 s.
  • v = 2πr/T = 2π × 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 2.4 m/s.
  • a = v²/r = 2.36² ÷ 1.5 = 3.7 m/s², toward the center.
Quick check
?Car X rounds a curve of radius 50 m and car Y rounds a curve of radius 100 m. Both cars move at the same constant speed. How does the magnitude of car X's centripetal acceleration compare with that of car Y?
Mission 1

Speeding up on a circle

  • Speed changing → an extra component along the path: tangential acceleration, at = rate of change of speed.
  • Direction changing → centripetal acceleration, ac = v²/r, toward the center.
  • The two are perpendicular. Net acceleration = their vector sum: a = √(ac² + at²).
  • Speeding up: a points ahead of the center. Slowing down: behind it.
  • Only at constant speed does the acceleration point exactly at the center.
Quick check
?A car moves around a circular track of radius 12 m. At one instant its speed is 6.0 m/s, and its speed is increasing at a rate of 4.0 m/s². What is the magnitude of the car's acceleration at that instant?
Mission check

?A rider on a large amusement ride moves at constant speed in a horizontal circle of radius 16 m. The ride completes 3 revolutions every 30 s. What is the magnitude of the rider's acceleration?

Constant speed is not constant velocity. The rider's direction keeps changing, so the rider accelerates toward the center of the circle, with ac = v²/r.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outward?

Make a guess: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outward?

Mission 2

Centripetal force is the net force

  • Circular motion at constant speed: acceleration v²/r toward the center.
  • Newton's second law: it needs a net force toward the center, often called the centripetal force.
  • Fnet = mac = mv²/r, toward the center.
  • It is perpendicular to the velocity: it changes the direction, not the speed.
  • It is not an extra force. It is the net force of real forces: tension, friction, gravity, normal force.
Mission 2

No outward force

  • Nothing pulls the ball outward. The string pulls it inward.
  • Cut the string: the ball flies off along the tangent, in a straight line (first law). Not outward along the radius.
  • The "flung outward" feeling in a turning car: your body keeps going straight while the car turns under you.
  • On a free-body diagram, never draw a "centripetal force" arrow or a "centrifugal force" arrow. Draw only real forces.
Quick check
?A puck of mass 0.30 kg slides on a frictionless horizontal table. It is tied to a string that passes through a small hole in the table and is attached to a block, which hangs at rest below the table. The puck moves in a circle of radius 0.40 m, centered on the hole, at a constant speed of 1.6 m/s. Use g = 10 m/s². What is the mass of the hanging block?
Quick check
?A car travels at constant speed around a curve on a flat horizontal road; its path is part of a horizontal circle. Air resistance is negligible. Which statement about the horizontal forces on the car is correct?
Mission 2

Worked example: a car on a flat bend

  • A 1200 kg car rounds a flat bend of radius 50 m at 15 m/s.
  • Only friction from the road acts sideways, so friction is the centripetal force.
  • F = mv²/r = 1200 × 15² ÷ 50 = 5400 N, toward the center.
  • If the greatest friction the tires can give is 7000 N: vmax = √(Fr/m) = √(7000 × 50 ÷ 1200) = 17 m/s.
  • Faster than that: friction cannot supply mv²/r, and the car skids toward the outside of the bend.
Mission 2

Vertical circles: top and bottom

  • Take toward the center as positive.
  • Bottom (center above): FT − Fg = mv²/r. Tension is more than the gravitational force.
  • Top (center below): FT + Fg = mv²/r. Tension is less than mv²/r.
  • Loop-the-loop, at the top: the track pushes down, FN + Fg = mv²/r.
  • Minimum speed at the top: FN = 0, so gravity alone gives mv²/r: mg = mv²/r → v = √(gr).
Quick check
?A toy car moves around the inside of a vertical circular loop of track with a diameter of 0.80 m. Friction is negligible. What is the minimum speed the car must have at the top of the loop to stay in contact with the track there? Use g = 10 m/s².
Mission 2

Banked curves and the conical pendulum

  • Banked curve, no friction: only FN and Fg act. The circle is horizontal, so resolve horizontally and vertically.
  • Horizontal: FN sin θ = mv²/r. Vertical: FN cos θ = mg.
  • Divide: tan θ = v²/(rg). One speed needs no friction.
  • Conical pendulum: string at angle θ to the vertical; ball in a horizontal circle.
  • Vertical: FT cos θ = mg. Horizontal: FT sin θ = mv²/r.
Quick check
?A road is banked at 37° to the horizontal on a curve, lower on the inside of the curve. A car travels around the curve in a horizontal circle of radius 120 m. At what speed can the car round the curve with no friction force exerted on it by the road? Use g = 10 m/s², sin 37° = 0.60, and cos 37° = 0.80.
Quick check
?A highway curve is banked so that a car moving at 25 m/s can round it with no friction force from the road. Another car rounds the same curve at a constant 15 m/s without sliding. Air resistance and rolling friction are negligible. Which statement describes the static friction force exerted by the road on this slower car?
Mission 2

Banked curves with friction

  • Slower than the no-friction speed: the car tends to slide down the bank. Static friction acts up the slope.
  • Faster: it tends to slide up. Static friction acts down the slope, adding to the inward force.
  • Greatest speed: friction down the slope at its maximum, f = μs FN.
  • Horizontal: FN sin θ + f cos θ = mv²/r. Vertical: FN cos θ − f sin θ = mg.
  • vmax² = rg(sin θ + μs cos θ)/(cos θ − μs sin θ).
Quick check
?A curve of radius 40 m is banked at 37° to the horizontal. The coefficient of static friction between a car's tires and the road is 0.25. Use g = 10 m/s². What is the greatest speed at which the car can round the curve without sliding?
Mission check

?A car drives at constant speed over the top of a rounded hill in the road. Near the top, the road's vertical cross-section is a circular arc. The car stays in contact with the road. At the top, how does the normal force from the road compare with the car's weight?

Nothing pulls the ball outward. The string's inward pull is the centripetal force; cut it and the ball flies off along the tangent.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Make a guess: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Mission 3

Gravity is the centripetal force

  • In a circular orbit, gravity is the only force on the satellite. It points to the center of the planet.
  • So gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • There is no outward "centrifugal" force. The satellite is in free fall all the way around.
  • m cancels: the orbit does not depend on the satellite's mass.
  • Orbital speed: v = √(GM/r). Smaller orbit → faster.
  • Satellite and planet pull each other equally hard, but the planet's huge mass makes its acceleration negligible. Treat it as fixed.
Quick check
?An astronaut floats inside a space station that moves in a circular orbit about 400 km above Earth's surface. Which statement about the forces exerted on the astronaut is correct?
Mission 3

Deriving T² ∝ r³

  • One orbit: distance 2πr in time T, so v = 2πr/T.
  • Substitute into GMm/r² = mv²/r:
  • GM/r² = 4π²r/T²
  • T² = (4π²/GM)r³
  • On the equation sheet: T² = (4π²/GM)R³, with R the orbit radius.
  • T² ∝ r³. The constant 4π²/GM depends only on the central mass.
  • Same law for moons of Jupiter or planets of another star; only the constant changes.
  • Double r: T² rises 2³ = 8 times, so T rises √8 ≈ 2.8 times.
Quick check
?Tethys and Rhea are moons of Saturn, and their orbits may be taken as circular. Tethys has an orbital radius of 2.95 × 10⁸ m and a period of 1.89 days. Rhea has an orbital radius of 5.27 × 10⁸ m. What is the period of Rhea?
Mission 3

Worked example: weighing the Earth

  • The Moon: r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, T = 27.3 days.
  • T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s.
  • Rearrange: M = 4π²r³/(GT²).
  • M = 4π² × (3.84 × 10⁸)³ ÷ (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.36 × 10⁶)²) = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Any orbiting body works: measure r and T (or v), get the central mass.
  • A graph of T² against r³ is a straight line through the origin; slope = 4π²/GM.
Mission check

?Two planets, P and Q, move in circular orbits around the same distant star. The orbital period of Q is 8.0 times the orbital period of P. What is the ratio (orbital radius of Q)/(orbital radius of P)?

T² ∝ r³ for anything orbiting the same central mass. Doubling the radius makes the period √8 ≈ 2.8 times longer.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A bat is thicker at one end. How do you find the exact point where it balances?

Make a guess: A bat is thicker at one end. How do you find the exact point where it balances?

Mission 4

From a sum to an integral

  • Point masses: xcm = Σmᵢxᵢ/Σmᵢ.
  • A continuous object: slice it into tiny elements dm, each at its own position x.
  • xcm = ∫x dm/∫dm. In 3D: rcm = ∫r dm/∫dm.
  • Rod: linear mass density λ = dm/dℓ, so dm = λ dx.
  • If m(ℓ) is the mass from one end up to ℓ, then λ = dm/dℓ, the slope of m(ℓ).
  • Uniform rod: λ = M/L. Nonuniform: λ(x) varies; the center of mass shifts toward the denser end.
Quick check
?A thin, nonuniform rod of length L and total mass M lies along an axis from ℓ = 0 to ℓ = L. The mass of the part of the rod between ℓ = 0 and any position ℓ is m(ℓ) = Mℓ³/L³. What is the linear mass density of the rod at position ℓ?
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Rod from x = 0 to L = 0.90 m; λ = λ0(1 + x/L), λ0 = 2.0 kg/m (2.0 kg/m at one end, 4.0 kg/m at the other).
  • Mass: M = ∫0ᴸ λ0(1 + x/L) dx = (3/2)λ0L = 2.7 kg.
  • Moment: ∫0ᴸ xλ0(1 + x/L) dx = λ0(L²/2 + L²/3) = (5/6)λ0L² = 1.35 kg·m.
  • xcm = 1.35 ÷ 2.7 = 0.50 m = 5L/9.
  • Past the middle (0.45 m), toward the denser end. Not at the dense end: the light end has mass too.
Quick check
?A thin rod lies along the x-axis, starting at x = 0. The graph shows the rod's linear mass density λ as a function of position x along its whole length. What is the x-coordinate of the rod's center of mass?
Mission 4

Mass from a density in 2D and 3D

  • M = ∫ρ dV (or ∫σ dA for a sheet, ∫λ dℓ for a rod).
  • Choose an element on which the density is constant:
  • Sphere with ρ(r): thin shell, dV = 4πr² dr.
  • Disk with σ(r): thin ring, dA = 2πr dr.
  • Density varying with height z: thin slice, dV = (area at z) dz.
  • Check units: the answer must come out in kg.
Quick check
?A solid sphere of radius R has a volume mass density that decreases from its center to its surface as ρ(r) = ρ0(1 − r²/R²), where r is the distance from the center and ρ0 is a positive constant. What is the total mass of the sphere?
Mission 4

Shapes by slicing

  • Symmetry first: the center of mass lies on every line of symmetry. Only integrate along the remaining axis.
  • Uniform right-angled triangular plate: apex at x = 0, vertical side of height h at x = b.
  • Strip at x: height hx/b, so dm = σ(hx/b) dx. More mass near the wide end.
  • xcm = ∫0ᵇ x · x dx ÷ ∫0ᵇ x dx = (b³/3) ÷ (b²/2) = 2b/3 from the apex.
  • Same method for any shape: pick strips, rings or slices whose position is a single coordinate.
Quick check
?A thin, uniform wire of linear mass density λ is bent into a semicircle of radius R, centered on the origin and lying in the region y ≥ 0 of the xy-plane. Where is the wire's center of mass?
Mission check

?Rods 1, 2 and 3 have the same length L and each lies along the x-axis from x = 0 to x = L. The graph shows the linear mass density λ of each rod as a function of x. x1, x2 and x3 are the x-coordinates of the rods' centers of mass. Which ranking is correct?

The balance point is the mass-weighted average position, ∫x dm/∫dm. A bat balances between its middle and its thick end: nearer the thick end the more its mass is concentrated there.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Drop a hammer and a feather where there is no air. Why do they hit the ground together?

Make a guess: Drop a hammer and a feather where there is no air. Why do they hit the ground together?

Mission 5

Two jobs for mass

  • Inertial mass: how strongly an object resists changes in its motion. a = Fnet/m.
  • Gravitational mass: how strongly it attracts, and is attracted by, other masses. Fg = Gm1m2/r², or Fg = mg near a planet.
  • Two different properties. Nothing in Newton's laws says they must be equal.
  • Inertia does not need gravity. A crate in deep space, far from any planet, is still hard to speed up.
  • A crate floating in a space station is in free fall: gravity still acts on it.
Quick check
?On the International Space Station, an astronaut has two closed toolboxes that look identical; one is full of tools and the other is empty. Both float freely. Which method would let her tell which toolbox is full?
Mission 5

Measuring each kind

  • Inertial: exert a known net force, measure the acceleration: m = Fnet/a.
  • Gravitational: an equal-arm balance compares the gravitational forces on two pans.
  • Balanced: mobject g = mstandard g, so the masses are equal, for any g.
Quick check
?On Earth, a rock on one pan of an equal-arm balance is balanced by 4.0 kg of standard masses on the other pan. The balance, the rock and the standard masses are taken to the Moon, where the gravitational field strength is 1.6 N/kg. Use g = 10 m/s² on Earth. What mass of standard masses balances the rock on the Moon?
Mission 5

Why everything falls together

  • Only gravity acts: a = Fg/minertial = (mgrav/minertial) × g.
  • If mgrav/minertial is the same for every object, every object falls with the same acceleration.
  • Tested for centuries, for many materials:
  • Galileo and Newton: pendulums of different materials.
  • Eötvös (early 1900s): torsion balance, equal to a few parts in a billion.
  • MICROSCOPE satellite (results 2022): equal to about 1 part in 10¹⁵.
  • So the two masses are experimentally equivalent. We use one word: mass.
Quick check
?Ball 1 has mass m and ball 2 has mass 3m. They are released from rest at the same height near Earth's surface, and air resistance is negligible. F1 and F2 are the magnitudes of the gravitational forces exerted on the balls by Earth, and a1 and a2 are the magnitudes of their accelerations. Which comparison is correct?
Mission 5

The equivalence principle

  • Closed room, no windows, at rest on Earth: scale reads mg; a dropped ball accelerates at 10 m/s² toward the floor.
  • Same room in a rocket far from any planet, accelerating at 10 m/s²: the floor pushes you, and rises to meet the ball. Same scale reading, same fall.
  • In free fall in a field, or floating in deep space: weightless either way.
  • Equivalence principle: in a noninertial (accelerating) frame, you cannot tell apparent weight from a gravitational force.
  • It works because inertial and gravitational mass are equivalent. Einstein built general relativity on it.
Quick check
?A student wakes in a closed, windowless room. A ball she releases accelerates toward the floor at 4 m/s², measured relative to the room, and a scale under her reads 40% of her weight on Earth. Which situation could explain both readings?
Mission check

?Suppose that for lead only, gravitational mass were 1% larger than inertial mass, while for wood the two were equal. A lead ball and a wooden ball are released together in a vacuum near Earth's surface. How would they fall?

The hammer feels a much bigger pull, but it has proportionally more inertia. Gravitational and inertial mass are equivalent, so the ratio, and the acceleration, is the same: the Apollo 15 hammer and feather landed together (1971).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Constant speed in a circle means acceleration toward the center: a = v²/r.
  • If the speed is changing too, add a tangential component; the net acceleration no longer points at the center.

Mission 2

  • The centripetal force is the net force of the real forces, toward the center: Fnet = mv²/r. There is no outward force.
  • Vertical circle: bottom FT − Fg = mv²/r; top FT + Fg = mv²/r; minimum speed at the top of a loop v = √(gr).
  • Banked curve with no friction: tan θ = v²/(rg).

Mission 3

  • Gravity alone provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so T² = (4π²/GM)r³; the satellite's mass cancels.
  • v = √(GM/r): lower orbits are faster.

Mission 4

  • rcm = ∫r dm/∫dm, with dm = λ dx (rod), σ dA (sheet) or ρ dV (solid); λ = dm/dℓ.
  • Choose elements of constant density (shells 4πr² dr, rings 2πr dr, slices); use symmetry to reduce to one integral.

Mission 5

  • Inertial mass resists changes in motion; gravitational mass sets the gravitational pull. Experiments show they are equal, so all objects fall with the same acceleration when only gravity acts.
  • Equivalence principle: inside an accelerating room you cannot tell apparent weight from gravity.
