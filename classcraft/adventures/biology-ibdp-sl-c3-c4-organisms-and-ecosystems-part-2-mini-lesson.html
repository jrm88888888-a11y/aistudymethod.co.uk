Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Make a guess: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Mission 1

Self, non-self and antigens

  • Every cell carries surface molecules, mostly proteins and glycoproteins, that identify it.
  • The immune system ignores self molecules and responds to non-self ones.
  • An antigen is a molecule that triggers an immune response, including antibody production.
  • Most antigens sit on the outer surface of pathogens. Antigens on red blood cells trigger antibodies if transfused into someone of a different blood group.
  • Say: "the antigen is recognised; the antibody is made against it" — not the other way round.
Quick check
?Which statement about antigens is correct?
Mission 1

One lymphocyte, one shape

  • Lymphocytes drive the adaptive (specific) response. They circulate in the blood and gather in lymph nodes.
  • Two kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells).
  • Each lymphocyte has receptors of one shape. Each B cell can make one type of antibody.
  • You have millions of different lymphocytes, made before any infection.
  • An antigen binds only the few with a complementary receptor. It selects them: clonal selection.
  • Say: "the receptor is complementary to the antigen" — not: "the same shape as the antigen".
  • Say: "the antigen selects a cell that already fits" — not: "the cell designs an antibody to fit".
Quick check
?Which statement about B-lymphocytes and antibodies is correct?
Mission 1

B cells: the antibody response

  • A B cell's receptor binds its complementary antigen. The B cell takes it in and displays it.
  • A T helper cell activated by the same antigen binds the B cell. Only now is the B cell activated.
  • The activated B cell divides by mitosis into a clone: clonal expansion.
  • Most become plasma cells: they secrete antibody. Every cell in the clone makes the same antibody.
  • Some become memory cells: long-lived and ready for next time.
Quick check
?In an experiment, B-lymphocytes specific to an antigen were cultured with that antigen and no other cells. No antibody was produced. When helper T-lymphocytes were added to the culture, antibody production began. What does this show?
Mission 1

Why the first response takes days

  • Only a few B cells fit any one antigen.
  • They must be activated, then divide many times, before there are enough plasma cells.
  • This takes days. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, and you feel ill.
  • Memory cells survive for years. Next time they divide at once: the pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms.
  • Immunity is the ability to eliminate an infectious disease. It comes from these memory cells.
Quick check
?After a person is exposed to a new pathogen for the first time, several days pass before antibodies against it can be detected in the blood. Which statement best explains this delay?
Mission check

?A child is born with no T lymphocytes. Her B lymphocytes are normal in number and type. Why does she make very little antibody against most pathogens?

The right cells already exist before infection. The antigen selects them, T helper cells switch them on, and mitosis builds the army.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A second infection with the same virus often passes without you noticing. What changed inside you?

Make a guess: A second infection with the same virus often passes without you noticing. What changed inside you?

Mission 2

First time slow, second time fast

  • Primary response (first exposure): few specific B cells must be selected, activated and cloned. Days pass; antibody peaks low. You may become ill.
  • Memory cells form and survive for years.
  • Secondary response (same antigen again): memory cells divide at once into plasma cells. Antibody rises sooner, much higher, and stays high longer.
  • The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms: you are immune.
  • Immunity is the ability to eliminate an infectious disease; it depends on memory cells.
  • Say: "memory cells persist" — not: "antibodies stay in the blood for life".

Mission check

?Illustrative data: a vaccine is given in two doses, one month apart. After dose 1, antibody rose slowly over two weeks to a low level. After dose 2, the same antibody rose within a few days to about ten times that level. What best explains the response to dose 2?

The second meeting is faster because memory cells are waiting, not antibodies. That is how a second infection, or a second vaccine dose, works.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Mission 3

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Protection lasts only while the pathogen's antigens stay the same. Flu antigens change, so its vaccine is remade each year.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 3

Herd immunity

  • A pathogen spreads only when an infected person passes it to someone susceptible.
  • Vaccines protect the individual and, at high coverage, the population.
  • When a high enough percentage is immune, most contacts are immune: chains of transmission break.
  • This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns, people with weakened immune systems.
  • More infectious diseases need higher coverage: about 95% for measles.
  • If coverage falls below that level, outbreaks return.
  • Members of a population are interdependent in building herd immunity.
Quick check
?For measles, transmission in a population is greatly impeded once about 95% of people are immune. A community's vaccination coverage falls from 96% to 85%. Which statement correctly describes a consequence?
Mission 3

Worked example: percentage change and difference

  • Percentage change compares a value over time: (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Illustrative data: weekly cases fall from 2500 to 1500: (1500 − 2500) ÷ 2500 × 100 = −40%, a fall of 40%.
  • Percentage difference compares two groups, using their mean as the reference: difference ÷ mean × 100.
  • Two regions: 300 and 500 cases per 100 000. Difference 200; mean 400. 200 ÷ 400 × 100 = 50%.
  • Change: divide by the initial value. Difference: divide by the mean.

Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) In a country, the number of new COVID-19 cases recorded per day fell from 2000 in one week to 1600 in the following week. What is the percentage change in the daily number of cases?
Mission 3

Ethics: the arguments

  • Ethics questions have arguments on several sides. Know them; weigh them.
  • Testing: vaccines are tested on animals, then on volunteers. How much risk is acceptable, and is consent fully informed?
  • Placebos: is it right to give a placebo when a vaccine might protect?
  • Side effects: serious ones are rare but not nil. The disease usually carries far greater risk. How should a small individual risk be weighed against benefit to the population?
  • Choice: should vaccination be compulsory to reach herd immunity, or an individual decision?
  • Fairness: who is vaccinated first when supply is short? Should richer countries fund vaccines for poorer ones?
  • Scientists publish so others can evaluate their work. News reports often appear before that evaluation is complete.
Quick check
?A news article reports a newly published study claiming that a vaccine causes a rare side effect, before other scientists have evaluated the study. Which response shows the best understanding of how science works?
Mission check

?Rubella is usually mild in children, but infection early in pregnancy can seriously harm the fetus. Many countries vaccinate all children, boys and girls. How does vaccinating children protect pregnant women who are not immune?

A vaccine protects the person who receives it and, at high coverage, the people around them.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Make a guess: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Mission 4

Atoms go round; energy flows through

  • Every element an organism needs is recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the rest.
  • Energy is not recycled. It leaves each trophic level as heat.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and wastes. They return elements as simple inorganic ions that plants can absorb.
  • Decomposers (saprotrophs) play the key role: without them, elements stay locked in dead matter.
Quick check
?Which statement about the recycling of chemical elements in ecosystems is correct?
Mission 4

Partners that feed plants

  • Mutualism: a relationship between two species in which both benefit.
  • Plants cannot use the N2 in air. They absorb only fixed nitrogen: ammonium or nitrate ions.
  • Root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover: the Fabaceae): Rhizobium bacteria fix N2. Plant gets ammonia / amino acids; bacteria get sugars and shelter.
  • Mycorrhizae: fungi living with plant roots. Hyphae spread far into the soil: a huge surface area for absorbing water and inorganic ions, especially phosphate. The plant supplies sugars.
  • Orchids: seeds have almost no food store. The fungus supplies the seedling with carbon compounds and ions; the green adult orchid can supply the fungus with sugars.
  • Hard corals: zooxanthellae (algae) in the polyp's cells photosynthesise and pass on sugars and oxygen; the coral gives shelter in sunlit water, CO2 and nitrogen-containing wastes.
  • Say: "bacteria in the nodules fix nitrogen" — not: "legumes fix nitrogen themselves".
Quick check
?What are the benefits to each partner in the relationship between a legume and the bacteria in its root nodules?
Mission 4

Leaching and eutrophication

  • Leaching: rain washes soluble nitrate and phosphate out of the soil into rivers and lakes.
  • The nutrient no longer limits growth: algal bloom at the surface.
  • Algae block light; plants below die. Algae die too.
  • Aerobic bacteria (decomposers) multiply and decompose the dead matter.
  • Their respiration raises BOD (biochemical oxygen demand) and lowers dissolved oxygen.
  • Fish and other aerobic animals die.
  • Say: "BOD rises, so dissolved oxygen falls" — not: "BOD falls" or "the fertiliser poisons the fish".
  • BOD: the oxygen used by microorganisms in a water sample kept in the dark over a set time (usually 5 days at 20 °C).

Mission check

?A farmer grows clover, a legume, in a field for a year, then ploughs the clover into the soil. Wheat sown next needs much less nitrogen fertiliser than wheat sown after wheat. Which explanation is correct?

Air is full of nitrogen, but plants can use it only after microorganisms have converted it. That is why crops run short.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Antigens select the few lymphocytes with complementary receptors; activated B cells form clones of plasma cells that all make one antibody, plus memory cells.

Mission 2

  • Memory cells make the secondary response sooner, larger and longer: that is immunity.

Mission 3

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • High coverage gives herd immunity, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.

Mission 4

  • Elements are recycled by microorganisms; energy is not. Plants cannot use N2 until bacteria fix it.
  • Leached nutrients → algal bloom → decomposition → BOD up, oxygen down → fish die.
