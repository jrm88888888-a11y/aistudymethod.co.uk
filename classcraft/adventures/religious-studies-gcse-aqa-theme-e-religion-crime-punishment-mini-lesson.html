Theme E: Religion, Crime & Punishment

This mini-lesson works through the AQA GCSE theme Religion, Crime and Punishment: good and evil, the reasons people commit crime, the three aims of punishment (retribution, deterrence, reformation) plus protection, the central place of forgiveness, the treatment of criminals, and the debate over the death penalty.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Christian, Muslim and non-religious views are all shown accurately — and where believers disagree, that range is shown too. Press Start when you're ready.

Good & evil

Good and evil intentions and actions

An action is what a person does; an intention is why they do it. Both matter when we judge whether something is good or evil. A crime usually joins a wrong action to a wrong intention.

  • Evil can be seen as the absence or opposite of good — deliberately causing harm or suffering.
  • Some evil is moral (caused by human choices, e.g. murder); some suffering is called natural evil (e.g. disease).
  • Believers ask a hard question: can it ever be good to cause suffering?

A live debate: most Christians and Muslims say causing suffering is only ever justified for a greater good — such as a punishment that protects others or helps an offender change. Causing suffering for its own sake, or out of cruelty, is always wrong.

Reasons for crime

Why do people commit crime?

The exam asks you to explain the causes of crime and how religions respond. People break the law for many reasons — and understanding the cause does not mean excusing the crime.

  • Poverty and greed — needing, or simply wanting, money.
  • Upbringing and environment — growing up around crime.
  • Mental illness and addiction — reduced control over choices.
  • Hate — including hate crimes targeting a person's race, religion or identity.
  • Opposition to an unjust law — sometimes people break a law they believe is deeply wrong.

Religious attitudes: Christianity and Islam both teach that lawbreaking is serious, but that offenders are still human beings loved by God who can repent and change. Some crimes (e.g. murder, hate crime) are seen as especially grave, yet the person is never beyond forgiveness.

The aims of punishment

Three aims — plus protection

Punishment can have several purposes. The three you must know are retribution, deterrence and reformation; protection is often added as a fourth.

  • Retribution — the offender gets their "just deserts": they pay for the crime. Linked to "an eye for an eye" (Exodus 21:24).
  • Deterrence — the punishment puts people off committing crime, both the offender and others.
  • Reformation — changing the offender for the better so they turn away from crime.
  • Protection — keeping society safe from the offender (e.g. by imprisonment).
Careful: "an eye for an eye" (retribution) is tempered for many Christians by Jesus' teaching to "turn the other cheek" (Matthew 5:39). Christianity generally leans towards justice with mercy and reform.
Religious views

Justice, mercy and forgiveness

Both religions value justice — but stress that it must be joined to mercy and the chance to reform.

Christianity: justice is tempered by forgiveness and mercy. Jesus said, "Let anyone among you who is without sin be the first to throw a stone" (John 8:7), and told Peter to forgive "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22). Many Christians therefore emphasise reformation over harsh retribution.
Islam: Islam strongly values justice. Shari'ah includes some fixed punishments, yet the Qur'an repeatedly stresses mercy, forgiveness and repentance — a wrongdoer who genuinely repents can be forgiven, and forgiveness is described as better than revenge.

Diversity: there is a range of views within each religion. Not every Christian or Muslim thinks the same — some emphasise strict justice, others mercy and reform.

Forgiveness

The importance of forgiveness

Forgiveness — showing grace and mercy, and pardoning someone for what they have done wrong — is central to both Christianity and Islam.

  • Christianity: Jesus taught his followers to forgive again and again ("seventy times seven", Matthew 18:21–22) and forgave those crucifying him.
  • Islam: Allah is Al-Ghaffur, "the Ever-Forgiving"; Muslims are encouraged to forgive, and repentance (tawbah) opens the way to Allah's mercy.
Key point: forgiving someone does not always mean removing all consequences. A person can be forgiven and still face a fair punishment — mercy and justice can go together.

Treatment of criminals

Prison, community service & dignity

Societies punish and manage offenders in different ways. You should be able to discuss these and how religious ideas of human dignity and rehabilitation apply.

  • Prison — protects the public and can deter, but can also be a place to help offenders reform.
  • Corporal punishment — causing physical pain as a punishment; widely opposed in the UK, though used in some countries.
  • Community service — unpaid work for the community, giving offenders a chance to make amends.
Religious view: because humans are made in the image of God, many Christians and Muslims say criminals must be treated with dignity and given the chance to rehabilitate — punishment should aim to reform, not simply to degrade.
The death penalty

Capital punishment: a range of views

The death penalty (capital punishment) is executing someone for a serious crime. Religious believers disagree about it — so you must show the range, not label a whole religion "for" or "against".

Arguments FOR

Retribution (a life for a life); deterrence (it may put people off); protection (the offender can never harm again).

Arguments AGAINST

The sanctity of life (life is God-given and precious); the risk of executing an innocent person; it gives no chance to reform; and it does not clearly deter.

Religious diversity: some Christians and Muslims accept capital punishment in principle (it appears in some readings of scripture and Shari'ah). Many others oppose it — including the modern teaching of the Roman Catholic Church — stressing the sanctity of life, mercy and the hope of reform.

Sanctity of life & "an eye for an eye"

Two ideas pulling on the debate

Two big ideas shape how believers weigh up punishment, especially the death penalty.

Sanctity of life: life is holy and God-given. Because God gives life, many believers argue only God should take it — a key argument against the death penalty and for treating every offender as valuable.
"An eye for an eye": from Exodus 21:24, this expresses retribution — a punishment that fits the crime. Originally it also limited revenge to being no worse than the crime. Many Christians read it alongside Jesus' call to "turn the other cheek", favouring mercy.

Both religions use the idea of the sanctity of life, and both hold justice and mercy in tension — which is why sincere believers reach different conclusions.

Recap

The ideas to know

Good & evil: actions + intentions both matter; causing suffering needs a genuine justification.

Reasons for crime: poverty, greed, upbringing, mental illness, addiction, hate, opposition to unjust law.

Aims of punishment: retribution (just deserts), deterrence (put people off), reformation (change the offender) — plus protection.

Religious views: Christianity — justice tempered by mercy and forgiveness; Islam — justice with mercy, forgiveness and repentance.

Forgiveness: central to both faiths, but does not always remove every consequence.

Treatment of criminals: prison, community service, corporal punishment; dignity and rehabilitation.

Death penalty: a range of views — some believers accept it, many (incl. modern Catholic teaching) oppose it on sanctity of life, mercy and reform.
