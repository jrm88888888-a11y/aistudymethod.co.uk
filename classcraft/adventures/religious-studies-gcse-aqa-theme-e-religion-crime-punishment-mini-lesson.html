Mission 1

Mission 1: Two people do the same thing for opposite reasons. Can one be right and the other wrong?

Make a guess: Two people do the same thing for opposite reasons. Can one be right and the other wrong?

Mission 1

Good, evil, sin, crime

  • Morality: ideas of right and wrong that guide how people act.
  • Good: what is right, kind and beneficial. Evil: what is wrong, harmful or against God's will.
  • Sin: an act (or thought) against God's will or religious law. A religious idea.
  • Crime: an act against the law of the state, which the state can punish.
  • Some acts are both (murder, theft). Some sins are not crimes (most lies, greed). Some crimes are not seen as sins by everyone (breaking an unjust law in protest).
Mission 1

Absolute or relative?

  • Absolute morality: some acts are always right or wrong, whatever the situation or culture. "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • Relative morality: what is right depends on the situation, culture or consequences.
  • Absolutists value clear, fair rules; relativists value flexibility and compassion.
  • Many religious believers hold both: some absolute rules, with care for each situation.
Mission 1

Intentions and actions

  • An intention is the reason or aim behind an action.
  • The same act can have good or evil intentions: a gift given out of love, or to bribe.
  • Christianity: "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7)
  • Islam: the Prophet taught that actions are judged by their intentions (niyyah) (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: actions are called skilful or unskilful, not sinful; the Buddha taught that intention (cetana) is what makes karma. "If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him" (Dhammapada 1, Müller).
  • Most traditions judge both: a good intention does not make every act right.

Mission 1

Can causing suffering ever be good?

  • Sometimes suffering is caused for good ends: surgery, a vaccine injection, punishment to protect society.
  • Joseph tells his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20).
  • But: causing suffering for pleasure, revenge or profit is evil in every tradition.
Mission 1Evaluate

"It can never be good to cause suffering."

  • Reasons for: suffering is the clearest sign of harm; Buddhists and Hindus value ahimsa (non-violence); Jesus taught love of enemies; once one hurt is justified, others may follow.
  • Reasons against: surgery and medicine cause pain to heal; just punishment protects others; Romans 5:3–4: suffering can build character; some believe God brings good out of evil (Genesis 50:20).
  • Judge it by: Does the intention matter more than the result? Is the suffering chosen by the person (as in surgery) or imposed? Is there another way to reach the good?

Religions judge both what we do and why we do it; good results do not make every motive good.

Mission 2: Why do people commit crimes, and is breaking the law always the same as doing wrong?

Mission 2

Crime and sin

  • A crime breaks the law of the state. The state punishes it (courts, fines, prison).
  • A sin breaks God's law or a religious teaching. It damages a person's relationship with God.
  • Many acts are both: murder and theft are crimes and sins ("You shall not murder", "You shall not steal": Exodus 20:13; Exodus 20:15).
  • Some sins are not crimes in the UK: e.g. lying to a friend, adultery, greed.
  • Some crimes may not be sins: e.g. breaking an unjust law out of conscience.
Mission 2

Why do people commit crime?

  • Poverty: stealing to survive or to feed a family.
  • Upbringing: parents or friends who see crime as normal; lack of care or guidance.
  • Mental health problems: an illness may affect judgement.
  • Addiction: to drugs, alcohol or gambling; crime pays for the habit, or happens under the influence.
  • Greed: wanting more than you need (fraud, theft of luxuries).
  • Hate: anger at a person or a group; it can lead to violence.
  • Opposition to an unjust law: breaking a law as a protest (civil disobedience).

Mission 2

Views about people who break the law

  • Most religious believers: condemn the crime, not the person. Every offender is still made in God's image, or has the same worth as anyone.
  • Causes such as poverty, addiction or mental illness call for compassion and help, though most believers say people remain responsible for free choices.
  • Greed and hate: harder to excuse; they come from the person's own choices, and many faiths name them as roots of wrongdoing.
  • Unjust law: Christians are taught to respect the state ("Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities", Romans 13:1). But "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
  • Martin Luther King (1929–1968), a Baptist minister, broke segregation laws peacefully and accepted the penalty; many Christians see this as right.
  • Humanists: look at causes and evidence; tackle poverty, addiction and mental illness; protest against unjust laws through peaceful, democratic means where possible.
Mission 2

Types of crime: hate crime, theft, murder

  • Hate crime: a crime motivated by hostility to someone's race, religion, sexual orientation, disability or transgender identity. Most believers condemn it: all people are equal before God (Genesis 1:27); "love your neighbour as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18).
  • Theft: taking property without permission. Forbidden in every major faith ("You shall not steal", Exodus 20:15). Believers ask why it happened: theft from need may be met with help as well as punishment.
  • Murder: the deliberate, unlawful killing of a person. Seen as one of the most serious crimes and sins: it takes a life that belongs to God (sanctity of life; Exodus 20:13).
  • Your spec asks for views about each type: be ready to explain why believers see them as wrong, and how they would respond to the offender.

  • Buddhism: crime grows from the three poisons: greed, hatred, ignorance.
  • Hinduism: crime breaks dharma (duty) and brings bad karma.
  • Sikhism: the Five Thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride) lead people away from God and into wrongdoing.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Someone who steals because they are poor should not be punished."

  • Reasons for: poverty limits real choice. Society shares the blame for poverty. Christians: Jesus showed mercy (John 8:11). Help with the cause (food, work) may stop it happening again.
  • Reasons against: theft still harms a victim, who deserves justice. Most poor people do not steal, so it is still a choice. The commandment "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15) makes no exception for need. Excusing it may encourage more crime.
  • Middle positions: a lighter or different sentence (community service, support) that takes need into account.
  • Judge it by: Is the person responsible? What does the victim deserve? Which response best prevents future crime?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two religious beliefs about people who commit crimes because of poverty.

  • Belief 1: Condemn the crime, not the person. Source: Jesus told the woman, "Neither do I condemn you … sin no more" (John 8:11). Development: so Christians offer offenders mercy and a fresh start, while still saying the act was wrong.
  • Belief 2: Society must tackle the cause. Source: "Allah enjoineth justice and kindness, and giving to kinsfolk" (Qur'an 16:90). Development: so Muslims give zakah and support projects that reduce poverty, the cause of the crime.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When someone breaks the law, what should punishment actually achieve?

Make a guess: When someone breaks the law, what should punishment actually achieve?

Mission 3

What punishment is for

  • Punishment: a penalty given by a lawful authority (a court, a judge) to someone who has broken the law.
  • It is not revenge: it follows fair rules, and fits the crime.
  • Why society needs it: to keep order, to protect people, and to show that laws matter.
  • Why it can be justice: the offender is treated fairly, and the victim's harm is recognised.
  • Religions agree that wrongdoing has consequences. They differ on what punishment should mainly aim to do.
Mission 3

Retribution, deterrence, reformation

  • Retribution: the offender gets what they deserve; the punishment fits the crime. Looks back at the crime.
  • Deterrence: punishment puts the offender, and others, off committing crimes. Looks forward.
  • Reformation: helping the offender change, so they become a law-abiding member of society (education, courses, counselling). Looks forward.
Quick check
?A judge says: "This sentence must send a clear message to anyone else thinking of carrying a knife." Which aim is the judge stressing?
Mission 3

Strengths and weaknesses

  • Retribution: strength: fair to victims; the punishment fits the crime. Weakness: can look like revenge; does not stop future crime.
  • Deterrence: strength: may prevent crime before it happens. Weakness: many crimes are done in anger, under the influence, or by people who expect not to be caught; punishing one person to warn others can seem unfair.
  • Reformation: strength: the offender may change, so less reoffending; respects human worth. Weakness: not every offender wants to change; victims may feel it is too soft.
Mission 3Read the source

Christian teachings on punishment

"eye for eye, tooth for tooth, hand for hand, foot for foot" (Exodus 21:24)

"he is a servant of God, an avenger for wrath to him who does evil." (Romans 13:4)

"Brothers, even if a man is caught in some fault, you who are spiritual must restore such a one in a spirit of gentleness" (Galatians 6:1)

  • Exodus 21:24 limits punishment to what fits the crime: retribution in proportion, not unlimited revenge.
  • Romans 13:4: the state has God-given authority to punish (retribution, deterrence).

Mission 3Diversity

Christian views on the aims

  • All Christians: the state may punish (Romans 13); punishment must be just and respect the offender's dignity.
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism 2266, paraphrase): punishment first redresses the disorder the crime caused (retribution), and should also protect public safety and help correct the offender.
  • Many Christians stress reformation: Jesus came to call sinners to repentance; no one is beyond change.
  • Some, often more conservative, Christians give more weight to retribution and deterrence (Exodus 21:24; Romans 13:4).
  • Quakers and many others: punishment should heal; they support reform and restorative justice (see Mission 5).

Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Shari'ah law sets out punishments. Hadd (fixed) penalties for certain crimes; qisas (equal retaliation) for murder and injury; other penalties left to the judge.
  • "Retaliation is prescribed for you in the matter of the murdered" (Qur'an 2:178, Pickthall); the victim's family may forgive and accept compensation: "an alleviation and a mercy from your Lord".
  • "And there is life for you in retaliation" (Qur'an 2:179, Pickthall): deterrence protects society.
  • Divergent views: many Muslims support Shari'ah penalties in an Islamic state with strict rules of evidence; many British Muslims accept UK law and stress mercy and reform; some scholars call for these penalties to be suspended today.
  • Judaism: "breach for breach, eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (Leviticus 24:20, JPS 1917). The rabbis taught that this means payment of compensation, not injury (Talmud, Bava Kamma 83b–84a): reparation in proportion.
  • Teshuvah (repentance, "returning"): every offender can change.
Mission 3Diversity

Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism

  • Buddhism: punishment should be compassionate (karuna) and aim at reform; violence breeds more hatred.
  • Hinduism: danda ("the rod"): the ruler's duty to punish justly to protect dharma.
  • Sikhism: justice and reform together.
Mission 3

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: no afterlife or divine judgement, so punishment should do good in this life: protect the public, reform offenders, reduce crime.
  • Many stress evidence: what actually lowers reoffending (education, treatment for addiction, restorative justice).
  • Utilitarian thinkers: punishment is justified only by its good results (deterrence, protection, reform).
  • Other non-religious thinkers defend retribution: offenders deserve a penalty that fits the crime, whatever the results.
  • Religious responses: many agree; some add that justice also matters before God.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Reformation is the most important aim of punishment."

  • Reasons for: offenders can change; Galatians 6:1: restore the wrongdoer. It protects society in the long run. It respects human worth.
  • Jewish teaching on teshuvah (repentance) holds that every offender can change.
  • Reasons against: victims deserve justice (retribution; Exodus 21:24). Some offenders will not change, so the public needs protection. Without deterrence, crime may rise. Romans 13:4 gives the state a duty to punish.
  • Another view: the aims work together: a just sentence can punish, protect and reform at once (Catholic teaching).
  • Judge it by: What does the victim need? Can the offender change? What best reduces future crime?

Mission 4

Mission 4: Should prisoners be treated well, even when they have done terrible things?

Make a guess: Should prisoners be treated well, even when they have done terrible things?

Mission 4

Prison

  • Prison: taking away an offender's liberty for a set time.
  • Aims served: protection (keeps dangerous people off the streets), retribution, deterrence, and, with education and courses, reformation.
  • Criticisms: overcrowding; drugs and violence inside; prisoners may learn more crime; families suffer; many people reoffend after release.
  • Most religious believers accept prison but say prisoners must be treated with dignity and given the chance to change.
  • Humanists agree: prison should be humane and focused on rehabilitation.
Mission 4

Community service and other sentences

  • Community service (a community order with unpaid work): the offender does useful work in the area, e.g. clearing litter, repairing buildings. Used for less serious crimes.
  • Strengths: the offender repays the community (reparation); keeps work and family; avoids learning crime in prison; cheaper.
  • Weaknesses: may seem too soft to victims; does not protect the public from dangerous offenders.
  • Many Christians and Humanists support it: it helps reform and repair.
Quick check
?A 19-year-old is caught spraying graffiti on a community centre for the first time. Many Christians would support community service rather than prison. Which reason fits that view best?
Mission 4

Corporal punishment

  • Corporal punishment: punishing someone by causing physical pain, e.g. caning, flogging.
  • Abolished as a court sentence in England and Wales in 1948 (Scotland 1949); banned in all UK schools.
  • Still used as a legal punishment in some countries, e.g. caning in Singapore; flogging in some states that apply Shari'ah law.
  • For: quick; may deter; cheaper than prison; the offender can carry on working.
  • Against: violates human dignity; can cause lasting harm; does not reform; may teach that violence solves problems; it is banned by human rights law in the UK.
Mission 4Diversity

Different beliefs on corporal punishment

  • Most Christians oppose it: every person is made in God's image; Jesus taught non-violence (Matthew 5:39) and "whatever you desire for men to do to you, you shall also do to them" (Matthew 7:12); it does not reform.
  • Some Christians have quoted "One who spares the rod hates his son" (Proverbs 13:24); most today read it as about loving discipline, not beating, and not about criminal law.
  • Islam: the Qur'an and Shari'ah set lashes for some crimes (e.g. Qur'an 24:2). Many Muslims accept this where an Islamic court applies strict rules of evidence; many others say it should not be applied today or outside such a system. British Muslims follow UK law.
  • Judaism: the Torah allowed lashes, limited to forty so the person is not degraded (Deuteronomy 25:3). Jewish courts have not used them for many centuries.
  • Buddhism: most oppose: it breaks ahimsa (non-harm) and the First Precept. Hinduism and Sikhism: many oppose because of ahimsa and human dignity; some accept firm punishment (danda) as the ruler's duty.

Mission 4

Prison reformers and chaplains

  • John Howard (1726–1790), The State of the Prisons (1777): inspected prisons and campaigned for clean, safe, healthy conditions.
  • Elizabeth Fry (1780–1845), a Quaker: visited women in Newgate Prison from 1813; set up schooling, sewing work and Bible reading; campaigned for reform.
  • Prison chaplains: faith leaders in every UK prison; they support prisoners of all faiths and none, lead worship, and help with grief, guilt and release.
  • Prison Fellowship (Christian): volunteers visit, pray with prisoners and run courses such as Sycamore Tree (offenders learn about the harm done to victims).
Mission 4Evaluate

"Prison is the best way to treat criminals."

  • Reasons for: protects the public; serious crimes deserve serious punishment (retribution); may deter; can offer education and courses that reform.
  • Reasons against: many reoffend after release; prisoners may learn crime; community service and restorative justice may reform better and repair harm; religious teaching stresses reform and dignity (Matthew 25:36; Galatians 6:1).
  • Judge it by: Which crimes? Which aim matters most? What does the evidence on reoffending show?

Mission 5

Forgiveness, repentance, reconciliation

  • Forgiveness: choosing to let go of anger and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you.
  • It is not pretending the wrong did not happen, and not saying it was acceptable.
  • Repentance: being truly sorry for wrongdoing and turning away from it (metanoia in Christianity; teshuvah in Judaism; tawbah in Islam).
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so both sides can live in peace again.
Mission 5Read the source

Jesus on forgiveness

"Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I don't tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven." (Matthew 18:21–22)

"For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if you don't forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." (Matthew 6:14–15)

  • On the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34)

Mission 5

Forgiveness and punishment

  • The tension: if Christians must forgive without limit, should offenders be punished at all?
  • Most Christians answer: forgiveness is personal; justice is the state's duty (Romans 13:4). A victim can forgive and still want a fair sentence.
  • Punishment can itself serve the offender's good: it helps them face the wrong and change.
  • Example: Pope John Paul II was shot in 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca. In 1983 he visited Ağca in prison and forgave him, yet Ağca stayed in prison.
  • Some argue the other way: punishing someone you have forgiven still looks like holding on to the wrong.

Mission 5Diversity

Different Christian and non-religious views

  • Unconditional forgiveness: many Christians forgive whether or not the offender repents, following Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34).
  • Conditional forgiveness: others point to "If he repents, forgive him" (Luke 17:3); full reconciliation needs repentance.
  • Catholic teaching: God's forgiveness is offered to all and received through repentance, especially in the Sacrament of Reconciliation; Catholics are called to forgive as they are forgiven.
  • Humanists: forgiveness can heal both victim and offender and help society; but it is a choice, not a duty, and victims should never be pressured to forgive.
  • Some non-religious people argue some acts should not be forgiven, out of respect for victims.
Mission 5Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Allah is al-Ghafur (the Forgiving) and ar-Rahim (the Merciful); forgiveness is offered to all who sincerely repent (tawbah).
  • "And if ye efface and overlook and forgive, then lo! Allah is Forgiving, Merciful." (Qur'an 64:14, Pickthall)
  • In qisas, the victim's family may forgive the offender (Qur'an 2:178): forgiveness and justice together.
  • Judaism: God is merciful to those who return (teshuvah). "Who is a God like unto Thee, that pardoneth the iniquity … because He delighteth in mercy." (Micah 7:18, JPS 1917)
  • Wrongs against people must be put right with them first: Yom Kippur does not atone for these until the person wronged is reconciled (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
Mission 5Diversity

Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism

  • Buddhism: holding on to anger harms yourself; forgiveness grows from metta (loving-kindness) and karuna (compassion).
  • "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller)
  • Hinduism: kshama (forgiveness, patience) is a virtue of dharma.
  • Sikhism: forgiveness (khima) is a mark of a God-centred (gurmukh) person; anger is one of the Five Thieves.

Mission 5

Restorative justice

  • Restorative justice: the offender and the victim (or the community) meet, with a trained facilitator, if both agree.
  • The victim can explain the harm; the offender takes responsibility, apologises and agrees how to repair it.
  • Aims: healing for victims; reformation of offenders; less reoffending; reconciliation.
  • Christian support: reconciliation is at the heart of the Gospel (Matthew 5:24; 2 Corinthians 5:18). Prison Fellowship's Sycamore Tree course teaches it in prisons.
  • Criticisms: not suitable for every crime; some victims find it painful; some say it is too soft without other punishment.
Mission 5

Examples of forgiveness

  • Gordon Wilson (Methodist): his daughter Marie was killed in the Enniskillen bombing in 1987. He said he bore the bombers no ill will and prayed for them; he later worked for peace.
  • Gee Walker (Christian): her son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in 2005. She said she forgave his killers, because of her faith.
  • Azim Khamisa (Muslim): his son Tariq was shot by a 14-year-old in 1995. He forgave the boy and set up a foundation with the boy's grandfather to stop youth violence.
  • Each acted from personal belief; none said the crime did not matter.
Quick check

Mission 5Evaluate

"Some actions are unforgivable."

  • Reasons for: some crimes (murder, genocide) cause harm that can never be repaired. Forgiving may seem to disrespect victims. Some offenders show no repentance. Humanists: forgiveness is a choice, not a duty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus taught "seventy times seven" and forgave on the cross. Examples like Gordon Wilson and Gee Walker show it is possible.
  • Muslims believe Allah is al-Ghafur (the Forgiving); Buddhists teach that "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5), so forgiving frees the victim from hatred.
  • Another view: forgiving the person is possible, while the act stays wrong and justice is still done.
  • Judge it by: Who has the right to forgive? Must the offender repent? Does forgiveness cancel punishment?

Forgiveness lets go of revenge; justice deals with the wrong. Most believers hold that you can offer one without cancelling the other.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Can a state ever be right to put someone to death for a crime?

Make a guess: Can a state ever be right to put someone to death for a crime?

Mission 6

What the death penalty is

  • Capital punishment (the death penalty): the state lawfully executes a person for a crime. A crime that can carry it is a capital offence.
  • Purpose: retribution (a life for a life), deterrence, and protection of society.
  • Great Britain: the last executions were in 1964; it was abolished for murder in 1965 (permanently from 1969). Northern Ireland: last execution 1961, abolished for murder 1973. Abolished across the UK for all crimes by 1998.
  • It is still used in some countries, e.g. China, Iran, Saudi Arabia and some US states.
  • Some people in the UK still call for it to return for the worst murders.
Mission 6

Arguments for

  • Retribution: some crimes are so evil that only death is a just penalty.
  • Deterrence: fear of death may stop people killing.
  • Protection: an executed murderer can never kill again.
  • Justice for victims' families: some feel the crime has been taken seriously.
  • Principle of utility (Jeremy Bentham, 1748–1832): the right act brings the greatest happiness for the greatest number. If executions deter murder and save many innocent lives, a utilitarian could support them.
Mission 6

Arguments against

  • Sanctity of life: many believers hold that all life is sacred and given by God, so only God may end it, a murderer's life included.
  • Mistakes cannot be undone: Timothy Evans was hanged in 1950 and pardoned in 1966; Derek Bentley was hanged in 1953 and his conviction quashed in 1998.
  • No clear evidence that it deters better than long prison sentences; many murders happen in anger or panic.
  • No chance to reform or repent.
  • Utility can argue against: executions may brutalise society and wrongful executions cause great harm, so it may not produce the greatest happiness.

Mission 6

How Catholic teaching developed

  • St Augustine (354–430): the state may use the sword (Romans 13), but in letters to officials (Letter 134 to Apringius; Letter 153 to Macedonius, c. 414) he begs for the lives of the condemned, so they have time to repent (paraphrase).
  • St Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 64, a. 2: if a person is dangerous to the community, "it is praiseworthy and advantageous that he be killed in order to safeguard the common good".
  • Pope John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae 56 (1995): only allowed when there is no other way to defend society; such cases are "very rare, if not practically non-existent".
  • Pope Francis (2018) revised the Catechism (2267): the death penalty is "inadmissible", an attack on the dignity of the person; the Church works for its abolition worldwide.
  • Catholics see this as a development in understanding: modern prisons can protect society without killing.
Mission 6Diversity

Christian views today

  • Most UK churches oppose it: Catholic, Church of England, Methodist, Quaker and others. Reasons: sanctity of life; Jesus' teaching on revenge and love of enemies (Matthew 5:38–48); no one is beyond redemption; mistakes.
  • Some Christians support it, often conservative Christians (e.g. many in some US churches): Genesis 9:6 comes before the Law of Moses and applies to all humanity; Romans 13:4 gives the state the sword.
  • Supporters add: justice is not revenge; the state, not the individual, punishes.

Mission 6Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: the Qur'an allows it for murder and certain very serious crimes: "slay not the life which Allah hath made sacred, save in the course of justice" (Qur'an 6:151, Pickthall).
  • The victim's family may forgive and accept compensation (Qur'an 2:178); forgiveness is praised.
  • Interpretations of Shari'ah differ: many Muslims support it for murder with strict proof; many scholars say the conditions make it very rare; some Muslims campaign against it. British Muslims follow UK law.
  • Judaism: the Torah allows it for murder: "He that smiteth a man, so that he dieth, shall surely be put to death." (Exodus 21:12, JPS 1917)
  • Mishnah Makkot 1:10 (paraphrase): a court that executed once in seven years was called destructive; Rabbi Eleazar ben Azariah said once in seventy; Rabbis Tarfon and Akiva said that had they sat on the court, no one would ever have been executed. Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel replied that they would then multiply murderers in Israel: the Mishnah keeps both views.
  • Israel abolished it for murder in 1954; it has been used once since, for the Nazi Adolf Eichmann (1962).
Mission 6Diversity

Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism

  • Buddhism: most Buddhists oppose it: the First Precept (not to take life), ahimsa, karuna and metta. Killing creates bad karma for those who carry it out.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa points against it; karma means the offender will face the results of the act anyway. Gandhi opposed the death penalty.
  • Some Hindus support it; India keeps it for the "rarest of rare" cases.
  • Sikhism: views vary. Many Sikhs oppose it: only God gives and takes life, and anyone can reform. Others accept it for the worst crimes, to protect the innocent and uphold justice.

Mission 6

Non-religious views and situation ethics

  • Humanists (e.g. Humanists UK) oppose it: this life is the only one; mistakes cannot be put right; no clear evidence it deters; it brutalises society.
  • Utilitarian arguments can go either way: it depends on the evidence about results.
  • Some atheists support it for the worst crimes, on grounds of retribution or protection.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The death penalty is never right."

  • Reasons for: sanctity of life (Exodus 20:13; Genesis 1:27); Jesus rejected revenge (Matthew 5:38–39); innocent people have been executed; no clear deterrent effect; Catholic teaching calls it inadmissible; Humanists oppose it.
  • Reasons against: retribution for the worst crimes (Genesis 9:6); Romans 13:4; protection of society; utility, if it deters.
  • Muslims point out that the Qur'an allows it for murder (6:151).
  • Another view: right in principle, but almost never in practice (Evangelium Vitae 56).
  • The rabbis of Mishnah Makkot 1:10 took a similar line.
  • Judge it by: Is life only God's to take? Can guilt ever be certain? What do victims and society need? What does the evidence show?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sin is against God's will; crime is against the law of the state; some acts are both.
  • Absolute morality: some acts always wrong; relative morality: depends on the situation.
  • Most traditions judge intentions as well as actions; conscience, scripture and religious law guide believers.

Mission 2

  • Crime breaks the law of the state; sin breaks God's law. Many acts are both; some are only one.
  • Reasons for crime: poverty, upbringing, mental health, addiction, greed, hate, opposition to unjust law. Believers condemn the crime, not the person.

Mission 3

  • Aims: retribution (deserved), deterrence (put others off), reformation (change the offender).
  • Christian sources: Exodus 21:24 (proportion), Romans 13:4 (authority), Galatians 6:1 (restore).
  • Islam (qisas, mercy), Judaism (compensation, teshuvah), Buddhism (compassion, reform), Hinduism (danda), Sikhism (justice and reform): each tradition balances justice and change.

Mission 4

  • Treatment of criminals: prison and community service; most believers want dignity and reform.
  • Most Christians and Buddhists oppose corporal punishment; Muslims and others differ on it.
  • Matthew 25:36, Hebrews 13:3, Surah 76:8: care for prisoners. Reformers (Howard, Fry), chaplains, Prison Fellowship.

Mission 5

  • Forgiveness: letting go of anger and revenge; repentance: turning from wrong; reconciliation: restoring the relationship.
  • Jesus: forgive "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22).
  • Muslims: Allah is al-Ghafur (64:14); Jews: teshuvah (Micah 7:18); Buddhists: "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5).
  • Forgiving the person can go with justice for the act.

Mission 6

  • Death penalty: execution by the state; aims: retribution, deterrence, protection. Abolished in the UK; used in some countries.
  • For: retribution, deterrence, utility (if it saves lives); against: sanctity of life, mistakes, no clear deterrent, no chance to reform.
  • Christians differ (Genesis 9:6 vs Matthew 5:38–39); Catholic teaching has developed to "inadmissible" (2018); other faiths and Humanists also differ.
