Mission 1

Mission 1: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Make a guess: An egg is a giant cell and a sperm is a tiny one. Why are the two cells built so differently?

Mission 1

The prime difference: one gamete travels

  • The male gamete travels to the female gamete. The egg stays put.
  • So the sperm is small, streamlined, with almost no food reserves.
  • The egg is large: its cytoplasm holds reserves for the embryo's first divisions.
  • Small and cheap → sperm made in vast numbers. Large → few eggs.
  • Both carry one haploid set of chromosomes. The size difference is cytoplasm, not DNA.
  • Human egg about 100 µm across; sperm head about 5 µm.
Mission 1

The male-typical system

  • Testis: makes sperm and testosterone. Held in the scrotum, below body temperature.
  • Epididymis: coiled tube on the testis; sperm mature and are stored here.
  • Sperm duct: carries sperm to the urethra.
  • Seminal vesicle and prostate gland: add fluid. Sperm + fluid = semen.
  • The seminal vesicle fluid contains fructose, a respiratory substrate for sperm.
  • Urethra: carries semen at ejaculation, urine at other times. Penis: erectile tissue.
  • Say: "seminal vesicles add fluid" — not: "seminal vesicles store sperm".
Mission 1

The female-typical system

  • Ovary: releases an egg each cycle (ovulation, around day 14); secretes oestrogen and progesterone.
  • Oviduct (fallopian tube): cilia move the egg; fertilisation happens here.
  • Uterus: muscular wall; the embryo implants in its lining.
  • Endometrium: the inner lining. Thickens each cycle; shed at menstruation.
  • Cervix: the neck of the uterus. Vagina: receives sperm; the birth canal.
  • Say: "fertilisation in the oviduct" — not: "fertilisation in the uterus".

Mission 1

Built for the job

  • Sperm acrosome: a sac of digestive enzymes in the head.
  • Sperm midpiece: packed with mitochondria → ATP for the flagellum (tail).
  • Sperm: haploid nucleus, little cytoplasm → small and fast.
  • Egg zona pellucida: a glycoprotein layer outside the membrane. Sperm bind to it.
  • Egg cortical granules: vesicles just under the membrane, ready to block extra sperm.
  • Egg cytoplasm: nutrient stores (yolk droplets) for the first divisions.
  • The cell released at ovulation is a secondary oocyte: meiosis is not yet finished (see Mission 2).
  • Say: "the zona pellucida is a glycoprotein layer" — not: "a cell wall".

Mission 1

Getting in: the acrosome reaction

  • Intercourse: ejaculation deposits semen in the vagina; sperm swim through the cervix and uterus to the oviducts.
  • (1) Many sperm reach the egg. One binds to the zona pellucida.
  • (2) Acrosome reaction: the acrosome releases its enzymes. They digest a path through the zona.
  • (3) The sperm's cell membrane fuses with the egg's cell membrane.
  • (4) The sperm nucleus enters the egg cytoplasm.
  • Say: "enzymes digest the zona pellucida" — not: "enzymes digest a hole in the egg membrane". Entry is by membrane fusion.
Mission 1

One sperm only: the cortical reaction

  • (5) Fusion triggers the cortical reaction.
  • Cortical granules fuse with the egg membrane and release enzymes into the zona (exocytosis).
  • The zona pellucida hardens; its sperm-binding sites are changed.
  • No further sperm can bind or pass through: polyspermy is blocked.
  • Say: "acrosome reaction lets one in; cortical reaction keeps the rest out" — not the reverse.

Mission 1

After entry

  • Sperm entry triggers the secondary oocyte to complete meiosis II. A second polar body forms.
  • Both nuclear membranes dissolve. All the condensed chromosomes join one mitosis.
  • That first mitosis makes two diploid nuclei: a two-cell embryo.
  • You may read "the nuclei fuse": a shorthand for these two steps.
Quick check

Mission check

?In a fish species the female lays a few hundred large eggs in a nest and the male releases many millions of tiny sperm over them. Which statement explains the difference in size and number?

One cell travels, the other supplies the embryo. Size, numbers and every special structure follow from that.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Make a guess: Meiosis in a testis gives four sperm, but meiosis in an ovary gives just one egg. Why?

Mission 2

Unequal cytokinesis

  • Nuclear division shares the chromosomes equally. Always.
  • Cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm) is usually equal, but not always.
  • Unequal example 1: making an egg. One big cell keeps almost all the cytoplasm; a tiny polar body gets almost none.
  • Each daughter cell must get at least one mitochondrion. Mitochondria form only by division of existing ones.
Quick check
?In human oogenesis, the first meiotic division produces a large secondary oocyte and a very small polar body. What does this unequal cytokinesis achieve?
Mission 2

Spermatogenesis

  • (1) Mitosis: spermatogonia (2n) divide, all through adult life.
  • (2) Growth: some enlarge into primary spermatocytes (2n).
  • (3) Meiosis I → two secondary spermatocytes (n).
  • (4) Meiosis II → four spermatids (n). Both divisions equal.
  • (5) Differentiation: each spermatid grows a flagellum and acrosome and loses most cytoplasm → spermatozoon.
  • Result: four sperm per meiosis; millions every day.
Mission 2

Inside the testis

  • The testis is packed with seminiferous tubules. Cut across, each is a ring of cells around a lumen.
  • Outer edge (basement membrane): spermatogonia.
  • Moving inwards: primary spermatocytes → secondary spermatocytes → spermatids.
  • In the lumen: spermatozoa, tails pointing in.
  • Sertoli cells: span the wall; nourish and protect developing sperm.
  • Interstitial (Leydig) cells: between the tubules; secrete testosterone.
Mission 2

Oogenesis

  • Before birth: oogonia (2n) divide by mitosis, then grow into primary oocytes (2n).
  • Primary oocytes start meiosis I, then pause. Each sits in a follicle.
  • From puberty, each cycle: one primary oocyte completes meiosis I → secondary oocyte (n) + first polar body. Unequal.
  • The secondary oocyte starts meiosis II, pauses again, and is released at ovulation.
  • Meiosis II finishes only if a sperm enters → ovum + second polar body. Unequal again.
  • Differentiation: the oocyte builds up cytoplasm, nutrient stores and cortical granules; the follicle cells grow around it.
  • Polar bodies degenerate. Result: one egg per meiosis.
Mission 2

Inside the ovary

  • Germinal epithelium: the outer layer.
  • Primordial follicles near the edge: a primary oocyte in one layer of flat follicle cells.
  • Developing follicles: more layers of follicle cells; a fluid space appears.
  • Mature (Graafian) follicle: large, fluid-filled; bulges at the surface. Its oocyte completes meiosis I just before ovulation.
  • Corpus luteum: the empty follicle after ovulation; secretes progesterone.
  • Rule of thumb: the bigger the follicle and its fluid space, the more mature it is. The corpus luteum has no oocyte.

Mission 2

From zygote to blastocyst to implantation

  • (1) The zygote divides by mitosis while cilia move it down the oviduct.
  • (2) About day 5: a blastocyst. A hollow ball with a fluid-filled cavity.
  • Inside: the inner cell mass → the embryo (later the foetus).
  • Outside: an outer layer of cells → attaches to the uterus; later helps form the placenta.
  • The outer layer is the trophoblast.
  • (3) About day 7: the blastocyst sheds the zona pellucida, attaches to the endometrium and sinks into it: implantation.
  • Say: "the blastocyst implants" — not: "the zygote implants".

Mission check

?Yeast can reproduce by budding. The nucleus divides by mitosis, and a small bud then pinches off a much larger parent cell. Which statement about the bud, as it separates, is correct?

Chromosomes are always shared equally; cytoplasm need not be. Give it all to one cell and you get one large egg, not four small ones.

Mission 3

The hormones and where they come from

  • Hypothalamus → GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) → anterior pituitary.
  • Anterior pituitary → FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) → ovary.
  • Ovary → oestrogen (mainly oestradiol) and progesterone → uterus lining (endometrium), hypothalamus and pituitary.
  • Ovary: a follicle grows and releases an egg; what is left becomes the corpus luteum.
  • Two linked cycles: ovarian (follicle → ovulation → corpus luteum) and uterine (lining shed → rebuilt → maintained). Together: the menstrual cycle.
Mission 3

One cycle, step by step

  • 1: Days 1–5, menstruation: the endometrium is shed. Progesterone is low, so FSH rises → follicles develop.
  • 2: Growing follicle secretes more and more oestradiol → endometrium thickens.
  • 3: About day 14: high oestradiol → LH surge (with a smaller FSH peak) → ovulation.
  • 4: Corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and oestradiol) → endometrium maintained; FSH and LH held low.
  • No pregnancy: corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → menstruation. FSH rises again.
  • Days are for a typical 28-day cycle; real cycles vary.
Mission 3

Two kinds of feedback

  • Negative feedback: low to moderate oestradiol, and progesterone, inhibit GnRH, FSH and LH. This stops new follicles developing in the second half.
  • Positive feedback: high, sustained oestradiol stimulates GnRH and LH release → more oestradiol → the LH surge → ovulation.
  • The fall in progesterone lifts the inhibition → FSH rises → a new cycle.
  • Say: "positive feedback: the response increases the stimulus" — not: "positive feedback is the good kind".
Quick check

Mission 3

Pregnancy, birth and milk

  • hCG: secreted by the embryo's outer cells (later the placenta). Keeps the corpus luteum secreting progesterone, so the lining is not shed. Pregnancy tests detect it.
  • Later, the placenta secretes progesterone and oestrogen itself.
  • Birth: stretching of the cervix → oxytocin from the posterior pituitary → stronger uterine contractions → more stretching. Positive feedback until the baby is born.
  • Lactation: prolactin from the anterior pituitary → milk production. Oxytocin → milk ejection when the baby suckles.
  • High progesterone in pregnancy holds back milk production; after birth progesterone falls and prolactin acts.
Quick check
?In early pregnancy the corpus luteum does not degenerate, and progesterone stays high. Which hormone keeps the corpus luteum active, and where does it come from?
Mission check

?The combined contraceptive pill contains an oestrogen and a progestogen (a progesterone-like hormone). Taken daily, it keeps both at a steady, moderate level. Why does ovulation not happen?

The egg comes mid-cycle because that is when oestradiol is high enough to flip feedback from negative to positive. Keep it steady and the flip never comes.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Every month the lining of the uterus is shed. What stops that happening once a pregnancy begins?

Make a guess: Every month the lining of the uterus is shed. What stops that happening once a pregnancy begins?

Mission 4

The embryo sends a signal

  • Without pregnancy: the corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → the endometrium is shed (see Mission 3).
  • After implantation, the embryo's outer cells secrete hCG.
  • hCG: human chorionic gonadotrophin.
  • hCG keeps the corpus luteum alive and secreting progesterone (and oestrogen).
  • Progesterone maintains the endometrium → no menstruation.
  • High progesterone and oestrogen also inhibit FSH and LH → no new follicles develop.
  • Say: "hCG is secreted by the embryo" — not: "by the mother's pituitary gland".
Quick check
?Which statement explains why hCG is needed early in pregnancy?
Mission 4

The hand-over

  • Weeks 1 to about 8: progesterone comes mainly from the corpus luteum, kept alive by hCG.
  • By about weeks 8 to 12: the placenta secretes enough progesterone and oestrogen itself.
  • hCG then falls; the corpus luteum degenerates; the pregnancy continues.
  • Progesterone: maintains the endometrium; keeps the uterus muscle relaxed (inhibits contractions).
  • Oestrogen: growth of the uterus muscle and the mammary glands.
  • Say: "progesterone from the corpus luteum, then the placenta" — not: "from the pituitary".
Mission 4Read the data

Hormones across pregnancy

Illustrative data: the shapes follow typical patterns; the levels are relative, not measurements.

  • hCG rises from implantation, peaks at about week 10, then falls to a low, steady level.
  • Progesterone rises all through pregnancy.
  • Its source changes: the corpus luteum share falls after about week 8 as the placenta share rises.
  • Oestrogen also rises to a peak near birth.
  • Read across: the hCG peak comes as the placenta takes over.

Mission 4

The placenta: an exchange organ

  • Villi of foetal tissue project into pools of maternal blood → large surface area.
  • Foetal capillaries run inside each villus. The two bloods are separated by a thin barrier: they do not mix.
  • To the foetus: oxygen, glucose, amino acids, water, ions, some antibodies.
  • To the mother: carbon dioxide, urea.
  • By diffusion (oxygen, carbon dioxide), facilitated diffusion (glucose), active transport (amino acids), endocytosis (antibodies).
  • Not a perfect filter: alcohol, nicotine, some drugs and some viruses cross.
  • The placenta lets the foetus stay in the uterus to a later stage of development. Compare marsupials: a brief, simple placenta, so the young are born tiny and finish developing in a pouch.
  • The placenta is also an endocrine organ: hCG, then progesterone and oestrogen.
Quick check
?A foetus in the uterus has fluid-filled, collapsed lungs, yet its blood is oxygenated. How does oxygen reach the blood of the foetus?
Mission 4

Birth: a positive feedback loop

  • (1) Late in pregnancy, progesterone falls. Its inhibition of contractions is removed.
  • Newer research: in humans, blood progesterone stays high until birth. What falls is its effect: the uterus responds to it less.
  • Rising oestrogen adds oxytocin receptors to the uterus muscle.
  • (2) The posterior pituitary releases oxytocin → the uterus muscle contracts.
  • (3) Contractions push the baby against the cervix. Stretch receptors send impulses to the hypothalamus → more oxytocin.
  • (4) Stronger contractions → more stretch → more oxytocin. Positive feedback.
  • The loop stops when the stimulus stops: the baby is born.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "negative feedback keeps contractions steady".
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the hormonal changes that bring about childbirth?
Mission 4

Lactation

  • In pregnancy, oestrogen and progesterone grow the mammary glands. High progesterone holds back full milk production.
  • After birth the placenta is lost → progesterone falls → milk production starts.
  • Prolactin (anterior pituitary): milk production.
  • Oxytocin (posterior pituitary): milk ejection ("let-down"): cells around the milk glands contract.
  • Suckling → impulses to the hypothalamus → release of both hormones.
  • Say: "prolactin makes milk; oxytocin releases it" — not the reverse.
Mission check

?In a mammal, removing the ovaries (and with them the corpus luteum) early in pregnancy ended the pregnancy, but the same operation late in pregnancy did not. What does this show?

The lining stays because progesterone stays high: first from the corpus luteum, kept alive by the embryo's hCG, then from the placenta.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The sperm travels, so it is small, cheap and numerous; the egg stays, so it is large and stocked with reserves. Both are haploid.
  • Acrosome reaction: enzymes digest the zona pellucida; membranes fuse. Cortical reaction: the zona hardens, blocking polyspermy.
  • Nuclear membranes dissolve and the chromosomes join one mitosis, giving two diploid nuclei.

Mission 2

  • Unequal cytokinesis: chromosomes are shared equally, cytoplasm is not; every daughter cell still needs a mitochondrion.
  • Spermatogenesis: mitosis, growth, two equal meiotic divisions, differentiation → four sperm, continuously. Oogenesis: begins before birth; two unequal divisions → one egg + polar bodies; meiosis II finishes only after a sperm enters.
  • About day 5 a hollow blastocyst forms; about day 7 it implants in the endometrium.

Mission 3

  • FSH grows follicles; rising oestradiol thickens the endometrium, then flips to positive feedback for the LH surge and ovulation; progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the lining and holds FSH and LH down.
  • hCG keeps the corpus luteum active in pregnancy; oxytocin drives birth by positive feedback; prolactin makes milk.

Mission 4

  • The embryo's hCG keeps the corpus luteum secreting progesterone; the placenta takes over by about weeks 8 to 12, so the endometrium is kept.
  • The placenta's villi give a large surface area for exchange between separate maternal and foetal bloods.
  • Birth: progesterone falls; oxytocin rises by positive feedback as the cervix stretches.
  • Lactation: prolactin makes milk; oxytocin ejects it.
