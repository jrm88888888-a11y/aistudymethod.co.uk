Statistics · sampling and types of data

Populations and samples

  • The population is the whole group you want to know about. A sample is the part of it you actually collect data from.
  • Samples are quicker and cheaper; sometimes testing destroys the item (testing how long batteries last).
  • You use the sample to infer (estimate) facts about the population.
  • Limitations: a sample gives only an estimate, and a different sample would give slightly different results.
Statistics · sampling and types of data

Random samples and bias

  • In a simple random sample, every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
  • Method: number every member, then use a random number generator (or names from a hat); ignore repeats.
  • A sample is biased if it is likely to misrepresent the population, e.g. asking people at a gym how often they exercise.
  • Larger random samples give more reliable estimates. A larger biased sample is still biased.

Statistics · sampling and types of data

Types of data

  • Qualitative (categorical) data are words or categories: favourite sport, eye colour.
  • Quantitative data are numbers. Discrete data are counted and take separate values (number of siblings). Continuous data are measured and can take any value in a range (height, time, mass).
  • The type of data decides which charts and averages make sense.
  • Primary data you collect yourself; secondary data were collected by someone else (a website, a census).

Yes, as an estimate. A simple random sample of 1,000 gives everyone an equal chance, so it usually represents the population well. A biased sample, such as 5,000 people from one website, can be badly wrong however large it is.

Statistics · charts and diagrams

Tally charts, frequency tables and pictograms

  • A tally chart records data as you collect it, in groups of five strokes.
  • A frequency table lists each category or value with its frequency (how many). A two-way table sorts by two features at once.
  • A pictogram uses a symbol for a fixed number of items, shown in a key: if one car symbol = 4 cars, then 321​31/2 symbols = 14 cars.
  • Part-symbols must be easy to read: halves or quarters.
Statistics · charts and diagrams

Bar charts: single, dual and composite

  • A bar chart shows categorical or discrete data: equal widths, gaps between bars, and an even frequency scale from 0.
  • A dual bar chart puts bars for two groups side by side, with a key.
  • A composite (stacked) bar chart stacks the groups in one bar: its total height is the total frequency.
  • A vertical line chart uses thin lines instead of bars, for ungrouped discrete data such as goals scored.
  • Check what each small square is worth first.
Calculate
?The dual bar chart shows the number of library books borrowed by Year 7 and Year 8 from Monday to Thursday. How many more books did Year 8 borrow than Year 7 altogether over the four days?

Statistics · charts and diagrams

Line graphs: time series and frequency polygons

  • A time series graph plots a quantity against time (horizontal axis), with points joined by straight lines.
  • Look for the trend (general rise or fall) and any seasonal pattern that repeats each year. Here sales peak every third quarter.
  • A frequency polygon shows grouped data: plot each frequency at its class midpoint (5, 15, 25, … for 0–10, 10–20, …) and join with straight lines.
  • Two polygons on the same axes are easy to compare.
Worked example Statistics · charts and diagrams

Reading a stem-and-leaf diagram

Question
An ordered stem-and-leaf diagram shows the ages of 11 people. Key: 3∣43 | 4 means 34. Rows: 1∣2551 | 2 5 5, 2∣1382 | 1 3 8, 3∣04443 | 0 4 4 4, 4∣14 | 1. Find the mode and the range.
  1. 1
    Use the key: the stem is the tens digit and each leaf a units digit, so the ages are 12, 15, 15, 21, 23, 28, 30, 34, 34, 34, 41
  2. 2
    Mode: the leaf 4 appears three times on stem 3, so the mode is 34
  3. 3
    Range: largest −− smallest =41−12=29= 41 − 12 = 29
Answer
mode 34 years, range 29 years
⚠️ Watch out
a stem-and-leaf diagram needs ordered leaves and a key. Subtracting the stems only (4−1=34 − 1 = 3) ignores the leaves.
Statistics · charts and diagrams

How graphs can mislead

  • Read the vertical axis before the picture. If it does not start at 0 (and has no break mark), small differences look huge.
  • In chart B the April bar looks 7 times as tall as January's, yet April's 1,030 members are only 3% more than January's 1,000.
  • Other tricks: unequal steps on a scale, missing labels, pictogram symbols of different sizes, tilted 3-D pie charts.

?Two papers plot the same members (1,000, 1,010, 1,020, 1,030). One says "soaring", the other "flat". How?

Statistics · averages and spread

Mean, median, mode and range

  • Mean: add all the values and divide by how many there are.
  • Median: put the values in order; the median is the middle one. With an even number of values it is halfway between the middle two. Its position is the 2n+1​(n+1)/2th value.
  • Mode: the value that occurs most often (there can be more than one, or none).
  • Range == largest value −− smallest value. It measures spread: how varied the data are.
Worked example Statistics · averages and spread

Four measures from a list

Question
8 students spent these times (minutes) on homework: 25, 30, 20, 30, 35, 30, 40, 110. Find the mean, median, mode and range.
  1. 1
    Order the list: 20, 25, 30, 30, 30, 35, 40, 110
  2. 2
    Mean: 320÷8=40320 ÷ 8 = 40 minutes
  3. 3
    Median: n=8n = 8, so halfway between the 4th and 5th values: 230+30​=30(30 + 30)/2 = 30 minutes
  4. 4
    Mode: 30 minutes (three times). Range: 110−20=90110 − 20 = 90 minutes
Answer
mean 40 min, median 30 min, mode 30 min, range 90 min
⚠️ Watch out
110 is an outlier. It lifts the mean to 40, above 6 of the 8 values. Without it the mean is 30. Finding the "middle" of the unordered list gives 230+35​=32.5(30+35)/2 = 32.5, which is wrong.
Statistics · averages and spread

Averages from a frequency table

  • In a frequency table each value xx occurs ff times, so each row totals fxfx.
mean=∑f∑fx​=number of valuestotal of all values​mean = (Σ fx)/(Σ f) = (total of all values)/(number of values)
  • Mode: the value with the highest frequency. Median: find its position, 2n+1​(n+1)/2, then use running totals.
  • Pets owned by 25 people (0 pets: 7, 1: 9, 2: 6, 3: 2, 4: 1): the median is the 13th person, and running totals 7, 16, … show they own 1 pet. The mode is also 1.
  • Divide by ∑fΣ f, not by the number of rows.

Statistics · averages and spread

Estimated mean from grouped data

  • In grouped data you do not know the exact values, so use the midpoint of each class to stand for every value in it.
  • Estimated mean =∑f∑(midpoint×f)​= (Σ (midpoint × f))/(Σ f). It is an estimate because the values are not all at the midpoint.
  • The modal class is the class with the highest frequency.
  • The median class is the class containing the 2n+1​(n+1)/2th value.
  • The range can only be estimated: largest possible value minus smallest possible value.
Worked example Statistics · averages and spread

Estimating a mean from a grouped table

Question
40 students recorded their journey time to school, tt minutes: 0<t≤100 < t ≤ 10: 8, 10<t≤2010 < t ≤ 20: 14, 20<t≤3020 < t ≤ 30: 12, 30<t≤4030 < t ≤ 40: 6. Estimate the mean and state the modal class.
  1. 1
    Midpoints: 5, 15, 25, 35
  2. 2
    Midpoint × frequency: 5×8=405 × 8 = 40, 15×14=21015 × 14 = 210, 25×12=30025 × 12 = 300, 35×6=21035 × 6 = 210
  3. 3
    Add: 40+210+300+210=76040 + 210 + 300 + 210 = 760
  4. 4
    Divide by the total frequency: 760÷40=19760 ÷ 40 = 19
Answer
estimated mean 19 minutes; modal class 10<t≤2010 < t ≤ 20
⚠️ Watch out
using the upper class limits (10, 20, 30, 40) gives 24 minutes, and dividing by 4 classes instead of 40 students gives 190.
Calculate
?A courier weighs 50 parcels. Mass mm kg: 0<m≤20 < m ≤ 2: 9 parcels, 2<m≤42 < m ≤ 4: 16, 4<m≤64 < m ≤ 6: 15, 6<m≤106 < m ≤ 10: 10. The courier claims the mean mass is under 4 kg. Work out an estimate of the mean mass. Give your answer to 2 decimal places.

Statistics · averages and spread

Choosing an average and comparing data

  • Mean: uses every value, but an outlier (far from the rest) can drag it a long way.
  • Median: not affected by a few extreme values; best for data like pay or house prices.
  • Mode: the only average for categorical data, such as favourite colour.
  • To compare two data sets, compare an average and the range, in context: "P spent less time on average, and their times were more consistent."
  • An outlier may be an error: check it before removing it.
Quick check
?Homework times: Class P mean 32 minutes, range 15 minutes; Class Q mean 35 minutes, range 60 minutes. Which comparison is correct?
Mission check

?A firm says average pay is £60,000, yet 9 of its 10 staff earn £25,000. How?

The owner earns £375,000. Total pay is 9×25,000+375,000=£600,0009 × 25,000 + 375,000 = £600,000, and 600,000÷10=£60,000600,000 ÷ 10 = £60,000: a true mean, but higher than 9 of the 10 salaries. The median and mode, both £25,000, describe a typical worker far better.

Statistics · scatter graphs

Scatter graphs and correlation

  • A scatter graph plots pairs of data (two variables for each item) as points, usually crosses. Each axis has its own scale.
  • Positive correlation: as one variable increases, the other tends to increase.
  • Negative correlation: as one increases, the other tends to decrease.
  • No correlation: no clear pattern.
  • Points close to a line show strong correlation; points loosely spread show weak correlation.

Statistics · scatter graphs

Lines of best fit

  • A line of best fit is a single straight ruled line, drawn by eye, that follows the trend.
  • It runs across the whole data set with roughly the same number of points on each side. It need not pass through any point or the origin.
  • Ignore any outlier (a point that does not fit the pattern).
  • To estimate, go from a known value to the line, then across to the other axis.
Worked example Statistics · scatter graphs

Estimating from a line of best fit

Question
The line of best fit for ice-cream sales passes through (15, 120) and (30, 300), where xx is temperature in °C and yy is sales. Estimate the sales on a day at 24 °C.
  1. 1
    Find the gradient: 30−15300−120​=15180​=12(300 − 120)/(30 − 15) = 180/15 = 12 more sales per °C
  2. 2
    Count up from the known point (15, 120): 24 °C is 9 °C warmer
  3. 3
    Add on: 120+9×12=120+108=228120 + 9 × 12 = 120 + 108 = 228
Answer
about 228 ice creams
⚠️ Watch out
on a paper you would read this straight off the line on the graph. Working out 24×12=28824 × 12 = 288 forgets that the line does not go through the origin.
Calculate
?Using the same line of best fit, estimate the temperature on a day when 192 ice creams were sold.
°C
Statistics · scatter graphs

Interpolation, extrapolation and outliers

  • Interpolation: estimating inside the range of the data. Usually reliable if the correlation is strong.
  • Extrapolation: estimating outside the range of the data. Risky: the trend may not continue.
  • An outlier may be an error or an unusual case (a day the shop shut early). Leave it out of the line.
  • A small sample or weak correlation gives only rough estimates.
Quick check
?The ice-cream data covers 12 °C to 31 °C. A student uses the line to predict 480 sales at 45 °C. How reliable is this?
Calculate
?A teacher plots hours of revision (xx) against test mark (yy) for 20 students who revised between 1 and 11 hours. Her line of best fit passes through (2, 40) and (10, 80). Estimate the mark for a student who revised for 7 hours.
Statistics · scatter graphs

Correlation does not prove causation

  • Correlation shows that two variables change together. It does not show that one causes the other.
  • Often a third factor drives both. Example: over a summer, sales of electric fans and of cold drinks rise together; hot weather causes both.
  • Sometimes the link is coincidence, especially with small data sets.
  • To show cause you need more than a scatter graph, such as a controlled experiment. In an exam, say "correlation does not mean causation" and name a possible third factor.
Mission check

?Ice-cream sales and sunburn cases show strong positive correlation. Does ice cream cause sunburn?

The two variables are correlated because both depend on the weather: sunny days bring more ice creams and more sunburn. Correlation shows variables move together; it never proves that one causes the other.

Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Quartiles and the interquartile range (Higher)

  • In ordered data the median splits it in half; the lower and upper quartiles (LQ, UQ) split it into quarters.
  • For nn listed values: LQ is the 4n+1​(n+1)/4th value, median the 2n+1​(n+1)/2th, UQ the 43(n+1)​(3(n+1))/4th.
  • Interquartile range (IQR) == UQ −− LQ: the spread of the middle half, unaffected by outliers.
  • For 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 12: LQ is the 2nd value (4), median the 4th (7), UQ the 6th (9): IQR =5= 5.
Calculate
?The marks of 11 students are 23, 15, 30, 18, 12, 28, 20, 35, 17, 25, 22. Find the interquartile range.
Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Cumulative frequency graphs (Higher)

  • Cumulative frequency is a running total: how many values are up to each upper class boundary.
  • Plot each total at the upper class boundary, start with 0 at the lowest boundary, and join with a smooth curve.
  • For nn values, read across from 2n​n/2 (median), 4n​n/4 (LQ) and 43n​3n/4 (UQ), then down.
  • For how many are more than a value, read up from it and subtract from nn.
Worked example Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Median and IQR from a cumulative frequency graph (Higher)

Question
The journey times tt (minutes) of 80 commuters: 0<t≤100 < t ≤ 10: 6, 10<t≤2010 < t ≤ 20: 14, 20<t≤3020 < t ≤ 30: 20, 30<t≤4030 < t ≤ 40: 20, 40<t≤5040 < t ≤ 50: 20. Draw a cumulative frequency graph and estimate the median and IQR.
  1. 1
    Running totals: 6, 20, 40, 60, 80
  2. 2
    Plot (0, 0), (10, 6), (20, 20), (30, 40), (40, 60), (50, 80) and join with a smooth curve
  3. 3
    Median: read across from 80÷2=4080 ÷ 2 = 40 to the curve, then down: 30 minutes
  4. 4
    LQ from 80÷4=2080 ÷ 4 = 20: 20 minutes. UQ from 43​×80=603/4 × 80 = 60: 40 minutes
  5. 5
    IQR =40−20=20= 40 − 20 = 20 minutes
Answer
median 30 minutes, IQR 20 minutes
⚠️ Watch out
plotting at the class midpoints (5, 15, 25, …) shifts the whole curve left by 5 minutes. Plot at the upper boundaries.
Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Comparing distributions (Higher)

  • Two data sets can share a median yet be very different. Compare an average (the median) and a spread (the IQR).
  • A smaller IQR means the middle half of the data is more consistent (closer together).
  • Write each comparison in context: "The median time for Group A was lower, so on average Group A was quicker. Group A's IQR was smaller, so their times were more consistent."
  • Use like for like: median with IQR, or mean with range.

Statistics · cumulative frequency and histograms

Histograms and frequency density (Higher)

  • A histogram shows continuous data with no gaps between bars. When class widths are unequal, the vertical axis is frequency density.
frequency density=class widthfrequency​frequency density = frequency/(class width)
  • The area of each bar represents the frequency: frequency=frequency density×class widthfrequency = frequency density × class width.
  • Example: the class 20<h≤3020 < h ≤ 30 has frequency 15 and width 10, so its bar has height 15÷10=1.515 ÷ 10 = 1.5.
  • With equal class widths the vertical axis can simply show frequency.
Calculate
?The histogram shows the heights hh cm of some plants. The frequency densities are: 0<h≤100 < h ≤ 10: 1.2, 10<h≤1510 < h ≤ 15: 3.6, 15<h≤2015 < h ≤ 20: 4, 20<h≤3020 < h ≤ 30: 1.5, 30<h≤5030 < h ≤ 50: 0.5. How many plants are taller than 15 cm?
Mission check

?Class X: median 62, LQ 55, UQ 68. Class Y: median 62, LQ 44, UQ 76. Were their results the same?

The same median only tells you the middle marks match. Class X's IQR is 68−55=1368 − 55 = 13 and Class Y's is 76−44=3276 − 44 = 32, so X's marks were much more consistent, while Y had both weaker and stronger students. Always compare an average and a spread.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A sample is used to estimate facts about a population; the estimate is only as good as the sample.
  • Random samples avoid bias; bigger random samples are more reliable, but size never cures bias.
  • Data are qualitative or quantitative; quantitative data are discrete (counted) or continuous (measured).
  • Choose the chart for the data: pictogram, bar chart (dual or composite) or pie chart for categories; read every value against the scale and key.
  • Pie chart angle = frequency ÷ total × 360°; reverse it to find a frequency.
  • A vertical axis that does not start at 0 can make a tiny change look dramatic.
  • Time series show trends and seasonal patterns; frequency polygons plot frequencies at class midpoints.
  • Stem-and-leaf diagrams keep every value: ordered leaves and a key.
  • Mean =number of valuestotal​= total/(number of values); median = middle value of the ordered list; mode = most common; range = largest −− smallest.
  • From a table, mean =∑f∑fx​= (Σ fx)/(Σ f); for grouped data use midpoints, and the answer is an estimate.
  • Outliers drag the mean, not the median: choose the average that fits the data, and compare an average and a spread in context.
  • Scatter graphs show positive, negative or no correlation.
  • Describe the strength too: strong or weak.
  • A line of best fit is a straight ruled line through the trend; use it to estimate, trusting interpolation more than extrapolation.
  • Correlation does not mean causation: look for a third factor.
  • Quartiles split ordered data into quarters; IQR = UQ − LQ measures the spread of the middle half.
  • Cumulative frequency: plot running totals at upper class boundaries; read across from 2n​n/2, 4n​n/4, 43n​3n/4.
  • A box plot shows lowest, LQ, median, UQ, highest on a scale; compare box plots on the same scale.
  • Histograms: frequency density = frequency ÷ class width, and bar area gives frequency.
