Mission 1: Why do Jews start their new year with ten days of saying sorry, ending in a 25-hour fast?

Make a guess: Why do Jews start their new year with ten days of saying sorry, ending in a 25-hour fast?

Mission 1

The Days of Awe

  • The Jewish year starts in autumn, in the month of Tishri.
  • Rosh Hashanah ("head of the year"): 1–2 Tishri. Yom Kippur ("Day of Atonement"): 10 Tishri.
  • Together with the days between, they are the Ten Days of Awe (Yamim Noraim), also called the Days of Repentance.
  • They are the High Holy Days: solemn, not merry, but hopeful.
  • The key idea is teshuvah ("returning"): repentance, turning back to God and to the right path.
  • Three things are said to change a bad decree: teshuvah (repentance), tefillah (prayer), tzedakah (charity).
  • Preparation begins a month before, in Elul: many hear the shofar each weekday morning and say early-morning prayers for forgiveness (Selichot).

Mission 1

Rosh Hashanah: birthday of the world, day of judgement

  • Tradition says Rosh Hashanah marks the creation of the world (or of the first humans).
  • God is hailed as King of the universe.
  • It is also Yom ha-Din, the Day of Judgement: every person's deeds of the past year are weighed.
  • The Talmud pictures books opened: the righteous written for life, the wicked for death, and most people "in between", with ten days to change the verdict (Rosh Hashanah 16b, paraphrase).
  • So: written on Rosh Hashanah, sealed on Yom Kippur.
  • Greeting: "May you be written and sealed for a good year."
  • Many Jews read the Book of Life as a picture of responsibility rather than a literal book.
Mission 1

The shofar

  • A shofar is a ram's horn (or another kosher animal's, never a cow's), blown in the synagogue on Rosh Hashanah.
  • Notes: tekiah (one long blast), shevarim (three broken), teruah (nine short), tekiah gedolah (a very long final blast). Many synagogues sound a hundred notes.
  • Meanings: a wake-up call to repent: Maimonides wrote that it says "wake up, you sleepers" (Laws of Repentance 3:4, paraphrase).
  • A trumpet at a king's coronation: God as King.
  • A ram recalls the ram Abraham offered instead of Isaac (Genesis 22:13).
  • In Orthodox synagogues the shofar is not blown when Rosh Hashanah falls on Shabbat (many Reform synagogues do blow it); it is blown once at the very end of Yom Kippur.

Mission 1

Sweet foods and tashlikh

  • Apple dipped in honey: a prayer for a sweet new year.
  • Round challah, often with raisins: the cycle of the year, and God's crown.
  • Pomegranate: its many seeds stand for many good deeds. A fish head: "be the head, not the tail".
  • Many avoid bitter or sour foods during the festival.
  • Tashlikh ("you will cast"): on the first afternoon, Jews go to a river, sea or pond and say verses including Micah 7:19, often emptying pockets or casting crumbs.
  • > "And Thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea." (Micah 7:19, JPS 1917)
  • The act is symbolic: sins are not washed away by water but by teshuvah.
Mission 1

Yom Kippur: the fast

  • The holiest day of the Jewish year, the last of the Ten Days.
  • "For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you" (Leviticus 16:30, JPS 1917).
  • A 25-hour fast, from before sunset to after nightfall: no food or drink.
  • Also no washing for pleasure, no perfumes or creams, no leather shoes, no marital relations; no work, as on Shabbat.
  • Many wear white (some men a white robe, the kittel): purity and forgiveness (Isaiah 1:18).
  • Pikuach nefesh (saving life) comes first: young children, the ill, and those whose health would be at risk must not fast.
  • The fast is not punishment: freed from bodily needs, Jews focus on prayer, like angels in white.
Mission 1

Five services, one long day

  • Kol Nidrei (evening, opening Maariv): an Aramaic declaration releasing vows made to God that could not be kept; sung three times with the Ark open.
  • Vidui (confession) in every service: "We have sinned…", said in the plural: the community repents together. Many tap their chest at each sin.
  • Shacharit (morning): long prayers and a Torah reading; Orthodox synagogues read Leviticus 16, the Temple ritual of the day.
  • Musaf recalls the high priest's service in the Temple on this day.
  • Minchah (afternoon): the book of Jonah: God forgives Nineveh when its people turn from evil (Jonah 3:10).
  • Neilah ("closing"): the final service as the day ends; the gates of heaven are pictured closing; the Ark stays open and many stand throughout.
  • It ends with the Shema, one long shofar blast, and often "Next year in Jerusalem!"
Mission 1

Making peace with people; kapparah

  • The Mishnah teaches: Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against other people, it atones only once the person has been reconciled (Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • So in the Ten Days Jews ask forgiveness from family and friends, and repay what they owe.
  • Many make new resolutions for the year ahead.
  • Kapparah means "atonement".
  • Kapparot: a custom on the eve of Yom Kippur among some Orthodox Jews: a chicken, or more often today a bag of money, is swung around the head and then given to the poor.
  • Some rabbis have long opposed using a chicken; animal-welfare concerns have made the money form more common.

Mission 1Diversity

Different ways, and changes

  • Orthodox: two days of Rosh Hashanah; services in Hebrew; separate seating; no phones, cars or electronic devices on these days.
  • Reform and Liberal: many communities keep one day of Rosh Hashanah; more English; mixed seating; some prayers rewritten; some services live-streamed so housebound members can join.
  • Some Reform Jews treat the Book of Life as a symbol of moral responsibility rather than a real heavenly record.
  • Many Jews who rarely attend synagogue still come on Yom Kippur: it is often the fullest service of the year.
  • Changes: kapparot with money instead of chickens; tashlikh in city parks; online learning in Elul; synagogues issuing tickets because of crowds and security.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Yom Kippur matters more than Rosh Hashanah."

  • For: Yom Kippur is the holiest day; it is when the verdict is sealed; Leviticus 16 makes atonement its whole purpose; more Jews attend Yom Kippur than any other service.
  • Against: Rosh Hashanah is when judgement begins; without the shofar's call to teshuvah, Yom Kippur would have no preparation.
  • Against: Rosh Hashanah celebrates creation and God as King: themes Yom Kippur assumes.
  • Another view: the Ten Days are one process: you cannot rank the start and the end of the same journey.
  • Judge it by: What does the Torah say each day is for? How do Jews in Britain actually keep them? Can parts of one process be ranked?

The Days of Awe join God and neighbour: repentance before God, and making peace with the people you have wronged.

Mission 2

From slavery to freedom

  • Pesach (Passover) remembers the Exodus: the Israelites leaving slavery in Egypt, led by Moses (Exodus 1–15).
  • Moses' life: hidden as a baby in a basket on the Nile, raised in Pharaoh's palace, he fled, then was called by God at the burning bush (Exodus 2–3).
  • Pharaoh refused to free the slaves; God sent ten plagues (blood, frogs … darkness).
  • The tenth plague: the death of the Egyptian firstborn. Israelites marked their doorposts with a lamb's blood, and God passed over their houses (Exodus 12:27).
  • They left in haste: no time for the dough to rise (Exodus 12:39).
  • At the Sea of Reeds (Red Sea), the waters parted; the Israelites crossed (Exodus 14).
  • For Jews, this is God's great act of redemption, which leads to Sinai and the covenant.

Mission 2

Chametz out

  • Chametz: leavened food from wheat, barley, spelt, rye or oats that has risen (bread, cakes, pasta, beer).
  • Jews may not eat or own chametz during Pesach (Exodus 12:15; 13:7).
  • Weeks of cleaning: cupboards, pockets, cars; special Pesach dishes or kashered ones.
  • The search (bedikat chametz): on the night before, by candle or torch, often with a feather and wooden spoon; ten wrapped pieces are hidden so children can find them.
  • Next morning, the chametz found is burned (biur chametz) and a formula declares any missed crumbs ownerless.
  • Large stocks can be sold to a non-Jew through a rabbi and bought back after.
  • Instead, matzah: flour and water, baked within 18 minutes so it cannot rise.
  • Meanings: the haste of the Exodus; the "bread of affliction" of slaves; and removing inner "puffed-up" pride.

Mission 2

The Seder plate

  • Seder means "order": a set meal on the first night (two nights for many outside Israel).
  • Zeroa: a roasted shank bone: the Passover lamb, no longer sacrificed since the Temple fell.
  • Beitzah: a roasted egg: the festival offering in the Temple; also mourning for the Temple.
  • Maror: bitter herbs (horseradish or bitter lettuce): the bitterness of slavery.
  • Charoset: apples, nuts, wine, cinnamon: the mortar slaves used for bricks.
  • Karpas: a green vegetable dipped in salt water: spring, and the slaves' tears.
  • Chazeret: a second bitter herb, used in a "sandwich" with matzah.
  • Beside the plate: three matzot, salt water, and wine for each person.
Mission 2

The Haggadah and four questions

  • Haggadah ("telling"): the book read at the Seder, from "thou shalt tell thy son in that day" (Exodus 13:8, JPS 1917).
  • The youngest child asks the four questions (Mah nishtanah: "Why is this night different?"):
  • Why only matzah? Why bitter herbs? Why dip twice? Why recline?
  • The answer is the story: "We were slaves to Pharaoh in Egypt…"
  • Four cups of wine: the four promises of Exodus 6:6–7 (bring out, deliver, redeem, take you as a people).
  • A drop of wine is spilled for each plague: joy is lessened because Egyptians suffered too.
  • Afikoman (hidden matzah) keeps children alert; Elijah's cup and the door opened express hope for the Messiah.
  • It ends: "Next year in Jerusalem!"

Mission 2

A pilgrim festival

  • Pesach is one of the three pilgrim festivals (shalosh regalim) with Shavuot and Sukkot: "Three times in a year shall all thy males appear before the LORD thy God" (Deuteronomy 16:16, JPS 1917).
  • In Temple times, families went up to Jerusalem to sacrifice the Passover lamb.
  • Since the Temple's destruction (70 CE) there is no sacrifice: the shank bone and the Haggadah take its place.
  • Pesach lasts seven days in Israel and for Reform Jews; eight days for most Orthodox Jews outside Israel. The first and last days are holy days.
  • On the second night, Jews begin counting the Omer: 49 days to Shavuot, linking freedom to the gift of the Torah (see Mission 3).
  • It is also a spring festival, in the month of Nisan.
Mission 2Diversity

Different ways of keeping Pesach

  • Orthodox: strict removal of all chametz; two Seders outside Israel; a fully traditional Haggadah in Hebrew.
  • Ashkenazi and Sephardi: many Ashkenazi Jews also avoid kitniyot (rice, beans, maize); most Sephardi Jews eat them.
  • Reform and Liberal: seven days; one Seder; Haggadot in English with new readings, e.g. on modern slavery or refugees.
  • Some families add Miriam's cup (water, for Moses' sister) to honour women in the story.
  • Many Jews who keep few other practices still hold or attend a Seder.
  • Understandings differ: many Orthodox Jews read the Exodus as history; some Reform and secular Jews read it mainly as a story that shapes Jewish identity and values.
Mission 2

Why Pesach matters today

  • Religious: it obeys the Torah's command "for ever" (Exodus 12:14) and thanks God as redeemer.
  • Memory: the Mishnah teaches that in every generation each Jew should see themselves as if they personally came out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase).
  • Teaching: the whole Seder is built around children's questions.
  • Social: family and community gather; the Seder joins generations.
  • Values: freedom, and remembering "you were strangers in Egypt": a reason to care for the oppressed today.
  • Hope: Elijah's cup and "next year in Jerusalem".
Mission 2Evaluate

"Pesach is the most important Jewish festival."

  • For: it remembers the founding event of the Jewish people; the Torah commands it "for ever"; the Exodus is recalled daily in prayer and in the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:2); it is very widely kept, even by non-observant Jews.
  • Against: Yom Kippur is the holiest day; Shabbat, kept weekly, is in the Ten Commandments and comes first in the Torah's list of holy days (Leviticus 23:3).
  • Against: Shavuot (the gift of the Torah) gives freedom its purpose.
  • Another view: festivals have different jobs (memory, atonement, joy); ranking them may miss the point.
  • Judge it by: What does the Torah stress? Which shapes Jewish identity most? Is "most kept" the same as "most important"?

Mission 3

Hanukkah: the story

  • Hanukkah ("dedication"): eight days from 25 Kislev, in winter.
  • Your spec spells it Chanukah.
  • About 167 BCE, the Greek-Syrian king Antiochus IV banned Shabbat, circumcision and Torah study, and set up pagan worship in the Temple (its desecration).
  • A priest, Mattathias, and his sons led a revolt; his son Judah Maccabee ("the hammer") led a small army to victory.
  • In about 164 BCE they recaptured Jerusalem, cleaned the Temple and rededicated it.
  • The Talmud tells of one jar of pure oil, enough for one day, that kept the Temple menorah burning for eight days (Shabbat 21b, paraphrase).
  • The history is in 1 Maccabees, which is not part of the Tenakh; the oil story comes from the Talmud.
Mission 3

Lighting the hanukkiah

  • A hanukkiah: eight branches plus a helper candle, the shammash, used to light the others.
  • One candle on the first night, two on the second … eight on the last.
  • Blessings are said; families sing Ma'oz Tzur ("Rock of Ages").
  • Placed in a window or doorway to make the miracle public.
  • Oil foods: latkes (potato pancakes) and doughnuts.
  • Dreidel: a spinning top with four Hebrew letters: "A great miracle happened there" (in Israel, "here").
  • Family gatherings, coins (gelt), and in many homes gifts.
  • A minor festival: not from the Torah, and work is allowed. Its message from the prophet read on its Shabbat: "Not by might, nor by power, but by My spirit" (Zechariah 4:6, JPS 1917).

Mission 3Diversity

Different ways, and changes

  • Hanukkah: public lightings of giant hanukkiot in city squares; in Britain and the USA it has grown in importance near Christmas, with more gifts.
  • Some Jews welcome this as pride in Jewish identity; others feel the miracle is lost in presents; some Zionists stress the Maccabees' bravery, many religious Jews God's miracle.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"Chanukah is a minor festival, so it should not be made into a major celebration."

  • For: it is not in the Torah; work is allowed; giving it more weight near Christmas may shift attention from Torah festivals.
  • Against: it remembers freedom of religion; the hanukkiah in the window proclaims a miracle publicly; it strengthens Jewish identity, especially for children in a mainly non-Jewish society.
  • Different Jews: many Orthodox Jews stress the miracle and the lights; some secular and Zionist Jews stress the Maccabees' struggle for freedom.
  • Judge it by: Is importance set by the Torah or by what a festival does for Jews today? Does more celebration change its meaning?

Mission 4Read the source

A land and a hope

> "Now the LORD said unto Abram: 'Get thee out of thy country, and from thy kindred, and from thy father's house, unto the land that I will show thee. And I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee" (Genesis 12:1–2, JPS 1917)

> "For the LORD hath comforted Zion; He hath comforted all her waste places, and hath made her wilderness like Eden" (Isaiah 51:3, JPS 1917)

  • The land (Eretz Yisrael) is part of God's covenant with Abraham and his descendants.
  • Zion is another name for Jerusalem, and for the land and people.
  • Isaiah speaks to exiles: God will restore the ruined land.

Mission 4

Jerusalem and the Temple

  • King David made Jerusalem his capital; Solomon built the First Temple there.
  • The Babylonians destroyed it in 586 BCE; the Second Temple was rebuilt and later expanded by Herod; the Romans destroyed it in 70 CE.
  • After 70 CE most Jews lived in exile (diaspora), but Jerusalem stayed at the centre of prayer.
  • Synagogues face Jerusalem; the Amidah asks God to rebuild it.
  • "If I forget thee, O Jerusalem, let my right hand forget her cunning." (Psalm 137:5, JPS 1917)
  • The fast of Tisha B'Av mourns both Temples; a glass is broken at weddings in their memory.
Mission 4

Keeping the Temple's memory alive

  • The Ark (aron hakodesh) holding the Torah scrolls recalls the Ark of the Covenant in the Temple's Holy of Holies.
  • Ner tamid ("eternal light") above the Ark recalls the Temple lamp that burned "continually" (Exodus 27:20).
  • Daily prayers follow the Temple's pattern: morning and afternoon services stand in place of the daily offerings; an extra service (Musaf) on Shabbat and festivals stands for the extra offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b, paraphrase).
  • Visiting the Western Wall: standing at the nearest place to where the Temple stood.
Mission 4

The Western Wall

  • The Western Wall (Hebrew Kotel): part of the retaining wall around the Temple Mount, built in Herod's rebuilding of the Second Temple.
  • It is the closest place Jews can pray to the site of the Holy of Holies. Many call it the last remnant of the Temple.
  • From 1948 to 1967 Jews could not reach it; since 1967 it has been under Israeli control.
  • Practices: prayer at any hour; reading the Psalms; notes (kvitlach) placed in the cracks, later collected and buried, never thrown away.
  • Bar Mitzvah ceremonies; services on festivals; some mourners tear a garment on first seeing the Temple site.
  • The main plaza is run under Orthodox rules: men and women pray in separate sections, divided by a screen.
  • Many Reform, Liberal and Masorti Jews pray in a mixed area at the southern end, by Robinson's Arch; groups such as Women of the Wall campaign for women to pray with Torah scrolls in the main women's section.

Mission 4

Pilgrimage in Judaism

  • In Temple times the Torah commanded three yearly pilgrimages to Jerusalem, at Pesach, Shavuot and Sukkot (Deuteronomy 16:16). Pilgrims sang the Psalms of Ascent (Psalms 120–134).
  • Since 70 CE there is no obligation to go; visiting is a personal choice.
  • Spiritual goals: to pray at holy places; to feel closer to God and to Jewish history; to celebrate a Bar or Bat Mitzvah; to remember the Shoah.
  • Physical goals: to see the land of the Bible; to climb Masada; to visit family; to study in Israel.
  • Challenges: cost and travel; heat; crowds at festivals; security worries; the emotional weight of Yad Vashem.
  • Effects: many return with stronger faith and Jewish identity; some choose to move to Israel (aliyah, "going up").
Mission 4

Yad Vashem and the Shoah

  • Yad Vashem in Jerusalem, set up by Israeli law in 1953: the world centre for remembering the Holocaust.
  • Its name comes from Isaiah: "a monument and a memorial (yad vashem)… an everlasting memorial, that shall not be cut off" (Isaiah 56:5, JPS 1917).
  • Roles: a memorial to about six million Jews murdered by Nazi Germany and its collaborators; a research archive; education for students, teachers and soldiers; collecting names of every victim.
  • Shoah (Hebrew: "catastrophe"): many Jews prefer it, because "holocaust" comes from a Greek word for a burnt offering to God, as if the murders were a sacrifice.
  • Remembering matters: to honour the dead, to warn against hatred, and to keep the command to remember.
Mission 4

Masada

  • Masada: a mountain-top fortress by the Dead Sea, built by Herod.
  • After the Temple fell, Jewish rebels held it against Rome until the Romans built a siege ramp (about 73–74 CE).
  • The historian Josephus (The Jewish War, Book 7) wrote that the defenders, led by Eleazar ben Yair, chose death rather than slavery; 960 people died.
  • In modern Israel, Masada became a symbol of resistance: "Masada shall not fall again." Some army units have held ceremonies there.
  • Visitors climb the Snake Path at dawn; some celebrate Bar Mitzvahs there.
  • Different views: Jewish law forbids taking one's own life, so some religious Jews do not hold Masada up as an example; some historians question parts of Josephus' account.

Mission 4Diversity

Different views of the land and the State of Israel

  • The State of Israel was founded in 1948. Most Jews worldwide see it as central to Jewish life; they differ on what it means.
  • Religious Zionists: the State is the beginning of redemption; Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935) taught that even secular pioneers were part of God's plan. Many say special prayers on Israel's Independence Day.
  • Secular Zionists: a homeland and safe refuge for a people, after centuries of persecution; Theodor Herzl (1860–1904), The Jewish State (1896).
  • Haredi non-Zionists: the land is holy, and many live there, but a Jewish state should wait for the Messiah; small groups (e.g. Satmar Hasidim) oppose the State outright.
  • Reform and Liberal: once saw Judaism as a religion, not a nation (e.g. American Reform's Pittsburgh Platform, 1885, did not seek a return to Zion); since the Shoah and 1948 most strongly support Israel, often with a stress on equality and justice.
  • Many Jews support the State while disagreeing with particular government policies; many diaspora Jews see their own country as home.
Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

"Every Jew should visit Israel at least once."

  • For: the land is part of the covenant; prayers face Jerusalem; seeing the Western Wall and Yad Vashem (Israel's Holocaust memorial) deepens faith and memory; it ties diaspora Jews to Jews in Israel.
  • Against: since 70 CE there is no command to go; God can be worshipped anywhere (Shekhinah); cost, health or safety may make it impossible.
  • Against: some Haredi and some progressive Jews feel uneasy about what a visit says about the State.
  • Another view: studying Torah and living Jewishly where you are can matter more than any journey.
  • Judge it by: Is there a religious duty, or a personal benefit? What about Jews who cannot go? Do different views of the State change the answer?
Mission check

Jerusalem and the land hold Jewish memory and hope; Jews agree they are holy but differ on what the modern State means.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Rosh Hashanah (1 Tishri): creation, God as King, judgement; shofar, apple and honey, tashlikh (Micah 7:19).
  • Yom Kippur (10 Tishri): 25-hour fast, white, confession, Kol Nidrei to Neilah; atonement with God, reconciliation with people.
  • Teshuvah, tefillah and tzedakah run through the Ten Days of Awe; Orthodox and Reform Jews keep them in different ways.

Mission 2

  • Pesach remembers the Exodus: slavery, plagues, the Passover, the Red Sea; "a memorial … for ever" (Exodus 12:14).
  • Chametz is cleared, searched for and burned; matzah is eaten; the Seder plate, Haggadah and four questions retell the story.
  • A pilgrim festival in Temple times; today kept by Orthodox, Reform and secular Jews in different ways.

Mission 3

  • Hanukkah (Chanukah): the Temple rededicated and the oil that lasted eight days; eight nights of lights, latkes, dreidel.

Mission 4

  • The land is part of God's covenant with Abraham (Genesis 12:1–3); Jerusalem and the Temple stay central in prayer and memory (Psalm 137:5; Isaiah 51:3).
  • The Western Wall is the closest place to the Temple's Holy of Holies; the Ark, ner tamid and daily prayers keep the Temple's memory alive.
  • Pilgrimage is voluntary today; Yad Vashem remembers the Shoah and teaches about it.
  • Masada stands for resistance, though its example is debated.
  • Religious Zionist, secular Zionist, Haredi and Reform Jews see the State of Israel differently.
