Mission 1

Mission 1: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Make a guess: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Quick check
?A 0.20 kg copper block at 80 °C is lowered into a large tank containing 200 kg of water at 20 °C. The water has far more internal energy than the block. Which statement describes the resultant thermal energy transfer?
Mission 1

Which way does energy flow?

  • Temperature decides the direction: net energy transfer is always from higher to lower temperature.
  • Not "more energy to less": a hot cup can lose energy to a huge cold bath.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. A body does not "contain heat".
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net energy transfer between bodies in contact. They are at the same temperature.
  • If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C (a thermometer), A and B are at the same temperature. This is why thermometers work.
Mission 1

The Kelvin scale and absolute zero

  • Absolute zero, 0 K (−273.15 °C): the lowest possible temperature. Particles have their minimum internal energy.
  • The Kelvin (absolute, thermodynamic) scale starts at absolute zero. It does not depend on the property of any one substance.
  • T (K) = θ (°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • No negative kelvin temperatures. 20 °C = 293 K.
Quick check
?Sample P is warmed from 10 °C to 30 °C. Sample Q is warmed from 290 K to 310 K. Which statement about the two temperature changes is correct?
Mission 1

Internal energy

  • Internal energy = total random kinetic energy + total potential energy of the particles.
  • KE: random motion (vibration in solids; moving about in liquids and gases).
  • PE: from the forces between particles. It depends on their separation.
  • The energies are randomly distributed: particles have a range of energies.
  • Not included: the KE of the whole object moving, or its gravitational PE.
  • Heat it → internal energy rises, and (unless it is changing phase) its temperature rises.
  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles → no PE. Internal energy is all random KE, so it depends only on temperature.
Quick check
?You have 1.0 kg of ice at 0 °C and 1.0 kg of liquid water at 0 °C. Which statement about their internal energies is correct?
Mission 1

Temperature and internal energy

  • Absolute temperature is a measure of the average random kinetic energy of the particles.
  • Mean KE ∝ T in kelvin. 27 °C → 54 °C is 300 K → 327 K: only about 9% more mean KE, not double.
  • Same temperature → same average translational KE per particle in any gas. Lighter particles move faster.
  • Temperature ≠ internal energy. A bath at 40 °C has far more internal energy than a cup at 40 °C: many more particles.
Mission check

?A thermometer at room temperature, 20 °C, is placed in a cup of water at 70 °C. Its reading rises for a while and then stays steady. Why does the steady reading give the temperature of the water?

Metal and wood left in the same room reach thermal equilibrium with it: same temperature.

Metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from your warmer hand much faster.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Pump air hard into a ball and the air inside gets warmer. Was the air heated?

Make a guess: Pump air hard into a ball and the air inside gets warmer. Was the air heated?

Mission 2

Two ways in: heating and work

  • Internal energy rises in two ways:
    • heating: energy transferred because of a temperature difference;
    • work done on it: a force moves its boundary, e.g. a piston compressing a gas, or rubbing.
  • Both are energy in transit across the boundary. Once inside, it is just internal energy. You cannot say how much "came as heat".
  • Qualitative first law: increase in internal energy = energy in by heating + work done on the system (and the reverse for energy out).
Quick check
?Gas is sealed in a cylinder whose walls and piston are insulated. The gas pushes the piston outward quickly, and the temperature of the gas falls. Which statement explains the fall?
Mission 2

Internal energy and temperature

  • Raise the temperature → mean random KE rises → internal energy rises.
  • Change of phase: internal energy changes at constant temperature (the potential energy of the particles changes).
  • Absolute zero: internal energy is at its minimum. It cannot be lowered further.
  • Internal energy depends on how much material too. Temperature does not.
Mission 2

Ideal gas: all kinetic

  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles, except during collisions.
  • So no intermolecular PE. Internal energy = total random KE of the particles.
  • For a fixed amount of ideal gas, internal energy depends only on temperature.
  • Real gases at ordinary pressures are close to ideal.
Quick check
?A sample of ideal gas in a cylinder expands slowly, pushing a piston outward, while its temperature is kept constant. How does the internal energy Uf of the gas at the end of the expansion compare with its internal energy Ui at the start?
Quick check
?A steel ball is dropped from rest and falls 2 m before it hits the floor. Air resistance is negligible. Which statement is correct about the ball during the fall, before it hits the floor?
Mission check

?Lead shot is sealed in a cardboard tube that is well insulated. The tube is turned end over end 100 times, so the shot falls the length of the tube and hits the end each time. The temperature of the lead rises by a few kelvin. Which statement explains the rise?

Same as pumping up the ball: the piston does work on the air. No temperature difference drove the energy in, so the air was not heated, yet its internal energy and temperature rose.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: Under the same Sun, beach sand gets scorching while the sea stays cool. Why?

Mission 3

Specific heat capacity

  • Specific heat capacity, c: energy needed to raise the temperature of 1 kg by 1 K. Unit: J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Q = mcΔθ
  • Water: c ≈ 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Dry sand: about 800. Same energy per kg → water warms about one fifth as much.
  • A change of 1 °C is a change of 1 K: Δθ needs no conversion.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 0.500 kg aluminium block, 20 °C → 45 °C. c = 900 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Temperature rise: 45 − 20 = 25 K.
  • Energy: 0.500 × 900 × 25 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J (11 250 J).
  • With a 50 W heater and no losses: t = 11 250 ÷ 50 = 225 s.
Quick check
?An electric kettle heats 250 g of water from 18 °C to 90 °C. The specific heat capacity of water is 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. How much energy is transferred to the water? Ignore energy transferred to the kettle and surroundings.
Mission 3

Method of mixtures

  • Hot object into cold water, insulated: energy lost by the hot = energy gained by the cold.
  • Both end at the same final temperature θf.
  • Worked: 0.200 kg copper (c = 390) at 100 °C into 0.300 kg water (c = 4180) at 20 °C.
    • 0.200 × 390 × (100 − θf) = 0.300 × 4180 × (θf − 20)
    • 7800 − 78θf = 1254θf − 25 080
    • θf = 32 880 ÷ 1332 = 24.7 °C
  • The water barely warms: its m × c is much bigger.
Quick check
?A 0.300 kg iron block at 80.0 °C is placed in 0.500 kg of water at 20.0 °C in an insulated container, and the block and the water reach a common temperature. The specific heat capacity of iron is 450 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, and the specific heat capacity of water is 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Energy transfer to the container and the surroundings is negligible. What is the common temperature?
Mission 3

Phase change: the particle picture

  • Heat a solid steadily: its temperature rises, then stops rising while it melts. Same again while it boils.
  • During a phase change, energy goes into potential energy: work is done against the forces between particles.
  • Melting: the fixed arrangement breaks up; the spacing changes only slightly. Boiling: the particles separate completely.
  • Mean kinetic energy stays the same, so the temperature is constant. Internal energy still rises.
  • Boiling: throughout the liquid, at the boiling point. Evaporation: only at the surface, at any temperature.
  • Cooling reverses it: freezing and condensing release energy at constant temperature.
Mission 3

Specific latent heat

  • Specific latent heat: energy needed to change the phase of 1 kg with no temperature change. Unit: J kg⁻¹.
  • Q = ml: l is the specific latent heat (of fusion or of vaporisation).
  • Water: l = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹ (fusion); l = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹ (vaporisation). Vaporising separates the particles completely, so it takes far more.
  • 10 g of steam condensing on skin gives out 0.010 × 2.26 × 10⁶ = 2.3 × 10⁴ J. The same water cooling from 100 °C to 37 °C gives only 2.6 × 10³ J more.
Quick check
?0.40 kg of ice at 0 °C is melted and the water is then warmed to 20 °C. Specific latent heat of fusion of ice = 3.34 × 10⁵ J kg⁻¹; specific latent heat of vaporisation of water = 2.26 × 10⁶ J kg⁻¹; specific heat capacity of water = 4.18 × 10³ J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is the total energy that must be supplied?
Mission check

?0.20 kg of water and 0.20 kg of cooking oil, both at 20 °C, are each supplied with 12 kJ of energy. Specific heat capacities: water 4180 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹, oil 2000 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. Assume no energy is lost. Which statement about their final temperatures is correct?

Same energy, same mass: the lower c, the bigger temperature rise. Sand's c is about a fifth of water's, so in the same sunshine each kilogram of sand warms about five times as much. The sea also spreads its energy through metres of mixing water and loses some by evaporation; dry sand keeps it in a thin top layer.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Make a guess: How do you measure the energy a material needs, when some always leaks away?

Mission 4

Electrical method for c

  • Metal block (known mass), wrapped in insulation. Holes for an immersion heater and a thermometer (a drop of oil helps contact).
  • Heater circuit: ammeter in series, voltmeter across the heater. Or a joulemeter.
  • Energy supplied: E = VIt.
  • Record temperature every 30 s. Plot θ against t.
  • Use the gradient of the straight part: c = VI ÷ (m × gradient). This avoids the early lag while the heater warms up.
  • A liquid: same method, in an insulated container with a lid; stir.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • 1.00 kg aluminium block. Heater: 12.0 V, 4.00 A → P = 48.0 W.
  • Gradient of θ–t graph: 0.0500 K s⁻¹.
  • c = 48.0 ÷ (1.00 × 0.0500) = 960 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Data-book value ≈ 900. Measured value is too high: some energy went to the surroundings, so less raised the block's temperature.
Quick check
?A 0.80 kg copper block is heated by a 24 W electrical heater. On the straight part of its temperature–time graph, the temperature rises at 0.075 K s⁻¹. What is the specific heat capacity of copper from these data?
Mission 4

Method of mixtures as a measurement

  • Heat a metal block in boiling water (≈ 100 °C). Transfer it quickly into cold water in an insulated cup. Stir; record the highest temperature.
  • Energy lost by block = energy gained by water (+ cup). Solve for c of the metal.
  • Errors: energy lost during the transfer; hot water carried over on the block; energy taken by the cup.
  • Quick and simple, so it is an estimate. The electrical method is more accurate.
Mission 4

Continuous flow

  • A liquid flows steadily past a heater. Each second, mass m/t is warmed by Δθ.
  • Power: P = (m/t) × c × Δθ.
  • Electric shower: 9.5 kW heats water from 10 °C to 40 °C (Δθ = 30 K).
    • m/t = 9500 ÷ (4180 × 30) = 0.076 kg s⁻¹: about 4.5 litres per minute.
  • Faster flow → smaller temperature rise. That is why a shower runs cooler when you turn up the flow.
Quick check
?Water flows through an electric water heater at a steady 0.050 kg s⁻¹. The heater's power is 8.4 kW, and all of it is transferred to the water. The specific heat capacity of water is 4200 J kg⁻¹ K⁻¹. What is the rise in the water's temperature?
Mission check

?A student uses the electrical method to find the specific heat capacity of a brass block. Her value is 10% higher than the data-book value. Which change to her method would most reduce this difference?

Two ways to beat the leak: insulate to make it small, and design it out (use the gradient of the straight part, not a single reading).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Net energy transfer goes from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium (same temperature, no net transfer).
  • Internal energy = random KE + intermolecular PE of the particles; absolute temperature measures the average random KE. T (K) ≈ temperature in °C + 273; temperature changes are the same in K and °C.

Mission 2

  • Internal energy rises by heating or by work done on the system; heat and work are energy in transit, not stores.
  • For an ideal gas, internal energy is entirely random kinetic energy, so it depends only on temperature.

Mission 3

  • Q = mcΔθ. Same energy and mass: lower c, bigger temperature rise.
  • During a phase change, energy raises particle PE at constant temperature: Q = ml.

Mission 4

  • Electrical method: E = VIt; c = P ÷ (m × gradient of θ–t). Energy lost to the surroundings makes c come out too high: insulate.
  • Continuous flow: P = (m/t) c Δθ.
