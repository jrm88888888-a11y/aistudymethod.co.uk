Make a guess: On a fast train, you throw a ball forwards. How fast is the ball moving?

Mission 1

Who is measuring?

  • A reference frame is an observer with a ruler and a clock.
  • Position and velocity are measured relative to a frame.
  • Different frames can give different values, all correct.
  • No frame is "the true one". Always say which frame you mean.
Mission 1

Adding velocities in a line

  • Frame S′ moves at velocity v relative to frame S. A body has velocity u in S. In S′: u′ = u − v.
  • Choose a positive direction. Train: +30 m s⁻¹ relative to the ground.
  • Ball thrown forwards at +10 m s⁻¹ relative to the train → +40 m s⁻¹ relative to the ground.
  • Thrown backwards (−10 m s⁻¹) → +20 m s⁻¹ relative to the ground.
  • Check with u′ = u − v: ground frame S, train frame S′, v = +30. u = +40 → u′ = 40 − 30 = +10 m s⁻¹ ✓
Quick check
?A cyclist rides east at 6 m s⁻¹ relative to the road. Observer X stands beside the road. Observer Y is in a car that moves east along the same road at a constant 10 m s⁻¹. Which statement correctly compares the cyclist's velocity as measured by Y with the velocity measured by X?
Mission 1

Acceleration is the same in both frames

  • Two frames moving at constant velocity relative to each other.
  • Every velocity measured in one differs by the same constant from the other.
  • So every change in velocity is the same → the acceleration is the same in both frames.
  • A ball dropped in a steady train falls with acceleration g for the passenger and for the platform.
Quick check
?Predict first. In a 1964 experiment, pions moving at 0.99975c relative to a laboratory emitted gamma-ray photons forwards. The Galilean rule predicts that the photons move at about 2c in the laboratory. Their measured speed was c. What should be concluded?
Mission 1

Where Galilean addition fails

  • u′ = u − v works for everyday speeds.
  • For light it fails: every inertial observer measures the same speed, c, whatever their motion.
  • So u′ = u − v is an approximation, valid when speeds are much less than c.
Mission check

?On a straight road, car A travels east at 35 m s⁻¹ and car B travels west at 22 m s⁻¹, both relative to the road. Take east as the positive direction. What is the velocity of car B in the reference frame of car A?

The ball in the hook moves at 10 m s⁻¹ relative to you and 40 m s⁻¹ relative to the track. Both are right: velocity belongs to a frame.

Mission 2

Reference frames

  • A reference frame: a set of axes to measure positions, plus clocks to time events.
  • An event happens at one place, at one instant: (x, t).
  • Inertial frame: a frame that is not accelerating. Newton's first law holds in it.
  • Constant velocity counts: speed and direction steady. Rest is a special case.
  • Non-inertial: speeding up, slowing down or turning. A ball on a table rolls with no horizontal force acting.
  • The ground is close enough to inertial for most experiments.
Quick check
?Treat the Earth's surface as an inertial reference frame. Which of the following is also an inertial reference frame?
Mission 2

Galilean relativity

  • Two inertial frames: a platform, and a train moving past it at constant velocity.
  • Velocities differ (u′ = u − v), but v is constant, so accelerations are the same.
  • Masses and forces are the same too, so F = ma holds in both.
  • Galilean relativity: Newton's laws are the same in all inertial frames.
  • So no mechanics experiment inside the closed train can reveal its steady motion.
  • Different frames still describe motion differently: a dropped ball falls straight down for the passenger, along a parabola for the platform.
Quick check
?On an aircraft flying straight and level at a constant 250 m s⁻¹, a flight attendant pours water from a jug held 20 cm above a cup on a tray. Where does the water land?
Mission 2

The Galilean transformation

  • Frame S′ moves at velocity v along +x relative to S. Origins coincide at t = t′ = 0.
  • x′ = x − vt · t′ = t
  • Worked example: v = 15 m s⁻¹. Event in S: x = 500 m, t = 20 s.
  • x′ = 500 − 15 × 20 = 200 m. t′ = 20 s.
  • t′ = t: time is absolute. Every inertial observer gives an event the same time, so all agree on time intervals and on which events are simultaneous.
Mission 2

The trouble with light

  • Maxwell's theory of electromagnetism predicts that light travels at c = 1/√(μ0ε0) = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • μ0 and ε0: the magnetic and electric constants of free space, fixed by experiments with magnets, currents and charges.
  • It names no frame: c comes from these two constants of nature.
  • Galilean addition says an observer moving towards the light should measure c + v.
  • Experiments (from Michelson and Morley, 1887, onwards) found no effect of the Earth's motion on the measured speed of light.
  • Either Maxwell's equations or the Galilean transformation must be wrong. Einstein (1905) kept Maxwell.
Mission 2

The two postulates

  • Postulate 1: the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames. All laws, including electromagnetism, not just Newton's.
  • Postulate 2: the speed of light in a vacuum, c, is the same for all inertial observers, whatever the motion of the source or the observer.
  • No experiment of any kind can pick out a frame that is "really" at rest.
  • Price: t′ = t must go. Time intervals and lengths depend on the frame.
Quick check
?Inside a spacecraft moving at a constant 0.50c relative to the Earth, a physicist measures the force between two parallel wires carrying known currents, with the apparatus at rest in the spacecraft. On Earth, the same apparatus gives a force F. What does she measure?
Quick check
?A spacecraft travels directly towards a distant star at 0.60c relative to the star. An astronaut measures the speed of the starlight arriving at the spacecraft. What does the astronaut find?
Mission check

?On a ship moving at constant velocity on a calm sea, a student pulls a glider along a level air track with a constant force and measures its acceleration. She repeats the experiment on land with the same glider and the same force. What does she find?

Same force, same mass, same acceleration, on the ship or on land: the same laws. That is why no experiment in the windowless train can show its steady motion: by the first postulate, not even one with light.

Mission 3

From Galileo to Lorentz

  • Frame S′ moves at velocity v along +x relative to S. Origins coincide at t = t′ = 0.
  • An event has coordinates (x, t) in S and (x′, t′) in S′.
  • Galilean: x′ = x − vt and t′ = t. These break the postulate that c is the same in every inertial frame.
  • Lorentz transformations keep c the same in both frames:
  • x′ = γ(x − vt)
  • t′ = γ(t − vx/c²)
  • The new piece: t′ depends on where the event happens, as well as when.
Mission 3

The Lorentz factor

  • γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²)
  • v = 0: γ = 1. γ is never less than 1.
  • 0.60c → 1.25 · 0.80c → 1.67 · 0.99c → 7.09.
  • v → c: γ grows without limit. No frame can move at c.
  • Small v: γ ≈ 1 and vx/c² ≈ 0. The Lorentz equations become Galileo's.
  • Derivation: not required.
Quick check
?Frame S′ moves at 0.90c relative to frame S. What is the Lorentz factor γ?
Mission 3

Worked example: one event, two frames

  • S′ moves at v = 0.60c relative to S → γ = 1.25.
  • Event in S: x = 1200 m, t = 5.0 μs. c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • vt = 0.60 × 3.00 × 10⁸ × 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ = 900 m.
  • x′ = 1.25 × (1200 − 900) = 375 m (3.8 × 10² m).
  • vx/c² = (0.60 × 3.00 × 10⁸ × 1200)/(3.00 × 10⁸)² = 2.4 μs.
  • t′ = 1.25 × (5.0 − 2.4) μs = 3.3 μs.
  • Check: Galileo would give 300 m and 5.0 μs.
Quick check
?Frame S′ moves at v = 0.80c in the positive x-direction relative to frame S, and the origins coincide at t = t′ = 0. An event occurs at x = 900 m, t = 1.0 μs in S. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the position x′ of the event in S′?
Mission 3

Intervals and simultaneity

  • For two events: Δx′ = γ(Δx − vΔt) and Δt′ = γ(Δt − vΔx/c²).
  • Two lamps 1200 m apart flash together in S: Δt = 0.
  • In S′ (0.60c): Δt′ = 1.25 × (0 − 2.4 μs) = −3.0 μs. Not together.
  • The flash further along +x happens first in S′.
  • Simultaneity is relative for events at different places.
  • Same place and same time in S (Δx = 0, Δt = 0) → same in every frame.
Mission 3

Adding velocities

  • An object has velocity u in S and u′ in S′.
  • u′ = (u − v)/(1 − uv/c²)
  • Signs matter: u and v are velocities along x, + or −.
  • A ship moves at v = +0.80c. It fires a probe forwards at u′ = +0.50c. Probe's velocity u in S?
  • Swap roles: in the ship's frame the probe has +0.50c, and the Earth moves at −0.80c.
  • Earth-frame velocity = (0.50c − (−0.80c))/(1 − (0.50)(−0.80)) = 1.30c/1.40 = 0.93c. Not 1.30c.
  • Same result from the rearranged form u = (u′ + v)/(1 + u′v/c²).
Quick check
?In the Earth's frame S, spacecraft A moves at +0.60c and spacecraft B moves at −0.50c along the same line, towards each other. Frame S′ is the rest frame of A. Using u′ = (u − v)/(1 − uv/c²), what is the velocity of B in S′?
Quick check
?In the Earth's frame S, spacecraft P moves at +0.90c and spacecraft Q moves at +0.60c along the same line. Frame S′ is the rest frame of P. Using u′ = (u − v)/(1 − uv/c²), what is the velocity of Q in S′?
Mission check

?A spacecraft moves at 0.70c relative to the Earth. It sends a light pulse forwards, at speed c relative to the spacecraft. What is the speed of the light pulse relative to the Earth?

Relativistic addition never gives a speed above c. Crew A sees ship B coming at 0.85c. In the Earth frame the gap closes at 1.10c, but that is a closing rate, not the velocity of any object, so it is allowed.

Mission 4

Moving clocks run slow

  • A clock moving relative to you is measured to tick slower than an identical clock at rest beside you.
  • It follows from the postulates: every observer must measure the same c.
  • An unstable particle is a clock: moving fast, its lifetime measured in the lab is longer than at rest.
  • Factor: γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²).
  • 0.10c: × 1.005 · 0.50c: × 1.15 · 0.90c: × 2.3 · 0.99c: × 7.1 · 0.999c: × 22
  • Everyday speeds: far too small to notice. Close to c: large.
Mission 4

Proper time and proper length

  • Proper time interval Δt0: between two events that happen at the same place in a frame. One clock, present at both, measures it.
  • In every other inertial frame, the interval is longer: Δt0 is the shortest.
  • Proper length L0: the length of an object measured in its own rest frame.
  • In frames where it moves along its length, it is shorter: L0 is the longest.
  • "Proper" means "own frame", not "correct". All frames' measurements are valid.
Quick check
?A lamp on the Earth flashes twice. A spacecraft passes the Earth at 0.80c between the flashes. Which observer measures the proper time interval between the two flashes?
Mission 4

Time dilation

  • Δt = γΔt0, with γ = 1/√(1 − v²/c²) ≥ 1.
  • Worked example: a lamp on a spacecraft flashes every 2.0 μs (spacecraft clock). The craft passes you at 0.60c.
  • γ = 1/√(1 − 0.60²) = 1/0.80 = 1.25.
  • You measure Δt = 1.25 × 2.0 = 2.5 μs between flashes.
  • Reciprocal: the astronaut measures your clocks running slow by the same factor. Neither frame is preferred.
Quick check
?A spacecraft moves at 0.50c relative to the Earth. A clock at rest on the Earth ticks once every 2.00 s, as measured on the Earth. What interval between ticks does the astronaut measure, using clocks at rest in the spacecraft's frame?
Mission 4

Length contraction

  • L = L0/γ: a moving object is measured shorter along its motion.
  • Lengths perpendicular to the motion are unchanged.
  • Worked example: a rod of proper length 100 m moves along its length at 0.80c.
  • γ = 1/√(1 − 0.80²) = 1/0.60 = 1.67. L = 100 ÷ 1.67 = 60 m.
  • Real, not an optical trick: it is the length you get by marking both ends at the same instant in your frame.
Quick check
?A spacecraft has a proper length of 60.0 m. It moves past a space station at 0.60c along its length. What length does an observer on the space station measure for the spacecraft?
Mission 4

The muon evidence

  • Cosmic rays hit the upper atmosphere and make muons, which fall at about 0.995c.
  • A muon at rest has a half-life of 1.5 μs: in 1.5 μs, half of a large group of muons decays. In 1.5 μs at nearly c it covers only about 450 m.
  • Without relativity, almost none should survive a few kilometres. Yet detectors at sea level count many.
  • Example (illustrative): a 3.0 km descent at 0.995c takes 10 μs in the Earth frame; γ = 10.0.
  • Earth frame, time dilation: half-life 10.0 × 1.5 = 15 μs. 10 μs is 0.67 half-lives: 63% survive. Without dilation: 6.7 half-lives, 1%.
  • Muon frame, length contraction: the muon's half-life is 1.5 μs, but the 3.0 km becomes 300 m, crossed in 1.0 μs: 0.67 half-lives, 63% again.
  • Both frames predict the same count. Experiments (e.g. Frisch and Smith, 1963) agree.
Quick check
?Muons are produced 4.5 km above the ground (measured in the Earth's frame) and travel straight down at 0.98c. What is the thickness of this 4.5 km layer of atmosphere in the muons' rest frame?
Mission check

?A spacecraft passes the Earth at 0.80c. A lamp on the spacecraft flashes once every 6.00 min, measured by a clock on the spacecraft. Earth observers time the flashes using clocks at rest in the Earth's frame. What time interval between flashes do they measure?

The spacecraft clock is the one present at every flash: it measures the proper time, and everyone else measures more. Muons are clocks too: from the ground their half-life is about 10 times longer, so many reach sea level.

Mission 5

Events and world lines

  • An event: one place, one instant. Plotted at (x, ct).
  • Space–time diagram: x across, ct up, same scale (both in metres or light-years).
  • World line: the path of an object through space–time.
  • At rest: a vertical line. Light: a line at 45°.
  • Constant velocity v: a straight line at angle θ to the ct axis, with tan θ = v/c.
  • Any object with mass: θ < 45°.
Quick check
?On a space–time diagram with axes x and ct, the world line of a particle moving at constant velocity makes an angle of 25° with the ct axis. What is the speed of the particle?
Mission 5

The space–time interval

  • Two events. Different observers measure different Δt and Δx.
  • They all agree on (Δs)² = (cΔt)² − (Δx)²: the space–time interval is invariant.
  • Note the minus sign: not Pythagoras.
  • Events at the same place in a frame (Δx = 0): Δs = cΔt0, the proper time × c.
  • In every other frame Δx ≠ 0, so cΔt is larger: time dilation, from the interval alone.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Frame S: a clock ticks twice at the same place, 4.0 μs apart. cΔt = (3.00 × 10⁸)(4.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1200 m; Δx = 0.
  • (Δs)² = 1200² − 0² = 1.44 × 10⁶ m².
  • Frame S′: the two ticks are 900 m apart.
  • (cΔt′)² = (Δs)² + (Δx′)² = 1.44 × 10⁶ + 0.81 × 10⁶ = 2.25 × 10⁶ m² → cΔt′ = 1500 m.
  • Δt′ = 1500 ÷ (3.00 × 10⁸) = 5.0 μs: longer than the proper time, 4.0 μs.
Quick check
?In frame S, two events are 2.0 μs apart in time and 300 m apart in space. In frame S′ they are 450 m apart in space. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the time between them in S′?
Mission 5

A second frame on the same diagram

  • S′ moves at v along +x. Draw its axes on S's diagram.
  • ct′ axis: the world line of S′'s origin (x′ = 0). Tilted from ct by θ, tan θ = v/c.
  • x′ axis: all events at ct′ = 0. Tilted up from x by the same θ.
  • The two primed axes close in on the 45° light line like scissors. Light stays at 45° in both frames.
  • To read an event's ct′: go parallel to x′ to the ct′ axis. For x′: go parallel to ct′.
Quick check
?On a space–time diagram of frame S (x across, ct up, equal scales), the ct′ axis of frame S′ makes an angle of 20° with the ct axis. Which describes the x′ axis of S′, and the speed of S′ relative to S?
Mission 5

Simultaneity is relative

  • Simultaneous in S: on a line parallel to the x axis.
  • Simultaneous in S′: on a line parallel to the x′ axis, which is tilted.
  • Two events on one horizontal line are not on one line parallel to x′. S′ says they happen at different times.
  • S′ moving along +x: of two events simultaneous in S, the one further along +x happens first in S′.
  • Not an illusion of light travel time: this is after allowing for it.
  • Events at the same place and same time are simultaneous for everyone.
Quick check
?A lamp at the exact centre of a railway carriage flashes once. The carriage moves at constant high speed past a platform. In the carriage frame, the light reaches the front and rear doors at the same time. According to an observer on the platform, what happens?
Mission check

?A space–time diagram for frame S has axes x and ct in light-years (ly). Frame S′ moves at 0.25c along +x, so its x′ axis rises 0.25 ly of ct for each 1 ly of x. Event A is at (x = 1.0 ly, ct = 3.0 ly) and event B at (x = 5.0 ly, ct = 3.5 ly). In which order do A and B happen in S′?

Yes. "At the same time" depends on the frame. Observers agree on the space–time interval between two events, not on the time between them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Galilean velocity addition: u′ = u − v; valid only for speeds much less than c.

Mission 2

  • Inertial frame: not accelerating. Galilean relativity: Newton's laws are the same in all inertial frames; x′ = x − vt, t′ = t.
  • Postulates: (1) the laws of physics are the same in all inertial frames; (2) c in a vacuum is the same for all inertial observers.

Mission 3

  • x′ = γ(x − vt), t′ = γ(t − vx/c²); events simultaneous in one frame need not be in another.
  • u′ = (u − v)/(1 − uv/c²): velocities never combine to more than c.

Mission 4

  • Proper time: two events at the same place (shortest interval). Proper length: rest frame (longest). Δt = γΔt0; L = L0/γ, along the motion only.
  • Muons: Earth frame, dilated half-life; muon frame, contracted atmosphere. Same survival in both.

Mission 5

  • Space–time diagrams: ct up, x across; world lines at θ to the ct axis with tan θ = v/c; light at 45°.
  • (Δs)² = (cΔt)² − (Δx)² is the same in every inertial frame; simultaneity is not.
