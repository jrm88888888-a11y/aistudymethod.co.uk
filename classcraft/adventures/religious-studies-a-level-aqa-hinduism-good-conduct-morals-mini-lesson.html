Mission 1

What "yoga" means

  • Yoga from Sanskrit yuj: "to yoke", "to join". A discipline that unites the self with the ultimate.
  • Also called a marga (path): a way to moksha, liberation from samsara (the cycle of rebirth).
  • The Bhagavad Gita teaches several yogas side by side; each chapter is titled a yoga.
  • Not mainly postures: the postures belong to hatha yoga (see Mission 2).
  • Key belief shared by all paths: karma and ignorance bind the atman; the right discipline frees it.
Mission 1

Different paths for different people

  • Hindus believe people differ in temperament, so different paths suit different characters:
  • Jnana yoga: path of knowledge, for the reflective mind.
  • Karma yoga: path of action, for the active person.
  • Bhakti yoga: path of devotion, for the emotional heart.
  • Raja yoga: path of meditation, for the contemplative (see Mission 2).
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) popularised this fourfold scheme in his lectures Karma Yoga, Bhakti Yoga, Jnana Yoga and Raja Yoga (1896). The Gita itself mainly names three.
  • AQA asks for suitability and links: most Hindus combine paths.
Mission 1

Karma yoga: selfless action

  • Karma yoga: doing one's dharma (duty) without attachment to results (nishkama karma).
  • Action itself does not bind; desire for its fruits does.
  • Offer every action to God; this purifies the mind (chitta-shuddhi) of selfish desire.
  • Aim: identification with the transcendent, the self no longer acting for itself.
  • AQA calls it selfless effort: good conduct done for its own sake, not to earn good karma.

> "Therefore, thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform, / Since in performance of plain duty man / Mounts to his highest bliss." (Bhagavad Gita 3:19, Arnold)

Mission 1Read the source

No one can stop acting

> "No man shall 'scape from act / By shunning action; nay, and none shall come / By mere renouncements unto perfectness. / Nay, and no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act" (Bhagavad Gita 3:4–5, Arnold)

  • Krishna answers Arjuna's wish to escape karma by not acting.

Q1 Why is "shunning action" impossible, according to Krishna?

Show answer

Our nature drives us to act all the time; even thinking is a kind of action.

Q2 What, then, is the way to escape karma?

Show answer

Act without attachment: karma yoga, the earlier screen.

Mission 1

Jnana yoga: the path of wisdom

  • Jnana: knowledge, not facts but direct realisation that atman is Brahman.
  • Method: viveka (discrimination between real and unreal), vairagya (renunciation), study of the Upanishads with a guru: hearing, reflecting, meditating.
  • Aim: identification with Brahman; ignorance (avidya) ends, and with it rebirth.
  • Classically linked to Advaita Vedanta and Shankara.

Mission 1

Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to a personal God, often Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Open to everyone, whatever caste or learning (Gita 9:32;).
  • Practices: puja, chanting the divine name, singing (kirtan, bhajan), pilgrimage.
  • Liberation is often by God's grace, and may be eternal loving union rather than merging (Ramanuja).

> "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known / And seen, and entered into, Indian Prince!" (Bhagavad Gita 11:54, Arnold)

Mission 2

Raja yoga: the path of meditation

  • Raja yoga, the "royal" path: control of the mind through meditation.
  • Main text: the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (about 195 short sutras; dating debated, many scholars place them around 400 CE).
  • Definition in Yoga Sutra 1.2 (paraphrase): yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind (chitta vritti nirodha).
  • When the mind is still, the self rests in its own true nature (1.3).
  • The name "raja yoga" for Patanjali's system was popularised by Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896).
  • Links: jnana yoga needs meditation to turn knowledge into realisation (see Mission 1).
Mission 2

The eight limbs (ashtanga)

Yoga Sutra 2.29 lists eight limbs, from outer to inner:

  • 1 Yama: restraints (ahimsa, truthfulness, non-stealing, brahmacharya, non-possessiveness).
  • 2 Niyama: observances (purity, contentment, austerity, self-study, surrender to Ishvara).
  • 3 Asana: a steady, comfortable seat (2.46). 4 Pranayama: breath control.
  • 5 Pratyahara: withdrawing the senses. 6 Dharana: concentration. 7 Dhyana: meditation.
  • 8 Samadhi: absorption.
  • Goal: kaivalya, the self freed from entanglement with matter.
  • Ethics first: postures and breath come only after yama and niyama.

Mission 2

Hatha yoga

  • Developed c. 11th–15th centuries, especially among the Nath yogis (traditionally founded by Gorakshanath).
  • Key text: Hatha Yoga Pradipika (Svatmarama, 15th century).
  • Practices: asanas, pranayama, cleansing techniques (shatkarma), locks (bandha) and seals (mudra).
  • Aim: purify the body and its subtle channels, awaken kundalini energy, and realise the body's divine nature; also strength and health.
  • Hatha means "force": a forceful, physical method.
  • The Hatha Yoga Pradipika presents hatha as a stairway to raja yoga (1.1–2, paraphrase).
Mission 2Read the source

Hatha serves raja

Hatha Yoga Pradipika 1.1–2 (paraphrase):

  • The author bows to Shiva, who taught hatha yoga as a stairway for those who wish to climb to raja yoga.
  • He teaches hatha only for the sake of raja yoga.

Q1 How does the text see the relationship between hatha and raja yoga?

Show answer

Hatha is a means; raja yoga, the stilled mind, is the goal.

Q2 What would this text say about postures practised only for fitness?

Show answer

They miss the point: the body is trained to serve meditation and liberation.

Mission 2

How yoga came West

  • 1893: Vivekananda speaks at the World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago; Raja Yoga (1896) presents yoga as meditation.
  • 1920: Paramahansa Yogananda arrives in the USA; Autobiography of a Yogi (1946).
  • 1930s–60s: T. Krishnamacharya (1888–1989) in Mysore develops dynamic posture sequences; students B. K. S. Iyengar (Light on Yoga, 1966), K. Pattabhi Jois, Indra Devi.
  • 1936: Swami Sivananda founds the Divine Life Society, Rishikesh; Swami Vishnudevananda opens Sivananda centres in the West from 1959.
  • 1960s–70s: Maharishi Mahesh Yogi; the Beatles in Rishikesh (1968).
  • 2015: first International Day of Yoga (21 June), set by the UN in 2014.
  • Ashrams such as the Sivananda ashram in Kerala.
Mission 2

New purposes

  • Much Western yoga focuses on fitness, flexibility and stress relief, often with no religious aim.
  • Scholar Mark Singleton, Yoga Body (2010), paraphrase: modern posture yoga blends hatha with Indian and Western physical culture (gymnastics, body-building) of the early 20th century.
  • Some Western teachers keep mantra, meditation and ethics; others teach postures alone.
  • Back in India, posture yoga is now also promoted for health and as national heritage.
  • Purpose changes significance: for liberation (moksha), health, or both.

Mission 3

Dharma: what holds things up

  • Dharma comes from the root dhr: to hold, uphold, support.
  • English needs many words for it: duty, righteousness, law, virtue, religion, the right way.
  • It also means a thing's nature: the dharma of fire is to burn; of water, to flow.
  • So dharma is at once cosmic (how the world is ordered), social (how society works) and personal (how I should live).
  • A much-quoted saying: dharma protects those who protect it (Manusmriti 8.15, paraphrase).
Mission 3

Sanatana dharma: the eternal way

  • Sanatana means eternal: without beginning or end.
  • Sanatana dharma has two common uses:
  • 1. A name for the religion. Many Hindus prefer it to "Hinduism" (a name from outside): their way is not founded at a point in time but is eternal truth.
  • 2. Universal dharma. Duties for everyone, whatever their class or stage of life (also called sadharana dharma, common dharma).
  • Manusmriti 10.63 (paraphrase) sums them up: non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, control of the senses.
  • Your spec: "Sanatana dharma (universal dharma)".
Mission 3

Universal and personal dharma

  • Alongside universal dharma, each person has svadharma: their own dharma.
  • Traditionally this is fixed by varna (class) and ashrama (stage of life): varnashrama dharma.
  • Krishna to Arjuna:

> "this is better, that one do / His own task as he may, even though he fail, / Than take tasks not his own, though they seem good." (Gita 3:35, Arnold)

  • Tension: Arjuna's universal dharma says "do not harm"; his svadharma as a warrior says "fight a just war". The Gita is built on that conflict.
  • Your spec asks for "the relationship between them".

When dharma declines

  • Adharma: the opposite of dharma: disorder, injustice, unrighteousness. It harms the world, society and the self, and brings bad karma.

> "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape, and move a man with men, / Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back, / And setting Virtue on her seat again." (Gita 4:7–8, Arnold)

  • Arnold translates dharma as "Righteousness" and adharma as "Wickedness".
  • Tradition pictures dharma as a bull that loses a leg in each of four ages (yugas); many Hindus believe we live in the last, the Kali Yuga.

Show answer

Mission 3Evaluate

Universal or personal dharma first?

  • Claim: Universal (sanatana) dharma matters more than the dharma of one's own class and stage of life.
  • For: It binds everyone; non-violence and truth are the "summary" of dharma (Manusmriti 10.63).
  • For: Reformers appealed to universal values such as non-violence and equal dignity to attack untouchability (e.g. Gandhi).
  • Against: The Gita (3:35, 18:47) says doing your own dharma, even imperfectly, is better than another's.
  • Against: Society works only if each role is filled; svadharma gives the universal a concrete shape.
  • Judgement criteria: Which fits the texts? Which guides real choices? Which fits Hinduism in a modern, plural society?

Mission 4

What ahimsa asks

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, non-harming (a- "not" + himsa "harm").
  • It covers thought, word and deed: hatred and cruel speech are himsa too.
  • It also covers harm you cause or approve through others.
  • Why: the same atman is in every living being.

> "… sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living, and all living things / In that Life-Soul contained." (Bhagavad Gita 6:29, Arnold)

  • Karma: harm returns as suffering, in this life or the next.
  • Positive side: compassion and respect for all life.
  • AQA calls ahimsa a virtue: a quality of character, not just a rule.
Mission 4

Ahimsa in Hindu tradition

  • Chandogya Upanishad 3.17.4: non-violence is listed with austerity, generosity, uprightness and truthfulness as the "gifts" of a life lived as sacrifice (paraphrase).
  • Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva): ahimsa is called the highest dharma (ahimsa paramo dharmah).
  • Yoga Sutras 2.30–2.35: ahimsa is the first yama (restraint); where it is firmly established, hostility ceases (paraphrase).
  • Manusmrti 10.63: ahimsa is among the duties common to all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • But Hindu ahimsa is usually weighed against other duties: sacrifice in the Vedic period, the warrior's duty, self-defence. It means minimising harm rather than an absolute vow.
  • By contrast, Jains (an Indian tradition with ahimsa at its heart) take it further: monks and nuns vow ahimsa absolutely, some sweeping their path to protect tiny beings; Jains hold that harm binds karma to the soul.
  • AQA spellings: smrti, Manusmrti.

Mission 4

Animals

  • Animals have atman and are part of samsara: a human may be reborn as an animal, and an animal as a human.
  • The cow is honoured as a mother (gau mata): she gives milk, and Krishna was a cowherd. Most Hindus do not eat beef; many states in India restrict cow slaughter.
  • Vegetarianism is widespread and seen as sattvic, but varies: many Bengali and coastal Hindus eat fish; some communities eat meat; some Shakta temples still offer animal sacrifice.
  • Many Hindus support gaushalas (cow shelters) and avoid leather, silk or products tested on animals; others accept animal testing for human medicine as a lesser harm.
  • Treatment of animals is named in your spec: give different Hindu views.
Mission 4Read the source

Manusmrti on killing animals

Manusmrti, chapter 5 (paraphrase):

  • 5.38: whoever kills an animal without lawful reason will suffer a violent death in later births, as many times as the animal has hairs.
  • 5.45: one who harms harmless creatures for his own pleasure finds no happiness, living or dead.
  • 5.48–51: meat cannot be got without harming living beings, so avoid it. The one who permits, cuts, kills, buys, sells, cooks, serves or eats the meat all share in the killing.
  • But 5.39–44: killing an animal in Vedic sacrifice is said not to be killing.

Q1 According to 5.51, does a person who only buys meat escape responsibility?

Show answer

No: buyer, cook and eater all count as slayers.

Q2 What tension does 5.39–44 show inside the text?

Show answer

Ahimsa is the ideal, but older ritual violence is allowed as an exception.

Mission 4

Harm you cannot avoid

  • Manusmrti 3.68–69 (paraphrase): every householder has five "slaughter-houses": the hearth, the grinding stone, the broom, the pestle and mortar, the water pot. Small creatures die through them every day.
  • So the householder performs five daily sacrifices (to the gods, the sages, the ancestors, all beings and guests) to make amends.
  • Point: Hindu ahimsa accepts that some harm is unavoidable; the duty is to keep it as small as possible and to act with care and intention not to harm.
  • Contrast: Jain monks organise their whole life to avoid even this harm.
Mission 4

The embryo and the unborn child

  • Many Hindus believe the atman is present from conception, so the embryo is a living being in samsara.
  • Dharma texts class killing an embryo (bhruna-hatya) among the gravest sins.
  • Many Hindus believe abortion creates bad karma for those who take part, and interrupts the karmic journey of the soul being born.
  • Sex-selective abortion of girls is widely condemned by Hindu leaders as himsa; it is illegal in India (PCPNDT Act, 1994).
  • Indian law allows abortion in set circumstances (Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971, amended 2021). Many Hindus accept it where the mother's life is at risk.

Mission 4Evaluate

Can a person live by ahimsa today?

  • Claim: Ahimsa, meaning never harming any living being, is a realistic standard in the modern world.
  • For: Millions of Jains and Hindus live as strict vegetarians; ahimsa shapes diet, work and medicine.
  • For: Ahimsa is about intention and minimising harm, which anyone can practise.
  • Against: Harm is built into life: farming, transport, medicine. Even the Manusmrti, a Hindu law text, admits that daily housework kills small creatures (3.68).
  • Against: Modern supply chains hide harm; the Manusmrti says the buyer of meat shares in the killing (5.51).
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa an absolute rule or an ideal to approach? Does intention or outcome matter more? Is Jain or Hindu ahimsa the standard being tested?

Mission 5

Ahimsa meets the warrior's duty

  • In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna, a kshatriya (warrior), refuses to fight his relatives at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna answers: a warrior's svadharma (own duty) is to fight a righteous war (dharma yuddha); refusing would be sin.

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul / Than lawful war" (Bhagavad Gita 2:31, Arnold)

  • The atman cannot be killed (2:19–20), so the soldier destroys only bodies.
  • So in traditional Hindu ethics, ahimsa is the general rule, but force to protect dharma can be a duty for those whose role it is.
Mission 5

Dharma yuddha: rules of righteous war

  • Just cause: to defend dharma, protect the weak, resist aggression; not greed.
  • Last resort: in the Mahabharata, Krishna goes as envoy to seek peace before the war.
  • Rules of conduct (Mahabharata; Manusmrti 7.90–93, paraphrase): fight only equals and armed opponents; do not strike those who surrender, flee, sleep or have lost their weapons; no hidden, poisoned or barbed weapons; spare non-combatants.
  • Even in war, the warrior should fight without hatred and without desire for gain.
  • AQA spelling: Manusmrti.
Mission 5Read the source

Two ways to read the battle

> "… if thou shunn'st / This honourable field--a Kshattriya-- / If, knowing thy duty and thy task, thou bidd'st / Duty and task go by--that shall be sin!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:33, Arnold)

  • Literal reading (e.g. Sri Aurobindo, 1872–1950, Essays on the Gita): the Gita really does teach that righteous war can be a duty.
  • Allegorical reading (Gandhi, Anasaktiyoga, 1929): Kurukshetra is the human heart; the battle is between good and evil impulses; the Gita teaches selfless action and so, in the end, non-violence.

Q1 What does Krishna say Arjuna's refusal would be?

Show answer

Sin: a failure in his duty as a kshatriya.

Q2 How can Gandhi accept this verse yet reject war?

Show answer

He reads the "field" and the "battle" as the inner struggle of the soul, not a call to physical fighting.

Mission 5

Weapons of mass destruction

  • The epics describe the brahmastra, a weapon able to destroy the world. In the Mahabharata, sages step between two such weapons and order them withdrawn: some weapons must never be used.
  • WMD break dharma yuddha rules: they cannot spare non-combatants, they poison land and future generations, and they kill without distinction.
  • J. Robert Oppenheimer recalled Gita 11:32 at the first atomic test (1945): "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds" (his own rendering, recalled in 1965).
  • Gandhi (paraphrase of his 1946 writing): the atom bomb was a devilish use of science and showed the futility of all violence; it will not be destroyed by counter-bombs, only by non-violence.
  • Other views: India tested nuclear weapons (1974, 1998) and holds a no-first-use policy; some Hindus defend this as deterrence to protect dharma and the nation; many others hold that WMD can never fit ahimsa.
Mission 5

Gandhi's ahimsa

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948).
  • Influences: his Gujarati Vaishnava home, Jain friends and teachers (e.g. Raychandbhai), the Gita, Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Ahimsa for Gandhi is active love, not passivity: refusing to hate, while resisting injustice.
  • It is the courage of the strong: he wrote (1920, paraphrase) that if the only choice were cowardice or violence, he would choose violence.
  • Linked with truth: he came to say that "Truth is God". Non-violence is the means; truth is the end.
  • Discipline: brahmacharya, simple living, vegetarianism, fasting.
Mission 5

Satyagraha: truth force

  • Satyagraha: satya (truth) + agraha (holding firmly): "truth force" or "soul force". Name chosen in South Africa, 1906–08.
  • Method: non-violent resistance to unjust laws: non-cooperation, boycott, strikes, marches, civil disobedience, fasting.
  • The satyagrahi accepts suffering (arrest, beatings) rather than inflicts it, aiming to convert the opponent, not to defeat them.
  • Rules: no hatred, no secrecy, no damage to persons, accept the legal penalty.
  • Salt March (12 March – 6 April 1930): about 385 km to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is ahimsa a workable standard in the modern world?

  • Claim: Ahimsa is a relevant and viable standard to live by today.
  • For: Gandhi's non-violent resistance (satyagraha, "truth force") helped win Indian independence (1947) and shaped the US civil rights movement.
  • For: Modern weapons make war's harm uncontrollable; non-violence may be the only rational path.
  • Against: Non-violence relies on the opponent's conscience. Gandhi's 1938 advice that Jews in Germany resist non-violently was strongly criticised (e.g. by Martin Buber, 1939).
  • Against: Independence came with the violence of Partition; India itself keeps an army and nuclear weapons.
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa a personal virtue or a state policy? Does it require results to be valid? Does dharma yuddha already make ahimsa realistic?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Yoga ("joining") is a disciplined path (marga) to moksha; different paths suit different temperaments.
  • Karma yoga: duty without attachment; jnana yoga: knowledge that atman is Brahman; bhakti yoga: loving devotion to a personal God.

Mission 2

  • Raja yoga (Patanjali) stills the mind through eight limbs, built on Samkhya dualism, aiming at kaivalya.
  • Hatha yoga purifies the body as a stairway to raja yoga; modern posture yoga grew from it and from physical culture.

Mission 3

  • Dharma (from dhr, "uphold"): the order that sustains the cosmos, society and the person; the successor to Vedic rta.
  • Sanatana dharma: the eternal way (a name for the religion) and the universal duties of all; svadharma: duties of one's own class and stage of life.

Mission 4

  • Ahimsa is non-harming in thought, word and deed, based on atman in all beings and on karma.
  • Applied to animals (vegetarianism, cow protection) and the embryo (life from conception; views differ on the lesser harm).

Mission 5

  • Traditional Hindu ethics weighs ahimsa against a warrior's duty: dharma yuddha allows just war under strict rules.
  • Gandhi read the Gita as an inner battle and made ahimsa a political force through satyagraha.
  • WMD break the rules of righteous war; some Hindus accept nuclear deterrence, many reject it.
