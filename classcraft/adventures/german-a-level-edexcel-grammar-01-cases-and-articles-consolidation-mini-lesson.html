A-Level Grammar

Cases & the article system

German marks the job a noun does in a sentence by changing the article (and sometimes the noun) — this is case. Master the four cases and the whole language falls into place: word order, prepositions, adjectives and pronouns all depend on them. This lesson consolidates Nominativ, Akkusativ, Dativ and Genitiv.

der Hund → den Hund → dem Hund → des Hundessame noun, four cases — the article carries the meaning

The big picture

Four cases, four jobs

Every noun phrase sits in one of four cases, decided by its role in the clause:

  • Nominativ — the subject (who does it) and the complement after sein / werden / bleiben: Der Hund schläft.
  • Akkusativ — the direct object (who receives the action): Ich sehe den Hund.
  • Dativ — the indirect object (to/for whom): Ich gebe dem Hund einen Knochen.
  • Genitiv — possession (whose): das Halsband des Hundes.

Ask two questions: Wer/Was? finds the Nominativ subject; Wen/Was? finds the Akkusativ; Wem? finds the Dativ; Wessen? finds the Genitiv.

Uses · Nom & Akk

Subject, object & time phrases

The Nominativ is the doer and the "equals" complement; the Akkusativ is the direct object — and also definite time expressions:

  • Der Zug ist pünktlich. — nominative subject.
  • Sie kauft einen neuen Wagen. — accusative object.
  • Wir bleiben einen Monat in Berlin. / jeden Tag, nächste Woche — accusative of time.

Exam gold: phrases like letzten Sommer, jeden Morgen, einen Monat lang are accusative even though there is no obvious "object" — a reliable way to show control of the case system.

Uses · Dativ

The dative & dative verbs

The Dativ is the indirect object (the receiver), but a group of common verbs governs it directly with no accusative at all:

  • helfen, danken, folgen, gefallen, gehören, antworten, begegnen, gratulieren, passen — all + Dativ.
  • Ich danke dem Lehrer. — I thank the teacher (dative, no accusative).
  • Das Buch gehört meiner Schwester. — the book belongs to my sister.
  • Der Film gefällt mir. — literally "the film is pleasing to me" = I like the film.

Trap: "I like the film" is Der Film gefällt mir — the thing liked is the subject, the person is dative. Never *"ich gefalle den Film".

Uses · Genitiv

The genitive — whose?

The Genitiv shows possession and follows a handful of prepositions. Masculine and neuter nouns add -s (or -es after one syllable):

  • das Auto des Vaters — the father's car.
  • die Meinung der Studenten — the students' opinion.
  • After prepositions: während der Woche, trotz des Regens, wegen des Wetters, statt eines Buches.

Register note: in speech the genitive is often replaced by von + dative (das Auto von meinem Vater), but in essays the true genitive reads as more formal and precise — use it.

Weak masculine nouns

The n-declension

A special group of masculine nouns (the "n-nouns") add -(e)n in every case except the nominative singular:

caseder Studentder Jungeder Herr
Nominativder Studentder Jungeder Herr
Akkusativden Studentenden Jungenden Herrn
Dativdem Studentendem Jungendem Herrn
Genitivdes Studentendes Jungendes Herrn

Members: people and animals such as der Student, der Junge, der Kollege, der Kunde, der Mensch, der Nachbar, der Löwe, der Präsident, der Journalist. Herr adds -n in the singular but -en in the plural (die Herren).

Recap

Your case toolkit

Nominativ: subject + complement after sein/werden/bleiben (Wer/Was?).

Akkusativ: direct object + definite time (den, einen; jeden Tag).

Dativ: indirect object + dative verbs (helfen, danken, gefallen, gehören); dative plural adds -n.

Genitiv: possession + während/trotz/wegen/statt; masc./neut. add -(e)s.

Weak nouns: Student, Junge, Herr, Mensch add -(e)n everywhere but the nominative singular.
