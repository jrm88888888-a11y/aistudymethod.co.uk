The umlauts

ä, ö, ü — and why the dots matter

The two dots change both the sound and the word. They are proper letters, not decoration:

älike "e" in bedMädchen, spät
ölips rounded, like "ur"schön, hören
üsay "ee" with rounded lipsüber, müde

Spelling rule: keep the umlaut. schön is not schoen, über is not ueber. In an exam, a missing umlaut can change or spoil the word.

The letter ß

ß — the "sharp s" (Eszett)

ß is a single letter that sounds like a sharp "ss". It follows a long vowel or a diphthong:

  • heißen (to be called), Straße (street), groß (big), weiß (white).
  • After a short vowel you write ss instead: Wasser, muss, Fluss.

Don't swap it for "ss" or "b": Straße is not Strasse in standard spelling, and never Strabe. Write the ß.

The famous vowel pair

ei vs ie — say the second letter

An easy trick: pronounce the name of the second letter:

ei = English "eye" · ie = English "ee"mein = "mine" · vier = "feer"
  • ei → mein, drei, Eis, weiß, Zeit (all with the "eye" sound).
  • ie → vier, sieben, Liebe, spielen, die (all with the "ee" sound).

This one small rule fixes a huge share of reading-aloud mistakes. Second letter = the sound.

Tricky consonants

w, v, z, ch, sch, j

w= English vWasser ≈ "Vasser"
v= English fVater ≈ "Fater"
z= tsZeit ≈ "tseit"
j= English yja ≈ "ya"
sch= English shSchule ≈ "shoole"

The big one: w = v and v = f. So wie viel sounds like "vee feel".

Word edges

st-, sp- and final -er

  • Initial st- and sp- are said "sht" and "shp": Stadt ≈ "shtat", Sport ≈ "shport", spielen ≈ "shpeelen".
  • Final -er softens to a quiet "-uh": Vater ≈ "Fah-tuh", Bruder ≈ "Broo-duh".
  • Final -d/-b/-g harden: Hund ≈ "Hunt", Tag ≈ "Tak".

These edge-sounds are exactly what examiners listen for in the read-aloud and photo card. Nail the "sht/shp" start and you sound instantly more German.

The four papers

How AQA 8662 is assessed

Four equally-weighted papers (25% each):

paperwatch out for
Listeningdistractors; note negatives & numbers
Speakingphoto card, role-play, conversation
Readingincl. translation into English
Writingincl. translation into German

Grammar earns marks everywhere: genders, cases, verb endings and word order are exactly what lifts writing and speaking into the top bands.

Speaking

Photo card & role-play

  • Photo card: start with Auf dem Foto sehe ich… ("in the photo I see…"), then say where, who, what they're doing and your opinion. Use a past and a future sentence to show tense range.
  • Role-play: read the prompts carefully — one needs a question (Wann…? Wie viel…?) and one is often unexpected. Keep answers short and correct.

Bank three "upgrade" phrases: meiner Meinung nach…, weil es … ist, and ich würde gern…. Drop one into every answer.

Translation & reading/listening

Working the receptive papers

  • Into German: check gender + case, verb ending, and word order (verb second; verb to the end after weil/dass). Translate the meaning, not word-for-word.
  • Into English: write natural English; watch tenses (Perfekt = simple past) and don't leave gaps.
  • Listening/Reading: beware distractors and negatives (nicht, kein, nie) — they flip the meaning. Note numbers and times carefully.

Golden rule: attempt every mark. A sensible guess beats a blank, and partial answers still score.

Recap

Your sounds & exam toolkit

Umlauts: ä ("bed"), ö ("ur"), ü ("ee" with round lips) — always keep the dots (schön, über).

ß after long vowels/diphthongs (heißen, Straße); ss after short vowels (muss).

ei = "eye", ie = "ee" — say the second letter (mein vs vier).

Consonants: w = v, v = f, z = ts, j = y, sch = sh; initial st-/sp- = "sht/shp"; final -er = soft "-uh".

Exam: photo card + role-play formulas, translate for meaning, watch negatives/distractors, and attempt every mark.
