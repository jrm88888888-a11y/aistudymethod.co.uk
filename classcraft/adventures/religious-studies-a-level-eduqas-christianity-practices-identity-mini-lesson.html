Mission 1

What is baptism for?

  • Baptism: the rite of entry into the Church, using water, "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Almost all Christians baptise. They disagree about who and what it does.
  • Sacrament view (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran): God acts in baptism; it gives grace, washes away sin and brings new birth.
  • Ordinance view (Baptists, many evangelicals and Pentecostals): an act of obedience and public witness to a faith already received.
  • If baptism gives grace, babies need it. If it declares faith, it should wait for faith.
  • Baptism is held to be once only. Catholics recognise any baptism with water in the name of the Trinity; most Baptist churches baptise believers who were baptised as infants, holding that infant baptism was not true baptism.

Baptism in the New Testament

> "Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are far off" (Acts 2:38–39, WEBBE)

> "He took them the same hour of the night and washed their stripes, and was immediately baptised, he and all his household." (Acts 16:33, WEBBE)

> "We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4, WEBBE)

Q1 Which words in Acts 2:38–39 support infant baptism, and which support believer's baptism?

Show answer

"To your children" for infants; "Repent" first, for believers.

Q2 Why might both sides cite Acts 16:33?

Show answer

A whole household is baptised, which may have included children; but no child is mentioned.

  • No New Testament text clearly records or forbids the baptism of an infant.
Mission 1

Infant baptism in the Catholic Church

  • Usually within weeks of birth, in the parish church at the font.
  • Parents and godparents reject sin and profess the Creed for the child, and promise to raise the child in the faith.
  • The priest or deacon pours water three times (or immerses): "I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit."
  • Chrism (anointing), a white garment (new life in Christ), a candle lit from the Easter candle (Christ the light).
  • Meaning: washes away original sin, gives sanctifying grace, makes the child a member of the Church; it leaves a permanent mark and is never repeated.
  • Faith is then completed later: First Communion and Confirmation.
Mission 1

Augustine: baptism and salvation

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), especially On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants (412), against the Pelagians.
  • Original sin: every human inherits Adam's guilt and a damaged nature, before any choice of their own.
  • So infants need forgiveness: baptism washes away the guilt of original sin.
  • Role in salvation: without baptism an infant cannot enter the Kingdom (John 3:5). He held that unbaptised infants are condemned, though to the mildest punishment (Enchiridion 93).
  • The Church's practice proves it: the Church already baptised infants "for the forgiveness of sins"; this only makes sense if infants have sin.
  • Parents and Church: the infant believes through the faith of the Church, voiced by those who bring the child (Letter 98).
Mission 1Read the source

Augustine on his own delayed baptism

Augustine was not baptised as a child. When he fell ill as a boy, his mother prepared his baptism, then delayed it when he recovered.

> "I beseech Thee, my God, I would fain know, if so Thou willest, for what purpose my baptism was then deferred? … How much better then, had I been at once healed" (Augustine, Confessions I.11, trans. Pusey)

  • Delay was common in his time: people waited so that baptism would wash away the sins of youth.

Q1 What does Augustine compare baptism to?

Show answer

Healing: like a cure for a wounded body.

Q2 How does this passage support infant baptism?

Show answer

Delay left him exposed to sin without grace; he judges it would have been better to be baptised at once.

Mission 1

Zwingli: the sign of God's covenant

  • Huldrych Zwingli (1484–1531), reformer of Zurich; Of Baptism (1525).
  • Baptism does not wash away sin or give grace by itself; it is a sign and a pledge.
  • Covenant: as Jewish boys received circumcision as the sign of God's covenant, Christian children receive baptism (Colossians 2:11–12).
  • Children of believers already belong to God's people; baptism marks it publicly.
  • Role of parents: baptism is the parents' and Church's pledge to bring up the child in the faith.
  • He defended infant baptism against the Anabaptists (re-baptisers) of Zurich, who baptised only believers from 1525.
  • So Zwingli supports infant baptism without Augustine's view that it saves.

Mission 1

Believer's baptism in Baptist churches

  • Baptist churches (from 1609, John Smyth's congregation in Amsterdam) baptise only those who profess faith themselves.
  • Usually teenagers or adults, by full immersion in a baptistry (pool) at the front of the church or in a river.
  • The candidate gives a testimony and answers questions about their faith.
  • Going under the water and rising shows dying and rising with Christ (Romans 6:4).
  • Most Baptists call it an ordinance: an act of obedience to Christ's command, not a means of saving grace. Salvation comes through faith.
  • For babies, many Baptist churches hold a service of thanksgiving and dedication: parents promise to raise the child; the child decides later.
Mission 1

Barth: the example of Christ and consent

  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Swiss Reformed theologian; The Teaching of the Church Regarding Baptism (1943 lecture) and Church Dogmatics IV/4 (1967).
  • A surprise: Barth belonged to a church that baptises infants, yet argued for believer's baptism.
  • The example of Christ: Jesus came to John at the Jordan and chose baptism himself (Matthew 3:13–15). Christian baptism follows that free, willing act.
  • Consent: baptism is a person's answer to God's grace. Someone who cannot answer cannot truly be baptised; infant baptism lacks the candidate's own responsible decision.
  • He held infant baptism was valid but seriously irregular, and did not call for re-baptism.
  • He also feared that baptising everyone at birth produced nominal Christians in state churches.
  • Eduqas names exactly these two themes for Barth: the example of Christ; the importance of consent.

Mission 1

What the early Church did

  • Didache 7 (c. 100): baptise in the name of the Trinity, in running water if possible, otherwise pour water three times; fast first. No mention of infants.
  • Justin Martyr, First Apology 61 (c. 155): those who are persuaded and believe are baptised; baptism is "illumination".
  • Hippolytus (attrib.), Apostolic Tradition 21 (early 3rd century): baptise the little ones first; if they cannot speak, parents or relatives answer for them.
  • Tertullian, On Baptism 18 (c. 200): delay is better, especially for little children; let them come when they can know Christ.
  • Cyprian of Carthage, Letter 64 to Fidus (c. 253): an African council rules that infants need not wait until the eighth day; baptise them at once.
  • So infant baptism was practised by about 200, and also debated.
Mission 1Evaluate

Should infants be baptised?

  • For: grace comes first; God's love does not wait for our choice (sacramental view).
  • For: covenant continuity with circumcision (Zwingli: as Jewish boys received circumcision as the covenant sign, Christian children receive baptism); household baptisms (Acts 16:15, 33); "to your children" (Acts 2:39).
  • For: parents rightly bring children into the family's faith, as into its language and home.
  • Against: no clear New Testament example; repentance and faith come first (Acts 2:38, 41).
  • Against: consent and the example of Christ (Karl Barth: Jesus came to baptism as an adult, and baptism needs the person's own yes); faith cannot be professed by proxy.
  • Against: risks nominal Christianity: baptised but never believing.
  • Is either just a symbolic act? Catholics: no, God really acts. Many Baptists: an act of obedience, though not merely symbolic, since it is commanded by Christ and marks a real commitment.
  • Judgement criteria: What does baptism do? How should silence in the New Testament be weighed? Whose faith counts: the person's or the Church's?
  • AO2 issues include "the extent to which both infant and adult baptism are just symbolic acts" and "the criteria for expressing the commitment to be baptised".

Mission 2: When Christians share bread and wine, what do they believe they are really receiving?

Make a guess: When Christians share bread and wine, what do they believe they are really receiving?

Mission 2

One meal, many names

  • Eucharist (Greek: "thanksgiving"), Mass (Catholic), Divine Liturgy (Orthodox), Holy Communion (Anglican, many Protestants), the Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread (Baptists, many evangelicals).
  • All look back to Jesus' Last Supper with his disciples, the night before he died.
  • Sacrament: "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace" (Book of Common Prayer, Catechism, 1662).
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help.
  • Catholics and Orthodox count seven sacraments; most Protestants two (baptism and the Eucharist); Baptists often say ordinance, not sacrament.
  • The big question: how, if at all, is Christ present in the bread and wine?
Mission 2Read the source

"This is my body"

> "He took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke and gave it to them, saying, "This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me." Likewise, he took the cup after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you."" (Luke 22:19–20, WEBBE)

> "For my flesh is food indeed, and my blood is drink indeed." (John 6:55, WEBBE)

  • Two key phrases: "This is my body" and "in memory of me" (Greek anamnesis, "remembrance"; Catholic and Orthodox teaching takes this as a remembrance that makes the saving event present, memorialists as a recalling of it).

Q1 Which phrase do Catholics stress most, and which do Baptists stress most?

Show answer

Catholics: "This is my body"; Baptists: "Do this in memory of me".

Q2 How does John 6:55 support a literal reading?

Show answer

It calls Jesus' flesh real food and his blood real drink.

Mission 2

Transubstantiation

  • Catholic teaching: at the consecration, the whole substance of the bread and wine is changed into the substance of Christ's body and blood.
  • The accidents (appearance, taste, texture, chemistry) remain those of bread and wine.
  • Terms from Aristotle: substance = what a thing is; accidents = how it appears to the senses.
  • Defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215); confirmed at the Council of Trent (1551) against the Reformers.
  • Christ is wholly present in each part, even a crumb; so the consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • This is the Catholic meaning of Real Presence: Christ truly, really and substantially present.
Mission 2Read the source

Aquinas on the change

> "the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body, and the whole substance of the wine into the whole substance of Christ's blood. Hence this is not a formal, but a substantial conversion; nor is it a kind of natural movement: but, with a name of its own, it can be called "transubstantiation."" (Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica III, Q. 75, Art. 4, English Dominican trans.)

  • In natural change, one form gives way to another in the same underlying matter (wood becomes ash). Here the whole substance changes, and the appearance remains.
  • Only God, the source of all being, can do this (Aquinas' argument in the same article).

Q1 Why does Aquinas say the change is not "a kind of natural movement"?

Show answer

No natural process changes a whole substance while leaving its appearance.

Q2 Why does the bread still taste like bread?

Show answer

The accidents remain; only the substance has changed.

Mission 2

Transignification and transfinalization

  • 1950s–60s: some Catholic theologians, e.g. Piet Schoonenberg (1911–1999) and Edward Schillebeeckx (1914–2009, The Eucharist, 1967), re-expressed the change without Aristotle's physics.
  • Transignification: the meaning of the bread changes. A thing's reality lies in what it is for persons; once Christ gives it as his self-gift, it means his body.
  • Transfinalization: the purpose (end) of the bread changes: no longer ordinary food, but the means of communion with Christ.
  • Analogy often used: a letter or a ring is "just paper" or "just metal", yet it really becomes a gift and a promise.
  • Pope Paul VI, Mysterium Fidei (1965): these terms are acceptable only if they include transubstantiation, a real change of being.
  • Schillebeeckx himself held that the new meaning rests on a real change made by Christ, not on believers' feelings.
Mission 2

Real Presence beyond Rome

  • Orthodox: a real change (metabole) of bread and wine into Christ's body and blood, through the Holy Spirit (the epiclesis, the prayer calling down the Spirit). How it happens is left a mystery.
  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): Christ's body and blood are truly present "in and under" the bread and wine (Large Catechism, 1529); the bread remains bread. Later Lutheran confessions say "in, with and under". Lutherans call this sacramental union.
  • Consubstantiation: the label others give to this view: both substances present at once. Many Lutherans reject the term as too Aristotelian.
  • Anglicans: a range. Article XXVIII (1571) rejects transubstantiation; the body of Christ is received "only after an heavenly and spiritual manner". Anglo-Catholics hold a strong Real Presence.
  • John Calvin (1509–1564): a spiritual Real Presence: by the Spirit, believers truly feed on Christ, who is in heaven.
Mission 2

Memorialism

  • Huldrych Zwingli (1484–1531): the Supper is a memorial of Christ's death and a pledge of the community's faith.
  • "This is my body" means "This signifies my body", as Jesus says "I am the door" (John 10:9).
  • Christ's risen body is at the right hand of God; it cannot be in many places at once.
  • "It is the spirit who gives life. The flesh profits nothing." (John 6:63, WEBBE)
  • Marburg Colloquy (1529): Luther and Zwingli agreed on 14 of 15 articles but split on this one.
  • Baptists today: the Lord's Supper is an ordinance and memorial; Christ is present spiritually among his people, not in the elements. Many stress it is not "merely" a memorial: it is a real meeting with the risen Lord in faith.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is there any common ground?

  • Claim: There is real common ground in contemporary understandings of the Eucharist.
  • For: all hold that Jesus commanded it, that it proclaims his death (1 Corinthians 11:26) and unites believers (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • For: ecumenical dialogue: Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (World Council of Churches, Lima, 1982) found agreement on thanksgiving, anamnesis, the Spirit's role and fellowship.
  • For: many memorialists speak of a real spiritual presence; many Catholics stress meaning and relationship.
  • Against: the core question, whether the bread becomes Christ's body, still divides; this affects worship (adoration) and who may share communion.
  • Against: Catholics and Orthodox do not, in general, share communion with Protestants, a sign that differences are not merely verbal.
  • Judgement criteria: Is agreement on practice enough without agreement on presence? Are the theories different answers or different languages?
  • AO2 issue: "the extent to which there is any common ground within contemporary understandings of the Eucharist".

Luke's Last Supper

> "He received a cup, and when he had given thanks, he said, "Take this and share it amongst yourselves, for I tell you, I will not drink at all again from the fruit of the vine, until God's Kingdom comes."" (Luke 22:17–18, WEBBE)

  • Setting: a Passover meal (Luke 22:15), remembering Israel's rescue from Egypt.
  • Luke alone has two cups: one before the bread (17), one "after supper" (20).

Show answer

Mission 3

The Catholic Mass

  • Celebrated daily; Catholics must attend on Sundays and holy days of obligation.
  • Liturgy of the Word (readings, homily), then Liturgy of the Eucharist.
  • Only an ordained priest may consecrate, using the words of institution at the altar.
  • Unleavened hosts; the cup is sometimes offered to the people too.
  • Receivers fast for one hour and should not be conscious of serious (mortal) sin; First Holy Communion at about age 7–8.
  • Remaining hosts are kept in the tabernacle; the red sanctuary lamp shows Christ present.
  • Importance: the "source and summit" of the Christian life (Second Vatican Council, Lumen Gentium 11, 1964).
Mission 3

The Baptist Lord's Supper

  • Held weekly in some churches, monthly in many; often after the sermon.
  • Led by the minister, or by elders or deacons: there is no priest; all believers are priests (1 Peter 2:9).
  • A communion table, not an altar: a meal, not a sacrifice.
  • The leader reads Jesus' words at the Last Supper (often 1 Corinthians 11:23–26).
  • Bread (sometimes an ordinary loaf) and small individual cups of wine or, often, non-alcoholic grape juice, passed to people in their seats.
  • Often everyone eats and drinks together, to show unity.
  • Usually an open table: all who love and trust Christ may share, whatever their church.
  • Importance: obedience to Christ's command, remembrance, fellowship. Less frequent sharing is meant to keep it special, not to make it unimportant.
Mission 3Diversity

Other practices

  • Orthodox Divine Liturgy: leavened bread and wine mixed with warm water, given together on a spoon; infants receive communion from baptism; the altar is behind the iconostasis (icon screen); blessed bread (antidoron) is shared with all, including visitors.
  • Anglican Holy Communion: kneeling at the altar rail; bread and a common cup; practice ranges from Anglo-Catholic to evangelical.
  • Pentecostal: usually a memorial meal, often with spontaneous prayer and praise.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army hold no outward Eucharist: all of life can be a meeting with Christ.
Mission 3

What is shared across traditions

  • Bread and wine (or grape juice), as Jesus used.
  • Words of institution from the Gospels or 1 Corinthians 11:23–26.
  • Thanksgiving prayer (the meaning of Eucharist).
  • Confession or self-examination before receiving.
  • The Lord's Prayer in most traditions.
  • A community act: never just private; "we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17, WEBBE).
  • Remembrance of Jesus' death and hope of his return.
  • Your spec: "the similarities in Eucharistic practice in Christian traditions".
Mission 3

Implications for communities

  • Identity: Catholics say "the Eucharist makes the Church"; a Catholic parish is built around the Mass.
  • Unity and division: Catholics and Orthodox do not, as a rule, share communion with other churches; many Protestants offer an open table. Mixed-church families and ecumenical services feel this division sharply.
  • Service: sharing bread at the table links to sharing food with the hungry (e.g. church food banks).
  • New questions: during the COVID-19 lockdowns (2020), churches debated online communion: some allowed people to share bread at home; Catholics encouraged spiritual communion (desiring Christ when unable to receive).

Mission 3Evaluate

Does belief shape practice more than anything else?

  • Claim: Differences in Eucharistic practice are caused mainly by differences in belief.
  • For: belief in Christ's lasting presence → tabernacle, adoration, reverence for crumbs; belief in a memorial → table, open sharing.
  • For: a sacrificial view needs a priest and altar; a meal view needs neither.
  • Against: history and culture matter too: e.g. individual cups and grape juice spread with 19th-century hygiene and temperance movements, not new doctrine.
  • Against: churches with similar beliefs (e.g. different Anglican parishes) still practise very differently.
  • Judgement criteria: Can each practice be traced to a belief? What else (history, culture, authority) explains it?
  • AO2 issue: "the extent to which theoretical beliefs about the Eucharist affect the practice of different denominations".

Mission 4

Same faith, two streams

  • Western churches: the Catholic Church, Anglicans and the Protestant churches (Lutheran, Methodist, Baptist, Pentecostal …).
  • Eastern Orthodox churches: a family of self-governing churches (Greek, Russian, Romanian, Serbian, Antiochian …) sharing one faith and liturgy.
  • East and West divided in 1054 after centuries of growing apart.
  • Same doctrine at both feasts: the incarnation at Christmas, the resurrection at Easter.
  • Different calendars, seasons of preparation and services.
Mission 4Read the source

What both streams celebrate

> "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us. We saw his glory, such glory as of the only born Son of the Father, full of grace and truth." (John 1:14, WEBBE)

> "Why do you seek the living amongst the dead? He isn't here, but is risen." (Luke 24:5–6, WEBBE)

  • Incarnation: Christians believe God the Son became fully human without ceasing to be God.
  • Resurrection: Christians believe Jesus rose bodily from the dead, defeating death.
  • Both streams confess these in the Nicene Creed at their services.

Q1 Which verse speaks of the incarnation, and which of the resurrection?

Show answer

John 1:14: the incarnation; Luke 24:5–6: the resurrection.

Q2 Why might a Christian say that Christmas only makes sense because of Easter?

Show answer

The one born is the one who dies and rises; both feasts concern who Jesus is and what he does for salvation.

Mission 4

Three calendars

  • Julian calendar (46 BC): a leap year every four years; drifts slowly against the sun.
  • Gregorian calendar (1582, Pope Gregory XIII): corrects the drift; used by the West and by most countries for civil life.
  • The Julian calendar is now 13 days behind (from 1900 to 2100).
  • Revised Julian calendar (1923): matches the Gregorian for fixed feasts until 2800; adopted by several Orthodox churches.
  • So 25 December (Julian) = 7 January (Gregorian).
Mission 4

Christmas: dates and the meaning of 6 January

  • West: Christmas on 25 December (attested in Rome by 336).
  • Orthodox on the Revised Julian calendar (e.g. Constantinople, Greece, Romania, Bulgaria, Antioch): 25 December.
  • Orthodox on the Julian calendar (e.g. Russia, Serbia, Georgia, Jerusalem, Mount Athos): 25 December Julian = 7 January.
  • In 2023 the Orthodox Church of Ukraine moved Christmas to 25 December.
  • 6 January means different things: West: Epiphany (the Magi: Christ shown to the nations); East: Theophany (19 January for churches on the Julian calendar; Jesus' baptism: the Trinity revealed, Matthew 3:16–17), with the Great Blessing of the Waters.
Mission 4

Preparing: Advent and the Nativity Fast

  • Western Advent: the four Sundays before Christmas; the start of the Church's year.
  • Focus: waiting and hope, for Christ's coming at Christmas and his second coming.
  • Signs: violet (or blue) vestments, the Advent wreath with four candles, Advent calendars, carols.
  • Orthodox Nativity Fast: 40 days, 15 November to 24 December (on each church's calendar).
  • Focus: fasting, prayer and almsgiving to prepare body and soul; no meat or dairy, with some days allowing fish, wine and oil.
  • The church year begins on 1 September, not with this fast.
  • Many Orthodox fast on Christmas Eve until the evening; in Slavic custom, "until the first star".

Mission 4

Christmas services

  • Western: Midnight Mass (Catholic and many Anglicans), crib scenes (Francis of Assisi, Greccio, 1223), carol services such as Nine Lessons and Carols (King's College, Cambridge, from 1918), Christingle, family services on Christmas morning.
  • Orthodox: the Royal Hours and the Vesperal Liturgy of St Basil on Christmas Eve; an All-Night Vigil with the hymn "God is with us" (Isaiah 8); the Divine Liturgy on the day.
  • The Nativity icon (Orthodox): the cave in darkness, a manger shaped like a tomb, swaddling bands like grave clothes, the ox and donkey (Isaiah 1:3), Joseph seated apart in doubt.
  • The icon links the birth to the death and resurrection; Western art often stresses tenderness and humility in the stable.
Mission 4

The date of Easter

  • Council of Nicaea (325): all churches should keep Easter on the same Sunday.
  • Rule (from Alexandria): the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the spring equinox (fixed at 21 March).
  • West applies it with the Gregorian calendar.
  • Orthodox apply it with the Julian calendar and older moon tables (the Paschalion); all Orthodox churches, except the Finnish, keep this date, even those using the Revised Julian calendar at Christmas.
  • Result: the dates coincide in some years (2025: 20 April; next in 2028: 16 April) and otherwise differ by one, four or five weeks (2026: 5 April and 12 April; 2027: 28 March and 2 May).
  • 2025 was the 1,700th anniversary of Nicaea; Catholic and Orthodox leaders spoke of seeking a common date.

Mission 4

Holy Week and Easter services

  • Western: Lent begins on Ash Wednesday; Palm Sunday; Maundy Thursday (foot washing); Good Friday (veneration of the cross, no Mass in Catholic churches); the Easter Vigil: new fire, the Paschal candle, the Exsultet, baptisms; Easter morning services, including Protestant sunrise services.
  • Orthodox: Great Lent begins on Clean Monday, with a stricter fast; on Holy Friday the epitaphios (an embroidered cloth showing Christ's burial) is carried round the church.
  • Pascha at midnight: the church is dark; the priest brings out a flame: "Come, receive the light"; a candlelit procession circles the church; at the doors: "Christ is risen!" "Truly he is risen!"
  • The Paschal hymn, sung again and again: Christ has risen, trampling down death by death and giving life to those in the tombs (paraphrase).
  • Red eggs, the Paschal greeting for 40 days, and the icon of the Anastasis: Christ raising Adam and Eve from the broken gates of death (shown in art; diagram uses symbols only).
Mission 4Diversity

Diversity within each stream

  • West: Catholics, Anglicans and Lutherans keep the full Lent and Holy Week liturgy; many Baptist and Pentecostal churches focus on Good Friday and Easter Sunday with little of Lent.
  • The English Puritans banned Christmas (1647) as unscriptural; some Reformed churches long kept no festivals.
  • East: "old calendar" and "new calendar" churches keep Christmas on different days; local customs differ (Greek roast lamb at Pascha; Russian kulich and paskha; Serbian badnjak oak branch at Christmas).
  • The Finnish Orthodox Church keeps the Western Easter date.
  • So "East" and "West" are each a family, not a single practice.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is Easter a different celebration in East and West?

  • Claim: Different emphases and practices make Easter a different celebration in the two streams.
  • For: different dates in most years; different seasons (Ash Wednesday vs Clean Monday); the midnight service and the Anastasis image stress Christ's victory over death for all humanity; the West has often stressed the cross and personal forgiveness.
  • Against: the same doctrine (bodily resurrection), the same Scriptures, the same creed; both treat Easter as the greatest feast (Orthodox: "Feast of Feasts"; West: the Easter Triduum as the summit of the year).
  • Against: the Western Easter Vigil and the Orthodox Pascha share ancient roots: darkness, light, procession, baptism.
  • Related issue: the relative importance of Easter and Christmas: liturgically Easter is greater in both; culturally, in the West, Christmas is often bigger.
  • Judgement criteria: Is a celebration defined by its doctrine or its practice? Do differences of emphasis amount to a different meaning?
  • AO2 issues: "whether the different emphases and practices mean that Easter is a different celebration"; "the relative importance of Easter and Christmas".
Mission check

?In 2027, a Greek Orthodox family and an Anglican family in the same street both keep Christmas on 25 December, but the Anglicans keep Easter on 28 March and the Greeks on 2 May. What explains this?

Doctrine unites the two streams at both feasts; calendars, fasting and liturgy give each its own shape.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Infant baptism (Catholic): a sacrament of grace that washes away original sin; parents and godparents speak for the child (Augustine).
  • Zwingli: infant baptism as a covenant sign and parents' pledge. Barth: believer's baptism, following the example of Christ and requiring consent.
  • Believer's baptism (Baptist): full immersion after a personal profession of faith; an ordinance and public witness.

Mission 2

  • Transubstantiation (Catholic): the substance becomes Christ's body and blood; the accidents remain (Lateran IV, Aquinas, Trent).
  • Transignification and transfinalization re-express the change as a change of meaning and purpose. Luther: sacramental union ("consubstantiation").
  • Memorialism (Zwingli, most Baptists): "Do this in memory of me"; Christ present spiritually among believers, not in the bread.

Mission 3

  • Luke 22:17–20: Passover setting, new covenant, "given for you", "Do this in memory of me".
  • Catholic Mass: daily, priest, altar, transubstantiation, reservation; "source and summit".
  • Baptist Lord's Supper: less frequent, table, open, remembrance.
  • Shared practices include words of institution, thanksgiving and self-examination; believers speak of reflection, forgiveness and belonging.

Mission 4

  • Same doctrine: incarnation at Christmas, resurrection at Easter (John 1:14; Luke 24:5–6).
  • Christmas: 25 December (West and New Calendar Orthodox) or 7 January (Julian); Advent (four Sundays, waiting) vs the Nativity Fast (40 days, fasting); Theophany vs Epiphany.
  • Easter: one Nicaea rule, two calendars; Easter Vigil vs midnight Pascha; diversity inside each stream.
