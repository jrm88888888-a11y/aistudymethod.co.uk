Mission 1

Microfinance

  • Microfinance: small loans, savings and insurance for people too poor for ordinary banks.
  • Poor borrowers have no collateral. Lenders use group lending: members share responsibility for repayment.
  • Many borrowers are women running small businesses.
  • Example: the Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) and its founder Muhammad Yunus shared the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize.
  • Evidence: six randomised studies on four continents found "modestly positive, but not transformative" effects (Banerjee, Karlan and Zinman, 2015).
  • Limits: high interest rates; loans may fund consumption, not businesses; risk of over-indebtedness; small firms rarely grow large.
  • Mobile banking: Kenya's M-Pesa lets people send and save money by phone. One study estimated it lifted about 194,000 households (about 2%) out of extreme poverty (Suri and Jack, 2016).
Quick check
?Why can microfinance lenders lend to poor borrowers that ordinary banks refuse?
Mission check

Microfinance helps households manage money, but it is not a full development strategy.

Mission 2

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 2

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • Lender of last resort: lends to banks that run short of cash.
  • Financial stability: watches for risks to the whole system and, in many countries, supervises banks.
Mission 2

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • UK: the government sets an inflation target of 2% (CPI). The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), nine members, sets Bank Rate to hit it.
  • The UK target is symmetric: inflation below 2% is treated as seriously as inflation above it.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 2

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • UK: Bank Rate (often called the base rate), set by the MPC; it feeds through to loan and savings rates (the transmission mechanism).
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • The Bank of England sets no minimum reserve ratio for monetary policy.

Mission 2

Lender of last resort

  • A bank can be solvent (assets worth more than it owes) but illiquid: it cannot turn its loans into cash fast enough.
  • If depositors panic and withdraw at once (a bank run), even a sound bank can fail.
  • The central bank can lend it cash against good collateral: it is the lender of last resort.
  • Classic rule (Walter Bagehot, 1873): lend freely, against good collateral, at a high rate.
  • Real-world: on 14 September 2007 the Bank of England gave emergency support to Northern Rock. Queues of savers still formed outside its branches.
  • Risk: moral hazard. If banks expect rescue, they may take bigger risks (see Mission 3).
Quick check
?A bank's loans are worth more than it owes, but depositors suddenly withdraw more cash than it holds. What can the central bank do as lender of last resort?
Mission 2

Central banks, banks and the money supply

  • Most money is bank deposits, created when commercial banks lend.
  • The central bank shapes that lending:
  • Policy rate: a higher rate makes loans dearer, so banks lend less and deposits grow more slowly.
  • QE: buying bonds creates reserves for banks and deposits for the sellers, so broad money rises directly.
  • Regulation: rules on capital and liquidity limit how far banks can expand lending.
  • So the central bank influences the money supply and the banking sector, but does not fix the quantity of money directly.
Mission 2

Keeping the system safe

  • Banks fail in ways that can spread to the whole economy. Central banks often help regulate them.
  • UK (since 2013): the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) looks at risks to the whole system.
  • The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England, supervises individual banks and insurers.
  • The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate from the Bank, regulates how firms treat customers.
  • More on regulation and crises in Mission 3.
Mission 2Evaluate

Should central banks be independent?

  • UK: the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997. The ECB's independence is written into EU treaties.
  • For: rate-setters cannot be pushed to cut rates before an election, so promises of low inflation are more credible. Inflation expectations may stay anchored.
  • Against: unelected officials make decisions that affect jobs, house prices and savers; accountability is weaker.
  • Against: monetary and fiscal policy may pull in opposite directions without coordination.
  • It depends on: how the target is set and reviewed, how open the central bank is, and how far it strays into areas such as QE that change who gains and loses.
Quick check
?Why might giving a central bank operational independence help to keep inflation expectations low?

Mission 3

What finance does for the real economy

  • The financial sector: banks, insurers, pension funds, markets.
  • It channels savings to firms and households that want to borrow and invest.
  • It runs the payments system: wages, card payments, bills.
  • It spreads and prices risk: insurance, diversified funds.
  • It turns short-term deposits into long-term loans (maturity transformation).
Mission 3

Two ways a bank fails

  • A bank's assets: loans, bonds, reserves. Its liabilities: deposits and other borrowing.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities: the owners' stake that absorbs losses.
  • Insolvency: loans go bad, assets fall below liabilities, capital is wiped out.
  • Illiquidity: the bank is solvent but cannot turn loans into cash fast enough when depositors withdraw (a run).
  • The two feed each other: rumours of losses start a run; selling assets in a hurry causes losses.
  • Say: "capital is the owners' funding that absorbs losses" — not: "capital is cash the bank keeps in the vault".
Mission 3Worked example

Reading a bank's ratios

Illustrative data, Bank A (billions):

AssetsLiabilities and capital
Cash and reserves5Deposits85
Government bonds15Other borrowing9
Loans80Capital6
Total100Total100
  • Leverage ratio = capital ÷ total assets = 6 ÷ 100 = 6%.
  • Capital ratio = capital ÷ risk-weighted assets. Safe assets count for less; say risk-weighted assets are 50. Ratio = 6 ÷ 50 = 12%.
  • Liquidity: liquid assets ÷ deposits = (5 + 15) ÷ 85 = 23.5%.
  • Stress: 8% of loans go bad. Loss = 0.08 × 80 = 6.4, more than capital of 6. Bank A is insolvent.
  • More capital = a bigger cushion. More liquid assets = more time in a run.

Mission 3

Systemic risk

  • Systemic risk: the risk that trouble at one bank spreads to the whole financial system.
  • Banks lend to each other, so one failure means losses for others (contagion).
  • Fire sales: a bank dumps assets, prices fall, other banks' assets lose value.
  • Panic: depositors run from banks that look similar.
  • Lending shrinks (a credit crunch), so firms cannot borrow; output and jobs fall.
  • These costs fall on people outside the bank: a negative externality, a reason to regulate.
Mission 3

Moral hazard and too big to fail

  • Moral hazard: being protected from the cost of a risk makes people take more risk.
  • If a bank is too big to fail, its owners and lenders expect a rescue.
  • So profits from risky lending stay private, while big losses fall on taxpayers.
  • Lenders charge such banks less, so they can grow even bigger and riskier.
  • Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort (see Mission 2) help to prevent runs, but can add to moral hazard.
  • Say: "moral hazard: behaviour changes after protection is given" — not: "adverse selection", which is hidden information before a deal.
Quick check
?In a crisis, a government rescues a large bank but lets a large clothing retailer go bust. Which is the strongest economic reason for treating them differently?
Mission 3

The crisis of 2007–08

  • (1) 2000s: US house prices boom. Lenders sell sub-prime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit.
  • (2) Mortgages are bundled into securities and sold worldwide; banks borrow heavily (high leverage) to buy them.
  • (3) From 2006, US house prices fall. Defaults rise; nobody knows which banks hold the losses.
  • (4) August 2007: banks stop lending to each other. September 2007: run on Northern Rock (UK). 15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers goes bankrupt.
  • (5) Governments rescue banks: in 2008–09 the UK government took large stakes in RBS and Lloyds Banking Group.
Mission 3

Who regulates UK finance

  • Before 2013 the FSA, the Bank of England and the Treasury shared the job; the gaps were blamed for missing the risks.
  • Since April 2013:
  • Financial Policy Committee (FPC), at the Bank of England: watches risks to the whole system (macroprudential), e.g. sets extra capital buffers when credit grows fast.
  • Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England: supervises the safety of individual banks and insurers (microprudential).
  • Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate: regulates how firms treat customers and keeps markets honest.
  • The Bank of England is still lender of last resort: it lends to solvent banks short of cash (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

The rules after the crisis

  • More capital (Basel III): banks must hold core capital of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% buffer, and more for the biggest banks.
  • Leverage ratio: capital of at least 3% of total assets, with no risk weights (UK: 3.25% for large banks).
  • Liquidity coverage ratio: enough liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows.
  • UK ring-fencing (from 2019): big banks separate everyday deposits from riskier investment banking.
  • Stress tests: regulators test whether banks could survive a severe recession.
  • Bail-in: losses fall on a failing bank's owners and lenders first, not taxpayers.
Quick check
?The Bank of England notices that mortgage lending across the whole UK is rising very fast and house prices look stretched. Which body's job is it to respond to this risk to the whole system?
Mission 3

The IMF and the World Bank

  • Both founded at Bretton Woods in 1944. The IMF has 191 member countries (2024).
  • IMF: watches the world economy and lends to countries with balance of payments crises, usually with conditions (conditionality): cut deficits, reform banks.
  • Examples: the UK borrowed 3.9 billion US dollars in 1976; Greece got an IMF and euro-area rescue in 2010.
  • World Bank: long-term loans and grants for development (roads, schools, health).
  • For: stops crises spreading between countries. Against: conditions can deepen recessions; moral hazard for governments and their lenders.
Mission 3Evaluate

Do tighter rules make us safer?

  • For: more capital and liquidity mean banks absorb losses and survive runs; fewer rescues; less moral hazard.
  • Against: capital costs banks more than deposits, so loans may be dearer and scarcer, slowing investment and growth.
  • Against: risk can move to shadow banks, where it is harder to see.
  • It depends on: how high the requirements are; whether rules are international; whether regulators can spot risks in time; and whether a failing bank can really be wound down without a rescue.
Mission check

?A country's government announces that it will cover every loss of its three largest banks, with no new rules on how much capital they hold. What does economic theory predict?

Protection from losses changes behaviour. Because bank failures spread to the whole economy, governments protect banks, and then regulate them to limit the extra risk this invites.

Mission 4

What counts as aid

  • Aid: a voluntary transfer of resources to a developing country on better-than-market terms.
  • Humanitarian aid: short-term relief after a disaster or conflict: food, shelter, medicine.
  • Development aid: longer-term help to raise living standards: schools, clinics, roads, water.
  • Bilateral aid: from one government to another. Multilateral aid: through bodies such as the World Bank.
  • Forms: grants (never repaid), concessional (soft) loans (low interest, long repayment), technical assistance (experts, training).
  • Tied aid: must be spent on goods or services from the donor country.
Mission 4

Official development assistance (ODA)

  • ODA: aid from governments, given to promote the economic development and welfare of developing countries, as grants or concessional loans.
  • Measured by the OECD's Development Assistance Committee (DAC).
  • UN target: donors give 0.7% of their GNI as ODA.
  • 2024 (OECD, preliminary): DAC donors gave 212.1 billion US dollars, 0.33% of their combined GNI. Aid fell 7.1% in real terms on 2023.
  • Only 4 donors went above 0.7%: Denmark, Luxembourg, Norway and Sweden.
  • Illustrative data: a donor with GNI of 3,000 bn giving 0.3% gives 9 bn. At 0.7% it would give 21 bn: a gap of 12 bn.
Quick check
?After an earthquake, a donor government flies in tents and medicine. Separately, it lends a poor country money at 1% interest over 40 years to build a power line, on condition that the cables are bought from the donor's own firms. Which description is correct?
Mission 4

How aid could raise growth

  • Poor countries often face a savings gap: low incomes → low saving → low investment.
  • They may also face a foreign currency gap: too little export revenue to import machinery and fuel.
  • Aid can fill both gaps.
  • Short run: aid spent in the economy raises AD: AD1 → AD2.
  • Long run: aid spent on infrastructure, health and education raises productive capacity: LRAS shifts right.
  • Aid spent on consumption, or lost to corruption, may give only the short-run boost.

Mission 4

The case for and against aid

  • For:
    • fills the savings and foreign currency gaps
    • saves lives quickly in a disaster
    • funds merit goods (vaccination, schooling) that markets under-provide
  • Against:
    • dependency: governments may rely on aid rather than build a tax system
    • corruption and fungibility: aid for clinics can free other funds for less useful spending
    • tied aid can cost more than buying at world prices
    • food aid can lower prices for local farmers and cut their output
    • large inflows can push up the exchange rate, making exports less competitive
  • Economists disagree on aid's overall effect on growth. Evidence varies by country, period and type of aid.
Mission 4

Why external debt can become a trap

  • External debt: money owed to foreign lenders, often in a foreign currency such as US dollars.
  • Repayments need foreign currency, earned mainly from exports.
  • Worked example (illustrative data):

1. Debt: 2 bn US dollars. Exchange rate: 100 local units per dollar → debt = 200 bn local units.

2. The currency depreciates to 150 per dollar → debt = 2 × 150 = 300 bn local units: 50% more, with no new borrowing.

3. Yearly interest at 5% = 0.1 bn dollars: 10 bn local units before, 15 bn after.

  • Debt service: yearly interest plus repayments. Every dollar spent on it is a dollar not spent on health, education or imports of capital goods.
Quick check

Mission 4

Debt relief

  • Debt relief: cancelling, reducing or rescheduling a country's debt.
  • HIPC Initiative (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries), launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996.
  • The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI), from 2005, added 100% relief on eligible debts to the IMF, World Bank and African Development Fund for countries completing HIPC.
  • Countries had to meet conditions, including a poverty reduction strategy. By February 2023, 36 countries had completed the process (IMF), most of them in Africa.
  • For: frees money for health, education and infrastructure; can restore access to credit.
  • Against: moral hazard (governments may borrow carelessly, expecting future relief); savings may not reach the poor; lenders may lend less in future.
  • Say: "debt relief cuts debt service payments" — not: "debt relief gives the country new money to spend".
Mission 4

The IMF and the World Bank

  • Both set up at Bretton Woods in 1944. Member countries own them; votes depend largely on each country's financial contribution (its quota at the IMF, its shares at the World Bank), so richer countries have more say.
  • IMF (International Monetary Fund):
    • watches members' economies and global financial stability (surveillance)
    • lends to countries with balance of payments problems, usually short to medium term
    • loans come with conditionality: e.g. cut the budget deficit, reform taxes or the exchange rate
  • World Bank:
    • finances long-term development projects: roads, power, water, schools
    • lends to middle-income countries (IBRD) and gives grants and very low-interest loans to the poorest countries (IDA)
  • Critics: in the 1980s and 1990s, structural adjustment conditions (spending cuts, privatisation, liberalisation) were blamed for hurting the poor. Supporters say they restored stability and growth. Conditions now place more weight on protecting social spending.
  • The IMF also acts as a lender to countries in financial crisis, supporting global financial stability.
Quick check
?A country cannot pay for its imports. Its currency is falling fast and it has run out of foreign currency reserves. Which body is it most likely to turn to, and on what terms?
Mission 4

NGOs and remittances

  • NGOs (non-governmental organisations): charities and other non-profit groups, independent of governments.
    • Strengths: local knowledge; work directly with communities; can reach people a weak government cannot.
    • Limits: small scale; patchy coordination; can depend on donations that change with the news.
  • Remittances: money sent home by people working abroad.
    • 2023 (World Bank): about 656 billion US dollars went to low- and middle-income countries: more than FDI, and more than ODA, that year.
    • They go straight to households: they pay for food, school fees and small businesses.
    • But they can fall in a downturn abroad, and they do not directly build public infrastructure.
Quick check
?A government receives large aid grants for its health service. It then cuts its own health budget by the same amount and spends the money on new military aircraft. What problem does this show?
Mission 4Evaluate

Does aid help growth and development?

It depends on:

  • What it is spent on: infrastructure, health and education can shift LRAS; consumption gives a short-run boost at most.
  • Governance: corruption and weak institutions cut the share that reaches its target.
  • Type and terms: humanitarian vs development; tied vs untied; grants vs loans that add to debt.
  • Size and timing: large inflows can push up the exchange rate; aid that stops suddenly can stall projects.
  • Alternatives: trade access, FDI and remittances may matter more for some countries.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two countries each receive aid worth 3% of GDP a year for ten years. Country P spends it on rural roads and child vaccination, and audits show little is lost. In country Q, much of it is spent on imported consumer goods and some is lost to corruption. Which outcome is most likely?

Aid can help growth when it builds productive capacity and good institutions make sure it is used well.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Microfinance reaches people without collateral; its effects are modest.

Mission 2

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.
  • It is lender of last resort, helps keep the financial system safe, and is often independent of government.

Mission 3

  • Banks fail through insolvency (losses wipe out capital) or illiquidity (a run); capital and liquidity ratios measure the cushions.
  • Bank failures spread (systemic risk), and expected rescues encourage risk (moral hazard, too big to fail): two reasons to regulate.
  • After 2007–08: FPC, PRA and FCA, Basel III capital and liquidity rules, ring-fencing and stress tests; regulation has costs and limits.
  • The IMF lends to countries in balance of payments crises, with conditions; the World Bank funds long-term development.

Mission 4

  • Aid: humanitarian or development; bilateral or multilateral; grants, soft loans, tied or untied. ODA target 0.7% of GNI.
  • Aid raises AD now; it raises LRAS only when spent on productive capacity. Debt in foreign currency gets heavier when the currency falls; debt relief cuts debt service.
  • IMF: balance of payments lending with conditions. World Bank: long-term development finance. NGOs and remittances also matter.
