Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body carries trillions of microbes, yet very few make you ill. What makes something a pathogen?

Make a guess: Your body carries trillions of microbes, yet very few make you ill. What makes something a pathogen?

Mission 1

What makes a pathogen

  • A pathogen is an organism that causes disease.
  • Diseases caused by pathogens are infectious: they can pass from host to host.
  • An organism able to cause disease is pathogenic.
  • Four groups include pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists (protists).
  • Most microbes on and in you cause no disease. Being a microbe does not make something a pathogen.
Mission 1

Direct and indirect transmission

  • Direct transmission: from host to host with nothing in between.
    • Contact: skin to skin, body fluids (HIV), plant touching plant (TMV).
    • Inoculation: through a break in the skin: an animal bite (rabies), a wound, a shared needle.
    • Ingestion: in contaminated food or water.
  • Indirect transmission: via something in between.
    • Fomites: objects such as towels and bedding (athlete's foot).
    • Droplets: coughed or sneezed, then inhaled (TB, influenza).
    • Vectors: a living organism that carries a pathogen between hosts (Anopheles mosquitoes: malaria).
    • Spores: carried by wind, water or soil (late blight, black sigatoka).
  • Living conditions change spread: overcrowding, poor ventilation, poor nutrition, poor sanitation; warm, humid climate (fungi, mosquitoes).
Quick check
?Athlete's foot spreads in changing rooms when people share towels. Which describes this transmission?
Mission check

?One form of sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense, a single-celled eukaryote. It lives in wild antelope and cattle and reaches humans through the bite of tsetse flies. Which statement is correct?

A pathogen is defined by causing disease, not by being small or being a microbe. Then the question is how it gets in.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Smallpox has been wiped out, yet malaria and flu are still with us. What makes a disease hard to beat?

Make a guess: Smallpox has been wiped out, yet malaria and flu are still with us. What makes a disease hard to beat?

Mission 2

Terms: who carries a disease

  • Pathogenic: able to cause disease.
  • Infectious: able to pass from one host to another.
  • Carrier: an infected individual with no symptoms who can still pass the pathogen on.
  • Disease reservoir: where a pathogen lives and multiplies between outbreaks: a host population or the environment.
  • Vector: a living organism that carries a pathogen between hosts, e.g. a mosquito.
  • Toxin: a poisonous substance made by a pathogen that damages host cells.
  • Antigenic types: strains of one pathogen species with different surface antigens.
Mission 2

Terms: how far a disease spreads

  • Endemic: always present in a population, at a fairly steady level.
  • Epidemic: a sudden rise in cases, well above the usual level, in one population or region.
  • Pandemic: an epidemic that spreads across many countries and continents.
  • Vaccine: antigens (or nucleic acid coding for them) that give active immunity without disease.
  • Antibiotic: a substance that kills or inhibits bacteria without harming the host.
  • Antigen triggers an immune response; an antibody is the protein made to bind it.
  • Resistance: a pathogen survives a drug that normally kills or inhibits it.
Quick check
?A woman has no symptoms of cholera, but Vibrio cholerae is found in her faeces. Three members of her household then develop cholera. Which term describes her?
Mission 2

The body as host

  • Your body is home to about 3.8 × 10¹³ bacteria: roughly as many as your own cells (3.0 × 10¹³).
  • Most live in the large intestine; others on skin and in the mouth.
  • Commensal: the microbe benefits; the host is unharmed (most skin flora).
  • Mutualistic: both benefit (gut bacteria make vitamin K and digest fibre).
  • Parasitic: the organism benefits; the host is harmed. Pathogens are parasites.
  • Opportunistic pathogens live harmlessly until defences fall, e.g. Candida (thrush).
  • Resident flora compete with pathogens for space and nutrients.
Mission 2

Cholera

  • Organism: Vibrio cholerae, a comma-shaped bacterium.
  • Source: faeces of infected people and carriers; also survives in brackish water.
  • Tissue: lining of the small intestine. Its toxin makes cells secrete chloride ions; water follows by osmosis. Result: severe watery diarrhoea and dehydration.
  • Transmission: faecal–oral, in contaminated water or food.
  • Prevention: clean water, sewage treatment, hand washing, well-cooked food.
  • Treatment: oral rehydration solution (water, glucose, salts); intravenous fluids if severe; antibiotics shorten severe illness.
  • Vaccine: oral, killed bacteria; partial protection for a few years.
Mission 2

Tuberculosis

  • Organism: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, a bacterium; M. bovis from cattle.
  • Source: people with active lung TB; infected cattle.
  • Tissue: mainly the lungs (it lives inside macrophages); can spread to other organs.
  • Transmission: inhaled droplets from coughs; M. bovis in unpasteurised milk. Overcrowding, poor ventilation, HIV and malnutrition raise risk.
  • Many infections stay latent: no symptoms, not infectious, but can become active later.
  • Prevention: BCG vaccine (live attenuated M. bovis; protection varies, best against severe childhood TB); pasteurising milk; testing cattle; tracing contacts.
  • Treatment: several antibiotics together for at least 6 months. Stopping early selects for multidrug-resistant TB.
Quick check
?TB is treated with several antibiotics taken together for at least six months. Why are several drugs used at once?
Mission 2

Smallpox

  • Organism: variola virus (a DNA virus).
  • Reservoir: humans only. No animal host.
  • Tissue: enters the lining of the airways; spreads in lymph and blood; pustules form on the skin and in the mouth and throat. About 30% of cases died.
  • Transmission: droplets, close contact, contaminated bedding and clothing.
  • Vaccine: live vaccinia virus, a close relative; one dose gives long-lasting protection.
  • Control: surveillance and containment: find each case, isolate it, vaccinate everyone around it (ring vaccination).
  • Treatment: none specific in the eradication era; supportive care only.
  • Last natural case: 1977. Declared eradicated by WHO in 1980.
Mission 2

Influenza

  • Organism: influenza virus (an RNA virus).
  • Reservoir: influenza A also lives in wild water birds, poultry and pigs.
  • Tissue: epithelial cells lining the airways.
  • Transmission: droplets, aerosols, contaminated surfaces.
  • Antigenic types: surface antigens H (haemagglutinin) and N (neuraminidase), e.g. H1N1, H3N2.
  • Antigenic drift: small changes every year. Antigenic shift: a new H–N combination, e.g. after mixing in pigs: risk of a pandemic (1957, 1968, 2009).
  • Prevention: a new vaccine each year, matched to the strains predicted to circulate; hand and respiratory hygiene.
  • Treatment: rest and fluids; antivirals for people at high risk. Antibiotics only for secondary bacterial infections.
Quick check
?A smallpox vaccination campaign eradicated smallpox. Influenza vaccination continues every year with no prospect of eradication. Which statement best explains the difference?
Mission 2

Malaria

  • Organism: Plasmodium, a protoctist, e.g. P. falciparum.
  • Vector: the female Anopheles mosquito. Reservoir: infected people.
  • Tissue: liver cells, then red blood cells, which burst in cycles of fever. P. falciparum can block brain capillaries.
  • Transmission: mosquito bite; also blood transfusion, mother to foetus.
  • Prevention: insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor spraying, draining standing water, preventive drugs for travellers.
  • Treatment: artemisinin-based combination therapy; older drugs (chloroquine) fail where Plasmodium is resistant.
  • Vaccines: two (RTS,S and R21) now recommended by WHO for children where malaria is common; protection is partial.
  • Why vaccines are hard: a complex life cycle, many antigens that differ between stages, and life hidden inside cells.
Mission 2

How viruses damage by reproducing

  • Viruses reproduce only inside host cells: they are obligate intracellular parasites.
  • Attach: viral proteins bind specific receptors, so only certain cells are infected (influenza: airway cells; HIV: helper T cells).
  • Take over: viral nucleic acid uses the cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to make viral parts.
  • Release: new viruses burst out (lysis) or bud off. The infected cell dies, or is killed by immune cells.
  • So disease follows the cells destroyed: influenza strips airway lining; HIV removes helper T cells.
  • Fast, error-prone copying of RNA genomes produces new antigenic types.
  • Some viruses lie dormant in cells and reactivate later (herpes viruses).
Quick check
?Influenza causes damage in the airways but rarely elsewhere in the body. Which statement best explains this?
Mission check

?Measles virus infects only humans and has one antigenic type; its vaccine protects for life. Yellow fever virus also has a highly effective vaccine, but it circulates among monkeys and is spread by mosquitoes. Why is measles considered possible to eradicate but yellow fever is not?

A human-only reservoir and a stable antigen make a disease beatable. Animal reservoirs, vectors, carriers and changing antigens keep it with us.

Mission 3

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Vaccination gives artificial active immunity.
  • Example: the rubella vaccine (part of MMR) protects without the person ever catching rubella.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 3

Herd immunity

  • A pathogen spreads only when an infected person passes it to someone susceptible.
  • Vaccines protect the individual and, at high coverage, the population.
  • When a high enough percentage is immune, most contacts are immune: chains of transmission break.
  • This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns, people with weakened immune systems.
  • More infectious diseases need higher coverage: about 95% for measles.
  • If coverage falls below that level, outbreaks return.
Quick check
?For measles, transmission in a population is greatly impeded once about 95% of people are immune. A community's vaccination coverage falls from 96% to 85%. Which statement correctly describes a consequence?
Mission 3

When antigens change

  • Memory cells bind only the antigen they were made against.
  • Antigen variability: mutations change a pathogen's surface antigens. Old memory cells no longer bind, so you can fall ill again.
  • Influenza antigens change often, so a new vaccine is made each year.
  • The common cold is caused by over 100 types of rhinovirus, each with different antigens: no single vaccine can cover them.
Quick check
?A person had influenza last winter and recovered fully. This winter they catch influenza again. Which is the best explanation?

Mission 3

Ethics: the arguments

  • Ethics questions have arguments on several sides. Know them; weigh them.
  • Testing: vaccines are tested on animals, then on volunteers. How much risk is acceptable, and is consent fully informed?
  • Placebos: is it right to give a placebo when a vaccine might protect?
  • Side effects: serious ones are rare but not nil. The disease usually carries far greater risk. How should a small individual risk be weighed against benefit to the population?
  • Choice: should vaccination be compulsory to reach herd immunity, or an individual decision?
  • Fairness: who is vaccinated first when supply is short? Should richer countries fund vaccines for poorer ones?
Quick check
?A news article reports a newly published study claiming that a vaccine causes a rare side effect, before other scientists have evaluated the study. Which response shows the best understanding of how science works?
Mission check

?A measles vaccine gives long-lasting protection. Over 100 types of rhinovirus cause the common cold, each with different surface antigens. Why would a vaccine made from one rhinovirus type give little protection against colds?

Long-lasting protection needs an antigen that stays the same. When antigens change or come in many types, memory cells miss.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 4

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 4

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 4

Kill or hold back?

  • Bactericidal: kills bacteria. E.g. penicillin: weakened wall, water enters by osmosis, the cell bursts (lysis).
  • Bacteriostatic: stops bacteria growing and dividing. E.g. tetracycline.
  • After a bacteriostatic drug, the immune system must clear the bacteria.
  • Viable count: bactericidal → falls; bacteriostatic → stays level.
  • Broad spectrum: acts on many types of bacteria (e.g. tetracycline).
  • Narrow spectrum: acts on a few types (e.g. benzylpenicillin, mainly Gram-positive).
  • Say: "bacteriostatic stops growth" — not: "bacteriostatic kills slowly".
Mission 4

Penicillin and tetracycline up close

  • Penicillin acts on growing bacteria, as they build new wall.
  • Without cross-links, the wall cannot resist water entering by osmosis: lysis.
  • Tetracycline binds the small (30S) subunit of 70S ribosomes. tRNA cannot bind: no protein synthesis.
  • Gram-negative bacteria: thin peptidoglycan under an outer membrane.
  • The outer membrane keeps many antibiotics (e.g. penicillin) and immune defences (e.g. lysozyme) away from the wall.
Quick check
?Antibiotics P and Q were added to separate cultures of one bacterium, each starting with 1 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³ (illustrative data). After 6 hours: culture P had 1 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³; culture Q had 2 × 10³; a culture with no antibiotic had 6 × 10⁷. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 4

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • Overuse (e.g. for viral infections, or routinely in farm animals) spreads resistance among pathogenic bacteria.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check
?In a weed population of a hypothetical plant species, an allele gives resistance to herbicide H. Where the herbicide is not used, resistant plants produce fewer seeds than susceptible plants. After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A pathogen is something that causes disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists include pathogens; most microbes do not.
  • Transmission is direct (contact, inoculation, ingestion) or indirect (fomites, droplets, vectors, spores).

Mission 2

  • Carriers, reservoirs, vectors and changing antigenic types decide how hard a disease is to control.
  • Cholera and TB are bacterial, smallpox and influenza viral, malaria protoctistan; viruses cause disease by destroying the cells they reproduce in.

Mission 3

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • High coverage gives herd immunity, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.
  • Changing antigens defeat old memory cells: why the flu vaccine changes yearly.

Mission 4

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.
