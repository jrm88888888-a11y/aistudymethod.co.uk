Mission 1

India in the 19th century

  • Foreign rule: after centuries of Muslim rulers (the Mughals), the British East India Company controlled much of India from the late 1700s; the British Crown ruled directly from 1858.
  • Islamic influence: Persian was the language of government; Islam's strict monotheism challenged image worship.
  • Christian missions: missionaries (e.g. at Serampore, near Calcutta, from 1800) criticised Hindu worship, caste and the treatment of women.
  • Western learning: English education spread (Macaulay's Minute, 1835); ideas of reason, science and progress questioned tradition. A first wave of secularisation.
  • Orientalists (e.g. William Jones, 1746–1794) translated Sanskrit texts and praised India's ancient wisdom.
  • Hindu responses ranged from defending tradition unchanged to reforming it. Two of the best-known reformers: Ram Mohan Roy and Dayananda Saraswati.
Mission 1

Ram Mohan Roy

  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), a Bengali brahmin; worked for the East India Company.
  • Learned Persian and Arabic (and met Islamic monotheism), Sanskrit (the Upanishads) and English (Christian and Enlightenment thought).
  • Translated Upanishads into Bengali and English: he argued that they teach one formless God, and that image worship was a later corruption.
  • Admired Jesus' moral teaching (The Precepts of Jesus, 1820) but rejected the Trinity; worked with Unitarian Christians.
  • Often called the "father of the Bengal renaissance". He died in Bristol in 1833, while visiting Britain.
Mission 1

The Brahmo Samaj

  • Roy founded the Brahmo Sabha in Calcutta in 1828; it became the Brahmo Samaj ("society of Brahman").
  • Beliefs: one eternal, formless God; the Upanishads read with reason; no images, no animal sacrifice, no priestly mediators.
  • Worship: congregational: Upanishad readings, hymns, prayers and sermons, like a Protestant service.
  • Universal in spirit: its worship was meant to be open to people of any faith who sought the one God.
  • Later leaders: Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905) gave it a covenant and creed and gave up belief in the Vedas' infallibility; Keshab Chandra Sen (1838–1884) took it in a more radical, universalist direction, and the movement split (1866, 1878).
  • Influential among educated Bengalis; always a small movement.
Mission 1

Roy as social reformer

  • Sati (a widow burning on her husband's pyre): Roy argued from the shastras that it was not required, and campaigned against it. The British banned it in Bengal in 1829.
  • Argued for women's property rights and education; against polygamy.
  • Criticised caste divisions and the power of priests.
  • Founded schools teaching both Western science and Indian learning.
  • His method: reform from within, appealing to Hindu scripture and to reason at the same time.

Mission 1

Dayananda Saraswati

  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), born in Gujarat.
  • The story told of his youth: at 14, keeping a night vigil for Shiva, he saw a mouse running over the image and eating the offerings. He decided a God who could not protect his own offerings was not in the image.
  • Became a wandering sannyasi; studied Sanskrit grammar and the Vedas under the blind teacher Virajananda at Mathura, who rejected post-Vedic texts.
  • Debated orthodox pandits, Christian missionaries and Muslim scholars.
  • Founded the Arya Samaj ("society of noble ones") in Bombay in 1875; it grew strongest in the Punjab.
  • Main work: Satyarth Prakash (The Light of Truth, 1875).
Mission 1

The Arya Samaj

  • Back to the Vedas: only the four Veda samhitas are revealed and infallible; later texts are accepted only where they agree with them.
  • One formless God. Rejects image worship, avatars and the Puranic stories.
  • Worship: havan (offerings into a sacred fire) with Vedic mantras.
  • Varna by merit, not birth: a person's varna depends on qualities and actions. Opposed untouchability and child marriage; supported widow remarriage and education for girls.
  • The Vedas are believed to contain all true knowledge, including the principles of science.
  • Shuddhi (purification): a rite to (re)admit converts to Christianity or Islam, and others, into the Hindu fold.
  • Sharp criticism of Christianity and Islam, and of much popular Hinduism. Founded schools and colleges (DAV schools, from 1886).

Mission 1

Significance and influence

  • Interfaith: Roy opened dialogue with Islam and Christianity; Dayananda answered them with polemic and debate.
  • Nationalism: the Arya Samaj gave many Hindus pride in a Vedic past; Arya Samajis such as Lala Lajpat Rai (1865–1928) were leading nationalists. Shuddhi and its stress on Hindu identity fed later Hindu nationalism.
  • Tolerance: the Brahmo ideal of one God behind all religions passed into Neo-Vedanta (see Mission 2).
  • Social reform: both helped to make the reform of caste, women's lives and education part of modern Hindu identity.
  • "Hinduism": both presented Hinduism as one religion with a clear scriptural core, a step towards seeing it as a world religion.

Mission 1Evaluate

Whose idea of Hinduism was more successful?

  • Claim: Dayananda's vision of Hinduism was more successful than Roy's.
  • For: The Arya Samaj became a mass movement, with schools and colleges across North India; the Brahmo Samaj stayed small.
  • For: Dayananda drew only on Indian sources, so he could not be accused of copying Christianity.
  • Against: Roy's ideas (reason, social reform, one God behind all faiths) passed into Neo-Vedanta and into modern Indian law and education.
  • Against: Dayananda's "Vedas alone" left out most living Hindu practice: murtis, Puranas, bhakti, festivals.
  • Against: both were selective; critics say each reshaped Hinduism to answer Western criticism.
  • Judgement criteria: numbers and lasting institutions; faithfulness to Hindu tradition; impact on social reform; effect on relations with other faiths.

Mission 2

Ramakrishna: a priest of the Mother

  • Sri Ramakrishna (1836–1886), born Gadadhar Chattopadhyay in a Bengal village; little schooling, no English.
  • From 1856, priest at the Dakshineswar Kali temple near Calcutta.
  • He longed to see the Goddess Kali as his Divine Mother; his disciples record that he had overwhelming visions of her.
  • He then followed many disciplines, guided by different teachers: Tantric practice; Vaishnava devotional moods; and Advaita, under the monk Totapuri, reaching nirvikalpa samadhi (absorption without any sense of difference).
  • His wife Sarada Devi (1853–1920) became his disciple and is revered as the Holy Mother.
  • His sayings were recorded by a disciple, "M" (Mahendranath Gupta), in the Kathamrita (The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna).
Mission 2

As many faiths, so many paths

  • According to his disciples, Ramakrishna also practised Islam (1866) and Christianity (1874) for short periods, and reported experiences of God through each.
  • His conclusion (paraphrase): "As many faiths, so many paths": each religion, sincerely followed, leads to the same God.
  • His image (paraphrase): one water is called jal, pani and "water" by different people; so one God has many names.
  • God is both formless and with form; Brahman and Shakti are one, like fire and its power to burn.
Mission 2

Images, caste, and the world

  • Image worship: unlike the Brahmos (see Mission 1), Ramakrishna worshipped the Mother in her murti; for him the image was a real meeting point with God.
  • He met Brahmo leaders, especially Keshab Chandra Sen, who made him known among educated Bengalis.
  • Caste: stories tell of him humbling his brahmin pride by cleaning the latrine of a low-caste servant.
  • A saying recorded by his disciples: serve living beings as Shiva, not out of pity (shiva jnane jiva seva). Vivekananda built on it.

Mission 2

Vivekananda

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta; Western-educated; a member of the Brahmo Samaj, sceptical of visions.
  • Met Ramakrishna in 1881 and became his leading disciple; after Ramakrishna's death (1886) he became a sannyasi and travelled across India, shocked by its poverty.
  • 1893: spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago; became famous overnight.
  • Lectured in the USA and Britain; founded the Vedanta Society of New York (1894).
  • Returned to India a hero; founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897). Died aged 39.

Mission 2

Neo-Vedanta

  • Neo-Vedanta (term used by Indologist Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern form of Vedanta taught by Vivekananda and his successors.
  • Advaita at the centre: Brahman is the one reality; every soul is potentially divine.
  • Experience (anubhava), not only scripture, is the ground of religion: religion is "realisation".
  • Four yogas (Vivekananda's lectures, 1895–96): karma, bhakti, jnana and raja yoga, suited to different temperaments, all leading to the same goal.
  • Universal: Vedanta is presented as the eternal truth behind all religions, and as friendly to science.
  • Continued by thinkers such as Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), The Hindu View of Life (1927).
Mission 2

Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission

  • Practical Vedanta (lectures in London, 1896): if Brahman is in every being, then serving people is worshipping God.
  • Vivekananda spoke of God in the poor (daridra narayana) and attacked "don't-touch-ism" (untouchability) and neglect of the masses and of women.
  • Shankara's Advaita centred on renunciation and knowledge; Vivekananda reinterpreted it as a call to social service.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897), with Belur Math near Kolkata as headquarters. Motto: "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world".
  • Today: hospitals, schools, colleges, rural development, relief after disasters, in India and beyond.
  • A liberationist approach: working to lift the poor and oppressed, seeing service to them as service to God.

Mission 2

Neo-Vedanta and Hindutva

  • Neo-Vedanta: universal, spiritual; all religions true paths; Hindu pride grounded in tolerance.
  • Hindutva (V. D. Savarkar, 1883–1966, Essentials of Hindutva, 1923): Hindu identity as a cultural and national identity; a Hindu is one for whom India is both fatherland and holy land (see Mission 3).
  • Overlap: Vivekananda called Indians to strength and national pride; many Hindutva organisations honour him.
  • Difference: his universalism (all paths true) sits uneasily with exclusivist readings of national identity; scholars and Hindus debate how far he can be claimed by either side.
  • Non-violence: Gandhi (see Mission 4) joined a Neo-Vedanta universalism to ahimsa and satyagraha; Vivekananda stressed strength and fearlessness more than non-violence.
  • Implications to weigh: interfaith relations, nationalism, tolerance.
Mission 2

Influence in India and the West

  • In the West: Vedanta Societies; yoga and meditation; writers drawn to Vedanta. Vedanta became the best-known Hindu philosophy outside India.
  • Popular image: Hinduism as tolerant, mystical and philosophical.
  • Hinduism as a world religion: a universal message, not only an Indian heritage.
  • In India: pride and confidence under colonial rule; a model of service; Vivekananda's birthday is India's National Youth Day.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Neo-Vedanta a faithful development of Hinduism?

  • Claim: Neo-Vedanta is a faithful development of Hindu tradition.
  • For: it rests on the Upanishads and Advaita; tolerance has deep Hindu roots.
  • For: Practical Vedanta draws a real ethical conclusion from "atman is Brahman".
  • Against: it centres one school (Advaita) and sidelines theistic and ritual Hinduism.
  • Against: Rambachan: it shifts authority from scripture to experience; Hacker: its inclusivism shaped Hinduism for Western audiences.
  • Judgement criteria: faithfulness to sources; fit with how most Hindus practise; fruits (service, tolerance); whether all living traditions change.

Mission 3

Savarkar: "Hindu-ness"

  • Hindutva = "Hindu-ness". Set out by V. D. Savarkar (1883–1966) in Essentials of Hindutva (1923), written in prison.
  • Savarkar, himself a rationalist, separated Hindutva (a people's culture, history and nation) from Hinduism (religious belief).
  • His definition (paraphrase): a Hindu is someone who regards India (Bharatvarsha) as both pitribhumi (fatherland) and punyabhumi (holy land).
  • So Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists count: their faiths began in India.
  • Muslims and Christians, even if Indian for generations, do not fully belong: their holy lands are elsewhere.

Mission 3

The Sangh Parivar

  • Sangh Parivar = "family of organisations" around the RSS (Rashtriya Swayamsevak Sangh, "National Volunteer Organisation"), founded 1925 in Nagpur by K. B. Hedgewar.
  • RSS: daily shakhas (branches) for physical training, discipline and "character-building"; M. S. Golwalkar (1906–1973) led it 1940–1973.
  • VHP (Vishva Hindu Parishad, 1964): religious leaders and campaigns, e.g. for a Rama temple at Ayodhya.
  • BJP (Bharatiya Janata Party, 1980): the political party; it has led India's government since 2014.
  • Others: student, labour and youth wings (e.g. Bajrang Dal), and social service bodies.
  • Ayodhya: the Babri Masjid was demolished in 1992; the Supreme Court gave the site for a temple (2019); the Ram Mandir was consecrated in 2024.
Mission 3

Neo-Vedanta and Hindutva compared

  • Shared roots: both look back to the 19th-century Hindu renaissance and to Vivekananda as a hero; both want Hindu pride after colonial rule.
  • Neo-Vedanta (Vivekananda, Ramakrishna Mission, Radhakrishnan): Hinduism is a universal spiritual truth; all religions are paths to one reality; the aim is spiritual and social service.
  • Hindutva: Hinduism is the national culture of India; the aim is a strong, united Hindu nation.
  • Tolerance: Neo-Vedanta accepts all faiths as true paths (inclusivism); Hindutva supporters say Hindus are tolerant but minorities must accept Hindu culture; critics call this exclusivism.
  • Politics: Gandhi's non-violent, interfaith nationalism vs Hindutva's cultural nationalism.

Mission 3Diversity

Hindus on Hindutva

  • Supporters: Hindutva is cultural, not theocratic; it includes all Indic faiths; it corrects centuries of foreign rule and an unfair secularism. India's Supreme Court in 1995 described Hindutva as indicating a "way of life".
  • Gandhian and Neo-Vedantin critics: true Hinduism is tolerant and universal; Hindutva turns a spiritual tradition into a political identity.
  • Secular and liberal Hindus: Hindutva threatens minorities' equal citizenship; they point to violence in Ayodhya (1992) and Gujarat (2002).
  • Dalit critics: Hindutva speaks for upper-caste Hinduism and hides caste inequality.
  • Many Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: reject being counted as "Hindu".
  • Scholars, e.g. Christophe Jaffrelot (The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics, 1996), study it as ethnic nationalism; supporters reject this label.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Hindutva is the only effective Hindu response to secularisation."

  • For: it has mass support and electoral success; it gives a clear identity in a fast-changing society; it has brought Hindu symbols back into public life.
  • Against: other responses also work: Neo-Vedanta's universal spirituality; devotional movements (ISKCON, Swaminarayan) that grow without politics; reform movements serving the poor (Ramakrishna Mission).
  • Against: "effective" for whom? Critics say it secures power but divides society and narrows Hinduism.
  • Judge it by: what counts as "effective" (numbers, depth of faith, social harmony)? Is Hindutva mainly religious at all?
Mission check

?A Jain businessman and a Muslim teacher were both born in Mumbai and have never left India. On Savarkar's definition of Hindutva, how would they be classed?

Hindutva draws its line by holy land, not by belief: that is what makes it a cultural and political identity rather than a creed.

Mission 4

A life in four places

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), born in Porbandar, Gujarat, into a Vaishnava family; his mother was deeply devout, with Jain neighbours and friends.
  • London (1888–1891): trained as a lawyer; first read the Bhagavad Gita (in Edwin Arnold's English) with Theosophist friends.
  • South Africa (1893–1914): campaigned for Indians' rights; first satyagraha campaigns; founded communal farms (Phoenix, Tolstoy Farm).
  • India (1915–1948): led the independence movement; Salt March (1930); Quit India (1942); independence and Partition (1947).
  • Shot on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for concessions to Muslims and Pakistan.
  • Called Mahatma ("great soul") and Bapu ("father").
Mission 4

Truth is God; all life is one

  • Gandhi's central idea: Satya (truth). He moved from "God is Truth" to "Truth is God" (c. 1929–31), so that even atheists seeking truth were included.
  • Advaita Vedanta: ultimate reality is one, Brahman; the self (atman) in each being is not different from it.
  • He drew a moral conclusion: he believed in "the essential unity of man and … of all that lives" (Young India, 4 Dec 1924).
  • So harming another is harming oneself: ahimsa (non-violence) follows from unity.
  • He held Advaita loosely: he also worshipped a personal God as Rama and called himself a sanatani (traditional) Hindu.
Mission 4

Satyagraha: holding firmly to truth

  • Satyagraha = satya (truth) + agraha (firm grasp): "truth-force" or "soul-force". Coined in South Africa (c. 1906–08).
  • Not "passive resistance": it is active, but never violent.
  • Aim: not to defeat the opponent but to convert them, by accepting suffering rather than inflicting it.
  • Methods: non-cooperation, civil disobedience, strikes, boycotts of foreign cloth, fasting.
  • Rules: openness (tell the authorities in advance), discipline, no hatred.
  • Example: the Salt March (1930): about 390 km to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.
Mission 4Read the source

Brahmacharya and the Gita

> "Dwelling 'mid solitudes, in diet spare, / With body, speech, and will tamed to obey, / Ever to holy meditation vowed, / From passions liberate, quit of the Self, / Of arrogance, impatience, anger, pride; / Freed from surroundings, quiet, lacking nought-- / Such an one grows to oneness with the BRAHM" (Bhagavad Gita 18:52–53, Arnold 1885)

  • Brahmacharya: literally "conduct leading to Brahman". Gandhi took a vow of celibacy in 1906, but used the word for control of all the senses: food, speech, sleep, sex.
  • He saw self-restraint as the source of the inner strength a satyagrahi needs.
  • Gandhi called the Gita his "dictionary of daily reference" (Autobiography, Part IV) and read Kurukshetra as the inner war of good and evil, not a call to fight.

Mission 4

Swaraj: rule over yourself

  • Swaraj = self-rule. For Gandhi it meant two things: India governing itself, and each person governing their own desires.
  • Hind Swaraj (1909, written in Gujarati): Indian freedom would be empty if India simply copied Western industrial civilisation.
  • Swadeshi ("of one's own country"): use local goods; the spinning wheel (charkha) and homespun cloth (khadi) became symbols of self-reliance.
  • Ideal: self-governing, simple village communities.
  • Science and medicine: in Hind Swaraj (ch. on doctors) he attacked Western medicine for treating bodies without changing the habits that made them ill; he preferred diet, nature cure and self-control. Later he accepted some modern treatment (e.g. an operation in 1924).
Mission 4

Untouchability, Partition, all religions

  • Untouchability: he called it a sin and not true Hinduism; used the name Harijan ("people of God") and ran temple-entry campaigns.
  • But he defended varna as a division of work by birth without high or low. B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) replied that caste must be destroyed, not reformed. Many Dalits find "Harijan" patronising.
  • Partition: he opposed dividing India ("vivisection"); in 1946–48 he walked through riot areas and fasted for Hindu–Muslim peace.
  • Universal religion: all religions are true, and all imperfect; equal respect for all religions (sarva dharma samabhava). His prayer meetings used the Gita, the Qur'an and Christian hymns.
  • Implications: interfaith, tolerance, a civic nationalism open to all faiths.

Mission 4Diversity

Who claims Gandhi?

  • Most Hindus in India: "Father of the Nation"; his birthday (2 October) is a national holiday.
  • Hindu nationalists: some honour his patriotism and cow protection; others, like Godse, blamed him for Partition and for favouring Muslims.
  • Orthodox Hindus of his day: opposed his temple-entry campaigns as breaking purity rules.
  • Dalit thinkers: Ambedkar saw him as a defender of caste in a reformer's clothes.
  • The non-Hindu world: an inspiration for Martin Luther King Jr (Montgomery 1955–56), the US civil rights movement and others; the UN marks 2 October as the International Day of Non-Violence.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Gandhi's achievements were more political than religious."

  • For: his lasting results were political: mass non-violent protest, independence, a secular constitution. Many followers used his methods without his religion. He himself was a skilled lawyer and organiser.
  • Against: he said his politics was his religion in action; satyagraha, ahimsa and brahmacharya are religious disciplines. His Gita reading changed how many Hindus see the text. His fasts were religious acts of self-purification.
  • Mixed record: Partition and violence he could not stop; caste reform judged too slow by Dalits; his late "experiments" testing his celibacy are widely criticised.
  • Judge it by: Can "political" and "religious" be separated in Gandhi's world view? Which achievements outlasted him? Whose verdict counts: Hindus, Dalits, or the wider world?

Mission 5

Why science is a test for any religion

  • Modern science asks for evidence and reason: claims are tested, and can be revised.
  • Religions often rest on revelation (for Hindus, shruti, "what is heard": the Vedas) and on tradition.
  • So science can challenge a tradition in three ways:
  • Method: is faith reasonable without evidence?
  • Discoveries: an old universe (Big Bang), evolution (Darwin).
  • Ethics: new powers (genetic engineering) raise new moral questions.
  • Hindus have answered in very different ways, from "no conflict at all" to firm rejection of parts of science.
Mission 5

Science inside the tradition

  • Many Hindus point out that Indian thinkers did science long before modern Europe.
  • Astronomy: Aryabhata (476–c. 550 CE), Aryabhatiya (499 CE): the earth turns on its axis; a value of π close to 3.1416; explained eclipses by shadows.
  • Brahmagupta (598–c. 668 CE): rules for calculating with zero and negative numbers.
  • Medicine: Ayurveda ("knowledge of long life"). The Charaka Samhita (internal medicine) and the Sushruta Samhita (surgery), compiled in the centuries around the turn of the era.
  • Astronomy grew partly from religious need: jyotisha set the right times for Vedic rituals.
Mission 5

Samkhya: classifying matter

  • Samkhya ("enumeration") is one of the six classical schools (darshanas); its key text is the Samkhya Karika (c. 4th–5th century CE).
  • Two ultimate realities: purusha (pure consciousness) and prakriti (matter, nature).
  • Prakriti is made of three gunas: sattva (clarity), rajas (energy), tamas (inertia).
  • It unfolds into twenty-four principles (tattvas), ending in the five gross elements: space, air, fire, water, earth.
  • Some Hindus see this as an early, reasoned classification of matter: an ancestor of science.
  • Others note it is a philosophy of liberation, not experiment: it classifies matter to free purusha from it.
Mission 5

"It was all in the Vedas"

  • Some Hindus hold that modern discoveries were revealed in the Vedas long ago.
  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), founder of the Arya Samaj, Satyarth Prakash (1875): the Vedas are the source of all true knowledge, including science (paraphrase).
  • Popular claims today: flying machines (vimanas) in the epics; nuclear weapons; "Vedic mathematics".
  • Critics, including many Hindu scientists and historians: these readings put modern ideas back into ancient texts; they can make Indian science look like myth and hide its real achievements.
  • Supporters: the Vedas are eternal knowledge, so modern science rediscovers what the rishis saw.
Mission 5Read the source

Days and nights of Brahma

Bhagavad Gita 8.18 (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "When that vast Dawn doth break, th' Invisible / Is brought anew into the Visible; / When that deep Night doth darken, all which is / Fades back again to Him Who sent it forth"

  • Hindu cosmology is cyclical: universes emerge and dissolve again and again.
  • A kalpa (a "day of Brahma") is usually reckoned at 4.32 billion years.
  • Many Hindus say the Big Bang fits: it can be the dawn of the present cycle, and the vast scale of time matches modern cosmology.

Mission 6

Vedanta: the end of the Veda

  • Vedanta means "end of the Veda": the Upanishads, the last and most philosophical part of the Vedas.
  • It became a darshana (school of philosophy), also called Uttara Mimamsa: inquiry into the later Veda.
  • Every Vedanta school comments on three texts, the prasthana-traya (triple foundation): the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras (Badarayana; date uncertain) and the Bhagavad Gita.
  • The central question: how are Brahman, the atman (self) and the world related?
  • Three great answers: Advaita (non-dual; Shankara), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual; Ramanuja), Dvaita (dual; Madhva). Ramanuja and Madhva.
Mission 6

Shankara in context

  • Shankara (also Sankara, Shankaracharya): c. 700–750 CE by many scholars; traditionally 788–820 CE. Born in Kerala.
  • Became a sannyasi (renouncer) young; disciple of Govinda, whose teacher Gaudapada had already taught non-duality (Mandukya Karika).
  • Wrote commentaries (bhashyas) on the Brahma Sutras, the main Upanishads and the Gita.
  • Rivals: Mimamsa ritualists, who held that ritual action is the heart of the Veda; and Buddhists, who denied a permanent self. Shankara argued against both: liberation comes through knowledge of the eternal self.
  • Tradition says he founded monasteries (mathas) at Sringeri, Puri, Dwarka and Jyotirmath, and organised orders of renouncers.
  • Sources: shruti above all (Upanishads read as the only valid means of knowing Brahman), the Brahma Sutras and Gita, and Gaudapada's teaching; reason supports scripture.
Mission 6

One without a second

  • For Shankara's Advaita, Brahman alone is ultimately real: "one only, without a second" (Chandogya 6:2:1, paraphrase).
  • Nirguna Brahman: without qualities; beyond thought and words. It can only be described by negation: neti, neti ("not this, not this", Brihadaranyaka 2:3:6).
  • Positively, it is described as sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss.
  • Saguna Brahman (with qualities) or Ishvara (the Lord): Brahman as seen from within the world: creator, worshipped as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess. Real at the everyday level, not the final truth.
  • Shankara teaches that atman is Brahman: the true self is not a part of Brahman but Brahman, fully.
Mission 6Read the source

"You are that"

  • Chandogya Upanishad 6 (paraphrase): Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu. Salt dissolved in water cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes of it (6:13). The subtle essence pervades everything; "that is the Self, and you are that" (tat tvam asi).
  • Shankara reads "you are that" as identity: the self and Brahman are one, not two.
  • The Gita on the self (Arnold):

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never; / Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:20, Arnold)

Mission 6

Adhyasa: superimposition

  • Adhyasa: seeing one thing as another. Shankara opens his Brahma Sutra commentary with it.
  • Rope and snake: at dusk a rope looks like a snake. The snake is superimposed on the rope. You feel real fear; but there was only ever a rope.
  • Shell and silver: mother-of-pearl glints like silver.
  • In the same way we superimpose body, mind and individuality onto the self ("I am tall", "I am sad"), and the qualities of the self onto body and mind.
  • Advaitins call the power by which the one Brahman appears as many maya.
  • Ignorance (avidya) does not make the rope vanish; knowledge removes the snake. So, for Shankara, liberation is by jnana (knowledge).

Mission 6

Maya and avidya

  • Maya (in Advaita): the power by which the one Brahman appears as many. Often translated "illusion", but the world is not "nothing".
  • Advaitins call maya indescribable: not real (it is not there in the final truth) and not unreal (it is experienced; it is not like a "son of a barren woman").
  • Avidya: ignorance in the individual; it hides Brahman and projects the world.
  • Samsara is the result: the self takes itself to be a body-mind with karma, and is reborn.
  • Brahman with a range of meanings: nirguna (beyond qualities), saguna (with qualities), Ishvara (Lord and creator), atman (the self within).
Mission 6

Three levels of reality

  • Advaitins distinguish three levels:
  • Illusory (pratibhasika): dreams, the snake in the rope. Real only while the mistake lasts.
  • Mundane, empirical (vyavaharika): the everyday world of people, gods, karma, dharma, science and worship. Fully real for us: you must still keep your promises and avoid the bus.
  • Ultimate (paramarthika): Brahman alone.
  • Each level is cancelled only by the one above: waking cancels the dream; knowing Brahman cancels the separateness of the world.
  • Shankara himself mostly contrasts two standpoints (everyday and ultimate); later Advaitins set out three.

Mission 6

Liberation, bhakti and moral action

  • Bondage (for Shankara): avidya. Liberation (moksha): direct knowledge that atman is Brahman. It is not produced by any action; it is the removal of ignorance.
  • Jivanmukti: one can be liberated while still alive.
  • Karma yoga and dharma: they do not liberate, but they purify the mind and make it ready for knowledge.
  • Bhakti is valuable but inferior for Shankara: devotion to a personal God belongs to the everyday level, where worshipper and worshipped seem two. It prepares the mind; it does not give final knowledge.
  • The Gita: Shankara reads it as teaching that knowledge is the direct means to moksha; action and devotion lead towards it.
Mission 6

Influence and scholars

  • Advaita became the most widely known school of Vedanta: carried by the Shankaracharyas of the mathas, the Smarta tradition and later Advaitins.
  • Scholars (e.g. Gavin Flood, An Introduction to Hinduism, 1996) note that this modern prominence can hide how many Hindus follow theistic Vedanta and bhakti.
  • J Hopkins (Thomas J. Hopkins, The Hindu Religious Tradition, 1971) tells Hindu history as a "great and growing synthesis" of traditions. Apply it: Shankara drew the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita into one system, against Buddhist and ritualist rivals.
  • P Bowes (Pratima Bowes, The Hindu Religious Tradition: A Philosophical Approach, 1977), paraphrase: she treats non-dualism as one of several Hindu ways of conceiving the divine, beside devotional theism, and finds the tradition's depth in not being confined to any one of them.
  • Read with the anthology extracts (3) Smart and (4) Brockington (paraphrase only).
Mission 6Evaluate

Does Advaita give the best account of Vedanta?

  • Claim: Shankara's Advaita is the most convincing reading of the Upanishads.
  • For: It takes "you are that" and "one without a second" at full strength.
  • For: Adhyasa explains how one reality can appear as many, using ordinary experience.
  • Against (Ramanuja): where does avidya live? Not in Brahman, who is pure knowledge; not in the individual self, which is itself a product of avidya.
  • Against (devotional): an impersonal Brahman leaves no one to love; bhakti, central to most Hindus, is demoted.
  • Against (ethical): if separateness is not ultimately real, why care for others? Reply: because the other is your own self, harming them harms you.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to shruti; coherence of avidya; fit with Hindu practice; consequences for ethics.

Mission 7

Ramanuja in context

  • Ramanuja: traditionally 1017–1137 CE; many scholars place him later in the 11th–12th centuries. Born near Chennai; taught at Srirangam, Tamil Nadu.
  • First studied Advaita, and disagreed with his teacher's reading of the Upanishads.
  • Became leader of the Sri Vaishnava tradition after Yamuna, devoted to Vishnu (Narayana) and Lakshmi (Sri).
  • Sources: the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; the Pancharatra temple scriptures; and the Tamil hymns of the Alvars, poet-saints of loving devotion to Vishnu.
  • Works: Sri Bhashya (on the Brahma Sutras), a Gita commentary, Vedartha Sangraha.
  • Context: he gives bhakti a Vedanta foundation, answering Shankara from within the same texts.
Mission 7

The world as God's body

  • Vishishtadvaita ("non-duality of the qualified") is the school of Ramanuja (11th–12th century), a devotee of Vishnu. For Ramanuja there is one reality, Brahman, but it is qualified by souls and matter.
  • Brahman is Vishnu: personal, with infinite good qualities (saguna). Not beyond all qualities.
  • Souls (chit) and matter (achit) are real and eternal. They are the body of God; God is their inner soul and controller.
  • As a body belongs to a person and cannot exist apart, the world belongs to God: inseparable, yet distinct.
  • "You are that" means: you, whose inner self is Brahman, belong to that Brahman. Not strict identity.
  • The world is not illusion. Maya is God's wonderful creative power.
Mission 7

Bondage and liberation for Ramanuja

  • Bondage: karma (beginningless) contracts the soul's knowledge; it identifies with bodies and is reborn.
  • Liberation (moksha): the soul's knowledge expands; after death it enjoys the bliss of God in Vaikuntha, Vishnu's heaven.
  • The liberated soul becomes like God in bliss and purity, but never identical with God. It remains itself, in loving service.
  • Means: bhakti (continuous loving meditation on God) and prapatti (surrender), with God's grace.
  • Dharma is not abandoned: duties are done as worship of God.
  • The difference from Shankara is radical: for Ramanuja, bhakti is the highest path, and the personal relationship with God is never dissolved.
Mission 7Read the source

The Gita's last word

Krishna's final teaching to Arjuna:

> "Give Me thy heart! adore Me! serve Me! cling / In faith and love and reverence to Me! / So shalt thou come to Me!" (Bhagavad Gita 18:65, Arnold)

> "And let go those— / Rites and writ duties! Fly to Me alone! / Make Me thy single refuge! I will free / Thy soul from all its sins!" (Bhagavad Gita 18:66, Arnold)

  • Ramanuja's claim: the Gita's climax is bhakti and surrender to a personal God who saves by grace. Sri Vaishnavas call 18:66 the "final verse" of surrender.
  • Shankara's claim: giving up all duties to "fly to Me" points to renunciation and knowledge of the one Self; devotion prepares for it.

Show answer

Mission 7

Madhva: five eternal differences

  • Madhva (traditionally 1238–1317 CE), from Udupi, Karnataka, where he set up the Krishna temple.
  • Dvaita: dualism. For Madhva, Vishnu alone is independent; everything else is dependent on him.
  • Five eternal differences: God and souls; God and matter; soul and soul; soul and matter; matter and matter.
  • He reads Chandogya 6:8:7 as "atat tvam asi": "you are not that". A soul is a reflection of God, never God.
  • Souls are graded: some are fit for liberation; some remain in samsara; some go to darkness. A unique and much-debated teaching.
  • Liberation: by bhakti grounded in right knowledge of God, through Vishnu's grace. Liberated souls enjoy bliss, each to its own capacity.

Mission 7

Influence: bhakti schools and Chaitanya

  • Sri Vaishnavism continues Ramanuja's teaching in South India; later it divided over how far grace needs human effort.
  • Ramanuja's thought gave philosophical respectability to devotion: a personal God, real souls, a real world.
  • Chaitanya (1486–1534), Bengal: Krishna (with Radha) as the supreme God; ecstatic sankirtan (congregational chanting of God's names).
  • Gaudiya (Bengal) Vaishnavas teach achintya-bhedabheda: souls are inconceivably one with and different from God. Like Ramanuja: personal God, real souls, bhakti as highest.
  • The Gaudiya tradition traces its lineage through Madhva's line (scholars debate the link). ISKCON comes from it.
  • Shankara's influence ran into Advaita and, in modern times, Neo-Vedanta (see Mission 2).
Mission 7

Scholars on Shankara and Ramanuja

  • J Hopkins (Thomas J. Hopkins, The Hindu Religious Tradition, 1971) tells Hindu history as a "great and growing synthesis". Apply it: Ramanuja joined the Tamil Alvars' devotion to Sanskrit Vedanta, giving temple bhakti an intellectual foundation.
  • P Bowes (Pratima Bowes, The Hindu Religious Tradition: A Philosophical Approach, 1977), paraphrase: non-dualism (Shankara) and devotionalism with "qualified monotheism" (Ramanuja) are both Hindu ways of conceiving the divine; the tradition's depth lies in not being confined to one.
  • Extract (4) (Brockington, on bhakti in the South) sets Ramanuja among the Alvars and Sri Vaishnava teachers; extract (3) (Smart) compares the Vedanta schools. Paraphrase only.
  • Strengths of Ramanuja: fits the devotion of most Hindus; keeps the world and persons real; reads the Gita's climax naturally.
  • Weaknesses: must explain how God can have a body without being limited or touched by its suffering; Advaitins say he weakens "you are that".
Mission 7Evaluate

Shankara or Ramanuja?

  • Claim: Ramanuja gives a better account of Hinduism than Shankara.
  • For: Most Hindus worship a personal God; Ramanuja makes that worship the highest path.
  • For: He avoids the problem of where avidya lives; the world is simply real.
  • Against: Shankara takes the identity texts ("you are that") more literally.
  • Against: Advaita can include bhakti at the everyday level, while pointing beyond it.
  • Against (Madhva's challenge to both): only eternal difference keeps God truly supreme.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to shruti; coherence; fit with Hindu practice; what it means for the relationship with God.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Foreign rule, Christian and Islamic challenges and Western reason pushed 19th-century Hindus to reform or defend their tradition.
  • Ram Mohan Roy (Brahmo Samaj, 1828): one formless God from the Upanishads and reason; against sati; open to other faiths. Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj, 1875): the four Vedas alone; havan; varna by merit; shuddhi.

Mission 2

  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): Kali devotee and mystic; experiences through many paths led him to teach "as many faiths, so many paths"; he accepted both images and the formless.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago 1893; Neo-Vedanta (Advaita, experience, four yogas, all religions true); Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897): serving people as God.
  • Neo-Vedanta and Hindutva share national pride but differ on universalism; Gandhi linked Neo-Vedanta to non-violence.

Mission 3

  • Savarkar (1923): a Hindu treats India as both fatherland and holy land; the Sangh Parivar (RSS, VHP, BJP) builds on this.
  • Hindus are divided: supporters see cultural pride and fair treatment; critics see exclusion of minorities and a politicised Hinduism.

Mission 4

  • Gandhi (1869–1948) joined religion and politics: Truth is God; all life is one; so non-violence (ahimsa) and satyagraha.
  • Brahmacharya (control of all senses, Gita 18:52–53) and swaraj (self-rule of person and nation) gave satyagraha its strength.
  • Hindus, Dalits and the wider world judge him differently: Father of the Nation, defender of caste, or global model of non-violence.

Mission 5

  • Science tests religion by its method, its discoveries and its new ethical powers; Hindus respond in many ways.
  • Indian astronomy and medicine (Aryabhata, Charaka, Ayurveda) and Samkhya's classification of matter show science within the tradition.

Mission 6

  • Vedanta interprets the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; Shankara's Advaita holds that Brahman alone is real and atman is Brahman.
  • The world appears through maya and avidya, by adhyasa (superimposition, like a rope seen as a snake); there are illusory, mundane and ultimate levels of reality.
  • Liberation is by knowledge; dharma, karma yoga and bhakti prepare the mind but are lower; critics question avidya, devotion and ethics.

Mission 7

  • Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita): souls and matter are real and form God's body; liberation is loving communion with Vishnu, like him but not him, reached by bhakti, surrender and grace.
  • Madhva (Dvaita): five eternal differences; God alone independent; souls graded; "you are not that".
  • Both claim the Gita; Ramanuja makes bhakti the highest path where Shankara makes it preparatory; his thought shapes later bhakti schools, including Chaitanya's.
