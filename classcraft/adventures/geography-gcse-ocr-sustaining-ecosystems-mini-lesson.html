Mission 1

What is a biome?

  • A biome is "a community of plants and animals living together in a certain kind of climate". Biomes are large-scale ecosystems.
  • An ecosystem links climate, soil, water, plants and animals. Change one and the others change: this is interdependence.
  • Climate (temperature and rainfall) does the most to decide which biome grows where.
Mission 1

The world map of biomes

  • Biomes form rough bands across the map, by latitude.
  • Near the equator: tropical forest. Either side: tropical grassland, then hot desert.
  • Further from the equator: temperate grassland and temperate forest, then boreal forest, then tundra and polar ice.
  • OCR's six to know: polar regions, coral reefs, grasslands, temperate forests, tropical forests, hot deserts.
Mission 1

Why the bands? Rising and sinking air

  • The equator gets more direct sunlight; the poles get less.
  • Near the equator warm air rises in the ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone), "forming thunderstorms": heavy rain all year feeds rainforest.
  • The air sinks over the subtropics (about 30°), "creating high-pressure regions over … deserts like the Sahara". Sinking air warms and dries, so clouds cannot form.
  • Near the poles there is "little or no solar energy in the winter", and the sun is at a low angle: cold biomes.
Quick check
?Why are many of the world's hot deserts found around 30° north and south?
Mission 1

Tropical rainforest

  • Where: between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (e.g. Amazon, Congo).
  • Climate: hot all year (at Manaus, Brazil, every month averages 27–28 °C); 2,000–10,000 mm of rain a year.
  • Plants: giant trees up to 75 m form the canopy; vines, ferns and palms below.
  • Soil: leaf litter rots fast in the heat and wet, "sending nutrients back into the soil".
  • Animals: most live in the canopy, where the food is (e.g. monkeys, toucans).
Mission 1

Hot deserts

  • Where: around 30° N and S under sinking air (e.g. Sahara).
  • Rain: about 250 mm a year; most get under 300 mm.
  • Temperature: days average 38 °C; nights fall to about −3.9 °C, because dry air lets heat escape.
  • Plants: cacti store water in their stems; some plants lie dormant until rain comes.
  • Animals: many shelter by day and are active at night (e.g. fennec fox).
Mission 1

Grasslands: tropical and temperate

  • Rain: about 500–900 mm a year: too little for dense forest.
  • Tropical grassland (savanna): warm all year with a wet and a dry season; between rainforest and desert.
  • Temperate grassland: cold winters, warm summers: prairies (North America), pampas (South America), steppes (Central Eurasia), veldt (South Africa).
  • Plants: grasses die back to their roots each year.
  • Animals: large grazing herds (e.g. zebra on the savanna, bison on the prairie) and the predators that hunt them.
Mission 1

Temperate and boreal forests

  • Temperate deciduous forest (eastern USA, Europe, China, Japan): 750–1,500 mm of rain; average 10 °C; four seasons; leaves fall in winter.
  • Boreal (coniferous) forest: about 50–60° N; winters very cold, summers around 10 °C; 300–900 mm of rain.
  • Conifers are evergreen, with needles that survive cold and dry conditions.
  • Animals in temperate forest (e.g. deer, squirrels) cope with winter: some store food or hibernate, many birds migrate.
Mission 1

Tundra and polar regions

  • Tundra: south of the Arctic ice caps, across North America, Europe and Siberia.
  • Below 0 °C for 6 to 10 months; only 150–250 mm of rain a year.
  • Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) and a short growing season mean almost no trees: mosses, lichens, grasses and shrubs.
  • Polar regions: little or no winter sun; some areas are so dry they are polar deserts.
  • Animals: most polar animals feed from the sea, e.g. seals and polar bears in the Arctic, penguins in Antarctica; thick fat and fur keep in heat.
Quick check
?A climate graph shows: total rain 200 mm a year; monthly temperatures below 0 °C for eight months; warmest month about 6 °C. Which biome is it?
Mission 1

Coral reefs

  • Where: shallow tropical and subtropical seas, 35° N to 35° S.
  • Climate: best in water of 23–29 °C; light reaches them to about 30 m.
  • Interdependence: reef corals live with tiny algae (zooxanthellae) that make food by photosynthesis.
  • Animals: about 25% of the ocean's fish depend on healthy reefs.
Mission check

?Imagine the belt of sinking air moved to about 25° north and south instead of 30°. Where would the hot deserts most likely have been?

Biomes form bands because air rises near the equator (rain, rainforest) and sinks around 30° (dry, deserts like the Sahara).

Mission 2

Where are tropical rainforests?

  • Between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, mostly close to the equator.
  • The largest areas: the Amazon (South America), the Congo Basin (Africa) and South-East Asia.
Mission 2

The climate: hot and wet all year

  • Manaus, Brazil (1970–2000):
    • every month 27–28 °C: an annual range of only 1 °C
    • 2,323 mm of rain a year; even the driest month (August) has 68 mm
  • Rainforests typically get 2,000 mm or more of rain a year.
Mission 2

Why: the equatorial climate

  • Latitude: near the equator, the sun is overhead or nearly overhead all year, so heating is strong every month.
  • Hot air rises, creating low pressure; the trade winds meet and rise in the ITCZ, forming thunderstorms.
  • Convectional rainfall: the sun heats the ground → warm, moist air rises → cools → condenses → towering clouds → afternoon storms.
Mission 2

A day in an equatorial climate

  • Singapore, 1° north (1991–2020):
    • monthly mean temperature 26.8–28.6 °C
    • 2,113 mm of rain a year, on 171 days
  • Storms come mostly in the afternoon, when solar heating is strongest; clouds tower 9–12 km high.
Quick check
?Singapore's warmest month averages 28.6 °C and its coolest 26.8 °C. What is its annual temperature range?
Mission 2

Layers of the forest

  • Emergents: the tallest trees, standing above the rest.
  • Canopy: a dense roof of leaves that blocks most sunlight from reaching the ground. Woody vines climb trees to reach the light.
  • Under canopy: smaller trees, vines, ferns and palms in dim light.
  • Shrub layer and forest floor: dark, with few plants, covered in leaf litter.
Mission 2

Soils: latosols

  • Latosols are deep: warm, wet conditions weather the rock far down.
  • They are red, from iron oxides.
  • They are poor in nutrients: heavy rain washes nutrients down and out (leaching).
  • Only a thin top layer is rich, where leaf litter rots.
  • Soil profile, top to bottom: leaf litter → thin humus-rich layer → thick red leached layer → weathered rock.
Mission 2

The nutrient cycle (Gersmehl model)

  • Nutrients are held in three stores: biomass (living things), litter (dead leaves) and soil.
  • Flows link them: leaf fall, decomposition, uptake by roots, plus leaching and runoff (out) and rain and weathered rock (in).
  • In a rainforest, the biomass store is largest. Litter rots fast in warm, wet conditions, and roots take the nutrients straight back up.
  • So the litter and soil stores are small.
Quick check
?In the Gersmehl model of a tropical rainforest, which store holds the most nutrients?
Mission 2

The water cycle

  • The canopy intercepts rain; much evaporates back into the air.
  • Trees take up water and release it through their leaves (transpiration).
  • This moist air makes more rain: air that has passed over forest produces at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • So the forest helps make its own rain: a regional-scale process.
Mission 2

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: the parts of an ecosystem depend on each other; change one and the others change.
  • Climate → plants: heat and rain all year let plants grow all year.
  • Plants → soil: leaf fall feeds the thin fertile layer; roots hold the soil.
  • Plants → water → climate: transpiration makes more rain.
  • Animals ↔ plants: animals eat fruit and spread seeds; plants give food and shelter.
  • People: forest peoples depend on it for food and materials; clearing breaks the links.
Mission check

?Farmers clear a patch of rainforest and burn the trees. Crops grow well for two or three years, then fail. What best explains this?

In the Gersmehl model the rainforest's nutrients sit mainly in biomass; litter rots fast and is taken straight back up. Clear the trees and heavy rain leaches the thin, poor soil, so crops soon fail.

Mission 3

The Amazon rainforest

  • The largest tropical rainforest on Earth. Most of it is in Brazil; it also covers parts of eight other South American countries and territories.
  • About 16,000 tree species.
  • Amazon trees store more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth.
  • This mission uses the Brazilian Amazon as the case study.
Mission 3

Goods and services

  • Goods: things people take from the forest: timber, Brazil nuts, rubber, fish, fruits and medicines.
  • Services: things the forest does for people: it stores carbon, recycles rainfall, shelters wildlife, and protects soils from erosion.
  • Goods can be sold. Services are harder to price, so they are easily ignored when forest is cleared.
Mission 3

Changing rates of deforestation

  • From 2004 to 2012 the rate of clearing in the Brazilian Amazon fell by 84%.
  • 2012: 4,571 km² cleared, the lowest on record until 2026.
  • The rate then rose again: 5,731 km² in August 2024–July 2025.
  • August 2025–July 2026: an estimated 3,335 km², the lowest since records began in 1988.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: By what percentage did clearing fall from 2024–25 (5,731 km²) to 2025–26 (3,335 km²)?
Mission 3

Cause: farming

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–15 was driven by agriculture.
  • In the Amazon, most cleared land first becomes cattle pasture (commercial farming).
  • Soy then spreads, mostly onto pastures first cleared for cattle.
  • Much soy is exported: in 2025 China imported 82.3 million tonnes of soybeans from Brazil, 74% of its soybean imports.
Mission 3

Cause: logging

  • Selective logging takes valuable trees and leaves the rest standing.
  • 1999–2002, in Brazil's top five timber states, 12,075–19,823 km² were logged each year: 60–123% of the area deforested.
  • Logging roads open up the forest to farmers and settlers.
Mission 3

Cause: roads, mining and energy

  • Roads: nearly 95% of deforestation happened within 5.5 km of a road or 1 km of a river. Over 190,000 km of the roads are unofficial.
  • Mining: small-scale gold mining (garimpo) in Brazil grew from about 218 km² (1985) to about 2,627 km² (2022). More than 91% is in the Amazon, and at least 77% of 2022 sites showed signs of illegality.
  • Energy (HEP): the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River flooded land and forced people in Altamira to move.
Quick check
?Which cause most often opens the forest up for the others?
Mission 3

Impact: rainfall and regional climate

  • Trees pump water vapour into the air (transpiration), which falls again as rain downwind.
  • Over more than 60% of tropical land, air that has passed over forest gives at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Replacing forest with monoculture crops or pasture means less moisture is recycled.
  • If clearing trends continued, Amazon rainfall could fall by 12% (wet season) and 21% (dry season) by 2050.
Mission 3

Impact: climate change

  • Trees store carbon. Clearing and burning release it as carbon dioxide.
  • Aircraft measurements in 2010–2018 found south-eastern Amazonia had become a net carbon source.
  • The causes: more deforestation and longer, drier dry seasons, bringing more fires.
Mission 3

Impact: biodiversity

  • Habitat loss shrinks each species' range.
  • Fragmentation: in 1999–2002, deforestation and logging created about 32,000 km and 38,000 km of new forest edge each year.
  • Small, separate fragments leave isolated populations that cannot mix or move.
  • Species found only in cleared areas can die out locally, or globally: extinction.
Quick check
?Which impact of clearing works at a regional scale, across much of the Amazon basin?
Mission 3

Tourism

  • Ecotourism brings visitors to forest lodges and river trips.
  • Impacts: boats, waste, trails and disturbance of wildlife, usually over small areas.
  • Benefits: income and jobs give local people a reason to keep the forest standing.
Mission check

?In September–October 2015, forest and peatland fires across maritime south-east Asia, mostly in Indonesia, released more CO₂ per day than all the fossil fuels burned in the EU. Which conclusion best fits?

In the Amazon, farming (cattle, then soy) is the main cause, helped by roads, logging, mining and HEP. The impacts reach far beyond Brazil: rainfall, carbon and biodiversity.

Mission 4

What makes management sustainable?

  • Sustainable management meets people's needs today without destroying the forest for the future.
  • Test any plan with three questions:
  • Environment: does forest cover and wildlife stay?
  • People: do local people earn a living from it?
  • Long term: will it still work in 20 years, with rules that are enforced?
Mission 4

The case: the Maya Biosphere Reserve, Guatemala

  • Petén, northern Guatemala: the largest expanse of continuous rainforest in Central America.
  • Guatemala's protected areas cover 32% of its land, yet only Nicaragua has lost forest faster in Latin America and the Caribbean since 2000.
  • The reserve is shared out in zones, each with different rules.
Mission 4

Zoning

  • Core zone: strictly protected; no logging. Its national parks include Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul.
  • Multiple-use zone: forest used under rules. Rights to timber and other forest products were given to 12 community groups and 2 timber companies.
  • Buffer zone: along the southern edge; farming and settlement, with some clearing allowed "under certain circumstances".
  • UNESCO's model: core = strict protection; buffer = activities compatible with conservation; transition = where communities live and work.
Mission 4

Local scale: Uaxactún's community forest

  • A village of about 800 people that manages 835 km² of forest in the multiple-use zone.
  • Selective logging since 2000; certified by the FSC since 2001.
  • Residents agreed hunting rules: no hunting at waterholes, no selling meat to outsiders.
  • Result: camera-trap surveys estimated 25 species of large ground animals in Uaxactún's forest and 24 in the strictly protected Mirador–Río Azul area next door.
  • But heavily hunted species, such as the great curassow, were scarcer near the village.
Mission 4

Does it work?

  • Success: Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul national parks stayed over 99% stable forest, 2000–2017.
  • Mixed: some concessions manage forests well; others "have had limited success or failed completely".
  • Failure: the buffer zone lost 1.4% of its forest a year; by 2017 only 17% was undisturbed forest.
  • Pressure on the core: Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared despite its protection.
  • Exam move: evaluate by naming a strength and a limitation, with evidence, then judge which matters more.
Quick check
?Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared, while Mirador–Río Azul National Park, in the far north, stayed almost untouched. Both are in the same strictly protected core zone. What does this suggest?
Mission check

?A government wants to cut deforestation in a rainforest region where many villagers depend on the forest for income. Which plan is most likely to be sustainable?

In the Maya Biosphere Reserve, a certified community forest at Uaxactún kept as many large animal species as the strictly protected forest next door. Where rules were not enforced, as in the buffer zone, the forest kept shrinking.

Mission 5

Where are the cold environments?

  • Polar: the ice-covered lands around the poles.
  • Antarctica: a continent around the South Pole, surrounded by the Southern Ocean. 98% of its land is covered by ice up to 4.7 km thick.
  • The Arctic: "largely an ocean surrounded by land".
  • Tundra: the treeless land north of the Arctic tree line, where the average July temperature does not rise above 10 °C.
  • Remember the difference: Antarctica = land (an ice-covered continent) ringed by sea; the Arctic = sea (sea ice that grows in winter and melts in summer) ringed by land.
Mission 5

Antarctica: the coldest, windiest, driest continent

  • Average yearly temperature: about −10 °C on the coast to −60 °C on the high interior.
  • Lowest temperature recorded on Earth's surface: −89.2 °C, Vostok station, 21 July 1983.
  • Precipitation is very low: about 150 mm of water a year over the whole continent; under 50 mm on the high plateau. Most falls as snow.
  • Katabatic winds: cold, dense air flows down off the ice sheet. Gusts "well over 200 km/h" have been measured.
Mission 5

Why so cold and so dry?

  • Latitude and sun angle: near the poles, sunlight arrives at a low angle, so its energy is spread over a wide area. In winter there is little or no sunlight at all.
  • High pressure: the interior of Antarctica is "dominated by high pressure". Sinking air is dry, so "little cloud" forms and little snow falls.
  • Altitude: the ice sheet is elevated, and the high plateau is the coldest part. Air is colder at height.
  • Albedo: snow reflects up to 90% of the Sun's energy back to space.
Quick check
?Why does the interior of Antarctica receive less than 50 mm of precipitation a year?
Mission 5

The Arctic and the tundra climate

  • Utqiaġvik, Alaska (tundra coast), 1991–2020 averages: February −24.4 °C, July 5.4 °C; precipitation about 137 mm a year.
  • Near the coast: a maritime climate, with cool, cloudy summers.
  • Inland: a continental climate, drier, with "frigid temperatures far below freezing" in winter; some parts of Siberia average below −40 °C in January.
  • At the North Pole there are about six months of daylight and six months of night.
Mission 5

Permafrost and tundra soils

  • Permafrost: ground that stays at or below 0 °C for at least two years.
  • It underlies about 25% of the exposed land in the northern hemisphere.
  • Active layer: the top layer that thaws each summer. Plants can only root here.
  • Below it the frozen ground stops water draining away, so soils are thin, nutrient-poor and waterlogged.
  • Water: in summer the thawed active layer sits on frozen ground, so the tundra is dotted with pools and bogs.
Mission 5

How plants survive

  • Antarctica: lichens (about 200 species), mosses and liverworts (over 50), and algae.
  • Only two flowering plants, a grass and a small cushion plant, both on the northern and western Antarctic Peninsula.
  • They must survive "repeated freezing and thawing and little fresh water"; water is the most important factor for where they grow.
  • Tundra: plants are low-growing. Some "hug the ground in tight little cushions, protecting them against wind and frost".
  • Short summers mean plants must grow and flower quickly while the top layer of the ground is thawed.
Mission 5

How animals survive

Emperor penguin (Antarctica):

  • Several layers of feathers for insulation; large reserves of body fat.
  • Small bill and flippers to cut heat loss.
  • Arteries and veins lie close together, so blood recycles heat before it reaches the feet.
  • Huddling: up to 10 birds per square metre; it cuts heat loss by up to 50%.
  • Arctic fox (tundra): a thick fur coat and special blood circulation in its paws.
  • Caribou (tundra): hollow hair that "holds heat well"; wide feet that spread out on snow and spongy tundra; long migrations between summer and winter ranges.
Quick check
?An emperor penguin has small flippers for its size. How does this help it survive?
Mission 5

Abiotic and biotic links in Antarctica

  • Abiotic = non-living (sea ice, light, temperature, water). Biotic = living (algae, krill, penguins, seals, whales).
  • Sea ice → algae → krill: in winter, krill feed on "algae growing on the underside of the pack ice".
  • Krill → larger animals: "Most of the larger Antarctic animals (seals, whales, seabirds, fish and squid) depend on Antarctic krill, directly or indirectly".
  • Land plants grow only where there is ice-free rock and liquid water in summer.
  • So a change in one abiotic factor, such as less sea ice, can pass up the whole food web.
Mission 5

Interdependence on the tundra

  • Climate → permafrost: long, very cold winters keep the ground frozen.
  • Permafrost → soils → plants: thin, waterlogged soils mean low shrubs, mosses and lichens, not forest.
  • Plants → animals: caribou eat lichen, moss, grass and other low-growing plants. Caribou are food for bears, wolverines and wolves.
  • Animals → people: caribou are "a main subsistence food source for Native residents in arctic Alaska".
  • People and animals → plants: caribou grazing "can have substantial effects on plant and lichen communities"; people who build on permafrost can thaw it.
Quick check
?Warmer summers thaw a deeper active layer, and woody shrubs spread across the tundra. Which chain follows most directly?
Mission check

?Antarctica's only two flowering plants grow on the northern and western Antarctic Peninsula, not on the high interior plateau. Which explanation fits best?

Life survives the cold where an abiotic need is just met (here, liquid water in a short summer) and when plants and animals are built and behave to save heat and water. The Australian Antarctic Program says the maritime Peninsula "generally receives more precipitation and has milder temperatures".

Mission 6

Case study: Alaska, USA

  • A US state in the Arctic and sub-Arctic, with tundra in the far north.
  • Some form of permafrost (ground frozen for at least two years) covers about 85% of Alaska.
  • Its North Slope, on the Arctic coast, holds oil fields far from Alaska's cities and ports.
  • This is your study of the Arctic. The North Slope, Utqiaġvik and Red Dog lie north of the Arctic Circle; Dutch Harbor and most national-park visits are further south, so use them only as Alaska-wide context.
Mission 6

Challenge: extreme temperature

  • Utqiaġvik, on the Arctic coast (1991–2020 averages): February −24.4 °C; July 5.4 °C.
  • Extreme cold freezes machinery and fuel, and makes outdoor work dangerous.
  • Short summers give little time to build.
Mission 6

Opportunity: oil and energy

  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay in 1968.
  • The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) runs 800 miles from Deadhorse to the port of Valdez. It has carried more than 18 billion barrels.
  • In fiscal year 2025 petroleum gave the State $1,910.5 million of its $6,342.2 million unrestricted revenue: about 30%.
Mission 6

Challenge: building on permafrost

  • When TAPS was designed, oil in the pipe was expected to be 70–80 °C (158–176 °F). That hot, it "would thaw a cylindrical area 20 to 30 feet in diameter within a decade".
  • Thawed permafrost turns to mud: "the pipeline [would] sink and break".
  • So TAPS is raised on supports for 420 miles and buried for 380 miles. Raised sections carry devices that release heat into the air.
  • Buildings face the same problem: on thawing ground, "buildings tilt, roads buckle and dip".
Quick check
?TAPS is 800 miles long and raised for 420 miles. What percentage of it is raised?
Mission 6

Challenge: inaccessibility

  • In summer the thawed top layer of tundra is marshy, so heavy trucks cannot cross it.
  • In winter, supplies travel on ice roads at least 100 cm thick, carrying trucks of up to 64 tonnes to mines and drill sites.
  • Ice roads melt each summer and must be rebuilt every winter.
  • Remoteness makes fuel, food, building materials and labour expensive.
Mission 6

Opportunity: mineral extraction

  • Red Dog, "one of the world's largest zinc mines", lies about 170 km north of the Arctic Circle, near Kotzebue.
  • It was developed under an agreement with the landowner, NANA, "a Regional Alaska Native corporation owned by the Iñupiat of northwest Alaska".
  • Local people own the land and share in the mine's income.
Mission 6

Opportunities: fishing and tourism

  • Fishing: Dutch Harbor, in Alaska's Aleutian Islands, has been the leading US port for seafood by volume for 26 years in a row (to 2024).
  • Catches and prices vary: Alaska's seafood industry lost an estimated $1.8 billion in revenue between 2022 and 2023.
  • Tourism: in 2023, 3.3 million visitors to Alaska's national parks spent $1.5 billion, supporting 21,274 jobs.
Quick check
?Which of these is a challenge of developing Alaska, not an opportunity?
Mission 6

Impacts on the Arctic: oil

  • Spills: on TAPS, spills of more than 55 gallons averaged about 7.6 a year in 1977–1994 and 1.5 a year in 1995–2025. Spills poison soil, water and wildlife.
  • Permafrost damage: building, vehicles and warm pipes can thaw frozen ground.
  • Climate change adds risk: a 2018 study projects that 550 km of TAPS lies where near-surface permafrost may thaw by 2050. This is a projection, not an observation.
Mission 6

Impacts: research and indigenous people

  • Scientific research: NOAA's Barrow Atmospheric Baseline Observatory, established in 1973, is about 8 km north-east of Utqiaġvik. NOAA's laboratory there researches greenhouse gases, aerosols and radiation. Research has a small footprint, and gives evidence of change.
  • Indigenous people: the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act (1971) settled Alaska Native land claims. Native corporations such as NANA now own land and earn income from it (Red Dog).
  • Development brings jobs and money, but can disturb hunting, fishing and traditional ways of life.
Mission 6

Impacts: whaling, fishing and tourism

  • Whaling: the IWC banned commercial whaling from the 1985/86 season.
  • The USA (Alaska) is one of four member countries that carry out aboriginal subsistence whaling. It "is not subject to the moratorium"; catches aim to be "appropriate to cultural and nutritional requirements in the long term".
  • Norway lodged a formal objection to the moratorium, sets its own catch limits and takes North Atlantic minke whales commercially in its own waters.
  • Fishing: large catches support jobs, but stocks must be limited so fish can breed and replace themselves.
  • Tourism: millions of visitors bring money, but also traffic, waste and wildlife disturbance to manage.
Quick check
?Which statement about whaling in the Arctic is correct?
Mission check

?A company plans a new pipeline to carry warm oil across Alaska's North Slope. Along which stretch is it most important to raise the pipe above the ground?

Warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost, so the pipe could sink and break. TAPS is raised for 420 miles where the permafrost is unstable and buried for 380 miles in thawed soils, bedrock or thaw-stable ground.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Over 100,000 tourists a year now visit Antarctica. Who decides how to keep it wild?

Make a guess: Over 100,000 tourists a year now visit Antarctica. Who decides how to keep it wild?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 4.3b one small-scale example of sustainable polar management, e.g. sustainable tourism
Mission 7

A wilderness

  • A wilderness is a large area where people have changed very little.
  • Antarctica is protected as a "natural reserve, devoted to peace and science".
  • It is fragile: in the cold, plants grow and waste breaks down very slowly, so damage lasts a long time.
  • Much of its wildlife depends on one small animal: krill.
Mission 7

Threats: tourism and fishing

  • Tourism: about 6,700 passengers landed in 1992–93; now over 100,000 people visit each year.
  • In 2025–26, 113,518 people travelled with members of the tour operators' association.
  • About 98% of tourist voyages go to the Antarctic Peninsula, so visits concentrate on a few sites.
  • Fishing: krill is fished commercially. A treaty body to manage it, CCAMLR, was set up in 1982 "in response to increasing commercial interest in Antarctic krill".
Mission 7

International agreements

  • Antarctic Treaty (1959): signed by 12 countries, now 58 Parties. Antarctica is for "peaceful purposes only" and science.
  • Environment Protocol (signed 1991, in force 1998): bans mineral resource activity except science. Neither it nor the Treaty has an end date: the claim that it "expires" in 2048 "is not correct".
Mission 7

Environmental impact assessment

  • The Protocol requires an environmental impact assessment (EIA) before any activity in Antarctica.
  • If an activity is likely to have "a minor or transitory impact", an Initial Environmental Evaluation is needed. If more, a Comprehensive Environmental Evaluation.
  • Draft comprehensive evaluations are made public and reviewed by an expert committee, which advises the Treaty Parties.
Mission 7

Rules for tourists

  • Treaty guidelines (2021): ships with more than 500 passengers "shall not make landings"; at most 100 passengers ashore at once; one guide per 20 passengers.
  • The tour operators' association, IAATO, schedules ships centrally "to prevent overcrowding" and sets whale slow-down zones.
  • IAATO attends the Treaty Parties' annual meetings as an invited expert.
Quick check
?Of 113,518 people who travelled with IAATO members in 2025–26, 27,217 were on cruise-only ships that did not land. What percentage were not on cruise-only ships?
Mission 7

Case study: is Antarctic tourism sustainable?

  • Small-scale management: each landing is limited in size, guided and scheduled.
  • Strengths: big ships cannot land; groups are small and guided; scheduling spreads visits between sites.
  • Weaknesses: visitor numbers grew from about 6,700 landed passengers in 1992–93 to over 100,000 travellers a year; IAATO's own measures rely on operators following them.
  • Judgement: managed to limit local harm, but growth puts pressure on the most-visited sites.
Mission check

?A company proposes to build a hotel on the Antarctic coast. What must happen before it could go ahead?

Many countries decide together, through the Antarctic Treaty and its Environment Protocol: no mining, impact assessments for every activity, and limits on landings. CCAMLR limits fishing, and the tour operators' association, IAATO, adds its own rules.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Seven countries claim parts of Antarctica, and some of the claims overlap. Can one treaty keep a whole continent safe?

Make a guess: Seven countries claim parts of Antarctica, and some of the claims overlap. Can one treaty keep a whole continent safe?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 4.3b case study of one global example of sustainable polar management, e.g. Earth Summits or the Antarctic Treaty
Mission 8

Why Antarctica needs managing

  • Seven countries claim parts of Antarctica: Argentina, Australia, Chile, France, New Zealand, Norway and the UK; some claims overlap.
  • Other countries recognise no claims.
  • 1957–58: scientists from 12 countries worked there in the International Geophysical Year.
  • Risks: rival claims, military use, mining, pollution, and now tourism.
  • Sustainable management: meet people's needs today (science, tourism) without damaging the continent for the future.
Mission 8

The Antarctic Treaty, 1959

  • Signed on 1 December 1959 by those 12 countries; in force 1961.
  • "Antarctica shall be used for peaceful purposes only" (Art. I).
  • Science is free, and results "shall be exchanged and made freely available" (Arts. II–III).
  • Nuclear explosions and radioactive waste banned (Art. V).
  • Covers everywhere south of 60° South, including ice shelves (Art. VI).
Mission 8

Claims frozen

  • Article IV: "No new claim, or enlargement of an existing claim … shall be asserted while the present Treaty is in force".
  • Claimants keep their claims; others need not recognise them.
  • The ATS says this "preserves the status quo": the argument is paused, not settled.
Quick check
?What did the 1959 Antarctic Treaty do about territorial claims?
Mission 8

Who decides, and how

  • 58 countries are Parties; 29 are Consultative Parties that make decisions.
  • A newcomer gets a vote only by "conducting substantial research activity there".
  • Decisions need every Consultative Party to agree (consensus).
  • Any Consultative Party can send inspectors, with "complete freedom of access at any time" to stations, ships and aircraft.
Mission 8

The Madrid Protocol, 1991

  • Signed in Madrid, 4 October 1991; in force 1998.
  • Makes Antarctica "a natural reserve, devoted to peace and science".
  • Article 7: bans all mining (mineral resource activities), except scientific research.
  • Environmental impact assessment: every activity must be assessed first; big impacts need a full evaluation, published and reviewed.
  • Waste reduced and removed; some products (e.g. PCBs) banned outright.
  • Wildlife: no taking, harmful disturbance or bringing in non-native species without a permit.
Quick check
?A country plans a large new research station on an ice-free coast near a penguin colony. Under the Madrid Protocol, what must happen first?
Mission 8

The tourism challenge

  • Tourists: "a few hundred" a season in the early 1990s → "over 100,000 passengers annually".
  • About 98% of tourist voyages go to the Antarctic Peninsula, October to April.
  • More than 20 sites "frequently host over 15,000 visitors" each season.
  • Management: visitor site guidelines, general guidelines for visitors (updated 2025) and post-visit reports.
  • Most tour operators belong to IAATO, an industry body that advises Treaty meetings.
Mission 8

The limits

  • Claims paused, not solved (Article IV).
  • Consensus is slow: rules on tour operators' contingency plans and insurance, Measure 4 (2004), are still "under consideration".
  • The 2005 annex on liability for environmental emergencies is not in force until all Consultative Parties approve it.
  • No Antarctic police: inspectors are sent by member states.
  • Outside forces: the Treaty cannot stop global warming reaching Antarctica; scientists report on it to Treaty meetings every year.

Mission check

The Antarctic Treaty (1959) and Madrid Protocol (1991) set rules for peace, science and a mining ban. Its limits are frozen claims, slow consensus on tourism rules, and threats like warming that no treaty can stop.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Biomes are large-scale ecosystems; their distribution follows climate: rainforest at the equator, deserts at about 30°, temperate and boreal forest further out, tundra and ice near the poles.
  • Climate, soil, water, plants and animals are interdependent; coral reefs need warm, clear, shallow water.

Mission 2

  • Characteristics: between the Tropics; hot (27–28 °C) and wet (over 2,000 mm) all year from overhead sun, low pressure and convectional rain; layers from emergents to forest floor; deep, red, leached latosols.
  • How it works: nutrients stored mainly in biomass and recycled fast; forests make their own rain; everything is interdependent.

Mission 3

  • Amazon clearing fell sharply after 2004, rose again after 2012, and in 2025–26 reached its lowest estimate since 1988.
  • Causes: farming (cattle, soy), logging, roads, mining, HEP.
  • Impacts: less rainfall, carbon release and biodiversity loss.

Mission 4

  • Zoning (strict core, managed multiple-use, buffer) works where people benefit and rules are enforced: Uaxactún kept its wildlife; the buffer zone lost most of its forest.

Mission 5

  • Cold environments are cold because of latitude and low sun angle, high pressure (sinking, dry air), altitude and high albedo; Antarctica is a polar desert.
  • Plants grow fast in short summers and need liquid water; animals insulate, cut heat loss and huddle. Tundra plants keep low, and caribou migrate.
  • On the tundra, climate → permafrost → thin, waterlogged soils → low plants → caribou → people: change one link and the rest change.

Mission 6

  • Alaska: opportunities in oil (TAPS, about 30% of State revenue), zinc (Red Dog), fishing (Dutch Harbor) and tourism (3.3 million park visitors). Challenges: extreme cold, inaccessibility (ice roads) and building on permafrost.
  • Human activity affects the Arctic through oil spills, permafrost thaw, fishing, tourism and whaling; research and Native land ownership shape how it is managed.

Mission 7

  • Antarctica is a fragile wilderness, protected as a natural reserve for peace and science. Tourism and krill fishing put pressure on it; mining is banned.
  • It is managed by international agreement (Treaty, Protocol, CCAMLR), impact assessment, visitor rules and industry self-regulation.
  • Small-scale case: IAATO and Treaty rules keep landings small, guided and scheduled, but numbers keep growing.

Mission 8

  • Treaty 1959: peace, free science, frozen claims (Art. IV), no nuclear tests; 58 Parties, 29 decide by consensus.
  • Madrid Protocol 1991: natural reserve, mining ban (Art. 7), impact assessment, waste and wildlife rules; no end date.
  • Limits: tourism (100,000+ a year) rules slow to agree, claims unresolved, enforcement by states, global warming.
