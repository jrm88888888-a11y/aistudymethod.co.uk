Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Make a guess: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Mission 1

Only differences can be measured

  • A voltmeter needs two electrodes, so it measures a potential difference between two half-cells.
  • No single half-cell's potential can be measured on its own.
  • So one half-cell is chosen as the reference and given the value 0 V.
  • Every other half-cell is measured against it.
Mission 1

The standard hydrogen electrode

  • Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): H2 gas bubbling over platinum in a solution of H⁺(aq).
  • Conditions: H2 at 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), 298 K.
  • Half-equation: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g). E⦵ = 0.00 V by definition.
  • Platinum is inert: it carries electrons and gives a surface for the reaction. It is often coated with fine platinum black.
  • Zero is an agreed value, not "no reaction".
Mission 1

Building a cell to measure E⦵

  • Electrodes: carry electrons between the half-cell and the wire.
  • Solutions: hold the ions that react in each half-cell.
  • Salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in KNO3(aq)): ions move through it to keep each solution neutral. No electrons pass through it.
  • High-resistance voltmeter: draws almost no current, so it reads the full potential difference.
  • In the lab: clean the metal with emery paper; use KNO3, not KCl, if Ag⁺ is present (AgCl would precipitate).

Mission 1

Measuring E⦵ for nickel

  • Connect a standard Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell to the SHE.
  • The voltmeter reads 0.25 V, and nickel is the negative electrode: electrons leave it.
  • So E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni) = −0.25 V. Negative electrode against the SHE → negative E⦵.
  • The whole difference is assigned to nickel, because the SHE is 0 V.
Mission 1

Why conditions matter

  • E changes with concentration, temperature and gas pressure.
  • Standard: 298 K, 100 kPa for gases, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ for ions. Only then is it E⦵.
  • More Ni²⁺(aq): the equilibrium Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni shifts right, so E becomes less negative.
Mission 1

Half-cells with no metal: platinum electrodes

  • Some couples have no solid metal: Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺, Cl2/Cl⁻.
  • Dip platinum into a solution of both forms: Fe³⁺(aq) and Fe²⁺(aq), 1.00 mol dm⁻³ each.
  • For a gas (Cl2), bubble it over platinum in Cl⁻(aq), like the SHE.
  • Measured against the SHE: E⦵(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.77 V.
Mission 1

Writing it down: cell diagrams

  • Half-equations are written as reductions: oxidised form + ne⁻ ⇌ reduced form (IUPAC convention).
  • Pt(s) | H2(g) | H⁺(aq) ‖ Ni²⁺(aq) | Ni(s)
  • | = phase boundary; ‖ = salt bridge. The SHE goes on the left.
  • Ion/ion couple: Pt(s) | H2(g) | H⁺(aq) ‖ Fe³⁺(aq), Fe²⁺(aq) | Pt(s); a comma separates species in the same phase.
  • E(cell) = E(right) − E(left). With the SHE on the left, the cell EMF equals E⦵ of the right half-cell.
Mission 1

Reading a table of E⦵ values

  • Listed in order, the values form the electrochemical series.
  • More positive E⦵: the species on the left is more easily reduced.
  • More negative E⦵: the species on the right is more easily oxidised.
  • So the strongest oxidising agent is on the left at the most positive end; the strongest reducing agent is on the right at the most negative end.
  • Compare signed values: −2.37 V is the lowest value, not the "largest".

Mission 2: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Make a guess: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Mission 2

Cell potential from two E⦵ values

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode). In a cell diagram: E⦵(right) − E⦵(left).
  • Reduction happens at the positive electrode (right-hand side of the diagram): the couple with the more positive E⦵.
  • Boards call E⦵cell the EMF, the standard cell potential or the standard emf.
  • Worked example: zinc and silver half-cells.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E⦵ = +0.80 V. Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), E⦵ = −0.76 V.
  • Ag⁺ is reduced; Zn is oxidised. E⦵cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.
  • Cell diagram: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) ‖ Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s).
  • Never multiply E⦵ by a coefficient: 2Ag⁺ or Ag⁺, the value is +0.80 V.

Mission 2

Will it react?

  • For an equation, find the species reduced: its E⦵ goes first.
  • E⦵cell positive: the reaction is feasible as written. Negative: the reverse is feasible.
  • Cl2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2Cl⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +1.36 − (+0.77) = +0.59 V. Chlorine oxidises Fe²⁺.
  • I2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2I⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +0.54 − (+0.77) = −0.23 V. No reaction; instead Fe³⁺ oxidises I⁻.
  • Shortcut, using the electrochemical series: the oxidised form of the more positive couple reacts with the reduced form of the less positive couple.

Mission 2

Why lithium gives a big voltage

  • Li⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Li(s): E⦵ = −3.04 V, the most negative common value.
  • So Li is the strongest reducing agent: it gives up electrons most readily.
  • E⦵cell is the gap between two E⦵ values. A very negative E⦵ makes a big gap with almost any partner.
  • Zinc (−0.76 V) is far less negative. An alkaline cell, with a zinc negative electrode, gives about 1.5 V.
  • A lithium coin cell reaches about 3 V with one cell, not two stacked.
  • Lithium reacts with water, so lithium cells use a non-aqueous electrolyte.
Mission 2

Background: E⦵cell and Gibbs energy

  • Beyond your course, but it explains the sign rule: ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell (F = charge on 1 mol of electrons).
  • n = electrons transferred in the balanced equation: Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag, n = 2.
  • Positive E⦵cell → negative ΔG → feasible. Your exams use E⦵cell only.
Mission 2

Limits of E⦵ predictions

  • E⦵ values apply only at standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³. Change any of these and E changes.
  • Concentration: MnO2 + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 2H2O (+1.23 V) vs ½Cl2 + e⁻ ⇌ Cl⁻ (+1.36 V). E⦵cell = −0.13 V, yet concentrated HCl is oxidised to Cl2 by MnO2.
  • High [H⁺] and [Cl⁻] shift the potentials enough to make it feasible.
  • Kinetics: E⦵ says nothing about rate. Mg + cold water: E⦵cell = +1.54 V, but the reaction is very slow (high activation energy).
  • So "feasible" means "can happen", not "will be seen to happen".
Mission 2

Measuring a cell's voltage

  • Clean each metal strip with emery paper to remove the oxide layer.
  • Dip each into a solution of its ions, 1.00 mol dm⁻³, at 298 K.
  • Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in saturated KNO3(aq). (KCl would precipitate AgCl in a silver half-cell.)
  • Use a high-resistance voltmeter: almost no current flows, so it reads the maximum voltage.
  • Ions of one element in two oxidation states (e.g. Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺): platinum electrode, equimolar solution.
  • Compare with the prediction. Small differences come from non-standard concentrations or temperature and oxide films.
Mission 2

Choosing a reducing agent: vanadium

  • VO2⁺ (+5, yellow) → VO²⁺ (+4, blue) → V³⁺ (+3, green) → V²⁺ (+2, violet).
  • VO2⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H2O, +1.00 V · VO²⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V³⁺ + H2O, +0.34 V · V³⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ V²⁺, −0.26 V.
  • Zinc (Zn²⁺/Zn, −0.76 V) in acid: E⦵cell = +1.76, +1.10 and +0.50 V. All positive, so Zn reduces V(V) all the way to V²⁺.
  • Fe²⁺ (Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺, +0.77 V): +0.23 V for the first step, −0.43 V for the second. It stops at VO²⁺.
  • Combine half-equations: Zn + 2VO2⁺ + 4H⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2VO²⁺ + 2H2O.

Mission 3: Why can you recharge a phone battery but not a standard AA battery?

Make a guess: Why can you recharge a phone battery but not a standard AA battery?

Mission 3

Building a zinc–copper cell

  • A cell turns the chemical energy of a feasible redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Two half-cells: zinc in Zn²⁺(aq); copper in Cu²⁺(aq).
  • Joined by a wire (through a voltmeter or bulb) and a salt bridge, e.g. KNO3(aq).
  • Zinc is oxidised more readily: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. The zinc electrode loses mass.
  • Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The copper electrode gains mass; the blue fades.
  • Zinc is the negative electrode; copper is the positive electrode. EMF = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.
Mission 3

Electrons in the wire, ions in the bridge

  • Electrons flow through the wire, from Zn to Cu. Never through the solutions or bridge.
  • Zn²⁺ builds up in the zinc half-cell: anions (NO3⁻) move in from the bridge.
  • Cu²⁺ is used up in the copper half-cell: cations (K⁺) move in.
  • This keeps each solution neutral, so current keeps flowing.
  • Lift the salt bridge out: the circuit breaks, and the reading falls to zero.
Quick check

Mission 3

Three kinds of cell

  • Non-rechargeable (primary): e.g. alkaline AA. Used once; its reactions can't practically be reversed.
  • Rechargeable (secondary, storage): e.g. lithium-ion phone cell, lead–acid car battery.
  • Charging: an external current drives each electrode reaction backwards, re-forming the reactants.
  • Fuel cell: fuel (e.g. H2) and O2 are fed in continuously. It never needs electrical recharging.
  • A cell stores chemical energy, not electricity.
Mission 3

The lithium cell

  • Simplified electrode reactions during use (discharge):
  • Negative electrode: Li → Li⁺ + e⁻.
  • Positive electrode: Li⁺ + CoO2 + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO2]⁻ (Co: +4 → +3).
  • Li⁺ moves through the electrolyte to the positive electrode; electrons go round the circuit.
  • Charging reverses both: Li⁺[CoO2]⁻ → Li⁺ + CoO2 + e⁻ and Li⁺ + e⁻ → Li.
  • EMF from data: Li⁺/Li −3.04 V; Li⁺ + CoO2/Li⁺[CoO2]⁻ +0.56 V (example value). EMF = 0.56 − (−3.04) = +3.60 V.

Mission 3

The hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell

  • A fuel cell uses the energy of the fuel + oxygen reaction to make a voltage, without burning.
  • Alkaline electrolyte (e.g. KOH):
  • Negative electrode: H2 + 2OH⁻ → 2H2O + 2e⁻ (E⦵ of 2H2O + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2 + 2OH⁻: −0.83 V).
  • Positive electrode: O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻ (E⦵ = +0.40 V).
  • Overall: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. EMF = 0.40 − (−0.83) = +1.23 V.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Only potential differences can be measured; the SHE is the 0 V reference.
  • E⦵ of a half-cell = the cell reading against the SHE under standard conditions, signed by direction of electron flow.
  • More positive E⦵: stronger oxidising agent on the left; more negative: stronger reducing agent on the right.

Mission 2

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative) = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left). Never multiply E⦵ by coefficients.
  • Positive E⦵cell: feasible. But kinetics and non-standard conditions can change what you see.
  • Lithium's very negative E⦵ makes a large gap, so lithium cells give a large voltage.

Mission 3

  • Electrons flow in the wire; ions flow in the salt bridge and solutions.
  • Rechargeable cells: a charging current reverses each electrode reaction. Fuel cells: fuel and O2 fed in continuously.
  • Alkaline fuel cell: H2 + 2OH⁻ → 2H2O + 2e⁻ and O2 + 2H2O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻; EMF 1.23 V.
