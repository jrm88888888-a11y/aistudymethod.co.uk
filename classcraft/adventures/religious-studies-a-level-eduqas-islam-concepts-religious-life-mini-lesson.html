Mission 1: If God sent many prophets and books before Muhammad, why do Muslims need the Qur'an?

Make a guess: If God sent many prophets and books before Muhammad, why do Muslims need the Qur'an?

Mission 1

Risalah: God's message through people

  • Risalah means "the message": God's guidance sent through chosen humans.
  • Muslims believe humans need guidance to know God and live rightly; sending it is God's mercy.
  • "For We assuredly sent amongst every People a messenger, (with the Command), 'Serve Allah, and eschew Evil'" (Qur'an 16:36, Yusuf Ali).
  • The core message is always the same: tawhid (one God) and accountability.
  • 25 prophets are named in the Qur'an (e.g. Adam, Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa, Dawud, Isa, Muhammad). A hadith (Musnad Ahmad) speaks of 124,000; scholars differ on its strength.
  • Belief in God's messengers is one of the articles of faith: "We make no distinction (they say) between one and another of His messengers." (Qur'an 2:285, Yusuf Ali)
Mission 1

Nabi, rasul, nadhir

  • Nabi (prophet): one who receives news (revelation) from God.
  • Rasul (messenger): one sent with a message to a people, often with a scripture or law.
  • Popular distinction: every rasul is a nabi, but not every nabi is a rasul.
  • The Qur'an uses both words without defining them: Musa "was a messenger (and) a prophet" (Qur'an 19:51, Yusuf Ali); 22:52 names "a messenger or a prophet" side by side. Scholars differ on how sharp the line is.
  • Nadhir (warner): every people has had one: "there never was a people, without a warner having lived among them" (Qur'an 35:24, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muhammad is called both bashir (bringer of good news) and nadhir (Qur'an 33:45).
  • Your spec writes nadir for warner.

Mission 1

Tahrif: were the earlier books altered?

  • The Qur'an condemns "those who write the Book with their own hands" and claim it is from God (Qur'an 2:79, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muslims call this tahrif (distortion). Two main views:
  • Altered text (tahrif al-nass): the words of the Torah and Gospel were changed. Argued strongly by Ibn Hazm (994–1064).
  • Altered meaning (tahrif al-ma'na): the texts largely survive, but their meaning was twisted in interpretation. Held by some classical and modern scholars.
  • Many Muslims add that the Injil was the message given to Isa, not the four Gospels written about him.
  • Shared conclusion: the Qur'an is the criterion for judging the earlier books.

Mission 1

Prophets in Sunni and Shi'a belief

  • Sunni: belief in messengers is one of six articles of faith (God, angels, books, messengers, Last Day, divine decree).
  • Shi'a (Twelvers): nubuwwah (prophethood) is one of five usul al-din (roots of religion): tawhid, adl (justice), nubuwwah, imamah, ma'ad (resurrection).
  • Ismah (protection from sin): Sunni scholars hold prophets are protected from major sins and from error in delivering the message. Shi'a scholars hold prophets are wholly sinless all their lives, and extend ismah to the Imams.
  • Both affirm Muhammad as the last prophet. For Shi'a Muslims, the Imams continue God's guidance, not prophecy.

Mission 1Evaluate

Has the idea of "messenger" been a success for Islam?

  • Claim: The concept of a messenger has been a success for Islam.
  • For: One pattern explains all religious history: one God, one message, many messengers (16:36). It gives Islam a place for Ibrahim, Musa and Isa.
  • For: A human messenger is a model to follow (33:21;), not only a voice.
  • Against: Jews and Christians do not accept the Qur'an's reading of their prophets and books; the claim of tahrif causes tension.
  • Against: Debates about what "messenger" means (nabi or rasul; ismah) divide Muslims.
  • Judgement criteria: Success for whom: Muslims or interfaith relations? In belief or in practice? Compare the Christian idea of incarnation.
  • Spec issue: "whether or not the concept of 'messenger' has been a success for Islam".

Mission 2: Muslims honour Abraham, Moses and Jesus. So why is Muhammad called the "seal" of the prophets?

Make a guess: Muslims honour Abraham, Moses and Jesus. So why is Muhammad called the "seal" of the prophets?

Mission 2

Ibrahim: the friend of God

  • "Abraham was not a Jew nor yet a Christian; but he was true in Faith, and bowed his will to Allah's (Which is Islam), and he joined not gods with Allah." (Qur'an 3:67, Yusuf Ali)
  • The Qur'an tells how this hanif (true monotheist) broke his people's idols and was saved from the fire (Qur'an 21:51–70).
  • "For Allah did take Abraham for a friend." (Qur'an 4:125, Yusuf Ali): Khalil Allah.
  • Tested: willing to sacrifice his son (Qur'an 37:102); most Muslims hold the son was Isma'il. Remembered at Id-ul-Adha.
  • Muslims believe he raised the Ka'bah with Isma'il (Qur'an 2:127). Hajj re-enacts his family's story.
  • Revelation: the Sahifa (see Mission 1). Founder: father of Isma'il (ancestor of the Arabs) and Ishaq (of the Israelites).
  • Example: trust, obedience, courage against idolatry. In every salah Sunni Muslims ask blessing on Muhammad as on Ibrahim.
Mission 2

Musa: the one God spoke to

  • Named in the Qur'an more often than any other prophet.
  • "and to Moses Allah spoke direct" (Qur'an 4:164, Yusuf Ali): Kalim Allah.
  • Confronted Pharaoh; led the Children of Israel out of Egypt; received the Tawrat.
  • Lawgiver and leader of a community: Muslims see a parallel with Muhammad.
  • In the account of the Mi'raj (night journey), Musa urged Muhammad to ask God to reduce daily prayers; they became five.
  • Ashura (10 Muharram): the Prophet fasted on it as the day God saved Musa; many Sunni Muslims still fast. For Shi'a Muslims Ashura is above all mourning for Husayn.
  • Example: courage before tyranny; patience with a difficult people.

Mission 2

Isa: messenger, Word and spirit

  • In the Qur'an, and in Muslim belief:
  • Born of the virgin Maryam (Surah 19 is named after her). As a baby he said: "I am indeed a servant of Allah: He hath given me revelation and made me a prophet;" (Qur'an 19:30, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Christ Jesus the son of Mary was (no more than) a messenger of Allah, and His Word, which He bestowed on Mary, and a spirit proceeding from Him" (Qur'an 4:171, Yusuf Ali).
  • Miracles "by Allah's leave": healing the blind and lepers, raising the dead (Qur'an 3:49). Given the Injil.
  • Muslims believe he was not divine: the same verse rejects "Trinity" and God "having a son".
  • Muslims believe he was not crucified: "they killed him not, nor crucified him, but so it was made to appear to them" (Qur'an 4:157, Yusuf Ali); "Allah raised him up unto Himself" (Qur'an 4:158, Yusuf Ali).
  • Most Muslims, Sunni and Shi'a, believe he will return before the Day of Judgement.
  • Example: humility, mercy, detachment from the world (much loved by Sufis).
Mission 2

Three founding figures

  • Muslims see Ibrahim, Musa and Isa as carriers of revelation whose followers became the Abrahamic traditions.
  • The People of the Book (ahl al-kitab): Jews and Christians, who received earlier scriptures.
  • Shared: one God, prophets, revelation, judgement; Ibrahim as father in faith.
  • Different: which son was to be sacrificed; Isa's death and divinity; the Qur'an as final revelation.
  • Muslims call their faith the original religion of Ibrahim, restored (3:67).

Mission 2Read the source

The seal of the prophets

> "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but (he is) the Messenger of Allah, and the Seal of the Prophets: and Allah has full knowledge of all things." (Qur'an 33:40, Yusuf Ali)

  • Khatam al-nabiyyin (AQA: Khatam an-Nabiyyin): "seal of the prophets".
  • A seal closes a document and confirms it is genuine: Muhammad ends the line of prophets and confirms those before him.
  • A hadith reported by both Sunni and Shi'a sources has the Prophet tell Ali (paraphrase): you are to me as Harun was to Musa, except that there is no prophet after me.

Q1 Which two roles does 33:40 give Muhammad?

Show answer

Messenger of God; seal of the prophets.

Q2 Why does the image of a seal suit both "last" and "confirming"?

Show answer

A seal comes at the end and guarantees what is sealed.

Mission 2

What "seal" means

  • Final: the message is complete: "This day have I perfected your religion for you" (Qur'an 5:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Universal: earlier prophets were sent to their own people; Muhammad was sent "as a universal (Messenger) to men" (Qur'an 34:28, Yusuf Ali).
  • Preserved: the Qur'an is guarded (15:9), so no new prophet is needed to restore it.
  • Isa's return is not new prophethood: he was a prophet before Muhammad and returns without a new revelation.
  • Ahmadi Muslims read "seal" as "best" or "authenticating" and believe Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (d. 1908) was a prophet without a new law. Mainstream Sunni and Shi'a scholars reject this reading.
  • Your spec pairs this with 46:9: Muhammad brings nothing new, only the same message to its completion.

Mission 2

The Prophet's example today

  • "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct)" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali).
  • Character: trustworthy (al-Amin), merciful, humble, just. "We sent thee not, but as a Mercy for all creatures." (Qur'an 21:107, Yusuf Ali)
  • Sunnah in daily life: how to pray, wash, eat, greet, trade, treat family, neighbours and animals.
  • Authority: after the Qur'an, his Sunnah is the second source of guidance.
  • Love: deep love for the Prophet is part of faith; this is why disrespect towards him causes Muslims great hurt.
  • Emphases differ: Sunni Muslims stress following his Sunnah; Shi'a Muslims also honour his family (ahl al-bayt) as guides; Sufis see him as the Perfect Human (al-insan al-kamil).
Mission 2Evaluate

Was Muhammad the ideal prophet and messenger?

  • Claim: Muhammad was the ideal prophet and messenger.
  • For (Muslim view): He united roles other prophets held separately: preacher (like Nuh), lawgiver and leader (like Musa), ruler (like Dawud), teacher of mercy (like Isa).
  • For: His whole life is recorded (Sunnah), so he can be followed in detail, unlike earlier prophets.
  • Against (critics): Some non-Muslims question whether war and political power fit a prophet; Muslims reply that justice sometimes requires leadership and defence (22:39).
  • Against (within Islam): Some Muslims are wary of ranking prophets, "We make no distinction" (Qur'an 2:285, Yusuf Ali), though 2:253 says some were raised above others.
  • Judgement criteria: Ideal by whose standards? Ideal as person, as messenger, or as model for all times and places?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which Muhammad was the ideal prophet and messenger".

Mission 3

Iman: six articles of faith

  • Iman (faith) in Sunni Islam has six articles. Muslims believe in:
  • 1. Allah, the One God (tawhid).
  • 2. The angels (malaikah).
  • 3. The revealed books: Sahifa of Ibrahim, Tawrat, Zabur, Injil, and the Qur'an.
  • 4. The messengers and prophets, from Adam to Muhammad.
  • 5. The Last Day: resurrection, judgement, heaven and hell (Akhirah).
  • 6. Al-Qadr: God's decree, "its good and its bad".
  • Eduqas spellings in this mission: malaikah, Mikail, Akhirah.
Mission 3Read the source

Where the list comes from

> "Any who denieth Allah, His angels, His Books, His Messengers, and the Day of Judgment, hath gone far, far astray." (Qur'an 4:136, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Hadith of Jibril (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, hadith 1, told by Umar; paraphrase): a stranger in white asks the Prophet about islam, iman and ihsan. Iman is to believe in Allah, His angels, books, messengers, the Last Day, and the decree, good and bad. The Prophet later says the questioner was Jibril.
  • So the Qur'an's lists (4:136; 2:177) name five; the Hadith adds the sixth, al-Qadr, which the Qur'an teaches elsewhere (54:49).

Q1 Which article is in the Hadith of Jibril but not in 4:136?

Show answer

Al-Qadr, the divine decree.

Q2 What does 4:136 say of someone who denies the angels?

Show answer

They have "gone far, far astray".

Mission 3

One web, not six boxes

  • Tawhid is the root: every other article is about what the One God does.
  • God sends angels → Jibril brings revelation → the books → given to prophets → who warn of the Last Day → when people answer for lives lived under God's decree.
  • Remove one and the others weaken: no angels, no revelation; no Last Day, no point to the books' warnings.
  • They shape life: prayer to the One God; trust in the Qur'an; following the Prophet's Sunnah; living as accountable; patience in hardship (qadr).

Mission 3

What angels are

  • Malaikah (singular malak: "messenger"): beings created by God from light (Sahih Muslim 2996, paraphrase).
  • Unseen (ghayb): normally invisible; they may take human form (Jibril in the Hadith of Jibril).
  • Obedient: they "do (precisely) what they are commanded" (Qur'an 66:6, Yusuf Ali). Most scholars say they have no free will to disobey.
  • Messengers with wings (35:1); neither male nor female (the Qur'an rejects calling them females, 43:19).
  • Servants, not partners: worshipping or praying to angels would be shirk.
  • Intermediaries: they carry God's commands to creation; they do not act on their own authority.
Mission 3

Jibril, Mika'il, Israfil

  • Jibril (Gabriel): brings revelation to the prophets: "he brings down the (revelation) to thy heart by Allah's will" (Qur'an 2:97, Yusuf Ali). Also called the Holy Spirit (16:102). Brought the Qur'an to Muhammad over about 23 years.
  • Mika'il (Michael): named in the Qur'an beside Jibril (2:98). Tradition gives him care of rain and provision for creation.
  • Israfil: not named in the Qur'an. In hadith and tradition he will blow the trumpet that ends the world and raises the dead (the trumpet: 39:68).
  • The Prophet's night prayer names all three: "Lord of Jibril, Mika'il and Israfil" (Sahih Muslim 770, paraphrase).
  • Eduqas spelling: Jibril, Mikail, Israfil.

Mission 3

Angels in a Muslim's life

  • Recording angels (Kiraman Katibin): "Kind and honourable,- Writing down (your deeds)" (Qur'an 82:11, Yusuf Ali). Many Muslims greet them at the end of salah.
  • The Angel of Death takes the soul at the appointed time (32:11).
  • Munkar and Nakir question the dead in the grave (hadith;).
  • Night of Power: "the angels and the Spirit" come down (97:4): Laylat al-Qadr is spent in prayer.
  • Effect: a Muslim is never unwatched; this encourages honesty and accountability, and comfort that God's care is active in the world.
  • Your spec: Akhirah as underlining the meaning and purpose of life (taught in a later lesson).
Mission 3Evaluate

"Belief in angels is essential for Islam."

  • Reasons for: angels are named in the Qur'an's own list (2:285, 4:136); revelation depends on Jibril, so the Qur'an's authority does; accountability depends on the recording angels and the Last Day.
  • Reasons against: tawhid is the foundation, and the Shahadah does not mention angels; the angels' role is instrumental: God could act directly. Some modernists, e.g. Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898), read angels as God-given forces in nature and the mind rather than separate beings.
  • Response: most scholars reject that reading as denying what the Qur'an plainly states; even Khan kept the word and the article of faith.
  • Judge it by: Is "essential" about logic (could Islam work without them?) or about authority (does the Qur'an require belief)? Can a belief be essential yet less central than tawhid?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the concept of Malaikah (angels) is essential for Islam".

Mission 4

Tawhid: God is One

  • Tawhid ("making one"): God is one and unique, in essence, in attributes and in actions.
  • Sole Creator and Sustainer: everything exists, and keeps existing, by God's will.
  • Omnipotent: "Allah has power over all things" (Qur'an 35:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Omniscient: "Not a leaf doth fall but with His knowledge" (Qur'an 6:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • Uncompromising: no partner, child, rival or equal; God alone is worshipped.
  • Later Sunni teaching (especially Ibn Taymiyya, 1263–1328) often divides tawhid into three: oneness of lordship, of worship, and of names and attributes. Ash'ari teaching frames it differently; the scheme is not universal.
Mission 4Read the source

Surah 112, al-Ikhlas

> "Say: He is Allah, the One and Only; Allah, the Eternal, Absolute; He begetteth not, nor is He begotten; And there is none like unto Him." (Qur'an 112:1–4, Yusuf Ali)

  • Al-Ikhlas means "sincerity" or "purity" (of faith).
  • A hadith says it equals a third of the Qur'an (Sahih al-Bukhari 5013, paraphrase).

Mission 4

Beyond us, yet near

  • Transcendent and indescribable: "there is nothing whatever like unto Him" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali). Human words fall short.
  • So Muslim theologians practise tanzih: declaring God free of every likeness to creatures.
  • Immanent: "We are nearer to him than (his) jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Yusuf Ali). God hears every prayer (2:186).
  • Not two gods or two moods: one God, beyond creation in being, close to it in knowledge, power and mercy.
  • Your spec: attributes as metaphors and symbols: a human word such as "hearing" points to a perfection in God that the word cannot contain.
Mission 4Read the source

The Throne verse (Ayat al-Kursi)

> "Allah! There is no god but He,-the Living, the Self-subsisting, Eternal. No slumber can seize Him nor sleep." (Qur'an 2:255, Yusuf Ali)

  • The verse goes on: all in the heavens and earth belong to God; none intercedes without His permission; He knows all that is before and behind people; His Throne (kursi) extends over heavens and earth; guarding them never tires Him (paraphrase).
  • Use: recited after salah, before sleep, and for protection; often hung in homes.

Mission 4

Shirk: the opposite of tawhid

  • Shirk ("association"): giving God a partner, or giving to anything what belongs only to God.
  • "Allah forgiveth not that partners should be set up with Him" (Qur'an 4:48, Yusuf Ali): the one sin not forgiven if a person dies without repenting.
  • Major shirk: worshipping idols, saints or angels; saying God has a son (112:3).
  • Minor shirk: doing good deeds to be seen (riya'), named by the Prophet as a hidden shirk (Musnad Ahmad, paraphrase).
  • Many Muslims also warn of shirk in modern forms: living as if money, fame or the self were the ultimate goal.
Mission 4

The Merciful, the Compassionate

  • 113 of the 114 surahs begin: "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Al-Rahman and al-Rahim both come from r-h-m, the root of "womb": care that gives and sustains life.
  • Many scholars explain al-Rahman as mercy to all creation, al-Rahim as mercy especially to believers.
  • "My mercy extendeth to all things" (Qur'an 7:156, Yusuf Ali); a hadith qudsi: God's mercy prevails over His anger (Sahih al-Bukhari 7404, paraphrase).
  • Significance: Muslims begin meals, journeys and work with the Bismillah; they never despair of forgiveness; they show mercy to receive mercy.
  • Mercy does not cancel justice: God is also Judge. Holding both is part of tawhid.
Mission 4

The 99 Beautiful Names

  • "The most beautiful names belong to Allah: so call on him by them" (Qur'an 7:180, Yusuf Ali). See also 59:22–24.
  • A hadith says God has ninety-nine names, and whoever learns them by heart enters Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari 2736, paraphrase). Lists vary; the best-known is in al-Tirmidhi.
  • Names of beauty (jamal): al-Rahman, al-Wadud (the Loving). Names of majesty (jalal): al-Aziz (the Mighty), al-Jabbar (the Compeller).
  • Practice: dhikr on prayer beads (subha, often 33 or 99 beads); naming children 'Abd al-Rahman ("servant of the Merciful").
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), The Best Means in Explaining the Beautiful Names of God: believers should reflect the names within human limits: mercy, patience, justice.

Mission 4Evaluate

"The Islamic concept of Allah is coherent."

  • Tension 1: transcendent and indescribable, yet near and named. Reply: the names describe God's relation to creation, not God's essence; nearness is of knowledge and care, not of place.
  • Tension 2: all-merciful, yet a Judge who punishes. Reply: mercy and justice are both perfections; 7:156 places mercy first; justice gives mercy meaning.
  • Tension 3: omnipotent, yet just. Replies differ: Ash'aris (the main Sunni school of theology) say God's will defines justice; the Mu'tazila (an early rationalist school) and Shi'a theologians say God freely always does what is just.
  • Critics: a God beyond all description can say nothing to us; or the names make God too human.
  • Judge it by: Is "coherent" the same as "fully understood"? Do the traditions' replies avoid contradiction or only relabel it?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which the Islamic concept of Allah is coherent".

Mission 5

Signs for people who think

  • Ayah means both "verse" and "sign": the Qur'an's verses and the features of nature both point to God.
  • The Qur'an repeatedly asks its hearers to reflect: on the sky, the seasons, their own bodies.
  • Later theologians (mutakallimun, practitioners of kalam, "speech", i.e. rational theology) built these hints into formal arguments.
  • Three are on the map here: from origination (3:190), from design (23:12–14), and from mutual hindrance (21:22).
  • Every argument aims at the God of tawhid: one, and unlike anything created.
  • In one line each: origination (the kalam cosmological argument): whatever begins to exist has a cause, and the world began, so it has a cause; design: ordered stages that reach a fitting goal point to a wise purpose, not chance; mutual hindrance: two all-powerful gods could block each other, so God's oneness follows.
Mission 5Read the source

What the arguments must reach

> "there is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the One that hears and sees (all things)" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali)

> "And there is none like unto Him." (Qur'an 112:4, Yusuf Ali)

  • Divine transcendence: God is not a very large thing inside the universe, nor its first link; God is beyond comparison.
  • So an argument that proves only "some cause" or "some designer" has not yet reached the God of the Qur'an.

Q1 What does 42:11 add after denying any likeness?

Show answer

That God hears and sees: transcendence does not mean God is unaware.

Q2 Why might 112:4 set a test for the other arguments?

Show answer

They must reach a being with no equal, not just a powerful cause.

Mission 5Read the source

21:22: no room for two gods

> "If there were, in the heavens and the earth, other gods besides Allah, there would have been confusion in both!" (Qur'an 21:22, Yusuf Ali)

  • Kalam name: the proof from mutual hindrance (burhan al-tamanu'), used by al-Ash'ari, al-Maturidi and al-Ghazali.
  • Suppose two all-powerful gods. One wills a body to move; the other wills it to stay still.
  • Both wills happen: impossible (moving and still at once). Neither: both are powerless. Only one: the other was not all-powerful.
  • So there can be only one omnipotent God; and the world's single, stable order fits that.
  • See also 17:42: other gods would have sought a way to the Lord of the Throne.

Q1 Why must the theologian only show that the gods could disagree?

Show answer

A god who could be blocked is not all-powerful.

Mission 5Evaluate

"The Qur'an's arguments show that one God exists."

  • Strengths: each starts from what anyone can observe (change, order, unity); together they form a cumulative case; 21:22 gives a reason for oneness, which many Western arguments lack.
  • Against the design argument: Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859): natural selection can explain ordered development without a designer.
  • Against oneness: Hume's Philo asks, "why may not several deities combine in contriving and framing a world?" (Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, 1779, Part V).
  • Reply: Philo's gods are limited workers; 21:22 is about omnipotent beings, which cannot share power. Design itself may be how God works, not a rival to God.
  • Against kalam: an infinite past may be possible; or the universe may need no cause. Replies defend both premises.
  • Judge it by: Were these verses meant as proofs, or as signs for those already reflecting? Does showing a "first cause" reach the God of 112? Can a cumulative case succeed where single arguments fail?

Mission 6

Why "pillars"?

  • The Prophet is reported to have said that Islam is built on five: the shahadah, prayer, zakah, Hajj and fasting in Ramadan (Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase).
  • A pillar (rukn, plural arkan) holds up a building: the pillars are what Islam stands on.
  • Purpose: to live in submission to God. Each pillar is ibadah (worship): "I have only created Jinns and men, that they may serve Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Yusuf Ali)
  • In the Hadith of Jibril, the five pillars define islam (practice), beside iman (belief: the six articles) and ihsan (worshipping God as if you see Him).
  • The pillars are fard (obligatory) for every adult Muslim who is able.
Mission 6

The five and their origins

  • Shahadah (witness): the declaration of faith (47:19; 48:29).
  • Salah (prayer) five times daily (4:103); the number five fixed, in hadith, on the Night Journey (c. 621).
  • Zakah (purifying alms) for the needy (2:43; 9:60); organised in Madinah.
  • Sawm (fasting) in Ramadan (2:183–185); prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Hajj (pilgrimage) to Makkah for all who can (3:97); the Prophet's Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632) is the model.
  • Eduqas spelling: shahadah, salah, zakah.
Mission 6Read the source

The shahadah

  • Ashhadu an la ilaha illa'llah, wa ashhadu anna Muhammadan rasulu'llah.
  • "I bear witness that there is no god but God, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the messenger of God."

> "Know, therefore, that there is no god but Allah" (Qur'an 47:19, Yusuf Ali)

> "Muhammad is the messenger of Allah" (Qur'an 48:29, Yusuf Ali)

  • Part one affirms tawhid and rejects shirk. Part two accepts Muhammad's message, so the Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Heard at birth (the adhan whispered to a baby), said in every salah, hoped for as a Muslim's last words.

Q1 Which part of the shahadah denies something, and what?

Show answer

The first: it denies any god but God.

Q2 Why does accepting Muhammad as messenger commit a Muslim to more than belief?

Show answer

It accepts his message and example as guidance for life.

Mission 6

Witness, public and private

  • Shahadah means witness: to declare what one knows to be true.
  • Conversion: a person becomes Muslim by saying the shahadah sincerely, with understanding. Often said before witnesses in a mosque; no further ceremony is required.
  • Public: a statement of truth to the world, and entry into the ummah.
  • Private: a personal commitment; without sincere intention the words alone are empty (63:1 calls hypocrites' testimony false).
  • Eduqas issue: whether the shahadah is more to do with private faith than public declaration.
  • Meaning and purpose: one God to worship; Muhammad as messenger and an exemplary Muslim.

Mission 6Diversity

Sunni pillars, Shi'a roots and branches

  • Sunni: the Five Pillars, as in the hadith.
  • Twelver Shi'a: five roots of faith (usul al-din): tawhid, adl (justice), nubuwwa (prophethood), imama (leadership of the Imams), ma'ad (resurrection).
  • And ten branches (furu' al-din): salah, sawm, zakah, khums, Hajj, jihad, enjoining good, forbidding evil, tawalla (loving God's friends) and tabarra (dissociating from their enemies).
  • Many Shi'a add to the shahadah "and Ali is the friend (wali) of God"; most Twelver jurists say it is not part of the obligatory words.
  • Isma'ilis list seven pillars, beginning with walaya (devotion to the Imam) and tahara (purity).
  • So Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the core practices; they group them differently, and Shi'a add duties such as khums.
Mission 6

Intention and action

  • "Actions are only by intentions; each person will have what they intended" (Bukhari, no. 1; paraphrase).
  • Niyyah (intention): each act of worship begins with it.
  • Outward act without inward sincerity is condemned: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali); charity "to be seen of men" is void (2:264).
  • But intention does not replace action: the pillars have conditions and set forms.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Risalah: God sent messengers to every people (16:36) with one message, tawhid. Nabi receives revelation; rasul is sent with a message (popular distinction); nadhir warns.
  • Books: Sahifa (Ibrahim), Tawrat (Musa), Zabur (Dawud), Injil (Isa), Qur'an (Muhammad), which confirms and guards the others (5:48). Tahrif: altered text or altered meaning.
  • Sunni six articles and Shi'a five roots both include prophethood; Shi'a add imamah; views on ismah differ.

Mission 2

  • Ibrahim (Khalil Allah, hanif, Ka'bah, 3:67), Musa (Kalim Allah, Tawrat, lawgiver), Isa (Word and spirit, virgin birth, not crucified, will return, 4:157–158, 4:171): carriers of revelation and founders of the Abrahamic faiths.
  • Muhammad is "the Seal of the Prophets" (33:40): last, confirming, universal; Isa's return brings no new revelation; Ahmadi reading rejected by mainstream scholars.
  • His example (33:21) guides daily life; he is commemorated in salawat (33:56), salah and, for many, Mawlid.

Mission 3

  • Sunni iman has six articles: Allah, angels, books, messengers, the Last Day, al-Qadr (4:136 and the Hadith of Jibril); they interlock as one chain from God to judgement.
  • Angels are obedient servants made of light: Jibril brings revelation, Mika'il oversees provision, Israfil will sound the trumpet.

Mission 4

  • Tawhid: the Muslim belief that God is one in essence, attributes and acts; Surah 112 and the Throne verse (2:255) express it; shirk is its opposite.
  • In Muslim teaching God is transcendent (42:11) yet near (50:16); al-Rahman and al-Rahim stand at the head of the Qur'an, and the 99 Names show how the One God relates to creation.

Mission 5

  • The Qur'an presents nature as signs (ayat); kalam theologians turned them into arguments.
  • 3:190: origination (kalam cosmological, al-Ghazali); 23:12–14: design and providence (Ibn Rushd); 21:22: mutual hindrance, for God's oneness.
  • Critics (Hume, Darwin) challenge the steps; defenders reply, and some read the verses as signs for reflection rather than formal proofs.

Mission 6

  • Islam is "built on five": shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm, Hajj; each is worship and each is fard for those able.
  • The shahadah witnesses to tawhid and to Muhammad as messenger; saying it sincerely makes a person Muslim.
