Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some cities spread out in houses with yards while others stack people in towers?

Make a guess: Why do some cities spread out in houses with yards while others stack people in towers?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • IMP-6.A explain how low-, medium- and high-density housing characteristics represent different patterns of residential land use
  • IMP-6.A.1 residential buildings and patterns of land use reflect and shape the city's culture, technological capabilities, cycles of development and infilling
Mission 1

Three housing densities

  • Low density: detached single-family houses on their own lots, with yards and driveways.
  • Medium density: townhouses, rowhouses, duplexes, small apartment buildings.
  • High density: large apartment blocks and towers; many households on a small area of land.
Mission 1

Density and land use

  • Each density is a different pattern of residential land use: how much land each home takes.
  • Land near the center usually costs most, so housing there is denser (bid-rent theory).
  • Low density uses more land per person and pushes the city's edge outward: sprawl.
Mission 1

What the USA builds

  • In 2025 the USA completed 1,005,000 single-family homes and 484,000 units in multifamily buildings.
  • 61% of those multifamily units were in buildings of four floors or more.
  • 41% of new single-family homes built for sale stood on lots under 7,000 square feet.
Quick check
?What share of all new US homes completed in 2025 were single-family homes?
Mission 1

Housing reflects culture

  • What people value shapes what they build: privacy, a yard, home ownership, or living close to work and family.
  • Rules shape it too: Minneapolis's plan says areas with little housing choice "were built that way intentionally, through zoning regulations".
  • In Singapore, 76.0% of residents live in public housing flats built by the government's Housing and Development Board.
  • Housing also shapes culture: who your neighbors are, how you travel, where children play.
Mission 1

Housing reflects technology

  • Elevators and steel frames made high-rise apartments possible.
  • Streetcars let cities grow outward along lines, often with rowhouses and small apartments.
  • Cars and highways opened land far from the center to low-density suburbs.
  • The range today is huge: in 2025 Mumbai had about 27,000 people per km²; Perth, fewer than 2,000. Of the world's 50 least dense cities of 1 million or more, 21 were in the USA.
Quick check
?Which technology did most to make large areas of low-density suburbs possible?
Mission 1

Cycles of development

  • Cities grow in waves. Each era builds its own housing type, and those buildings last for decades.
  • Minneapolis's plan describes how, on the city's outskirts, "a post-depression development pattern emerged with little variation in housing types and density".
  • Later, old areas decline, are renovated or are replaced: a new cycle begins.
Mission 1

Infilling

  • Infilling: building new housing on vacant or underused land inside the existing city.
  • It raises density without spreading the city outward.
  • Minneapolis's 2040 plan aimed to allow up to three homes on a lot in neighborhoods of mostly single-family houses, and the highest-density housing in and near downtown.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of infilling?
Mission check

?An imaginary city has three areas: old rowhouses near the center, built in the streetcar era; large single-family houses with two-car garages at the edge, built in the 1990s; and new apartment buildings on former parking lots next to a new rail station. What do these areas show?

Cities spread out or build up because of culture (what people value and plan for), technology (streetcars, cars, elevators), cycles of development (each era's housing lasts) and infilling (building up inside the city).

Mission 2

What infrastructure is

  • Infrastructure: the networks a city runs on: transportation, water supply, sanitation, power, waste disposal and telecommunications.
  • Its location decides which places can grow; its quality decides how well people there live and work.
  • Building it is political: governments decide who pays and which areas are served first.
Mission 2

Hong Kong's water problem

  • Local reservoirs supply only 20–30% of the water Hong Kong uses.
  • Its "undulating terrain" makes rainwater hard to collect and store.
  • Local yield can differ between years by up to 200 million m³.
Mission 2

Building a supply

  • 1960: an agreement with Guangdong Province to import water; 1965: imports from the Dongjiang (East River) began.
  • Since 1982: 24-hour uninterrupted supply.
  • Use in 2018 (1,292 million m³): local rainwater 21%, Dongjiang water 57%, seawater for toilet flushing 22%.
  • Since December 22, 2023, a desalination plant has supplied drinking water, planned to meet about 5–10% of fresh water demand.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: About how much Dongjiang water did Hong Kong use in 2018?
Mission 2

Railways steer development

  • Hong Kong's MTR: 245.3 km of routes and about 5.64 million passenger journeys on weekdays.
  • Under its "Rail plus Property" model, the MTR "develops land around railway lines" and uses the value to help pay for new lines.
  • So homes, offices and malls cluster at stations: location of infrastructure shapes where economic development happens.
Mission 2

Unequal infrastructure, unequal development

  • 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements in 2022: 24.8% of the world's urban population.
  • Districts without piped water, sewers or transit attract less investment, and residents face more health risks and longer trips to work.
  • Well-served districts attract firms and higher-income residents: infrastructure quality helps create spatial inequality inside a city.
Quick check
?A city opens a new metro line to a low-income district on its eastern edge. What is the most likely result over the next decade?
Mission 2

Infrastructure, politics and environment

  • Politics: Hong Kong depends on agreements with Guangdong, and the Dongjiang basin is itself "an area of water scarcity".
  • Environment: Hong Kong's Harbour Area Treatment Scheme now treats sewage from both sides of Victoria Harbour; construction "spanned two decades" at a cost of HK$25.8 billion.
  • Society: costs fall on taxpayers or bill payers, so governments must choose which areas and services come first.
Mission check

?London's old sewers carry rain and sewage together, and in storms they spilled into the River Thames. In February 2025 a 25 km tunnel was fully connected to catch these overflows; the operator reports overflow volumes fell by about 86% in 2025, a modeled figure in an unusually dry year. What does this case show?

Hong Kong has water 24 hours a day because it built infrastructure beyond its own reservoirs: Dongjiang imports since 1965 (57% of use in 2018), seawater for flushing (22%) and desalination since 2023. Where infrastructure goes, development follows.

  • IMP-6.D explain the effects of urban design initiatives and practices

Mission 3

Why urban design matters

  • Urban areas use 75% of global energy and produce 75% of greenhouse gas emissions.
  • About 60% of the land turned into urban space since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Urban sprawl: low-density growth spreading outward, built around cars. Design and zoning rules can steer growth another way.
Mission 3

Smart growth

  • Smart growth: policies that steer growth into compact, connected places. The US EPA's principles include:
    • "Mix land uses." (mixed land use: homes, shops and offices together)
    • "Create walkable neighborhoods." (walkability)
    • "Provide a variety of transportation choices."
    • "Preserve open space, farmland, natural beauty and critical environmental areas."
    • "Create a range of housing opportunities and choices."
Mission 3

New Urbanism

  • New Urbanism: a design movement for compact, walkable neighborhoods; its Charter dates from 1996.
  • "Neighborhoods should be compact, pedestrian friendly, and mixed-use."
  • "Many activities of daily living should occur within walking distance."
  • Typical features: front porches, narrow streets, a town center, a mix of housing types.
Mission 3

Transportation-oriented development

  • Transportation-oriented development (TOD): dense, mixed-use building clustered around transit stations, so people can live without driving everywhere.
  • It joins zoning (what may be built where) with transit planning (where stations go).
Mission 3

Example: Vauban, Freiburg (Germany)

  • About 41 ha, with about 5,300 residents (5,267 in December 2020): about 128 people per hectare (our calculation).
  • "Not a car-free but a car-reduced district" (translated): no public parking on residential streets; car owners buy a space in district garages (about 470 spaces).
  • A tram line has served the district since April 2006: transit at the heart of the plan.
  • Result: about 215 private cars per 1,000 residents in 2020.
Quick check
?Which feature of Vauban is the clearest example of transportation-oriented development?
Mission 3

Greenbelts

  • Greenbelt: a ring of protected open land around a city where building is restricted.
  • England's Green Belt: about 1.63 million hectares, around 12.5% of England's land area (March 31, 2026).
  • Its stated purpose: "to prevent urban sprawl by keeping land permanently open".
Mission 3

Slow-growth cities

  • Slow-growth cities limit how fast and how far a city can expand, often with an urban growth boundary.
  • Portland, Oregon, region: Metro manages an urban growth boundary that "protects farms and forests and helps ensure the efficient use of land".
  • The region's first boundary was approved in 1980; most later moves were small, "20 acres or less".
Mission 3

Praise

  • Less sprawl: greenbelts and growth boundaries protect farmland and open space.
  • Walkability and transportation: compact, mixed-use districts with transit cut car dependence; Vauban has about 215 cars per 1,000 residents.
  • Housing options and livability: New Urbanism calls for "a broad range of housing types and price levels".
Mission 3

Criticism

  • Higher housing costs: limiting land for building can push prices up. A study of 353 English planning areas found that "regulatory constraints have a substantive positive impact" on how strongly house prices rise with local earnings.
  • Possible de facto segregation: if new walkable districts become expensive, lower-income households may be priced out, so neighborhoods divide by income without any law requiring it.
  • Loss of historical or place character: rebuilding at higher density can replace older buildings and street patterns.
  • Ambition vs delivery: China's Tianjin Eco-City was planned for 350,000 residents by 2020 but had about 40,000 in 2016.
Quick check
?A city's greenbelt stops new housing on its edge, and house prices inside the city rise faster than incomes. Which criticism of urban design does this illustrate?
Mission check

?A US suburb rezones land around a new rail station for apartments, shops and offices, and protects the farmland at its edge. Can this make a real difference?

Sustainable design can make a real difference when it changes how existing places grow: mixed land use, walkability, transit and protected open land. But new eco-districts house few people, and the gains must be weighed against housing costs and who gets to live there. Do not confuse smart growth with the "smart cities" in the question: AP tests design and zoning ideas such as smart growth, not city technology.

Mission 4

Two kinds of urban data

  • Quantitative data: numbers. Counts, rates, percentages. They show how much a city changed.
  • Qualitative data: words, images and observations. They show how people experience change and what they think of it.
  • Geographers use both to explain the causes and effects of urban change.
Mission 4

Census and survey data

  • Decennial census: every ten years; counts every person; a short set of questions such as age, sex and owner or renter.
  • American Community Survey (ACS): every month, every year; sent to a sample of about 3.5 million addresses; asks about education, jobs, internet access, transportation and more.
  • Together they show changes in a city's population size and composition.
Mission 4

New Orleans by the numbers

  • Population of New Orleans (Orleans Parish):
    • 1980: 557,515 · 1990: 496,938 · 2000: 484,674
    • 2010: 343,829 · 2020: 383,997 · 2025 (estimate): 362,154
  • 2000 to 2010: down 140,845, a fall of 29.1%, nearly 3 in 10 (our calculation).
  • A slow decline, a sudden collapse, a partial recovery, then decline again.
Quick check
?By what percentage did New Orleans's population grow from 2010 (343,829) to 2020 (383,997)?
Mission 4

Numbers point to a cause

  • In August 2005, when the levees protecting New Orleans failed after Hurricane Katrina, about 80% of the city flooded.
  • About 134,000 housing units, 70% of occupied units, were damaged.
  • The census drop from 2000 to 2010 matches the timing: data show the effect and point to the cause.
Mission 4

Composition changed, too

  • Children under 18 in New Orleans: 129,167 in 2000; 70,737 in 2025.
  • Across the metro, households with children fell from 33% (2000) to 22% (2024).
  • Survey data are estimates from a sample: researchers flag differences that are "not significant at 95% confidence interval".
Quick check
?A planner wants to know how many households in New Orleans had internet access in 2024. Which source should she use?
Mission 4

Field studies and narratives

  • Field studies: going to the place. Observing, photographing, mapping land use, recording which buildings are rebuilt or empty, talking to people.
  • Narratives: people's own accounts. Interviews, oral histories, diaries, letters, social media posts.
  • These show individual attitudes: why people stayed, left or returned, and how they feel about the changes.
Mission 4

Stories after Katrina

  • In 2005 George Mason University and the University of New Orleans started the Hurricane Digital Memory Bank to "collect, preserve, and present the stories" of Hurricanes Katrina and Rita.
  • It holds over 25,000 items.
  • Stories like these can show what the census cannot: what it felt like to leave, and how people view rebuilding.
Quick check
?Which question needs qualitative data to answer?
Mission check

?In 2021 an imaginary US city opened a rail line through an old neighborhood. In 2025 planners want to know (1) what share of the neighborhood's residents now commute by public transit, and (2) how longtime residents feel about the change. Which pair of sources fits best?

Census and survey numbers show that New Orleans shrank by almost 3 in 10, then partly recovered, and that it has fewer children. They cannot show how people felt: that needs qualitative data from field studies and narratives.

Mission 5

People move within cities

  • As people and money move within a city (for example, from the center to the suburbs), some neighborhoods gain and others lose.
  • The neighborhoods that lose face housing, service, crime and environmental problems.
  • This mission: how housing discrimination caused some of these patterns, and how cities respond.
Mission 5

Redlining

  • Redlining: refusing loans, or charging more, for property in neighborhoods judged "high risk".
  • In the late 1930s the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) graded neighborhoods in US cities from green ("Best") to red ("Hazardous").
  • Grades used housing age and condition, nearness to polluting industry, residents' incomes, and residents' race and ethnicity.
  • Red areas were often denied the loans that pay for buying and repairing homes.
Mission 5

A lasting pattern

  • A 2018 study compared HOLC maps of 115 cities with recent census data.
  • 74% of areas graded "Hazardous" are low-to-moderate income today; nearly 64% are minority neighborhoods.
  • Researchers still debate how far the HOLC used its own maps; the maps record what local lenders already believed.
  • Redlining became illegal under the Fair Housing Act of 1968.
Mission 5

Blockbusting

  • Blockbusting: real-estate agents persuaded owners to sell cheaply by warning that people of another race were moving in, then resold the homes at higher prices.
  • Federal law now bans inducing sales, "for profit", through "representations regarding the entry or prospective entry into the neighborhood of a person or persons of a particular race, color, religion, sex, handicap, familial status, or national origin".
  • Effect: rapid turnover, falling investment, and neighborhoods that resegregated block by block.
Quick check
?A real-estate agent knocks on doors saying "sell now before values crash", then resells the houses for much more. What is this?
Mission 5

Affordability and services

  • A household is cost-burdened if it spends more than 30% of its income on housing (HUD).
  • In 2023, over 21 million US renter households, 49.7% of those measured, were cost-burdened.
  • Low-income neighborhoods can also lack services: supermarkets, clinics, transit. The USDA counts an urban tract as low access when many residents live more than 1 mile (or 0.5 mile) from a food store.
  • Concentrated poverty and disinvestment are linked with rising crime, which drives more people and businesses away.
Mission 5

Environmental injustice and abandonment

  • Environmental injustice: poorer or minority neighborhoods carrying more pollution, heat or hazards.
  • Across 108 US urban areas, formerly redlined "D" areas are on average 2.6 °C warmer in summer land surface temperature than "A" areas, linked to more paved surface and fewer trees.
  • Disamenity zones / zones of abandonment: areas so disinvested that buildings stand empty and services leave.
  • Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and an estimated 645,705 in 2024.
Quick check
?Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 645,705 in 2024. By about what percentage did its population fall?
Mission 5

Response: inclusionary zoning

  • Inclusionary zoning: a rule that new housing developments must include a share of homes priced for low- or moderate-income households.
  • Montgomery County, Maryland, passed one of the first such programs in the USA in 1973 (in effect 1974).
  • Today any new development there with 20 or more units must make 12.5% to 15% of them Moderately Priced Dwelling Units.
  • Aim: mixed-income neighborhoods instead of separate rich and poor areas.
Mission 5

Response: local food movements

  • Local food movements: community gardens, urban farms, farmers' markets and cooperatives that grow or sell food close to where people live.
  • In low-access areas they add fresh food, local jobs and use for vacant lots.
  • Limits: small scale, and they cannot by themselves fix low incomes.
Quick check
?A county requires every new apartment complex of 50 units to rent 7 units at below-market prices to moderate-income households. Which response is this?
Mission 5

Fragmented government

  • In 2022 the USA had 90,837 local governments: 3,031 counties, 19,491 municipalities, 16,214 townships, 12,546 school districts and 39,555 special districts.
  • Illinois alone had 6,930; Hawaii had 21.
  • Geographic fragmentation: a metro area is split among many cities and suburbs, each with its own taxes and zoning.
  • Functional fragmentation: different agencies run water, transit, schools and housing. Problems that cross borders (traffic, segregation, sprawl) have no single owner.
Mission check

?An imaginary metro area: a central city with old housing, many vacant lots and few supermarkets; 60 suburbs, each setting its own zoning and keeping its own property taxes. A regional plan to build affordable homes in the suburbs stalls because each suburb must approve its own share. Which explanation fits best?

Redlining, blockbusting and unaffordable housing helped concentrate poverty; heat, pollution and abandonment followed. Responses like inclusionary zoning and local food movements help, but fragmented government makes metro-wide action hard.

Mission 6

Squatter settlements and land tenure

  • Squatter settlement: homes built on land the residents do not own or rent legally; also called informal settlements.
  • Land tenure: the right to occupy and use land (ownership, a lease, or customary rights).
  • Without secure tenure, residents can be evicted, and they find it hard to get loans, services or compensation.
Mission 6

A growing challenge

  • 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements in 2022, 130 million more than in 2015.
  • That is 24.8% of the world's urban population.
  • Over 85% were in three regions: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia (362 million), Central and Southern Asia (334 million) and sub-Saharan Africa (265 million).
Mission 6

Lagos: waterfront settlements

  • Amnesty International reports a 2013 Lagos State estimate that over 70% of Lagos's population lived in informal settlements.
  • On October 9, 2016, the State Governor announced that informal settlements along the waterways and creeks would be demolished; he claimed they were "used as criminal hideouts".
  • At least 40 such settlements are home to at least 300,000 people (enumeration by the NGO Justice and Empowerment Initiatives, reported by Amnesty).
Mission 6

Otodo-Gbame, Lagos

  • Amnesty estimates that over 30,000 residents were forcibly evicted in three operations: November 2016, March 2017 and April 2017.
  • Amnesty found no prior consultation, no adequate notice and no compensation, and that the evictions went ahead in violation of court orders.
  • Amnesty (2017) reports that luxury housing was being developed on part of the cleared land.
Quick check
?Why were Otodo-Gbame's residents so easily evicted?
Mission 6

Urban renewal

  • Urban renewal: clearing and rebuilding older, run-down districts, usually led by government.
  • London example: the Heygate estate, a public-housing estate in Southwark completed in 1974, originally home to about 3,000 people.
  • A late-1990s survey found its buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment"; the council chose demolition, completed in July 2014.
Mission 6

The Heygate: outcomes and views

  • In 2005 all 595 council tenants were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in the borough, and all were offered the right to return.
  • The approved plan (January 2013) included 3,000 new homes.
  • The council: housing problems plus "regeneration opportunities" justified demolition.
  • Some residents: "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • Across London, 50 estates were regenerated in a decade: total homes almost doubled, but there was a net loss of some 8,000 social rented homes.
Quick check
?What does the London-wide evidence earlier in this mission show about urban renewal?
Mission 6

Gentrification

  • Gentrification: higher-income households move into and reinvest in a lower-income neighborhood.
  • New York City: of 22 neighborhoods that were low-income in 1990, NYU's Furman Center classed 15 as gentrifying.
  • Average rent rise, 1990 to 2010–2014: gentrifying 34.3%; whole city 22.1%; non-gentrifying low-income 13.2%.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: The rent rise in gentrifying neighborhoods was about how many times that in non-gentrifying low-income neighborhoods?
Mission 6

Positive and negative consequences

  • Positive: investment, repaired housing, new shops and services, higher tax revenue, rising values for owners.
  • Negative: higher rents and prices; long-term lower-income renters may be displaced; local businesses change.
  • Evidence limit: the Furman Center says, "We cannot directly observe if low-income residents are moving out of increasingly high-cost neighborhoods".
Mission 6

Responses that shift power

  • Secure tenure and enforced court orders protect informal residents.
  • Rehousing rights: Heygate tenants were offered the right to return.
  • Resident ballots: since July 18, 2018, London landlords seeking city (GLA) funding for estate regeneration that demolishes any social homes and builds 150 or more homes must win a resident ballot.
  • London's mayor also requires "no net loss of affordable homes" in such schemes.
Mission check

?A city plans to replace an informal settlement on valuable riverfront land with offices. Most residents have no legal title. Who is most likely to decide what replaces it?

Power over land decides. Amnesty estimates over 30,000 people were evicted from Otodo-Gbame; at the Heygate, the council redeveloped amid long controversy but gave tenants rehousing rights. Since 2018, London requires resident ballots for city-funded schemes that demolish social homes and build 150 or more homes.

Mission 7

Why cities face sustainability challenges

  • Urban areas use 75% of global energy and produce 75% of global greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Challenges named in the AP course: sprawl, sanitation, climate change, air and water quality, a large ecological footprint and energy use.
Mission 7

Footprint, sprawl and sanitation

  • Ecological footprint: the land and water a population needs to produce what it consumes and absorb its waste, especially carbon emissions, measured in global hectares.
  • Sprawl eats land: about 60% of land converted to urban use since 1970 was farmland.
  • Sanitation and water quality: 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements in 2022, many without safe water or sewers.
Mission 7

Air quality

  • In 2019, 99% of the world's people lived where WHO air quality guidelines were not met.
  • Outdoor air pollution caused an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths in 2019; 89% were in low- and middle-income countries.
  • WHO: air pollution is "one of the greatest environmental risks to child health".
Mission 7

London: a coroner's findings

  • A 2021 coroner's report on Ella Kissi-Debrah, who "died at the age of 9", lists "Air pollution exposure" among the causes; "The principal source of her exposure was traffic emissions".
  • London, 2019: where the most deprived Londoners were likely to live, average nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) was 13% higher than in the least deprived areas; fine particulate matter (PM2.5) was 6% higher.
  • The authors warn this "does not mean that any individual is necessarily exposed to higher or lower levels".
Quick check
?In London in 2019, the gap between the most and least deprived areas was 13% for NO₂ but only 6% for PM2.5. Which best explains the bigger gap for NO₂?
Mission 7

Four responses in the AP course

  • Regional planning: one plan across a whole metro area, so cities and counties do not compete to sprawl.
  • Brownfield remediation and redevelopment: cleaning up and reusing a property where reuse "may be complicated by the presence or potential presence of a hazardous substance, pollutant or contaminant".
  • Urban growth boundary (UGB): a line outside which urban development is not allowed.
  • Farmland protection: rules that keep farmland in farming.
Mission 7

Portland, Oregon: a UGB

  • Oregon's 1973 Senate Bill 100 created the nation's first statewide land-use planning laws.
  • Metro, a regional government for the Portland area, manages the UGB, which protects "farms and forests".
  • Metro is an example of regional planning: one boundary for the whole region.
  • The UGB must hold enough land for 20 years of growth; the best farmland is the last land considered for adding.
Quick check
?An old factory site downtown has contaminated soil. The city cleans it and builds housing there instead of on farmland at the edge. Which response is this?
Mission 7

How effective are the responses?

  • London's Ultra Low Emission Zone (ULEZ), a charge on older, more polluting vehicles: in 2024 roadside NO₂ was estimated to be 27% lower across London than without the ULEZ.
  • About 97% of vehicles seen driving in London were compliant.
  • Caution: the 27% compares measurements with a modeled no-ULEZ scenario, not with a real city.
  • Portland's UGB has been expanded "about three dozen times" since it was first drawn: it decides where growth may go, but the line itself keeps moving.
  • Exam move: "describe effectiveness" means say what changed, by how much, for whom, and what the evidence cannot show.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the effectiveness of Portland's UGB?
Mission check

?In a US city, a freeway runs through a lower-income neighborhood on the east side, while wealthier suburbs lie upwind to the west. Who is likely to breathe the dirtiest air?

Exposure is uneven: in London, NO₂ was 13% higher where the most deprived residents lived (2019), and the coroner named traffic as the main source of a child's exposure. Responses such as the ULEZ and urban growth boundaries help, but evidence of their effects has limits.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Low, medium and high density housing use land differently; low density spreads the city, high density concentrates it.
  • Housing reflects and shapes a city's culture, technology, cycles of development and infilling.

Mission 2

  • The location and quality of infrastructure shape where jobs, homes and investment go, and who is left without services.
  • Infrastructure is political (who pays, which areas first) and environmental (water, sewage, emissions).

Mission 3

  • Design initiatives: mixed land use, walkability, transportation-oriented development, smart growth, New Urbanism, greenbelts, slow-growth cities (urban growth boundaries).
  • Praise: less sprawl, more walking and transit, more housing choice. Criticism: higher housing costs, possible de facto segregation, loss of place character.

Mission 4

  • Quantitative: census (everyone, every ten years) and surveys such as the ACS (a sample, every year) show changes in urban population size and composition.
  • Qualitative: field studies and narratives show individual attitudes toward urban change. Use both to explain causes and effects.

Mission 5

  • Intra-urban movement and housing discrimination (redlining, blockbusting) left lasting patterns of unaffordable housing, poor services, environmental injustice and zones of abandonment.
  • Responses include inclusionary zoning and local food movements; fragmentation of government across counties, cities and districts makes regional fixes hard.

Mission 6

  • Squatter settlements are growing (1.12 billion slum residents in 2022); insecure land tenure leaves residents open to eviction.
  • Urban renewal and gentrification bring investment and new homes, but can reduce affordable housing and displace lower-income residents.

Mission 7

  • Challenges: sprawl, sanitation, climate change, air and water quality, ecological footprint, energy use.
  • Responses: regional planning, brownfield redevelopment, urban growth boundaries, farmland protection; judge them by measured change and its limits.
