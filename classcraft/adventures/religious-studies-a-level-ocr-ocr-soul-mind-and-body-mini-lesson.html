Soul, Mind and Body

Are we purely physical, or is there a soul or mind distinct from the body? This topic runs from Plato and Aristotle, through Descartes' substance dualism, to modern materialism. What survives — if anything — depends on the answer.

Plato · the soul

Plato: the immortal soul

For Plato the soul is distinct from the body, immortal, and belongs to the realm of the Forms. The body is a temporary, distracting prison; real knowledge is the soul recollecting the Forms it knew before birth.

  • The soul is tripartite: reason, spirit (emotion/will) and appetite (desire).
  • In the Phaedrus Plato pictures the soul as a charioteer (reason) steering two horses (spirit and appetite).
  • A just, harmonious person lets reason rule the other two parts.

Weigh it: Plato assumes the Forms exist and the soul pre-existed — both are hard to demonstrate.

Aristotle · the soul

Aristotle: the soul as the form of the body

Aristotle rejects a separable soul. In De Anima the soul is the form of the body — what makes a living thing the kind of living thing it is. Soul and body are one substance, like the imprint and the wax.

  • The soul is the formal and final cause of a living body — its structure and purpose.
  • Because the soul is the form of this body, it cannot normally exist without the body (unlike Plato's soul).
  • An analogy Aristotle uses: if an axe had a soul, it would be its capacity to chop — lose the axe and you lose the soul.

Contrast: Plato = soul survives death; Aristotle = soul is bound to the body it forms.

Descartes · dualism

Descartes: substance dualism

Descartes argues that mind and body are two distinct substances — substance dualism.

  • The mind is res cogitans — a thinking, non-extended thing.
  • The body is res extensa — an extended (space-occupying) thing.
  • Certainty: he can doubt he has a body, but not that he is thinking — cogito ergo sum ('I think, therefore I am').
  • Indivisibility: the body has parts and is divisible; the mind seems to have no parts, so they must be different in kind.

Key problem — interaction: if mind is non-physical, how does it cause the physical body to move? Descartes' answer (via the pineal gland) is widely rejected.

Materialism

Materialism: no separate soul

Materialism holds there is only the physical: the mind is not a distinct substance but a feature of the brain and body.

  • Gilbert Ryle mocks dualism as belief in a 'ghost in the machine'. He says dualists make a category mistake — treating 'the mind' as a separate thing rather than as a way of describing behaviour and abilities.
  • Identity theory: mental states simply are brain states (pain = a particular neural firing).
  • Reductive materialists (e.g. Richard Dawkins) argue that consciousness is fully explained by physical processes, so no soul is needed.

Challenge to materialism: it struggles to explain first-person conscious experience (qualia) and personal identity over time.

Weighing the debate

How you decide shapes belief in life after death:

  • For dualism: consciousness seems genuinely different from matter; near-death and religious experience suggest the self can be more than the brain.
  • For materialism: brain damage changes personality and thought, tying mind tightly to the body; the interaction problem undermines dualism.
  • Religious believers need not be dualists — some argue for the resurrection of the body rather than an immortal soul, closer to Aristotle than Plato.

The big ideas to take into the exam

Plato: soul is immortal, tripartite (reason, spirit, appetite), survives the body

Aristotle: soul is the form of the body and cannot exist without it

Descartes: substance dualism: res cogitans (mind) vs res extensa (body); cogito ergo sum

Materialism: only the physical exists; Ryle ghost in the machine and category mistake; identity theory

Key problems: interaction (for dualism); explaining consciousness and identity (for materialism)
