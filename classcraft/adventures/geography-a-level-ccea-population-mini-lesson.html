Mission 1

Mission 1: How do you count every person in a country, and how do you know who was missed?

Make a guess: How do you count every person in a country, and how do you know who was missed?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.1a.i distinguish national census taking from vital registration
  • AS2.1a.ii MEDC–LEDC contrasts in data reliability, collection methods and use (one MEDC and one LEDC national case study)
Mission 1

Two ways to count people

  • Census: counts everyone in a territory at one moment, repeated every few years. A stock snapshot.
  • Vital registration: records each birth, death, marriage (and divorce) as it happens. A continuous flow of events.
  • A census tells you how many people there are; registration tells you how the number is changing.
Mission 1

What makes a census

UN key features:

  • Individual enumeration: each person and dwelling recorded separately.
  • Universality within a defined territory.
  • Simultaneity: one reference moment.
  • Defined periodicity: the UN recommends a census "at least every 10 years".
  • Can produce small-area statistics.
Mission 1

What makes vital registration

  • UN definition: "the continuous, permanent, compulsory, and universal recording of the occurrence and characteristics of vital events".
  • Produces vital statistics: births, deaths, marriages, divorces.
  • Gives each person a legal identity, which opens access to benefits and services.
  • Needs an office, a law, and people who use it.
Quick check
?Which is an example of vital registration rather than a census?
Mission 1

How the two fit together

  • A census is "a fundamental baseline" for registration systems; comparing the two reveals gaps in registration.
  • Where registration is weak, census data can be used to estimate mortality and fertility.
  • Globally, only 73% of countries and areas register at least 90% of births; only 68% register at least 90% of deaths.
Mission 1

MEDC: Northern Ireland's Census 2021

  • Census day 21 March 2021: 1,903,175 people in 768,810 households.
  • First census to actively encourage online returns: just over 80% of households responded online.
  • Field staff used hand-held devices to follow up non-responding households.
  • Household return rate 97.1%, up from 93.6% in 2011.
  • Gaps estimated with a Census Coverage Survey; administrative data support the census.
Mission 1

MEDC: registration in the UK

  • All births in England, Wales and Northern Ireland "must be registered within 42 days".
  • In Northern Ireland a death "should be registered within five days" (unless referred to the coroner).
  • Completeness of birth registration: 100% (2022); deaths with cause recorded: 100% (2016).
  • Reliable, timely vital statistics, year by year.
Mission 1

LEDC: Kenya's 2019 census

  • Enumeration 24/25–31 August 2019, plus a mop-up on 1–2 September.
  • Total enumerated: 47,564,296 people.
  • Kenya's "first paperless" census: mobile technology for mapping and enumeration; data encrypted and sent to secure servers.
  • A big, modern operation, but a snapshot of one week.
Mission 1

LEDC: registration in Kenya

Children under 5 whose births are reported registered (Kenya Demographic and Health Surveys):

  • 60% (2008–09) → 66.9% (2014) → 76% (2022).
  • Only 33.8% had a birth certificate (2022).
  • 2022 gaps: urban 81.3%, rural 73.2%; poorest fifth 62.6%, richest fifth 88%.
  • By county: Wajir 49.6%, Nyeri 96.2%.
  • Note: these registration rates themselves come from a sample survey, because registration data cannot measure their own gaps.
Quick check
?In Kenya, which births are most likely to be missing from the registration system?

Mission 1

How the data are used

  • Census: small-area statistics for planning schools, housing and services; a baseline every decade.
  • Vital statistics: fertility and mortality rates, causes of death, health targets and life expectancy.
  • Legal identity: registration underpins access to "public benefits, social protections, and human rights".
  • Where registration is incomplete, census and survey data are used to estimate births and deaths.
  • Exam move: for each case, link method → reliability → use in one chain.
Quick check
?A health ministry wants annual causes of death by district. Which country's system can deliver this directly, and why?
Mission check

?Country X registers about half of its deaths, mostly in towns. Its last census was 12 years ago. A minister claims rural mortality is lower than urban mortality "because fewer rural deaths are recorded". Which response is strongest?

A census is a periodic snapshot of everyone; vital registration is a continuous record of events. In LEDCs registration is often incomplete and uneven, so data must be checked against each other.

Mission 2

Mission 2: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Make a guess: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.1b.i crude birth rate, crude death rate, total fertility rate, infant mortality rate
  • AS2.1b.ii the demographic transition model and the epidemiological transition
Mission 2

Vital rates

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase rate (% a year) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10. Negative = natural decrease.
  • "Crude" means per 1,000 of the whole population, whatever its age.
Mission 2

Fertility and infant mortality

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's age-specific birth rates.
  • Replacement level is about 2.1: "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
  • Infant mortality rate (IMR): deaths of infants under 1 year per 1,000 live births. It is a sensitive measure of health care, nutrition and clean water.
  • World TFR in 2024: 2.25.

Quick check
?In 2024 Niger's CBR was 41.4 and its CDR 8.7 per 1,000. What was its natural increase rate?
Mission 2

Age structure drives the crude rates

  • Japan's CDR (12.4) is higher than Niger's (8.7), although Japanese people live 23 years longer.
  • The reason is age structure: a population with many old people has many deaths each year, even with excellent health care.
  • A young population has few deaths and many future parents. Births stay high for decades even after TFR falls: population momentum.
Mission 2

The demographic transition model

  • Stage 1: high, fluctuating birth and death rates; slow growth.
  • Stage 2: death rates fall (food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination); birth rates stay high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3: birth rates fall (lower infant mortality, urbanisation, girls' education, contraception); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 5 (added later): births below deaths; decline.
Mission 2

Applying the DTM

  • Niger: CDR already low (8.7) but CBR very high (41.4): late stage 2.
  • Japan: deaths exceed births (CBR 6.1, CDR 12.4); population falling by 0.51% a year in 2024: stage 5.
  • Exam move: CCEA wants general reference to places: name a country for each stage you describe.
Mission 2

Limits of the DTM

  • Based on the history of Western Europe and North America.
  • In many lower-income countries death rates fell faster, using imported medicine and public health, before industrialisation.
  • It ignores migration, culture and policy (such as China's birth limits).
  • It does not say when, or whether, a country moves on.
Mission 2

The epidemiological transition

  • Omran (1971): "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases as the chief form of morbidity and primary cause of death".
  • Age of Pestilence and Famine: mortality "high and fluctuating"; life expectancy 20–40 years.
  • Age of Receding Pandemics: mortality falls; life expectancy rises "from about 30 to about 50 years".
  • Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases: mortality low; then "fertility becomes the crucial factor in population growth".
Quick check
?Which DTM stage best matches Omran's "Age of Receding Pandemics"?
Mission check

?In 2024 Bangladesh's TFR was 2.14, close to replacement, yet its CBR (20.0) was four times its CDR (5.1). Why is it still growing by natural increase?

Natural change depends on fertility and age structure. Niger has high fertility and a very young population, so births far exceed deaths (natural increase 3.27% a year: doubling in about 21 years). Japan has low fertility and an old population, so deaths exceed births.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 2000, non-communicable diseases caused 58% of the world's deaths. In 2023 they caused 74%. Good news or bad news?

Make a guess: In 2000, non-communicable diseases caused 58% of the world's deaths. In 2023 they caused 74%. Good news or bad news?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.1b.ii the epidemiological transition
Mission 3

Basic needs: food, water and sanitation

  • 2024: 3.4 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation; 354 million practised open defecation.
  • Safely managed sanitation: WHO African Region 27%, Europe 80%.
  • 23.2% of children under five were stunted in 2024.
  • Without sanitation, faeces reach water; diarrhoea and undernutrition then feed each other and kill infants. Skilled care in childbirth cuts maternal deaths.
Quick check
?A low-income country wants the fastest fall in infant mortality. Which change would do most?
Mission 3

The epidemiological transition (Omran, 1971)

  • Omran's three stages: "The Age of Pestilence and Famine"; "The Age of Receding Pandemics"; "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases".
  • Over time "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
  • It runs alongside the demographic transition: death rates fall first, then people live longer.
  • Driver: rising living standards (more food, clean water, sanitation) cut susceptibility to infection, so more people survive childhood.
Mission 3

The evidence

  • NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • In 2023, for the first time, communicable diseases caused less than half of all deaths in low-income countries.
  • Life expectancy 73.3 years, but healthy life expectancy (HALE) 62.8 (2023): many extra years are lived in poor health.
Mission 3

Diseases of poverty and of affluence

  • Diseases of poverty (mostly communicable): unsafe water and sanitation, crowded housing, undernutrition, warm climates suiting vectors, young populations, weak health care.
  • Diseases of affluence (NCDs): older populations, tobacco, physical inactivity, harmful use of alcohol and unhealthy diets.
  • The label misleads: 82% of the 18 million NCD deaths before age 70 (2021) were in low- and middle-income countries.
Quick check
?In a country, the risk of dying of heart disease at each age has not changed, yet heart disease's share of all deaths has doubled. What best explains this?
Mission 3

Limits of the model

  • Omran's classical model is based on Western Europe (England and Wales, Sweden); he added an accelerated model (Japan) and a delayed model (Chile, Ceylon).
  • Many poorer countries now face infections and NCDs together: a double burden of disease.
  • Olshansky and Ault (1986) added a fourth stage: "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • New and re-emerging infections (HIV, COVID-19) break the one-way pattern.
Mission check

?Over 40 years in one middle-income country, child deaths from diarrhoea fell sharply, life expectancy rose, and the share of deaths from heart disease, diabetes and dementia doubled. Good news or bad news?

Both. The world's rise from 58% to 74% is Omran's epidemiological transition: infections recede as living standards rise, people live longer, and NCDs take over, while the poorest countries and groups carry both burdens at once.

Mission 4

Mission 4: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Make a guess: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.1c.i differences between underpopulation, overpopulation and optimum population
  • AS2.1c.ii evaluate Malthus's and Boserup's theories of population sustainability
Mission 4

Under-, over- and optimum population

  • Each is defined against resources and technology, not against a fixed number of people:
    • Overpopulation: too many people for the resources and technology available; living standards fall.
    • Underpopulation: too few people to use the resources fully; more people would raise living standards.
    • Optimum population: the number that gives the highest living standard per person.
  • The optimum moves: new technology or trade can raise it; resource depletion can lower it.
Quick check
?A country has rich farmland and minerals but too few workers to develop them, so output per person is below what it could be. Which term fits?
Mission 4

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Malthus (1798): "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Population outgrows food until checks act:
    • preventive checks lower births (for example, later marriage)
    • positive checks raise deaths (for example, famine and disease).
Mission 4

Boserup: the optimistic view

  • Ester Boserup (The Conditions of Agricultural Growth, 1965): in Turner and Ali's summary, "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • Pressure on land drives new methods, such as irrigation for double and triple cropping and "green revolution" inputs.
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.
Quick check
?As population density rose, farmers shortened fallow periods and built terraces to grow more crops on the same land. Which view does this support?
Mission 4

Testing the views with data

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961), 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 kg per hectare (1961), 4,229 kg per hectare (2023).
  • World food supply: 2,196 kcal per person per day (1961), 2,884 (2013), on FAO's older series.
  • Yields grew about 3.0 times while population grew about 2.6 times (our calculation).
Quick check
?World food supply was 2,196 kcal per person per day in 1961 and 2,884 in 2013. By what percentage did it rise?
Mission 4

Evaluating the views

  • Against Malthus: food per person rose 31% (1961–2013), as yields tripled; he did not foresee the Green Revolution or large-scale trade in food.
  • For a Malthusian caution: 645 million people were still undernourished in 2025; six of nine planetary boundaries (Earth-system limits that mark the "safe operating space for humanity") were transgressed in 2023, seven by 2025.
  • Limits of Boserup: she assumed gradual growth and time to adapt; rapid growth or degraded soils can outpace intensification.
  • Exam move: say which view the evidence supports, at what scale, and for whom.
Mission check

?A study of Bangladeshi villages (1950–1986) found that small farmers "kept pace with the demands on production", but the share of landless households increased. Which conclusion is best supported?

Globally, food per person rose, from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal a day (1961–2013), because cereal yields roughly tripled while population grew 2.6 times. That fits Boserup better than Malthus, but hunger and planetary limits remain, and the answer depends on scale.

Mission 5

Mission 5: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Make a guess: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.1c.iii need for anti-natalist and pro-natalist fertility policies
  • AS2.1c.iv evaluate one fertility policy introduced in response to a population–resource imbalance (national case study: China)
Mission 5

Prospects for the global population

  • 8.2 billion in 2024; projected peak of about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s; about 10.2 billion by 2100.
  • The UN puts the chance of a peak this century at 80%. In 2013 it was about 30%.
  • The earlier peak is partly due to lower-than-expected fertility in some of the largest countries, "particularly China".
Mission 5

Why fertility policies?

  • Anti-natalist policy aims to reduce births: used where rapid growth outruns food, jobs, schools or resources (a population–resource imbalance).
  • Pro-natalist policy aims to raise births: used where fertility is far below replacement, the workforce shrinks and the population ages.
  • Examples of need: Niger's TFR is 5.94; Japan's 1.22 (2024).
Quick check
?Which is a pro-natalist measure?
Mission 5

China's one-child policy, 1980

  • Its rationale was "to reduce the growth rate of China's enormous population": a response to a population–resource imbalance.
  • 25 September 1980 is often cited as its official start.
  • Some exceptions: some ethnic minority groups, and parents whose first child was disabled.
  • Enforcement: economic and other sanctions, and in places "forced abortions and sterilizations".
Mission 5

Evaluation 1: did the policy cause the fall?

  • China's TFR had already fallen from 6.09 (1970) to 2.75 (1979), before the policy.
  • The 1970s "Longer, Later, Fewer" (wan xi shao) campaign came first. Researchers estimate it explains 27% of China's modern fertility decline.
  • So the one-child policy added to a fall already under way, alongside economic change and education.
Mission 5

Evaluation 2: the costs

  • Sex ratio at birth: about 117 boys per 100 girls in 2000, against about 105 normally, reflecting son preference under birth limits.
  • Ageing: 23.0% of China's people were aged 60 or over at the end of 2025.
  • Human costs: coercive enforcement, including forced abortions and sterilisations in places. Researchers call Chinese population policy "extreme in global experience".
Quick check
?Which statement is the strongest evaluation of the one-child policy?
Mission 5

The U-turn

  • 2016: two children allowed for all couples, after the law was revised in 2015.
  • 2021: three children; the 2020 fertility rate "dropped to 1.3 children per woman", and the shift "addresses the challenges of a graying populace".
  • 2025: 7.92 million births, 11.31 million deaths; population 1,404.89 million, 3.39 million fewer than a year earlier.
  • The imbalance had reversed: from too many births for resources to too few workers for an ageing population.
Mission 5

Can governments raise fertility?

  • UN review: child and family allowances "appear to have a positive but weak effect on fertility".
  • Short-term baby bonuses have "a limited effect, mostly affecting the timing of childbearing".
  • Raising births is harder than lowering them: the costs of raising children, housing and work still decide.
Mission check

?A country's TFR has fallen to about 1.0. Its government offers a one-off cash bonus for each new baby. What does the evidence predict?

China switched from anti-natalist to pro-natalist policy because fertility fell far below replacement (1.3 in 2020) and the population began to age and shrink: in 2025 deaths exceeded births by 3.39 million. Policy helped push births down; raising them again is much harder.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Census: individual, universal, simultaneous, periodic (at least every 10 years). Vital registration: continuous, compulsory, universal recording of births, deaths, marriages.
  • UK: online-first census (NI 97.1% return); births within 42 days; 100% complete registration.
  • Kenya: paperless 2019 census (47.6 million); 76% of under-5s registered, 33.8% with certificates; large gaps by wealth, place and county.

Mission 2

  • CBR, CDR, TFR and IMR measure natural change; age structure decides the crude rates. The DTM describes the sequence; its pace differs between settings.
  • The epidemiological transition tracks the shift from infectious to degenerative causes of death.

Mission 3

  • As living standards rise, infections recede and NCDs dominate: the epidemiological transition.

Mission 4

  • Malthus: population outruns food until checks act.
  • Boserup: population pressure drives intensification.
  • Since 1961 food per person has risen globally, but hunger, inequality and environmental limits keep the debate open.
  • Under-, over- and optimum population are defined against resources and technology, and the optimum changes.

Mission 5

  • Fertility policy responds to population–resource imbalances. China's one-child policy accelerated an existing fall at high cost; its U-turn shows how hard it is to raise fertility.
