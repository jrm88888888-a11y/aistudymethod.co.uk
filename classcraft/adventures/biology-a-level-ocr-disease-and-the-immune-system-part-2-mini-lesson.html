Mission 1

Mission 1: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Mission 1

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Vaccination gives artificial active immunity.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 1

Herd immunity

  • A pathogen spreads only when an infected person passes it to someone susceptible.
  • Vaccines protect the individual and, at high coverage, the population.
  • When a high enough percentage is immune, most contacts are immune: chains of transmission break.
  • This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns, people with weakened immune systems.
  • More infectious diseases need higher coverage: about 95% for measles.
  • If coverage falls below that level, outbreaks return.
Quick check
?For measles, transmission in a population is greatly impeded once about 95% of people are immune. A community's vaccination coverage falls from 96% to 85%. Which statement correctly describes a consequence?
Mission 1

When antigens change

  • Memory cells bind only the antigen they were made against.
  • Antigen variability: mutations change a pathogen's surface antigens. Old memory cells no longer bind, so you can fall ill again.
  • Influenza antigens change often, so a new vaccine is made each year.

Mission 1

Routine programmes, and why they change

  • Countries run routine programmes: in the UK, babies get combined vaccines (including diphtheria, tetanus, whooping cough, polio) and children get MMR.
  • Programmes change when:
    • a new vaccine arrives (UK: meningitis B added in 2015; HPV extended to boys in 2019);
    • a pathogen changes (a new influenza vaccine each year);
    • patterns of disease change: outbreaks, falling coverage, new groups at risk;
    • a disease is eradicated (smallpox vaccination stopped).
  • Global issues: travel carries pathogens between countries; cost, cold storage and access limit coverage; eradication (smallpox, polio) needs every country to take part.
Mission check

?A measles vaccine gives long-lasting protection. Over 100 types of rhinovirus cause the common cold, each with different surface antigens. Why would a vaccine made from one rhinovirus type give little protection against colds?

Long-lasting protection needs an antigen that stays the same. When antigens change or come in many types, memory cells miss.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 2

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 2

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 2

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • MRSA: Staphylococcus aureus resistant to methicillin and many other antibiotics.
  • Clostridium difficile: antibiotics kill normal gut bacteria; resistant C. difficile multiplies and releases toxins: severe diarrhoea.
  • Same pattern in insects: pesticide resistance.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check

After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission 2

Infections caught in hospital

  • Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), e.g. MRSA, C. difficile.
  • Causes: heavy antibiotic use (strong selection); patients with weak immunity; wounds, catheters, surgery; staff and equipment moving between patients.
  • Prescribing codes: no antibiotics for viral or minor infections; identify the bacterium and the drugs it is sensitive to; narrow spectrum where possible; take exactly as prescribed; keep some antibiotics in reserve.
  • Infection-control codes: wash hands between patients; isolate infected patients; screen new patients (e.g. for MRSA); clean wards; sterilise equipment; bare below the elbows.
  • Alcohol gel does not kill C. difficile spores. Wash hands with soap and water.
Quick check
?Cases of C. difficile infection rise on a hospital ward. Staff already clean their hands with alcohol gel between patients. Which change to practice would best reduce the spread?
Mission 2

Antibiotics: benefits and risks

  • 1928: Fleming sees a Penicillium mould stop bacteria growing.
  • 1940s: Florey and Chain purify penicillin; mass production follows.
  • Benefits: once-deadly infections cured; safer surgery, childbirth, cancer treatment and transplants.
  • Risks: resistance; allergic reactions; loss of helpful gut bacteria (C. difficile).
  • Implications: few new classes of antibiotic found in decades; resistant infections are harder and costlier to treat; some are untreatable.
Mission 2

Where medicines come from

  • Microorganisms: penicillin (Penicillium, a fungus); streptomycin (Streptomyces, a bacterium).
  • Plants: morphine (opium poppy); digoxin (foxglove); paclitaxel, a cancer drug (Pacific yew); aspirin was developed from a chemical in willow bark.
  • Most species have never been tested. Lost biodiversity means lost medicines.
  • Personalised medicine: a person's genome guides the choice of drug and dose.
  • Synthetic biology: engineering organisms to make medicines, e.g. yeast making a precursor of the antimalarial artemisinin.
Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Mission 3

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).

Mission 3

What is driving the losses?

  • Overarching cause: human population growth. It drives every specific cause.
  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
  • Climate change: species' ranges shift; some cannot move.
Quick check
?Which factor is the overarching cause of the current biodiversity crisis, driving each of the specific causes?
Mission 3

Farming and biodiversity

  • Farming techniques that reduce biodiversity:
  • Monoculture: one crop over large areas; few habitats, few food sources.
  • Removing hedgerows, ponds and field margins; draining wetlands.
  • Pesticides and herbicides kill non-target species; fertilisers let a few fast-growing plants dominate.
  • Intensive farming gives more food and income, at a cost to biodiversity.
  • Continuous monoculture also depletes the soil.
Quick check
?A farmer removes the hedgerows between three small fields to make one large field of wheat, sprayed with herbicide. Which statement best explains the effect on biodiversity?
Mission 3

Climate change moves species

  • As climate warms, many species shift their ranges: towards the poles and uphill.
  • Real data (Chen et al. 2011, many species and regions combined): median shift 16.9 km per decade to higher latitude and 11.0 m per decade uphill.
  • Species that cannot move fast enough, or have nowhere to go (mountain tops, islands), suffer local extinctions.
  • Arriving species change communities; timing mismatches (e.g. flowering and pollinators) can follow.
Quick check
?Illustrative mountain. A plant grows between 1800 m and 2000 m altitude. The summit is at 2100 m. Assume its range moves uphill at 11.0 m per decade (the median rate above). About how long until the top of its range reaches the summit?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Mission 4

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
  • Social and ethical arguments are also made: e.g. that species have a right to exist, or that we should not leave a poorer world to future generations.
Mission 4

Medicines from living things

  • Penicillin: from the mould Penicillium, a microorganism.
  • Aspirin: developed from salicylic-acid compounds in willow bark.
  • Paclitaxel (Taxol, an anticancer drug): first isolated from the bark of the Pacific yew.
  • Most species have never been tested for useful compounds. Each extinction loses possible medicines.
  • Personalised medicine: a person's genome guides the choice of drug and dose.
  • Synthetic biology: engineering organisms to make drugs, e.g. yeast engineered to make a precursor of the antimalarial artemisinin.
Mission 4

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • At sea: marine conservation zones.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
Mission 4

Conservation versus human needs

  • Preservation: keeping an area untouched, with no human use.
  • Conservation: active management that maintains biodiversity while allowing sustainable use.
  • People need land for food, homes and income. Conservation that ignores this rarely lasts.
  • Compromises: sustainable timber and fishing quotas; payments to farmers; ecotourism income for local people.
  • Reasons for conservation: economic (resources, tourism), social (recreation, livelihoods), ethical.
Mission 4

Ex situ conservation

  • Ex situ: conserving a species outside its natural habitat.
  • Zoos: captive breeding; studbooks pair unrelated animals to limit inbreeding; reintroduction.
  • Botanic gardens: living collections of rare plants; propagation; research.
  • Seed banks: seeds dried and frozen, then germinated and tested at intervals. Kew's Millennium Seed Bank holds seeds of over 40,000 species.
Quick check
?Seeds of many tropical rainforest trees are killed by the drying and freezing used in seed banks. A conservation programme wants to conserve one such tree species, whose natural forest is being logged. Which approach to conserving this species is appropriate?
Mission 4

Conservation agreements

  • CITES (Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species, 1973): controls or bans international trade in listed species and their products, e.g. ivory. Trade needs permits.
  • CBD (Convention on Biological Diversity, Rio Earth Summit 1992): countries agree to conserve biodiversity, use it sustainably and share fairly the benefits of genetic resources. Each produces a national biodiversity strategy.
  • Countryside Stewardship Scheme (England, from 1991): paid farmers to manage land for wildlife and landscape, e.g. restoring hedgerows and field margins. Later replaced by newer stewardship schemes.
  • International agreements set the rules; local schemes change what happens on the land.
Mission check

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

A species kept only in a zoo or a seed bank survives, but it is not truly saved. Conservation needs the habitat as well.

Mission 5

Reading whole genomes

  • Genome sequencing finds the order of nucleotides in all of an organism's DNA.
  • Human Genome Project (1990–2003): about 3 billion base pairs; about 20 000–21 000 protein-coding genes.
  • Thousands of genomes are now sequenced: bacteria, crops, the malaria mosquito, and extinct Neanderthals, from bone.
Mission 5

Sanger sequencing: chain termination

  • Mix: template DNA, a primer, DNA polymerase, normal nucleotides, and a few terminator nucleotides.
  • Each terminator base (A, T, C, G) carries a different fluorescent label.
  • When a terminator is added, the new strand stops.
  • Result: fragments of every length, each ending in a labelled base.
  • Capillary electrophoresis separates them by length, one base apart.
  • A laser reads the label at the end of each fragment, shortest first: that is the sequence.
Mission 5

Faster and cheaper

  • High-throughput ("next-generation") sequencing:
    • DNA is cut into millions of short fragments.
    • All are sequenced at the same time.
    • Computers assemble overlapping reads into the genome.
  • A human genome now takes about a day.

Mission 5

From genome to proteome

  • Using the genetic code, a gene's base sequence predicts the amino acid sequence of its polypeptide.
  • Proteome: the full range of proteins a cell can produce.
  • Complex organisms: introns, other non-coding DNA and regulatory genes. The proteome cannot easily be read from the genome.
Quick check
?Why is it easier to predict a bacterium's proteome from its genome than to predict a human's?
Mission 5

Comparing genomes: bioinformatics

  • Bioinformatics: storing and analysing sequence data in databases, using computer software.
  • Computational biology: using computers to model and analyse biological systems.
  • Between individuals: single-base differences (SNPs) are linked to traits and disease risk.
  • Between species: more similar sequences mean a more recent common ancestor. This tests evolutionary relationships.
  • Epidemiology: sequencing pathogens tracks how an outbreak spreads and when new variants appear.
Mission 5

Synthetic biology

  • Sequence knowledge lets scientists design and build new DNA, then put it into cells.
  • 2010: a bacterial cell controlled by a genome made entirely by chemical synthesis.
  • 2013: yeast engineered with plant genes to make a precursor of artemisinin, an antimalarial drug.
  • Also: microorganisms designed to make medicines, fuels or biosensors.
Mission 5

Genomes in medicine

  • Personalised medicine: choose a treatment or dose to suit a person's genotype.
  • Cancer: sequence a tumour; choose drugs that target its particular mutations.
  • Rare diseases: a genome can reveal the cause, ending years of uncertain diagnosis.
  • A genome shows risks and likely responses, not a fixed future.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • High coverage gives herd immunity, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.
  • Changing antigens defeat old memory cells: why the flu vaccine changes yearly.

Mission 2

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.

Mission 3

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Human population growth is the overarching cause.
  • Intensive farming raises food production but lowers biodiversity.

Mission 4

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.
  • CITES controls trade; the CBD commits countries to conservation; stewardship schemes pay farmers to manage land for wildlife.

Mission 5

  • Sequencing reads nucleotide order; it has become automated, faster and vastly cheaper, so whole genomes are compared routinely.
  • Uses: relationships between species, outbreaks, personalised medicine; a genome gives risks, not a fixed future.
  • The genome predicts polypeptides, but non-coding DNA, regulation and cell-specific expression mean it does not simply give the proteome.
