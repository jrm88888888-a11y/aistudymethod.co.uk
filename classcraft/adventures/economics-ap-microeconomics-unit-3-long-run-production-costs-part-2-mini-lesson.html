Mission 1

Mission 1: If a seller must cut its price to sell one more, why does that extra sale bring in less than the price?

Make a guess: If a seller must cut its price to sell one more, why does that extra sale bring in less than the price?

Mission 1

Three measures of revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold.
  • Average revenue (AR) = TR ÷ quantity. With one price for all units, AR = price.
  • Marginal revenue (MR) = the change in TR from selling one more unit: MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • Say: "marginal revenue is the extra revenue from one more unit" — not: "the price of one more unit".

Mission 1

Why MR is below price

  • To sell a 3rd unit, the stall cuts its price from 8 to 6, for every buyer.
  • Gain: the 3rd unit sells for +6.
  • Loss: the first 2 units now earn 2 less each: 2 × 2 = −4.
  • MR = 6 − 4 = 2, below the price of 6.
  • So when a firm must lower its price to sell more, MR < AR for every unit after the first.
  • Imperfect competitors face a downward-sloping demand curve: to sell more they must lower the price, so MR < P.
  • Say: "AR is the demand curve; MR lies below it" — not: "MR equals the price".

Mission 1

A price taker

  • In a perfectly competitive market, a firm is a price taker: it can sell as much as it likes at the market price.
  • It never has to cut its price to sell more, so each extra unit adds exactly the price to TR.
  • So P = MR = AR = D, all one horizontal line.
  • TR is a straight line from the origin, rising by the price with each unit.
  • Illustrative data: price 5 → TR 5, 10, 15, 20 …; MR = 5 every time.
Mission 1

Drawing the curves

  • D = AR (and P): the downward-sloping demand curve.
  • With a straight-line demand curve, MR starts at the same point on the price axis and is twice as steep.
  • MR meets the quantity axis halfway to where AR does.
  • TR is an arch: it rises while MR > 0, peaks where MR = 0, and falls once MR < 0.
  • Say: "TR is maximized where MR = 0" — not: "where AR = 0", where TR is zero.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can't one wheat farmer charge even slightly more than the market price?

Make a guess: Why can't one wheat farmer charge even slightly more than the market price?

Mission 2

The assumptions

  • Perfect competition assumes:
    • many buyers and sellers, each too small to affect the price
    • identical (homogeneous) products: one seller's output is a perfect substitute for another's
    • perfect information: everyone knows every price
    • free entry and exit: no barriers
  • No firm has market power.
  • Real markets come close, but never fully: wheat and other crops, some currency markets.
Mission 2

A price taker

  • Two diagrams:
    • Industry: market D and S set the price, Pe.
    • Firm: sells any amount it wants at Pe. Its demand curve is horizontal at Pe.
  • Charge above Pe? Buyers switch to identical products: the firm sells nothing.
  • Charge below Pe? Pointless: it can already sell all it wants at Pe.
  • So the firm is a price taker: every extra unit sells for Pe.
  • P = MR = AR = D, a horizontal line.
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve is perfectly elastic" — not: "market demand is perfectly elastic". Market demand slopes down as normal.
Quick check
?In a perfectly competitive market, why is each firm's demand curve horizontal at the market price?
Mission 2

Case: the world wheat price

  • Wheat (US hard red winter), world price, annual averages:
    • 2021: about 315 dollars per metric ton
    • 2022: about 430 dollars per metric ton
    • 2023: about 340 dollars per metric ton
  • 2022 was the year Russia invaded Ukraine; both are major wheat exporters.
  • No single farmer caused these swings, and none could resist them.
  • Each farmer's only choice: how much to grow and sell at the world price.
Mission 2

How much to produce?

  • Profit is maximized where MC = MR.
  • For a price taker, MR = P. So produce where MC = P, on the rising part of MC.
  • Why:
    • MC below P: the next unit adds more to revenue than to cost → produce it.
    • MC above P: the unit adds more to cost than to revenue → don't produce it.
  • Rule: MR = MC, and in perfect competition P = MR = MC.
  • Buyers buy up to where MB = P; firms produce up to where MC = P. So at the competitive equilibrium P = MB = MC: allocative efficiency.
Mission 2

Profit, normal profit (zero economic profit) or loss

  • Find output where MC = MR. Then compare AR (= P) with ATC at that output.
  • P > ATC: economic profit = (P − ATC) × Q, a rectangle.
  • P = ATC: normal profit (break-even): just enough to keep the firm in this industry.
  • P < ATC: loss = (ATC − P) × Q.
  • AP terms: economic profit (P > ATC), zero economic profit (P = ATC), economic loss (P < ATC). ATC = average total cost.
  • Say: "economic profit" — not: "a big profit". Zero economic profit (normal profit) is already counted in ATC as a cost.

Mission 2

Shut down or carry on?

  • In the short run, fixed costs must be paid even at zero output.
  • Shut down → loss = fixed cost.
  • Carry on → loss = TC − TR.
  • Rule: carry on if TR ≥ TVC, i.e. P ≥ AVC. Revenue covers variable costs and pays part of the fixed cost.
  • Shut down if P < AVC at every output.
  • Shutdown price = minimum AVC.
  • The firm from an earlier screen: shutdown price = minimum AVC = 10.
  • AP wording: operate if TR ≥ TVC (P ≥ AVC); shut down if TR < TVC (P < AVC).
Mission 2Graph lab

Same firm. Fixed cost 360. Shutdown loss = 360.

  • Takeaway: produce as long as P ≥ AVC. At 15: TR 300 > TVC 11 × 20 = 220, so the extra 80 pays part of the fixed cost and the loss is 280 < 360: carry on. Below 10, shutting down loses less.

Mission 2

The firm's supply curve

  • At each price, the firm produces where P = MC.
  • So its MC curve shows how much it supplies at each price.
  • Below minimum AVC it supplies nothing.
  • Firm's short-run supply curve = its MC curve above minimum AVC.
  • Industry supply = the firms' supply curves added sideways (horizontally).
Mission check

?A firm sells standard cardboard boxes in a perfectly competitive market. The market price is 15 per box. At its current output, the marginal cost of its last box is 12. What should it do to maximize profit?

A price taker cannot choose its price. Its only lever is output: produce up to where MC equals the market price.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a firm make goods at the lowest possible cost and still waste resources?

Make a guess: Can a firm make goods at the lowest possible cost and still waste resources?

Mission 3

Two questions about waste

  • Efficiency: getting the most out of scarce resources.
  • Ask two separate questions:
    • How is it made? At the lowest possible cost per unit? → productive efficiency
    • What is made, and how much? The goods society values most? → allocative efficiency
  • One can hold without the other.
Mission 3

Productive efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output where average cost (ATC) is at its minimum.
  • AP label: ATC (average total cost). Productive efficiency: output at minimum ATC.
  • At that output, MC = ATC: the MC curve cuts the ATC curve at its lowest point.
  • Why? If the next unit costs less than the average (MC < ATC), it pulls ATC down. If it costs more (MC > ATC), it pushes ATC up.
  • Say: "productively efficient at minimum ATC" — not: "at the lowest total cost" (that is zero output).

Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm's total costs are: 10 units, 300; 20 units, 440; 30 units, 570; 40 units, 720; 50 units, 950. At which output is the firm productively efficient?
Mission 3

Allocative efficiency

  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods and services society values most, in the right amounts.
  • Condition: P = MC.
    • Price shows what buyers will pay for the last unit: its marginal benefit (MB) to them.
    • MC shows the cost of the resources used for it: the value of what else they could have made.
  • P > MC: buyers value one more unit above its cost → too little is made.
  • P < MC: the last unit costs more than it is worth to buyers → too much is made.
  • Price signals both MB and MC: allocative efficiency is P = MB = MC.
  • Say: "allocatively efficient where P = MC" — not: "where P = ATC" (that is normal profit).
Mission 3Graph lab

Can both happen at once?

A price-taking firm always produces where P = MC. You set the market price.

  • Takeaway: P = MC holds at every price; minimum ATC only when the price equals minimum ATC (here 26).
Quick check
?A firm sells its product for 30. At its current output, the marginal cost of the last unit is 18. What does this show?
Mission 3

Comparing market structures

  • Perfect competition (long run): P = MC (allocatively efficient) and P = minimum ATC (productively efficient).
  • Imperfect competition (monopoly, oligopoly, monopolistic competition): to sell more, a firm must lower its price.
    • So it produces where P > MC. The efficient condition, P = MB = MC, is not met: allocatively inefficient.
  • Monopolistic competition (long run): zero economic profit, but output is below the efficient scale (left of minimum ATC). This gap is excess capacity.
    • So it is neither productively nor allocatively efficient.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A firm produces exactly at the lowest point of its ATC curve. Its price is 12. At this output, its marginal cost is 7. Which statement is correct?

Low cost and the right amount are two different tests. A firm can pass the first and fail the second.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Make a guess: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Mission 4

The long run

  • Long run: all factors can change. Firms can enter or leave the industry.
  • In perfect competition there are no barriers, so entry and exit are free.
  • Normal profit: the minimum return needed to keep the firm's resources in this industry. It is their opportunity cost.
  • Normal profit is counted inside ATC. So at P = ATC, the owners earn just enough to stay.
  • AP terms: zero economic profit = normal profit. ATC includes it.
  • Say: "normal profit" — not: "no profit". The owners are still paid their opportunity cost.
Mission 4

Economic profit attracts entry

  • Build it in steps (two panels: industry and firm):
  • 1. Industry: D and S1 set price P1, above minimum ATC. Firm: economic profit rectangle.
  • 2. Profit is a signal: new firms enter. Industry supply shifts right: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price falls to P2 = minimum ATC. Each firm cuts output back to the lowest point of ATC.
  • 4. Economic profit is gone: normal profit only. Entry stops.
  • AP term: economic profit attracts entry until it is zero.
Quick check
?A perfectly competitive industry is earning economic profit. New firms enter until the long run is reached. What happens to output?
Mission 4

Losses drive exit

  • The mirror image:
  • 1. Price below minimum ATC: firms make losses.
  • 2. In the long run, loss-making firms leave. Industry supply shifts left: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price rises back to minimum ATC.
  • 4. The firms that remain make normal profit. Exit stops.
  • Firms exit when they expect long-run losses: in the long run every cost is variable, so the price must cover ATC.
Mission 4

Long-run equilibrium

  • Entry and exit stop only when price = minimum ATC.
  • P = MR = MC = minimum ATC: zero economic profit, at the efficient scale.
  • Each firm makes normal profit: no reason to enter or leave.
  • Industry: D and S set this price; the number of firms adjusts to match demand.
Mission 4Graph lab

A demand boom

Illustrative data. 240 identical firms, each making normal profit at a price of 26. Assume each firm's costs do not change as the industry grows.

  • Takeaway: entry continues until price is back at minimum ATC (26). More firms; same output each.
Mission 4

Worked example: the boom, step by step

  • Illustrative data. Each firm: minimum ATC = 26 at 30 units.
  • Start: market demand at 26 is 7,200 units → 7,200 ÷ 30 = 240 firms.
  • Short run: demand rises. Still 240 firms. Price jumps to 43; each firm makes 40 units.
    • Industry output: 240 × 40 = 9,600.
    • Profit per firm: (43 − 28) × 40 = 600 (economic profit). ATC at 40 units = 28.
  • Long run: firms enter until price = 26 again.
    • Market demand at 26 is now 14,700 units → 14,700 ÷ 30 = 490 firms.
  • Industry output doubles and more; each firm is back to 30 units and normal profit.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a perfectly competitive industry, each firm's minimum ATC is 4 at an output of 500 units. Today 100 firms each produce 550 units and sell at 6. At a price of 4, market demand would be 60,000 units. Costs do not change as firms enter. How many firms will there be in the long run?
Mission 4

Why the long run is efficient

  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC. The value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost.
  • Productive efficiency: output at minimum ATC, the lowest cost per unit.
  • Long-run perfect competition is both allocatively (P = MC) and productively (P = minimum ATC) efficient.
  • Consumers pay the lowest price that firms can sustain: minimum ATC.
Mission 4

Constant, increasing, decreasing cost

  • After a demand increase, where does long-run price settle? It depends on what happens to input prices as the industry grows.
  • Constant-cost industry: input prices do not change. Long-run price returns to the same minimum ATC. Long-run supply (LRS) is horizontal.
  • Increasing-cost industry: expansion bids up input prices (e.g. scarce specialized labor or land). Every firm's ATC shifts up. Long-run price ends higher. LRS slopes up.
  • Decreasing-cost industry: expansion lowers input prices (e.g. suppliers gain economies of scale). Long-run price ends lower. LRS slopes down.
  • In all three, each firm still ends at zero economic profit.
Quick check
?Demand for a good in an increasing-cost, perfectly competitive industry rises and stays higher. Compared with the price before the rise in demand, what is the new long-run equilibrium price?
Mission check

?A city's market for bicycle repairs is perfectly competitive and in long-run equilibrium. The city then charges every repair firm a fixed yearly fee, whatever its output. Demand is unchanged. What happens in the long run?

Free entry and exit push price to minimum ATC. Any change in costs or demand is eventually competed into the price.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • TR = P × Q; AR = TR ÷ Q = price, so the AR curve is the demand curve; MR = ΔTR ÷ ΔQ.
  • When a firm must cut its price to sell more, MR is below AR; a price taker has P = AR = MR.
  • TR is highest where MR = 0, at unit elasticity; MR is positive where demand is elastic and negative where it is inelastic.

Mission 2

  • A perfectly competitive firm is a price taker: P = AR = MR = D, a horizontal line.
  • It produces where MC = MR = P. Compare P with ATC: economic profit, normal profit or loss.
  • In the short run it keeps producing while P ≥ AVC; its supply curve is MC above minimum AVC.

Mission 3

  • Productive efficiency: minimum ATC (MC = ATC).
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC.
  • Market power lets a firm set P above MC: allocative inefficiency.

Mission 4

  • Economic profit attracts entry; losses cause exit. Both stop at price = minimum ATC.
  • Long-run equilibrium: P = MR = MC = minimum ATC; normal profit (zero economic profit).
  • It is allocatively and productively efficient.
  • Long-run supply is horizontal (constant cost), upward (increasing cost) or downward (decreasing cost).
