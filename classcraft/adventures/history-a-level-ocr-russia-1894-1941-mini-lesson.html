1894–1905 · the strains of autocracy

Industrialisation without political change

Nicholas II came to the throne in 1894 convinced that autocracy was a sacred trust. The problem was that his ministers were modernising the economy of a state whose politics could not modernise with it.

  • Witte's industrialisation (finance minister 1892–1903): foreign loans, the gold standard, the Trans-Siberian Railway. It created a concentrated, urban, and highly combustible industrial working class — in a country with no legal parties, no parliament and no unions.
  • The land question was never solved. Peasant land hunger, redemption payments and communal tenure kept the countryside poor and resentful.
  • Defeat abroad: the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05) was meant to be a short victorious war. It ended in humiliation — Port Arthur, Mukden, and the annihilation of the Baltic Fleet at Tsushima.
  • 1905: Bloody Sunday (January 1905), when troops fired on a peaceful petitioning march to the Winter Palace, destroyed the myth of the Tsar as the people's protector. Strikes, mutiny and the St Petersburg Soviet followed. Nicholas conceded the October Manifesto — civil liberties and an elected Duma.

The key judgement: 1905 was a survived revolution, not a defeated one. The Fundamental Laws (1906) restored the Tsar's autocratic title and he dissolved the first two Dumas at will. Concession without conviction bought time — and taught a generation that the regime yielded only to force.

1906–1917 · reform, war, collapse

Stolypin, the war, and February

Between 1906 and 1914 the regime had one serious reformer and one catastrophic war.

  • Stolypin (PM 1906–11) combined repression — the field courts-martial whose noose was nicknamed 'Stolypin's necktie' — with land reform: peasants could leave the commune and consolidate holdings, creating, he hoped, a conservative class of prosperous farmers. He was assassinated in 1911, and the reform was incomplete.
  • The First World War destroyed the monarchy. Catastrophic losses, transport collapse, urban food shortages, and Nicholas's fatal decision in 1915 to take personal command at the front — which meant every defeat was now personally his, and left government to the Tsarina and Rasputin.
  • February 1917: bread queues, strikes and mutiny in Petrograd. The garrison would not fire. The Duma formed a Provisional Government; the Petrograd Soviet issued Order Number 1. Nicholas abdicated on 2 March 1917 (Old Style; 15 March New Style). Three hundred years of Romanov rule ended in a week, and nobody planned it.

Dual power is the key concept: the Provisional Government had the formal authority; the Soviet had the loyalty of the soldiers and workers. Neither could govern alone — and the Provisional Government's decision to continue the war destroyed it.

1917 · October

Coup, or revolution?

Between February and October, the Bolsheviks went from a fringe faction to power.

  • Lenin's April Theses (April 1917) broke with all other socialists: no support for the Provisional Government, all power to the Soviets, an immediate end to the war. 'Peace, land, bread' was a programme aimed precisely at the three things the Provisional Government would not deliver.
  • The July Days — a premature rising — nearly destroyed the party; Lenin fled to Finland.
  • The Kornilov affair (August 1917) saved them: Kerensky armed the Bolsheviks to resist an apparent military coup, and they never gave the weapons back. Bolshevik support in the Soviets surged.
  • 25–26 October 1917 (Old Style; 7–8 November New Style): the Military Revolutionary Committee, organised by Trotsky, seized the key points of Petrograd. The Winter Palace fell with very little fighting.
  • Consolidation: the Constituent Assembly, in which the Bolsheviks were a minority, met once and was dissolved by force in January 1918. The Treaty of Brest-Litovsk (March 1918) bought peace at an enormous territorial price.

The central quarrel of this unit. Was October a coup by a disciplined conspiratorial party — the reading associated with Richard Pipes — or a genuine revolution from below, resting on real support among workers, soldiers and sailors, as Sheila Fitzpatrick and the social historians argue? Note the evidence each side uses: Pipes reads the party's own organisational record; Fitzpatrick reads factory committees, garrison meetings and soviet elections. Different archives, different revolutions.

1918–1928 · civil war and NEP

Survival, terror, and a strategic retreat

The Bolsheviks won the Civil War (1918–1921) and were changed by winning it.

  • Why the Reds won: interior lines and control of the industrial centres and railways; Trotsky's Red Army; a single command against divided, geographically scattered Whites with no common political programme; and the peasantry's grim calculation that a Red victory was less likely to bring back the landlords.
  • War Communism: grain requisitioning, nationalisation, labour discipline — and the Cheka. Famine and industrial collapse followed.
  • Kronstadt (March 1921): the mutiny of the sailors who had been the revolution's most loyal supporters, demanding soviets without Bolsheviks. It was crushed — and it terrified Lenin.
  • The New Economic Policy (1921): requisitioning replaced by a tax in kind; small-scale private trade legalised. Economically it worked; ideologically it was a retreat, and the party never stopped arguing about it.
  • Lenin died in January 1924. Stalin, as General Secretary from 1922, used control of appointments to build a machine, allied with the right against Trotsky, then destroyed the right. By 1928–29 he was dominant.

1928–1941 · Stalin's revolution

Collectivisation, the plans, and the Terror

Stalin's 'revolution from above' transformed the Soviet Union at a colossal human cost.

  • Collectivisation (from 1929): peasants were forced into collective farms; the kulaks — supposedly rich peasants, in practice anyone who resisted — were 'liquidated as a class'. Peasants slaughtered livestock rather than surrender it. Grain was requisitioned even as the countryside starved: the famine of 1932–33, at its most lethal in Ukraine and Kazakhstan, killed millions.
  • The Five-Year Plans (from 1928): breakneck heavy industry — steel, coal, machinery, the new cities of Magnitogorsk and Dnieprostroi. Targets were fantastical and statistics unreliable; consumer goods and housing were sacrificed. But the industrial base that survived 1941 was built here.
  • The Great Terror (1936–1938): the show trials of Zinoviev and Kamenev (1936), Pyatakov (1937) and Bukharin (1938); the destruction of the Red Army officer corps; the mass operations of 1937–38 with regional quotas for arrest and execution. The purge consumed the party, the army, the intelligentsia and vast numbers of ordinary people.
  • 1939–41: the Nazi–Soviet Pact (August 1939) bought time and territory; Operation Barbarossa began on 22 June 1941, and the unit ends there.

Explaining the Terror. The totalitarian school — Robert Conquest, The Great Terror (1968) — saw a centrally directed campaign flowing from Stalin's will. The revisionists — J. Arch Getty, Origins of the Great Purges (1985) — argued for a more chaotic process, with local officials, denunciation and centre–periphery conflict driving the killing. The archives opened after 1991 confirmed both that Stalin signed the orders and that the machine ran on willing participation from below. The mature position holds both.

Exam technique · Unit Group 2

The non-British period study

OCR Unit Group 2 is a 1 hour paper worth 30 marks — 15% of the A-level. It is assessed by essay: no sources, no extracts, just your knowledge and your judgement.

  • Because there is no source booklet to hide behind, precision is everything. Dates, names, institutions, decrees. 'The Bolsheviks took power' is nothing; 'the Military Revolutionary Committee seized the bridges, telegraph and railway stations on 25 October Old Style' is a mark.
  • Beware the calendar. Russia used the Julian calendar until February 1918, thirteen days behind the West. The February Revolution happened in March, and the October Revolution in November, by our calendar. Signal that you know this — examiners notice.
  • Every question is a judgement question. Decide, structure around your reasons, and give the counter-case its best shot before you defeat it.

Key question · why Stalin, not Trotsky

The succession struggle, 1924–1929

Trotsky was the more brilliant man, and he lost. Explaining that is a classic essay.

  • The office: Stalin became General Secretary in 1922 — a post everyone else considered dull. It gave him control of appointments, and therefore of the party congresses that would decide his fate.
  • The tactics: he allied with Zinoviev and Kamenev against Trotsky; then, having destroyed the Left, turned with Bukharin against Zinoviev and Kamenev; then destroyed Bukharin and the Right over the pace of industrialisation. Each opponent was isolated separately.
  • The ideology: 'Socialism in One Country' was electorally shrewd inside the party — it promised achievable construction at home, against Trotsky's 'permanent revolution', which promised endless struggle and depended on revolutions abroad that had not come.
  • Trotsky's failings: arrogance, a late arrival to Bolshevism that was never forgotten, an unwillingness to organise a faction, and his absence from Lenin's funeral.
  • Lenin's Testament, criticising Stalin, was suppressed by a Politburo whose members had all been criticised in it.

The insight: Stalin won because he understood that in a one-party state, the party apparatus is the electorate. He was not underestimated by accident — he was underestimated because his rivals were thinking about ideas while he was thinking about personnel.

Key question · continuity

Was Stalinism the child of Leninism?

This is the question the whole unit is designed to make you answer — because it spans 1894 to 1941 precisely so you can trace continuities.

The continuity case. Lenin created the one-party state: he dissolved the Constituent Assembly in January 1918, founded the Cheka, banned rival parties, and at the Tenth Party Congress (1921) banned factions within the Bolshevik party. The machinery Stalin used was built by Lenin. On this reading, Stalin was the logical heir.
The discontinuity case. Lenin's repression was directed against class enemies in a civil war; Stalin's Terror consumed the party itself, killing most of Lenin's own Central Committee. The scale, the arbitrariness, the cult of personality and the murder of loyal communists were new. Lenin's Testament warned against Stalin precisely because he saw the danger.

How to handle it: refuse the binary. Lenin built a state capable of Stalinism; he did not make Stalinism inevitable. Structures create possibilities; individuals choose among them. That formulation, evidenced from 1918, 1921 and 1937, is a first-class answer.
