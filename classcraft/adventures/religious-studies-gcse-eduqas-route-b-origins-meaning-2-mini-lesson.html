Mission 1

Where animals stand

  • Animal rights: the view that animals have moral claims of their own, such as not to be made to suffer or killed for human benefit.
  • Animal welfare: animals may be used, but must be treated well and spared needless suffering.
  • Sentience: being able to feel pain and pleasure. Most people agree many animals are sentient.
  • Christianity, Islam and Judaism teach that humans have a special place (image of God; khalifah) but must care for animals as God's creatures.
  • Hinduism, Sikhism and Buddhism stress that all beings have a soul or can be reborn, so harming animals has spiritual consequences.

Christianity and animals

"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you." (Genesis 9:3)

"A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10)

  • Catechism of the Catholic Church 2415–2418 (paraphrase): animals are God's creatures, entrusted to human stewardship. Humans may use them for food and clothing. Medical and scientific experiments are acceptable within reasonable limits if they help to care for or save human lives. It is against human dignity to make animals suffer or die needlessly.

Show answer

Eating animals.

Q2 What limit does Proverbs 12:10 set?

Mission 1

Singer and speciesism

  • Peter Singer (born 1946), Australian philosopher, Animal Liberation (1975): a utilitarian (someone who judges actions by how much suffering and happiness they produce) who argues for equal consideration of interests.
  • What matters morally is the ability to suffer, not species. (Jeremy Bentham made this point in 1789.)
  • Speciesism (a term coined by Richard Ryder, 1970): favouring the interests of one's own species just because it is one's own, like racism or sexism.
  • Singer urges vegetarianism and opposes most experiments. An experiment is only justified if we would accept it on a human with similar capacities (paraphrase).
  • Singer also argues that not every human life is more valuable than every animal life: this challenges the sanctity of life (more in Mission 5).
  • Catholic responses: humans alone are made in the image of God (imago Dei) with a spiritual soul, so human life has a unique value; but cruelty to animals is still wrong (CCC 2418).

Mission 2

Theist, atheist, agnostic

  • Theist: believes in God (or gods).
  • Atheist: believes there is no God (some atheists say simply that they lack belief in God).
  • Agnostic: believes we cannot know whether God exists, or has not decided. The word was coined by T. H. Huxley (1825–1895) in 1869.
  • These are answers to one question: does God exist?
  • Humanism goes further: a whole non-religious approach to life. Most Humanists are atheists or agnostics.
Quick check
?Sam says: "There might be a God, there might not. I don't think anyone can know for sure." Which term best describes Sam?
Mission 2

What Humanists believe

  • Humanists see no good evidence for God or the supernatural; they understand the world through reason, evidence and science.
  • One life: there is no afterlife, so this life matters all the more.
  • Meaning is made, not given: people create purpose through relationships, work, learning and helping others.
  • Morality comes from people: reason, empathy and concern for human flourishing (wellbeing).
  • Humanists support human rights, equality and freedom of belief for everyone.
  • Groups: Humanists UK (Britain); Humanists International (Amsterdam Declaration, 2002).
Mission 2

How Humanists decide what is right

  • Ask: What are the consequences? Who is helped, who is harmed?
  • Empathy: treat others as you would want to be treated (the Golden Rule, found in many cultures).
  • Respect for choice: people should run their own lives if they harm no one else.
  • Examples: many Humanists support legal assisted dying, a woman's right to choose on abortion, and equal marriage.
  • Humanists hold non-religious ceremonies: weddings, naming ceremonies, funerals.
  • Humanists do not all agree: each person must think the issue through.

?A Humanist is deciding whether to give a kidney to a stranger. Which line of reasoning is most typically Humanist?
Mission 2

Soul and afterlife: non-religious views

  • Most atheists and Humanists see the mind as the activity of the brain. No separate soul survives death.
  • Death is the end of personal existence. Evidence: damage to the brain changes memory and personality.
  • People live on in other ways: memories, children, work and the good they have done; the body's matter returns to nature.
  • The ancient Greek Epicurus (341–270 BCE): death is nothing to us, because when death comes, we are no longer there to experience it (paraphrase).
  • Humanist funerals celebrate the person's life, without prayers or talk of an afterlife.
Mission 2Diversity

Is death the end?

  • Catholic: each person has an immortal soul; after death comes judgement and, at the end of time, the resurrection of the body. "I am the resurrection and the life." (John 11:25)
  • Jewish: many focus on this life and keeping God's commandments; there is belief in olam ha-ba (the world to come), with a range of views on what it is. Many Orthodox Jews also believe in the resurrection of the dead.
  • Humanist / atheist: death is the end of personal existence.
  • Agnostic: an open question.
Mission 2

Singer: speciesism

  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Australian philosopher; Animal Liberation (1975), Practical Ethics (1979).
  • Speciesism: favouring members of your own species just because they are human, as racism favours one race.
  • Singer says what matters is the ability to suffer and to have interests and plans, not species.
  • So he rejects the sanctity of life: human life is not sacred simply because it is human.
  • He argues that some killing (e.g. voluntary euthanasia) can be right, and that equal suffering counts equally, whatever the species.
  • Many Humanists do not share all his conclusions; Humanists UK does support legal assisted dying.
Mission 2Diversity

Catholic responses to Singer

  • Image of God: "God created man in his own image." (Genesis 1:27). Human dignity comes from God, not from abilities.
  • Sanctity of life: every human life is sacred from conception to natural death; no one may deliberately take innocent life.
  • Dignity does not depend on capacities: an unborn child, a person with dementia or in a coma has full human worth.
  • Animals matter, but differently: humans are stewards; cruelty to animals is wrong, but humans and animals are not equal in dignity (Catechism 2415–2418).
  • Shared ground: Catholics and Singer both oppose needless animal suffering and both care for the poor.
Mission 2Diversity

Shared values and disagreements

  • Shared: human dignity and rights; care for the vulnerable; honesty; justice; freedom of belief.
  • Disagree about: where morality comes from (God or humans); life after death; assisted dying and abortion; faith schools; bishops in the House of Lords.
  • Dialogue means understanding each other's reasons, not winning.
Mission 2Evaluate

"You can be a good person without believing in God."

  • Reasons for: many atheists and Humanists live honest, generous lives; empathy and reason can guide action; Paul says Gentiles without the Jewish law can show "the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15), and some Christians apply this to non-believers.
  • Reasons against: many believers argue that without God there is no objective standard of goodness (one that holds whatever anyone thinks); religion gives motivation, community and forgiveness; Catholic teaching sees moral law as rooted in God, even when people do not know it.
  • Another view: many religious people agree non-believers can act well, but say true goodness includes loving God.
  • Judge it by: What does "good" mean: acting well, or a relationship with God? Must a standard be objective to be real?
Mission check

?At a hospital ethics meeting, a Humanist and a Catholic both oppose a plan to cut care for elderly patients. Which statement best describes their positions?

Non-religious people build ethics on reason and empathy; they often reach the same actions as believers, for different reasons.

Mission 3: If the universe began with a Big Bang, is there still room for a Creator?

Make a guess: If the universe began with a Big Bang, is there still room for a Creator?

Mission 3

Two kinds of question

  • Science asks how: what happened, in what order, by what processes. It answers from evidence that can be tested.
  • Religion often asks why: is there a purpose? Who, if anyone, is behind it?
  • A creation account (creation story) is a sacred text about the beginning of the world and its meaning.
  • Many religious people say the two kinds of question fit together. Others think one side has to give way.
  • The key word is interpretation: how a sacred text is read decides whether it clashes with science.
Mission 3Read the source

Genesis 1

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)

"God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)

  • Genesis 1: God creates by speaking, over six days, then rests on the seventh (Genesis 2:1–3). Humans come last.
  • Genesis 2 (from 2:4): a second account. God forms the man from dust before plants and animals, then the woman.
  • Your spec compares the two: Genesis 1:1–2:3 (God transcendent, creating by word) and 2:4–24 (God close at hand, shaping the man and breathing life into him).

Q1 In Genesis 1, how does God create?

Show answer

By speaking: "God said… and there was…"

Q2 Which two things in Genesis 1:1 and 1:31 matter most for belief?

Show answer

God is the source of everything; what God made is good.

Mission 3

The Big Bang

  • Scientists' best account: about 13.8 billion years ago the universe was extremely hot and dense, and it has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  • Evidence: distant galaxies are moving away from us (red shift); the cosmic microwave background, faint radiation left over from the early universe.
  • Stars, then galaxies and planets, formed over billions of years. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago.
  • The theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, in 1927 and 1931.
  • Lemaître kept science and faith distinct: the theory describes the physical beginning; it neither proves nor disproves God.
  • Brian Cox (born 1968), physicist and broadcaster, explains the universe's history from the Big Bang using evidence alone; he does not appeal to God to explain it (paraphrase of his public view).

Mission 3Diversity

Christians read Genesis differently

  • Literal reading (creationists, e.g. some evangelical Christians): Genesis is accurate history; God made everything in six days; many say the earth is only thousands of years old (young earth). The Big Bang is rejected or reinterpreted.
  • Non-literal reading (most Catholics, Anglicans, Orthodox, many Protestants): Genesis is true about who and why, not a science report. The "days" can be stages or a poetic framework.
  • These Christians see the Big Bang as how God created: science and faith are compatible.
  • Shared belief: God is Creator; the universe depends on God and is good; humans have a special place in it.
  • Catholic and most other Christian teaching: God creates ex nihilo (out of nothing). Science studies how the universe develops; it cannot explain why there is something rather than nothing.
Mission 3Read the source

Augustine: out of nothing

Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions (c. 397–400), Book XII, chapter 7 (Pusey translation, 1838):

> "Thou createdst heaven and earth; not out of Thyself, for so should they have been equal to Thine Only Begotten Son … And aught else besides Thee was there not, whereof Thou mightest create them, … and therefore out of nothing didst Thou create heaven and earth"

  • Creation ex nihilo: God did not shape existing material, nor make the world from God's own being.
  • Augustine's reason: before creation there was nothing besides God to make it from.

Q1 Why could God not make the world "out of Thyself", for Augustine?

Show answer

Then heaven and earth would have been equal to the Son, and so to God, which nothing created can be.

Q2 Does the Big Bang clash with this?

Show answer

Many Catholics say no: the Big Bang describes a beginning; ex nihilo says where that beginning ultimately came from.

Mission 3

Does science count against God?

  • Atheists and Humanists (non-religious people who live by reason and human values) point out that the universe can be explained by science without God.
  • Argument: if physics can describe the universe from its earliest moments, a Creator is an unnecessary extra. Creation stories were early attempts to explain what science now explains.
  • Many add that a literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence.
  • Religious replies:
    • The Big Bang needs a cause: why is there a universe at all? (the First Cause argument)
    • The laws of physics are finely balanced for life; some see design in this.
    • Science explains how, not why: it cannot rule out a Creator.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Science has shown that God did not create the universe."

  • Reasons for: the Big Bang explains the universe's development by physical laws. A literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence. Explanations that need fewer causes are often preferred (no need for God).
  • Reasons against: science explains how the universe developed, not why there is anything at all. The Big Bang suggests a beginning, which fits belief in creation (Lemaître; creation ex nihilo). Many scientists are believers.
  • Augustine (Confessions XII, 7) adds that God made heaven and earth from nothing, not from God's own being.
  • Other views: Buddhists see the question as unimportant; many Hindus and Sikhs see the universe's vastness as fitting their scriptures.
  • Judge it by: Do science and religion answer the same question? Does a beginning need a cause? Does it depend on how scripture is read?
Mission check

?A science teacher who is a practising Catholic explains the Big Bang in class, then goes to Mass and says the Creed: "maker of heaven and earth". Which belief best explains why she sees no clash?

Whether science and religion clash depends largely on how sacred texts are read, and on which question each is answering.

Mission 4

Evolution in brief

  • Evolution: living things change over many generations; new species develop from earlier ones.
  • Natural selection: individuals with features that suit their environment survive and reproduce more, so those features spread.
  • "Survival of the fittest" (Herbert Spencer's phrase, later used by Darwin): "fittest" means best suited, not strongest.
  • Evidence: fossils, shared anatomy, and DNA showing all life is related.
  • Life on Earth began over 3.5 billion years ago. Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared about 300,000 years ago, sharing ancestors with other apes.
Mission 4Read the source

Darwin

Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859), closing sentence:

> "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one; … from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved."

  • Darwin also wrote The Descent of Man (1871), applying evolution to humans.
  • Later editions read "breathed by the Creator into a few forms". Darwin moved towards agnosticism in later life.

Q1 What does Darwin say all life began from?

Show answer

A few forms, or one.

Q2 Does this sentence attack religion?

Show answer

Not directly: it expresses wonder; but it explains the variety of life without special creation of each species.

Mission 4

Dawkins

  • Richard Dawkins (born 1941), biologist and atheist: The Selfish Gene (1976), The Blind Watchmaker (1986), The God Delusion (2006).
  • Natural selection explains living things that look designed without any designer: a "blind watchmaker".
  • So, he argues, there is no need for God to explain human origins, and belief in God is very probably mistaken.
  • Humans are not specially created; they are one product of the same process as all life.
  • Darwin and Dawkins are your spec's named non-religious views on evolution.

Made in God's image

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

  • Imago Dei (image of God): humans can reason, love, choose and relate to God.
  • Humans are made last in Genesis 1 and given responsibility for creation (Genesis 1:26–31).

Mission 4Diversity

Christian responses

  • Creationists (some evangelical and other Christians): God created each kind directly and Adam and Eve as the first humans. Human evolution from earlier species is rejected.
  • Theistic evolution (most Catholics, Anglicans, Orthodox and many Protestants): evolution is how God created life; God gives each human a soul.
  • Catholic teaching (Pope Pius XII, Humani Generis, 1950): the body may have evolved; the spiritual soul is created directly by God.
  • John Paul II, Message to the Pontifical Academy of Sciences (22 October 1996, paras 3–4): there is no opposition between evolution and faith, rightly understood; new knowledge shows evolution to be "more than a hypothesis" (short phrase; paraphrase).

Mission 4Evaluate

"Evolution and belief that God created humans cannot both be true."

  • Reasons for: a literal Genesis (six days, Adam first) conflicts with evolution. Natural selection explains apparent design without God (Dawkins). Evolution involves suffering and extinction, which some find hard to fit with a loving Creator.
  • Reasons against: many believers accept theistic evolution: God creates through natural processes. Catholic teaching: the body may evolve; the soul is God's direct creation (Pius XII, John Paul II). Genesis teaches meaning, not biology (many Christians, Reform Jews).
  • Other views: Buddhists see no conflict because they hold no creator; many Hindus and Sikhs see evolution as part of the divine order.
  • Judge it by: Must sacred texts be read literally? Can a process be both natural and God-given? Does evolution explain the soul and moral value?
Mission check

?A Catholic doctor studies the human genome and says: "Our DNA tells me how we came to be. My faith tells me who we are." Which belief is she expressing?

For many believers, evolution answers how humans came to be; belief in God and the soul answers who humans are and why they matter.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If every life is sacred, should anyone ever judge how good a life is?

Make a guess: If every life is sacred, should anyone ever judge how good a life is?

Mission 5

Sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: the belief that human life is holy (sacred) and has special value, because it is given by God.
  • So life must be protected and respected from its beginning to its natural end.
  • Many believers add: only God may decide when a life begins and ends. "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away." (Job 1:21)
  • The value is intrinsic: it does not depend on health, ability, age or usefulness.
  • Non-religious people may also say every human life has great value, but not because it is God-given.
Mission 5Read the source

Made by God

"God created man in his own image." (Genesis 1:27)

"Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Before you were born, I sanctified you." (Jeremiah 1:5)

"Don't you know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God? You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19)

Q1 Which verse says God knows a person before birth?

Show answer

Jeremiah 1:5.

Q2 Why would "you are not your own" matter for decisions about life and death?

Show answer

Life belongs to God, so it is not simply ours to end.

Mission 5

Quality of life

  • Quality of life: how good a person's life is: their health, freedom from pain, independence, relationships, ability to enjoy life.
  • Doctors use it when choosing treatments; patients use it when deciding what they want.
  • Arguments based on quality of life say that a life's value can depend on what it is like for the person living it.
  • Questions: who decides? Can a life with great suffering still be worth living? Can quality of life be measured?
  • Many believers use both ideas: life is sacred, and suffering should be relieved (e.g. by good medical care).

Mission 5

Imago Dei and Catherine of Siena

  • Imago Dei: humans are made in the image of God, with reason, free will, and the capacity to love and know God.
  • St Catherine of Siena (1347–1380), The Dialogue (c. 1378), in the section on discretion: she asks God why he gave humans such dignity, and answers that it was out of his immeasurable love; God saw his creature in himself and fell in love with her (paraphrase).
  • So human dignity comes from being loved and created by God, not from what a person can do.
  • Catholic teaching: every human life is sacred from conception to natural death (Pope John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae, 1995, paraphrase).
Mission 5Diversity

Christian views

  • Strong sanctity of life (Catholic teaching; many evangelical and fundamentalist Christians, who read the Bible as literally true in all it teaches): innocent human life must never be deliberately ended, whatever its quality.
  • Weaker sanctity of life (many liberal Protestants, some Anglicans): life is God's gift, but love (agape) and compassion can mean that quality of life matters in hard cases.
  • All agree that the sick, disabled and dying are equally valuable, and that suffering should be relieved (hospices, medical care).
  • Fundamentalist Christians often oppose abortion in all cases, using Jeremiah 1:5; Catholic teaching reaches a similar conclusion through Scripture, Tradition and natural law.
Mission 5

Non-religious views and Singer

  • Humanists: human life is valuable because it is the only life we have; there is no God and no afterlife. Value comes from human experience, relationships and choices.
  • So quality of life and personal autonomy (the right to make decisions about your own life) matter greatly.
  • Peter Singer (born 1946), Practical Ethics (1979), Rethinking Life and Death (1994): the traditional sanctity of life gives value to human life just because it is human. He calls this speciesism.
  • Singer argues that the capacity to suffer, and to be self-aware and plan a future, are what matter morally; so some animals have more of these than some humans (paraphrase).
  • Catholic responses: every human, at any stage or ability, is made in God's image and loved by God; value does not depend on capacities. Basing worth on abilities would put the weakest at risk.
Quick check
?Which response would most Catholics give to Peter Singer's critique of the sanctity of life?
Mission 5Evaluate

"The sanctity of life matters more than the quality of life."

  • Reasons for: life is God's gift (Job 1:21; 1 Corinthians 6:19). Judging lives by quality risks treating disabled, sick or old people as less valuable. Quality is hard to measure and can change.
  • Reasons against: compassion (agape; karuna, Buddhist compassion) means suffering matters.
  • Humanists stress autonomy: people should judge their own lives. Singer: the sanctity doctrine is speciesist and ignores suffering.
  • Middle positions: many believers hold both: life is sacred, so it is never ended deliberately, but quality of life guides care (e.g. refusing burdensome treatment).
  • Judge it by: Is value given by God or by experience? Who decides? Can the two ideas be held together?
Mission check

?A hospital chaplain visits a young man badly injured in a rugby accident. He says: "My life is worth nothing now." Which reply best reflects a sanctity-of-life view?

Sanctity of life says a life's worth is given, not earned; quality of life asks what the life is like for the person living it.

Mission 6

Abortion and the law

  • Abortion: the deliberate ending of a pregnancy so that it does not result in the birth of a child. (A miscarriage is the natural loss of a pregnancy.)
  • Abortion Act 1967 (England, Wales, Scotland): legal if two doctors agree it is needed on set grounds, such as risk to the woman's physical or mental health.
  • Since 1990 the limit for most grounds is 24 weeks. No limit if the woman's life is at risk, to prevent grave permanent injury, or for serious fetal abnormality.
  • Since the Crime and Policing Act 2026, a woman in England and Wales cannot be prosecuted for ending her own pregnancy; the 1967 Act's rules (two doctors, 24 weeks) still govern how abortions are provided.
  • Laws differ worldwide: from a total ban (e.g. Malta allows it only to save the woman's life) to abortion on request.
Mission 6

When does a life begin?

  • Conception (fertilisation): egg and sperm join to form a genetically unique embryo. Many pro-life believers say a human life with full status starts here.
  • Ensoulment: when the soul enters the body. Some Muslim teaching places this at 120 days; the Catholic Church treats the embryo as a person from conception.
  • Viability: when the fetus could survive outside the womb (around 24 weeks with medical help).
  • Birth: in Jewish law, the fetus becomes a full person once it is born.

Mission 6

Pro-life and pro-choice

  • Pro-life: the unborn child has a right to life; abortion is wrong (some allow exceptions, e.g. to save the mother's life). Groups: SPUC (Society for the Protection of Unborn Children), LIFE.
  • Arguments: sanctity of life; the fetus is a human being from conception; disabled people's lives are equally valuable; alternatives exist.
  • Pro-choice: a woman has the right to decide what happens to her body. Groups: BPAS (British Pregnancy Advisory Service), Abortion Rights, Humanists UK.
  • Arguments: autonomy (personal choice); quality of life of the woman and the child; safe legal abortion prevents dangerous illegal ones.
Mission 6Read the source

Before birth

"For you formed my inmost being. You knit me together in my mother's womb." (Psalm 139:13)

"Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you." (Jeremiah 1:5)

"When Elizabeth heard Mary's greeting, the baby leapt in her womb" (Luke 1:41)

  • Some Christians point to Exodus 21:22–23: if a pregnant woman is hurt and "no harm follows", there is a fine; serious harm is "life for life". Older translations read the verse as a miscarriage, so some argue the fetus is valued but not equal to the mother; the WEBBE reads "gives birth prematurely", which others take to protect both lives equally.

Q1 What do these three passages suggest about the unborn?

Show answer

God knows and forms them before birth; the unborn child responds to Mary's greeting.

Q2 Do they directly mention abortion?

Show answer

No: they are applied to it by interpretation, which is why Christians disagree.

Mission 6Diversity

Christian views

  • Catholic teaching: life begins at conception; direct abortion is always gravely wrong (CCC 2270–2272).
  • Mother's life at risk: Catholic teaching allows life-saving treatment (e.g. removing a cancerous womb, or the damaged tube in an ectopic pregnancy) even if the unborn child dies as a foreseen but unintended result: the principle of double effect.
  • Fundamentalist Christians (who read the Bible as literally true in all it teaches) mostly oppose abortion in all or almost all cases, using Psalm 139 and Jeremiah 1:5.
  • Church of England: strongly opposed in principle, but accepts strictly limited cases where abortion may be the lesser evil (e.g. risk to the mother's life, serious abnormality).
  • Some liberal Protestants (e.g. many Methodists and Quakers) accept abortion more widely, out of compassion and respect for conscience.

Mission 6

Non-religious arguments

  • Humanists: most support the right to choose; the woman's autonomy and wellbeing matter most; an early fetus is not yet a person who can feel or have interests.
  • Many Humanists accept limits later in pregnancy, as the fetus develops.
  • Utilitarianism: weigh the happiness and suffering of everyone affected (woman, child, family); abortion may be right if it leads to less suffering.
  • Some non-religious people are pro-life: they argue the fetus is a human being, and killing it is wrong whatever one's religion.
  • Religious replies: the unborn's value does not depend on what it can do (sanctity of life); utilitarian sums can overlook the weakest.
Mission check

?A Catholic and a member of the Church of England are asked about a 30-year-old woman who will die unless her pregnancy is ended. Both say treatment to save her life is acceptable. Which shared belief best explains this?

Disagreement about abortion comes mainly from different answers to two questions: when does a person begin, and whose rights come first?

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Animal welfare allows careful use; animal rights opposes using animals as resources. UK law controls experiments (1986 Act, the 3Rs).
  • Christians, Muslims and Jews generally allow necessary experiments and eating meat, with duties of kindness; many Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs are vegetarian because of ahimsa, rebirth and compassion.
  • Peter Singer (utilitarian) argues speciesism is like racism: equal suffering counts equally.

Mission 2

  • Theist: believes in God; atheist: believes there is no God; agnostic: cannot know.
  • Humanists: no God, one life, ethics from reason, empathy and human flourishing.
  • Non-religious view: death is the end of personal existence.
  • Singer's speciesism challenges the sanctity of life; Catholics answer with the image of God.

Mission 3

  • Creation accounts (Genesis 1–2, the Qur'an, Hindu scriptures) say the universe has a source and meaning; Buddhists have no creation story, and Sikh scripture describes creation without a step-by-step account.
  • The Big Bang (first proposed by Lemaître, a Catholic priest) is accepted by many believers as how creation happened; literalists reject it where it conflicts with scripture.
  • Atheists and Humanists say science explains the universe without God; believers reply that science answers how, not why.

Mission 4

  • Evolution by natural selection (Darwin, 1859) explains how species, including humans, developed; Dawkins argues it removes the need for God.
  • Creationists reject human evolution; most Christians accept theistic evolution, and Catholic teaching says the soul is created directly by God (Pius XII, 1950; John Paul II, 1996).
  • Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs and Buddhists respond in different ways; for many there is no conflict.

Mission 5

  • Sanctity of life: life is holy and God-given (imago Dei; Job 1:21); quality of life: how good a life is for the person.
  • Christians differ: strong sanctity (Catholic and many fundamentalist Christians) or weaker sanctity, where agape lets quality of life count in hard cases.
  • Humanists and Singer stress quality of life, autonomy and suffering; Catholics reply that value never depends on abilities.

Mission 6

  • UK law: Abortion Act 1967, 24 weeks for most grounds, no limit when the woman's life is at risk; since 2026 women in England and Wales cannot be prosecuted for ending their own pregnancy.
  • Catholic teaching: life from conception; direct abortion always wrong; double effect when the mother's life is at risk. The Church of England accepts strictly limited cases.
  • Pro-life (sanctity) vs pro-choice (autonomy, quality of life); Humanists mostly pro-choice; situation ethics and utilitarianism weigh consequences.
