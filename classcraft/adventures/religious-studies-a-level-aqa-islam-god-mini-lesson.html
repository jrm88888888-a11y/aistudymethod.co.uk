Mission 1

Mission 1: Muslims say nothing is like God, yet call God by ninety-nine names. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Muslims say nothing is like God, yet call God by ninety-nine names. How can both be true?

Mission 1

Tawhid: God is One

  • Tawhid ("making one"): God is one and unique, in essence, in attributes and in actions.
  • Sole Creator and Sustainer: everything exists, and keeps existing, by God's will.
  • Omnipotent: "Allah has power over all things" (Qur'an 35:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Omniscient: "Not a leaf doth fall but with His knowledge" (Qur'an 6:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • Uncompromising: no partner, child, rival or equal; God alone is worshipped.
  • Later Sunni teaching (especially Ibn Taymiyya, 1263–1328) often divides tawhid into three: oneness of lordship, of worship, and of names and attributes. Ash'ari teaching frames it differently; the scheme is not universal.
  • Your spec: God as "the omnipotent creator and controller of all things".
Mission 1Read the source

Surah 112, al-Ikhlas

> "Say: He is Allah, the One and Only; Allah, the Eternal, Absolute; He begetteth not, nor is He begotten; And there is none like unto Him." (Qur'an 112:1–4, Yusuf Ali)

  • Al-Ikhlas means "sincerity" or "purity" (of faith).
  • A hadith says it equals a third of the Qur'an (Sahih al-Bukhari 5013, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Beyond us, yet near

  • Transcendent and indescribable: "there is nothing whatever like unto Him" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali). Human words fall short.
  • So Muslim theologians practise tanzih: declaring God free of every likeness to creatures.
  • Immanent: "We are nearer to him than (his) jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Yusuf Ali). God hears every prayer (2:186).
  • Not two gods or two moods: one God, beyond creation in being, close to it in knowledge, power and mercy.
Mission 1Read the source

The Throne verse (Ayat al-Kursi)

> "Allah! There is no god but He,-the Living, the Self-subsisting, Eternal. No slumber can seize Him nor sleep." (Qur'an 2:255, Yusuf Ali)

  • The verse goes on: all in the heavens and earth belong to God; none intercedes without His permission; He knows all that is before and behind people; His Throne (kursi) extends over heavens and earth; guarding them never tires Him (paraphrase).
  • Use: recited after salah, before sleep, and for protection; often hung in homes.

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 1

Shirk: the opposite of tawhid

  • Shirk ("association"): giving God a partner, or giving to anything what belongs only to God.
  • "Allah forgiveth not that partners should be set up with Him" (Qur'an 4:48, Yusuf Ali): the one sin not forgiven if a person dies without repenting.
  • Major shirk: worshipping idols, saints or angels; saying God has a son (112:3).
  • Minor shirk: doing good deeds to be seen (riya'), named by the Prophet as a hidden shirk (Musnad Ahmad, paraphrase).
  • Many Muslims also warn of shirk in modern forms: living as if money, fame or the self were the ultimate goal.
Mission 1

The Merciful, the Compassionate

  • 113 of the 114 surahs begin: "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Al-Rahman and al-Rahim both come from r-h-m, the root of "womb": care that gives and sustains life.
  • Many scholars explain al-Rahman as mercy to all creation, al-Rahim as mercy especially to believers.
  • "My mercy extendeth to all things" (Qur'an 7:156, Yusuf Ali); a hadith qudsi: God's mercy prevails over His anger (Sahih al-Bukhari 7404, paraphrase).
  • Significance: Muslims begin meals, journeys and work with the Bismillah; they never despair of forgiveness; they show mercy to receive mercy.
  • Mercy does not cancel justice: God is also Judge. Holding both is part of tawhid.
Mission 1

The 99 Beautiful Names

  • "The most beautiful names belong to Allah: so call on him by them" (Qur'an 7:180, Yusuf Ali). See also 59:22–24.
  • A hadith says God has ninety-nine names, and whoever learns them by heart enters Paradise (Sahih al-Bukhari 2736, paraphrase). Lists vary; the best-known is in al-Tirmidhi.
  • Names of beauty (jamal): al-Rahman, al-Wadud (the Loving). Names of majesty (jalal): al-Aziz (the Mighty), al-Jabbar (the Compeller).
  • Practice: dhikr on prayer beads (subha, often 33 or 99 beads); naming children 'Abd al-Rahman ("servant of the Merciful").
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), The Best Means in Explaining the Beautiful Names of God: believers should reflect the names within human limits: mercy, patience, justice.

Mission 1Evaluate

"The Islamic concept of Allah is coherent."

  • Tension 1: transcendent and indescribable, yet near and named. Reply: the names describe God's relation to creation, not God's essence; nearness is of knowledge and care, not of place.
  • Tension 2: all-merciful, yet a Judge who punishes. Reply: mercy and justice are both perfections; 7:156 places mercy first; justice gives mercy meaning.
  • Tension 3: omnipotent, yet just. Replies differ: Ash'aris (the main Sunni school of theology) say God's will defines justice; the Mu'tazila (an early rationalist school) and Shi'a theologians say God freely always does what is just.
  • Critics: a God beyond all description can say nothing to us; or the names make God too human (see Mission 2).
  • Judge it by: Is "coherent" the same as "fully understood"? Do the traditions' replies avoid contradiction or only relabel it?

Mission 2: The Qur'an says nothing is like God, yet speaks of God's hand and face. How do Muslims read it?

Make a guess: The Qur'an says nothing is like God, yet speaks of God's hand and face. How do Muslims read it?

Mission 2

The puzzle

  • The Qur'an insists: "there is nothing whatever like unto Him" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali).
  • Yet it speaks of God's hand(s), face, eyes, and of God being established on the Throne.
  • These are anthropomorphic descriptions: speaking of God in human terms.
  • Two errors every school wanted to avoid:
  • Tashbih (likening): treating God as having a body like creatures.
  • Ta'til (stripping): emptying God of the attributes the Qur'an gives.
  • AQA spellings: Mutazili, Ashari, Hanbali.
Mission 2Read the source

Hand and face

> "(Allah) said: 'O Iblis! What prevents thee from prostrating thyself to one whom I have created with my hands?'" (Qur'an 38:75, Yusuf Ali)

> "But will abide (for ever) the Face of thy Lord,- full of Majesty, Bounty and Honour." (Qur'an 55:27, Yusuf Ali)

  • Also: "both His hands are widely outstretched" in generosity (5:64); "the Hand of Allah is over their hands" at a pledge of loyalty (48:10); "(Allah) Most Gracious is firmly established on the throne" (20:5).

Q1 In 55:26–27, what is the face of God contrasted with?

Show answer

Q2 What does 38:75 suggest about Adam's creation?

Show answer

Mission 2

Clear verses and ambiguous verses

  • 3:7 divides the Qur'an into muhkamat (clear, decisive verses), "the foundation of the Book", and mutashabihat (ambiguous, allegorical ones).
  • It warns against those who chase the ambiguous verses "seeking discord".
  • Key dispute: the verse says no one knows their hidden meanings (ta'wil) "except Allah". Does the next phrase, "those who are firmly grounded in knowledge", begin a new sentence (only God knows) or continue it (God and the learned know)?
  • Reading 1 supports leaving the meaning to God. Reading 2 supports interpreting the verses by reason.
  • All three schools treat 42:11 as a clear verse that governs how the others are read; they disagree on what follows from it.
Mission 2

The Mu'tazila: interpret by reason

  • Rationalist theologians, 8th–10th centuries; traced to Wasil ibn 'Ata (d. 748); later systematised by Qadi 'Abd al-Jabbar (d. 1025).
  • First of their five principles: tawhid. A God with a body, or with eternal attributes distinct from Himself, would not be truly one.
  • So they practise ta'wil (figurative interpretation): hand = power or grace; face = God's own self (so 55:27 is read as: God Himself remains); established on the Throne = rules over creation.
  • Reason is a God-given test: a reading that makes God bodily must be wrong, since reason and the clear verses rule it out.
  • Also held that the Qur'an is created (not eternal). Caliph al-Ma'mun enforced this in the Mihna (inquisition), from 833.
Mission 2

The Hanbalis: affirm, without asking how

  • Followers of Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), who was imprisoned and flogged in the Mihna for refusing to call the Qur'an created.
  • Affirm the hand and face as real attributes of God because God affirms them; do not explain them away.
  • But they are not body parts: God's hand is not like a creature's hand (42:11).
  • Formula: bi-la kayfa, "without (asking) how". A saying attributed to Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) on the Throne: its meaning is known, its "how" is unknown, belief in it is required, and asking about it is innovation (paraphrase).
  • Later Hanbalis differ: many leave the meaning itself to God (tafwid); Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) held the meaning is known, only the "how" is unknown.
  • Critics called them anthropomorphists; Hanbalis reject tashbih as firmly as their critics.

Mission 2

The Ash'aris: a middle way

  • Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (873/4–935/6) was a Mu'tazili until about 912, then turned against them.
  • Uses reason (kalam) like the Mu'tazila, but to defend the creed of the Hadith scholars.
  • In al-Ibana: God has a hand and a face as attributes, bi-la kayfa; not power, not a body.
  • Attributes are "neither God nor other than God": real, eternal, yet not separate gods.
  • Later Ash'aris (al-Juwayni, d. 1085; al-Ghazali; Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, d. 1210) allowed either tafwid (leave the meaning to God) or ta'wil (interpret, e.g. hand as power) when the literal sense would imply a body.
  • The Ash'ari school became the most widespread Sunni theology, alongside the Maturidi.
Mission 2Diversity

Three readings side by side

  • Hand (38:75): Mu'tazili: power, grace. Hanbali: a real attribute, not a limb, how unknown. Ash'ari: an attribute bi-la kayfa; later, also "power" by ta'wil.
  • Face (55:27): Mu'tazili: God's self. Hanbali: a real attribute. Ash'ari: an attribute, or God's self.
  • Method: Mu'tazili: reason governs reading. Hanbali: text governs; reason may not reinterpret. Ash'ari: text first, reason to defend and sometimes interpret.
  • Main fear: Mu'tazili: tashbih. Hanbali: ta'til. Ash'ari: both.
  • Today, the debate continues: e.g. Salafi teachers broadly follow Ibn Taymiyya; many seminaries (e.g. al-Azhar) teach Ash'ari and Maturidi theology.

Mission 2Evaluate

"The Mu'tazili approach best protects tawhid."

  • For: ta'wil removes every hint of a body, so God is clearly one and incomparable (42:11); reason is honoured as God-given.
  • Against: it can slide into ta'til: if "hand" just means "power", why did God choose the word? Reason is put above revelation; 3:7 may mean only God knows these meanings.
  • For the Hanbali view: most faithful to the text and the early community. Against: "without how" may avoid the question; critics see a risk of tashbih.
  • For the Ash'ari view: keeps both text and reason; avoids both errors. Against: inconsistent: who decides which verses to interpret?
  • Judge it by: Which approach best fits both 42:11 and 38:75? What should guide reading: the text, reason, or the early community? Is "protecting tawhid" the only goal, or also faithfulness to revelation?

Mission 3: A Sufi cried out "I am the Real" and was executed. What did he mean, and what do Sufis say of God?

Make a guess: A Sufi cried out "I am the Real" and was executed. What did he mean, and what do Sufis say of God?

Mission 3

God beyond comparison

  • Sufis begin from Tawhid: God is One, and nothing is like Him.
  • Incomparable (tanzih): "Naught is as His likeness; and He is the Hearer, the Seer." (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall)
  • God's essence (dhat) is unknowable in Himself: no mind can grasp it.
  • Yet the same verse also names God as Hearer and Seer: God is known through His names and signs in creation and the self (41:53).
  • Ibn 'Arabi (1165–1240) taught that both are needed: incomparability (tanzih) and likeness (tashbih) together give true knowledge of God.
Mission 3

Creation as overflowing light; God within

  • A saying Sufis love, "I was a hidden treasure and loved to be known, so I created creation" (paraphrase; a hadith qudsi not found in the main collections).
  • So creation is God's self-disclosure (tajalli): God's names shown in the world, like light shining from the sun.
  • Emanation: creation flows from God as light overflows from its source. The idea came through Muslim philosophers (al-Farabi, Ibn Sina) who drew on Neoplatonism. Theologians, al-Ghazali among them, insisted that creation is God's free act, not a necessary overflow.
  • Things have no being of their own: they borrow it from God, moment by moment.
  • Immanent: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall); "whithersoever ye turn, there is Allah's Countenance" (Qur'an 2:115, Pickthall).
  • Debate: Ibn 'Arabi's followers spoke of the "oneness of being" (wahdat al-wujud). Critics such as Ibn Taymiyya called this blurring God and the world; Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624) taught instead the "oneness of witnessing" (wahdat al-shuhud): the oneness is in the mystic's vision, not in being.

The verse of Light

> "Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The similitude of His light is as a niche wherein is a lamp. The lamp is in a glass. The glass is as it were a shining star. (This lamp is) kindled from a blessed tree, an olive neither of the East nor of the West, whose oil would almost glow forth (of itself) though no fire touched it. Light upon light." (Qur'an 24:35, Pickthall)

Q1 List the five images in the parable.

Show answer

Niche, lamp, glass, blessed olive tree, oil.

Q2 Which phrase suggests that the light is already present before any fire touches it?

Show answer

"whose oil would almost glow forth (of itself) though no fire touched it".

Mission 3Diversity

Reading the Light

  • Early commentary (attributed to Ibn 'Abbas and Ubayy ibn Ka'b): God is the guide of all in heaven and earth; the lamp is the light of faith in the believer's heart.
  • Al-Ghazali, The Niche of Lights (Mishkat al-Anwar, c. 1106): God is the only true Light; every other light is borrowed. He maps the images onto five levels of the human spirit: niche = senses; glass = imagination; lamp = intellect; tree = reflective reason; oil = the holy prophetic spirit, which "almost glows" by itself.
  • Many Sufi commentators: niche = the breast; glass = the purified heart; lamp = the inmost secret where God's light shines; oil = the natural disposition to know God (fitra).
  • "Light upon light": for Sufis, the light of revelation meeting the light of a purified heart.
  • Common thread: God is not a light among lights, but the Light by which all else is seen.

Mission 3

Fana: passing away in God

  • Fana' ("passing away", annihilation): the self's awareness of itself dissolves, until only God is in view.
  • Qur'anic root: "Everyone that is thereon will pass away; There remaineth but the Countenance of thy Lord" (Qur'an 55:26–27, Pickthall).
  • A hadith qudsi (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Softening of Hearts, no. 6502): God draws near to the servant who draws near through extra worship, until God becomes "the hearing with which he hears, the sight with which he sees" (paraphrase).
  • What fana is not: the soul does not become God (ittihad), and God does not move into a body (hulul). Mainstream Sufis rejected both.
  • Sober Sufis such as al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910) stress what follows: after fana comes baqa' ("remaining"): the self returns, transformed, to live in God and keep the law.
  • This is the sense in which Sufis speak of the soul as one with God: oneness of awareness, not of essence.
Mission 3

Drunken Sufism: ecstatic sayings

  • Drunken (sukr, intoxication) Sufism stresses being overwhelmed by God in fana.
  • In that state some spoke shatahat (ecstatic utterances) that sound like claims to divinity:
  • Abu Yazid al-Bistami (d. c. 874): "Glory be to me! How great is my majesty!" (paraphrase of the Arabic subhani).
  • Al-Hallaj (c. 858–922): "I am the Real" (Ana al-Haqq; al-Haqq is a name of God). Executed in Baghdad in 922: in Sufi tradition, for these words; historians also point to political charges.
  • Readings: (1) blasphemy, as his accusers and later critics held; (2) the human "I" had passed away, so God was speaking: as glowing iron in a fire might say "I am fire" (a classic Sufi image); (3) words of the drunk: excused, but not to be repeated (al-Junayd, al-Ghazali).
Mission 3Evaluate

Can the soul be one with God without denying Tawhid?

  • Claim: Sufi teaching that the soul becomes one with God is compatible with Tawhid.
  • For: oneness in fana is of awareness, not essence; mainstream Sufis reject hulul and ittihad.
  • For: Qur'anic support: 50:16 (nearness), 55:26–27 (all passes but God's Face), the hadith qudsi of the servant God loves.
  • Against: sayings such as al-Hallaj's blur Creator and creature; his accusers and later critics (Ibn Taymiyya) judged them unbelief.
  • Against: "oneness of being" can seem to deny the real difference between God and the world.
  • Judgement criteria: Are Sufi words to be read literally or as language of experience? Who interprets: jurists, theologians, Sufi masters? Does sober Sufism answer the objection?
  • Skill tip: in an AO2 answer, compare two readings of "oneness" before you judge.

Mission check

?After years of dhikr, a Sufi describes a moment in prayer: "I was not there. There was only God. Then I came back, and I was more myself than ever, and went to work." Which pair of terms fits her two moments?

Sufis describe union with God as the self's vanishing from its own view, followed by a return to life in God, while God remains beyond comparison.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Tawhid: the Muslim belief that God is one in essence, attributes and acts; Surah 112 and the Throne verse (2:255) express it; shirk is its opposite.
  • In Muslim teaching God is transcendent (42:11) yet near (50:16); al-Rahman and al-Rahim stand at the head of the Qur'an, and the 99 Names show how the One God relates to creation.

Mission 2

  • The Qur'an speaks of God's hand and face, yet says nothing is like God (42:11); schools steer between tashbih (likening) and ta'til (stripping).
  • Mu'tazila: ta'wil by reason (hand = power, face = God's self). Hanbalis: affirm as real attributes, bi-la kayfa. Ash'aris: affirm bi-la kayfa; later also tafwid or ta'wil.
  • 3:7 (clear and ambiguous verses) lies behind the dispute about who can know the meaning.

Mission 3

  • God is incomparable and unknowable in essence, yet shows Himself in His names and in creation; God is nearer than the jugular vein.
  • The verse of Light (24:35): for al-Ghazali, God alone is true Light; the heart, purified, receives it.
  • Fana' (passing away) and baqa' (remaining) describe the soul's oneness with God in awareness, not essence.
