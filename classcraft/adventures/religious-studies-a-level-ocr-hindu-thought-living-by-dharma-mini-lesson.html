Mission 1

Virtue and two kinds of dharma

  • A virtue is a settled quality of character that shows itself in action.
  • Hindu texts set out two levels of duty:
  • Sadharana dharma: duties common to everyone (non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, self-control). Manusmriti 10.63 gives a list like this for all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • Varnashrama dharma (svadharma): duties of your class and stage of life.
  • The three virtues in this mission belong to the first level: non-violence, detachment, self-restraint.
  • Each is thought to purify the mind and so lead towards moksha (liberation).
Mission 1

Non-violence (ahimsa)

  • Ahimsa: a- (not) + himsa (harm): not harming any living being in thought, word or deed.
  • Basis: the same atman (self) is present in every living being, so harming another is harming life that is one with yours.
  • Karma: Hindus believe harm done returns as suffering in this or a later life.
  • Its positive side: compassion (daya) and kindness to all.
  • Many Hindus apply it to diet: vegetarianism, and avoiding beef in particular.
  • It is weighed against other duties: a warrior may still have to fight (see Mission 3).
Mission 1

Detachment (vairagya)

  • Vairagya: freedom from craving for objects, pleasure and results.
  • Not indifference and not laziness: the Gita's ideal is to act fully while letting go of the fruits (nishkama karma, desireless action).
  • Tyaga: renouncing the fruit of action (Gita 18). Sannyasa: renouncing the world itself, the last ashrama.
  • Why it matters: desire for results binds the self to karma and rebirth.
  • Gita 2:47 (Arnold): "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."
Mission 1

Self-restraint (dama)

  • Dama or indriya-nigraha: control of the senses and appetites.
  • Image in Gita 2:58: the wise person draws back the senses from their objects "as the wise tortoise draws its four feet safe / Under its shield" (Arnold).
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad V.2: the creator's one syllable "Da" is heard by gods, humans and demons as three lessons: restrain yourselves, give, be compassionate (paraphrase).
  • Practised as fasting, celibacy (brahmacharya) in the student stage, moderation in food and speech.
  • Self-restraint makes the other two virtues possible: anger restrained becomes non-violence; desire restrained becomes detachment.

Divine qualities

Bhagavad Gita 16:1–3 (Edwin Arnold, 1885), Krishna speaking:

> "… opened hand / And governed appetites; and piety, / And love of lonely study; humbleness, / Uprightness, heed to injure nought which lives, / Truthfulness, slowness unto wrath, a mind / That lightly letteth go what others prize …" (Bhagavad Gita 16:1–3, Arnold)

  • Krishna calls this list the divine endowment (daivi sampad): "The Heavenly Birth brings to deliverance" (16:5, Arnold).

Mission 1

The three gunas

  • Prakriti (matter, nature, including mind) is woven from three gunas (strands, qualities). The idea comes from Samkhya philosophy.
  • Sattva: light, purity, calm, goodness, knowledge.
  • Rajas: passion, energy, desire, restlessness.
  • Tamas: darkness, inertia, dullness, delusion.
  • All three are present in everything and everyone; their balance shifts and shapes character.
  • They belong to prakriti, not to the atman, which is beyond them.

> "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature, 'Soothfastness,' / 'Passion,' and 'Ignorance.' These three bind down / The changeless Spirit in the changeful flesh." (Bhagavad Gita 14:5, Arnold)

Mission 1

The virtues of each guna

The Gita sorts food, worship and action by guna (chapters 14, 17, 18):

SattvaRajasTamas
Qualitiescalm, knowledge, contentment, truthfulnessgreed, ambition, restless activitysloth, confusion, carelessness, cruelty
Food (17:8–10)fresh, mild, nourishingvery spicy, salty, sour, heatingstale, left overnight, rotten
Action (18:23–25)done as duty, without craving the resultdone for gain or show, with great straindone in delusion, careless of harm to others
  • So the three virtues are sattvic: they grow as sattva grows.
  • Many Hindus choose a sattvic diet (often vegetarian, no onion or garlic for some) to support a calm mind.

Mission 1Read the source

As you act, so you become

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV.4.5–6 (paraphrase):

  • As a person acts and behaves, so they become: good action makes one good; bad action makes one bad.
  • A person is made of desire: as the desire, so the will; as the will, so the deed; as the deed, so the result they reap.
  • One who still desires is bound to return to the world of action.
  • One who is free from desire, whose only desire is the Self, does not return: being Brahman, they go to Brahman.

Q1 Why does this passage make detachment central to liberation?

Show answer

Desire drives will and deed, and so rebirth; only one free of desire is liberated.

Q2 What link does it make between virtue and karma?

Show answer

Character is built by actions: what we repeatedly do, we become.

Mission 1

Virtues, varna, ashrama and the aims of life

  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90) describes the four varnas coming from the cosmic person's mouth, arms, thighs and feet.
  • The Gita links varna to the gunas: duties are "fixed by reason of the Qualities / Planted in each" (18:41, Arnold). A Brahmin's virtues are "serenity, / Self-mastery, religion, purity" (18:42).
  • Ashramas train the virtues in turn: student (self-restraint, celibacy) → householder (non-violence, generosity, wealth and pleasure within dharma) → forest-dweller (loosening ties) → sannyasi (complete detachment).
  • With the purusharthas: virtues keep artha (wealth) and kama (pleasure) within dharma, and prepare for moksha.
  • Many modern Hindus read guna-based varna as character, not birth; critics note it was used to justify birth-based caste.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is virtue the way to liberation?

  • Claim: Living virtuously is enough to achieve moksha.
  • For: Virtue purifies the mind; the "divine endowment" leads to deliverance (Gita 16:5).
  • For: Brihadaranyaka IV.4.6: the one free of desire is not reborn.
  • Against: Even sattva binds, by attachment to happiness and knowledge (Gita 14:6). The liberated person is gunatita, beyond all three gunas (14:22–25).
  • Against: Advaita Vedanta (the non-dualist school of Shankara): only knowledge of atman as Brahman liberates; virtue only prepares. Many bhakti (devotional) traditions: liberation comes by God's grace, not merit.
  • Judgement criteria: What does each school mean by moksha? Is virtue a cause of liberation or a sign of it? Can a person be detached while still doing worldly duty?

Mission 2

What ahimsa asks

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, non-harming (a- "not" + himsa "harm").
  • It covers thought, word and deed: hatred and cruel speech are himsa too.
  • It also covers harm you cause or approve through others.
  • Why: the same atman is in every living being.

> "… sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living, and all living things / In that Life-Soul contained." (Bhagavad Gita 6:29, Arnold)

  • Karma: harm returns as suffering, in this life or the next.
  • Positive side: compassion and respect for all life.
Mission 2

Ahimsa in Hindu tradition

  • Chandogya Upanishad 3.17.4: non-violence is listed with austerity, generosity, uprightness and truthfulness as the "gifts" of a life lived as sacrifice (paraphrase).
  • Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva): ahimsa is called the highest dharma (ahimsa paramo dharmah).
  • Yoga Sutras 2.30–2.35: ahimsa is the first yama (restraint); where it is firmly established, hostility ceases (paraphrase).
  • Manusmrti 10.63: ahimsa is among the duties common to all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • But Hindu ahimsa is usually weighed against other duties: sacrifice in the Vedic period, the warrior's duty, self-defence. It means minimising harm rather than an absolute vow.
  • By contrast, Jains (an Indian tradition with ahimsa at its heart) take it further: monks and nuns vow ahimsa absolutely, some sweeping their path to protect tiny beings; Jains hold that harm binds karma to the soul.

Mission 2

Animals

  • Animals have atman and are part of samsara: a human may be reborn as an animal, and an animal as a human.
  • The cow is honoured as a mother (gau mata): she gives milk, and Krishna was a cowherd. Most Hindus do not eat beef; many states in India restrict cow slaughter.
  • Vegetarianism is widespread and seen as sattvic, but varies: many Bengali and coastal Hindus eat fish; some communities eat meat; some Shakta temples still offer animal sacrifice.
  • Many Hindus support gaushalas (cow shelters) and avoid leather, silk or products tested on animals; others accept animal testing for human medicine as a lesser harm.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can a person live by ahimsa today?

  • Claim: Ahimsa, meaning never harming any living being, is a realistic standard in the modern world.
  • For: Millions of Jains and Hindus live as strict vegetarians; ahimsa shapes diet, work and medicine.
  • For: Ahimsa is about intention and minimising harm, which anyone can practise.
  • Against: Harm is built into life: farming, transport, medicine. Even the Manusmrti, a Hindu law text, admits that daily housework kills small creatures (3.68).
  • Against: Modern supply chains hide harm; the Manusmrti says the buyer of meat shares in the killing (5.51).
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa an absolute rule or an ideal to approach? Does intention or outcome matter more? Is Jain or Hindu ahimsa the standard being tested?

Mission 3

Ahimsa meets the warrior's duty

  • In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna, a kshatriya (warrior), refuses to fight his relatives at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna answers: a warrior's svadharma (own duty) is to fight a righteous war (dharma yuddha); refusing would be sin.

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul / Than lawful war" (Bhagavad Gita 2:31, Arnold)

  • The atman cannot be killed (2:19–20), so the soldier destroys only bodies.
  • So in traditional Hindu ethics, ahimsa is the general rule, but force to protect dharma can be a duty for those whose role it is.
Mission 3Read the source

Two ways to read the battle

> "… if thou shunn'st / This honourable field--a Kshattriya-- / If, knowing thy duty and thy task, thou bidd'st / Duty and task go by--that shall be sin!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:33, Arnold)

  • Literal reading (e.g. Sri Aurobindo, 1872–1950, Essays on the Gita): the Gita really does teach that righteous war can be a duty.
  • Allegorical reading (Gandhi, Anasaktiyoga, 1929): Kurukshetra is the human heart; the battle is between good and evil impulses; the Gita teaches selfless action and so, in the end, non-violence.

Q1 What does Krishna say Arjuna's refusal would be?

Show answer

Sin: a failure in his duty as a kshatriya.

Q2 How can Gandhi accept this verse yet reject war?

Show answer

He reads the "field" and the "battle" as the inner struggle of the soul, not a call to physical fighting.

Mission 3

Gandhi's ahimsa

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948).
  • Influences: his Gujarati Vaishnava home, Jain friends and teachers (e.g. Raychandbhai), the Gita, Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Ahimsa for Gandhi is active love, not passivity: refusing to hate, while resisting injustice.
  • It is the courage of the strong: he wrote (1920, paraphrase) that if the only choice were cowardice or violence, he would choose violence.
  • Linked with truth: he came to say that "Truth is God". Non-violence is the means; truth is the end.
  • Discipline: brahmacharya, simple living, vegetarianism, fasting.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is ahimsa a workable standard in the modern world?

  • Claim: Ahimsa is a relevant and viable standard to live by today.
  • For: Gandhi's non-violent resistance (satyagraha, "truth force") helped win Indian independence (1947) and shaped the US civil rights movement.
  • For: Modern weapons make war's harm uncontrollable; non-violence may be the only rational path.
  • Against: Non-violence relies on the opponent's conscience. Gandhi's 1938 advice that Jews in Germany resist non-violently was strongly criticised (e.g. by Martin Buber, 1939).
  • Against: Independence came with the violence of Partition; India itself keeps an army and nuclear weapons.
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa a personal virtue or a state policy? Does it require results to be valid? Does dharma yuddha already make ahimsa realistic?
Mission check

Hindus agree that ahimsa matters; they differ on whether protecting dharma can ever require force. A later mission: the paths of yoga.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Hindu teaching, non-violence, detachment and self-restraint are duties for all (sadharana dharma) that purify the mind.
  • In the Gita and Samkhya, the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas) make up nature; the virtues are sattvic, but liberation lies beyond all three.
  • Brihadaranyaka IV.4.5–6: as one desires and acts, so one becomes; freedom from desire frees from rebirth.

Mission 2

  • Ahimsa is non-harming in thought, word and deed, based on atman in all beings and on karma.

Mission 3

  • Traditional Hindu ethics weighs ahimsa against a warrior's duty: dharma yuddha allows just war under strict rules.
  • Gandhi read the Gita as an inner battle and made ahimsa a political force through satyagraha.
