Algebra · expressions

Terms, expressions and like terms

  • An expression is a collection of terms such as 5a5a, −9b−9b or x2x². The number in front of a letter is its coefficient.
  • Algebra shorthand: abab means a×ba × b, 3y3y means y+y+yy + y + y, a2ba²b means a×a×ba × a × b, ba​a/b means a÷ba ÷ b.
  • Like terms have exactly the same letters and powers. Collect them by adding their coefficients: 2a+3b+5a−9b=7a−6b2a + 3b + 5a − 9b = 7a − 6b.
  • x2x² and xx are not like terms: 3x2+2x3x² + 2x cannot be simplified.
  • To expand a single bracket, multiply every term inside by the term outside: 3x(2x−4y)=6x2−12xy3x(2x − 4y) = 6x² − 12xy and −4(x−3)=−4x+12−4(x − 3) = −4x + 12.

Algebra · expressions

Expanding two brackets

  • Multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second. A grid keeps track: four boxes, four products.
  • (x+5)(x+2)(x + 5)(x + 2): the boxes are x2x², 2x2x, 5x5x and 1010. Collect the like terms: x2+7x+10x² + 7x + 10.
  • The grid is also an area picture: a rectangle (x+5)(x + 5) by (x+2)(x + 2) splits into four smaller rectangles.
  • Higher: for three brackets, expand two of them first, then multiply the result by the third: (x+1)(x−1)(2x+1)=(x2−1)(2x+1)=2x3+x2−2x−1(x + 1)(x − 1)(2x + 1) = (x² − 1)(2x + 1) = 2x³ + x² − 2x − 1.

Algebra · expressions

Factorising: expanding in reverse

  • Factorise means write as a product. Take out the highest common factor of every term: 9x2+15xy=3x(3x+5y)9x² + 15xy = 3x(3x + 5y). "Factorise fully" means nothing more can come out.
  • A quadratic x2+bx+cx² + bx + c factorises to (x+p)(x+q)(x + p)(x + q) where pp and qq multiply to cc and add to bb: x2+7x+10=(x+5)(x+2)x² + 7x + 10 = (x + 5)(x + 2).
  • Difference of two squares: x2−a2=(x−a)(x+a)x² − a² = (x − a)(x + a), so x2−16=(x−4)(x+4)x² − 16 = (x − 4)(x + 4).
  • Check any factorisation by expanding it again.

Algebra · formulae

Expressions, equations, formulae

  • An expression has no equals sign: 3x+53x + 5.
  • An equation is true only for particular values of the letter: 3x+5=203x + 5 = 20 is true only when x=5x = 5.
  • A formula links two or more quantities, so you can work one out from the others: v=u+atv = u + at.
  • An identity is true for every value of the letter. It is often written with ≡≡: 2(x+3)≡2x+62(x + 3) ≡ 2x + 6.

Algebra · formulae

Substituting into formulae

  • Substitute means replace each letter by its value, then follow the order of operations: brackets, powers, then ×× and ÷÷, then ++ and −−.
  • Put negative numbers in brackets: if x=−3x = −3, then x2=(−3)2=9x² = (−3)² = 9 and 2x2=2×9=182x² = 2 × 9 = 18.
  • Watch the minus: 5−2x=5−2×(−3)=5+6=115 − 2x = 5 − 2 × (−3) = 5 + 6 = 11.
  • A formula in words works the same way: write it with letters first, then substitute.

Algebra · formulae

Changing the subject

  • The subject is the letter on its own on one side, like vv in v=u+atv = u + at.
  • To change the subject, undo the operations on the letter you want, in reverse order, doing the same to both sides.
  • v=u+atv = u + at: subtract uu, then divide by aa, so t=av−u​t = (v − u)/a.
  • y=3x−2y = 3x − 2: add 2, then divide by 3, so x=3y+2​x = (y + 2)/3.
  • If the subject is squared, undo the square last with a square root: A=πr2A = π r² gives r=πA​​r = √(A/π).

Algebra · linear equations and inequalities

Solving linear equations

  • Keep the equation balanced: whatever you do to one side, do to the other.
  • Brackets: expand them first. 7(x+3)=5x−87(x + 3) = 5x − 8 becomes 7x+21=5x−87x + 21 = 5x − 8.
  • Unknown on both sides: subtract the smaller xx term from both sides so xx appears once: 2x+21=−82x + 21 = −8, so x=−14.5x = −14.5.
  • Fractions: multiply every term by the denominator: 24x+5​=3(4x + 5)/2 = 3 gives 4x+5=64x + 5 = 6, so x=0.25x = 0.25.

Algebra · linear equations and inequalities

Forming an equation from words

  • Choose a letter for the unknown and say what it stands for: "let mm be the number of months".
  • Write each quantity as an expression in that letter. A fixed charge plus a rate per unit is "fixed + rate × units".
  • When two quantities are equal, write expression = expression and solve.
  • Finish by answering the question asked, in context and with units.

Question

Algebra · linear equations and inequalities

Linear inequalities

  • << less than, >> greater than, ≤≤ at most, ≥≥ at least.
  • Number line: open circle for << or >> (end value not included), closed circle for ≤≤ or ≥≥.
  • Solve an inequality like an equation: 3x−2<103x − 2 < 10 gives 3x<123x < 12, so x<4x < 4.
  • If you multiply or divide by a negative number, reverse the inequality sign: −2x<6−2x < 6 gives x>−3x > −3.

Algebra · linear equations and inequalities

Regions on a graph

  • An inequality in xx and yy, such as x+y≤5x + y ≤ 5, describes a region of the grid.
  • Draw the boundary line x+y=5x + y = 5: solid for ≤≤ or ≥≥, dashed for << or >>.
  • Test a point not on the line, such as (0,0)(0, 0): 0+0≤50 + 0 ≤ 5 is true, so the region is on the side containing (0,0)(0, 0).
  • Several inequalities together give the region where all are true.

Algebra · quadratic equations

Solving by factorising

  • Make one side 0: write the equation as ax2+bx+c=0ax² + bx + c = 0.
  • Factorise, then use: if AB=0AB = 0, then A=0A = 0 or B=0B = 0.
  • Each bracket gives one root, so there are usually two solutions.
  • The roots are where the graph of y=ax2+bx+cy = ax² + bx + c crosses the xx-axis; a graph gives approximate solutions.
  • Foundation: x2+bx+c=0x² + bx + c = 0. Higher: also ax2+bx+c=0ax² + bx + c = 0 and equations you must rearrange first.

Algebra · quadratic equations

The quadratic formula (Higher)

  • For ax2+bx+c=0ax² + bx + c = 0:
x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/2a
  • Use it when the quadratic does not factorise, or when you need decimals or surds.
  • Rearrange to …=0… = 0 first, and take each of aa, bb, cc with its sign.
  • If b2−4acb² − 4ac is negative there are no solutions: the graph does not cross the xx-axis.
  • The formula is given on the formula sheet, but practise using it until it is quick and accurate.

Algebra · quadratic equations

Completing the square (Higher)

  • Halve the coefficient of xx: x2+bx+c=(x+2b​)2−(2b​)2+cx² + bx + c = (x + b/2)² − (b/2)² + c
  • Solve x2−8x+13=0x² − 8x + 13 = 0: (x−4)2−16+13=0(x − 4)² − 16 + 13 = 0, so (x−4)2=3(x − 4)² = 3 and x=4±3​x = 4 ± √3.
  • Keep the ± when you square-root. Leave surds exact unless told to round.
  • Choosing a method: factorise if you can; use the formula for decimals; complete the square for exact surds or a turning point.

Algebra · simultaneous equations

Two unknowns, two equations

  • One equation in xx and yy has endless solutions. Simultaneous equations are two equations that must both be true at once.
  • The solution is the pair xx, yy that fits both.
  • Graphically, each linear equation is a line; the solution is where the lines intersect. y=2x−1y = 2x − 1 and x+y=5x + y = 5 meet at (2,3)(2, 3).
  • Always check your pair in both original equations.
  • Reading the solution as the point where two lines intersect is Higher tier (2.6B).

Algebra · simultaneous equations

Solving by substitution

  • Use substitution when one equation already says y=…y = … or x=…x = ….
  • Replace that letter in the other equation, using brackets: y=2x−1y = 2x − 1 into x+y=5x + y = 5 gives x+(2x−1)=5x + (2x − 1) = 5.
  • Solve for one letter: 3x=63x = 6, so x=2x = 2; then y=2×2−1=3y = 2 × 2 − 1 = 3.
  • Elimination and substitution give the same answer; pick whichever is quicker.

Algebra · simultaneous equations

One linear, one quadratic (Higher)

  • Rearrange the linear equation to y=…y = … (or x=…x = …) and substitute it into the quadratic one.
  • You get a quadratic equation in one letter: make it =0= 0 and solve.
  • Find the partner value for each root using the linear equation. Give the answers as pairs.
  • A line can meet a curve at two points, one point (it touches) or no points.
  • The curve might be a circle such as x2+y2=25x² + y² = 25: the same method works.

Algebra · algebraic fractions

Simplifying algebraic fractions (Higher)

  • An algebraic fraction has algebra in the numerator, the denominator or both.
  • To simplify, factorise the numerator and denominator fully, then cancel common factors (whole brackets or whole factors).
  • x2−5x+6x2−2x​=(x−3)(x−2)x(x−2)​=x−3x​(x² − 2x)/(x² − 5x + 6) = (x(x − 2))/((x − 3)(x − 2)) = x/(x − 3)
  • You can only cancel factors, never single terms: in xx+3​(x + 3)/x nothing cancels.

Algebra · proof

Writing integers with algebra (Higher)

  • Let nn be any integer. Then 2n2n is always even and 2n+12n + 1 is always odd.
  • Consecutive integers: nn, n+1n + 1, n+2n + 2. Consecutive even numbers: 2n2n, 2n+22n + 2. Consecutive odd numbers: 2n+12n + 1, 2n+32n + 3.
  • A multiple of 3 can be written 3n3n; any expression of the form 3×(integer)3 × (integer) is a multiple of 3.
  • The goal is to write the result in a form like 4(…)4(…) or 2(…)+12(…) + 1 where the bracket is an integer.
Algebra · proof

Show that, proof and counterexample (Higher)

  • Show that two expressions are equivalent: start from one side and work to the other. (x+1)2+2=x2+2x+1+2=x2+2x+3(x + 1)² + 2 = x² + 2x + 1 + 2 = x² + 2x + 3.
  • Checking examples is not a proof: the next number might fail.
  • Higher: a proof uses general expressions (nn, 2n+12n + 1, ...), simplifies them, and ends with a sentence saying why the result follows.
  • One counterexample is enough to prove a statement false.

Proving a result about consecutive integers (Higher)

Question
Prove that the sum of any three consecutive integers is a multiple of 3.
  1. 1
    Write three consecutive integers in general: nn, n+1n + 1, n+2n + 2 where nn is an integer
  2. 2
    Add them: n+(n+1)+(n+2)=3n+3n + (n + 1) + (n + 2) = 3n + 3
  3. 3
    Factorise to show the factor 3: 3n+3=3(n+1)3n + 3 = 3(n + 1)
  4. 4
    Conclude in words: n+1n + 1 is an integer, so the sum is 3 times an integer

The sum is 3(n+1)3(n + 1), which is a multiple of 3 for every integer nn.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Collect only like terms (same letters, same powers); expand by multiplying every term in one bracket by every term in the other.
  • (a+b)2=a2+2ab+b2(a + b)² = a² + 2ab + b², never just a2+b2a² + b²: the grid shows the two missing strips.
  • Factorising undoes expanding: common factor first, then look for x2+bx+cx² + bx + c or a difference of two squares, and check by expanding.
  • Substitute with brackets round negatives, then follow the order of operations.
  • Change the subject by undoing operations in reverse order; if the subject appears twice, collect those terms and factorise.
  • An equation is true for some values, an identity (≡≡) for every value, and a formula links quantities.
  • Expand brackets, collect the unknown on one side, and keep the equation balanced; check by substituting.
  • To compare two deals, write each as an expression, set them equal, and interpret what happens either side of the solution.
  • Inequalities solve like equations, except that multiplying or dividing by a negative reverses the sign.
  • Make one side 0, factorise, and set each bracket to 0: each bracket gives a root.
  • Higher: if it will not factorise, use x=2a−b±b2−4ac​​x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac))/2a.
  • Completing the square gives exact surd solutions in the form a±b​a ± √b.
  • A quadratic graph can cross a line twice, so a quadratic equation can have two solutions.
  • Two unknowns need two equations; the solution fits both and is where their graphs intersect.
  • Eliminate by matching coefficients and adding or subtracting; substitute when one equation gives y=…y = ….
  • Higher: for a line and a curve, substitute to get a quadratic, then find a yy for each xx.
  • Factorise first, then cancel whole factors; never cancel single terms.
  • Add or subtract algebraic fractions over a common denominator, and multiply every term by it to solve an equation.
  • For a quadratic inequality, find the critical values, sketch the curve, and read off where it is above or below the axis.
  • Write general numbers with algebra: even 2n2n, odd 2n+12n + 1, consecutive nn, n+1n + 1.
  • A proof simplifies the general expression into the form you need and finishes with a sentence; examples never prove, but one counterexample disproves.
