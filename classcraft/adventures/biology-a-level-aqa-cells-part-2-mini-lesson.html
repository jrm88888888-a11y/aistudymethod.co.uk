Mission 1

Mission 1: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Make a guess: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Mission 1

A virus is not a cell

  • A virus particle is acellular: no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, few or no enzymes.
  • It has genetic material, DNA or RNA, never both.
  • The genetic material sits inside a capsid: a coat of protein.
  • Attachment proteins on the surface bind to receptors on a host cell.
  • Viruses are non-living: they cannot replicate outside a host cell.
  • Viruses do not divide. The host cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP make new virus particles.
  • Say: "virus particle" — not: "virus cell".
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all viruses?
Mission 1

Two very different viruses

  • Bacteriophage (phage): infects bacteria.
    • DNA inside a protein head; a protein tail injects the DNA.
  • HIV: infects human helper T cells.
    • RNA (two single strands) inside a protein capsid.
    • An envelope: a lipid bilayer taken from the host cell, studded with glycoproteins.
    • Carries the enzyme reverse transcriptase: HIV is a retrovirus.

Quick check
?A newly discovered particle 30 nm across contains one molecule of single-stranded RNA inside a protein shell. It has no ribosomes and it multiplies only inside living cells. How does the particle multiply?
Mission 1

How a host cell makes viruses

  • (1) Attachment: viral proteins bind to complementary receptors on the host cell.
  • (2) Entry: the nucleic acid enters. A phage injects its DNA; the capsid stays outside.
  • (3) Synthesis: the host's ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP make viral nucleic acid and proteins.
  • (4) Assembly: new particles self-assemble.
  • (5) Release: phage enzymes break down the wall; the cell bursts (lysis), releasing new phages.
  • One cell can release hundreds of new particles: that is how numbers explode.
Quick check
?Bacteriophage lambda does not multiply in a sterile nutrient broth containing amino acids, nucleotides and ATP, but does multiply when living E. coli cells are added to the broth. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?The antibiotic tetracycline blocks bacterial ribosomes but does not block the ribosomes in the cytoplasm of human cells. A doctor is asked whether tetracycline would stop influenza viruses multiplying in the cells lining a patient's airways. Which answer is correct?

One virus becomes millions because each infected cell becomes a factory, using its own ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to build new particles.

Mission 2

Mission 2: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Make a guess: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Mission 2

Why take cells apart?

  • A microscope shows what an organelle looks like. To find out what it does, you need it on its own, in quantity.
  • Cell fractionation: break cells open, then separate their organelles into fractions.
  • Isolated organelles keep working: e.g. isolated mitochondria use oxygen and make ATP.
Mission 2

Step 1: homogenise in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution

  • Chop the tissue; put it in solution; break the cells in a homogeniser (a blender).
  • Cold (ice-cold): reduces enzyme activity, so enzymes released from the cell (e.g. from lysosomes) cannot digest the organelles.
  • Isotonic: same water potential as the tissue, so organelles neither gain nor lose water by osmosis: they do not burst or shrivel.
  • Buffered: keeps the pH constant, so the organelles' proteins are not denatured.
  • Filter the homogenate: removes unbroken cells and large debris.
  • Say: "cold reduces enzyme activity" — not: "cold denatures the enzymes".
  • Say: "stops the organelles bursting" — not: "stops the cells bursting" (the cells are broken on purpose).
Quick check
?Liver tissue is homogenised in an ice-cold, isotonic, buffered solution. Why must the solution be isotonic?
Mission 2

Step 2: ultracentrifugation

  • Spin the filtered homogenate in an ultracentrifuge.
  • The largest, densest organelles settle first, into a pellet at the bottom.
  • The liquid above, the supernatant, is poured into a fresh tube and spun faster and longer.
  • Repeat. Each spin brings down smaller, less dense organelles.
  • This is differential centrifugation.
  • Usual order: nuclei → (plant tissue: chloroplasts) → mitochondria → lysosomes → ER fragments → ribosomes.
  • Typical (illustrative) conditions: about 1 000 × g for nuclei; about 10 000–20 000 × g for mitochondria; about 100 000 × g and above for ER and ribosomes.
Quick check
?A homogenate of leaf cells is filtered and then spun at increasing speeds. In which order do these organelles form the main part of each pellet?

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Make a guess: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Mission 3

One cell becomes two

  • A parent cell (often called the mother cell) divides into two daughter cells.
  • The parent does not survive as a third cell: its contents are shared out.
  • First the nucleus divides: mitosis.
  • Then the cytoplasm divides: cytokinesis.
  • Not every cell keeps dividing: most mature nerve cells never do.
Mission 3

What mitosis is for

  • Growth: more cells.
  • Repair and replacement: cells lost by wounds or wear.
  • Each daughter cell gets the same chromosome number and the same genes: genetic constancy.
  • Say: "growth needs cell division to add cells" — not: "organs grow just by their cells getting bigger".
Mission 3

Copy first: two chromatids

  • Before a cell divides, every DNA molecule is replicated.
  • This happens in S phase of interphase.
  • Each chromosome is then two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • They stay joined until anaphase.
  • One chromatid = one DNA molecule. Two joined chromatids = one chromosome.
  • Say: "replication gives each chromosome two sister chromatids" — not: "replication doubles the chromosome number".
Quick check
?A cell of an organism has 8 chromosomes before its DNA is replicated. How many chromosomes and how many DNA molecules are in its nucleus at metaphase of mitosis?
Mission 3

The cell cycle

  • Interphase (G1 → S → G2), then mitosis, then cytokinesis.
  • G1: the cell grows, makes proteins, duplicates organelles.
  • S: DNA is replicated.
  • G2: more growth and protein synthesis; the cell prepares to divide.
  • Interphase takes most of the cycle. It is not a resting stage.
  • Many cells leave the cycle and stop dividing; some can be stimulated to divide again.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly gives the sequence of phases in the cell cycle?
Mission 3

Mitosis in four stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense (shorter, thicker, visible). The nuclear envelope breaks down. The spindle forms.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Spindle fibres from both poles attach at each centromere.
  • Anaphase: centromeres divide. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles; the spindle fibres shorten.
  • Telophase: chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. A nuclear envelope forms round each set. The spindle breaks down.
  • Say: "sister chromatids separate" — not: "homologous chromosomes separate".
Quick check
?A body cell has 4 chromosomes. It is in anaphase of mitosis. What is moving towards each pole of the cell?
Mission 3

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
  • It usually begins during telophase.
  • Animal cell: the membrane is pulled in at the equator, forming a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two.
  • Plant cell: the rigid wall cannot be pinched. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse into a cell plate.
  • The cell plate becomes new membranes and a new wall. It grows outwards until it joins the old wall.
  • Say: "spindle microtubules move chromosomes" — not: "the spindle pinches the cell in two".
Quick check
?A dividing cell is examined after its two new nuclei have formed. Many small vesicles are seen gathering and fusing in a line across the equator of the cell, midway between the nuclei. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission check

?A population of a hypothetical freshwater plant reproduces only by fragmentation: pieces break off a parent plant and grow into new plants by mitosis. Which claim about the new plants is best supported?

Copy every chromosome, then pull the copies apart: each new cell gets a full, identical set. Four million times a second, or once in a broken-off fragment.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Make a guess: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Mission 4

Read the stage from the chromosomes

  • Interphase: intact nucleus; no separate chromosomes (DNA uncoiled). Most cells look like this.
  • Cells divide out of step, so the commonest stage is the one that lasts longest.
  • Prophase: thread-like chromosomes, scattered; nuclear envelope disappearing.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes in a single line across the equator.
  • Anaphase: two groups of V-shaped chromatids moving apart.
  • Telophase: two clusters at the poles; nuclear envelopes re-forming. In plants, a cell plate between them.
  • Why visible? Condensed chromosomes are short and thick, so they take up stain densely.
  • Say: "intact nucleus, no visible chromosomes = interphase" — not: "= prophase".
Quick check
?Two cells are seen in a stained micrograph of a root tip. Cell 1 has an intact nucleus in which no separate chromosomes can be distinguished. Cell 2 has no nuclear envelope, and its condensed chromosomes lie in a single line across the middle of the cell. Which identification is correct?
Mission 4Practical

Root-tip squash

  • AQA required practical 2.
  • Cut the last 5 mm of a growing root (onion or garlic). Dividing cells are in the meristem, near the tip.
  • Warm in hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1 mol dm⁻³, about 60 °C, a few minutes). This breaks the middle lamella, so cells separate, and stops division.
  • Rinse; add a stain that binds DNA (e.g. toluidine blue or acetic orcein).
  • Cover with a coverslip and squash with firm, vertical pressure: no sideways slide. Aim for one layer of cells.
  • Find the meristem at low power, then identify stages at high power (×400).
  • Key controls: use only the tip; same acid time and temperature for every root; count every cell in a field, not just the dividing ones.
  • Supports: the mitotic index.
  • Supports: actual size of cells.
Quick check
?In a stained root-tip squash, one cell shows two groups of V-shaped chromatids, their points facing opposite ends of the cell. Which explains this appearance?
Mission 4

Counting cells measures time

  • Mitotic index = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells counted.
  • A higher index means a larger share of cells dividing.
  • Cells in a tissue divide out of step. So the fraction of cells in a stage ≈ the fraction of the cycle spent in it.
  • Time in a stage = (cells in that stage ÷ total cells) × length of one cycle.
  • Most cells are in interphase: it is the longest stage.
  • Say: "divide by the total number of cells" — not: "by the cells not in that stage".
Mission 4

Worked example: from counts to hours

Illustrative data: one field of a root-tip squash. One cell cycle takes 20 hours.

StageCells
Interphase440
Prophase30
Metaphase12
Anaphase8
Telophase10
Total500
  • Cells in mitosis = 30 + 12 + 8 + 10 = 60.
  • Mitotic index = 60 ÷ 500 = 0.12 (12%).
  • Time in mitosis = 0.12 × 20 h = 2.4 h.
  • Prophase: 30 ÷ 500 × 20 h = 1.2 h, the longest stage of mitosis here.
  • Assumes cells are out of step, and every stage was identified correctly.
Quick check
?A student examined 240 cells in a micrograph of a growing tissue. 204 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes, and the remaining 36 cells had condensed chromosomes visible. What is the mitotic index of the sample?
Mission 4

Worked example: actual size

  • Actual size = size of image ÷ magnification.
  • A cell measures 8 mm across on a photomicrograph taken at ×400.
  • Convert first: 8 mm = 8 × 1000 = 8000 µm.
  • Actual size = 8000 µm ÷ 400 = 20 µm.
  • Check: root-tip cells are tens of micrometres across. A result in millimetres is a warning sign.
Quick check
?On a photomicrograph taken at ×600, a root-tip cell measures 15 mm across. What is the actual width of the cell?
Mission check

?A student counted 100 cells in a micrograph of a growing root tip. 85 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes; 6 had condensed chromosomes scattered through the cell; 3 had chromosomes aligned across the equator; 4 had two groups of chromatids moving apart; 2 had two groups of chromosomes at the poles with nuclear envelopes forming. Which conclusion is correct?

More cells in a stage means more time spent in it. One frozen moment, counted carefully, is a clock.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Make a guess: Many cells in your body could still divide, yet most of the time they hold back. What keeps them in check?

Mission 5

Division on demand

  • Cells divide only when signals and internal checks allow it.
  • Genes that control the cycle make proteins that push it on or hold it back.
  • If control fails, a cell divides without restraint. Its descendants pile up into a tumour.
  • Uncontrolled division can lead to cancer.
Mission 5

When control fails

  • A mutation can disable a gene that controls the cycle.
  • A damaged cell then keeps dividing. Each division copies the damage to its descendants.
  • More mutations build up in that cell line.
  • Usually several mutations in one cell line are needed before a tumour forms.
  • The genes involved, oncogenes and tumour suppressor genes, are in the next mission.
  • Say: "cancer cells divide without the normal controls" — not: "cancer is just fast division".
Quick check
?The risk of developing most cancers rises steeply with age. Which explanation fits how tumours form?
Mission check

?A mutation occurs in a gene that controls the cell cycle in one cell lining a man's colon. Which statement about the consequences is correct?

Division is held in check by genes. Damage one in a body cell and that cell's line can escape control.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Cancer starts in the body's own genes. How does a normal gene turn into a cancer gene?

Make a guess: Cancer starts in the body's own genes. How does a normal gene turn into a cancer gene?

Mission 6

Benign or malignant

  • Both kinds of tumour are masses of cells formed by uncontrolled division.
  • Benign: stays in one place, often inside a capsule. Does not invade or spread. Does not cause cancer, but can still press on organs.
  • Malignant: invades neighbouring tissue. Cells can break away, travel in blood or lymph and form new tumours: metastasis. This is cancer.
  • Malignant cells often lose their specialised features and have abnormal nuclei; benign cells usually look like the normal tissue.
  • Malignant tumours often grow faster, but rates overlap. Invasion and spread define malignancy.
  • Say: "malignant = invades and can spread" — not just: "malignant = grows faster".
Quick check
?The mitotic index was determined for three samples (illustrative data): cells from a root-tip meristem, 0.12; cells from a tumour removed from a patient, 0.10; cells from a mature leaf, 0.00. A student concluded that the tumour must be benign because its mitotic index is lower than that of a healthy meristem. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 6

Accelerators and brakes

  • Proto-oncogenes: normal genes. Their proteins stimulate division when the cell is signalled to divide.
  • A mutation can turn one into an oncogene: the protein is overactive or always on. Division is driven with no signal.
  • One mutated copy can be enough.
  • Tumour suppressor genes: their proteins slow division, repair DNA or trigger cell death.
  • A mutation that inactivates them removes the brake. Usually both copies must be lost.
  • Either change → uncontrolled division. Several such mutations together → a tumour.
  • Say: "an oncogene is a mutated version of the cell's own proto-oncogene" — not: "a foreign cancer gene".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how mutations in genes that control the cell cycle lead to uncontrolled cell division?
Mission 6

Mutagens and carcinogens

  • Mutagen: anything that increases the rate of mutation.
  • Carcinogen: anything that causes cancer. Many carcinogens are mutagens.
  • Examples: UV light; ionising radiation (X-rays, gamma rays); chemicals in tobacco smoke; some viruses (e.g. human papillomavirus).
  • Mutations that hit proto-oncogenes or tumour suppressor genes raise cancer risk.
  • Genetic factors matter too: an inherited faulty tumour suppressor allele (e.g. BRCA1) raises the risk of breast cancer.
Mission 6

Methylation and oestrogen

  • Epigenetic change: gene expression altered without changing the base sequence.
  • Increased methylation (hypermethylation) of a tumour suppressor gene → not transcribed → brake lost.
  • Decreased methylation (hypomethylation) of an oncogene → transcribed → division driven.
  • Methylation can be reversed, so drugs that remove methylation are used for some cancers.
  • Oestrogen binds its receptor in some breast cells. The complex acts as a transcription factor, switching on genes that stimulate division.
  • Raised oestrogen concentration (e.g. made by fat tissue after the menopause) is linked to the development of some breast cancers.
  • Some breast tumours make oestrogen themselves, stimulating their own growth.

Mission 6

Drugs that target the cycle

  • Cancer cells divide often, so drugs that block division hit them hardest.
  • Normal dividing cells are hit too: hair follicles, gut lining, bone marrow. Hence the side effects.
  • Mitotic poisons, e.g. vincristine: stop microtubules forming, so no spindle. Cells cannot complete mitosis.
  • Antimetabolites, e.g. 5-fluorouracil: block DNA synthesis in S phase. Cells cannot replicate their DNA.
  • Using oncogene and tumour suppressor knowledge: drugs that block an oncogene's overactive protein; testing for inherited faulty alleles (e.g. BRCA1) to guide screening and prevention.
  • Tamoxifen blocks oestrogen receptors in breast cells: used for oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer.
Quick check
?5-fluorouracil prevents the synthesis of DNA. At which point in the cell cycle are dividing tumour cells stopped?
Mission check

?In chronic myeloid leukaemia, a mutated gene in blood-forming cells makes an overactive protein that keeps the cells dividing. A drug that blocks this protein stops most of the cancer cells dividing. Which explanation is correct?

A cancer gene is the cell's own gene, stuck on (oncogene) or broken (tumour suppressor). Know which, and you know what a treatment must do.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A virus is an acellular particle: DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, with no metabolism; host cells make and assemble new particles, often hundreds per cell.

Mission 2

  • Homogenise in ice-cold (less enzyme activity), isotonic (no osmosis into or out of organelles), buffered (no pH change) solution; filter; spin at increasing speeds: nuclei, then mitochondria, then ER and ribosomes.
  • Isolating one type of organelle lets its function be tested directly: a new technique opened a new field.

Mission 3

  • Every chromosome is replicated first (two sister chromatids); mitosis then sends one chromatid of each chromosome to each pole, so both daughter cells get an identical, complete set. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm (cleavage furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).
  • The cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) → cytokinesis.

Mission 4

  • Stages are identified from the chromosomes: none visible (interphase), scattered (prophase), in a line (metaphase), V-shaped groups moving apart (anaphase), clusters at the poles (telophase).
  • Cells divide out of step, so the fraction of cells in a stage gives the fraction of the cycle spent in it.
  • Mitotic index = cells in mitosis ÷ total cells counted.

Mission 5

  • Cell division is controlled by genes whose proteins push the cycle on or hold it back; when mutations disable these controls, a cell line divides without restraint and can form a tumour.

Mission 6

  • A proto-oncogene mutated into an oncogene drives division; a tumour suppressor gene inactivated removes the brake. Several such changes in one cell line give a tumour.
  • Benign tumours stay put; malignant tumours invade and metastasise to form secondary tumours.
  • Abnormal methylation (more on tumour suppressors, less on oncogenes) and raised oestrogen can also drive tumours.
  • Vincristine stops the spindle forming; 5-fluorouracil blocks DNA synthesis in S phase.
