Issues of Good and Evil

This mini-lesson works through the Eduqas theme Issues of Good and Evil: crime and its causes, the aims of punishment, the death penalty, forgiveness, the origin of evil, and the problem of suffering. You'll compare a genuine range of religious and non-religious (Humanist / atheist) views.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. These are serious moral questions on which sincere people disagree. Press Start when you're ready.

Crime

Crime and its causes

A crime is an act that breaks the law. People commit crimes for many reasons:

  • Causes of crime: poverty, upbringing, addiction, mental illness, greed, peer pressure, or opposition to an unjust law.
  • Types of crime: against the person (e.g. assault), against property (e.g. theft), and against the state.
  • Religious response: many believers accept that difficult circumstances play a part, while still holding people responsible for their choices; they emphasise justice and compassion.

Non-religious view: Humanists tend to focus on the social causes of crime and on rehabilitation and prevention, based on evidence about what reduces reoffending.

Punishment

The aims of punishment

Why does society punish people who break the law? There are several aims of punishment:

  • Deterrence — to put people off committing crime.
  • Retribution — the wrongdoer "pays back" for what they did.
  • Reformation (rehabilitation) — to help the offender change and become a better person.
  • Protection — to keep society safe from harm.

Religious emphasis: many Christians stress reformation and mercy ("go and sin no more"); Islam and Christianity both value justice, so retribution and protection also matter.

Death penalty

The death penalty

The death penalty (capital punishment) is the most debated form of punishment. There is a genuine range of views:

  • Arguments for: some argue it delivers justice for the worst crimes, deters others, and protects society.
  • Arguments against: many argue it is wrong to take life, that mistakes cannot be undone, and that it does not clearly deter crime.
  • Religious views differ: the Roman Catholic Church now teaches the death penalty is inadmissible; many Christians oppose it, valuing sanctity of life and forgiveness. Some believers point to scriptural support for it. Views also vary among Muslims.
  • Non-religious view: many Humanists oppose the death penalty, focusing on human rights and the risk of executing the innocent.
Forgiveness

Forgiveness

Forgiveness means letting go of blame or the desire for revenge. It is a central value in many faiths.

  • Christianity: Jesus taught forgiving "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:21–22) and prayed for those who crucified him. Forgiveness does not always mean removing all consequences.
  • Islam: Allah is "the Most Merciful"; Muslims are encouraged to forgive, and mercy is highly valued.
  • Non-religious view: many Humanists value forgiveness and reconciliation for the good it does to people and relationships, without a religious basis.

Key idea: forgiveness and justice can go together — a person can be forgiven and still face fair consequences.

Origin of evil

The origin of evil

Where does evil come from? Two types are often distinguished:

  • Moral evil — suffering caused by human choices (e.g. cruelty, war).
  • Natural evil — suffering caused by nature (e.g. earthquakes, disease).

Religious explanations include:

  • Free will: God gave humans genuine freedom, and moral evil results from misusing it (linked to the Fall in Genesis 3).
  • Test / soul-making: some argue struggle and suffering can help people grow.

Non-religious view: Humanists usually explain evil and suffering through human behaviour and natural processes, without reference to God.

Suffering

The problem of suffering

If God is all-powerful and all-loving, why is there suffering? This is the problem of evil (often linked to Epicurus).

  • Free will response: genuine love and goodness require freedom, and freedom allows evil.
  • Trust in God: the Book of Job suggests humans cannot always understand God's reasons, but can still trust him.
  • Responding to suffering: many believers respond with prayer, charity and practical help for those who suffer.
  • Non-religious view: some atheists argue suffering is strong evidence against an all-loving, all-powerful God; Humanists focus on reducing suffering through human effort.
Good vs evil

Good, evil and conscience

How do people decide what is good? Sources of moral guidance include:

  • Sacred texts and teachings — e.g. the Ten Commandments, Jesus' teaching, the Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Conscience — an inner sense of right and wrong; many believers see this as a God-given guide.
  • Reason and consequences — Humanists especially stress using reason, evidence and empathy to work out how to reduce harm and increase wellbeing.

Shared ground: religious and non-religious people often agree on many moral basics — such as that murder, cruelty and dishonesty are wrong.

Recap

What to know

Crime: causes include poverty, addiction, upbringing; believers balance responsibility with compassion.

Aims of punishment: deterrence, retribution, reformation, protection — many Christians stress reformation and mercy.

Death penalty: a range of views; the Catholic Church now teaches it is inadmissible; many oppose it, some support it.

Forgiveness: central to Christianity and Islam; can go together with justice.

Origin of evil: moral evil (human choices) vs natural evil (nature); free will and soul-making responses.

Suffering: the problem of evil (Epicurus); free-will and trust responses; Humanists focus on reducing suffering.

Range: give religious AND non-religious views, and show believers disagree among themselves.
