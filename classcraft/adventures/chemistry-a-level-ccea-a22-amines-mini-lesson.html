Mission 1

Mission 1: Fish smells fishy because of amines. Why does a squeeze of lemon juice take the smell away?

Make a guess: Fish smells fishy because of amines. Why does a squeeze of lemon juice take the smell away?

Mission 1

What is an amine?

  • Take ammonia, NH3. Replace H atoms with alkyl or aryl groups: an amine.
  • Primary: one C on the N, e.g. butylamine, CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, and phenylamine, C6H5NH2.
  • Secondary: two C on the N, e.g. (CH3)2NH. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3N.
  • Every amine N has a lone pair. That drives all its chemistry.
  • Amide, not amine: N bonded to a C=O carbon (–CONH2, –CONHR).
Quick check
?Which statement about the compound (CH3)3CNH2 is correct?
Mission 1

Hydrogen bonding in amines

  • Primary and secondary amines have N–H bonds: they form hydrogen bonds between molecules.
  • So their boiling points are higher than alkanes of similar Mr...
  • ...but lower than alcohols: N is less electronegative than O, so the H bonds are weaker.
  • Tertiary amines have no N–H: no hydrogen bonds between their molecules.
  • Small amines dissolve in water: the N lone pair accepts hydrogen bonds from water.
Mission 1

Amines are bases

  • The N lone pair accepts H⁺: amines are bases, like ammonia.
  • In water, a weak base: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺ + OH⁻. The solution is alkaline.
  • With acids, salts: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻ (butylammonium chloride). No water forms.
  • Add NaOH to the salt and the free amine comes back: RNH3⁺ + OH⁻ → RNH2 + H2O.
  • Back to the opening question: fishy-smelling amines are volatile. Lemon's acid turns them into ionic salts, which don't evaporate.
Mission 1

Which is the strongest base?

  • Base strength depends on how available the N lone pair is to accept H⁺.
  • Alkyl groups push electrons towards N (positive inductive effect). Lone pair more available: stronger base than NH3.
  • Phenyl: the lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring. Less available: much weaker base than NH3.
  • Order: primary aliphatic amine > ammonia > phenylamine.
  • More alkyl groups, more available lone pair: 3° > 2° > 1° by this argument. (In water, measured values put 3° below 2°, and sometimes below 1°: how well the ion is hydrated also counts.)
  • Amides are barely basic: the N lone pair is delocalised onto the C=O group.
Quick check
?Which lists ethylamine, ammonia and phenylamine in order of base strength, strongest first?
Mission 1

Making aliphatic amines

  • Route 1: halogenoalkane + excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube.
  • CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + NH4Br
  • Excess NH3 makes the primary amine the main product, but some 2° and 3° amine still forms.
  • Route 2: reduce a nitrile: CH3CH2CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2. Only the primary amine forms.
  • Reducing agent: LiAlH4 (lithal) in dry ether.
Mission 1

Making aromatic amines

  • Reduce nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated HCl, heated under reflux.
  • C6H5NO2 + 6[H] → C6H5NH2 + 2H2O
  • In all that acid, the product is the phenylammonium ion, C6H5NH3⁺: a salt.
  • Add NaOH(aq) to free phenylamine: C6H5NH3⁺ + OH⁻ → C6H5NH2 + H2O.
  • Nitrobenzene comes from nitrating benzene.
Quick check
?Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. Excess sodium hydroxide solution is then added before the phenylamine is separated. Why is the NaOH needed?
Mission 1

Amines + acyl chlorides: amides

  • The N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride.
  • Ammonia gives a primary amide, RCONH2. A primary amine gives a secondary (N-substituted) amide, RCONHR′.
  • CH3COCl + 2CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 → CH3CONHCH2CH2CH2CH3 + CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻
  • Product: N-butylethanamide. Adding CH3CO– to N is ethanoylation.
  • Identify an aromatic amine (e.g. phenylamine): make its N-substituted amide (a crystalline solid), recrystallise it, and match its melting point to data. Amides of small aliphatic amines, such as N-butylethanamide, are liquids, so this works best for aromatic amines.
Mission 1

Amides: make, split, dehydrate

  • From acids: RCOOH + NH3 → RCOO⁻NH4⁺; heat strongly → RCONH2 + H2O.
  • Acid hydrolysis, heat: CH3CONH2 + H2O + HCl → CH3COOH + NH4Cl
  • Alkaline hydrolysis, heat: CH3CONH2 + NaOH → CH3COONa + NH3 (turns damp red litmus blue).
  • Dehydration: heat with P4O10 (phosphorus(V) oxide): CH3CONH2 → CH3CN + H2O.
Mission check

?A reaction mixture contains phenylamine and some unreacted nitrobenzene, dissolved in an organic solvent. It is shaken with dilute hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel. Which compound moves into the water layer, and why?

Same chemistry as lemon on fish. An acid turns an amine into a salt, which stays in water instead of escaping.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Make a guess: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Mission 2

Building the skeleton

  • Most organic reactions only swap functional groups. The carbon chain stays the same length.
  • To make a longer chain, you need a reaction that forms a new C–C bond.
  • The trick: a carbon nucleophile, a carbon atom with a lone pair or a δ− charge.
  • Cyanide, ⁻:C≡N, is the simplest: it adds one carbon.
Mission 2

Cyanide + halogenoalkane

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻
  • Product: propanenitrile. The chain grows from 2 C to 3 C.
  • Naming: count the C≡N carbon in the chain. CH3CH2CN is propanenitrile.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic substitution. Lone pair on the C of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ C; the C–Br electrons go to Br.
  • KCN is highly toxic. Acid turns cyanide into HCN gas, so cyanide and acid only ever meet in a fume cupboard, in small amounts, under controlled conditions.
Mission 2

Cyanide + carbonyl

  • Aldehydes and ketones add cyanide across C=O: hydroxynitriles.
  • Reagent: HCN, with KCN to supply CN⁻.
  • CH3CHO → CH3CH(OH)CN, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. Chain: 2 C → 3 C.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic addition. Product is racemic when the new C is chiral.
  • Hazards: HCN is a very toxic gas, and cyanide salts are toxic too. Fume cupboard, gloves, small amounts.
Quick check
?1-Bromobutane is heated under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol. What is the organic product?
Mission 2

Nitrile → carboxylic acid

  • Acid hydrolysis: heat under reflux with dilute HCl.
  • CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + HCl → CH3CH2COOH + NH4Cl
  • The C≡N carbon becomes the –COOH carbon.
  • Alkaline hydrolysis: reflux with NaOH(aq). CH3CH2CN + NaOH + H2O → CH3CH2COONa + NH3
  • This gives the salt. Add dilute acid to release the carboxylic acid.
  • From a hydroxynitrile: CH3CH(OH)CN → CH3CH(OH)COOH, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).
Mission 2

Nitrile → amine

  • Reduction with lithal (LiAlH4) in dry ether: CH3CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2NH2
  • Product: propylamine (propan-1-amine), one carbon longer than bromoethane.
  • Unlike ammonia + halogenoalkane, this gives only the primary amine: no further substitution.
Mission 2

Amides and nitriles

  • Acid → amide: make the acyl chloride (e.g. with PCl5), then add concentrated ammonia. CH3COCl + 2NH3 → CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl
  • Or make the ammonium salt with ammonia, then heat it: CH3COOH + NH3 → CH3COONH4; CH3COONH4 → CH3CONH2 + H2O
  • Amide → nitrile: heat with phosphorus(V) oxide, P4O10, which removes water. CH3CONH2 → CH3CN + H2O
  • This route keeps the same number of carbons. Cyanide + halogenoalkane adds one.
Quick check
?2-Hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN, is heated under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid. What is the organic product?
Mission check

?Bromomethane, CH3Br, has one carbon. Which route turns it into ethylamine, CH3CH2NH2?

Cyanide adds the extra carbon, then you choose what the C≡N becomes: an amine, or (by hydrolysis) an acid.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Benzene is as colourless as water. Why do two benzene rings joined by two nitrogen atoms make an orange solid?

Make a guess: Benzene is as colourless as water. Why do two benzene rings joined by two nitrogen atoms make an orange solid?

Mission 3

Making benzenediazonium chloride

  • Nitrous acid, HNO2, is unstable: make it in the flask from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid.
  • NaNO2 + HCl → HNO2 + NaCl
  • Phenylamine in HCl, kept below 10 °C (ice bath; in practice about 0–5 °C), reacts with it:
  • C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl → C6H5N2⁺Cl⁻ + 2H2O
  • The product is benzenediazonium chloride. Its ion has the group –N⁺≡N on the ring.
Mission 3

Keep it cold

  • Above about 10 °C, the diazonium ion decomposes: nitrogen gas bubbles off and phenol forms.
  • C6H5N2⁺ + H2O → C6H5OH + N2 + H⁺
  • It survives when cold because the –N⁺≡N group is delocalised with the ring.
Quick check
?Benzenediazonium chloride is made and used below 10 °C. What happens if the solution warms up?
Mission 3

Coupling with phenol

  • Dissolve phenol in NaOH(aq) and keep it cold.
  • Add cold benzenediazonium chloride: a yellow-orange solid forms at once.
  • The product is 4-hydroxyazobenzene: two rings joined by –N=N–, the azo group. (The parent compound, azobenzene, is an orange solid.)
  • C6H5N2⁺Cl⁻ + C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5N=NC6H4OH + NaCl + H2O
  • The nitrogen is kept. The new bond is at the 4-position of the phenol ring, opposite the OH.
Mission 3

Coupling with phenylamine

  • Phenylamine couples the same way, in weakly acidic solution.
  • Product: 4-aminoazobenzene, a yellow dye.
  • C6H5N2⁺ + C6H5NH2 → C6H5N=NC6H4NH2 + H⁺
  • Both couplings are electrophilic substitutions: the diazonium ion is a weak electrophile.
  • It reacts only with rings activated by –OH (or –O⁻) or –NH2.
Quick check
?Cold benzenediazonium chloride is added to a cold solution of phenol in NaOH(aq). Which is the organic product?
Mission 3

Why azo compounds are coloured

  • A substance is coloured if it absorbs some visible light: electrons move to a higher electronic level.
  • Benzene's gap between levels is large: it absorbs only ultraviolet, so it looks colourless.
  • Joining two rings through –N=N– gives one extended delocalised system.
  • More delocalisation → electronic levels closer together → smaller ΔE.
  • Smaller ΔE → longer wavelength absorbed (E = hc/λ), now in the visible.
  • The –N=N– group that brings this about is the chromophore.
Mission 3

The colour you see

  • You see the wavelengths that are not absorbed: the complementary colour.
  • Absorbs violet → looks yellow. Absorbs blue → looks orange. Absorbs green → looks red.
  • More extended delocalisation shifts absorption to longer wavelength: yellow → orange → red.
  • Groups like –OH and –NH2 on the rings extend the delocalisation further.
Quick check
?An azo dye absorbs most strongly at about 480 nm, in the blue part of the visible spectrum. What colour does its solution appear in white light?
Mission 3

Indicators

  • Methyl orange is an azo dye.
  • In acid it gains H⁺. That changes the extent of delocalisation and the gap between levels.
  • Acid form absorbs a longer wavelength → red. Alkaline form → yellow.
  • Any change in delocalisation changes the colour.
Quick check
?Which reagents and conditions, in two steps, convert phenylamine into the yellow-orange azo dye 4-hydroxyazobenzene?
Mission check

?Benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol to give a yellow-orange dye. With naphthalen-2-ol (two fused rings) it gives an orange-red dye. Why is the second dye redder?

Benzene alone absorbs only ultraviolet. Linking rings through –N=N– extends delocalisation, shrinks ΔE and moves absorption into the visible.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Make a guess: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Mission 4

Work backwards

  • Start from the target. Name its functional groups and count its carbons.
  • Ask: which one known reaction makes this group? That gives the compound one step back.
  • Repeat until you reach the starting material you were given.
  • Carbon count goes up? You need a C–C bond-forming step (see Mission 2).
  • Then write the route forwards, with reagents and conditions for every step.
Mission 4

Your reaction map

  • Alkene → halogenoalkane (HBr) · → alcohol (steam, acid catalyst).
  • Halogenoalkane → alcohol (NaOH(aq)) · → amine (NH3) · → nitrile (KCN, +1 C) · → alkene (KOH in ethanol).
  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (oxidation). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Acid + alcohol → ester.
  • Aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile (cyanide, +1 C) · → alcohol (reduction).
  • Nitrile → amine (reduction).
  • Nitrile → carboxylic acid (hydrolysis).
Mission 4

Worked example: three steps

  • Target: propylamine, CH3CH2CH2NH2 (3 C). Start: ethene (2 C).
  • Back 1: an amine from a nitrile by reduction → propanenitrile, CH3CH2CN.
  • Back 2: a nitrile from a halogenoalkane, adding 1 C → bromoethane, CH3CH2Br.
  • Back 3: bromoethane from ethene + HBr. Reached the start.
  • Forwards: (1) HBr, room temperature. (2) KCN in ethanol, reflux. (3) Reduce the nitrile.
  • Step 3 reagent: LiAlH4 in dry ether.
Quick check
?Which compound reacts with KCN in ethanol to give butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CN?
Mission 4

Nitriles in routes

  • Adding a carbon: bromoethane → (KCN) propanenitrile → (hydrolyse) propanoic acid, or → (LiAlH4) propylamine.
  • Keeping the carbons: ethanoic acid → (PCl5) ethanoyl chloride → (NH3) ethanamide → (P4O10, heat) ethanenitrile → (LiAlH4) ethylamine.
  • Count carbons at every step: they only change at a C–C step.
Quick check
?Ethanamide, CH3CONH2, is heated with phosphorus(V) oxide. The organic product is then reduced with LiAlH4. What is the final product?
Mission check

?A chemist needs 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, CH3CH2CH(OH)CN, and has propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH. Which two-step plan works?

Start from the target, find the one reaction that makes it, then step back again. The route writes itself.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Amines: the N lone pair makes them bases (salts with acids, freed by alkali) and nucleophiles.
  • Make them: halogenoalkane + excess NH3; nitrile reduction; nitroarene + Sn / conc. HCl, then NaOH.
  • Base strength: aliphatic amine > NH3 > phenylamine (lone-pair availability).
  • Amines + acyl chlorides → N-substituted amides.

Mission 2

  • CN⁻ adds one carbon: with halogenoalkanes (substitution) or carbonyls (addition).
  • Nitriles can be reduced to amines.
  • Nitriles can be hydrolysed to carboxylic acids.

Mission 3

  • Phenylamine + HNO2 (NaNO2 + HCl) below 10 °C → benzenediazonium chloride. Warm: phenol + N2.
  • Couple with phenol (alkaline) or phenylamine → azo dye, –N=N– between two rings.
  • Extended delocalisation → smaller ΔE → visible light absorbed → complementary colour seen.
  • Indicators like methyl orange change colour as H⁺ changes the delocalisation.

Mission 4

  • Work backwards from the target, one known reaction per step; count carbons to spot a C–C step.
