Mission 1

Mission 1: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Mission 1

The plum-pudding model

  • 1897: Thomson discovers the electron. Atoms must contain positive charge too.
  • Plum-pudding model: positive charge spread evenly through the whole atom, electrons dotted inside.
  • Charge spread out → only weak electric fields anywhere in the atom.
  • Prediction: fast alpha particles pass through with only tiny deflections.
Mission 1

The experiment

  • Geiger and Marsden, in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory (1909–1913).
  • A narrow beam of alpha particles aimed at a very thin gold foil.
  • In a vacuum, so air does not stop the alpha particles.
  • A zinc sulfide screen flashes where an alpha particle hits. Counted through a microscope at many angles.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement about the results of the Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment is correct?
Mission 1

Results and conclusions

  • Almost all went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some were deflected through small angles → they are repelled by positive charge (plum pudding allows this too).
  • A very few were deflected by more than 90° → the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny core. Plum pudding cannot do this.
  • Rutherford (1911): the nuclear model. A small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bouncing back needs a strong electric repulsion from something much more massive than an alpha. Electrons are far too light.
Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 1

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimetres: about a pea on the centre spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Quick check
?A gold nucleus has radius 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and a gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly what fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy?
Mission 1

Distance of closest approach

  • Head-on: the alpha slows, stops for an instant at distance d, then returns.
  • At d, all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy.
  • Eₖ = Q1Q2/(4πε0d), with Q1 = 2e and Q2 = Ze.
  • Gold (Z = 79), Eₖ = 7.7 MeV = 7.7 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.23 × 10⁻¹² J.
  • d = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² ÷ 1.23 × 10⁻¹² = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.
  • The alpha never touches the nucleus, so d is an upper limit for its radius. Nuclear recoil ignored.
Quick check
?Alpha particles of kinetic energy Ek are fired head-on at gold-197 nuclei (proton number 79), and their distance of closest approach is d. Alpha particles of kinetic energy 2Ek are then fired head-on at silver-107 nuclei (proton number 47). In both cases only the electric repulsion acts and nuclear recoil is negligible. What is the new distance of closest approach?
Mission 1

How models changed

  • Early 1800s: Dalton, solid indivisible atoms.
  • 1897–1904: Thomson, electron discovered → plum pudding.
  • 1909–1911: alpha scattering → Rutherford's nuclear model.
  • 1913: Bohr adds energy levels. 1932: Chadwick finds the neutron.
  • Each model was replaced when new evidence did not fit it, after other scientists checked the results.
Mission check

?A gold nucleus is about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m across and a gold atom is about 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. In a scale drawing the nucleus is a dot 1 mm across. How wide must the atom be drawn?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a sheet of paper and a strip of aluminium reveal what a radioactive source emits?

Make a guess: How can a sheet of paper and a strip of aluminium reveal what a radioactive source emits?

Mission 2

Identifying radiation by absorption

  • Geiger–Müller (GM) tube and counter. Measure the background first.
  • Source a few cm from the tube (α travels only a few cm in air).
  • Count with: nothing → paper → a few mm aluminium → a few cm lead.
  • Big drop with paper: α present.
  • Further drop with aluminium: β present.
  • Counts left after aluminium, reduced by lead: γ present.
Mission 2

Getting reliable counts

  • Decay is random: counts vary. Count for long enough to get hundreds of counts each time.
  • A count N has a spread of about √N: 400 ± 20.
  • Subtract the background from every reading.
  • Keep the source–tube distance and position fixed; change only the absorber.
  • Handle the source with tongs; return it to its box straight after.
Quick check
?A student measures a background count rate of 30 counts per minute. With a source in front of a GM tube she records these count rates, each counted over one minute, in counts per minute: no absorber 1530; paper 330; 5 mm aluminium 325; 25 mm lead 105. What does the source emit?
Mission 2

The inverse-square law for γ

  • A point source emits γ photons equally in all directions.
  • Air absorbs almost none. They spread over a sphere of area 4πx².
  • So intensity I = k / x².
  • Double the distance → one quarter of the intensity. Triple → one ninth.
  • α and β do not follow this: air absorbs them.
Mission 2

Testing it (practical)

  • Measure the background. Measure count rate C at several distances d from the source holder to the tube.
  • Problem: the source sits inside its holder, so the true distance is x = d + e (e unknown).
  • Corrected rate C = k/(d + e)². So 1/√C = (d + e)/√k.
  • Plot 1/√C against d: a straight line confirms the law. Its intercept on the d axis is −e.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Background: 20 counts per minute.
  • At 0.20 m, measured 420 per minute → corrected 400.
  • At 0.50 m: corrected = 400 × (0.20/0.50)² = 400 × 0.16 = 64.
  • Measured there: 64 + 20 = 84 per minute.
  • Apply the law to the corrected count only. Background does not depend on the source distance.
Quick check
?A GM tube 0.30 m from a γ source records 1250 counts per minute. The background count rate is 50 counts per minute. The tube is moved to 0.60 m from the source. What count rate does it now record?
Mission 2

Hazards and safe handling

  • Ionising radiation damages cells and DNA: cell death, or mutations that can cause cancer.
  • Outside the body: β and γ are the hazard. α cannot get through the dead outer layer of skin.
  • Inside the body (swallowed, breathed in): α is the most hazardous. All its energy goes into a small volume of tissue.
  • Safe handling: long tongs; keep your distance (γ falls as 1/x²); minimise time; never point a source at anyone; store in a lead-lined box.
Mission 2

Radiation in medicine: risk and benefit

  • Diagnosis: a γ-emitting tracer (e.g. technetium-99m) is imaged by a γ camera. Benefit: a diagnosis without surgery. Risk: a small dose.
  • Treatment: radiotherapy beams or implanted sources kill tumour cells. Healthy tissue nearby is also exposed.
  • Any use must be justified: the expected benefit to the patient must outweigh the risk.
  • Choose a source to keep the dose low: a half-life just long enough for the scan, γ only for imaging, the lowest activity that gives a clear result.
Quick check
?Technetium-99m emits γ rays only and has a half-life of 6.0 h. It is injected into patients so that the organs that take it up can be imaged by a γ camera outside the body. Which statement is a correct reason for choosing technetium-99m?
Mission check

?A worker's radiation badge holds a strip of photographic film behind four windows: open; thin plastic (stops α, like paper); 3 mm of aluminium; 2 mm of lead. After a month, the film is equally dark behind the open window and the plastic, much fainter behind the aluminium, and slightly fainter still behind the lead. Which radiations reached the badge?

Each material stops one radiation: paper (or thin plastic) stops α, a few mm of aluminium stops β, and only thick lead much reduces γ. The step where the count (or the darkening) drops tells you what is there.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Make a guess: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Quick check
?Predict first. Radioactive decay is described as random and spontaneous. Which statement correctly describes what this means?
Mission 3

Random and spontaneous

  • Random: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or when.
  • Spontaneous: nothing outside triggers it. Temperature, pressure and chemistry make no difference.
  • Every undecayed nucleus of an isotope has the same, constant chance of decaying per second. A nucleus does not "age".
  • With huge numbers of nuclei, the fraction that decays in a given time is predictable.
Mission 3

Half-life

  • Half-life: the time for the number of undecayed nuclei of an isotope to halve.
  • Same half-life whatever the starting amount: each nucleus has the same chance.
  • After n half-lives, the fraction left is (½)ⁿ: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16 …
  • The decay curve never quite reaches zero.
  • Half-lives range from far less than a second (polonium-214: 164 μs) to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 × 10⁹ years).
Mission 3

Worked example: whole half-lives

  • A sample has 6.4 × 10⁶ undecayed nuclei. Half-life 2.0 hours.
  • After 8.0 hours: 8.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4 half-lives.
  • Left: 6.4 × 10⁶ × (½)⁴ = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 16 = 4.0 × 10⁵.
  • Decayed: 6.4 × 10⁶ − 4.0 × 10⁵ = 6.0 × 10⁶.
  • Read the question: "left" or "decayed"?
Quick check
?A hospital prepares a dose of technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) that has an activity of 800 MBq at 08:00. What will its activity be at 14:00 the next day?
Mission 3

Activity and count rate

  • Activity A: the number of nuclei that decay per second. Unit: becquerel, 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
  • Count rate: the number of decays a detector records per second (or minute).
  • Count rate < activity: the detector catches only some of the radiation.
  • Both are proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei, so both halve every half-life.
Mission 3

Measuring a half-life

  • Measure the background count rate first.
  • Record the count in equal time intervals. Subtract the background from each: corrected count rate.
  • Plot corrected count rate against time.
  • Read the time to fall from any value to half of it. Repeat from several starting points; average.
  • If you forget to subtract the background, the curve levels off at the background, not zero, and the half-life you read off is too long.
Quick check
?The background count rate in a laboratory is 20 counts min⁻¹. A detector near a source records 340 counts min⁻¹ at t = 0 and 100 counts min⁻¹ at t = 6.0 h, background included both times. What is the half-life of the source?
Mission 3

The decay constant

  • Decay constant λ: the probability per unit time that a nucleus decays. Unit: s⁻¹ (or h⁻¹, yr⁻¹).
  • Larger λ → faster decay → shorter half-life.
  • Activity A = λN: more nuclei, or a likelier decay, means more decays per second.
  • Rate of decay: ΔN/Δt = −λN.
Mission 3

The exponential decay law

  • N = N0e−λt: N0 nuclei at t = 0; N at time t.
  • A = A0e−λt.
  • Half-life: T½ = ln 2 / λ.
  • Works for any time, not just whole half-lives.
Mission 3

Worked example: carbon dating

  • Old wood has 0.40 of the carbon-14 per gram of carbon found in living wood. Half-life 5730 years.
  • λ = ln 2 ÷ 5730 = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ yr⁻¹.
  • N/N0 = e−λt → t = ln(N0/N) ÷ λ = ln(1/0.40) ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴.
  • t = 0.916 ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ = 7.6 × 10³ years.
  • Same answer from activities: A/A0 = N/N0 = 0.40.
  • Assumes the carbon-14 fraction in living things has stayed the same.
Quick check
?A sample contains 8.0 × 10²⁰ nuclei of iodine-131, which has a half-life of 8.0 days. How many iodine-131 nuclei remain after 20 days?
Mission check

?A sample of radon-222 is sealed in a tube. What is the probability that a given nucleus in the original sample has decayed after three half-lives?

Each surviving nucleus keeps the same chance of decaying. So in the next hour, half of what is left decays: a quarter of the original sample, not the other half.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a straight-line graph reveal the half-life of a source whose count falls along a curve?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the half-life of a source whose count falls along a curve?

Mission 4

Making the curve straight

  • A = A0e−λt. Take natural logs of both sides:
  • ln A = ln A0 − λt.
  • Compare y = c + mx: plot ln A against t.
  • Straight line. Gradient = −λ. Intercept = ln A0.
  • Works for N, for activity, and for corrected count rate C.
  • A straight line also tests the model: random decay gives exponential fall.
Mission 4

Why half-life = ln 2 / λ

  • After one half-life, N = N0/2.
  • So ½ = e−λT½. Take logs: ln ½ = −λT½.
  • ln ½ = −ln 2. So λT½ = ln 2.
  • T½ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • ln(C / s⁻¹) is 6.20 at t = 0 and 4.47 at t = 30 min (best-fit line).
  • Gradient = (4.47 − 6.20) ÷ 30 = −0.0577 min⁻¹. So λ = 0.0577 min⁻¹.
  • T½ = ln 2 ÷ 0.0577 = 12.0 min.
  • Use the line, not two data points: it averages out the random scatter.
Quick check
?To determine the half-life of a nuclide, a student plots a graph of ln(corrected count rate) against time. The graph is a straight line with a gradient of −0.0231 min⁻¹. What is the half-life of the nuclide?
Mission 4

From mass to activity

  • Number of nuclei: N = (mass ÷ molar mass) × NA.
  • 2.0 mg of cobalt-60 (molar mass 60 g mol⁻¹, half-life 5.27 years):
  • N = (2.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 60) × 6.02 × 10²³ = 2.0 × 10¹⁹.
  • λ = ln 2 ÷ (5.27 × 3.16 × 10⁷ s) = 4.16 × 10⁻⁹ s⁻¹. λ must be in s⁻¹ to give Bq.
  • A = λN = 8.4 × 10¹⁰ Bq.
Quick check
?Iodine-131 has a half-life of 8.0 days and a molar mass of 131 g mol⁻¹. What is the activity of 1.0 μg of iodine-131? Take NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ and 1 day = 8.64 × 10⁴ s.
Mission 4

Modelling decay step by step

  • In a short time Δt: ΔN = −λNΔt.
  • Spreadsheet: start with N0. Each row: Nnew = N − λNΔt. Repeat.
  • The numbers trace out an exponential curve.
  • Smaller Δt → closer to N0e−λt. Large Δt overestimates the decays, because N falls during each step.
Mission 4

The dice model

  • Each die is a nucleus. Each throw, remove dice showing a six: probability 1/6 per throw.
  • Number left after n throws: N0(5/6)ⁿ. Plot it: a decay curve.
  • The "half-life" is about 3.8 throws, for any starting number of dice.
  • Like nuclei: each die's chance is constant and you can't predict which die goes.
Mission 4

Dating and waste

  • Carbon dating: a living thing keeps the carbon-14 fraction of the air; after death it falls (half-life 5730 years). Useful up to roughly 50 000 years; then too little is left to measure.
  • Rocks: longer-lived isotopes, e.g. potassium-40 (half-life 1.25 × 10⁹ years), date rocks billions of years old.
  • Waste: short-lived isotopes are very active but soon decay. Long-lived ones stay radioactive for thousands to hundreds of thousands of years: they need secure storage for many half-lives.
Quick check
?Charcoal from an ancient hearth has a carbon-14 activity per gram that is 30% of the value for living wood. Carbon-14 has a half-life of 5730 years. Assuming the carbon-14 fraction in living wood has not changed, how old is the charcoal?
Mission check

?A hospital technician checks a short-lived medical tracer. She plots ln(activity / MBq) against time t in hours. The best-fit straight line falls from 6.0 at t = 0 to 4.6 at t = 6.0 h. What is the half-life of the tracer?

Taking logs turns e−λt into a straight line. Its gradient is −λ, and the half-life is ln 2 ÷ λ.

Mission 5

Mission 5: From where a nucleus sits on a single chart, can you predict how it will decay?

Make a guess: From where a nucleus sits on a single chart, can you predict how it will decay?

Mission 5

N against Z for stable nuclei

  • Plot neutron number N against proton number Z for every stable nucleus: a narrow band of stability.
  • Light stable nuclei: N ≈ Z (⁴2He, ¹²6C, ¹⁶8O).
  • Heavier stable nuclei: N > Z. Lead-208: N = 126, Z = 82.
  • Why: every proton repels every other proton; the strong force acts only between neighbours. Extra neutrons add attraction without repulsion.
  • No stable nuclei beyond lead (Z = 82).
Mission 5

Where a nucleus sits sets its decay

  • Above the band (too many neutrons): β⁻. A neutron → proton: N − 1, Z + 1.
  • Below the band (too many protons): β⁺ or electron capture. A proton → neutron: N + 1, Z − 1.
  • Very heavy (most with Z > 82): α. N − 2, Z − 2.
  • β⁻, β⁺ and electron capture move a nucleus towards the band. α makes a very heavy nucleus smaller but leaves it neutron-rich, so α decays in a chain are followed by β⁻ decays.
  • γ: no change in N or Z.
Mission 5

Electron capture

  • The nucleus captures one of the atom's inner electrons.
  • p + e⁻ → n + νₑ.
  • ⁷4Be + ⁰₋1e → ⁷3Li + νₑ
  • Same result as β⁺: Z − 1, N + 1, A unchanged. Only a neutrino leaves the nucleus.
  • The atom then has a gap in an inner shell. An outer electron drops into it and emits an X-ray.
Quick check
?The chart shows neutron number N against proton number Z. Starting from the nucleus marked with a dot, four arrows (one of them a loop back to the same nucleus) are meant to show the change produced by each of the four types of decay, as labelled. One of the four is drawn incorrectly. Which one?
Mission 5

Excited nuclei and γ rays

  • After α or β decay, the new nucleus is often in an excited state.
  • It falls to its ground state by emitting a γ photon. Photon energy = difference between the levels.
  • Nuclear energy levels are discrete, so the γ energies are too: a "fingerprint" of the nuclide.
  • Example: cobalt-60 β⁻ decays to an excited nickel-60 nucleus at 2.51 MeV. It drops to 1.33 MeV, then to 0: γ photons of 1.17 MeV and 1.33 MeV.
Quick check
?The energy-level diagram shows a parent nucleus and two energy states of the new nucleus it becomes. The parent nucleus undergoes beta-minus decay to the excited state of the new nucleus, which then emits a gamma-ray photon as it drops to its ground state. What is the frequency of the gamma-ray photon? Use h = 4.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ eV s.
Mission 5

Technetium-99m

  • "m" = metastable: an excited state of technetium-99 that lasts unusually long.
  • ⁹⁹ᵐ43Tc → ⁹⁹43Tc + γ. Half-life 6.0 hours; γ energy 140 keV.
  • Made in the hospital from molybdenum-99 (half-life 66 hours), which β⁻ decays to Tc-99m.
  • Ideal for diagnosis: emits γ but no α or β particles, so a low dose to tissue; γ escapes the body to a γ camera; short half-life: activity falls quickly; it can be attached to many chemicals that target organs.
Mission check

?The table shows the number of neutrons in each of six isotopes of carbon (each has 6 protons) and how each isotope decays. Which claim is supported by the data in the table?

Yes. Above the band of stability (neutron-rich): β⁻. Below it (proton-rich): β⁺ or electron capture. Very heavy: α. β decays move the nucleus towards the band.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Make a guess: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Mission 6

Two ways to measure a nucleus

  • Alpha particles: closest approach gives an upper limit on R.
  • But alphas are affected by the strong force when they get close, which spoils the simple calculation.
  • Electrons: not affected by the strong force. Their diffraction by nuclei gives a more accurate R.
Mission 6

Electron diffraction by nuclei

  • High-energy electrons (hundreds of MeV): λ ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m, about the size of a nucleus.
  • Beam through a thin target; count scattered electrons at each angle.
  • Intensity falls with angle, with a first minimum (a dip, not zero).
  • First minimum: sin θ ≈ 0.61λ/R (given in questions).
  • At these energies E ≈ pc, so λ ≈ hc/E.
Quick check
?Electrons of de Broglie wavelength 4.1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m are scattered by oxygen-16 nuclei. The first minimum of intensity is at 57°. The first minimum is at sin θ = 0.61λ/R. What is the radius R of an oxygen-16 nucleus?
Mission 6

Radius and nucleon number

  • Measured radii fit one rule:
  • R = R0A1/3, A = nucleon number, R0 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Use the value of the constant given in the question.
  • From data: R against A1/3 is a straight line through the origin.
  • 8 times the nucleons → 2 times the radius. 27 times → 3 times.
  • Typical radii: about 2.7 fm (carbon-12) to about 7.4 fm (uranium-238). 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • Gold-197: A = 197.
  • A1/3 = 1971/3 = 5.82.
  • R = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.82 = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • About 7 fm: roughly 20 000 times smaller than the gold atom.
Quick check
?A nucleus of lead-208 has proton number 82 and nucleon number 208. Using R = R0A1/3 with R0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, what is the radius of this nucleus?
Quick check
?A carbon-12 nucleus contains 12 nucleons, each of mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. R0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. What is the density of the nucleus?
Mission 6

Every nucleus has the same density

  • Volume: V = ⁴⁄3πR³ = ⁴⁄3πR0³A. So V ∝ A.
  • Mass: m ≈ A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. So m ∝ A.
  • Density = m/V: the A cancels. The same for every nucleus.
  • ρ = 3 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ ÷ (4π × (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • Nucleons are packed at a fixed spacing, like molecules in a drop of liquid.
Mission 6

Nucleus versus atom

  • Solids, made of atoms: about 10³ to 2 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³ (gold: 1.9 × 10⁴).
  • Nuclear matter: 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, about 10¹³ times denser.
  • Why: the nucleus is 10⁴–10⁵ times narrower than the atom, so its volume is about 10¹² to 10¹⁵ times smaller, yet it holds nearly all the mass.
  • A teaspoon (5 cm³) of nuclear matter: 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.3 × 10¹⁷ ≈ 1 × 10¹² kg, about a billion tonnes.
Mission check

?A neutron star has a mass of 2.8 × 10³⁰ kg. Model it as a sphere of nuclear matter, with the same density as any nucleus. Estimate its radius.

Nuclear matter has the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, in any amount. A whole star of it is only about 14 km in radius, and a teaspoon of it has a mass of about 10¹² kg: a billion tonnes.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Alpha scattering: most pass straight through, a very few bounce back → a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.
  • Closest approach: Eₖ = electric PE at d; d is an upper limit for the nuclear radius.

Mission 2

  • Identify radiation by which absorber causes a drop in the corrected count rate.
  • γ from a point source: I = k/x². Plot 1/√C against d to test it.
  • α is most hazardous inside the body; β and γ outside. Use tongs, distance, short times and shielding.

Mission 3

  • Decay is random and spontaneous; each nucleus has a constant chance of decaying.
  • Half-life: the time for N to halve. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ is left.
  • Activity (Bq) and count rate halve each half-life; subtract the background before reading a half-life.
  • N = N0e−λt; half-life = ln 2 / λ.
  • A = λN.

Mission 4

  • ln A = ln A0 − λt: plot ln A against t; gradient −λ; half-life = ln 2/λ.
  • N = (mass ÷ molar mass) × NA; use λ in s⁻¹ for activity in Bq.
  • ΔN = −λNΔt in small steps rebuilds the exponential.

Mission 5

  • Stable nuclei: N ≈ Z when light, N > Z when heavy. Neutron-rich → β⁻; proton-rich → β⁺ or electron capture; very heavy → α.
  • γ photons come from discrete nuclear energy levels; Tc-99m emits γ but no α or β particles, half-life 6 hours.

Mission 6

  • R = R0A1/3, R0 ≈ 1.2 fm; nuclear radii are a few fm.
  • V ∝ A and m ∝ A, so all nuclei have the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³: about 10¹³ times that of ordinary matter.
  • Electron diffraction (first minimum) measures R more accurately than alpha scattering.
