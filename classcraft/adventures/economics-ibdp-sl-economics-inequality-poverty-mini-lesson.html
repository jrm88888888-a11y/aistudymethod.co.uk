Make a guess: If almost everyone in a country gets richer, can more people end up counted as poor?

Mission 1

Two meanings of poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a line that is fixed in real terms (it rises only with prices).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income of the same society.
    • Measured against a line that moves with the median income, e.g. 60% of median household income.
  • Median: the middle income when all households are lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Absolute poverty asks: can you survive? Relative poverty asks: can you take part in normal life where you live?
  • Say: "relative poverty is low income compared with the median" — not: "a milder form of absolute poverty".
Mission 1

Worked example: the line moves

  • Illustrative data. Nine households, weekly income:
    • 150 · 220 · 280 · 340 · 400 · 460 · 520 · 650 · 900
  • Median = 400. Relative line = 0.6 × 400 = 240.
  • Below 240: two households (150, 220). Relative poverty rate = 2/9 = 22%.
  • Ten years later, in real terms: the bottom three gain 2%; everyone else gains 20%.
    • New median = 400 × 1.2 = 480. New line = 0.6 × 480 = 288.
    • Bottom three now: 153 · 224.4 · 285.6. All below 288.
    • Relative poverty rate = 3/9 = 33%.
  • Absolute line fixed at 200 (real terms): one household below it before (150), one after (153).
  • Everyone got richer. Relative poverty rose; absolute poverty did not.
Quick check
?Over 20 years, the real income of every household in a country doubles, including the median household's. The poverty lines are 60% of the median (relative) and a fixed basket of basic needs (absolute). What happens?
Mission 1

Poverty lines in practice

  • International poverty line (World Bank): $3.00 a day per person, at 2021 prices, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).
    • PPP: adjusts for what money buys in each country, so $3.00 means the same basket everywhere.
    • It is an absolute line. It replaced $2.15 a day (2017 prices) in June 2025.
    • Higher lines for richer countries: $4.20 and $8.30 a day.
  • UK: relative low income = below 60% of median household income, before or after housing costs (BHC/AHC).
  • The UK also uses an "absolute low income" line: 60% of the 2010/11 median, held fixed in real terms.
  • Elsewhere: the EU counts people below 60% of their country's median; the OECD compares countries at 50%.
Mission 1

Case: the numbers

  • World, below the international line:
    • 1990: around 2.3 billion people.
    • 2025: around 831 million: about 1 in 10 people.
  • UK, 2023/24, median income £650 a week (BHC). Line: 0.6 × 650 = £390 a week.
    • Relative low income: 17% of people BHC; 21% AHC.
    • Children: 31% AHC. Pensioners: 16% AHC.
  • These figures are for a couple with no children. So the UK relative line is about £28 a day each (390 ÷ 7 ÷ 2): far above the World Bank's $3.00 a day.
  • One country can cut absolute poverty fast while relative poverty barely moves.
Mission 1

Minimum income standards

  • A minimum income standard (MIS) asks: what income does a household need for an acceptable standard of living?
  • Built from a basket of goods and services: food, housing, clothing, transport, a phone, a modest social life.
  • UK MIS (Joseph Rowntree Foundation, Loughborough University): members of the public agree the basket in group discussions.
  • UK 2025: a single person needs to earn about £30,500 a year; a couple with two children about £74,000 between them.
  • Strength: based on real costs and needs, not just a % of the median.
  • Weakness: what counts as "acceptable" is a judgement, and baskets differ by family type and place.
Mission 1

The Multidimensional Poverty Index

  • Income is not the only thing that matters. The MPI (UNDP and Oxford's OPHI) counts deprivations.
  • 3 dimensions, 10 indicators:
    • Health (1/3): nutrition, child mortality. Each 1/6.
    • Education (1/3): years of schooling, school attendance. Each 1/6.
    • Living standards (1/3): cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, electricity, housing, assets. Each 1/18.
  • A person is multidimensionally poor if their weighted deprivations add up to 1/3 or more.
  • MPI = H × A
    • H = share of people who are poor (headcount ratio).
    • A = average share of weighted deprivations among the poor (intensity).
  • Illustrative data: H = 0.30, A = 0.45 → MPI = 0.30 × 0.45 = 0.135.
  • 2025 Global MPI: 1.1 billion of 6.3 billion people in 109 countries are multidimensionally poor.
Quick check
?A household is deprived in five MPI indicators: cooking fuel, sanitation, drinking water, housing and assets. It has no health or education deprivations. Using the MPI weights, is it multidimensionally poor?
Mission 1

Why poverty is hard to measure

  • Where to draw the line: 60% or 50% of the median? $3.00 or $4.20? Each choice changes the count.
  • Surveys miss people: homeless people, refugees, people in conflict zones.
  • Households, not individuals: income may not be shared fairly within a household.
  • Housing costs: two families with the same income can have very different amounts left (BHC vs AHC).
  • Income vs consumption: poor countries often measure spending; incomes can swing month to month.
  • PPP and prices: estimates change when price data are updated (the $2.15 → $3.00 change).
  • MPI weights are a judgement: is sanitation worth less than schooling?
  • Snapshots: a one-year count does not show who is poor for many years.
Mission 1

Causes of poverty

  • Individuals and households:
    • unemployment, low pay, part-time or insecure work
    • low human capital: few skills or qualifications
    • sickness, disability, caring duties, old age
    • large families or one earner (e.g. lone parents)
    • discrimination (gender, ethnicity, disability) and unequal opportunity
    • owning little wealth or property: no income from rent, interest or profit
  • Economy and government:
    • recession, rising prices of essentials (food, energy, rents)
    • changes to taxes and benefits
    • globalisation and technological change (fewer low-skill jobs); market-based supply-side policies (e.g. weaker unions)
    • conflict, weak institutions, climate shocks (low-income countries)
Mission 1

What changes the poverty count?

  • Absolute poverty falls when the real incomes of the poor rise.
  • Relative poverty falls only when the poor's incomes rise faster than the median.
  • So a recession can cut relative poverty: the median falls while benefits and pensions hold steady.
  • And fast growth can raise relative poverty, if gains go mostly to middle and high earners.
  • Say: "relative poverty fell because the median fell" — not: "the poor became better off".
Mission 1

Effects of poverty and inequality

  • Individuals: worse health and shorter lives, poorer diet, stress, lower school results, debt.
  • Firms: lower demand for most goods; workers with less training and lower productivity.
  • Economy:
    • low human capital → lower productivity and growth
    • higher spending on benefits and health care; less tax paid
    • weaker social stability: crime and political unrest can rise where inequality is high
  • Counter-argument: some inequality gives incentives to work, train and take risks.
  • So the effect of inequality on growth depends on how large it is and where it comes from.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can growth alone end poverty?

  • Growth helps: much of the fall in world absolute poverty since 1990 came in fast-growing economies such as China and India.
  • It depends on:
    • the measure: growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty
    • who gains: "inclusive" growth raises low incomes; growth in a few high-pay sectors may not
    • prices of essentials: rising rents or food prices can absorb income gains
    • public services: health, schooling and clean water reach people that higher average incomes may not
Quick check
?During a recession, median household income falls by 5% in real terms. State pensions are held constant in real terms, and most pensioners' income comes from the state pension. What is most likely to happen to measured relative poverty among pensioners?
Mission check

?Over ten years, the real incomes of the poorest fifth of a country's households rise by 15%. Median real household income rises by 40%. Which outcome is most likely?

Absolute poverty tracks the real incomes of the poor. Relative poverty tracks the gap between the poor and the median, so it can rise while almost everyone gets richer.

Mission 2

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is levied on.
    • Income tax, corporation tax (on company profits), taxes on wealth.
  • Direct taxes fall on personal income, corporate income and wealth.
  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government, but can pass some or all of it on to buyers in higher prices.
    • VAT (a sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports.
  • How much of an indirect tax buyers bear depends on elasticity.
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 2

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Judge a tax by the share of income it takes, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the share of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the share stays the same at every income.
  • Regressive: the share falls as income rises.
  • A regressive tax can still take more money from richer people.
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a larger proportion of a low income" — not: "poor people pay more money".
Mission 2

Average and marginal tax rates

  • Average tax rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
  • Marginal tax rate (MRT) = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100: the tax on the next unit of income.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income.
  • Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • Regressive: ART falls as income rises.
  • Moving into a higher band raises the rate only on income inside that band.
Mission 2

Worked example: income tax bands

  • Illustrative data.

  • Income 60,000:
    • first 10,000: 0
    • next 30,000 × 20% = 6,000
    • last 20,000 × 40% = 8,000
    • total tax = 14,000
  • ART = 14,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 23.3%.
  • Earn 1,000 more: tax rises by 400. MRT = 400 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 40%.
  • Income 30,000: tax 4,000, ART 13.3%, MRT 20%.
  • ART rises from 13.3% to 23.3% as income rises: progressive.

Mission 2

Flat taxes

  • A flat tax: one rate on all income above a tax-free allowance.
  • Illustrative data: 20% on income above 12,000.

  • MRT is 20% for everyone above the allowance, yet ART rises with income.
  • With an allowance, a flat tax is mildly progressive. With no allowance, it is proportional.
  • Real case: Estonia introduced a flat income tax in 1994; several central and eastern European countries followed.
  • Supporters: simple, cheap to collect, keeps the reward for earning more. Critics: less redistribution than several rising bands.
Mission 2Evaluate

Should a country make its taxes more progressive?

It depends on:

  • How people respond: if high earners cut work, move or avoid tax, revenue may rise less than planned.
  • Value judgements about equity: how much inequality is acceptable is a normative question.
  • Which taxes change: raising income tax rates at the top differs from cutting regressive taxes on spending.
  • Administration: complex systems cost more to run and leave more room for avoidance.
Mission check

?A local council charges every adult the same fixed amount each year, whatever their income. How is this tax best described?

Judge a tax by the share of income it takes. A tax can take more money from richer people and still be regressive.

Mission 3

Equity is not equality

  • Equality: everyone gets the same (income, wealth or opportunity).
  • Equity: a fair distribution. What is fair is a value judgement (normative).
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same (same income → same tax).
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently (higher income → pay more).
  • Equality of outcome vs equality of opportunity: many argue fairness needs the second, not the first.
  • Say: "an equitable distribution" (fair) — not: "an equal distribution", unless you mean identical shares.
Mission 3

Why redistribute?

  • In a free market, income depends on the factors you own and what they earn: wages, rent, interest, profit.
  • Some own little: low skills, no property, unable to work. Their market income may be very low.
  • Many economists call a very unequal outcome a market failure: markets can be efficient yet produce an inequitable result.
  • Redistribution: government moves income or wealth from richer to poorer households, using:
    • taxes, transfers and benefits, state-provided services, and laws on pay.
Mission 3

Transfers: universal or means-tested?

  • Transfer payments: money paid by government with nothing produced in return: pensions, unemployment and disability benefits, child benefit.
  • Universal benefit: paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income (e.g. a state pension for all over an age).
  • Means-tested benefit: paid only to those with low income or wealth (e.g. the UK's Universal Credit).

Mission 3

The poverty trap

  • Poverty trap: as a low earner earns more, benefits are withdrawn and taxes start, so they keep little of the rise.
  • Effective marginal tax rate (EMTR): share of an extra unit of earnings lost to tax and benefit withdrawal.
  • Case: UK, Universal Credit. For each extra £1 of net earnings, benefit falls by 55p (the taper rate, cut from 63% in November 2021).
  • Worked example: a basic-rate taxpayer on Universal Credit earns £100 more.
    • Income tax 20% + National Insurance 8% = £28. Net earnings rise by £72.
    • Benefit withdrawn: 55% × £72 = £39.60.
    • Kept: £72 − £39.60 = £32.40.
    • EMTR = 1 − 0.72 × 0.45 = 67.6%.
  • Say: combine the rates by multiplying what is kept (0.72 × 0.45) — not: by adding them (28% + 55% = 83%), since the taper applies to net earnings.
Mission 3Graph lab

Change the taper

Line: net income (after tax and benefits) against gross earnings. Flat line = nothing kept; 45° line = everything kept.

  • Takeaway: a higher taper flattens the line (stronger trap) but ends benefit sooner (cheaper). A lower taper helps work incentives but stretches benefit to higher earners.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker pays income tax at 20% on extra earnings. A means-tested benefit is withdrawn at 60% of each extra unit of net (after-tax) earnings. What is the worker's effective marginal tax rate?
Mission 3

Universal basic income

  • Universal basic income (UBI): a regular cash payment to every adult, with no means test and no work condition.
  • For:
    • no withdrawal as earnings rise, so no poverty trap from the benefit itself
    • simple; full take-up; no stigma
    • security when work is irregular
  • Against:
    • very costly: paid to all, so higher taxes, which raise marginal tax rates for earners
    • may reduce work for some (an income effect)
    • a flat payment ignores different needs (disability, housing costs)
  • Case: Finland, 2017–2018. 2,000 unemployed people received €560 a month, tax-free, with no conditions.
    • Employment: recipients worked 78 days on average in the second year, about 6 more than a control group.
    • Recipients reported better wellbeing and less financial stress.
    • Limits: not universal (unemployed only), two years, small sample; other benefit rules changed in 2018, which blurs the result.
Mission 3

Minimum wages

  • A legal minimum wage: a price floor in the labour market.
  • Competitive labour market: a minimum above the equilibrium wage raises pay for those in work but may cut jobs (excess supply of labour).
  • Evidence is mixed:
    • Card and Krueger (1994), New Jersey fast food: no fall in employment after a rise.
    • Other studies find job losses, especially for young and low-skilled workers or after large rises.
  • Targeting: helps the low-paid, but many poor households have no one in work, and some low-paid workers live in better-off households.
Mission 3

Services, skills and fair treatment

  • State provision of merit goods: free or subsidised education and health care benefit poorer households most, relative to income.
  • Human capital: training and schooling raise future earnings: tackles a cause of poverty, not just the symptom.
  • Targeted spending: free school meals, housing support, help with energy bills.
  • Infrastructure: roads, water and electricity in poor regions raise access to jobs and markets.
  • Anti-discrimination laws: equal pay for equal work; open hiring and promotion.
  • Trade-off: slow to work (years for education to raise pay), but can raise efficiency as well as equity.
Quick check
?A government replaces a means-tested benefit with a universal payment of the same maximum amount, paid to everyone whatever their income. What is the most likely effect?
Mission 3Evaluate

Equity or efficiency?

  • Redistribution often involves a trade-off: more equity, but weaker incentives and some lost output.
  • It depends on:
    • rates: high tax rates and high withdrawal rates weaken incentives more
    • how people respond: if labour supply hardly changes with pay, little output is lost
    • design: universal vs targeted benefits
    • use of the money: spending on education and health can raise productivity, so equity and efficiency go together
  • Moral and political judgement: how much inequality is fair, and how much output to give up for it, is normative. Economics measures the trade-off; it cannot settle it.
Mission check

?A government wants to weaken the poverty trap for low-income working families on a means-tested benefit. Which change would do this most directly?

The poverty trap is about the share of each extra unit that is lost, not the level of support. Lower the tax and withdrawal rates on the next unit, and work pays more, though usually at a higher cost to government.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed real line for basic needs. Relative poverty: below a share (often 60%) of the median.
  • Growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty; a recession can even cut relative poverty.
  • Measures: poverty lines (World Bank $3.00 a day), minimum income standards, the MPI (H × A). All involve judgements.

Mission 2

  • Direct taxes fall on income and wealth. Indirect taxes fall on spending.
  • Progressive, proportional or regressive: judged by the share of income paid (ART), not the amount.
  • ART = total tax ÷ income. MRT = tax on the next unit of income.

Mission 3

  • Equity is fairness, a value judgement; equality means identical shares.
  • Means-tested benefits target the poor but create a poverty trap: a high effective marginal tax rate.
  • UBI, minimum wages and human capital policies trade off cost, targeting and incentives.
