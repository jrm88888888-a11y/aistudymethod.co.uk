Mission 1

Long profile and cross profile

  • Long profile: the river's height from source to mouth. Steep near the source, gentle near the mouth.
  • Cross profile: a slice across the valley and channel.
  • Upper course: steep, narrow V-shaped valley; small, shallow channel.
  • Lower course: wide, flat valley floor; wide, deep channel.

Mission 1

What changes downstream

  • A river's load is the rock and soil it carries.
  • Increase: discharge, width, depth, cross-sectional area, velocity, load amount.
  • Decrease: gradient, bed roughness, load size. Stones become smaller and rounder.
  • Upper Tees: "gravel and rock bed on steep gradient".
Quick check
?Use the Tees table. Middleton is 211.2 m high and 37.72 km from the furthest source; Barnard Castle is 133.0 m high and 54.95 km from it. What is the average gradient between them, in metres of drop per km?
Mission 1

Weathering and mass movement

  • Weathering breaks rock down where it is: e.g. freeze–thaw (water freezes in cracks and expands) and chemical weathering by rainwater.
  • Mass movement: material moves downhill under gravity. Sliding: rock or soil moves along a straight slip surface. Slumping: saturated soil slips along a curved surface.
  • Both feed rock and soil into the river: its load.
Mission 1

Erosion

  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, pushing air into cracks in the bed and banks.
  • Abrasion: the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
  • Attrition: stones hit each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: water dissolves soluble rock, such as limestone.
  • Vertical erosion deepens the channel; lateral erosion widens it.
Mission 1

Transportation

  • Traction: large boulders roll along the bed.
  • Saltation: smaller stones and sand bounce along the bed.
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried in the water, making it look muddy.
  • Solution: dissolved minerals are carried, invisible.
  • Big boulders "may only be moved during rapid flows under flood conditions".
Quick check
?A river carries sand grains that bounce along its bed. Which process is this?
Mission 1

Why rivers deposit

A river deposits (drops) its load when it loses energy:

  • velocity falls, e.g. on the inside of a bend or where it enters a lake or the sea;
  • discharge falls after a flood or in a dry spell;
  • the water becomes shallow;
  • it has more load than it can carry.
  • The largest, heaviest particles are dropped first.
Mission 1

Why fine grains are hard to pick up

  • One textbook reading of a velocity graph: a 1 mm sand grain is picked up at about 20 cm/s; a 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s. Versions of the graph differ.
  • Fine grains, especially clay, "stick together": they are cohesive.
  • They also lie in a thin layer of slow water against the bed, which shelters them.
  • Once lifted, fine grains stay in suspension even in slow water, so they are deposited last.
Quick check
?A river slows from 30 cm/s to 5 cm/s as it enters a lake. What is most likely?
Mission 1

Processes change downstream

  • Upper course: steep gradient, large angular load: vertical erosion dominates.
  • Middle course: more discharge: lateral erosion on the outside of bends, deposition on the inside.
  • Lower course: most discharge, fine load: deposition dominates, especially in floods and near the mouth.
Quick check
?Which describes the upper course of a river such as the Tees near Middleton?
Mission 1

Storms, droughts and climate on the Tees

  • Storms: on 5 December 2015, during Storm Desmond (5–6 December), the Tees at Darlington reached its highest daily mean flow on record, 439 m³/s: about 24 times its mean. High velocity means more erosion and transport, and floodplain deposition.
  • Droughts: low discharge and velocity: little erosion, more deposition; gravel bars dry out and plants grow on them.
  • Climate: the wet uplands get more rain (1,585 mm a year above Middleton; 1,019 mm above Low Moor, 1991–2020), so upland streams have energy to erode.
  • The Tees is managed: its flow is "heavily influenced by reservoir storage and abstractions".
Mission check

?In a flood, water runs at 30 cm/s past a sandy bar and a bank of firm clay. The sand is being picked up, but the clay bank is barely eroded. Why?

Fine grains are cohesive and sheltered at the bed, so silt needs about 60 cm/s to be picked up, against about 20 cm/s for 1 mm sand. Once lifted, they are the last to be deposited.

Mission 2

V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs

  • In the upper course the river cuts down: vertical erosion.
  • Weathering and mass movement wear the valley sides back, giving a V-shaped valley.
  • The river winds around ridges of harder rock. Seen from upstream, the ridges overlap: interlocking spurs.
Mission 2

How a waterfall forms

  • A band of hard (resistant) rock lies over soft rock.
  • The soft rock erodes faster, so the hard rock is left overhanging.
  • Falling water and swirling rocks erode a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The soft rock is undercut; the unsupported overhang collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall back upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
Mission 2

Niagara Falls

  • Hard dolostones and limestones lie over weaker shales and limestones.
  • About 12,000 years ago the river took its present course; since then the falls have moved about 11 km (7 miles) upstream.
  • Recent retreat: about 0.9–1.5 m (3–5 feet) a year over the last 500+ years; now 0.3 m (1 foot) a year, largely because water is taken for hydroelectric power.
Quick check
?Niagara has retreated about 7 miles in about 12,000 years. 1 mile = 1.609 km. What was its average rate of retreat?
Mission 2

A UK waterfall: High Force

  • High Force, on the River Tees, "plunges over the Whin Sill", a hard rock called dolerite.
  • "Below the Whin Sill you can see layers of sandstone and limestone": softer rocks that are undercut.
  • "Torrential meltwaters carved gorges like those at Low Force and High Force".
  • This shows the role of geology: hard rock over softer rock.
Mission 2

Meanders

  • On a bend the fastest water swings to the outside.
  • Outside bank: lateral erosion cuts a steep river cliff.
  • Inside bank: slow water deposits sand and gravel: a gentle slip-off slope, built of a point bar.
  • In cross-section the channel is deep on the outside and shallow on the inside.
  • Learn the terms river cliff and point bar.
Mission 2

Oxbow lakes

  • Erosion on the outside of two bends narrows the neck of land between them.
  • In a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter route.
  • Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an oxbow lake.
Quick check
?In a cross-section across a meander, where is the river deepest?
Mission 2

Floodplains and levees

  • Floodplain: the wide, flat valley floor that floods. It is widened as meanders migrate and built up by layers of alluvium (silt) left by floods.
  • When the river floods, water slows sharply as it leaves the channel, so the coarsest sediment is dropped first, beside the channel.
  • Flood after flood, this builds raised banks: natural levees.
  • Finer silt and clay settle further out on the floodplain.
Quick check
?A river floods and spreads across its floodplain. Where is the coarsest sediment deposited?
Mission check

?An upland river flows over a band of hard sandstone lying on top of soft shale. Over thousands of years its waterfall moves upstream. Which explanation is correct?

Hard rock over soft rock, undercutting in the plunge pool, and collapse move a waterfall upstream. Niagara has moved about 11 km in about 12,000 years, leaving a gorge.

Mission 3

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph shows a river's discharge after a rainfall event. Rainfall is drawn as bars.
  • Rising limb: discharge increases. Peak discharge: the highest flow.
  • Falling limb: discharge drops back.
  • Lag time: the time from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow: the normal, slow flow from groundwater, below the storm water.
Quick check
?What is lag time?
Mission 3

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: basin 277 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234 km².
  • Rain on 23 December 2013: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow jumped from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, its highest daily mean since records began in 1958 (the record has some gaps).
  • Lambourn: 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. After about 317 mm more rain, it peaked at 9.53 m³/s on 15 February 2014.
Quick check
?The Beult's mean flow is 2.126 m³/s. Its peak on 24 December 2013 was 79.4 m³/s. How many times its mean flow was the peak?
Mission 3

Rock type: chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain soaks in, is stored as groundwater, and reaches the river slowly.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Little water can soak in, so it runs quickly to the river over and through the soil.
  • So geology controls the shape of the hydrograph: impermeable rock gives a short lag time and a high peak.
Mission 3

Other physical factors

  • Precipitation: intense or long-lasting rain gives more runoff.
  • Antecedent conditions: rain on already wet ground runs off faster. The Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed water to the river.
  • Basin shape and size: in a small, round basin water arrives together, so the peak is earlier.
  • Snow: a sudden thaw releases stored water quickly; frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission 3

Land use

  • Urban areas: roads, roofs and car parks are impermeable; drains carry water straight to rivers: shorter lag time, higher peak.
  • Forest: leaves intercept rain and roots help water soak in: longer lag time, lower peak.
  • Farmland: bare or compacted soil and field drains speed runoff; crops and grass slow it.
  • Land-use change and flood causes are covered in more depth in another mission.
Quick check
?Which basin would have the shortest lag time and highest peak after the same storm?
Mission 3

Exam move: describe, then explain

  • Describe using numbers from the graph: peak discharge, lag time, how steep the rising limb is.
  • Explain using named factors: geology, relief, soils, land use, rainfall.
  • Compare: here rainfall and basin size were similar, so geology made the difference.
  • Draw a hydrograph: time on the x-axis; discharge (m³/s) on the left y-axis; rainfall bars (mm) on the right y-axis. Label lag time from peak rain to peak flow.
Mission check

?Two UK basins of the same size get the same 40 mm storm. Basin X is on granite with thin soils. Basin Y is on sandstone that stores water. Which describes their hydrographs?

Geology. The Beult's clay sent the rain straight to the river (79.4 m³/s, about 37 times its mean). The Lambourn's chalk stored it and released it slowly; its February peak was a seasonal groundwater peak, not a storm lag time.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

Mission 4

Why rivers flood: physical causes

  • A flood happens when a river's discharge is more than its channel can hold, so water spills onto land that is usually dry.
  • Intense rain: falls faster than the ground can absorb. Long rain: soaks the ground until it is saturated.
  • Relief: steep slopes send water to the river quickly.
  • Impermeable rock (e.g. clay) stops water soaking in.
Quick check
?Which situation is most likely to cause a river flood without any human cause?
Mission 4

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) stop rain infiltrating, so more becomes surface runoff.
  • Drains and sewers carry the water straight to the river.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way.
Mission 4

Houston, 2017

  • Hurricane Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast for four days; the highest storm rainfall, in south-east Texas, was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches).
  • A study in Nature found that the city's growth made the flood response and the rainfall worse.
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely (25–30 August 2017).
  • One study of one event: strong evidence, but not proof for every city.
Quick check
?A new housing estate is planned upstream of a town. Which design would best stop it raising flood peaks downstream?
Mission 4

Towns in the UK

  • In England, about 4.6 million properties are in areas at risk of flooding from surface water (rain that cannot drain away).
  • The Environment Agency notes that "in some places urban drainage systems do not have the capacity to manage surface water".
  • Paving over gardens and building new estates add impermeable surfaces; building on floodplains raises exposure.
Mission 4

Farming, forests and industry

  • Deforestation: fewer leaves to intercept rain and fewer roots to open the soil, so more runoff and soil erosion. Eroded soil can fill river channels.
  • A review by FAO and CIFOR found that forests can reduce flooding from "frequent, low-intensity, short-duration storms".
  • But "forests cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods": in long rain, forest soils become saturated too.
  • Agriculture: heavy machinery and grazing compact soil; ploughing downslope and field drains speed runoff.
  • Industry: factories, yards and car parks add impermeable surfaces, like towns.
Quick check
?Which statement about forests and floods is best supported by the evidence on the last screen?
Mission 4

Impacts at different scales: Harvey

  • Local, social: 36 direct deaths in Harris County (Houston), out of at least 68 across Texas.
  • Regional, economic: over 300,000 structures flooded across south-eastern Texas.
  • National, economic: damage estimated at $125 billion; flooded oil refineries went offline for days and US petrol prices rose to their highest level in two years.
  • Environmental: floodwater spreads sediment and pollution, and erodes banks and habitats.
Quick check
?Which Harvey impact is national in scale?
Mission check

?Fields upstream of a town are replaced by houses, roads and storm drains that empty into a stream. The same storm now gives a bigger flood. How has the stream's hydrograph changed, and why?

Impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and lag time and raise peak discharge. In Houston, urbanisation also increased the rainfall, making Harvey-scale floods about 21 times as likely.

Mission 5

Two ways to manage floods

  • Hard engineering: building structures to control the river.
  • Soft engineering: working with natural processes, or changing how people use the land and prepare.
  • Hard schemes usually cost more to build and maintain; soft schemes usually cost less but may protect less.
Mission 5

Hard engineering

  • Dams and reservoirs: store floodwater upstream and release it slowly. Cost: very expensive; they flood land and trap sediment.
  • Straightening (channelisation): water leaves faster. Cost: it can send the flood downstream sooner and harms habitats.
  • Embankments or artificial levees (raised banks) and flood walls: hold more water in the channel. Cost: if they fail or are overtopped, flooding is sudden.
  • Deepening (dredging): more water fits in the channel, but sediment soon refills it.
  • Flood relief channels: carry extra water round a town. Cost: expensive to dig, and they protect only the town they bypass.
Mission 5

A UK flood relief channel: the Jubilee River

  • Built by the Environment Agency and opened in 2002: an 11.6 km man-made channel taking Thames floodwater round Maidenhead, Windsor, Eton and Cookham.
  • Benefit: it reduces flood risk to about 3,000 properties.
  • Limit: it "is not designed to reduce flood risk to communities upstream or downstream".
  • Landscape change: it was built as a "naturalistic river and habitats", so a new river now runs through former farmland.
Quick check
?A council straightens a meandering river through its town. What is the most likely cost?
Mission 5

Soft engineering

  • Flood warnings and preparation (flood plans, flood doors, moving valuables upstairs): cheap; they save lives and some property, but the water still comes.
  • Flood plain zoning (land-use zoning): keep houses off the riskiest land; use it for fields, parks or playing fields. Hard where towns are already built.
  • Planting trees (afforestation): trees intercept rain and soils absorb more. Cost: they take years to grow and the land is lost to farming. Most useful for smaller floods: forests "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Washlands: parts of the floodplain left to flood on purpose, storing water. Cost: that land floods, so it cannot be built on or farmed all year.
  • River restoration: return a straightened river to a natural course, so it floods safe land and slows down. Cost: land beside the river floods more often.
Mission 5

Flood warnings in England

  • Three levels from the Environment Agency:
  • Flood alert: "flooding is possible so you should prepare now".
  • Flood warning: "flooding is expected so you should act now".
  • Severe flood warning: flooding "could cause danger to life".
  • People can sign up to get warnings by phone call, text or email.
Mission 5

A UK soft scheme: Slowing the Flow, Pickering

  • In Pickering, North Yorkshire, the scheme planted 40,000 trees, restored heather moorland and built over 300 leaky dams in streams and drains.
  • It also built an upstream flood storage area (a small reservoir).
  • Over Christmas 2015 it cut the flood flow by 15–20%, about half from the land management and half from the storage area.
  • It "prevented the flooding of a number" of homes and the town's museum.
Mission 5

Zoning and new homes

  • In England, about 6.3 million properties are in areas at risk of flooding; this could reach about 8 million by the middle of the century.
  • Planning rules use a sequential test "to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding".
  • An exception test allows some building at risk only if, among other things, it is safe for its lifetime and does not increase flood risk elsewhere.
  • This is land-use zoning in practice.
Quick check
?Which measure is soft engineering?
Mission 5

The Dutch answer: Room for the River

  • The floods of 1993 and 1995, when more than 250,000 people had to leave, were "the direct reason" for the Room for the River programme.
  • Aim: "give rivers space again", not only raise dikes.
  • Works at 34 places along the Rhine branches; total cost €2.3 billion.
  • Methods: move dikes back, lower floodplains, dig high-water side channels, return reclaimed land to the river.
Mission check

?A town's river floods every few years. Planners want lower flood levels, no extra flooding downstream and more wildlife habitat. Which plan matches the Dutch approach?

The Dutch gave the rivers room. Dikes were moved back, floodplains lowered and side channels dug at 34 places.

Mission 6

The River Tees: source to sea

  • Rises in the North Pennines and flows south-east to the North Sea at Teesside.
  • Gauges show the fall: 211 m above sea level at Middleton-in-Teesdale, 12 m at Low Moor, about 85 km further on.
  • Upland course: moorland and dales. Lowland course: a broad, low plain around Darlington, Yarm and Stockton.
Mission 6

Why this landscape matters

  • Water supply: high rainfall and impervious rocks make the North Pennines a key catchment; reservoirs such as Cow Green supply Teesside.
  • Geology and wildlife: the North Pennines is a UNESCO Global Geopark; Upper Teesdale's rare "Teesdale Assemblage" of plants grows here.
  • The estuary: Teesmouth's mudflats and salt marsh are internationally designated for birds; heavy industry grew around the estuary.
Mission 6

The rock that makes the waterfalls

  • The Whin Sill is dolerite: molten rock that cooled underground about 295 million years ago.
  • It is hard, "resistant to erosion".
  • It sits above softer layers of sandstone, limestone and shale.
  • Where the Tees crosses it: High Force, Low Force and Cauldron Snout waterfalls.
Mission 6

Erosional landforms: waterfall and gorge

  • Water and the rocks it carries erode the softer rock beneath the dolerite faster (hydraulic action and abrasion).
  • A plunge pool forms; the hard cap is undercut, then collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
  • At Low Force, the Whin Sill "forms the rocky gorge beneath the bridge"; torrential meltwaters at the end of the ice age carved the gorges at Low Force and High Force.
Quick check
?Why does High Force exist where it does?
Mission 6

Lowland landforms: meanders

  • In the Tees Lowlands the "meandering, slow moving River Tees" crosses a broad plain.
  • Outside of each bend: faster, deeper flow erodes a river cliff.
  • The lower Tees winds between "steep, well-wooded banks".
  • Inside of each bend: slower flow deposits a slip-off slope (point bar).
  • Where it cuts through glacial deposits, the middle Tees forms "local bluffs with gorge like characteristics".
Mission 6

Depositional landforms: floodplain and estuary

  • Floodplain: in floods the river spreads out, slows and drops alluvium across the valley floor. The lowlands are covered in "glacial and alluvial drift material".
  • Estuary: where river meets sea, fine sediment settles out as mudflats, as at Seal Sands, with salt marsh and sand dunes nearby.
  • Upstream, Teesdale's grassy mounds are drumlins: clay and boulders shaped by moving ice, not by the river.
Mission 6

Processes at different scales

  • Hours: on 5 December 2015, during Storm Desmond, the Tees at Darlington reached its highest daily mean flow on record, 439 m³/s, about 24 times its average. Floods do most of the eroding and moving.
  • Years to centuries: meander bends migrate as cliffs erode and slip-off slopes grow.
  • Thousands of years: meltwater at the end of the last ice age carved the gorges; waterfalls retreat upstream.
  • Millions of years: the Whin Sill formed 295 million years ago.

Mission 6

How geology and climate shape the processes

  • Geology: hard dolerite makes waterfalls; impervious rocks mean rain runs off quickly into rivers.
  • Climate: the North Pennines has "high rainfall, low temperatures and strong winds". Average rainfall above Middleton is 1,585 mm a year; for the whole catchment above Low Moor, 1,019 mm.
  • More rain upstream means more water and energy to erode in the uplands.
  • Natural England expects "more frequent and intense rainfall" to increase erosion and "flood risk downstream".
Mission 6

People and the Tees: upstream

  • Reservoirs in the dales, including Cow Green, store water for Teesside.
  • The Tees's flow is "heavily influenced by reservoir storage and abstractions": people change how much water, and energy, the river has.
Mission 6

People and the Tees: downstream

  • Flood defences, including works at Croft-on-Tees and the Tees Barrage, have altered the river's natural course and flow.
  • The Tees Barrage (early 1990s) controls tidal flow and helped regeneration in Stockton, "but may also have influenced sedimentation in the estuary".
  • Much of Teesside's industry is on reclaimed land; mudflats and salt marsh have been lost to it.
  • Dredging in the estuary "may be depleting sediment" at Redcar, adding to coastal erosion.
  • On the tributary River Skerne, a section has been restored with new meanders.
Quick check
?Which change to the lower Tees was mainly caused by people working with river and tidal processes?
Mission check

?Another UK river rises on high, wet moorland made only of soft shale and sandstone, then crosses a wide, flat plain to the sea. Which landform is it least likely to have?

On the Tees, the hard Whin Sill over softer rock makes High Force and its gorge in the uplands; on the low plain, the slow river meanders, floods and drops its load in the estuary.

Mission 7: Between 1941 and 2004, Alaska's Muir Glacier retreated 12 km. All that time, its ice kept flowing towards the sea. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Between 1941 and 2004, Alaska's Muir Glacier retreated 12 km. All that time, its ice kept flowing towards the sea. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.11 past glacial processes in UK uplands, erosion (plucking, abrasion), transport on/within ice, deposition
  • 1.12 erosional landforms, truncated spurs, corries, glacial troughs, tarns, arêtes, hanging valleys, roches moutonnées
Mission 7

Britain under ice

  • The last British–Irish Ice Sheet was at its maximum around 27,000 years ago. The timing varied from place to place, between about 30,000 and 22,000 years ago.
  • It stretched from the edge of the continental shelf in the north to South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York.
  • Scotland, Northern Ireland, most of Wales and northern England lay under the ice. Most of southern England lay beyond it.
  • The ice melted away, but it left the UK's uplands carved and its lowlands covered in its deposits.
Quick check
?At the last glacial maximum, which of these places lay beyond the edge of the ice sheet?
Mission 7

Freeze-thaw weathering

  • Water seeps into cracks in rock.
  • When the water freezes it expands, pushing the crack wider. When the ice thaws, water seeps deeper into the crack.
  • Repeated freezing and thawing widens the crack until angular pieces break off.
  • On cold, bare upland slopes this supplies much of the rock that glaciers carry.
Mission 7

Glacial erosion: plucking and abrasion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto rock at the bed; as the ice moves on, it tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys".
  • Abrasion: rocks held in the base of the ice scrape the bedrock like sandpaper, leaving scratches called striations.
  • Plucking leaves rough, jagged rock; abrasion leaves smooth, polished rock.
Quick check
?A rock surface is smooth and polished, with long parallel scratches. Which process formed it?
Mission 7

How ice moves

  • Ice flows downhill under its own weight.
  • "Glaciers move by internal deformation of the ice, and by sliding over the rocks and sediments at the base".
  • "A glacier may slide on a thin layer of water at its base". This basal sliding makes erosion strong.
Mission 7

Moving forward, melting back

  • Snow falls high up, builds into ice, and the ice flows downhill.
  • At the snout, ice melts. If more ice melts than arrives, the snout retreats up the valley.
  • NSIDC: "Even as it retreats, the glacier still deforms and moves down slope, like a conveyor belt".
  • Retreat describes where the snout is, not which way the ice flows.
Mission 7

Transport

  • Glaciers carry rock on the ice (from freeze-thaw and rockfall on the valley sides), within it, and beneath it.
  • Learn the phrase: material is transported on and within the ice, and dragged along beneath it.
  • The ice carries everything from clay to huge boulders, then drops it "at the far edge of the glacier where the ice is melting".
Mission 7

Deposition: till and outwash

  • Glaciers deposit when the ice melts and can no longer carry its load.
  • Till: dropped directly by the ice. Unsorted: angular rocks of all sizes mixed together. In west Cumbria the tills are "extremely compact and stony" and "up to 10 m thick".
  • Outwash: carried away by meltwater streams, then deposited as the water slows. Sorted into layers, with rounded stones.
  • Example: "Extensive deposits of glaciofluvial sand and gravel were laid down in the Tyne Gap and Vale of Eden".
Quick check
?A pit in a lowland shows layers of rounded gravel and sand, each layer a similar grain size. How was it most likely deposited?
Mission 7

Landforms of erosion 1: corries, tarns and arêtes

  • Corrie: a deep, steep-sided hollow carved at the head of a glacier, with a rock lip at the front.
  • Tarn: a small lake left in a corrie after the ice melts. Red Tarn, below Helvellyn in the Lake District, covers 9.8 ha and is 25.6 m deep.
  • Arête: a knife-edged ridge between two corries. At Helvellyn, the corries are separated by the arêtes of Striding Edge and Swirral Edge.
Mission 7

Landforms of erosion 2: the glacial trough

  • Glacial trough: a glacier scours a V-shaped river valley "into a U-shape": steep sides, flat floor.
  • Truncated spurs: the ice cuts off the ends of the river's interlocking spurs, leaving cliffs along the trough.
  • Hanging valley: a smaller tributary glacier cuts less deeply, so its valley is left high above the trough. Cwm Idwal in Snowdonia is "a striking example of a glacial hanging valley".
  • Roche moutonnée: a rock outcrop, smoothed by abrasion on the side the ice came from and plucked steep and jagged on the downstream side.
Quick check
?A rock outcrop has a gentle, polished slope on one side and a steep, jagged face on the other. Which way did the ice flow?
Mission check

?Scientists drill a line of stakes into a glacier. Every year the stakes move several metres downhill, yet every year the snout is further up the valley. How can both be true?

A glacier is a moving store of ice. "A retreating glacier does not flow uphill; it simply melts faster than it flows". Muir Glacier's ice kept flowing towards the sea while its snout retreated 12 km.

Mission 8: A 16,500-tonne boulder sits on the flat prairie of Alberta, Canada, far from the mountains it came from. How did it get there?

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

Mission 8

Reading a glaciated upland

  • In glaciated mountains: "Massive U-shaped valleys, cirque lakes or tarns, horns, moraines, and arêtes".
  • Erosional landforms dominate the high ground, where the ice formed and was most powerful.
  • Depositional landforms lie lower down, where the ice melted and dropped its load.
  • Each landform is evidence of a process: read it like a clue.
Mission 8

Moraines

  • Moraine: till (unsorted rock debris) deposited by a glacier.
  • Terminal moraine: a ridge across the valley at the snout's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot … the larger this moraine will be".
  • Ground moraine: an uneven sheet of till spread across the valley floor as the ice melts back.
Quick check
?A curved ridge of unsorted boulders, sand and clay crosses a valley floor. It is the lowest of several such ridges down the valley. What is it most likely to be?
Mission 8

Drumlins

  • Drumlin: a smooth, oval hill of till, shaped beneath moving ice; often found in groups, a "basket of eggs".
  • Moving ice deposits till, then moulds and streamlines it as it keeps moving: deposition and erosion working together.
  • The blunt, steep end faces up-ice; the long, tapered end points the way the ice flowed.
  • The Vale of Eden, Cumbria, has elongate drumlins; their shape shows that ice "flowed down the Vale of Eden".
Mission 8

Crag and tail

  • A hard rock crag resists erosion; softer rock and sediment in its shelter form a long, sloping tail.
  • Edinburgh: the Castle stands on a basalt plug. Ice "moved from the west and impinged on the western face" of it.
  • The tail, made of sediments "protected by the 'crag'", forms the gentle slope of the Royal Mile to Holyrood.
  • Like drumlins, it shows both erosion and deposition, and the direction of ice flow.
Quick check
?In a field of drumlins, every hill has its steep, blunt end facing north-west and its tapered end facing south-east. Which way did the ice flow?
Mission 8

Erratics

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on a different type of rock.
  • Big Rock, Okotoks, Alberta: a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 9 m tall.
  • It came from a mountain formation in what is now Jasper National Park. A rockslide dropped it onto a glacier, which carried it to the plains between 10,000 and 30,000 years ago.
  • Erratics show which way the ice moved, and how far.
Mission check

?At Norber, in the Yorkshire Dales, hundreds of large, angular sandstone and siltstone boulders sit on limestone, more than 1 km from their source rock and more than 120 m above it. What is the best explanation?

The Norber boulders are erratics. Ice moved them "for more than 1 km and more than 120 m above their source outcrop". Big Rock is the same: a glacier carried it out of the mountains and left it on the prairie as the ice melted.

Mission 9: Walkers, sheep farmers, quarries and boats all want the same fells. Who gets to use the Lake District?

Make a guess: Walkers, sheep farmers, quarries and boats all want the same fells. Who gets to use the Lake District?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.13 how farming, forestry and settlement have affected physical processes in glaciated uplands
  • 1.13 pros and cons of development (water supply, renewables, tourism, conservation) and resulting landscape change
Mission 9

A glaciated upland that people use

  • The Lake District National Park in Cumbria covers 2,362 km². Ice carved its corries, troughs and ribbon lakes.
  • 38,993 people live there (Census 2021).
  • 17.45 million tourists visited in 2025.
  • It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site: "an unrivalled example of a northern European upland agro-pastoral system" (sheep and cattle farming).
Mission 9

Four ways to earn a living

  • Tourism: visitors spent £1.784 billion directly in 2025.
  • Farming: centuries of upland sheep farming, including the Herdwick breed. Thin soils, steep slopes and heavy rain suit grazing, not crops.
  • Forestry: woodland covers 13% of the park; 95 km² is owned or managed by the Forestry Commission.
  • Quarrying: slate has long been dug here. Honister Slate Mine sells slate and now also mine tours, a via ferrata and a zip wire.
Quick check
?Honister Slate Mine now runs mine tours, a via ferrata and a zip wire. What does this show?
Mission 9

When land uses clash

  • Tourism v. local people: demand for holiday homes pushes up house prices. In 2011, 24% of homes were holiday or second homes.
  • Tourism v. farming: walkers, dogs and cars share narrow lanes and grazing land with sheep and farm traffic.
  • Tourism v. jobs: tourism jobs are often seasonal and low-paid.
  • Traffic: visitors bring congestion, parking problems and litter.
Mission 9

Development v. conservation

  • UNESCO lists the risks: "development pressures, changing agricultural practices and diseases, and tourism".
  • Windermere: fast powerboats and water-skiing clashed with quiet enjoyment and wildlife. A 10 mph speed limit came into force in 2005.
  • Honister: new adventure attractions bring jobs, but add structures and visitors high on the fells.
  • The question is always the same: does new development (jobs, homes, attractions) harm what makes the place worth conserving?
Quick check
?Which is a conflict between development and conservation?
Mission 9

People change upland processes

  • Farming: heavy grazing removes vegetation, so less rain is intercepted and more runs over bare soil; soil erosion speeds up. Drainage ditches move water off the fells faster.
  • Forestry: trees intercept rain and their roots bind soil; but clear-felling leaves bare ground that erodes.
  • Settlement: roofs, roads and drains are impermeable, so rain reaches rivers faster.
  • Land managers around Windermere work to "reduce catchment run-off from rural and urban land".
Mission 9

Development: pros and cons

  • Water storage: Haweswater is a reservoir. It drowned the village of Mardale, which reappeared when the reservoir was 89% empty in the 1995 drought.
  • Renewable energy: high rainfall and steep streams suit hydro-electric power; pipes, buildings and power lines change a protected view.
  • Tourism: £1.784 billion of spending, but erosion and congestion.
  • Conservation: woodland planting and path repair change the landscape too, slowly and on purpose.
Quick check
?A fellside is clear-felled and then heavily grazed by sheep. What happens to the physical processes there?
Mission check

?A company wants to reopen a closed slate quarry inside a national park. It would create jobs, but lorries would use narrow valley roads used by walkers and farm traffic. Which statement best describes the issue?

The Lake District has 38,993 residents but 17.45 million tourists a year. Every use (farming, forestry, quarrying, tourism, water) competes for the same land, so the answer is managed sharing: rules, repair and planning decisions that weigh development against conservation.

Mission 10: Why does the land around Helvellyn look as if a giant scooped it out with a spoon?

Make a guess: Why does the land around Helvellyn look as if a giant scooped it out with a spoon?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.11 processes on today's relict glacial uplands: freeze-thaw weathering, mass movement (soil movement, rock falls/slides)
  • 1.11 how past climate and current UK weather and climate (seasonal, diurnal variation) affect processes in glaciated uplands
  • 1.14 one named UK glaciated upland (igneous here): significance of its location, how it formed, key factors in its change
Mission 10

The Lake District: where and why it matters

  • North-west England, in Cumbria. England's largest National Park and a UNESCO World Heritage Site; 2,362 km².
  • Scafell Pike (978 m), England's highest mountain, is here; Wastwater (74 m) is England's deepest lake.
  • The fells and lakes "radiate outwards" from Dunmail Raise, like the spokes of a wheel.
  • Significance: about 17.45 million tourists visited in 2025; the landscape is a "text book example" of glacial landforms.
Mission 10

The rocks

  • Skiddaw Group (north, about 500 million years old): sea muds, "crumpled and squeezed"; mostly smooth mountains.
  • Borrowdale Volcanics (centre, about 450 million years old): "very hard lavas and ashes" from eruptions. They form the "highest and craggiest mountains: Scafell, Helvellyn and the Langdale Pikes".
  • These peaks are not old volcanoes: they are "the harder volcanic rocks that have withstood erosion".
  • Windermere Group (south): softer sedimentary rocks, gentler scenery.
  • So this is an igneous (volcanic) glaciated upland.
Mission 10

How the ice came

  • About 2 million years ago Earth's climate cooled; glaciers and ice sheets "covered most of mainland Britain".
  • Up to 10 cold glacials alternated with warmer interglacials.
  • The last British–Irish ice sheet was at its biggest about 27,000 years ago. The last Ice Age ended about 10,000 years ago.
  • After the ice sheet melted, small glaciers returned to Helvellyn's hollows in a short cold spell, the Loch Lomond Stadial.
Mission 10

Erosional landforms at Helvellyn

The Lake District National Park's list of features around Helvellyn:

  • Corrie (local name "cove"): the east face of Helvellyn. There are nine corries on the eastern slopes; Nethermost Cove has a headwall 280 m high.
  • Tarn: Red Tarn, 9.8 ha and 25.6 m deep, fills a corrie.
  • Arête: Striding Edge and Swirral Edge, the ridges between corries.
  • Glacial trough: Grisedale. Ribbon lake: Ullswater.
  • U-shaped valley: Honister, "carved out of volcanic rock by glacial action".
Mission 10

How a corrie forms

  • Snow collects in a north- or east-facing hollow and turns to ice.
  • Plucking: ice freezes onto rock and tears it out as it moves.
  • Abrasion: rocks in the ice scrape the floor deeper.
  • The ice turns in a rotational slide, deepening the back and leaving a lip at the front.
  • When two corries cut back towards each other, the ridge between them becomes an arête.
Quick check
?At Helvellyn, which landform is Striding Edge?
Mission 10

Depositional landforms

  • Moraine: the lip of Red Tarn, debris dumped at the front of the corrie glacier.
  • Till: red, "extremely compact and stony" glacial deposits, up to 10 m thick, in north-west England.
  • Drumlins: elongated hills of till in the Vale of Eden and around the north of the Lake District.
  • Glaciofluvial sand and gravel, laid down by meltwater in the Vale of Eden.
Quick check
?Which pair gives one erosional and one depositional landform from the Lake District?
Mission 10

Processes today: freeze-thaw

  • The glaciers have gone, but the uplands are still changing. This is a relict glacial landscape.
  • Freeze-thaw: water in cracks freezes and expands, then thaws, "prying the rock apart".
  • The higher Lake District has over 75 days of air frost a year; ground frost on 75 to over 130 days in NW England.
  • Broken rock collects below cliffs as scree, e.g. on the south flank of Swirral Edge.
  • On the summits, frost heave sorts stones into patterned ground.
Mission 10

Processes today: mass movement

  • Soil movement: soil and stones creep or flow slowly downslope, especially when waterlogged or thawing. Helvellyn has "soliflucted scree" (scree moved by flowing, thawing soil).
  • Rock falls: blocks loosened by freeze-thaw drop from crags onto scree slopes.
  • Rock slides: slabs slip along cracks or layers, often after heavy rain.
  • Rain is heavy here: over 3,200 mm a year on the higher fells; 253 mm fell at Seathwaite in 24 hours on 19 November 2009.
Mission 10

Weather: seasons and days

  • Altitude: temperature drops about 0.5 °C for every 100 m of height.
  • Seasonal: in January, mean daily minimum temperatures are below 0 °C on the highest ground; snow lies on over 30 days a year in the Lake District uplands.
  • Diurnal (daily): the coldest time is usually around sunrise; the warmest is 2–3 hours after midday.
  • So in winter and spring, rock on the fells freezes at night and thaws by day, again and again: ideal for freeze-thaw.
  • Past climate made the big landforms; today's weather slowly breaks and moves them.
Quick check
?On a winter morning the valley floor is at 3 °C. A summit is 800 m higher. Using a fall of 0.5 °C per 100 m, what is the summit temperature?
Mission 10

What changes the landscape

  • Geology: hard volcanic rock makes high, craggy fells; softer rocks make gentler land.
  • Past climate: glaciers and meltwater carved and filled the valleys.
  • Today's weather: frost and heavy rain drive freeze-thaw, scree and mass movement.
  • People: clearing trees in the 11th century led to soil erosion; quarrying for slate and building stone; walkers' boots erode paths: Fix the Fells repairs 344 upland paths.
Mission check

?A walker in Snowdonia finds a deep, armchair-shaped hollow facing north-east, with a steep back wall, a small lake, and a low ridge of jumbled boulders holding the lake in. What best explains it?

The hollows around Helvellyn are corries. Small glaciers plucked and scraped them out of hard volcanic rock, left knife-edge arêtes between them, and dumped moraines at their lips.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Downstream: discharge, width and depth rise; gradient and load size fall (Tees: 211 m to 12 m high; 9.1 to 21.0 m³/s).
  • Four erosion and four transport processes; rivers deposit when they lose energy; fine cohesive grains resist erosion.

Mission 2

  • Erosion landforms: V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges; erosion and deposition: meanders and oxbow lakes; deposition: floodplains and levees.

Mission 3

  • Short lag time and high peak: impermeable rock, steep slopes, wet soil, round basins, urban land.

Mission 4

  • Physical causes: intense or long rain, saturated ground, steep relief, impermeable rock.
  • Human causes: urbanisation, deforestation, farming; they shorten lag time and raise peaks.

Mission 5

  • Hard engineering controls the river with structures; soft engineering works with nature and changes land use, warnings and preparation.

Mission 6

  • Upland Tees: hard dolerite (Whin Sill) over softer rocks gives waterfalls (High Force) and gorges; lowland Tees: meanders, floodplain, estuary mudflats (Seal Sands).
  • High rainfall and impervious rock drive the processes; reservoirs, flood defences, the Tees Barrage, reclamation and dredging now change the river and its estuary.

Mission 7

  • The last ice sheet reached South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York around 27,000 years ago. Ice erodes by plucking and abrasion, helped by freeze-thaw, and deposits till (unsorted) and outwash (sorted by meltwater).
  • Erosion carved corries, tarns, arêtes, troughs, truncated spurs, hanging valleys and roches moutonnées.

Mission 8

  • Deposition: ground and terminal moraine. Erosion and deposition together: drumlins and crag and tail, such as Edinburgh's Castle Rock and Royal Mile.

Mission 9

  • Glaciated uplands support tourism, farming, forestry and quarrying; these uses conflict with each other and with conservation.
  • Grazing, felling and building speed runoff and erosion; reservoirs, renewables, tourism and conservation all change the landscape.

Mission 10

  • Lake District: hard Borrowdale volcanic rocks make the highest, craggiest fells; Skiddaw Group rocks make smoother hills.
  • Erosional landforms at Helvellyn: corries (east face), Red Tarn, Striding and Swirral Edge (arêtes), Grisedale (trough), Ullswater (ribbon lake). Depositional: moraine at Red Tarn's lip; drumlins and till in the Vale of Eden.
  • Today: freeze-thaw (75+ frost days on high ground; night freeze, day thaw), scree, soil creep and rock falls; heavy rain. Change also comes from tree clearance, quarrying and footpath erosion.
