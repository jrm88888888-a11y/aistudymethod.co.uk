Mission 1

Three attitudes to other faiths

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true or leads to God.
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth, but others contain truth and their followers can be "saved" or righteous.
  • Pluralism: many religions are equally valid responses to God.
  • Rabbinic Judaism is often described as unusual: it is exclusive about the Torah's truth for Jews, but inclusive about salvation: the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2).
  • Many Orthodox Jews: inclusivist. Many Reform and Liberal Jews: closer to pluralism. Some Haredi Jews: exclusivist about truth, wary of any religious contact.

Each in the name of its god

> "For let all the peoples walk each one in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the LORD our God for ever and ever." (Micah 4:5, JPS 1917)

> "Have we not all one father? Hath not one God created us?" (Malachi 2:10, JPS 1917)

  • Micah's vision of peace (4:1–4) is followed by this verse about the nations' own ways.

  • Malachi's verse was originally about Israelites; it is now often quoted in interfaith settings for the shared humanity of all people.

Mission 1Diversity

Interfaith dialogue

  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) saw Christianity and Islam as helping spread monotheism; strong support for dialogue and joint action for tikkun olam.
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964): cooperate on shared moral and social concerns, but no debate about the content of faith (paraphrase).
  • Sacks, The Dignity of Difference (2002): one God made a diverse world; difference is a blessing (paraphrase). Some Haredi rabbis objected, and he revised passages in a 2003 edition.
  • Haredi: many avoid interfaith dialogue altogether.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can Judaism be truly pluralist?

  • Claim: Judaism can accept other faiths as equally valid without losing its own identity.
  • For: the teaching that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come means non-Jews need not become Jews; Micah 4:5 can be read pluralistically.
  • For: Reform and Liberal Judaism practise pluralism and keep a strong Jewish identity.
  • Against: chosenness and Sinai claim a unique revelation; full pluralism seems to dissolve it.
  • Against: even within Judaism, most Orthodox bodies do not recognise other movements' rabbis or conversions.
  • Judgement criteria: pluralism about salvation or about truth? Does respect require equal validity? Which movement?

Mission 2

The home: a small sanctuary

  • Judaism is lived as much at home as in the synagogue: Shabbat meals, festivals, kashrut, prayer, study.
  • The home is often called a mikdash me'at ("small sanctuary"): the table like the Temple altar.

> "and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thy house" (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917)

  • Parents are the first teachers of Torah; the Pesach seder is built around children's questions.
  • Shalom bayit (peace in the home) is a high value: family harmony is a religious goal.
  • Spec issue: is family life Judaism's main strength?
Mission 2

Traditional roles

  • Orthodox Judaism teaches distinct but complementary roles for men and women.
  • Women are exempt from most time-bound positive mitzvot (e.g. tefillin, fixed public prayer) (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:7); a common explanation is their role in the home and with children.
  • Three mitzvot traditionally linked with women (Mishnah Shabbat 2:6): niddah (family purity), separating challah from dough, and lighting Shabbat candles.
  • Jewish identity passes through the mother in Orthodox and Masorti Judaism.
  • Men: public prayer in a minyan, Torah study as a lifelong duty, leading services.
Mission 2Read the source

A woman of valour

> "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917)

> "She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and the law of kindness is on her tongue." (Proverbs 31:26, JPS 1917)

  • Eshet chayil ("woman of valour", Proverbs 31:10–31) is sung by many husbands and families at the Friday night table.
  • The woman in the poem runs a household, trades, buys a field and teaches with wisdom.

Q1 Which qualities does verse 26 praise?

Show answer

Wisdom and kindness in speech: teaching.

Q2 How could a feminist and a traditionalist read the poem differently?

Show answer

Traditional: honour for the wife's home role; feminist: praise only in relation to a husband's household, or evidence of women's economic power.

Mission 2

Why roles have changed

  • Society outside religion: women's education, paid work and leadership became normal in the 20th century.
  • Secular rights: UK votes for women (1918, equal in 1928); Sex Discrimination Act 1975; Equality Act 2010. Religious bodies have some exemptions, but expectations changed.
  • Different understandings of Torah and Halakhah:
  • Orthodox: halakhah is divine and binding; change happens only within it.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): Torah reflects its time; equality is a Jewish value that can override old rules.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhah binding but developing; many egalitarian rulings since the 1970s.
  • Interpretations of equality: equal dignity with different roles (Orthodox), or equal roles (Progressive)?

Mission 2

Agunah: the chained woman

> "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand, and sendeth her out of his house" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish divorce needs a get: a document the husband gives freely to his wife, before a beth din (rabbinic court).
  • A civil divorce alone does not end a Jewish marriage in Orthodox law.
  • Agunah (plural agunot): a "chained" woman whose husband refuses to give a get, or has disappeared.
  • She cannot remarry in Orthodox law; children of a new relationship would be mamzerim, restricted in whom they may marry.
  • A man without a get faces fewer consequences: the system is unequal.
Mission 2

Breaking the chains

  • Prenuptial agreements: the husband agrees in advance to pay daily support until he gives a get (e.g. used by the United Synagogue and in the USA).
  • UK law: the Divorce (Religious Marriages) Act 2002 lets a civil court delay the final divorce until a religious divorce is given.
  • Pressure: some batei din name refusers publicly; Israel's rabbinical courts can impose sanctions, even prison.
  • Masorti: a clause in the ketubah (marriage contract) committing both to the beth din (the "Lieberman clause", 1954); in some cases annulment.
  • Reform and Liberal: civil divorce, or their own egalitarian get, so the problem largely does not arise.

Mission 2

Minyan: who counts?

  • Minyan: a quorum of ten adults needed for parts of public prayer (e.g. Kaddish, Kedushah, Torah reading).
  • Orthodox: ten men over bar mitzvah; women may attend but do not count. A mechitzah (partition) separates seating.
  • Masorti / Conservative: a 1973 ruling allowed women to count; most UK Masorti communities now count women, some do not.
  • Reform and Liberal: women and men count equally; mixed seating.
  • Women's prayer groups and partnership minyanim (from 2002) let Orthodox women read Torah and lead some parts of the service, while ten men still form the minyan.
Mission 2

The Jewish Orthodox Feminist Alliance

  • JOFA, founded 1997 in New York by Blu Greenberg (b. 1936); a UK branch followed.
  • Aim: expand women's roles within halakhah. Greenberg's motto: where there is a rabbinic will, there is a halakhic way.
  • On agunot: promotes prenuptial agreements, supports International Agunah Day (the Fast of Esther), campaigns against get refusal.
  • On minyan: supports women's prayer groups and partnership minyanim; women's Torah reading and leadership in ritual.
  • Critics: many Orthodox rabbis say partnership minyanim break with tradition; some feminists say working within halakhah leaves women unequal.
Mission 2Diversity

Sexual and Jewish identity

  • Orthodox: gender roles and sexual norms are given by halakhah; same-sex sexual acts are forbidden, though many communities now welcome LGBT Jews as people.
  • Masorti / Conservative: practice varies; the US Conservative movement has approved same-sex wedding ceremonies since 2012.
  • Reform and Liberal: egalitarian roles; same-sex marriage celebrated; transgender Jews welcomed.
  • Family life: many Jews see a stable home as Judaism's strength, while recognising more diverse families today.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can women be equal to men in Judaism?

  • Claim: Women can be fully equal to men in Judaism.
  • For: Progressive Judaism already gives equal roles; women in the home are central to Jewish life.
  • For: JOFA and Masorti show change within halakhah is possible (prenups, partnership minyanim).
  • Against: in Orthodox law only men give a get or form a minyan: structural inequality remains.
  • Against: if equality means identical roles, much of traditional Judaism must change; many women choose distinct roles.
  • Judgement criteria: equal dignity or equal roles? Who decides: halakhah, the community, women themselves?

Mission 3

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • 1960s–70s: second-wave feminism changed women's roles in work, law and education. Many Jewish women asked why their religious lives had not changed too.
  • Their questions: women could not be counted in a minyan, lead most prayer, be rabbis, or end their own marriages.
  • 1971: Rachel Adler, then Orthodox, published "The Jew Who Wasn't There": women in halakhah treated like those exempt from many commandments.
  • 1972: the group Ezrat Nashim presented a "Call for Change" to Conservative rabbis.
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: it asks how texts, memory and images of God were shaped by men, and how they could include women.
Mission 3Read the source

Who was spoken to at Sinai?

Before the Torah is given, Moses prepares the people:

> "And he said unto the people: 'Be ready against the third day; come not near a woman.'" (Exodus 19:15, JPS 1917)

  • Judith Plaskow (b. 1947), Standing Again at Sinai: Judaism from a Feminist Perspective (1990), chapter 2.
  • Her reading (paraphrase): at the moment the covenant is made, "the people" are addressed as men. Women are present, but invisible as hearers.

Q1 Why does Plaskow find this verse so troubling?

Show answer

It is the central moment of Jewish memory, and the speaker seems to assume that the covenant community is male.

Q2 What does her book title suggest women must do?

Show answer

Stand again at Sinai: claim their own place in receiving and shaping Torah.

Mission 3

Plaskow: reshaping Jewish memory

  • Torah for Plaskow is more than the Five Books: it is the whole of Jewish memory and teaching, still being written.
  • That Torah is partial: it records men's experience and leaves women's mostly unspoken.
  • So women must reclaim Torah, not reject it, in three ways (paraphrase):
  • History: recover what women actually did in Jewish life, often missing from the record.
  • Midrash: tell new stories that fill the gaps, as the rabbis did. Her own example: "The Coming of Lilith" (1972).
  • Liturgy and ritual: new prayers and ceremonies that mark women's lives.
  • Covenant: the covenant is made with the whole people, so women must be full members of the community that receives it.

Mission 3

Margaret Wenig: God imagined as an old woman

  • Margaret Moers Wenig: Reform rabbi in New York; taught preaching at Hebrew Union College.
  • 1976: with Naomi Janowitz, Siddur Nashim, an early Sabbath prayer book using female language for God.
  • Sermon "God Is a Woman and She Is Growing Older" (first preached on Yom Kippur, 1990), paraphrase: God as an elderly mother waiting for her children to come home, sad at their absence, ready to forgive.
  • Roots in the Tanakh: "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you" (Isaiah 66:13, JPS 1917).
  • Her contribution: new images of God in prayer and preaching, so women (and older people) can see themselves in Jewish worship.
  • Critics, especially Orthodox: female images risk changing how God is understood; tradition already holds that God has no gender.
Mission 3

Traditional marriage: acquisition and the agunah

  • Jewish marriage begins with kiddushin. Classical halakhah describes it as kinyan (acquisition): "a woman is acquired in three ways", by money, by document or by intercourse (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:1, paraphrase).
  • Today: the groom gives a ring before witnesses; the ketubah sets out his duties to his wife.
  • Divorce: only the husband can give a get (bill of divorce): "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • If he refuses, or disappears, she cannot remarry under halakhah: she is an agunah ("chained"; plural agunot).
  • Defenders: kinyan is a legal form, not ownership of a person; the ketubah protects wives.
Mission 3

Adler: Brit Ahuvim, the lovers' covenant

  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Engendering Judaism: An Inclusive Theology and Ethics (1998), chapter 5.
  • Her diagnosis (paraphrase): a marriage built on acquisition makes one partner the subject and the other the object. The agunah problem follows from that.
  • Her proposal: Brit Ahuvim ("covenant of lovers"), a marriage between two subjects.
  • She builds it from a different part of halakhah: the law of partnership (shutafut), used for business partners.
  • In the ceremony both partners place a symbolic item of value into a shared bag and lift it together: a partnership, not a purchase.
  • Both make promises; neither acquires the other.

Mission 3

Dissolving and reconfiguring

  • Dissolution: a partnership can be ended by the partners. So a Brit Ahuvim can be dissolved without a husband's get: no one can be left an agunah.
  • Adler also proposes a ritual for ending, so that a broken covenant is marked honestly before the community.
  • Inclusive: the ceremony works for same-sex couples as well as for a woman and a man.
  • Reconfiguring the tradition (paraphrase): Adler does not invent from nothing. She reads Jewish sources again and rebuilds from materials already inside them.
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive responses

  • Orthodox: Torah and halakhah are given by God, so change must come within halakhah. Responses to agunot: prenuptial agreements that put pressure on a husband to give a get; rabbinic courts using sanctions.
  • Orthodox feminists (e.g. JOFA, founded 1997) push for women's learning and leadership within halakhah; some Orthodox women now serve as clergy (e.g. graduates of Yeshivat Maharat, from 2013). Many Orthodox rabbis reject this.
  • Conservative: the Lieberman clause (1954) added to the ketubah lets a rabbinic court act if a husband refuses a get.
  • Reform and Liberal: egalitarian worship, women rabbis, inclusive God-language, women's names (the matriarchs) in the Amidah. Many accept civil divorce or use egalitarian marriage documents.
  • Reconstructionist and Renewal: most open to Plaskow's new midrash and to ceremonies like Brit Ahuvim.
  • Critics of Plaskow and Adler: a covenant "reshaped" by each generation may stop being the covenant given at Sinai.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can Torah be reshaped and still be Torah?

  • Claim: Feminist theology like Plaskow's reshapes Judaism so far that it is no longer the same tradition.
  • For: Orthodox Judaism holds Torah as given by God (Torah min ha-shamayim); new texts cannot share its authority.
  • For: New God-language (Wenig) and new ceremonies may change what Jews mean by God and covenant.
  • Against: Judaism has always reinterpreted: midrash, Talmud and changes such as the ban on polygamy (attributed to Rabbenu Gershom, c. 1000 CE).
  • Against: Plaskow and Adler work with Jewish sources, aiming to include women in the covenant, not to leave it.
  • Judgement criteria: Who has authority to interpret? Is change of law different from change of memory and prayer? Do women in each movement feel included?

Mission 4

Diaspora

  • Diaspora (Greek, "scattering"): Jewish communities living outside the Land of Israel.
  • Two Hebrew words: galut ("exile", a punishment and a loss) and tefutzot ("dispersion", a neutral fact).
  • Began with the Babylonian exile (586 BCE); grew after the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE).
  • Today the two largest Jewish communities are in Israel and the USA; Britain has one of Europe's larger communities.
  • Every diaspora community faces one question: how to belong to the wider society and stay Jewish.
Mission 4Read the source

"Seek the peace of the city"

> "And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray unto the LORD for it; for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace." (Jeremiah 29:7, JPS 1917)

  • Jeremiah's letter to the exiles in Babylon: build houses, plant gardens, raise families there.
  • The Talmud's principle dina de-malkhuta dina ("the law of the kingdom is the law"): Jews obey the civil law of the land they live in, unless it forces them to break the Torah.
  • British synagogues pray each Shabbat for the Royal Family and the country.

Q1 What does Jeremiah tell exiles to do about the city they live in?

Show answer

Pray for it and work for its peace.

Q2 Why?

Show answer

Mission 4

Migration and Jews in Britain

  • 1070s: Jews come from Normandy after the Norman Conquest.
  • 1190: massacre at Clifford's Tower, York. 1290: Edward I expels all Jews from England.
  • 1656: Jews resettle under Cromwell, after petitions by Menasseh ben Israel; Sephardi Jews build Bevis Marks synagogue (1701), still in use.
  • 18th century: Ashkenazi Jews from Germany and Holland; the Board of Deputies (1760).
  • 1858: Lionel de Rothschild is the first practising Jew to sit in Parliament.
  • 1881–1914: Jews fleeing Russian pogroms settle in London's East End, Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow; the Aliens Act (1905) restricts entry.
  • 1930s: refugees from Nazism; the Kindertransport (1938–39) brings nearly 10,000 children.
  • After 1945: Jews from Iraq, Egypt, Iran, South Africa, Israel and, recently, France.
Mission 4

Diversity today

  • Census 2021: about 271,000 people in England and Wales gave their religion as Jewish (0.5%); many more are Jewish by family but not by religion.
  • Main centres: London (e.g. Barnet, Hackney's Stamford Hill), Greater Manchester, Gateshead, Leeds, Glasgow.
  • Orthodox (central): the United Synagogue (Chief Rabbi of the United Hebrew Congregations of the Commonwealth); the Federation of Synagogues; the Sephardi community.
  • Haredi: e.g. the Union of Orthodox Hebrew Congregations; Hasidic groups in Stamford Hill and Salford; the fastest-growing part of the community.
  • Progressive: Reform and Liberal (recently joined as Progressive Judaism); Masorti.
  • Many Jews are secular or "just Jewish": identity by culture, family and Israel, not synagogue.

Mission 4

How this context shapes Jewish thought

  • Integration models: Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), Chief Rabbi 1991–2013, The Home We Build Together (2007): society as a home all groups build together, each keeping its own identity (paraphrase).
  • The Dignity of Difference (2002): God is found in human difference, not only in sameness (paraphrase).
  • Enclave models: many Haredi communities limit contact with secular culture to protect Torah life.
  • Pluralistic Judaism: respect between Orthodox, Progressive and secular Jews, and with other faiths (own mission).
  • Freedom also allows assimilation: no law keeps anyone Jewish, so identity must be chosen.
Mission 4

Segregation or assimilation? Four pressure points

  • Kashrut: eating at work, school or friends' homes; kosher food costs more outside big centres. Too strict a line can isolate; dropping it removes a daily marker of identity.
  • Dress: kippah, tzitzit, Haredi black coats, married women covering hair. Visibility expresses faith; it can also attract antisemitic abuse (recorded by the Community Security Trust).
  • Practice: Shabbat and festivals clash with Friday-evening work, Saturday sport and some exam dates.
  • Education: Jewish state schools (often oversubscribed); Haredi independent schools, some in conflict with Ofsted over secular curriculum and unregistered settings.
  • Problems of segregation: isolation, poverty, misunderstanding. Problems of assimilation: intermarriage, loss of practice, shrinking communities.
Mission 4

The Jewish Leadership Council

  • Jewish Leadership Council (JLC), founded in 2003: brings together the leaders of major British Jewish organisations.
  • Members include communal bodies (e.g. the Board of Deputies, synagogue movements across Orthodox and Progressive) and major charities.
  • Role: strategy and co-ordination for the community: Jewish schools (through its schools network), welfare, security funding, and speaking with government alongside the Board of Deputies.
  • The Board of Deputies (1760) is the elected representative body; the JLC is a council of organisational leaders. Critics ask whether an unelected body should speak for the community.
  • Neither speaks for most Haredi communities, which have their own bodies.
Mission 4Evaluate

Does assimilation mean loss of identity?

  • Claim: For Jews in Britain, assimilation equals loss of Jewish identity.
  • For: intermarriage and dropping Shabbat and kashrut often lead, within a generation or two, to no Jewish practice at all.
  • For: Nehemiah's covenant shows separation as the historic defence of identity.
  • Against: many Jews integrate: full citizens at work and in public life, while keeping Shabbat, kashrut and Jewish schooling.
  • Against: identity can be cultural, ethnic or tied to Israel as well as religious; secular Jews often still identify strongly.
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as "Jewish identity": practice, belief, descent or belonging? Is integration different from assimilation?
  • Your spec's issues: "the possibility of assimilation into a secular society for Jews in Britain"; "the extent to which assimilation equates to a loss of identity".

Mission 5

The Shoah

  • Shoah ("catastrophe"): the murder of about six million Jews by Nazi Germany and its collaborators, 1941–45.
  • Centuries of Christian anti-Judaism and modern racial antisemitism prepared the ground.
  • 1933 Hitler in power · 1935 Nuremberg Laws strip Jews of citizenship · 1938 Kristallnacht, a nationwide pogrom (9–10 November) · 1941 mass shootings in the east · 1942 Wannsee Conference coordinates the "Final Solution" · death camps such as Auschwitz-Birkenau and Treblinka.
  • About one third of the world's Jews were killed; whole communities of Eastern Europe vanished.
  • Jews also use churban ("destruction"), the word for the destruction of the Temples.
Mission 5

Why it shakes belief

  • Classical Jewish belief: God is all-powerful, just and acts in history: God brought Israel out of Egypt.
  • The covenant: God chose Israel and promised to protect it; Israel keeps the mitzvot.
  • So where was God at Auschwitz? Four possible answers:
  • Punishment: a traditional prayer says "because of our sins we were exiled" (festival Musaf). A few Haredi leaders applied this to the Holocaust; most Jews find it unbearable.
  • Loss of faith: many survivors stopped believing.
  • God hidden or limited (see Mission 6).
  • A purpose within God's plan (Maybaum, later in this mission).

Mission 5

Rubenstein: the "death of God"

  • Richard L. Rubenstein (1924–2021), American rabbi, After Auschwitz (1966; 2nd edition 1992).
  • In 1961 a German Protestant dean, Heinrich Grüber, told him the Holocaust was part of God's will for the Jews. Rubenstein concluded: if God controls history, then Hitler was God's instrument, and he could not worship such a God.
  • So the God of history who chose Israel and acts in events is "dead" (paraphrase): belief in that God is no longer possible.
  • The covenant and chosenness die with it: being "chosen" made Jews a target.
  • "Death of God" describes a cultural event in human belief, not a claim that God once lived and then died.
  • Yet he kept Jewish community, ritual and festivals: they help people face life and death together.
Mission 5

After the death of God: Holy Nothingness

  • Rubenstein did not become a simple atheist.
  • God as the Holy Nothingness: the ground of all being, from which we come and to which we return; not a person who intervenes (paraphrase, influenced by Kabbalah's En Sof).
  • So Judaism still matters as a religious community, not as a covenant with a God who directs history.
  • He valued the land of Israel and a return to nature's rhythms.
  • Critics: many Jews felt he gave Hitler the last word by giving up the covenant (Fackenheim); others found his honesty more faithful than easy answers.

Mission 5

Maybaum: the third churban

  • Ignaz Maybaum (1897–1976), Reform rabbi from Vienna who led communities in Germany, came to London in 1939; his mother and two sisters were murdered. The Face of God after Auschwitz (1965).
  • Churban: a destruction that ends an old era and makes way for a new one.
  • 1st churban (586 BCE): Temple destroyed; exile spread Jews and their God among the nations.
  • 2nd churban (70 CE): sacrifice ended; the synagogue and rabbinic Judaism grew.
  • 3rd churban: the Holocaust ended the medieval world of East European Jewry and moved Jews into the modern world.
  • God's providential plan: God is still Lord of history, bringing progress through catastrophe.
Mission 5Read the source

The suffering servant

> "Surely our diseases he did bear, and our pains he carried; whereas we did esteem him stricken, smitten of God, and afflicted. But he was wounded because of our transgressions, he was crushed because of our iniquities" (Isaiah 53:4–5, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish tradition (e.g. Rashi) reads the servant as the people of Israel, suffering among the nations; Christians read it as Jesus.
  • Maybaum: Israel is the suffering servant; the six million died as an innocent sacrifice (vicarious atonement), not for their own sins.
  • Like the crucifixion for Christians, he said, Auschwitz should shock the Western world into turning from evil (paraphrase).

Q1 In verse 5, whose wrongdoing causes the servant's suffering?

Show answer

Others': "our transgressions".

Q2 Why does this suit Maybaum but not the punishment view?

Show answer

The servant is innocent; he suffers for others, not for his own sins.

Mission 5

"Hitler, my servant" and the remnant

> "Nebuchadrezzar the king of Babylon, My servant" (Jeremiah 25:9, JPS 1917)

  • Role of Hitler: as God used Babylon, Maybaum said, God used Hitler as an instrument, without making him any less guilty (paraphrase).
  • Not punishment: Maybaum explicitly rejects the idea that the victims were punished for sin.

> "A remnant shall return, even the remnant of Jacob, unto God the Mighty." (Isaiah 10:21, JPS 1917)

  • Remnant: the survivors, especially Jews in the West, carry Judaism into the new era.
  • Critics: making God use genocide for "progress" seems monstrous; the Christian imagery troubles many Jews; Rubenstein said the God of history could not be worshipped.

Mission 5Evaluate

Maybaum or Rubenstein?

  • Claim: Rubenstein's response takes the Holocaust more seriously than Maybaum's.
  • For: Rubenstein refuses to make Auschwitz part of God's plan; he faces the evidence.
  • For: Maybaum's "Hitler, my servant" and sacrifice for progress seem to justify the unjustifiable.
  • Against: Maybaum keeps the covenant and gives survivors hope and purpose, as the prophets did after 586 BCE.
  • Against: Rubenstein lets the Holocaust destroy Judaism's core belief; critics call this a posthumous victory for Hitler.
  • Judgement criteria: faithful to the victims? to Jewish tradition? morally acceptable? does it help Jews live after it?
Mission 5Worked answer

Comparing two responses

Question: "Compare Maybaum's and Rubenstein's responses to the Holocaust."

  • Shared starting point: both accept that the traditional God of history is challenged by Auschwitz.
  • Difference 1 (God): Rubenstein: the God of history is "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness. Maybaum: God still directs history (Jeremiah 25:9).
  • Difference 2 (covenant): Rubenstein: the covenant and chosenness end. Maybaum: Israel remains God's servant (Isaiah 53).
  • Difference 3 (meaning): Rubenstein: no purpose. Maybaum: churban as painful progress; the remnant goes on.

Mission 6

What the Holocaust did to the Jewish world

  • About one third of the world's Jews were murdered; Eastern Europe's Yiddish-speaking world of towns, yeshivot and Hasidic courts was largely destroyed.
  • The centres of Jewish life moved to the USA and to Israel (state founded 1948).
  • Antisemitism did not end: in Kielce, Poland, a pogrom in 1946 killed survivors returning home; Holocaust denial later spread.
  • Commemoration: Yom HaShoah (27 Nisan, set by Israel's Knesset in 1951), observed by many Jews; some Haredi Jews mourn the Holocaust on Tisha B'Av instead.
  • : Rubenstein and Maybaum. Now: Fackenheim, Berkovits, Wiesel.
Mission 6

Fackenheim: the commanding voice of Auschwitz

  • Emil L. Fackenheim (1916–2003), rabbi and philosopher; held in Sachsenhausen after Kristallnacht (1938); later a professor in Toronto; moved to Israel in 1983. God's Presence in History (1970).
  • Jewish tradition holds that at Sinai God's commanding voice gave the Torah's 613 commandments.
  • From Auschwitz, Fackenheim says, comes a commanding voice too: a new revelation, though without any explanation of why it happened.
  • Its command is the 614th commandment (first set out in 1967).
  • Eduqas: the Holocaust as a "new revelation experience of God".
Mission 6Read the source

The 614th commandment

Fackenheim, God's Presence in History (1970), summary of the commandment (paraphrase except the quoted phrase):

  • Jews are "forbidden to hand Hitler posthumous victories".
  • They must survive as Jews, lest the Jewish people perish.
  • They must remember the victims, lest their memory perish.
  • They must not despair of humanity and the world, retreating into cynicism.
  • They must not despair of the God of Israel, lest Judaism perish.

Q1 Why is giving up Judaism a "victory" for Hitler, on this view?

Show answer

Hitler aimed to end the Jewish people; Jews who abandon Judaism complete his work.

Q2 Does the commandment explain why God allowed the Holocaust?

Show answer

No: it commands a response, not an explanation.

Mission 6

Berkovits: the hidden face

  • Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), Orthodox rabbi (Berlin, Leeds, Sydney, Boston, Chicago; Israel from the mid-1970s). Faith after the Holocaust (1973).
  • Your spec spells his name Berkovitz.

> "Verily Thou art a God that hidest Thyself, O God of Israel, the Saviour." (Isaiah 45:15, JPS 1917)

  • Hester panim ("hiding of the face"): in Deuteronomy 31:17 God says "I will hide My face from them" as a response to sin; Berkovits uses the idea without the sin.
  • Free will: for humans to be truly free and responsible, God must hold back and not stop every evil act.
  • So the Holocaust is a human and historical event: the crime of the Nazis, not an act of God.
  • The hidden God is still the Saviour: Israel's survival through history shows God's presence.
Mission 6

Job and the modern Jew

  • Job lost everything and argued with God, but did not let go of God: "Though He slay me, yet will I trust in Him" (Job 13:15, JPS 1917).
  • Berkovits (paraphrase): those who were not in the camps are not Job; we are Job's brother. We must not judge either the faith or the loss of faith of those who suffered.
  • Both the believer who prayed in the camps and the one who lost faith in them are honoured.
  • No theodicy fully justifies the suffering; faith lives with the unanswered question.
  • Critics: if God's hiding cost six million lives, is human freedom worth that price? Why does God hide then and not at other times?

Mission 6

Wiesel: Night

  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016), from Sighet (Romania); deported to Auschwitz in 1944 at 15; survived Buchenwald; Nobel Peace Prize 1986.
  • Night (Yiddish 1956; French 1958; English 1960): his memoir of the camps.
  • On the first night, he says, the flames consumed his faith for ever (paraphrase).
  • At the hanging of a young boy, a prisoner asks where God is; a voice within Eliezer answers that God is there, on the gallows (paraphrase): faith dying, or God suffering with the victim?
  • At Rosh Hashanah he will not bless God: he feels like the accuser, and God the accused.
Mission 6

Wiesel: The Trial of God

  • A play (1979), set at Purim 1649 in the village of Shamgorod, after a pogrom.
  • Wandering actors arrive at an inn; the innkeeper Berish, whose family was murdered, puts God on trial.
  • No one will defend God, until a stranger, Sam, does so brilliantly. At the end he is revealed as Satan.
  • Point: easy defences of God can be diabolical; protest can be a form of faith.
  • Wiesel said it was inspired by a trial of God he witnessed in Auschwitz, where the judges found God guilty and then went to pray.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Judaism usually combines unique truth (the Torah for Israel) with open salvation (the righteous of the nations, through the Noahide laws).
  • Orthodox views range from Haredi caution to Soloveitchik's limited cooperation and Sacks' "dignity of difference"; Reform and Liberal Jews lean to pluralism.

Mission 2

  • The home is a "small sanctuary"; Orthodox Judaism teaches complementary roles, Progressive Judaism equal roles; society, secular rights and readings of Torah drive the debate.
  • An agunah is "chained" when a husband refuses a get; remedies include prenups, the UK 2002 Act and court pressure; Reform and Liberal Jews avoid the problem.
  • Minyan: Orthodox counts ten men; most Masorti and all Reform and Liberal communities count women; Orthodox feminists (JOFA) seek change within halakhah.

Mission 3

  • Plaskow: women were unseen at Sinai (Exodus 19:15); Torah must be reshaped through history, midrash and ritual.
  • Wenig: new images of God, such as God as an ageing mother.
  • Adler: traditional marriage rests on acquisition, which leaves agunot; Brit Ahuvim is a partnership of equals that the partners can dissolve.

Mission 4

  • The diaspora began with the Babylonian exile and grew after 70 CE; a portable Judaism of Torah, prayer and synagogue let Jews live anywhere (Jeremiah 29:7; dina de-malkhuta dina).
  • Jews in Britain: medieval community, expulsion (1290), resettlement (1656), migration waves; diverse today (Orthodox, Haredi, Progressive, secular); freedom of religion under Article 9 and the Equality Act.
  • Segregation and assimilation both bring problems in kashrut, dress, practice and education; the JLC co-ordinates communal strategy.

Mission 5

  • The Shoah challenges belief in a just, all-powerful God who acts in history and keeps covenant with Israel.
  • Rubenstein (After Auschwitz, 1966): the God of history and the covenant are "dead"; God as Holy Nothingness; Jewish community still matters.
  • Maybaum (The Face of God after Auschwitz, 1965): the third churban; Hitler as God's instrument; Israel as suffering servant (vicarious atonement); the remnant carries Judaism into a new age.

Mission 6

  • Fackenheim: from Auschwitz comes a commanding voice: the 614th commandment forbids handing Hitler posthumous victories (survive, remember, do not despair of humanity or of God); Israel as return into history.
  • Berkovits: hester panim; God hides to allow human free will, so the Holocaust is a human crime; we are "Job's brother".
  • Wiesel: Night shows faith wounded and protesting; The Trial of God (Eduqas) gives God's defence to Satan.
