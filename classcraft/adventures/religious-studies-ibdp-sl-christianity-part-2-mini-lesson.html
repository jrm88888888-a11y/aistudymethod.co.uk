Make a guess: Doctors sometimes bring back the dead. Is that what Christians mean by Jesus' resurrection?

Mission 1

What is at stake

  • Paul makes the resurrection the hinge of Christian faith:
  • "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Three separate questions:
  • Did it happen? (history)
  • Can a historian show it? (method)
  • What kind of event was it? (theology: a body revived, a body transformed, or a change in the disciples)
  • Christians answer these differently. This mission maps the answers.
Mission 1

The scientific challenge

  • Modern science studies a world of regular natural laws. A dead man rising looks like the clearest violation of them.
  • David Hume (1711–1776), An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding (1748), Section X: "it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed in any age or country."
  • So, for Hume, uniform experience always outweighs testimony to such an event.
  • Ernst Troeltsch (1865–1923), "On Historical and Dogmatic Method" (1898): historians judge the past by analogy with the present. Dead people do not rise now, so a historian cannot conclude that one did then (paraphrase).
  • Reply from many believers: the resurrection claims to be unique, an act of God, so analogy cannot rule it out in advance.
Mission 1

Experience: Bultmann

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941).
  • The New Testament world-view is mythical (heaven above, demons, miracles). Modern people cannot share it, so the message must be demythologised: interpreted for what it says about human existence.
  • The resurrection is not an event of past history that a historian could check (paraphrase). Easter is the rise of faith in the disciples: they came to see the cross as God's saving act.
  • The risen Christ meets people now in the kerygma (the preached message), which calls for a decision of faith.
  • Related: Willi Marxsen (1919–1993), The Resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth (1968): "the cause of Jesus continues" (paraphrase).

Mission 1

N. T. Wright: the best explanation

  • N. T. Wright (b. 1948), The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003).
  • In Jewish and pagan usage, "resurrection" meant a new bodily life after death, not a spirit surviving. Wright's phrase: "life after life after death".
  • Early Christians held a remarkably consistent, changed form of Jewish hope: one man raised before the end of the age, in a transformed body (Wright: "transphysical").
  • Historian's question: what explains this belief?
  • An empty tomb alone suggests theft; visions alone would be read as a ghost. Together, the empty tomb and the meetings with Jesus are, in his view, the best historical explanation (paraphrase).

> "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44, WEBBE)

  • Luke also says the risen Jesus vanished (24:31) and stood among them suddenly (24:36).

Q1 Which features in Luke stress continuity with the body that died?

Show answer

Q2 What does Paul's contrast suggest?

Show answer

Mission 1

Resuscitation or resurrection

  • Resuscitation: a dead body returns to the same mortal life. The person will die again: the widow's son at Nain (Luke 7:15), Jairus' daughter, Lazarus (John 11:44).
  • Resurrection: raised to a new, transformed, imperishable life beyond death.
  • "Christ, being raised from the dead, dies no more. Death no longer has dominion over him!" (Romans 6:9, WEBBE)
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 646): Christ's resurrection was not a return to earthly life like the raisings he worked (paraphrase).
  • Contemporary critique: some read Luke's fish and flesh as a crude "resuscitation"; others (Rudolf Bultmann, 1884–1976, who read Easter as the rise of the disciples' faith; John Shelby Spong, 1931–2021, Resurrection: Myth or Reality?, 1994) reject a bodily event altogether and read resurrection as transformed faith.

Mission 1Evaluate

Can history decide the resurrection?

  • Claim: Historical study can show whether Jesus rose from the dead.
  • For (Wright): historians explain events by their best causes; early belief, the empty tomb and the meetings need explaining, and natural theories fail.
  • For: Paul's early list (1 Corinthians 15:3–8) names witnesses, some still living: a claim open to checking.
  • Against (Bultmann): faith built on historical proof is no longer faith; the resurrection's meaning is met in decision, not evidence.
  • Against (Troeltsch, Hume): history works by analogy and probability; a unique act of God lies outside its method.
  • Weaknesses each way: Bultmann seems to cut loose from what Paul meant (1 Corinthians 15:14); Wright's "best explanation" depends on whether God's action is allowed as an explanation.
  • Judgement criteria: What can historians prove? What does faith need? What did the first witnesses claim?

Mission 2: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Make a guess: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Mission 2

The puzzle

  • Christians inherited strict monotheism: "The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Yet the New Testament shows early Christians worshipping Jesus: in John's Gospel, Thomas calls him "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • So: is Jesus God, or something less than God?
  • In the 4th century this became a crisis that split the Church and reached the emperor.
Mission 2

Arius: the Son is created

  • Arius (c. 256–336), a priest in Alexandria, Egypt; dispute from c. 318.
  • God alone is unbegotten, unchanging, without beginning.
  • So the Son (the Word, Logos) must have a beginning: created by God before time, the first and highest creature, through whom God made everything else.
  • Slogan of his party, condemned at Nicaea and attacked by Athanasius: "there was when he was not".
  • Texts he used: "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15); "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28); Proverbs 8:22 (Wisdom "created" in the Greek version).
  • His aim: to protect the oneness and changelessness of God.
Mission 2

Athanasius: only God can save

  • Athanasius (c. 296–373), deacon at Nicaea, then bishop of Alexandria from 328; exiled five times.
  • On the Incarnation (c. 318 or c. 335; date debated).
  • Humans, made in God's image, were sliding back into death and corruption through sin.
  • Only the Creator can re-create: a creature cannot restore the image of God.
  • "For He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54): sharing God's life (theosis, deification).
  • So if the Son is a creature, we are not saved. The Son must be fully God.
Mission 2Read the source

The Word before the world

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, WEBBE)

> "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us." (John 1:14, WEBBE)

> "who, existing in the form of God, didn't consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6–7, WEBBE)

Q1 Which phrase in John 1:1 did Athanasius' side stress?

Show answer

"The Word was God."

Q2 What does Philippians 2 say the Son did to become human?

Show answer

He "emptied himself", taking the form of a servant.

Mission 2

Nicaea, 325

  • Emperor Constantine called the first ecumenical (worldwide) council to end the dispute: roughly 250–300 bishops at Nicaea (ancient counts vary; tradition says 318).
  • The creed of Nicaea: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father".
  • Key word homoousios: of the same substance (being) as the Father; not homoiousios, "of similar substance".
  • So the Son is co-equal with the Father and has no beginning.
  • Arius was condemned and exiled. Arian views stayed strong for decades; the Council of Constantinople (381) confirmed Nicaea (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Read the source

The Chalcedonian Definition (451)

  • After Nicaea (Jesus is fully God), the Church asked how he is also fully human. The Council of Chalcedon (451) answered:

> "one and the same Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, to be acknowledged in two natures, inconfusedly, immutably, indivisibly, inseparably"

  • Two natures: fully divine and fully human, "truly God and truly man".
  • One person (hypostasis): not two sons, not a mixture.
  • Four "without"s: no confusion (against Eutyches), no change, no division, no separation (against Nestorius).
  • Eutyches said Jesus' human nature was absorbed into the divine; Nestorius seemed to split Christ into two persons.

Q1 Which phrase rules out Jesus being a blend of God and man?

Show answer

"Inconfusedly".

Q2 Which phrase rules out two separate persons?

Show answer

"Indivisibly, inseparably".

Mission 2Diversity

Who accepts Chalcedon?

  • Accept: Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
  • Oriental Orthodox (Coptic, Armenian, Ethiopian, Eritrean, Syriac, Malankara): did not accept Chalcedon. They teach one united nature of the Word incarnate (miaphysite), fully divine and fully human, and reject the label "monophysite" (the view of Eutyches). Joint statements with the Eastern Orthodox (1989, 1990) found the faith largely shared.
  • Church of the East (Assyrian): did not accept Ephesus (431, the council that condemned Nestorius); its 1994 Common Christological Declaration with Rome affirmed a shared faith in Christ.
  • Non-Trinitarian groups today: e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses hold that Jesus is God's first creation, a view close to Arius'.

Mission 3: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Make a guess: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Mission 3

One God, three persons

  • The Trinity: one God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Christians believe each person is fully God, yet there is one God, not three.
  • "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three views the Church rejected:
  • Tritheism: three separate gods.
  • Modalism: one person appearing in three modes (associated with Sabellius, 3rd century).
  • Subordinationism: the Son and Spirit as lesser than the Father.
Mission 3Read the source

Biblical basis

The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Christians find the pattern in it:

> "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, WEBBE)

> "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all." (2 Corinthians 13:14, WEBBE)

  • At Jesus' baptism, the Spirit descends "as a dove" and the Father's voice speaks (Matthew 3:16–17).

Q1 In Matthew 28:19, how many "names" are there?

Show answer

One "name", shared by three.

Mission 3

The Son and the Spirit as God

  • Son: "the Word was God" (John 1:1); "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • Spirit: lying to the Holy Spirit is lying "to God" (Acts 5:3–4); "Where could I go from your Spirit?" (Psalm 139:7).
  • Christians read the Spirit as personal: teaches and reminds (John 14:26); not just a force.
  • Early Christians worshipped and baptised in the threefold name before the doctrine was defined.
  • The doctrine explains what Christians already did in prayer.

Mission 3

From Nicaea to Constantinople

  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios (of one substance) with the Father (see Mission 2).
  • Cappadocian Fathers: Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395).
  • Their formula: one ousia (being, substance), three hypostaseis (persons).
  • Constantinople (381) completed the creed: the Spirit is "the Lord and Giver of life", who "proceeds from the Father", and "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified".
  • This Nicene Creed is shared by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
Mission 3

The filioque

  • Latin filioque: "and the Son". The West added it to the creed: the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son.
  • First used in Spain (Council of Toledo, 589); spread in the Frankish church; sung at Mass in Rome from 1014.
  • West (Augustine, On the Trinity): the Spirit is the bond of love between Father and Son; "the Spirit of his Son" (Galatians 4:6).
  • East (Photius, 9th century): the Father is the one source (monarchia) of Son and Spirit; John 15:26 says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father".
  • East also objected that a council's creed was changed without an ecumenical council.
  • A cause of the East–West Schism (1054), with papal authority.
  • Today: the Catholic Church omits the filioque when it uses the Greek creed; Lambeth Conferences (1978, 1988) asked Anglicans to consider omitting it.

Mission 4

What is the soul?

  • Hebrew Bible: a human is a "living soul" (Hebrew nephesh): dust given God's breath (Genesis 2:7). The soul is the whole living person, not a separate part.
  • Greek thought (Plato, Phaedo): the soul is immortal and is freed from the body at death.
  • Christian dualism: the soul is created by God, spiritual, and survives death; Jesus distinguishes those who "kill the body" from the soul they cannot kill (Matthew 10:28).
  • Aquinas (1225–1274): the soul is "the form of the body" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 76, a. 1): it makes the body a living human. A soul without its body is not a complete person.
  • Some modern Christians are physicalists: no separate soul; God re-creates the whole person (e.g. Nancey Murphy, Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?, 2006).
Mission 4

Resurrection, not just immortality

  • Christian hope is the resurrection of the body, not only the survival of a soul.
  • The Apostles' Creed in Latin says carnis resurrectionem: "the resurrection of the flesh".
  • Roots: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2).
  • Jesus' resurrection is the "firstfruits": the pattern of what will happen to believers (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Traditional timeline: at death the soul goes to God; at the last day the dead are raised and soul and body are reunited.
  • The key question: what will the raised body be like?
Mission 4

Augustine: the resurrection of the flesh

  • Augustine (354–430), City of God (413–426), Book XXII; also the Enchiridion.
  • The same flesh will rise: whatever was lost from the body, in life or after death, will be restored (XXII.21).
  • Even a body burnt, eaten or scattered is not beyond the Creator's power: "not a hair of its head shall perish" (XXII.20–21; Luke 21:18).
  • Bodies will be perfected: deformities and blemishes removed (XXII.19); everyone raised at the age of full maturity, about thirty, Christ's age (XXII.15).
  • Sex remains: women rise as women, though there is no marriage (XXII.17; Matthew 22:30).
Mission 4Read the source

1 Corinthians 15

> "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:42–44, WEBBE)

> "flesh and blood can't inherit God's Kingdom" (1 Corinthians 15:50, WEBBE)

> "For this perishable body must become imperishable, and this mortal must put on immortality." (1 Corinthians 15:53, WEBBE)

  • Paul's image (15:36–38): a seed is sown and a plant rises. Continuity, but transformation.
  • 15:51–52: "we will all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye".

Q1 List Paul's four contrasts.

Show answer

Perishable/imperishable; dishonour/glory; weakness/power; natural/spiritual.

Q2 Does the seed image suggest the same body, or a different one?

Show answer

Both: the same seed, but a body as different as a plant.

Mission 4Diversity

Christian views today

  • Catholic: at death the soul is judged and separated from the body; at the last day God reunites it with its own, transformed body (Catechism 997–1001). The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) taught that all will rise with their own bodies.
  • Orthodox: the same hope; the body sanctified now through the sacraments; icons and relics honour the body's destiny.
  • Many Protestants: the soul is with Christ at death (Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23); bodily resurrection at Christ's return.
  • Soul sleep / conditional immortality (e.g. Seventh-day Adventists, Christadelphians): no immortal soul; the dead "sleep" until resurrection.
  • Liberal Christians: may hold the soul's survival or a spiritual resurrection, reading bodily language as symbolic.
  • All share the belief that death is not the end and that the whole person, in some form, is raised by God.
Mission check

?A Christian woman whose husband was lost at sea says: "His body is gone, but I believe God will raise him, changed but still him." Which belief best fits her words?

Christians hold together continuity (the same person) and transformation (a glorified body). Augustine and spiritual-resurrection readings differ on how much of the old body is kept.

Mission 5: How did a small prayer meeting in Los Angeles become a quarter of world Christianity?

Mission 5

Roots: Topeka and Azusa Street

  • 1901, Topeka, Kansas: at Charles Parham's Bible school, Agnes Ozman speaks in tongues. Parham teaches tongues as the "initial evidence" of baptism in the Spirit.
  • 1906–c. 1909, Azusa Street, Los Angeles: William J. Seymour (1870–1922), an African American preacher, leads a revival in a former stable building. Meetings for hours each day, Black and white, rich and poor, women and men together: unusual in segregated America.
  • Missionaries and a newspaper, The Apostolic Faith, spread it to Europe, Africa, Asia and Latin America within a few years.
  • Pentecostal denominations form: Church of God in Christ (Pentecostal from 1907), Assemblies of God (USA, 1914), Elim (UK, 1915).
  • Roots also in the Holiness movement and earlier revivals (e.g. Wales, 1904–05).
Mission 5Read the source

The Spirit at Pentecost

> "Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them. They were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak with other languages, as the Spirit gave them the ability to speak." (Acts 2:3–4, WEBBE)

> "But to each one is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the profit of all." (1 Corinthians 12:7, WEBBE)

  • Paul then lists gifts: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healings, miracles, prophecy, discerning of spirits, languages (tongues) and their interpretation (1 Corinthians 12:8–10).

Q1 Why do Pentecostals take their name from Acts 2?

Show answer

They expect the same filling with the Spirit, with tongues, today.

Q2 According to 1 Corinthians 12:7, who are the gifts for?

Show answer

For the good of the whole community, not for private status.

Mission 5

Main beliefs

  • Baptism in the Holy Spirit: a powerful experience of being filled with the Spirit. Classical Pentecostals: a second experience after conversion, usually shown by speaking in tongues (glossolalia).
  • Gifts of the Spirit (charismata, hence "charismatic") are for today: tongues, prophecy, healing, words of knowledge.
  • The "Fourfold Gospel": Jesus as Saviour, Baptiser in the Spirit, Healer and Coming King.
  • The Bible as the authority; personal conversion; evangelism.
  • Worship: lively, participatory, expectant: raised hands, spontaneous prayer, testimony, healing prayer.
  • Not all Pentecostals agree that tongues are the necessary evidence; many charismatics do not.
Mission 5

The Charismatic Movement after 1960

  • Charismatic = Pentecostal-style experience inside the historic churches, without leaving them.
  • 1960: Dennis Bennett, an Episcopal priest in Van Nuys, California, tells his congregation he has spoken in tongues; the news spreads nationally.
  • 1964 (UK): Anglican Michael Harper founds the Fountain Trust to support renewal across denominations.
  • 1967: the Duquesne Weekend (Pittsburgh) begins the Catholic Charismatic Renewal.
  • 1980s–90s: John Wimber's Vineyard churches ("signs and wonders"); the "Toronto Blessing" (1994); the Alpha course (Holy Trinity Brompton, London) with its Holy Spirit weekend.
  • Charismatics in sacramental churches often see Spirit baptism as a release of grace already given in baptism and confirmation, not a second blessing.
Mission 5

Global growth

  • Evangelicalism: defined by historian David Bebbington (Evangelicalism in Modern Britain, 1989) by four marks: Bible authority, the cross, conversion, activism. Global networks such as the Lausanne Movement (1974).
  • Pentecostal and Charismatic Christians: about 584 million in 2010, over a quarter of all Christians (Pew Research Center, 2011).
  • US: Assemblies of God and Church of God in Christ; televangelism and megachurches; strong growth among Hispanic Americans.
  • Beyond: Latin America (e.g. Brazil's Assemblies of God), Africa (e.g. Nigeria, Ghana), Asia (e.g. Yoido Full Gospel Church, Seoul). Most Pentecostals now live in the Global South.
  • Why it spreads: direct experience, healing, lay leadership, local languages and music, help for people moving to cities.

Mission 5

Challenge 1: can the experience be verified?

  • Verification: a claim is meaningful or justified only if it can be checked by experience others can test (logical positivism: A. J. Ayer, 1910–1989, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936).
  • A person who says "the Holy Spirit filled me" reports a private experience. How could anyone else, or even the person, check that the cause is God?
  • Counterfeits: Paul himself requires "discerning of spirits" (1 Corinthians 12:10) and order in worship (1 Corinthians 14:27, 40).
  • Response: Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): the principle of credulity: trust how things seem unless there is a special reason not to; and the principle of testimony: believe others' reports unless there is reason to doubt them.
  • Reply: experiences of God are unlike sense experience: there are no agreed checks, so credulity may not apply.
Mission check

The same experiences of the Spirit can lead people out into new churches (Pentecostal) or renew the churches they already belong to (Charismatic).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Bultmann: the resurrection is the rise of faith, met now in the kerygma; Wright: the empty tomb and the meetings together best explain early belief in a transformed bodily resurrection.
  • Resuscitation returns a person to mortal life; resurrection, as Christians mean it, is a transformed life that cannot die.

Mission 2

  • Arius: the Son is the first, highest creature ("there was when he was not"). Athanasius: only God can re-create and save, so the Son is fully God.
  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios, "of one substance with the Father", "begotten, not made".
  • Chalcedon (451): one person in two natures, without confusion, change, division or separation; accepted by most churches, not by the Oriental Orthodox.

Mission 3

  • Christians believe in one God in three co-equal, co-eternal persons; not three gods, not three modes, not ranked.
  • Biblical basis: the threefold name (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14), the divinity of Son and Spirit, and Jewish monotheism (Deuteronomy 6:4).
  • Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) defined it; the West's filioque helped divide East and West.

Mission 4

  • Soul: Hebrew whole-person view; Greek immortal soul; Christian dualism (soul survives); Aquinas: soul as form of the body.
  • 1 Corinthians 15:42–44, 50–54: sown perishable, raised imperishable; "spiritual body"; flesh and blood cannot inherit; all will be changed.

Mission 5

  • Pentecostalism: Topeka (1901) and Azusa Street (1906, Seymour); Spirit baptism, tongues, healing, the gifts of 1 Corinthians 12.
  • Charismatic Movement (from 1960): renewal within historic churches (Bennett, Fountain Trust, Catholic Renewal 1967).
