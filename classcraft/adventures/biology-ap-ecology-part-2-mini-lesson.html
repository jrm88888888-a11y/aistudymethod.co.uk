Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Make a guess: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Mission 1

Less at every level

  • Each trophic level passes on only a small share of its energy: often around 10%, measured from about 5% to 20%.
  • After 3 or 4 transfers (4 or 5 levels), too little is left to support another population.
  • So top predators are few, and each needs a large area to find enough food.
  • Eat or harvest lower in a chain and fewer transfers come between the producers and you: more of their energy reaches you as food.
  • Say: "less energy reaches each higher level" — not: "top predators hold the most energy".
Quick check
?Food chains in most ecosystems have no more than four or five trophic levels. What is the reason for this restriction?
Mission 1

Three kinds of pyramid

  • Pyramid of numbers: organisms per area at each level. Can be inverted: one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars.
  • Pyramid of biomass: dry mass per area (g/m²) at one moment. Usually upright. In some seas it is inverted: a small, fast-dividing stock of phytoplankton feeds a larger stock of zooplankton.
  • Pyramid of energy: energy flow per area per year (kJ/(m²·yr)). Never inverted: each level receives less than the level below.
  • Decomposers are not drawn as a bar.
Mission 1

When the energy supply changes

  • Less energy captured by producers means less for every level above.
  • Populations shrink; the top level may disappear altogether.
  • Causes include less sunlight (e.g. volcanic ash), fewer producers (e.g. sediment clouding a lake) and poor seed crops.
  • More food energy → larger populations, e.g. more mice after big acorn crops.

Mission check

?In a lake, the main food chain is phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → pike. A fishery could harvest either the small fish or the pike. Which harvest can be the larger each year, and why?

Every step up a chain loses most of its energy, mainly as heat. That keeps top predators rare, and it is why shorter chains, and lower respiratory losses, give more food.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Make a guess: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Mission 2

Levels of ecology

  • Population: all the organisms of one species in one area at one time.
  • Members interact with each other and with their environment.
  • Community: all the populations of every species in an area: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • Ecosystem: a community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment.
  • Biome: a large region with a characteristic climate, containing many ecosystems (e.g. tundra, desert).
Quick check
?An ecologist is studying a lake. Which statement correctly describes a level of ecological organization in the lake?
Mission 2

Abiotic and biotic factors

  • Abiotic factors: non-living. Temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, oxygen, wind.
  • Biotic factors: living. Food, predators, competitors, parasites, pathogens.
  • Both control where a species is found (distribution) and how many (abundance).
  • Abiotic and biotic factors together shape which populations an ecosystem can hold.
Quick check
?A student finds a woodland plant only on the shaded side of a hedge. Which pair names one abiotic factor and one biotic factor that could explain this?
Mission check

?A fallen, rotting log in a wood holds beetle larvae, woodlice, fungi and bacteria. Inside it are damp, decaying wood and still air. Which statement is correct?

Like a rock pool and a rainforest, the log is living things plus the non-living world they depend on. That is all an ecosystem needs.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Make a guess: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Mission 3

Four ways numbers change

  • Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove them.
  • Change in population = (births + immigrants) − (deaths + emigrants).
  • With immigration and emigration negligible: dN/dt = B − D. N = population size; B = births; D = deaths per unit time.

Mission 3

Exponential growth

  • Few individuals, plentiful resources, weak limiting factors (little competition, predation or disease).
  • Births exceed deaths by a constant proportion of the population.
  • So the number added grows each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … A J-shaped curve.
  • Each individual does not breed faster. There are simply more individuals breeding.
  • dN/dt = rmax N. rmax = maximum per capita growth rate. The slope is proportional to N.
Mission 3

Sigmoid growth and carrying capacity

  • Real populations slow down and level off: an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
  • Carrying capacity: the largest population the environment's resources can support sustainably.
  • Limiting resources: food, water, light, space, nest sites, mineral ions.
  • As numbers rise, resources per individual fall. Births fall; deaths rise. At the plateau, births ≈ deaths.
  • Births and deaths do not stop at the plateau: they balance.
  • Logistic growth: dN/dt = rmax N (K − N)/K, where K = carrying capacity.

Mission 3

Worked example: the logistic equation

  • Illustrative values: rmax = 0.5 per year; K = 1000.
  • N = 200: dN/dt = 0.5 × 200 × (1000 − 200)/1000 = 80 per year.
  • N = 500: 0.5 × 500 × 500/1000 = 125 per year. Fastest growth near K/2.
  • N = 900: 0.5 × 900 × 100/1000 = 45 per year.
  • N = 1000: (K − N) = 0, so dN/dt = 0.
  • The factor (K − N)/K is the fraction of K still "unused". It shrinks as N rises.
Quick check
?A population has rmax = 0.20 per year and carrying capacity K = 2400. When N = 600, what is dN/dt, using logistic growth?
Mission 3

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

  • Density-dependent: affects a larger proportion of the population as it gets denser.
    • Competition for limited resources, predation, disease and parasites spread faster when crowded.
  • Density-independent: affects a similar proportion whatever the density.
    • Frost, drought, flood, fire, storms.
  • Density-dependent factors act as negative feedback: above K they push numbers down; below K they relax, so numbers recover.
  • Both kinds of limit together produce logistic growth.
  • Say: "a storm kills the same proportion" — not: "density-dependent because it killed so many".
Mission 3

Energy sets the numbers

  • More energy available (food, light) → larger populations. Less → populations shrink.
  • Example: in years with a big acorn crop, forest mice are more numerous the following summer.
  • Reproductive strategy can switch with energy. Water fleas (Daphnia): food plentiful → females make eggs by mitosis that develop unfertilized (asexual), fast growth.
  • Food scarce → males appear; eggs made by meiosis are fertilized, giving resting eggs: more genetic variation for a harder future.

Mission check

?A grassland has supported a population of a hypothetical grazing mammal near its carrying capacity for many years. A change in climate then reduces the amount of grass the grassland produces each year for the foreseeable future. Which prediction is best supported?

Growth stops where resources balance births and deaths. Change the resources and that balance point moves.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two kinds of barnacle can both live low on the shore. Why is only one of them ever found there?

Make a guess: Two kinds of barnacle can both live low on the shore. Why is only one of them ever found there?

Mission 4

Niche: a role, not a place

  • Habitat = where a species lives. Niche = how it lives there: its role in the ecosystem.
  • A niche includes its biotic interactions: what it eats, what eats it, what it competes with.
  • And its abiotic interactions: the temperature, water, light and pH it tolerates.
  • Say: "the niche is the species' role" — not: "the niche is where it lives".
Quick check
?Koalas (Phascolarctos cinereus) feed on Eucalyptus leaves, are active mainly at night, and are found in eucalypt woodland in eastern Australia where rainfall and temperature stay within particular ranges. Which statement about the ecological niche of the koala is correct?
Mission 4

One niche, one species

  • Two species with the same niche compete for the same limited resources.
  • Competitive exclusion: the better competitor survives; the other is eliminated from that area.
  • Gause (1934): Paramecium aurelia and P. caudatum, both fed on bacteria. Alone, each grew to a steady population.
  • Together, P. caudatum declined until almost none remained.
  • Example: Balanus barnacles keep Chthamalus barnacles off the lower shore, although Chthamalus can live there.
  • Species that coexist usually partition the niche: they use resources differently.
  • So no two species in a community have exactly the same niche.
Quick check
?Two species of Paramecium, P. aurelia and P. caudatum, both feed on bacteria. Grown in separate cultures with the same food supply, each species reached a stable population. Grown together in one culture, P. aurelia reached a similar density to when alone, while the population of P. caudatum declined steadily until almost none remained. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 4

Sharing by dividing: niche partitioning

  • MacArthur (1958): five warbler species, all eating insects in the same spruce trees, in New England.
  • Each fed mostly in a different part of the tree: higher or lower, on outer twigs or inner branches near the trunk.
  • Different zones, different insects: less overlap, so less competition.
  • Niche partitioning: coexisting species divide a resource by place, time or food type.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Make a guess: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Mission 5

Two kinds of competition

  • Competition: individuals use the same resource, and it is in limited supply.
  • Intraspecific: between members of the same species. They need exactly the same food, light, water, space, nest sites and mates.
  • Interspecific: between different species whose needs overlap.
  • No fight is needed. Seedlings shading each other are competing.
  • Say: "competition for a limited resource" — not: "competition means fighting".
Quick check
?Why does competition occur between members of the same population?
Mission 5

What competition does to growth curves

  • Intraspecific competition rises with density. It is what bends the curve into a plateau at carrying capacity.
  • Interspecific, grown together: each species may level off lower than when alone.
  • Or one keeps rising while the other falls to zero: competitive exclusion (Gause's Paramecium).
  • Remove a competitor: the other population rises to a new, higher plateau. It does not grow for ever.

Mission 5

Cooperation within a species

  • Members of one species can also cooperate: each gains by helping, or by helping relatives.
  • Meerkats take turns as sentinels, warning the group of predators.
  • Wolves hunt large prey together as a pack.
  • Emperor penguins huddle through the Antarctic winter, sharing warmth.
  • Honeybee workers forage for the whole colony.
  • The same group still competes: for mates, for food, for the best positions.

Mission check

?A forester plants beech seedlings very close together in a cleared plot with no other plant species. Over ten years, most of the seedlings die and the survivors are tall and spaced out. Which explanation is best?

Same species, same needs, same limited light: that is why competition within a species is so fierce.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Make a guess: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Mission 6

Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together

  • Prey plentiful → more predators survive and breed. Predator numbers rise, a little later.
  • More predators → more prey eaten. Prey numbers fall.
  • Less food → predators starve and breed less. Predator numbers fall.
  • Fewer predators → prey recover. The cycle repeats.
  • The predator peak lags the prey peak: breeding and starving take time.
  • Case study: snowshoe hare and Canada lynx, Canada. Fur-trade records show a cycle of about 10 years.

Mission 6

Living together: mutualism and other symbioses

  • Mutualism (+/+): both partners benefit.
  • Legume root nodules: Rhizobium bacteria fix N2 into ammonia for the plant; the plant gives them sugars and shelter.
  • Commensalism (+/0) and parasitism (+/−) are symbioses too.
  • These partnerships give access to energy and matter: the legume gets nitrogen; the bacteria get energy in sugars.
Quick check

The bacteria convert nitrogen gas (N2) from the air into compounds the plant can use, and the plant supplies the bacteria with sugars. A soil treatment kills the bacteria but does not otherwise harm the plants. Which prediction about the legumes growing in this soil is most likely correct?
Mission 6

Control from the top, control from the bottom

  • Bottom-up: the supply of nutrients or producers limits the levels above.
  • Top-down: predators limit their prey, which releases the level below.
  • Both act in every community; usually one dominates.
  • Trophic cascade: a change at the top passes down, alternating, level by level.
  • North Pacific kelp forests: sea otters eat sea urchins; urchins graze kelp.
  • Where otters were hunted out, urchins multiplied and grazed the kelp to bare rock: an urchin barren.
  • Name control by where it starts: here, at the top.
Mission 6

Keystone species

  • Keystone species: effect on community structure far larger than its abundance.
  • Remove it and the community changes sharply or collapses.
  • Paine (1966), Washington State shore: he removed the starfish Pisaster. Mussels spread over the rock; the number of species fell from 15 to 8.
  • Sea otters: a small fraction of the biomass, yet they keep the whole kelp forest.
  • Keystone species, producers, and essential abiotic and biotic factors maintain diversity.
  • Say: "disproportionate effect" — not: "the most common species".
Quick check
?In a lake, bass eat minnows, minnows eat zooplankton, and zooplankton eat algae. Anglers remove nearly all of the bass. Which prediction about the algae is most likely correct?
Mission check

?Illustrative scenario. In a woodland, owls eat voles, and voles eat tree seedlings. A disease kills most of the owls. What is the most likely effect on the tree seedlings over the next few years?

Remove a predator and the effect skips down the food chain: otters, urchins and kelp; owls, voles and seedlings.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Most energy is lost at each transfer, so higher levels hold less energy and biomass, and chains rarely exceed four or five levels. Harvesting lower in a chain gives more food energy.
  • Pyramids of numbers and biomass can be inverted; pyramids of energy never are.

Mission 2

  • Population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment); ecosystems can be any size.
  • Biome: a large region of many ecosystems with a characteristic climate.

Mission 3

  • Plentiful resources: exponential growth. As resources per individual shrink, births fall and deaths rise until they balance at the carrying capacity.
  • Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease) push numbers back toward carrying capacity; density-independent factors (weather) knock them about.
  • dN/dt = B − D; exponential dN/dt = rmax N; logistic dN/dt = rmax N (K − N)/K.

Mission 4

  • A niche is a species' role: its biotic and abiotic interactions. Two species cannot share one niche for long; competition eliminates one or squeezes both.
  • Interactions are scored + / − / 0: competition − / −; predation, herbivory, parasitism + / −; mutualism + / +.

Mission 5

  • Intraspecific competition: same species, identical needs; it levels a population off at carrying capacity and cuts each individual's breeding success.
  • Interspecific competition lowers both species' numbers or excludes one; removing a competitor raises the other to a new, higher plateau.

Mission 6

  • Predator and prey numbers cycle, with the predator peak lagging; predation is density-dependent and lowers the prey's numbers.
  • Mutualism is +/+; predation, grazing and parasitism are +/−.
  • A keystone species has a disproportionate effect; removing a top predator can cascade down to the producers.
