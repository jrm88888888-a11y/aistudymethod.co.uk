Mission 1

Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

Make a guess: Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

IB areas of exploration · time and spaceScreen 1

Two contexts

  • Context of production: when, where, by whom and for whom a text was made.
  • Context of reception: when, where and by whom it is read, and what those readers bring.
  • Space means place and culture, not just date: a work read far from where it was written meets new expectations.
  • The area asks how important context is to a text's making and reading, and how its meaning and impact change over time.

Pride and Prejudice (Jane Austen, 1813)

IB areas of exploration · time and spaceScreen 3

Context that does work

  • An entail kept an estate in the male line. Mr Bennet's passes to a male cousin, Mr Collins, so his five daughters will lose their home when he dies.
  • For women like them, marriage was the main route to security.
  • That context sharpens the opening: the "truth" is less about what rich men want than about what families with daughters need.
  • Context helps explain a choice; it isn't a history lesson added on.

Read from another time and place

  • A reader in 2026, in Lagos or Toronto, may not know what an entail is, and may read the marriage plot as romance, as social satire, or both.
  • Adaptations move the story: Gurinder Chadha's film Bride and Prejudice (2004) sets it partly in Amritsar.
  • The words stay the same; their impact changes. Your argument should hold both contexts.

How your exam asks this

  • No exam question names the area, but every component rewards context that serves analysis.
  • Your works span at least 3 periods and 3 places on 2 continents, so contexts differ across your course.
  • Paper 2 (25 marks) asks you to compare two works; different contexts often explain why they treat an issue differently.
  • Individual Oral: one global issue across texts from different times or places.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Would this work mean the same if written somewhere else, or now?

Probably not. A work's meaning is shaped by when and where it was made (the entail behind Austen's opening "truth") and by when and where it is read. The words stay the same; their impact changes, and strong IB answers use context to explain how the text's choices work.

Mission 2

A novel written in Russian, read in English: whose words are they?

Make a guess: A novel written in Russian, read in English: whose words are they?

IB works in translation · reading a translationScreen 1

Two makers, one text

  • A work in translation has two makers: the writer, who made the original, and the translator, who chose every English word.
  • Plot, structure, characters and many images come from the writer. Exact words, rhythm and sound are the translator's choices.
  • Name the translator once and analyse the translation you studied: in Constance Garnett's translation…
  • Treat the gap between languages as part of the work's context.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A novel written in Russian, read in English: whose words are they?

Both, in different ways. The writer made the story, structure, characters and most images; the translator chose every English word. Strong answers build big claims on what survives translation and say "the translation" when they analyse a single word, as Keats credits Chapman.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Context of production (when, where, for whom it was made) and context of reception (who reads it, when and where).
  • Context earns its place when it explains a choice in the text, like the entail behind Austen's ironic opening.
  • Your works span at least 3 periods and 3 places on 2 continents.
  • A work in translation has two makers: the writer and the translator.
  • Plot, structure, point of view, characters and most images survive; sound, puns and exact connotations may not.
  • The IO pairs an original work with a work in translation.

Key quotations to learn

  • It is a truth universally acknowledged (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 1) The ironic "truth": certain in tone, local in fact.
  • must be in want of a wife (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 1) Whose want? The families', as the context shows.
  • unfortunately for his daughters, was entailed (Pride and Prejudice, Ch. 7) The legal context behind the marriage plot.
  • Till I heard Chapman speak out loud and bold (Keats, "On first looking into Chapman's Homer", line 8) A poet honouring his translator.
  • Yet did I never breathe its pure serene (Keats, line 7) Before the translation, Homer was only something he'd been told about.
  • A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread—and Thou (FitzGerald, "From Omar Khayyám") FitzGerald's English rhythm on a Persian idea.
