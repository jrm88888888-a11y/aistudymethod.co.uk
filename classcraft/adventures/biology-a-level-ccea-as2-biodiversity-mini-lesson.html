Mission 1

Mission 1: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Mission 1

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
Mission 1

The species: the unit everything rests on

  • Every classification is built on the species.
  • CCEA: a group of individuals of common ancestry that closely resemble each other and are normally capable of interbreeding to produce fertile offspring.
Mission 1

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
  • CCEA meanings: genus: similar, closely related species · family: apparently related genera · order: apparently related families · class: a group of orders within a phylum · phylum: organisms built on a similar plan · kingdom: the largest, most inclusive group.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 1

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Mission 1

Why not just use common names?

  • A binomial is universal: the same in every country and language.
  • Common names mislead. "Robin" means Erithacus rubecula in Britain but Turdus migratorius in North America.
  • One species can have many local names; a "starfish" is not a fish.
  • The genus shows at once which species are close relatives.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 1

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

Mission 2

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • cell structure: prokaryotic or eukaryotic;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Make a guess: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Mission 3

One chemistry, many cell plans

  • Every living thing contains the same four classes of biochemicals: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • All store genetic information in DNA and use ATP.
  • The biochemical basis of life is similar for all organisms: a sign of common ancestry.
  • The big differences are in cell structure and in how organisms get energy and carbon.
  • These differences sort life into its largest groups.
Mission 3

Five kingdoms: the small and simple

  • The five-kingdom system was built on observable features: cell type, nucleus, cell wall, body form, nutrition.
  • Prokaryotae:
    • no nucleus, no membrane-bound organelles; circular DNA;
    • small (70S) ribosomes; cell wall usually present (in bacteria, made of peptidoglycan);
    • mostly single cells; reproduce by division (binary fission).
    • Example: a rod-shaped bacterium.
  • Protoctista:
    • eukaryotic;
    • mostly single cells, or little cell differentiation;
    • some autotrophs, some heterotrophs.
    • Paramecium: heterotroph. Pleurococcus: autotroph.
Mission 3

Five kingdoms: fungi, plants, animals

  • Fungi:
    • eukaryotic; threads called hyphae, with chitin cell walls;
    • heterotrophs: release enzymes onto food, digest it outside the body, absorb the products (many are saprotrophs).
    • CCEA term: lysotrophs (decomposers), e.g. moulds.
  • Plantae: eukaryotic, multicellular; autotrophs (producers); chlorophyll in chloroplasts; cellulose cell walls.
  • Animalia: eukaryotic, multicellular; heterotrophs; no cell walls; capable of locomotion.
  • Say: "fungi are heterotrophs with chitin walls" — not: "fungi are plants".
Quick check
?A newly described organism grows as a mass of branching threads through dead leaves. Its cells have nuclei and walls containing chitin. It releases enzymes onto the leaves and absorbs the digested products. In which kingdom should it be placed?
Mission 3

Three domains

  • 1977: Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences from many organisms.
  • Prokaryotes fell into two groups, as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes.
  • So life has three domains, a level above kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • Archaea: a separate domain (a "super-kingdom"), distinct from both Bacteria and Eukarya.
  • You and a mushroom: both Eukarya, one domain.
  • Archaea look like bacteria: no nucleus. But their key molecules differ, and some are closer to ours.
  • Say: "archaea are a separate domain" — not: "archaea are unusual bacteria".
Quick check
?Which evidence was used in 1977 to propose that all organisms should be classified into three domains?
Mission 3

Kingdoms or domains? New data on trial

  • Five kingdoms: based on observable features. All prokaryotes: one kingdom.
  • Three domains: based on molecular evidence. Prokaryotes: two domains. Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia all sit inside Eukarya.
  • Domains add a level above kingdoms. The eukaryote kingdoms stay; kingdom Prokaryotae is split into two domains.
Mission check

?A single-celled organism from a salt lake has no nucleus. Its cell wall contains no peptidoglycan, and its rRNA base sequence is closest to those of known archaea. How is it classified in the two systems?

Looks put it with the bacteria; its molecules put it in a domain of its own.

Mission 4

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island. Which of these findings describe the island's biodiversity?
Mission 4

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

Buttercup1031

Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 4

Simpson's index (CCEA form)

  • D = Σn(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1)
  • N = total number of organisms (or total percentage cover) of all species; n = number (or percentage cover) of each species.
  • Values from 0 to 1 (1 = one species only).
  • Lower value = more diverse. It is the reverse of most textbooks' forms: read the scale carefully.
  • Meadow A: 0.23. Meadow B: 0.61.
Mission 4

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20. N(N − 1) = 20 × 19 = 380.
  • Σn(n − 1) = (12 × 11) + (6 × 5) + (2 × 1) = 164.
  • D = 164 ÷ 380 = 0.43.
  • For plants, use percentage cover in place of counts.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rock pool holds limpets 8, barnacles 6, periwinkles 4 and sea anemones 2. Using D = Σn(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1), what is Simpson's index?
Mission 4

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Plants: use percentage cover or frequency in quadrats. Animals: count individuals.
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating Simpson's Diversity Index for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 2

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.

Mission 3

  • Five kingdoms (Prokaryotae, Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia) rest on observable features; three domains (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) rest on rRNA sequences and sit above them.

Mission 4

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • D = Σn(n − 1) ÷ N(N − 1): lower = more diverse.
