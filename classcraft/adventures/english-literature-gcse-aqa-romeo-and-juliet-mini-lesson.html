Mission 1

The Prologue gives away the ending. Why would Shakespeare do that?

Make a guess: The Prologue gives away the ending. Why would Shakespeare do that?

Romeo and Juliet · structure and fateScreen 1

The story in five acts

  • The play takes place in under a week, in Verona and once in Mantua. Learn what happens in each act.
ActWhat happens
1A street brawl; the Prince warns that the next fight will cost lives. Romeo pines for Rosaline. At the Capulets' feast he meets Juliet; they kiss, then learn each other's family.
2The balcony scene: they vow love. Friar Lawrence agrees to marry them; the Nurse carries messages; they marry in secret.
3Tybalt kills Mercutio; Romeo kills Tybalt and is banished. After one night together, Romeo leaves for Mantua. Capulet orders Juliet to marry Paris.
4The Friar gives Juliet a potion to make her seem dead. Capulet brings the wedding forward a day. She is found "dead" and laid in the family tomb.
5The Friar's letter never reaches Romeo. Believing Juliet dead, Romeo kills Paris at the tomb and takes poison. Juliet wakes, finds him and kills herself with his dagger. The families make peace.
  • The turning point is Act 3 Scene 1: after two deaths, the story cannot return to comedy.
Extract Romeo and Juliet · structure and fateScreen 2

The Prologue

Romeo and Juliet · structure and fateScreen 3

A sonnet that announces death

  • The Chorus speaks 14 lines of iambic pentameter rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG: a Shakespearean sonnet, ending in a rhyming couplet.
  • The sonnet was the form of love poetry. Here it announces deaths, so love and death are bound together from the start.
  • It tells us the ending, so we know more than the lovers do: dramatic irony shadows every hopeful moment.
  • "star-cross’d" draws on astrology: their fortunes are thwarted by the stars.

Romeo and Juliet · structure and fateScreen 5

Fate or choice?

  • Fate: Romeo fears "Some consequence yet hanging in the stars," (1.4); after killing Tybalt he cries "O, I am fortune’s fool!" (3.1); in Mantua, "Then I defy you, stars!" (5.1).
  • Choices and chance: Tybalt's challenge, Romeo's revenge, Capulet's rushed wedding, the Friar's plan, a letter stopped by plague fears (5.2).
  • Context: Brooke's 1562 poem, Shakespeare's source, warned readers against the lovers' disobedience. Shakespeare's Prologue blames the feud: only "their children’s end" ends their "parents’ rage".

?The Prologue gives away the ending. Why would Shakespeare do that?

Telling us the ending turns the play from "what happens?" into "how, and why?". Dramatic irony makes us watch every hopeful moment knowing it leads to death, and the play keeps asking whether fate, the feud or the characters' own choices are to blame.

Mission 2

Romeo loves Rosaline, then Juliet, in one night. Fickle or changed?

Make a guess: Romeo loves Rosaline, then Juliet, in one night. Fickle or changed?

Romeo and Juliet · RomeoScreen 1

Romeo before Juliet

  • Romeo first appears lovesick for Rosaline, who "hath forsworn to love" (1.1). She never appears on stage.
  • His father says he shuts himself away and "makes himself an artificial night" (1.1).
  • He is a Petrarchan lover: like the Italian poet Petrarch, who wrote love poems to the unattainable Laura, he suffers for a cold lady in polished, paradoxical lines.
  • Mercutio mocks it: "Now is he for the numbers that Petrarch flowed in." (2.4)

Romeo and Juliet · RomeoScreen 4

The change

  • When he sees Juliet, Romeo's images become fresh and full of light: "O, she doth teach the torches to burn bright!" (1.5)
  • He rejects his old love at once: "Did my heart love till now? Forswear it, sight!" (1.5)
  • With Juliet he shares a sonnet (1.5) instead of reciting one, and she answers back.
  • Friar Lawrence doubts him: "Young men’s love then lies / Not truly in their hearts, but in their eyes." (2.3)
Romeo and Juliet · RomeoScreen 5

Romeo's choices

  • Haste: "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." (2.3)
  • Peace, then rage: he refuses Tybalt's challenge, then after Mercutio's death chooses "fire-ey’d fury" (3.1).
  • Masculinity: he blames love for softening him: "Thy beauty hath made me effeminate" (3.1). Elizabethan ideas of manly honour expected a man to answer an insult.
  • Despair: he falls weeping in 3.3; the Friar calls his tears "womanish".

Mission 3

Juliet obeys her parents in Act 1. By Act 3 she defies them. Why?

Make a guess: Juliet obeys her parents in Act 1. By Act 3 she defies them. Why?

Romeo and Juliet · Juliet and the familyScreen 1

Juliet in Act 1

  • Juliet is not yet fourteen. Her mother raises marriage to Paris; Juliet's reply is polite and careful: "It is an honour that I dream not of." (1.3)
  • She promises to obey: "I’ll look to like, if looking liking move:" but no further "Than your consent gives strength to make it fly." (1.3)
  • The Nurse, who breast-fed her, talks far more and more warmly than her mother: she is Juliet's closest adult.
Romeo and Juliet · Juliet and the familyScreen 2

Fathers, daughters and marriage

  • In Elizabethan England the father ruled the household. Rich families arranged marriages for property and alliance; marrying without approval was a great risk.
  • Girls could legally marry at 12, but most women married in their mid-twenties.
  • Capulet first seems gentle: "My will to her consent is but a part;" (1.2). By 3.4 he promises Paris: "I think she will be rul’d / In all respects by me;"

Romeo and Juliet · Juliet and the familyScreen 5

The Nurse turns

  • The Nurse carries messages for the lovers and fetches the rope ladder (2.4–2.5).
  • After Romeo is banished she advises: "I think it best you married with the County." and says Romeo is "a dishclout" compared with Paris (3.5).
  • Juliet, alone: "Ancient damnation! O most wicked fiend!" and "Thou and my bosom henceforth shall be twain." (3.5)
  • Mother, father and Nurse have all failed her: she turns to the Friar.
Romeo and Juliet · Juliet and the familyScreen 6

Juliet's growing voice

  • At the balcony she sets the terms: "If that thy bent of love be honourable, / Thy purpose marriage, send me word tomorrow," (2.2)
  • With her mother she uses double meanings (3.5).
  • She pretends obedience to her father: "Henceforward I am ever rul’d by you." (4.2)
  • Then she acts alone: "My dismal scene I needs must act alone." (4.3)

Mission checkScreen 8

?Juliet obeys her parents in Act 1. By Act 3 she defies them. Why?

Love for Romeo gives Juliet a loyalty that competes with her father's authority. When her father threatens to disown her and the Nurse advises bigamy with Paris, she is left alone, and her language shows her growing control: double meanings, pretended obedience and decisions she makes herself.

Mission 4

The greatest love story ever, or a warning about rushing in?

Make a guess: The greatest love story ever, or a warning about rushing in?

Romeo and Juliet · loveScreen 1

Kinds of love

  • Courtly (Petrarchan) love: Romeo's suffering for the distant Rosaline (1.1).
  • Sexual love and bawdy jokes: Sampson and Gregory, Mercutio and the Nurse treat love as physical.
  • Practical, arranged love: Paris and the Capulets see marriage as a good match. The Nurse: "Women grow by men." (1.3)
  • Mutual love: Romeo and Juliet, who speak to each other as equals.
  • Friendship: Romeo and Mercutio.
Extract Romeo and Juliet · loveScreen 2

The lovers' first words

Romeo and Juliet · loveScreen 3

A shared sonnet

  • Their first 14 lines form a Shakespearean sonnet (three quatrains and a couplet), split between them. Its rhymes run ABAB CBCB DEDE FF: Juliet's quatrain reuses Romeo's "this/kiss" rhyme, as if answering him. They kiss at the couplet.
  • The extended metaphor of pilgrim, saint, shrine and prayer makes their love holy, not merely physical.
  • Juliet is no silent Petrarchan lady: she answers, corrects and steers him.
  • They start a second sonnet; the Nurse interrupts it. Family breaks into love.

Romeo and Juliet · loveScreen 5

Love and the body

  • Mercutio mocks romantic love as only physical: "If love be blind, love cannot hit the mark." (2.1)
  • Juliet is frank about desire within marriage, longing for the wedding night: "O, I have bought the mansion of a love, / But not possess’d it;" (3.2)
  • The play sets spiritual and physical love side by side: neither cancels the other.
Romeo and Juliet · loveScreen 6

Warning signs

  • Juliet: "It is too rash, too unadvis’d, too sudden," (2.2)
  • Friar: "These violent delights have violent ends" (2.6)
  • Love is tied to death from the start: "My grave is like to be my wedding bed." (1.5)
  • Context: Brooke's 1562 poem, Shakespeare's source, condemned the lovers as slaves to dishonourable desire. Shakespeare gives them his finest poetry; the Prince blames the feud: "See what a scourge is laid upon your hate," (5.3)

Mission 5

No one says why the feud began. So why does everyone keep fighting?

Make a guess: No one says why the feud began. So why does everyone keep fighting?

Romeo and Juliet · conflictScreen 1

How the first fight starts

  • Capulet servants provoke Montague servants with a rude gesture (biting a thumb). Words lead to swords (1.1).
  • Benvolio tries to stop it: "I do but keep the peace, put up thy sword,"
  • Tybalt answers: "I hate the word / As I hate hell, all Montagues, and thee:"
  • Citizens, then the old heads of both houses, join in. The Prince: "Three civil brawls, bred of an airy word," and he threatens death for the next.
Romeo and Juliet · conflictScreen 2

Honour and the sword

  • Gentlemen were expected to defend their honour: an insult had to be answered.
  • The new Italian rapier style was fashionable: Saviolo's fencing manual, with a section on honour and quarrels, was published in London in 1595.
  • Mercutio mocks Tybalt as one who "fights by the book of arithmetic" (3.1).
  • Tybalt at the feast: "Now by the stock and honour of my kin, / To strike him dead I hold it not a sin." (1.5)

The turning point

  • Mercutio fights Tybalt and is wounded under Romeo's arm. Dying, he curses both families: "A plague o’ both your houses." (3.1)

Mission checkScreen 9

?No one says why the feud began. So why does everyone keep fighting?

The cause of the "ancient grudge" doesn't matter: the code of honour does. Each insult must be answered and each death avenged, so a thumb-biting quarrel grows into the deaths of Mercutio, Tybalt and, in the end, the families' own children.

Mission 6

Meet on Sunday, wed on Monday, dead within the week. Why the rush?

Make a guess: Meet on Sunday, wed on Monday, dead within the week. Why the rush?

Romeo and Juliet · time and hasteScreen 1

A story squeezed into days

  • Shakespeare wrote the play around 1595–96; it was first printed in 1597. His source, Arthur Brooke's 1562 poem, spreads the story over about nine months.
  • Shakespeare compresses it into under a week:
WhenWhat happens
SundayBrawl; feast; the lovers meet; balcony scene
MondaySecret wedding; Mercutio and Tybalt die; Romeo banished; Capulet fixes the wedding to Paris for Thursday
TuesdayThe lovers part at dawn; the Friar's potion; the wedding moved to Wednesday; Juliet drinks the potion that night
WednesdayJuliet found "dead"; the news reaches Romeo in Mantua
The last nightThe tomb (Act 5)
Romeo and Juliet · time and hasteScreen 2

Everyone is in a hurry

  • Romeo: "O let us hence; I stand on sudden haste." The Friar: "Wisely and slow; they stumble that run fast." (2.3)
  • Juliet worries: "It is too rash, too unadvis’d, too sudden," (2.2)
  • Capulet: "Monday! Ha, ha! Well, Wednesday is too soon," so Thursday (3.4). Then he brings it forward: "I’ll have this knot knit up tomorrow morning." (4.2)
  • Even the Friar, to Paris: "On Thursday, sir? The time is very short." (4.1)

Romeo and Juliet · time and hasteScreen 5

Elizabethan marriage

  • Girls could legally marry at 12 and boys at 14, but most people married in their mid-twenties; rich families sometimes matched children young for property and alliance.
  • Juliet is not yet fourteen. Capulet first wants "two more summers" (1.2); Paris replies "Younger than she are happy mothers made."
  • A secret marriage arranged by a friar, without parents' knowledge, defied a father's authority. Brooke's preface blamed the lovers for ignoring their parents and trusting a friar.

Mission 7

The lovers only meet in the dark. Why is their love always light?

Make a guess: The lovers only meet in the dark. Why is their love always light?

Romeo and Juliet · languageScreen 1

Verse, prose and rhyme

  • Blank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter, ten syllables with five stresses (2.2): "But soft, what light through yonder window breaks?" reads but SOFT, what LIGHT through YON-der WIN-dow BREAKS. Lovers and nobles mostly speak it.
  • Prose: the servants' jokes, much of the Nurse, Mercutio's banter.
  • Rhyming couplets end many scenes and mark Romeo's showy Rosaline speeches.
  • Sonnets: the Prologue, the Act 2 Chorus and the lovers' first shared lines (1.5).

Light in the dark

Romeo and Juliet · languageScreen 6

Opposites everywhere

  • Oxymoron links love and hate from the start: Romeo's "O brawling love! O loving hate!" (1.1); Juliet's "My only love sprung from my only hate!" (1.5).
  • In 3.2, hearing Romeo killed Tybalt, Juliet's oxymorons pour out: "Beautiful tyrant, fiend angelical, / Dove-feather’d raven, wolvish-ravening lamb!"
  • Opposites in the language mirror a play where love grows from hate and a wedding bed becomes a tomb.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The lovers only meet in the dark. Why is their love always light?

Their love is light in a dark world: torches, sun and stars make it brilliant against the night, the feud and danger. But light also shows how brief it is ("Too like the lightning"), and in the end even the sun won't shine.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The Prologue is a Shakespearean sonnet spoken by the Chorus: a love-poem form that announces the lovers' deaths and creates dramatic irony.
  • "star-cross’d" and "death-mark’d" suggest fate; the play also shows choices and chance driving events, so the fate-or-choice debate stays open.
  • Your exam sets an extract or essay on the play: know the five-act shape and the turning point in Act 3 Scene 1.
  • Act 1 Romeo is a Petrarchan lover of Rosaline, speaking in oxymorons that sound recited.
  • Meeting Juliet changes his language (light imagery, a shared sonnet), but the Friar doubts him and his haste and rage drive the tragedy.
  • Act 1 Juliet is careful and obedient; in a patriarchal household her father can arrange her marriage.
  • Capulet's fury in 3.5 and the Nurse's advice to marry Paris leave Juliet alone; she answers with double meanings, pretence and independent action.
  • Link context to the text: Elizabethan fathers' authority explains why Juliet's refusal is so shocking.
  • The play shows many kinds of love: Petrarchan, bawdy, arranged and mutual, plus friendship.
  • Romeo and Juliet's shared sonnet makes their love holy and equal; the Nurse interrupts their second sonnet.
  • Warnings about speed and links between love and death keep the "celebration or warning?" debate open.
  • The feud has no stated cause: in 1.1 an insult becomes a brawl, and Benvolio's peace-making fails.
  • Honour (Tybalt, Mercutio) and revenge (Romeo, Lady Capulet) keep it going; 3.1 is the turning point from comedy to tragedy.
  • Mercutio's repeated curse on "both your houses" is fulfilled: peace comes only through the lovers' deaths.
  • Shakespeare compressed Brooke's nine-month poem (1562) into under a week; the play was written c.1595–96 and first printed in 1597.
  • Haste runs through the language (Romeo, Juliet, Capulet, the Friar) and through the plot, where choices and chance squeeze the timetable.
  • Elizabethan marriage context: fathers' authority and arranged matches explain why a secret marriage and a refused match matter so much.
  • The play uses blank verse, prose, rhyming couplets and sonnets: notice who speaks which and when.
  • Light imagery (torches, sun, stars, lightning) presents the lovers' love as brilliant against darkness, and brief.
  • Oxymoron and antithesis tie love to hate and light to danger.
