Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a medicine's level in the blood halve every few hours, however high it starts?

Make a guess: Why does a medicine's level in the blood halve every few hours, however high it starts?

Mission 1

Concentration–time graphs

  • Zero order: a straight line sloping down. The rate stays constant.
  • First order: a curve. [A] falls by the same fraction in equal times.
  • Second order: steeper at first, then a longer, flatter tail.
  • Rate at any moment = slope of the tangent (made positive).
Mission 1

The halving-time test

  • First order: rate = k[A]. Halve [A] and the rate halves too.
  • So losing half of what's left always takes the same time, whatever [A] you start with.
  • That constant time is the half-life, t½.
  • Zero order: each halving takes less time than the last.
  • Second order: each halving takes longer than the last.
  • The medicine: a fixed halving time means its removal is first order.
Mission 1

Rate–concentration graphs

  • Plot rate (y) against [A] (x). Data: initial rates of separate runs, or tangents to one curve.
  • Zero order: a horizontal line. Rate does not depend on [A].
  • First order: a straight line through the origin. Slope = k, when A is the only species in the rate law.
  • Second order: a curve rising more and more steeply from the origin.
  • Don't carry shapes across: a falling straight line on a concentration–time graph is zero order, but a straight line on a rate–concentration graph is first order.
Quick check
?A hypothetical reaction is first order with respect to its only reactant, X. Which statement correctly describes the graph of [X] as a function of time for this reaction?
Mission 1

Half-life from a graph

  • Half-life, t½: the time for [A] to fall to half its value.
  • Read it from the graph: 0.80 → 0.40, then 0.40 → 0.20. Check the times match.
  • Constant t½ shows first order.
  • t½ = 0.693/k. If t½ = 40 s, k = 0.693 ÷ 40 s = 1.73 × 10⁻² s⁻¹.
  • Radioactive decay is first order: each isotope has a constant half-life.
Mission 1

Integrated rate laws

  • Zeroth order: [A]ₜ − [A]0 = −kt. Plot [A] vs t: linear, slope = −k.
  • First order: ln[A]ₜ − ln[A]0 = −kt. Plot ln[A] vs t: linear, slope = −k.
  • Second order: 1/[A]ₜ − 1/[A]0 = kt. Plot 1/[A] vs t: linear, slope = +k.
  • Make all three plots from one run. The linear one gives the order.
  • Example: k = 1.73 × 10⁻² s⁻¹, [A]0 = 0.800 M. After 100 s: ln[A] = ln 0.800 − 1.73, so [A] = 0.14 M.
Quick check
?A hypothetical compound, A, decomposes in a reaction that is first order with respect to A. At a certain temperature the rate constant is k = 0.020 s⁻¹. In one trial at this temperature the initial concentration of A is 0.80 M. What is [A] after 25 s?
Mission 1

Two ways to measure rate

  • Continuous monitoring: follow one run over time; plot a concentration–time (or volume–time) graph.
  • Monitor gas pressure, mass loss, or absorbance with a spectrophotometer (Beer's law).
  • Initial-rate method: several runs, changing one starting concentration at a time; find each initial rate.
  • Compare initial rates: double [A] → rate ×1 (order 0), ×2 (order 1), ×4 (order 2).
Mission check

?A hypothetical compound X decomposes at constant temperature.
Results: t = 0, 200, 400, 600 s; [X] = 0.640, 0.320, 0.160, 0.080 M. A new trial at the same temperature starts at [X] = 0.320 M. How long does [X] take to fall to 0.080 M?

The level halves in a fixed time because the rate of removal is proportional to the level: first order. Most medicines are cleared this way.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why doesn't adding more bromine speed up the bromination of propanone?

Make a guess: Why doesn't adding more bromine speed up the bromination of propanone?

Mission 2

Elementary steps

  • Most reactions happen in a series of elementary steps: single collisions or break-ups.
  • The sequence of steps is the mechanism.
  • The steps must add up to the overall equation.
  • They must also fit the rate law found by experiment.
  • Rate-determining step (RDS), also called the rate-limiting step: the slowest step. It limits the rate of the whole reaction.
Mission 2

Intermediates and transition states

  • An intermediate forms in one step and is used up in a later one.
  • It cancels when the steps are added, so it is never in the overall equation.
  • It sits in an energy minimum, so in principle it can be detected.
  • A transition state sits at an energy maximum: bonds partly made, partly broken. It cannot be isolated.
  • A catalyst is used in one step and re-formed in a later one.
  • Detecting a proposed intermediate is evidence for one mechanism over another.
Quick check
?The decomposition of hydrogen peroxide in the presence of iodide ions is proposed to occur by the following mechanism. Step 1: H2O2(aq) + I⁻(aq) → H2O(l) + IO⁻(aq). Step 2: H2O2(aq) + IO⁻(aq) → H2O(l) + O2(g) + I⁻(aq). Which statement correctly identifies the roles of I⁻ and IO⁻ in this mechanism?
Mission 2

Molecularity

  • Molecularity: the number of particles that collide (or break up) in one elementary step.
  • Unimolecular: one. (CH3)3CBr → (CH3)3C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Bimolecular: two. (CH3)3C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH. Also 2HI → H2 + I2: count particles, not species.
  • Termolecular: three at once. Rare: three particles seldom collide together.
  • It applies to elementary steps, never to an overall equation.
  • An elementary step's rate law follows its particles: A + B → rate = k[A][B]; 2A → rate = k[A]².
Quick check
?The reaction 2 NO(g) + O2(g) → 2 NO2(g) has the experimentally determined rate law rate = k[NO]²[O2]. A student claims that this rate law shows that the reaction occurs in a single elementary step. Which evaluation of the claim is best?
Mission 2

How the mechanism sets the rate law

  • The rate law contains species in the RDS, and in any fast step before it.
  • Species that react only after the RDS are zero order.
  • If the RDS is the first step, the orders = the numbers of each particle in it.
  • Example: (CH3)3CBr + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH + Br⁻ has rate = k[(CH3)3CBr].
  • So the RDS contains one (CH3)3CBr and no OH⁻. OH⁻ reacts in a later, fast step.
Mission 2

When the slow step is not first

  • Overall: P + 2Q → PQ2.
  • Step 1: P + Q ⇌ PQ (fast). Step 2: PQ + Q → PQ2 (slow).
  • The slow step gives rate = k[PQ][Q]. But PQ is an intermediate: replace it.
  • PQ is made from P and Q, so [PQ] is proportional to [P][Q].
  • Rate = k[P][Q]²: P and both Q particles show up.
  • This is the pre-equilibrium approximation: step 1's forward and reverse rates are equal.
Quick check
?The overall equation for a reaction is 2X + Y → X2Y. The experimental rate law is rate = k[X][Y]. Which mechanism is consistent with both?
Quick check
?A proposed mechanism for the hypothetical reaction 2 A(g) + B(g) → C(g) has two steps. Step 1: A + A ⇌ A2 (fast, reversible). Step 2: A2 + B → C (slow). The rate law predicted by the mechanism is rate = k[A]²[B]. Which statement explains why [A] appears in this rate law although A is not a reactant in the slow step?
Mission 2

Energy profile of a multistep reaction

  • One maximum (transition state) per step; one minimum per intermediate.
  • Each step's Ea runs from the species entering that step up to its own maximum.
  • A later step starts from the intermediate's dip, not from the reactants.
  • The step with the largest Ea (usually also the highest peak) is the slow step.
Quick check
?A hypothetical reaction occurs by a three-step mechanism. Relative to the reactants, the energies of the species along the reaction coordinate are: first transition state, +30 kJ/mol; first intermediate, +20 kJ/mol; second transition state, +105 kJ/mol; second intermediate, −10 kJ/mol; third transition state, +40 kJ/mol; products, −70 kJ/mol. Which step is the rate-determining step, and why?
Mission 2

Back to bromine

  • CH3COCH3 + Br2 → CH3COCH2Br + HBr (in acid).
  • Rate law: rate = k[CH3COCH3][H⁺]. Zero order in Br2.
  • So the RDS (and any fast step before it) involves propanone and H⁺ only.
  • Accepted outline: fast protonation; slow change to an enol, CH2=C(OH)CH3; then fast reaction of the enol with Br2.
  • Br2 waits for the slow step to make the enol. More Br2 can't make that step faster.
  • H⁺ is a catalyst: used, then re-formed. It is in the rate law.
Mission check

?Below about 500 K (225 °C), NO2(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO2(g) has rate = k[NO2]². Doubling [CO] does not change the rate. Which statement explains this?

Same idea as bromine. A reactant that only joins in after the slow step can't speed the reaction up.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Could you measure an activation energy with a cricket?

Make a guess: Could you measure an activation energy with a cricket?

Mission 3

k rises with temperature

  • k is constant only at one temperature. Raise T and k rises.
  • Hotter: a much larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea.
  • The Arrhenius equation, k = Ae−Ea/RT, links k to Ea and T. You use it qualitatively; no calculations.
Mission 3

Larger Ea, bigger effect of temperature

  • For the same A, a larger Ea means a smaller fraction with enough energy, so a smaller k.
  • But a large Ea makes k more sensitive to T: a 10 K rise multiplies k by a bigger factor.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea, so k is larger at the same T.
Quick check
?For a reaction with Ea = 50 kJ/mol, raising the temperature from 300 K to 310 K almost doubles the rate. Which statement gives the main reason for this large increase?
Quick check
?At a fixed temperature, a catalyst is added that lowers the activation energy of a reaction. What happens to the rate constant, k?
Mission 3

What A means

  • A accounts for how often collisions happen with the right orientation.
  • A stricter orientation requirement means a smaller A.
  • A is not the fraction with enough energy: that is e−Ea/RT.
Mission 3

Background: how chemists measure Ea

  • Not assessed on the AP Exam: no Arrhenius calculations.
  • Measure k at several temperatures. Plot ln k against 1/T (T in K).
  • The points lie on a straight line. Its slope is −Ea/R, so a steeper line means a larger Ea.
  • Two temperatures give two points: enough for an estimate.
Mission check

?Two hypothetical elementary reactions, X and Y, have equal rates at 300 K.
The activation energy of reaction X is 50 kJ/mol and that of reaction Y is 100 kJ/mol. Both reaction mixtures are heated to 310 K with no change in concentrations. Which statement about the rates at 310 K is correct?

So yes, a cricket could do it. Time its chirps at several temperatures: the more its chirp rate rises with temperature, the larger the apparent activation energy of the processes behind it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Make a guess: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Mission 4

What a catalyst does

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up overall.
  • It gives a different route with a lower activation energy.
  • Or it makes more collisions effective (e.g. a better orientation).
  • It does react: it is used in one step and re-formed in a later one.
  • So its overall amount stays the same. One catalyst particle works again and again.
  • It does not change ΔH or the equilibrium yield.
Mission 4

How a solid surface works

  • Adsorption: reactant molecules bond to active sites on the surface.
  • Bonds in the reactants weaken, and the molecules are held close, in a good orientation.
  • They react on the surface.
  • Desorption: products leave, freeing the sites for more reactants.
  • Surface catalysis: the bound molecules are new intermediates, with new elementary steps.
  • More exposed surface (e.g. a powder) means more sites, so a faster reaction.
Quick check
?The decomposition of a gas on the surface of a solid metal catalyst is carried out twice at the same temperature: once with a single lump of the metal and once with the same mass of the metal as a fine powder. The reaction is faster with the powder. Which statement best explains this observation?
Mission 4

Catalysts that bind or swap protons

  • Enzymes bind the reactant (substrate) at an active site: the enzyme–substrate complex is a new intermediate.
  • Binding holds the reactant in a favorable orientation, or gives a path with a lower Ea.
  • Acid–base catalysis: a reactant or intermediate gains or loses a proton (H⁺).
  • Example: bromination of propanone in acid. H⁺ is added, then lost again in a later step.
  • New intermediates mean new elementary steps.
  • A catalyst used in the rate-limiting step appears in the rate law, though not in the overall equation.
Quick check
?A hypothetical reaction, A + B2 → AB2, is catalyzed by X. The proposed mechanism is shown. Step 1: A + X → AX (slow). Step 2: AX + B2 → AB2 + X (fast). Which rate law is consistent with the proposed mechanism?
Mission check

?High in the atmosphere, chlorine atoms destroy ozone in two steps. Overall: O3 + O → 2O2. Which statement is correct?
  • Step 1: Cl + O3 → ClO + O2. Step 2: ClO + O → Cl + O2.

That's how a catalyst joins in, changes, and comes out unchanged. One chlorine atom can go around this cycle many thousands of times.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Concentration–time: straight line = zero order; constant halving time = first order.
  • Rate–concentration: horizontal = 0; straight through origin = 1; upward curve = 2.
  • Linear plot: [A] (zeroth), ln[A] (first), 1/[A] (second); t½ = 0.693/k.

Mission 2

  • The slowest step sets the rate law. Species that react after it are zero order.
  • Fast, reversible first step: replace the intermediate (pre-equilibrium approximation).

Mission 3

  • k rises with T because a larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea. Larger Ea, more sensitive to T.

Mission 4

  • A catalyst reacts in one step and is re-formed in a later one, by a route with lower Ea.
  • Catalysts may bind reactants (enzymes, surfaces) or swap protons (acid–base).
