Mission 1

Assimilate or integrate?

  • Diaspora: a community living outside its homeland (for Sikhs, outside Punjab).
  • Assimilation: a minority becomes like the majority, losing distinctive practices (e.g. cutting hair, dropping Punjabi).
  • Integration: full participation in society while keeping a distinct identity.
  • Secular society: the state does not favour one religion; religion is often seen as private.
  • Sikh identity is visible (turban, uncut hair, kara, kirpan): it cannot simply be kept private.
  • The question for the Rehat Maryada: which rules must hold everywhere, and which can bend?
Mission 1

The panj kakke in UK law

  • Kesh and turban: Motor-Cycle Crash Helmets (Religious Exemption) Act 1976; Employment Act 1989 (turban-wearing Sikhs exempt from hard hats on construction sites).
  • Race and religion: Mandla v Dowell Lee (1983): Sikhs are an ethnic group; the Equality Act 2010 now protects both race and religion.
  • Kirpan: carrying a blade in public is an offence, but the Criminal Justice Act 1988 gives a defence for carrying it for religious reasons. Schools, courts and airports may set their own rules.
  • Kara: in 2008 the High Court ruled that a Welsh school had discriminated against Sarika Watkins-Singh by banning her kara.
  • Pattern: Sikhs won exemptions mostly case by case, through campaigns and courts.
  • Your spec: "the legal treatment of the panj kakke" (the 5Ks).

Mission 1

Beyond Britain

  • France (2004): a law banning conspicuous religious symbols in state schools led to Sikh boys being excluded for wearing turbans.
  • Canada (2006): the Supreme Court (Multani) allowed a Sikh pupil to wear a kirpan in school, with safety conditions.
  • USA (2017): the US Army allowed turbans and beards as a standard religious accommodation.
  • Different secular models: France's laïcité keeps religion out of public institutions; British and Canadian approaches accommodate visible faith.
Mission 1

Mixed marriages

  • The Rehat Maryada: a Sikh's daughter should be married to a Sikh, by Anand Karaj (the Sikh wedding: the couple walk four times around the Guru Granth Sahib during the four laavan hymns).
  • Strict view: Anand Karaj is for two Sikhs only; a non-Sikh partner should not take vows before the Guru they do not accept. The Sikh Council UK (2014) issued guidance that both partners should be Sikh. In 2016 protesters disrupted an interfaith Anand Karaj at a Leamington Spa gurdwara; many arrests followed.
  • Accommodating view: some gurdwaras and families accept a partner who commits to Sikh ways; others accept civil marriage with a blessing.
  • Issue: identity and integrity of the rite vs love, freedom and integration.
Mission 1

Same-sex marriage

  • UK law: the Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 allows same-sex marriage; religious bodies must opt in to conduct it.
  • Akal Takht (2005) issued an edict telling Sikhs not to support same-sex marriage (in response to Canadian law).
  • Traditional arguments: the Rehat Maryada speaks of marriage between a Sikh man and woman; the family is the setting for spiritual life (grihastha).
  • Inclusive arguments: the Guru Granth Sahib does not address same-sex relationships directly; the soul (atma) is beyond gender; the laavan are about the soul's union with God. Groups such as Sarbat (LGBT Sikhs, UK) argue for acceptance.
  • Responses differ by generation, country and institution.

One light in two bodies

Guru Amar Das, Guru Granth Sahib 788 (paraphrase):

  • They are not husband and wife who merely sit together. They alone are called husband and wife who have one light in two bodies.

Q1 What, for this verse, makes a marriage?

Show answer

Spiritual union: one divine light shared, not simply living together.

Q2 How might the verse be used on each side of the same-sex marriage debate?

Show answer

Traditional: it speaks of husband and wife. Inclusive: what matters is shared light, which is beyond gender.

Mission 1

Punjabi or English?

  • The Guru Granth Sahib is written in Gurmukhi script; its language mixes old Punjabi, Braj, Sant Bhasha, Persian and Sanskrit words.
  • For Punjabi: it is the language of the Gurus' bani; kirtan in the original raags keeps sound and meaning together; Punjabi links Sikhs worldwide.
  • For English: many British-born Sikhs understand little Punjabi; they need katha and teaching they can follow, or they may drift away.
  • Practice: Punjabi classes at gurdwaras; English katha; screens projecting transliteration and English translation during kirtan.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib itself is always read in Gurmukhi in worship; translation is for understanding.
Mission 1

G Singh and D S Tatla

  • Gurharpal Singh and Darshan Singh Tatla, Sikhs in Britain: The Making of a Community (2006).
  • They describe British Sikhs as a community that made itself: through gurdwaras, campaigns (turbans, kirpans), media and politics (paraphrase).
  • They stress diversity: caste, generation, degree of observance and political views; no single "Sikh position".
  • The gurdwara is at the centre, but also a site of conflict (committee elections, caste, language).
  • Use them to argue that Sikh responses to the diaspora are negotiated, not simply "assimilation" or "resistance".
Mission 1Evaluate

"Sikh identity cannot survive in a secular diaspora without compromise."

  • For: many first-generation men cut hair for work; Punjabi is declining among the young; mixed marriages are increasing; some gurdwaras use English and chairs.
  • Against: legal campaigns protected the 5Ks; many young Sikhs have adopted the turban and taken amrit; secular law protects religious freedom; miri-piri gives Sikhs a basis for public engagement rather than withdrawal.
  • Distinguish: compromise of essentials (amrit, Guru Granth Sahib, 5Ks for the Khalsa) vs adaptation of customs (Sunday diwan, English katha).
  • Judge it by: who defines the essentials (Akal Takht, Rehat Maryada, local sangat)? Does adaptation weaken or renew identity?
  • AO2 tip: weigh positions against the Rehat Maryada and a scholar (Singh and Tatla); reach your own judgement.
Mission check

Sikh identity in the diaspora is negotiated: keeping the essentials, adapting the customs, and using the law to protect both.

Mission 2: Guru Nanak said no one knows when the world began. Does that leave room for the Big Bang?

Make a guess: Guru Nanak said no one knows when the world began. Does that leave room for the Big Bang?

Mission 2

Creation in the Guru Granth Sahib

  • Before creation (Guru Nanak, Maru Solhe, Guru Granth Sahib 1035, paraphrase): for countless ages there was only darkness (dhundhukar); no earth, no sky; only the infinite hukam (divine order, will).
  • Creation by one command (Japji 16, Guru Granth Sahib 3, paraphrase): with one word, God made the whole expanse, and from it flowed countless rivers of life.
  • Countless worlds (Japji 22, Guru Granth Sahib 5, paraphrase): there are worlds beneath worlds and hundreds of thousands of skies above; searchers grow weary trying to count them.
  • God remains: Waheguru is Karta Purakh (Creator) in the Mul Mantra, and sustains creation continuously.
Mission 2Read the source

When did it begin?

Guru Nanak, Japji 21, Guru Granth Sahib 4 (paraphrase):

  • What was the time, the moment, the date, the season, the month, when creation came to be?
  • The pandits (Hindu scholars) do not know, or it would be written in their texts; the qazis (Muslim judges) do not know, or it would be in the Qur'an. The yogis do not know.
  • Only the Creator, who made the world, knows.

Q1 Which religious authorities does Guru Nanak say do not know the date?

Show answer

Pandits, qazis and yogis.

Q2 Why might Sikhs say this leaves room for scientific cosmology?

Show answer

The scripture fixes no date or method, so the age of the universe is open to inquiry.

Mission 2

Big Bang and continuous creation

  • Big Bang: the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Many Sikhs see parallels: a "before" with no matter (GGS 1035); creation from a single command (Japji 16); an immense, expanding cosmos (Japji 22).
  • Continuous creation: God did not create once and withdraw. Creation unfolds under hukam and God sustains it at every moment.
  • Caution: parallels are interpretations; the Gurus wrote devotional poetry, not science.
Mission 2

Evolution and 8.4 million life forms

  • Sikh scripture speaks of the soul passing through 8.4 million (84 lakh) life forms before human birth.
  • Many Sikhs read this as compatible with evolution: many forms of life; humans arriving late; the human birth as a precious opportunity.
  • No literal Adam and Eve and no fixed six days: so the conflicts some Christians and Muslims face over Genesis or the Qur'an's creation accounts do not arise in the same way.
  • The rebirth of the soul is a religious claim that science neither confirms nor tests: it concerns meaning and the soul, not biology.
Mission 2

Challenges science still raises

  • Miracle stories in the janamsakhis (e.g. Guru Nanak stopping a boulder with his hand at Panja Sahib): many Sikhs read them as symbolic; Sikh teaching itself warns against seeking miracles.
  • Rebirth and karma: cannot be shown scientifically; scientific naturalism may treat the self as only the brain.
  • Bioethics: IVF, organ donation, end of life.
  • Rationalist Sikh writers (e.g. D. S. Chahal) argue Guru Nanak's teaching is scientific in spirit; critics say this reads modern ideas into the text.
  • Your spec: Sikh responses to "the challenges of science", with reference to G Singh and D S Tatla.
Mission 2

What is secularisation?

  • Secularisation: religion losing influence over society, institutions and individuals' lives.
  • Secularism: a political principle: the state is neutral between religions.
  • In Britain: falling attendance; religion seen as private; some young Sikhs cutting hair, not reading Gurmukhi, or attending only for weddings and festivals.
  • But also: revival among some young Sikhs after 1984; youth camps; online katha; new visibility of the turban.
  • Sikh values often line up with secular ones: equality, freedom, democracy.
Mission 2

Sikh responses to secularisation

  • Social obligations and reform: sewa, langar, Sarbat da bhala (the good of all): faith shown in action others can see and value.
  • Duties as a citizen: Kirat Karo (honest work), obeying just laws, military and public service.
  • Freedom of religion: Guru Tegh Bahadur (martyred 1675) is remembered for defending Hindus' right to worship: freedom for others, not just for Sikhs.
  • Equality: rejection of caste; equality of women and men; langar open to all.
  • Democracy: the Panj Piare decide collectively; the gurmatta (resolution) of the sangat; elected gurdwara committees.
Mission 2Diversity

Is Sikhism compatible with secular democracy?

  • Yes: Sikh values support equality, freedom of conscience and collective decision-making; Sikhs serve in Parliament and armed forces; India's constitution and Britain's law protect Sikh practice.
  • Tension: miri-piri means religion is not merely private; the Akal Takht has spiritual and temporal authority; some Sikhs seek a Sikh homeland (Khalistan).
  • Middle view: Sikhs accept a neutral state that protects all religions (like Guru Tegh Bahadur's defence of others' freedom) but reject a secularism that pushes religion out of public life.
  • Singh and Tatla show British Sikhs using democratic tools (campaigns, courts, elections) to defend religious identity (paraphrase).

Mission 2Evaluate

"Sikh responses to science and secularisation are effective."

  • For (science): no date or method of creation to defend; openness to Big Bang and evolution; Sikhs prominent in medicine and science.
  • Against (science): "parallels" can be selective reading; rebirth and miracle stories remain untestable; rationalist readings may change the tradition.
  • For (secularisation): Sikh values fit equality and freedom; visible service (langar, disaster relief) earns public respect; legal campaigns succeeded.
  • Against (secularisation): declining Punjabi and observance among some young Sikhs; tension between miri-piri and a privatised secularism.
  • Judge it by: effective at what: keeping young Sikhs, persuading outsiders, or keeping teaching intact? Evidence from Britain vs Punjab?
Mission check

?A science teacher says: "Religions must choose between their creation stories and the Big Bang." Which reply would many Sikhs give, based on their scripture?

Sikh scripture leaves the when and how of creation open; the bigger challenge of modern life is secularisation, which Sikhs meet with service, citizenship and visible identity.

Mission 3

Peace comes first

  • Sikh teaching begins with peace: one God (Waheguru) is present in every person.
  • Guru Arjan: no one is my enemy, and no one is a stranger; I get along with all (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1299; paraphrase).
  • The goal is sarbat da bhala, the welfare of all, prayed for in every Ardas.
  • Inner peace (freedom from anger and ego, haumai) comes before outer peace.
  • So war is never good in itself: at most it can be necessary.
Mission 3

Dharam yudh: war for righteousness

  • Dharam yudh: a war fought for dharam (righteousness, justice), never for gain.
  • Conditions usually given (drawn from the Gurus' example and the Zafarnama):
  • Last resort: every peaceful means has failed.
  • Just cause: to defend the oppressed or freedom of faith; never revenge, conquest or land.
  • Minimum force; fighting stops when the enemy stops.
  • No harm to civilians; no looting; property taken is returned.
  • Disciplined soldiers: fighters must be sant sipahi, acting without hatred.

Mission 3

Social justice and liberation

  • Guru Nanak denounced injustice by rulers: the Babarvani hymns lament the suffering caused by Babur's invasion (e.g. Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 360, 722).
  • Kings are like tigers and their officials like dogs that wound the people (Ang 1288; paraphrase).
  • Liberation has two senses in Sikh thought:
  • Mukti: spiritual liberation, union with God by grace.
  • Freedom from oppression: social and political justice for the poor, the low-caste, the persecuted.
  • Liberationist approaches link the two: the person freed from haumai is free to stand against injustice (teg and deg).
Mission 3

Guru Arjan, the first Sikh martyr

  • 1606, Lahore: Guru Arjan, compiler of the Adi Granth (1604), dies in custody on the orders of the emperor Jahangir.
  • Jahangir's own memoirs record that he ordered the Guru's punishment and death.
  • Sikh tradition: the Guru was seated on a hot iron plate with burning sand poured over him; he accepted God's will calmly, then died in the river Ravi.
  • He is remembered with words of his own hymn: your will seems sweet to me (Ang 394; paraphrase).
  • Significance: martyrdom as acceptance of hukam (God's will) without hatred; it led directly to Guru Hargobind's miri-piri.
  • Sikhs mark the anniversary by offering cool drinks (chhabeel) to passers-by in the summer heat.
Mission 3

Guru Tegh Bahadur: dying for others' freedom

  • 1675, Delhi: the ninth Guru is executed under the emperor Aurangzeb.
  • Kashmiri Hindu Brahmins (Pandits), facing forced conversion, had asked for his help.
  • He died defending another community's right to its own faith.
  • Sikhs call him Hind di Chadar, "the shield of India"; Gurdwara Sis Ganj in Delhi marks the place.
  • Significance: Sikh martyrdom is about freedom of conscience for all, not only for Sikhs.
Mission 3

18th-century martyrs

  • After 1708, the Khalsa fights for self-determination under Mughal and Afghan persecution.
  • Sahibzade: Guru Gobind Singh's four sons. The elder two die in battle at Chamkaur; the younger two (aged about 9 and 6) are executed at Sirhind for refusing to convert (1704–05).
  • Banda Singh Bahadur: leads the Khalsa to power in Punjab; executed in Delhi, 1716.
  • Bhai Mani Singh: scholar and granthi of the Harmandir; executed by dismemberment, 1734.
  • Bhai Taru Singh: scalped rather than cut his hair, 1745.
  • Baba Deep Singh: died fighting his way to the Harmandir after its desecration, 1757.
  • Mass killings (ghallughara, "holocaust") of 1746 and 1762.
  • This struggle ends in Sikh rule in Punjab under Maharaja Ranjit Singh (from 1799;).
Mission 3Read the source

The game of love

  • Guru Nanak (paraphrase): if you want to play the game of love with me, come to my path with your head on your palm; once you set foot on this way, give your head and do not count the cost (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1412).
  • Shahid (martyr) means "witness": the martyr's death bears witness to the truth.

Q1 What does "head on your palm" ask of a Sikh?

Show answer

Total commitment: readiness to give one's life for the path of truth and love.

Q2 Why might Sikhs see this verse fulfilled in Guru Gobind Singh's call for heads in 1699?

Show answer

The Panj Piare offered their heads, as the verse asks;.

Mission 3

Two scholars

  • Louis E. Fenech, Martyrdom in the Sikh Tradition: Playing the "Game of Love" (2000).
  • Fenech (paraphrase): the idea of martyrdom is rooted in the Gurus' teaching of self-giving love (Ang 1412), but the martyr tradition as Sikhs now tell it was shaped over time. The word shahid came from Islamic usage; many martyr narratives were written down or expanded later, especially by Singh Sabha writers. The tradition is a living one, still added to in the 20th century.
  • Piara Singh Sambhi (with W. Owen Cole), The Sikhs: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices (1978).
  • Cole and Sambhi (paraphrase): Sikh teaching sees the Gurus' use of arms as defensive, bound by strict conditions; peace and the welfare of all remain the aim.
  • Debate: Fenech's historical approach can unsettle believers who read the martyr accounts as straightforward history; others value his showing how martyrdom shapes identity.
Mission 3

Martyrdom in Sikh life and the 20th century

  • Every Ardas remembers those who gave their heads, were broken on wheels, sawn apart or scalped, and did not give up their faith.
  • Martyrs' anniversaries (shaheedi gurpurabs) are kept in gurdwaras worldwide.
  • The theme returns in the 20th century: the gurdwara reform movement, Partition, and the struggle for Khalistan after 1984 (see Mission 4).
  • Some Sikhs call those killed in 1984 martyrs; the Indian state sees some of them as militants. Which deaths count as martyrdom is itself disputed.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Dharam yudh can still justify Sikhs using force today."

  • For: the principle is timeless: oppression and persecution still exist, and the sant sipahi must protect the weak.
  • For: the criteria (last resort, just cause, minimum force) are strict enough to prevent misuse.
  • Against: modern war cannot meet the criteria: weapons cannot protect civilians or be limited.
  • Against: in a democracy, non-violent action, law and courts are always "other means".
  • Against: who may apply the label is disputed: in the 1980s Punjab conflict, Sikhs disagreed sharply over whether armed militancy met the criteria (see Mission 4).
  • Judgement criteria: who has authority to declare dharam yudh? can the criteria be met by a modern state army or only by individuals? what do the Gurus' examples actually show?

?A community faces a government that bans its worship and arrests its leaders. Its peaceful protests and legal appeals have failed. Some young Sikhs who support the community want to attack the homes of officials' families. Which response best applies Sikh teaching on dharam yudh?

For Sikhs, dharam yudh is a narrow door, not an open gate: it can never justify hatred or harm to the innocent. Martyrs show the same faith from the other side: accepting death rather than giving up truth.

Mission 4

What "Khalistan" means

  • Khalistan: "land of the Khalsa" (the pure): a proposed independent Sikh state in Punjab.
  • Background memory: the Khalsa's 18th-century struggle and the Sikh kingdom founded by Maharaja Ranjit Singh (1799–1849; he died in 1839), ended by British conquest.
  • Many gurdwaras recite "Raj karega Khalsa" ("the Khalsa shall rule") after Ardas. Sikhs read it differently: as political sovereignty, or as the rule of righteousness.
  • The demand for a separate state became prominent only in the 1970s–80s.
  • Sikhs hold many views on it. This mission explains the history; it takes no side.
Mission 4

1984: Operation Blue Star

  • June 1984: the Indian army attacks the Harmandir Sahib complex in Amritsar to remove Bhindranwale and armed militants.
  • It falls on the anniversary of Guru Arjan's martyrdom: many pilgrims are inside.
  • The Akal Takht is badly damaged; the Sikh Reference Library burns; hundreds die, including many pilgrims (figures are disputed). Bhindranwale is killed.
  • Indian government view: the action was needed to end an armed occupation and violence in Punjab.
  • Many Sikhs' view: an assault on their holiest shrine; a wound to the whole community.
Mission 4

After Blue Star

  • 31 October 1984: Prime Minister Indira Gandhi is assassinated by two Sikh bodyguards.
  • November 1984: mobs kill thousands of Sikhs in Delhi and other cities, while police largely fail to act; many Sikhs call it a genocide; justice was slow, with some convictions decades later.
  • 1984 to the mid-1990s: armed insurgency in Punjab for Khalistan. Civilians are killed by militants; many young Sikhs are killed or "disappeared" by security forces.
  • 1985: Canada-based militants bomb Air India Flight 182, killing 329 people.
  • Result: Khalistan becomes a demand for some; 1984 becomes a defining memory for almost all Sikhs.
Mission 4Diversity

Sikh views on self-rule

  • Independence: some Sikhs, especially in parts of the diaspora, campaign for a sovereign Khalistan, citing 1984 and "Raj karega Khalsa".
  • Autonomy within India: mainstream Akali politics has sought more powers for Punjab, not separation.
  • Justice, not a state: many Sikhs want accountability for 1984 and human-rights abuses, but reject separatism.
  • Religious objection: some argue Sikhism's message is universal; tying it to a territory narrows Guru Nanak's vision.
  • Everyday priorities: many Sikhs in Punjab focus on farming, jobs, drugs and education.
  • Governments differ too: India treats several pro-Khalistan groups as terrorist organisations; the issue still strains India's relations with Canada, the UK and the USA.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • UK law protects the 5Ks largely case by case (helmets, hard hats, Mandla 1983, kirpan defence, kara case 2008).
  • Mixed and same-sex marriage draw diverse Sikh responses; Punjabi vs English is a live debate; Singh and Tatla stress a community that made itself.

Mission 2

  • The Guru Granth Sahib speaks of darkness before creation, creation by one command and countless worlds, and says only the Creator knows when it began.
  • Many Sikhs see no conflict with the Big Bang, continuous creation or evolution (8.4 million life forms); some challenges remain.
  • Responses to secularisation: service, citizenship, freedom and equality, democracy; tension remains with a privatised secularism (miri-piri).

Mission 3

  • Peace and sarbat da bhala come first; dharam yudh allows force only as a last resort, for justice, with minimum force and no harm to civilians.
  • Guru Arjan (1606), Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) and the 18th-century martyrs shape a Sikh identity of "head on the palm"; Fenech shows how the martyr tradition was shaped over time; Cole and Sambhi stress the defensive use of arms.
  • Liberation means both mukti and freedom from oppression; martyrdom remains a live and disputed theme in the 20th century.

Mission 4

  • Khalistan ("land of the Khalsa") is a proposed independent Sikh state; the demand grew mainly in the 1970s–80s.
  • Sikh views differ: independence, autonomy within India, justice without separation, and a universal, non-territorial reading of the faith.
