Mission 1

Two goals: better rebirth and nirvana

  • Nirvana (Pali nibbana): the end of greed, hatred and delusion, and so of rebirth. The ultimate goal.
  • Better rebirth: a happier human life or a heaven realm, gained by merit (punya): giving, keeping precepts, devotion. A proximate goal.
  • Many lay Buddhists in Theravada countries aim mainly at better rebirth; many monastics aim at nirvana.
  • Anthropologist Melford Spiro, Buddhism and Society (1970), called these nibbanic and kammatic Buddhism (plus "apotropaic": protective ritual).
  • Most Buddhists see them as stages on one road, not rivals: a good rebirth gives the chance to practise; merit supports the path.
  • Your spec term: Nirvana.
Mission 1

The arhat, "worthy one"

  • Arhat (Pali arahant): "worthy one": a person who has reached nirvana in this life by following the Buddha's teaching.
  • Greed, hatred and delusion are destroyed; no new karma is made; at death there is no further rebirth (parinirvana).
  • Standard canonical description (paraphrase): birth is ended, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done.

> "There is no suffering for him who has finished his journey, and abandoned grief, who has freed himself on all sides, and thrown off all fetters." (Dhammapada 90, Müller 1881)

  • In Theravada the arhat is the ideal of the Buddha's disciple; the Buddha himself is also an arahant, but one who found the path unaided.
Mission 1

The ten fetters

  • Progress is measured by breaking ten fetters (samyojana) that bind beings to rebirth:
  • Lower five: 1 belief in a permanent self · 2 doubt (in the Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) · 3 clinging to rites and rituals as liberating in themselves · 4 sensual desire · 5 ill will.
  • Higher five: 6 craving for fine-material existence · 7 craving for immaterial existence · 8 conceit ("I am") · 9 restlessness · 10 ignorance.
  • "Fetters" in Müller's Dhammapada 90 are these ties.
Mission 1

The four stages of awakening

  • 1 Stream-winner (sotapanna, "stream-enterer"): breaks fetters 1–3. At most seven more lives, none in the lower realms; unshakeable confidence in the Three Refuges.
  • 2 Once-returner (sakadagami): fetters 4–5 weakened. One more human birth.
  • 3 Non-returner (anagami): fetters 4–5 broken. Reborn in the Pure Abodes, a heaven realm, and reaches nirvana there.
  • 4 Arhat: fetters 6–10 broken. Full awakening in this life.
  • Each stage has a path moment and a fruit: "four pairs, eight kinds of person", the noble Sangha honoured in the refuge chant.

The first step

> "Better than sovereignty over the earth, better than going to heaven, better than lordship over all worlds, is the reward of the first step in holiness." (Dhammapada 178, Müller 1881)

  • "The first step in holiness" = the fruit of stream-entry (sotapatti-phala).

Mission 1

Renunciation and the monastic path

  • Theravada emphasises renunciation: leaving home for the monastic life gives the best conditions to reach nirvana.
  • Richard Gombrich (b. 1937), Theravada Buddhism: A Social History (1988): the Theravada tradition has held that the full path is normally lived by monastics, while lay people support them and make merit; but nirvana is not barred to lay people, and the canon records lay disciples who reached the early stages, some even arhatship.
  • Gombrich stresses that the Buddha ethicised karma: what matters is intention, not ritual (cf. fetter 3).
  • Today: forest monasteries (e.g. the Thai Forest tradition) and lay meditation movements (e.g. in Myanmar, from the 20th century) both make awakening a live aim, not only a distant ideal.

Mission 1Diversity

Arhat and bodhisattva

  • Mahayana holds up the bodhisattva: one who vows to reach full Buddhahood for the sake of all beings, through the perfections (see Mission 2).
  • Some Mahayana texts call the arhat's goal lesser: liberation for oneself only.
  • Theravada replies: arhats teach and serve; the Buddha sent the first arahants out "for the welfare of the many, out of compassion for the world" (Vinaya, Mahavagga I.11, paraphrase). Ending one's own greed and hatred is good for others.
  • Theravada also honours the bodhisattva as the path the Buddha himself took (the Jataka tales), but as rare.
  • Your spec: the ideal of the arhat and bodhisattva in Theravada and Mahayana.
Mission 1Evaluate

Better rebirth or nirvana?

  • Claim: For most Buddhists, better rebirth matters more in practice than nirvana.
  • For: Most lay merit-making (dana, festivals, chanting) aims at a happier rebirth (Spiro).
  • For: Nirvana seems remote; many Buddhists hope to meet the future Buddha Maitreya and awaken then.
  • Against: Merit and better rebirth are means: every Buddhist hopes to reach nirvana in the end; the refuge chant honours the noble disciples.
  • Against: Stream-entry (Dhammapada 178) is ranked above heaven, and lay meditation movements make awakening a present aim.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "importance" measured by belief or by practice? Lay or monastic? Theravada or Mahayana?

Mission 2

Bodhisattva: a being set on awakening

  • Bodhisattva: bodhi (awakening) + sattva (being): a being bound for full Buddhahood.
  • In the Pali texts the word names Gautama before his awakening, through many lives (the Jataka stories).
  • Tradition tells how, ages ago, the ascetic Sumedha met the Buddha Dipankara and resolved to become a Buddha himself.
  • In Mahayana Buddhism the bodhisattva becomes the ideal for everyone: not only one future Buddha, but all who make the vow.
  • Your spec pairs this with the arhat ideal of Theravada Buddhism.
Mission 2

The arhat: the worthy one

  • Arhat ("worthy one"): one who has fully ended greed, hatred and delusion, and so ends rebirth at death.
  • Four stages of awakening:
  • Stream-enterer: has seen the Dhamma; at most seven more lives.
  • Once-returner: one more human birth.
  • Non-returner: no return to the human world.
  • Arhat: free.
  • Many lay Buddhists aim first at a better rebirth through merit; Nirvana remains the final goal.
Mission 2

Bodhicitta and the vow

  • Bodhicitta, "the awakening mind": the wish to become a Buddha for the sake of all beings.
  • It is made formal in the bodhisattva vow, often taken in a ceremony before a teacher.
  • A common East Asian form, the Four Great Vows (paraphrase): beings are countless, I vow to free them; delusions are endless, I vow to end them; teachings are boundless, I vow to learn them; the Buddha way is unsurpassed, I vow to reach it.
  • Not "I postpone nirvana forever". Many Mahayana texts teach that a Buddha reaches a non-abiding nirvana: free, but never withdrawn from beings.

Mission 2

The six perfections

  • Paramita, "perfection" or "gone to the far shore": a quality practised to its fullest.
  • Dana: generosity, giving even one's life.
  • Sila: ethical conduct.
  • Ksanti: patience, enduring harm without anger.
  • Virya: energy, joyful effort.
  • Dhyana: meditation, a stable mind.
  • Prajna: wisdom, seeing the emptiness of all things.
Mission 2

Wisdom and compassion: two wings

  • Compassion (karuna) without wisdom becomes attachment: "I am the helper; you are the helped."
  • Wisdom (prajna) without compassion becomes cold: seeing emptiness, but doing nothing.
  • So the perfection of wisdom guides the other five, like a sighted person leading others on a road (an image from the Perfection of Wisdom texts, paraphrase).
  • True giving sees no fixed giver, gift or receiver: all are empty. It gives freely, without pride.
Mission 2Diversity

Arhat and bodhisattva compared

  • Goal: arhat — nirvana, the end of rebirth; bodhisattva — full Buddhahood.
  • Motive: arhat — freedom from suffering, then teaching others; bodhisattva — freedom of all beings from the start.
  • Length: arhat — possible in this life; bodhisattva — countless lives.
  • Who: Theravada — the arhat is the goal for most; a few aim at Buddhahood. Mahayana — the bodhisattva path is for all.
  • Fairness check: calling arhats "selfish" is a Mahayana polemic. In the Pali texts the Buddha sent his first arhat disciples out to teach "for the welfare of the many" (Vinaya, Mahavagga I.11, paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the bodhisattva ideal higher than the arhat ideal?

  • Claim: The bodhisattva ideal is a higher ideal than that of the arhat.
  • For: It joins wisdom to universal compassion; no one is left behind.
  • For: It follows the Buddha's own example: he chose Buddhahood and taught for 45 years.
  • Against: Arhats also teach and serve; the contrast is overdrawn.
  • Against: A goal that is reachable in this life may motivate more than one that is countless lives away.
  • Judgement criteria: Which ideal fits the Buddha's teaching as recorded? Which is reachable? Does a higher aim make a better path?

Mission 3

What anatta denies

  • Anatta (Sanskrit anatman): not-self, the third mark of existence.
  • In the Brahmanical thought of the Buddha's day (e.g. the Upanishads), the atman is an eternal, unchanging self, the true owner of experience, one with ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • The Buddha taught that no such self is found in any part of experience: nothing permanent, independent and fully under our control.
  • Anatta does not say people do not exist. "Ruth" is a real, changing person; what is denied is a fixed core behind her.
Mission 3

The five khandhas

  • A person is analysed into five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates"; Sanskrit skandhas):
  • rupa: form: the body and the physical world it meets.
  • vedana: feeling: each experience as pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • sanna: perception: recognising and labelling ("red", "friend").
  • sankhara: mental formations: will, habits, intentions; this is where karma is made.
  • vinnana: consciousness: awareness through the six senses (the mind counts as the sixth).
  • Buddhists teach that all five are impermanent and depend on each other, and that none of them, and nothing beyond them, is a self.
  • Your spec says five aggregates.
Mission 3Read the source

The second sermon

Anattalakkhana Sutta ("Discourse on the Characteristic of Not-self"), Samyutta Nikaya 22.59, given to the five ascetics after the first sermon (paraphrase):

  • If form were self, it would not lead to affliction, and one could say: "Let my form be thus; let it not be thus." Because form is not-self, it leads to affliction and cannot be ordered about. The same holds for feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.
  • The Buddha asks: is form permanent or impermanent? Impermanent. Is what is impermanent dukkha or happiness? Dukkha. Is it fitting to regard what is impermanent, dukkha and subject to change as "this is mine, this I am, this is my self"? No.

Q1 What two tests for a self does the sutta use?

Show answer

A self would be under our control; a self would be permanent and not dukkha.

Q2 Which mark does the argument begin from?

Show answer

Anicca: it moves from impermanence to dukkha to not-self, as in an earlier lesson.

Mission 3

Neither eternalism nor annihilationism

  • Eternalism (sassatavada): an eternal self survives death unchanged.
  • Annihilationism (ucchedavada): a self exists now but is destroyed at death, so nothing carries on and deeds bear no further fruit.
  • The Buddha rejects both. In Samyutta Nikaya 44.10 the wanderer Vacchagotta asks "Is there a self?" and "Is there no self?" The Buddha stays silent.
  • He tells Ananda why (paraphrase): "yes" would side with the eternalists; "no" would side with the annihilationists and confuse Vacchagotta further.
  • The middle way between them is dependent origination: a process of causes and effects continues, with no lasting owner (Samyutta Nikaya 12.15;).

Nagasena's chariot

The Questions of King Milinda (Milinda Panha), Book II, chapter 1, section 1 (paraphrase). The Greek king Milinda (Menander I, 2nd century BCE) meets the monk Nagasena.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name, a way of speaking. No permanent individual is found here."
  • The king objects: then who keeps the precepts, who meditates, who reaches nibbana? Is the hair Nagasena? The body? Feeling, perception, formations, consciousness? All together? Something apart from them? To each, Nagasena says no. "Then 'Nagasena' is an empty sound!"
  • Nagasena asks how the king came. "By chariot." Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The yoke? All together? Something apart? No to each. "Then 'chariot' is an empty sound!"
  • The king: "No: 'chariot' is the name used because of all these parts." Nagasena: "Just so: because of the body and the five khandhas, I am called Nagasena."
  • He quotes the nun Vajira (Samyutta Nikaya 5.10): as "chariot" is said when the parts come together, so "a being" is said when the khandhas are present.

Q1 Does Nagasena say that he does not exist?

Show answer

No: "Nagasena" is a correct name for a real, changing process; there is just no separate owner.

Mission 3

Two levels of truth

  • Theravada Abhidhamma distinguishes:
  • conventional truth (sammuti-sacca): persons, chariots, names; true for everyday speech.
  • ultimate truth (paramattha-sacca): momentary physical and mental events (dhammas) with no self among them.
  • The two levels let Buddhists speak of people, choices and responsibility without positing a self.
Mission 3

Not-self or no-self? Different Buddhist views

  • Mainstream Theravada: analysis finds no self at all; anatta is a statement about reality (see Steven Collins, 1951–2018, Selfless Persons, 1982).
  • "Not-self" as a strategy: some teachers, e.g. Thanissaro Bhikkhu (b. 1949), read anatta as a practice: stop identifying with anything, without asserting "there is no self" (the Buddha's silence in SN 44.10).
  • Personalists (Pudgalavada, early Indian schools): a "person" exists that is neither the same as nor different from the khandhas. Other schools criticised this as a disguised self.
  • Mahayana: some Buddha-nature texts speak of a "true self"; most Mahayana thinkers read this as skilful means, not an atman. Emptiness extends anatta to all things.
  • Dhammakaya (Thailand) teaches nibbana as true self (atta); many Thai scholar-monks, e.g. P. A. Payutto, reject this as contrary to the Pali Canon.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does anatta undermine moral responsibility?

  • Claim: If there is no lasting self, no one can be held responsible for past actions.
  • For: Milinda's own worry: if there is no Nagasena, who keeps precepts or does wrong? Reward and blame seem to need a lasting agent.
  • For: If the person who acted and the person punished are different moments, punishment looks unfair.
  • Against: Causal continuity is enough. Nagasena's image (Milinda Panha, Book II, paraphrase): a man who steals mangoes cannot plead that they are not the ones the owner planted, because they grew from them.
  • Against: The khandhas include sankhara, intention: actions shape the same continuing stream that experiences their results.
  • Judgement criteria: Does responsibility need sameness or only connection? Does anatta make compassion easier or harder (less "me" to defend)?

Mission 4

The Wheel of Life

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and craving, with no first beginning that can be found.
  • Tibetan Buddhists paint it as the Wheel of Life (Sanskrit bhavachakra, "wheel of becoming"), often at a monastery entrance.
  • From the centre out: hub (three poisons) → ring of beings rising and falling → six realms → rim of twelve links.
  • The wheel is gripped by Yama, lord of death: all of samsara is subject to impermanence and death.
  • Outside the wheel, in the upper corner, a figure points to the moon or a Dhamma wheel: release is possible.
  • Your spec: "the wheel of becoming".
Mission 4

The hub: three poisons

  • At the centre, three animals chase each other, each biting the next one's tail:
  • cockerel: greed, craving (Pali lobha or raga);
  • snake: hatred, aversion (dosa; Sanskrit dvesha);
  • pig: delusion, ignorance (moha).
  • Delusion is often called the root: not seeing anicca and anatta, beings grasp and push away.
  • The Buddha's Fire Sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 35.28, paraphrase): everything is burning with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion.
  • Their opposites are the wholesome roots: generosity, loving-kindness, wisdom.
Quick check
?Why are the three animals at the hub drawn biting each other's tails?
Mission 4

Rising and falling

  • Around the hub, a ring split in two:
  • light half: beings rising to higher rebirths through skilful (kusala) actions;
  • dark half: beings falling to lower rebirths through unskilful (akusala) actions.
  • In the painting, and in Buddhist teaching, karma moves beings up and down the wheel, but never off it. Even the highest rebirth is still samsara.
  • Leaving the wheel needs nibbana: uprooting the three poisons, not just earning good karma.
Mission 4

The six realms

  • As Buddhists describe them:
  • Gods (devas): long, blissful lives; risk pride and complacency; they fall when merit runs out.
  • Titans (asuras): powerful but driven by envy; endless conflict with the gods.
  • Humans: a mix of pleasure and pain; the best realm for liberation.
  • Animals: driven by instinct, fear and ignorance; little chance to practise.
  • Hungry ghosts (pretas): huge bellies, tiny throats: craving that can never be satisfied.
  • Hell beings: intense pain, linked to hatred and cruelty. Hells are not eternal: beings leave when the karma is used up.
  • A common Tibetan pairing: gods–pride, titans–jealousy, humans–desire, animals–ignorance, hungry ghosts–greed, hells–anger.
Mission 4

A precious human birth

  • The blind turtle simile (Majjhima Nikaya 129, paraphrase; compare Samyutta Nikaya 56.47–48): a yoke with one hole drifts on the ocean; a blind turtle surfaces once in a hundred years. The chance that it puts its head through the hole is greater than the chance of a human birth for a being in the lower realms.
  • Why is the human realm best for liberation? Buddhist tradition gives these reasons:
  • Enough suffering to want release; enough freedom and intelligence to practise.
  • Gods are too comfortable to seek release; beings in hell, the ghost realm or the animal realm are too pained or too dull.
  • Buddhas awaken as humans.

Mission 4

Three ways to read the realms

  • Literal: the realms are real places or states of rebirth. The traditional view of most Theravada and Tibetan teachers, found throughout the Pali Canon.
  • Metaphorical: the realms picture human conditions: a hungry ghost as an addict; hell as war or torture; gods as the very rich.
  • Psychological: the realms are states of mind we pass through daily, even moment by moment: rage is the hell realm; envy, the titans; numb routine, the animals.
  • Chögyam Trungpa (1939–1987) taught the six realms as psychological states for Western students (Transcending Madness, 1992, from talks of the 1970s).
  • Many teachers hold more than one reading: literal rebirth and present states of mind.
Mission 4

Different Buddhist emphases

  • Pali Canon: often lists five destinations (e.g. Majjhima Nikaya 12): hell, animals, hungry ghosts, humans, gods. Titans are added as a sixth in other lists.
  • The painted wheel is mainly a Tibetan image, though an early Indian version survives at Ajanta. It is rarely painted in Theravada temples.
Mission 4Evaluate

Should the realms be read psychologically?

  • Claim: The six realms are best understood psychologically, not literally.
  • For: Fits a modern scientific worldview; useful now, whatever happens after death.
  • For: The Buddha was practical: what matters is ending craving here (poisoned arrow).
  • Against: The suttas present rebirth in the realms as real; reading them as only mental changes what the Buddha taught.
  • Against: Without literal rebirth, karma's results across lives and the urgency of the path weaken.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to the texts; usefulness for practice; fit with the rest of Buddhist teaching (karma, nibbana); whether the readings must exclude each other.

Mission 5

Karma is intention

  • Karma (Pali kamma) means action: in Buddhist teaching, intentional action.
  • The Buddha (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase): it is intention (cetana) that I call karma; having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind.
  • Karma is not fate. It is what you are doing now, and it shapes what you will become.
  • Contrast: the Jains of the Buddha's day treated karma as a kind of matter that sticks to the soul even through unintended acts. The Buddha put intention at the centre.
  • karma is a technical term on your spec: no translation is given in exams.
Mission 5

Seeds and fruits

  • In Buddhist teaching, an intentional act plants a seed; its fruit (vipaka) ripens later: in this life, the next, or a later one.
  • Kusala (skilful, wholesome) acts spring from generosity, kindness and wisdom; they bring happiness and good rebirths. Merit (punna; Sanskrit punya) is their store of good results.
  • Akusala (unskilful, unwholesome) acts spring from greed, hatred and delusion (see Mission 4); they bring suffering and lower rebirths.
  • Which acts make karma? Intentional acts of body, speech and mind. A truly accidental act makes little or none.
  • An arhat's acts make no new karma: with no craving left, there is no seed to sprout. Old karma can still ripen in the arhat's body until death.

The Dhammapada on karma

From Chapter 1, "The Twin-Verses" (Dhammapada 1, Müller, 1881):

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage."

Verse 2 (paraphrase): with a pure thought, happiness follows "like a shadow". From Chapter 9, "Evil" (Dhammapada 127, Müller, 1881):

> "Not in the sky, not in the midst of the sea, not if we enter into the clefts of the mountains, is there known a spot in the whole world where a man might be freed from an evil deed."

Mission 5

Rebirth without a soul

  • Rebirth is not a soul moving house (reincarnation of an atman). With anatta (see Mission 3), there is no traveller.
  • Buddhists explain that what continues is a stream of causes: the khandhas of one life condition the khandhas of the next, shaped by karma and craving.
  • The Questions of King Milinda, Book II, chapter 2 (paraphrase): Milinda asks whether the one reborn is the same or another. Nagasena: neither the same nor another.
  • His lamp: a flame burns all night. The flame of the last watch is not the same flame as the first, nor a different one: it depends on it.
  • His milk: milk becomes curds, then butter, then ghee: not the same, yet not unconnected.

Mission 5

Different Buddhist views of the moment of rebirth

  • Theravada (orthodox teaching): rebirth is immediate; there is no in-between state. Many lay Theravadins also make merit and dedicate it to dead relatives, who may be among the hungry ghosts.
  • Tibetan Buddhism: an intermediate state (bardo) of up to 49 days between death and rebirth.
  • Texts such as the Bardo Thodol ("Liberation through Hearing in the Intermediate State", often called The Tibetan Book of the Dead) are read to guide the dying and the dead.
  • Tibetan Buddhists also recognise tulkus: teachers reborn on purpose out of compassion.
  • Both agree: the state of mind at death strongly shapes the next rebirth.

Rebirth in Buddhism is continuity without a traveller: intentions shape a stream of causes that carries on.

Mission 6

Vajrayana: the diamond vehicle

  • Tibetan Buddhism is Mahayana in its goal: awakening for the sake of all beings, as a bodhisattva.
  • Its distinctive method is Vajrayana, the "diamond (or thunderbolt) vehicle", based on Indian Buddhist tantras.
  • Vajrayana claims a faster path: using body, speech and mind, and powerful symbols, under a qualified teacher (lama).
  • Practice begins with an empowerment (initiation) from the lama.
  • Four main schools: Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug. The Dalai Lamas belong to the Gelug.
Mission 6Read the source

Mantra: Om mani padme hum

  • The best-known Tibetan mantra is the six syllables of Avalokitesvara (Tibetan Chenrezig), the bodhisattva of compassion:

> Om maṇi padme hūṃ

  • Maṇi = "jewel" (linked with compassion and method); padme = "lotus" (linked with wisdom). The popular Western gloss "the jewel in the lotus" is disputed by scholars; Tibetans usually leave the mantra untranslated, as the sound matters.
  • It is recited on malas (108 beads), carved on mani stones, printed on prayer flags and rolled inside prayer wheels: each turn counts as a recitation.
  • Its purpose: to purify the mind and cultivate compassion for all beings.
  • Many Tibetan Buddhists believe the Dalai Lama is an emanation of the same bodhisattva.
  • Tibetan monks also build sand mandalas (diagrams of an awakened being's palace) over days, then sweep them away and pour the sand into water: a lesson in impermanence (anicca).

Q1 Why would someone turn a prayer wheel rather than chant?

Show answer

Each turn is believed to send out the mantra; it joins body and mind in practice, for instance while walking.

Q2 Which quality is the mantra meant to grow?

Show answer

Compassion, Avalokitesvara's quality, joined with wisdom.

Mission 6

Avalokitesvara and the Dalai Lama

  • Avalokitesvara (Chenrezig): the bodhisattva of compassion, regarded as Tibet's patron.
  • A tulku is a teacher believed to be reborn deliberately, out of compassion, to continue his work; after a death, a search finds the child.
  • The Dalai Lamas (Gelug school) are regarded as tulkus and as emanations of Avalokitesvara. The title dates from 1578; earlier holders were counted back.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso (b. 1935), was recognised as a small child in 1937; he has lived in exile in India since 1959.
  • His seat in Lhasa, the Potala, is named after Avalokitesvara's mythical mountain home.
  • Link to samsara: Tibetan Buddhists teach that a bodhisattva is not driven back into rebirth by karma, but chooses rebirth to help beings.
Mission 6Diversity

What does "emanation" mean?

  • Literal: many Tibetans revere the Dalai Lama as Avalokitesvara present in a human life; this shapes devotion and his authority.
  • Symbolic / exemplary: others stress that he embodies compassion: the title points to the ideal he lives.
  • Psychological: Avalokitesvara as the compassion every practitioner can awaken in their own mind.
  • The Dalai Lama usually calls himself a simple Buddhist monk; in 2025 he said the institution will continue and that his own office alone may recognise his successor.
  • China's government claims the right to approve the next Dalai Lama; most Tibetan Buddhists reject this.
  • The three readings parallel the literal, metaphorical and psychological readings of the realms (see Mission 4).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Better rebirth (through merit) is a near goal; nirvana is the ultimate goal; Spiro called these kammatic and nibbanic Buddhism.
  • Four stages break ten fetters: stream-winner (1–3), once-returner (4–5 weakened), non-returner (4–5 broken), arhat (all ten).
  • Mahayana prefers the bodhisattva ideal; Theravada replies that arhats serve "the welfare of the many".

Mission 2

  • A bodhisattva vows to reach Buddhahood for the sake of all beings (bodhicitta); the arhat reaches nirvana through four stages.
  • Six perfections: generosity, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom; wisdom guides the rest, and compassion moves them.

Mission 3

  • Anatta: no permanent, independent self in the five khandhas (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) or beyond them.
  • The Buddha rejects eternalism and annihilationism; Nagasena's chariot shows "person" is a valid name for parts in relation, with no owner.
  • Buddhists differ on how to read anatta (Theravada no-self, not-self as strategy, Personalists, Buddha-nature, Dhammakaya).

Mission 4

  • The Wheel of Life (bhavachakra): three poisons at the hub (cockerel greed, snake hatred, pig delusion), rising and falling beings, six realms, twelve links, all gripped by Yama; a figure outside points to release.
  • For Buddhists, the human realm is the precious one for liberation: enough suffering to seek release, enough freedom to practise.
  • The realms can be read literally, metaphorically or psychologically; many Buddhists hold more than one reading.

Mission 5

  • In Buddhist teaching, karma is intentional action of body, speech and mind; it plants seeds whose fruits ripen later. Accidents make little or none; an arhat makes none.
  • Rebirth is "neither the same nor another" (Milinda): a stream of causes, not a soul.

Mission 6

  • The Dalai Lama is regarded as a tulku and an emanation of Avalokitesvara, read literally, symbolically or psychologically.
