Mission 1

Mission 1: A laser beam is a flood of light waves all marching in perfect step. How does a laser make them?

Make a guess: A laser beam is a flood of light waves all marching in perfect step. How does a laser make them?

Mission 1

Absorption and spontaneous emission

  • Two energy levels, E1 (lower) and E2 (upper). N1 and N2: numbers of atoms in each.
  • Absorption: a photon with hf = E2 − E1 is absorbed; an electron moves up to E2.
  • Spontaneous emission: an excited electron drops back to E1 on its own, at a random time, giving a photon with hf = E2 − E1.
  • Spontaneous photons have random directions and phases: ordinary lamp light.
Mission 1

Stimulated emission

  • A photon with hf = E2 − E1 passes an atom that is already excited to E2.
  • It triggers the electron to drop to E1, emitting a second photon.
  • The new photon is a copy: same frequency, same phase, same direction (and polarisation).
  • The first photon is not absorbed: one photon in, two identical photons out.
  • Each copy can trigger more copies: a chain. All in step, so the light is coherent.
Quick check
?An atom has only two energy levels, 1.8 eV apart, and its electron is in the upper one. A photon of energy 2.1 eV passes it. What happens?
Mission 1

Population inversion

  • Normally N1 > N2: most atoms are in the lower level.
  • Then a passing photon is more likely to meet an unexcited atom and be absorbed than to stimulate emission. Light is weakened.
  • To amplify light, stimulated emission must win: need N2 > N1.
  • Population inversion: more atoms in the upper level of the transition than in the lower level.
  • Laser = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.
Mission 1

Metastable state and pumping

  • Most excited states last about 10⁻⁸ s before spontaneous emission empties them.
  • A metastable state is an excited state that lasts far longer, around 10⁻³ s.
  • Atoms pile up in the metastable state instead of dropping back at once.
  • With enough pumping, it can then hold more atoms than the level below: a population inversion.
  • Energy must be supplied continually to lift atoms up: pumping (with light, or an electric current or discharge).
Quick check
?In a medium, 6.0 × 10¹⁸ atoms are in the lower level of a transition and 4.0 × 10¹⁸ in the upper level. A beam of photons with hf = E2 − E1 passes through. What happens to the beam?
Mission 1

Structure of a laser

  • Amplifying medium (gas, crystal or semiconductor) with a population inversion, kept up by a pump.
  • A mirror at each end, facing each other.
  • One mirror reflects (almost) 100%. The other is partially transmitting: it lets out a few per cent.
  • Photons travelling along the axis bounce back and forth, stimulating more emission on every pass. Photons in other directions leave through the sides.
  • The fraction let out through the partial mirror is the laser beam: coherent, monochromatic, narrow.
Quick check
?A laser's partially transmitting mirror is replaced by a clear window that reflects nothing. The other mirror is unchanged. What happens?
Mission check

?Along the axis of a ruby laser, a photon of energy 1.79 eV passes three chromium ions in turn. Each ion has an electron in a metastable level 1.79 eV above its ground level, and each is stimulated to emit once. What travels on past the third ion?

The copy is made by stimulated emission: an excited atom, nudged by a matching photon, emits an identical photon. Mirrors and a population inversion keep the copying going.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How does slamming electrons into a lump of metal make X-rays?

Make a guess: How does slamming electrons into a lump of metal make X-rays?

Mission 2

The X-ray tube

  • Heater (cathode): a hot filament releases electrons by thermionic emission.
  • High-voltage supply: tens of kV to about 150 kV between cathode and anode.
  • Electrons accelerate across the vacuum and hit the target metal (usually tungsten) on the anode.
  • Each electron arrives with kinetic energy eV: at 100 kV, 100 keV.
  • A lead housing absorbs X-rays except through a small window.
Mission 2

Braking radiation: the continuous spectrum

  • An electron passing close to a nucleus is decelerated and emits an X-ray photon.
  • It can lose any amount of energy, up to all of it → photons of all energies up to a maximum: a continuous spectrum.
  • Maximum photon energy = eV (the electron's whole kinetic energy).
  • Minimum wavelength: λmin = hc/eV. At 100 kV, λmin = 1.2 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
  • Low-energy photons are mostly absorbed in the tube window and filter, so the curve falls to zero at low energy.
Mission 2

Characteristic lines: jumps between energy levels

  • A fast electron knocks an inner-shell electron out of a tungsten atom.
  • An electron from a higher energy level drops into the vacancy.
  • It emits one X-ray photon: hf = ΔE, the difference between the two levels.
  • The levels are fixed for tungsten → sharp lines at fixed energies on top of the continuous spectrum.
  • Tungsten's K lines: about 59 keV and 67 keV. They appear only if eV exceeds the K-shell binding energy (about 69.5 keV).
Quick check
?In a tungsten atom, an electron drops from an energy level at −11.5 keV into a vacancy in the level at −69.5 keV. What is the wavelength of the X-ray photon emitted? (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission 2

Controlling the beam

  • Tube current (more electrons per second) → more photons: higher intensity, same spectrum shape, same maximum energy.
  • Tube voltage higher → higher maximum photon energy, more penetrating ("harder") beam, and higher intensity.
  • An aluminium filter absorbs low-energy photons. They would be absorbed in the patient, adding dose, and never reach the detector.
  • A lead collimator narrows the beam to the area being imaged.
Quick check
?An X-ray tube operates at 60 kV. What is the maximum energy of an X-ray photon it produces? (e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission check

?A tungsten-target X-ray tube is first run at 80 kV, then at 100 kV, with the same tube current. Which statement describes how the X-ray spectrum changes?

Two processes: electrons braked by nuclei give a continuous spread up to eV; electrons knocking out inner electrons give lines at energies set by the target's energy levels.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does bone cast a sharp shadow on an X-ray image while most soft organs barely show?

Make a guess: Why does bone cast a sharp shadow on an X-ray image while most soft organs barely show?

Mission 3

A conventional X-ray image

  • An X-ray image is a shadow: X-rays pass through the patient onto a detector.
  • Tissue that attenuates (absorbs or scatters) more lets fewer X-rays through.
  • Fewer X-rays reach the detector → that area shows light. Bone looks white; air in the lungs looks dark.
  • Detector: a digital flat panel, or film behind an intensifying screen that turns X-rays into light.
  • It is 2D: structures at different depths overlap.
Mission 3

Why bone stands out

  • Photoelectric absorption: an X-ray photon is absorbed by an atom and ejects one of its inner electrons.
  • Photoelectric absorption rises steeply with proton number Z (roughly Z³) and falls as photon energy rises.
  • Bone contains calcium (Z = 20) and phosphorus (Z = 15). Soft tissue is mostly H, C, N, O (Z ≤ 8).
  • So bone absorbs far more strongly than soft tissue: high contrast.
  • Soft tissues are all similar in Z and density: low contrast between organs.
Mission 3

The CT (CAT) scanner

  • An X-ray tube rotates around the patient, producing a thin fan-shaped beam.
  • A ring of detectors measures the transmitted intensity from every angle.
  • Computer software combines these measurements to reconstruct a cross-sectional slice.
  • The patient moves through on a table → many slices → a 3D image on a display.
  • Principle: the attenuation of each small region is worked out from many intensity measurements at different angles.
Mission 3

CT scan or ordinary X-ray?

  • CT advantages: separates soft tissues with only small differences in attenuation.
  • No overlap: each slice shows structures at one depth, so a tumour's size and position can be measured accurately.
  • The 3D image can be viewed from any angle.
  • Disadvantages: much higher dose (dose to the body, in millisieverts, mSv). A chest CT gives about 6.6 mSv; a chest X-ray about 0.014 mSv: roughly 500 times more.
  • 6.6 mSv ≈ 3 years of average UK natural background radiation. CT is also much more expensive.
Quick check
?Why does the X-ray tube in a CT scanner rotate around the patient during a scan?
Mission check

?A child has swallowed a small metal coin and a plastic bead of the same size. On the X-ray image the coin shows clearly, but the bead can hardly be seen. Which is the best explanation?

Contrast comes from differences in attenuation. High-Z atoms (calcium in bone, metal, barium, iodine) absorb strongly by the photoelectric effect; soft tissues are alike, so they barely show.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 4

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
  • Wave model: diffraction, interference, polarisation. Photon model: photoelectric effect, emission and absorption by atoms. Both: reflection, refraction.
Mission 4

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • λ = h/p, where p is the momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: Eₖ = p²/2m, so p = √(2mEₖ) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the electron as non-relativistic. What is the de Broglie wavelength of the electron?
Quick check
?Predict first. In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a potential difference of a few kilovolts pass through a thin film of graphite and strike a fluorescent screen, where bright concentric rings are seen. How does this experiment provide evidence for the wave nature of matter?
Mission 4

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 4

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√V. Four times the p.d. halves λ.
Mission 4

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A cricket ball at 30 m s⁻¹: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission check

?A beam of electrons and a beam of protons are each accelerated from rest through 100 V and aimed at the same crystal, whose rows of atoms are 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Which beam shows clear diffraction?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. They diffract when it is close to the size of the gap, as with the spacing of carbon atoms.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Make a guess: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Quick check
?Predict first. Imagine a wavefront diagram of the sound from a source moving at constant speed through still air towards a stationary observer X. Each wavefront is drawn as a circle centred on the point where the source was when that wavefront was emitted. Compared with the waves the same source emits when it is at rest, how do the waves reaching X differ?
Mission 5

A moving source

  • A source emits wavefronts at a steady frequency. Each spreads out from where the source was when it was emitted.
  • Moving source: wavefronts bunched ahead (shorter λ), spread out behind (longer λ).
  • Wave speed through the air is unchanged. So ahead: higher f; behind: lower f.
  • Approaching: higher pitch. Receding: lower pitch. Constant velocity → constant (shifted) pitch.
  • Faster relative motion → bigger shift. Same velocity as the listener → no shift.
Mission 5

Sound: how big is the shift?

  • v = wave speed in the air; source moves at us; observer at uo.
  • Moving source, observer at rest. Each period the source moves us T, so ahead λ′ = λ(v − us)/v; behind λ′ = λ(v + us)/v.
  • Example: siren 700 Hz, source 25 m s⁻¹, v = 340 m s⁻¹.
  • λ = 340 ÷ 700 = 0.486 m. Approaching: λ′ = 0.486 × 315 ÷ 340 = 0.450 m (f′ = 340 ÷ 0.450 = 756 Hz).
Quick check
?A source of sound of frequency 500 Hz moves directly towards a stationary observer at 20 m s⁻¹. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. What wavelength does the observer receive?
Mission check

?A train sounds a 400 Hz horn as it moves directly away from a stationary listener. The listener receives a wavelength of 0.925 m. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. How fast is the train moving?

Approaching: waves bunched, higher f; receding: stretched, lower f. The size of the shift grows with the relative speed, so measuring it measures the speed.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Light from almost every distant galaxy arrives stretched towards the red. How fast is it moving away?

Make a guess: Light from almost every distant galaxy arrives stretched towards the red. How fast is it moving away?

Mission 6

Doppler shift of light

  • Source moving away: waves stretched → longer wavelength, lower frequency: red shift.
  • Source moving towards us: waves squashed → blue shift.
  • Measure it with absorption lines: compare each line's wavelength with the laboratory value.
  • The whole pattern of lines shifts together, so we know which line is which.
Mission 6

The red-shift equation

  • z = Δλ/λ, and z = v/c for v ≪ c.
  • λ: the wavelength emitted (laboratory value). Δλ: observed − emitted.
  • v: the speed of the source along the line of sight.
  • Positive Δλ (longer) → receding.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A hydrogen line: 656.3 nm in the laboratory, 676.0 nm in a distant galaxy's spectrum.
  • Δλ = 676.0 − 656.3 = 19.7 nm.
  • Δλ/λ = 19.7 ÷ 656.3 = 0.030.
  • v = 0.030 × c = 0.030 × 3.00 × 10⁸ = 9.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, away from us.
  • Divide by the emitted wavelength, not the observed one.
Quick check
?A galaxy recedes from the Earth at 1.5 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹. A line in its spectrum has a laboratory wavelength of 486.1 nm. At what wavelength is the line observed?
Mission 6

Cosmological vs Doppler red shift

  • Doppler red shift: the galaxy moves through space away from us.
  • Cosmological red shift: space itself expands while the light is travelling. The light's wavelength is stretched with it.
  • The longer the journey, the more stretching: more distant galaxies show larger red shifts.
  • For nearby galaxies (small red shifts), both give Δλ/λ ≈ v/c.
  • A galaxy's own motion through space adds a Doppler shift, towards or away from us.
Quick check
?Andromeda, our nearest large neighbour galaxy, shows a blue shift. Does this contradict the expansion of the universe?
Mission 6

What the red shifts tell us

  • Almost every distant galaxy is red-shifted: almost all are receding.
  • The further away, the faster they recede.
  • Not because we are at the centre: space is expanding everywhere.
Mission check

?A hydrogen absorption line has a laboratory wavelength of 656.28 nm. In the spectrum of a star in our own galaxy, it is observed at 656.08 nm. What is the star's velocity along the line of sight?

The same measurement works for any source: a shift of 0.03% means about 90 km s⁻¹. For nearly all distant galaxies the shift is to the red: they are receding, because space itself is expanding while their light travels to us.

Mission 7

Mission 7: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Make a guess: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Mission 7

Hubble's law

  • Plot recession speed (from red shift) against distance (measured independently, e.g. from stars of known luminosity).
  • Distant galaxies: a straight line through the origin. Speed ∝ distance.
  • v = H0d
  • H0, the Hubble constant: the gradient of the graph.
  • Twice as far → receding twice as fast.
  • Nearby galaxies scatter about the line: their own motions through space matter more.
Mission 7

Worked example: how far?

  • A galaxy's red shift gives v = 6.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.
  • d = v/H0 = 6.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.5 × 10²⁴ m.
  • In light-years: 2.5 × 10²⁴ ÷ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 2.6 × 10⁸ ly.
  • The light we see left that galaxy about 260 million years ago.
Quick check
?A galaxy at a distance of 1.2 × 10²⁴ m recedes from the Earth at 2800 km s⁻¹. What value of the Hubble constant does this give?
Mission 7

The age of the universe

  • Run the expansion backwards: every galaxy was once in the same place as every other.
  • Time for a galaxy to cover distance d at speed v: d/v.
  • Hubble's law: d/v = 1/H0, the same for every galaxy.
  • T = 1/H0: T is the age of the universe.
  • H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ (data sheet) → T = 4.2 × 10¹⁷ s ÷ 3.16 × 10⁷ s per year = 1.3 × 10¹⁰ years.
  • Only an estimate: it assumes the galaxies' speeds have never changed.
Quick check
?A measured value of the Hubble constant is 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. What is the estimated age of the universe? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)
Mission check

?Galaxy P is 2.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 5.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Galaxy Q is 6.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 1.56 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹. Assuming each has always moved at its present speed, how long ago was each at our position? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)

Yes: every galaxy gives about the same distance ÷ speed, so they were all together about 1/H0 ago: roughly 13 to 15 billion years.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Stimulated emission: a photon with hf = E2 − E1 triggers an excited atom to emit an identical photon (same f, phase, direction): coherent light.
  • Amplification needs a population inversion (N2 > N1), made possible by a long-lived metastable state and pumping; mirrors (one partially transmitting) feed the light back through the medium.

Mission 2

  • X-ray tube: heated cathode, high p.d., tungsten target on the anode.
  • Continuous spectrum up to a maximum photon energy eV; characteristic lines at fixed energies from inner-shell transitions (hf = ΔE).
  • Current controls the number of photons; voltage controls their maximum energy (and the number).

Mission 3

  • X-ray image = shadow; high-Z, dense material (bone) absorbs more by the photoelectric effect and shows white.
  • CT: rotating tube, fan beam, ring of detectors, computer reconstruction of slices; better soft-tissue detail, much higher dose.

Mission 4

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).

Mission 5

  • Approaching → higher f, shorter λ; receding → lower f, longer λ; bigger relative speed → bigger shift.

Mission 6

  • Red shift: z = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c for v ≪ c; a longer observed wavelength means the source is receding.
  • Cosmological red shift: space expands while light travels, stretching its wavelength.
  • Almost all distant galaxies are red-shifted: the universe is expanding.

Mission 7

  • Hubble's law v = H0d: recession speed is proportional to distance.
  • Age of the universe ≈ 1/H0, roughly 13–15 billion years (assumes constant expansion).
