Mission 1

Mission 1: How can one rock tell you whether it was born in fire, under the sea or under crushing pressure?

Make a guess: How can one rock tell you whether it was born in fire, under the sea or under crushing pressure?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.1 characteristics and distribution of the UK's main rock types: sedimentary (chalk, sandstone), igneous (basalt, granite), metamorphic (schist, slate)
  • 1.1 role of geology and past tectonics in forming upland (igneous/metamorphic) and lowland (sedimentary) landscapes
Mission 1

Three rock families

  • A rock is a solid collection of minerals.
  • Rocks are grouped by how they formed:
  • Igneous: from molten rock that cooled.
  • Sedimentary: from layers of sediment.
  • Metamorphic: changed by heat and pressure.
Mission 1

Igneous: born in fire

  • Molten rock is magma underground and lava at the surface.
  • Extrusive (at the surface): cools quickly → small crystals (fine-grained).
  • Intrusive (underground): cools slowly → large crystals (coarse-grained).
  • Rule: bigger crystals = slower cooling.
Mission 1

Basalt

  • Extrusive igneous rock: dark and fine-grained.
  • The Giant's Causeway, County Antrim: about 40,000 basalt columns, from volcanic activity about 50–60 million years ago.
  • A lava flow cooled and cracked into joints, creating the columns.
Mission 1

Granite

  • Intrusive igneous rock: cooled slowly deep underground.
  • Coarse-grained, usually light in colour, with crystals you can see: quartz, feldspar, mica.
  • Very resistant to erosion: it forms high, rugged moors such as Dartmoor and Bodmin Moor.
Quick check
?Granite has large crystals; basalt has tiny crystals. Why?
Mission 1

Sedimentary: born in layers

  • Weathering and erosion break rocks into sediment.
  • Rivers, wind and the sea carry it and deposit it in layers (strata), usually in seas and lakes, sometimes in deserts.
  • Buried layers are compacted and cemented into rock.
  • Clues: layers, rounded grains, sometimes fossils.
Mission 1

Sandstone

  • Sand grains (often quartz, feldspar, mica) compacted and cemented together.
  • Feels gritty; often shows layers.
  • The red Old Red Sandstone of the Brecon Beacons was laid down mainly by rivers, about 419–359 million years ago.
  • Some sandstones are very hard: Exmoor's sandstones resist erosion.
Mission 1

Chalk

  • A soft limestone, usually white, made from the shells of tiny marine algae (coccoliths).
  • Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, is a stack of pure white chalk.
  • Chalk is not very hard, but rain soaks through it quickly, so little runs over it to erode it. It forms lines of hills, e.g. the North and South Downs.
Quick check
?A rock has thin layers of rounded grains glued together and feels gritty. What is it, and how did it form?
Mission 1

Metamorphic: changed by heat and pressure

  • Rocks buried deep, or squeezed at plate boundaries, face high temperatures and pressures for millions of years.
  • They change physically or chemically without melting.
  • Rocks next to hot magma can be "baked": contact metamorphism.
  • Result: harder rocks, often with crystals or layers in a new direction.
Mission 1

Slate

  • Shale or clay squeezed intensely becomes slate.
  • Soft clay becomes hard and resistant to erosion.
  • Fine-grained, dark grey, and splits into thin flat sheets: ideal for roofs.
  • Parts of the high ground of the Lake District and Wales are slate. The highest Lake District fells are hard volcanic rock.
Mission 1

Schist

  • Forms from mudstone or shale under stronger heat and pressure than slate.
  • New flaky minerals such as mica grow, giving shiny, wavy layers.
  • Much of the Grampian Highlands of Scotland is metamorphic rock, including schists.
Quick check
?Which pair shows a rock before and after metamorphism?
Mission 1

Where are the UK's rocks?

  • North and west: older, harder igneous and metamorphic rocks: Highlands (gneiss, schist), Lake District (slate and volcanic rock) and Wales (slate), Dartmoor (granite).
  • South and east: younger, softer sedimentary rocks: chalk Downs and clay vales such as the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury.
  • Geographers often draw a rough Tees–Exe line between upland and lowland Britain.
Mission 1

Past tectonics built the uplands

  • About 440–410 million years ago, plate collision in the Caledonian mountain-building period moved huge slabs of rock in north-west Scotland.
  • Before that, an ocean (the Iapetus Ocean) separated Scotland from England and Wales.
  • Volcanic activity 50–60 million years ago produced the Causeway basalts.
  • The African and European plates' collision (the Alps, 15–35 million years ago) folded the chalk of south-east England; erosion left the North and South Downs.
  • So: hard rocks + mountain building = uplands; soft sediments = lowlands.
Mission check

?A hill rock is very hard, splits into thin flat sheets, and its tiny crystals are lined up. A geologist says it was once mud on a sea floor. What happened to it?

Crystals tell you a rock cooled from molten rock (big = slow, small = fast). Grains, layers and fossils tell you it built up as sediment. Hardened, crystalline or sheet-like rock tells you heat and pressure changed it.

The hard igneous and metamorphic rocks shaped by old mountain building form the uplands of the north and west; softer sedimentary rocks form the lowlands of the south and east.

Mission 2: Why are most of Britain's mountains in the north and west, and its flattest land in the south and east?

Make a guess: Why are most of Britain's mountains in the north and west, and its flattest land in the south and east?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.2 upland and lowland landscapes result from interacting physical processes (glacial erosion/deposition, weathering, climate, post-glacial river and slope processes)
  • 1.2 distinctive landscapes result from human activity over time (agriculture, forestry, settlement)
Mission 2

Uplands and lowlands

  • Uplands (high, steep, rugged): mainly in the north and west: the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, the Pennines, Wales; plus Dartmoor, Bodmin Moor and Exmoor in the south-west.
  • Lowlands (low, gentle): mainly in the south and east: clay vales such as the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury, broken by hills of more resistant chalk and limestone (the Downs; the Cotswolds, "hard limestones rising above mudstones").
  • England's highest mountain, Scafell Pike (978 m), is in the Lake District.
Mission 2

The link to geology

  • Relief (differences in height) "is greatly affected by the underlying geology".
  • Hard rocks resist erosion → uplands: granite (Dartmoor), slate (Lake District, Wales), gneiss (Western Highlands), hard sandstone (Exmoor).
  • Soft rocks wear away → lowlands: clays of the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury are soft and impermeable, so streams erode them fast.
  • Chalk is not very hard, but rain soaks straight through it, so it forms chains of hills like the Downs.
Quick check
?A student marks four places on a UK map. Which one is in an upland area?
Mission 2

Glaciated landscapes

  • At its biggest, about 27,000 years ago, the last ice sheet reached as far south as South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York.
  • Ice eroded the uplands into corries (cirques), U-shaped troughs and steep valley heads; in the Brecon Beacons this happened in the late Devensian (26,000 to 15,000 years ago).
  • Ice deposited till (boulder clay) over lowlands; much of the Holderness coast is late Devensian till.
  • So glaciated landscapes are mainly in the north and west; the far south was never covered by this ice.
Mission 2

Climate shapes landscapes

  • West and north: wetter; east and south: "drier, warmer, sunnier and less windy".
  • Rain falls where moist Atlantic air rises over hills, so a rainfall map "looks very similar to a topographic map".
  • Over 3,500 mm a year falls on the high west Highlands; around 500 mm in the driest parts of eastern England.
  • More rain → more river erosion and more frost and water to weather rock in the uplands.
Mission 2

After the ice: weathering, rivers and slopes

  • Weathering: in freeze-thaw, water in cracks "will expand and push the cracks apart".
  • Post-glacial rivers cut down into glacial troughs and till, and build floodplains with the sediment.
  • Slope processes: when gravity beats a slope's strength, material moves downhill as a mass movement.
  • These processes interact: climate supplies water and frost; geology decides how fast rock breaks down.
Quick check
?Why is Dartmoor upland while the Weald clay is lowland?
Mission 2

Human activity shapes the land

  • Agriculture: farmland covers 69% of the UK's land; in England 63.1% of land is agricultural.
  • Forestry: woodland covers 14% of the UK (20% of Scotland, 10% of England).
  • Settlement: 8.7% of England's land is in developed use (homes, roads, industry).
  • Over centuries, grazing, tree planting and felling, and town growth have changed vegetation, drainage and the look of the land.
Quick check
?Which change is an example of human activity making a landscape distinctive?
Mission check

?In south-east England, a ridge of chalk stands above a clay vale. Chalk is not a very hard rock. Why does the chalk form the higher ground?

Relief follows geology: hard rocks of the north and west stand up as uplands; soft, impermeable clays of the south and east are worn down, while permeable chalk survives as low hills. Ice, rain, rivers and slopes shaped both; farming, forestry and settlement finish the picture.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Make a guess: Between 1951 and 2004 the cliffs at Aldbrough, on England's Holderness coast, retreated about 2 m a year. Why do they disappear so fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.3 weathering (3 types), mass movement (sliding, slumping), erosion (4 types), transport (incl. longshore drift), deposition
  • 1.3 influence of rock type and constructive/destructive waves on landforms
  • 1.3 UK weather and climate (seasonality, storm frequency, prevailing winds) and erosion rates, retreat and landforms
Mission 3

Where waves get their energy

  • Waves are made by wind blowing over the sea.
  • Bigger waves come from stronger winds, winds that blow for longer, and a longer fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • When a wave breaks, water rushes up the beach (swash) and drains back (backwash).
Mission 3

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive waves: low and gentle. Strong swash, weak backwash, so they add sediment and build beaches.
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, often in storms. Weak swash, strong backwash, so they remove sediment and attack cliffs.
  • Destructive waves cut cliffs and lower beaches; constructive waves build beaches, spits and bars.
Quick check
?A beach is getting wider and higher over a calm summer. Which waves are most likely to be doing this?
Mission 3

Four ways the sea erodes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water, and air forced into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: sea water dissolves some rocks, such as chalk and limestone.
Mission 3

Transport, longshore drift and deposition

  • Four ways sediment moves: traction (big pebbles roll), saltation (small pebbles and sand bounce), suspension (fine particles carried in the water), solution (dissolved).
  • Longshore drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash pushes sediment up the beach at that angle, and the backwash drags it straight down. Sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped where waves and currents lose energy, such as in sheltered bays.
Quick check
?A beach faces south. The prevailing waves arrive from the south-west. Which way does longshore drift move the sand?
Mission 3

Weathering: breaking rock where it stands

  • Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion carries it away.
  • Mechanical: freeze-thaw (water in a crack freezes, expands and widens it) and salt weathering (salt crystals grow in tiny gaps and split the rock).
  • Chemical: rainwater is a weak acid. It slowly dissolves chalk and limestone (carbonation).
  • Biological: plant roots widen cracks, and animals burrow into the cliff.
Mission 3

Mass movement: cliffs that fall and slump

  • Mass movement: rock and soil move downhill under gravity.
  • Rock fall: blocks break off a steep cliff, often after waves undercut it or freeze-thaw loosens them.
  • Sliding: material slides down a straight slip surface.
  • Slumping: soft, wet rock such as clay slips along a curved surface, rotating as it goes.
Mission 3

Holderness: soft clay against hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly 18,000–13,000 years old, and about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • Above the waves, the till fails mainly by rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls. More rainfall could make the till more likely to fail.
  • To the north, Flamborough Head is chalk, which "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004: about 114 m in total (2.16 × 53, calculated).
Mission 3

UK weather, climate and erosion

  • Prevailing winds: the UK's prevailing winds are west to south-westerly, so west- and south-west-facing coasts face the longest fetch, across the Atlantic.
  • Seasonality: "Winter is the windiest time of year in the UK", when more Atlantic storms reach the country. Most erosion happens in winter storms.
  • Storm frequency: winter 2013/14 was judged the stormiest on record for the British Isles. In 2014, one storm removed up to 10 m of dunes at Studland, Dorset, and a tidal surge breached Spurn in December 2013.
  • Exam move: explain how a stormy winter speeds up retreat, then link it to a landform (a cliff, a spit).
Quick check
?At Aldbrough, the cliff retreated 1.16 m a year from 1852 to 1951 and 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004. What was the mean rate over the whole period, 1852 to 2004?
Quick check
?Why does Holderness retreat so much faster than Flamborough Head, just to the north?
Mission check

?Two cliffs face the same North Sea storms. Cliff X is resistant chalk. Cliff Y is soft glacial clay, soaked after a wet winter. Which retreats faster, and why?

Aldbrough's cliffs are soft till. Waves attack the foot at high tide, the wet clay slumps from above, and longshore drift carries the debris south. That is why they retreated 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 4

Headlands and bays

  • Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles, the sea erodes the soft rock faster.
  • Soft rock is worn back into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • This is a discordant coast: rock bands run across the coastline.
  • Bays are sheltered, so waves there lose energy and drop sand: bay-head beaches.
Mission 4

When the rocks run along the coast

  • On a concordant coast the rock bands run parallel to the coastline.
  • At Lulworth, Dorset, hard Portland Stone lies on the seaward side, with softer rocks behind it.
  • Lulworth Cove formed "after a break in the Portland and Purbeck rocks" let the sea erode the softer rocks behind: a small, round cove.
  • Joints (cracks) and faults (fractures where rocks have moved) are weaknesses that the sea exploits first.
Mission 4

Cliffs, notches and platforms

  • Waves attack the foot of a cliff between high and low tide, cutting a wave-cut notch by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The rock above is left overhanging; it collapses and the cliff retreats.
  • A gently sloping wave-cut platform is left, exposed at low tide.
Mission 4

Cave, arch, stack, stump: Old Harry Rocks

  • At Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, the sea exploits "a crack in the chalk joints", slowly creating a cave.
  • Erosion along joints and bedding planes turns the cave into an arch; when the arch collapses, a stack is left.
  • Old Harry's Wife "was still a stack until her eventual collapse in 1896 to render her a stump": a stump is a stack worn down until it is covered at high tide.
  • Exam move: name the process at each stage (hydraulic action, abrasion) and the weakness it exploits (joints, faults, bedding planes).
Quick check
?What is the correct order as a headland is eroded?
Mission 4

Why the sea drops sediment: beaches

  • The sea deposits sediment where it loses energy: in sheltered bays, in shallow water, behind obstacles, and where constructive waves dominate.
  • Deposition also needs a supply of sediment, from eroding cliffs, rivers and the seabed.
  • A beach builds up where more sediment arrives than is removed.
  • Sandy beaches have gentle slopes; shingle beaches (pebbles) are steeper. Slapton Sands, Devon, is shingle: "flint, chert and quartz pebbles".
Mission 4

Spits

  • Where the coast changes direction, sediment carried along the coast keeps going and is dropped in the calmer water beyond: a spit grows out from the land.
  • The end often curves round as waves bend into the calmer water behind it: a hooked (recurved) spit. Saltmarsh forms in the shelter behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Mission 4

Bars

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment that joins two headlands, cutting off the water behind as a lagoon.
  • Slapton Sands, Devon, is a shingle bar linking Strete Gate with Torcross. It dammed an old estuary and formed a freshwater lagoon, Slapton Ley.
  • Its main formation probably began about 5,000 years ago, as the rising sea pushed shingle landward. Longshore drift still replenishes it.
Quick check
?What is the key difference between a spit and a bar?
Quick check
?Why do beaches often form in bays rather than on headlands?
Mission check

?Shingle drifts east along a straight coast. Then the coastline turns sharply north into a river estuary. What will most likely form, and why?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment carried south along the Holderness coast by longshore drift keeps going where the coast turns at the Humber mouth, and is dropped in the calmer water. Its end hooks into the estuary, with saltmarsh behind.

Mission 5

Mission 5: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Make a guess: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.5 how urbanisation, agriculture and industry change coastal landscapes; effects of recession and flooding
  • 1.5 pros and cons of UK defences (sea walls, groynes, rip rap; beach nourishment, managed retreat) and resulting landscape change
Mission 5

How people change coasts

  • Urbanisation: Selsey was an island until a causeway was completed in 1809; the B2145 is still its only road. Towns and roads now depend on defences.
  • Leisure: the land behind Medmerry's shingle bank held "one of the largest caravan parks in Europe".
  • Agriculture and industry: low land behind defences is farmed; ports and power stations build on the coast and need protecting.
  • Defences, in turn, change the landscape: groynes hold beaches in place but can starve the coast beyond them.
Mission 5

What erosion and flooding do

  • Recession takes land: before defences were built in the 1950s, parts of the Manhood Peninsula were eroding at 8 m a year.
  • If Selsey's defences failed, 250 properties close to the sea would be lost to erosion over 100 years.
  • Flooding: without winter repairs, about 50 properties, sewage pumping stations and electricity sub-stations at Medmerry would flood within a year.
  • Environment: habitats are lost to the sea, or squeezed against defences as sea level rises.
Mission 5

Four policies for each stretch of coast

  • Hold the line: keep the defences where they are.
  • Advance the line: build new defences further out to sea.
  • Managed retreat (managed realignment): let the sea in, behind a new line of defence inland.
  • No active intervention: "Let nature take its course – no work will be carried out to repair defences".
Mission 5

Hard engineering

  • Sea walls: concrete walls that reflect waves. Strong, but very costly, and waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Rock armour (rip rap): big boulders that break up waves. Cheaper than a sea wall, but can look out of place and make the beach harder to reach.
  • Gabions: wire cages filled with rocks; cheaper than walls, but the wire rusts, so they do not last as long.
  • Groynes: barriers across the beach that trap longshore drift and keep the beach wide. Down-drift, the beach can be starved: groynes at one point on the Sussex coast "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
  • Revetments: sloping walls or fences that absorb wave energy.
Mission 5

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment (replenishment): add sand or shingle to the beach, which absorbs wave energy. It looks natural, but must be repeated.
  • Managed retreat: give low land to the sea; it becomes saltmarsh, which absorbs wave energy.
Quick check
?Groynes keep one town's beach wide. What is their most likely cost?
Mission 5

Medmerry: the problem

  • Medmerry lies west of Selsey. A shingle bank there protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 hectares of land.
  • The flood risk was 1 in 1 in any given year, and the bank had about 1 year of life left.
  • The B2145 and the utilities that serve Selsey cross the low land behind the bank.
Mission 5

The options and their costs (2007)

  • No active intervention: £8–10 million. Without winter repairs, the bank was likely to breach within a year.
  • Hold the line: £80 million; judged "not practical".
  • Managed realignment: £10 million, for benefits valued at £40 million.
  • Costs are present values: today's value of the spending over the strategy's time span.
Quick check
?Managed realignment at Medmerry was estimated to cost £10 million and to bring benefits worth £40 million. How many pounds of benefit for each £1 spent?
Mission 5

Hold the line or let the sea in?

  • For holding the line: all property and land keep some protection, and losses are delayed.
  • Against: rising flood risk, more future funding, and "increasingly out of step with natural processes".
  • For managed realignment: it works with natural processes, cuts long-term spending, manages flood risk and may create a large area of wildlife habitat.
  • Against: flooding of land and property, possible loss of beach access, and a possible cut in shingle supply to the Witterings, along the coast.
Mission 5

What happened: effects and conflicts

  • Completed in 2014, with 7 km of new embankments inland, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey.
  • The £28 million scheme was then "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe"; it created a new wetland, with the RSPB's support.
  • The government also had a duty to create new intertidal habitat, to replace losses elsewhere.
  • Conflict: realignment meant "the flooding of a relatively large area of land".
  • Evaluate: £28 million spent is not directly comparable with the 2007 present-value estimate of £10 million.
Mission check

?On a low farmland coast, an old sea wall is failing. Rebuilding it would cost far more than the land behind it is worth. A town sits on higher ground nearby, but its only road crosses the low land. Which policy fits best?

At Medmerry, holding a failing bank was costed at £80 million against £10 million to realign (2007 estimates). New banks inland protect Selsey's road and services, and the flooded land became wetland. The price was a "relatively large area of land" given to the sea.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Over 1,000 years, one English coast has lost 26 villages to the sea. Where did all that land go?

Make a guess: Over 1,000 years, one English coast has lost 26 villages to the sea. Where did all that land go?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 1.6 one named distinctive UK coastal landscape: location significance, formation, most influential factors in its change
Mission 6

Holderness: where and why it matters

  • East Yorkshire's North Sea coast, from the chalk of Flamborough Head in the north to Spurn at the mouth of the Humber in the south.
  • The till cliffs retreat 1–4.5 m a year on average.
  • Over the last 1,000 years the coast has retreated about 2 km, losing 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey of 1086.
  • Significance: Spurn protects the Humber estuary and the port of Hull, and is "an important site for migratory birds". Defences protect Bridlington, Hornsea, Withernsea, Mappleton and the Easington gas terminals.
Mission 6

Two rocks, two speeds

  • Flamborough Head: chalk, "more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Holderness cliffs: glacial till (boulder clay), left by ice 18,000–13,000 years ago; about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • Geology sets the speed: hard chalk stands out as a headland; soft till retreats fast.
Mission 6

Erosional landforms 1: Flamborough Head

  • Headland: resistant chalk juts out into the sea.
  • Caves: more than 200 sea caves, mainly around the headland; the largest go back more than 50 m.
  • Waves exploit cracks (joints) in chalk: a crack → cave → arch → stack → stump, as at Old Harry Rocks in Dorset.
  • Storms "could impact the chalk at wave level, creating undercutting and cavities".
Mission 6

Erosional landforms 2: the till cliffs

  • At high tide, waves erode the foot of the cliff.
  • The wet clay above slips along curved surfaces: rotational slumping is the main way these cliffs fail.
  • Waves then carry the slumped debris away, and the cycle repeats.
  • In front of the cliffs, waves wear down a gently sloping shore platform.
Quick check
?Which landform would you expect at Flamborough Head but not on the clay cliffs at Aldbrough?
Mission 6

Where the land goes

  • Waves arrive at an angle, so longshore drift moves eroded sediment south along Holderness.
  • Net drift: about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year.
  • Using records back to 1786, up to 2 million m³ a year has been eroded from the cliffs.
  • Fine clay is carried off in suspension; sand and gravel move along the beach.
Mission 6

Depositional landforms: Spurn

  • Spurn is a spit: "a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast".
  • Over three miles long, but in places only 50 m wide.
  • Made of shingle beach and tidal flat deposits.
  • It grows where the coast turns at the Humber mouth: drift carries sediment on into open water, where it is dropped.
  • Holderness's eroding cliffs supply sand and gravel to Spurn: "The continuation of erosion along the Holderness coast is crucial for the downdrift frontages".
Mission 6

Processes at different scales

  • Small scale, hours: one high tide cuts the foot of a cliff.
  • Medium scale, years: slumps, then removal of debris; retreat of 2.16 m a year at Aldbrough, 1951–2004.
  • Large scale, decades to centuries: drift moves sediment the length of the coast to Spurn; the coast has retreated about 2 km in 1,000 years.
Mission 6

Climate's influence

  • Storms: winter is the UK's windiest season, with more Atlantic storms. In December 2013 a tidal surge broke through Spurn's narrowest part and destroyed its road.
  • Rain: rainwater soaks into till; more rain "could increase the sensitivity of the till deposits to destabilisation".
  • Freezing and thawing of wet clay in winter also loosens the cliff face.
Quick check
?Holderness's cliffs erode quickly, yet Spurn, at the southern end of the same coast, is a long ridge of sand and shingle. Why?
Mission 6

People: defend some places, not all

  • The shoreline management plan holds the line at Bridlington, Hornsea, Withernsea, Mappleton and the Easington gas terminals.
  • Elsewhere, the cliffs are left to erode, so beaches down-drift keep their supply.
  • Mappleton, 1991: two rock groynes and a rock revetment (rock armour laid as a sloping bank along the foot of the cliff), built "to prevent cliff recession", with a design life of 50 years.
Mission 6

How management changes the coast

  • Defended Mappleton is becoming a headland: the undefended cliffs either side keep retreating.
  • A bay is forming between Hornsea and Mappleton.
  • The beach and shore platform in front of the defences are being lowered.
  • Defended stretches may cut the sediment supplied down-drift: people and processes act together.
Mission 6

The future

  • Global sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, letting waves reach higher up the cliffs.
  • In modelled UKCP18 scenarios, sea-level rise could completely cut off Spurn Head by 2050.
Quick check
?Which factor has most influenced how fast the Holderness cliffs change?
Mission check

?On another coast, soft cliffs erode and longshore drift carries the sediment to a spit at the far end. A town halfway along builds groynes that trap the drifting sand. What is most likely to happen?

Holderness's lost land did not vanish: the sea eroded the till, longshore drift carried sand and gravel south, and Spurn grew from it. That is why the plan defends only a few places and lets the rest erode.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Igneous (basalt: fast cooling, small crystals; granite: slow cooling, large crystals), sedimentary (sandstone; chalk from shells: layers, grains, fossils), metamorphic (slate from shale; schist: changed by heat and pressure).
  • North and west uplands: igneous and metamorphic rocks shaped by past tectonics; south and east lowlands: sedimentary rocks such as chalk and clay.

Mission 2

  • Uplands north and west on hard rocks; lowlands south and east on soft rocks; chalk forms low hills because it is permeable.
  • Ice reached South Wales, north Norfolk and the Vale of York; the wet west and dry east shape processes.
  • Farming (69% of UK land), forestry (14%) and settlement make landscapes distinctive.

Mission 3

  • Destructive waves erode and constructive waves build. Erosion and slumping wear cliffs back; longshore drift moves the sediment along the coast.
  • Rock type sets the pace: weak till retreats fast; chalk resists.
  • Prevailing south-westerlies and winter storms speed up erosion: weight each period by its length when you work out a mean rate.

Mission 4

  • Hard rock makes headlands; soft rock makes bays. Headlands are eroded along joints: cave, arch, stack, stump.
  • Where waves lose energy, sediment builds beaches; where the coast turns, it builds spits.
  • A spit that crosses a bay becomes a bar.

Mission 5

  • Hard engineering holds the line but is costly and can harm the coast down-drift; soft engineering works with natural processes.
  • Medmerry chose managed retreat: cheaper and lower-risk than holding a failing bank, with habitat gained and land lost.

Mission 6

  • Holderness: soft till cliffs retreat 1–4.5 m a year; resistant chalk forms Flamborough Head, with over 200 caves; Spurn spit is built from the eroded sediment.
  • Geology sets the rate; winter storms and rain drive it; defences at Mappleton create a headland and can starve the coast down-drift.
