Mission 1Topics 4.1–4.5

Mission 1: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Make a guess: Can burning 1 kg of charcoal really make more than 3 kg of carbon dioxide?

Mission 1

What an equation tells you

  • Atoms are rearranged, never made or destroyed: each element balances.
  • Coefficients give the ratio of amounts in moles, not masses.
  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l): 1 mol : 2 mol : 1 mol : 2 mol.
  • A coefficient multiplies the whole formula: 2O2 is four O atoms.
  • Total mass is conserved. Total moles need not be.
  • Total charge is conserved too.
Mission 1

Formulae of ionic compounds

  • Charges must total zero: Na⁺ and SO4²⁻ → Na2SO4.
  • Charge from the group: groups 1, 2 → 1+, 2+; Al → 3+; groups 16, 17 → 2−, 1−.
  • Learn the common ions: NO3⁻, CO3²⁻, SO4²⁻, OH⁻, NH4⁺.
  • Brackets for two or more of a polyatomic ion: Ca(OH)2, (NH4)2SO4, Al2(SO4)3.
Mission 1

Balancing, with state symbols

  • Write correct formulae first. H2, N2, O2 and Cl2 are molecules.
  • Balance by changing coefficients only, never subscripts.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), (aq). A precipitate is (s); water formed at room temperature is (l); a gas given off is (g).
  • Unfamiliar reaction? Turn the names you're given into formulae, then balance.
  • "Sodium reacts with water to give sodium hydroxide solution and hydrogen": 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g).
  • Physical changes have equations too: H2O(s) → H2O(l). So does dissolving: NaCl(s) → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) (is that physical or chemical? See "Physical or chemical change?").
Quick check
?A student writes the equation Cu(s) + Ag⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Ag(s) to represent the reaction of copper metal with aqueous silver ions. Which statement about the student's equation is correct?
Mission 1

Ionic equations

  • In solution, soluble ionic compounds and strong acids are present as separate ions. Precipitates, water and gases keep their full formulae.
  • Ions unchanged on both sides are spectator ions. Leave them out.
  • Precipitation: Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s).
  • Neutralization: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l).
  • Displacement: Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
  • Check that atoms and charges balance.
  • Three forms: molecular (full formulae), complete ionic (every ion shown), net ionic (spectators removed).
Quick check
?When aqueous solutions of lead(II) nitrate, Pb(NO3)2, and potassium iodide, KI, are mixed, a yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide, PbI2, forms. Which of the following is the balanced net ionic equation for the reaction?
Mission 1

Physical or chemical change?

  • Physical: properties change, composition doesn't. Phase changes; forming or separating mixtures.
  • Chemical: new substances form, with new compositions.
  • Possible evidence of chemical change: heat or light, gas, precipitate, color change. Evidence, not proof: boiling water makes bubbles, but it's physical.
  • Bond view: chemical bonds broken or formed → chemical. Only intermolecular interactions change → physical.
  • Gray area: dissolving NaCl breaks ionic bonds but forms ion–dipole attractions. You can argue either way.
Mission 1

Particle pictures

  • Each symbol is one particle. Every atom in "before" appears in "after".
  • 2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(g): 4 H2 + 2 O2 → 4 H2O.
  • Numbers of particles follow the coefficient ratio, not the coefficients themselves.
  • A reactant in excess appears in "after" too.
  • (aq) ionic compounds: separate ions. (s): a packed lattice. (g): far-apart particles.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a particulate representation of the reaction represented by the equation 2 Al(s) + 3 Cl2(g) → 2 AlCl3(s). The student's "before" box contains 6 Cl2 molecules. How many Al atoms should the "before" box contain, and how many AlCl3 formula units should the "after" box contain, if no reactant is to be left over?
Mission 1

Three steps for any reacting quantity

  • Step 1: given quantity → amount in moles.
  • Step 2: × mole ratio (wanted coefficient ÷ given coefficient).
  • Step 3: moles → the quantity asked for.
  • Mass: n = m/M. M is for one formula unit: the coefficient is used only in step 2.
  • Gas: n = PV/RT. Solution: n = molarity × V (V in L).
Mission 1

Worked example: reacting masses

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). What mass of CaO forms from 25.0 g of CaCO3?
  • Step 1: M(CaCO3) = 40.08 + 12.01 + 3(16.00) = 100.09 g/mol; n = 25.0 ÷ 100.09 = 0.2498 mol.
  • Step 2: ratio 1 : 1, so n(CaO) = 0.2498 mol.
  • Step 3: m = 0.2498 × 56.08 = 14.0 g.
  • Mass check: the other 11.0 g leaves as CO2.
Quick check
?Excess AgNO3(aq) is added to 25.0 mL of 0.100 M CaCl2(aq), and all of the chloride ions precipitate as AgCl(s) (molar mass 143.32 g/mol). What mass of AgCl(s) forms?
Mission 1

Gravimetric analysis

  • Add excess of a reagent that precipitates the ion you want.
  • Filter, wash, dry to constant mass, weigh.
  • Moles of precipitate → moles of ion → mass or % in the sample.
  • 0.250 g of impure calcium chloride, dissolved; excess Na2CO3(aq) gives 0.220 g CaCO3.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.220 ÷ 100.09 = 2.198 × 10⁻³ mol = n(CaCl2). Mass = 2.198 × 10⁻³ × 110.98 = 0.244 g: 97.6% pure.
  • Masses can give a formula too: burn a known mass of Mg; the mass gained gives the Mg : O mole ratio.
Mission check

?A strip of magnesium burns brightly in air and leaves white magnesium oxide: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s). What mass of MgO forms from 2.43 g of Mg?
  • (Molar masses: Mg 24.31 g/mol, MgO 40.30 g/mol)

Same with charcoal. 1.00 kg C is 83 mol; C : CO2 is 1 : 1, so about 3.7 kg of CO2 forms. The extra 2.7 kg is oxygen taken from the air.

Mission 2Topics 4.2, 4.3, 4.7

Mission 2: When two clear solutions make a cloudy solid, which particles actually react?

Make a guess: When two clear solutions make a cloudy solid, which particles actually react?

Mission 2

Three kinds of reaction

  • Acid–base: a proton (H⁺) is transferred from one species to another.
  • Redox (oxidation–reduction): electrons are transferred; oxidation numbers change.
  • Precipitation: ions in solution combine into an insoluble ionic solid.
  • To classify: look for H⁺ moving, oxidation numbers changing, or a new solid forming from ions.
Mission 2

Solubility: the rule you need

  • All sodium, potassium, ammonium and nitrate salts are soluble in water.
  • So Na⁺, K⁺, NH4⁺ and NO3⁻ stay dissolved, and are usually spectator ions.
  • Any other solubility you need is given to you, or shown by what you see ("a precipitate forms").
  • Two solutions make a solid? Look for a new pairing of ions that is not one of these always-soluble salts.
Mission 2

Three ways to write one reaction

  • Lead(II) nitrate and potassium iodide solutions make a yellow precipitate of lead(II) iodide.
  • Molecular: Pb(NO3)2(aq) + 2 KI(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 KNO3(aq).
  • Complete ionic: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 NO3⁻(aq) + 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI2(s) + 2 K⁺(aq) + 2 NO3⁻(aq).
  • K⁺ and NO3⁻ appear unchanged on both sides: spectator ions. Cancel them.
  • Net ionic: Pb²⁺(aq) + 2 I⁻(aq) → PbI2(s). Atoms and charge balance.
  • Split into ions: soluble ionic compounds and strong acids, (aq). Keep whole: solids, liquids, gases, weak acids and weak bases.
  • Use the molecular form for what you measure out; the net ionic form for what actually changes.
Quick check
?When aqueous solutions of AgNO3 and (NH4)2CrO4 are mixed, a red precipitate forms. Which of the following is the balanced net ionic equation for the reaction?
Mission 2

Drawing the particles

  • A soluble ionic compound (aq) is drawn as separate ions, spread through the water.
  • A polyatomic ion such as NO3⁻ stays in one piece.
  • A precipitate is drawn as ions packed together in a solid.
  • Spectator ions are still there after the reaction: draw them, still separate.
  • Count atoms and charges: the after picture must match the before picture and the equation.
Quick check
?A student draws a particulate diagram of the mixture that results from the reaction represented by the equation AgNO3(aq) + NaCl(aq) → AgCl(s) + NaNO3(aq). The diagram shows Ag⁺ and Cl⁻ ions packed together in a solid at the bottom of the beaker and, in the water above it, NaNO3 units in which each Na⁺ ion is joined to an NO3⁻ ion. Which evaluation of the diagram is best?
Mission 2

Acid–base: follow the proton

  • Strong acid + strong base. Molecular: HCl(aq) + NaOH(aq) → NaCl(aq) + H2O(l).
  • Net ionic: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l). Na⁺ and Cl⁻ are spectators.
  • A weak acid stays as molecules: CH3COOH(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → CH3COO⁻(aq) + H2O(l).
  • No hydroxide or water needed: NH3(g) + HCl(g) → NH4Cl(s). H⁺ moves from HCl to NH3.
  • Test: did an H⁺ move from one species to another, with no oxidation-number change?
Mission 2

Redox: follow the electrons

  • Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s).
  • Zn: 0 → +2. It loses 2 electrons: Zn is oxidized.
  • Cu: +2 → 0. It gains 2 electrons: Cu²⁺ is reduced.
  • Electrons move from the species oxidized to the species reduced.
  • Combustion is redox with O2: CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O. C goes −4 → +4; O goes 0 → −2. Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon gives CO2 and H2O.
Quick check
?Zinc metal reacts with hydrochloric acid: Zn(s) + 2 HCl(aq) → ZnCl2(aq) + H2(g). A student claims that this is an oxidation-reduction reaction. Which of the following best evaluates the claim?
Mission check

?Colorless sodium hydroxide solution is added to blue copper(II) sulfate solution. A pale blue precipitate of copper(II) hydroxide, Cu(OH)2, forms. Which is the balanced net ionic equation for the reaction?

In a precipitation, only the ions that pack into the solid react. The rest are spectators, still dissolved and unchanged.

Mission 3Topic 4.5

Mission 3: Mix 10 g of hydrogen with 10 g of oxygen. Which runs out first?

Make a guess: Mix 10 g of hydrogen with 10 g of oxygen. Which runs out first?

Mission 3

Limiting and excess

  • The limiting reactant is used up first, and the reaction stops.
  • So it fixes the maximum amount of product.
  • The other reactant is in excess: some is left over.
  • 2CO + O2 → 2CO2, starting with 6 CO and 4 O2: 6 CO2 form, and 1 O2 is left over.
Mission 3

Finding the limiting reactant

  • Convert every reactant to moles. Masses alone tell you nothing.
  • Divide each amount by its coefficient in the equation. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.
  • Calculate all products from the limiting reactant only.
  • Excess left over = amount present − amount that reacted.
  • Compare moles, not masses: the smaller mass is often not the limiting reactant.
Mission 3

Worked example: burning methane

  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l), with 8.00 g of CH4 and 24.0 g of O2.
  • n(CH4) = 8.00 g ÷ 16.04 g/mol = 0.499 mol; ÷ 1 = 0.499.
  • n(O2) = 24.0 ÷ 32.0 = 0.750 mol; ÷ 2 = 0.375. Smaller, so O2 limits.
  • n(CO2) = 0.750 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol; mass = 0.375 × 44.0 = 16.5 g at most.
  • CH4 left = 0.499 − 0.375 = 0.124 mol = 1.99 g.
  • The smaller mass (methane) was the one in excess.
Quick check
?A 12.0 g sample of Mg(s) and 12.0 g of O2(g) are allowed to react according to the equation 2 Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2 MgO(s). Which statement about the limiting reactant is correct and correctly justified?
Mission 3

Theoretical and experimental yield

  • Theoretical yield: the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reactant.
  • Experimental (actual) yield: the mass of product actually obtained. It is normally less.
  • In the lab, percent yield = actual yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100%.
  • Why less: incomplete or reversible reaction, side reactions, product lost in transfer and purification.
  • Above 100%? The product is impure, for example still wet.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the theoretical yield of a product in a reaction?
Mission check

?Thermite is a mixture of aluminum and iron(III) oxide. 2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). A mixture contains 10.0 g of Al and 10.0 g of Fe2O3. Which statement identifies the limiting reactant with a correct reason?
Molar masses: Al 27.0 g/mol, Fe2O3 159.6 g/mol.

Now the hydrogen. 10 g of H2 is about 5 mol; 10 g of O2 is only about 0.31 mol, which needs just 0.63 mol of H2. Oxygen runs out first, and most of the hydrogen is left over.

Mission 4Topic 4.6

Mission 4: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Make a guess: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Mission 4

Three ways to moles

  • From a mass: n = m ÷ M.
  • From a solution: n = M × V, with V in L (mL ÷ 1000).
  • From a gas: PV = nRT (P in atm, V in L, T in K, R = 0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹).
  • Back again: use the same equation, rearranged.
Mission 4

Worked example: mass to gas volume

  • Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g), acid in excess. What volume of H2 forms from 1.30 g of zinc?
  • n(Zn) = 1.30 g ÷ 65.38 g/mol = 0.01988 mol = n(H2). At 298 K and 1.00 atm: V = nRT ÷ P = 0.01988 × 0.08206 × 298 ÷ 1.00 = 0.486 L.
  • Mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → the quantity you want.
Mission 4

Carrying out a titration

  • A pipet (with a filler) puts a fixed volume of one solution into a Erlenmeyer flask. Add a few drops of indicator.
  • A buret adds the other solution, with swirling, over a white tile. Go dropwise near the color change.
  • Read the buret at the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level, to the nearest 0.01 mL.
  • The solution in the buret is the titrant (known concentration). The solution in the flask is the analyte (the amount you want).
Mission 4

Equivalence point and end point

  • Equivalence point: the analyte has been totally consumed by the titrant.
  • Endpoint: the observable event, usually a color change, that signals the equivalence point.
  • So the color change tells you when the moles match the equation. The buret tells you how much was needed.
Quick check
?A student titrates a sample of HCl(aq) with standardized NaOH(aq), using phenolphthalein as the indicator. Instead of stopping at the first faint pink color that persists, the student keeps adding NaOH until the solution is dark pink and records that buret reading. How does this error affect the calculated number of moles of HCl in the sample?
Mission 4

Worked example: a titration calculation

  • A 25.0 mL sample of NaOH(aq) needs 21.40 mL of 0.0500 M H2SO4 to reach the endpoint.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • Step 1, known solution: n(H2SO4) = 0.0500 × 0.02140 = 1.070 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 2, ratio: n(NaOH) = 2 × 1.070 × 10⁻³ = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 3, molarity: [NaOH] = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol ÷ 0.0250 L = 0.0856 M.
Quick check
?Now the mission: vinegar.
A 10.0 mL sample of vinegar is titrated with 0.500 M NaOH, using phenolphthalein. The endpoint is reached after 16.60 mL. CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l). What is the molarity of acetic acid in the vinegar?

The indicator turns pink once all the acid has reacted. The titer gives n(NaOH); the 1 : 1 ratio gives n(acid). That is about 50 g of acid per liter: typical of 5% vinegar.

Quick check
?A student titrates a 25.00 mL sample of a weak acid, HA, with 0.100 M NaOH and records the volume of NaOH needed to reach the equivalence point. The student then titrates an identical 25.00 mL sample of HA to which 50.0 mL of distilled water has been added. How does the volume of NaOH needed to reach the equivalence point in the second titration compare with that in the first, and why?
Mission check

?Limewater is a solution of calcium hydroxide. Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
A 25.0 mL sample of limewater is titrated with 0.0500 M HCl, using phenolphthalein. The endpoint is reached after 18.40 mL. What is the molarity of the calcium hydroxide?

As with the vinegar: the color change marks when the moles match the equation; the buret gives the volume; the ratio does the rest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coefficients are mole ratios. Balance with coefficients; ionic equations leave out spectator ions; check atoms and charge.
  • Three steps: given → moles; × mole ratio; moles → what's asked.
  • Molecular, complete ionic and net ionic equations are three forms of one reaction.

Mission 2

  • Acid–base: a proton moves. Redox: electrons move (oxidation numbers change). Precipitation: ions form an insoluble solid.
  • Net ionic equation: split soluble ionic compounds and strong acids into ions, cancel spectators, check atoms and charge.

Mission 3

  • Moles ÷ coefficient: the smallest is the limiting reactant. It fixes the theoretical yield.
  • Excess left = amount present − amount reacted.

Mission 4

  • Moles from a mass, a solution or a gas volume, then the mole ratio, then back.
  • The indicator changes color at the end point, which a suitable indicator makes match the equivalence point.
  • At the equivalence point, moles of titrant and analyte match the equation (1 : 1 for monoprotic acids).
