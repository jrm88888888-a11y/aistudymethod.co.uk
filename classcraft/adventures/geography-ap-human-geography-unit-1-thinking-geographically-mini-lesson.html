Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does Greenland look bigger than South America on so many world maps?

Make a guess: Why does Greenland look bigger than South America on so many world maps?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • IMP-1.A identify types of maps, the information they show, and the spatial patterns and relationships they portray
  • IMP-1.A.1 reference maps and thematic maps
  • IMP-1.A.2 absolute and relative distance and direction, clustering, dispersal, elevation
  • IMP-1.A.3 all maps are selective; projections distort shape, area, distance and direction
Mission 1

Two kinds of map

  • Reference map: shows where things are: boundaries, cities, roads, rivers, physical features. Examples: a road atlas, a topographic map, a political world map.
  • Thematic map: shows one topic or pattern across space: population density, income, religion, rainfall.
  • Ask: "Is this map for finding places, or for showing a pattern?"
Mission 1

Kinds of thematic map

  • Choropleth: areas shaded by a value (e.g., % of people who are farmers in each country).
  • Dot distribution: one dot = a set number of things (e.g., 1 dot = 1,000 people).
  • Graduated symbol: bigger symbol = bigger value (e.g., circles sized by city population).
  • Isoline: lines joining equal values (e.g., contour lines for elevation, isotherms for temperature).
  • Cartogram: area of each country resized by a value (e.g., population).
Quick check
?A map of the United States shades each state from light to dark by median household income. What type of map is this?
Mission 1

Distance and direction

  • Absolute distance: measured in standard units (miles, kilometers).
  • Relative distance: measured by time, cost or connection. Example: two towns 50 miles apart are "closer" by highway than two towns 20 miles apart across a mountain range.
  • Absolute direction: cardinal directions (north, south, east, west).
  • Relative direction: depends on where you stand (left, right, "past the school").
Mission 1

Patterns on the map

  • Clustering: things grouped close together (e.g., houses along a river).
  • Dispersal: things spread out over a large area (e.g., farmhouses on the Great Plains).
  • Elevation: height above sea level, shown by contour lines, colors or shading.
  • Patterns help explain why things are where they are.
Quick check
?A dot map shows most of a country's people packed into a narrow strip along one river, with almost no dots in the desert on either side. Which pattern does the map show?
Mission 1

Every map is selective

  • No map shows everything. Mapmakers choose:
    • what to include and what to leave out,
    • how much to simplify coastlines and roads,
    • which projection, colors and categories to use.
  • So every map reflects choices. Read the title, legend and source.
Mission 1

Why projections distort

  • A map projection turns the round Earth into a flat map. This "cannot be done without distortion".
  • The mapmaker "must choose the characteristic which is to be shown accurately at the expense of others".
  • Four things can be distorted: shape, area, distance, direction.
  • "No map can be both equal-area and conformal" (true in shape).
Mission 1

The Mercator projection

  • Mercator: local angles are shown correctly, so small shapes are "essentially true".
  • Lines of constant direction are straight, so it is "very useful in navigation".
  • But area is badly stretched toward the poles.
  • "Greenland appears larger, although it is only one-eighth the size of South America."
Mission 1

Other choices

  • Equal-area projections: every place keeps its true area; shapes are distorted, especially near the edges.
  • Azimuthal projections: directions from the map's center are correct.
  • Equidistant projections: true distance from one or two points, or along every meridian.
  • Compromise projections (e.g., Robinson): small errors in everything, nothing perfect.
Quick check
?A ship's navigator wants to steer one constant compass direction between two ports. Which projection suits this best?
Mission check

?A student wants to compare how much land is covered by forest in Canada and in Brazil, using shading on a world map. Which map should she use, and why?

Flattening a globe always distorts something. Mercator keeps directions and small shapes but swells areas near the poles, so Greenland looks bigger than South America, which is about eight times larger.

Mission 2

Mission 2: After Nepal's 2015 earthquake, how did thousands of people help map where help was needed?

Make a guess: After Nepal's 2015 earthquake, how did thousands of people help map where help was needed?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • IMP-1.B identify different methods of geographic data collection
  • IMP-1.B.1 field data gathered by organizations or individuals
  • IMP-1.B.2 geospatial technologies: GIS, satellite navigation, remote sensing, online mapping and visualization
  • IMP-1.B.3 spatial information from written sources: field notes, media, travel narratives, policy documents, interviews, landscape and photo analysis
  • IMP-1.C explain geographical effects of decisions made using geographical information
  • IMP-1.C.1 census data and satellite imagery inform personal, business/organizational and government decisions at all scales
Mission 2

Field data

  • Field data: information collected in person, at the place itself.
  • Gathered by organizations: census bureaus, government agencies, aid groups.
  • Gathered by individuals: researchers, students, volunteers.
  • Example: after Nepal's earthquake, engineers inspected rebuilt houses and recorded results on a tablet app.
Mission 2

Geospatial technologies

  • GIS (geographic information systems): software that stacks layers of data (roads, population, hazards) on one map to analyze them.
  • Satellite navigation (GPS): the USA commits to at least 24 operational GPS satellites, 95% of the time.
  • Remote sensing: gathering data from a distance, by satellite or aircraft. Landsat satellites have imaged Earth's land since July 1972.
  • Online mapping and visualization: web maps that anyone can view, and often edit.
Quick check
?A city planner overlays maps of flood zones, schools and population on one screen to choose a site for a new school. Which tool is this?
Mission 2

Written and visual sources

  • Spatial information also comes from words and pictures:
    • field observations (notes and sketches), personal interviews;
    • media reports, travel narratives, policy documents;
    • landscape analysis and photographic interpretation.
  • These are often qualitative: they show how people see and use places.
Quick check
?A geographer asks farmers how a new road changed where they sell their crops, and records their answers. Which method is this?
Mission 2

Nepal 2015: images from space

  • On April 25, 2015, a magnitude 7.8 earthquake struck Gorkha, Nepal.
  • Scientists mapped landslides using high-resolution satellite imagery: the earthquake and aftershocks caused at least 25,000 landslides.
  • Maps like these show which roads and villages may be cut off.
Mission 2

Nepal 2015: mapping by individuals

  • More than 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, a free online map.
  • Their mapping covered more than 80% of the earthquake-hit zones. Anyone online can edit OpenStreetMap.
  • The map was used by Nepal's military, the Red Cross and many other organizations.
Mission 2

Nepal 2015: phones as sensors

  • Researchers used movements of 12 million de-identified mobile phones.
  • They estimated that about 390,000 more people than normal left the Kathmandu Valley.
  • Limits: phone ownership "is skewed towards certain population groups", and some areas had too little network coverage.
Quick check
?Why might relying only on mobile phone data miss some people who need help?
Mission 2

Census data drive decisions

  • Government: after the 2020 US Census, Texas gained two House seats; Colorado, Florida, Montana, North Carolina and Oregon gained one; seven states each lost one.
  • Business: companies use census data on population and income to choose where to open stores.
  • Personal: people use online maps and GPS to choose routes, homes and schools.
  • Decisions at every scale, from a household to a nation.
Mission 2

Geographical effects

  • Decisions based on data change places: where aid goes, where roads are rebuilt, where political power shifts.
  • If data miss some people or places, decisions miss them too.
  • Combining sources (satellite imagery, census, fieldwork, interviews) gives a fuller picture.
Mission check

?After a river flood, a city must decide where to send rescue boats and food first. Which pair of sources gives the best basis for that decision?

In Nepal, satellite images mapped 25,000 landslides, more than 6,000 volunteers added data to OpenStreetMap, and phone data tracked movement. Organizations and individuals, using remote sensing, online mapping and census-style data, decide where help goes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Planes and the internet have "shrunk" the world. So why do most people who move stay close to home?

Make a guess: Planes and the internet have "shrunk" the world. So why do most people who move stay close to home?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • PSO-1.A define major geographic concepts that illustrate spatial relationships
  • PSO-1.A.1 absolute and relative location, space, place, flows, distance decay, time-space compression, pattern
Mission 3

Absolute and relative location

  • Absolute location: a precise position, usually given by latitude and longitude.
  • The USGS gives the geographic center of the lower 48 states as 39°50' N, 98°35' W.
  • Relative location: a position described in relation to other places. The USGS: "Near Lebanon, Smith County, Kansas".
  • Even this point is an approximation: the USGS says there is "no generally accepted definition of a geographic center".
Mission 3

Space, place and pattern

  • Space: the area between and around things, and how things are arranged within it.
  • Place: a specific location with physical and human characteristics that give it meaning (a sense of place).
  • Pattern: the general arrangement of things in space: clustered, dispersed or linear.
  • Geographers ask where things are, then why they are arranged that way.
Quick check
?Which of these describes a relative location?
Mission 3

Flows

  • Flows: movements of people, goods, money and information between places.
  • Goods: world container port traffic rose from 547.1 million TEU (twenty-foot equivalent units) in 2010 to 913.3 million in 2024.
  • Flows link places, so a place's relative location can change when new routes, ports or cables open.
Mission 3

Distance decay

  • Distance decay: interaction between two places declines as the distance between them increases.
  • US movers in 2019:
    • same county: 55.3%
    • different county, same state: 23.7%
    • different state: 16.7%
    • from abroad: 4.3%
  • Most moves are short: people keep jobs, family, friends and schools within reach.
Quick check
?Using the 2019 figures earlier in this mission, what share of US movers moved within one state?
Mission 3

Time-space compression

  • Time-space compression: the shrinking of the time (and cost) needed to move people, goods and information between places, as transport and communication improve.
  • Air passengers worldwide: 310.4 million in 1970, 4,455.7 million in 2019, about 14 times as many (4,455.7 ÷ 310.4).
  • Internet users: 15.6% of people in 2005, 73.6% in 2025 (estimate).
Mission 3

Compression is uneven

  • People online, 2025: 94% in high-income countries, 23% in low-income countries.
  • Urban areas 85%; rural areas 58%.
  • So the world has "shrunk" much more for some places than for others.
Quick check
?Which change is the best example of time-space compression?
Mission 3

Putting the concepts together

  • Time-space compression weakens distance decay: far places are easier to reach and contact.
  • It does not remove it: moving home still means leaving jobs, family and friends, and costs money.
  • Exam move: on a free-response question, define the concept, then explain it with a real example and a spatial effect (where, and how far).
Mission check

?A bakery sells online and ships nationwide, yet most of its sales come from people living within a few miles. Which explanation fits best?

The world has shrunk in time and cost, but unevenly, and distance decay still shapes most interactions. It shows in the data: 79% of US movers in 2019 moved within one state.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a quarter of a country lies below sea level, why do millions of people choose to live there?

Make a guess: If a quarter of a country lies below sea level, why do millions of people choose to live there?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • PSO-1.B explain how major geographic concepts illustrate spatial relationships
  • PSO-1.B.1 nature and society concepts include sustainability, natural resources and land use
  • PSO-1.B.2 theories of human–environment interaction have evolved from environmental determinism to possibilism
Mission 4

Natural resources

  • Natural resources: things in nature that people use: water, soil, forests, minerals, fuels.
  • Renewable: replaced naturally within a human lifetime if not overused (solar energy, wind, forests, fresh water).
  • Nonrenewable: limited stocks that take millions of years to form (coal, oil, natural gas, metal ores).
Mission 4

Land use and sustainability

  • Land use: how people use an area of land: farming, housing, industry, parks, transportation.
  • Sustainability: using resources so they last. The UN's 1987 report described development that "meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs".
  • Land use choices decide whether resources are used sustainably or used up.
Quick check
?A town draws water from an aquifer faster than rain can refill it. Which statement is best?
Mission 4

Environmental determinism

  • Environmental determinism: the idea that the physical environment (climate, landforms) controls how people and societies develop.
  • A leading US example: geographer Ellen Churchill Semple, whose 1911 book was based on the German geographer Friedrich Ratzel's work.
  • Her opening line: "Man is a product of the earth's surface."
Mission 4

Determinism in its own words

  • Semple claimed that on the mountains the earth "has given him leg muscles of iron", while "along the coast she has left these weak and flabby".
  • She claimed river-valley farming "narrows his outlook to the cramped horizon of his farm".
  • These are claims, not findings: the book offers no measurements that test them.
Mission 4

Why geographers moved on

  • Determinism predicts that similar environments produce similar societies. They often do not.
  • It leaves out culture, technology, trade and choice.
  • People change environments too: they irrigate deserts, drain wetlands, build cities on reclaimed land.
Mission 4

Possibilism

  • Possibilism: the environment sets limits and possibilities, but people choose how to use them, using culture and technology.
  • French geographers set it against determinism: "Against the geographical determinism of Ratzel he sets the possibilism of Vidal de la Blache".
  • A key line: "There are no necessities, but everywhere possibilities".
Quick check
?Which statement is an example of environmental determinism?
Mission 4

Possibilism on the map

  • The Netherlands: "26 per cent of the country is below sea level". People built dikes, pumps and drained land (polders) and use it for farms and cities. About 18 million people lived in the Netherlands in 2024.
  • Singapore: an island city that imports part of its water from Johor, Malaysia. It added recycled water, called NEWater, in 2002.
  • The limits were real. The land use came from human choices and technology.
Quick check
?How does the Netherlands best illustrate possibilism?
Mission check

?Two desert regions have the same hot, dry climate. In one, people herd camels and goats. In the other, people grow crops on large farms watered by canals and wells. Which explanation fits the possibilist view?

Environmental determinism said the environment controls people. Possibilism says it offers limits and possibilities, and people choose, as the Dutch did on land below sea level.

Mission 5

Mission 5: The world's population is growing, Japan's is shrinking, and Tokyo's is growing. Which is the true story?

Make a guess: The world's population is growing, Japan's is shrinking, and Tokyo's is growing. Which is the true story?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • PSO-1.C define scales of analysis used by geographers
  • PSO-1.C.1 scales of analysis include global, regional, national and local
  • PSO-1.D explain what scales of analysis reveal
  • PSO-1.D.1 patterns and processes at different scales reveal variations in, and different interpretations of, data
Mission 5

Four scales of analysis

  • Scale of analysis: the size of the area a geographer studies.
  • Global: the whole world.
  • Regional: a group of countries (such as sub-Saharan Africa), or a large part of one country.
  • National: one country.
  • Local: a city, county, town or neighborhood.
Mission 5

Global scale: growing

  • In 2024 the world's population grew by about 0.96%.
  • At this scale the story is simple: the world still adds people every year.
Mission 5

Regional scale: fast and slow

  • Population growth in 2024:
    • Sub-Saharan Africa: about 2.44%.
    • East Asia and Pacific: about 0.16%.
  • One world total hides very different regional rates.
Mission 5

National scale: Japan shrinks

  • On October 1, 2024, Japan had 123,802 thousand people, 550 thousand fewer than a year before.
  • That is a fall of 0.44%, the fourteenth year of decline in a row.
  • At the national scale, Japan's story is decline, the opposite of the world's.
Mission 5

Local scale: Tokyo grows

  • In 2024, population rose in only two of Japan's 47 prefectures: Tokyo and Saitama, next to it.
  • Tokyo had the highest rate of increase; 45 prefectures shrank, with Akita shrinking fastest.
  • Inside a shrinking country, people are concentrating in and around the capital.
Quick check
?Which statement about population change in 2024 is accurate?
Mission 5

Zooming in on a city: Mumbai

  • Global: in 2022, 24.8% of the world's urban people lived in slums or informal settlements.
  • City: in India's 2011 census, 52.5% of Greater Mumbai's people lived in slums (6,534,460 of 12,442,373).
  • Ward (city district): 10.0% in D ward, but 84.9% in M/East ward.
  • Each zoom reveals differences the larger scale averaged away.
Mission 5

Why scale changes the interpretation

  • A large-scale average hides variation inside it.
  • A pattern at one scale can come from a process at another: if people move within a country, one city can grow while the nation shrinks.
  • The same data can support different conclusions: "Japan is shrinking" (national) and "Tokyo needs more housing" (local) are both true.
Quick check
?Mumbai's city leaders must choose where to spend money on new water and sanitation for slum areas. Which data would help them most?
Mission 5

Choosing the right scale

  • Match the scale to the question: world trends need global data; a city plan needs local data.
  • Compare more than one scale before drawing a conclusion.
  • Ask: what does a larger scale hide, and what does a smaller scale miss about the bigger picture?
Mission check

?In an imaginary country, the national unemployment rate fell over five years, but in its northern region unemployment rose. The government must decide where to open new job-training centers. Which approach fits best?

All three stories are true; which one matters depends on the question. The world grew about 0.96% in 2024, Japan shrank 0.44%, and Tokyo grew. Data at global, regional, national and local scales show different patterns, so match the scale to the decision.

  • SPS-1.A.2 types of regions are formal, functional and perceptual/vernacular
  • SPS-1.A.3 regional boundaries are transitional and often contested and overlapping
  • SPS-1.A.4 geographers apply regional analysis at local, national and global scales
Mission 6

What is a region?

  • A region is an area that geographers group together because it shares something.
  • That "something" can be:
    • one or more unifying characteristics (language, climate, crop, government), or
    • a pattern of activity (commuting, shopping, broadcasting).
Mission 6

Formal regions

  • Formal region: defined by a shared, measurable characteristic.
  • Example: Quebec as a French-speaking region. In 2021, 82.2% of people in Quebec had French as their first official language, against 21.4% in Canada as a whole.
  • Countries, states and counties are formal regions too: defined by government.
Mission 6

Functional regions

  • Functional region: a central node plus the area linked to it by activity.
  • Example: a US metropolitan area: a core plus "adjacent communities having a high degree of economic and social integration with that core".
  • Outlying counties join if enough people commute to or from the core counties.
  • Other examples: a TV station's viewing area, a newspaper's delivery area, an airport's catchment.
Mission 6

Functional boundaries move

  • In 2023 the official New Orleans metro area was redrawn to exclude St. Tammany Parish.
  • Reason: a smaller share of St. Tammany's workers were commuting to jobs in New Orleans and nearby parishes.
  • When the activity changes, the region changes.
Quick check
?A pizza shop delivers to every home within a 20-minute drive. What kind of region is its delivery area?
Mission 6

Perceptual (vernacular) regions

  • Perceptual or vernacular region: defined by how people think and feel about a place.
  • Examples: "the South", "the Midwest", "the Middle East".
  • Built from shared images: accents, food, history, landscape, sports.
  • No official line: each person's map is a little different.
Mission 6

Transitional boundaries

  • Most regional boundaries are transitional: one region fades into the next across a zone, not a line.
  • Example: moving from a mostly French-speaking area to a mostly English-speaking one, the share of French speakers falls gradually.
  • A line on a map is a simplification of that zone.
Mission 6

Contested and overlapping

  • Overlapping: one place belongs to many regions at once. A city can be in a Census region, a metro area and a perceptual region all together.
  • The Census Bureau groups US states into four regions: Northeast, Midwest, South, West. Other definitions of "the Midwest" differ from it.
  • Contested: people disagree about where boundaries lie, or who belongs.
  • The UN says its world regions are "for statistical convenience" and imply no "political or other affiliation".
Quick check
?Ask 100 people to draw "the West Coast's surfer culture region" on a map, and the outlines all differ. What type of region is this, and why do the outlines differ?
Mission 6

Regions at every scale

  • Local: neighborhoods, school attendance zones, a store's market area.
  • National: Census regions; Quebec as a language region within Canada.
  • Global: world regions such as sub-Saharan Africa. The UN has moved away from labeling whole world regions "developed" or "developing": definitions change with purpose.
Mission check

?In an imaginary state, people argue about where "Upstate" begins. A state agency uses a line along county borders for its reports. Which statement is best?

No single line ends the Midwest. The Census Bureau draws one formal definition, commuting draws functional metro areas, and people's perceptions draw others. Who decides depends on the purpose of the region.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Reference maps show where things are; thematic maps show patterns. Maps show distance and direction (absolute and relative), clustering, dispersal and elevation.
  • All maps are selective; every projection distorts shape, area, distance or direction. Choose the projection for the job.

Mission 2

  • Data come from fieldwork (organizations and individuals), geospatial technologies (GIS, GPS, remote sensing, online maps) and written sources (interviews, media, policy documents, photos).
  • Census data and satellite imagery guide personal, business and government decisions; gaps in data become gaps in decisions.

Mission 3

  • Absolute location = coordinates; relative location = position compared with other places. Space, place, pattern and flows describe how places are arranged and linked.
  • Time-space compression shrinks travel and communication time, unevenly; distance decay means interaction still falls with distance.

Mission 4

  • Nature–society concepts: natural resources (renewable, nonrenewable), land use and sustainability.
  • Thinking moved from environmental determinism (the environment controls society) to possibilism (the environment sets limits; people choose).

Mission 5

  • Geographers analyze at global, regional, national and local scales.
  • Patterns vary by scale (world growing, Japan shrinking, Tokyo growing; Mumbai 52.5% overall, 10.0% to 84.9% by ward), so conclusions depend on the scale chosen.

Mission 6

  • Regions are defined by shared characteristics (formal), activity around a node (functional) or perceptions (perceptual/vernacular).
  • Boundaries are transitional, often contested and overlapping; regional analysis works at local, national and global scales.
