Mission 1

One compound, five formulae

  • Empirical: simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Hexane: C3H7.
  • Molecular: actual number of each atom. Hexane: C6H14.
  • Structural: the arrangement of atoms, carbon by carbon: CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2CH3 or CH3(CH2)4CH3.
  • Displayed: every atom and every bond drawn.
  • Skeletal: a zig-zag of C–C bonds; H on carbon is left out.
  • A general formula fits a whole series. Alkanes: CnH2n+2 (more in the next mission).
Mission 1

Writing structural and displayed formulae

  • Structural: write each carbon with its own atoms, in chain order.
  • Put a branch in brackets after the carbon it hangs from: CH3CH(CH3)CH3.
  • Put a group on an inside carbon in brackets too: propan-2-ol is CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Write a carboxyl group as COOH and an ester group as COO: CH3COOH, CH3COOCH3.
  • Displayed: draw every bond, including the O–H bond. Don't shortcut to "–OH".
Mission 1

Reading a skeletal formula

  • Every line end and corner is a carbon, unless a line ends in a label (OH, Cl): that end is the labelled atom.
  • A group drawn on a line off a corner is attached to that carbon.
  • Give each carbon enough H for four bonds. H on O or N is always drawn.
  • Lines are bonds: count the points, not the lines.
  • Example: five zig-zag lines, the last ending in OH. That is 5 C and 1 O.
  • H: 3 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 1 (on O) = 12. So C5H12O, CH3CH2CH2CH2CH2OH.
  • Rings use your specification's symbols: a plain hexagon for cyclohexane, a hexagon with a circle inside for benzene.

  • A functional group is the group of atoms responsible for a compound's typical reactions. It decides the compound's class.

Mission 1

Telling look-alikes apart

  • Aldehyde: C=O carbon also carries an H. Ketone: C=O carbon carries two carbons.
  • Carboxyl: C=O carbon also carries –OH. It counts as one group, not a ketone plus an alcohol.
  • Ester: the single-bonded O joins a second carbon, not H.
  • Ether: C–O–C with no C=O.
  • So C2H6O fits two compounds: CH3CH2OH (alcohol, boils at 78 °C) and CH3OCH3 (ether, boils at −24 °C).
  • Ethanol's O–H lets its molecules hydrogen-bond to each other; the ether has no O–H.
Mission 1

Words for carbon skeletons

  • Aliphatic: carbon and hydrogen in straight chains, branched chains or non-aromatic rings.
  • Alicyclic: aliphatic, arranged in non-aromatic rings (with or without side chains). Cyclohexane.
  • Aromatic: contains a benzene ring. Methylbenzene.
  • Saturated: single C–C bonds only.
  • Unsaturated: has a multiple carbon–carbon bond: C=C, C≡C or an aromatic ring.
  • Cyclohexane, C6H12, is saturated: its ring, not a double bond, lowers the H count.

Mission 2

Homologous series

  • A homologous series is a family of compounds with the same functional group.
  • Each member differs from the next by CH2.
  • One general formula fits every member.
  • Stays the same: the functional group, so chemical properties are similar.
  • Changes: chain length, so physical properties (boiling point, melting point) change gradually.

Mission 2

Using a general formula

  • An alkane with 20 carbons: n = 20, H = 2(20) + 2 = 42. C20H42.
  • An alcohol with 7 carbons: C7H15OH, molecular formula C7H16O.
  • An alkene with Mr = 70.0: CnH2n has Mr = 12.0n + 2.0n = 14.0n. So n = 5: C5H10.
  • An alkyl group is CnH2n+1: methyl CH3–, ethyl C2H5–, propyl C3H7–.
  • R stands for an alkyl group, or any part of a molecule not taking part in the reaction: R–OH is any alcohol.

Mission 2

What changes along a series

  • Boiling and melting separate whole molecules. No covalent bonds break.
  • Each extra CH2 adds electrons and surface contact: stronger induced dipole–dipole forces.
  • More energy is needed to separate the molecules: boiling point rises.
  • Your specification calls these induced dipole–dipole interactions (London forces).
  • Petrol: mostly about C5–C10 alkanes. Candle wax: about C20–C40. Far longer chains: solid at room temperature.
Quick check
?The boiling points of four successive alcohols are: methanol 65 °C, ethanol 78 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C and butan-1-ol 118 °C. Which statement explains this trend?
Mission check

?Butane, C4H10, boils at −1 °C and is sold as camping gas. Dodecane, C12H26, boils at 216 °C and is a liquid in diesel fuel. Which explains the difference?

Same reason as petrol and wax. One series, one functional group; chain length sets the strength of the forces between molecules.

Mission 3

The parts of a name

  • Stem = carbons in the longest chain: meth 1, eth 2, prop 3, but 4, pent 5, hex 6.
  • Learn to 10: hept 7, oct 8, non 9, dec 10.
  • Alkyl groups follow the stems: methyl, ethyl, propyl … decyl.
  • -an- only single C–C bonds; -en- a C=C.
  • Suffix for the main group: -ol, -al, -one, -oic acid. Alkanes just end in -e.
  • Prefixes for side groups: methyl, ethyl; fluoro, chloro, bromo, iodo.
  • Locants are carbon numbers: 2-methylpentan-3-ol.
  • A ring adds cyclo: cyclohexane, cyclohexene.
Mission 3

Four steps to a name

  • 1. Find the longest chain that contains the functional-group carbon. It may bend through a branch.
  • 2. Number from the end that gives the suffix group the lowest locant.
  • 3. Next, lowest locant to the C=C; then to the prefixes.
  • 4. Prefixes in alphabetical order. Repeats: di-, tri- (ignored for alphabetical order). Commas between numbers; hyphens between numbers and letters.
Mission 3

Worked examples: prefixes only

  • CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH(CH3)CH3: longest chain 5 → pentane.
  • From the right, methyls on C2 and C3; from the left, C3 and C4. Lower set wins.
  • Name: 2,3-dimethylpentane.
  • CH3CH(CH3)CHBrCH2CH3: from the left {2, 3}; from the right {3, 4}.
  • Name: 3-bromo-2-methylpentane. Alphabetical order (b before m), not number order.
  • OCR calls these compounds haloalkanes; the prefixes are the same.
Quick check
?What is the IUPAC name of CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2Cl?
Mission 3

Groups that take a suffix

  • -ol: CH3CH2CH2CH(OH)CH3 is pentan-2-ol, not pentan-4-ol.
  • -one: CH3COCH2CH2CH3 is pentan-2-one. Propanone needs no number.
  • -al and -oic acid: that carbon is always C1, so no number: CH3CH2CH2COOH is butanoic acid.
  • With a branch: (CH3)2CHCOOH is 2-methylpropanoic acid.
  • -ene: number the C=C by its lower carbon: CH2=CHCH2CH2CH3 is pent-1-ene.
Mission 3

More than one group

  • The suffix group gets the lowest locant first; the C=C locant goes before -en-.
  • CH2=CHCH2CH(OH)CH3: from the CH3 end, OH on C2, C=C from C4: pent-4-en-2-ol. Not pent-1-en-4-ol.
  • Branch plus suffix: CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH2OH is 3-methylbutan-1-ol.
  • Two of the same group: 1,2-dibromoethane; ethane-1,2-diol (keep the "e" before a consonant).
Quick check
?What is the IUPAC name of CH3CH2CH(CH3)CHO?
Mission 3

Naming aromatic compounds

  • One group on benzene: chlorobenzene, nitrobenzene, methylbenzene.
  • Some take a new parent name: phenol (C6H5OH), benzoic acid (C6H5COOH), phenylamine (C6H5NH2).
  • More groups: the ring carbon carrying the parent's group is C1. Number round the ring to give the lowest locants.
  • CH3 on C1, NO2 on C2 and C4: 2,4-dinitromethylbenzene.
  • OH on C1, Cl on C2, C4 and C6: 2,4,6-trichlorophenol.
Mission 3

Names for later classes

  • Ester: alcohol part first, then acid part ending -oate. CH3COOCH2CH3 is ethyl ethanoate.
  • Acyl chloride: -oyl chloride. CH3COCl is ethanoyl chloride.
  • Nitrile: count the C of C≡N. CH3CH2CN is propanenitrile.
  • Amine: CH3CH2NH2 is ethylamine (also called ethanamine).
  • Amide: CH3CONH2 is ethanamide.
Quick check
?What is the IUPAC name of CH3CH2COCH2CH(CH3)CH3?
Mission check

?Which structural formula is 3-methylpentan-2-one?

Yes. Every part of a name is an instruction: stem = chain length, suffix + locant = which group and where, prefixes = branches.

Mission 4

Structural isomers

  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural formula. The atoms are connected differently.
  • Chain isomers: different carbon skeleton. Hexane and 2-methylpentane, both C6H14.
  • Position isomers: same group, different place. Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol, both C3H8O.
  • Functional group isomers: different families. Propanal and propanone, both C3H6O.
  • Other family pairs: carboxylic acid / ester (C2H4O2); alcohol / ether (C2H6O).
Mission 4

New molecule, or the same one drawn again?

  • Rotating, flipping or bending a drawing does not make a new isomer.
  • Test: trace the connections. Same atoms bonded to the same atoms = same compound.
  • Quick test: name both. Same name, same compound.
  • "3-methylbutane" is 2-methylbutane numbered from the wrong end.
  • Coming next: stereoisomers have the same connections but a different arrangement in space.
Mission 4

Worked example: every isomer of C5H12

  • Work systematically: longest chain first, then shorten it by one carbon at a time.
  • 5-carbon chain: CH3CH2CH2CH2CH3, pentane.
  • 4-carbon chain + one CH3: it can only go on C2: CH3CH(CH3)CH2CH3, 2-methylbutane.
  • 3-carbon chain + two CH3: both on C2: C(CH3)4, 2,2-dimethylpropane.
  • A CH3 on an end carbon just lengthens the chain. So C5H12 has three isomers.
Quick check
?Which pair of compounds are position isomers?
Mission 4

Primary, secondary, tertiary

  • Find the carbon carrying the –OH (or halogen). Count the carbons bonded to it.
  • One → primary (1°); two → secondary (2°); three → tertiary (3°).
  • Propan-1-ol 1°; propan-2-ol 2°; 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH, 3°.
  • Haloalkanes the same way: (CH3)3CBr is tertiary.
  • The class is not the locant: hexan-3-ol is secondary.
  • Amines, met later, count differently: carbons bonded to the N. CH3CH2NH2 1°, (CH3)2NH 2°, (CH3)3N 3°.
Mission 4

Worked example: the four isomers of C4H9Cl

  • 4-carbon chain, Cl on C1: CH3CH2CH2CH2Cl, 1-chlorobutane: primary.
  • 4-carbon chain, Cl on C2: CH3CHClCH2CH3, 2-chlorobutane: secondary.
  • 3-carbon chain + CH3, Cl on an end: (CH3)2CHCH2Cl, 1-chloro-2-methylpropane: primary.
  • 3-carbon chain + CH3, Cl on the middle: (CH3)3CCl, 2-chloro-2-methylpropane: tertiary.
  • Chain isomers and position isomers together: four in all.

Mission 5

Radicals

  • A radical is a species with an unpaired electron.
  • A dot marks the unpaired electron: Cl•, CH3•.
  • The dot is not a charge. These radicals are neutral.
  • Odd number of outer electrons → one is unpaired. CH3•: 4 + 3 = 7.
  • Radicals are highly reactive: the unpaired electron pairs up by forming a new bond.
  • Put a dot on every species with a single unpaired electron in your equations.
Quick check
?Which statement about the chlorine radical, Cl•, is correct?
Mission 5

Two ways a bond can break

  • Homolytic fission: one electron to each atom → two radicals. Cl–Cl → Cl• + Cl•
  • Heterolytic fission: both electrons to one atom → two ions. H–Cl → H⁺ + Cl⁻
  • The atom that takes the pair becomes negative.
  • Radical reactions use homolytic fission. (Heterolytic fission comes up again in a later mission.)
Mission 5

Background: fish-hook arrows

  • A fish-hook (half) arrow has one barb: it moves one electron.
  • A full curly arrow (two barbs) moves an electron pair.
  • Cl2 splitting: two fish-hooks, each from the Cl–Cl bond to a different Cl atom.
  • Your exam does not require half-arrows here. Write each step as an equation with dots.
Mission 5

Initiation

  • Methane and chlorine don't react in the dark. In UV light they do.
  • Initiation: UV light splits the halogen homolytically: Cl2 → 2Cl•
  • Cl–Cl breaks because it is the weakest bond present: Cl–Cl 243, C–H 413 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Br–Br is weaker still (193 kJ mol⁻¹): even sunlight splits it.
  • Initiation makes radicals.
Quick check
?Methane reacts with bromine in ultraviolet (UV) light. Which equation represents the initiation step?
Mission 5

Propagation: the chain

  • Propagation: radical + molecule → new molecule + new radical.
  • Step 1: CH4 + Cl• → CH3• + HCl (Cl• removes an H atom)
  • Step 2: CH3• + Cl2 → CH3Cl + Cl•
  • Cl• is regenerated, so steps 1 and 2 repeat: a chain reaction.
  • Add the two steps: CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl.
  • No H• ever forms.
Mission 5

Termination

  • Termination: two radicals combine. Radicals are removed.
  • Cl• + Cl• → Cl2
  • CH3• + Cl• → CH3Cl
  • CH3• + CH3• → C2H6 (so a trace of ethane appears)
  • Radicals are scarce, so termination is rare: most product forms in propagation.

Mission 5

A mixture of products

  • Cl• attacks CH3Cl too: CH3Cl + Cl• → CH2Cl• + HCl, then CH2Cl• + Cl2 → CH2Cl2 + Cl•.
  • Further substitution goes on to CHCl3 and CCl4.
  • In longer chains, Cl• can remove an H from different positions, giving isomers.
  • So radical substitution is a poor way to make one pure product: a limitation in synthesis.
Mission 5

Back to the hook: bromine in hexane

  • In the dark: no light to split Br2, so no radicals. No reaction; it stays orange.
  • In sunlight: Br2 → 2Br•.
  • C6H14 + Br• → C6H13• + HBr, then C6H13• + Br2 → C6H13Br + Br•.
  • Overall: C6H14 + Br2 → C6H13Br + HBr. Br2 is used up, so the orange fades.
  • Br• can take an H from C1, C2 or C3: 1-, 2- and 3-bromohexane all form.
Quick check
?The products of the reaction of methane with chlorine in UV light include CH2Cl2, CHCl3 and CCl4. How are most of these more highly substituted products formed?
Mission 5

Why alkanes are so unreactive

  • C–C and C–H bonds are strong: breaking them needs a lot of energy.
  • C and H have similar electronegativities: the bonds are almost non-polar.
  • No δ+ or δ− sites, so polar reagents are not attracted.
  • Only very reactive species, such as radicals, attack alkanes.
  • That's why the bromine needs light: light makes the radicals.
Mission check

?A mixture of methane and chlorine does not react in the dark. A single brief flash of UV light splits a few Cl2 molecules, yet many CH3Cl molecules form for each Cl2 split. Which explains this?

Light is needed only to start the chain by splitting the halogen. That's why bromine in hexane fades only in sunlight.

Mission 6

Electrophiles

  • An electrophile is an electron pair acceptor.
  • It is electron-deficient: H⁺, or the δ+ atom of a polar molecule.
  • No charge needed: HBr (through Hδ+) and Br2 act as electrophiles.
  • Heterolytic fission: a bond breaks and both electrons go to one atom, forming two ions.
  • Radical reactions (see Mission 5) use homolytic fission instead: one electron to each atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as electrophiles?
Mission 6

Why alkenes attract electrophiles

  • Alkenes are unsaturated: they contain a C=C bond, so they can add more atoms.
  • The C=C bond is a region of high electron density.
  • It attracts electrophiles and donates an electron pair to them.
  • Alkanes have only C–C and C–H σ bonds: no electron-rich site. They react with bromine only when light makes Br• radicals.
  • The reagent adds across C=C. Double becomes single; nothing is lost: electrophilic addition.
Mission 6

Inside the double bond: σ and π

  • σ bond: orbitals overlap directly between the two carbon nuclei.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p orbitals, above and below the C–C axis.
  • The π bond stops rotation about C=C: restricted rotation (the origin of E/Z isomers).
  • Estimate from average bond enthalpies: π ≈ 612 − 347 = 265 kJ mol⁻¹, less than a C–C σ bond.
  • So the π bond breaks easily. Its exposed electrons sit where electrophiles can reach them.
Mission 6

The test for C=C

  • Shake with bromine water, at room temperature, in the dark.
  • Alkene: orange → colourless. Alkane: stays orange.
  • With Br2 in an organic solvent: CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br.
  • In bromine water, water also adds and much of the product is BrCH2CH2OH. The colour change is the same.
Quick check
?Ethene is bubbled through a solution of bromine in an organic solvent at room temperature, in the dark. Which statement is correct?
Mission 6

Adding halogens and hydrogen halides

  • Room temperature. Each reagent adds one part to each C=C carbon.
  • CH2=CH2 + Br2 → CH2BrCH2Br, 1,2-dibromoethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + Cl2 → CH2ClCH2Cl, 1,2-dichloroethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + HBr → CH3CH2Br, bromoethane.
  • CH2=CH2 + HCl → CH3CH2Cl, chloroethane.
  • Cyclohexene + HBr → bromocyclohexane.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes, such as propene, can give two products: a later lesson.
Mission 6

Adding water

  • Steam + an acid catalyst (e.g. H3PO4) adds H to one carbon and OH to the other.
  • CH2=CH2 + H2O → CH3CH2OH, ethanol. This is how ethanol is made industrially.
  • Cyclohexene + H2O → cyclohexanol.
Mission 6

Adding hydrogen

  • Hydrogenation: H2 adds across C=C, with a nickel catalyst.
  • CH2=CH2 + H2 → CH3CH3. Typical conditions: about 150 °C.
  • Margarine: vegetable oils have many C=C bonds. Hydrogenating some of them raises the melting point, so the oil becomes a soft solid.
Quick check
?2,3-dimethylbut-2-ene, (CH3)2C=C(CH3)2, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the product?
Mission 6

Mechanism: alkene + HBr

  • H–Br is already polar: Hδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br. Heterolytic fission: Br leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms: a C⁺ with only three bonds.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: bromoethane.
  • HCl reacts in the same way.
Mission 6

Mechanism: alkene + bromine

  • The electron-rich C=C repels the Br–Br electrons: an induced dipole, Brδ+–Brδ−.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to Brδ+.
  • Arrow 2: from the Br–Br bond to Brδ−, which leaves as Br⁻.
  • A carbocation forms. Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to the C⁺. Product: CH2BrCH2Br.
  • No light and no radicals: the C=C itself splits Br2.
  • A three-membered bromonium ion intermediate is also acceptable in place of the carbocation.
  • Cl2 adds the same way.
Quick check
?Ethene reacts with hydrogen bromide by electrophilic addition. Which describes the curly arrows in the first step of the mechanism?
Mission check

?In the dark, at room temperature, but-2-ene quickly decolourises a solution of iodine monochloride, ICl. Butane does not react with it. The product is CH3CHICHClCH3. Which statement explains why but-2-ene reacts without light?

The electron-rich C=C pulls an electrophile towards it and splits it into ions. So bromine (or ICl) adds in the dark. An alkane has no such site: it waits for light to make Br• radicals.

Mission 7

Sigma bonds

  • σ bond: direct (head-on) overlap of orbitals between two nuclei.
  • Electron density lies on the axis between the nuclei.
  • Every single bond is a σ bond: all C–C and C–H bonds in alkanes.
  • Groups can rotate freely about a σ bond (free rotation).
Mission 7

Pi bonds

  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p orbitals, one on each atom.
  • Electron density lies above and below the axis, not on it.
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π. Triple bond = 1σ + 2π.
  • The π bond stops rotation: groups on each C are fixed in place.
  • Restricted rotation about C=C gives E/Z (cis/trans) isomers when each C carries two different groups.
  • Each C of C=C: three bonding regions, trigonal planar, about 120°.
Quick check
?The displayed formula of propenenitrile (acrylonitrile), C3H3N, is shown: CH2=CH–C≡N. How many σ bonds and how many π bonds are in one molecule?
Mission 7

Why C=C is shorter and stronger, but not twice as strong

  • C–C 154 pm, 347 kJ mol⁻¹. C=C 134 pm, 612 kJ mol⁻¹ (mean, or average, bond enthalpies).
  • Two shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: C=C is shorter and stronger than C–C.
  • But not twice as strong: the π bond is weaker than the σ bond (612 − 347 ≈ 265).
  • Sideways overlap of p orbitals is less effective than head-on overlap.
  • π electrons are exposed above and below the bond: C=C is a region of high electron density. Electrophiles attack it.
  • So alkenes are far more reactive than alkanes.

Mission check

?A C≡C bond is only about 2.4 times as strong as a C–C bond, not 3 times. Which statement explains this?
  • Mean (average) bond enthalpies: C–C 347 kJ mol⁻¹ · C=C 612 kJ mol⁻¹ · C≡C 838 kJ mol⁻¹.

The first bond between two atoms is a σ bond. Every extra bond is a π bond, from weaker sideways overlap, so each one adds less strength than the σ bond.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Five formulae for one compound: empirical, molecular, structural, displayed, skeletal.
  • Plus a general formula for a whole series.
  • The functional group decides the class; check what each C=O carbon and each O is bonded to.

Mission 2

  • Homologous series: same functional group, each member + CH2, one general formula, similar chemistry.
  • Longer chain → stronger induced dipole–dipole forces → higher melting and boiling points.

Mission 3

  • Longest chain with the functional-group carbon; lowest locant to the suffix group, then C=C, then prefixes.
  • Prefixes alphabetical; -al and -oic acid carbons are always C1.

Mission 4

  • Structural isomers: same molecular formula, different structural formula (chain, position, functional group).
  • 1°/2°/3°: count the carbons bonded to the carbon carrying –OH or –X.

Mission 5

  • Light splits Cl2 or Br2 homolytically into radicals (initiation).
  • Propagation: radical + molecule → molecule + radical, so the chain repeats; termination: two radicals combine.
  • Further substitution and attack at different positions give a mixture: a limitation in synthesis.

Mission 6

  • C=C is electron-rich, so alkenes undergo electrophilic addition with Br2, HX and H2O (acid catalyst).
  • Mechanism: C=C → δ+ atom; bond → δ− atom; lone pair of the anion → C⁺.
  • Bromine water: orange → colourless with C=C.
  • H2 adds over a nickel catalyst.

Mission 7

  • σ: head-on overlap, on the axis. π: sideways p overlap, above and below; weaker; stops rotation. Double = σ + π; triple = σ + 2π.
