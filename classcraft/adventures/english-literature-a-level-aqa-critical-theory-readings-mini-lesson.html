Critical theory readings

The NEA (Theory and independence) is where AQA English Literature B asks you to read through theory. You study two texts — one poetry and one prose — informed by the Critical Anthology, and write two essays of 1250–1500 words, each on a different text and each linked to a different aspect of the Anthology.

  • The Critical Anthology covers: narrative theory · feminist theory · Marxist theory · eco-critical theory · post-colonial theory · literary value and the canon.
  • One essay may be re-creative — a creative transformation of the text, accompanied by a commentary that explains the critical thinking behind it.
  • AO5 dominates the NEA, but theory also earns marks in both exam papers — a lens sentence, well aimed, can lift a whole paragraph.

Theory · first principles

What a critical theory actually is

A critical theory is not a set of opinions about books. It is a set of questions — a position from which certain features of a text become visible that were invisible from elsewhere.

Test for using theory well: a lens must let you see something in the text you could not otherwise see. If your feminist paragraph and your Marxist paragraph could be swapped without either becoming false, you have not used either lens — you have merely named them.

  • Theory is a tool, not an authority. You are not obliged to agree with a theory to use it, and the strongest essays put two lenses into conflict with each other over the same passage.
  • Theory must land on evidence. Every theoretical claim ends in a quotation, a structural fact, or a silence.
  • Beware the summary trap. Two paragraphs explaining what Marxism is will earn you nothing. Explain nothing; do it.

Narrative theory

Reading the telling, not the tale

Narrative theory asks not what is told but how, and therefore who controls what you are allowed to know.

  • Story vs discourse (or fabula vs syuzhet) — the events as they happened, versus the order and manner in which they are presented. The gap between the two is where meaning is manufactured.
  • Narrator and focalisation — who speaks is not the same as who sees. A third-person narrator may see the world through one character's eyes, which quietly makes that character's assumptions the reader's own.
  • Unreliability — a narrator whose account the text invites us to doubt. Ask what the text lets us see over the narrator's shoulder.
  • Gaps and closure — what is withheld, and whether the ending resolves it. Structuralists (Todorov, Propp, Genette) describe recurring narrative shapes; the interesting question is always what a text does with the shape it inherits.
  • Time — analepsis (flashback), prolepsis (flashforward), duration, frequency. A text that keeps returning to one event is making an argument about trauma or obsession without ever stating it.

Narrative theory is the lens that combines with all the others. Feminist, Marxist and post-colonial readings all end up asking a narrative question: who is granted a point of view, and who is only ever looked at?

Feminist theory

Reading gender

Feminist criticism is not one thing. Know at least the range:

  • Images-of-women criticism — how are women represented? The angel, the monster, the madwoman, the muse, the corpse. Useful, but the weakest and most easily exhausted version.
  • The gaze — who looks, and who is looked at? Laura Mulvey's account of the male gaze (developed for cinema, transferable with care) describes a structure in which the man acts and the woman is displayed.
  • Gynocriticism — Elaine Showalter's term for the study of women's writing as a tradition in its own right, rather than women as objects in men's writing.
  • The construction of gender — Simone de Beauvoir's claim that one is not born but rather becomes a woman; later, Judith Butler's argument that gender is performative, produced through repeated acts rather than expressing an inner essence.
  • Intersection — class and race cut across gender. A reading that treats all women in a text as occupying one position will usually be wrong.

The most productive feminist question at A-level is a narrative one: who is granted interiority? Count the soliloquies, the free indirect passages, the moments of private thought. A text may adore its heroine and still never let her think.

Marxist theory

Reading class and economy

Marxist criticism reads literature as produced by, and participating in, an economic order.

  • Base and superstructure — the claim that a society's economic arrangements shape its culture. In its crude form this is determinism; in its useful form it is a question: what economic arrangement does this text need to be true in order to work?
  • Ideology — Louis Althusser's argument that ideology is not a set of lies but the frame within which reality is experienced as natural. The most ideological thing in a text is what it does not think worth mentioning.
  • Hegemony — Antonio Gramsci's term for rule maintained by consent rather than force: the ruled accept the order as common sense. Comedy and romance are extraordinarily good at manufacturing it.
  • Commodity and labour — who works in this text, and is their work visible? Wealth in fiction very often appears as a property of persons rather than a product of anyone's hands.
  • Literature as a commodity — books are also produced, priced, sold and reviewed. A Marxist reading includes the marketplace the text was made for.

The killer Marxist question: who does the work in this book, and where does the prose put them? Count the pages. The answer is almost always an argument.

Post-colonial theory

Reading empire

  • Orientalism — Edward Said's argument that the West produced the East as an object of knowledge: exotic, irrational, feminised, in need of governing. Once you have the idea, you find its grammar everywhere in nineteenth-century writing.
  • Othering — the construction of a group as fundamentally different, so that the observing culture can define itself as the norm.
  • The subaltern — Gayatri Spivak's question, can the subaltern speak?, presses whether the most powerless can be heard at all, or whether even sympathetic representation talks over them.
  • Hybridity and mimicry — Homi Bhabha's account of the colonised subject who imitates the coloniser: almost the same, but not quite — an imitation that unsettles the original by revealing it to be a performance.
  • Writing back — texts from formerly colonised nations that rewrite the canon from the other side, turning a silent figure into a narrator.

Post-colonial reading is not confined to texts set in colonies. A country-house novel whose income comes from a plantation nobody visits is doing colonial work precisely by not mentioning it. The absence is the evidence.

Eco-critical theory

Reading the non-human

Eco-criticism asks how a text represents the natural world and the human relation to it — and it is the youngest lens in the Anthology, which makes it a demonstration of the point that new contexts of reception create new readings.

  • Nature as setting vs nature as agent — is the landscape a backdrop for human feeling, or does it have its own life the text must reckon with?
  • The pathetic fallacy — weather that agrees with the hero. Eco-criticism treats this suspiciously: it makes the non-human world a mirror for human emotion, which is a subtle form of erasure.
  • Pastoral and anti-pastoral — the idealised countryside is very often a landowner's countryside: no labour, no mud, no wages. Raymond Williams's The Country and the City exposed exactly this, and it is where eco-criticism meets Marxism.
  • Anthropocentrism — the assumption that the human is the centre and everything else is resource, scenery or symbol.
  • The Anthropocene and climate — reading older texts in the light of ecological crisis: not to condemn them, but to notice what they could take for granted.

Best eco-critical question at A-level: does the land in this text belong to anybody, and does the text notice? That one question turns a nature-description paragraph into an argument.

NEA craft

Building the theory essay

The task: two essays of 1250–1500 words, each on a different text (one poetry, one prose) and each linked to a different aspect of the Critical Anthology. One may be re-creative, with a commentary.

What a good theory essay does:

  1. States a reading, not a theory. Your title should promise a claim about the text, not a summary of a school of criticism.
  2. Applies the lens to specific, close evidence — and stays close. 1500 words is short; there is no room for a page of Marxism-in-general.
  3. Tests the lens. The best essays find the place where the theory struggles with the text — a woman who is both objectified and given the last word; a labourer the novel both pities and cannot imagine. That resistance is your argument.
  4. Uses critics sparingly and accurately. One well-understood idea, applied twice, beats six name-drops. Never attribute a quotation you cannot verify.
  5. Bibliography and citation. Referencing is part of the assessment of academic practice — record everything you consult as you go, not the night before.

The re-creative option: the creative piece is only as good as its commentary. The commentary must show the critical thinking — what you changed, which theoretical claim the change tests, and what it reveals about the original.

Recap

The big ideas to take into the exam

The NEA (7717): two texts — one poetry, one prose — informed by the Critical Anthology; two essays of 1250-1500 words, each on a different text and a different aspect; one may be re-creative with a commentary

The Anthology: narrative theory · feminist theory · Marxist theory · eco-critical theory · post-colonial theory · literary value and the canon

Narrative theory: story vs discourse · narrator vs focaliser · unreliability · gaps and closure · time. The lens that underpins all the others: WHO IS GRANTED A POINT OF VIEW?

Feminist: the gaze · gynocriticism · gender as constructed and performative · intersection with class and race. Best question: who is granted interiority?

Marxist: base and superstructure · ideology as the unsaid (Althusser) · hegemony by consent (Gramsci) · invisible labour. Best question: who does the work, and where does the prose put them?

Post-colonial: Orientalism (Said) · othering · the subaltern (Spivak) · hybridity and mimicry (Bhabha) · writing back. The silence about empire IS the evidence

Eco-critical: nature as agent not backdrop · the pathetic fallacy · the pastoral as a landowner's landscape (Williams) · anthropocentrism

Discipline: a lens must SEE something. If your feminist and Marxist paragraphs are interchangeable, you have used neither
