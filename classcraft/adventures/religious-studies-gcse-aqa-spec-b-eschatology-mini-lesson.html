Mission 1

Mission 1: What do Christians believe happens after we die, and why don't they all agree?

Make a guess: What do Christians believe happens after we die, and why don't they all agree?

Mission 1

Death is not the end

  • Christians believe in eternal life: life with God that death cannot end.
  • The reason: Jesus rose from the dead. Christians believe his resurrection is the first of many.
  • The creeds: "I believe in … the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting" (Apostles' Creed).
  • Belief about the future shapes life now: hope, comfort and responsibility.
Mission 1Read the source

"I am the resurrection" (John 11)

  • Context: Jesus' friend Lazarus has been dead four days. His sister Martha meets Jesus (John 11:17–20).
  • Martha believes Lazarus will rise "in the resurrection at the last day" (John 11:24).
  • "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies." (John 11:25)
  • "Whoever lives and believes in me will never die." (John 11:26)

Q1 What does Jesus claim about himself?

Show answer

He is the source of resurrection and life.

Q2 What does Jesus promise to those who believe in him?

Show answer

They will live, even though they die.

Mission 1Read the source

A new kind of body

  • Paul: "Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruit of those who are asleep." (1 Corinthians 15:20): Jesus' resurrection is the first of a harvest.
  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable." (1 Corinthians 15:42)
  • "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44)
  • Like a seed and the plant it becomes: the same person, transformed (1 Corinthians 15:36–38).
  • Q: What does "spiritual body" mean here? (A real, transformed body raised by God's Spirit, not a ghost.)
Mission 1

Judgement

  • Christians believe all people will be judged by God through Christ.
  • Catholic teaching: a particular judgement at death, and the Last (final) Judgement at the end of time.
  • Matthew 25:31–46: the sheep and the goats: people are judged by how they treated the hungry, thirsty, stranger, sick and prisoner.
  • Most Christians hold that judgement looks at faith and how it was lived; they differ on the balance.
Quick check

Mission 1

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: being with God for ever in perfect happiness. Catholics call it the beatific vision: seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12).
  • Hell: being cut off from God for ever.
  • "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." (Matthew 25:46)
  • Catholic teaching: hell is a state of self-exclusion: a person's own final choice to refuse God's love. God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1033, 1037).
Mission 1

Purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: purgatory is a state of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully holy. All in purgatory will reach heaven.
  • Catholics pray for the dead and offer Mass for them (2 Maccabees 12:46; 1 Corinthians 3:15 "saved, but as through fire").
  • Most Protestants reject purgatory: Jesus' death is enough; scripture does not teach it.
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead, but do not define purgatory as Catholics do.
Mission 1Diversity

Traditional and contemporary views

  • Traditional: heaven and hell as places, often pictured as paradise above and fire below; hell is eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46).
  • Contemporary (many Catholics and Protestants): heaven and hell as states of being with or apart from God; fire is a picture, not a literal flame. Pope John Paul II taught this in 1999 talks.
  • Resurrection: most Christians expect a bodily resurrection at the end of time; some liberal Christians speak of the soul living on with God.
  • Christians may show these beliefs in funerals, gravestones and prayers for the dead.

Mission 1

Why it matters now

  • Hope and comfort: death is not the final word; Christian funerals speak of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection" (Book of Common Prayer).
  • Responsibility: judgement means how people live matters (Matthew 25).
  • Value of life: each person is made for eternal life with God.
  • Catholic practice: prayers and Masses for the dead; All Souls' Day (2 November); the last rites prepare the dying.
  • Some Christians warn against being good only to reach heaven: love should be its own reason.
Mission 1Evaluate

"A loving God would not send anyone to hell."

  • Reasons for: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4). Eternal punishment for limited wrong seems unjust. Universalists and some theologians hope all will be saved.
  • Reasons against: Jesus speaks of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46; Luke 16). Love respects free will: people may finally refuse God. Catholic teaching: God sends no one; hell is self-exclusion (CCC 1033). Justice for victims requires that evil be judged.
  • Another view: annihilation: the unsaved cease to exist, with no endless suffering.
  • Judge it by: Does "send" describe what hell is? How should Jesus' warnings be read: literally or as pictures? Can love and justice both be fully satisfied?
Mission check

?At a funeral the priest says: "We pray for Maria, that God will cleanse her of any remaining sin, so that she may soon see God face to face." Which tradition's belief is this prayer expressing?

Christians share hope in resurrection, judgement and eternal life with God. They differ on purgatory, on what heaven and hell are like, and on who will finally be saved.

Make a guess: If God judges everyone at death, why do Catholics believe in a second judgement at the end of time?

Mission 2

The four last things

  • Catholic teaching names four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell.
  • Death: the end of earthly life; the soul is separated from the body. It is the end of the time in which a person can choose for or against God.
  • Judgement: each life is seen in the light of Christ.
  • Heaven: eternal life with God.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God.
  • Purgatory is not one of the four: it is a stage on the way to heaven.
  • The hope behind them: Jesus' resurrection (see Mission 1). Death is real, but not the last word.
Mission 2

Two judgements

  • Particular judgement: at the moment of death, each soul is judged by Christ, and goes to heaven (perhaps through purgatory) or to hell (CCC 1021–1022, paraphrase).
  • Final (Last) judgement: at the end of time, when Christ returns (the Parousia). The dead are raised; all humanity is judged together, body and soul (CCC 1038–1041, paraphrase).
  • "We must all be revealed before the judgement seat of Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:10).
  • Why two? The particular judgement concerns the individual. The final judgement makes public the meaning of all of history, and includes the body, and every effect a life has had on others.
  • The final judgement does not overturn the first; it completes it.

Mission 2Read the source

"Saved, but as through fire"

"If any man's work is burnt, he will suffer loss, but he himself will be saved, but as through fire." (1 Corinthians 3:15)

  • Paul pictures each life as a building on the foundation of Christ (3:11). Some materials survive the "fire" of the Day; some burn (3:12–14).
  • Catholic reading: some people die in God's friendship but are not yet fully purified. Purgatory is their final cleansing before heaven (CCC 1030–1031, paraphrase).
  • Purgatory is not a second chance and not a lesser hell: all in purgatory are saved.
  • The living can help by prayer and Masses for the dead (2 Maccabees 12:45;).

Mission 2Read the source

The Rich Man and Lazarus

"There was a certain rich man … living in luxury every day. A certain beggar, named Lazarus, was taken to his gate, full of sores" (Luke 16:19–20).

  • Both die. Lazarus is carried to "Abraham's bosom"; the rich man is "in torment" (16:22–23).
  • "Between us and you there is a great gulf fixed" (16:26): after death, the choice made in life stands.
  • "If they don't listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead" (16:31).
  • Catholic significance: judgement follows death at once (the brothers are still alive: a sign of particular judgement, CCC 1021); ignoring the poor is a serious failure of love; people already have God's word (Moses and the prophets) to guide them, and a miracle would not persuade those who ignore it; a hint of the Resurrection.
  • Q: What does the "great gulf" suggest about choices after death? (They can no longer be changed.)

Mission 2

Resurrection of the body, not just a surviving soul

  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. … It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:42, 44)
  • Catholics believe the soul survives death; but the full hope is the resurrection of the body: the whole person, body and soul, reunited and transformed at the end of time, like the risen Jesus.
  • Because Jesus rose bodily, his resurrection is the pattern for ours (1 Corinthians 15:20–23).
  • Significance: the body matters (care for the sick, respect for the dead, burial).
Mission 2

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: perfect, eternal union with God, seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12): the beatific vision.
  • Not a place in the sky, but a state of complete happiness and love with God, Mary and the saints (CCC 1023–1029, paraphrase).
  • Hell: eternal self-exclusion from communion with God, by freely and finally refusing God's love (CCC 1033, paraphrase).
  • Its chief pain is separation from God, for whom humans were made (CCC 1035, paraphrase).
  • Jesus warns: "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46).
Mission 2Read the source

Vatican II: LG 48 and CCC 1037

  • Lumen Gentium 48: the Church is a pilgrim; it will reach its perfection only in the glory of heaven, when all things are restored and the whole world is renewed in Christ (Acts 3:21; Ephesians 1:10) (paraphrase).
  • That renewal has already begun in Christ and carries on in the Church; the end of the ages has already come upon us.
  • We must keep watch: at the end of life all must appear before Christ's judgement seat (2 Corinthians 5:10), and hell is a real possibility (LG 48, paraphrase).
  • CCC 1037: God predestines no one to hell. That needs a free, deliberate turning away from God (a mortal sin) and persisting in it to the end. The Church prays daily for God's mercy, since God wants none to perish (2 Peter 3:9) (paraphrase).
  • Importance: eschatology is about the whole cosmos, not only individual souls; and hope, not fear, is the keynote.
  • Q: According to CCC 1037, does God send anyone to hell against their will? (No: only persistent free refusal leads there.)
Mission 2Read the source

Julian of Norwich: "All shall be well"

  • Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416), an anchoress (a woman living a life of prayer in a cell attached to a church) in Norwich. In 1373 she received visions, written in Revelations of Divine Love.
  • Chapter 32: God said, "All thing shall be well", and "Thou shalt see thyself that all manner of thing shall be well" (Warrack, 1901).
  • God cares for small things as well as great.
  • There is a "Great Deed" that the Trinity will do on the last day, hidden from us now, "by which He shall make all things well".
  • Julian also holds the Church's faith that some will be condemned, so she cannot see how all can be well. God answers: "That which is impossible to thee is not impossible to me."
  • Cosmic reconciliation: God will reconcile "all things" in Christ (Colossians 1:20), in a way we cannot yet see.
  • Q: Does Julian say she understands how all will be made well? (No: the Great Deed is hidden; she trusts God.)
Mission 2

Michelangelo's The Last Judgement

  • Fresco on the altar wall of the Sistine Chapel, Vatican; painted 1536–1541, for Pope Paul III.
  • Centre top: Christ as judge, raising his arm; Mary at his side; saints around him holding the instruments of their martyrdom. Theme: Christ the judge, the communion of saints.
  • Bottom left: the dead climb out of graves, skeletons regaining flesh. Theme: resurrection of the body (compare Ezekiel 37:12).
  • Left side: the saved rise to heaven, some pulled up by others, two by a rosary. Theme: prayer and intercession help.
  • Centre below: angels blow trumpets and hold the books of the deeds of the dead (Revelation 20:12). Theme: judgement of all.
  • Right side and bottom right: the damned fall; Charon's boat and Minos (from Dante's Inferno). Theme: hell is real; human choices matter.
  • Significance: placed behind the altar, it reminds those at Mass that history ends in Christ's judgement and mercy.
Mission 2Diversity

Christians disagree

  • Catholic: particular and final judgement; purgatory; resurrection of a transformed body; prayer for the dead.
  • Orthodox: prays for the dead and believes in a state before the final judgement, but does not define purgatory as Catholics do.
  • Protestant: most reject purgatory (Church of England's 39 Articles, Article 22: a "fond thing vainly invented"); many believe the saved go straight to be "at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8).
  • Nature of resurrection: most Christians: a real, transformed body. Some liberal Christians: a "spiritual body" (1 Corinthians 15:44) means a new, non-physical existence.
  • Hell: traditional eternal hell; some evangelicals: the lost cease to exist (annihilation); some Christians: hell as separation chosen, not torment; a few: all will finally be saved (universalism).
Mission 2Evaluate

"Catholics should hope that everyone will be saved."

  • Reasons for: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4); CCC 1037: God predestines no one to hell, and the Church prays daily for mercy for all; the medieval mystic Julian of Norwich: a hidden "Great Deed" will make all things well; the theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar (1905–1988, Dare We Hope "That All Men Be Saved"?, 1986) argued Catholics may hope, though not know, that hell is empty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus' own warnings of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46) and the "great gulf" (Luke 16:26); Catholic teaching affirms that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035); love cannot be forced, so free creatures may refuse God for ever; Julian herself accepted that some are condemned; critics say universal hope weakens the urgency of conversion.
  • Judge it by: Is there a difference between hoping something and teaching it? Can God's mercy override human freedom? How should Jesus' warnings be read: as predictions or as urgent calls?
Mission check

?Sam says: "If my nan was judged by Christ the moment she died, a Last Judgement at the end of time is pointless. Her verdict is already known." Which reply best reflects Catholic teaching?

Death, judgement, heaven and hell frame Catholic hope: each life judged at death, all creation renewed at the end, with purgatory and prayer in between.

Mission 3

Before death: the "last rites"

  • When a Catholic is dying, a priest is called to give the last rites: the sacraments that prepare for the journey to God.
  • Reconciliation (confession): sins forgiven; peace with God and the Church.
  • Anointing of the Sick: the priest lays hands on the person and anoints the forehead and hands with the Oil of the Sick (James 5:14–15).
  • Viaticum (Latin, "food for the journey"): Holy Communion as the last Eucharist, with Christ's promise "I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:54).
  • Family and friends pray with the dying person (prayers of commendation).
Mission 3

Reconciliation, healing, hope

  • Reconciliation: sins forgiven, relationships healed; the person can die at peace with God and others. The priest may give the Apostolic Pardon.
  • Healing: Anointing gives strength, peace and courage; sometimes physical healing if God wills (CCC 1520, paraphrase). Since Vatican II, it is for anyone seriously ill or frail, not only the dying (formerly "Extreme Unction").
  • Hope: the person's suffering is joined to Christ's; Viaticum is a pledge of resurrection.
  • Sacramental: outward signs (oil, hands, bread) of inward grace.

Mission 3

The funeral in three stations

The Catholic funeral rite (Order of Christian Funerals) follows the body from home to church to cemetery, like a journey.

  • 1. Vigil (home): the evening before; prayers, Scripture, perhaps the Rosary, at home, in a funeral home or in church. A time of quiet support.
  • 2. Funeral liturgy (church): usually a Requiem Mass; sometimes a Liturgy of the Word.
  • 3. Committal (cemetery or crematorium): the body is laid to rest.
  • Each stage expresses the belief that the person is passing from death to eternal life with Christ.
Mission 3

In church: symbols of baptism and resurrection

  • Holy water sprinkled on the coffin as it enters: recalls baptism, when the person first shared Christ's death and rising (Romans 6:4).
  • White pall (cloth) over the coffin: the white baptismal garment; "you … have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27). All are equal in death.
  • Paschal candle beside the coffin: Christ, the light who has overcome death (see Mission 4).
  • Crucifix, Book of the Gospels or Bible on the coffin: life shaped by Christ's word.
  • Incense at the end: respect for the body, once a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19), and prayers rising to God.
  • Vestments: white (resurrection), violet or black (mourning, prayer for mercy).
Mission 3

Prayers, readings and the farewell

  • Readings often chosen: "The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" (Wisdom 3:1); "In my Father's house are many homes" (John 14:2); "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25).
  • A homily proclaims the hope of resurrection; it is not just a eulogy.
  • The Mass is offered for the person who has died.
  • Final commendation: the community says farewell and commends the person to God.
  • Traditional prayer: "Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them."
  • As the coffin leaves, the In paradisum asks that angels lead the person into paradise (paraphrase).
  • Committal: at the grave or crematorium, the body is entrusted to the earth "in sure and certain hope" of resurrection. Catholics may choose cremation (allowed since 1963), but ashes are kept in a sacred place, not scattered (2016 guidance).

Mission 3

Praying for the dead

  • Catholics believe in the communion of saints: the Church on earth, the souls in purgatory and the saints in heaven are united in Christ.
  • So the living can help the dead by prayer: "it would be superfluous and idle to pray for the dead" if they were not expected to rise (2 Maccabees 12:44).
  • Masses for the dead: a family asks a priest to offer Mass for a person (a Mass intention), often at the funeral, a month later, and on anniversaries.
  • All Souls' Day (2 November): the whole Church prays for all the dead. November is the month of the Holy Souls.
Mission 3

Care of the dying and sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: Catholics believe human life is holy, a gift from God (Genesis 2:7; "You shall not murder", Exodus 20:13), and that the moment of death is for God to decide: "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away" (Job 1:21).
  • Euthanasia (deliberately ending a life to end suffering): Catholic teaching holds it is morally unacceptable, whatever the motive (CCC 2277, paraphrase).
  • But Catholics need not prolong life at all costs: treatment that is burdensome or disproportionate may be refused (CCC 2278).
  • Painkillers may be used even if they might shorten life, if death is not intended (CCC 2279).
  • Palliative care (relieving pain and supporting the whole person) is a special form of charity: Catholic hospices, chaplains, the last rites.
  • Other views: some Christians and many non-religious people support assisted dying out of compassion and respect for autonomy; Catholics reply that true compassion accompanies, and does not end, a life, while supporters reply that compassion can include respecting a request to die.
Quick check
?An elderly Catholic with terminal cancer decides to stop a harsh course of chemotherapy that would only extend her life a few weeks, and to receive pain relief in a hospice. According to Catholic teaching, how should this decision be seen?
Mission 3Evaluate

"A Catholic funeral is more for the living than for the dead."

  • Reasons for: much of the rite consoles the bereaved: the vigil's "quiet support", readings of hope, the gathered community; the aims include supporting the mourners.
  • Reasons against: the Mass is offered for the dead person; prayers and the final commendation entrust them to God's mercy; Catholics believe prayer really helps those in purgatory (communion of saints), so the dead are its first concern.
  • Another view: for Catholics the distinction is false: the living and the dead form one communion in Christ; the rite serves both and above all worships God.
  • Weigh: how far do the funeral's prayers and actions show belief in resurrection, judgement and purgatory, and how far are they shaped by grief and custom?
  • Judge it by: Who are the prayers addressed to and about? What would be lost if the dead were not prayed for? Do non-Catholic funerals share the same aims?

A Catholic funeral completes what baptism began: water, white garment and light proclaim that the person who died with Christ will rise with him.

Mission 4

The Paschal candle: what you see

  • A large candle, new each year, blessed at the Easter Vigil ("Paschal" = of Easter, from the Hebrew Pesach, Passover).
  • A cross: Christ died and rose.
  • Alpha (Α) and Omega (Ω), first and last letters of the Greek alphabet: "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End" (Revelation 22:13).
  • The numbers of the current year in the angles of the cross: Christ is Lord of all time, including now ("Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever", Hebrews 13:8).
  • Five grains of incense (often red "nails") in the cross: the five wounds of Christ (hands, feet, side), now glorious.
  • The flame: Christ, risen and alive, the Light of the world (John 8:12).
Mission 4

Light in the darkness: the Easter Vigil

  • The Vigil begins after nightfall, in a dark church.
  • Outside, a new fire is blessed; the priest may cut the cross, Alpha, Omega and year into the candle and insert the five grains, praying that Christ's wounds guard and protect us (paraphrase).
  • The candle is lit from the new fire and carried in: "The Light of Christ" — "Thanks be to God". Everyone's small candles are lit from it until the church glows.
  • The Exsultet is sung beside it: a hymn of praise for the night when Christ broke the chains of death (paraphrase).
  • Belief: resurrection: light conquers darkness; "the darkness hasn't overcome it" (John 1:5).
Mission 4

The same light at baptism and at death

  • The candle stands in the sanctuary through the 50 days of Easter, then beside the font.
  • Baptism: a baptismal candle is lit from it and given to the newly baptised (or parents and godparents): "Receive the light of Christ" (Rite of Baptism). The new Christian shares Christ's risen life.
  • Funerals: it stands beside the coffin (see Mission 3): the light received at baptism is still burning; the person who died with Christ will rise with him (Romans 6:4).
  • So one artefact links Easter, baptism and death: the whole Christian life is lived in the light of the Resurrection.
Quick check
?Why are the numbers of the current year marked on the Paschal candle?
Mission 4

Memorials: tombstones, monuments, gardens

  • Catholic tombstones: cross or crucifix; IHS or Chi-Rho; "RIP" (Requiescat in pace, "may he/she rest in peace"); "Pray for the soul of …" (belief in purgatory and the communion of saints); angels; a candle or Alpha and Omega.
  • Protestant memorials: often a plain cross and a hope-filled verse, e.g. "asleep in Jesus" (1 Thessalonians 4:14) or "at home with the Lord" (2 Corinthians 5:8); usually no request for prayer, since most Protestants do not pray for the dead.
  • Orthodox graves: often a cross (three-barred in Russian and some other traditions), icons, and a lamp; prayers for the dead.
  • Monuments: war memorials with crosses; the Stone of Remembrance inscribed "Their name liveth for evermore" (from Sirach 44:14).
  • Remembrance gardens (often at crematoria): roses, plaques, a book of remembrance, where ashes may be interred.
  • Non-religious memorials: celebrate a life ("in loving memory"), often with no reference to an afterlife.
Quick check
?A gravestone reads: "Pray for the soul of Patrick Byrne, died 2019. RIP." Which belief does this wording most clearly show?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Objects and images teach faith better than words."

  • Reasons for: a candle or carving speaks to everyone, including those who cannot read (much of the Christian world in history); symbols engage the senses and memory.
  • A cross and "RIP" on a gravestone speak of hope to everyone who walks past.
  • Reasons against: objects can be misunderstood or treated superstitiously; some Protestants argue faith comes by hearing the Word (Romans 10:17) and warn against images (Exodus 20:4); words can explain why, not just that.
  • Symbols need explaining: few visitors know what Alpha and Omega means.
  • Another view: Catholics see word and image as partners.
  • The Paschal candle is lit while the Exsultet is sung.
  • Judge it by: Who is the audience? What happens when no one explains the symbol? Do objects add something words cannot?
Mission check

?A Catholic family chooses a gravestone for their grandmother carved with a lit candle, the letters Α and Ω, and the words "The Light of Christ." Which belief are they expressing?

Catholic artefacts of death all point the same way: through Christ's Passion to the light of the Resurrection.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Because Jesus rose, Christians believe in resurrection, judgement, and heaven or hell; 1 Corinthians 15 describes a transformed "spiritual body".
  • Catholics believe in purgatory and pray for the dead; most Protestants reject it (39 Articles, XXII).
  • Views differ: places or states; eternal punishment, annihilation or universal salvation.

Mission 2

  • Four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell. Particular judgement at death; final judgement at Christ's return, when bodies rise.
  • Purgatory (1 Corinthians 3:15): final purification of the saved, helped by prayer.
  • Luke 16, LG 48, CCC 1037, Julian's "all shall be well" and Michelangelo's Last Judgement show judgement, mercy and the hope of a renewed creation.

Mission 3

  • The last rites (Reconciliation, Anointing, Viaticum) prepare the dying; the funeral follows the body from home to church to cemetery.
  • Holy water, white pall and Paschal candle link death to baptism and proclaim resurrection; the Mass and commendation pray for the dead.
  • Sanctity of life: no euthanasia, but palliative care and no obligation to burdensome treatment.

Mission 4

  • The Paschal candle (cross, Alpha and Omega, year, five grains) proclaims the risen Christ at the Easter Vigil, at baptism and at funerals.
  • Memorials show different beliefs: Catholic prayer for the dead, Protestant assurance, non-religious remembrance.
