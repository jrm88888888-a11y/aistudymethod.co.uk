Mission 1

Mission 1: A city is made of concrete, glass and roads. Can it really make its own weather?

Make a guess: A city is made of concrete, glass and roads. Can it really make its own weather?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.4 impact of urban forms and processes on local climate and weather
  • 3.2.3.4 urban heat island; urban precipitation (frequency and intensity), fogs and thunderstorms; effects of urban structures and layout on wind speed, direction and frequency
Mission 1

How urban form changes the climate

  • Surfaces: roofs and roads absorb the sun's heat by day and release it slowly.
  • Geometry: tall buildings and narrow streets form urban canyons.
  • Impermeable ground: rain drains away, so less water evaporates to cool the air.
  • Processes: waste heat from vehicles, buildings and air conditioning; particles from traffic and industry.
Mission 1

The urban heat island

  • Urban heat island (UHI): a built-up area warmer than the countryside around it.
  • In the United States, urban areas are 0.6–3.9 °C (1–7 °F) warmer by day and 1.1–2.8 °C (2–5 °F) warmer by night than outlying areas.
  • The effect is greatest in humid regions and in larger, denser cities.
Mission 1

Why cities are warmer

  • Roofs and roads release stored heat slowly, so heat islands are "more pronounced after sunset".
  • Less vegetation and water mean less shade and less evaporative cooling.
  • Urban canyons trap heat and "can block natural wind flow".
  • Waste heat comes from vehicles, air conditioning, buildings and industry.
Quick check
?On a clear, calm night, the countryside cools quickly. Why does the city stay warmer?
Mission 1

Heat: impacts and responses

  • Electricity demand for air conditioning rises 1–9% for each 1.1 °C (2 °F) of warming.
  • Conventional roofs can reach 37 °C (66 °F) warmer than the surrounding air.
  • Responses: trees and vegetation, green roofs, cool (reflective) roofs and cool pavements.
Mission 1

Urban precipitation

  • Heat: warm surfaces heat the air, which rises (convection).
  • Roughness: buildings slow and lift the wind, forcing air upwards.
  • Particles: pollution adds condensation nuclei on which droplets form.
  • Evidence: satellite radar around several southern US cities found monthly rainfall rates about 28% higher 30–60 km downwind of the city.
  • The greatest increases were found on average 39 km from the edge of the urban centre.
Quick check
?Why was the largest rainfall increase found downwind of the cities, not over their centres?
Mission 1

Fogs

  • Radiation fog: on clear, calm nights the ground cools; moist air above it cools to its dew point and condenses.
  • Cities add particles for droplets to form on; their warmth works against fog. Which wins depends on the pollution.
  • London, 5–9 December 1952: an anticyclone created an inversion that trapped coal smoke in the fog.
  • About 4,000 people were known to have died, "but it could be many more". The Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 followed.
Mission 1

Thunderstorms

  • Thunderstorms need rising, moist, unstable air. Urban heat adds to the uplift on summer afternoons.
  • In the satellite study, maximum rainfall rates downwind exceeded the upwind average by 48–116%: intense, convective rain.
  • Impermeable surfaces turn intense rain quickly into runoff, raising flash-flood risk.
Mission 1

Wind

  • Speed: rough cities lower average wind speeds, but create strong gusts.
  • Direction: streets channel wind along them, whatever the regional direction.
  • Frequency: more calm periods at street level, broken by sudden gusts.
  • Tall buildings deflect wind down to the street (downdraught); gaps between them speed it up (Venturi effect).
  • The City of London requires wind studies: "Adverse wind effects can reduce the quality and usability of outdoor areas".
Quick check
?On a windy day, the gap between two tall towers is gustier than a nearby park. Why?

Mission check

?Researchers compare summer rainfall upwind of a large inland city with rainfall 40 km downwind. Downwind areas get noticeably more. What best explains this?

Yes. Cities are warmer, especially at night (US urban areas 2–5 °F, or 1.1–2.8 °C, warmer at night); they boost rainfall downwind (about 28% in the satellite study); and they change fog and wind.

Make a guess: In 2021 a coroner's report named air pollution exposure as a cause of a 9-year-old London girl's death. Who breathes a city's dirtiest air?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.4 urban air quality: particulate and photochemical pollution; pollution reduction policies
Mission 2

Particulate pollution

  • PM10: particles 10 micrometres (µm) across or smaller. PM2.5: 2.5 µm or smaller.
  • A human hair is about 70 µm across.
  • Primary particles are emitted directly (construction, roads, fires, chimneys); secondary particles form in the air from sulfur dioxide and nitrogen oxides.
  • Particles under 10 µm can get deep into the lungs, and some into the bloodstream.
Mission 2

Photochemical pollution

  • Ground-level ozone is not emitted directly. It forms when nitrogen oxides (NOx) and volatile organic compounds (VOCs) react in sunlight.
  • Sources of these: cars, power plants, industrial boilers, refineries and chemical plants.
  • Ozone is most likely to reach unhealthy levels "on hot sunny days in urban environments", and can be carried far downwind.
  • Photochemical smog: a haze of ozone and secondary particles over sunny, traffic-heavy cities.
Quick check
?Which conditions are most likely to produce photochemical smog?
Mission 2

The global picture

  • In 2019, 99% of the world's population lived where WHO air quality guideline levels were not met.
  • Outdoor air pollution caused an estimated 4.2 million premature deaths in 2019; 89% were in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Average population-weighted exposure to PM2.5 was 35.7 µg/m³ in 2015–2019, against "the recommended air quality guideline of 5 μg/m3".
Quick check
?Average population-weighted PM2.5 exposure in 2015–2019 was 35.7 µg/m³; WHO's guideline is 5 µg/m³. How many times the guideline is that?
Mission 2

Patterns within a city

  • Place: roadside sites beside heavy traffic record more nitrogen dioxide (NO₂) than background sites away from main roads; London monitors both.
  • Time: peaks at rush hours; in still, cold weather an inversion traps pollution near the ground.
  • Sources: road transport is the largest single source of fine particles from within London, about 30% of emissions.
  • But over half of London's PM2.5 comes from regional, often transboundary (non-UK) sources.
Mission 2

A coroner's findings

  • A coroner's report (Inner South London, 20 April 2021) concerned Ella Kissi-Debrah, who "died at the age of 9" after an asthma attack in 2013.
  • The recorded causes include "Air pollution exposure".
  • She was exposed to NO₂ and particulate matter above WHO guidelines; "The principal source of her exposure was traffic emissions".
Mission 2

Who is most exposed?

  • London, 2019: where the most deprived Londoners were likely to live, average NO₂ was 3.8 µg/m³ (13%) higher than in the least deprived areas; PM2.5 was 0.7 µg/m³ (6%) higher.
  • The NO₂ gap was 7.6 µg/m³ in an earlier (2013) study; the authors say the two studies are not exactly comparable.
  • It "does not mean that any individual is necessarily exposed to higher or lower levels".
  • Most vulnerable: over 600,000 Londoners live with a lung condition; WHO calls air pollution "one of the greatest environmental risks to child health".
Quick check
?Between the two studies, the deprivation gap narrowed more for NO₂ than for PM2.5. Why?
Mission 2

Policy 1: the Clean Air Acts

  • London's Great Smog, 5–9 December 1952: about 4,000 people were known to have died, "but it could be many more".
  • The Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 "banned emissions of black smoke" and required urban residents and factories to switch to smokeless fuels.
  • Coal smoke was the old problem; traffic is the main local source now.
Mission 2

Policy 2: the Ultra Low Emission Zone

  • The world's first 24-hour ULEZ began in central London on 8 April 2019; inner London from 25 October 2021; all London boroughs from 29 August 2023.
  • Cars below the Euro 4 (petrol) or Euro 6 (diesel) standards pay £12.50 a day.
  • GLA estimate for 2024: roadside NO₂ 27% lower across London than without the ULEZ (54% central, 29% inner, 24% outer).
  • Around 97% of vehicles seen driving in London were ULEZ compliant; scrappage had removed over 15,000 non-compliant vehicles by February 2023.
Mission 2

Policy 3: national targets

  • England's legal target: annual mean PM2.5 at or below 10 µg/m³ by the end of 2040. That is twice the WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³.
  • The GLA notes WHO's latest guidelines "are more stringent than UK legal limits".
  • The coroner warned that national particulate limits are "far higher than the WHO guidelines".
Mission 2

Evaluating the policies

  • Strengths: large falls in roadside NO₂; the method was independently peer reviewed.
  • Method: "without ULEZ" figures are estimates, and reflect "all the Mayor's policies to reduce emissions from transport".
  • Who pays: owners of older vehicles, often lower-income drivers and small firms; scrappage offsets part of it.
  • Limits: city policy cuts local NO₂ most; much PM2.5 comes from outside London.
Quick check
?Why can a city's own policies cut NO₂ more easily than PM2.5?
Mission check

?A city brings in a zone that charges older, dirtier vehicles. Before it starts, NO₂ is far higher at roadside monitors than at background ones, while PM2.5 is similar at both, and over half of the PM2.5 blows in from outside the city. Whose air should improve most?

Exposure is uneven, so a traffic policy helps the most exposed most. In 2019, NO₂ was 13% higher where London's most deprived residents were likely to live, and a coroner named traffic as the principal source of a child's exposure. Children and people with lung conditions are most vulnerable.

Mission 3: When rain falls on a city, where does it go, and why does it reach the river so fast?

Make a guess: When rain falls on a city, where does it go, and why does it reach the river so fast?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.5 urban precipitation, surfaces and catchments; impacts on drainage basin storage; the urban water cycle shown by hydrographs
  • 3.2.3.5 urban catchment management issues; sustainable urban drainage systems (SUDS)
Mission 3

Urban precipitation

  • Cities can add rain: heat drives convection; buildings lift air; pollution adds condensation nuclei.
  • Satellite radar around six US cities: monthly rainfall rates about 28% higher 30–60 km downwind.
  • More intense, shorter downpours fall onto surfaces that cannot absorb them.
Mission 3

Surfaces and storage: the urban water cycle

  • Impermeable roofs, roads and paving: little infiltration, so less soil and groundwater storage.
  • Fewer plants: less interception and evapotranspiration.
  • Drains and sewers: fast, smooth channels straight to rivers.
  • Result: more surface runoff, delivered faster; less water released slowly as baseflow.
Mission 3

The urban hydrograph

  • Compared with a rural catchment of the same size:
  • Shorter lag time (peak rainfall to peak discharge).
  • Higher peak discharge and a steeper rising limb.
  • Lower baseflow between storms (less groundwater recharge).
  • The diagram is schematic, not measured data.
Quick check
?In a model catchment, peak rainfall is at 10:00. Before development, peak discharge came at 18:00; after paving and piped drainage, at 13:00. What happened to lag time?
Mission 3

Issue 1: surface water flooding

  • England: 6.3 million properties in areas at risk of flooding from rivers, the sea or surface water.
  • About 4.6 million at risk from surface water, when rain overwhelms drainage ("flash flooding").
  • With climate change: around 8 million by mid-century, about 1 in 4 properties.
Mission 3

Issue 2: combined sewers and overflows

  • Old cities have combined sewers: foul sewage and rainwater in the same pipe.
  • London's ran at "80% or more" of capacity in dry weather, so "even light rainfall can trigger spills".
  • Combined sewer overflows sent about 39 million tonnes of untreated waste water into the Thames, 50–60 times a year.
  • Effects: low oxygen, fish deaths, health risks, sewage litter.
Quick check
?Why does more paving in an old city make river pollution worse, not just flooding?
Mission 3

SUDS: managing water where it falls

  • Sustainable drainage systems (SUDS) slow, store, soak away or reuse rainwater, mimicking natural drainage.
  • England's national standards set a hierarchy for runoff:
  • 1 collected for non-potable use → 2 infiltrated to ground → 3 to a surface water body → 4 to a surface water sewer → 5 to a combined sewer (last resort).
  • Everyday rain: at least the first 5 mm of most rainfall events should not run off the site.
  • Planning policy: development affecting drainage "should incorporate sustainable drainage systems".
Mission 3

SUDS features

  • Green roofs and walls: intercept and store rain.
  • Rainwater harvesting: collect for reuse (toilet flushing, watering).
  • Permeable surfaces: let water infiltrate.
  • Swales (shallow grassy channels) and infiltration trenches.
  • Detention basins: hold storm water and release it slowly.
  • Also "multifunctional benefits": water quality, biodiversity and amenity.
Quick check
?A developer can send roof water to (1) the nearest combined sewer, (2) a soakaway, or (3) a rainwater harvesting tank. Which order do England's SUDS standards require them to consider?
Mission 3

Is SUDS enough?

  • For the Thames, a review concluded "SuDS alone would not reduce spills from CSOs sufficiently".
  • So London added a 25 km storage tunnel, the Tideway, fully operational in Feb 2025.
  • Overflow volumes fell about 86% in 2025 (modelled; an unusually dry year).
  • Judgement: SUDS reduce runoff at source and add benefits; big infrastructure handles the legacy of combined sewers. Most cities need both.
Mission check

?Two identical housing estates are built on clay in the same town. Estate P drains roofs and roads into pipes to the river. Estate Q uses green roofs, permeable paving and a detention basin. After the same heavy storm, how will the river hydrograph below each estate differ?

In cities, rain hits impermeable surfaces and is piped away, so it reaches rivers faster and in larger peaks, and can overflow combined sewers. SUDS keep water where it falls (use, infiltrate, store, release slowly), lengthening lag time and lowering peaks; large cities with old sewers also need storage tunnels.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.5 a specific urban river restoration project in a damaged urban catchment: reasons, aims, stakeholder attitudes, activities, evaluation of outcomes
Mission 4

The damaged catchment

  • Mayes Brook, east London: flows to the River Roding, then the Thames.
  • The catchment is "highly responsive to storm events".
  • Mayesbrook Park (45 ha) is surrounded by dense housing and infrastructure.
  • Its Water Framework Directive water body (named Seven Kings Water) is classed as heavily modified.
Mission 4

Reasons for restoration

  • The brook ran in a concrete channel, fenced off and "largely invisible" from the park.
  • Dredged spoil piled on the park bank formed an embankment: a possible flood risk to lower-lying houses opposite.
  • Park lakes were polluted by a storm water overflow; 3 m of polluted sediment in the top lake.
  • Mostly short-mown grass: poor habitat, little used by neighbours; the park had "suffered from a lack of investment".
Mission 4

Aims

  • Make a rundown park "a showcase of how public greenspace can help a community to cope with the risks from climate change".
  • Risks named: more flooding and higher summer temperatures.
  • Flood risk was "not the primary driver", but the scheme had to reduce it.
  • Improve wildlife and recreation value.
Mission 4

Activities

  • 1.6 km of brook brought out of its channel into the park.
  • 1.5 ha of new floodplain storage; a backwater; SUDS and wetland areas.
  • 3 ha of woodland planted ("equivalent of 3 football pitches").
  • Separate Thames Water work fixed misconnected drains, "dramatically" improving water quality.
  • Built March 2011 – October 2012; river and floodplain works £750,000; whole park scheme about £3.8 million.
Quick check
?Which activity most directly reduces flood peaks downstream?
Mission 4

Stakeholders

  • Lead: Environment Agency and the London Borough of Barking and Dagenham (landowner).
  • Partners: Thames Rivers Trust, Queen Mary University of London, Natural England, Greater London Authority, London Wildlife Trust, RSA (insurance), SITA Trust, Mayesbrook Park Friends group.
  • Neighbours: moving the fence line beside homes "needed legal interpretation as to ownership".
  • Community: a Natural England-funded ranger (2011–13) ran events, volunteering and monitoring.
Mission 4

Attitudes: what the record shows

  • Funders were persuaded by an ecosystem services valuation; it "proved influential in convincing funders".
  • More funders brought more money, but the scope "needed to be tightly controlled" to keep core aims.
  • Contrast, the River Quaggy (south-east London): in 1990 the National Rivers Authority proposed more channelisation; local campaigners (now QWAG) argued instead for restoring the floodplain in Sutcliffe Park.
  • A survey for Greenwich Council found 78% of local people in favour; there was "much argument" over planning permission.
  • Attitudes shift: from taming rivers in concrete to giving them space.
Quick check
?Why might an insurance company (RSA) help fund an urban river restoration?
Mission 4

Evaluation: flooding

  • Modelling shows lower peak water levels, most for 1-in-100-year (plus climate change) and 1-in-1,000-year events.
  • Modelled reductions reach homes on the west bank in a 1-in-100 + climate change event.
  • But: this modelling "has not yet been validated through an actual flood event".
  • Number of properties protected: "Not available".
Mission 4

Evaluation: ecology, people, money

  • 2012–13: most restored sections had more habitat and flow types than in the 2008–09 baseline.
  • Drought in 2011–12 limited regrowth of water plants after the works.
  • Whole water body (not just the restored reach): moderate ecological status in 2019 and 2022; phosphate poor.
  • Estimated lifetime benefits about £27 million against £3.8 million costs: about £7 per £1.
  • Maintenance: about £10,000 a year before; expected saving of £5,000 a year. Phase 2 (lakes) on hold.

Mission check

?A council removes a culvert from a city stream. The Mayes Brook restoration (2011–12) turned a fenced concrete channel into a 1.6 km brook with 1.5 ha of floodplain. It is greener (more habitat, woodland, more use) and modelled to be safer, but the flood benefit is not yet proven by a real event, and the wider water body remains of "moderate" status.

Make a guess: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

Mission 5

Where urban waste comes from

  • Sources: industrial activity (factories, construction), commercial activity (shops, offices, restaurants) and personal consumption (households).
  • Waste streams are the separate flows a city collects and treats: dry recyclables, food waste, garden waste, residual ("black bag") waste.
  • England, 2024: households produced 21.9 million tonnes of waste, 376 kg per person.
Mission 5

Waste, wealth, lifestyles and attitudes

  • The world produced 2.56 billion tonnes of municipal solid waste in 2022; on current trends, 3.86 billion tonnes by 2050.
  • The World Bank: waste generation, "Driven by urbanization, rising incomes, and increased consumption", is growing faster than population.
  • Richer lifestyles add packaging and electronics: e-waste was 17.6 kg per person in Europe against a world average of 7.8 kg (2022).
  • Attitudes shape the streams: willingness to sort, buy less, repair or reuse.
Quick check
?Which change would most likely increase a city's waste per person?
Mission 5

Disposal options and their impacts

  • Unregulated (open dumping and burning): in low-income countries "the vast majority of waste is openly dumped"; smoke, disease, blocked drains.
  • Burial (landfill): methane, leachate into groundwater, land take.
  • Incineration and recovery: burning with energy recovery cuts landfill but emits CO₂ and air pollutants and leaves ash.
  • Recycling: saves raw materials, but plastics come in many types and can be recycled only a limited number of times.
  • Submergence (dumping at sea) and trade (exporting waste) move the impact to the sea or to another country.
Mission 5

England's balance

  • In 2024/25, English local authorities managed 25.2 million tonnes of waste:
    • incineration: 12.7 Mt, 50.3%
    • recycling: 10.4 Mt
    • landfill: 1.4 Mt, 5.5%.
  • England's waste-from-households recycling rate: 43.8% in 2024.
Quick check
?English local authorities managed 25.2 million tonnes of waste in 2024/25 and sent 10.4 million tonnes for recycling. What percentage was recycled?
Mission 5

Recycling varies by product

  • Plastics: only about 9% of all plastic waste had been recycled (estimate cited in 2018).
  • E-waste: 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled in 2022.
  • Food waste collected separately for recycling in England rose 6.7% to 535 thousand tonnes in 2024.
  • Recycling reduces resource consumption and ecological footprints only when recycled material replaces new material. The EU's circular material use rate was 12.2% in 2024.
Mission 5

Trade: why waste crossed oceans

  • "Upward of half of the plastic waste intended for recycling has been exported".
  • High-income countries supplied 87% of plastic waste exports (1988–2016).
  • China had imported a cumulative 45% of the world's plastic waste since 1992.
Mission 5

China closes its door

  • In 2017 China announced a policy "permanently banning the import of nonindustrial plastic waste", in force from the end of 2017.
  • Imports, thousand tonnes, 2016 → 2018:
    • China 8,801.6 → 51.4 (UN Comtrade, all plastic scrap, weight estimated; Brooks et al., using the same UN database, give 7.35 million tonnes for 2016)
    • Malaysia 287.7 → 872.5; Thailand 69.5 → 552.7; Türkiye 152.9 → 424.6.
  • Exporters also had to handle more at home, where the alternatives were landfill or incineration.
Mission 5

Rules tighten

  • Basel Convention: plastic-waste amendments adopted in 2019, effective 1 January 2021.
  • EU: exports of plastic waste to non-OECD countries banned from 21 November 2026.
  • Limits: Türkiye's imports still rose to 677.7 thousand tonnes by 2024. Rules shifted flows rather than stopping them.
Quick check
?Malaysia imported 287.7 thousand tonnes of plastic waste in 2016 and 872.5 thousand tonnes in 2018. How many times as much did it import in 2018?
Mission check

?In 2022, 5.1 billion kg of e-waste crossed borders; an estimated 3.3 billion kg went from high-income to middle- and low-income countries through uncontrolled movements. If a major importer banned e-waste imports, what does the plastic case suggest?

China banned most plastic waste imports from the end of 2017. Its imports fell 99.4%, while Malaysia's tripled and Thailand's and Türkiye's rose. Exporters had to recycle, incinerate or landfill more at home, and the Basel Convention amendments (2021) now control these flows more tightly.

Mission 6: Singapore burns the rubbish it can't recycle and buries the ash offshore. Why not simply landfill it all?

Make a guess: Singapore burns the rubbish it can't recycle and buries the ash offshore. Why not simply landfill it all?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.3.6 comparison of incineration and landfill approaches to waste disposal in a specified urban area
Mission 6

The specified urban area: Singapore

  • A city-state with a population density of 8,332 people per km² (June 2026).
  • Waste disposed rose "over a 7-fold" from 1,260 tonnes a day in 1970 to a peak of 9,412 tonnes a day in 2025.
  • NEA: the "main challenge" is "the setting aside of land for waste disposal in view of the limited land stock".
Mission 6

How the system works

  • Recycle first: overall recycling rate 52% in 2025; domestic only 11%.
  • Incinerate the rest in four waste-to-energy (WTE) plants.
  • Ash + non-incinerable waste → Tuas Marine Transfer Station → covered barges, a 3-hour, 30 km trip → Semakau Landfill.
  • So Singapore combines the two methods: incineration first, landfill for what is left.
Quick check
?Which material is landfilled at Semakau?
Mission 6

Incineration: benefits

  • Cuts waste volume by about 90%; ash is "about 10 per cent of its original volume".
  • Heat raises steam that drives turbines: up to 3% of Singapore's electricity.
  • Furnaces run at 850–1,000 °C.
  • Flue gas passes electrostatic precipitators, lime dosing and bag filters, then 100–150 m chimneys.
  • Ferrous scrap is recovered from the ash and recycled.
Mission 6

Incineration: costs and limits

  • It does not remove the need for landfill: ash still has to go somewhere.
  • Plants wear out: Ulu Pandan closed in 2009 after 30 years; Tuas Incineration Plant closed in 2022 after 36 years; both were replaced.
  • New capacity is large and slow to build: the Integrated Waste Management Facility (5,800 t/day of incinerable waste) opens in phases from 2027.
  • Burning releases gases that must be cleaned before release.
  • Recyclable materials that are burned are lost to reuse: the domestic recycling rate is only 11%.
Mission 6

Landfill: Semakau

  • Opened 1 April 1999; Singapore's only landfill; 350 ha.
  • Built about 8 km south of the main island: a 7 km rock bund encloses sea off Pulau Semakau and Pulau Sakeng.
  • The bund is lined with an impermeable membrane and marine clay to contain leachate.
  • Received 2,098 t/day in 2021; expected to meet needs "up to 2035 and beyond".
  • Coral reefs on the western shore "remain largely intact"; replanted mangroves are "thriving".
Mission 6

Landfill in general: methane

  • Landfill gas comes from decomposing organic material: about 50% methane, 50% CO₂.
  • Methane is "at least 28 times more effective than CO₂ at trapping heat" over 100 years.
  • In the USA, municipal landfills are the third-largest human source of methane: about 14.4% (2022).
  • Semakau mostly receives ash, which has already been burned, so it should produce far less gas than a landfill of raw waste.
Quick check
?A landfill cell would be full after 1 year of untreated waste. If the same waste is incinerated first, cutting its volume by about 90%, roughly how long would the cell last?
Mission 6

Side by side

  • Land: incineration ~90% less volume; landfill needs space, which Singapore lacks.
  • Energy: incineration up to 3% of electricity; landfill gas, roughly half methane and half carbon dioxide, can be captured; if not, the methane escapes.
  • Emissions: incineration needs flue-gas cleaning; raw-waste landfill emits methane.
  • Residues: incineration still needs landfill for ash; landfill needs leachate containment.
  • Lifespan: WTE plants last ~30–36 years; Semakau to 2035 and beyond.
Quick check
?Which reason does NEA itself give as the main driver of Singapore's choice to incinerate before landfilling?
Mission 6

Exam move: compare, then judge

  • Compare on the same criteria: land, energy, emissions, residues, lifespan.
  • Locate the judgement: in a dense city-state with little land, incineration plus a small, lined landfill makes sense.
  • Qualify: in a city with abundant land and gas capture, the balance could shift.
  • Singapore shows the two methods are complements, not just rivals.
Mission check

?A city has cheap, remote land with clay geology, captures methane at its landfill to generate electricity, and its people oppose a new incinerator near housing. Which conclusion best fits the evidence?

Singapore incinerates first because land is scarce: burning cuts volume by about 90% and yields some electricity, and Semakau takes the ash. Where land is plentiful and gas is captured, landfill can compete. The best method depends on the urban area's constraints.

Mission 7: Smog, sewage and abandoned land: why do the same three problems need different fixes in different cities?

Mission 7

Three urban problems, two contrasting cities

  • Atmospheric pollution: gases and particles (NO₂, PM2.5) at harmful levels. In 2019, 99% of the world's people lived where WHO guideline levels were not met.
  • Water pollution: sewage, industrial effluent and run-off entering rivers.
  • Dereliction: land and buildings left empty and decaying after their use ends.
  • Contrast: London (high-income country) and Delhi (India, lower-middle income).
Mission 7

Delhi's air: a winter problem

  • Delhi's air is tracked by the CPCB's network of 39 monitoring stations.
  • PM2.5 rises from September and peaks post-monsoon and in winter; the highest daily city average in 2019–2023 was 544.71 µg/m³ on 3 November 2019. India's daily standard is 60.
  • Causes: emissions from stubble burning, vehicles and industry are trapped by a shallow boundary layer and low winds.
  • Across South Asia, crop residue burning is 40–60% of peak pollution in the winter harvest months.
Mission 7

Why the weather makes it worse

  • The North China Plain emits 65% more particulates and precursors a year than the Indo-Gangetic Plain, where Delhi lies.
  • Yet the Indo-Gangetic Plain has the higher aerosol pollution.
  • Reason: stagnant winds (< 6 km/h) and a shallow boundary layer (< 300 m) give poor ventilation.
  • So the same emissions give higher concentrations in Delhi. Geography sets the city's "carrying capacity" for pollution.
Quick check
?Why are Delhi's worst PM2.5 days in winter rather than in the monsoon?
Mission 7

London's air: from smoke to traffic

  • Great Smog, December 1952: about 4,000 known deaths; the Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 banned black smoke.
  • Today the main local problem is traffic: road transport is about 30% of PM2.5 emitted within London.
  • But over half of London's PM2.5 comes from outside the city, often from outside the UK.
  • Exposure is unequal: in 2019, NO₂ was 13% higher in the most deprived areas than the least deprived.
Mission 7

Strategies for urban air

  • London: Ultra Low Emission Zone (2019; inner London 2021; London-wide 29 Aug 2023) charges older vehicles.
  • Roadside NO₂ in 2024 was 27% lower than modelled without the ULEZ.
  • Delhi: Graded Response Action Plan — forecast-triggered bans on construction and trucks, and some industrial closures. Six GRAP orders in winter 2024, with "abrupt and often negative impacts on local economies".
  • Crop burning is illegal but widespread; fires rise 15% when the wind would carry smoke to a neighbouring district. Enforcement is the weak link.
  • Beijing cut PM2.5 by 35% in 2013–2017 through energy changes, coal controls and vehicle rules.

Mission 7

London's water: a Victorian sewer

  • The sewers were built for a city of 4 million; they now serve over 9 million.
  • Combined sewers carry rain and sewage together; overflows put about 39 million tonnes of raw sewage into the Thames in 50–60 events a year.
  • Strategy: the Thames Tideway Tunnel, 25 km, fully operational February 2025.
  • 2025: overflow volumes fell about 86% (modelled; an unusually dry year).
Mission 7

Delhi's water: the Yamuna

  • In Delhi the Yamuna runs about 48 km, 2% of its length, but receives almost 79% of its pollutant load (CPCB figures).
  • Drains bring about 3,026 million litres a day of domestic sewage; industry about 35.9.
  • Of 41 sewage treatment plants, some are at times non-operational through power cuts and maintenance problems.
  • Strategy: the Yamuna Action Plans (I and II) were judged unsuccessful by one study; interceptor sewers to carry drain flows to treatment plants are proposed.

Mission 7

Dereliction: east London and Detroit

  • London: before 2012 the Olympic Park site in the Lower Lea Valley was "a largely derelict, polluted and inaccessible site".
  • Detroit: population fell from 1,849,568 (1950) to 639,111 (2020); empty homes and lots followed.
  • The Detroit Land Bank Authority aims to "return Detroit's blighted and vacant properties to productive use"; its website lists 59,039 vacant-land parcels and 32,692 vacant lots sold.
  • Causes differ: east London's dereliction came from industrial decline in a growing city; Detroit's from population loss.
Mission 7

Strategies for dereliction

  • Flagship regeneration (London): 2.5 km² of brownfield remediated, more than 200 buildings demolished, 98% of demolition material reused or recycled.
  • Brownfield-first planning: in England, 54% of new residential addresses in 2021–22 were on previously developed land.
  • Land banking (Detroit): sell side lots to neighbours, auction homes for renovation.
  • Trade-off: flagship schemes can draw better-off households in and raise fears of gentrification.
Mission check

?An inland city in an emerging economy has: winter smog that forms when air is still; raw sewage from unsewered districts; and a closed steelworks. Which plan best matches strategies to causes?

Atmospheric pollution, water pollution and dereliction have different causes, so strategies must be matched to each. Delhi shows that regional sources and weather matter for air, and that sewage treatment must work in practice; London shows that big engineering and planning schemes can work but are costly and uneven in who benefits.

Mission 8

Urban impacts: local and global

  • Global: urban areas consume 75% of global energy and produce 75% of greenhouse gas emissions (UN, citing the EU Joint Research Centre).
  • Land: built-up area per person rose from 43 to 63 m²; about 60% of land converted to urban use since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Local: heat islands, air and water pollution, waste, and loss of habitat at the edge.
Mission 8

Ecological and carbon footprints

  • Ecological footprint: the biologically productive area a population needs to produce what it consumes and absorb its waste, especially carbon emissions, in global hectares.
  • Carbon footprint: the greenhouse gases caused by an activity or population, in tonnes of CO₂-equivalent.
  • A city's footprint lies mostly outside it: food, goods, energy and waste sinks elsewhere.
  • Production-based accounts count emissions released in the city; consumption-based accounts add those embodied in what residents buy.
  • Globally, Earth Overshoot Day fell on 30 July 2026: by then humanity had used a whole year's biocapacity.
Quick check
?A city has closed its factories and now imports most goods. What happens to its two carbon accounts?
Mission 8

Dimensions of sustainability

  • AQA's four dimensions: natural (ecosystems, resources), physical (buildings, infrastructure), social (equity, community, health), economic (jobs, viable services).
  • A sustainable city meets residents' needs now without stopping future generations meeting theirs.
  • Features: compact form, public transport, efficient buildings, renewable energy, green space, mixed and affordable housing, local participation.
Mission 8

Social sustainability

  • Equity: affordable homes and services for all incomes, not only for those who can pay a premium for "green" housing.
  • Community: mixed neighbourhoods, safe public space, a say in planning.
  • Health and access: clean air, walkable services, transport that does not need a car.
  • Exam move: an eco-district that only the well-off can afford is environmentally strong but socially weak.
Mission 8

Liveability

  • Liveability: how good a place is to live in, now, for its residents.
  • The EIU ranks 173 cities on more than 30 indicators in five categories: stability, healthcare, culture and environment, education and infrastructure.
  • Its 2025 average index score was 76.1 out of 100.
  • Liveability is not sustainability: a highly liveable city can have a large footprint.

Mission 8

Eco-district: Vauban, Freiburg (Germany)

  • A former French army barracks, redeveloped after the forces left in 1992.
  • About 41 ha and 5,267 residents (December 2020).
  • "Not a car-free but a car-reduced district" (translated): no parking on residential streets; car owners buy a space in one of two district garages (about 470 spaces).
  • A tram line has served it since April 2006.
Mission 8

Vauban: cutting the footprint

  • Low-energy construction is compulsory (65 kWh/m²); 277 homes were built to passive-house standard (at most 15 kWh/m² a year).
  • In the Solar Settlement's plus-energy houses, more energy is produced than used.
  • Heat comes from a woodchip-fired combined heat and power plant, whose electricity covers about 700 households.
  • No rainwater sewers: rain runs in open channels and soaks into the ground.
  • December 2020: 1,132 private cars, about 215 per 1,000 residents.
Mission 8

Eco-city: Sino-Singapore Tianjin (China)

  • Agreed by China and Singapore in November 2007; built on 34.2 km² of "non-arable land, deserted salt pans, and polluted water bodies" about 40 km from Tianjin's centre.
  • Targets for 2020 included 100% green buildings, 90% of trips by walking, cycling or public transport, and 20% renewable energy.
  • Planned for "350,000 residents and 190,000 jobs by 2020".
  • World Bank completion report (2017): "about 40,000 residents" by 2016; about 93% of buildings exceeded the green building standard; renewable energy in the public housing pilot 9.9% against a 60% target.
Quick check
?Tianjin Eco-City had about 40,000 residents in 2016 against 350,000 planned for 2020. What percentage of the planned population was that?
Mission 8

Smart cities: retrofitting and purpose-built

  • Smart city: digital technology (sensors, data platforms) to run traffic, lighting, energy and waste. The UN lists such technologies among ways to build resilience.
  • Retrofitting: Barcelona's council began a smart-city project in November 2012; its Sentilo platform is open-source software for city sensor data. Superblocks turned parts of four Eixample streets into green hubs with four squares of about 2,000 m² each.
  • Purpose-built: Masdar City (Abu Dhabi), begun in 2008, claims buildings "use 40% less energy and water"; its own 2024 report gives a 22.7% cut in energy use intensity and 13.1% water savings.
Mission 8

Opportunities, challenges and strategies

  • Opportunities: density cuts travel and heating per person; new districts test ideas; data can cut waste.
  • Challenges: delivery lags ambition (Tianjin: about 40,000 of 350,000); developers' claims need independent checks (Masdar: 40% v 22.7%); cost and affordability; privacy in smart cities.
  • Strategies: building standards, car-reduced streets and public transport, renewable heat, water-sensitive design, and retrofitting existing cities, where most people already live.
Mission check

?A national government has money for one programme to cut urban carbon emissions. Which choice is most likely to make the biggest difference, and why?

Eco-districts such as Vauban prove the ideas (compulsory low-energy building, car-reduced streets), but new eco-cities house few people (Tianjin: about 40,000 of a planned 350,000 by 2016). The real difference comes from retrofitting existing cities, judged by independent evidence, not developer claims.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Urban form (materials, canyons, impermeable ground) and processes (waste heat, pollution) change local weather.
  • The heat island is strongest at night; cities boost convective rain downwind; fog depends on pollution; tall buildings make wind gusty and channelled.

Mission 2

  • Particulate pollution (PM10, PM2.5) is primary or secondary; photochemical pollution (ozone) forms from NOx and VOCs in sunlight.
  • Exposure is highest beside busy roads and, in London, in more deprived areas.
  • Policies: Clean Air Acts (smoke), ULEZ (traffic), national targets; local action cannot fix pollution that drifts in.

Mission 3

  • Urban surfaces cut infiltration, interception and storage; drains speed runoff: shorter lag, higher peak, lower baseflow. Cities can also intensify rainfall downwind (+28%, six US cities).
  • Issues: surface water flood risk (4.6m properties in England), combined sewer overflows (Thames 39 Mt/yr). SUDS hierarchy (use → infiltrate → water body → sewer) helps; Tideway shows legacy sewers also need large storage.

Mission 4

  • Mayes Brook, Mayesbrook Park (east London): reasons (concrete channel, polluted lakes, run-down park, flood-risk embankment); aims (climate-change park: flooding and heat, wildlife, recreation); activities (1.6 km restored, 1.5 ha floodplain, SUDS, 3 ha woodland, 2011–12).
  • Stakeholders: EA and council led many partners and a Friends group; funders persuaded by valuation; Quaggy shows campaigners overturning channelisation. Evaluation: more habitat; modelled (unvalidated) flood reduction; status still "moderate"; estimated £7 benefit per £1.

Mission 5

  • Urban waste comes from industry, commerce and households; its amount and streams rise with income and consumption and reflect lifestyles and attitudes.
  • Each disposal option (unregulated, recycling, recovery, incineration, burial, submergence, trade) moves impacts somewhere: air, land, sea or another country.

Mission 6

  • Singapore incinerates non-recycled waste in four WTE plants (≈90% volume cut, up to 3% of electricity) and landfills the ash at offshore Semakau (opened 1999, 350 ha, lined bund, to 2035 and beyond).
  • Incineration saves land but still needs landfill, wears out and burns resources; landfill needs space and controls for leachate and methane.

Mission 7

  • Problems differ by city: Delhi's air is driven by regional burning and poor winter ventilation; London's by traffic. The Yamuna's load is mostly domestic sewage; the Thames's comes from overflows of a Victorian system. Dereliction follows industrial decline (London) or population loss (Detroit).
  • Strategies: ULEZ, GRAP, enforcement on crop burning; Tideway, treatment plants and interceptor sewers; remediation, brownfield-first planning and land banks. Judge them by evidence and by who gains.

Mission 8

  • Cities use 75% of global energy; their ecological and carbon footprints lie mostly outside them.
  • Sustainability has natural, physical, social and economic dimensions; liveability is a different measure.
  • Eco-districts and eco-cities test ideas; retrofitting existing cities spreads them.

Mission 9

  • Dhaka's growth (almost 37 million in 2025 on the UN definition) is driven by natural increase and migration to garment and informal jobs; informal settlements and services lag behind.
  • Well-being, physical environment, sustainability and residents' experience all show benefits and costs falling on different people.

Mission 10

  • London (8.8m; 40.6% born abroad): highest GDP per head (£72,201) but least affordable housing (10.6×), high renting, east London deprivation.
  • Environment and sustainability improving (ULEZ −27% NO₂; Tideway −86% overflow volume, modelled) but with weak recycling (28.8%); residents report slightly lower life satisfaction (7.35 v 7.45) and many move out.
