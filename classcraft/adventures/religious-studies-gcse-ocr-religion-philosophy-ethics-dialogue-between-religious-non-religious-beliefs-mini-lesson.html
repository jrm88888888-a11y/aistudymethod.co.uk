Religious & Non-Religious Dialogue

The big question

What is this topic about?

OCR calls this topic Dialogue Between Religious and Non-Religious Beliefs. It asks: how do religious and non-religious people understand the world, and how can they talk and live together well despite their differences?

  • Some people are religious — for example theists, who believe in God or gods.
  • Some people are non-religious — for example atheists, agnostics and Humanists.
  • The topic looks at how each group finds meaning, purpose and morality, and how they answer life's big questions.

Spec focus: you should be able to explain the key worldviews, compare how religious and non-religious people find meaning and morality, and discuss the value of respectful dialogue between them.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Theism — belief in God or gods (for example Christians, Muslims, Jews).
  • Atheism — the belief that there is no God.
  • Agnosticism — the view that we cannot know whether God exists.
  • Humanism — a non-religious worldview that bases meaning, morality and value on human reason, evidence and compassion rather than on God.
  • Dialogue — respectful conversation between people of different beliefs to build understanding.

Common mix-up: an atheist is sure there is no God; an agnostic says we cannot know either way. Being non-religious does not mean having no morals — Humanists take morality very seriously; they just base it on human wellbeing rather than God.

Meaning & purpose

Where do people find meaning?

Everyone wants a sense of meaning and purpose in life. Religious and non-religious people often find it in different places — but both can live full, purposeful lives.

Key idea: a religious person may say life's purpose comes from God; a Humanist may say we make our own purpose and should make the most of the one life we have. Both can be deeply meaningful ways to live.
Morality

Where does morality come from?

A big question in the dialogue is: what makes something right or wrong?

  • Religious ethics often base morality on God — through scripture, religious teachings and the example of key figures (for example the Bible, the Qur'an, or the teachings of Jesus).
  • Secular / Humanist ethics base morality on human reason, empathy, the consequences of actions, and human wellbeing — asking "will this help or harm people?"

Important: the two approaches often reach the same conclusions — for example, that murder, cruelty and dishonesty are wrong, and that kindness and fairness are good. Religious people are not the only ones with morals, and non-religious people are not immoral; they simply give different reasons for their shared values.

Big question 1

How did the universe begin?

One classic question in the dialogue is the origin of the universe.

  • Many religious believers say the universe was created by God.
  • Scientists explain the universe beginning with the Big Bang around 13.8 billion years ago.

These are not always in conflict. Many religious believers accept the Big Bang and see it as how God created the universe — God as the ultimate cause behind the science. Many atheists and Humanists accept the Big Bang as a full, natural explanation with no need for God.

Big question 2

Where did humans come from?

A second big question is the origin of humans.

  • Some religious believers hold to special creation — that God directly created humans (some read creation stories, such as Adam and Eve, literally).
  • Science explains humans arising through evolution by natural selection over millions of years.

Again, these need not clash. Many religious believers accept evolution, seeing it as the process God used to bring about human life; they may read creation stories as expressing deeper truths rather than as science. Atheists and Humanists generally accept evolution as a complete natural explanation.

Fair to both sides: accepting evolution does not make someone non-religious, and holding a religious view of creation does not make someone "anti-science". People hold a range of views within each group.
Big question 3

What happens after death?

A third big question is what happens when we die. Here the views differ more sharply:

  • Many religious believers hold that there is some form of afterlife — for example heaven, resurrection, or rebirth, depending on the religion.
  • Most atheists and Humanists believe there is no afterlife: death is the end of consciousness, and this is the one life we have.

This difference shapes how people live. A believer may find comfort and hope in an afterlife; a Humanist may argue that because this life is all we have, it is all the more precious and worth living well.

Common ground: both often agree that how we treat others in this life matters enormously — whether the reason is answering to God or valuing the one life each person has.

Humanism up close

What Humanists believe

Humanism is one of the most important non-religious worldviews for this topic. Humanists:

  • do not believe in God or an afterlife, and rely on evidence and reason to understand the world;
  • base morality on human empathy, the consequences of our actions, and human wellbeing — treating others as we would wish to be treated;
  • believe this one life gives us the responsibility to make it a good one, for ourselves and others.

Represent them fairly: Humanists are not nihilists (who think nothing matters) and are not immoral. They care deeply about human rights, kindness, honesty and living a meaningful life — they simply do this without religious belief.

Why dialogue matters

The value of dialogue

Dialogue is respectful conversation between people who believe different things. It is at the heart of this topic — and it comes in two main forms:

  • Interfaith dialogue — conversation between different religions (for example Christians and Muslims).
  • Religious / non-religious dialogue — conversation between believers and non-believers (for example a Christian and a Humanist).

Dialogue is not about winning an argument or converting the other person. Its purpose is to listen, understand, find common ground, and learn to live well together in a society of many beliefs.

Benefits of dialogue: it reduces prejudice and misunderstanding, builds friendship and cooperation, helps people work together on shared goals (like helping the poor), and supports a peaceful, tolerant society.

Living together

Tolerance and freedom of belief

For dialogue to work, people need to be able to disagree respectfully. Two important ideas here are:

  • Tolerance — accepting that others have the right to hold beliefs different from your own, even when you disagree with them.
  • Freedom of belief — the right of each person to choose, follow, change, or leave a religion or worldview.

Tolerance does not mean thinking every belief is equally true, or never disagreeing. It means treating people with respect and dignity while still being honest about your own views.

Disagreeing respectfully: attack ideas, not people; listen carefully; avoid stereotypes; and remember most people are trying, sincerely, to live good and meaningful lives.

Why it matters

Why dialogue matters in real life

Exam questions often ask about the value and purpose of dialogue. In diverse societies, people of many beliefs live and work side by side, so dialogue has real benefits:

  • It builds understanding and breaks down stereotypes about "the other side".
  • It reveals common ground — shared values like compassion, honesty and fairness.
  • It lets religious and non-religious people work together on shared goals, such as charity, justice and caring for the environment.
  • It reduces conflict and prejudice, supporting a peaceful, tolerant society.
  • It helps individuals examine and strengthen their own beliefs by hearing them challenged respectfully.

Recap

The ideas to know

Theism: belief in God or gods. Atheism: the belief there is no God.

Agnosticism: the view that we cannot know whether God exists.

Humanism: a non-religious worldview based on reason, evidence and human wellbeing.

Meaning: from God/faith, or from relationships, achievements and human flourishing.

Morality: from God/scripture, or from reason, empathy and wellbeing — often the same conclusions.

Big questions: universe (God / Big Bang), humans (creation / evolution), death (afterlife / none).

Dialogue: respectful conversation to understand, find common ground and live well together.
