Mission 1

Two tests for any good

  • Rival: one person's use leaves less for others. A sandwich you eat cannot be eaten by me.
  • Excludable: the seller can stop people who have not paid from using it.
ExcludableNon-excludable
RivalPrivate good: a sandwich, a bus seatCommon resource: fish in the open sea
Non-rivalA film on a streaming servicePublic good: national defence, a flood barrier, street lighting
  • Ask the two questions separately. A good can pass one test and fail the other.
Mission 1

Private goods and public goods

  • Private good: rival and excludable. The price mechanism works: pay, or go without.
  • Public good: non-rival and non-excludable.
  • Non-rival: one more user adds nothing to cost, so the marginal cost of an extra user is zero.
  • Say: "non-excludable: non-payers cannot be stopped from benefiting" — not: "it is free". Someone pays, usually through taxes.

Mission 1

The free rider problem

  • Free rider: someone who benefits from a good without paying for it.
  • Non-excludable → a firm cannot charge, because people can benefit anyway.
  • Each person thinks: "If others pay, I benefit free. If they don't, my payment alone won't be enough."
  • So too few pay. A firm cannot cover its costs and does not supply the good.
  • Result: the good is under-provided or not provided at all: a missing market.
  • A missing market is a complete market failure: the market does not exist, although the good would raise welfare.

Mission 1

Real world: who pays for lighthouses?

  • A lighthouse beam is non-rival (one more ship sees it at no extra cost) and non-excludable at sea.
  • Textbook result: no firm could make ships pay, so the state must provide.
  • In the British Isles, lighthouses are paid for by light dues: a charge on commercial ships when they call at a port.
  • The money goes into the General Lighthouse Fund, overseen by the UK Department for Transport.
  • A ship cannot be excluded from the light. It can be excluded from the port. The charge is tied to something excludable.
  • The dues are compulsory and set by government: closer to a tax than a market price.
Mission 1

How governments respond

  • Direct provision: government supplies the good and pays through taxation. Taxes are compulsory, so free riding is overcome.
  • Examples: national defence, flood defences, street lighting.
  • Government may produce the good itself (state employees, state-owned assets).
  • Or government pays a private firm to produce the good. The state still finances it; users still pay nothing at the point of use.
  • This is contracting out to the private sector.
  • The hard part: with no market price, how much does society value the good? Government must estimate it (cost-benefit analysis).
Quick check
?A city council pays a private company to install and maintain all its street lights. Residents pay nothing directly. What has happened?
Mission 1Evaluate

Must the state provide public goods?

It depends on:

  • How non-excludable the good really is. Charging may be possible where users can be shut out (light dues at ports), or once technology allows it (electronic road tolls).
  • Information. Government must guess how much people value the good; it can provide too much or too little (government failure).
  • The cost of taxation and of state production. Contracting out may cut costs, but contracts are hard to write and monitor.
  • Group size. A few neighbours may agree to share a cost; with millions of people, free riding dominates.

Mission 2

The cost nobody pays for

  • A firm pays for labour, materials, energy: its private costs.
  • Its production may also harm third parties, who are outside the trade: polluted air and rivers, noise, carbon emissions.
  • That harm is an external cost. Nobody pays the firm's victims through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost.
  • At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC (marginal external cost).
  • A negative production externality = an external cost created by producing a good.
  • Examples: a chemical plant's waste in a river harms fishing downstream; an airport's noise lowers the value of nearby homes; a coal power station's emissions harm health.
Mission 2

Worked example: adding up social cost

  • Illustrative data: a paper mill dumps waste in a river. Per tonne of paper:
  • Marginal private cost (wood, labour, energy) = 300.
  • Marginal external cost (lost fishing, water cleaning downstream) = 80.
  • MSC = MPC + MEC = 300 + 80 = 380.
  • Buyers value the last tonne at 360 (MPB). No external benefit, so MSB = 360.
  • The mill makes the tonne: to it, 360 > 300. To society: 360 − 380 = −20. Society loses 20 on that tonne.
  • If all 1,000 tonnes cost the same: private cost 300,000; external cost 80,000; social cost 380,000.

Mission 2

The diagram

  • Step 1: the market. Supply = MPC; demand = MPB = MSB (no external benefit). Market equilibrium at Qm, Pm.
  • Step 2: add the external cost. MSC lies above MPC; the vertical gap = MEC.
  • Step 3: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC: Q* (less), P* (higher).
  • Firms respond to private costs only, so the market produces Qm > Q*: overproduction.
  • The price Pm is too low: it does not include the external cost.
  • Say: "the market overproduces compared with the social optimum" — not: "the market is in disequilibrium".
Mission 2

The welfare loss

  • For each unit from Q* to Qm: MSC > MSB. It costs society more than it is worth.
  • Adding up these losses gives a triangle:
  • corners: the social optimum (MSB = MSC); MSC at Qm; MSB at Qm.
  • The triangle points towards the social optimum.
  • Called the welfare loss.
  • Say: "welfare loss = the triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q* to Qm" — not: "the whole area between MSC and MPC". That bigger area is the external cost of all output, most of which is still worth bearing.

Mission 2

Worked example: calculating the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q; MEC = 20 per unit, so MSC = 40 + Q.
  • Market: 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so Qm = 40, Pm = 60.
  • Social optimum: 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so Q* = 30, P* = 70.
  • At Qm = 40: MSC = 80; MSB = 60. Height = 80 − 60 = 20 (= MEC).
  • Base = Qm − Q* = 40 − 30 = 10.
  • Welfare loss = ½ × 10 × 20 = 100.
  • Compare: total external cost at Qm = 20 × 40 = 800. The welfare loss is only 100: most of the external cost (20 × 30 = 600) falls on units that are still worth producing.

Mission 2

Putting a number on the external cost

  • Carbon dioxide is a global external cost: emissions anywhere add to climate damage everywhere.
  • The social cost of carbon estimates the damage from one more tonne of CO₂, in today's money.
  • Two US government estimates for emissions in 2020: $51 per tonne (2021) and $190 per tonne (2023).
  • The gap comes mostly from assumptions, above all how much weight to give damage to people in the future.
  • So the MEC used for policy depends on value judgements, not only on science.
Mission 2Evaluate

How big is the problem?

  • It depends on measuring MEC. Health, nature and future harm have no market price, so estimates vary widely.
  • It depends on elasticity. For a given MEC, the more price elastic demand and supply are, the bigger the overproduction (Qm − Q*) and the welfare loss.
  • It depends on who and where. Many small polluters, or harm across borders, make the external cost hard to trace and charge.
  • It depends on time. Some damage builds up over years (carbon, soil), so today's MEC may understate the harm.
  • Ways to correct it (taxes, regulation, permits, property rights) are in Mission 7.

Mission 3

Benefit to you, cost to others

  • Marginal private benefit (MPB): the benefit to the buyer of one more unit. It is the buyer's demand curve.
  • Some consumption harms third parties: people outside the deal.
  • Marginal external cost (MEC) of consumption: the harm to third parties from one more unit consumed.
  • Marginal social benefit (MSB) = MPB − MEC. The external cost cuts the benefit to society.
  • Buyers respond to private, not external, costs and benefits.
  • Example: second-hand smoke. Tobacco kills over 7 million people a year, including over 1.6 million non-smokers exposed to second-hand smoke (WHO).
  • Other cases: loud music at night, litter, driving after drinking alcohol.
Mission 3

Overconsumption on the diagram

  • Step 1: no production externality, so MPC = MSC. The market settles where MPB = MPC: quantity Q1, price P1.
  • Step 2: MSB lies below MPB by the external cost. The social optimum is where MSB = MSC: quantity Q*.
  • Q1 > Q*: the market overconsumes.
  • Step 3: each unit from Q* to Q1 has MSC above MSB. Society loses on every one.
  • The shaded triangle between Q* and Q1 is the welfare loss. It points to the social optimum.
  • Say: "the welfare loss is the triangle between Q* and Q1" — not: "the whole area under MSB".

Mission 3

Demerit goods

  • A demerit good is more harmful to the consumer than they realise.
  • It is overconsumed if left to the market.
  • Examples often given: tobacco, alcohol, gambling, recreational drugs.
  • Why overconsumed:
  • Imperfect information: buyers underestimate the harm to themselves.
  • Short-termism and addiction: today's pleasure outweighs tomorrow's cost.
  • External costs: most demerit goods also harm third parties.
  • Calling a good "demerit" is a value judgement: someone decides it is bad for people.
Mission 3

The demerit-good diagram

  • Step 1: buyers act on MPB (perceived). Market at Q1, where it meets MPC = MSC.
  • Step 2: with full information, buyers would value it less: MPB (full information) lies lower. Quantity falls to Q2.
  • Step 3: adding the external cost gives MSB, lower still. Social optimum Q* where MSB = MSC.
  • Overconsumption: Q1 > Q2 > Q*. Welfare loss: the triangle between Q* and Q1.
  • Say: "overconsumed" for a demerit good — not: "overproduced because firms make too much".
Mission 3

Who decides what is "bad"?

  • Positive statement: can be tested against evidence. "Smokers underestimate the risk of lung cancer."
  • Normative statement (value judgement): an opinion about what ought to be. "People should not smoke."
  • Case for treating a good as demerit:
  • Evidence of information failure, addiction, harm to others.
  • Case against:
  • Consumer sovereignty: adults are the best judges of their own welfare.
  • Fears of paternalism: the state imposing its tastes.
  • Which goods count changes over time and between societies.
  • Policy responses (taxes, bans, information) are in Mission 7.
Quick check
?Which of these statements about a product is a value judgement, not a positive statement?
Mission 3Evaluate

How serious is the overconsumption?

  • It depends on the size of the external cost: hard to measure, so Q* is an estimate.
  • It depends on information: if buyers learn the true harm, perceived MPB falls on its own.
  • It depends on who is harmed: harm to the buyer alone is a weaker case than harm to others.
  • It depends on the value judgement: views differ on how far the state should overrule personal choices.

Mission 4

Benefits that spill over

  • Marginal private benefit (MPB): the benefit to the buyer of one more unit. It is the demand curve.
  • Marginal external benefit (MEB): the benefit to third parties of one more unit.
  • Marginal social benefit (MSB) = MPB + MEB.
  • Buyers respond to private benefits only. The external benefit is ignored.
  • Example: vaccination. Each vaccinated person also lowers the chance that others catch the disease.
  • Measles vaccination averted an estimated 60 million deaths worldwide in 2000–2023.
  • Goal: 95% coverage with 2 doses. Actual global coverage in 2023: 83% for dose 1, 74% for dose 2.
  • Many things cause that shortfall, such as access, cost and conflict. The spillover is one reason why free choice alone may not close it.
  • Other cases: education, training, restoring old buildings.
Mission 4

Underconsumption on the diagram

  • Step 1: MPC = MSC (no production externality). The market settles where MPB = MPC: Q1, P1.
  • Step 2: MSB lies above MPB by the external benefit. Social optimum Q* where MSB = MSC.
  • Q1 < Q*: the market underconsumes.
  • Step 3: units from Q1 to Q* have MSB above MSC, but are not consumed. The lost surplus is the welfare loss triangle, pointing to the social optimum.
  • Say: "underconsumption" or "underprovision" — not: "demand is too low", which makes it sound like a demand shift.

Mission 4

Merit goods

  • A merit good is more beneficial to the consumer than they realise.
  • It is underconsumed if left to the market.
  • Examples often given: education, healthcare, vaccination, pensions.
  • Why underconsumed:
  • Imperfect information: people underestimate the long-run benefit to themselves.
  • Short-termism: the cost is now, the benefit years later.
  • External benefits: most merit goods also benefit others.
  • Calling a good "merit" is a value judgement.
  • Merit goods are not public goods: they are rival and excludable (see Mission 1).
Mission 4

The merit-good diagram

  • Step 1: buyers act on MPB (perceived). Market at Q1, where it meets MPC = MSC.
  • Step 2: with full information, they would value it more: MPB (full information) lies higher. Quantity Q2.
  • Step 3: adding the external benefit gives MSB, higher still. Social optimum Q* where MSB = MSC.
  • Underconsumption: Q1 < Q2 < Q*. Welfare loss: the triangle between Q1 and Q*.
  • Say: "underconsumed because of imperfect information" — not: "underconsumed because it is too expensive".

Mission 4Evaluate

Should the government step in?

  • It depends on measuring the MEB: hard to value, so the right subsidy is uncertain.
  • It depends on information: if perceived MPB is the problem, information may be cheaper than a subsidy.
  • It depends on opportunity cost: money spent here cannot be spent elsewhere; risk of government failure.
  • It depends on the value judgement: some argue people should be free to choose; others that the state should ensure access.

Mission 5

What markets need to know

  • Markets allocate resources well when buyers and sellers know the prices, the quality and the consequences of what they trade.
  • Imperfect information: people lack some of this knowledge, so they make choices they would not make if fully informed.
  • Information failure: missing or uneven information leads to a misallocation of resources: a market failure.
Mission 5

Asymmetric information

  • Asymmetric information: one party to a deal knows more than the other.
  • The seller knows more:
    • a used car's history
    • whether a repair was needed
    • the risks of a financial product
  • The buyer knows more:
    • an insurance customer knows their own health or driving
    • a borrower knows how likely they are to repay
  • The better-informed side can gain at the other's expense, so the other side trades less, or not at all.
  • Say: "asymmetric information" — not: "imperfect information", when one side knows more than the other.
Quick check
?Which situation is an example of asymmetric information?
Mission 5

Hidden quality: the used-car problem

  • Used cars are either good or poor ("lemons"). The seller knows which. The buyer cannot tell.
  • So buyers will pay only what an average car is worth to them.
  • Owners of good cars get less than their car is worth, so many keep them.
  • Fewer good cars on sale → the average falls → buyers offer even less.
  • The market can shrink until mostly poor cars are traded. Good cars that both sides would gladly trade are not sold.
  • Idea from George Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons" (1970).
Mission 5

Worked example: what will a buyer pay?

  • Illustrative data.
Worth to a buyerLowest price its owner accepts
Good car10,0008,000
Poor car4,0003,000
  • Half the cars are good, half are poor. A buyer cannot tell which is which.
  • Expected value to a buyer = 0.5 × 10,000 + 0.5 × 4,000 = 7,000.
  • So buyers offer at most 7,000.
  • Good-car owners want at least 8,000. 7,000 < 8,000 → they withdraw.
  • Now only poor cars are left, so buyers will pay at most 4,000.
  • Every good car is worth more to a buyer (10,000) than to its owner (8,000), yet none is sold.

Mission 5

Government responses

  • Provide information: make it public, free and easy to use.
    • Food hygiene ratings: in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, restaurants are inspected and rated from 0 to 5.
    • Nutrition labels; health campaigns on smoking.
  • Legislation and regulation: minimum standards, compulsory disclosure, consumer rights (e.g. a refund for faulty goods), bans on misleading adverts.
  • Private fixes also exist: warranties, independent inspections, online reviews.
  • Limits: information costs money to collect; people may ignore it; government may not know better than the market (government failure).

Should government close information gaps?

  • The cost of information: inspections and labels cost money; is the gain worth it?
  • Whether people use it: information is ignored if it is complex or people are biased (smokers already see warnings).
  • Whether government knows more: regulators may lack information too (government failure).
  • Private fixes: warranties, reviews and inspections may already close much of the gap.

Mission 6

Rival, but no one can be kept out

  • Recall the two tests (see Mission 1): rival? excludable?
  • Some resources are rival but non-excludable:
    • fish in the open sea: a fish one boat catches, another cannot
    • groundwater that any landowner may pump
    • common grazing land; a forest anyone may log
    • the atmosphere's capacity to absorb pollution
  • Often called common access or common resources.
  • Say: "rival but non-excludable" — not: "a public good". A public good is non-rival.
Quick check
?Which of these is a common pool (open-access) resource?
Mission 6

The tragedy of the commons

  • Tragedy of the commons: users of a common resource, each acting in their own interest, over-use it until it is depleted.
  • Each user gets all the benefit of using more.
  • The cost (a smaller stock, lower catches for everyone, now and later) is shared among all users.
  • So each user's private gain from using more is larger than the gain to society.
  • Everyone reasons the same way. Total use exceeds the sustainable level: the stock shrinks year after year.
  • Nobody owns the stock, so nobody gains from conserving it.
  • The root cause is missing property rights: if the stock had an owner, that owner would gain from keeping it.
Mission 6

Worked example: one more tonne

  • Illustrative data. 10 boats share a fishing ground.
  • One extra tonne caught today shrinks the breeding stock, so future catches fall by 2 tonnes in total.
  • That loss is shared equally among the 10 boats.
  • The boat that catches the extra tonne:
    • gains 1 tonne
    • loses its share of the future loss: 2 ÷ 10 = 0.2 tonnes
    • net: 1 − 0.2 = +0.8 tonnes → it fishes more
  • Society: 1 − 2 = −1 tonne → the extra catch makes everyone worse off overall.
  • With 100 boats, each boat's share is only 0.02 tonnes. The more users, the weaker each one's reason to hold back.

Mission 6

Unsustainable use is a negative externality

  • Each boat's private cost (MPC): fuel, crew, nets.
  • It ignores the external cost it imposes on others: lower catches for other boats and for future fishers.
  • Market catch Q1 > socially optimal Q*: over-use.

Mission 6

Real world: the northern cod

  • Off Newfoundland, Canada, the northern cod stock supported fishing for centuries.
  • After the Second World War, factory freezer trawlers and sonar made catches far bigger.
  • Quotas existed, but stock estimates were too high, and a 1989 advice to cut quotas by half was not followed.
  • On 2 July 1992 Canada stopped the fishery: a moratorium.
  • About 40,000 fishers and related workers were affected: the largest layoff in Canadian history.
  • The stock did not quickly recover. A limited commercial fishery reopened only in 2024.
  • Lesson: over-use can destroy a resource. Government limits can also fail when information is poor.
Mission 6

Managing the commons

  • Legislation and regulation: catch quotas, closed seasons, minimum net mesh sizes, protected areas.
  • Tradable permits (tradable quotas): a cap on the total catch, split into shares that can be bought and sold.
  • Property rights: give a user, a group or the state ownership of the resource, so the owner gains from conserving it.
Mission 6Evaluate

Can over-use be stopped?

It depends on:

  • Information. How big is the stock and how fast does it regrow? The cod quotas were set on estimates that were too high.
  • Monitoring and enforcement costs. Oceans are vast; illegal catches are hard to spot.
  • Political pressure. Quotas mean lost jobs now for gains later.
  • The users. Small groups who know each other can often govern a common well; thousands of anonymous users rarely can.

Mission 7

The aim: make the polluter face the full cost

  • Governments intervene to correct market failure, and also to raise revenue, redistribute income and protect consumers.
  • A negative externality: the market produces Qm, more than the social optimum Q* (see Mission 2).
  • The fix: make decision-makers face the external cost. This is internalising the externality.
  • The target is Q*, not zero. Some pollution is worth its cost.
  • Two families of tools:
    • market-based: taxes, subsidies, tradable permits, property rights. They change prices or incentives.
    • command and control: regulation, bans, limits. They set rules.
  • Others: information, government provision, agreements between countries.
Mission 7

A tax on each unit

  • A Pigouvian tax: a tax per unit equal to the MEC at the social optimum.
  • Firms now pay MPC + tax. Supply shifts up, from MPC to MPC + tax.
  • If MEC is the same for every unit, MPC + tax = MSC.
  • Quantity falls from Qm to Q*. Price rises from Pm to P*.
  • Consumers and producers share the tax; the government gains tax revenue.
  • The welfare loss triangle disappears.
  • Say: "the tax internalises the external cost" — not: "the tax ends the pollution".
Mission 7

Worked example: setting the tax

  • Illustrative data (from Mission 2): MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q; MEC = 20 per unit.
  • No tax: 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so Qm = 40, Pm = 60. Welfare loss = 100.
  • Tax = MEC = 20. New supply: 40 + Q.
  • 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so Q = 30 = Q*.
  • Buyers pay 70 (up 10). Firms keep 70 − 20 = 50 (down 10).
  • Here the burden splits 10: 10, because the curves have equal slopes.
  • Tax revenue = 20 × 30 = 600.
  • Welfare loss = 0: the market now reaches the social optimum.

Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q; MEC = 20, so MSC = 40 + Q.

  • Takeaway: the tax must match the MEC. Too low leaves overproduction; too high causes underproduction.

Mission 7

Carbon taxes

  • A carbon tax: a tax per tonne of CO₂ emitted, charged on fuels by their carbon content.
  • On a polluting industry, e.g. coal-fired electricity: S1 → S2 (S + tax). Price rises, quantity falls.
  • Low-carbon producers become relatively cheaper. Firms and households switch.
  • Real world: British Columbia, Canada.
    • 2008: tax of C$10 per tonne CO₂e, with the revenue meant to be returned through other tax cuts.
    • 2024: C$80 per tonne.
    • A 2015 review of studies put emissions 5–15% lower than without the tax.
    • 1 April 2025: the tax on consumers was ended, as Canada dropped its national requirement for one.
  • Lesson: a carbon tax can cut emissions, but it lasts only while it keeps political support.
Mission 7

Subsidies for positive externalities

  • Some goods give external benefits (see Mission 4): MSB > MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • A subsidy per unit equal to the MEB at Q*: supply shifts down to MPC − subsidy.
  • Output rises to Q*; price to buyers falls.
  • Cost to the government = subsidy × Q*.
  • Examples: vaccinations, public transport fares, home insulation.
  • Subsidising a clean substitute (e.g. electric buses) also cuts demand for the polluting good.
Mission 7

Rules: regulation and legislation

  • Command and control: the law sets limits, enforced by inspection and fines.
    • emissions standards; limits on output or a cap at Q*
    • bans where the harm is severe: leaded petrol, CFCs
  • Real world: leaded petrol, still sold in 117 countries in 2002, was used for the last time in 2021 (UNEP).
  • Strengths: simple, certain, quick; best when the harm is dangerous at any level.
  • Limits: the same rule for every firm ignores their different costs of cutting; no reward for cutting more than the rule asks; inspection is costly.
Quick check
?A pesticide sold to farmers is found to poison rivers and wildlife even in very small amounts. What is the strongest economic case for banning it rather than taxing it?
Mission 7

Tradable permits

  • The government sets a cap: the total pollution allowed. It issues that many permits (1 permit = 1 tonne).
  • Permits are given out or auctioned. Firms may buy and sell them.
  • Firms that can cut cheaply cut more and sell spare permits. Firms that find cutting costly buy.
  • Illustrative data: firms A and B each emit 100 t. Cap = 150 permits, 75 each.
    • Cost of cutting 1 t: A 20, B 60.
    • No trade: each cuts 25 t. Cost = 25 × 20 + 25 × 60 = 2,000.
    • Trade at 40 a permit: A cuts 50 t (cost 1,000) and sells 25 permits to B (+1,000). B cuts nothing and pays 1,000.
    • Same total emissions (150 t). Total cost of cutting falls to 1,000.
  • The permit price rises as the cap tightens: a price on pollution, set by the market.
Mission 7

Real world: the EU Emissions Trading System

  • Started 2005: power stations and heavy industry in the EU.
  • The cap falls every year.
  • After the 2008 crisis, demand for permits fell. By 2013 there was a surplus of about 2 billion permits, and prices stayed low for years.
  • Fix: a Market Stability Reserve (from 2019) takes surplus permits off the market.
  • In February 2023 the price reached about €100 per tonne.
  • Lesson: permits fix the quantity; the price depends on how tight the cap is.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: firms X and Y each emit 60 t. Each is given 40 permits, so together they must cut 40 t. Cutting 1 t costs X 15 and Y 45. By how much does permit trading lower the total cost of cutting 40 t?
Mission 7

Property rights and bargaining

  • Externalities often arise because property rights are missing or unclear: no one owns the river or the air.
  • Assign the right, and the parties can bargain. This is the Coase idea.
  • Illustrative data: a factory gains 300 a year by dumping waste in a river. A fish farm downstream loses 500.
    • Fish farm owns the river: the factory would pay at most 300 to dump; the farm needs at least 500. No dumping.
    • Factory owns the river: the farm pays it between 300 and 500 to stop. No dumping.
  • Either way the efficient outcome is reached. The right decides who pays.
  • Limits: many victims (city air), high bargaining costs, harm hard to trace or measure.
Mission 7

Information, provision and agreements

  • Information and education: campaigns, labels, warnings.
    • For a demerit good, people overvalue it. Education lowers MPB towards MSB.
  • Government provision: the state supplies a good with external benefits (vaccinations, public transport), to reach Q*.
  • Road pricing: drivers pay to use busy roads at busy times. London's congestion charge began in 2003 at £5 a day; traffic entering the zone fell by about 18% in the first year.
Mission 7Evaluate

Which tool works best?

It depends on:

  • Measuring the externality. A tax needs the MEC; a cap needs the right total. Both are hard to value (see Mission 2).
  • Elasticity of demand. If demand is price inelastic, a tax cuts output little but raises much revenue.
  • Enforcement and admin costs. Monitoring emissions, collecting taxes and policing bans all cost money.
  • Consequences for stakeholders. Carbon taxes take a bigger share of poorer households' income; returning the revenue can offset this. Firms may move production abroad.
  • Certainty. A permit cap fixes the quantity, not the price; a tax fixes the price, not the quantity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Public goods are non-rival and non-excludable; private goods are rival and excludable.
  • Non-excludability creates free riders, so the market under-provides or fails to provide: a missing market.
  • Government usually provides public goods through taxation.
  • It can also contract out production to private firms.

Mission 2

  • A negative production externality makes MSC = MPC + MEC lie above MPC.
  • The market produces where MPB = MPC: more than the social optimum where MSB = MSC.
  • The welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from Q* to Qm, pointing to the social optimum.

Mission 3

  • A negative consumption externality puts MSB below MPB, so the market consumes Q1 > Q* and creates a welfare loss.
  • Demerit goods are overconsumed because of imperfect information and external costs; calling a good "demerit" is a value judgement.

Mission 4

  • A positive consumption externality puts MSB above MPB, so the market stops at Q1 < Q*, with a welfare loss.
  • Merit goods are underconsumed because of imperfect information and external benefits; calling a good "merit" is a value judgement.

Mission 5

  • Information failure: missing or uneven information leads to a misallocation of resources.
  • Asymmetric information: one side knows more. Hidden quality can drive good products out of a market.
  • Governments respond with information provision and regulation.

Mission 6

  • Common pool (open-access) resources are rival but non-excludable.
  • Each user gains all the benefit but shares the cost, so the resource is over-used: the tragedy of the commons.
  • Responses: regulation, tradable permits, property rights, collective self-governance.

Mission 7

  • Correcting an externality means making decision-makers face the external cost or benefit, to reach Q*, not zero.
  • Taxes and subsidies change the price; regulation and bans set rules.
  • Permits cap the total and let trading find the cheapest cuts.
  • With clear property rights and low bargaining costs, the parties can bargain to the efficient outcome.
