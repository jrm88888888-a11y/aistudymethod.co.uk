Mission 1

Four questions for every belief

  • AQA tests each belief with four questions:
  • Reasonable: based on reason, or at least consistent with it?
  • Meaningful: do the statements say anything, and for whom?
  • Coherent: consistent in itself and with the rest of the belief system?
  • Belief in / belief that: is faith trust in someone, or agreement that something is true?
  • Hinduism has its own philosophy: the darshanas ("viewpoints"), including Nyaya (logic), Samkhya, Yoga, Mimamsa and Vedanta.
  • Its pramanas (valid means of knowledge) include perception (pratyaksha), inference (anumana) and testimony (shabda: for most schools, including the Vedas).
  • So the dialogue is not only Hinduism v. Western philosophy: it is also an argument inside Hinduism.
Mission 1

Ultimate reality: Brahman

  • Brahman: the one ultimate reality behind and within all things.
  • Nirguna Brahman: without qualities, beyond description and understanding.
  • Saguna Brahman: with qualities; a personal God who can be loved and worshipped, e.g. as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess (Devi), and the Trimurti (Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva).
  • Avatars such as Krishna and Rama: God descending in visible form.
  • Three Vedanta schools disagree:
  • Advaita (non-dualism), Shankara (8th century CE): only nirguna Brahman is ultimately real; saguna is Brahman as seen through maya.
  • Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism), Ramanuja (traditionally 1017–1137): Brahman is personal, Vishnu, and souls and world are his "body".
  • Dvaita (dualism), Madhva (13th century): God, souls and matter are eternally distinct.
Mission 1Read the source

Beyond speech and mind

Kena Upanishad, Part First, IV–V (Paramananda translation, 1919):

> "That which speech does not illumine, but which illumines speech: know that alone to be the Brahman (the Supreme Being), not this which people worship here."

> "That which cannot be thought by mind, but by which, they say, mind is able to think: know that alone to be the Brahman, not this which people worship here."

Q1 Why can Brahman not be described?

Show answer

It is what makes speech and thought possible, so it cannot be one of their objects.

Q2 What does "not this which people worship here" suggest about saguna images?

Show answer

They are not Brahman as it ultimately is; for Advaita, they are a lower, provisional view.

Mission 1

Is talk of Brahman meaningful and coherent?

  • Meaningful? A. J. Ayer (1910–1989), Language, Truth and Logic (1936): statements that cannot be verified are meaningless. Talk of nirguna Brahman seems a clear case.
  • Hindu replies: neti neti, "not this, not this" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 2.3.6): a negative way, like the via negativa. Brahman is known by direct realisation (anubhava), not by sense-verification; meaningful for the seeker on the path.
  • Coherent? One Brahman, many gods: the Rig Veda says the one truth is called by many names (1.164.46, paraphrase). Saguna is how the one appears to devotees.
  • Coherent with evil? Karma explains suffering by past action; Max Weber called it the most consistent theodicy in history. Critics: it can blame victims, and seems to need an infinite past.
  • Reasonable? Nyaya philosophers (e.g. Udayana, 10th–11th century, Nyayakusumanjali) argued for Ishvara (God) from the world as an effect. Mimamsa and classical Samkhya denied a creator God: Hindu reasoning reached both answers.

Mission 1

The self: atman

  • Atman: the real, eternal self, distinct from body and mind.
  • Advaita: atman is Brahman: "tat tvam asi", "you are that" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7, paraphrase).
  • Samkhya (dualism): many purushas (conscious selves) are distinct from prakriti (matter, including mind).
  • Dialogue with philosophy:
  • Like Plato and Descartes: a non-physical self survives the body.
  • Buddhist challenge, ancient: anatta (no permanent self): Hindu and Buddhist philosophers debated this for centuries.
  • Hume's challenge: looking inward, he found only a "bundle" of perceptions, no self (Treatise, 1739).
  • Materialist challenge: the ancient Charvaka school and modern neuroscience treat consciousness as a product of the body.
  • Hindu reply: atman is the witness (sakshi) of perceptions, so it cannot be found as one of them.
Mission 1Read the source

The chariot

Katha Upanishad, Part Third, III–IV (Paramananda translation, 1919):

> "Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins."

> "The senses are called the horses; the sense objects are the roads; when the Atman is united with body, senses and mind, then the wise call Him the enjoyer."

Q1 Which part of the chariot is the true self?

Show answer

The lord, the passenger: atman, distinct from body, mind and intellect.

Q2 How does the parable answer Hume's "bundle" objection?

Show answer

The self is not one of the parts or perceptions, but their owner and witness, so introspection would not find it as an object.

Mission 1

Death, rebirth and evidence

  • Samsara: the cycle of rebirth; karma shapes the next life; moksha: release.
  • What is reborn? Not memories or personality, but the atman with the subtle body (sukshma sharira), which carries karmic tendencies.
  • Personal identity problem: if no memory carries over, is it the same person? (Compare Locke on memory and identity.)
  • Evidence? Ian Stevenson (1918–2007), Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966): children's reported memories of past lives.
  • Critics: Paul Edwards, Reincarnation: A Critical Examination (1996): cultural expectation, leading questions, and the population problem (more people now than ever).
  • Hindu replies: souls also come from other realms and species; and rebirth is accepted on shruti, not mainly on case studies.
  • Coherence: in bhakti traditions God's grace can free the devotee from karma (Bhagavad Gita 18:66, paraphrase); critics ask whether grace fits a strict law of karma.
Mission 1

Sources of wisdom and authority

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): the Vedas, including the Upanishads. Mimamsa: they are apaurusheya (not of human, or even divine, authorship) and eternal; Nyaya: they are authored by Ishvara.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): the Ramayana, the Bhagavad Gita, the Manusmriti; authority below shruti.
  • Gurus: living authority, e.g. Swami Sivananda (1887–1963), Divine Life Society.
  • Reason and scripture: shabda (testimony) is a pramana. Vedanta says reasoning alone has no firm foundation on matters beyond the senses (Brahma Sutra 2.1.11, paraphrase).
  • Reformers:
  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833): reason and the Upanishads; rejected image worship and sati.
  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), Arya Samaj: the Vedas alone, read rationally.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): direct experience, not books, is the final proof.
  • B. R. Ambedkar publicly burned the Manusmriti (1927) as a source of caste injustice.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Hindu beliefs about ultimate reality are coherent."

  • For: nirguna and saguna are two ways of relating to one reality (Advaita), or one personal God with infinite qualities (Ramanuja). "One truth, many names" unites many gods. Karma gives a consistent account of suffering. Hindu logicians tested these ideas from within.
  • Against: if Brahman has no qualities, how can it be one, real or the cause of the world? Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita contradict each other on God and soul. Karma needs an infinite past and can seem to blame victims. Grace and karma pull against each other.
  • Another view: coherence is judged within each school; "Hinduism" is a family of systems, not one system.
  • Judge it by: Does "beyond description" make nirguna Brahman incoherent, or only beyond concepts? Can coherence be tested across schools, or only within one? Does the four-question test fit a tradition that values realisation over argument?
Mission 1Worked answer

Linking belief, philosophy and evaluation

Question: "Hindu beliefs about the self cannot survive philosophical criticism." Evaluate this view.

  • Belief: the Katha Upanishad presents atman as the lord of the chariot, distinct from body and mind.
  • Philosophical challenge: Hume found only a bundle of perceptions; materialists say consciousness depends on the brain.
  • Hindu response: atman is the witness, not an object of introspection; Advaita appeals to realisation, not sense evidence.
  • Evaluation: the reply avoids Hume's objection, but at the price of making atman unverifiable, so the meaningfulness question returns. How far that matters depends on whether belief in a path of realisation needs belief that atman can be proven.
  • Skill tip: the 25-mark dialogue essay rewards linking Component 1 philosophy (Hume, Ayer, life after death) directly to named Hindu teaching.

Mission 2

Religious experience in Hinduism

  • Anubhava: direct experience or realisation; for Advaita the final way of knowing Brahman.
  • Samadhi: deep meditative absorption, the goal of the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (c. 2nd century BCE–4th century CE; dates disputed).
  • Darshan: "seeing" the deity in a murti (sacred image), temple or holy place, and being seen by it.
  • Bhakti ecstasy: e.g. Chaitanya (1486–1534), chanting Krishna's names.
  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): visions of the goddess Kali; he also reported experiences while following Christian and Muslim practice.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): religion is realisation, not doctrine; experience is its proof.
  • So in Hinduism experience is often the source of authority, not only evidence for it.
Mission 2Read the source

Vivekananda, quoted by William James

William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), Lecture XVI–XVII, quoting Vivekananda's Raja Yoga (1896):

> "That the mind itself has a higher state of existence, beyond reason, a superconscious state, and that when the mind gets to that higher state, then this knowledge beyond reasoning comes...."

James adds that the Vedantists' test of such states is "empirical: its fruits must be good for life."

Q1 What kind of knowledge does Vivekananda claim samadhi gives?

Show answer

Knowledge "beyond reasoning": direct, not inferred.

Q2 What test do James and the Vedantists share?

Show answer

The fruits: a changed, better life.

Mission 2

Philosophy tests the claim

  • For:
  • James: mystical states are ineffable and noetic (they feel like knowledge); they carry authority for the person who has them.
  • W. T. Stace (1886–1967), Mysticism and Philosophy (1960): a common core: the introvertive experience of undifferentiated unity, found across religions. Advaita fits this well.
  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): the principle of credulity: if it seems to someone that something is present, it probably is, unless there are special reasons to doubt it.
  • Against:
  • Steven Katz, "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism" (1978): there are no unmediated experiences; a Hindu mystic's experience is shaped by Hindu concepts, so it cannot confirm them independently.
  • Naturalistic challenges: brain-stimulation studies (e.g. Michael Persinger, 1980s); experiences may have physical causes.
  • Advaita's reply to Katz: nirvikalpa samadhi is claimed to be beyond all concepts, so it is the test case Katz must explain.

Mission 2

Miracles in Hindu tradition

  • Avatars' deeds: e.g. Krishna lifting Govardhan hill to shelter villagers from a storm (Bhagavata Purana 10.25, paraphrase).
  • Siddhis: powers said to arise from advanced yoga (Yoga Sutras book III). Patanjali warns they are obstacles to samadhi (3.37; numbering varies).
  • Modern claims: the "milk miracle" of 21 September 1995, when murtis of Ganesha were reported to drink milk worldwide; scientists offered capillary action as an explanation. Gurus such as Sathya Sai Baba (1926–2011) were said to materialise objects; sceptics alleged sleight of hand.
  • A different frame: with no sharp line between natural and supernatural, many Hindus see siddhis as natural powers of a disciplined mind. Vivekananda (Raja Yoga, 1896) said such powers follow laws not yet understood.
  • Dialogue: this treats wonders as within nature's (not yet understood) laws, so Hume's definition of a miracle as a violation of natural law fits poorly (contrast Holland, for whom a miracle may be a natural coincidence read as a sign).
Mission 2

Hume's challenge and Hindu replies

  • Hume, Enquiry (1748), Section X: testimony for miracles is never strong enough; miracle stories abound among "ignorant and barbarous nations".
  • His fourth argument: "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary": each religion's miracles cancel out the others'.
  • Hindu replies:
  • Most Hindu traditions do not rest faith on miracles; Patanjali treats powers as distractions.
  • Inclusivism: wonders in other traditions need not refute Hindu ones, because "the truth is one", so the cancelling-out argument has less force.
  • Critique of Hume: the "ignorant and barbarous" claim is widely criticised as prejudice; India had long traditions of logic and debate (Nyaya).
  • But: Hindu rationalists (e.g. the Indian rationalist movement) also test claimed miracles and often reject them.
Mission 2

Science and religious discourse

  • Compatibility claims:
  • Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975): Hinduism as a science of the spirit (paraphrase): based on experience, open to testing.
  • Some claim the Vedas revealed modern discoveries (Dayananda Saraswati argued this). Critics: e.g. Meera Nanda, Prophets Facing Backward (2003): reading science back into texts.
  • Cosmology: vast cycles of creation and dissolution (kalpas lasting billions of years) are often compared with deep time and Big Bang cosmology.
  • Evolution: some read the ten avatars (fish, tortoise, boar, man-lion…) as an evolutionary sequence; ISKCON rejects Darwinian evolution (Cremo and Thompson, Forbidden Archaeology, 1993).
  • Ian Barbour, Religion in an Age of Science (1990): four relations: conflict, independence, dialogue, integration. Hindu responses span all four.

Mission 2Diversity

The truth claims of other religions

  • "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names" (Rig Veda 1.164.46, paraphrase).
  • Vivekananda at the World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago (1893): "We believe not only in universal toleration, but we accept all religions as true."
  • Ramakrishna taught that many paths lead to the same goal, from his own practice.
  • Inclusivism, not pluralism? Indologist Paul Hacker (1913–1979) coined "inclusivism": other faiths are accepted as lower forms of Hindu (often Advaita) truth.
  • Dvaita (Madhva) held that some souls are eternally bound; ISKCON holds Krishna to be the supreme God, other gods subordinate.
  • John Hick's pluralism draws on Hindu ideas, but critics (e.g. Gavin D'Costa) argue that every pluralism privileges one view, here the Advaitic.
  • Coherence question: can contradictory claims (one God / many; personal / impersonal; one life / many) all be true?

Mission 2

Belief in and belief that

  • H. H. Price (1899–1984), "Belief 'In' and Belief 'That'" (1965): belief in is trust and commitment; belief that is accepting a proposition.
  • Bhakti marga: chiefly belief in: loving surrender to Krishna, Rama or Devi.
  • Jnana marga: knowledge, but realisation, not assent to propositions; neither simply "belief that" nor "belief in".
  • Karma marga: right action without attachment; belief shown in practice.
  • Relevance of philosophy: Hindu darshanas treat reasoning as part of the spiritual path, but Advaita holds that reason alone cannot reach Brahman; a devotee may find philosophy irrelevant to bhakti.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Religious experience gives Hindus good reason to believe in Brahman."

  • For: experience is the claimed source of the Upanishads; Swinburne's credulity applies; Stace's common core fits Advaita; lives are changed (James' "fruits"); experience is a pramana in Vedanta.
  • Against: Katz: experiences are shaped by prior concepts; naturalistic explanations; Hindu experiences conflict (personal Krishna in bhakti, impersonal Brahman in Advaita); experience is private and unverifiable.
  • Another view: for many Hindus experience is not evidence for a belief but the goal itself; the demand for proof misreads the path.
  • Judge it by: Does the variety of Hindu experiences count for or against them? Can a concept-free experience exist? Is "good reason" the same for the experiencer and for an observer?

Mission 3

Key Hindu moral principles

  • Dharma: duty, right order, the way things should be.
  • Sanatana dharma (universal dharma): duties for everyone, e.g. non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, self-control.
  • Varnashrama dharma: duties of one's class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama). One's own duty is svadharma.
  • Karma: every intentional act has results that return to the agent, in this life or the next.
  • Ahimsa (non-harming), satya (truthfulness) and asteya (not stealing): three of the five yamas (restraints), Yoga Sutras 2.30.
  • Four aims of life: dharma, artha (wealth), kama (pleasure), moksha (liberation from rebirth).
  • No single Hindu authority: Vedanta schools, bhakti traditions, reformers and law books weigh these differently.
Mission 3Read the source

Action without craving its fruit

Krishna to Arjuna, Bhagavad Gita (Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, 1885):

> "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Gita 2:47)

> "There is 'right' Action: that which being enjoined— / Is wrought without attachment, passionlessly, / For duty, not for love, nor hate, nor gain." (Gita 18:23)

  • This is nishkama karma: desireless action, the heart of karma yoga.

Q1 According to 18:23, what makes an action "right"?

Show answer

It is enjoined, i.e. it is one's duty, and it is done without attachment, love, hate or wish for gain.

Q2 Which ethical theory from Component 1 seems closest, and why?

Show answer

Kant: the act is done because it is one's duty, not for its results or out of inclination.

Mission 3

Hindu responses to Kant

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785): only a good will acting from duty has moral worth; "Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law" (Abbott translation).
  • Common ground:
  • Nishkama karma ≈ acting from duty, not from inclination or for reward.
  • Sanatana dharma's common duties look universal: Manusmriti 10.63 gives non-injury, truthfulness, not stealing, purity and self-control as duties for all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • Tensions:
  • Source: for Kant, duty comes from autonomous reason; for most Hindus, dharma is known through shruti, smriti, the example of the good, and gurus.
  • Universality: svadharma differs by varna and stage of life. "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently" (Gita 18:47, Arnold).
  • Motive: the Gita asks for action offered to God and leading to moksha; Kant would call any further end heteronomous.

Mission 3

Hindu responses to Bentham

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789): right acts produce the greatest pleasure over pain for the greatest number; the hedonic calculus measures it.
  • Common ground:
  • Karma ties every act to its results: Hindus are taught to think ahead about consequences.
  • Kama (pleasure) and artha (wealth) are legitimate aims of life.
  • The Gita commends action for lokasangraha, the welfare of the world (Gita 3:20, 3:25).
  • Tensions:
  • Karma is not a calculation of pleasure: it follows intention and attachment, and works across lifetimes.
  • Pleasure and wealth are pursued within dharma; the highest aim, moksha, goes beyond pleasure and pain.
  • Ahimsa limits sacrifice of the few: M. K. Gandhi (1869–1948) wrote that a follower of ahimsa cannot accept "the greatest good of the greatest number" and must seek the good of all (paraphrase; Young India, 1926). His word: sarvodaya, welfare of all.
Mission 3

Hindu responses to virtue ethics

  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics: virtues are habits of character, learned by practice, aiming at the mean; the goal is eudaimonia (flourishing); phronesis (practical wisdom) guides choice.
  • Common ground:
  • The Gita lists divine qualities: "Fearlessness, singleness of soul, … / Uprightness, heed to injure nought which lives, / Truthfulness, slowness unto wrath" (Gita 16:1–2, Arnold).
  • Its ideal is the sthitaprajna, the person of steady wisdom (Gita 2:55–72), who acts calmly in success and failure.
  • Character grows by practice: yoga, discipline, the company of the good, a guru's example.
  • Tensions:
  • The goal is moksha, release from rebirth, not flourishing in this life.
  • Equanimity is the ideal, not anger "at the right time".
  • In bhakti traditions, God's grace transforms character, not habit alone.
  • The three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas: the qualities of nature) shape character as well as choice (Gita 14).

Mission 3

Test case: lying

  • Satya is a yama for all. Manusmriti 4.138 (paraphrase): speak what is true and pleasant; do not speak a truth that wounds, nor a pleasant falsehood.
  • Kaushika (Mahabharata, Karna Parva): an ascetic vowed always to tell the truth. He told robbers where travellers were hiding; they were killed. Krishna says he fell into hell: truth that causes great harm is not dharma.
  • Yudhishthira (Drona Parva): he says "Ashvatthama is dead" (meaning an elephant) to stop his teacher Drona fighting. His chariot, which never touched the earth, sinks to the ground.
  • Compare Kant, On a Supposed Right to Lie (1797): never lie, even to a murderer at the door.
  • The tradition both condemns lying and sees cases where rigid truth-telling fails: closer to judgement in context than to an exceptionless rule.
Mission 3

Test case: theft

  • Asteya (not stealing): a yama and a duty for all varnas.
  • Vishvamitra (Mahabharata, Shanti Parva): in a twelve-year famine, the sage steals dog meat from an outcaste's hut to stay alive. He argues that life must be preserved so that dharma can be kept.
  • This is apad-dharma: dharma in times of distress, when normal rules bend.
  • Analysed three ways:
  • Deontological reading: the rule stands; emergencies are an exception the tradition itself lays down.
  • Consequential reading: survival outweighs the harm.
  • Character reading: a wise person knows when the rule's purpose is better served by bending it.
  • Compare: stealing bread to survive.
Mission 3Diversity

Other perspectives that changed Hindu ethics

  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833): used reason and humane values, alongside scripture, against sati (widow-burning); banned in Bengal in 1829.
  • Gandhi: drew on the Gita, Tolstoy, Ruskin and the Sermon on the Mount; re-read the Gita's battle as an inner struggle and made ahimsa central.
  • B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), Annihilation of Caste (1936): duty assigned by birth denies equal human dignity; he publicly burned the Manusmriti in 1927.
  • Like Kant's humanity formula, Ambedkar's critique asks that every person be treated as an end, never as a means only.
  • Many Hindus reply: varna should rest on qualities, not birth (Gita 4:13, read by reformers); sanatana dharma, not caste duty, is the core.
  • Implications: if a text such as Manusmriti is criticised, is the authority of smriti weakened, or only one reading of it?
Mission 3Evaluate

How far is Hindu ethics deontological?

  • Claim: Hindu ethics is basically deontological.
  • For: dharma is duty; nishkama karma rejects acting for results; the Gita stresses doing one's own duty.
  • For: rules such as the yamas and Manusmriti's duties are binding on all.
  • Against: karma makes consequences central, across lifetimes; lokasangraha aims at the good of the world.
  • Against: the Gita's divine qualities and the sthitaprajna point to character; apad-dharma and the Kaushika story allow judgement in context.
  • Against: dharma comes from scripture, role and God, not autonomous reason: "deontological" in a non-Kantian sense.
  • Judgement criteria: Which texts count most (Gita, Manusmriti, epics)? Is the goal (moksha) part of the ethics? Can a Western label fit a tradition with no single founder?

Building a synoptic paragraph

  • Source: Gita 2:47: right deeds as motive, "not the fruit".

Make a guess: If every living being has a soul, how do Hindus decide about abortion, meat, gene editing and marriage?

Mission 4

Three starting points

  • Hindus usually start from three teachings:
  • Atman in all beings: every living thing, human or animal, carries an eternal self on its journey through samsara.
  • Ahimsa: avoid harm to any being in thought, word and deed. The more harm, the heavier the karma.
  • Karma: Hindus hold that suffering and life-span are tied to past actions; interfering may affect both the sufferer's karma and the actor's.
  • Alongside these: daya (compassion), the duty to relieve suffering, and dharma of one's role (e.g. a doctor's or a ruler's).
  • These can pull in different directions. Hindus weigh them differently: there is no single ruling body.
Mission 4

The embryo and abortion

  • Many Hindus hold that the atman enters at conception: the embryo is a person on its journey.
  • Dharma texts list killing an embryo (bhruna-hatya) among the gravest wrongs.
  • Most Hindu teachers oppose abortion; many accept it to save the mother's life (ahimsa weighed against ahimsa).
  • Sex-selective abortion: condemned by Hindu leaders and reformers; banned in India (Pre-Conception and Pre-Natal Diagnostic Techniques Act, 1994).
  • Practice and law differ from teaching: abortion is legal in India (Medical Termination of Pregnancy Act, 1971) and widely used.
  • Compare natural law: also protects the embryo, by the primary precept of preserving life, not by karma.
Mission 4

Ending life: euthanasia and capital punishment

  • Euthanasia, most common view: ending life early cuts across karma; the atman must still work through it; the one who kills takes on karma; doctors' dharma is to heal.
  • Another view: releasing someone from unbearable pain is an act of daya, and good karma.
  • Prayopavesa: fasting to death when one's duties are complete and the body is failing; accepted in some traditions as a calm, non-violent end (e.g. Vinoba Bhave, 1982). Supporters distinguish it from suicide.
  • India's Supreme Court allowed passive euthanasia under safeguards (Aruna Shanbaug case, 2011) and recognised living wills (Common Cause, 2018). Active euthanasia remains illegal.
  • Capital punishment: Manusmriti gives the king danda (the rod of punishment) to protect dharma; Gandhi rejected it as contrary to ahimsa. India keeps it for the "rarest of rare" cases (Bachan Singh, 1980).

Mission 4Read the source

One self in every being

Bhagavad Gita (Edwin Arnold, The Song Celestial, 1885):

> "To him who wisely sees, / The Brahman with his scrolls and sanctities, / The cow, the elephant, the unclean dog, / … are all one." (Gita 5:18)

  • Here "Brahman" means a priest of the brahmin class.
  • The Ramayana opens with the sage Valmiki cursing a hunter who shot one of a pair of mating cranes; his grief became the first verse (Bala Kanda; paraphrase).

Q1 What does the wise person see in a priest, a cow and a dog alike?

Show answer

The same self or divine reality in each: they are "all one".

Q2 How might a Hindu use this verse against intensive farming?

Show answer

If the same atman is in a cow as in a human, treating animals as production units ignores what they are.

Mission 4

Animals as food and farming

  • Vegetarianism is widely practised, especially among Vaishnavas and many brahmin families: meat cannot be had without killing (Manusmriti 5.48, paraphrase).
  • Not universal: many Hindus eat meat, though most avoid beef. Some Shakta temples still offer animal sacrifice to the Goddess (e.g. Kamakhya, Assam).
  • The cow is honoured as a giver of life (milk, ghee); cow slaughter is banned in many Indian states.
  • Intensive farming: ahimsa and karma condemn needless suffering; some Hindus now question even dairy, when calves and old cows are killed.
  • Response: cow protection farms, e.g. ISKCON's Bhaktivedanta Manor (Hertfordshire) keeps cows for life ("ahimsa milk").
Mission 4

Animals in science, organs and blood sports

  • Scientific procedures: ahimsa opposes causing pain; Gandhi opposed vivisection. Some Hindus accept animal testing to save human lives, as the lesser harm.
  • Animal organs: a pig heart valve or organ may trouble some Hindus over ahimsa and purity; others accept it to preserve life. No single ruling.
  • Cloning animals: concern about suffering and failed attempts.
  • Blood sports: hunting for pleasure is hard to reconcile with ahimsa. In the epics, kings hunt (King Dasharatha's hunt kills a young ascetic by mistake, named Shravana in later retellings, and brings a curse). The stories show its danger rather than ban it outright.
  • Compare utilitarianism: may count animal pain (Bentham: "can they suffer?") but may also justify harm for greater benefit; ahimsa resists that trade.

Mission 4

Genetic engineering

  • Embryo research: if the atman is present at conception, destroying embryos is himsa (harm). Others give weight to daya: research may relieve great suffering.
  • Cloning: some Hindus see no conflict with scripture; the epics tell of unusual births (e.g. the hundred Kauravas, grown in pots). Others fear harm to the clone and pride in "playing creator".
  • "Designer" babies: choosing traits (especially sex) worries many Hindus, given India's history of sex-selection.
  • Therapy vs enhancement: treating disease fits the duty to heal; enhancement for status raises concerns of greed and ahimsa.
  • GM crops: debated in India; activists such as Vandana Shiva (b. 1952) argue against GM seed on ecological and social grounds.
  • Some Hindus, following Radhakrishnan's view that Hinduism is scientific, welcome research as knowledge.
Mission 4

Marriage

  • Vivaha (marriage) is a samskara, one of the life-cycle rites; it begins the grihastha (householder) stage.
  • Marriage serves dharma (duty, children, family worship), artha (a household) and kama (love and pleasure) together.
  • Saptapadi: seven steps around the sacred fire, each a promise.
  • Many marriages are arranged by families, with the couple's consent; love marriages are now common.
  • The Hindu Marriage Act 1955 (India) made marriage monogamous for Hindus and allowed divorce. Traditionally, marriage was a lifelong bond.
  • Compare virtue ethics: marriage as a school of character (patience, loyalty); Kant: each spouse treated as an end, not a means.
Mission 4Diversity

Same-sex relationships and transgender matters

  • Traditional view: marriage in the grihastha stage exists for children and family dharma; Manusmriti prescribes penances for same-sex acts (e.g. a ritual bath for men, 11.175).
  • Other readings: the Kama Sutra speaks of a "third nature" (tritiya prakriti); the Mahabharata's Shikhandi changes sex; Arjuna lives a year as Brihannala; Shiva as Ardhanarishvara is half-male, half-female.
  • Hijras: a recognised third-gender community for centuries, with roles at weddings and births.
  • Indian courts: third gender recognised (NALSA, 2014); consensual same-sex acts decriminalised (Navtej Singh Johar, 2018); same-sex marriage left to Parliament (2023).
  • Hindu views today range from opposition to full acceptance; some scholars (e.g. Ruth Vanita) trace a long tolerance of same-sex love.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can ahimsa settle modern issues?

  • Claim: Ahimsa gives Hindus a clear answer to modern life-and-death issues.
  • For: one principle covers humans and animals alike; it is more consistent than theories that ignore animals.
  • For: it explains why many Hindus oppose abortion, euthanasia and cruel farming.
  • Against: ahimsa conflicts with itself (mother vs embryo; animals vs patients) and needs a way to weigh harms.
  • Against: practice often differs from teaching (abortion, meat-eating, sex-selection), so "Hindu ethics" is hard to state.
  • Other perspectives: utilitarianism supplies a weighing method; virtue ethics asks who acts well; feminist and LGBT critics challenge traditional readings.
  • Judgement criteria: Is internal diversity a strength (flexibility) or a weakness (no guidance)? Which sources count: Gita, Manusmriti, gurus, law?

Mission 5

Wealth: a proper aim, with limits

  • Artha (wealth, success) is one of the four aims of life; householders (grihastha) are expected to earn, support family and give.
  • At Diwali many Hindus worship Lakshmi, goddess of prosperity.
  • Limits: artha must be pursued within dharma; greed binds the soul to samsara.
  • Dana (giving) is a duty; aparigraha (non-possessiveness) is one of the yamas.
  • Gandhi's trusteeship: the rich hold wealth as trustees for society, keeping only what they need.
  • Other perspectives: Peter Singer (b. 1946), "Famine, Affluence and Morality" (1972), argues from consequences that the affluent must give far more. Hindu ethics agrees on giving, but grounds it in dharma and detachment, not a calculation.
  • Kant would class helping others as an imperfect duty: binding, but leaving freedom over how and when. Hindu dana is also a duty, chiefly of the householder.

> "All this, whatsoever exists in the universe, should be covered by the Lord. Having renounced (the unreal), enjoy (the Real). Do not covet the wealth of any man."

Show answer

If all belongs to the Lord, the owner is a steward who should use wealth for others; Gandhi often cited this verse.

Mission 5

Tolerance: one truth, many names

  • Rig Veda 1.164.46: the one reality is called by many names by the wise (paraphrase).
  • "Whoso worship me, / Them I exalt; but all men everywhere / Shall fall into my path" (Gita 4:11, Arnold).
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), Chicago Parliament of Religions, 1893: different religions are like streams that reach the same sea; he asked not only for tolerance but acceptance of all religions as true.
  • Neo-Vedanta view: all faiths are partial expressions of one truth.
  • Compatibility: this fits liberal ideals of toleration and Hick's pluralism.
Mission 5Diversity

Questions about Hindu tolerance

  • Inclusivism, not equality? Saying "all paths lead to my truth" may rank other faiths below Vedanta. Some Christians and Muslims reject being absorbed this way.
  • Diversity within: Vaishnava, Shaiva and Shakta traditions each hold their deity supreme; ISKCON teaches Krishna as the Supreme Person.
  • Caste: critics such as Ambedkar argued that tolerance of beliefs sat beside intolerance of persons, through untouchability.
  • Hindutva: V. D. Savarkar, Essentials of Hindutva (1923), defined Hindu identity by land, culture and descent; supporters see it as national self-respect; critics see it as excluding Muslims and Christians.
  • Communal conflict, e.g. the demolition of the Babri Masjid (1992), is condemned by many Hindus as against ahimsa and tolerance; defended by others as reclaiming a sacred site.
Mission 5

Freedom of religious expression

  • India: the Constitution (Article 25) protects freedom "to profess, practise and propagate" religion, subject to public order, morality and health.
  • Conversion: several states have anti-conversion laws against conversion by force, fraud or inducement. Supporters see protection of the poor; critics see a limit on freedom.
  • Offence: some Hindus campaign against art or books they see as insulting deities (e.g. protests against paintings by M. F. Husain; Penguin India's withdrawal of Wendy Doniger's The Hindus in 2014). Others defend free expression.
  • Britain: Hindus share the protection of Article 9, European Convention on Human Rights (Human Rights Act 1998).
  • Other perspective: J. S. Mill, On Liberty (1859): only harm to others justifies limiting freedom. Does offence count as harm?

Mission 5

Karma and free will

  • In Hindu teaching, karma is not fate: it is the law that intentional acts bring results.
  • Three kinds: sanchita (stored from past lives), prarabdha (already bearing fruit: one's birth, body, circumstances), kriyamana or agami (being made now, by present choices).
  • Prarabdha sets the scene; present choices remain the agent's own and shape the future.
  • Moral responsibility rests on intention and attachment: the same outward act can bind or free, depending on motive (nishkama karma).
  • In many Vedanta schools, Ishvara (God) dispenses the fruits of karma justly; in others karma works impersonally.
Mission 5Read the source

Is Arjuna free?

Bhagavad Gita (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "There lives a Master in the hearts of men / Maketh their deeds, by subtle pulling-strings, / Dance to what tune HE will." (Gita 18:61)

> "Meditate! / And—as thou wilt—then act!" (Gita 18:63)

  • Earlier Krishna warns that Arjuna's own nature would "spur thee to the war" even if he refused (18:59–60).

Q1 Which verse suggests determinism, and which suggests free choice?

Show answer

18:61 suggests God moves all deeds; 18:63 leaves the decision to Arjuna.

Q2 How might a Hindu hold both together?

Show answer

As compatibilists: God and nature set the conditions, yet the choice flows from Arjuna's own understanding and will.

Mission 5

Three Western positions in Hindu terms

  • Hard determinism: if gunas, prarabdha and God's will fix every act, no one is free; but then karma, praise and blame lose their point.
  • Libertarianism: present choice is genuinely open; many modern Hindu teachers (e.g. Vivekananda) stress self-effort over fate.
  • Compatibilism: choices are shaped by nature and past karma, yet are the agent's own because they flow from the agent's desires and understanding.
  • Reward and punishment: karma acts like an impersonal moral law; future births reward and correct. Critics (e.g. Ambedkar) say this can blame the poor and low-caste for their condition.
  • Implications for authority: Ambedkar went further, publicly burning the Manusmriti (1927) and rejecting its authority. Many Hindu reformers instead argue that caste rules in smriti do not bind where they conflict with shruti or the conduct of the good: the authority of such sources is limited, not lost.
  • Advaita adds a twist: the true self (atman) is not the doer at all; agency belongs to the body-mind.
Mission 5

Conscience in Hindu decision-making

  • Manusmriti 2.6 and 2.12 (paraphrase): the sources of dharma are the Veda, smriti, the conduct of the good, and what satisfies one's own self (atmatushti).
  • So inner conviction has a place, but last, after scripture and good example.
  • Buddhi (the discerning intellect) in a sattvic state "comprehends … / What must be done, and what must not be done" (Gita 18:30, Arnold); a darkened intellect "Looks upon wrong as right" (18:32).
  • Gandhi acted on his "inner voice", e.g. in his 21-day fast of 1933.
  • Compare: Aquinas (conscience as reason applying principles; can err), Butler (conscience as God-given, with supreme authority), Freud (the superego, formed in childhood).
  • Like Aquinas, the Gita expects conscience to be trained: purity of mind is needed before the inner voice can be trusted.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is karma compatible with real moral responsibility?

  • Claim: Belief in karma undermines moral responsibility.
  • For: if present circumstances and character come from past lives one cannot remember, it seems unfair to be held responsible.
  • For: Gita 18:61 and the gunas suggest God and nature drive action.
  • Against: karma presupposes responsibility: only free, intentional acts generate karma.
  • Against: prarabdha limits circumstances, not present choice; Gita 18:63 leaves the choice to Arjuna.
  • Other perspectives: compatibilists (soft determinists) argue that a caused act is still free if it flows from the agent's own wants; Locke's locked room shows that acting willingly and being free to do otherwise can come apart.
  • Judgement criteria: Does fairness need memory? Is God's control coherent with human choice? Does the belief in practice encourage effort or resignation?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Brahman is understood as nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (personal God); Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita relate these differently.
  • Atman and rebirth meet challenges from Buddhism, Hume, materialism and critics of reincarnation evidence; Hindu thinkers reply from within their own philosophy.
  • Authority rests on shruti, smriti, gurus and experience; reformers and critics have weighed reason against scripture.

Mission 2

  • Religious experience (anubhava, samadhi, darshan, bhakti) is central in Hinduism; James, Stace and Swinburne support it, Katz and naturalism challenge it.
  • Hindu miracle claims (avatars' deeds, siddhis, the 1995 milk event); many Hindus read siddhis as natural powers rather than violations; Hume's challenges meet inclusivist replies.
  • Hindu responses to science span Barbour's four models; Hindu views of other religions range from pluralist-sounding inclusivism (Vivekananda) to exclusivist strands (Madhva, ISKCON).

Mission 3

  • Key principles: dharma (sanatana and varnashrama), karma, ahimsa, satya, asteya, the four aims.
  • Kant: nishkama karma parallels acting from duty; but dharma comes from scripture and role, not autonomous reason. Bentham: karma and lokasangraha value results, but ahimsa and moksha resist the calculus. Virtue: Gita 16 and the sthitaprajna, aimed at moksha.
  • Lying (Kaushika, Yudhishthira) and theft (Vishvamitra, apad-dharma) show judgement in context; Gandhi, Roy and Ambedkar show other perspectives reshaping Hindu ethics.

Mission 4

  • Starting points: atman in all beings, ahimsa, karma, daya, role duty.
  • Many Hindus oppose abortion and euthanasia (karma, ahimsa), with exceptions and wide differences in practice; vegetarianism is common but not universal; genetic engineering is judged by ahimsa against compassion.
  • Marriage is a samskara serving dharma, artha and kama; views on same-sex relationships and gender range widely, drawing on texts, hijra tradition and Indian law.

Mission 5

  • Wealth (artha) is a proper aim within dharma, with dana, aparigraha and Gandhi's trusteeship (Isha 1); tolerance rests on "one truth, many names" (Rig Veda 1.164.46; Gita 4:11; Vivekananda), but critics question inclusivism, caste and Hindutva; freedom of expression is debated (conversion, offence).
  • Hindus teach that karma is not fate: prarabdha sets circumstances; present (kriyamana) choices are free; Gita 18:61 and 18:63 pull towards determinism and freedom.
  • Conscience (atmatushti; sattvic buddhi; Gandhi's inner voice) is a real but subordinate source, needing a purified mind.
