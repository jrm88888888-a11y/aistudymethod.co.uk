Prose Theme: Crime and Detection

Component 2 is the Prose paper: one comparative essay on two thematically linked texts, at least one written pre-1900. This lesson teaches the theme of Crime and Detection as conventions and critical frames, plus the comparative method — for any pairing your centre studies.

  • One comparative essay; AO4 (connections) is heavily weighted.
  • At least one text is pre-1900 — the birth of detection is not the modern crime novel.
  • The theme is the lens: the detective and method, the crime and the criminal, order and justice.

Named works are illustrative only, and no quotations are invented. Press Start.

AO4 · the comparative method

How to compare two prose texts

AO4 wants the texts to argue with each other, not run in parallel.

  1. Lead with a thematic proposition: e.g. both texts use the solving of a crime to reassure a society that guilt can be located in one person and expelled.
  2. Interleave in theme-paragraphs (the detective; the criminal; justice) drawing on both texts.
  3. Prize the productive difference — where one restores order and the other leaves it broken.
  4. Respect the pre-1900 text’s justice system and its ideas of guilt and punishment.

Stem: “Where the golden-age puzzle expels the criminal to cleanse society, the later text implicates society itself in the crime.”

Theme · the detective and method

The figure and the puzzle

The detective story has a distinctive machinery:

  • The detective — often brilliant, eccentric and outside official authority; ratiocination (reasoning) as spectacle.
  • The puzzle — clues, deduction and the climactic reveal; the reader invited to solve alongside the sleuth.
  • Fair play — the “golden age” convention (codified in rules by Knox and Van Dine) that the reader must have access to every clue; red herrings mislead but never cheat.
  • The sidekick and the confession — the less-clever narrator, the final gathering and explanation.

Tzvetan Todorov argued that detective fiction always contains two stories: the story of the crime (which is over) and the story of the investigation that reconstructs it.

Theme · the crime and the criminal

Transgression and its shadow

The second strand is the crime itself:

  • Transgression — the violation that disrupts the ordered world and sets the plot going.
  • The criminal — motive and psychology; the sympathetic criminal who blurs the moral line; the criminal as the detective’s double.
  • The victim and the community — the closed circle (country house, village) whose secrets the crime exposes.
  • Sensation and the Newgate tradition — earlier fiction’s fascination with the criminal before the detective took centre stage.

Comparative angle: ask whether each text is finally more interested in catching the criminal or in understanding the conditions that produced the crime.

Theme · order, justice and restoration

What solving the crime does

Detective fiction is deeply concerned with order:

  • Restoration — the classic form ends by re-sealing the disrupted community, guilt safely located in one outsider.
  • Law versus justice — the gap between what is legal and what is right; the detective who lets a “guilty” party go.
  • Ideology — whose order is being restored, and at whose expense?

W.H. Auden, in his 1948 essay The Guilty Vicarage, described the golden-age puzzle as a fantasy in which guilt is discovered in a single individual and expelled, restoring the innocence of the community — a quasi-religious pattern of sin and absolution.

AO3 · context

The rise of detection

Context tracks how crime and its policing change across your texts’ periods:

  • Nineteenth century — the new Metropolitan Police and detective branch, sensation fiction, anxieties about the city and the crowd.
  • Golden age — the interwar puzzle novel and its reassuring, orderly closure.
  • Hard-boiled and after — American crime fiction relocating the story to a corrupt society where order is never really restored.

Weld it: because the golden-age text emerges from a society craving order after war, its neat solution consoles; a later text written amid distrust of institutions can no longer offer that comfort — AO3 driving AO4.

AO5 · interpretation

Reading crime through theory

Bring a lens that yields a textual claim:

  • Marxist — crime as a symptom of class and property; who is criminalised and who is protected?
  • Feminist — the female victim as spectacle; the woman detective disrupting a masculine genre.
  • Structuralist — Todorov’s two-story model and the genre’s rules as a system of expectations to fulfil or break.

Discipline: not “a Marxist would say crime is about class”, but “the novel makes the servant the first suspect and the master the last, so its very order of suspicion encodes a class hierarchy of guilt.”
