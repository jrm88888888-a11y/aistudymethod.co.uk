Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can't a government get fast growth, plenty of jobs and steady prices all at once?

Mission 1

The big four objectives

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks likeWhy it matters
Economic growthSteady, sustainable rise in real GDPHigher living standards; more tax revenue
Low unemploymentFew people who want work are without itLost output; lost income; poverty and poor health
Price stabilityLow and stable inflationUncertainty and lost spending power when inflation is high or erratic

  • UK: the Bank of England's inflation target is 2% (CPI), set by the government.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 1

More objectives

  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one economy this year: real GDP growth 2.1%, unemployment 4.0%, inflation 6.5% (target 2%), current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is furthest from being met?
Mission 1

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve.
  • Some economists stress demand (Keynesian); others say low unemployment needs supply-side reform, not more demand (free-market view).
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.

Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 1

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
  • Watch for unintended consequences: e.g. higher interest rates to cut inflation can also hit investment and future growth.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 1Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.
Mission check

?An economy is already close to full capacity. The government cuts income tax to give growth a boost. Which outcome is most likely in the short run?

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Mission 2

Mission 2: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Make a guess: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Mission 2

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 2

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
Mission 2

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a negative output gap: real output and employment rise; the price level rises a little.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close a positive output gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes.
Mission 2Graph lab

Close the gap

Illustrative data. Real output is 460; full-employment output is 500. The gap is 40.

  • Takeaway: because of the multiplier, a rise in spending smaller than the gap can close it, if prices stay steady.
Quick check
?An economy has output above full capacity and inflation well above target. Which policy is contractionary fiscal policy?
Mission 2

Two views: Keynesian vs monetarist and new classical

  • Keynesian view: AS is flat when there is lots of spare capacity.
    • In a deep recession, expansionary fiscal policy raises real output and jobs with little inflation.
    • Near full capacity, it mainly raises the price level.
  • Monetarist / new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output.
    • In the short run, more G raises output and the price level.
    • In the long run, wages and costs adjust: SRAS shifts left. Output returns to potential, at a higher price level.
    • Government borrowing may push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • So the two views disagree on how far fiscal policy can raise output for long.
Mission 2

Fiscal policy on the supply side

  • Some fiscal measures also shift LRAS right:
    • capital spending on infrastructure, broadband, transport
    • spending on education, training and health
    • tax incentives for investment or research
  • These raise productive capacity: more output with less pressure on prices.
  • But they take years, and the first effect is on AD (spending now).
Mission 2

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilisers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
  • Political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; tax rises and spending cuts are not.
  • Sustainable debt: high debt raises interest costs and may limit room for future stimulus.
Mission 2

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • UK 2008–09: VAT was cut from 17.5% to 15% from December 2008 to December 2009, to support spending during the recession. Its effect is debated: some spending was brought forward rather than added.
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary.
Mission 2

Fiscal and monetary policy together

  • Fiscal instruments: government spending and taxes, set by the government (HM Treasury).
  • Monetary instruments: Bank Rate, set by the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) to hit the 2% inflation target; and quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds with new central bank money to lower longer-term interest rates.
  • Both work on AD. Both have time lags: an interest rate change can take up to about two years to have its full effect on inflation.
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?
Mission 2Evaluate

How effective is fiscal policy?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, extra demand raises output; near capacity it mostly raises prices.
  • Size of the multiplier: high saving or imports weaken the effect.
  • Time lags: stimulus can arrive too late.
  • Confidence and debt: if people expect future tax rises to repay debt, they may save more. If markets doubt debt is sustainable, interest rates may rise.
  • Crowding out: borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment (stronger near full capacity).
Mission check

?A government spots a recession and plans a large spending programme. It takes 18 months to pass and start, and by then the economy is already recovering strongly. What is the main risk?

Fiscal policy can lift a slump, but its effect depends on timing and on how much spare capacity is left.

Mission 3

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 3

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 3

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.
Mission 3

  • Market-based: free markets and incentives work better with fewer barriers.
  • Interventionist: markets under-provide some things (positive externalities, public goods), so the government steps in.
Mission 3

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 3

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 3

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.

Mission 3

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 3Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why can the right cure for one slump be the wrong cure for the next?

Make a guess: When an economy slumps, what should a government do first?

Mission 4

The toolkit and the policy mix

  • Three sets of tools:
    • Fiscal policy: government spending and taxes.
    • Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply, QE.
    • Supply-side policies: raise productive capacity (education, incentives, competition).
  • Policy mix: how these are combined at one time.
  • Fiscal and monetary policy can push the same way (both expansionary) or opposite ways (one expansionary, one contractionary).
  • Policies that shift AD are demand-side; policies that shift LRAS are supply-side.
Mission 4

Combining fiscal and monetary policy

  • Step 1: start with real output below full employment.
  • This is a negative output gap.
  • Step 2: both expansionary: AD shifts right for two reasons. Real output and the price level both rise.
  • Step 3: the interest rate:
    • Fiscal expansion raises incomes and spending, so demand for money rises: rates are pushed up.
    • Monetary expansion raises the money supply: rates are pushed down.
    • Overall change: indeterminate. It depends on which effect is bigger.
  • Opposite directions flip this: the interest-rate change is certain, the AD change is not.
    • Expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary: interest rate up; AD uncertain.
    • Contractionary fiscal + expansionary monetary: interest rate down; AD uncertain.
  • Say: "the change in the interest rate is indeterminate" — not: "it stays the same".

  • Takeaway: two pushes the same way make the AD change certain; two pushes in opposite ways make the interest-rate change certain.
Quick check
?A government cuts its spending to reduce a budget deficit. At the same time, the central bank buys government bonds to increase the money supply. Which outcome is certain?
Mission 4

Demand shocks and supply shocks

  • An external shock starts outside the economy and outside the government's control.
  • Demand shock: e.g. a world recession cuts demand for exports.
    • AD shifts left. Real output and the price level fall.
    • Expansionary policy can push both back up: no conflict.
  • Supply shock: e.g. world energy prices jump.
    • SRAS shifts left. Real output falls and the price level rises: stagflation.
    • Raising AD helps jobs but adds to inflation. Cutting AD slows inflation but deepens the slump: a policy dilemma.
  • First step in any crisis: diagnose the shock.
  • Say: "a cost-push supply shock" — not: "inflation caused by too much spending".
Mission 4

Conflicts and trade-offs

  • Hitting one objective can make another harder:
    • Inflation vs unemployment: in the short run, along the SRPC, lower inflation may mean higher unemployment.
    • Growth vs the current account: faster growth raises imports.
    • Growth vs the environment: more output can mean more pollution and resource use.
    • Growth vs equality: gains may go mostly to some groups.
  • Unintended consequences: e.g. very low interest rates for years can push up house and share prices.
  • Macroeconomic perspectives differ:
    • Keynesian: markets can stay in a slump for a long time; demand-side policy helps.
    • Classical / monetarist: markets self-correct; policy should keep inflation low and boost supply.
  • Say: "a trade-off" when one objective is given up for another — not: "the policy failed".
Quick check
?A country that imports most of its energy is hit by a sharp rise in world energy prices. Unemployment and inflation both rise. Which statement is most accurate?
Mission 4

The Great Depression

  • USA, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Thousands of banks failed. The money supply fell by about a third.
    • The Federal Reserve did little to stop it.
    • The Smoot–Hawley tariffs (1930) raised taxes on imports; other countries hit back.
  • Recovery began after 1933: the US left the gold standard; the New Deal brought public works and bank reform.
  • UK: governments aimed to balance the budget and cut spending in 1931.
    • The UK left the gold standard in 1931. Bank Rate was cut to 2% in 1932: "cheap money".
  • Keynes's General Theory (1936) argued that governments should raise spending in a slump.
  • Two readings, both still argued:
    • Keynesian: a collapse in spending that budget-balancing made worse.
    • Monetarist: a collapse in money that the central bank allowed.
Mission 4

The 1970s: when the old cure stopped working

  • After 1945, full employment was the top priority. Governments managed AD to keep unemployment low.
  • 1973–74: oil prices roughly quadrupled. A supply shock.
  • Result: stagflation. UK inflation (RPI) peaked at about 27% in 1975.
  • Raising AD to cut unemployment now pushed inflation even higher.
  • Direct controls on inflation were tried:
    • Incomes policies: legal or agreed limits on pay rises (UK, 1970s).
    • Price controls: the US froze wages and prices for 90 days in 1971.
  • Problems with direct controls:
    • They treat symptoms, not causes.
    • Shortages and black markets where prices are held below equilibrium.
    • Hard to enforce; pent-up pay and price rises when they end.
Mission 4

Monetarism and new priorities

  • Late 1970s: inflation became the top priority.
  • USA: from 1979, the Fed (chair Paul Volcker) cut money growth. The federal funds rate reached nearly 20% in 1980–81.
    • Inflation fell from 13.5% (1980) to 3.2% (1983).
    • The cost: a deep recession; unemployment hit 10.8% in 1982.
  • UK: from 1980, the government set targets for money supply growth (monetarism).
    • Inflation fell, but unemployment rose above 3 million in the 1980s.
    • Money targets proved hard to hit and were dropped.
  • 1992: the UK set an inflation target. 1997: the Bank of England was given control of interest rates.
  • Since 2008, financial stability has moved up the list.
Mission 4

2008: the response

  • The global financial crisis began in the banks: lending froze, AD collapsed.
  • UK:
    • Bank Rate cut from 5% (2008) to 0.5% (March 2009).
    • Quantitative easing: the Bank of England bought £200bn of assets, mainly government bonds, by early 2010.
    • VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% for 13 months.
    • Government stakes in failing banks (RBS, Lloyds).
  • USA:
    • Federal funds rate cut to 0–0.25% (December 2008); large-scale asset purchases (QE).
    • $700bn fund to rescue banks (TARP, 2008).
    • $787bn stimulus package (2009).
  • Lessons drawn from the 1930s: stop the money supply and banks collapsing; support AD.
Mission 4

After 2008: the austerity debate

  • From 2010, the UK and many other countries cut deficits: fiscal consolidation ("austerity").
  • Arguments for:
    • High debt can raise borrowing costs. In 2010, Greece's borrowing costs soared.
    • A credible plan may boost confidence and keep interest rates low.
    • Smaller deficits leave room to respond to the next shock.
  • Arguments against:
    • Cutting spending in a weak economy reduces AD and growth.
    • A study by IMF economists (2013) found the multiplier in the crisis was larger than forecasters assumed, so cuts hit output harder.
    • With interest rates near zero, monetary policy could not offset the cuts.
  • Your judgement depends on: the size of the multiplier, the level of debt, and market confidence.
Quick check
?In the USA from 1929 to 1933, the money supply fell by about a third as thousands of banks failed. Which response in 2008 most clearly reflects that lesson?
Mission 4

Problems and constraints for policymakers

  • Time lags: spotting a problem, acting and the effect can take 18 months or more.
  • Poor data and forecasts: GDP figures are revised; the size of the output gap is uncertain.
  • The zero lower bound: rates near 0% leave little room to cut.
  • High public debt limits fiscal room.
  • A global economy:
    • External shocks (energy prices, world recessions, pandemics) are outside any one government's control.
    • Capital flows and exchange rates can undo interest-rate changes.
    • Low-income countries face extra limits: poverty, weak tax bases, costly borrowing.
    • Competitiveness depends on supply-side factors that change slowly.
  • Expectations and politics: short-termism before elections; rising inflation expectations.
  • Result: government failure in macro policy is possible: a policy that arrives late or is the wrong size can destabilise the economy.
Mission 4Evaluate

Which policy works best?

  • Criteria for success: are the targets met (e.g. 2% inflation)? At what cost? How fast? With what side-effects? Who gains and who loses?
  • Relative effectiveness:
    • Monetary: quick to change, but weak near 0% rates and slow to work (about 18–24 months).
    • Fiscal: strong in a deep slump, but slow to agree and limited by debt.
    • Supply-side: lasting gains in capacity, but they take years.
  • It depends on:
    • The cause of the problem: demand shock or supply shock?
    • Spare capacity: a big gap favours stimulus; little spare capacity means inflation.
    • The state of public finances and how open the economy is.
Mission check

?Two economies both face rising unemployment. In Economy X, demand for its exports has collapsed and inflation is falling below target. In Economy Y, the price of imported food and fuel has doubled and inflation is rising above target. Which judgement is best?

Diagnose before you prescribe: a demand shock calls for stimulus, a supply shock forces a choice.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.

Mission 2

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.
  • Crowding out may limit it too.

Mission 3

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.

Mission 4

  • The policy mix combines fiscal, monetary and supply-side tools. When two policies push the same way, the AD change is certain and the interest-rate change is not, and the reverse.
  • Diagnose the shock first: demand shocks allow stimulus without conflict; supply shocks force a trade-off.
  • Priorities shifted: full employment after 1945, inflation from the late 1970s, financial stability after 2008. Each crisis was fought with lessons from the last.
