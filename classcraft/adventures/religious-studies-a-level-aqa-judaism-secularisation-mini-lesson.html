Make a guess: In a society where religion seems optional, should Jews live apart, blend in, or something in between?

Mission 1

What secularisation is

  • Secularisation: religion loses influence over society, institutions and individuals.
  • Three features:
  • Replacement: science, law and reason, not scripture, become the accepted sources of truth and moral values.
  • Relegation: religion is pushed into the personal sphere: fine at home, kept out of public life.
  • Militant atheism: the view that religion is irrational and a threat to social stability.
  • For Jews, the challenge began with emancipation: citizenship came with the expectation that religion was private.
Mission 1

Militant atheism and Jewish replies

  • "New Atheists": Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006); Christopher Hitchens (1949–2011), God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything (2007); Sam Harris, The End of Faith (2004).
  • Their charges: faith is belief without evidence; religion divides people and can fuel violence; it should have no special place in law or education.
  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science explains how, religion asks why; they need each other (paraphrase).
  • Sacks also argued that religious communities build trust, volunteering and care: they strengthen social stability rather than threaten it (paraphrase).
  • Many Jews add: Judaism values questioning and argument (the Talmud records disagreement), so it does not depend on blind faith.
Mission 1

Secularisation inside Jewish life

  • 19th-century formula (Judah Leib Gordon, 1860s, common translation): "Be a man in the street and a Jew at home": relegation in a single line.
  • Secular Jews: Jewish by people, culture, family, food, humour or Israel; little belief or observance.
  • Humanistic Judaism (Rabbi Sherwin Wine, 1963): Jewish culture and ethics without belief in God.
  • In Israel many secular Jews keep festivals as national culture.
  • So secularisation is not only a pressure from outside; it is also a way many Jews now live.
Mission 1Read the source

Nehemiah's covenant

After the return from Babylon, Nehemiah and the people sign a covenant (Nehemiah 10:28–31, English verse numbering; JPS 1917 prints 10:29–32):

> "And the rest of the people… and all they that had separated themselves from the peoples of the lands unto the law of God" (Nehemiah 10:28; 10:29 in JPS 1917)

> "and that we would not give our daughters unto the peoples of the land, nor take their daughters for our sons" (Nehemiah 10:30; 10:31 in JPS 1917)

> "and if the peoples of the land bring ware or any victuals on the sabbath day to sell, that we would not buy of them on the sabbath, or on a holy day; and that we would forego the seventh year" (Nehemiah 10:31; 10:32 in JPS 1917)

Q1 Name three things the people promise.

Show answer

No intermarriage; no trading on Shabbat or holy days; keep the seventh year.

Q2 What does "separated themselves… unto the law of God" suggest about why they separate?

Show answer

Separation serves keeping the Torah, not hostility for its own sake.

Mission 1

Ghettoism

  • Ghettoism: preserving Jewish identity by choosing to live separately from secular society.
  • Not the forced ghettos of history (the word comes from Venice, 1516), but a voluntary separation.
  • Today: close Haredi communities (e.g. Stamford Hill, Gateshead, Bnei Brak): own schools and yeshivot, Yiddish or Hebrew, distinctive dress, limits on television and internet, marriage within the community.
  • Aim: to protect Torah life from secular values, as Nehemiah's covenant protected Shabbat and family.
  • Strengths: high observance and strong community; large families mean rapid growth.
  • Criticisms: isolation, poverty from limited secular education, little dialogue with others.
Mission 1

Assimilation

  • Assimilation: becoming like the majority so fully that a distinct Jewish identity fades.
  • Routes: dropping Shabbat and kashrut; intermarriage without raising children as Jews; in the 19th century, conversion (the poet Heinrich Heine called baptism his entry ticket to European culture: paraphrase).
  • Some see it as a free personal choice in an open society.
  • Most Jewish leaders see it as the greatest threat in free societies: Jews disappearing not by persecution but by choice.
Mission 1

Integration

  • Integration: full participation in society while keeping Jewish belief and practice.
  • Samson Raphael Hirsch (1808–1888): Torah im Derekh Eretz: Torah together with the culture and work of the wider world.
  • Modern Orthodoxy: university, professions, civic life, with halakhah kept in full.
  • Progressive Judaism: adapts practice to modern life and values, while keeping a distinct religious identity.
  • Jonathan Sacks: integration, not assimilation: every group helps build the shared "home".
  • Jeremiah 29:7: "seek the peace of the city": the scriptural root of integration.

Mission 1

The Haredi response

  • Haredi Jews see secular culture as a challenge to be resisted, not adopted.
  • Torah study at the centre: many men study in yeshiva or kollel for years.
  • Own institutions: schools, courts, charities (e.g. free-loan societies and volunteer ambulances).
  • In Israel: Haredi parties defend Shabbat, kashrut and marriage law in public life; long dispute over exemption of yeshiva students from army service (the Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that there was no legal basis for a blanket exemption).
  • Variety: some reject the secular State outright (Satmar, Neturei Karta); most are non-Zionist and take part pragmatically (see Mission 4).
  • Chabad takes another path: outreach to secular Jews, meeting secularisation by engagement.
Mission 1Diversity

Four responses compared

  • Haredi: secular society is a threat; separate to protect Torah. Source: Nehemiah 10. Risk: isolation.
  • Modern Orthodox: secular knowledge is valuable; integrate with halakhah intact. Source: Hirsch. Risk: compromise under pressure.
  • Progressive: adapt practice to modern values; Judaism is evolving. Source: Reform principles. Risk: losing distinctiveness.
  • Secular: identity by culture and peoplehood, not belief. Source: Haskalah, Zionism. Risk: weaker transmission to children.
  • All four are Jewish answers to the same question.
Mission 1Worked answer

Using Nehemiah in an essay

Question: "Ghettoism is the most effective Jewish response to secularisation." Evaluate.

  • Belief: ghettoism preserves identity by separating from secular values.
  • Source: Nehemiah 10:28–31: the returning exiles "separated themselves… unto the law of God", refused intermarriage and Shabbat trade.
  • Development: separation guarded Shabbat and family when assimilation threatened; Haredi communities today keep high observance and grow fast.
  • Counter: integration (Hirsch; Jeremiah 29:7) argues Torah can thrive inside society; separation can bring poverty and isolation.
  • Judgement criterion: "effective" at what: survival in numbers, depth of practice, or influence on society?
Mission 1Evaluate

Is separation the best way to preserve Jewish identity?

  • Claim: Separation (ghettoism) is the most effective Jewish response to secularisation.
  • For: Nehemiah's precedent; Haredi communities show strong observance and retention.
  • For: secular values (individualism, consumerism) clash with a life shaped by mitzvot.
  • Against: integration has kept Modern Orthodox and Progressive communities strong and engaged.
  • Against: separation can bring poverty, limited education and mutual suspicion; it cannot answer militant atheism's challenge in public debate.
  • Judgement criteria: what is being preserved: practice, belief, peoplehood? At what cost to individuals and to wider society?

Mission 2: Judaism does not call wealth a sin. So why does it set so many limits on what you do with it?

Make a guess: Judaism does not call wealth a sin. So why does it set so many limits on what you do with it?

Mission 2

Wealth as gift and trust

  • "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917): ultimately, everything belongs to God.
  • Wealth is not evil: Abraham, Isaac and Jacob were rich; prosperity can be a blessing.
  • But wealth is held in trust: owners are stewards with duties to God and to the poor.
  • Pirkei Avot 4:1 (paraphrase): who is rich? The one who is content with their portion.
  • Proverbs 30:8 asks for "neither poverty nor riches": poverty can tempt to steal, riches to forget God.
Mission 2Read the source

"My power… hath gotten me this wealth"

> "and thou say in thy heart: 'My power and the might of my hand hath gotten me this wealth.'" (Deuteronomy 8:17, JPS 1917)

> "But thou shalt remember the LORD thy God, for it is He that giveth thee power to get wealth" (Deuteronomy 8:18, JPS 1917)

Q1 What danger does verse 17 warn against?

Show answer

Pride: believing wealth is one's own achievement alone.

Q2 How does verse 18 change the meaning of earning money?

Show answer

The ability to earn is a gift from God; wealth carries covenant duties.

Mission 2

Responses to materialism

  • Materialism: valuing possessions and consumption above everything; a mark of secular consumer culture.
  • Jewish responses:
  • Shabbat: one day a week with no buying, selling or work. Abraham Joshua Heschel called Shabbat "a palace in time" (The Sabbath, 1951): holiness in time, not things.
  • Tzedakah and ma'aser: the custom of giving a tenth of income.
  • Bal tashchit ("do not destroy", from Deuteronomy 20:19): against waste.
  • Honest business and limits on showy spending: some communities set guidelines for simple weddings.
  • "He that loveth silver shall not be satisfied with silver" (Ecclesiastes 5:10 in English Bibles; 5:9 in JPS 1917).
Mission 2

Tzedakah: justice, not charity

  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek, "justice": giving is a duty, not an optional kindness.
  • Even a person who receives tzedakah should give some.
  • Maimonides' eight levels (Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14): the highest is to help someone become self-supporting (a loan, partnership or job).
  • Gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness): personal help that money cannot replace: visiting the sick, comforting mourners.
  • Communities run free-loan societies (gemachs), food banks and care homes.

Mission 2

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberation theology began among Latin American Catholics (Gustavo Gutiérrez, 1971): God sides with the poor and oppressed.
  • Jews point out that the Exodus is the original liberation story: God frees slaves from Pharaoh.
  • The prophets demand justice for the widow, orphan, stranger and poor (Isaiah 1:17; Amos 5:24).
  • Abraham Joshua Heschel (1907–1972) marched with Martin Luther King at Selma (1965); he said afterwards that he felt as if his legs were praying (paraphrase).
  • Marc Ellis, Toward a Jewish Theology of Liberation (1987): Jews, as a people who suffered, must stand with the oppressed today (paraphrase); his application to Palestinians is controversial among Jews.
  • Liberationist Judaism: social action for the poor as a religious duty, not only charity.
Mission 2Read the source

"To let the oppressed go free"

> "Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the fetters of wickedness, to undo the bands of the yoke, and to let the oppressed go free, and that ye break every yoke?" (Isaiah 58:6, JPS 1917)

> "Is it not to deal thy bread to the hungry, and that thou bring the poor that are cast out to thy house?" (Isaiah 58:7, JPS 1917)

  • Read publicly on Yom Kippur morning (haftarah).

Q1 According to Isaiah, what does God want more than fasting alone?

Show answer

Freeing the oppressed and feeding the hungry.

Q2 Why might a liberationist approach choose this passage?

Show answer

It makes justice for the poor part of worship itself.

Mission 2

The Jewish Social Action Forum

  • Jewish Social Action Forum (JSAF): a coalition of British Jewish organisations working together on social justice.
  • Brings together synagogue movements across the community and social-action charities, co-ordinated with the Board of Deputies.
  • Campaign areas have included refugees and asylum seekers, poverty and food banks, a fair wage, and climate.
  • Jewish charities working in this field include World Jewish Relief (disasters and poverty abroad), René Cassin (human rights) and Mitzvah Day (volunteering).
  • Grounding: the Torah's repeated command to protect the stranger: "for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 10:19, JPS 1917).
  • Shows the social relevance of Judaism in a secular society: faith acting in public life.
Mission 2

Tikkun olam

  • Tikkun olam: "repair of the world".
  • Mishnah (Gittin 4): rabbinic rulings made "for the repair of the world": to keep society fair and orderly.
  • Aleinu prayer: to perfect the world under God's kingship.
  • Kabbalah (Luria): every mitzvah repairs a broken cosmos.
  • Modern use: social justice and social action, especially in Reform Judaism.
  • Critics: some Orthodox thinkers say "tikkun olam" has become a political slogan detached from Torah and mitzvot.
Mission 2Diversity

Different Jewish ways of social action

  • Orthodox: strong chesed (kindness) within and beyond the community: welfare charities, volunteer ambulances, free-loan societies; tzedakah as halakhah; often wary of party politics.
  • Haredi: dense networks of mutual aid within the community.
  • Progressive: social action and campaigning central to identity (refugees, poverty, climate); tikkun olam as a core value.
  • Secular Jews: many work for justice as part of a Jewish ethical and historical identity.
  • Shared basis: tzedakah, the prophets, the stranger, and pikuach nefesh (saving life).

Mission 2Evaluate

Is social action now the heart of Judaism?

  • Claim: In a secular society, social action is the most important expression of Judaism.
  • For: the prophets put justice above ritual (Isaiah 58); social action shows Judaism's relevance to people who no longer worship.
  • For: campaigns (JSAF, Mitzvah Day) involve Jews of every stream, including the non-observant.
  • Against: Torah, prayer, Shabbat and kashrut are commandments too; social action without them may be ethics, not Judaism.
  • Against: "tikkun olam" can become a political slogan, and Jews disagree about which policies justice requires.
  • Judgement criteria: what makes an act Jewish: its motive, its source, or its community? Can justice and ritual be separated?
Mission check

?A Jewish business owner earns a large profit. She gives a tenth to tzedakah, funds an apprenticeship for an unemployed refugee, and closes her shop every Shabbat. Which teaching is shown by the apprenticeship in particular?

In Judaism, wealth is a trust: its use is measured by justice for the poor and the stranger.

Make a guess: Jews prayed for 1,800 years to return to Zion. Why did a modern movement decide not to wait?

Mission 3

Zion in Jewish memory

  • Zion: a hill in Jerusalem; then a name for Jerusalem and the whole Land of Israel.
  • After exile, Jews kept Zion at the centre of prayer:
  • the Amidah, three times daily, asks God to gather the exiles and rebuild Jerusalem;
  • synagogues face Jerusalem; the Pesach Seder ends "Next year in Jerusalem";
  • at weddings a glass is broken to remember the destroyed Temple.
  • Traditional belief: the return would come through God, in the days of the Messiah, not by human politics.
  • "Zion theology": the Hebrew Bible's hope that God will restore the people to Zion.
Mission 3Read the source

Amos 9:14–15

> "And I will turn the captivity of My people Israel, and they shall build the waste cities, and inhabit them; and they shall plant vineyards, and drink the wine thereof" (Amos 9:14, JPS 1917)

> "And I will plant them upon their land, and they shall no more be plucked up out of their land which I have given them, saith the LORD thy God." (Amos 9:15, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who acts in these verses: the people, or God?

Show answer

God: "I will turn… I will plant"; the people then build and farm.

Q2 Which phrase could later Zionists read as a promise that a return would be permanent?

Show answer

They "shall no more be plucked up out of their land".

  • Religious Zionists read verses like these as a promise now being fulfilled; most Haredi Jews read them as for the Messiah's time (see Mission 4).
Mission 3

Rabbis who said: start now

  • Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai (1798–1878, Sarajevo) and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer (1795–1874, Prussia), Derishat Tziyon ("Seeking Zion", 1862).
  • Their argument: redemption can begin naturally, through human effort: buying land, farming, settling. The miraculous stage follows.
  • Settling the Land is itself a mitzvah.
  • Roots of later Religious Zionism (see Mission 4).
  • Most rabbis of their time disagreed: forcing the end is forbidden; wait for God.

Mission 3

The Dreyfus affair

  • 1894, France: Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish army officer, is convicted of spying for Germany. He was innocent; the real spy was another officer, Esterhazy.
  • January 1895: Dreyfus is publicly stripped of rank in Paris; crowds shout against the Jews.
  • France was the first European country to emancipate its Jews (1791). If antisemitism could flare there, could assimilation ever protect Jews?
  • Theodor Herzl, reporting from Paris for a Vienna newspaper, saw the case unfold. He later said it made him a Zionist.
  • Émile Zola's open letter J'accuse…! (1898) defended Dreyfus; he was fully cleared in 1906.
Mission 3

Herzl's The Jewish State (1896)

  • Theodor Herzl (1860–1904): Budapest-born, Vienna-based, secular journalist and playwright.
  • Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State, 1896): the Jewish question "is a national question" (tr. Sylvie d'Avigdor, 1896).
  • "We are a people—one people." (same translation)
  • Antisemitism will not disappear through assimilation; the answer is sovereignty over a territory, secured by international agreement.
  • Method: diplomacy with the great powers, not small-scale settlement first.
  • Territory: Palestine ("our ever-memorable historic home") or Argentina; he did not yet insist on one.
  • This is Political Zionism: national, mostly secular, aiming at a recognised state.
Mission 3

Basel, 1897

  • First Zionist Congress, Basel, Switzerland, 29–31 August 1897: about 200 delegates.
  • Founds the Zionist Organisation (later World Zionist Organisation); Herzl its president.
  • Basel Programme (paraphrase): Zionism aims to create for the Jewish people a home in Palestine secured by public law.
  • The German word was Heimstätte ("home"), not "state": a cautious word for the Ottoman Empire's sake.
  • Means: settlement of farmers and workers; organising Jews worldwide; strengthening national feeling; seeking governments' consent.
  • Herzl in his diary (paraphrase): at Basel he had founded the Jewish State; in fifty years everyone would see it. Israel was declared in 1948.

Mission 4

Many Zionisms

  • All Zionists wanted a Jewish national home in the Land of Israel. They disagreed on why, how and what kind.
  • Political (Herzl): a recognised state, through diplomacy (see Mission 3).
  • Practical: settle and build now, without waiting for a charter.
  • Cultural: a centre for Hebrew culture and Jewish values.
  • Labour: a socialist society built by Jewish workers.
  • Revisionist: a state on both sides of the Jordan, defended by strength.
  • Religious: return to the Land as part of God's redemption, under the Torah.
  • Against them all, for different reasons: most Haredi Jews and, until the 1930s, most Reform Jews.
Quick check

Mission 4

Religious Zionism

  • Mizrachi (founded 1902) by Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines (1839–1915): Orthodox Jews should work inside the Zionist movement.
  • Its slogan (paraphrase): the Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel.
  • Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of the Land of Israel (1921):
  • even secular pioneers are unknowingly part of God's plan; their work is the beginning of redemption (atchalta de-geulah).
  • Settling the Land is a mitzvah; the state is a religious event, not only a political one.
  • After 1967 his son's followers founded Gush Emunim.
Mission 4Diversity

Political and Religious Zionism

  • Why a state? Political: Jews are a nation that needs safety. Religious: God promised the Land; return is part of redemption.
  • Who acts? Political: Jews, through diplomacy and settlement. Religious: Jews act, but God works through them.
  • What kind of state? Political: modern, mainly secular, for all Jews. Religious: shaped by Torah, Shabbat and halakhah in public life.
  • The Messiah? Political: not needed. Religious: the state is a step towards the Messianic age.
  • Shared ground: both support the State of Israel, Hebrew, immigration (aliyah) and defence.
  • Your spec's two named responses: Political Zionism and Religious Zionism.
Mission 4Read the source

"That ye awaken not"

> "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles, and by the hinds of the field, that ye awaken not, nor stir up love, until it please." (Song of Songs 2:7, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Ketubot 110b–111a, paraphrase) reads the repeated oath as three oaths made at the start of exile:
  • Israel would not go up to the Land en masse ("as a wall");
  • Israel would not rebel against the nations;
  • the nations would not oppress Israel too much.

Q1 According to this reading, what was forbidden?

Show answer

A mass, forced return before God's time: "awakening love" too early.

Q2 How do religious Zionists answer?

Show answer

Many say the oaths are aggadah, not binding law, or lapsed when the nations broke their oath through persecution.

Mission 4

Haredi opposition

  • Haredi ("trembling" before God; ultra-Orthodox) leaders mostly opposed Zionism:
  • it "forces the end": only the Messiah may restore Jewish rule;
  • it replaced Torah with secular nationalism: "Jewish" became a nationality, not a covenant;
  • secular pioneers broke Shabbat and kashrut in the Holy Land.
  • Agudat Israel (1912): anti-Zionist at first, later non-Zionist: lives in the state and sits in parliament, without giving it religious meaning.
  • Satmar Hasidim (Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, Vayoel Moshe, 1961): the state is a grave sin.
Mission 4

Neturei Karta

  • Neturei Karta: Aramaic for "guardians of the city", founded in Jerusalem in 1938, leaving Agudat Israel.
  • The name comes from a Talmudic story: the true guardians of a city are its teachers of Torah, not its soldiers.
  • Beliefs: a Jewish state before the Messiah is rebellion against God; exile ends only by divine redemption.
  • Practice: refuse to vote, take state money or recognise Israel; some join anti-Israel protests abroad.
  • A small group: most Haredi Jews reject its political actions, even while sharing its non-Zionist theology.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Secularisation: religion replaced as a source of truth and morals, relegated to private life, and attacked by militant atheism.
  • Responses: ghettoism (Nehemiah 10:28–31; Haredi separation), assimilation (identity fades), integration (Hirsch, Modern Orthodoxy, Progressive Judaism, Sacks).

Mission 2

  • Wealth is a gift held in trust (Psalm 24:1; Deuteronomy 8:17–18); Shabbat, tzedakah, ma'aser and bal tashchit answer materialism.
  • Liberationist approaches read the Exodus and the prophets (Isaiah 58:6–7) as a call to free the oppressed.
  • JSAF co-ordinates British Jewish social action.
  • Tikkun olam moved from Mishnaic law and Kabbalah to modern social justice; Jews differ on how political it should be.

Mission 3

  • Jews prayed daily for a return to Zion, traditionally expected through God and the Messiah; Amos 9:14–15 promises a permanent replanting in the land.
  • 19th-century nationalism, racial antisemitism, pogroms and the Dreyfus affair convinced Herzl that Jews were a nation needing a homeland secured by public law (The Jewish State, 1896; Basel, 1897).

Mission 4

  • Zionism took many forms: political (Herzl), practical and Labour (settlement, kibbutz), cultural (Ahad Ha'am's spiritual centre), Revisionist (Jabotinsky) and Religious (Mizrachi, Rav Kook).
  • Political Zionism: Jews as a nation needing a safe state; Religious Zionism: the return to the Land as part of God's redemption under Torah.
  • Most Haredim reject any religious meaning in the State; Neturei Karta reject the State entirely; Reform moved from opposition (1885) to support (1937).
