Mission 1

Mission 1: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Make a guess: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Mission 1

Coulomb's law

  • The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and to 1/r²: an inverse-square law.
  • It acts along the line joining them: like charges repel, unlike attract. Each exerts an equal and opposite force on the other.
  • F = Qq/(4πε0r²)
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, the permittivity of free space.
  • Treat air as a vacuum.
Mission 1

Spheres act as points

  • A uniformly charged sphere acts, outside itself, like a point charge at its centre.
  • So r is measured centre to centre.
  • Two spheres 2.0 cm across with a 3.0 cm gap: r = 1.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 5.0 cm.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • +3.0 nC and +5.0 nC, centres 4.0 cm apart, in air.
  • F = (3.0 × 10⁻⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040²) = 8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N.
  • Both positive: repulsion.
  • Halve r: force × 2² = 4 times: 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • Double one charge: force × 2.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres whose centres are 0.60 m apart exert electric forces of magnitude 1.0 N on each other. Their charges do not change. At what centre-to-centre separation would the forces have a magnitude of 9.0 N?
Mission 1

More than two charges

  • Find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • A (+2.0 nC) at x = 0; B (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m; C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Force on B:
  • From A: repulsion, pushes B in +x: 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ N.
  • From C (0.20 m away): attraction, pulls B towards C, also +x: 6.7 × 10⁻⁷ N.
  • Resultant: 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ N in +x.
  • Not in a line? Resolve into components, add, then use Pythagoras.
Quick check
?Three point charges are fixed on the x-axis: P (+4.0 nC) at x = 0, Q (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m and R (+2.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric force on Q?
Mission check

?Two small charged spheres exert electric forces of magnitude F on each other. The charge on each sphere is halved, and the distance between them is reduced to one third of its value. What is the magnitude of the force now?

Force ∝ 1/r². Halving the distance makes the force 4 times bigger; a third of the distance makes it 9 times bigger.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Make a guess: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Mission 2

The electric field

  • Electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Charges create it; it is there even when nothing is placed in it.
  • E = F/q: the force per unit positive charge.
  • A vector. Unit: N C⁻¹.
  • Direction: the push on a positive charge: away from +, towards −.
  • A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.
Quick check
?At point P, a charge of +2.0 µC experiences an electric force of 5.0 × 10⁻³ N directed east. This charge is removed and a charge of −6.0 µC is placed at P; the charges that produce the field do not move. Taking east as positive, what is the electric force on the −6.0 µC charge?
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sketches the electric field lines around two equal positive point charges a short distance apart. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 2

Field lines

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines start on + charges and end on − charges (or run to or from far away).
  • Lines never cross: the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines are drawn so their density matches E.
  • Patterns: single + (radially out) · single − (radially in) · + and − pair · two + (a neutral point between) · parallel plates.
  • A field line is not the path a charge follows.
Mission 2

The field of a point charge

  • E = Q/(4πε0r²)
  • Inverse square: double r, E falls to ¼.
  • Field lines agree: the same lines pass through every sphere round the charge, area 4πr². Line density ∝ 1/r².
  • A charged sphere: outside, its field is that of a point charge at its centre.
  • +4.0 nC, at 0.20 m: E = 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.20²) = 9.0 × 10² N C⁻¹, away from the charge.
Mission 2

Adding fields

  • Several charges: add their fields as vectors.
  • +4.0 nC and +1.0 nC, 0.40 m apart. Where between them is E = 0?
  • There the two fields point opposite ways. Equal when 4.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)², x measured from the 4.0 nC charge.
  • Square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 2(0.40 − x) = x and x = 0.27 m.
  • The neutral point is nearer the smaller charge.
Quick check
?The diagram shows two point charges, Q1 = +4.0 nC and Q2 = −2.0 nC, fixed on a line, and a point P on the same line. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric field strength at P?
Mission 2

The uniform field between plates

  • Two parallel plates, p.d. V, separation d.
  • Between them, away from the edges: a uniform field, the same strength and direction everywhere. Lines parallel, evenly spaced, + to −.
  • E = V/d. Unit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
  • 200 V across plates 4.0 cm apart: E = 200 ÷ 0.040 = 5.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹.
  • Force on a proton: eE = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, the same anywhere between the plates.
Mission check

?The field lines of a long, straight charged wire spread out radially, in planes at right angles to the wire: they spread in two dimensions, not three. Point P is 2.0 cm from the wire and point Q is 6.0 cm from it, both far from the wire's ends. How does the electric field strength at P compare with that at Q?

Lines are drawn so that their density matches E. Closer lines, stronger field. A point charge's lines spread over spheres (area ∝ r²), so E ∝ 1/r²; a long wire's spread over cylinders (area ∝ r), so E ∝ 1/r.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Make a guess: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Mission 3

What sets C for parallel plates

  • Larger plate area A: the same charge is spread more thinly, so the field and potential difference are smaller. C ∝ A.
  • Smaller gap d → C ∝ 1/d.
  • Vacuum: C = ε0A/d. With an insulator between the plates: C = εA/d.
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹: the permittivity of free space.
  • Plate thickness and the charge already stored do not matter.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Air-filled plates, each 0.040 m², 1.0 mm apart.
  • C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ F (350 pF).
  • Connected to 12 V: Q = CV = 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ C.
  • The field between the plates is uniform: E = V/d = 12 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.2 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
Quick check
?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has a capacitance of 16 pF. A second air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has plates with three times the area of the first capacitor's plates, and its plates are twice as far apart. What is the capacitance of the second capacitor?
Mission 3

Dielectrics

  • Fill the gap with an insulator, a dielectric: the capacitance increases.
  • Permittivity ε = εᵣε0. εᵣ is the relative permittivity: how many times larger C becomes when the material fills the gap.
  • Each multiplies C by about: air 1.0006 · polythene 2.3 · glass 5 to 10 · water 80.
Mission check

?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor is charged and then disconnected from the supply. Its plates are then pulled apart to twice their separation. What happens to the charge on each plate, the potential difference across the capacitor and the energy it stores?

Bigger plates, a smaller gap or a dielectric all raise C.

At a fixed p.d., Q = CV: more C stores more charge.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Make a guess: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Mission 4

Electric potential

  • Electric potential at a point: the work done per unit charge to bring a small positive test charge from infinity to that point.
  • Zero at infinity, by definition.
  • A scalar: it has a sign but no direction.
  • Unit: J C⁻¹ = volt (V).
  • Set by the source charges, not by the test charge: any test charge at P, of any size or sign, sees the same V.
Mission 4

Potential of a point charge

  • V = Q/4πε0r
  • Falls as 1/r, not 1/r² (field strength falls as 1/r²).
  • Positive charge: V positive, highest near the charge. Negative charge: V negative.
  • Example: +4.0 nC. At 0.20 m, V = 180 V.
  • Isolated charged sphere, radius R: V = Q/4πε0R at its surface. C = Q/V gives C = 4πε0R. R = 0.10 m: C = 11 pF.
Mission 4

Potentials add as numbers

  • Several charges: add the potentials, with their signs. No angles, no components.
  • Two charges of +4.0 nC, 0.30 m apart. Midpoint M is 0.15 m from each.
  • Field at M: equal and opposite → E = 0.
  • Potential at M: 240 V + 240 V = 480 V. Not zero.
  • E = 0 here because the two forces on a test charge cancel. V just adds, so it does not cancel.
Mission 4

Work and potential difference

  • Work done moving charge q: W = qΔV
  • Worked example: move +2.0 nC from 0.20 m to 0.10 m from the +4.0 nC charge.
  • ΔV = 360 − 180 = 180 V. Work done on it by an external force (moved slowly) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 180 = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J.
  • Released at 0.10 m, it is pushed outwards. By 0.20 m it has gained 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J of kinetic energy.
  • A negative charge does the opposite: it speeds up moving towards higher V.
  • Only the start and end potentials matter, not the path.
Mission 4

Work from a force–distance graph

  • The force on the +2.0 nC charge changes as it moves: F = Qq/4πε0r².
  • Work done = area under the force–distance graph between the two distances.
  • Between 0.10 m and 0.20 m that area is 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J: the same as q × ΔV.
Quick check
?A point charge of +5.0 nC is fixed in place. A second charge of +2.0 nC is moved slowly from a point 0.30 m from the fixed charge to a point 0.10 m from it. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the work done on the +2.0 nC charge by the external force?
Quick check
?Predict first. Two small spheres, each carrying a positive charge, are brought from infinite separation to a separation r. Which statement about the electric potential energy of the system is correct?
Mission 4

Potential energy of two charges

  • Electric PE = Vq = Qq/4πε0r
  • It is the work done by an external force to bring the charges together from infinity.
  • It belongs to the pair, not to one charge.
  • Like charges: positive. Work is needed to push them together.
  • Unlike charges: negative. Work is needed to pull them apart to infinity.
  • +4.0 nC and +2.0 nC, 0.10 m apart: +7.2 × 10⁻⁷ J.
Quick check
?Which statement is true for the electric field of a point charge, but never true for the gravitational field of a point mass?
Mission 4

Electric and gravitational fields compared

  • Source: mass ↔ charge.
  • Force: F = −GMm/r², always attractive ↔ F = Qq/4πε0r², attractive or repulsive.
  • Field strength: g = −GM/r² ↔ E = Q/4πε0r². Both inverse-square.
  • Potential: Vg = −GM/r, always negative ↔ V = Q/4πε0r, sign of Q.
  • Potential energy: −GMm/r ↔ Qq/4πε0r.
  • Shared: potential zero at infinity; field lines show both fields.
  • Between two protons, the electric force is about 10³⁶ times the gravitational force.
Mission check

?Point charges +Q and −Q are fixed a short distance apart, and M is the midpoint between them. A student claims: 'The potential at M is zero, so no work is needed to bring a small test charge from infinity to M, and the test charge feels no electric force at M.' Which evaluation of the claim is correct?

Potential and field strength are different things. Between two equal positive charges, E = 0 but V is not zero; between +Q and −Q, V = 0 but E is not zero.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Mass, charge and moving charge all make fields. Why is a uniform electric field simply V divided by d?

Make a guess: Mass, charge and moving charge all make fields. Why is a uniform electric field simply V divided by d?

Mission 5

Fields: one idea, several kinds

  • A field is a region where an object feels a non-contact force.
  • Gravitational field: acts on mass. Created by mass.
  • Electric field: acts on charge. Created by charge.
  • Magnetic field: acts on moving charge. Created by moving charge (currents).
  • Field strength is always force per unit of what the field acts on: a vector.
Quick check
?A beam of electrons passes between the poles of a strong magnet. A charged dust grain sits at rest between the same poles. Which of them feels a force from the magnetic field?
Mission 5

Why E = V/d

  • A charge Q crosses the gap d between parallel plates with p.d. V.
  • The field is uniform, so the force F = QE is constant. Work done: W = Fd = QEd.
  • By the definition of p.d., the work done is also W = QV.
  • QEd = QV, so E = V/d.
  • Units agree: V m⁻¹ = J C⁻¹ m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
Mission 5

Worked example: two routes, one answer

  • Plates 3.0 mm apart, p.d. 12 V. An electron (charge e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) crosses the gap.
  • Field: E = 12 ÷ (3.0 × 10⁻³) = 4.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹.
  • Route 1, force × distance: F = eE = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ N; W = Fd = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁶ × 3.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • Route 2, charge × p.d.: W = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 12 = 1.9 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • The two routes agree. That agreement is E = V/d.
Quick check
?Two parallel plates 5.0 mm apart have a p.d. of 2.0 kV between them. A proton (charge 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) crosses from one plate to the other. How much work does the field do on it?
Mission check

?Two parallel plates are connected to a fixed p.d. They are pulled apart until the gap between them is doubled. For a proton crossing the whole gap, what happens to the force on it and to the work done on it by the field?

The work done crossing depends only on Q and V. Spread the same p.d. over a wider gap and the force is weaker in proportion: that is why E = V/d.

Mission 6

Mission 6: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Make a guess: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Mission 6

The force on a charge in a field

  • F = qE, the same everywhere in a uniform field.
  • Positive charge: force along the field (towards the negative plate).
  • Negative charge: force against the field (towards the positive plate).
  • Between parallel plates: E = V/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
  • Electron or proton there: |F| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N. Same size, opposite directions.
Mission 6

Same force, different acceleration

  • Constant force → constant acceleration: a = F/m.
  • Electron: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻².
  • Proton: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.9 × 10¹² m s⁻².
  • The electron's mass is about 1/1800 of the proton's, so its acceleration is about 1800 times greater.
  • Gravity is negligible: the electron's weight is about 10⁻²⁹ N, some 10¹⁴ times smaller than 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N.
Quick check
?Two horizontal parallel plates, 2.5 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 150 V between them. What is the magnitude of the acceleration of an electron between the plates? Gravity is negligible. Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission 6

Accelerating through a potential difference

  • Work done by the field = qV = gain in kinetic energy.
  • From rest: ½mv² = eV (electron or proton).
  • Worked example: electron through 2000 V.
  • v = √(2eV/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.
  • That is about 9% of the speed of light, so ½mv² is still accurate.
Quick check
?Two parallel plates, 2.0 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 500 V between them. An electron is released from rest at the negative plate and accelerates across the gap. What is its speed when it reaches the positive plate? Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Quick check
?An electron moves horizontally into the region between two horizontal parallel plates, midway between them and at right angles to the uniform electric field between the plates. It is deflected towards the positive plate. Gravity is negligible. Which describes the motion of the electron while it is between the plates?
Mission 6

Deflection: a projectile in an electric field

  • Particle enters at right angles to the field (parallel to the plates).
  • Along the plates: no force → constant velocity v.
  • Across the plates: constant force → constant acceleration a = F/m.
  • Path between the plates: a parabola, like a ball thrown horizontally.
  • Time between plates: t = L/v. Sideways shift: y = ½at².
  • After the plates: no force → straight line.
Mission 6

Worked example: deflection

  • Electron at 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹ enters midway between plates 5.0 cm long, 2.0 cm apart, 400 V.
  • a = eV/(md) = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻² (as for the electron on the earlier screen).
  • t = L/v = 0.050 / 2.65 × 10⁷ = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹ s.
  • y = ½at² = ½ × 3.5 × 10¹⁵ × (1.9 × 10⁻⁹)² = 6.3 mm. Less than 10 mm, so it misses the plate.
  • Sideways velocity at exit: vy = at = 6.6 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹.
  • A proton at the same speed: about 1800 times less deflection.
Mission check

?A proton and an alpha particle are placed, one at a time, at the same point in a uniform electric field. The alpha particle has twice the charge of the proton and about four times its mass. Gravity is negligible. What is the ratio (acceleration of the alpha particle) / (acceleration of the proton)?

Same field, acceleration = charge × E ÷ mass. The electron and proton feel equal forces, but the electron has about 1/1800 of the mass: about 1800 times the acceleration, so it swerves far more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coulomb's law F = Qq/(4πε0r²): inverse-square, along the line of centres; like repel, unlike attract.

Mission 2

  • E = F/q: force per unit positive charge; field lines show its direction, and their density its strength.
  • Point charge: E = Q/(4πε0r²); parallel plates: uniform, E = V/d.

Mission 3

  • C = εA/d with ε = εᵣε0.

Mission 4

  • Potential = work done per unit positive charge from infinity; V = Q/4πε0r; a scalar, so potentials simply add.
  • Electric potential takes the sign of Q; gravitational potential is always negative.

Mission 5

  • Fields: gravitational (mass), electric (charge), magnetic (moving charge); field strength is force per unit mass or per unit charge.
  • E = V/d follows from W = Fd = QEd and W = QV: the work done crossing the gap is QV, whatever the gap.

Mission 6

  • In a uniform field F = qE (positive charges along E, negative against it), so a = qE/m is constant; light particles accelerate most.
  • Through a potential difference V, a charge gains kinetic energy qV.
  • Entering at right angles: constant velocity along the plates, constant acceleration across them, so a parabola.
