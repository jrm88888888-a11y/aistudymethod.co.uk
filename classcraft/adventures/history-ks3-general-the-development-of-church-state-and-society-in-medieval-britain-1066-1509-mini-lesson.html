Medieval Britain, 1066-1509

This mini-lesson tells the story of how Church, state and society changed in medieval Britain — from the Norman Conquest of 1066 to the end of the Middle Ages at Bosworth in 1485.

The Norman Conquest

1066 and the Battle of Hastings

When King Edward the Confessor died in 1066 with no son, three men claimed the English throne. Duke William of Normandy (from northern France) invaded and defeated King Harold Godwinson at the Battle of Hastings in October 1066.

William was crowned king on Christmas Day 1066 and became known as William the Conqueror. This is one of the most important dates in English history.

Controlling a conquered land

Castles and the Domesday Book

Ruling England after 1066 was hard — the Normans were a small group ruling a much larger, unhappy population. William used two powerful tools:

  • Castles — first quick motte-and-bailey castles of earth and wood, later huge stone keeps like the Tower of London. They let a few soldiers control a whole area.
  • The Domesday Book (1086) — a giant survey recording who owned what land, so the king could tax people fairly and know exactly what his kingdom was worth.
Society: the feudal system

The feudal pyramid

To reward his followers and keep control, William shared out the land in a system historians call feudalism. Everyone owed something to the person above them, and gave protection to those below.

Watch out: most people were peasants at the bottom. They were not free to leave — they worked their lord's land in return for a small strip of their own.

Church: the power of religion

Christendom and the Church

Almost everyone in medieval Britain was a Christian. Western Europe was called Christendom — a family of kingdoms that all followed the Pope in Rome, the head of the Church.

  • The Church ran everyday life: baptisms, weddings, funerals, and Sunday services in the local parish church.
  • Monasteries and abbeys were centres of learning, medicine and copying books.
  • The Church was hugely wealthy and powerful — it collected a tax (the tithe) of one tenth of what people produced.

Church vs Crown: kings and the Church did not always agree. In 1170 Archbishop Thomas Becket was murdered after clashing with King Henry II — a famous struggle over who had more power.

State: limiting the king

King John and Magna Carta, 1215

King John lost wars in France, raised heavy taxes and quarrelled with the Church. His angry barons forced him to seal Magna Carta ("the Great Charter") in 1215.

Magna Carta said that even the king must obey the law. It promised that free men could not be imprisoned without a fair trial (the idea behind "justice for all").

Common mistake: people often think Magna Carta gave rights to everyone. In fact it mostly protected the barons and "free men" — and there weren't many free men in 1215. Its lasting importance is the principle that the king is not above the law.

State: the birth of Parliament

Simon de Montfort and Parliament

Kings needed money, and money meant asking powerful people for agreement. These meetings grew into Parliament (from the French parler, "to talk").

In 1265, the baron Simon de Montfort called a Parliament that, for the first time, invited not just lords and bishops but also ordinary townspeople and knights from the shires. This is a key step towards the Parliament we know today.

Society: the Black Death

The Black Death, 1348

In 1348 a terrible plague — the Black Death — reached England. Within two years it killed around a third to a half of the population. People did not understand germs; many blamed God's anger or "bad air".

Society: rebellion

The Peasants' Revolt, 1381

Lords tried to freeze wages and a new poll tax (a charge on every person) made things worse. In 1381 thousands of peasants, led by Wat Tyler, marched on London in the Peasants' Revolt.

The 14-year-old King Richard II met the rebels. Wat Tyler was killed and the revolt was crushed — but the hated poll tax was dropped, and over time lords could no longer treat peasants exactly as before.

State: the end of the Middle Ages

The Wars of the Roses

In the late 1400s two branches of the royal family — Lancaster (red rose) and York (white rose) — fought a series of civil wars for the throne, known as the Wars of the Roses.

They ended at the Battle of Bosworth in 1485, where Henry Tudor defeated King Richard III. Henry became Henry VII, the first Tudor king, and married Elizabeth of York to unite the two sides.

Skill: using evidence

How do we know all this?

Historians can't visit the past, so they use sources — evidence left behind:

  • Primary sources — made at the time: the Bayeux Tapestry (showing 1066), the Domesday Book, chronicles written by monks, coins and buildings.
  • Secondary sources — written later, like a modern textbook or this lesson.

Historians ask: who made this source, when, and why? A monk writing about a rebellion might be biased against the rebels. Weighing up sources like this is what makes history a subject, not just a list of dates.

Big idea: how much changed?

What 1066 really changed

The Norman Conquest didn't just swap one king for another. It brought a whole new ruling class from France, new castles, and lasting changes to the English language — hundreds of French words (like beef, parliament and royal) entered English.

Common mistake: some people think 1066 only changed who was king. In fact it changed who owned the land, how the country was governed, and even the language ordinary people would eventually speak.

Recap

The story in a nutshell

1066: Battle of Hastings — William the Conqueror takes England.

Society: feudalism (king → barons → knights → peasants) + castles + Domesday Book.

Church: Christendom, the Pope, tithes; Church vs Crown (Becket).

State: Magna Carta 1215 (king must obey the law) → Parliament 1265 (Simon de Montfort).

Black Death 1348 → labour shortage → Peasants' Revolt 1381.

1485: Wars of the Roses end at Bosworth — the Tudors begin.
