Mission 1

From verifying to falsifying

  • Karl Popper (1902–1994), The Logic of Scientific Discovery (1934; English 1959): a scientific theory must be falsifiable: some possible observation could show it wrong. He used this to mark off science from non-science, not to define meaning.
  • Antony Flew (1923–2010) applied the idea to religious assertions: to assert something is to deny something else.
  • "Theology and Falsification", a symposium in the journal University (1950–51), with R. M. Hare and Basil Mitchell; reprinted in New Essays in Philosophical Theology (1955).
  • Your anthology: extract 2 (Flew and Hare) and extract 3 (Flew and Mitchell), from Mitchell (ed.), The Philosophy of Religion (OUP). Paraphrased here.
Mission 1Read the source

Flew's gardener

Flew, adapting a parable by John Wisdom ("Gods", 1944) (paraphrase):

  • Two explorers find a clearing with flowers and weeds. One says a gardener tends it; the other says no.
  • They wait. No gardener. They put up a barbed-wire electric fence and patrol with bloodhounds. No scream, no movement of the wire, no bark.
  • The believer says: the gardener is invisible, intangible, gives off no scent, makes no sound.
  • The sceptic asks how such a gardener differs from an imaginary one, or from no gardener at all.

Q1 What does each new "invisible, intangible" step do to the believer's claim?

Show answer

It removes another way the claim could be tested, until nothing could count against it.

Q2 What is the gardener a picture of?

Show answer

God, believed to care for the world, though no test detects God.

Mission 1

Flew's challenge

  • A real assertion rules something out. If nothing could count against it, it asserts nothing.
  • Believers qualify their claims to protect them: God's love is "not merely human love", God's plan is "inscrutable".
  • Flew's example (paraphrase): a child dies of cancer; the believer's heavenly Father shows no visible concern; still the believer says God loves us.
  • Each qualification kills the claim a little: "the death by a thousand qualifications" (Flew).
  • His question to believers (paraphrase): what would have to happen to count, for you, as a disproof of God's love, or of God's existence?
  • Your spec: "religious claims are false because nothing can count against them". Flew's own conclusion: they risk being empty, not shown false.
Quick check

Mission 1

Hare: bliks

  • R. M. Hare (1919–2002) agreed with Flew that religious claims are not falsifiable assertions, but said they still matter.
  • His parable (paraphrase): a student is convinced that all the university's dons want to murder him. Friends introduce him to the kindest dons; he says it only shows how cunning they are. Nothing can shake him.
  • He has a blik: an unfalsifiable way of seeing the world that frames how all evidence is read.
  • Everyone has bliks: Hare's own example is trusting that his car's steering will not suddenly fail.
  • Bliks can be sane or insane; the difference between them matters a great deal, though neither is an assertion.
  • Religious language, on this view, expresses a blik: non-cognitive, yet meaningful.
  • Anthology extract 2.
Mission 1

Mitchell: the partisan and the stranger

  • Basil Mitchell (1917–2011) said religious claims are assertions, and evidence does count against them.
  • His parable (paraphrase): in an occupied country, a resistance fighter (a partisan) meets a Stranger who says he leads the resistance, and asks for trust whatever happens.
  • Sometimes the Stranger is seen helping partisans; sometimes he is seen in enemy uniform, handing patriots over.
  • The partisan feels the strain, but keeps trusting because of that first meeting.
  • So suffering really does count against belief in God's love, but not decisively: faith rests on trust in a person.
  • Mitchell: believers' claims can be provisional hypotheses (dropped at the first counter-evidence), significant articles of faith (challenged, but not easily abandoned), or vacuous formulae (nothing counts). Faith is the middle one.
  • Anthology extract 3; your spec contrasts bliks with "significant articles of faith".

Mission 1

Flew replies

  • To Hare: if religious language expresses a blik and not an assertion, it is not what believers mean. Most Christians claim God really exists and really loves.
  • To Mitchell: the Stranger is a man: limited, perhaps unable to help, perhaps forced to hide. God is omnipotent and omniscient, so these excuses do not apply.
  • So, Flew argues, Mitchell is pushed back towards qualifications.
  • Mitchell could reply: the parable shows the logic of trust, not God's reasons; theodicy tries to supply those.
Mission 1Diversity

Realist and anti-realist readings

  • Mitchell: realist. "God loves us" corresponds (or fails to correspond) to how things really are. Evidence matters.
  • Hare: non-cognitive, often read as anti-realist: the blik is a way of seeing, not a report of hidden facts.
  • Flew: realist demands: a claim must say how the world is.
  • Hick: realist; meets Flew by naming what would verify belief, after death.
  • Your spec: "strengths and weakness of these approaches, including realist and anti-realist views". Ayer and Mitchell are your named scholars here.
Mission 1Evaluate

Did anyone win the symposium?

  • Flew: strong: asks believers to say what their claims rule out. Weak: Popper's test is for science, not for meaning; some unfalsifiable claims are plainly understood.
  • Hare: strong: explains why believers are not moved by counter-evidence; everyone has bliks. Weak: makes religion non-cognitive; it is hard to tell sane from insane bliks without evidence.
  • Mitchell: strong: keeps belief as assertion and takes suffering seriously. Weak: Flew's reply: God, unlike the Stranger, has no limits to excuse him.
  • Judgement criteria: Which account fits what believers mean? Which takes suffering seriously? Is falsifiability the right test of meaning?

Mission 2

Early Wittgenstein: the picture theory

  • Ludwig Wittgenstein (1889–1951), Austrian-British philosopher, Cambridge.
  • Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921): a meaningful sentence is a picture of a possible fact: its parts match things in the world.
  • What cannot be pictured (ethics, God, the meaning of life) cannot be said; it can only show itself. The book ends by telling us to be silent about it (paraphrase).
  • The Vienna Circle read it as support for verification. Wittgenstein later rejected the picture theory.
  • Your spec: "critique of picture theory".
Mission 2

Meaning is use

  • Philosophical Investigations (published 1953, after his death): words are like tools in a toolbox: they do many different jobs (§11).
  • His builders (§2, paraphrase): one calls "Slab!" and the other brings a slab. The word is not a picture; it is part of an activity.
  • "The meaning of a word is its use in the language" (§43).
  • To find what a word means, ask how it is used, not what object it names.
Mission 2

Language games and forms of life

  • A language game: an activity with its own rules for how words are used. Wittgenstein's list (§23, paraphrase) includes giving orders, describing, telling a joke, thanking, greeting and praying.
  • "Love" means one thing in a tennis score, another in a wedding vow.
  • A form of life: the shared practices and way of living within which a language game makes sense.
  • Religious language belongs to a religious form of life: worship, prayer, confession, care for others.
  • So believers' words are meaningful to participants who know the rules, even if not cognitive in the scientific sense.
Quick check
?A doctor says a patient has "faith" in the treatment; a priest says a person has "faith" in God. What would Wittgenstein say about the word "faith" here?
Mission 2Read the source

Believing in a Last Judgement

From Wittgenstein's 1938 lectures on religious belief, published from students' notes as Lectures and Conversations (1966) (paraphrase):

  • Suppose someone believes in a Last Judgement, and I do not. Am I contradicting him? Wittgenstein says no.
  • The believer has a picture that guides his whole life: it is in his thoughts in every decision.
  • It is not like a weather forecast that evidence could make more or less likely. It is not reasonable in that sense, but not unreasonable either.
  • The unbeliever is not denying the same thing; he does not use that picture at all.

Q1 Why does Wittgenstein say he is not contradicting the believer?

Show answer

They are not making rival claims in one game; the believer's words play a different role in his life.

Q2 How does this differ from Hick's view of belief in life after death?

Show answer

Hick treats it as a factual claim verifiable in future; Wittgenstein treats it as a picture that regulates life.

Mission 2

D. Z. Phillips

  • D. Z. Phillips (1934–2006), Welsh philosopher, The Concept of Prayer (1965), Death and Immortality (1970), Religion Without Explanation (1976).
  • Prayer: asking God for something is not an attempt to change God's mind like a request to a powerful person. It expresses the believer's desires and dependence on God.
  • Eternal life: not more life after death, but a quality of life now: self-giving love and dying to the self.
  • "God exists" is not a claim that one more being exists. Its meaning is shown in the believer's life of worship.
  • Religious language is distinctive: you cannot judge it by the rules of science or history.
Mission 2

Fideism and coherence

  • Fideism (Latin fides, faith): religious belief can be understood and judged only from within faith.
  • Kai Nielsen (1926–2021) called the view of D. Z. Phillips (a follower of Wittgenstein) and others "Wittgensteinian fideism" (1967). Phillips rejected the label.
  • Your spec: "criteria of coherence in the relevant language game": a religious statement is judged by whether it fits the game's rules and practice.
  • Religious statements become expressions of belief and commitment.
Quick check
?Why do supporters of the language-game view say Ayer's verification principle does not defeat religious language?
Mission 2Evaluate

Strengths and weaknesses of language games

  • Strengths: answers Ayer and Flew: religious language is meaningful within its game; respects how believers actually use words; explains why believers and atheists often talk past each other.
  • Weakness: most believers mean realist claims. "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE)
  • Weakness: it rejects religious claims that could be empirically checked (e.g. historical claims about Jesus).
  • Weakness: it may allow no conversation between groups: outsiders cannot criticise, and believers cannot persuade.
  • Weakness: any coherent practice could be shielded from criticism, however odd (a worry raised by Kai Nielsen).
  • Weakness: it may not give adequate meaning to the word "God", as a real being who acts.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit what believers mean? Does it allow dialogue and criticism? Does it answer the verification challenge?
Mission 2Worked answer

Judging a non-cognitive view

Task: evaluate whether language games show that religious language is meaningful.

  • Point: Meaning is use (Wittgenstein, Philosophical Investigations, §43); prayer and worship have clear rules for those who take part.
  • Counter-point: Paul treats the resurrection as a fact (1 Corinthians 15:14); a game-only meaning loses that claim.
  • Develop: A Wittgensteinian such as D. Z. Phillips would reply that "fact" means something different in faith; critics answer that believers mean the ordinary sense.
  • Judgement: decide whether "meaningful to participants" is enough, or whether meaning must include the claim to be true.

Mission 3

Three answers to one question

  • Question: does God exist?
  • Theism: yes, God exists.
  • Atheism: from Greek a-theos, "without God": rejection of belief in God or gods.
  • Agnosticism: from Greek a-gnōstos, "not known": we do not (or cannot) know whether God exists.
  • The word agnostic was coined by T. H. Huxley (1825–1895) in 1869; he explained it in his essay "Agnosticism" (1889).
  • Atheism answers "what do you believe?"; agnosticism answers "what can we know?". So one person can be both.
Mission 3

Kinds of atheism and agnosticism

  • Positive (strong) atheism: asserts "there is no God".
  • Negative (weak) atheism: simply lacks belief in God; makes no claim to disprove God.
  • Practical atheism: lives as if God does not exist, whatever is said.
  • Protest atheism: rejects God on moral grounds, often because of suffering (e.g. Ivan in Dostoevsky's The Brothers Karamazov, 1880).
  • Strong (permanent) agnosticism: God's existence cannot be known by anyone.
  • Weak (temporary) agnosticism: it is not yet known; evidence may settle it.

Mission 3

New Atheism

  • A movement after the attacks of 11 September 2001; best-selling books, debates, media.
  • Leading writers: Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006); Christopher Hitchens, God Is Not Great (2007); Sam Harris, The End of Faith (2004); Daniel Dennett, Breaking the Spell (2006).
  • Older atheism mostly said religion is false. New Atheism adds: religion is harmful and should be challenged openly.
  • Treats "God exists" as a hypothesis to be tested by the standards of science.
Mission 3Read the source

Dawkins' case

Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006), paraphrased:

  • God's existence is a scientific question; the "God hypothesis" should be judged on evidence.
  • A designer God would need to be more complex than what it designs, so it explains nothing and is itself very improbable ("Ultimate Boeing 747"; chapter 4).
  • He places beliefs on a spectrum of theistic probability from 1 (strong theist: "I know God exists") to 7 (strong atheist: "I know there is no God"); 4 is a completely even-handed agnostic.
  • He calls himself a 6: God very improbable, but not disproved ("leaning towards 7").

Q1 Does Dawkins claim to know that there is no God?

Show answer

No: he thinks God very improbable and lives on that assumption, but claims no certainty: a 6, not a 7.

Q2 Why does he think "God designed it" is not a good explanation?

Show answer

The designer would need explaining even more than the design.

Mission 3

The criticisms of religion

  • Non-thinking: faith is belief without evidence, so it discourages questioning (Dawkins).
  • Infantile worldview: religion is a comfort from humanity's childhood (Hitchens: religion comes from the "infancy" of our species).
  • Impedes scientific progress: religion gives "God did it" answers and has resisted science (e.g. the Galileo affair, 1633; opposition to evolution; limits on embryo research).
  • Also: harm from religious violence and from teaching children that doubt is wrong.
  • Believers reply that these describe some religion, not faith as most traditions understand it.
Quick check
?Which claim is most typical of New Atheism rather than of older forms of atheism?
Mission 3

Strengths and weaknesses, other explanations, probability, postmodernism

  • Strengths of religious beliefs, as believers see them: they give life meaning and purpose, hang together coherently, and claim explanatory power (why there is a universe at all, and why it is ordered).
  • Weaknesses, as critics see them: the evil and suffering in the world, the lack of public evidence that anyone can check, and the diversity of religions whose claims contradict each other.
  • Alternative explanations of religion: projection (Feuerbach), wish fulfilment (Freud), "opium" of the oppressed (Marx), evolutionary by-product: we over-detect agents behind events (e.g. Justin Barrett, Why Would Anyone Believe in God?, 2004).
  • Probability: neither side claims proof. Dawkins: God is very improbable. Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): taken together, the evidence makes God more probable than not.
  • Postmodern interpretations: doubt any single "big story" that explains everything (Jean-François Lyotard, The Postmodern Condition, 1979). Religion becomes one way of living and speaking among many.
  • Non-realism: Don Cupitt (1934–2025), Taking Leave of God (1980): God is a human ideal that guides life, not a being "out there".
  • Postmodernism cuts both ways: it weakens religious claims to final truth, but also the New Atheists' confidence in science as the only story.
Mission 3

Westphal: suspicion as a gift

  • Merold Westphal (b. 1940), Suspicion and Faith: The Religious Uses of Modern Atheism (1993).
  • Two kinds of atheism: evidential (sceptical) atheism asks "is it true?"; the atheism of suspicion asks "what hidden motive does belief serve?"
  • The "masters of suspicion": Marx (religion serves the powerful), Nietzsche (religion serves resentment), Freud (religion serves wishes).
  • Westphal: believers should not just refute them. He proposes reading them "as a Lenten penance": a call to self-examination, like the Hebrew prophets and Jesus attacking hypocrisy.
  • So the critique can purify faith, even where it fails to disprove God.
  • Contrast with Dawkins: Dawkins argues mainly about evidence; Westphal shows why the critique of motives matters more for believers.
Mission 3Diversity

Religious responses to New Atheism

  • Science and religion are compatible: many religious groups reject the claim that they conflict. Francis Collins (geneticist, led the Human Genome Project), The Language of God (2006); John Lennox, God's Undertaker (2007).
  • Apologists in the media: Alister McGrath and Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? (2007); public debates (e.g. Dawkins and Lennox, 2007). Main replies: faith is not "belief without evidence"; Dawkins attacks a God few theologians believe in.
  • Fundamentalist activity: some groups respond by stressing a firm, separate moral community (e.g. on marriage, education, creation teaching).
  • Self-critique: following Westphal, some believers accept parts of the critique as prophetic.

Mission 3Evaluate

Have the New Atheists made their case?

  • Claim: New Atheism has successfully shown that religious belief is unreasonable.
  • For: Religion makes testable claims (e.g. prayer, design), and science explains much that once needed God.
  • For: Real harm has been done in religion's name; probability (Dawkins) puts the burden of proof on believers.
  • Against: It attacks a "God of the gaps" that most theologians reject; it ignores philosophy of religion (critics: McGrath, and the philosopher Alvin Plantinga).
  • Against: Faith is often trust based on reasons and experience, not "belief without evidence"; many leading scientists are religious believers.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the critique describe faith as believers hold it? Is science the only route to truth? Do harmful acts show that a belief is false?
  • Weigh strengths and weaknesses of religious beliefs: meaning, coherence, explanatory power vs evil, lack of public evidence, diversity of religions.

Mission 4

The 1948 broadcast

  • BBC Third Programme, 28 January 1948; a radio debate on the existence of God.
  • Frederick Copleston SJ (1907–1994): Jesuit priest, historian of philosophy (A History of Philosophy, from 1946); a Thomist (follower of Aquinas).
  • Bertrand Russell (1872–1970): logician and philosopher; author of "Why I Am Not a Christian" (lecture, 1927).
  • Context: after the Second World War; logical positivism (A. J. Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936) said talk of God was meaningless.
  • Published in Humanitas (1948); reprinted in Russell's Why I Am Not a Christian (1957). Your extract: anthology extract 4.
  • Three topics: the argument from contingency, religious experience, the moral argument.
Mission 4

Starting positions

  • They first agree a definition of God (paraphrase): a supreme personal being, distinct from the world and its creator.
  • Copleston: God's existence can be proved philosophically.
  • Russell: he is an agnostic, not an atheist: he does not claim God's non-existence can be proved, but thinks the case for God fails.
  • So the burden falls on Copleston: Russell only has to show the arguments do not work.
  • Their deeper disagreement: what philosophy is allowed to ask.
Mission 4

Copleston: the argument from contingency

  • Builds on Leibniz (1646–1716) and the principle of sufficient reason: everything that exists has an adequate explanation.
  • Step 1: Some things in the world do not contain the reason for their own existence (e.g. Copleston depends on his parents, air, food).
  • Step 2: The world is the total of such contingent things; there is no world apart from them.
  • Step 3: So the total needs a reason outside itself.
  • Step 4: An infinite chain of contingent causes would explain nothing; adding more contingent beings never gives a necessary one.
  • Conclusion: there must be a being that contains its own reason for existing and cannot not exist: God.
  • Compare Aquinas' Third Way.
Quick check
?In the debate, what does Copleston mean by calling a being "contingent"?
Mission 4

Russell: "necessary" is a word for statements

  • Russell (paraphrase): "necessary" can only be used meaningfully of propositions (statements), not of things.
  • A necessary proposition is analytic: true by its meanings, and denying it is a contradiction (e.g. "all triangles have three sides").
  • But no statement of the form "X exists" is analytic. So a "necessary being" is a confused idea.
  • He adds that existence is not a predicate (following Kant's reply to the ontological argument): saying God exists does not add a property to the idea of God.
  • Copleston: the necessary being is one whose essence includes existence; we know it from the world, not by defining it.
Mission 4

Russell: the universe is just there

  • Copleston asks: why is there something rather than nothing?
  • Russell (paraphrase): the universe is simply there, and that is all; it has no explanation, and does not need one.
  • Mother analogy (paraphrase): every human being has a mother, but it does not follow that the human race has a mother. Moving from members to the whole is a different "logical sphere": the fallacy of composition.
  • Causes: physicists (on quantum transitions) need not assume every single event has a cause; looking for causes does not prove there are causes everywhere.
  • Copleston: refusing the question is not answering it. Science itself assumes the world is intelligible.
  • The two reach an impasse: Copleston thinks the question is legitimate; Russell thinks it has no meaning.
Quick check
?What is Russell's point when he says that every human has a mother but the human race does not?
Mission 4

Copleston: religious experience

  • Not a strict proof, but evidence best explained by God (compare –).
  • He means a loving but unclear awareness of a transcendent object, not visions or pictures.
  • His "pure type": St Francis of Assisi (c. 1181–1226), whose experience overflowed in creative, self-giving love.
  • Also Plotinus (c. 204–270): a mystic who, according to his pupil Porphyry, was kind and gentle.
  • Argument: the experience comes to very different people and transforms lives for good; the most reasonable explanation is a real objective cause.
Mission 4

Russell: experiences prove nothing outside the mind

  • Experiences that only one person has are private, unlike a clock everyone in a room can see. They may have subjective causes.
  • Mystics also report seeing devils and Satan, with equal conviction; so testimony would be as good an argument for Satan as for God (paraphrase).
  • Good effects on a life are no evidence that a belief is true: false beliefs can inspire too.
  • Example (paraphrase): a boy may be inspired by reading about the Spartan lawgiver Lycurgus, though Lycurgus probably never existed; readers are moved by imaginary heroines of novels.
  • Copleston's reply: good effects do not prove truth, but they do support the mystic's honesty; and what inspires the reader is a real value.
  • Link: Persinger and Dawkins (–) also offer natural explanations of religious experience.
Quick check
?Copleston points to the good effects of St Francis's experience of God. Which of Russell's replies addresses this most directly?
Mission 4Evaluate

Who has the stronger case?

  • Claim: Copleston gives a more convincing account than Russell.
  • For Copleston: "Why is there something rather than nothing?" seems a real question; science assumes the world is intelligible; religious experience is widespread and life-changing.
  • For Russell: We cannot move from parts to the whole; "necessary being" may be incoherent; private experiences have other explanations.
  • Applying it today: Russell's "brute fact" view is still defended by many atheist philosophers; contingency arguments are still defended (e.g. by Richard Swinburne, Alexander Pruss).
  • Judgement criteria: Must every existing thing have a reason? Is "necessary being" meaningful? Can personal experience count as evidence for others?
Mission 4Worked answer

Using the extract

Task: explain how Copleston uses the argument from contingency in the extract.

  • Locate: Copleston begins from things that depend on others to exist (his parents, air, food).
  • Explain: The world is the total of such things, so it needs a reason outside itself; that reason must be a being that cannot not exist.
  • Context: He is a Thomist drawing on Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason.
  • Link: Russell answers that "necessary" fits only statements and that the universe may just be there.
  • Skill tip: cite the extract by its argument and paraphrase it accurately; then link it to the wider topic (Aquinas, Leibniz, the cosmological argument).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Flew (1950): a claim nothing could count against asserts nothing ("the death by a thousand qualifications").
  • Hare: religious belief is a blik, unfalsifiable but significant; Mitchell: belief is an assertion that evidence counts against, but not decisively; Flew replied to both.

Mission 2

  • Later Wittgenstein (Philosophical Investigations, 1953): meaning is use; language games within forms of life; religious language is meaningful to participants, though often read as not cognitive.
  • Phillips: prayer, eternal life and "God exists" understood within the life of faith.
  • Critics call this fideism.
  • Strengths: answers verification and falsification; weaknesses: believers mean realist claims, no dialogue between games, thin meaning for "God".

Mission 3

  • Atheism answers what you believe; agnosticism answers what can be known. Atheism can be positive or negative; agnosticism strong or weak.
  • New Atheism (antitheism: Dawkins, Hitchens, Harris, Dennett) says religion is false and harmful; religious responses stress science-religion compatibility and public apologetics.
  • Westphal: the atheism of suspicion can be read "as a Lenten penance": prophetic self-examination.

Mission 4

  • 1948 BBC debate: Copleston (Thomist, God provable) vs Russell (agnostic).
  • Contingency: the world of dependent things needs a necessary being (Copleston); "necessary" applies only to statements, and the universe is just there (Russell; mother analogy).
  • Religious experience: best explained by God (Copleston); private, ambiguous and no proof of truth even when it does good (Russell).
