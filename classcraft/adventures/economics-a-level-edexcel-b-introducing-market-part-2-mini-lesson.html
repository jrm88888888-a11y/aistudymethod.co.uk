Mission 1

Mission 1: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Make a guess: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Mission 1

No one is in charge

  • Every day, millions of buyers and sellers decide what to buy, make and sell.
  • No one gives the orders. Yet shops are mostly stocked with things people want.
  • The price mechanism: changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, decide how scarce resources are used.
  • It answers the basic questions: what to produce, how, and for whom.
  • Prices do three jobs: signalling, incentive and rationing.
Mission 1

Signalling

  • A price change is a signal: it carries information.
  • A rising price says: buyers want more than is on offer here. The good has become scarcer.
  • A falling price says: there is too much on offer. Use resources elsewhere.
  • Buyers and sellers need not know why the price changed (a drought, a fashion, a new use). The price alone tells them how to react.
Mission 1

Incentive (and the role of profit)

  • A higher price makes producing the good more profitable.
  • Firms already in the market produce more: quantity supplied rises (a movement along S).
  • Call this an extension of supply.
  • In time, new firms enter (market supply shifts right), and resources (workers, land, machines) move into the industry.
  • A falling price cuts profit. Firms produce less, and resources move out to better-paid uses.
  • Profit is the reward that drives this. Losses are the warning to leave.
  • Say: "the higher price gives existing firms an incentive to extend supply" — not: "the higher price shifts the supply curve".
Mission 1

Rationing

  • Scarce goods must be shared out somehow.
  • A higher price cuts quantity demanded: some buyers buy less, or stop buying.
  • Call this a contraction of demand.
  • The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price.
  • Price rationing removes a shortage without queues, lotteries or ration books.
  • But "able to pay" depends on income. Price rationing is not the same as giving goods to those who need them most.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls", for a movement along D.

Mission 1

One shock, three jobs: Europe's gas, 2022

  • 2022: Russia cut pipeline gas supplies to Europe. Wholesale gas prices soared.
  • Signal: the price told every user and supplier that gas was now very scarce.
  • Incentive: suppliers shipped in more liquefied natural gas from other countries, by sea.
  • Rationing: factories and households cut their use.
  • Real data: EU gas use, August 2022 to March 2023, was 17.7% lower than the average for those months in 2017–2022 (Eurostat, 2023). The EU had set a voluntary target of 15%.
  • Caution: a mild winter and government campaigns to save energy also played a part. Price was not the only cause.

Mission 1Evaluate

How well does the price mechanism allocate resources?

Strengths

  • Uses information spread across millions of people. No planner could gather it all (Hayek, 1945).
  • Automatic: signals and incentives work without orders.
  • Rewards efficiency and new ideas through profit.

Limits. It depends on …

  • Income distribution: price rations by ability to pay, so the poorest can be priced out of essentials.
  • Market failure: prices miss external costs and benefits (pollution), and public goods may not be provided at all.
  • Information and competition: monopoly power or poor information distort the signals.
  • Speed of response: factors move slowly (new skills, new mines), so prices can swing sharply.
  • Judge: a powerful way to coordinate choices, but its results are only as good as the prices it works with.

Mission 2

Why research the market?

  • Market research: collecting and analysing information about customers, competitors and the market.
  • It answers: Who will buy? How many? At what price? What do rivals offer?
  • It reduces risk: a firm can test an idea before spending heavily on it.
  • It cannot remove risk: tastes change, and people do not always do what they say.
Mission 2

Mass markets and niche markets

  • Mass market: a large market for a fairly standard product sold to many types of customer.
  • Niche market: a small, specialised part of a market with particular needs.

  • Small firms often start in a niche; a niche that grows can attract larger rivals.
Mission 2

Primary and secondary research

  • Primary (field) research: new data collected first-hand, for this firm's own purpose.
  • Methods: surveys, interviews, focus groups, observation, test marketing.
  • Secondary (desk) research: data that already exist, collected by someone else for another purpose.
  • Sources: government statistics, published market reports, newspapers, competitors' websites and accounts, the firm's own sales records.

  • Quantitative data are numbers (how many, how much). Qualitative data are opinions and reasons (why).
  • Say: "primary research is collected first-hand for this purpose" — not: "primary research is the most important research".

Mission 2

Sampling

  • The population is everyone the firm wants to know about. A sample is the smaller group actually asked.
  • Random sample: every member of the population has an equal chance of being chosen.
  • Stratified sample: the population is split into groups (strata); each group is sampled in proportion to its size.
  • Quota sample: researchers must find set numbers of people from each group, but choose who to ask themselves: not random.
  • Convenience sample: whoever is easiest to reach. Cheap, but often biased.
  • Worked example, illustrative data: a gym has 5,000 members: 3,000 aged under 35, 2,000 aged 35 or over.
  • Stratified sample of 200: under 35: 3,000 ÷ 5,000 × 200 = 120; 35 or over: 2,000 ÷ 5,000 × 200 = 80.
Mission 2

Bias, and why size is not enough

  • Sampling bias: the sample does not represent the population.
  • Question bias: leading questions push people towards an answer ("Wouldn't you agree that …?").
  • Response bias: people give the answer they think sounds good, or say what they would buy but then do not.
  • Non-response bias: the people who reply differ from those who do not.
  • A larger sample cuts random error, but costs more. It does not remove bias from a biased method.
  • Real case: in 1985 Coca-Cola replaced its recipe with a sweeter "New Coke" after taste tests of nearly 200,000 people, in which the new flavour beat both the old Coke and Pepsi.
  • Many loyal buyers protested. The original returned 79 days later.
  • The tests measured a sip of taste; they did not measure buyers' attachment to the brand.
  • Say: "a larger sample is more reliable" — not: "a larger sample removes bias".
Quick check
?A fitness app emails a survey to its existing users. Of the 2,000 who reply, 85% say they would pay for a premium version. The firm predicts that 85% of all gym-goers in the country would pay. What is the main weakness of this prediction?
Mission 2

Market segmentation

  • Market segmentation: dividing a market into groups of customers with similar characteristics or needs.
  • Demographic: age, gender, income, family size, occupation.
  • Geographic: region, country, town or rural area, climate.
  • Psychographic (lifestyle): values, interests, attitudes, e.g. health-conscious or eco-minded.
  • Behavioural: how often people buy, brand loyalty, the benefit they look for.
  • Why segment? To target a group with the right product, price, promotion and place, and to spot niches.
Mission 2

Market mapping and positioning

  • A market map places rival brands on two axes that matter to customers, e.g. price (low to high) and healthy to indulgent.
  • Illustrative data: six cereal bar brands, A to F, are mapped. No brand sits at high price, very healthy.
  • A gap may be a niche, but a gap can also mean no one wants that mix. Research must check demand.
  • A map shows positions, not market share or sales.
  • Positioning: how a firm wants customers to see its product compared with rivals'.
  • A firm can reposition a brand, e.g. by changing its price, recipe or advertising.
Mission 2

Competitive advantage and differentiation

  • Competitive advantage: something that lets a firm beat its rivals over time.
  • Two routes: lower costs than rivals, or a product customers see as different and better.
  • Differentiation: making a product stand out: brand, design, quality, features, service, a unique selling point (USP).
  • Differentiation adds value: customers will pay more than the cost of the bought-in inputs.
  • Illustrative data: two bars both use inputs costing 0.40. The plain bar sells for 0.90; the branded, high-protein bar sells for 1.80.
  • Added value: 0.90 − 0.40 = 0.50; 1.80 − 0.40 = 1.40.
  • An advantage that rivals can copy quickly does not last.

Mission 2Evaluate

How far can a firm trust its research?

It depends on …

  • The sample: how it was chosen, more than how big it is.
  • The questions: neutral questions give more reliable answers than leading ones.
  • Say versus do: stated intentions often overstate real buying; test marketing shows real behaviour.
  • Timing: tastes and competitors change, and secondary data age quickly.
  • Cost: better research costs more; a small firm must weigh the cost against the risk of a costly mistake.
  • Judge: research reduces risk, but only if it asks the right people the right questions, and the results are read with care.

Mission 3: How can a tiny online shop survive when giant retailers sell almost everything?

Mission 3

Mass markets and niche markets

  • Mass market: a large market of buyers with broadly similar wants, served with standard products in high volume (e.g. bottled water, basic phone chargers).
  • Niche market: a small, specialised segment of a larger market with particular wants (e.g. left-handed guitars, gluten-free baking kits).
  • Mass markets: high sales volume, low margins, strong price competition, economies of scale matter.
  • Niche markets: low volume, higher prices and margins, fewer rivals, but the market can be small or disappear.
  • The price mechanism works in both: a rise in demand in a niche signals profit and draws in new firms.
Mission 3

Market information in the digital economy

  • Price comparison sites: show many sellers' prices on one screen, so search costs fall.
  • Social media: lets firms reach and talk to customers cheaply; customers post reviews that others read.
  • Viral marketing: content that users share themselves, so a message spreads at almost no cost to the firm.
  • Result: buyers have better information about prices and quality than ever before.
  • Say: "consumers are better informed" — not: "consumers have perfect information".
Mission 3

Online retail grows

  • Internet sales as a share of all retail sales in Great Britain:
    • 2008: 4.9%
    • 2019: 19.2%
    • 2020: 28.1% (shops closed in the pandemic)
    • 2021: 30.7%
    • 2025: 27.5%
  • The share jumped in the pandemic, fell back a little, then stayed far above its 2019 level.
Mission 3

Worked example: points or per cent?

  • 2008 → 2025: online share rose from 4.9% to 27.5%.
  • Change in percentage points: 27.5 − 4.9 = 22.6 percentage points.
  • Percentage change: 22.6 ÷ 4.9 × 100 = 461%.
  • So the online share is about 5.6 times as big as in 2008 (27.5 ÷ 4.9).
  • Say: "rose by 22.6 percentage points" — not: "rose by 22.6%".

Mission 3

The supply side goes digital

  • Micromarketing: targeting very small groups, even single customers, using data on what they search and buy.
  • Online retailing and distribution: no shop rent; one warehouse can serve the whole country, or the world.
  • Wider geographical markets: a shop in a small town can sell to buyers anywhere.
  • Digital skills: firms must recruit and train staff to run websites, data and online advertising.
  • Impact: lower fixed costs and lower prices for buyers, but tougher price competition squeezes profit margins.
Mission 3

The long tail

  • Line up products from best-selling to least-selling: a few hits sell a lot; a very long tail of niche items each sell a little.
  • A shop has limited shelf space, so it stocks the hits only.
  • Online, storage and display cost little, so a seller can offer millions of niche items.
  • The long tail (Chris Anderson, 2004): many small niche sales can add up to a large share of the market.
  • Evidence: a large share of Amazon's book sales came from titles a typical bookshop did not stock (Brynjolfsson, Hu and Smith, 2003).
Quick check
?Why can an online music service profit from the long tail when a high-street record shop could not?
Mission 3

Firm creation and destruction

  • Digital markets have low barriers to entry: a website and social media page cost little to set up.
  • So many firms start up, and many close: creative destruction.
  • All UK industries, 2023: about 316,000 business births (birth rate 11.0%) and 309,000 business deaths (death rate 10.8%).
  • Old models can be destroyed: video-rental chain Blockbuster filed for bankruptcy in the US in 2010 as viewers moved to streaming and online rental.
  • New firms and new jobs appear in their place: app developers, online sellers, delivery firms.
Mission 3

How small firms compete

  • Product differentiation and a USP (unique selling point): something buyers cannot get elsewhere, e.g. handmade, personalised, local.
  • Flexibility: small firms can change products or orders fast to fit what customers want.
  • Customer service: knowing customers by name, quick replies, easy returns.
  • Targeting niche markets: too small or specialised for big firms to bother with.
  • Relationships with stakeholders: loyal suppliers, staff and the local community give an edge.
  • Say: "competes through differentiation" — not: "competes by always being cheapest".
Mission 3Evaluate

Does the digital economy help small firms?

  • It depends on whether the firm has a niche: online reach helps a specialist find buyers worldwide; a firm selling standard goods faces price comparison against giants.
  • It depends on who controls the platform: selling through a large marketplace brings customers but also fees and the platform's rules.
  • It depends on the evidence: some studies find sales spread into the tail; others find that online, best-sellers grab an even bigger share (Elberse, 2008).
  • It depends on costs: lower rent, but spending on digital skills, delivery and online advertising.

Mission 4

The spectrum of competition

  • Example of monopolistic competition: hairdressers, cafés, pizza restaurants in a town.
  • Oligopoly: firms are interdependent: each must think about how rivals will react.
  • Contestable market: entry and exit are free and costless (no sunk costs), so even a few firms must behave competitively or be undercut by new entrants.
Mission 4

Barriers to entry

  • A barrier to entry is anything that makes it hard for new firms to enter a market.
TypeExamples
LegalPatents, licences, the sole right to run a service (e.g. a local water or power network)
CostEconomies of scale, high start-up costs, sunk costs (costs that cannot be recovered on leaving)
MarketBrand loyalty, heavy advertising, product differentiation
PhysicalControl of a key resource, the best locations, owning the network
  • Branding, intellectual property (IP) and heavy R&D spending all make entry harder.
  • Why they matter: without barriers, supernormal profit attracts entrants, whose extra supply competes it away. With high barriers, firms can keep supernormal profit in the long run.
Mission 4

Sunk costs and barriers to exit

  • A sunk cost cannot be recovered if the firm leaves the market: advertising, research, specialist equipment with no resale value.
  • High sunk costs deter entry: an entrant that fails loses them for good.
  • Barriers to exit: costs of leaving, e.g. sunk costs written off, redundancy pay, long contracts.
  • If sunk costs are low, a market is contestable: a new firm could enter, take profit and leave (hit-and-run entry).
  • So even a market with few firms may have prices close to average cost, because incumbents fear entry. (Contestable markets are covered fully in a later lesson.)
Quick check
?Building a new steel works costs billions. The two existing producers each make so much steel that their average cost is far below what any new, smaller rival could achieve. Which barrier to entry does this describe?
Mission 4

Minimum efficient scale and the number of firms

  • Minimum efficient scale (MES): the lowest output at which long-run average cost reaches its minimum.
  • Compare MES with the size of the market:
  • MES small relative to market demand → room for many firms → competitive structure.
  • MES large → room for only a few firms → oligopoly.
  • MES close to the whole market → one firm can supply it most cheaply → natural monopoly.
  • So economies of scale act as a barrier to entry: a small entrant would face higher average cost.

Mission 4

Concentration ratios

  • Market share = firm's sales ÷ total market sales × 100.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.
  • Great Britain grocery shares (Kantar, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025): Tesco 28.2%, Sainsbury's 15.7%, Asda 11.6%, Aldi 10.6%, Morrisons 8.3%.
  • 4-firm CR = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
  • 5-firm CR = 66.1 + 8.3 = 74.4%.
  • A high ratio suggests oligopoly. There is no single official cut-off, so always state n.
  • Limits: the result depends on how the market is defined (all groceries or one product? national or local?). It does not show whether the top firms are equal in size or one dominates.

Mission 4

Structure is not conduct

  • Market structure: the number and size of firms, measured by the concentration ratio.
  • Market conduct: how firms actually behave: price wars, collusion, advertising, innovation.
  • Two markets with the same concentration ratio can behave very differently: one fiercely competitive, one quietly colluding.
  • Interdependence: in an oligopoly each firm's best price or output depends on how rivals react, so firms face uncertainty.
  • Say: "this market is highly concentrated" — not: "this market is uncompetitive", until you have evidence on conduct.
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful are the models?

  • Perfect competition assumes identical products, perfect information and free entry. Few markets fit; some farm commodities come closest.
  • It is still useful as a benchmark: it shows what efficient outcomes look like.
  • Pure monopoly is rare; many "monopolies" are dominant firms with some rivals.
  • Real firms try to escape competition by differentiation and branding, which add value and give a competitive advantage: customers pay more for a product they see as different.
  • It depends on the question: the models predict the direction of effects (entry erodes profit) better than exact prices.
  • The threat of entry (contestability) can matter as much as the number of firms.

Mission 5

Banks: from savers to borrowers

  • Banks are financial intermediaries: they take deposits from savers and make loans to households and firms.
  • This channels savings into investment: new machines, buildings and businesses.
  • Banks pay savers a lower interest rate than they charge borrowers. The gap covers costs, losses on bad loans, and profit.
  • Illustrative data: a bank pays 2% on deposits and charges 7% on loans. On each 10,000 lent: 700 received − 200 paid = 500.
  • Credit: money lent now, to be repaid later with interest.
  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit: lending creates deposits, and so new money (Bank of England, 2014).
Mission 5

Interest and collateral

  • Interest is the price of borrowing. The rate depends on the risk of not being repaid, the length of the loan, and the general level of rates (e.g. Bank Rate).
  • Collateral: an asset the borrower pledges, which the lender can take and sell if the loan is not repaid. Example: a building.
  • A loan backed by collateral is secured; one without is unsecured, and usually costs more.
  • Illustrative data: borrow 20,000 for one year. Secured at 6%: interest = 1,200. Unsecured at 11%: interest = 2,200.
  • A new firm with no assets and no track record is risky, so it may pay more or be refused.
  • Say: "collateral" — not: "a deposit" (a deposit is money placed in a bank).
Quick check
?A bank offers a loan to a large, long-established manufacturer at 6% a year. It offers the same amount to a new online start-up with no property at 12% a year. What best explains the difference?
Mission 5

Unlimited liability

  • Sole traders and ordinary partnerships are unincorporated: the law does not separate the owner from the business.
  • So owners have unlimited liability: they are personally responsible for all the business's debts.
  • If the business cannot pay, creditors can claim the owners' personal assets: savings, car, even their home.
  • Partners can also be liable for debts that another partner ran up.
  • Real case: the insurance market Lloyd's of London relied on wealthy individuals, called Names, who backed insurance policies with unlimited liability.
  • Losses in 1989, 1990 and 1991 were each over £2 billion. Many Names faced heavy losses, and some were ruined.
  • From 1994, Lloyd's admitted limited liability companies as members.
Mission 5

Limited liability

  • A company is incorporated: a separate legal person that can own assets, sign contracts and be sued.
  • Its owners, the shareholders, have limited liability: they can lose only what they paid for their shares.

  • But lenders know it too. Banks often ask directors of small companies for a personal guarantee, which puts their own assets back at risk.

Mission 5

Worked example: borrow or sell a share?

  • Illustrative data. A company needs 50,000.
  • Option 1: a bank loan at 8% a year. Interest = 50,000 × 0.08 = 4,000 a year.
  • Option 2: sell 20% of the company to a venture capitalist.
  • Good year, profit before interest = 60,000:
  • Loan: owners keep 60,000 − 4,000 = 56,000.
  • Shares: owners keep 80% × 60,000 = 48,000.
  • Bad year, profit before interest = 2,000:
  • Loan: 2,000 − 4,000 = −2,000. The interest is still due.
  • Shares: owners keep 80% × 2,000 = 1,600. The investor shares the bad year.
  • Debt is cheaper when profits are high, riskier when they are low. (The loan must also be repaid.)
Quick check
?A small shop needs to buy extra stock for the busy winter season. It expects to sell the stock, and so be able to pay for it, within six weeks. Which source of finance is most suitable?
Mission 5Evaluate

Which source of finance is best?

It depends on …

  • Legal form: a sole trader cannot sell shares; only a plc can sell them to the public.
  • Amount and time: match short-term needs to overdrafts or trade credit; long-term assets to loans, leasing or shares.
  • Cost and control: debt costs interest but keeps ownership; shares cost a share of profit and some control.
  • Risk: existing debts, collateral, and how certain future profits are. Debt is dangerous if profits may fall.
  • The economy: when interest rates are high or banks are cautious, loans are dearer and harder to get.
  • Judge: there is no single best source; the best fit depends on the firm's form, its need and how much risk its owners can bear.

Legal form decides who carries the risk; the source of finance must fit the risk, the amount and the time.

Mission 6

What banks do

  • Commercial (retail) banks:
    • Accept deposits and keep them safe.
    • Make loans to households and firms.
    • Run payments: cards, transfers.
  • They channel savings into investment: firms borrow to build and expand.
  • Borrowers pay interest: the price of credit. Banks charge more for riskier loans.
  • Collateral: an asset the bank can take if a loan is not repaid (e.g. a house for a mortgage).
Mission 6

A bank's balance sheet

  • Liabilities: what the bank owes.
    • Customers' deposits (the bank owes them on demand or at a set date).
    • Borrowing from other banks.
  • Assets: what the bank owns or is owed.
    • Reserves: cash and deposits at the central bank. Most liquid, earn least.
    • Liquid assets: e.g. short-term government bills.
    • Loans to customers. Least liquid, earn most.
    • Investments such as bonds.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities. It absorbs losses.
Mission 6

Loans create deposits

  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit.
  • That deposit is new money: no one else's balance went down.
  • Example (illustrative): a bank lends 10,000 to a café owner.
    • Assets: loans +10,000.
    • Liabilities: deposits +10,000.
    • Broad money rises by 10,000 at once.
  • When loans are repaid, deposits fall: money is destroyed.
  • What limits lending? Demand for loans, the risk of non-payment, the interest rate set by the central bank, and capital and liquidity rules.
  • Say: "banks create deposits when they lend" — not: "banks just lend out savers' money".
Mission 6

The money multiplier model

  • A textbook model of how deposits spread through the banking system.
  • Fractional reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
  • Worked example (illustrative): reserve ratio 10%; a new cash deposit of 1,000.
    • Round 1: keep 100; lend 900. The 900 is spent and deposited in another bank.
    • Round 2: keep 90; lend 810.
    • Round 3: keep 81; lend 729 … and so on.
  • Maximum total deposits = 1,000 × (1 ÷ 0.10) = 10,000.
  • Bank credit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio = 10.

Rounds of lending

Illustrative data. A new deposit of 1,000 enters the banking system.

  • Takeaway: a lower reserve ratio means more of each deposit is lent on, so deposits grow further.
Mission 6

Why the multiplier overstates

  • The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every penny of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit.
  • In reality:
    • Banks may keep excess reserves, e.g. when they fear losses.
    • People and firms hold some cash instead of deposits.
    • Lending depends on demand for loans and on capital rules.
  • Real world:
    • The Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0% since March 2020.
    • Central banks today mostly set interest rates and supply the reserves banks need.
  • So the multiplier is a maximum, not a forecast.
  • Link to prices (quantity theory): if money grows faster than real output, inflation may follow.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The price mechanism allocates scarce resources with no one in charge: prices signal, give incentives and ration.
  • It works well when prices reflect all costs and benefits; it struggles with market failure and unequal incomes.

Mission 2

  • Primary research is new data collected for the firm's own question; secondary research already exists. Each has costs and limits.
  • A sample must represent the target population. A bigger sample cuts random error but not bias from leading questions or the wrong people.
  • Segmentation, market maps and positioning help a firm find a niche and a competitive advantage; differentiation adds value.

Mission 3

  • Digital markets give buyers better information, so demand is more price elastic and price competition is tougher.
  • Online sellers can serve a long tail of niche products, and low entry costs mean many firms are born and many die.
  • Small firms compete through USPs, flexibility, customer service, niches and stakeholder relationships.

Mission 4

  • Market structures range from perfect competition to monopoly, by number of firms, product differentiation, barriers to entry and control over price.
  • Barriers to entry (legal, cost, market, physical) let firms keep supernormal profit in the long run.
  • n-firm concentration ratio = combined market share of the n largest firms.
  • The larger the minimum efficient scale relative to the market, the fewer firms it can hold.

Mission 5

  • Banks channel savings to investment and create credit; interest reflects risk, and collateral lowers it.
  • Sole traders and partners have unlimited liability; shareholders in Ltds and plcs can lose only their investment.
  • Loans, overdrafts, trade credit and leasing keep ownership but must be paid; shares, venture capital and crowdfunding share profit and control.

Mission 6

  • Banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a matching deposit.
  • The multiplier model: maximum deposits = new reserves × 1 ÷ reserve ratio. It overstates because of idle reserves and cash leakages.
