Mission 1

Ummah: a community of faith

  • Ummah: a people or community. The Qur'an uses it for every people that received a messenger, and especially for the community of Muslims.
  • Its bond is faith, not tribe, race, language or nation.
  • Every Muslim belongs to it by saying the shahadah.
  • "Verily, this brotherhood of yours is a single brotherhood, and I am your Lord and Cherisher: therefore serve Me (and no other)." (Qur'an 21:92, Yusuf Ali)
  • Pickthall translates the same verse: "this, your religion, is one religion". The Arabic is ummah wahidah: one community.
Mission 1

Madinah: the first ummah

  • 622: after the Hijrah, the Prophet unites the emigrants (Muhajirun) from Makkah and the helpers (Ansar) of Madinah as brothers.
  • The Constitution of Madinah (Sahifah) declares the believers "one community (ummah) apart from other people" (paraphrase).
  • Loyalty to God and His Messenger now outranks loyalty to clan; disputes go to the Prophet.
  • The Constitution also includes Madinah's Jewish tribes as allies in defending the city, each keeping its own religion.
  • So the ummah began as both a religious community and a political one.
Mission 1Read the source

The ummah's purpose

> "Thus, have We made of you an Ummat justly balanced, that ye might be witnesses over the nations" (Qur'an 2:143, Yusuf Ali)

> "Ye are the best of peoples, evolved for mankind, enjoining what is right, forbidding what is wrong, and believing in Allah." (Qur'an 3:110, Yusuf Ali)

> "The Believers are but a single Brotherhood: So make peace and reconciliation between your two (contending) brothers" (Qur'an 49:10, Yusuf Ali)

  • Balanced (the "middle" community): avoiding extremes; a witness to humanity.
  • Best because of what it does: enjoining good, forbidding wrong, believing in God.
  • Brotherhood: when Muslims quarrel, the rest must make peace.

Mission 1

The ummah's role today

  • Shared worship: one qiblah, the same prayers in Arabic, one month of fasting, one Hajj that gathers Muslims from every nation.
  • Care: zakah, sadaqah and Islamic aid charities serve Muslims and others in need.
  • One body: the believers are like one body: when one part hurts, the whole body responds (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Witness and justice: enjoining good and forbidding wrong (3:110) in public life.
  • Cooperation between states: the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (founded 1969).
  • Minority life: in Britain the ummah is local (the mosque) and global (concern for Muslims elsewhere, news, online learning).
  • Tensions: national, ethnic and sectarian divisions; conflicts between Muslim states.
Mission 1

A Sunni understanding

  • Full name: Ahl al-Sunnah wa'l-Jama'ah: "people of the Sunnah and the community".
  • After the Prophet, guidance passes to the community as a whole, through the Qur'an, the Sunnah and scholars.
  • Ijma (consensus) is authoritative: "my community will not agree on error" (Ibn Majah; paraphrase).
  • Historically the caliph symbolised the ummah's unity; today unity rests on shared law, scholarship and worship.
  • Role today: to hold to the Sunnah, keep unity and avoid division (3:103), and witness to the world.
Mission 1

A Shi'a understanding

  • The ummah is rightly guided when it follows the Imams of the Prophet's family.
  • After Karbala, Shi'a memory shapes the ummah as a community of the oppressed that must stand for justice ("We wished to be Gracious to those who were being depressed in the land, to make them leaders (in Faith)", Qur'an 28:5, Yusuf Ali).
  • In the occultation, the marja' guides the faithful on the hidden Imam's behalf.
  • The ummah awaits the Mahdi, who will unite it and fill the earth with justice.
  • Unity: Shi'a and Sunni scholars have long worked for rapprochement (taqrib); in 1959 al-Azhar's Shaykh Mahmud Shaltut declared the Ja'fari school valid to follow.

Mission 1Diversity

What it means to be a Muslim

  • Shared: the shahadah; islam (submission), iman (faith), ihsan (excellence) from the Hadith of Jibril; the Qur'an; prayer, fasting, zakah, Hajj.
  • Sunni: belonging to the jama'ah (community): following the Sunnah as handed on by the Companions; six articles and five pillars.
  • Shi'a: belonging includes walayah, loyal love for the Imams: a Shi'a hadith says Islam is built on five, prayer, zakah, fasting, Hajj and walayah (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase). Five roots and ten branches.
  • Many Shi'a scholars: a Muslim says the shahadah; a mu'min (full believer) also accepts the Imamate.
  • Mutual recognition: the Amman Message (2004–2005) affirms both as Muslims and condemns takfir (declaring Muslims unbelievers); extremists who do so are a small minority.
  • Your spec: "similarities and differences between Shi'a and Sunni views about what it means to be a Muslim".
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the ummah more ideal than reality?

  • Claim: Today the ummah is an ideal, not a real community.
  • For: Muslims live in rival nation states; sectarian and ethnic conflicts divide them; there is no single leader.
  • Against: every day the ummah prays to one qiblah; Hajj gathers millions from every nation; aid flows across borders.
  • Against: unity was never uniformity; spiritual unity can coexist with political diversity.
  • Criteria: Must a community be political to be real? Is shared practice enough? How much division can a community hold?

Mission 2

Why "pillars"?

  • The Prophet is reported to have said that Islam is built on five: the shahadah, prayer, zakah, Hajj and fasting in Ramadan (Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase).
  • A pillar (rukn, plural arkan) holds up a building: the pillars are what Islam stands on.
  • Purpose: to live in submission to God. Each pillar is ibadah (worship): "I have only created Jinns and men, that they may serve Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Yusuf Ali)
  • In the Hadith of Jibril, the five pillars define islam (practice), beside iman (belief: the six articles) and ihsan (worshipping God as if you see Him).
  • The pillars are fard (obligatory) for every adult Muslim who is able.
Mission 2

The five and their origins

  • Shahadah (witness): the declaration of faith (47:19; 48:29).
  • Salah (prayer) five times daily (4:103); the number five fixed, in hadith, on the Night Journey (c. 621).
  • Zakah (purifying alms) for the needy (2:43; 9:60); organised in Madinah.
  • Sawm (fasting) in Ramadan (2:183–185); prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Hajj (pilgrimage) to Makkah for all who can (3:97); the Prophet's Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632) is the model.
Mission 2Read the source

The shahadah

  • Ashhadu an la ilaha illa'llah, wa ashhadu anna Muhammadan rasulu'llah.
  • "I bear witness that there is no god but God, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the messenger of God."

> "Know, therefore, that there is no god but Allah" (Qur'an 47:19, Yusuf Ali)

> "Muhammad is the messenger of Allah" (Qur'an 48:29, Yusuf Ali)

  • Part one affirms tawhid and rejects shirk. Part two accepts Muhammad's message, so the Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Heard at birth (the adhan whispered to a baby), said in every salah, hoped for as a Muslim's last words.

Show answer

Q2 Why does accepting Muhammad as messenger commit a Muslim to more than belief?

Show answer

Mission 2

Witness, public and private

  • Shahadah means witness: to declare what one knows to be true.
  • Conversion: a person becomes Muslim by saying the shahadah sincerely, with understanding. Often said before witnesses in a mosque; no further ceremony is required.
  • Public: a statement of truth to the world, and entry into the ummah.
  • Private: a personal commitment; without sincere intention the words alone are empty (63:1 calls hypocrites' testimony false).

Mission 2Diversity

Sunni pillars, Shi'a roots and branches

  • Sunni: the Five Pillars, as in the hadith.
  • Twelver Shi'a: five roots of faith (usul al-din): tawhid, adl (justice), nubuwwa (prophethood), imama (leadership of the Imams), ma'ad (resurrection).
  • And ten branches (furu' al-din): salah, sawm, zakah, khums, Hajj, jihad, enjoining good, forbidding evil, tawalla (loving God's friends) and tabarra (dissociating from their enemies).
  • Many Shi'a add to the shahadah "and Ali is the friend (wali) of God"; most Twelver jurists say it is not part of the obligatory words.
  • Isma'ilis list seven pillars, beginning with walaya (devotion to the Imam) and tahara (purity).
  • So Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the core practices; they group them differently, and Shi'a add duties such as khums.
Mission 2

One building, many dimensions

  • The pillars are a unity: each trains one part of life for God.
  • Word: shahadah. Time: salah shapes each day. Wealth: zakah. Body: sawm. Place and lifetime: Hajj.
  • Individual and ummah: each pillar is personal, yet shared (prayer lines, Ramadan, Hajj).
Mission 2

Intention and action

  • "Actions are only by intentions; each person will have what they intended" (Bukhari, no. 1; paraphrase).
  • Niyyah (intention): each act of worship begins with it.
  • Outward act without inward sincerity is condemned: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali); charity "to be seen of men" is void (2:264).
  • But intention does not replace action: the pillars have conditions and set forms.

Mission 2Evaluate

Are the pillars the whole of Islam?

  • Claim: Keeping the five pillars is all it takes to be a good Muslim.
  • For: The hadith says Islam is "built on" them; they are the duties every Muslim shares.
  • For: Done sincerely, they shape the whole of life: prayer "restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (29:45).
  • Against: "It is not righteousness that ye turn your faces Towards east or West" (Qur'an 2:177, Yusuf Ali): righteousness includes belief, kindness and keeping promises.
  • Against: The Hadith of Jibril adds iman and ihsan; ethics and the wider shari'ah go beyond the five.
  • Criteria: What does "good Muslim" mean: practice, belief, character? Can ritual be sincere without moral change? Do Sunni and Shi'a lists change the answer?

Make a guess: Muslims pray at set times, in set words, facing one place. Is there room left for the heart?

Mission 3

Salah within ibadah

  • Ibadah (worship, service): any lawful act done for God's sake. "Truly, my prayer and my service of sacrifice, my life and my death, are (all) for Allah" (Qur'an 6:162, Yusuf Ali).
  • Salah: the fixed ritual prayer, five times a day; the second pillar.
  • The five daily prayers: Fajr (dawn), Zuhr (after midday), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (after sunset), Isha (night).
  • Other prayer surrounds it: nafila (extra), tahajjud (night prayer), du'a (personal calling on God), tasbih (glorifying), wird (Sufi litany), dhikr (remembrance).
Mission 3

Preparing to pray

  • Wudu (washing): "wash your faces, and your hands (and arms) to the elbows; Rub your heads (with water); and (wash) your feet to the ankles" (Qur'an 5:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ghusl (full washing) after major impurity; tayammum (dry earth) when there is no water (5:6).
  • Niyyah (intention) in the heart: this prayer, for God.
  • Face the qiblah, the Ka'bah in Makkah (2:144); a clean place; modest covering.
  • The adhan (call) and iqamah (call to stand) begin congregational prayer.
  • Purity of body points to purity of heart.
Mission 3

One rak'ah

  • Rak'ah: one cycle of set words and positions.
  • Takbir ("Allahu akbar": God is greatest) opens the prayer.
  • Qiyam (standing): al-Fatiha in Arabic; in the first two rak'ahs, another passage of the Qur'an follows.
  • Ruku' (bowing), then standing again.
  • Sujud (prostration) twice: "bow down in adoration, and bring thyself the closer (to Allah)!" (Qur'an 96:19, Yusuf Ali). A hadith says a servant is closest to God in prostration (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Tashahhud (sitting, with the shahadah) and taslim ("peace be on you") to right and left end the prayer.

Mission 3Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a salah

  • Same pillar, same five prayers, same core words and movements.
  • Times: many Shi'a combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at three times a day; Sunnis combine mainly when travelling.
  • Hands: most Sunnis fold them (Hanafi, Shafi'i, Hanbali); Shi'a, and many Maliki Sunnis, pray with arms at their sides.
  • Prostration: Shi'a place the forehead on earth or something natural, often a small clay turbah (frequently from Karbala). It is prostrated on, not to.
  • Wudu: Shi'a wipe the feet; Sunnis wash them. Both cite 5:6.
  • Adhan: Shi'a add "Come to the best of deeds" and often a witness to Ali.
  • AQA: "differences in Sunni and Shi'a practices".
Mission 3

Jumu'ah, the Friday prayer

> "O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday (the Day of Assembly), hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business (and traffic)" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

  • Replaces Zuhr on Friday: a khutbah (sermon, in two parts) and two rak'ahs in congregation.
  • After it: "disperse through the land, and seek of the Bounty of Allah" (62:10): Friday is not a day of rest.
  • Sunni law: a duty for adult men who are resident and well; women may attend.
  • Twelver Shi'a: in the Imam's absence (occultation), many jurists hold Friday prayer may be prayed instead of Zuhr rather than required; Iran revived it publicly after 1979.
  • Purpose: unity of the ummah, teaching, news of the community.
  • AQA spelling: Jummah.
Mission 3

Outward act, inward state

  • "The believers must (eventually) win through,- Those who humble themselves in their prayers" (Qur'an 23:1–2, Yusuf Ali). Khushu': humble attention.
  • Warning: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ihsan: worship God as if you see Him; if you do not see Him, He sees you (Hadith of Jibril, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Effect on life: "Prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (Qur'an 29:45, Yusuf Ali).
  • The set form supports the heart: the body bows so the self learns to bow.
  • AQA: "the importance of the outward actions of prayer and the underlying intentions and state of mind".

Mission 4

The fifth pillar

  • Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah, 8–13 Dhu'l-Hijjah, the last month of the lunar year.
  • "Pilgrimage thereto is a duty men owe to Allah,- those who can afford the journey" (Qur'an 3:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • Once in a lifetime, for adult Muslims who are able: health, money (debts paid, family provided for) and a safe route.
  • Origins: Ibrahim and Isma'il raised the foundations of the Ka'bah (2:127); God told Ibrahim to "proclaim the Pilgrimage among men" (22:27).
  • The Prophet made one Hajj, the Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632). Its rites are the model.
  • Umrah: the "lesser pilgrimage", at any time, with fewer rites; it does not replace Hajj.
Mission 4

Ihram: the state of consecration

  • At the miqat (boundary points) pilgrims enter ihram with an intention.
  • Men wear two plain white unstitched cloths; women wear simple modest clothes, face and hands uncovered.
  • Forbidden in ihram: perfume, cutting hair or nails, hunting, sexual relations, quarrelling (2:197: "no obscenity, nor wickedness, nor wrangling in the Hajj").
  • The talbiyah: "Here I am, O God, here I am; You have no partner; praise, blessing and sovereignty are Yours."
  • Meaning: rich and poor look the same; ihram is often compared to the burial shroud: the pilgrim leaves the world behind.
Mission 4

The rites, day by day

  • Arrival: tawaf, seven circuits of the Ka'bah anticlockwise; sa'y, seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah.
  • 8th: to Mina, the tent city.
  • 9th: 'Arafat: wuquf (standing) in prayer from midday to sunset; the Prophet's Farewell Sermon was given here. Night at Muzdalifah, gathering pebbles.
  • The standing at 'Arafat is often seen as a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: all stand equal before God, asking forgiveness.
  • 10th (Eid-ul-Adha): stoning the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah); sacrifice; men shave or trim, women cut a lock; tawaf al-ifadah and sa'y.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three jamarat each day.
  • Farewell tawaf before leaving. Many also visit the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah (not part of Hajj).

Mission 4

What Hajj means

  • Tawhid: the whole journey answers God's call ("Here I am").
  • Ibrahim's faith: his family's obedience is re-lived at the Ka'bah, Safa and Marwah, the jamarat and in the sacrifice.
  • Equality: "the most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is (he who is) the most righteous of you" (Qur'an 49:13, Yusuf Ali).
  • Unity of the ummah: Muslims of every nation, in one place, one dress, one prayer.
  • Judgement and forgiveness: a Hajj done without wrongdoing returns the pilgrim "as on the day his mother bore him" (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Malcolm X (1925–1965), on Hajj in 1964, wrote that seeing pilgrims of every colour as brothers changed his view of race (letter from Makkah; paraphrase).
Mission 4

Hajj in the 20th century

  • Rule of the Hijaz: Ibn Saud took Makkah and Madinah (1924–25); the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (1932) took charge of the Hajj.
  • Travel: the Hejaz Railway reached Madinah in 1908 but was wrecked in the First World War; from the mid-century, air travel replaced months-long journeys by land and sea.
  • Building: major expansions of the Grand Mosque from the 1950s; a Jamarat bridge (1963; rebuilt on several levels from 2006); fire-resistant tents at Mina after a fire in 1997.
  • Management: country quotas from 1988 (about one pilgrim per thousand Muslims); visas; health rules.
  • Tensions: the 1979 seizure of the Grand Mosque; a deadly clash with Iranian pilgrims in 1987; crushes in crowds.
  • Heritage: Saudi authorities demolished many historic sites (e.g. tombs at al-Baqi', 1925–26), seeing them as risks of shirk; many Shi'a and some Sunnis regard this as a grievous loss.

Mission 4Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims

  • Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims perform the same Hajj together, at the same places and times.
  • Twelver Shi'a add tawaf al-nisa', a further full tawaf (seven circuits, with its two-rak'ah prayer) needed before marital relations are lawful again.
  • Small differences in prayer (see Mission 3) continue during Hajj.
  • Shi'a also value ziyarah (visiting) to the shrines of the Prophet's family: Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad. These are highly honoured but never replace Hajj.
  • Politics have touched Hajj: e.g. Iran's boycott after 1987; disputes over the treatment of Shi'a pilgrims and holy sites.
Mission 4Evaluate

More than a personal journey?

  • Claim: Hajj is above all a personal spiritual journey.
  • Personal: intention, repentance and forgiveness are individual; each pilgrim stands alone at 'Arafat.
  • Personal: many describe it as the high point of their own faith.
  • More than personal: it unites the ummah across nations (49:13); millions act as one.
  • More than personal: it re-enacts a shared history (Ibrahim, Hajar, the Prophet) and shapes Muslim identity worldwide.
  • Criteria: Can the personal and communal be separated? What does the Qur'an stress (22:27–28)? Has modern mass travel made Hajj more communal or more of an individual experience?
  • AQA: the significance and importance of Hajj today.

Mission 5

Masjid: a place of prostration

  • Masjid (Arabic): "place of sujud", of prostration. The English word mosque comes from it.
  • So the core of a masjid is simple: a clean place where Muslims bow to the ground before God.
  • A hadith says the whole earth was made a masjid for the Prophet's community: Muslims may pray anywhere clean (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • So a building is not required for salah. What, then, is a mosque for?
  • Jami' (gathering) mosque: a larger mosque where Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) is held.
Mission 5

The first masjid in Madinah

  • 622 CE, after the Hijrah: first the mosque at Quba, then the Prophet's own mosque in Madinah.
  • Built by the community together: mud-brick walls, palm-trunk pillars, palm-leaf roof, an open courtyard.
  • One building, many roles:
  • Worship: the five prayers, Friday prayer and khutbah; Bilal's adhan called people in.
  • Teaching: the Qur'an recited and explained; the suffa (shaded platform) sheltered poor Companions who studied there.
  • Government: disputes judged, decisions taken, delegations from other tribes received.
  • Welfare: charity given out; the needy fed and housed.

Mission 5

Mosques in Britain: from house to centre

  • 1889: Britain's first purpose-built mosque, the Shah Jahan Mosque in Woking; the same year, the Liverpool Muslim Institute opened in converted terraced houses.
  • 1950s–1970s: migrant workers, mostly men, opened house mosques in rooms and terraces: prayer first.
  • As families arrived, mosques added madrasah classes (Qur'an and Arabic for children, often after school).
  • From the 1970s–80s, more purpose-built mosques with domes and minarets.
  • Today: many are community centres: youth clubs, food banks, advice, sport, marriage and funerals, open days and interfaith visits.
  • Debates: space and roles for women; the language of the khutbah; how imams are trained (abroad or in the UK).
  • Your spec: "the changing role of the mosque in the community".
Mission 5

The London Central Mosque: its history

  • 1940: the British government sets aside £100,000 for a site for a mosque in London, in recognition of Muslims across the Empire. A site by Regent's Park is chosen.
  • 1944: King George VI opens the Islamic Cultural Centre in the house on the site.
  • 1969: design competition won by architect Sir Frederick Gibberd.
  • 1977: the mosque is completed: a gold-coloured dome, a minaret, a large main hall, with the Centre beside it. Built largely with gifts from Muslim governments.
  • Run by a board of trustees: ambassadors of Muslim countries in London.
  • 2018: listed Grade II* (a particularly important building of more than special interest).
  • Also called Regent's Park Mosque.
Mission 5

The London Central Mosque: its work today

  • Worship: five daily prayers; Friday prayer for thousands; Eid prayers in several sittings.
  • Life events: Islamic marriages (nikah), funeral prayers, guidance for new Muslims taking the shahadah.
  • Education: Qur'an and Arabic classes, lectures, a library and bookshop.
  • Visits and dialogue: school groups, tours, interfaith events; a public face of Islam in London.
  • Advice and welfare: religious and family guidance; support for those in need.
  • The work has grown from prayer for diplomats and visitors to a national centre serving a diverse London ummah.
  • Your spec: "the history and developing work of the London Central Mosque".
Mission 5Diversity

Roles of the masjid in the UK today

  • Religious: salah, Jumu'ah, Ramadan tarawih prayers, Eid, Qur'an recitation, nikah, funerals.
  • Educational: madrasah classes, adult study circles, talks.
  • Social: community meals (e.g. iftar), food banks, youth and sports clubs, care for the elderly.
  • Political (civic): voter registration, meetings with MPs and police, statements after local events.
  • Women: some mosques have full facilities, women trustees and classes; others little or no space. Muslim women's groups campaign for access, recalling that women prayed in the Prophet's mosque.
  • Shi'a centres (imambargah, husayniyyah) hold majlis gatherings, especially in Muharram.
  • Supported by the community: donations, sadaqah, volunteers, committees (9:18).

Mission 5Evaluate

Has the mosque kept its first purpose?

  • Claim: Today's mosques have kept the function of the first masjid in Madinah.
  • For: prayer and prostration remain central; many UK mosques again teach, feed and advise, as in Madinah.
  • For: the London Central Mosque's growth from a prayer room to a national centre mirrors that breadth.
  • Against: some mosques are prayer halls only, run by small committees; few have political or judicial roles.
  • Against: in Madinah women prayed and learned in the mosque; in some UK mosques women have little space.
  • Prostration first? Yes: the word masjid means place of sujud, and the earth itself is a masjid. Or no: the Prophet's mosque shows the community, not only the prayer, as its purpose.
  • Criteria: What counts as the mosque's "function": its acts, or its role as hub of the ummah? Should a minority community's mosque match a city-state's?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The ummah is the community of all Muslims, joined by faith (21:92, 49:10); it began in Madinah and spread through rule, trade, Sufis and migration.
  • Sunnis stress the Sunnah and the community's consensus; Shi'a stress guidance through the Imams and justice for the oppressed.

Mission 2

  • Islam is "built on five": shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm, Hajj; each is worship and each is fard for those able.
  • The shahadah witnesses to tawhid and to Muhammad as messenger; saying it sincerely makes a person Muslim.
  • The pillars form a unity of outward act and inward intention; Twelver Shi'a group the same practices as roots and branches.

Mission 3

  • Salah: five fixed prayers, with wudu, niyyah and qiblah; each rak'ah moves from standing to prostration.
  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the pillar but differ in timing, hand position, prostration surface and wudu.

Mission 4

  • Hajj: a once-in-a-lifetime duty for those able (3:97), retracing Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il and the Prophet's Farewell Hajj.
  • Ihram, tawaf, sa'y, 'Arafat, Muzdalifah, stoning, sacrifice: equality, obedience and a foretaste of Judgement.
  • 20th century: Saudi control, air travel, expansion, quotas and safety, and disputes over heritage.

Mission 5

  • Masjid means place of prostration; the first masjid in Madinah was also school, court, welfare centre and meeting place.
  • The London Central Mosque grew from a 1940s gift of land to a national centre for worship, learning and dialogue.
