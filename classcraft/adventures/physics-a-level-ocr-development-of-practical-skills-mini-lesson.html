Mission 1

Mission 1: A newton, a joule, a watt: what are they actually made of?

Make a guess: A newton, a joule, a watt: what are they actually made of?

Mission 1

Number × unit

  • A physical quantity = number × unit. 3.0 m means 3.0 × (1 metre).
  • No unit, no meaning: "the length is 3.0" could be 3.0 mm or 3.0 km.
  • Units obey algebra: m ÷ s = m s⁻¹; m × m = m².
  • Table headings and axis labels: quantity / unit, e.g. speed / m s⁻¹. The entries below are then pure numbers.
Mission 1

The base units you need

  • Every SI unit is built from base units.
  • Mass: kg · length: m · time: s · current: A · temperature: K · amount of substance: mol.
  • Every unit you will use is built from these six. (The SI has a seventh, the candela, for luminous intensity; you will not need it.)
Mission 1

Derived units

  • A derived unit comes from a defining equation. Replace each quantity by its unit.
  • Velocity = displacement ÷ time → m s⁻¹. Acceleration → m s⁻².
  • Density = mass ÷ volume → kg m⁻³. Momentum = mass × velocity → kg m s⁻¹.
  • Force = mass × acceleration → kg m s⁻² = N (newton).
  • Work = force × distance → N m = kg m² s⁻² = J (joule).
  • Charge = current × time → A s = C (coulomb).
Mission 1

Worked example: the watt

  • Power = energy ÷ time.
  • Unit: J s⁻¹.
  • J = kg m² s⁻² (from work = force × distance).
  • W = kg m² s⁻² ÷ s = kg m² s⁻³.
  • Method: write the defining equation, then substitute units you already know, down to base units.
Quick check
?A spring's stiffness is its spring constant: the force needed per unit change in its length. What is the unit of the spring constant in base units?
Mission 1

Prefixes and powers of ten

  • T tera 10¹² · G giga 10⁹ · M mega 10⁶ · k kilo 10³
  • c centi 10⁻² · m milli 10⁻³ · µ micro 10⁻⁶ · n nano 10⁻⁹ · p pico 10⁻¹²
  • d deci 10⁻¹
  • Standard form: 0.000 52 m = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴ m. One non-zero digit before the point.
  • Prefix on a squared unit: square the factor too. 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, so 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².
Quick check
?The cross-sectional area of a wire is 0.50 mm². What is this area in m²?
Mission 1

Converting units

  • Multiply by a fraction equal to 1, and cancel units.
  • 72 km h⁻¹ = 72 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 20 m s⁻¹.
  • Electronvolt: the energy gained by one electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V. 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • 5.0 eV = 5.0 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 1

Units test an equation

  • In a correct equation, every term has the same base units: the equation is homogeneous.
  • Check v² = u² + 2as:
  • v² and u²: (m s⁻¹)² = m² s⁻². 2as: m s⁻² × m = m² s⁻². All match.
  • Not homogeneous → certainly wrong.
  • Homogeneous → possibly right. Pure numbers (2, ½, π) have no units, so s = ut + at² passes the test but has lost its ½.
Quick check
?Which equation cannot be correct, because it is not homogeneous? (s displacement, u and v velocities, a acceleration, t time)
Mission check

?Pressure is force ÷ area, and its unit is the pascal (Pa). What is the pascal in base units?

Newton = kg m s⁻². Joule = kg m² s⁻². Watt = kg m² s⁻³. Every one is built from base units, by the equation that defines it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can readings agree perfectly with each other and still all be wrong?

Make a guess: Can readings agree perfectly with each other and still all be wrong?

Mission 2

Random and systematic errors

  • Random error: readings scatter unpredictably either side of the true value.
  • Sources: reaction time, judging a moving pointer, small changes in conditions.
  • Reduce its effect: repeat and average; measure a larger quantity.
  • Systematic error: every reading is off in the same direction, by a similar amount.
  • Sources: a zero error, a badly calibrated instrument, reading a scale from the same wrong angle.
  • Repeating does not help. Fix the method: check the zero, calibrate, change the technique.
Mission 2

Precise, accurate and the rest

  • Precise: repeated readings are close together (little random error).
  • Accurate: close to the true value (little systematic and random error).
  • Repeatable: the same person, method and equipment give the same results.
  • Reproducible: a different person, method or equipment gives the same results.
  • Resolution: the smallest change an instrument can show (a rule: 1 mm).
  • Precise does not mean accurate.
Quick check
?A student times a trolley rolling down a ramp with a hand-held stopwatch that reads to 0.01 s. Her five times vary by about 0.2 s. Which change would most reduce the effect of this random error on her result?
Mission 2

Absolute, fractional, percentage

  • Absolute uncertainty: same unit as the value. l = 2.40 ± 0.05 m.
  • Fractional: 0.05 ÷ 2.40 = 0.021. Percentage: × 100 = 2.1%.
  • Where it comes from:
  • One reading on a scale: often ± half the smallest division.
  • A length from two marks (zero and end): ± one division.
  • Digital display: ± the last digit.
  • Repeated readings: ± half the range, i.e. ± (largest − smallest) ÷ 2.
Quick check
?A ball's fall is timed four times: 0.62 s, 0.57 s, 0.65 s, 0.60 s. The stopwatch reads to 0.01 s. Which is the best way to write the result?
Mission 2

Combining uncertainties

  • Add or subtract quantities → add absolute uncertainties.
  • Multiply or divide → add percentage uncertainties.
  • Power: xⁿ → n × percentage uncertainty of x. A square root is a power of ½.
  • Constants (2, π) add no uncertainty.
  • Example: d = 2.40 ± 0.05 m (2.1%), t = 1.60 ± 0.04 s (2.5%).
  • v = d ÷ t = 1.50 m s⁻¹, uncertainty 2.1% + 2.5% = 4.6% → ± 0.07 m s⁻¹.
Mission 2

Worked example: density of a cube

  • m = 50.0 ± 0.1 g → 0.2%.
  • Side L = 2.00 ± 0.02 cm → 1.0%.
  • ρ = m ÷ L³ = 50.0 ÷ 8.00 = 6.25 g cm⁻³ = 6250 kg m⁻³.
  • % uncertainty = 0.2% + 3 × 1.0% = 3.2%.
  • Absolute: 0.032 × 6250 ≈ 200 kg m⁻³.
  • ρ = (6.3 ± 0.2) × 10³ kg m⁻³. The cube's side dominates: measure it more precisely first.
Quick check
?The resistivity of a wire is ρ = RA/L, where A = πd²/4. The percentage uncertainties are: R 3.0%, d 1.0%, L 0.5%. What is the percentage uncertainty in ρ?
Mission 2

Reporting a result: significant figures

  • Give a final answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data.
  • Give an uncertainty to 1 (or 2) significant figures; round the value to match: 1.50 ± 0.07 m s⁻¹.
  • Record raw readings to the resolution of the instrument, consistently: 2.40, 2.45, not 2.4, 2.45.
  • Percentage difference = |measured − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%.
  • If the accepted value lies inside your range (value ± uncertainty), your result is consistent with it.
Mission 2

Evaluating results

  • Anomaly: a result far from the pattern. Check it, repeat it, or leave it out of the mean, and say so.
  • Limitation: name the specific cause, e.g. "the reaction time is large compared with a 0.6 s fall".
  • Improve precision: reduce random error (repeat, larger quantities, data logger).
  • Improve accuracy: remove systematic error (zero check, calibration, better technique).
  • "Be more careful" is never an improvement. Name the change and the error it reduces.
Mission check

?A student measures the diameter of a wire five times with a micrometer: 0.55, 0.56, 0.55, 0.55, 0.56 mm. Later she notices that the micrometer reads 0.03 mm when fully closed. Which statement is correct?

Readings that agree are precise. Only removing systematic error makes them accurate.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a straight line expose a law that is hiding inside a curve?

Make a guess: How can a straight line expose a law that is hiding inside a curve?

Mission 3

Plotting a good graph

  • What you change (independent variable): x-axis. What you measure: y-axis.
  • Label: quantity / unit, e.g. time / s.
  • Scale: points fill more than half the grid; steps of 1, 2 or 5.
  • Plot points as small, sharp crosses.
  • Line of best fit: a straight line or smooth curve, points scattered evenly either side. Never join the dots.
Mission 3

Gradient, intercept, area

  • Gradient = Δy ÷ Δx. Use a large triangle, at least half the line.
  • Gradient has units: y-unit ÷ x-unit.
  • y-intercept: the value of y where x = 0.
  • Area under the line: its units are y-unit × x-unit. On a velocity–time graph it is displacement.
  • On a curve: draw a tangent, then take its gradient.
Quick check
?A graph shows the force F needed to stretch a spring against how far it is stretched, x. F is in N and x in cm. The line passes through the origin and through (8.0 cm, 2.0 N). What is the spring constant in N m⁻¹?
Mission 3

Making a straight line

  • Match the equation to y = mx + c.
  • Object starting from rest, constant acceleration:
  • s = ½at² (a kinematics equation)
  • Displacement against t²: straight line through the origin, gradient ½a.
  • Displacement against t: a curve. The law was hiding in it.
  • Rule: rearrange the law into y = mx + c. Transform the measured variable (y) or the changed variable (x), whichever the law needs. Constants end up in the gradient and intercept.
Mission 3

Worked example: gradient and intercept

  • A ball rolls down a long ramp past light gates at different distances from a start line. Plot v² against distance.
  • v² = u² + 2as → y = v², x = s, gradient = 2a, intercept = u².
  • The best-fit line has gradient 1.6 m s⁻² → acceleration = 1.6 ÷ 2 = 0.80 m s⁻².
  • Intercept: 4.0 m² s⁻² → initial speed = √4.0 = 2.0 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?The intensity I at a distance r from a small source radiating power P equally in all directions is I = P/(4πr²). Which graph is a straight line through the origin, and what is its gradient?
Mission 3

Log graphs: power laws

  • Unknown power: y = kxⁿ.
  • Take logs: lg y = n lg x + lg k.
  • Plot lg y against lg x: straight line, gradient = n, intercept = lg k.
  • Example: pendulum period T and length L. Gradient 0.50 → T ∝ L0.5.
  • Logs have no units: take the log of the number, e.g. lg (L / m).
Mission 3

Log graphs: exponentials

  • Exponential decay: y = y0e−kt.
  • Take natural logs: ln y = ln y0 − kt.
  • Plot ln y against t: straight line, gradient = −k, intercept = ln y0.
  • Capacitor discharge: ln Q = ln Q0 − t/RC. Gradient = −1/RC.
  • Radioactive decay: ln N = ln N0 − λt. Gradient = −λ.
  • A straight ln–t graph tests that the decay is exponential.
Quick check
?A quantity decays as y = y0e−kt. A graph of ln y against t (in s) is a straight line with gradient −0.040 s⁻¹ and y-intercept 3.0. What are k and y0?
Mission 3

Error bars and worst lines

  • Error bar: ± the absolute uncertainty of each point.
  • Best line: passes through all (or nearly all) error bars.
  • Worst line: the steepest or the shallowest line that still passes through all the error bars.
  • Uncertainty in gradient = |best gradient − worst gradient|.
  • % uncertainty = that ÷ best gradient × 100%.
  • Intercept: the same method, using where the two lines cross the y-axis.
Quick check
?A best-fit line has gradient 2.50. The steepest line through all the error bars has gradient 2.65 (same units). What is the percentage uncertainty in the gradient?
Mission check

?The period of a mass on a spring is T = 2π√(m/k), where k is the spring constant. A student varies m and measures T. Which graph gives a straight line through the origin, and what is its gradient?

Rearrange the law into a straight-line form, plot what it tells you to plot, and the curve becomes a line whose gradient and intercept hold the constants.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do physicists time 20 swings of a pendulum instead of just one?

Make a guess: Why do physicists time 20 swings of a pendulum instead of just one?

Mission 4

Planning an experiment

  • Independent variable: the one you change.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure.
  • Control variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them fixed.
  • Take at least 6 values, spread over a wide range.
  • Repeat each reading and average.
  • Plan the graph first: what will you plot to get a straight line?
Mission 4

Choosing instruments

  • Metre rule: 1 mm. Vernier callipers: 0.1 mm (some 0.02 mm). Micrometer: 0.01 mm.
  • Top-pan balance: 0.01 g. Stopwatch: 0.01 s, but reaction time (about 0.2 s) dominates.
  • Light gates and a timer or data logger: times to 1 ms or better, no human reaction. A logger records many readings quickly.
  • Analogue meter: read the pointer face-on to avoid parallax. Digital: ± the last digit.
  • Pick an instrument whose resolution is small compared with the value measured.
Mission 4

Micrometer and vernier

  • Micrometer: first check the zero reading with the jaws closed.
  • Close it with the ratchet, so the force is the same each time.
  • Reading = sleeve (to 0.5 mm) + thimble division × 0.01 mm.
  • Sleeve 5.5 mm, thimble 23 → 5.73 mm.
  • Vernier: main scale up to the vernier's zero + the vernier mark that lines up × 0.1 mm.
  • Measure a wire at several points and in two directions at each. Average.
Quick check
?On a pair of vernier callipers, the vernier's zero lies between the 31 mm and 32 mm marks of the main scale. Vernier mark 6 lines up exactly with a main-scale mark. What is the reading?
Mission 4

Measure many, then divide

  • One swing of a pendulum: about 1.4 s. Hand timing: about ± 0.2 s. That is 14%.
  • Time 20 swings: about 28 s, still ± 0.2 s. That is 0.7%.
  • T = 28.0 ÷ 20 = 1.40 s; uncertainty 0.2 ÷ 20 = ± 0.01 s.
  • The same reading uncertainty is shared out over many cycles.
  • Same idea: weigh 50 ball bearings and divide by 50.
  • Start and stop timing as the bob passes a marker at the centre of the swing, where it moves fastest.
Mission 4

Using an oscilloscope

  • Vertical axis: y-gain, in V per division. Horizontal: time-base, in s (or ms) per division.
  • Peak voltage = divisions from the centre line to a peak × y-gain.
  • 3.0 div × 2.0 V div⁻¹ = 6.0 V peak.
  • Period = divisions for one full cycle × time-base.
  • 4.0 div × 5.0 ms div⁻¹ = 20 ms → f = 1/T = 50 Hz.
  • A d.c. voltage shifts the trace up or down: a horizontal line.
Quick check
?One complete cycle of a wave on an oscilloscope screen spans 2.5 divisions. The time-base is set to 2.0 ms div⁻¹. What is the frequency of the signal?
Mission 4

Safety and records

  • Before starting: list each hazard, its risk, and how you will control it.
  • Lasers: never look along the beam; watch for reflections.
  • Radioactive sources: handle with tongs, keep them away from the body, minimise the time out of the lead box.
  • Stretched wires: wear eye protection. Heavy masses: clamp the stand, sand tray below.
  • Record raw data at once in a table: units in the headings, every reading to the instrument's resolution.
  • Endorsement: keep a dated lab record; cite any source you use.
Mission 4

Conclusions and evaluation

  • Conclusion: state what the data show, only within the range tested. Refer to the graph.
  • Compare with theory, allowing for the uncertainties.
  • Evaluate: find the largest source of uncertainty and say why.
  • Improve with a specific change and the error it reduces: "light gates remove reaction time", not "be more careful".
  • Systematic error → change the method or correct it. Random error → repeat, or measure a larger quantity.
Mission 4

Practicals in every topic

  • Each named practical is taught in its own topic mission, using the skills here.
  • Practical activity groups PAG1–PAG12. The endorsement (CPAC) is assessed separately.
Quick check
?A student investigates how the time t for a ball to fall from rest depends on the drop height h. (h = ½gt², with g constant.) Which plan is best?
Mission check

?A student has a ruler with 1 mm divisions and wants the thickness of one sheet of paper, about 0.1 mm. Which choice and reason are both correct?

The reading uncertainty stays about the same however much you measure. Measure 20 swings, or 200 sheets, and it becomes a tiny fraction of the total.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every derived unit reduces to base units (kg, m, s, A, K, mol) through its defining equation: N = kg m s⁻², J = kg m² s⁻², W = kg m² s⁻³.
  • Prefixes: square or cube the factor with the unit (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²).
  • An equation whose terms have different base units must be wrong; matching units do not prove it right.

Mission 2

  • Random error scatters readings (repeat and average); systematic error shifts them all (fix the method). Precise ≠ accurate.
  • Uncertainty from repeats = half the range.
  • Add absolute uncertainties for + and −; add percentages for × and ÷; multiply by n for a power.

Mission 3

  • Gradient = Δy/Δx with units; area = y × x; tangent for a curve. Linearise by rearranging the law into a straight-line form.
  • lg y against lg x gives a power n.
  • ln y against t gives an exponential's −k.
  • Gradient uncertainty = |best − worst| gradient, worst line through all error bars.

Mission 4

  • Plan: independent, dependent and control variables; at least 6 values over a wide range; repeats; a straight-line graph.
  • Measure many cycles or items and divide: the same reading uncertainty becomes a smaller percentage.
  • Micrometer: check the zero, use the ratchet.
  • Oscilloscope: divisions × y-gain for voltage, divisions × time-base for period.
