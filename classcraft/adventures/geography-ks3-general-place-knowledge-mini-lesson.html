What is "place knowledge"?

Comparing places, not just naming them

A place is a part of the Earth's surface that has its own character. Place knowledge is the geographer's skill of comparing two or more places to explain how they are alike, how they differ, and how they are linked.

  • Similarities — things the places share (e.g. both grow crops, both have a capital city).
  • Differences — things that set them apart (e.g. climate, average income).
  • Links — connections between them (e.g. trade, migration, tourism, colonial history).

Key idea: good comparison is always like-for-like — you compare climate with climate, economy with economy. Comparing "Kenya's climate" with "London's population" tells you nothing useful.

Locate before you compare

Always start by locating your places on a world map. Notice how latitude (distance from the Equator) already hints at climate.

Region in Africa

Kenya — the Nairobi region 🇰🇪

Kenya is a country in East Africa. Our focus region is around the capital, Nairobi.

  • Physical features: highlands and savanna grassland; the Great Rift Valley; Mount Kenya (5,199 m).
  • Climate: tropical highland — warm all year (about 18–26 °C in Nairobi) with two rainy seasons.
  • Population: Kenya has about 55 million people; Nairobi is a fast-growing city of over 4 million.
  • Economy: important primary sector (farming: tea, coffee, cut flowers) plus growing tourism and tech ("Silicon Savannah").
  • Culture & development: many languages (Swahili & English official); a developing economy with a lower GDP per person than the UK.

Careful: a region is not a whole continent. "Nairobi" tells us far more than "Africa" — Africa has 54 countries and huge variety, from deserts to rainforests.

Region in Asia

India — the Kerala region 🇮🇳

India is a huge country in South Asia. Our focus region is Kerala, on the tropical south-west coast.

  • Physical features: a coastal plain with lagoons ("backwaters"), rising to the Western Ghats mountains; the Arabian Sea to the west.
  • Climate: tropical monsoon — hot (25–33 °C) with very heavy monsoon rains from June to September.
  • Population: India has about 1.4 billion people; Kerala alone has around 35 million and is densely settled.
  • Economy: farming (rice, spices, coconut, tea), fishing, tourism, and money sent home by workers abroad (remittances).
  • Culture & development: Malayalam language; a mix of religions; Kerala has unusually high literacy and life expectancy for a developing region.

Careful: Kerala is not the same as "India" or "Asia". Northern India is much drier and cooler in winter — one region can never stand for a whole continent.

Comparison region — UK

The UK — London & the South-East 🇬🇧

The UK is a developed country in north-west Europe. It's our familiar "measuring stick" for comparison.

  • Physical features: gentle lowlands, the River Thames, a mild coastline; no mountains near London.
  • Climate: temperate maritime — cool and changeable (about 4–23 °C), with rain spread fairly evenly all year.
  • Population: the UK has about 68 million people; London is a global city of around 9 million.
  • Economy: mostly tertiary and quaternary sectors — finance, services, media, technology; little farming employment.
  • Culture & development: very diverse and multicultural; a high GDP per person and long life expectancy.

Why compare with the UK? Using a place you already know helps you spot what is genuinely different (and what is surprisingly similar) about Kenya and Kerala.

Both at once

Places share similarities AND differences

A common mistake is to think two places are either "the same" or "totally different". In reality, almost every pair of places has both.

  • Similarities (Kenya & UK): both have a large capital city, both grow crops, both have coasts and rivers.
  • Differences: climate, average wealth, and which economic sector employs most people.
  • Kerala & UK similarity: both have high literacy — a reminder that "developing" doesn't mean "poor at everything".

Avoid stereotypes: don't assume an African or Asian region is only about poverty or wildlife. Nairobi has a booming tech scene; Kerala has world-class education. Look for the full picture.

Measuring development

How "developed" is a place?

Development means how wealthy and comfortable life is in a place. Geographers use indicators to compare it fairly:

  • GDP per person — average wealth made per person per year.
  • Life expectancy — how long people typically live.
  • Literacy rate — the share of adults who can read and write.
HDI = wealth + health + educationthe Human Development Index combines income, life expectancy and schooling into one score from 0 to 1

Watch out: one indicator alone can mislead. Kerala has a lower income than the UK but very high literacy and life expectancy — so it is more developed than its income alone suggests.

Common mistakes

Three traps to avoid

  • A region is not a whole continent. "Kerala" ≠ "Asia"; "Nairobi" ≠ "Africa". Continents are hugely varied.
  • Compare like-for-like. Match climate to climate, economy to economy — never climate to population.
  • Places have similarities AND differences. It's never "all the same" or "totally different".

Bonus trap: don't rely on stereotypes. Developing regions can lead the world in some things (Kerala's literacy, Nairobi's tech). Judge places on evidence, using indicators.
