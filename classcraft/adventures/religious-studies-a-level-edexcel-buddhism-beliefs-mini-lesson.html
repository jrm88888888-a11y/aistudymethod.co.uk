Mission 1

The Wheel of Life

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and craving, with no first beginning that can be found.
  • Tibetan Buddhists paint it as the Wheel of Life (Sanskrit bhavachakra, "wheel of becoming"), often at a monastery entrance.
  • From the centre out: hub (three poisons) → ring of beings rising and falling → six realms → rim of twelve links.
  • The wheel is gripped by Yama, lord of death: all of samsara is subject to impermanence and death.
  • Outside the wheel, in the upper corner, a figure points to the moon or a Dhamma wheel: release is possible.
Mission 1

The hub: three poisons

  • At the centre, three animals chase each other, each biting the next one's tail:
  • cockerel: greed, craving (Pali lobha or raga);
  • snake: hatred, aversion (dosa; Sanskrit dvesha);
  • pig: delusion, ignorance (moha).
  • Delusion is often called the root: not seeing anicca and anatta, beings grasp and push away.
  • The Buddha's Fire Sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 35.28, paraphrase): everything is burning with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion.
  • Their opposites are the wholesome roots: generosity, loving-kindness, wisdom.

Mission 1

Rising and falling

  • Around the hub, a ring split in two:
  • light half: beings rising to higher rebirths through skilful (kusala) actions;
  • dark half: beings falling to lower rebirths through unskilful (akusala) actions.
  • In the painting, and in Buddhist teaching, karma moves beings up and down the wheel, but never off it. Even the highest rebirth is still samsara.
  • Leaving the wheel needs nibbana: uprooting the three poisons, not just earning good karma.
Mission 1

The six realms

  • As Buddhists describe them:
  • Gods (devas): long, blissful lives; risk pride and complacency; they fall when merit runs out.
  • Titans (asuras): powerful but driven by envy; endless conflict with the gods.
  • Humans: a mix of pleasure and pain; the best realm for liberation.
  • Animals: driven by instinct, fear and ignorance; little chance to practise.
  • Hungry ghosts (pretas): huge bellies, tiny throats: craving that can never be satisfied.
  • Hell beings: intense pain, linked to hatred and cruelty. Hells are not eternal: beings leave when the karma is used up.
  • A common Tibetan pairing: gods–pride, titans–jealousy, humans–desire, animals–ignorance, hungry ghosts–greed, hells–anger.
  • Many Buddhists read the realms both as places of rebirth and as states of mind we pass through now: craving can be the ghost realm, rage the hell realm.
Mission 1

Different Buddhist emphases

  • Pali Canon: often lists five destinations (e.g. Majjhima Nikaya 12): hell, animals, hungry ghosts, humans, gods. Titans are added as a sixth in other lists.
  • The painted wheel is mainly a Tibetan image, though an early Indian version survives at Ajanta. It is rarely painted in Theravada temples.
  • Theravada: the three poisons are uprooted by the path; their end is nibbana (see Mission 5).
  • Mahayana and Vajrayana: they are also uprooted, but tantric teaching adds that their energy can be transformed into wisdom (e.g. anger into clear, "mirror-like" wisdom).
  • Contemporary teachers apply the poisons to consumerism (greed), polarised politics (hatred) and misinformation (delusion).

For many Buddhists, the wheel works on two scales at once: lives across time, and moods within a day. Either way, the poisons at the hub keep it turning.

Mission 2

Why conduct matters

  • Good conduct (sila) is the first of the three trainings and the base of the whole path (see Mission 6).
  • It is not obedience to a creator: no god commands it. It is training: conduct that leads to less suffering for oneself and others.
  • One verse sums up the Buddha's teaching in three parts:

> "Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened." (Dhammapada 183, Müller 1881)

  • "Sin" is Müller's 1881 word; Buddhists today say unskilful or unwholesome action, not offence against God.
  • Ethics matters for this life (calm, trust, no remorse), for future lives (karma) and for awakening (a clear mind).
Mission 2

Intention is karma

  • "It is intention (cetana), monks, that I call karma": having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind (paraphrase, Nibbedhika Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 6.63).
  • So karma is not the outward act alone: it is the will behind the act.
  • Two people may do the same thing (give money) with different intentions (kindness; showing off): the karmic weight differs.
  • An accident with no intention to harm is not karmically the same as deliberate harm.
  • Intention shapes the mind that acts again: habits form, and character is built.
  • Pali texts write kamma.
  • With no fixed self, responsibility follows the stream of intentions: the one who acts and the one who meets the result are "neither the same nor another" (Questions of King Milinda, paraphrase). Its example: a mango thief is still guilty, because the stolen fruit grew from what the owner planted.
Mission 2Read the source

The mind comes first

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage." (Dhammapada 1, Müller 1881)

  • Verse 2 is the pair: speak or act "with a pure thought" and happiness follows "like a shadow that never leaves him".

Q1 What does the image of the wheel and the ox say about how results follow actions?

Show answer

They follow as surely as a cart wheel follows the ox: the result is tied to the mind that acted.

Q2 Where does the verse place the source of good and bad action?

Show answer

In the mind: thought comes first, then speech and action.

Mission 2

Kusala and akusala

  • Kusala: healthy, skilful, wholesome. Akusala: unhealthy, unskilful, unwholesome.
  • The test is the root in the mind, not a rule. Three akusala roots: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa), delusion (moha): the three poisons (see Mission 1).
  • Three kusala roots: non-greed (generosity), non-hatred (loving-kindness), non-delusion (wisdom).
  • Kusala acts lead to happiness and are praised by the wise; akusala acts lead to harm for oneself and others (Kalama Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 3.65, paraphrase).
  • "Healthy" fits the image of the Buddha as a doctor: akusala states are a sickness of the mind.

Mission 2

Keown: kusala as virtue

  • Damien Keown, The Nature of Buddhist Ethics (1992): an action is kusala because it expresses and builds virtue, not because its results are good.
  • He compares this with virtue ethics: the goal is a person whose intentions are wholly rooted in generosity, kindness and wisdom.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944): wholesome roots are trained through sila, so good conduct becomes natural rather than forced (paraphrase of his teaching on the path).
  • Other readings: some scholars see karma-based ethics as closer to consequentialism (kusala because it leads to happiness).
  • Keown and Bodhi are your named scholars for the ethics of the path.

Mission 3

Three marks of existence

  • The Buddha taught three marks (Pali tilakkhana) of conditioned existence:
  • anicca: impermanence; dukkha: suffering, unsatisfactoriness; anatta: not-self.
  • The classic summary (Dhammapada 277–279): all conditioned things (sankhara) are impermanent; all conditioned things are dukkha; all dhammas are not-self.
  • Note the shift: dhammas is wider than sankhara. For Theravada, nibbana is unconditioned: not impermanent, not dukkha, but still not-self.
Mission 3

Translating dukkha

  • No English word fits. Common translations:
  • suffering (the usual one); unsatisfactoriness; pain, ill; stress (Thanissaro Bhikkhu).
  • "Suffering" is too strong for a mild let-down; "unsatisfactoriness" is too weak for grief or torture. Dukkha covers both.
  • A traditional explanation (folk etymology, not certain): du- (bad) + kha (axle-hole): a wheel that fits badly, so the ride always jars.
  • The Buddhist point is not that every moment hurts, but that nothing conditioned can give lasting, secure satisfaction.
Mission 3Read the source

The first truth at the Deer Park

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta ("Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion"), Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (paraphrase): the noble truth of dukkha is this: birth, ageing, sickness and death are dukkha; sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha; being joined with what is unpleasant, being parted from what is pleasant, and not getting what one wants are dukkha. In brief, the five clinging-aggregates are dukkha.

And Dhammapada 278 (Müller, 1881):

> "'All created things are grief and pain,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity."

  • "Passive in pain" renders a Pali word meaning turns away from it, grows disenchanted: seeing dukkha is a step to freedom, not despair.

Q1 Which items on the list can no one avoid, however lucky?

Show answer

Birth, ageing, death; parting from what is pleasant. Sickness for almost everyone.

Q2 Why does the list end with the five aggregates?

Show answer

Dukkha is not just bad events: it is built into the conditioned, grasping personality itself.

  • At the end of the sermon Kondañña, one of the five listeners, sees that whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing (paraphrase): anicca in one line.
Mission 3

Three kinds of dukkha

  • Dukkha-dukkha: the dukkha of pain itself: physical and mental pain, illness, grief.
  • Viparinama-dukkha: the dukkha of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • Sankhara-dukkha: the dukkha of conditioned existence: everything depends on causes that shift, so nothing gives a secure footing. The subtlest kind; often seen only through insight meditation.
  • Source: e.g. Dukkhata Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 38.14); explained by Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga XVI.

Mission 3

Anicca: gross and subtle

  • Gross (obvious) impermanence: seasons turn, bodies age, buildings crumble, kingdoms fall, everyone dies.
  • Subtle or momentary impermanence: every bodily and mental event arises and ceases moment by moment. What looks solid is a fast series, like the frames of a film or a flame renewed each instant.
  • The Abhidhamma and its commentators, including Buddhaghosa (5th century CE, Visuddhimagga, "The Path of Purification"), analyse experience into momentary events.
  • In Buddhaghosa's account of insight, the meditator comes to see formations breaking up moment by moment (Visuddhimagga XXI, paraphrase).
  • Anicca is not bad news in itself: change also makes growth, healing and awakening possible.
Mission 3

How the marks link

  • The Buddha's own chain (e.g. Samyutta Nikaya 22.15, paraphrase): what is impermanent is dukkha; what is dukkha is not-self.
  • Anicca → dukkha: change is not painful by itself; dukkha comes from craving and clinging to what changes.
  • Dukkha → anatta: what is unstable and beyond our control cannot be a lasting self ("this is mine, this I am, this is my self").
  • So the three marks are one insight from three angles, not three separate facts.
  • Your spec links the marks to the rejection of a permanent self and of an eternal creator God: if everything conditioned is impermanent, nothing conditioned is an eternal ground. The Buddha's path does not depend on a creator.

Mission 3

From anicca to emptiness

  • Early Abhidharma schools analysed experience into momentary dharmas; some (e.g. the Sarvastivada) held that each dharma has its own own-nature (svabhava).
  • Mahayana wisdom texts (Prajnaparamita) and Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE) pushed anicca further: even dharmas are empty (sunya) of own-nature.
  • Emptiness (sunyata) is not nothingness: things exist dependently, arising from conditions, with no fixed core.
  • The Heart Sutra: "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (short phrase in common use).
  • So emptiness can be read as anicca and dependent origination taken all the way down. Full study.
  • In Tibetan teaching, contemplating impermanence and death is one of the "four thoughts that turn the mind" to the Dharma. Your spec pairs Buddhaghosa (Theravada) with T. W. Rinpoche, a modern Tibetan teacher: compare their readings of the marks.
Mission 3

Is Buddhism pessimistic?

  • The charge: the first teaching is suffering; even pleasure is counted as dukkha; the goal is to end rebirth. Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860, The World as Will and Representation, 1818/1844) admired Buddhism as agreeing with his pessimism. Many 19th-century Western readers took nibbana to mean annihilation.
  • The reply: Walpola Rahula (1907–1997), What the Buddha Taught (1959): Buddhism is "neither pessimistic nor optimistic" but realistic (short phrase).
  • Like a doctor, the Buddha names an illness in order to cure it: the third truth says dukkha can end (see Mission 4).
  • Joy has a place: calm and happiness in meditation; "Nirvana the highest happiness" (Dhammapada 204, Müller).
Mission 3Evaluate

Do the three marks describe reality?

  • Claim: The three marks are an accurate account of reality.
  • For: Science finds change everywhere: cells, stars, even "solid" matter.
  • For: Experience confirms that pleasures fade and that we cannot control body or mind.
  • Against: Some things seem lasting in human terms (mathematical truths, love that endures); "all is dukkha" seems overstated.
  • Against: If all is impermanent, what about nibbana? Theravada answers: nibbana is unconditioned, so outside the first two marks. Critics say the system then has one exception.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the claim descriptive or a tool for practice? Does it fit experience in meditation as well as ordinary life?

Mission 4

The first sermon

  • After his awakening, the Buddha walked to the Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath), near Varanasi (Benares), to his five former companions in asceticism.
  • The sermon is the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion" (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).
  • It opens with the Middle Way: avoid both indulgence in sense pleasure and harsh self-torment.
  • Then it sets out the Four Noble Truths.
  • One listener, Kondanna, understood: whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing. He became the first disciple to "see the Dhamma".
Mission 4

Four truths, four tasks

  • In the sermon, the Buddha teaches:
  • Dukkha: there is suffering, unsatisfactoriness. Task: fully understand it.
  • Samudaya (origin): dukkha arises from craving (tanha). Task: abandon it.
  • Nirodha (cessation): dukkha ends when craving ends. Task: realise it.
  • Magga (path): the Noble Eightfold Path leads there. Task: develop it.
  • In the sutta the Buddha claims full awakening only after knowing each truth, its task, and that the task is done: twelve aspects in all.
  • They are "noble" (ariya) truths: truths as seen by the noble ones, who have realised them.
  • Your spec's terms: dukkha, samudaya/tanha, nirodha, magga.
Mission 4

The doctor analogy

  • A traditional comparison treats the Buddha as a physician:
  • Illness = dukkha · Cause = tanha · Cure is possible = nirodha · Prescription = magga.
  • Buddhaghosa uses it in the Visuddhimagga (XVI): the truths are like a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine.
  • What it teaches: the Buddha diagnoses in order to heal; the first truth is not the last word.
  • Its limits: the patient must take the medicine personally. No one can be cured by someone else's practice.
Mission 4Read the source

The second and third truths

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (paraphrase):

  • Origin: it is craving that leads to renewed existence, bound up with delight and lust, seeking pleasure now here, now there: craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.
  • Cessation: the complete fading away and ending of that same craving: giving it up, letting it go, being free of it.

Q1 Does the sutta say pleasure itself causes dukkha?

Show answer

No: craving, the thirst that chases pleasure "here and there", is the origin.

Show answer

Mission 4

Three kinds of craving

  • Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: food, comfort, sex, entertainment, possessions.
  • Bhava-tanha: craving for existence or becoming: to be someone, to be admired, to live on for ever.
  • Vibhava-tanha: craving for non-existence: to get rid of what we dislike, even to stop existing.
  • All three grasp at what is impermanent, so all three bring dukkha: the pleasure fades, the identity cracks, the escape never arrives.
  • Vibhava-tanha shows that pushing away is craving too, not freedom from it.
Mission 4

Tanha in the chain of causes

  • In dependent origination, tanha is link 8 of the twelve nidanas: conditioned by feeling, it conditions grasping.
  • So the second truth is a short form of the whole chain: craving is the most practical point to work on, while ignorance is its root.
  • Ending craving needs wisdom, not just willpower: craving thrives on not seeing anicca and anatta.

Mission 4

Nirodha and magga

  • Nirodha: the "cutting off" of craving through detachment: no longer gripping what changes. This is nibbana (see Mission 5).
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path, in three trainings: wisdom (right view, right intention), ethics (right speech, action, livelihood), meditation (right effort, mindfulness, concentration). Full study.
  • Damien Keown (Buddhism: A Very Short Introduction, 1996) presents the Four Noble Truths as the framework that holds the rest of Buddhist teaching together.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), The Noble Eightfold Path (1984): the path is the fourth truth put into practice; its factors are developed together, as components, not climbed one by one.
Mission 4

Disciples and arhats

  • After the Buddha's second sermon (on not-self, Samyutta Nikaya 22.59), all five companions became arhats ("worthy ones"). The Vinaya says there were now six arhats in the world, the Buddha included.
  • Disciples who follow the teaching pass through four stages: stream-entry, once-return, non-return, arhat.
  • Arhat: has ended craving and ignorance, realised nibbana, and will not be reborn.
  • Buddha and arhat both realise nibbana; a Buddha discovers and teaches the path, an arhat follows it.
  • Enlightenment (awakening, bodhi) is full, direct knowledge of the Four Noble Truths. Mahayana schools add the bodhisattva, who aims at full Buddhahood for the sake of all beings.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is Buddhist detachment positive or negative?

  • Claim: The ideal of detachment is negative: it makes people cold and withdrawn.
  • For: Monastics leave family life; the goal is to stop "being attached" even to loved ones.
  • For: If craving for existence is a fault, it may seem that caring about life is too.
  • Against: Detachment means non-clinging, not indifference: one can love without trying to own or freeze what one loves.
  • Against: It is paired with metta (loving-kindness) and karuna (compassion); the Buddha and his arhats taught for decades out of compassion.
  • Judgement criteria: What exactly is given up: people, or grasping? How do detached Buddhists actually treat others? Is non-clinging possible for lay people?

Mission 5

Blowing out

  • Nibbana (Pali; Sanskrit nirvana) means "blowing out" or "extinguishing", as a flame goes out.
  • What goes out are the three fires of greed, hatred and delusion (see Mission 1). Samyutta Nikaya 38.1 (paraphrase): the destruction of greed, hatred and delusion: this is called nibbana.
  • It is the third noble truth, nirodha: craving "cut off" through detachment, letting go of what changes (see Mission 4).
  • It is not the destruction of a self: by anatta, there was no lasting self to destroy.
Mission 5

In this life and after death

  • Nibbana with remainder (sa-upadisesa): reached in this life. The fires are out, but the "remainder", the five khandhas, continues: the awakened one still has a body and feelings.
  • Nibbana without remainder (anupadisesa): at the death of an awakened one. The khandhas end and there is no rebirth.
  • Source: Itivuttaka 44 (paraphrase) names these two "elements" of nibbana.
  • The Buddha: awakening at Bodh Gaya (about age 35) = nibbana with remainder; death at Kusinara (about age 80) = parinibbana, final nibbana (Digha Nikaya 16).

Mission 5

The unconditioned

  • Everything in samsara is conditioned: it arises from causes and so changes.
  • Nibbana is unconditioned (asankhata): not produced by causes, not subject to arising and passing.
  • Udana 8.3 (paraphrase): there is an unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned; if there were not, there would be no escape from the born, become, made and conditioned.
  • So the path does not manufacture nibbana; it removes what obscures it, and nibbana is realised.
Mission 5

Where does the fire go?

  • In Majjhima Nikaya 72 (paraphrase), the wanderer Vacchagotta asks where an awakened one goes after death: is he reborn? not reborn? both? neither? To each, the Buddha says the question does not apply.
  • He asks: when a fire goes out, which direction did it go: east, west, north or south? Vacchagotta: the question does not fit; the fire went out because its fuel ran out.
  • So too the awakened one: no longer reckoned by the khandhas, "deep, immeasurable, hard to fathom, like the ocean" (paraphrase).
  • Scripture speaks of nibbana in three ways:
  • negatively: the end of craving, the unborn, cessation;
  • positively: peace, the highest happiness (Dhammapada 204, Müller), the cool;
  • by images: the further shore, an island, a refuge.
Mission 5

Theravada and Mahayana emphases

  • Theravada: nibbana is the unconditioned, realised by the arhat; after parinibbana the awakened one is neither "somewhere" nor simply annihilated: the question does not apply.
  • Mahayana: a Buddha does not rest in a nirvana apart from the world but keeps helping beings: non-abiding nirvana (apratisthita-nirvana).
  • Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE): there is not the slightest difference between samsara and nirvana when both are seen as empty (Mulamadhyamakakarika 25, paraphrase;).
  • The Lotus Sutra treats the arhat's nirvana as a resting place on the way to Buddhahood (the "magic city").
  • Buddhist texts say nibbana's form cannot be described, but similes can point to its qualities (coolness, peace).

Mission 6

The Middle Way

  • In his first sermon (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11) the Buddha names two extremes to avoid (paraphrase):
  • Indulgence in sense pleasures: low, worldly, unprofitable.
  • Self-mortification (harsh asceticism): painful, unprofitable.
  • Between them, the Middle Way leads to calm, insight, awakening and nirvana. The Buddha says that Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • It mirrors his life: palace luxury, then six years of harsh ascetic practice, including extreme fasting, then awakening after accepting food.
  • The path is the fourth Noble Truth: magga, the way to the end of dukkha (see Mission 4).
  • Your spec writes nibbana, dhamma, arahant.
Mission 6

Eight factors, three trainings

  • Each factor is samma: "right" in the sense of complete, fitting, skilful; not "right" as opposed to a rule broken.
  • Wisdom (panna): 1 right view (understanding the Four Noble Truths) · 2 right intention (renunciation, goodwill, harmlessness).
  • Ethics / morality (sila): 3 right speech · 4 right action · 5 right livelihood.
  • Meditation (samadhi): 6 right effort · 7 right mindfulness · 8 right concentration.
  • The nun Dhammadinna teaches that the path is included in the three trainings, not the reverse: each factor sorts under one training (Culavedalla Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 44).
  • Practice usually begins with ethics, which steadies the mind for meditation, which ripens into wisdom; but the list opens with view.
  • Your spec: the threefold way (ethics, meditation, wisdom).

Mission 6

Inter-reliant, not linear

  • "Path" does not mean eight steps in a row. In the Mahacattarisaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 117) the Buddha says (paraphrase):
  • Right view comes first: it lets you tell right intention, speech, action and livelihood from wrong.
  • Right view, right effort and right mindfulness "run around" each of the other factors: you see what is skilful, strive for it, and stay aware of it.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), The Noble Eightfold Path (1984): the factors are better called components than steps; with maturity all eight are practised together.
  • A common image: a spiral. Ethics, meditation and wisdom feed each other, and each turn goes deeper.
Mission 6

Speech, action, livelihood

  • Defined in the Magga-vibhanga Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 45.8) (paraphrase):
  • Right speech: no lying, no divisive speech, no harsh speech, no idle chatter.
  • Right action: no killing, no taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct.
  • Right livelihood: giving up wrong ways of earning a living. Lay followers should not trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poison (Vanijja Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.177).
  • These overlap with the five precepts. Practice today: a Buddhist may refuse work in an arms factory, avoid gossip at work, or choose a caring profession.
  • Your spec names these three as the ethical principles of the path.
Mission 6

Metta, karuna, khanti

  • Metta (loving-kindness): goodwill to all beings without exception. The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8) compares it to a mother protecting her only child with her life (paraphrase).
  • Karuna (compassion): the wish that beings be free from suffering. Metta and karuna are two of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abidings"), with sympathetic joy and equanimity.
  • Khanti (patience, forbearance): not repaying harm with hatred. In the simile of the saw, even someone being cut apart by bandits who gives way to hate "is not following my teaching" (paraphrase, Kakacupama Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 21).

> "The Awakened call patience the highest penance, long-suffering the highest Nirvana" (Dhammapada 184, Müller 1881)

  • These qualities express right intention and are trained in meditation.
  • Khanti is one of the ten perfections in Theravada; in Mahayana, kshanti is one of the six perfections.

Mission 6Diversity

One path, different settings

  • Theravada: the path leads to arahantship. Ethics, meditation and wisdom are often taught in that order (Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga, 5th century CE, is structured as sila, samadhi, panna).
  • Mahayana: the path sits inside the bodhisattva way; the six perfections (giving, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom) map onto the three trainings. Karuna stands beside wisdom as one of the two wings of awakening.
  • Zen: the path is lived in daily tasks and zazen rather than taught as a list; Pure Land: many rely on Amida's vow more than on their own effort.
  • Contemporary: the path is taught in the West as a practical ethic for lay people; Engaged Buddhists apply right livelihood and right action to social issues.
Mission 6

Keown: is ethics left behind?

  • An older view (e.g. Winston King, Melford Spiro) held that ethics is only a raft: useful for the crossing, then left behind by the enlightened.
  • Damien Keown, The Nature of Buddhist Ethics (1992), argues against this: ethics and wisdom are both part of the goal. Nirvana is the full perfection of virtue as well as knowledge.
  • Keown compares Buddhist ethics to virtue ethics (Aristotle): the path forms character, and the arahant is the model of perfect virtue.
  • Bodhi: the path's ethical factors are not preliminary; they are kept, made effortless, in the awakened person.
  • Critics: some verses praise one "who in this world is above good and evil" (Dhammapada 412, Müller 1881), which suggests ethics is transcended. Keown reads such verses as going beyond merit-seeking goodness, not beyond virtue.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is any part of the path more important?

  • Claim: Some factors of the Eightfold Path matter more than others.
  • For: Right view comes first and guides the rest (Majjhima Nikaya 117).
  • For: Meditation (samadhi) is where insight happens; without it ethics may stay on the surface.
  • Against: The factors are inter-reliant components (Bodhi); remove one and the others weaken.
  • Against: Ethics is the foundation most lay Buddhists actually practise; and on Keown's view it is part of the goal itself.
  • Judgement criteria: What does the text say about order? What do lay and monastic Buddhists practise? Is the path a moral guide for every Buddhist, including Pure Land and Zen?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Wheel of Life (bhavachakra): three poisons at the hub (cockerel greed, snake hatred, pig delusion), rising and falling beings, six realms, twelve links, all gripped by Yama; a figure outside points to release.

Mission 2

  • Good conduct (sila) is training, not obedience; it is the base of meditation and wisdom.
  • Intention (cetana) is karma: acts are kusala (healthy) or akusala (unhealthy) according to their roots in greed, hatred, delusion or their opposites.

Mission 3

  • Three marks: anicca, dukkha, anatta. Dukkha has three kinds: pain, the dukkha of change, and the dukkha of conditioned existence.
  • Anicca works at a gross level (ageing, death) and a subtle, momentary level; Buddhists teach that craving for what changes is what turns anicca into dukkha.

Mission 4

  • The Deer Park sermon (SN 56.11): dukkha (understand), samudaya: craving (abandon), nirodha (realise), magga: the Eightfold Path (develop).
  • The doctor analogy: illness, cause, cure, prescription.
  • Three cravings: for sense pleasures, for existence, for non-existence.
  • Tanha is link 8 of the twelve nidanas.
  • The five companions became the first arhats: disciples who realise nibbana by following the path the Buddha discovered.

Mission 5

  • In Buddhist teaching, nibbana ("blowing out") is the end of greed, hatred and delusion: with remainder in this life, without remainder (parinibbana) at death.
  • It is unconditioned; questions about where an awakened one "goes" do not apply. Mahayana stresses non-abiding nirvana.

Mission 6

  • The Middle Way avoids indulgence and self-mortification; the Buddha identifies it with the Noble Eightfold Path, the fourth Noble Truth.
  • Eight factors in three trainings: wisdom (view, intention), ethics (speech, action, livelihood), meditation (effort, mindfulness, concentration); practised together, not one by one.
  • Metta, karuna and khanti are the qualities the path cultivates; Keown argues ethics is part of the goal, not just a raft.
