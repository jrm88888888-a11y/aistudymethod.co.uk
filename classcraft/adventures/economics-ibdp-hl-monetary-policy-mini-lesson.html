Mission 1

Mission 1: When a bank lends you money, whose money are you actually borrowing?

Make a guess: When a bank lends someone 10,000, where does that money come from?

Mission 1

What banks do

  • Commercial (retail) banks:
    • Accept deposits and keep them safe.
    • Make loans to households and firms.
    • Run payments: cards, transfers.
  • They channel savings into investment: firms borrow to build and expand.
  • Borrowers pay interest: the price of credit. Banks charge more for riskier loans.
  • Collateral: an asset the bank can take if a loan is not repaid (e.g. a house for a mortgage).
Mission 1

A bank's balance sheet

  • Liabilities: what the bank owes.
    • Customers' deposits (the bank owes them on demand or at a set date).
    • Borrowing from other banks.
  • Assets: what the bank owns or is owed.
    • Reserves: cash and deposits at the central bank. Most liquid, earn least.
    • Liquid assets: e.g. short-term government bills.
    • Loans to customers. Least liquid, earn most.
    • Investments such as bonds.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities. It absorbs losses.
Mission 1

Loans create deposits

  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit.
  • That deposit is new money: no one else's balance went down.
  • Example (illustrative): a bank lends 10,000 to a café owner.
    • Assets: loans +10,000.
    • Liabilities: deposits +10,000.
    • Broad money rises by 10,000 at once.
  • When loans are repaid, deposits fall: money is destroyed.
  • What limits lending? Demand for loans, the risk of non-payment, the interest rate set by the central bank, and capital and liquidity rules.
  • Say: "banks create deposits when they lend" — not: "banks just lend out savers' money".
Mission 1

The money multiplier model

  • A textbook model of how deposits spread through the banking system.
  • Fractional reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
  • Worked example (illustrative): reserve ratio 10%; a new cash deposit of 1,000.
    • Round 1: keep 100; lend 900. The 900 is spent and deposited in another bank.
    • Round 2: keep 90; lend 810.
    • Round 3: keep 81; lend 729 … and so on.
  • Each round is smaller, so deposits grow to a limit set by the reserve ratio.
Mission 1Graph lab

Rounds of lending

Illustrative data. A new deposit of 1,000 enters the banking system.

  • Takeaway: a lower reserve ratio means more of each deposit is lent on, so deposits grow further.
Mission 1

Why the multiplier overstates

  • The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every penny of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit.
  • In reality:
    • Banks may keep excess reserves, e.g. when they fear losses.
    • People and firms hold some cash instead of deposits.
    • Lending depends on demand for loans and on capital rules.
  • Real world:
    • The Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0% since March 2020.
    • Central banks today mostly set interest rates and supply the reserves banks need.
  • So the multiplier is a maximum, not a forecast.
Quick check

In Economy A, banks lend all their excess reserves, and every loan comes back as a deposit. In Economy B, banks keep some extra reserves, and people keep part of their loans as cash. Using the multiplier model, which is correct?
Mission check

?A bank holds 1,000,000 of its customers' savings deposits. It then agrees a new loan of 50,000 to a firm and credits the firm's account. What happens at once to the savers' balances and to the money supply?

A bank loan is not someone else's savings passed on. It is a new deposit, so it is new money.

Make a guess: Why can't you open an account at a central bank, and what does it do instead?

Mission 2

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 2

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
Mission 2

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • The ECB aims for 2% inflation over the medium term, also symmetric.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 2

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • IB: base rate / discount rate / refinancing rate.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • Reserve requirements: a minimum share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
Quick check
?Which of these is a job of a central bank, and not of a commercial bank?
Mission check

?On a busy shopping day, customers of Bank X pay by card at retailers that bank with Bank Y. At the end of the day, Bank X owes Bank Y 2 billion. How is this usually settled?

The central bank is the bank for banks: their payments to each other settle in its reserve accounts.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If your savings grow every year, how can you still end up able to buy less?

Make a guess: If your savings grow every year, how can you still end up able to buy less?

Mission 3

Nominal and real

  • You save 1,000 for one year at an interest rate of 5% (illustrative data).
  • After a year you have 1,050. That 5% is the nominal interest rate: the rate in money terms, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Over the same year, inflation is 3%. A basket that cost 1,000 now costs 1,030.
  • 1,050 ÷ 1,030 = 1.019. You can buy only about 2% more than a year ago.
  • That 2% is the real interest rate: the rise in what your savings can buy.
  • Say: "the real interest rate fell" — not: "interest rates fell", when the nominal rate stayed the same and only inflation rose.
Mission 3

The Fisher relation

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Named after the economist Irving Fisher.
  • Example: nominal 5%, inflation 3% → real ≈ 5 − 3 = 2%.
  • If inflation is higher than the nominal rate, the real rate is negative: savings buy less each year.
  • The exact answer here is 1.94%, not 2%. The subtraction is close when rates are low, and it is the form you use in calculations.
  • Say: "real interest rate = nominal rate minus inflation" — not: "nominal rate minus the interest".
Quick check
?A bank pays a nominal interest rate of 6% on savings. Inflation over the year is 8%. What is the real interest rate?
Mission 3

When real rates go negative

  • Real-world data, euro area, October 2022:
  • The European Central Bank's deposit rate was 0.75%. Euro area inflation was 10.6%.
  • Real rate on that deposit ≈ 0.75 − 10.6 = −9.85%.
  • Nominal rates had started to rise, but real rates were still deeply negative.
  • Borrowers care about the real rate too: a negative real rate means a debt shrinks in real terms.
  • So a rise in the nominal rate is not always "tighter" money: it depends on what happens to inflation.
Mission 3

Expected and actual inflation

  • Lenders set the nominal rate before they know the inflation rate.
  • A lender wants a real return of 3% and expects inflation of 2%. It charges 3 + 2 = 5% nominal (illustrative data).
  • Inflation turns out to be 6%.
  • Real rate actually earned = 5 − 6 = −1%, not the 3% planned.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders: the money repaid buys less than both expected.
  • Unexpectedly low inflation does the reverse.
Quick check
?A firm borrows at a fixed nominal rate of 7% for one year. When the loan is agreed, both sides expect inflation of 3%. Actual inflation turns out to be 1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A central bank raises its nominal policy rate from 3% to 5%. Over the same period, inflation rises from 2% to 7%. What happened to the real policy rate?

A higher nominal rate can still mean a lower real rate, if inflation rises by more.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Make a guess: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Mission 4

Holding money has a cost

  • You can keep your wealth as money (cash, current accounts) or as interest-bearing assets such as bonds.
  • Money is the most liquid asset: you can spend it at once.
  • But money pays little or no interest.
  • So the opportunity cost of holding money is the interest you give up.
  • The higher the nominal interest rate, the more it costs to hold money.
Mission 4

The demand for money

  • At a high nominal interest rate, people hold less money and more bonds.
  • At a low rate, holding money costs little, so they hold more.
  • So money demand (MD) slopes down against the nominal interest rate.
  • A change in the interest rate → a movement along MD.
  • MD shifts right when people need more money for spending: a higher price level or higher real output (more transactions).
  • IB: label the curve Dm.
Mission 4

Money supply and equilibrium

  • The money supply (MS) is set by the central bank. It does not depend on the interest rate.
  • So MS is a vertical line.
  • IB: label it Sm.
  • Equilibrium: the nominal interest rate where the quantity of money demanded = the money supply.
  • Illustrative data: MS = 600 (billion); MD gives an equilibrium rate of 4%.
  • Say: "money demand shifted right" — not: "money supply increased", when people simply want to hold more money.
Mission 4

Getting back to equilibrium

  • Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.
  • Step 1: rate 5%, above equilibrium. People want to hold only 500 of the 600: a surplus of money of 100.
  • They use spare money to buy bonds → bond prices rise → interest rates fall back to 4%.
  • Step 2: rate below equilibrium → a shortage of money.
  • People sell bonds to get money → bond prices fall → interest rates rise.
  • The nominal rate adjusts; the money supply stays where the central bank put it.
Quick check
?In the money market, the nominal interest rate is below its equilibrium level. What happens next?
Mission 4Graph lab

Prices up, rate?

  • Takeaway: a higher price level raises money demand, so with a fixed money supply the nominal rate rises.
Mission 4

The central bank moves the money supply

  • To raise the money supply, the central bank buys government bonds (open market operations or quantitative easing). It pays with new money.
  • MS shifts right: MS1 → MS2. The nominal rate falls (illustrative data: 600 → 700, rate 4% → 3%).
  • To reduce the money supply, it sells bonds: MS shifts left, the rate rises.
  • Say: "an increase in the money supply lowers the nominal interest rate" — not: "lower interest rates increase the money supply" (wrong direction of cause).
Quick check
?Real output grows strongly, so people make more transactions. The central bank leaves the money supply unchanged. What happens in the money market?
Mission 4

From the money market to AD

  • Step 1: the central bank buys bonds. MS shifts right; the nominal rate falls.
  • Step 2: borrowing is cheaper, so investment and interest-sensitive consumption rise.
  • Step 3: AD shifts right: real output and the price level rise.
Quick check
?To cut inflation, the central bank sells government bonds. Which chain is correct?
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful is this model?

It depends on:

  • What the central bank targets: most central banks set a policy interest rate and supply whatever money is demanded at it. Then the money supply is not fixed.
  • Stable money demand: if money demand shifts a lot (new payment methods, panics), a fixed money supply gives an unstable rate.
  • Very low rates: near zero, extra money may not cut rates further. Real-world data: the European Central Bank's deposit rate was at or below 0% from July 2012 to September 2022.
  • The link to spending: a lower rate only raises AD if firms and households want to borrow.
Mission check

?After a wave of online payment fraud, people decide to hold more cash. Money demand rises by 50 billion at every interest rate. The central bank wants to keep the nominal interest rate unchanged. What should it do?

To hold the rate steady, a central bank must supply the extra money people want to hold.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 5

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • A central bank (such as the European Central Bank or the Reserve Bank of India) sets a policy interest rate (base rate).
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 5

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • Many central banks target about 2% inflation (e.g. the euro area and the US). Other goals: low unemployment, a smaller business cycle, long-term growth, external balance.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 5

The central bank's tools

  • Policy rate (base rate, Bank Rate, discount rate): the rate the central bank pays or charges banks.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds money to the banking system; selling drains it.
  • Minimum reserve requirements: the share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): large-scale bond buying when rates are near zero (see Mission 6).
Mission 5

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
Mission 5

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • IB names: deflationary (recessionary) gap and inflationary gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 5

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • Euro area: the European Central Bank raised its deposit rate from −0.5% (2022) to 4.0% (September 2023). Inflation had peaked at 10.6% (October 2022).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 5

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 5

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance (see Mission 6).
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 5Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 6

Mission 6: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Make a guess: When interest rates can go no lower, how can a central bank still get people spending?

Mission 6

When the usual tool runs out

  • The usual tool: cut the policy interest rate to make borrowing cheaper.
  • Many central banks cut their policy rates close to zero after the 2008 financial crisis.
  • Rates cannot go far below zero: people could hold cash at 0% instead. This floor is the zero (or effective) lower bound.
  • With the economy still weak, central banks turned to unconventional tools.
  • Main one: quantitative easing (QE).
Mission 6

What QE is

  • QE: the central bank creates new central bank reserves (electronic money) and uses them to buy assets, mainly government bonds.
  • It buys from investors such as pension funds and insurers, not straight from the government.
  • The seller's bank account is credited: broad money (bank deposits) rises; banks' reserves rise.
  • So QE increases the money supply while the policy rate is stuck near zero.
  • Say: "the central bank creates reserves to buy bonds" — not: "prints banknotes and gives them away".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes quantitative easing?
Mission 6

How QE reaches spending

  • 1. Bond prices up, yields down. Big purchases raise bond prices. A bond's yield (its return) moves the opposite way to its price.
  • 2. Lower long-term interest rates. Government bond yields are a benchmark, so mortgage and business loan rates tend to fall.
  • 3. Portfolio rebalancing. Sellers use their new money to buy other assets (shares, company bonds): their prices rise, firms can borrow more cheaply.
  • 4. Wealth effect. Higher asset prices make owners feel richer: more consumption.
  • 5. Exchange rate. Lower yields can make the currency less attractive: it tends to fall, exports become cheaper.
  • 6. Confidence. It signals the central bank will act.
  • Result: AD shifts right, raising real output and the price level towards the inflation target.
Mission 6

Worked example: price up, yield down

  • Illustrative data. A bond pays a fixed 3 a year, for ever.
  • Yield = annual payment ÷ price × 100.
  • Price 100: 3 ÷ 100 × 100 = 3%.
  • QE buying pushes the price to 120: 3 ÷ 120 × 100 = 2.5%.
  • Price 150: 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = 2%.
  • The payment is fixed, so paying more for it means a lower return.
  • Say: "higher bond prices mean lower yields" — not: "bond prices and interest rates rise together".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bond pays a fixed 4 a year, for ever. After central bank purchases, its price rises from 100 to 125. What is its yield now?

Mission 6

Reversing QE

  • Quantitative tightening (QT): the central bank shrinks its bond holdings.
  • Two ways: stop reinvesting as bonds mature, or sell bonds back to investors.
  • Reserves and deposits shrink: the money supply contracts.
  • The chain runs in reverse: bond prices fall, yields rise, borrowing costs rise: AD tends to shift left.
  • Central banks try to reverse QE gradually and predictably, so markets are not shocked.
Mission 6

Other ways the money supply changes

  • Open market operations (OMO): the central bank buys short-term government securities to add reserves (expand), or sells them to drain reserves (contract).
  • Minimum reserve requirements: a higher required share of deposits held as reserves cuts banks' lending (contract); a lower one lets them lend more (expand).
  • Policy rate (base or discount rate): a higher rate makes borrowing costlier, so less new lending and slower money growth.
  • QE is OMO on a huge scale, in long-term bonds, when the policy rate is at the floor.
Mission 6

Real world: QE in practice

  • The Bank of Japan first used QE in March 2001.
  • The US Federal Reserve's assets reached about 4.5 trillion dollars by the time it stopped buying in October 2014.
  • The European Central Bank began large-scale bond buying in 2015: 60 billion euros a month.
  • Question to weigh: why did central banks buy on this scale, and what happens when they stop?
Mission 6Evaluate

Does QE work?

It depends on:

  • Confidence. If households and firms want to save and banks will not lend, new reserves sit idle: "pushing on a string".
  • Who gains. Higher bond, share and house prices help people who own them, which may widen wealth inequality. Supporters reply that QE protected jobs and incomes.
  • Timing and exit. Too much for too long risks inflation; unwinding risks bond-market disruption and losses for the central bank.
  • Evidence. Most studies find early QE lowered long-term yields. How much it raised output and inflation is still debated.
Mission check

?A central bank bought large amounts of government bonds during a slump. Years later, with inflation above target, it starts selling some of the bonds back to investors. Other things equal, what is the most likely effect?

Buying bonds pushes long-term rates down; selling them pushes rates up. The same chain works in both directions.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a matching deposit.

Mission 2

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.

Mission 3

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation; it can be negative.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.

Mission 4

  • The opportunity cost of holding money is the interest given up, so money demand slopes down.
  • Money supply is vertical, set by the central bank; the nominal interest rate is where they meet.
  • MD shifts with the price level and real output; MS shifts with central bank action.
  • A lower rate raises investment and consumption, so AD shifts right.

Mission 5

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 6

  • At the zero lower bound, QE creates reserves to buy bonds: bond prices rise, yields fall, borrowing gets cheaper, AD shifts right.
  • Reversing QE (QT) contracts the money supply and pushes yields up.
