Mission 1: What four ingredients does every product in the world need before it can be made?

Make a guess: What four ingredients does every product in the world need before it can be made?

Mission 1

The four factors of production

  • An economic resource (a factor of production) is anything used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, oil, fish, minerals.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to make other goods: machines, tools, factories, delivery vans.
  • Enterprise: the entrepreneur who organises the other three and takes the risk of the business.
  • Every product needs a combination of factors. A bakery: land (a site, wheat), labour (bakers), capital (ovens), enterprise (the owner).
  • Say: "capital = machines, tools, buildings" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital, but money cannot make anything.
Quick check
?A fishing firm uses a boat, a crew, the fish in the sea, and an owner who borrowed to buy the boat. Which is classed as capital?
Mission 1

More factors, better factors

  • Quantity of factors can change:
    • Land: reclamation turns sea or marsh into farmland. The Netherlands drained part of an inland sea to create the province of Flevoland.
    • Labour: population growth and migration add workers; emigration removes them.
    • Capital: firms invest in more machines and buildings.
  • Quality can change too:
    • Land: fertiliser and irrigation raise crop yields; over-farming can wear soil out.
    • Labour: education and training build human capital (skills and knowledge).
    • Capital: new technology makes machines more productive.
  • More or better factors → the economy can produce more.
Mission 1

Worked example: productivity

  • Productivity: output per unit of a factor (per worker, per hectare, per machine).
  • Illustrative data: a farm has 200 hectares of land.
  • Before irrigation: 3 tonnes of wheat per hectare → 200 × 3 = 600 tonnes.
  • After irrigation: 4.5 tonnes per hectare → 200 × 4.5 = 900 tonnes.
  • Change: (900 − 600) ÷ 600 × 100 = +50%.
  • Same quantity of land, better quality: higher land productivity.
  • Say: "productivity rose" (output per unit) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more land or workers.
Quick check
?Which change raises the quality of a country's labour rather than its quantity?
Mission check

?A family runs a strawberry farm. Which list correctly matches each item to its factor of production?

Ask "natural, human, man-made, or risk-taking?" and every input finds its factor.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do most workers in rich countries never make anything you can touch?

Make a guess: Why do most workers in rich countries never make anything you can touch?

Mission 2

What is a market?

  • A market: any place or system where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods or services.
  • It can be a street market, a shop, a website, an app or a phone call.
  • Buyers want low prices; sellers want high prices. Trade happens at a price both accept.
  • Markets help answer the basic questions: prices tell firms what to make, and decide who gets it (those willing and able to pay).
Mission 2

Three sectors of the economy

  • Goods: physical things you can touch (bread, cars). Services: things done for you (a haircut, a bus ride, banking).
  • Primary sector: takes natural resources from the earth or sea: farming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction.
  • Secondary sector: makes or builds goods from raw materials: manufacturing and construction.
  • Tertiary sector: provides services: shops, transport, banking, health care, education, tourism.
  • A chain of production: wheat (primary) → flour and bread made in a bakery (secondary) → sold in a shop and delivered (tertiary).
  • Say: "delivery and transport are tertiary" (a service) — not: secondary, even when they carry goods.
Quick check
?A new school is being built in a town. Which of these workers is in the secondary sector?

Mission check

?Illustrative data: over 30 years, average income in a country doubles. The share of its workers in factories falls from 30% to 18%, yet its factories make more goods than before. Which explanation fits best?

A sector's share of jobs can fall while its output rises. Machines free workers, and richer consumers buy more services.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some countries have far higher living standards than others, and how can we measure the gap?

Make a guess: One country produces nearly twice as much per person as another. What does that tell you about how well its people live?

Mission 3

Living standards and development

  • Living standards: how well people live: their income, health, education and the goods and services they can enjoy.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's living standards and well-being over time.
  • Developed countries: high incomes per person, long lives, most people well educated.
  • Developing (less developed) countries: lower incomes per person, and often shorter lives and less schooling.
  • Development is more than money: no single number captures it.
Mission 3

Real case: richer per head, shorter lives

  • GDP per head = GDP ÷ population: the average output per person.
  • 2023, World Bank data (GDP per head in US dollars):
GDP per headLife expectancy
Equatorial Guineaabout $6,80063.7 years
Sri Lankaabout $3,80077.5 years
  • Equatorial Guinea produced about 1.8 times as much per person.
  • Yet people in Sri Lanka could expect to live about 14 years longer.
  • A large share of Equatorial Guinea's output comes from oil and gas. GDP per head is an average: it does not show who receives the income, or whether it pays for clinics and schools.
  • So: higher GDP per head does not always mean higher living standards.
Mission 3

How sectors change as countries develop

  • Primary sector: farming, fishing, mining. Secondary: manufacturing and construction. Tertiary: services.
  • Share of jobs in farming, 2023 (World Bank, ILO estimates):
CountryJobs in farmingGDP per head
Niger74%about $650
Sri Lanka26%about $3,800
Japan3%about $35,200
UKunder 1%about $49,900
  • Developing economies: the primary sector is large; often most people work on farms.
  • As they develop, workers move into secondary (factory) jobs, where output per worker is usually higher.
  • Developed economies: the tertiary sector is largest. The secondary share often falls (deindustrialisation).
Mission 3

Trade patterns: developed and developing

  • Developing countries often export primary products (crops, oil, metals) and import manufactured goods.
    • Nigeria, 2023: fuel was about 92% of its goods exports; manufactures under 3%.
  • Developed countries export mainly manufactured goods and services.
    • Japan, 2023: manufactures were about 84% of its goods exports.
  • Much world trade is between developed countries, but some developing countries, especially in Asia, now export many manufactures.
  • Risk for primary exporters: world prices of crops, oil and metals swing a lot, so export earnings swing too.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Crude oil makes up 90% of Country R's exports. The world price of oil halves. What is the most likely effect on Country R?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Today, 70% of Country Z's workers are in farming, and cocoa and coffee make up most of its exports. If Country Z develops over the next 30 years, which change is most likely?

Development moves workers from the primary sector into secondary and tertiary jobs, and changes what a country exports.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can the same workers make more without working longer hours, and why does it matter?

Make a guess: How can the same workers make more without working longer hours, and why does it matter?

Mission 4

Production vs productivity

  • Production: the total output of goods and services. E.g. 1,000 chairs a week.
  • Productivity: output per unit of input, over a period. E.g. chairs per worker per week.
  • Labour productivity = total output ÷ number of workers (or ÷ hours worked).
  • Production can rise just by hiring more workers. Productivity rises only if each input produces more.
  • Say: "productivity rose" only when output per worker (or per hour) rose — not when total output rose.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Two furniture factories, one week.
  • Factory A: 20 workers make 1,000 chairs. Productivity = 1,000 ÷ 20 = 50 chairs per worker.
  • Factory B: 10 workers make 600 chairs. Productivity = 600 ÷ 10 = 60 chairs per worker.
  • A has the higher production. B has the higher productivity.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bakery employs 10 workers who make 500 loaves a day. It hires 2 more workers, and output rises to 540 loaves a day. What has happened?
Mission 4

What raises productivity?

  • Education and training: skills make each worker produce more. Skills, knowledge and experience are called human capital.
  • Investment in capital: new machines, computers and buildings. Workers with better tools produce more per hour.
  • Technology: better methods, e.g. robots on a production line, or software that speeds up paperwork.
  • Management and organisation: e.g. specialisation and division of labour, better layout, less waste.
  • Motivation: better pay, conditions and recognition can raise effort and cut absence.
  • Migration: workers who move to fill skill shortages can raise productivity in the country they join; the country they leave may lose skills.
Mission 4

Productivity of land

  • Land productivity = output per hectare (e.g. tonnes of wheat per hectare).
  • Fertiliser: adds nutrients so each hectare yields more.
  • Irrigation: brings water to dry land, so crops grow in dry seasons, or more than once a year.
  • Reclamation: turns unusable land (swamp, desert, land under the sea) into land that can be farmed or built on.
  • Limits: overuse of fertiliser and water can damage soil and rivers over time.
Mission 4Graph lab

Higher wages, lower costs?

Illustrative data. Each worker is paid £100 a day and makes 20 units a day.

  • Takeaway: labour cost per unit = wage ÷ output per worker. If productivity rises faster than wages, cost per unit falls.
Mission 4

Why higher productivity matters

  • Firms: lower average cost → more profit, or lower prices to win customers. More competitive, including against firms abroad.
  • Workers: firms can afford higher wages without raising cost per unit.
  • Consumers: lower prices, better quality.
  • The economy: more output from the same resources → economic growth and higher living standards.
  • But: if machines replace workers, some may lose their jobs and need to retrain.
Quick check
?Which change is most likely to raise labour productivity in a car factory?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Last year, a call centre had 50 staff who handled 20,000 calls a month. It bought new software. This year, 40 staff handle 20,000 calls a month. Which statement is correct?

Investment in new technology let fewer workers produce the same output: productivity rose even though production did not.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How could ten workers make thousands of pins a day when one alone could barely make twenty?

Make a guess: How could ten workers make thousands of pins a day when one alone could barely make twenty?

Mission 5

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Workers: a dentist, an electrician, a web designer.
    • Firms: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Regions: a city known for finance, or for films.
    • Countries: one exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 5

Adam Smith's pin workshop

  • In 1776, Adam Smith described a small pin workshop in The Wealth of Nations.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 separate operations: one worker draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons:
    • Skill: repeating one task makes a worker faster and better at it.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machines: a small, simple task is easier to do with a machine, so machines get invented.
Mission 5

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity (output per worker) rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same pay, same hours: more output and a lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 5 workers, each paid 80 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 100 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 5 workers make 160 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 5

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower cost per unit, more output from the same workers. Lower costs can mean more profit.
  • Workers: quicker to train for one task; can become expert; may earn more as their productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices and more products, often of better quality.
  • The economy: more output from the same scarce resources.
Mission 5

The downsides

  • Boredom: doing the same task all day can lower motivation and quality, and raise absence and staff turnover.
  • Narrow skills: if a machine takes over the task, or demand changes, a worker may struggle to find another job.
  • Interdependence: each stage depends on the others. If one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Less variety: products made in large, standard batches can all look the same.
  • Smith himself warned that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can dull a worker's mind.
Quick check
?A car factory buys one key electronic part from a single supplier abroad. A fire stops that supplier's production, and within a week the car factory has to stop too. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show?
Mission 5

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of most other things.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Barter: swapping goods directly, with no money.
  • Barter needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other has, at the same time.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange quicker and easier. another lesson looks at money in depth.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show?
Mission check

?A hospital laundry keeps the same 6 workers, the same hours and the same machines. Instead of each worker washing, drying and folding whole batches, each now does one stage only. Sheets cleaned per worker rise by a quarter. Which explanation fits?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Four factors: land (natural), labour (human effort), capital (man-made aids to production), enterprise (organising and risk-taking).
  • Quantity and quality of factors can change: reclamation, migration, investment, irrigation, training, technology.

Mission 2

  • A market brings buyers and sellers together.
  • Primary: natural resources. Secondary: making and building. Tertiary: services.
  • In rich economies most jobs are in services.
  • In many developing economies, most jobs are in farming.

Mission 3

  • As countries develop, the primary sector shrinks and secondary, then tertiary, sectors grow.
  • Developing countries often export primary products; developed countries export mainly manufactures and services.

Mission 4

  • Production = total output. Productivity = output per unit of input (e.g. per worker or per hour).
  • Training (human capital), investment, technology, good management and motivation raise productivity.
  • Higher productivity lowers cost per unit, which can mean higher wages, lower prices and economic growth.

Mission 5

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (skill, time saved, machines) and cut cost per unit.
  • Downsides: boredom, narrow skills, interdependence, less variety.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.
