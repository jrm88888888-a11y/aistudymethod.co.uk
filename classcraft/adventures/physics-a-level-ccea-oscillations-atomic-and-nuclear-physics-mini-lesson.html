Mission 1

Mission 1: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Make a guess: Take a pendulum clock to the Moon. Does it run fast, run slow, or keep time?

Mission 1

Mass on a spring

  • T = 2π√(m/k)
  • m = oscillating mass; k = spring constant.
  • Bigger mass → longer T. Stiffer spring → shorter T.
  • Not on g: hung vertically, gravity only shifts the equilibrium position. Same T as horizontal.
  • Not on amplitude.
Mission 1

Simple pendulum

  • T = 2π√(l/g)
  • Length from the pivot to the centre of mass of the bob.
  • Small angles only. Then the restoring force is (nearly) proportional to the displacement.
  • Not on the bob's mass. Not on the (small) amplitude.
Mission 1

Worked examples

  • Spring: m = 0.25 kg, k = 40 N m⁻¹. T = 2π√(0.25/40) = 0.50 s.
  • Pendulum, length 0.80 m:
  • T = 2π√(0.80/9.81) = 1.8 s (g = 9.81 m s⁻²).
  • To make the pendulum's period three times as long: nine times the length.
Quick check
?A trolley hung from a newton meter gives a reading of 14.7 N. The trolley is then attached to a spring system on a horizontal track and oscillates with negligible friction. The spring constant of the system is 7.50 N m⁻¹. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². What is the time period of the oscillation?
Mission 1

What sets the period?

  • Mass on a spring: longer with more mass; shorter with a stiffer spring. Unchanged by g and by amplitude.
  • Pendulum: longer with a longer string; shorter where g is larger. Unchanged by the bob's mass and by small changes of amplitude.
  • Large swings (beyond about 10°): a pendulum's period grows slightly. The motion is no longer quite SHM.
Mission 1

Measuring the period

  • Time many oscillations (e.g. 20) and divide. Repeat and average.
  • Start and stop as the mass passes equilibrium, at a fiducial marker: it moves fastest there, so the instant is sharp.
  • Pendulum: keep angles small; measure l to the centre of the bob.
  • Pendulum: plot T² against l. Straight line through the origin, gradient 4π²/g → g = 4π² ÷ gradient.
  • Spring: plot T² against m. Gradient 4π²/k → k = 4π² ÷ gradient.
Quick check
?A long simple pendulum hangs in the stairwell of a school. Swinging with a small amplitude, its bob takes 2.10 s to move from one extreme position to the other. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². What is the length of the pendulum?
Mission check

?A simple pendulum and a mass–spring oscillator each have a period of 1.0 s on Earth. Both are taken to a planet where the gravitational field strength is four times as large as on Earth, and both oscillate with small amplitude. What are their new periods?

A pendulum's period depends on its length and on g. On the Moon, g is about one-sixth of Earth's, so each swing takes longer: the clock runs slow. A watch regulated by a balance wheel and hairspring would keep time.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can small pushes, timed just right, build up a huge swing?

Make a guess: Why can small pushes, timed just right, build up a huge swing?

Mission 2

Free and forced oscillations

  • Free oscillation: displace it, let go, no further force. It oscillates at its natural frequency, f0.
  • Forced oscillation: a periodic driving force keeps acting.
  • Once steady, a forced system oscillates at the driving frequency, not at f0.
Quick check
?A lightly damped mass–spring system has a natural frequency of 2.0 Hz. It is driven by a periodic force of constant amplitude with a frequency of 3.5 Hz. Which describes its oscillation once the amplitude has become steady?
Mission 2

Resonance

  • Resonance: driving frequency = natural frequency → maximum amplitude.
  • Why: each push is in step with the motion, so the driver does positive work on every cycle. Energy builds up.
  • Amplitude–driving frequency graph: small at low frequency, a peak at f0, then falls towards zero at high frequency.
  • The peak height is limited by damping: the amplitude stops growing when energy lost per cycle = energy supplied per cycle.
Quick check
?In its spin cycle, a washing machine's drum speeds up steadily. At one rotation rate the whole machine shakes violently; at higher rates it runs smoothly again. Why does it shake at that one rate?
Mission 2

Damping

  • Damping: a resistive force (air resistance, friction, a fluid) takes energy out of the oscillation. The energy ends up as thermal energy of the surroundings.
  • Undamped: kinetic ⇄ potential energy, total energy constant. Damped: total energy falls, so the amplitude falls.
  • Light damping: keeps oscillating; amplitude decays gradually; period almost unchanged.
  • Critical damping: returns to equilibrium in the shortest time, without oscillating.
  • Heavy damping (also called over-damping): no oscillation; creeps back more slowly than critical.
Quick check
?The graph shows displacement–time curves X, Y and Z for the same mass–spring system, released from rest at the same displacement with three different amounts of damping. Which statement about the curves is correct?
Mission 2

Damping changes the resonance curve

  • More damping:
    • lower peak (smaller maximum amplitude),
    • broader, flatter curve: the resonance is less sharp,
    • peak at a slightly lower frequency.
  • Light damping: tall, sharp peak, very close to f0.
  • Far from f0 (very low or very high driving frequency), damping makes little difference.
Quick check
?The same oscillator is driven by a periodic force of the same constant amplitude in two experiments, X and Y, with different damping. In X the maximum amplitude is 25 mm, at 5.00 Hz, and the amplitude falls to half this value at 4.82 Hz and 5.17 Hz. In Y the maximum amplitude is 3.4 mm, at 4.89 Hz, and the amplitude falls to half this value at 3.3 Hz and 6.1 Hz. Which conclusion do the data support?
Mission check

?A singer shatters a wine glass by singing one steady note close to it. Which condition is essential for this to happen?

Same idea as the swing. A driver at the natural frequency adds energy on every cycle, so small pushes build a large amplitude; at other frequencies the pushes work against the motion for part of each cycle.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Engineers add damping to bridges, cars and doors. How much damping is just right?

Make a guess: Engineers add damping to bridges, cars and doors. How much damping is just right?

Mission 3

Seeing resonance: Barton's pendulums

  • One heavy driver pendulum and several light pendulums of different lengths, all hung from one string.
  • The driver forces every light pendulum at the driver's frequency.
  • The light pendulum of the same length as the driver (same f0) swings with the largest amplitude: resonance.
  • Make the light bobs from paper cones (more damping): every amplitude falls, and the "winner" stands out less. The resonance is less sharp.
Mission 3

Investigating damping of a spring

  • Mass on a spring. Add a card disc to increase air resistance.
  • Record amplitude after each oscillation: a motion sensor and data logger, or video against a ruler.
  • Light damping: the amplitude falls by roughly the same fraction each cycle (an exponential-style decay).
  • Illustrative data: 80, 64, 51, 41 mm. Each is 0.8 × the one before.
  • Bigger card → bigger fraction lost each cycle.
Quick check
?A lightly damped mass–spring system is released with an amplitude of 60 mm. After one complete oscillation its amplitude is 45 mm. The amplitude falls by the same fraction in each oscillation. What is the amplitude after three complete oscillations?
Mission 3

Where the energy goes

  • Damped: work done against resistive forces → thermal energy in the surroundings. Total energy falls, so A falls (energy ∝ amplitude²).
Quick check
?Damping reduces the amplitude of a mass–spring oscillator from 40 mm to 20 mm. What fraction of the original oscillation energy has been transferred to the surroundings?
Mission 3

Critical damping at work

  • Car suspension: spring + shock absorber (a damper: oil forced through small holes).
    • Too little damping: the car keeps bouncing after a bump.
    • Too much: the wheel cannot follow the road; bumps are passed straight to the car body.
    • Designed close to critical: returns to level quickly with little or no bounce.
  • Door closers: close without slamming or swinging past.
  • Analogue meters: the pointer settles on its reading quickly, without wobbling.
Mission 3

Designing against resonance

  • Avoid: bridges, buildings, machinery and vehicle parts must not have f0 near a driving frequency they meet (walkers, wind, engines, earthquakes).
  • Fix 1: damp it. Millennium Bridge (2000): dampers added under the deck; the sway stopped.
  • Fix 2: move f0. Stiffer structure → higher f0; added mass → lower f0.
  • Useful: tuning circuits (radio); musical instruments.
Quick check
?A car's wing mirror buzzes violently at one engine speed. A rubber damper is fitted to the mirror's mounting. What happens now as the engine passes that speed?
Mission check

?A seismometer has a heavy mass on a spring, with adjustable damping. After a sudden jolt, with the damping set low, the mass swings past its rest position and back several times before it settles. The damping is then increased in small steps. Which describes how the mass's motion changes?

"Just right" is close to critical: no overshoot, shortest settling time. Less damping bounces; more damping is sluggish.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Mission 4

The plum-pudding model

  • 1897: Thomson discovers the electron. Atoms must contain positive charge too.
  • Plum-pudding model: positive charge spread evenly through the whole atom, electrons dotted inside.
  • Charge spread out → only weak electric fields anywhere in the atom.
  • Prediction: fast alpha particles pass through with only tiny deflections.
Mission 4

The experiment

  • Geiger and Marsden, in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory (1909–1913).
  • A narrow beam of alpha particles aimed at a very thin gold foil.
  • In a vacuum, so air does not stop the alpha particles.
  • A zinc sulfide screen flashes where an alpha particle hits. Counted through a microscope at many angles.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement about the results of the Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment is correct?
Mission 4

Results and conclusions

  • Almost all went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some were deflected through small angles → they are repelled by positive charge (plum pudding allows this too).
  • A very few were deflected by more than 90° → the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny core. Plum pudding cannot do this.
  • Rutherford (1911): the nuclear model. A small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bouncing back needs a strong electric repulsion from something much more massive than an alpha. Electrons are far too light.
Mission 4

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 4

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimetres: about a pea on the centre spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Quick check
?A gold nucleus has radius 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and a gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly what fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy?
Mission check

?A gold nucleus is about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m across and a gold atom is about 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. In a scale drawing the nucleus is a dot 1 mm across. How wide must the atom be drawn?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Make a guess: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Mission 5

Radius and nucleon number

  • Measured radii fit one rule:
  • r = r0A1/3, A = nucleon number, r0 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Use the value of the constant given in the question.
  • From data: r against A1/3 is a straight line through the origin.
  • 8 times the nucleons → 2 times the radius. 27 times → 3 times.
  • Typical radii: about 2.7 fm (carbon-12) to about 7.4 fm (uranium-238). 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Gold-197: A = 197.
  • A1/3 = 1971/3 = 5.82.
  • r = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.82 = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • About 7 fm: roughly 20 000 times smaller than the gold atom.
Quick check
?A nucleus of lead-208 has proton number 82 and nucleon number 208. Using r = r0A1/3 with r0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, what is the radius of this nucleus?
Quick check
?A carbon-12 nucleus contains 12 nucleons, each of mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. r0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. What is the density of the nucleus?
Mission 5

Every nucleus has the same density

  • Volume: V = ⁴⁄3πr³ = ⁴⁄3πr0³A. So V ∝ A.
  • Mass: m ≈ A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. So m ∝ A.
  • Density = m/V: the A cancels. The same for every nucleus.
  • ρ = 3 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ ÷ (4π × (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • Nucleons are packed at a fixed spacing, like molecules in a drop of liquid.
Mission 5

Nucleus versus atom

  • Solids, made of atoms: about 10³ to 2 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³ (gold: 1.9 × 10⁴).
  • Nuclear matter: 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, about 10¹³ times denser.
  • Why: the nucleus is 10⁴–10⁵ times narrower than the atom, so its volume is about 10¹² to 10¹⁵ times smaller, yet it holds nearly all the mass.
  • A teaspoon (5 cm³) of nuclear matter: 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.3 × 10¹⁷ ≈ 1 × 10¹² kg, about a billion tonnes.
Mission check

?A neutron star has a mass of 2.8 × 10³⁰ kg. Model it as a sphere of nuclear matter, with the same density as any nucleus. Estimate its radius.

Nuclear matter has the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, in any amount. A whole star of it is only about 14 km in radius, and a teaspoon of it has a mass of about 10¹² kg: a billion tonnes.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 6

Nuclear notation

  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 6

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Make a guess: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Mission 7

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable. They decay: they emit radiation and become more stable.
  • Decay is spontaneous: nothing triggers it. Heat, pressure and chemistry do not change it.
  • The emitted particle or photon is the radiation: α, β or γ.
  • The type of decay is set by the isotope: carbon-14 decays by β⁻; uranium-238 by α.
Mission 7

Meet the radiations

  • α particle: a helium-4 nucleus. 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e. Symbol ⁴2He or ⁴2α.
  • β⁻ particle: a fast electron from the nucleus. Charge −e. Symbol ⁰₋1e or ⁰₋1β.
  • γ ray: a high-energy photon. No charge, no mass.
Mission 7

Balancing decay equations

  • Top numbers (nucleon number, A) add up the same on both sides.
  • Bottom numbers (proton number or charge, Z) add up the same on both sides.
  • An electron counts as ⁰₋1e: no nucleons, charge −1.
Mission 7

Worked example: α and β⁻

  • α decay: ²³⁸92U → ²³⁴90Th + ⁴2He
  • A: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓. Z: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
  • New element: Z falls by 2 (uranium → thorium). A falls by 4.
  • β⁻ decay: ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰₋1e + ν̄
  • ν̄ is an antineutrino: uncharged, almost no mass, emitted with the β⁻ particle.
  • A: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Z: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓.
  • Inside: a neutron → proton. Z rises by 1; A unchanged.
Quick check
?A nucleus has nucleon number A and proton number Z. It undergoes an alpha decay, and the nucleus formed then undergoes a beta-minus decay. Which expression gives the number of neutrons in the final nucleus?
Mission 7

γ decay

  • γ decay: after α or β decay the new nucleus is often left excited. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a γ photon.
  • ⁶⁰28Ni* → ⁶⁰28Ni + γ. A and Z do not change. Same nuclide, less energy.

Quick check
?A steel works checks the thickness of steel sheet, about 5 mm thick, with a radioactive source on one side of the sheet and a detector on the other. Which type of source is most suitable, and why?
Mission check

?Thorium-232 (²³²90Th) decays through a chain of α and β⁻ decays to stable lead-208 (²⁰⁸82Pb). How many α decays and how many β⁻ decays are there in the chain?

An α decay lowers Z by 2; two β⁻ decays raise it by 2. So α, β⁻, β⁻ returns a nucleus to the same element: an isotope with 4 fewer nucleons.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • T = 2π√(m/k); T = 2π√(l/g), for small angles.
  • Spring: no g, no amplitude. Pendulum: no mass, no (small) amplitude.
  • Plot T² against l (or m): gradient 4π²/g (or 4π²/k).

Mission 2

  • A forced oscillator settles at the driving frequency; resonance (maximum amplitude) happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency.
  • More damping: lower, broader resonance peak at a slightly lower frequency. Light damping oscillates and decays; critical returns fastest without oscillating; heavy (over-) damping returns slowly.

Mission 3

  • Damping removes energy (E ∝ A²); light damping cuts the amplitude by roughly the same fraction each cycle. Suspensions, door closers and meters are damped close to critical.
  • Harmful resonance is prevented by adding damping or moving f0 away from the driving frequency.

Mission 4

  • Alpha scattering: most pass straight through, a very few bounce back → a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

Mission 5

  • r = r0A1/3, r0 ≈ 1.2 fm; nuclear radii are a few fm.
  • V ∝ A and m ∝ A, so all nuclei have the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³: about 10¹³ times that of ordinary matter.

Mission 6

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.

Mission 7

  • α: ⁴2He, A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: neutron → proton, Z + 1. γ: no change to A or Z.
  • α: most ionising, least penetrating; γ: least ionising, most penetrating.
