Mission 1

What AO1 rewards

  • AO1: knowledge and understanding of religion and belief.
  • Every board uses the same four strands:
  • Thought and teaching: religious, philosophical and ethical ideas.
  • Influence: how beliefs shape individuals, communities and societies.
  • Similarities and differences: their causes and significance.
  • Approaches to the study of religion and belief.
  • AO1 is not "write all you know". It is select, explain, support.
Mission 1

Your AO1 tasks

  • Each two-part question: first part AO1, 10 marks; second part AO2, 15 marks.
  • Typical wording: Explain, Examine, Compare.
  • Questions may span more than one topic (see Mission 3).
  • Use the scholars prescribed for the topic and specialist language.
Mission 1

Select: answer this question, not the topic

  • Underline the focus: whose view? which aspect? which part of the topic?
  • "Examine Hick's soul-making theodicy" is not "write about the problem of evil".
  • Choose 3–4 main points that answer it fully; drop the rest.
  • Watch small words: "different views", "two ways", "the influence of", "compare".
  • Material that is accurate but off-focus earns little or nothing.
Quick check
?The question is "Examine Hick's soul-making theodicy." Which material is most relevant?
Mission 1

Develop each point

  • A point alone is identification. Marks come from development.
  • Point → explain it (what does it mean?) → support it (scholar, text, example) → significance (why does it matter to the belief or to believers?).
  • Two well-developed points beat six bare ones.
  • Use connectives that show understanding: "because", "this means", "so", "which is why".
Mission 1

Scholars, sources and terms

  • Name the scholar and the idea: "Hick (Evil and the God of Love, 1966) argues…", not just "Hick says evil is good".
  • Paraphrase accurately; quote only short phrases you are sure of.
  • Use sources of wisdom and authority where relevant: scripture with reference, a council, a creed.
  • Specialist terms, used correctly and explained once: e.g. "epistemic distance", "theodicy", "eschatological".
  • Show difference within traditions where the question allows (e.g. Catholic and Protestant; Sunni and Shi'a).
Mission 1Read the source

What a top AO1 answer shows

CCEA's A2 grade descriptions (spec section 5) say that at grade A, for AO1, candidates characteristically:

  • "address the question specifically"
  • "use extensive relevant evidence, examples and scholarship"
  • "use an extensive range of technical language and vocabulary"
  • Eduqas' top-band AO1 descriptor uses similar words: thorough, accurate, relevant knowledge, with an extensive range of scholarly views (paraphrase).

Q1 Which phrase rewards selection rather than quantity?

Show answer

"address the question specifically"; "relevant".

Q2 Name one thing both descriptors reward besides knowledge.

Show answer

Scholarship or scholarly views; also accurate technical language.

Mission 1Worked answer

One developed AO1 paragraph

Question: Examine Hick's soul-making theodicy.

  • Point: Hick (1922–2012) builds on Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202): humans are made in God's image but must grow into his likeness.
  • Explain: goodness that is freely developed is worth more than goodness simply given.
  • Support: in Evil and the God of Love (1966), Hick says the world is a "vale of soul-making" (a phrase he takes from Keats), where challenges and suffering make growth possible.
  • Significance: so God creates at an epistemic distance: not obvious, so humans can freely choose to love him.
  • 10-mark tip: two or three points developed like this, with accurate terms.

Mission 1

Common faults

  • Narrative: telling a story (a scholar's life; a parable retold) without explaining its point.
  • Listing: many names or terms, none explained.
  • Generalising: "Christians believe…" where traditions differ.
  • Name-dropping: a scholar's name with no idea attached, or the wrong idea attached.
  • Drifting into evaluation: "This is a weak argument because…" in an AO1 part.
  • Long introductions: start answering in the first sentence.
Mission 1

Similarities, differences and influence

  • If a question says compare or different views: set ideas side by side and say why they differ (different sources, different starting points, different contexts).
  • If it says influence: show the belief changing something: worship, ethics, community life, law.
  • These strands are named in every board's AO1, so they can be asked directly.
  • E.g. Augustine and Hick both defend God's goodness, but Augustine looks back to the Fall; Hick looks forward to growth.

Mission 2

What AO2 rewards

  • AO2: analyse and evaluate aspects of, and approaches to, religion and belief, including their significance, influence and study.
  • Analyse: break an issue into parts; see how they connect; spot assumptions.
  • Evaluate: weigh arguments and reach a judgement you can justify.
  • Marks reward the quality of reasoning, not which side you take.
  • Across the full A-level, AO2 is the larger share: 60% on AQA, Edexcel and Eduqas; 56% on CCEA (AS units split evenly).
Mission 2

Your AO2 tasks

  • The second part of each two-part question: AO2, 15 marks.
  • Usually a statement to evaluate, e.g. "'…' Evaluate this claim."
  • Use the views and arguments of the prescribed scholars.
Mission 2

Read the statement first

  • Underline the key terms and decide what they mean before you argue.
  • Strength words: "most", "only", "fully", "fails", "proves", "irrelevant". They decide what you must show.
  • "The most convincing" requires a comparison with other views.
  • "Proves" sets a high bar: is "makes probable" enough?
  • Define terms in the introduction; return to them in the conclusion.
Quick check
?"Hick's theodicy is the most convincing response to the problem of evil." Evaluate this view. What does the word "most" require?
Mission 2

A line of argument

  • State your thesis early: a one-sentence answer to the question.
  • Each paragraph argues, not just reports: a reason for your thesis, or a challenge you then answer.
  • End each paragraph with a mini-judgement that links back: "This shows that…".
  • Your conclusion should follow from the paragraphs; no new arguments at the end.
Mission 2

Counter-arguments and responses

  • Give the strongest form of the opposing view, not a weak one ("steel-man", not "straw-man").
  • Then respond: Is it valid? Is it based on a false assumption? Does it prove less than it claims?
  • Use scholars as voices in a debate: "Phillips objects that…; Hick could reply that…".
  • Avoid "Some people think… Others think…" lists that never weigh anything.
Mission 2Evaluate

Two justified judgements

  • Claim: "Hick's theodicy successfully defends the goodness of God."
  • For: Freely chosen goodness needs a world with real challenge; Irenaeus' image-to-likeness gives a long tradition behind it.
  • Against: D. Z. Phillips: a loving God would not use suffering such as the Holocaust as a means to growth (paraphrase); much suffering destroys rather than builds people.
  • Criteria: Can a good end justify such means? Does an afterlife hope (Hick's eschatology) answer the problem, or depend on what cannot be shown?
  • Student 1 judges that Hick succeeds, if his eschatology is accepted. Student 2 judges that he fails, because the means are unjustifiable. Both can reach the top band.
Mission 2

Making the judgement

  • A judgement can be qualified: "succeeds only if…", "succeeds for moral evil but not for natural evil".
  • It must be justified: name the criterion that decided it.
  • It must be consistent with your paragraphs.
  • Sitting on the fence ("both sides have good points") is not a judgement; a reasoned partial agreement is.
  • Avoid "I believe" without reasons; personal insight means your reasoning, not your preference.
Quick check
?Which sentence is evaluation rather than description?
Mission 2Worked answer

One evaluative paragraph

Statement: "Hick's theodicy successfully defends the goodness of God."

  • Point: Hick's strongest case concerns moral evil: growth needs real choices with real consequences.
  • Counter: But Phillips argues that a loving God would not use suffering such as the Holocaust as a means to anyone's growth.
  • Response: Hick could reply that the world needs real, not token, danger, and that growth is completed after death.
  • Weigh: That reply depends on an eschatology that cannot be verified, so it convinces those who already accept it more than it answers the critic.
  • Mini-judgement: So Hick defends God's goodness only if his afterlife hope is granted.

Mission 3

What "synoptic" means

  • Synoptic (Greek: "seeing together"): showing how parts of the course connect.
  • It rewards breadth (knowing several areas) and depth (using them to sharpen one argument).
  • A link is worth marks only if it does work: it explains, supports or challenges your point.
  • A link that is only mentioned ("this also comes up in ethics") does no work.
Mission 3

Your synoptic demands

  • Component 1 questions may span more than one topic in a section, e.g. conscience and free will, or religious experience and miracles.
  • Both parts still apply: AO1 10 marks, AO2 15 marks.
  • Component 2's dialogue questions are fully synoptic.
  • Only bring in another topic when the question names it or the link directly answers the question.
Mission 3

Four kinds of link

  • Shared concept: one idea in two topics. Free will in the problem of evil and in moral responsibility.
  • Shared method: the same kind of argument. The design argument and the argument from religious experience both reason inductively from experience.
  • Influence: one development reshaping another. Darwin's theory affected the design argument and debates on biblical authority.
  • Contrast: the same question answered differently. Conscience for Aquinas (reason) and Freud (super-ego).
Quick check
?Which sentence makes a genuine synoptic link rather than a bolt-on mention?
Mission 3

AQA questions that span topics

  • Read for two topics in one question: e.g. "Aquinas' natural moral law and his view of conscience", or "religious experience and miracles as evidence for God".
  • Plan both halves: give each topic its share.
  • Show the connection: for Aquinas, conscience applies the precepts of natural law to particular acts.
  • Use each topic's prescribed scholars and terms.
Mission 3Worked answer

A cross-topic paragraph

Question: Explain how Aquinas' natural moral law relates to his view of conscience.

  • Topic 1: Natural law: the primary precepts are known by synderesis, the natural habit of knowing that good is to be done and evil avoided.
  • Topic 2: Conscience (conscientia) is the act of applying those principles to a particular case.
  • Connection: So conscience is natural law at work: errors come from faulty reasoning from the precepts, not from the precepts themselves.
Quick check
?Which pair of arguments share the same method, giving a sound "shared method" link?
Mission check

?A student writing on conscience wants one synoptic link to philosophy of religion. Which link does the most work?

A synoptic link earns marks when it changes the argument. Name the concept, method, influence or contrast that connects the topics, and show what follows.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • AO1 rewards selected, accurate, developed knowledge: thought and teaching, influence, similarities and differences.
  • Develop every point: explain → support (scholar, source, example) → significance; use specialist terms correctly.
  • The first part, 10 marks, is AO1.

Mission 2

  • AO2 rewards analysis and evaluation: a line of argument, the strongest counter-argument with a response, and a justified, consistent judgement.
  • Read strength words in the statement ("most", "only", "proves"); they decide what you must show.

Mission 3

  • Synoptic means seeing together: links by shared concept, shared method, influence or contrast, each used to explain, support or challenge.
  • Questions may span topics: plan both halves and show the connection.
