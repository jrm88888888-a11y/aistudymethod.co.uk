Mission 1

Mission 1: A lamp glows while charge flows through it. Does any of the charge get used up on the way?

Make a guess: A lamp glows while charge flows through it. Does any of the charge get used up on the way?

Mission 1

Charge and current

  • Charge, Q, in coulombs (C). One electron: −e, with e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C: tiny.
  • 1 C is the charge of about 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
  • Current: the rate of flow of charge through a cross-section.
  • I = ΔQ/Δt
  • 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹. So 1 C is the charge passing a point when 1 A flows for 1 s.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A charger supplies a steady 1.5 A for 20 minutes.
  • Δt = 20 × 60 = 1200 s.
  • ΔQ = IΔt = 1.5 × 1200 = 1800 C.
  • Number of electrons = 1800 ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.1 × 10²².
Quick check
?In an electron beam in a vacuum tube, 1.5 × 10¹⁵ electrons pass through a cross-section of the beam in 8.0 s at a steady rate. What is the magnitude of the current in the beam? Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
Mission 1

What actually moves

  • Metals: free electrons, already present throughout the wire.
  • No cell connected: carriers move fast but randomly. Net flow through any cross-section: zero, so no current.
  • Connect a cell: the carriers already in the wire start to drift. The cell does not supply the charge.
Quick check
?Predict first. A copper wire forms part of a circuit carrying a steady direct current. Which statement about the charge carriers in the wire is correct?
Mission 1

Which way is the current?

  • Conventional current: the direction positive charge would move. Outside the cell: from + to −.
  • In metals, electrons move the opposite way.
Mission 1

Charge is conserved

  • Charge cannot be created, destroyed or used up.
  • In a steady current, charge cannot build up anywhere.
  • So the current into a lamp equals the current out of it. Same all round a single loop.
  • The lamp transfers energy, not charge.
  • At a junction, the currents divide so that total current in = total current out: conservation of charge.
  • Example: 3.0 A in; 1.2 A out along one branch → 1.8 A out along the other.
Quick check
?Several wires meet at a junction in a circuit with steady currents. A student measures a current of 1.20 A in one wire leading into the junction, and currents of 0.70 A and 0.40 A in two wires leading out of it. The ammeters are ideal and read correctly. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?A copper wire has a thick section X joined to a thin section Y, and there is a steady current in the wire. How do the currents IX and IY in the two sections compare?

Charge is never used up. As much charge leaves a lamp each second as enters it; what the lamp takes is energy.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Electrons in a wire drift more slowly than a snail. How can the wire still carry a large current?

Make a guess: Electrons in a wire drift more slowly than a snail. How can the wire still carry a large current?

Mission 2

Drift velocity

  • Free electrons in a metal move randomly at about 10⁵–10⁶ m s⁻¹. Net flow: zero.
  • A pd sets up a field in the wire. Each electron gains a little speed along the wire, then collides with a lattice ion.
  • Result: a slow average velocity along the wire, the mean drift velocity, v.
  • Conduction in a metal = the drift of free electrons.
Mission 2

Deriving the equation

  • Wire: cross-sectional area A; n charge carriers per unit volume (number density, m⁻³); each of charge q.
  • In time Δt, each carrier drifts a distance vΔt.
  • All carriers in a length vΔt pass the end face: number = n × A × vΔt.
  • Charge passing: ΔQ = nAvΔt × q.
  • I = ΔQ/Δt = nAvq.
  • I = nqvA
Mission 2

Worked example: how slow?

  • Copper: n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³. Wire area A = 1.0 mm² = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². Current 2.0 A.
  • v = I ÷ (nAe) = 2.0 ÷ (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)
  • v = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹: about 0.15 mm each second.
  • To drift 1 m: about 6800 s (nearly 2 hours).
  • So why does a lamp light at once? The field is set up all round the circuit almost instantly; electrons everywhere start drifting together.
Quick check
?A wire consists of two sections made of the same metal and joined end to end. The diameter of section 2 is half the diameter of section 1. The wire carries a steady current, which is the same in both sections. What is the ratio v2/v1 of the drift speed of the free electrons in section 2 to that in section 1?
Mission 2

Number density decides

  • Same current, same area: v ∝ 1/n.
  • Metals: n ≈ 10²⁸–10²⁹ m⁻³. Slow drift carries a large current.
  • Semiconductors (pure silicon at room temperature): n ≈ 10¹⁶ m⁻³. Far fewer carriers; n rises with temperature.
  • Insulators: almost no free carriers.
  • This huge range of n is why resistivities range from ~10⁻⁸ Ω m (metals) to over 10¹² Ω m (insulators).
Quick check
?A copper wire of diameter 1.6 mm carries a steady current of 3.0 A. Measurements show that its free electrons, each with a charge of magnitude e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, drift at 1.1 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹. What is the number of free electrons per unit volume in the copper?
Mission 2

Using the equation

  • Rearrange for what you need: v = I/(nAq); n = I/(Avq).
  • Convert: mm² → m² (× 10⁻⁶); diameter → radius before A = πr².
  • Compare two wires: write I = nAvq for each and divide. Unchanged quantities cancel.
Mission check

?A silver wire and a copper wire are connected in series, so they carry the same current. Silver has 5.9 × 10²⁸ free electrons per m³; copper has 8.5 × 10²⁸. The silver wire has twice the cross-sectional area of the copper wire. What is the ratio (drift speed in silver) / (drift speed in copper)?

A copper wire has about 10²⁹ free electrons in each cubic metre. Each drifts at well under a millimetre a second, but so many drift together that a few coulombs pass every second.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Make a guess: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Mission 3

Potential difference

  • Potential difference (pd) between two points: the work done (energy transferred) per unit charge moving between them.
  • V = W/Q
  • 1 volt = 1 J C⁻¹: 1 J transferred for each coulomb passing between the points.
Mission 3

Energy in a lamp

  • Charge passing through a lamp or resistor transfers electrical energy to internal energy and light.
  • The charge comes out; the energy stays behind.
  • Worked example: potential difference across a lamp 6.0 V; 3.0 C passes through.
  • W = VQ = 6.0 × 3.0 = 18 J transferred in the lamp.
Quick check
?A steady current of 0.50 A flows through a small electric motor for 2.0 minutes. In this time, 360 J of energy is transferred from electrical energy to other forms in the motor. What is the potential difference across the motor?
Mission check

?While a kettle is heating water, 2.0 × 10³ C of charge passes through its heating element and 4.6 × 10⁵ J of energy is transferred in the element. What is the potential difference across the element?

A volt counts joules per coulomb. The element takes 230 J from each coulomb that passes through it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Make a guess: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Mission 4

Resistance

  • Resistance: how strongly a component opposes the flow of charge.
  • R = V/I: the pd across it ÷ the current through it.
  • Unit: the ohm, 1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹.
  • Same potential difference: bigger R → smaller current.
Quick check
?Predict first. What is the origin of the electrical resistance of a metal wire?
Mission 4

Where resistance comes from

  • The potential difference sets up an electric field in the wire. Free electrons are accelerated by it (opposite to the field: they are negative)…
  • …then collide with the vibrating positive ions of the lattice, giving them energy.
  • Speed up, collide, speed up again: a steady average drift.
  • Ions gain energy → vibrate more → the conductor warms: the heating effect of a current.
  • The energy comes from the charges' electrical energy. No charge is used up.
Mission 4

Ohm's law

  • Ohm's law: the current in a metallic conductor is proportional to the pd across it, provided its temperature (and other physical conditions) stays constant.
  • Ohmic I–V graph: a straight line through the origin, same in both directions.
  • R = V/I defines resistance for any component, at any point. Ohm's law is the special case where R stays constant.
Mission 4

Worked example: is it ohmic?

  • Readings: 2.0 V, 0.40 A and 6.0 V, 1.2 A.
  • R = V/I: 2.0 ÷ 0.40 = 5.0 Ω; 6.0 ÷ 1.2 = 5.0 Ω. Same → consistent with ohmic.
  • Graph of I (vertical) against V (horizontal): gradient = 1.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.20 A V⁻¹ = 1/R.
  • With V on the vertical axis, the gradient is R itself.
Quick check
?The I–V characteristic of a filament lamp is plotted with current I on the vertical axis and pd V on the horizontal axis. Up to 0.10 V the graph is a straight line through the origin, and at 0.10 V the current is 0.050 A. At 6.0 V the current is 0.50 A, and the tangent to the curve at this point has a gradient of 0.040 A V⁻¹. What is the resistance of the lamp at 6.0 V, and how is it found?
Mission 4

The filament lamp

  • I–V graph: through the origin, curving towards the V axis; the same shape for reversed pd.
  • Larger current → filament hotter → ions vibrate more → more collisions → R rises.
  • So equal steps in V give smaller and smaller steps in I. Non-ohmic.
  • Resistance at a point = V/I at that point. Not the gradient of the tangent.
  • A metal wire kept at constant temperature: a straight line. Same metal, different temperature behaviour.
Mission check

?Three components X, Y and Z are tested. The currents at pds of 1.0 V, 2.0 V and 4.0 V are: X — 0.10 A, 0.20 A, 0.40 A; Y — 0.05 A, 0.15 A, 0.35 A; Z — 0.20 A, 0.30 A, 0.40 A. Which of the components are ohmic, and why?

The current doubles exactly when the resistance stays the same: an ohmic conductor, such as a metal wire kept at constant temperature.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Heat a metal wire and its resistance rises. Heat a thermistor and it falls. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Heat a metal wire and its resistance rises. Heat a thermistor and it falls. Why the difference?

Mission 5

The semiconductor diode

  • A diode lets current through in one direction only.
  • Forward bias: almost no current until the pd reaches the threshold (≈ 0.6 V for silicon). Then the current rises steeply.
  • Reverse bias: a tiny current: very high resistance.
  • Resistance = V/I at each point: very high below threshold, low above it.
Quick check
?The pd across a forward-biased silicon diode is raised from 0.2 V to 0.8 V. What happens to its resistance?
Mission 5

The NTC thermistor

  • NTC: negative temperature coefficient. Temperature up → resistance down.
  • R–T graph: falls steeply at low temperatures, then less steeply. Not a straight line.
  • I–V graph: curves upwards (towards the I axis, I vertical). A larger current heats it, R falls, so I rises faster than V.
  • Use: temperature sensor, e.g. in a potential divider.
Mission 5

Metal or thermistor: why opposite?

  • Two effects of warming:
    • ions vibrate more → more collisions → R up;
    • more charge carriers freed → R down.
  • Metal: carrier number (n) hardly changes. Collisions win → R rises (roughly in proportion to temperature rise).
  • NTC thermistor (semiconductor): n rises hugely. Carriers win → R falls.
  • In I = nqvA terms: n grows far faster than v falls.
Quick check
?A copper wire has a fixed pd across it. As it warms up, the current falls. A student explains: "Warming reduces the number of free electrons in the copper." Which correction is right?
Mission 5

The LDR

  • LDR: light-dependent resistor, made of a semiconductor.
  • Light intensity up → resistance down: very high in the dark, much lower in bright light.
  • Why: absorbed light (photons) frees more conduction electrons. n rises, so R falls.
  • Use: light sensors, e.g. switching street lights.
Quick check
?With a fixed pd across an LDR, the current is 0.20 mA in the dark and 10 mA in bright light. Assume the carriers' drift velocity is about the same in both. By what factor has the number of charge carriers per unit volume changed?
Mission check

?An NTC thermistor senses the temperature inside a fridge. After the door is closed, the thermistor cools from 15 °C to 5 °C. What happens to its resistance, and why?

Temperature changes both the ions' vibration and the number of charge carriers. In a metal the vibration wins; in a thermistor the carrier number wins, so its resistance moves the opposite way.

Mission 6

Mission 6: One dead bulb can put out a whole string of lights, yet home lamps work on their own. Why?

Make a guess: One dead bulb can put out a whole string of lights, yet home lamps work on their own. Why?

Mission 6

Closed, open, short

  • Charge flows only round a closed loop.
  • A gap anywhere in a loop (open circuit): no current in that loop.
  • Short circuit: a wire straight across a component. No potential difference across it, so no current through it.
Mission 6

Circuit diagrams

  • A circuit diagram shows what connects to what, not where things sit.
  • A diagonal arrow through a symbol = variable component.
  • Ideal wires have no resistance. An ideal cell has no internal resistance.
Quick check
?Three circuits are each made from an identical cell of negligible internal resistance, a resistor and a lamp. In circuit 1 the three form a single closed loop. Circuit 2 is the same loop with an open switch added. Circuit 3 is the same loop as circuit 1 with a wire of negligible resistance connected directly across the lamp's terminals. Which ranks the currents I1, I2 and I3 in the lamps of circuits 1, 2 and 3?
Mission 6

Two conservation laws

  • Junction: total current in = total current out. Charge is conserved: none piles up.
  • Closed loop: sum of emfs = sum of potential differences. Energy is conserved: each coulomb gains in the cell what it loses in the components.
  • Current is not used up. Energy is transferred.
Quick check
?A 9.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance is connected to three resistors in series. The potential differences across two of them are 2.0 V and 4.5 V. What is the potential difference across the third resistor?
Mission 6

Series and parallel

  • Series: one path. Same current everywhere. pds add: V = V1 + V2.
  • Parallel: separate branches. Same pd across each. Currents add: I = I1 + I2.
  • Series: R = R1 + R2 + … Parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …
  • Parallel: more paths, so the total is less than the smallest branch resistance.
  • Home lamps are in parallel: each has the full supply potential difference, whatever the others do.
Mission 6

Where the formulas come from

  • Series, same I. Energy: V = V1 + V2.
  • So IR = IR1 + IR2 → R = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel, same V. Charge: I = I1 + I2.
  • So V/R = V/R1 + V/R2 → 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
Quick check
?Resistors of 3.0 Ω, 6.0 Ω and 8.0 Ω are connected in parallel. What is the equivalent resistance of the combination?
Mission 6

Worked example

  • 6.0 V supply, negligible internal resistance. A 1.0 Ω resistor in series with 3.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in parallel.
  • Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 → 2.0 Ω.
  • Total: 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0 Ω. Supply current: 6.0 ÷ 3.0 = 2.0 A.
  • Potential difference across the pair: 2.0 × 2.0 = 4.0 V. Across the 1.0 Ω: 2.0 V. Sum: 6.0 V.
  • Branch currents: 4.0 ÷ 3.0 = 1.3 A; 4.0 ÷ 6.0 = 0.67 A. Sum: 2.0 A.
Mission 6

Meters

  • Ammeter: in series. Ideal: zero resistance, so it does not change the current.
  • Voltmeter: in parallel, across the component. Ideal: infinite resistance, so it draws no current.
  • Treat meters as ideal unless told otherwise.
  • Real meters change the circuit a little: a real ammeter adds resistance; a real voltmeter draws some current.
Quick check
?A student wants to measure the current in a lamp and the pd across it, but connects the meters the wrong way round. The circuit is: a cell, a voltmeter and the lamp connected in series in a single loop, with an ammeter connected in parallel with the lamp. Both meters are ideal. What does the student observe?
Mission check

?Three identical lamps are connected to a supply of negligible internal resistance: lamp A is in series with lamps B and C, which are in parallel with each other. The filament of lamp C breaks. What happens to the brightness of lamps A and B? (Assume each lamp's resistance stays the same.)

Parallel branches share the same potential difference, so each lamp works on its own. In series, one break stops the current in the whole loop.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Put one cell in backwards in a pack of three. What happens to the total voltage?

Make a guess: Put one cell in backwards in a pack of three. What happens to the total voltage?

Mission 7

Applying the two laws

  • Junctions: current in = current out (charge). Loops: Σ emf = Σ pd (energy).
  • Pick a direction round the loop. Crossing a cell from − to + in that direction: +emf. From + to −: −emf.
  • A current you guessed the wrong way comes out negative. That is fine.
Mission 7

Cells together

  • Series, all the same way: emfs add. Three 1.5 V cells → 4.5 V.
  • One cell reversed: its emf subtracts. 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V.
Quick check
?Three cells are connected in series in a single line: two of emf 1.5 V face one way, and one of emf 2.0 V faces the other way. What is the total emf of the line?
Quick check
?A 12 V dc supply of negligible internal resistance is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel combination of a 6.0 Ω resistor and a 12 Ω resistor. What is the current in the 12 Ω resistor?
Mission check

?A 15 V supply charges a 12 V battery. They are connected in a single loop, positive terminal to positive terminal, and the total resistance in the loop is 2.0 Ω. What is the current, and which way does it pass through the battery?

Opposing emfs subtract, whether in a battery pack or a charger. Three identical cells with one reversed: the total is one cell's emf, a third of the full pack.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current = rate of flow of charge; 1 A = 1 C s⁻¹.
  • I = ΔQ/Δt.
  • Conventional current: the way positive charge would move; electrons in metals move the other way.
  • Charge is conserved: the current into a component equals the current out.
  • At a junction: total current in = total current out.

Mission 2

  • I = nqvA: the vast range of n (metals to insulators) explains the vast range of resistivities.
  • Same current and area: v ∝ 1/n. In copper, v is only about 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹.

Mission 3

  • Potential difference = energy transferred per unit charge; 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹.
  • V = W/Q.

Mission 4

  • R = V/I, in ohms (1 Ω = 1 V A⁻¹). Ohm's law: I ∝ V at constant temperature.
  • Resistance comes from collisions of free electrons with vibrating lattice ions, which also heat the conductor.
  • Filament lamp: R rises as it heats; find R from V/I, not from a tangent.
  • NTC thermistor and LDR: R falls with more heat or light (more charge carriers).

Mission 5

  • Diode: current in one direction only, after a threshold of about 0.6 V (silicon).
  • Warming: metal R rises (more collisions); NTC thermistor R falls (many more carriers).
  • LDR: brighter light frees more conduction electrons, so R falls.

Mission 6

  • Junction: current in = current out (charge). Loop: emfs = sum of potential differences (energy).
  • Series: same I, pds add, R = R1 + R2. Parallel: same V, currents add, 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
  • Ideal ammeter: zero resistance, in series. Ideal voltmeter: infinite resistance, in parallel.

Mission 7

  • Emfs in series add; a reversed emf subtracts.
