Mission 1

Mission 1: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Make a guess: Astronauts float on the space station. How much weaker is Earth's gravity up there?

Mission 1

Newton's law of gravitation

  • Every pair of masses attracts. Gravity never repels.
  • F = GMm/r²
  • r: distance between the centres. G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
  • Acts along the line joining the centres. Each mass pulls the other equally hard (third law).
  • Inverse-square law: double r → force ÷ 4.
Mission 1

Spheres act as point masses

  • Outside a uniform sphere (or one made of uniform shells), its field is exactly that of a point mass at its centre.
  • So for planets and stars, r is measured from the centre, never from the surface.
Quick check
?Two objects exert gravitational forces of magnitude F on each other. The mass of each object is doubled, and the distance between their centres is tripled. What is the new magnitude of the gravitational force that each object exerts on the other?
Mission 1

Gravitational field strength

  • Gravitational field: a region where a mass experiences a force.
  • Field strength g = F/m: force per unit mass on a small test mass. Unit N kg⁻¹. Towards the mass.
  • Divide F = GMm/r² by m: g = GM/r².
  • Free fall: g in N kg⁻¹ equals the acceleration in m s⁻².
  • Weight W = mg.
Quick check
?A student sketches the gravitational field lines around an isolated planet that is a uniform sphere. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 1

Field lines and the uniform field near the surface

  • Field lines: the direction of the force on a small mass. Around a planet: radial, pointing inwards.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Radial lines spread out: g falls with distance.
  • Near the surface, over a few kilometres, g barely changes: treat the field as uniform.
  • Uniform field: parallel, equally spaced lines.
Mission 1

Worked example: the space station

  • Earth's radius R = 6.37 × 10⁶ m. Station about 4.0 × 10⁵ m up: r = 6.77 × 10⁶ m from the centre.
  • g ∝ 1/r²: gstation/gsurface = (6.37/6.77)² = 0.89.
  • gstation = 0.89 × 9.81 ≈ 8.7 N kg⁻¹.
  • Gravity there is only about 11% weaker. Astronauts float because they are in free fall with the station.
  • Turn it round to find a mass: M = gR²/G = 9.81 × (6.37 × 10⁶)² ÷ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg (Earth).
Quick check
?The Earth exerts a gravitational force of 2.0 × 10²⁰ N on the Moon. The mass of the Moon is about 1.2% of the mass of the Earth. Which statement about the gravitational force that the Moon exerts on the Earth is correct?
Mission check

?The diagram shows a planet of radius R and two points, P and Q, on a line through the planet's centre. The arrow at P represents the planet's gravitational field at P, of magnitude 4.5 N kg⁻¹. What is the magnitude of the planet's gravitational field at Q?

g ∝ 1/r², with r from the centre. At 400 km up, r is only 6% larger than Earth's radius, so g is about 89% of its surface value. Astronauts float because they are falling freely, not because gravity has gone.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Make a guess: Double a satellite's orbital radius. How many times longer does one orbit then take?

Mission 2

Gravity is the centripetal force

  • In a circular orbit, gravity is the only force on the satellite. It points to the centre of the planet.
  • So gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r.
  • There is no outward "centrifugal" force. The satellite is in free fall all the way around.
  • m cancels: the orbit does not depend on the satellite's mass.
  • Orbital speed: v = √(GM/r). Smaller orbit → faster.
Quick check
?An astronaut floats inside a space station that moves in a circular orbit about 400 km above Earth's surface. Which statement about the forces exerted on the astronaut is correct?
Mission 2

Deriving T² ∝ r³

  • One orbit: distance 2πr in time T, so v = 2πr/T.
  • Substitute into GMm/r² = mv²/r:
  • GM/r² = 4π²r/T²
  • T² = (4π²/GM)r³
  • T² ∝ r³. The constant 4π²/GM depends only on the central mass.
  • Same law for moons of Jupiter or planets of another star; only the constant changes.
  • Double r: T² rises 2³ = 8 times, so T rises √8 ≈ 2.8 times.
Quick check
?Tethys and Rhea are moons of Saturn, and their orbits may be taken as circular. Tethys has an orbital radius of 2.95 × 10⁸ m and a period of 1.89 days. Rhea has an orbital radius of 5.27 × 10⁸ m. What is the period of Rhea?
Mission 2

Worked example: weighing the Earth

  • The Moon: r = 3.84 × 10⁸ m, T = 27.3 days.
  • T = 27.3 × 86 400 = 2.36 × 10⁶ s.
  • Rearrange: M = 4π²r³/(GT²).
  • M = 4π² × (3.84 × 10⁸)³ ÷ (6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × (2.36 × 10⁶)²) = 6.0 × 10²⁴ kg.
  • Any orbiting body works: measure r and T (or v), get the central mass.
  • A graph of T² against r³ is a straight line through the origin; gradient = 4π²/GM.
Quick check
?Astronomers measure the orbital period T and orbital radius R of several moons that move in circular orbits around a planet. The graph shows T² plotted against R³, with a best-fit line through the origin. What is the mass of the planet? Use G = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ N m² kg⁻².
Quick check
?Which set of conditions must a satellite meet to be geostationary?
Mission 2

Geostationary and low orbits

  • A geostationary satellite stays above one point on the equator. It needs:
  • period = Earth's rotation period, about 24 h;
  • orbit in the equatorial plane;
  • moving west to east, the same way as Earth spins.
  • From T² = (4π²/GM)r³: r ≈ 4.2 × 10⁷ m, height ≈ 3.6 × 10⁷ m (36 000 km).
  • Uses: communications and TV, weather images. Ground dishes stay fixed.
Mission check

?Two planets, P and Q, move in circular orbits around the same distant star. The orbital period of Q is 8.0 times the orbital period of P. What is the ratio (orbital radius of Q)/(orbital radius of P)?

T² ∝ r³ for anything orbiting the same central mass. Doubling the radius makes the period √8 ≈ 2.8 times longer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Make a guess: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Mission 3

Coulomb's law

  • The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and to 1/r²: an inverse-square law.
  • It acts along the line joining them: like charges repel, unlike attract. Each exerts an equal and opposite force on the other.
  • F = q1q2/(4πε0r²) = kq1q2/r²
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, the permittivity of free space.
  • Treat air as a vacuum.
Mission 3

Spheres act as points

  • A uniformly charged sphere acts, outside itself, like a point charge at its centre.
  • So r is measured centre to centre.
  • Two spheres 2.0 cm across with a 3.0 cm gap: r = 1.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 5.0 cm.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • +3.0 nC and +5.0 nC, centres 4.0 cm apart, in air.
  • F = (3.0 × 10⁻⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040²) = 8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N.
  • Both positive: repulsion.
  • Halve r: force × 2² = 4 times: 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • Double one charge: force × 2.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres whose centres are 0.60 m apart exert electric forces of magnitude 1.0 N on each other. Their charges do not change. At what centre-to-centre separation would the forces have a magnitude of 9.0 N?
Mission 3

More than two charges

  • Find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • A (+2.0 nC) at x = 0; B (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m; C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Force on B:
  • From A: repulsion, pushes B in +x: 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ N.
  • From C (0.20 m away): attraction, pulls B towards C, also +x: 6.7 × 10⁻⁷ N.
  • Resultant: 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ N in +x.
  • Not in a line? Resolve into components, add, then use Pythagoras.
Quick check
?Three point charges are fixed on the x-axis: P (+4.0 nC) at x = 0, Q (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m and R (+2.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric force on Q?
Mission check

?Two small charged spheres exert electric forces of magnitude F on each other. The charge on each sphere is halved, and the distance between them is reduced to one third of its value. What is the magnitude of the force now?

Force ∝ 1/r². Halving the distance makes the force 4 times bigger; a third of the distance makes it 9 times bigger.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Make a guess: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Mission 4

The electric field

  • Electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Charges create it; it is there even when nothing is placed in it.
  • E = F/q: the force per unit positive charge.
  • A vector. Unit: N C⁻¹.
  • Direction: the push on a positive charge: away from +, towards −.
  • A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.
Quick check
?At point P, a charge of +2.0 µC experiences an electric force of 5.0 × 10⁻³ N directed east. This charge is removed and a charge of −6.0 µC is placed at P; the charges that produce the field do not move. Taking east as positive, what is the electric force on the −6.0 µC charge?
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sketches the electric field lines around two equal positive point charges a short distance apart. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 4

Field lines

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines start on + charges and end on − charges (or run to or from far away).
  • Lines never cross: the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines are drawn so their density matches E.
  • Patterns: single + (radially out) · single − (radially in) · + and − pair · two + (a neutral point between) · parallel plates.
  • A field line is not the path a charge follows.
Mission 4

The field of a point charge

  • E = q/(4πε0r²) = kq/r²
  • Inverse square: double r, E falls to ¼.
  • Field lines agree: the same lines pass through every sphere round the charge, area 4πr². Line density ∝ 1/r².
  • A charged sphere: outside, its field is that of a point charge at its centre.
  • +4.0 nC, at 0.20 m: E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.20² = 9.0 × 10² N C⁻¹, away from the charge.
Mission 4

Adding fields

  • Several charges: add their fields as vectors.
  • +4.0 nC and +1.0 nC, 0.40 m apart. Where between them is E = 0?
  • There the two fields point opposite ways. Equal when 4.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)², x measured from the 4.0 nC charge.
  • Square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 2(0.40 − x) = x and x = 0.27 m.
  • The neutral point is nearer the smaller charge.
Quick check
?The diagram shows two point charges, Q1 = +4.0 nC and Q2 = −2.0 nC, fixed on a line, and a point P on the same line. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric field strength at P?
Mission 4

The uniform field between plates

  • Two parallel plates, p.d. V, separation d.
  • Between them, away from the edges: a uniform field, the same strength and direction everywhere. Lines parallel, evenly spaced, + to −.
  • E = V/d. Unit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
  • 200 V across plates 4.0 cm apart: E = 200 ÷ 0.040 = 5.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹.
  • Force on a proton: eE = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, the same anywhere between the plates.
Mission check

?The field lines of a long, straight charged wire spread out radially, in planes at right angles to the wire: they spread in two dimensions, not three. Point P is 2.0 cm from the wire and point Q is 6.0 cm from it, both far from the wire's ends. How does the electric field strength at P compare with that at Q?

Lines are drawn so that their density matches E. Closer lines, stronger field. A point charge's lines spread over spheres (area ∝ r²), so E ∝ 1/r²; a long wire's spread over cylinders (area ∝ r), so E ∝ 1/r.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Gravity and the electric force obey the same inverse-square rule. Where do they part company?

Make a guess: Gravity and the electric force obey the same inverse-square rule. Where do they part company?

Mission 5

Force fields

  • Force field: a region in which a body experiences a non-contact force.
  • The field at a point is a vector: a size and a direction.
  • Gravitational fields are one of several kinds of field that give a force: electric and magnetic fields are others.
  • Field lines show the direction of the force on a test object; closer lines mean a stronger field.
Mission 5

Side by side

  • Field strength: g = F/m (small test mass) · E = F/q (small positive test charge).
  • Force law: F = GMm/r² · F = q1q2/(4πε0r²).
  • Point source: g = GM/r² · E = q/(4πε0r²).
  • Same: both inverse-square; both radial around a point source; field lines used for both.
  • Different: masses always attract; charges attract or repel.
  • Different: field lines point into a mass; out of a positive charge, into a negative one.
Quick check
?Which statement is true for the gravitational field of a point mass but not for the electric field of a point charge?
Mission 5

g against r

  • Outside a planet of radius R: g ∝ 1/r², falling from its surface value gs.
  • r = R → gs. r = 2R → gs/4. r = 3R → gs/9.
  • The curve never reaches zero: gravity has no range limit.
  • Distances are from the centre: "a height R above the surface" means r = 2R.
Quick check
?The gravitational field strength at the surface of a planet of radius R is 12 N kg⁻¹. What is the gravitational field strength at a height R above the planet's surface?
Quick check
?Two electrons are held a short distance apart, far from all other objects. Which statement correctly describes the forces the electrons exert on each other?
Mission check

?Two equal masses are fixed a distance d apart. Separately, a charge +Q and a charge −Q are fixed a distance d apart. Which statement about the resultant field at the midpoint of each pair is correct?

Same inverse-square rule. They part company on sign: masses only attract, while charges attract or repel. Two masses' fields cancel midway; opposite charges' fields add.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Make a guess: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Quick check
?Predict first. An uncharged parallel-plate capacitor is connected to a battery and left until it is fully charged. Which statement correctly describes how the capacitor becomes charged?
Mission 6

What a capacitor is

  • Two conducting plates, separated by an insulator (air, a vacuum or a plastic film).
  • Connect a cell: electrons are pulled off one plate, pass round the external circuit, and are pushed onto the other plate.
  • No charge crosses the gap.
  • One plate ends at +Q, the other at −Q. Net charge: zero.
  • "The charge on a capacitor" means Q, the charge on each plate.
Mission 6

Capacitance

  • C = Q/V: capacitance is the charge stored per unit p.d.
  • Unit: the farad. 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹: a capacitor storing 1 C on each plate for every 1 V across it.
  • 1 F is huge. Real values: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • For one capacitor, Q ∝ V. C is fixed by its construction, not by the p.d. you apply.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.
  • Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C on each plate.
  • Electrons moved from one plate to the other: 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹⁶.
  • Charge it to 18 V instead: Q doubles; C stays 470 μF.
Quick check
?A parallel-plate capacitor is connected to an ideal 12 V battery. When the capacitor is fully charged, its positive plate carries a charge of 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ C. What is the capacitance of the capacitor?
Mission 6

What it stores: energy

  • Separating +Q from −Q takes work. That work is stored as electric potential energy.
  • Graph of p.d. against charge: a straight line through the origin (V = Q/C).
  • Moving a small charge ΔQ across a p.d. V takes work VΔQ: a thin strip under the line.
  • Add the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle.
  • Energy = ½QV
Mission 6

Three forms, one example

  • Use Q = CV: W = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C.
  • The 470 μF capacitor at 9.0 V: energy = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = 1.9 × 10⁻² J.
  • At 18 V: the charge doubles and the potential difference doubles, so the energy is four times as much (7.6 × 10⁻² J).
  • Why ½? The cell moves charge Q through p.d. V and transfers QV. The capacitor keeps ½QV; the other ½QV is dissipated as heat in the circuit's resistance.
Quick check
?A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a potential difference of 12 V. How much energy does it store?
Mission 6

Capacitors as energy stores

  • A capacitor can release its energy very quickly: a large current for a short time, so a high power.
  • A battery stores far more energy but delivers it slowly.
  • Camera flash: a battery charges a capacitor over a few seconds; the flash tube then takes the energy in about a millisecond.
  • Defibrillator: a capacitor charged to a few thousand volts delivers about 200 J through the patient's chest in a few milliseconds.
  • How fast it empties depends on the time constant.
Mission check

?The graph shows the magnitude Q of the charge on each plate of a capacitor as a function of the potential difference V across it. What energy is stored in the capacitor when V = 6.0 V?

The plates' charges cancel, so a capacitor stores no net charge. It stores energy: the work done separating +Q from −Q, equal to ½QV.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • F = GMm/r²: always attractive, inverse square, r between centres.
  • g = F/m = GM/r²; outside a sphere, treat it as a point mass at its centre.

Mission 2

  • Gravity alone provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, so T² = (4π²/GM)r³; the satellite's mass cancels.
  • v = √(GM/r): lower orbits are faster.
  • Geostationary: 24 h period, equatorial plane, west to east, r ≈ 4.2 × 10⁷ m.

Mission 3

  • Coulomb's law F = q1q2/(4πε0r²) = kq1q2/r²: inverse-square; like repel, unlike attract.

Mission 4

  • E = F/q: force per unit positive charge; field lines show its direction, and their density its strength.
  • Point charge: E = q/(4πε0r²) = kq/r²; parallel plates: uniform, E = V/d.

Mission 5

  • Gravitational and electric fields: both inverse-square and radial from a point source; gravity only attracts, so g points towards the mass.
  • Outside a planet, g ∝ 1/r² falls from gs at r = R to gs/4 at r = 2R.

Mission 6

  • A capacitor's plates carry +Q and −Q; C = Q/V, in farads, set by construction, not by the p.d.
  • Energy stored W = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C: the area under the V–Q graph.
