Mission 1

Roots: Chaitanya and Gaudiya Vaishnavism

  • ISKCON: the International Society for Krishna Consciousness, founded in New York in 1966. Popularly "the Hare Krishnas".
  • It belongs to Gaudiya Vaishnavism, the Bengal ("Gauda") devotional tradition.
  • Founder of that tradition: Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (1486–1534), who led ecstatic group chanting of Krishna's names (sankirtan) in the streets.
  • Key texts: the Bhagavad Gita and the Bhagavata Purana (Srimad Bhagavatam).
  • Here Krishna is not only an avatar of Vishnu: he is the supreme God himself, the source of Vishnu and all forms. This is why your spec calls Krishna the heart of the bhakti movements.
  • ISKCON sees itself as part of an unbroken chain of teachers (parampara) back to Chaitanya and Krishna.
Mission 1

Krishna consciousness

  • Krishna consciousness: every soul's original nature is to love and serve Krishna. We forget this and identify with the body.
  • The soul (jiva) is eternal, both one with Krishna in quality and different from him: achintya-bhedabheda ("inconceivable oneness and difference", Chaitanya's teaching).
  • So ISKCON rejects Advaita's impersonal Brahman as the highest truth: God is a person.
  • Goal: pure loving devotion (prema bhakti) and return to Krishna's eternal realm, not merging into Brahman.
  • In this age of quarrel (Kali Yuga) the easiest path is chanting God's names.
  • The maha-mantra: "Hare Krishna, Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna, Hare Hare / Hare Rama, Hare Rama, Rama Rama, Hare Hare".

Mission 1

Why did it grow?

Religious reasons

  • A clear, personal God and a simple daily practice (chanting) open to anyone.
  • An authority: an ancient lineage, a guru, and English translations with commentary.

Cultural reasons

  • 1960s counterculture: young Westerners looking beyond materialism and drugs; communal living.
  • Celebrity: George Harrison produced the "Hare Krishna Mantra" single (1969) and gave Bhaktivedanta Manor (Hertfordshire, 1973).
  • Diaspora: from the 1970s, Indian Hindus in Britain and America joined temples in large numbers: Vaishnava worship they knew, explained in English to their children.

Mission 2

Three theistic traditions

  • Theistic: centred on a personal Supreme God (saguna Brahman).
  • Vaishnavism: Vishnu (and his avatars) supreme. Shaivism: Shiva supreme. Shaktism: the Goddess, Devi or Shakti, supreme.
  • Each has its own scriptures (beside the Vedas), temples, festivals, marks, saints and schools.
  • They grew over many centuries, especially from the time of the epics and Puranas (c. 300 BCE–1000 CE).
  • Smartas worship several deities as equal forms of one Brahman.
  • Many Hindus respect all the deities while devoting themselves to one.
Mission 2

Vaishnavism

  • Supreme deity: Vishnu, with Lakshmi; worshipped especially as his avatars Krishna and Rama.
  • Bhakti: loving devotion and surrender (prapatti) to God, who saves by grace; chanting the divine names.
  • Ritual worship: temple and home puja to the murti; offering food that becomes prasad; the tulsi plant; great temples such as Tirupati, Srirangam and Puri.
  • Moral and ethical values: dharma as Vishnu's order; many Vaishnavas are strict vegetarians; humility and service of other devotees.
  • Schools: Ramanuja's Sri Vaishnavism; Madhva; Gaudiya (Chaitanya, ISKCON); Pushti Marg; Swaminarayan.
  • Mark: a vertical U- or V-shaped tilak on the forehead.
Mission 2

Shaivism

  • Supreme deity: Shiva, with Parvati; worshipped in the lingam, a smooth, rounded stone pillar, symbol of formless divine presence.
  • Bhakti: Tamil poet-saints, the 63 Nayanars; the five-syllable mantra Om Namah Shivaya; festival Maha Shivaratri.
  • Ritual worship: pouring water and milk over the lingam and offering bilva leaves; temples such as Varanasi's Kashi Vishwanath and the twelve Jyotirlingas.
  • Asceticism: Shiva is the great yogi; many sadhus are Shaiva, with ash (vibhuti), matted hair and rudraksha beads.
  • Schools: Shaiva Siddhanta (Tamil, based on the Agamas); Kashmir Shaivism (non-dual; Abhinavagupta, c. 950–1016); Lingayats or Virashaivas (Basava, 12th century), who wear a small lingam on the body.
  • Mark: three horizontal lines of ash (tripundra).

Mission 2

Shakti: power as Goddess

  • Shakti: "power", "energy": the creative power of the divine.
  • In many traditions every god has a shakti, pictured as his consort: Lakshmi with Vishnu, Parvati with Shiva, Saraswati with Brahma.
  • A common saying (from the Saundarya Lahari, paraphrase): Shiva united with Shakti can create; without her he cannot even stir.
  • In Shaktism, the Goddess, Devi or Mahadevi, is not only a consort but the Supreme reality: she brings forth Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
  • She is worshipped as the Divine Mother: both gentle and fierce.
  • "In some contexts" one manifestation, such as Durga or Kali, is worshipped as the true form of the Divine.

Uma teaches the gods

Kena Upanishad (Swami Paramananda's translation, 1919). The gods cannot recognise a mysterious being. Indra runs towards it, but it disappears:

> "Then he saw in that very space a woman beautifully adorned, Uma of golden hue, daughter of Haimavat (Himalaya)." (3:12)

> "She (Uma) said: 'It is Brahman. It is through the victory of Brahman that ye are victorious.'" (4:1)

  • Uma is a name of Parvati, Shiva's consort.

Mission 2

Forms of the Goddess

  • Durga: the warrior, riding a lion. In the Devi Mahatmya (c. 5th–6th century; part of the Markandeya Purana) the gods' powers join to form her, and she slays the buffalo-demon Mahishasura.
  • Kali: dark, fierce, garlanded with skulls; destroys evil and ego; loved as Mother (e.g. by Ramakrishna, 1836–1886).
  • Lakshmi (wealth, good fortune), Saraswati (learning, music), Parvati (devoted wife and mother).
  • Village goddesses (grama-devatas) protect local communities.
  • Festivals: Navaratri (nine nights), Durga Puja (Bengal), Kali Puja. Shakti Peethas: shrines where, the story says, parts of the goddess Sati's body fell.

Mission 2

Lipner and Sivananda on the Goddess

  • J Lipner (Hindus, 1994/2010), paraphrase:
  • The divine feminine is central to Hinduism, not an add-on: the Goddess can be gentle consort and independent, fierce power.
  • Worship of a goddess as supreme fits his polycentric picture: one of many centres of devotion, each able to stand for the whole.
  • S Sivananda (1887–1963), paraphrase:
  • Shakti is the dynamic power of the one Brahman; Shiva (pure consciousness) and Shakti (power) cannot be separated.
  • Worship of the Divine Mother, e.g. at Navaratri, is a valid path: the devotee approaches the one God as Mother.

Mission 2Evaluate

Separate religions?

  • Claim: Vaishnavism, Shaivism and Shaktism are best seen as separate religions.
  • For: Each has its own Supreme God, scriptures, temples, saints, theology and initiation.
  • For: In history they sometimes competed; some devotees worship only their own deity.
  • Against: They share the Vedas, dharma, karma, samsara and moksha, and the idea of one Brahman.
  • Against: Many Hindus move between them: a Vaishnava family may visit a Shiva temple at Shivaratri.
  • Against: "Religion" is a Western category that may not fit.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion: beliefs, practices, institutions, self-understanding? Do devotees see themselves as Hindus first?

Mission 3

Four aims of life

  • The purusharthas: "aims of a person", four goals a full human life may pursue.
  • Dharma: right living, duty, virtue.
  • Artha: wealth, security, success, power.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, beauty, enjoyment of the senses.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara.
  • All four are legitimate. The question is how they fit together.
Mission 3

Artha: wealth and power

  • Earning a living, owning property, supporting a family, building a business, and for rulers, governing well.
  • Without artha, a household cannot give to the poor, support temples or pay for ritual.
  • The Arthashastra (attributed to Kautilya, compiled c. 2nd century BCE–3rd century CE): a manual of statecraft and economics; wealth as the basis of a stable kingdom.
  • Artha is good when gained and used within dharma: honestly, generously, without harm.
Mission 3

Kama: pleasure and love

  • Delight in the senses, music, art, food, friendship, romantic and sexual love within marriage.
  • The Kama Sutra (Vatsyayana, c. 3rd century CE) is a guide to the art of pleasant living, not only to sex.
  • Kama shows that Hindu tradition does not reject pleasure: it is one of life's aims.

> "These am I, free from passion and desire; / Yet am I right desire in all who yearn" (Gita 7:11, Arnold)

  • Krishna is present in desire that is not contrary to dharma (the Sanskrit of 7:11): kama, too, needs dharma's limits.
Mission 3

How the four fit together

  • Trivarga: the first three, the "group of three" for life in the world.
  • Dharma frames artha and kama, like the banks of a river: they give the water its course.
  • Moksha goes beyond the three: it is freedom from the whole cycle in which they are pursued.
  • Two directions of life: pravritti (engagement with the world: dharma, artha, kama) and nivritti (withdrawal: moksha).
  • Most Hindus live engaged lives and turn more towards moksha with age.
Mission 3Read the source

  • Some say the chief good is dharma and artha; others, kama and artha; others, dharma alone; others, artha alone.
  • The text's answer: the chief good is the three together (trivarga).

Mission 3

The aims across the stages of life

  • Brahmacharya (student): learn dharma; self-discipline.
  • Grihastha (householder): pursue artha and kama within dharma; raise a family; support society.
  • Vanaprastha (retirement): loosen ties; growing vairagya (detachment, freedom from craving).
  • Sannyasa (renunciation): give up everything for moksha.
  • Vairagya is the bridge: detachment turns a person from the three worldly aims towards liberation.
  • Your spec lists the ashrama values as dharma, artha, kama and vairagya, and their relationship with moksha.
  • Bilimoria (extract 1, paraphrase): the aims form a scheme in which worldly goods are pursued under dharma, while the ideal of moksha pulls the other way, towards renunciation; ethics must manage that tension.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is moksha the most important aim?

  • Claim: Moksha is the most important of the four aims.
  • For: It alone ends samsara; the others are temporary and bind (even punya).
  • For: Upanishads and Vedanta make liberation the highest goal (parama purushartha).
  • Against: Dharma is the foundation: without it, no aim is pursued rightly, and moksha is not reached.
  • Against: For most Hindus the householder's three aims fill most of life; Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together.
  • Against: For some bhakti traditions, loving God matters more than liberation itself.
  • Judgement criteria: Most important for whom, and at which stage of life? For the individual, or for society?

Mission 4

Virtue and two kinds of dharma

  • A virtue is a settled quality of character that shows itself in action.
  • Hindu texts set out two levels of duty:
  • Sadharana dharma: duties common to everyone (non-violence, truthfulness, not stealing, purity, self-control). Manusmriti 10.63 gives a list like this for all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • Varnashrama dharma (svadharma): duties of your class and stage of life.
  • The three virtues in this mission belong to the first level: non-violence, detachment, self-restraint.
  • Each is thought to purify the mind and so lead towards moksha (liberation).
Mission 4

Non-violence (ahimsa)

  • Ahimsa: a- (not) + himsa (harm): not harming any living being in thought, word or deed.
  • Basis: the same atman (self) is present in every living being, so harming another is harming life that is one with yours.
  • Karma: Hindus believe harm done returns as suffering in this or a later life.
  • Its positive side: compassion (daya) and kindness to all.
  • Many Hindus apply it to diet: vegetarianism, and avoiding beef in particular.
  • It is weighed against other duties: a warrior may still have to fight (see Mission 6).
Mission 4

Detachment (vairagya)

  • Vairagya: freedom from craving for objects, pleasure and results.
  • Not indifference and not laziness: the Gita's ideal is to act fully while letting go of the fruits (nishkama karma, desireless action).
  • Tyaga: renouncing the fruit of action (Gita 18). Sannyasa: renouncing the world itself, the last ashrama.
  • Why it matters: desire for results binds the self to karma and rebirth.
  • Gita 2:47 (Arnold): "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."
Mission 4

Self-restraint (dama)

  • Dama or indriya-nigraha: control of the senses and appetites.
  • Image in Gita 2:58: the wise person draws back the senses from their objects "as the wise tortoise draws its four feet safe / Under its shield" (Arnold).
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad V.2: the creator's one syllable "Da" is heard by gods, humans and demons as three lessons: restrain yourselves, give, be compassionate (paraphrase).
  • Practised as fasting, celibacy (brahmacharya) in the student stage, moderation in food and speech.
  • Self-restraint makes the other two virtues possible: anger restrained becomes non-violence; desire restrained becomes detachment.
Mission 4Read the source

  • Krishna calls this list the divine endowment (daivi sampad): "The Heavenly Birth brings to deliverance" (16:5, Arnold).

Mission 4

The three gunas

  • Prakriti (matter, nature, including mind) is woven from three gunas (strands, qualities). The idea comes from Samkhya philosophy.
  • Sattva: light, purity, calm, goodness, knowledge.
  • Rajas: passion, energy, desire, restlessness.
  • Tamas: darkness, inertia, dullness, delusion.
  • All three are present in everything and everyone; their balance shifts and shapes character.
  • They belong to prakriti, not to the atman, which is beyond them.

> "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature, 'Soothfastness,' / 'Passion,' and 'Ignorance.' These three bind down / The changeless Spirit in the changeful flesh." (Bhagavad Gita 14:5, Arnold)

Mission 4

Virtues, varna, ashrama and the aims of life

  • The Gita links varna to the gunas: duties are "fixed by reason of the Qualities / Planted in each" (18:41, Arnold). A Brahmin's virtues are "serenity, / Self-mastery, religion, purity" (18:42).
  • Ashramas train the virtues in turn: student (self-restraint, celibacy) → householder (non-violence, generosity, wealth and pleasure within dharma) → forest-dweller (loosening ties) → sannyasi (complete detachment).
  • Many modern Hindus read guna-based varna as character, not birth; critics note it was used to justify birth-based caste.
Mission 4

Bilimoria on Indian ethics

  • Purushottama Bilimoria (philosopher of Indian ethics), "Indian ethics", in P. Singer (ed.), A Companion to Ethics (1991): anthology extract 1. Paraphrase only:
  • Indian ethics grows from the Vedic idea of rta (cosmic order) into dharma, the order of duties that upholds society and the cosmos.
  • He distinguishes universal duties binding on all from the particular duties of one's station, and notes the tension between them.
  • He reads the Gita as a synthesis: act according to your dharma, but without desire for the fruits, which joins the active life to the ideal of renunciation.
  • He notes the critical question whether such ethics can be separated from a hierarchical social order.
  • Use him to show why the gunas matter: they explain different natures, and so different duties and virtues.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is virtue the way to liberation?

  • Claim: Living virtuously is enough to achieve moksha.
  • For: Virtue purifies the mind; the "divine endowment" leads to deliverance (Gita 16:5).
  • For: desireless action (nishkama karma, Gita 2:47) frees the self from the craving that binds it to karma and rebirth.
  • Against: Even sattva binds, by attachment to happiness and knowledge (Gita 14:6). The liberated person is gunatita, beyond all three gunas (14:22–25).
  • Against: Advaita Vedanta (the non-dualist school of Shankara): only knowledge of atman as Brahman liberates; virtue only prepares. Many bhakti (devotional) traditions: liberation comes by God's grace, not merit.
  • Judgement criteria: What does each school mean by moksha? Is virtue a cause of liberation or a sign of it? Can a person be detached while still doing worldly duty?

Mission 5

What ahimsa asks

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, non-harming (a- "not" + himsa "harm").
  • It covers thought, word and deed: hatred and cruel speech are himsa too.
  • It also covers harm you cause or approve through others.
  • Why: the same atman is in every living being.

> "… sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living, and all living things / In that Life-Soul contained." (Bhagavad Gita 6:29, Arnold)

  • Karma: harm returns as suffering, in this life or the next.
  • Positive side: compassion and respect for all life.
Mission 5

Ahimsa in Hindu tradition

  • Chandogya Upanishad 3.17.4: non-violence is listed with austerity, generosity, uprightness and truthfulness as the "gifts" of a life lived as sacrifice (paraphrase).
  • Mahabharata (Anushasana Parva): ahimsa is called the highest dharma (ahimsa paramo dharmah).
  • Yoga Sutras 2.30–2.35: ahimsa is the first yama (restraint); where it is firmly established, hostility ceases (paraphrase).
  • Manusmrti 10.63: ahimsa is among the duties common to all four varnas (paraphrase).
  • But Hindu ahimsa is usually weighed against other duties: sacrifice in the Vedic period, the warrior's duty, self-defence. It means minimising harm rather than an absolute vow.
  • By contrast, Jains (an Indian tradition with ahimsa at its heart) take it further: monks and nuns vow ahimsa absolutely, some sweeping their path to protect tiny beings; Jains hold that harm binds karma to the soul.

Mission 5

Animals

  • Animals have atman and are part of samsara: a human may be reborn as an animal, and an animal as a human.
  • The cow is honoured as a mother (gau mata): she gives milk, and Krishna was a cowherd. Most Hindus do not eat beef; many states in India restrict cow slaughter.
  • Vegetarianism is widespread and seen as sattvic, but varies: many Bengali and coastal Hindus eat fish; some communities eat meat; some Shakta temples still offer animal sacrifice.
  • Many Hindus support gaushalas (cow shelters) and avoid leather, silk or products tested on animals; others accept animal testing for human medicine as a lesser harm.
Mission 5Evaluate

Can a person live by ahimsa today?

  • Claim: Ahimsa, meaning never harming any living being, is a realistic standard in the modern world.
  • For: Millions of Jains and Hindus live as strict vegetarians; ahimsa shapes diet, work and medicine.
  • For: Ahimsa is about intention and minimising harm, which anyone can practise.
  • Against: Harm is built into life: farming, transport, medicine. Even the Manusmrti, a Hindu law text, admits that daily housework kills small creatures (3.68).
  • Against: Modern supply chains hide harm; the Manusmrti says the buyer of meat shares in the killing (5.51).
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa an absolute rule or an ideal to approach? Does intention or outcome matter more? Is Jain or Hindu ahimsa the standard being tested?

Mission 6

Ahimsa meets the warrior's duty

  • In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna, a kshatriya (warrior), refuses to fight his relatives at Kurukshetra.
  • Krishna answers: a warrior's svadharma (own duty) is to fight a righteous war (dharma yuddha); refusing would be sin.

> "Nought better can betide a martial soul / Than lawful war" (Bhagavad Gita 2:31, Arnold)

  • The atman cannot be killed (2:19–20), so the soldier destroys only bodies.
  • So in traditional Hindu ethics, ahimsa is the general rule, but force to protect dharma can be a duty for those whose role it is.
Mission 6Read the source

Two ways to read the battle

> "… if thou shunn'st / This honourable field--a Kshattriya-- / If, knowing thy duty and thy task, thou bidd'st / Duty and task go by--that shall be sin!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:33, Arnold)

  • Literal reading (e.g. Sri Aurobindo, 1872–1950, Essays on the Gita): the Gita really does teach that righteous war can be a duty.
  • Allegorical reading (Gandhi, Anasaktiyoga, 1929): Kurukshetra is the human heart; the battle is between good and evil impulses; the Gita teaches selfless action and so, in the end, non-violence.

Q1 What does Krishna say Arjuna's refusal would be?

Show answer

Sin: a failure in his duty as a kshatriya.

Q2 How can Gandhi accept this verse yet reject war?

Show answer

He reads the "field" and the "battle" as the inner struggle of the soul, not a call to physical fighting.

Mission 6

Gandhi's ahimsa

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948).
  • Influences: his Gujarati Vaishnava home, Jain friends and teachers (e.g. Raychandbhai), the Gita, Tolstoy and the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Ahimsa for Gandhi is active love, not passivity: refusing to hate, while resisting injustice.
  • It is the courage of the strong: he wrote (1920, paraphrase) that if the only choice were cowardice or violence, he would choose violence.
  • Linked with truth: he came to say that "Truth is God". Non-violence is the means; truth is the end.
  • Discipline: brahmacharya, simple living, vegetarianism, fasting.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is ahimsa a workable standard in the modern world?

  • Claim: Ahimsa is a relevant and viable standard to live by today.
  • For: Gandhi's non-violent resistance (satyagraha, "truth force") helped win Indian independence (1947) and shaped the US civil rights movement.
  • For: Modern weapons make war's harm uncontrollable; non-violence may be the only rational path.
  • Against: Non-violence relies on the opponent's conscience. Gandhi's 1938 advice that Jews in Germany resist non-violently was strongly criticised (e.g. by Martin Buber, 1939).
  • Against: Independence came with the violence of Partition; India itself keeps an army and nuclear weapons.
  • Judgement criteria: Is ahimsa a personal virtue or a state policy? Does it require results to be valid? Does dharma yuddha already make ahimsa realistic?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ISKCON (1966) brings Chaitanya's Gaudiya Vaishnava bhakti to the world: Krishna is the supreme person; chant his names.

Mission 2

  • Vaishnavism (Vishnu supreme; bhakti, puja, ethical life), Shaivism (Shiva supreme; lingam, bhakti, asceticism) and Shaktism (Devi supreme) are the main theistic traditions.
  • Shakti is the creative power of the divine, pictured as Goddess; for Shaktas she is supreme, worshipped as Durga, Kali and others; right-hand devotion and left-hand Tantra.
  • Lipner: the divine feminine is central and polycentric; Sivananda: Shakti is Brahman's power, approached as Mother.
  • Whether they are separate religions or one family is debated.

Mission 3

  • Four purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama, moksha; dharma frames artha and kama; moksha goes beyond all three.
  • Relative importance is debated: Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together; Vedanta makes moksha the highest aim.
  • Ashrama values: dharma, artha, kama, then vairagya (detachment) turning towards moksha.

Mission 4

  • In Hindu teaching, non-violence, detachment and self-restraint are duties for all (sadharana dharma) that purify the mind.
  • In the Gita and Samkhya, the three gunas (sattva, rajas, tamas) make up nature; the virtues are sattvic, but liberation lies beyond all three.

Mission 5

  • Ahimsa is non-harming in thought, word and deed, based on atman in all beings and on karma.

Mission 6

  • Traditional Hindu ethics weighs ahimsa against a warrior's duty: dharma yuddha allows just war under strict rules.
  • Gandhi read the Gita as an inner battle and made ahimsa a political force through satyagraha.
