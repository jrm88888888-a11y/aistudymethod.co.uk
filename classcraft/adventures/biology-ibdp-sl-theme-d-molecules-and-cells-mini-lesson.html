Mission 1

PCR: copy one stretch of DNA

  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies one chosen stretch of DNA, in a tube.
  • It needs:
    • the template DNA sample
    • two primers: short single strands of DNA
    • free DNA nucleotides
    • a heat-stable DNA polymerase
  • The usual enzyme is Taq polymerase.
  • The cycle repeats, about 25–35 times. Each cycle doubles the target.
Mission 1

One cycle, three steps

  • 1 Denature, about 95 °C. Hydrogen bonds between bases break. The strands separate.
  • 2 Anneal, about 55 °C. Primers bind by complementary base pairing to each end of the target, one on each strand.
  • 3 Extend, about 72 °C. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer, building new complementary strands.
  • The new strands become templates in the next cycle.
  • Say: "heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands" — not: "heat breaks the DNA into nucleotides".
Mission 1

Why primers? Why a heat-stable enzyme?

  • DNA polymerase can only add to an existing strand. Primers give it a start.
  • The two primers sit either side of the target. Only the DNA between them is amplified.
  • Primers also stop the separated strands re-pairing at once.
  • Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs.
  • It is not denatured at 95 °C, so it works through every cycle. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first cycle.
  • 72 °C is close to Taq's optimum temperature.
  • Say: "primers anneal to complementary sequences" — not: "primers copy the DNA" or "primers separate the strands".
Quick check
?Why is Taq polymerase, rather than a DNA polymerase from human cells, used in the polymerase chain reaction?
Mission 1

Worked example: how many copies?

  • Every molecule is copied in every cycle, so the number doubles each cycle.
  • Copies after n cycles = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Example: 1 molecule, 30 cycles.
    • 1 × 2³⁰ = 1 073 741 824
    • About 1.1 × 10⁹ copies: over a billion.
  • Example: 5 molecules, 3 cycles: 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 5 × 2³ = 40.
  • This assumes every strand is copied each cycle. In a real tube, copying slows in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.
  • Say: "multiply by 2ⁿ" — not: "multiply by 2 × n".
Quick check
?A PCR reaction starts with 3 double-stranded DNA molecules of the target section. How many double-stranded molecules of the target are present after 4 complete cycles?
Mission 1

Gel electrophoresis: sorting by length

  • DNA samples go into wells at one end of a gel. A voltage is applied across the gel.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups). Every fragment moves towards the positive electrode.
  • The gel is a mesh. Shorter fragments slip through more easily and travel further.
  • Fragments of the same length travel the same distance: they form one band.
  • A ladder of fragments of known length runs in one lane, for comparison.
  • Say: "shorter fragments travel further" — not: "longer fragments travel further because they carry more charge".
Quick check
?Three DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1200 base pairs are loaded into the same well of a gel and a voltage is applied. Which statement describes the result?
Mission 1

What PCR and gels are used for

  • Forensics: amplify DNA from a hair, a drop of blood or skin cells, then compare it (see Mission 2).
  • Paternity and relationships: compare amplified regions between people, animals or plants.
  • Diagnosing infections: detect a pathogen's DNA or RNA, even at low levels.
  • Genetic testing: amplify a gene, then test it for a particular allele.
  • Ancient and degraded DNA: amplify the little that survives.
  • Before sequencing: make enough copies of a gene to read its base sequence.
Mission check

?The human ACE gene has two common alleles. Allele I contains an extra stretch of about 300 base pairs that allele D lacks. One pair of primers on either side of this stretch gives a 490 base pair product from allele I and a 190 base pair product from allele D. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, one person's lane shows two bands. Which interpretation is correct?

Primers pick the stretch to copy; the gel sorts the copies by length. Together they turn a trace of DNA into a genotype.

Mission 2

Repeats that vary between people

  • Much of the genome does not code for proteins. Some of it is tandem repeats: a short sequence repeated end to end, e.g. GATA GATA GATA.
  • Forensic profiles use short tandem repeats (STRs): units of 2–6 bases.
  • The number of repeats at a locus varies between people. More repeats: a longer fragment.
  • You inherit one allele at each locus from each parent: one or two bands per locus.
  • Many loci, many alleles at each: the chance that two people share all of them is very low. Identical twins are the exception.
Mission 2

Making a profile

  • The method is called DNA profiling.
  • 1 Extract DNA from the sample.
  • 2 Amplify several repeat loci by PCR. Primers bind either side of each repeat.
  • 3 Separate the fragments by length: electrophoresis.
  • 4 Detect the bands: labelled primers or labelled probes make them visible. Compare samples.
Quick check
?Two people have different alleles at one short tandem repeat locus. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, their bands for this locus are in different places. What difference between the alleles does the gel detect?
Mission 2

Worked example: why test many loci?

  • Illustrative: suppose 1 in 10 unrelated people share a profile at one locus by chance.
  • Loci on different chromosomes are inherited independently. Multiply the chances.
  • 2 loci: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1 in 100.
  • 10 loci: (1/10)¹⁰ = 1 in 10¹⁰: about one person in 10 000 000 000.
  • Real casework: the UK system tests 16 repeat loci; the US core set has 20.
  • Relatives share more alleles, so their chance of matching is higher.
  • Say: "the chance of a coincidental match is very low" — not: "the profile proves the suspect did it".
Quick check
?A forensic laboratory compares a sample from a crime scene with a suspect's DNA using 20 different markers rather than one. Why does this make the result more reliable?
Mission 2

Reading a profile

  • Forensic match: the sample could come from a person only if every band matches.
  • One band different: that person is excluded.
  • Paternity: each band in a child came from the mother or the father.
    • Cross out the child's bands that match the mother.
    • Every band left must be in the father's profile.
    • The father has other bands the child did not inherit: that is expected.
  • Matching bands are consistent with a relationship. Different bands exclude one.
Quick check
?A DNA profile was prepared for a mother, her child and a man who may be the father. The fragment lengths (in base pairs) in each profile were: mother 100, 220, 260, 340; child 100, 150, 220, 300; man 150, 180, 300, 330. What can be concluded?
Mission 2

Uses: identity and relationships

  • Forensics: link a sample from a scene to a person, or exclude a suspect.
  • Paternity and family relationships; identifying human remains.
Mission check

?A captive breeding programme needs to know which of two males fathered a chick. Profiles at three repeat loci (fragment lengths in base pairs; illustrative data):
BirdLocus ALocus BLocus C
Mother120, 128210, 21895, 101

Same logic as a crime scene: one mismatch excludes; matches at many loci make a coincidence very unlikely.

Mission 3

From gene to ribosome

  • DNA holds the genetic information. In eukaryotic cells it stays in the nucleus.
  • mRNA carries a copy of one gene's information to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Translation: the base sequence of mRNA is used to build the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
  • The mRNA is read in codons: three bases = one amino acid (or a stop signal).
  • Amino acids are not made from the bases. They already float in the cytoplasm.
  • Say: "translation makes a polypeptide on a ribosome" — not: "the ribosome converts bases into amino acids".
Mission 3

Two RNAs, two shapes

  • mRNA: a single, linear strand; hundreds to thousands of nucleotides; a run of codons. No folded clover-leaf.
  • tRNA: a single strand of about 80 nucleotides, folded into a clover-leaf shape.
  • The fold is held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in the same strand.
  • One loop carries the anticodon: three bases.
  • The 3′ end is the amino acid attachment site. Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid.
Mission 3

The ribosome

  • Made of rRNA and protein, in two subunits: small and large.
  • The small subunit binds the mRNA.
  • The large subunit holds two tRNAs at the same time.
  • rRNA gives the ribosome its shape and catalyses peptide bond formation.
Quick check
?A cell contains many different kinds of tRNA molecule. What is the role of a tRNA during translation?
Mission 3

Codon meets anticodon

  • The anticodon is complementary to the codon: A–U, U–A, G–C, C–G.
  • RNA has uracil, not thymine. So codon AUG pairs with anticodon UAC.
  • Codon and anticodon are held by hydrogen bonds: weak, so the tRNA can leave later.
  • The ribosome checks only this pairing. It never checks the amino acid.
  • Say: "complementary to the codon" — not: "the same as the codon".
Mission 3

Elongation, one codon at a time

  • (1) Two tRNAs sit on the ribosome, each paired with its codon.
  • (2) A peptide bond forms between their amino acids, by condensation. The chain now hangs on the second tRNA.
  • (3) The ribosome moves one codon along the mRNA. The empty tRNA leaves.
  • (4) The next tRNA pairs with the newly exposed codon. Repeat.
Quick check
?An antibiotic stops the ribosome from moving along the mRNA but does not affect the binding of tRNAs or the formation of peptide bonds. What effect will this have on translation of an mRNA?
Mission 3

Start, stop and the energy bill

  • Start codon AUG: translation begins here. AUG also codes for methionine, the first amino acid.
  • Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA): no tRNA pairs with them. The finished polypeptide is released.
  • Many ribosomes can translate one mRNA at once, so many copies of the polypeptide are made quickly.
Mission 3

Worked example: decoding a gene

  • Template DNA strand: 3′–TAC AAG CCT TTC ACT–5′.
  • Step 1. mRNA is complementary to the template, with U for T: AUG UUC GGA AAG UGA.
  • Step 2. Read from AUG in threes. Use the table: AUG Met · UUC Phe · GGA Gly · AAG Lys · UGA stop.
  • Step 3. Polypeptide: Met–Phe–Gly–Lys. Four amino acids; the stop codon adds none.
  • Step 4. Anticodons used (each written 3′→5′): UAC, AAG, CCU, UUC. None for UGA.
  • Notice: the anticodons have the template's sequence, with U for T.
Quick check
?The coding region of an mRNA, from the first nucleotide of the start codon to the last nucleotide of the stop codon, is 903 nucleotides long. How many amino acids does the polypeptide translated from this region contain?
Mission 3

Read the data: the first word of the code

  • 1961: Nirenberg and Matthaei mixed a cell-free extract of E. coli (ribosomes, tRNAs, enzymes, ATP, amino acids) with an artificial RNA made only of uracil: poly-U.
  • In each tube, one amino acid (or one group) was radioactive. They measured radioactivity built into protein.
  • Real data (Nirenberg and Matthaei, 1961), counts per minute per mg protein:

  • In another run, an RNA-digesting enzyme cut the poly-U effect to near background (29 500 → 120).

Mission check

?In a test tube translation system, the tRNAs with the anticodon 3′-ACG-5′ normally carry the amino acid cysteine, and they are the only tRNAs in the system that pair with the codon 5′-UGC-3′. Before translation, the cysteine attached to these tRNAs is chemically changed into a different amino acid, alanine; the tRNAs themselves are unchanged. An mRNA containing the codon 5′-UGC-3′ is then translated. Which prediction about the polypeptide made is correct?

The ribosome reads codons, never amino acids. The tRNA is the adapter that links the two, so whatever it carries goes in.

Mission 4

What is a gene mutation?

  • A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Many arise as errors during DNA replication that are not corrected.
  • The changed DNA is transcribed into changed mRNA. That may change the polypeptide.
  • Larger changes, to whole chromosomes, are chromosome mutations.
Mission 4

Three ways to change a sequence

  • (1) Substitution: one base replaced by another. Same length.
  • (2) Deletion: a base removed. One shorter.
  • (3) Insertion: a base added. One longer.
  • Compare the sequences base by base, and count their lengths.
Quick check
?Part of the base sequence of a gene is GAT TCA GGC ATT before a mutation and GAT TCG GCA TT after it. Which type of gene mutation has occurred?
Mission 4

A substitution: three possible outcomes

  • A substitution changes one codon only. The reading frame is untouched.
  • Outcome 1: the new codon codes for the same amino acid. No change. The code is degenerate.
  • Outcome 2: the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid changes.
  • Outcome 3: the new codon is a stop codon. The polypeptide is cut short.
  • You may also see: silent (1), missense (2), nonsense (3).
Mission 4

Worked example: four mutations, one gene

  • Code table: AUG Met · GCA, GCU Ala · UGG Trp · AAG Lys · GAG Glu · CUU Leu · CAU His · GGA Gly · AGC Ser · UGA stop.
  • Normal mRNA: AUG GCA UGG AAG CUU → Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu.
  • (a) GCA → GCU: Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu. No change.
  • (b) AAG → GAG: Met–Ala–Trp–Glu–Leu. One amino acid changed.
  • (c) UGG → UGA: Met–Ala–stop. Two amino acids only.
  • (d) Delete the G of codon 2: AUG CAU GGA AGC UU… → Met–His–Gly–Ser… Every codon after the deletion changes.
Quick check
?In mRNA the codons GCU, GCC, GCA and GCG all code for alanine. A base substitution in a gene changes the codon GCU in its mRNA to GCC. What is the effect on the polypeptide?
Mission 4

Deletions and insertions: the frame shifts

  • The ribosome reads in threes from the start codon. There are no gaps or commas.
  • Delete one base and every later base moves up one place: a frameshift.
  • Inserting one base does the same.
  • Every downstream codon is regrouped. Codons before the mutation are unchanged.
  • A new stop codon usually appears early. The polypeptide almost always stops working.
  • Three bases added or removed: the frame is kept. One amino acid is gained or lost (a neighbour may change too).
  • Large insertions or deletions remove or add many amino acids: the polypeptide is unlikely to fold or work.
  • Say: "frameshift: all downstream triplets change" — not: "one base, one amino acid".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, strain A has a deletion of one nucleotide in codon 10 of a gene that has 300 codons. Strain B has a deletion of the three adjacent nucleotides that make up codon 10 of the same gene. Which prediction about the polypeptides translated from the two genes is best supported?
Mission 4

A real point mutation: sickle cell

  • Gene: β-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin.
  • DNA coding strand: GAG → GTG. mRNA: GAG → GUG.
  • Amino acid 6: glutamic acid (charged) → valine (non-polar).
  • At low oxygen, these haemoglobin molecules stick together into long fibres.
  • Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, rigid, and block capillaries.
  • One base out of hundreds. Two copies of this allele: sickle cell anaemia.
Quick check
?In the allele that causes sickle cell anaemia, the mRNA codon for amino acid 6 of the beta chain of haemoglobin is GUG instead of GAG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG codes for valine. What is the effect of this mutation on the protein?
Mission 4

Harmful, neutral or beneficial?

  • Harmful: the protein no longer works, or works worse.
  • Neutral: no detectable effect on the organism. Silent mutations are usually neutral.
  • Beneficial: the new protein gives an advantage.
  • The environment decides: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic helps only where that antibiotic is present.
  • Say: "silent: same amino acid sequence" — not: "silent: no effect on the phenotype". That is neutral.
Mission check

?A gene codes for a polypeptide of 400 amino acids. A single base substitution changes the fifth codon of its mRNA from UAC to UAA. In the code table, UAC codes for tyrosine and UAA is a stop codon. What is the most likely effect?

One letter can do nothing, change one amino acid, or stop the whole chain. It depends on which codon it creates.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • PCR repeats denature, anneal and extend; primers mark the target and each cycle doubles it (starting number × 2ⁿ).
  • Taq polymerase survives 95 °C; annealing about 55 °C; extension about 72 °C.
  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments travel further.

Mission 2

  • Repeat loci vary in length between individuals; PCR copies them and a gel sorts them by length.
  • Chances at independent loci multiply, so more loci make a chance match less likely.
  • One band that cannot be explained excludes; matching bands are consistent with, not proof of, identity or relationship.

Mission 3

  • mRNA codons pair with complementary tRNA anticodons on the ribosome; each tRNA brings its amino acid, and peptide bonds join them one codon at a time from AUG to a stop codon.

Mission 4

  • Substitutions change one codon: same amino acid (degenerate code), one different amino acid, or an early stop. Deletions and insertions not in multiples of three shift the frame and change every downstream codon.
