Mission 1

Ending a marriage

  • Divorce: the legal ending of a marriage. Separation: living apart while still married.
  • Annulment: a declaration that a marriage was never valid in the first place.
  • Remarriage: marrying again while a former spouse is still alive.
  • Law in England and Wales: since April 2022, no-fault divorce (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020). The only ground: the marriage has broken down irretrievably; no one has to be blamed.
  • Common reasons: adultery, abuse, addiction, money worries, growing apart.
Mission 1

What Jesus taught

  • Marriage is God's work: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; Genesis 2:24).
  • Matthew 19:1–12: Pharisees ask whether divorce is allowed. Jesus says Moses allowed it "because of the hardness of your hearts" (Matthew 19:8), but it was not God's plan.
  • In Matthew, one exception: sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9; 5:32). Mark has no exception (Mark 10:11–12).

Mission 1

Catholic teaching: annulment, not divorce

  • A valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power (CCC 2382, paraphrase).
  • Divorce is a grave offence against the natural law (CCC 2384, paraphrase), but civil divorce may be tolerated to protect legal rights or children (CCC 2383); separation is allowed.
  • A spouse unjustly abandoned is not to blame (CCC 2386).
  • Remarriage while a former spouse lives is not accepted; the person cannot usually receive Holy Communion.
  • Annulment (declaration of nullity): a Church tribunal finds the marriage was never valid, e.g. no free consent, force or fear, no intention of a lifelong faithful union open to children. Both may then marry in church.

Mission 1Diversity

Other Christians

  • Orthodox: marriage is meant for life, but out of mercy (economy) the Church allows divorce and up to three marriages; a second wedding has a penitential tone.
  • Church of England: since 2002 (General Synod), divorced people may remarry in church at the priest's discretion.
  • Methodists and many Protestants: divorce is a sad last resort; God forgives; remarriage can be a fresh start.
  • Some evangelicals: divorce only for adultery (Matthew 19:9) or desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15).
Mission 1

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, Situation Ethics, 1966): do the most loving (agape) thing. If staying together causes lasting harm, divorce may be the loving act.
  • Humanists and atheists: marriage is a human commitment; if it has failed, divorce can be best for adults and children; remarriage is fine.
  • They support no-fault divorce: less conflict, less blame.
Mission 1Evaluate

Sanctity of vows or compassion?

  • Sanctity of vows: the promise was made before God for life; Jesus taught against divorce (Matthew 19:6); stability for children; easy divorce weakens commitment.
  • Compassion: no one should stay in abuse or misery; God forgives and offers new starts; children can suffer more from conflict; Moses allowed divorce because of human weakness (Matthew 19:8).
  • Another view: both matter: work hard to save the marriage, but allow separation or divorce when it truly fails.
  • Judge it by: Is a vow unbreakable or a commitment to try? Whose welfare counts most? Does allowing divorce show mercy or weaken marriage?
Mission 1Diversity

Islam

  • Divorce is allowed as a last resort; a hadith calls it the most disliked of permitted things (Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, try to reconcile: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk." (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall)
  • Talaq (divorce by the husband), then iddah: a waiting time of three monthly cycles, to allow reconciliation and check for pregnancy (Qur'an 2:228).
  • "Divorce must be pronounced twice and then (a woman) must be retained in honour or released in kindness." (Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall)
  • A wife can seek divorce by khul' (returning her dowry) or through a judge or Shari'ah council.
Mission 1Diversity

Judaism

  • Divorce is allowed, though sad. Deuteronomy 24:1: the husband "writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • The get (bill of divorce) is given by the husband before a Beth Din (rabbinic court). The wife must accept it freely.
  • Orthodox: a civil divorce is not enough; without a get neither may remarry in Orthodox Judaism. A woman whose husband refuses is an agunah ("chained" woman).
  • Reform: divorce and remarriage accepted; a get is encouraged but the movement's own procedures help where a husband refuses.
Mission 1Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: marriage is a sacred samskara, ideally binding beyond this life; divorce was not part of traditional Hindu law.
  • Buddhists: marriage is a social contract, not a sacrament; no religious ban on divorce. Reduce suffering; act with compassion (karuna).
  • Sikhs: the Anand Karaj joins two souls; divorce is strongly discouraged and families and the sangat try to reconcile the couple. Civil divorce is reluctantly accepted.

Mission 1Worked answer

Vows and compassion

Question: "Religious people should never divorce." Evaluate this statement.

  • For: Catholics believe a valid marriage cannot be dissolved (CCC 2382); Jesus said "What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart" (Matthew 19:6). Vows before God are sacred.
  • Against: Muslims, Jews and most Protestants allow divorce as a last resort; staying in abuse is not loving (situation ethics); God forgives.
  • Weighing: the Catholic view rests on the sacrament being permanent; the other view rests on compassion for real suffering. Which is stronger depends on whether a vow can be broken by the other partner's behaviour.
  • 12-mark: arguments for and against, using religious teaching, and a justified conclusion.

Christians share Jesus' teaching on lifelong marriage, but differ on how to apply it: annulment, mercy, or pastoral discretion.

Mission 2

Three ways to decide

  • An ethical theory is a method for deciding what is right.
  • Rules and duties: some acts are right or wrong in themselves (e.g. Natural Law: preserve life, never kill the innocent). Often absolutist.
  • Consequences: the right act produces the best results (e.g. utilitarianism, situation ethics). Often relativist.
  • Character: become a good person, and good acts follow (virtue ethics).
  • Religious believers use all three, alongside scripture and religious authority.
Mission 2

Situation ethics

  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), American Episcopal priest; Situation Ethics: The New Morality (1966).
  • Only one absolute rule: agape, unselfish love for others.
  • In each situation, do the most loving thing. Other rules are useful guidelines, not absolutes.
  • Fletcher points to Jesus putting people before Sabbath rules: "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27)
  • Criticisms: hard to predict results; people may call selfish choices "loving"; it may allow acts many see as always wrong (e.g. torture "for the greater good").
Mission 2Read the source

Love fulfils the law

"Love doesn't harm a neighbour. Love therefore is the fulfilment of the law." (Romans 13:10)

  • Jesus: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Matthew 22:39)

Q1 How could a situationist use Romans 13:10?

Show answer

Love is the aim of every rule, so love can decide when rules clash.

Q2 How might a Catholic reply?

Show answer

Love works through God's commands, not against them: "fulfilment", not replacement.

Mission 2

Utilitarianism

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789): the principle of utility: the right act gives the greatest happiness to the greatest number.
  • John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), Utilitarianism (1863): "actions are right in proportion as they tend to promote happiness, wrong as they tend to produce the reverse of happiness."
  • Death penalty: a utilitarian may support it if it deters crime and protects many; or oppose it if it does not deter, or risks executing the innocent.
  • Criticisms: a majority's happiness could justify harming a minority; results are hard to predict; justice and rights can be ignored.

Mission 2Diversity

One issue, several theories

  • Divorce: sanctity of vows: a promise before God must be kept (absolutist) · compassion: love may mean letting an unhappy couple part (situation ethics).
  • Death penalty: principle of utility (deterrence, protection) · sanctity of life: only God gives and takes life.
  • Racial discrimination: all people are equal (image of God; Humanist human rights) · positive discrimination: favouring under-represented groups may produce a fairer outcome (utilitarian), but critics say it is still treating people by race.
Mission 2Worked answer

Two ethical arguments

Question: Explain two religious beliefs about divorce. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer.

  • Belief 1: many Christians argue from the sanctity (holiness) of marriage vows. Development: the vows are made before God for life, and Jesus taught "What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate" (Mark 10:9), so divorce breaks a sacred promise.
  • Belief 2: other Christians argue from compassion. Development: if a marriage has broken down or is abusive, the most loving act may be to end it and allow a fresh start, as situation ethics teaches.
  • 6-mark tip: explain two beliefs and develop each; refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching, and show how it supports your point, not just its name.

Mission 3

Types of family

  • Nuclear: two parents and their children.
  • Extended: wider relatives (grandparents, aunts, cousins) living together or closely involved.
  • Single-parent: one parent raising the children.
  • Blended (step-family): parents with children from earlier relationships.
  • Same-sex parents: two mothers or two fathers.
  • Polygamous: one person with more than one spouse. Illegal in the UK (bigamy); legal in some countries.
  • Also adoptive and foster families.
Mission 3

What families are for

  • Procreation: having children: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28).
  • Stability: a secure, loving place to grow up.
  • Protection of children: care, safety, food, home.
  • Education: values and, for believers, educating children in a faith (more in Mission 4).
  • Care across generations: children care for ageing parents: "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12).
  • Families are the building block of society: where people first learn love, duty and forgiveness.
Mission 3Read the source

Parents and children

"You fathers, don't provoke your children to wrath, but nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord." (Ephesians 6:4)

Mission 3

Catholic teaching: the domestic church

  • The family is the domestic church (CCC 1656, paraphrase): the first place children meet God, learn to pray and live the faith.
  • Familiaris Consortio (John Paul II, 1981), sections 17 and 36–85 (paraphrase): four tasks of the family:
  • (1) forming a community of persons in love; (2) serving life: having and educating children (parents are the first educators, FC 36); (3) serving society; (4) sharing in the life and mission of the Church.
  • The ideal: a married man and woman, open to children, faithful for life.
  • The Church supports every family pastorally, including single-parent and blended families.

Mission 3Diversity

Christians and new family types

  • Catholic: every child deserves a mother and father; a child is a gift, not a right (CCC 2378, paraphrase). So the Church opposes same-sex couples adopting, and surrogacy; it rejects IVF for every couple, married or not (CCC 2376–2377).
  • Catholics still welcome and support single-parent, blended and same-sex-parent families in parish life.
  • Liberal Protestants (many Anglicans, Methodists, URC): what matters is love, stability and care, not the family's shape.
  • Polygamy: Christians teach monogamy: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8).
  • Humanists: judge a family by how well it cares for children, not its structure; same-sex couples and single people have an equal right to raise children.
Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: the family is the foundation of the ummah (worldwide community of Muslims); procreation and raising Muslim children strengthen it.
  • "We have commended unto man kindness toward parents." (Qur'an 46:15, Pickthall)
  • Extended families are common. Polygyny: Qur'an 4:3 allows up to four wives only if treated with equal justice; most Muslims are monogamous, and UK Muslims follow UK law.
  • Most Muslims do not accept same-sex parenting; single parents are supported by the wider family.
  • Judaism: "Lo, children are a heritage of the LORD; the fruit of the womb is a reward." (Psalm 127:3, JPS 1917)
  • The home is a small sanctuary (mikdash me'at): Shabbat meals, festivals, teaching children (Deuteronomy 6:7). Orthodox Jews hold the man–woman ideal; Reform Jews welcome same-sex parents.
Mission 3Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: the householder stage (grihastha) is for marriage and raising children; joint (extended) families are common. Purposes: procreation, security, education.
  • Buddhists: the Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31) lists five duties each way. Parents: keep children from wrong, guide them to good, train them for work, find a suitable spouse, hand over the inheritance in time.
  • Children: support their parents, do their duties, keep up the family line, be worthy of the inheritance, make offerings for them after death.
  • Sikhs: family life (grihasth) is the path to God; nine of the ten Gurus married, and they rejected living as hermits.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The nuclear family is the best kind of family."

  • Reasons for: Catholic teaching and many Christians see married mother and father as God's plan (Genesis 2:24); stability; a child's link to both birth parents.
  • Reasons against: extended families give more support (many Hindu, Muslim and Sikh families); single, blended and same-sex-parent families can be equally loving; Humanists judge by care, not shape.
  • Another view: the ideal and the reality differ; communities should support every family.
  • Judge it by: What do children most need? Is there evidence that structure, or quality of care, matters most? Should religious ideals shape law?
Mission 3Worked answer

Purpose with a source

Question: Explain two reasons why the family is important for Christians. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: it is where children are brought up in the faith. Source: fathers should "nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). Development: prayer, worship and values are learned at home first.
  • Reason 2: for Catholics, the family is the domestic church. Development: Familiaris Consortio teaches the family forms a community of love and serves society, so a strong family builds a strong Church and society.

Mission 4

What a religious upbringing involves

  • Rites at birth: baptism (Christian), the adhan whispered and aqiqah (Muslim), brit milah or a baby-naming (Jewish), naming in the gurdwara (Sikh), namakarana (Hindu).
  • At home: prayer, scripture stories, food rules, festivals.
  • In the community: worship, classes (Sunday school, madrassah, cheder), faith schools.
  • Coming of age: confirmation, bar/bat mitzvah; later, for Sikhs, taking Amrit.
  • Values: honesty, compassion, service.
  • AQA calls this "educating children in a faith": one purpose of the family.

Mission 4

A Christian upbringing and belief in God

  • At baptism, parents and godparents promise to bring the child up in the faith.
  • Children learn to pray, hear Bible stories, join worship, attend Sunday school or a church school.
  • First Communion and confirmation: the young person affirms the faith for themselves.
Mission 4Evaluate

The non-religious challenge

  • Humanists and atheists: children should learn about many beliefs and choose for themselves; a single-faith upbringing can be indoctrination.
  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006), paraphrase: there is no such thing as a "Christian child" or "Muslim child", only a child of Christian or Muslim parents.
  • Being pushed may cause rejection of God later: teenagers rebel against what was forced on them.
  • Christian responses: every upbringing passes on values, including a non-religious one; faith is offered, not forced, and confirmation is a free choice; good religious education teaches children to think and question.
  • Parents' right to guide a child's religion is recognised in the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child (1989), article 14, alongside the child's own freedom of thought.
  • Judge it by: Can anyone raise a child "neutrally"? Where is the line between teaching and pressure?

The ummah supports families

  • Worship: Jumu'ah prayer, Ramadan iftars, Eid.
  • Rites of passage: adhan at birth, aqiqah, nikah (marriage), funeral prayers.
  • Classes and groups: madrassah (Qur'an and Arabic), youth groups, parenting and marriage courses.
  • Counselling: imams and community elders help couples reconcile (Qur'an 4:35).
  • "And hold fast, all of you together, to the cable of Allah, and do not separate." (Qur'an 3:103, Pickthall)
Mission 4Diversity

Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish and Sikh communities

  • Buddhists: the sangha is one of the Three Refuges: Buddhists go for refuge to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha (Khuddakapatha 1, paraphrase). Support: vihara worship, Wesak, blessing ceremonies, dhamma school, counselling.
  • Hindus: samskaras (namakarana, upanayana), mandir worship, festivals (Diwali), Bal Vihar classes, help from elders.
  • Jews: brit milah or naming, cheder, bar/bat mitzvah, Jewish schools, Shabbat hospitality, Jewish care charities.
  • Sikhs: naming in the gurdwara, Punjabi and kirtan classes, langar, the sangat (congregation) supporting families.

Mission 4

Communities and family breakdown

  • When families struggle, communities offer: food and money help, childcare, mediation, bereavement and marriage counselling.
  • Non-religious groups (e.g. Humanist pastoral carers, secular charities) offer similar support without faith.
  • Religious believers add: shared worship, prayer for families, a sense of belonging to God's people.

Mission 5

Roles then and now

  • Traditional roles: man as breadwinner and head; woman as homemaker and main carer.
  • Today: most couples both work; more fathers share childcare or stay at home; roles are negotiated.
  • Gender equality: men and women have equal worth, rights and opportunities.
  • Two religious approaches:
  • Complementarian: equal in worth, different and complementary roles.
  • Egalitarian: equal in worth, roles shared or interchangeable, decided by gifts and circumstances.
Mission 5

Genesis: equal in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Both share God's image and the same blessing and task (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is "a helper comparable to him". The Hebrew word (ezer) is also used of God helping Israel, so it does not mean inferior.
  • Genesis 3:16: after the Fall, "he will rule over you". Many Christians and Jews read this as the result of sin, not God's original plan.

Mission 5Diversity

Christian views on roles

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals, some traditional Christians): the husband is a loving, serving head; the wife supports and respects him (Ephesians 5:22–25).
  • Egalitarian (many Anglicans, Methodists, liberal Christians): 5:21 means mutual submission; "neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28); roles by gifts.
  • Catholic: man and woman are equal in dignity and complementary. John Paul II (Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988) read Ephesians 5 as mutual submission.

Mission 5Diversity

Islam

  • Equal before Allah: "Whosoever doeth right, whether male or female, and is a believer..." (Qur'an 16:97, Pickthall); both created "from a single soul" (Qur'an 4:1).
  • Different responsibilities: the husband must provide for the family (Qur'an 4:34); a wife's own earnings and property remain hers; mahr (dowry) is paid to her.
  • "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them." (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall) Scholars differ on what the "degree" means (e.g. extra responsibility).
  • Diversity: traditional Muslims stress distinct roles; many British Muslims share work and childcare.
Mission 5Diversity

Judaism

  • "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917) This poem (31:10–31) is sung in many Jewish homes on Friday night, often by the husband; the woman runs a home, trades and buys a field (31:16).
  • Orthodox Jews: equal worth, different roles. Women are exempt from many time-bound commandments, partly to allow for family duties; the home is a woman's special sphere of holiness (e.g. Shabbat candles).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews: egalitarian: roles in home and synagogue are shared.
Mission 5Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: Manusmriti 3.55–60 (paraphrase): women must be honoured by fathers, brothers and husbands; where women are honoured the gods rejoice, and where they are not, rites bring no reward.
  • The wife is ardhangini ("half of the body") of her husband; stridharma (women's duty) traditionally centred on home and family. Many Hindus today share roles; others keep traditional ones.
  • Buddhists: Sigalovada Sutta: the husband honours her, does not look down on her, is faithful, gives her authority in the home and gives her gifts (ornaments); the wife manages the home well, is hospitable to relatives, faithful, protects the family's wealth and is skilful.
  • Sikhs: Guru Granth Sahib 788 (paraphrase): those who merely sit together are not husband and wife; husband and wife are those who have one light in two bodies. Equal partners in grihasth.

Mission 5

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: equal rights and opportunities; roles decided by the couple, not by sex or scripture.
  • Feminist critiques: "different roles" can hide inequality (e.g. women doing most unpaid housework).
  • Many atheists and Humanists support shared parental leave and equal pay.
  • Religious responses: complementarity values women's distinct gifts; Catholic teaching says work in the home deserves recognition; many believers already share roles.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Men and women should have different roles in the family."

  • Reasons for: complementarian reading of Ephesians 5:22–25 and Qur'an 4:34; Orthodox Jewish and traditional Hindu teaching; biological differences (pregnancy, breastfeeding).
  • Reasons against: Genesis 1:27 and Galatians 3:28; Ephesians 5:21 mutual submission; GGS 788 "one light in two bodies"; Humanist equality; modern families need flexibility.
  • Another view: equal worth is essential; whether roles differ should be the couple's free choice.
  • Judge it by: Does "different" mean "unequal"? Are scripture's roles timeless or cultural? Who decides: tradition or the couple?

Mission 6

Prejudice, discrimination, equality

  • Prejudice: judging someone unfairly before knowing them (an attitude). Sexism: prejudice based on sex.
  • Discrimination: treating someone unfairly because of a prejudice (an action).
  • Gender equality: equal rights, status and opportunities for women and men.
  • UK law: Equal Pay Act 1970; Sex Discrimination Act 1975; now the Equality Act 2010.
  • Examples: unequal pay for the same work; women passed over for promotion; girls denied schooling (Malala Yousafzai, shot in 2012 for campaigning for girls' education).
Mission 6

Christian teaching on equality

  • Both sexes are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)
  • Jesus treated women as disciples and witnesses: Mary Magdalene first told the others he had risen (John 20:18).
Mission 6Read the source

Equality and leadership

"But I don't permit a woman to teach, nor to exercise authority over a man, but to be in quietness." (1 Timothy 2:12)

  • Compare Galatians 3:28: "neither male nor female".

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 6Diversity

Women's ministry in the churches

  • Catholic: only men can be ordained priests. John Paul II (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994): the Church has no authority to ordain women. Reasons: Jesus chose twelve men as apostles; the priest acts in the person of Christ.
  • Catholics stress this is about roles, not worth; women lead as religious sisters, teachers, theologians; since 2021 they can be formally installed as readers and acolytes.
  • Orthodox: male priesthood, for similar reasons of tradition.
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994, bishops since 2014 (first, Libby Lane, 2015); in 2025 Sarah Mullally was chosen as the first woman Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • Methodists, Salvation Army, URC, many Baptists: women ministers; the Salvation Army from its start (Catherine Booth).
  • Some evangelical churches: women lead in many ways but not as senior pastors (1 Timothy 2:12).

Mission 6Diversity

Islam

  • Equal reward for faith and good deeds: Qur'an 33:35 lists "men who surrender unto Allah, and women who surrender" through ten pairs (Qur'an 33:35, Pickthall).
  • Worship: men and women pray in separate rows or rooms; women may lead other women; most scholars teach only men lead mixed congregational prayer. Attending the mosque is a duty for men at Jumu'ah, optional for women.
  • Equality in action: Khadijah, successful businesswoman; Aisha, a leading scholar; Fatima al-Fihri founded al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859), often called the oldest university still working.
  • Many Muslims say discrimination against women comes from culture, not Islam; groups such as Muslim Women's Network UK campaign for women's rights.
Mission 6Diversity

Judaism

  • Both made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Orthodox: separate seating (mechitzah); only men count for a minyan (prayer quorum of ten) and lead public prayer; rabbis are men. Women's learning and leadership roles are growing.
  • Reform and Liberal: mixed seating; women rabbis and cantors. First woman rabbi: Regina Jonas (Germany, 1935); in Britain, Jackie Tabick (1975).
Mission 6Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: the same atman (self) is in all, so all deserve equal regard. The Gita's true yogi "sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living" (Bhagavad Gita 6.29, Arnold).
  • God is worshipped as female too (Durga, Lakshmi, Saraswati). Women increasingly train as priests; some temples still limit women's roles.
  • Buddhists: women and men can both reach enlightenment; the Buddha founded an order of nuns.
  • Sikhs: Guru Granth Sahib 473 (paraphrase): we are born of woman, and through woman the family goes on; so why call her inferior, from whom kings are born? Women may lead any service, read the Guru Granth Sahib and serve as granthi.
  • Divergent Sikh practice: few women in senior Sikh bodies; women are not permitted to perform kirtan inside the Harmandir Sahib. Bibi Jagir Kaur became the first woman president of the SGPC (1999).

Mission 6

Does equal mean the same?

  • Equality as sameness: the same rights, roles and opportunities everywhere, including religious leadership. Held by many Humanists and egalitarian believers.
  • Equality with difference (complementarity): equal worth and rights, but some roles linked to sex. Held by Catholic and Orthodox teaching, Orthodox Judaism and many Muslims.
  • Humanists: no reason, apart from tradition, to bar women from any role; tradition should change when it causes unfairness.
  • Catholic response: the male priesthood follows Christ's choice and does not judge women's worth; Mary is honoured above all saints.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Religions treat women as equals."

  • Reasons for: equal image of God (Genesis 1:27), Galatians 3:28, Qur'an 33:35, GGS 473, Soma Sutta; women priests, rabbis and leaders in many traditions; faiths campaign against sex discrimination.
  • Reasons against: male-only priesthood (Catholic, Orthodox) and rabbinate (Orthodox Judaism); separate roles in mosques; some texts (1 Timothy 2:12; some Manusmriti verses); practice often lags behind teaching.
  • Another view: equal worth is taught everywhere; the debate is whether different roles are fair.
  • Judge it by: Equal worth or equal roles? Teaching or practice? Which tradition or group?
Mission check

?On one street, the Anglican church's vicar is a woman, while the Catholic parish next door has only ever had male priests. Both churches teach that women and men are made equally in God's image. What best explains the difference?

Faiths teach equal worth and oppose sex discrimination; they disagree about whether every role in worship should be open to both, and why.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Divorce ends a marriage in law; annulment declares it was never valid. Catholics accept annulment, not remarriage after divorce; most other Christians allow remarriage.
  • The key debate: sanctity of vows (Matthew 19:6) vs compassion (Matthew 19:8).
  • Situation ethics and Humanists put compassion and the welfare of those involved first.
  • Islam (talaq, iddah, khul'), Judaism (get, agunah) and, reluctantly, Dharmic traditions allow divorce as a last resort.

Mission 2

  • Situation ethics: do the most loving thing (agape). Utilitarianism: the greatest happiness of the greatest number.

Mission 3

  • Types: nuclear, extended, single-parent, blended, same-sex parents. Purposes: procreation, stability, protecting and educating children.
  • Christians: Ephesians 6:1–4; Catholics: domestic church (Familiaris Consortio), child as gift, not right.
  • Polygamous families too. Other faiths: ummah (46:15), Psalm 127, grihastha, Sigalovada Sutta.
  • Sikhs: family life (grihasth) as the path to God.

Mission 4

  • A religious upbringing: rites at birth, prayer at home, classes, worship, coming-of-age rites (Proverbs 22:6; Matthew 19:14).
  • Humanists warn of indoctrination; believers reply that every upbringing passes on values and faith must be freely chosen.
  • Parish, ummah, sangha, mandir, synagogue and sangat support families through worship, rites, classes, groups and counselling.

Mission 5

  • Equal in God's image (Genesis 1:27); Ephesians 5 read as headship (complementarian) or mutual submission (egalitarian).
  • Other faiths: Qur'an 2:228 and 4:34; Proverbs 31; Manusmriti 3.55–60; Sigalovada Sutta.
  • Sikhs: GGS 788, one light in two bodies.

Mission 6

  • Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination an action; sex discrimination is illegal under the Equality Act 2010.
  • Christians: Genesis 1:27, Galatians 3:28; Catholic and Orthodox churches ordain only men; Anglicans and most Protestants ordain women.
  • Other faiths: Qur'an 33:35; Orthodox vs Reform Judaism; Gita 6.29; Buddhist nuns; practice does not always match teaching.
  • Sikhs: GGS 473; women may lead any service.
