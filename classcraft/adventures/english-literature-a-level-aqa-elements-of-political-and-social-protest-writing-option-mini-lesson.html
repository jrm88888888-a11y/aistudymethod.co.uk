Mission 1

Can a poem or a novel change a law?

Make a guess: Can a poem or a novel change a law?

Protest writing · power and powerlessnessScreen 1

What makes a text protest writing?

  • Power and powerlessness are at its core: who rules, who suffers, who resists.
  • It may be public (government, war, the factory) or private (the family, marriage, the school).
  • Your PD texts: Blake's Songs of Innocence (1789) and of Experience (1794); Dickens's Hard Times (1854), set in industrial Coketown; Shakespeare's Henry IV Part 1 (c.1597), a rebellion against a king who seized the throne.
Protest writing · power and powerlessnessScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2, Option 2B (open book, clean copies; 3 hours; 75 marks; 40%).
  • Section A: an unseen passage (25 marks). Section B: an essay on one set text (25). Section C: an essay connecting two texts (25).
  • You study three texts: one post-2000 prose text, one poetry text and one more, at least one pre-1900. AQA stresses elements: what each text does with power.
  • Essay practice is for class.

Protest writing · power and powerlessnessScreen 7

Power in a history play

  • Henry IV Part 1 sets a king who seized the crown against nobles who rebel, but its comic scenes put the poor in view.
  • Falstaff calls his ragged recruits "food for powder" (Act 4 Scene 2), and mocks the nobles' language: "What is honour? A word." (Act 5 Scene 1).
  • Did any text change a law? Rarely directly. Dickens visited the Preston strike in January 1854. F. R. Leavis later called Hard Times a moral fable. The protest works on readers.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Can a poem or a novel change a law?

Protest writing rarely changes a law by itself. What it does is expose how power works, who holds it and who suffers, and so change how readers see their own world. Strong answers explore how each text does this, through setting, voice and structure.

Mission 2

Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

Make a guess: Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.
  • AQA: even a single-text answer can place the text among others of its period or genre.
A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature B: AO4 counts in every question (about 12%). Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section C each set one essay connecting two texts (25 marks), through the genre you've studied.

Mission 3

Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

Make a guess: Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 1

Two kinds of context

  • Context of production: when, where and how the text was written: its society, beliefs, genre conventions, theatre or publishing, the writer's situation.
  • Context of reception: how readers and audiences have received it since, from first reviews to modern productions and criticism.
  • The words on the page stay the same; what audiences notice, fear and sympathise with changes.
  • Context earns marks when it explains something in the text: ‘this helps explain why…’.
A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature B: AO3 counts in every question (about 24%). Genre is a key context: how your text uses or breaks the conventions of tragedy, comedy, crime or protest writing.

Jane Eyre's restlessness

A-level skills · contexts of production and reception (AO3)Screen 7

Jane Eyre's first readers and later ones

  • 1847: published under the name Currer Bell.
  • 1848: Elizabeth Rigby, in the Quarterly Review (December), condemned the novel as irreligious and linked its spirit to the revolutions abroad and Chartism at home, the unrest of that year.
  • 1966: Jean Rhys's novel Wide Sargasso Sea retells Bertha's story from the Caribbean.
  • 1979: Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar read Bertha as Jane's dark double, her suppressed anger.
  • 1985: Gayatri Spivak argued that Jane's freedom is bought at the colonised Bertha's expense.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does The Merchant of Venice play differently now than in 1600?

The text hasn't changed; its audiences have. Written in a society that had expelled its Jews and was gripped by the Lopez affair, the play is now received after the Holocaust, so many audiences hear Shylock's speech as a plea for shared humanity. The strongest answers use both contexts to explain what the text does.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Protest writing has power and powerlessness at its core, in public settings (factory, state, war) and private ones (school, family).
  • Blake's "mind-forg'd manacles" place oppression in minds as well as laws; Dickens's repetitions make Coketown's people as uniform as its streets; Falstaff mocks the language of the powerful.
  • Paper 2 is open book: Section A unseen, B one text, C two texts connected; each 25 marks.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • AQA marks AO4 in every question, as well as the linking essays.
  • Context of production: when, where and how a text was written. Context of reception: how it has been read and staged since.
  • The Merchant of Venice came from an England without a Jewish community and fresh from the Lopez affair; after the Holocaust, its antisemitism is staged and read very differently.
  • Jane Eyre was attacked in 1848 as irreligious and rebellious; feminist and post-colonial critics since 1966 have reread it, including through Bertha.
  • Context earns marks only when it explains something in the text.
  • In Lit B, genre is a context too: ask how the text uses or breaks its genre's conventions.

Key quotations to learn

  • The mind-forg'd manacles I hear. (Blake, "London", stanza 2) Oppression in ideas as well as institutions.
  • Runs in blood down Palace walls. (Blake, "London", stanza 3) The powerless soldier's suffering stains the rulers.
  • Now, what I want is, Facts. (Hard Times, Book I Chapter I (Gradgrind)) Utilitarian control of education.
  • people equally like one another (Hard Times, Book I Chapter V) Industrial uniformity erases individuals.
  • food for powder, food for powder, (Henry IV Part 1, Act 4 Scene 2 (Falstaff)) Poor recruits as fodder for nobles' wars.
  • What is honour? A word. (Henry IV Part 1, Act 5 Scene 1 (Falstaff)) Mockery of the ruling class's ideals.
