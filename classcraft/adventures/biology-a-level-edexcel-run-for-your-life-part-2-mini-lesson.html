Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 1Practical

The respirometer

  • Edexcel core practical 16: investigate rate of respiration.
  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 1

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check

Mission check

?A student set up a respirometer with blowfly larvae above potassium hydroxide solution, and the bead moved steadily towards the larvae. In a second run, identical except that the potassium hydroxide was replaced with water, the bead hardly moved. Which explanation is best?

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Make a guess: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Mission 2

A steady inside

  • Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment within limits, whatever happens outside.
  • Internal environment = blood and tissue fluid around the cells.
  • Regulated variables include core temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and the water potential of the blood.
  • Each variable fluctuates around a set point. It is never perfectly constant.
  • This is a dynamic equilibrium: constant change, constant correction.
Mission 2

Why the limits matter

  • Temperature too high: enzymes denature. Too low: too few successful collisions, so reactions slow.
  • pH away from the optimum: ionic and hydrogen bonds in the enzyme alter, so the active site changes shape. Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
  • Blood glucose too low: cells lack respiratory substrate. The brain is hit first.
  • Blood glucose too high: blood water potential falls, so water leaves cells by osmosis.
  • Say: "lower water potential" — not: "the blood is more concentrated with water".
Mission 2

The control loop

  • Stimulus: a change in a variable.
  • Receptor detects it.
  • Coordinator (e.g. hypothalamus, pancreas) compares it with the set point.
  • Effector (a muscle or a gland) makes the response.
  • The parts are linked by nerves (fast) or hormones in the blood (slower, longer-lasting).
  • Say: "receptors detect; effectors respond" — never swap them.
Quick check
?Which statement about homeostasis in humans is correct?
Mission 2

Negative feedback

  • Negative feedback: the response reverses the change.
  • Variable rises above the set point → response lowers it.
  • Variable falls below the set point → response raises it.
  • So it works from both sides, and the variable oscillates around the set point.
  • The response starts only after a change, so small swings are normal.
  • Say: "negative feedback opposes the change" — not: "negative feedback lowers the level".
Quick check
?During a long run, a person's blood glucose concentration falls to 3.5 mmol dm⁻³. Glucagon secretion increases and the concentration returns to 5 mmol dm⁻³. Which description of this sequence is correct?
Mission 2

Exercise: feedback in action

  • Working muscles respire faster: more CO2, more heat.
  • CO2 dissolves to form carbonic acid, so blood pH falls.
  • Chemoreceptors detect it: in the medulla (brainstem) and in the aorta and carotid arteries.
  • Baroreceptors in the aorta and carotid artery walls monitor blood pressure.
  • The medulla sends more impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (faster ventilation) and to the heart (higher heart rate and stroke volume).
  • More CO2 is removed; pH returns towards its set point: negative feedback.
  • The hypothalamus detects the rise in core temperature and triggers cooling (thermoregulation in Mission 3).
Quick check
?During a run, a student's blood carbon dioxide concentration rises, blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.32 and ventilation rate rises from 12 to 30 breaths per minute. Which statement correctly links these changes?
Mission 2

Positive feedback

  • Positive feedback: the response increases the original change.
  • The loop runs until an end point removes the stimulus.
  • It cannot hold a set point, so it is not used to keep variables steady. That is why homeostasis uses negative feedback.
  • Childbirth: the baby stretches the cervix → oxytocin → stronger uterine contractions → more stretching … until birth.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "positive feedback is the helpful kind".
Quick check
?Why are homeostatic variables controlled by negative feedback rather than by positive feedback?
Mission check

?Two hormones help regulate blood calcium in mammals. When blood calcium falls below normal, the parathyroid glands secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), which raises it. When blood calcium rises above normal, the thyroid secretes calcitonin, which lowers it. Which statement is correct?

New variable, same logic: a response that reverses the change is negative feedback, whichever way it pushes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your core temperature barely moves in snow or in sun. How does your body manage that, and at what cost?

Make a guess: Your core temperature barely moves in snow or in sun. How does your body manage that, and at what cost?

Mission 3

The control centre

  • Peripheral thermoreceptors in the skin detect skin and outside temperature: an early warning.
  • Thermoreceptors in the hypothalamus monitor the temperature of the blood: core temperature.
  • The hypothalamus compares this with the set point (about 37 °C) and sends nerve impulses to effectors.
  • Effectors: skin arterioles, sweat glands, hair erector muscles, skeletal muscles.
  • This is negative feedback: each response opposes the change.
  • Mammals and birds hold core temperature nearly constant like this. In many other animals, e.g. lizards, body temperature follows the surroundings.
Mission 3

Too hot

  • Vasodilation: smooth muscle in skin arterioles relaxes. More blood flows through surface capillaries; more heat is lost to the air.
  • Sweating: water in sweat evaporates, taking latent heat from the skin.
  • Hairs lie flat: erector muscles relax; less air is trapped.
  • Say: "arterioles dilate, so more blood flows near the surface" — not: "blood vessels move closer to the skin".
Mission 3

Too cold

  • Vasoconstriction: skin arterioles constrict. Less blood reaches the surface; less heat is lost.
  • Shivering: rapid, involuntary contractions of skeletal muscle. More respiration, more heat.
  • Hair erection: erector muscles contract, trapping air. Effective in furry mammals; little effect in humans.
  • Sweating falls.
Quick check
?How does vasodilation of the arterioles in the skin lower body temperature?
Mission 3

Exercise heat

  • Working muscles release far more heat: most of the energy from respiration ends up as heat, not movement.
  • Core temperature rises a little; the hypothalamus detects it.
  • It increases sweating and vasodilation in the skin.
  • Conflict: skin and muscles both need blood. Cardiac output rises to supply both.
  • Sweat loses water. Dehydration reduces sweating, and humid air slows evaporation: core temperature can keep rising.
  • The body is in dynamic equilibrium: temperature shifts, then is corrected.
  • The price of a steady core: water lost in sweat, and extra respiration (e.g. shivering) to make heat in the cold.
Quick check
?On a hot, very humid day a runner sweats heavily, but their body temperature keeps rising. Why is sweating less effective in these conditions?
Mission check

?A person walks from a warm room into freezing air. Within a minute their skin arterioles constrict, although their core temperature has not yet changed. Which explanation is correct?

Two sets of sensors: the skin warns early, the hypothalamus checks the core. Together they keep the core steady.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Exercise protects your health. So can doing far more of it ever start to harm you?

Make a guess: Exercise protects your health. So can doing far more of it ever start to harm you?

Mission 4

Too little exercise

  • Inactivity lowers energy expenditure. If intake stays the same: energy surplus, fat stored, obesity.
  • Inactivity is linked to higher risk of:
    • Cardiovascular disease (CVD): higher blood pressure, more LDL cholesterol, atherosclerosis.
    • Type 2 diabetes: cells respond less to insulin, so blood glucose stays high.
  • Regular exercise does the reverse: lowers blood pressure, raises HDL, improves insulin sensitivity, helps control body mass.
  • These are risk factors: they raise the probability of disease, not certainty.
Mission 4

Too much exercise

  • Wear and tear on joints:
    • Repeated high-impact loading wears articular cartilage: raises risk of osteoarthritis, especially after a joint injury.
    • Injuries to ligaments (e.g. the anterior cruciate ligament, ACL), menisci (cartilage pads in the knee) and tendons.
  • Suppression of the immune system after very prolonged, intense exercise:
    • For hours to a day: fewer lymphocytes and natural killer cells in the blood; less IgA antibody in saliva.
    • Linked to more reported upper respiratory tract infections (colds, sore throats).
    • Disputed: some scientists argue the cells move into tissues rather than being lost, and that many reported "infections" were never confirmed.
  • Moderate regular exercise is linked to fewer infections. Risk is thought to rise again at very high loads: a J-shaped curve.
Mission 4

Correlation or cause?

  • Correlation: two variables change together.
  • Causation: a change in one variable produces the change in the other.
  • A correlation can arise because:
    • a third factor (confounding variable) affects both;
    • the cause runs the other way (illness makes people exercise less);
    • chance, in a small sample.
  • Example: 1950s London buses. Drivers (sitting) had more heart disease than conductors (climbing stairs). But drivers were also issued larger uniforms, even when newly hired: body size differed from the start.
  • Evidence for cause: a plausible mechanism, plus a controlled or randomised study.
  • Say: "is associated with" or "is correlated with" — not: "causes", unless other variables were controlled.

Mission 4

Keyhole surgery

  • Keyhole surgery on a joint: arthroscopy.
  • Two or three small cuts, about 1 cm each.
  • An arthroscope (a thin tube with a light and camera) shows the inside of the joint on a screen.
  • Fine instruments go in through the other cuts: repair a torn meniscus, trim damaged cartilage, rebuild a torn ACL with a tendon graft.
  • Compared with open surgery:
    • less damage to surrounding tissue, less blood loss, less pain;
    • lower risk of infection, smaller scars;
    • shorter hospital stay, faster recovery: earlier return to sport.
  • Physiotherapy is still needed to rebuild strength.
Mission 4

Prostheses

  • Prosthesis: an artificial device that replaces a missing or damaged body part.
  • Joint replacements: worn knee or hip surfaces replaced with metal and plastic parts. Restore pain-free movement.
  • Limb prostheses: a socket fits the remaining limb.
    • Running blades (carbon fibre) bend under load, store elastic energy and return it in each stride.
    • Microprocessor knees sense movement and adjust resistance as the user walks or runs.
  • Result: people with injuries or disabilities can train and compete, including at the Paralympic Games.
Quick check
?A footballer has a torn meniscus repaired by keyhole surgery rather than open surgery. Why can they usually return to training sooner?
Mission 4

Performance-enhancing substances

  • Anabolic steroids (testosterone-like):
    • Enter cells, bind receptors, switch on genes: more protein synthesis, more muscle mass and strength, faster recovery.
    • Risks: liver damage, raised CVD risk, reduced fertility.
  • EPO (erythropoietin, a hormone from the kidneys):
    • More red blood cells: more oxygen carried, more endurance.
    • Risks: thicker blood, clots, heart attack and stroke.
  • Both are banned in competition by the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA).
  • Some substances are permitted, such as creatine and caffeine.
Mission 4

Ethical positions

  • Absolutist: an action is always right or always wrong, whatever the consequences.
  • Relativist: it depends on the situation and the consequences.
  • Frameworks used to reason:
    • Rights and duties: what we owe others, e.g. fair competition.
    • Maximising the amount of good (utilitarian): the greatest good for the greatest number.
    • Making decisions for yourself (autonomy): informed people may decide for themselves.
    • Leading a virtuous life (virtue ethics): what would a person of good character do?
  • Arguments that their use can be acceptable: personal choice; supervised use safer than hidden use; natural advantages (e.g. inherited high red cell counts) and altitude training are already allowed.
  • Arguments that their use is not acceptable: health risks; breaks agreed rules; pressures others to use drugs too; young people copy athletes.
  • Your answer should name the position and support it with an argument.
Quick check
?A sports official argues: "Banning these drugs is right because, across all athletes, it prevents far more harm to health than the benefits it removes." Which ethical framework is the official using?
Mission check

?A survey finds that retired professional footballers report knee osteoarthritis more often than men of the same age who never played sport at that level. Which statement is best supported?

Too much and too little exercise are both linked to harm, but the data are full of correlations: naming the cause needs controlled evidence.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Make a guess: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Mission 5

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system: electrical impulses along neurones to specific cells. Fast (milliseconds), short-lived, localised.
  • Endocrine system: hormones secreted into the blood. Slower to start, longer-lasting, often widespread.
  • The two systems work together, e.g. in the "fight or flight" response.
Mission 5

Glands, blood, target cells

  • Endocrine glands are ductless: they secrete hormones directly into the blood.
  • The blood carries each hormone to almost every cell.
  • Only target cells respond: they have receptors with a shape complementary to that hormone.
  • Cells without the receptor are unaffected, however much hormone reaches them.
  • Say: "the hormone binds to complementary receptors on target cells" — not: "the blood takes the hormone to its target".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes the roles of the nervous system and the endocrine system in sending messages between organs?
Mission check

?In humans, cells of the pituitary gland at the base of the brain release human growth hormone, and target cells in many tissues respond, leading to growth. Which flowchart best represents how this signal reaches and affects its target cells?

Every hormone is broadcast to the whole body; the receptor decides who listens.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.
  • Lipids and amino acids enter respiration mostly via acetyl coenzyme A and the Krebs cycle; lipids give about twice the energy per gram.

Mission 2

  • Homeostasis keeps internal variables within limits around a set point; negative feedback reverses a change from either side, so small oscillations are normal.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point; it drives processes such as birth or ripening, not steady states.

Mission 3

  • The hypothalamus compares blood temperature with the set point and drives effectors: vasodilation and sweating to cool; vasoconstriction and shivering to warm.

Mission 4

  • Too little exercise is linked to obesity, CVD and type 2 diabetes; too much to joint wear and short-term immune suppression. A correlation is not a cause until confounding is ruled out.
  • Keyhole surgery and prostheses help injured and disabled people take part in sport; whether performance-enhancing substances are acceptable depends on the ethical position taken.

Mission 5

  • Hormones are secreted into the blood and reach almost every cell; only target cells with complementary receptors respond.
