Mission 1

A Jew in Enlightenment Berlin

  • Moses Mendelssohn (1729–1786), born in Dessau, son of a Torah scribe.
  • 1743: follows his teacher, Rabbi David Fränkel, to Berlin. Teaches himself German, Latin and philosophy.
  • Berlin is a centre of the Enlightenment (German Aufklärung): reason, tolerance, "natural religion" open to all.
  • Jews in Prussia had limited rights: residence only by royal privilege, taxes, restricted trades.
  • Friend of the playwright Lessing; wins the Berlin Academy prize (1763) ahead of Kant; called "the German Socrates".
  • He stays an observant Jew all his life.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 1

Truths that reason can reach

  • Mendelssohn's eternal truths: God exists; God's providence; the soul is immortal.
  • These are open to every human being by reason. A good God would not leave most of humanity without the truths needed for happiness.
  • Existence of God: he argued it by reason, not by appeal to scripture (prize essay, 1763; Morning Hours, 1785).
  • Freewill: by the same rational method he argued humans are free and so morally responsible.
  • So for Mendelssohn there is harmony between reason and Judaism: Judaism demands no belief that reason contradicts.
Mission 1Read the source

Jerusalem (1783)

Mendelssohn, Jerusalem, or on Religious Power and Judaism (1783), Part II (paraphrase):

  • Judaism is not a revealed religion in the Christian sense: it does not reveal saving doctrines that reason could not reach.
  • What Israel received at Sinai was revealed legislation: laws and commandments for the Jewish people.
  • The eternal truths are taught alongside the laws, but believed because reason shows them, not because they are commanded.

Q1 What, for Mendelssohn, is unique to Judaism?

Show answer

Its divinely revealed law, not special doctrines.

Q2 Why could he think this defends Judaism?

Show answer

Judaism asks no one to believe against reason, so an Enlightenment critic cannot call it irrational.

Mission 1

Why keep the commandments?

  • The ceremonial law binds Jews because God gave it at Sinai. Only God can release them from it, as publicly as it was given.
  • The commandments are a "living script" (paraphrase): actions that keep the eternal truths alive and point to them, unlike written creeds that can harden into idols.
  • They bind Jews together and sustain Israel's loyalty to God.
  • No coercion: neither state nor religious authority may force belief. He opposed the rabbis' power of excommunication (herem).
  • So Mendelssohn defends observance of Halakhah and rationalism together.

Mission 1

The Biur: Torah in German

  • Sefer Netivot ha-Shalom (1780–1783): the Five Books of Moses translated into German, printed in Hebrew letters, with a Hebrew commentary, the Biur ("explanation"), written with collaborators.
  • Aims: teach Jews pure German in place of Yiddish; teach Hebrew grammar and the plain sense (peshat) of the text.
  • 1783: translation of the Psalms into German.
  • Effect: a bridge from the traditional study house into German culture: the start of modernising Jewish education.
  • Some rabbis objected that boys would study German instead of Talmud; the work was banned in some communities.
Mission 1

Immortality as endless progress

  • Phaedon (1767): a reworking of Plato's Phaedo in modern form.
  • Simplicity: the soul is simple (not made of parts), so it cannot fall apart as bodies do.
  • God's goodness: a wise, good God would not create beings who long for perfection and then end them.
  • So immortality is unending moral progress: the soul keeps growing towards perfection after death.
  • Critics: Kant (Critique of Pure Reason, 2nd edition, 1787) argued the simplicity argument fails: a simple soul could still fade away by degrees.
  • Note the method: a rational proof, not a proof from scripture.

Mission 1Diversity

Hero or danger?

  • Critics: the Hatam Sofer (Moses Sofer, 1762–1839, Pressburg) urged his followers not to read Mendelssohn's books: openness to German culture would lead Jews away from Torah.
  • Assimilation: several of Mendelssohn's children and most of his grandchildren became Christians (e.g. the composer Felix Mendelssohn Bartholdy). Critics see the fruit of his approach; defenders say he kept the law and cannot be blamed for others' choices.
  • Samson Raphael Hirsch (Orthodox) praised his learning but said he did not build a Jewish philosophy from within Judaism.
  • Reform later claimed him as a founder, though he never questioned the law's binding force.
  • D Sorkin (Moses Mendelssohn and the Religious Enlightenment, 1996), paraphrase: Mendelssohn was a religious enlightener, rooted in medieval Jewish philosophy and the Hebrew Bible, not a secularist.
Mission 1Evaluate

Did Mendelssohn transform Jewish life and thought?

  • Claim: Mendelssohn transformed Jewish life and thought.
  • For: the Biur moved Jews into German language and culture; modern Jewish schools followed (Berlin Freischule, 1778, founded by his circle).
  • For: he showed a Jew could be a leading European philosopher; Lessing's Nathan the Wise (1779) was modelled on him.
  • For: his separation of religion and state helped the case for emancipation (equal civil rights for Jews;).
  • Against: his own theology was traditional: reason plus revealed law, much like medieval Jewish philosophy.
  • Against: the Maskilim and Reformers went where he did not: he never gave up observance.
  • Against: Eastern European Jewry, the majority, was barely touched in his lifetime.
  • Judgement criteria: transform life (language, school, culture) or thought (beliefs)? His intentions or his influence? Which Jews?
  • Spec issue: "the extent to which Mendelssohn and the Haskalah transformed Jewish life and thought".

Mission 2

The Haskalah

  • Haskalah: Hebrew for "enlightenment" (from sekhel, reason). Its followers: Maskilim ("enlightened ones").
  • Began in Berlin in the 1770s–80s around Mendelssohn (see Mission 1); spread to Galicia, then Russia, in the 19th century, until about the 1880s.
  • Core idea: Jews should join European learning and culture and remain Jews.
  • Tools: reason, secular study, European languages, a revived Hebrew.
  • It challenged the traditional world in which Torah and Talmud study was the only learning that counted.
Mission 2

The Maskilim's programme

  • Schools: the Jewish Free School in Berlin (1778) taught German, maths and trades alongside Hebrew.
  • Naphtali Herz Wessely (1725–1805), Words of Peace and Truth (1782): Jews need "the teaching of man" (general knowledge) as well as "the teaching of God".
  • Ha-Me'assef ("The Gatherer", from 1783–84): first modern Hebrew journal: science, poetry, history.
  • In the East: Isaac Baer Levinsohn (1788–1860) urged secular study within Torah; Joseph Perl (1773–1839) satirised the Hasidim.
  • Judah Leib Gordon (1830–1892), poem "Awake, my people!" (1863), paraphrase: be a person in the street and a Jew at home.
  • Maskilim opposed Hasidism and attacked Yiddish as a "jargon"; they promoted Hebrew and the local language.
Mission 2

Secular Jewish culture

  • From the Haskalah came a Jewish identity not built on religious practice.
  • History: the "Science of Judaism" (Wissenschaft des Judentums, from 1819; Leopold Zunz, 1794–1886) studied Jewish texts as history; Heinrich Graetz wrote a History of the Jews (1853–1876).
  • Language and literature: Hebrew novels and poetry; later Yiddish literature.
  • Politics: later movements built on this identity: cultural Zionism (Ahad Ha'am) and Jewish socialism (the Bund, 1897).
  • Result: many Jews defined themselves by history and identity rather than by observance.

Mission 2

Emancipation: Jews as citizens

  • Emancipation: Jews given equal civil rights as citizens.
  • 1781: Christian Wilhelm von Dohm, On the Civil Improvement of the Jews (written at Mendelssohn's request).
  • 1782: Joseph II's Edict of Tolerance (Austria): partial rights.
  • 1791: France grants full citizenship to all Jews; Napoleon's Assembly of Jewish Notables (1806) and "Grand Sanhedrin" (1807) asked Jews to affirm loyalty to France.
  • 1812 Prussia (partial) · 1858 Britain (Jews may sit in Parliament) · 1867 Austria-Hungary · 1871 German Empire · Russia only in 1917.
  • Emancipation was slow, uneven and often reversed.
Mission 2Read the source

"Everything as individuals"

Count Stanislas de Clermont-Tonnerre, speech to the French National Assembly, December 1789 (paraphrase of his French):

  • The Jews should be refused everything as a nation and granted everything as individuals.
  • They must not form a separate political body or "a nation within the nation"; if they refuse, they should leave.

Q1 What were Jews offered?

Show answer

Full rights as individual citizens.

Q2 What were they asked to give up?

Show answer

Their own communal self-government and their status as a separate nation.

Mission 2

What emancipation changed

  • Civil liberties: the vote, professions, universities, freedom to live anywhere.
  • Communities: the old kehillah lost its legal powers (its own courts, taxes, the ban). Belonging became voluntary.
  • Jewish–Christian relations: more contact and friendship (Lessing and Mendelssohn); but also pressure to convert. Heinrich Heine (baptised 1825) called baptism the "entry ticket" to European culture (paraphrase).
  • Responses: assimilation and conversion; Reform (see Mission 3); neo-Orthodoxy (Hirsch); the Positive-Historical school (Zacharias Frankel, 1801–1875), root of Conservative/Masorti.
  • Backlash: a new, racial antisemitism (the word popularised in Germany in 1879).
  • Suggested reading: E Kessler, An Introduction to Jewish-Christian Relations (2010), chapter 6.

Mission 2

Who are we? Jewish self-consciousness

  • Emancipation forced a new question: are Jews a religion, a nation or a culture?
  • Religion: "German citizens of the Jewish faith" (the Central Association, founded 1893). Judaism as one confession among others.
  • People/nation: Jews as a people with a history and future (Zionism; the Bund).
  • Culture: identity through history, language and literature, with or without belief.
  • D Vital (David Vital, A People Apart, 1999), paraphrase: emancipation split European Jewry over whether to remain a distinct people or to dissolve into the nations.
  • Result: pluralism and diversity within Judaism, never fully resolved.
Mission 2Diversity

From Haskalah to Reform and Progressive

  • Haskalah ideas Reform drew on: reason (keep what reason and ethics support); history (Judaism has always developed: Wissenschaft); aesthetics (decorum, sermons, the vernacular).
  • Reform (Germany): Seesen temple (1810), Hamburg Temple (1818), Abraham Geiger (see Mission 3).
  • Progressive in Britain: West London Synagogue (1840); Liberal Judaism (Jewish Religious Union, 1902; Claude Montefiore and Lily Montagu).
  • Not all Maskilim became Reform: in the East many became secular nationalists or writers; some stayed observant.
  • Orthodoxy also answered the Haskalah: Hirsch's neo-Orthodoxy took secular study on board while holding all of halakhah.

Mission 2Evaluate

Did the Haskalah strengthen Judaism?

  • Claim: The Haskalah strengthened Judaism.
  • For: it gave Jews tools to live as equals in modern Europe: education, languages, professions.
  • For: it revived Hebrew and created modern Jewish scholarship, literature and history.
  • For: it produced new forms (Reform, neo-Orthodoxy, cultural Zionism): pluralism let Jews choose how to be Jewish.
  • Against: it weakened observance and community; for many, acculturation led to assimilation (losing a distinct Jewish identity) and conversion.
  • Against: science and rational thought challenged belief in God and revealed law; some Jews left religion.
  • Against: it divided Jews, and it did not stop modern antisemitism.
  • Judgement criteria: strengthen Judaism (the religion) or Jews (the people)? Short term or long term? Germany or Eastern Europe? Is diversity a strength or a weakness?
  • Spec issues: Haskalah in the context of the European Christian enlightenment; the challenge of science and rational thought; pluralism and diversity.

Mission 3

Why Reform began

  • 19th-century Germany: emancipation was opening citizenship (see Mission 2); many young Jews were drifting away or converting.
  • Reformers wanted a Judaism modern Jews could stay in.
  • Israel Jacobson opens a temple in Seesen (1810); the Hamburg Temple follows (1818).
  • Changes: sermons in German, an organ and choir, shorter services, decorum, some prayers in German.
  • The Hamburg prayer book altered prayers for a return to Zion and the restoration of sacrifice.
  • Reformers drew on the Haskalah: reason and the historical study of Judaism (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

Progressive revelation

  • Abraham Geiger (1810–1874): Judaism has always developed; each age expresses its eternal core in new forms.
  • Progressive revelation: God's revelation continues through history; the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans in their time.
  • The ethical heart (the prophets' justice and compassion) is lasting; many ritual laws belonged to an earlier age.
  • Today Reform speaks of informed choice: Jews study the tradition and decide which mitzvot to keep.
  • Contrast: Orthodox Torah from heaven, binding as given.

Mission 3Evaluate

Development or break?

  • Claim: Reform Judaism is a legitimate development of Judaism, not a break from it.
  • For: Judaism has always changed: prayer replaced sacrifice; the rabbis adapted law (Geiger, Meyer).
  • For: Reform keeps the ethical core of the prophets and has drawn more Jews to stay Jewish in modern societies.
  • Against: Orthodox critics: if the Torah is not from heaven and halakhah not binding, the basis of Judaism has changed.
  • Against: classical Reform dropped hope of return to Zion and much ritual; later Reform restored much of it, suggesting the first changes went too far.
  • Judgement criteria: what is Judaism's "core"? Who decides what is legitimate? Development by halakhic process or by community choice?
Mission check

Reform says change is part of Judaism's history; its critics say some changes alter the foundation. Both sides argue about what Judaism's core is.

Mission 4

Zion in Jewish memory

  • Zion: a hill in Jerusalem; then a name for Jerusalem and the whole Land of Israel.
  • After exile, Jews kept Zion at the centre of prayer:
  • the Amidah, three times daily, asks God to gather the exiles and rebuild Jerusalem;
  • synagogues face Jerusalem; the Pesach Seder ends "Next year in Jerusalem";
  • at weddings a glass is broken to remember the destroyed Temple.
  • Traditional belief: the return would come through God, in the days of the Messiah, not by human politics.
  • "Zion theology": the Hebrew Bible's hope that God will restore the people to Zion.

Q2 Which phrase could later Zionists read as a promise that a return would be permanent?

  • Religious Zionists read verses like these as a promise now being fulfilled; most Haredi Jews read them as for the Messiah's time (see Mission 5).
Mission 4

Why the 19th century changed things

  • Haskalah and emancipation (see Mission 2): Jews became citizens in much of western Europe; many hoped to be accepted as Germans or Frenchmen "of the Mosaic faith".
  • Assimilation: some left Jewish practice or converted; yet hostility did not end.
  • Nationalism: Italians, Germans, Poles and Greeks were claiming nations. Were Jews a nation too?
  • Modern antisemitism: hatred of Jews as a "race", not only as a religion (the word was popularised in Germany c. 1879).
  • Persecutions: pogroms in the Russian Empire (1881–82) and the restrictive May Laws (1882).
  • Response: Hovevei Zion ("Lovers of Zion") societies; Leon Pinsker's Autoemancipation (1882): Jews must liberate themselves.
  • First Aliyah (1882–1903): small groups settle farming colonies (e.g. Rishon LeZion, 1882), many helped by Baron Edmond de Rothschild. Immigration stayed limited: Ottoman restrictions, poverty, disease; most Jews leaving Russia went to America.
Mission 4

Rabbis who said: start now

  • Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai (1798–1878, Sarajevo) and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer (1795–1874, Prussia), Derishat Tziyon ("Seeking Zion", 1862).
  • Their argument: redemption can begin naturally, through human effort: buying land, farming, settling. The miraculous stage follows.
  • Settling the Land is itself a mitzvah.
  • Roots of later Religious Zionism (see Mission 5).
  • Most rabbis of their time disagreed: forcing the end is forbidden; wait for God.

Mission 4

The Dreyfus affair

  • 1894, France: Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish army officer, is convicted of spying for Germany. He was innocent; the real spy was another officer, Esterhazy.
  • January 1895: Dreyfus is publicly stripped of rank in Paris; crowds shout against the Jews.
  • France was the first European country to emancipate its Jews (1791). If antisemitism could flare there, could assimilation ever protect Jews?
  • Theodor Herzl, reporting from Paris for a Vienna newspaper, saw the case unfold. He later said it made him a Zionist.
  • Émile Zola's open letter J'accuse…! (1898) defended Dreyfus; he was fully cleared in 1906.
Mission 4

Herzl's The Jewish State (1896)

  • Theodor Herzl (1860–1904): Budapest-born, Vienna-based, secular journalist and playwright.
  • Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State, 1896): the Jewish question "is a national question" (tr. Sylvie d'Avigdor, 1896).
  • "We are a people—one people." (same translation)
  • Antisemitism will not disappear through assimilation; the answer is sovereignty over a territory, secured by international agreement.
  • Method: diplomacy with the great powers, not small-scale settlement first.
  • Territory: Palestine ("our ever-memorable historic home") or Argentina; he did not yet insist on one.
  • This is Political Zionism: national, mostly secular, aiming at a recognised state.
Mission 4

Basel, 1897

  • First Zionist Congress, Basel, Switzerland, 29–31 August 1897: about 200 delegates.
  • Founds the Zionist Organisation (later World Zionist Organisation); Herzl its president.
  • Basel Programme (paraphrase): Zionism aims to create for the Jewish people a home in Palestine secured by public law.
  • The German word was Heimstätte ("home"), not "state": a cautious word for the Ottoman Empire's sake.
  • Means: settlement of farmers and workers; organising Jews worldwide; strengthening national feeling; seeking governments' consent.
  • Herzl in his diary (paraphrase): at Basel he had founded the Jewish State; in fifty years everyone would see it. Israel was declared in 1948.
Mission 4

Herzl the diplomat

  • 1898: Herzl's only visit to the "Holy Land", timed to meet Kaiser Wilhelm II there; the Kaiser offered no support.
  • 1901: meets Sultan Abdul Hamid II of the Ottoman Empire; asks for a charter for Palestine; refused.
  • Institutions: Jewish Colonial Trust (1899) and Jewish National Fund (1901) to buy land.
  • 1903: after the Kishinev pogrom, Britain offers land in East Africa (the "Uganda programme", in present-day Kenya) as a refuge.
  • The Sixth Congress (1903) votes only to investigate; many Russian delegates walk out: only Zion would do.
  • Herzl dies in 1904; the Seventh Congress (1905) rejects East Africa.
  • His novel Altneuland (1902) pictures a future Jewish society; its motto (paraphrase): if you will it, it is no fairy tale.
Mission 4

Reviving Hebrew

  • For centuries Hebrew was leshon ha-kodesh ("the holy tongue"): for prayer and study, not everyday speech.
  • Eliezer Ben-Yehuda (1858–1922) settled in Jerusalem (1881), raised his son speaking only Hebrew, coined new words and began a great dictionary.
  • Hebrew schools and the Second Aliyah settlers (1904–14) made it the daily language of the new Jewish community.
  • Some Orthodox Jews objected: using the holy tongue for daily, secular life profanes it.
  • Modern Hebrew became an official language of Israel (1948): the only ancient spoken language revived on this scale.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is Zionism a "solution" to antisemitism?

  • Claim: Zionism is the solution to antisemitism.
  • For: Herzl: assimilation did not stop hatred (Dreyfus); only a state can protect Jews.
  • For: since 1948, Jews fleeing persecution (e.g. from the Soviet Union, Arab states, Ethiopia) have had a state guaranteed to take them in.
  • Against: antisemitism has continued, in the diaspora and in hostility towards Israel; a state has not ended it.
  • Against: Ahad Ha'am: a state cannot solve the "problem of Judaism", the loss of identity (see Mission 5).
  • Against: Haredi critics: the true remedy for exile is repentance and the Messiah, not politics.
  • Judgement criteria: "Solution" as refuge, or as an end to hatred? Safety for whom: Jews in Israel, or everywhere?
  • Your spec's wording: "if Zionism is a 'solution' to anti-Semitism".

Mission 5

Many Zionisms

  • All Zionists wanted a Jewish national home in the Land of Israel. They disagreed on why, how and what kind.
  • Political (Herzl): a recognised state, through diplomacy (see Mission 4).
  • Practical: settle and build now, without waiting for a charter.
  • Cultural: a centre for Hebrew culture and Jewish values.
  • Labour: a socialist society built by Jewish workers.
  • Revisionist: a state on both sides of the Jordan, defended by strength.
  • Religious: return to the Land as part of God's redemption, under the Torah.
  • Against them all, for different reasons: most Haredi Jews and, until the 1930s, most Reform Jews.
Mission 5

Ahad Ha'am: a spiritual centre

  • Asher Ginzberg (1856–1927), pen name Ahad Ha'am ("one of the people"), Hebrew essayist from Ukraine.
  • "This Is Not the Way" (1889): settlement without spiritual preparation will fail.
  • Visits to the Land (first in 1891): "Truth from the Land of Israel" criticised poor planning, dependence on charity and settlers' treatment of the Arab population, whose presence many ignored.
  • Herzl solves the "problem of the Jews" (persecution); the deeper problem is the "problem of Judaism": modern Jews losing their identity.
  • Aim: a centre in the Land for Jewish value, spirit and ethics, reviving Hebrew and Jewish culture, radiating to the diaspora. Most Jews would stay where they were.
  • Paraphrase of his famous remark: more than the Jews have kept the Sabbath, the Sabbath has kept the Jews.
  • He saw ethics, not ritual law, as Judaism's core, and himself as non-observant; Orthodox critics objected.
Mission 5

Labour Zionism and the kibbutz

  • Practical Zionism: build facts on the ground: land, villages, schools. Chaim Weizmann (1874–1952) joined practical and political work ("synthetic Zionism").
  • Labour Zionism: Jewish national rebirth through Jewish work on the land and a socialist society.
  • A. D. Gordon (1856–1922): a "religion of labour"; manual work heals the people and binds it to the land.
  • Kibbutz ("gathering"): first at Degania (1910), by the Jordan near the Sea of Galilee. Shared property, equal work, decisions by assembly; children often raised together.
  • Labour leaders, above all David Ben-Gurion (1886–1973), led the Jewish community and then Israel until 1977.
  • Most were secular: the pioneer, not the rabbi, would redeem the people. There were also religious kibbutzim (from the 1930s).
Mission 5

Revisionist and Liberal Zionism

  • Ze'ev (Vladimir) Jabotinsky (1880–1940) founded the Revisionist movement (1925): "revise" the cautious policy of Weizmann.
  • Goals: a Jewish majority and state on both banks of the Jordan; mass immigration; a Jewish defence force.
  • "The Iron Wall" (1923, paraphrase): Arab acceptance would come only once Jewish strength was unbreakable.
  • Youth movement Betar; later Herut and Likud (Menachem Begin, prime minister 1977).
  • General (Liberal) Zionists: middle-class, liberal in economics, between Labour and the Revisionists; later merged into Likud.

Mission 5

Religious Zionism

  • Mizrachi (founded 1902) by Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines (1839–1915): Orthodox Jews should work inside the Zionist movement.
  • Its slogan (paraphrase): the Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel.
  • Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of the Land of Israel (1921):
  • even secular pioneers are unknowingly part of God's plan; their work is the beginning of redemption (atchalta de-geulah).
  • Settling the Land is a mitzvah; the state is a religious event, not only a political one.
  • After 1967 his son's followers founded Gush Emunim.
Mission 5Diversity

Political and Religious Zionism

  • Why a state? Political: Jews are a nation that needs safety. Religious: God promised the Land; return is part of redemption.
  • Who acts? Political: Jews, through diplomacy and settlement. Religious: Jews act, but God works through them.
  • What kind of state? Political: modern, mainly secular, for all Jews. Religious: shaped by Torah, Shabbat and halakhah in public life.
  • The Messiah? Political: not needed. Religious: the state is a step towards the Messianic age.
  • Shared ground: both support the State of Israel, Hebrew, immigration (aliyah) and defence.

"That ye awaken not"

> "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles, and by the hinds of the field, that ye awaken not, nor stir up love, until it please." (Song of Songs 2:7, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Ketubot 110b–111a, paraphrase) reads the repeated oath as three oaths made at the start of exile:
  • Israel would not go up to the Land en masse ("as a wall");
  • Israel would not rebel against the nations;
  • the nations would not oppress Israel too much.

Mission 5

Haredi opposition

  • Haredi ("trembling" before God; ultra-Orthodox) leaders mostly opposed Zionism:
  • it "forces the end": only the Messiah may restore Jewish rule;
  • it replaced Torah with secular nationalism: "Jewish" became a nationality, not a covenant;
  • secular pioneers broke Shabbat and kashrut in the Holy Land.
  • Agudat Israel (1912): anti-Zionist at first, later non-Zionist: lives in the state and sits in parliament, without giving it religious meaning.
  • Satmar Hasidim (Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, Vayoel Moshe, 1961): the state is a grave sin.
Mission 5

Neturei Karta

  • Neturei Karta: Aramaic for "guardians of the city", founded in Jerusalem in 1938, leaving Agudat Israel.
  • The name comes from a Talmudic story: the true guardians of a city are its teachers of Torah, not its soldiers.
  • Beliefs: a Jewish state before the Messiah is rebellion against God; exile ends only by divine redemption.
  • Practice: refuse to vote, take state money or recognise Israel; some join anti-Israel protests abroad.
  • A small group: most Haredi Jews reject its political actions, even while sharing its non-Zionist theology.
Mission 5

Progressive responses and non-Jewish views

  • Reform at first rejected Jewish nationhood: Pittsburgh (1885) said Jews were "no longer a nation, but a religious community" (see Mission 3). Columbus Platform (1937): supports building a Jewish homeland.
  • British Liberal Judaism (Claude Montefiore) and Edwin Montagu, the Jewish Secretary of State for India in 1917, opposed Zionism: it would make Jews strangers in their own countries.
  • Today most Orthodox and Progressive Jews support Israel; Progressive Jews press for religious pluralism there.
  • Non-Jewish support: Christian "restorationists" (Lord Shaftesbury, 1801–1885); the Balfour Declaration (1917): Britain favours "a national home for the Jewish people" in Palestine, without prejudice to the "civil and religious rights of existing non-Jewish communities"; Christian Zionism among many evangelicals today.
  • Non-Jewish opposition: Arab and Palestinian nationalism; some churches and human-rights groups; UN Resolution 3379 (1975) called Zionism racism, revoked in 1991.
Mission 5Evaluate

How strong are the links between Zionism and Judaism?

  • Claim: Zionism is a Jewish religious movement.
  • For: the Land is central to the covenant (Genesis 12; 15); prayer for Zion is daily; religious Zionists see the State as the beginning of redemption.
  • For: cultural Zionism revived Hebrew and Jewish learning.
  • Against: its founders (Herzl, Labour) were mainly secular; it was a modern nationalism, like others in Europe.
  • Against: many religious Jews (Haredim, early Reform) rejected it on religious grounds; some non-Jews (Christian Zionists) support it.
  • Judgement criteria: the founders' motives, or the movement's meaning for Jews today? Is "Jewish" a religion, a people, or both?
  • Further issues in your spec: whether Zionism has done more damage than good; whether anti-Zionism is "merely" antisemitism. Arguments compared: some definitions (e.g. the IHRA working definition, 2016) treat denying Jews any right to self-determination as antisemitic, while saying criticism of Israel like that of any other country is not; Haredi anti-Zionism shows opposition can be religious, not hostile to Jews.
  • Damage or good? Supporters point to a refuge for persecuted Jews, the revival of Hebrew and Jewish self-government; critics point to continuing conflict, the displacement of Palestinians, and division among Jews over the state. The issue is how to weigh these.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mendelssohn (1729–1786): eternal truths (God, providence, immortality) by reason; Judaism is revealed legislation, binding on Jews, with no coercion of belief.
  • The Biur (1780–1783) and Psalms translation brought Jews into German language and culture; Phaedon (1767) argued for immortality as unending moral progress.
  • Critics (Hatam Sofer) feared assimilation; scholars debate whether he transformed Jewish thought or only Jewish life.

Mission 2

  • The Haskalah (from the 1770s) joined Jewish life to reason, secular study and European languages; Maskilim revived Hebrew and created a secular Jewish culture of history and identity.
  • Emancipation (France 1791 onwards) gave Jews civil rights as individuals but weakened communal authority; it raised the question whether Jews are a religion, a nation or a culture.
  • Haskalah rationalism and historical study fed Reform and Progressive Judaism; Orthodoxy and secular nationalism were other answers.

Mission 3

  • Reform began in early 19th-century Germany (Seesen 1810, Hamburg 1818), shaped by emancipation and the Haskalah; Geiger's progressive revelation sees Judaism developing with an ethical core.

Mission 4

  • Jews prayed daily for a return to Zion, traditionally expected through God and the Messiah; Amos 9:14–15 promises a permanent replanting in the land.
  • 19th-century nationalism, racial antisemitism, pogroms and the Dreyfus affair convinced Herzl that Jews were a nation needing a homeland secured by public law (The Jewish State, 1896; Basel, 1897).
  • Herzl's diplomacy (Kaiser, Sultan, Britain) failed in his lifetime; the Uganda programme split the movement because many would accept only Zion.

Mission 5

  • Zionism took many forms: political (Herzl), practical and Labour (settlement, kibbutz), cultural (Ahad Ha'am's spiritual centre), Revisionist (Jabotinsky) and Religious (Mizrachi, Rav Kook).
  • Most Haredim reject any religious meaning in the State; Neturei Karta reject the State entirely; Reform moved from opposition (1885) to support (1937).
