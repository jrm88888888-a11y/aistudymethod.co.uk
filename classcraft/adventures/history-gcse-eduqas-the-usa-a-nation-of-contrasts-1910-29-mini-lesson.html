The USA: A Nation of Contrasts 1910-29

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole Eduqas study of the USA, 1910–29: mass immigration and hostility to it, the Red Scare, racism and the Ku Klux Klan, the great economic boom of the Roaring Twenties, Prohibition and gangsters, changing lives for women, the popular culture of jazz and cinema — and how it all came crashing down in the Wall Street Crash of 1929.

Immigration · the melting pot

A nation of immigrants

For decades millions of immigrants poured into the USA, drawn by the promise of jobs and freedom. So many peoples mixed together that the USA became known as a "melting pot".

But by the 1910s and 1920s many Americans grew hostile to newcomers. Older, established Americans feared:

  • immigrants would take jobs and drive down wages;
  • new arrivals (many from southern and eastern Europe) had different languages, religions and customs;
  • some immigrants brought radical politics — communism and anarchism.

Key idea: the USA moved from an "open door" to closing the door — putting strict limits on who could come in.

Immigration · closing the door

The quota system

In the 1920s Congress passed laws to cut immigration sharply and control who was allowed in:

  • Emergency Quota Act, 1921 — set the first strict limits on the number of immigrants allowed each year.
  • Immigration Act (National Origins Act), 1924 — tightened the quotas further, deliberately favouring immigrants from northern and western Europe and cutting numbers from southern and eastern Europe and Asia.

Why it mattered: the quota system showed a growing mood of intolerance and a wish to keep the USA "white, Protestant and English-speaking". It was rooted in prejudice about which peoples were "desirable".

The Red Scare

Fear of communism

After the Russian Revolution of 1917, many Americans feared that communists and anarchists ("Reds") were plotting revolution in the USA too. This panic became known as the Red Scare.

  • A wave of strikes and a few anarchist bombings in 1919 fuelled the fear.
  • In the Palmer Raids (1919–1920) the Attorney General, A. Mitchell Palmer, ordered thousands of suspected radicals to be arrested; many immigrants were deported, often with little evidence.

Misconception alert: the Red Scare was driven more by fear and prejudice than by real evidence of revolution. Many of those arrested had done nothing wrong.

The Red Scare · a famous case

Sacco and Vanzetti

The Red Scare's atmosphere shaped one of the most famous trials in US history.

  • Nicola Sacco and Bartolomeo Vanzetti were Italian immigrants and anarchists, arrested in 1920 for a robbery and murder in Massachusetts.
  • The evidence against them was weak, but the judge and jury were hostile to their politics and background.
  • Despite worldwide protests, they were executed in 1927.

Many people at the time and since believed the pair were convicted for who they were — immigrants and radicals — rather than for what they had done. The case became a symbol of prejudice and intolerance.

Racism · the 1920s

The Klan and Jim Crow

The 1920s were years of deep racism, especially against Black Americans:

  • In the South, Jim Crow laws enforced strict segregation — separate schools, transport and facilities — and denied Black Americans the vote and fair treatment.
  • The Ku Klux Klan (KKK), revived from 1915, spread rapidly and peaked in the mid-1920s with millions of members. It used terror and violence against Black people, immigrants, Catholics and Jews.

Contrast: while the 1920s are remembered as a decade of freedom and fun, for millions of Black Americans it was a decade of segregation, poverty and fear.

Racism · the Great Migration

Moving north for a better life

Seeking work and hoping to escape the worst of Southern racism, huge numbers of Black Americans moved from the rural South to the industrial cities of the North (like Chicago, Detroit and New York). This is called the Great Migration.

  • The North offered factory jobs and no Jim Crow segregation laws.
  • But newcomers still faced discrimination, poor housing and low pay, and were often confined to crowded districts.
  • Districts like Harlem in New York became centres of Black culture — the Harlem Renaissance in music, writing and art.

Life in the North was often better than the South, but it was far from equal — proof that the USA remained a nation of stark contrasts.

The Roaring Twenties · the boom

The economic boom

The 1920s brought a spectacular economic boom. American industry roared ahead and many people became far better off:

  • Mass production — Henry Ford's assembly line made the Model T car cheap enough for ordinary families; by the late 1920s millions were sold.
  • Booming demand for consumer goods — cars, radios, vacuum cleaners, fridges.
  • Hire purchase (credit) let people "buy now, pay later", fuelling spending.
  • Clever advertising and a soaring stock market boom made shares look like a sure bet.

Contrast again: the boom left many behind — farmers, coal miners and Black Americans in the South often saw little of the new wealth.

Prohibition

Banning alcohol

In 1917 the 18th Amendment to the Constitution was passed (ratified 1919); from 1920 it made the making, selling and transporting of alcohol illegal across the USA. This was Prohibition.

  • Supporters (the "drys") argued alcohol caused crime, poverty and broken families.
  • But millions still wanted to drink, so illegal bars ("speakeasies") sprang up everywhere, supplied by bootleggers who smuggled and made alcohol.

Prohibition largely failed: instead of ending drinking, it created a huge illegal trade and made criminals rich and powerful.

Prohibition · gangsters

Gangsters and organised crime

Prohibition handed organised crime a fortune. Gangs fought to control the illegal alcohol business, and corruption spread to police, judges and politicians.

  • Al Capone ran a criminal empire in Chicago, making millions from bootlegging and using violence to crush rivals.
  • Gang warfare (such as the 1929 St Valentine's Day Massacre) shocked the public.

Prohibition was so unpopular and unenforceable that it was finally repealed by the 21st Amendment in 1933.

Key link: Prohibition is often blamed for increasing crime and corruption rather than reducing them — a law that backfired.

Changing lives · women

Women in the 1920s

The 1920s brought real changes for many American women:

  • The 19th Amendment (1920) gave women across the USA the right to vote.
  • Some young, mostly middle-class women — the "flappers" — shocked older generations by wearing shorter skirts, cutting their hair, smoking, drinking and dancing to jazz.
  • More women worked, drove cars and enjoyed new freedoms.

Contrast: flappers were a minority. For most women — especially poorer, rural and Black women — daily life changed far less, and old attitudes remained strong.

Popular culture

Jazz, cinema and radio

A booming popular culture united millions of Americans in new leisure and entertainment:

  • Jazz — born in Black communities — became the sound of the decade (the "Jazz Age").
  • Cinema boomed: silent films gave way to "talkies" — The Jazz Singer (1927) was the first major film with sound.
  • Radio brought music, news and sport into millions of homes; sports stars became national heroes.

Mass entertainment helped create a shared national culture — but jazz and the flappers also worried more conservative Americans who felt standards were slipping.

Cracks in the boom

Not everyone was roaring

Beneath the glittering boom, serious weaknesses were building up:

  • Farmers struggled with falling prices and debt throughout the 1920s.
  • Overproduction — factories made more goods than people could buy once demand slowed.
  • Too much buying on credit, and reckless speculation on the stock market (often with borrowed money), left the economy fragile.
  • Wealth was very unevenly shared — millions stayed poor even at the height of the boom.

These weaknesses meant the boom was built on shaky foundations — and when confidence collapsed, the whole system could tumble.

The end · 1929

The Wall Street Crash

In October 1929 the New York stock market (Wall Street) crashed. Panicking investors sold millions of shares at once, share prices collapsed, and fortunes were wiped out overnight.

  • Banks and businesses failed; savings vanished; unemployment soared.
  • The Crash triggered the Great Depression of the 1930s.

The Crash of 1929 is the end-point of this study: the "boom" decade ended in catastrophe, exposing how fragile the good times had really been.

Exam skill

Sources and interpretations

In the exam you handle sources and interpretations about 1920s America. Two quick habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A cheery advert selling cars and a photograph of a Southern shack tell very different stories about the "boom" — because each shows only part of a nation of contrasts.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know (dates, events, who benefited and who did not).

Interpretations differ: some see the 1920s as a golden age of freedom and prosperity; others stress racism, intolerance and inequality. Knowing that both pictures are partly true — and why — is exactly what the exam rewards.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Immigration: "melting pot"; growing hostility; Emergency Quota Act 1921; Immigration (National Origins) Act 1924.

Red Scare: fear of communism after 1917; Palmer Raids 1919–20; Sacco & Vanzetti (arrested 1920, executed 1927).

Racism: Jim Crow segregation; KKK revived 1915, peaked mid-1920s; the Great Migration north.

The boom: mass production, Ford's Model T & assembly line; consumer goods; hire purchase; stock market boom.

Prohibition: 18th Amendment (1919, in force 1920); speakeasies, bootlegging, Al Capone; repealed 1933 (21st Amendment).

Women & culture: 19th Amendment 1920 (the vote); flappers; jazz, cinema (The Jazz Singer 1927), radio.

The end: weaknesses beneath the boom → Wall Street Crash October 1929 → Great Depression.
