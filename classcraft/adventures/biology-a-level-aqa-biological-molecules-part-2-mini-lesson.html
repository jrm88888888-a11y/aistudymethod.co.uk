Mission 1

Mission 1: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Make a guess: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Mission 1

Lipids are more than fat

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water: they are hydrophobic.
  • Two groups: triglycerides and phospholipids.
  • Not every lipid is an energy store.
Mission 1

Building a phospholipid

  • Start with a triglyceride: glycerol + three fatty acids.
  • Swap one fatty acid for a phosphate-containing group.
  • So: glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group.
  • Result: a head (phosphate) and two tails (fatty acids).
Mission 1

Amphipathic: two personalities

  • Head: phosphate group, charged. It is hydrophilic: it interacts with water.
  • Tails: hydrocarbon chains, non-polar. They are hydrophobic.
  • This is called amphipathic.
  • Say: "the tails are excluded from water" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Which components are linked together by condensation reactions to form one phospholipid molecule?
Mission 1

The bilayer builds itself

  • (1) On water, phospholipids form a single layer: heads in the water, tails in the air.
  • (2) Under water, tails cannot stay in the air. They hide by pairing up tail to tail.
  • (3) Result: a phospholipid bilayer. Heads face the water on both sides; tails form a non-polar core.
  • No enzymes, no energy from the cell: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude the tails.
  • A sheet's edges still expose tails, so it closes into a sphere.
  • The molecules are not covalently bonded to each other, so the bilayer is fluid.
Quick check
?Phospholipids are shaken with water in a test tube, with no cells or enzymes present. What happens, and why?
Mission 1

Triglyceride or phospholipid?

  • Triglyceride: many C–H bonds, so a high energy content; insoluble, so no effect on water potential.
  • Phospholipid: the bilayer's non-polar core stops water-soluble molecules and ions crossing freely.
Mission check

?Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and triglycerides in blood plasma, which is mostly water. Each lipoprotein has an oily core of cholesterol and triglyceride, surrounded by a single layer of phospholipids (with some proteins). How are the phospholipids in this layer most likely arranged?

One rule builds membranes, vesicles and lipoproteins: heads to the water, tails away from it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Make a guess: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Mission 2

One plan, twenty versions

  • Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  • A central carbon atom carries four groups:
  • Amine group (–NH2), carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • The twenty amino acids in proteins differ only in their R group.
  • The simplest R group is a single hydrogen atom.
  • Say: "amine group, –NH2" — not: "–NH3". Nitrogen's third bond is to the central carbon.
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all 20 of the amino acids that are coded for in the genetic code?
Mission 2

The peptide bond

  • (1) Line up two amino acids: the carboxyl group of one beside the amine group of the next.
  • (2) –OH from the carboxyl and –H from the amine leave as one water molecule.
  • (3) C joins N: a peptide bond, –CO–NH–. A covalent bond. Two amino acids make a dipeptide.
  • This is a condensation reaction.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it: adding water breaks the peptide bond.
  • The R groups take no part.
Quick check
?Two amino acids react to form a dipeptide. Which statement describes this reaction?
Mission 2

Worked example: counting bonds and water

  • Many amino acids joined in a chain: a polypeptide.
  • A chain has two ends: a free amine group at one, a free carboxyl group at the other.
  • n amino acids → n − 1 peptide bonds → n − 1 water molecules released.
  • 3 amino acids: 2 bonds, 2 H2O.
  • Masses: glycine (Mr 75) + alanine (Mr 89) → dipeptide.
  • Mr = 75 + 89 − 18 = 146. Subtract one water per bond.
  • Hydrolysis of the chain uses the same number of water molecules.
  • Any of the twenty can sit at any position, repeats allowed, in any order, in chains of any length. So the number of possible sequences is effectively unlimited.
Quick check
?A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is assembled by condensation reactions. How does the number of water molecules change as the whole chain is formed?
Mission check

?Two short polypeptides, P and Q, are each made of the same five amino acids, but the amino acids are joined in a different order in P and in Q. Which statement about P and Q is correct?

Twenty kinds, joined in any order, any number of times, to any length. That is how so few make so many.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Make a guess: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Mission 3

Sequence, shape, job

  • A polypeptide folds into a precise three-dimensional shape.
  • Bonds between parts of the chain hold that shape. Most are weak; together they are strong.
  • The shape decides the function: enzymes, hormones, antibodies, transport, support.
  • The same sequence folds the same way, every time.
  • Different sequences fold differently. So endless sequences give endless proteins.
  • Four levels describe the shape: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
Mission 3

Primary structure

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Held by peptide bonds. Set by the gene.
  • The sequence, with the exact position of each amino acid, determines every later level.
  • Change one amino acid, anywhere, and the shape can change.
  • Say: "primary structure = the order of amino acids" — not: "which amino acids are present".
Mission 3

Secondary structure

  • The backbone carries polar C=O and N–H groups.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another, at regular positions.
  • α-helix: the chain coils; each turn is held to the next.
  • β-pleated sheet: lengths of chain lie side by side, folded like pleats, held to each other.
  • R groups point away from the backbone and take no part in these bonds, so helices and sheets form in many sequences.
  • Say: "backbone hydrogen bonds" — not: "bonds between R groups".
Quick check
?What stabilises the alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets of a protein's secondary structure?
Mission 3

Tertiary structure: R groups lock the fold

  • The chain, with its helices and sheets, folds further into its overall 3D shape: tertiary structure.
  • Held by bonds between R groups:
  • Hydrogen bonds: between polar R groups. Weak, but many.
  • Ionic bonds: between a –COO⁻ and an –NH3⁺ R group. pH-sensitive.
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between two cysteines. Strong.
Quick check
?In one region of a polypeptide there are two cysteines, an amino acid whose R-group contains a carboxyl group and an amino acid whose R-group contains an amine group. Which statement about the bonds these could form in the tertiary structure is correct?
Mission 3

Quaternary structure

  • Some proteins are built from more than one polypeptide: quaternary structure.
  • The polypeptides are held together by the same kinds of bond as tertiary structure.
  • Example: haemoglobin, four polypeptides (see Mission 4).
  • So a functional protein may contain one polypeptide, or several.
  • All four levels together decide what the protein does.
Mission 3

Denaturation

  • (1) A folded protein: its shape held by many weak bonds.
  • (2) High temperature: more vibration breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. The chain unfolds.
  • (3) pH change: gaining or losing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break.
  • This loss of shape is denaturation. The protein can no longer do its job.
  • Peptide bonds stay intact: the sequence is unchanged.
  • Usually permanent: the chains do not refold correctly. A cooked egg white stays white.
  • Cold does not denature: it slows molecules down, and activity returns on warming.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?When egg white is heated, its soluble proteins turn into a white solid. What has happened to the protein molecules?
Mission 3

Enzymes: globular proteins with an active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: compact, folded, usually soluble.
  • The active site is a small pocket made of only a few amino acids.
  • But the rest of the folded chain holds those few in exactly the right places.
  • So the active site's shape and properties come from the enzyme's tertiary structure.
  • The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Denature the enzyme: the active site changes and the substrate no longer binds.
Quick checkRead the data
?(Illustrative data.) An enzyme's activity at its optimum pH of 7 was 100 units. One sample was placed at pH 2 for 10 minutes, then returned to pH 7: its activity was 4 units after 1 hour and 4 units after 24 hours, and its polypeptide chains were found to be intact. A second sample was cooled to 5 °C for 10 minutes, then returned to 37 °C: its activity was 100 units. What do these results show?
Mission check

?A mutation replaces one amino acid in an enzyme at a position on the surface far from the active site in the folded protein. The enzyme loses most of its activity. What is the best explanation?

Sequence sets shape, and shape sets function. One amino acid in the wrong place can be enough.

Mission 4

  • Both have a precise, repeatable 3D structure.
  • Say: "fibrous proteins have an elongated structure" — not: "fibrous proteins are unfolded".
Mission 4

Haemoglobin

  • A globular protein of four polypeptides: two α and two β. So it has quaternary structure.
  • Each polypeptide holds one haem group: a ring with an Fe²⁺ ion at its centre.
  • Each haem binds one O2. One haemoglobin carries up to four O2.
  • Haem is not made of amino acids. It is a prosthetic group.
  • Polar R groups on the surface keep it soluble inside red blood cells.
  • The haemoglobins are a group of chemically similar molecules found in many different organisms.
Mission 4

Insulin and a named enzyme

  • Insulin: a small globular hormone.
  • Two polypeptides (21 and 30 amino acids), linked by disulfide bridges.
  • Soluble, so it travels in blood plasma.
  • Its precise shape fits its receptor on target cells.
Quick check
?A haemoglobin molecule is made of four polypeptides, each holding one haem group that contains an Fe²⁺ ion. Which statement about haemoglobin is correct?
Quick check
?Protein X is compact and roughly spherical, dissolves in blood plasma and binds to a specific receptor on target cells. Protein Y forms long insoluble fibres with high tensile strength in tendons. Which statement correctly relates the form of each protein to its function?
Mission check

?Spider dragline silk is a protein. Its long polypeptide chains lie parallel to one another, with many stretches folded into β-pleated sheets that are stacked and held together by hydrogen bonds. It does not dissolve in water. Which statement best explains why it suits the frame of a web?

Long and cross-linked for strength, compact and soluble for work in solution. Shape fits job.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Make a guess: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Mission 5

Enzymes are catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst.
  • A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
  • It is not used up: it leaves the reaction unchanged, so one molecule catalyses the same reaction again and again.
  • The reaction can happen without the enzyme, but at body temperature it would be far too slow.
  • Benefit: reactions in cells run fast enough to sustain life.
  • Say: "speeds up a reaction" — not: "makes a reaction possible".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how an enzyme acts as a catalyst in a cell?
Mission 5

Metabolism: a web of reactions

  • Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism — a network of linked pathways.
  • Almost every step is catalysed by its own enzyme. So an organism needs thousands of different enzymes.
  • Control the enzyme → control the step. That is how cells regulate metabolism.
  • Enzymes build structures (e.g. DNA polymerase copies DNA) and run functions (e.g. respiration), from single cells to the whole organism.
Mission 5

Lowering the energy barrier

  • To react, bonds in the substrate must first be broken. That needs energy: the activation energy.
  • An enzyme gives the reaction a route with a lower activation energy.
  • At the same temperature, more substrate molecules now have enough energy to react. So the rate rises.
  • The energy of the substrate and of the products is unchanged. So is the energy released overall.
  • The enzyme supplies no energy.
  • Say: "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" — not: "the enzyme provides the activation energy".
Mission 5

Worked example: reading an energy diagram

Illustrative values, read from an energy diagram:

LevelWithout enzymeWith enzyme
Substrate5050
Peak11075
Products2020
  • Energies in kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Activation energy = peak − substrate level. Not the peak value read from the axis.
  • Without enzyme: 110 − 50 = 60. With enzyme: 75 − 50 = 25.
  • Energy released = substrate − products = 50 − 20 = 30, with or without the enzyme.
Quick check
?In an energy diagram for a reaction, the substrate is at 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and the products are at 15 kJ mol⁻¹. The highest energy reached during the reaction is 90 kJ mol⁻¹ without an enzyme and 60 kJ mol⁻¹ with the enzyme. For the enzyme-catalysed reaction, what are the activation energy and the overall energy yield?
Mission 5

The active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: one or more polypeptide chains folded into a compact 3D shape (tertiary structure).
  • The active site is a small pocket or groove where the substrate binds.
  • Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions between R groups fix the shape of the active site.
  • The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site; its charged groups are attracted to opposite charges there.
  • Substrate + active site → enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme free again.
  • Say: "complementary shape" — not: "the same shape".
Mission 5

Specificity

  • Each enzyme catalyses one reaction, or a few very similar ones: specificity.
  • Reason: only a molecule whose shape and charges suit the active site can bind.
  • Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Amylase hydrolyses starch but not cellulose.
  • The glucose units are linked differently (α-glucose in starch, β-glucose in cellulose), so cellulose does not fit amylase's active site.
  • Size alone does not decide: many small molecules do not bind.
  • Say: "the active site is complementary to one substrate" — not: "any molecule small enough fits".
Quick check
?Lipase catalyses the hydrolysis of triglycerides but has no effect on starch. What is the reason?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical bacterium, enzyme E catalyses the conversion of compound A to compound B. The conversion of A to B releases energy. A mutation stops the bacterium from making enzyme E. No other enzyme in the bacterium acts on compound A. Which prediction about compound B in the mutant bacterium is best supported?

An enzyme changes the route, not the destination: a lower barrier, the same start and finish, and the enzyme itself unchanged.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Make a guess: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Mission 6

Meeting by chance

  • Molecules in solution are in constant, random motion.
  • A reaction can only happen when a substrate collides with the active site, the right way round.
  • Enzymes do not seek, pull or grab their substrates.
  • More collisions per second → more enzyme–substrate complexes → faster rate (used again in a later lesson).
  • Say: "substrate and active site collide by random motion" — not: "the enzyme attracts its substrate".
Mission 6

Model 1: lock and key

  • Proposed by Emil Fischer, 1894.
  • The active site is a rigid shape. Only a substrate of exactly complementary shape fits, like one key in one lock.
  • Strength: explains specificity.
  • Limits: a rigid site does not explain how bound substrate is strained into reacting; and later evidence showed many enzymes change shape when they bind.
  • This is how science works: a model is kept while it explains the evidence, then revised.
Mission 6

Model 2: induced fit

  • Proposed by Daniel Koshland, 1958.
  • The active site is flexible. As the substrate binds, the active site changes shape and moulds closely around it.
  • Both the enzyme and the substrate change shape as they bind.
  • The tight fit strains bonds in the substrate, which lowers the activation energy.
  • Enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme returns to its original shape.
  • Later evidence for the newer model: X-ray structures of the same enzyme with and without its substrate show different shapes.
  • Say: "the active site changes shape as the substrate binds" — not: "the active site is a rigid exact match".
Quick check
?According to the induced-fit model, what happens when a substrate binds to the active site of an enzyme?
Mission 6

Inside or outside the cell?

  • Intracellular enzymes act inside the cell that made them.
  • e.g. catalase breaks down toxic hydrogen peroxide inside cells.
  • Extracellular enzymes are made inside cells, secreted, and act outside them.
  • e.g. amylase (in saliva and pancreatic juice) digests starch; trypsin (from the pancreas) digests protein in the small intestine.
  • Together, intracellular and extracellular enzymes determine structures and functions, from single cells to the whole organism.
  • Say: "extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted" — not: "made outside cells".
Quick check
?Trypsin is made by cells in the pancreas and digests proteins in the small intestine. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Hexokinase is the enzyme that adds a phosphate group to glucose. X-ray structures show that the two halves of the enzyme are further apart when no glucose is bound. When glucose binds, the two halves close around it. What does this evidence show?

Not a rigid lock: the enzyme and its substrate adjust to each other, and that close, strained fit is part of what speeds the reaction.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic tails; in water they form bilayers by themselves.

Mission 2

  • Every amino acid: central carbon + amine group + carboxyl group + H + R group; only R differs.
  • Condensation joins carboxyl to amine: a peptide bond and one water per bond; hydrolysis reverses it.

Mission 3

  • The amino acid sequence decides how a chain folds; bonds between parts of the chain hold the shape; the shape decides the job.
  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H bonds: α-helix, β-pleated sheet) → tertiary (R-group bonds) → quaternary (more than one polypeptide).
  • Heat and pH changes break the weak bonds: the protein is denatured, though its peptide bonds survive.

Mission 4

  • Globular proteins (haemoglobin, insulin, enzymes) are compact and soluble; fibrous proteins (collagen) are long, insoluble and strong.
  • Haemoglobin: four polypeptides, each with a haem prosthetic group holding Fe²⁺; one O2 per haem.

Mission 5

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts: they lower the activation energy, leave the energy released unchanged, and are not used up.
  • The active site, held in shape by the whole tertiary structure, is complementary in shape and charge to one substrate: hence the enzyme–substrate complex and specificity.

Mission 6

  • Substrate and active site meet by random collisions; in induced fit the active site changes shape as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.
  • Intracellular enzymes act inside cells; extracellular enzymes are made inside cells and secreted to act outside.
