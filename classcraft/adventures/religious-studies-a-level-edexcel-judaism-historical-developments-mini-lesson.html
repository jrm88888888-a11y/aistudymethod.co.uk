Mission 1: How have Jews in Britain stayed Jewish for 350 years without either hiding or shutting themselves away?

Make a guess: How have Jews in Britain stayed Jewish for 350 years without either hiding or shutting themselves away?

Mission 1

Diaspora

  • Diaspora (Greek, "scattering"): Jewish communities living outside the Land of Israel.
  • Two Hebrew words: galut ("exile", a punishment and a loss) and tefutzot ("dispersion", a neutral fact).
  • Began with the Babylonian exile (586 BCE); grew after the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE) (see Mission 2).
  • Today the two largest Jewish communities are in Israel and the USA; Britain has one of Europe's larger communities.
  • Every diaspora community faces one question: how to belong to the wider society and stay Jewish.
Mission 1

Without a Temple: a new Jewish identity

  • Before 70 CE: the Temple, priests and sacrifice stood at the centre.
  • After: Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai at Yavneh and the rabbis rebuilt Judaism around Torah study, prayer and the synagogue; "the offering of our lips" replaces bullocks (Hosea 14:3 in JPS numbering).
  • Judaism becomes portable: it can be lived anywhere a community keeps Torah.
  • Authority moves from priests to rabbis, interpreters of the Oral Torah.
  • Isaiah Gafni (Land, Center and Diaspora, 1997): rabbis built ideas that let a diaspora community, above all Babylonia, claim religious authority while the Land stayed central (paraphrase).
Mission 1Read the source

"Seek the peace of the city"

> "And seek the peace of the city whither I have caused you to be carried away captive, and pray unto the LORD for it; for in the peace thereof shall ye have peace." (Jeremiah 29:7, JPS 1917)

  • Jeremiah's letter to the exiles in Babylon: build houses, plant gardens, raise families there.
  • The Talmud's principle dina de-malkhuta dina ("the law of the kingdom is the law"): Jews obey the civil law of the land they live in, unless it forces them to break the Torah.
  • British synagogues pray each Shabbat for the Royal Family and the country.

Show answer

Mission 1

Migration and Jews in Britain

  • 1070s: Jews come from Normandy after the Norman Conquest.
  • 1190: massacre at Clifford's Tower, York. 1290: Edward I expels all Jews from England.
  • 1656: Jews resettle under Cromwell, after petitions by Menasseh ben Israel; Sephardi Jews build Bevis Marks synagogue (1701), still in use.
  • 18th century: Ashkenazi Jews from Germany and Holland; the Board of Deputies (1760).
  • 1858: Lionel de Rothschild is the first practising Jew to sit in Parliament.
  • 1881–1914: Jews fleeing Russian pogroms settle in London's East End, Manchester, Leeds, Glasgow; the Aliens Act (1905) restricts entry.
  • 1930s: refugees from Nazism; the Kindertransport (1938–39) brings nearly 10,000 children.
  • After 1945: Jews from Iraq, Egypt, Iran, South Africa, Israel and, recently, France.
  • Three ways a minority can live: segregation (living apart), assimilation (identity fades into the majority), integration (full part in society while keeping Jewish practice).

Mission 1

The Jewish Leadership Council

  • Jewish Leadership Council (JLC), founded in 2003: brings together the leaders of major British Jewish organisations.
  • Members include communal bodies (e.g. the Board of Deputies, synagogue movements across Orthodox and Progressive) and major charities.
  • Role: strategy and co-ordination for the community: Jewish schools (through its schools network), welfare, security funding, and speaking with government alongside the Board of Deputies.
  • The Board of Deputies (1760) is the elected representative body; the JLC is a council of organisational leaders. Critics ask whether an unelected body should speak for the community.
  • Neither speaks for most Haredi communities, which have their own bodies.
Mission 1

Who speaks for Judaism?

  • Edexcel's phrase: interpretations about revelation and the authentic voice of Judaism.
  • After 70 CE: the rabbis' claim: the Oral Torah given at Sinai, passed on in a chain (Pirkei Avot 1:1); the Karaites (from the 8th century) rejected it and read only the written Torah.
  • Migration moved authority: from the Land to Babylonia (the Geonim; Saadia Gaon, 882–942, moved from Egypt to lead the academy at Sura); then Spain (Maimonides, 1138–1204, who fled to Egypt), then Ashkenazi Europe.
  • Modern diaspora: Orthodox, Reform and secular Jews each claim to continue the authentic tradition.
  • Key figures: Yohanan ben Zakkai, Saadia, Maimonides, and for Britain Menasseh ben Israel.
Mission 1Evaluate

Was the diaspora a loss or a strength?

  • Claim: The diaspora strengthened Jewish identity.
  • For: a portable, text-centred Judaism survived empires; Babylonia produced the Talmud; migration spread learning.
  • For: diaspora communities gave Judaism variety (Sephardi, Ashkenazi, Mizrahi traditions).
  • Against: exile brought persecution, expulsions (England 1290; Spain 1492) and the Holocaust.
  • Against: many lost their Jewish identity through assimilation, especially in free societies.
  • Judgement criteria: survival, creativity, safety, or unity? Is "galut" (exile) or "tefutzot" (dispersion) the right word?

Make a guess: For most of 1,900 years few Jews lived in the Land of Israel. Why did it stay at the centre of Judaism?

Mission 2

A land in a covenant

  • The Land enters Jewish history as part of God's covenant with Abraham.
  • "Unto thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 12:7, JPS 1917).
  • Hebrew: Eretz Yisrael, "the Land of Israel"; the Torah calls it Canaan before the Israelites enter it.
  • The Promised Land: promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, entered under Joshua.
  • The Land is holy: some commandments apply only there (e.g. tithes of produce, the sabbatical year): the mitzvot ha-teluyot ba-aretz.
  • So the Land is not only a homeland: it is a place where the covenant can be lived in full.
Mission 2

Exile to Babylon, and return

  • 586 BCE: the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar destroy Jerusalem and the First Temple; many are taken into exile (Hebrew: galut).
  • The Torah and the prophets explain exile as the result of breaking the covenant, and promise return (e.g. Deuteronomy 30:3–5; Jeremiah 29:10).
  • "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." (Psalm 137:1, JPS 1917)
  • 538 BCE: Cyrus of Persia lets exiles return; the Second Temple is completed c. 515 BCE; Ezra and Nehemiah renew the covenant (see Mission 3).
  • Many Jews stayed in Babylonia: a lasting community outside the Land begins.
Mission 2

70 CE: the great dispersion

  • 70 CE: Roman armies under Titus destroy Jerusalem and the Second Temple.
  • 132–135 CE: the Bar Kokhba revolt is crushed; Jews are barred from Jerusalem, renamed Aelia Capitolina.
  • Without Temple or sacrifice, the rabbis rebuild Judaism around Torah, prayer and synagogue.
  • The diaspora (dispersion) grows: Babylonia, then North Africa, Spain, Europe, the Middle East.
  • Mourning built into the year: Tisha B'Av fast for both Temples.
  • How the Temple's destruction shaped identity and the diaspora.

Mission 2

Still at the centre, 70–1948

  • Prayer: the Amidah asks for the ingathering of exiles and the rebuilding of Jerusalem; grace after meals blesses God "who builds Jerusalem".
  • Law: the calendar was long fixed from the Land; rabbis debated which mitzvot apply outside it.
  • Presence: Jewish communities never fully left (e.g. Tiberias, Safed, Jerusalem, Hebron).
  • Journeys: Judah Halevi (d. 1141) set out for Zion; Nachmanides (1267) rebuilt a community in Jerusalem; Spanish exiles (after 1492) made Safed a centre of learning.
  • Hope: the Messiah would gather the exiles; Jews were to wait, not force the end.
  • Babylonia and later centres led Jewish learning; the Land led Jewish longing.
Mission 2

Debates about covenant and promise

  • Unconditional? The promise to Abraham is "for ever"; many religious Zionists read the modern return as its fulfilment.
  • Conditional? Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28–30 tie life in the Land to keeping the covenant; exile follows sin, return follows repentance.
  • Who brings the return? God alone, in the Messiah's time (most Haredim); or God through human action (religious Zionists); or human action alone (secular Zionists).
  • Is exile only geography? Some Progressive Jews and diaspora thinkers say the covenant is lived wherever Jews keep it; the Land is precious but not required for a full Jewish life.
  • Isaiah Gafni shows that even in late antiquity rabbis argued about whether a diaspora centre (Babylonia) could rival the Land (see Mission 1).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the Land central to Judaism?

  • Claim: The Land of Israel is central to Judaism.
  • For: covenant promise to Abraham; many mitzvot tied to the Land; daily prayer for Zion; festivals follow its harvests (Sukkot, Shavuot).
  • For: exile itself is defined by distance from the Land.
  • Against: for most of history most Jews lived outside it; rabbinic Judaism flourished in Babylonia and Europe.
  • Against: Torah and covenant can be kept anywhere; Jeremiah told the exiles to "seek the peace of the city" where they were (Jeremiah 29:7).
  • Judgement criteria: central to belief, to practice, or to identity? For which Jews: Orthodox, Progressive, secular?

Mission 3

What secularisation is

  • Secularisation: religion loses influence over society, institutions and individuals.
  • Three features:
  • Replacement: science, law and reason, not scripture, become the accepted sources of truth and moral values.
  • Relegation: religion is pushed into the personal sphere: fine at home, kept out of public life.
  • Militant atheism: the view that religion is irrational and a threat to social stability.
  • For Jews, the challenge began with emancipation: citizenship came with the expectation that religion was private.
Mission 3

Militant atheism and Jewish replies

  • "New Atheists": Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006); Christopher Hitchens (1949–2011), God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything (2007); Sam Harris, The End of Faith (2004).
  • Their charges: faith is belief without evidence; religion divides people and can fuel violence; it should have no special place in law or education.
  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science explains how, religion asks why; they need each other (paraphrase).
  • Sacks also argued that religious communities build trust, volunteering and care: they strengthen social stability rather than threaten it (paraphrase).
  • Many Jews add: Judaism values questioning and argument (the Talmud records disagreement), so it does not depend on blind faith.
Mission 3

Secularisation inside Jewish life

  • 19th-century formula (Judah Leib Gordon, 1860s, common translation): "Be a man in the street and a Jew at home": relegation in a single line.
  • Secular Jews: Jewish by people, culture, family, food, humour or Israel; little belief or observance.
  • Humanistic Judaism (Rabbi Sherwin Wine, 1963): Jewish culture and ethics without belief in God.
  • In Israel many secular Jews keep festivals as national culture.
  • So secularisation is not only a pressure from outside; it is also a way many Jews now live.

Mission 3

Ghettoism

  • Ghettoism: preserving Jewish identity by choosing to live separately from secular society.
  • Not the forced ghettos of history (the word comes from Venice, 1516), but a voluntary separation.
  • Today: close Haredi communities (e.g. Stamford Hill, Gateshead, Bnei Brak): own schools and yeshivot, Yiddish or Hebrew, distinctive dress, limits on television and internet, marriage within the community.
  • Aim: to protect Torah life from secular values, as Nehemiah's covenant protected Shabbat and family.
  • Strengths: high observance and strong community; large families mean rapid growth.
  • Criticisms: isolation, poverty from limited secular education, little dialogue with others.
Mission 3

Assimilation

  • Assimilation: becoming like the majority so fully that a distinct Jewish identity fades.
  • Routes: dropping Shabbat and kashrut; intermarriage without raising children as Jews; in the 19th century, conversion (the poet Heinrich Heine called baptism his entry ticket to European culture: paraphrase).
  • Some see it as a free personal choice in an open society.
  • Most Jewish leaders see it as the greatest threat in free societies: Jews disappearing not by persecution but by choice.
  • A secular society tests whether Judaism can survive without outside pressure to stay separate.
Mission 3

Integration

  • Integration: full participation in society while keeping Jewish belief and practice.
  • Samson Raphael Hirsch (1808–1888): Torah im Derekh Eretz: Torah together with the culture and work of the wider world.
  • Modern Orthodoxy: university, professions, civic life, with halakhah kept in full.
  • Progressive Judaism: adapts practice to modern life and values, while keeping a distinct religious identity.
  • Jonathan Sacks: integration, not assimilation: every group helps build the shared "home" (see Mission 1).
  • Jeremiah 29:7: "seek the peace of the city": the scriptural root of integration.

Mission 3

The Haredi response

  • Haredi Jews see secular culture as a challenge to be resisted, not adopted.
  • Torah study at the centre: many men study in yeshiva or kollel for years.
  • Own institutions: schools, courts, charities (e.g. free-loan societies and volunteer ambulances).
  • In Israel: Haredi parties defend Shabbat, kashrut and marriage law in public life; long dispute over exemption of yeshiva students from army service (the Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that there was no legal basis for a blanket exemption).
  • Variety: some reject the secular State outright (Satmar, Neturei Karta); most are non-Zionist and take part pragmatically.
  • Chabad takes another path: outreach to secular Jews, meeting secularisation by engagement.
Mission 3Diversity

Four responses compared

  • Haredi: secular society is a threat; separate to protect Torah. Source: Nehemiah 10. Risk: isolation.
  • Modern Orthodox: secular knowledge is valuable; integrate with halakhah intact. Source: Hirsch. Risk: compromise under pressure.
  • Progressive: adapt practice to modern values; Judaism is evolving. Source: Reform principles. Risk: losing distinctiveness.
  • Secular: identity by culture and peoplehood, not belief. Source: Haskalah, Zionism. Risk: weaker transmission to children.
  • All four are Jewish answers to the same question.
Mission 3Worked answer

  • Counter: integration (Hirsch; Jeremiah 29:7) argues Torah can thrive inside society; separation can bring poverty and isolation.
  • Judgement criterion: "effective" at what: survival in numbers, depth of practice, or influence on society?

Mission 4

Tzedakah: justice, not charity

  • Jewish teaching sees wealth as a gift held in trust from God ("The earth is the LORD'S", Psalm 24:1), so it carries limits: ma'aser (the custom of giving a tenth), Shabbat rest from trade, bal tashchit (do not waste).
  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek, "justice": giving is a duty, not an optional kindness.
  • Even a person who receives tzedakah should give some.
  • Maimonides' eight levels (Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14): the highest is to help someone become self-supporting (a loan, partnership or job).
  • Gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness): personal help that money cannot replace: visiting the sick, comforting mourners.
  • Communities run free-loan societies (gemachs), food banks and care homes.
Mission 4

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberation theology began among Latin American Catholics (Gustavo Gutiérrez, 1971): God sides with the poor and oppressed.
  • Jews point out that the Exodus is the original liberation story: God frees slaves from Pharaoh.
  • The prophets demand justice for the widow, orphan, stranger and poor (Isaiah 1:17; Amos 5:24).
  • Abraham Joshua Heschel (1907–1972) marched with Martin Luther King at Selma (1965); he said afterwards that he felt as if his legs were praying (paraphrase).
  • Marc Ellis, Toward a Jewish Theology of Liberation (1987): Jews, as a people who suffered, must stand with the oppressed today (paraphrase); his application to Palestinians is controversial among Jews.
  • Liberationist Judaism: social action for the poor as a religious duty, not only charity.

Mission 4

Tikkun olam

  • Tikkun olam: "repair of the world".
  • Mishnah (Gittin 4): rabbinic rulings made "for the repair of the world": to keep society fair and orderly.
  • Aleinu prayer: to perfect the world under God's kingship.
  • Kabbalah (Luria): every mitzvah repairs a broken cosmos.
  • Modern use: social justice and social action, especially in Reform Judaism.
  • Critics: some Orthodox thinkers say "tikkun olam" has become a political slogan detached from Torah and mitzvot.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is social action now the heart of Judaism?

  • Claim: In a secular society, social action is the most important expression of Judaism.
  • For: the prophets put justice above ritual (Isaiah 58); social action shows Judaism's relevance to people who no longer worship.
  • For: campaigns (JSAF, Mitzvah Day) involve Jews of every stream, including the non-observant.
  • Against: Torah, prayer, Shabbat and kashrut are commandments too; social action without them may be ethics, not Judaism.
  • Against: "tikkun olam" can become a political slogan, and Jews disagree about which policies justice requires.
  • Judgement criteria: what makes an act Jewish: its motive, its source, or its community? Can justice and ritual be separated?

Mission 5

How science challenges religion

  • Modern science asks for evidence and reason: claims are tested, not accepted on authority.
  • Some discoveries seem to clash with a plain reading of Genesis:
  • The Big Bang (from the 1920s–60s): the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Evolution (Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859): life developed over billions of years by natural selection.
  • Genetics: humans can now read and edit DNA, raising new ethical questions.
  • Jewish responses range from rejection of some findings to full acceptance with a re-reading of Torah.
Mission 5Read the source

Bereshit

> "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Bereshit: "in the beginning", the first word of the Torah and the Hebrew name of Genesis.
  • Rashi (1040–1105), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): the plain sense is "At the beginning of God's creating the heaven and the earth…"; the verse does not set out to teach the order of creation.
  • Nachmanides (Ramban, 1194–1270), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): God created from absolute nothing a tiny substance, no bigger than a mustard seed, from which everything else came.
  • Creatio ex nihilo (creation from nothing): God alone is eternal; the world had a beginning (Saadia Gaon; Maimonides).
  • Maimonides also taught that where reason proves something, a verse that seems to contradict it should be read figuratively (Guide for the Perplexed II.25, paraphrase); for him, studying nature leads to love of God.

Q1 According to Rashi, what is Genesis 1 not trying to teach?

Show answer

Mission 5Diversity

Responses to evolution

  • Rejection (many Haredi Jews): six literal days; species created separately. Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902–1994), the Lubavitcher Rebbe, argued in letters that science offers theories about the distant past, not proof.
  • Embrace through mysticism: Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine: evolution, as gradual ascent towards perfection, fits kabbalistic teaching (paraphrase).
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science explains how things work; religion asks what they mean (paraphrase). Rabbi Natan Slifkin (b. 1975), The Challenge of Creation (2006): Torah and evolution are compatible; some Haredi rabbis banned his books in 2004–05.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): accept evolution fully; Genesis teaches values (one Creator, a good world, human dignity), not biology.
  • Most Jewish movements found evolution less of a crisis than many Christian groups did: the Oral Torah had long allowed non-literal readings.
Mission 5

Cantor and Swetlitz

  • Geoffrey Cantor and Marc Swetlitz (eds), Jewish Tradition and the Challenge of Darwinism (2006).
  • Main ideas (paraphrase):
  • Jewish responses to Darwin were diverse: rabbis, scientists and writers in Europe, America and Palestine/Israel reacted differently.
  • Responses were shaped by context, not only theology: emancipation, antisemitism, social Darwinism and racial science made "evolution" a social and political issue for Jews.
  • The debate was less about biblical literalism than about design, ethics and human dignity; there was no Jewish equivalent of the American "creation versus evolution" battles until some recent Haredi disputes.
  • Use them to answer "how far is Jewish belief compatible with science?": it depends which Jews, where and when.
Mission 5

How old is the universe?

  • The Jewish calendar counts years from creation: Rosh Hashanah 2026 began the year 5787.
  • Science: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old; the Earth about 4.5 billion.
  • Jewish responses:
  • Literal: the world is under 6,000 years old; it was made mature (with apparent age), so evidence of age does not show real age (Schneerson's view).
  • Earlier worlds: a midrash says God "built worlds and destroyed them" before this one (Bereshit Rabbah 3:7, paraphrase); Rabbi Israel Lipschutz (1782–1860) linked it to fossils.
  • Day = age: yom (day) can mean a long period; the calendar counts from Adam, not from the first moment.
  • Relativity: Gerald Schroeder (next screen).
Mission 5

Gerald Schroeder: six days and 15 billion years

  • Gerald Schroeder (b. 1938), MIT-trained physicist living in Jerusalem; Genesis and the Big Bang (1990), The Science of God (1997).
  • Einstein's relativity: time passes at different rates in different frames of reference.
  • The six days are told from the perspective of creation's beginning; the universe has since stretched by a factor of about a million million (10¹²).
  • So six days "then" equal about 15 billion years "now". Each day is half the one before: about 8, 4, 2, 1, ½ and ¼ billion years.
  • From Adam, time is counted in human terms: hence about 5,800 years.
  • Critics: scientists say the calculation uses relativity loosely; some religious critics say it reads modern science into Torah (when science changes, so must the reading).

Mission 5Evaluate

Is Judaism compatible with science?

  • Claim: Jewish belief is fully compatible with scientific thinking.
  • For: Maimonides made studying nature a path to loving God; reason can reshape how a verse is read.
  • For: Rashi and the Oral Torah never required a literal, scientific Genesis; Kook and Sacks welcome evolution.
  • Against: many Haredi Jews reject evolution and an old universe; the Slifkin ban shows real conflict.
  • Against: Schroeder-style harmonising may change Torah to fit each new theory (critics' view).
  • Cantor and Swetlitz: compatibility depends on context; the strongest clashes were about ethics and dignity, not dates.
  • Judgement criteria: Which Jews? Compatible with scientific findings, or with science's method (evidence over authority)? Does science answer the questions Judaism asks?
Mission check

?A student says: "Judaism must reject the Big Bang, because Genesis 1:1 says God created the heaven and the earth." Which reply best reflects mainstream Jewish thought?

The clash is about how to read Genesis, not whether God created. Jews divide over evolution and the age of the world, but almost all welcome science as the study of God's works.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The diaspora began with the Babylonian exile and grew after 70 CE; a portable Judaism of Torah, prayer and synagogue let Jews live anywhere (Jeremiah 29:7; dina de-malkhuta dina).
  • The Temple's fall moved authority to rabbis and diaspora centres (Gafni); migration kept reshaping who speaks for Judaism.

Mission 2

  • In Jewish belief, the Land is part of God's covenant with Abraham: a holy land where the covenant can be lived in full.
  • Exile to Babylon (586 BCE) and the Roman destruction (70 CE) scattered the Jews; prayer, law and festivals kept the Land at the centre of Jewish life.

Mission 3

  • Secularisation: religion replaced as a source of truth and morals, relegated to private life, and attacked by militant atheism.
  • Responses: ghettoism (Nehemiah 10:28–31; Haredi separation), assimilation (identity fades), integration (Hirsch, Modern Orthodoxy, Progressive Judaism, Sacks).

Mission 4

  • Liberationist approaches read the Exodus and the prophets (Isaiah 58:6–7) as a call to free the oppressed.
  • Tikkun olam moved from Mishnaic law and Kabbalah to modern social justice; Jews differ on how political it should be.

Mission 5

  • Science's evidence-based method and discoveries (Big Bang, evolution, genetics) challenge literal readings of Genesis; Jewish responses run from rejection (many Haredim) to full acceptance (Progressive), with Kook, Sacks and Slifkin in between.
  • Bereshit: Rashi says Genesis 1 does not teach the order of creation; Ramban's tiny first substance and creatio ex nihilo fit a universe with a beginning; Schroeder uses relativity to make six days equal about 15 billion years.
