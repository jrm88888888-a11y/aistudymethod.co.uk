Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Needs: things required to survive (food, water, shelter). Wants: things we desire beyond them.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Is anything not scarce?

  • Economic good: uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Almost everything.
  • Free good: needs no scarce resources; more is available than people want, even at a zero price. Zero opportunity cost.
  • Examples: air on a remote mountain, seawater, sunlight.
  • Free of charge is not the same as a free good. A state school uses teachers, buildings and tax revenue: an economic good given at a zero price.
  • Free goods can become scarce: clean air in a polluted city.
  • Say: "a free good has zero opportunity cost" — not: "a free good is anything with a zero price".
Quick check
?Which of the following is most likely to be a free good?
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Firm: money spent on a new machine cannot also pay for an advert campaign.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. A later lesson compares market, planned and mixed economies.
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is opportunity cost in practice?

It depends on:

  • Information: the next best alternative may be unknown or uncertain when the choice is made.
  • Valuation: many alternatives have no price (time, health, a clean environment), so they are hard to compare.
  • Time: some costs fall on future generations, as when a resource is over-used.
  • Whose cost: a firm's choice may impose costs on others (pollution) that it does not count.
  • Judgement: the idea always applies, but measuring it can be hard.
Mission check

?A city makes its buses free to ride. The buses are paid for from the city's tax revenue. Which statement about this choice is correct?

Scarce resources have other uses, so even a "free" service costs society the next best thing those resources could have made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When economists disagree, is it about the evidence or about values?

Make a guess: Two economists study the same data and still disagree about what the government should do. Why?

Mission 2

Ceteris paribus

  • Ceteris paribus = "other things being equal".
  • Economists change one variable and hold all others constant, to isolate its effect.
  • Example: if bus fares rise, ceteris paribus, fewer bus journeys are demanded. Incomes, fuel prices and the weather are held fixed.
  • In the real world other things rarely stay equal, so data must be read with care.
  • A market is in equilibrium when quantity demanded equals quantity supplied: it clears. Otherwise it is in disequilibrium (excess demand or excess supply).
Quick check
?A student notices that one summer the price of ice cream rose and so did the quantity sold. The student concludes that higher prices make people buy more ice cream. What is the main flaw?
Mission 2

Positive and normative

  • A positive statement is about what is, was or will be. It can be tested against evidence.
  • A normative statement is about what ought to be. It rests on a value judgement, so evidence alone cannot settle it.
  • A positive statement can be false and still be positive: "Inflation was 90% last year" (illustrative) can be checked and refuted.
  • A prediction is positive: it can be tested once the future arrives.
  • Clue words for normative: should, ought, fair, too high, better. But check the meaning, not just the word.
  • Say: "positive = testable" — not: "positive = true" or "positive = optimistic".
Quick check
?Which of these statements is normative?
Mission 2

Value judgements in policy

  • A value judgement is a view based on what someone believes is right, fair or important.
  • Policy needs both: positive analysis (what will happen?) and normative choices (is that outcome good?).
  • Example: economists may agree a tax will cut pollution and raise prices for poorer households. Whether that trade-off is worth it is a value judgement.
  • So economists can agree on the evidence and still disagree on the policy.
Mission check

?Two economists discuss fuel duty (illustrative figures).
Economist 1: "A 10% rise in fuel duty would cut fuel use by 3% in the long run."
Economist 2: "Fuel duty should not rise, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income on fuel."
Which classification is correct?

A fact inside a statement doesn't make its conclusion positive; the word "should" carries a value.

Mission 3

The four factors of production

  • Factors of production: the resources used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Enterprise: organising the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.

Mission 3

Economic agents and what they want

  • Households: own the factors of production and sell their services; buy goods and services. Usual aim: maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms: hire factors to produce goods and services. Usual aim: maximise profit; others include growth, market share, survival.
  • Government: taxes, spends, provides public services, makes laws. Aims include growth, jobs, stable prices and a fairer distribution of income.
  • Aims can conflict: a firm's profit vs the local environment; buyers' low prices vs workers' high wages.
Mission 3

The circular flow: everyone depends on everyone

  • Households sell factor services to firms. Firms pay factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit).
  • Households spend that income on goods and services. Their spending is firms' revenue.
  • Real flows (factors, goods) go one way; money flows go the other.
  • One agent's spending is another agent's income: interdependence.
  • Government joins the flow by taxing incomes and spending on services.

Mission 4

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
  • Governments aim to maximise social welfare (the well-being of society as a whole).
Mission 4

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.

  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

Mission 4

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".
Quick check

Mission 4

  • Marginal benefit (MB): extra benefit from one more unit of an activity. Marginal cost (MC): extra cost of that unit.
  • Rule: do one more unit while MB ≥ MC. Stop where MB = MC.

Mission 4

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: Mission 5.
Mission check

?A hiker has had no water all day on a hot trail and would pay a large amount for one bottle. At home, after drinking plenty, she would not pay even a small amount for one more bottle. Which explanation is best?

What we pay depends on how much one more unit adds, not on how much the good matters in total.

Mission 5

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 5

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 5

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
Mission 5

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 5

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 5

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?
Mission check

?Many workers say they should save more for retirement but do not start. In one scheme, workers sign up now to put part of each future pay rise into their pension; nothing leaves today's pay. Many more workers saved more under the scheme. Which bias does the design best get round?

People who mean well today often fail tomorrow; designs that commit the future self can close the gap.

Mission 6

Why aim for maximum profit?

  • Economic theory usually assumes firms maximise profit: output where MC = MR.
  • Reasons a firm might:
  • Owners take the risk and want the reward (dividends, a higher share price).
  • Retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Profit builds a cushion for bad years and makes lenders and investors keen.
  • High profit signals success and pays for growth.
  • Each economic agent has an objective: consumers maximise utility, firms are assumed to maximise profit, governments aim at social welfare.
Mission 6

Stakeholders pull in different directions

  • Stakeholder: anyone with an interest in what the firm does.
StakeholderWhat they may want
Shareholders (owners)High profit, dividends, rising share price
ManagersHigh pay, status, a bigger firm, an easy life
WorkersGood pay, secure jobs, safe conditions
CustomersLow prices, quality, good service
SuppliersSteady orders, prompt payment
Local community, governmentJobs, low pollution, taxes paid
  • Conflicts: higher pay for workers can mean lower dividends; lower prices for customers can mean lower profit.
  • The firm's objectives depend on who has the most power over it.
  • When owners do not run the firm, managers may pursue managerial utility: their own pay, status and perks.
Mission 6

Three targets on one diagram

  • Profit maximisation: MC = MR.
  • Sales revenue maximisation: MR = 0. The last unit adds nothing to TR, so TR is at its peak.
  • Sales (volume) maximisation: sell as many units as possible while still making normal profit: AR = AC.
  • Moving from profit max to revenue max to sales max: more output, a lower price, less profit.
  • Your course calls these sales revenue and sales volume maximisation.
  • Say: "revenue is maximised where MR = 0" — not: "where MC = MR".
Mission 6

Worked example: three outputs

  • Illustrative data. AR = 24 − Q, so MR = 24 − 2Q. TC = 42 + 0.5Q², so MC = Q.
  • Profit max: 24 − 2Q = Q → Q = 8, P = 16. TR = 128, TC = 74, profit = 54.
  • Revenue max: 24 − 2Q = 0 → Q = 12, P = 12. TR = 144 (the highest), profit = 30.
  • Sales max: AR = AC at Q = 14, P = 10. TR = TC = 140: normal profit only.
  • Each target gives up some profit for size.

Mission 6

Growth, market share, survival

  • Growth: a bigger firm can gain economies of scale and market power, and spreads its risks. Managers often gain pay and status from size.
  • Market share: a larger share of sales can bring power over price and a strong brand. Firms may cut prices now to win it.
  • Survival: the first aim for many new firms and in recessions. Cover costs, keep cash flowing.
  • Many of these can serve long-run profit while lowering profit today.
Mission 6

Satisficing

  • Satisficing: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit, not the maximum. The term comes from the economist Herbert Simon.
  • Why firms satisfice:
  • Owners and managers are different people. Managers need only enough profit to keep shareholders content.
  • Firms have several stakeholders to keep happy at once.
  • Information is limited. Finding the exact MC = MR point is costly.
  • Satisficing is a minimum target, not a lack of effort. Profit below it brings pressure for change.
Mission 6

CSR and social objectives

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a firm takes account of its impact on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
  • Examples: cutting emissions, fair supplier contracts, community projects, staff welfare.
  • Social enterprises and not-for-profit organisations put a social aim first.
  • Real case: in 2022 the founder of the outdoor clothing firm Patagonia transferred its ownership to a trust and a non-profit body. Profit not reinvested in the firm goes to fighting climate change.
  • For CSR: it can attract customers and staff, and protect the brand: long-run profit.
  • Against: Milton Friedman (1970) argued a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules; owners, not managers, should choose how to spend on good causes.
  • Some public and not-for-profit bodies aim at social welfare directly.
Quick check
?A new food-delivery firm sets its prices below its costs to sign up as many customers as possible. It expects to make losses for several years. Which objective best explains this?
Mission 6Evaluate

Do firms really maximise profit?

It depends on …

  • Who controls the firm: an owner-manager may maximise profit; managers of a large company with many small shareholders have more room to pursue other goals.
  • Competition: in fiercely competitive markets, firms that do not aim at maximum profit may not survive.
  • Time horizon: growth, market share and CSR can be routes to long-run profit, so they may not be alternatives at all.
  • Information: firms rarely know MC and MR exactly; rules of thumb can approximate maximum profit.
  • Judge: profit maximisation is a useful assumption for predicting behaviour, but real firms often balance several objectives.
Mission check

?The managers of a large hotel chain aim each year for a level of profit that keeps its shareholders content. Once they reach it, they do not push for more. They avoid price rises and cost cuts that would upset staff and regular guests, even though these would raise profit. Which objective best describes their behaviour?

A firm can earn less than the maximum on purpose when other goals, or other people, matter to whoever runs it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Positive statements can be tested (even if false); normative statements rest on value judgements.
  • Ceteris paribus: change one thing, hold the rest equal.

Mission 3

  • Four factors: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.
  • Rewards: rent, wages, interest, profit.
  • Households, firms and government depend on each other.

Mission 4

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.

Mission 5

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.

Mission 6

  • Theory assumes profit maximisation (MC = MR), but firms may aim for growth, market share, survival, satisfactory profit or social goals.
  • Revenue maximisation is at MR = 0 and sales maximisation at AR = AC: more output, a lower price, less profit.
  • Objectives depend on which stakeholders hold power, and stakeholders' aims often conflict.
