Mission 1

Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Firm: money spent on a new machine cannot also pay for an advert campaign.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.
Quick check
?On Saturday, Kai can work a café shift that pays 60 or a tutoring session that pays 45 (same currency, same hours, and he enjoys both equally). Instead, he goes to a festival with free entry. Measured in money, what is the opportunity cost of going to the festival?

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. A later lesson compares market, planned and mixed economies.
Quick check
?A government decides that hospital treatment will be given according to medical need, not ability to pay. Which basic economic question is this decision mainly answering?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is opportunity cost in practice?

It depends on:

  • Information: the next best alternative may be unknown or uncertain when the choice is made.
  • Valuation: many alternatives have no price (time, health, a clean environment), so they are hard to compare.
  • Time: some costs fall on future generations, as when a resource is over-used.
  • Whose cost: a firm's choice may impose costs on others (pollution) that it does not count.
  • Judgement: the idea always applies, but measuring it can be hard.
Mission check

?A city makes its buses free to ride. The buses are paid for from the city's tax revenue. Which statement about this choice is correct?

Scarce resources have other uses, so even a "free" service costs society the next best thing those resources could have made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why might a business choose to earn less profit than it could?

Make a guess: Why might a business choose to earn less profit than it could?

Mission 2

Why aim for maximum profit?

  • Economic theory usually assumes firms maximise profit: output where MC = MR.
  • Reasons a firm might:
  • Owners take the risk and want the reward (dividends, a higher share price).
  • Retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Profit builds a cushion for bad years and makes lenders and investors keen.
  • High profit signals success and pays for growth.
Mission 2

Stakeholders pull in different directions

  • Stakeholder: anyone with an interest in what the firm does.
StakeholderWhat they may want
Shareholders (owners)High profit, dividends, rising share price
ManagersHigh pay, status, a bigger firm, an easy life
WorkersGood pay, secure jobs, safe conditions
CustomersLow prices, quality, good service
SuppliersSteady orders, prompt payment
Local community, governmentJobs, low pollution, taxes paid
  • Conflicts: higher pay for workers can mean lower dividends; lower prices for customers can mean lower profit.
  • The firm's objectives depend on who has the most power over it.
Mission 2

Three targets on one diagram

  • Profit maximisation: MC = MR.
  • Sales revenue maximisation: MR = 0. The last unit adds nothing to TR, so TR is at its peak.
  • Sales (volume) maximisation: sell as many units as possible while still making normal profit: AR = AC.
  • Moving from profit max to revenue max to sales max: more output, a lower price, less profit.
  • Say: "revenue is maximised where MR = 0" — not: "where MC = MR".
Mission 2

Worked example: three outputs

  • Illustrative data. AR = 24 − Q, so MR = 24 − 2Q. TC = 42 + 0.5Q², so MC = Q.
  • Profit max: 24 − 2Q = Q → Q = 8, P = 16. TR = 128, TC = 74, profit = 54.
  • Revenue max: 24 − 2Q = 0 → Q = 12, P = 12. TR = 144 (the highest), profit = 30.
  • Sales max: AR = AC at Q = 14, P = 10. TR = TC = 140: normal profit only.
  • Each target gives up some profit for size.
Mission 2Graph lab

Change the target

  • Takeaway: revenue peaks where MR = 0 and sales volume peaks where AR = AC; both mean more output, a lower price and less profit.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a firm's cost and revenue diagram: MC = MR at an output of 30; MR = 0 at an output of 50; AR = AC at an output of 60; AC is lowest at an output of 40. The managers' bonuses depend on the firm's sales revenue. Which output will they choose?
Mission 2

Growth, market share, survival

  • Growth: a bigger firm can gain economies of scale and market power, and spreads its risks. Managers often gain pay and status from size.
  • Market share: a larger share of sales can bring power over price and a strong brand. Firms may cut prices now to win it.
  • Survival: the first aim for many new firms and in recessions. Cover costs, keep cash flowing.
  • Further objectives: cost efficiency, return on investment, employee welfare, customer satisfaction, social objectives.
  • Many of these can serve long-run profit while lowering profit today.
Mission 2

Satisficing

  • Satisficing: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit, not the maximum. The term comes from the economist Herbert Simon.
  • Why firms satisfice:
  • Owners and managers are different people. Managers need only enough profit to keep shareholders content.
  • Firms have several stakeholders to keep happy at once.
  • Information is limited. Finding the exact MC = MR point is costly.
  • Satisficing is a minimum target, not a lack of effort. Profit below it brings pressure for change.
Mission 2

CSR and social objectives

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a firm takes account of its impact on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
  • Examples: cutting emissions, fair supplier contracts, community projects, staff welfare.
  • Social enterprises and not-for-profit organisations put a social aim first.
  • Real case: in 2022 the founder of the outdoor clothing firm Patagonia transferred its ownership to a trust and a non-profit body. Profit not reinvested in the firm goes to fighting climate change.
  • For CSR: it can attract customers and staff, and protect the brand: long-run profit.
  • Against: Milton Friedman (1970) argued a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules; owners, not managers, should choose how to spend on good causes.
Quick check
?A new food-delivery firm sets its prices below its costs to sign up as many customers as possible. It expects to make losses for several years. Which objective best explains this?
Mission 2Evaluate

Do firms really maximise profit?

It depends on …

  • Who controls the firm: an owner-manager may maximise profit; managers of a large company with many small shareholders have more room to pursue other goals.
  • Competition: in fiercely competitive markets, firms that do not aim at maximum profit may not survive.
  • Time horizon: growth, market share and CSR can be routes to long-run profit, so they may not be alternatives at all.
  • Information: firms rarely know MC and MR exactly; rules of thumb can approximate maximum profit.
  • Judge: profit maximisation is a useful assumption for predicting behaviour, but real firms often balance several objectives.
Mission check

?The managers of a large hotel chain aim each year for a level of profit that keeps its shareholders content. Once they reach it, they do not push for more. They avoid price rises and cost cuts that would upset staff and regular guests, even though these would raise profit. Which objective best describes their behaviour?

A firm can earn less than the maximum on purpose when other goals, or other people, matter to whoever runs it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would someone risk their savings to start a business, and who else gets a say once it exists?

Make a guess: Why would someone risk their savings to start a business, and who else gets a say once it exists?

Mission 3

The entrepreneur

  • Four factors of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise.
  • The entrepreneur supplies enterprise. They:
  • spot an opportunity: a want that is not being met
  • organise land, labour and capital to produce
  • take decisions and bear the risk of loss
  • often innovate: a new product, process or way of selling.
  • The reward for enterprise is profit, which is never guaranteed. Other factors earn rent, wages and interest.
  • Say: "the entrepreneur bears the risk" — not: "the workers bear the risk": workers are paid wages even when the firm makes a loss.
Mission 3

Adding value

  • Added value = selling price − cost of bought-in materials and services.
  • Illustrative data: a café's coffee uses beans, milk and a cup costing 0.80. It sells for 3.20.
  • Added value per cup = 3.20 − 0.80 = 2.40. At 200 cups a day: 200 × 2.40 = 480 a day.
  • Added value is not profit: wages, rent and other costs are paid out of it.
  • Ways to add value: branding, convenience, quality, design, a unique selling point (USP).
  • Say: "added value = price − cost of bought-in inputs" — not: "price − all costs" (that is profit).
Mission 3

Creative destruction

  • Joseph Schumpeter (1942): entrepreneurs' innovations destroy old products, firms and jobs, and create new ones.
  • Real case: video rental chain Blockbuster had 9,094 stores at its peak in 2004.
  • It filed for bankruptcy in 2010, facing competition from Netflix's mail-order and streaming service, rental kiosks and video on demand.
  • Gains: new products, lower prices, new firms and jobs.
  • Losses: failing firms, lost jobs and skills that no longer match the new jobs.
  • Creative destruction is how the economy renews itself, but its costs fall on particular workers and places.
Quick check
?A mechanic quits her job, puts her own savings into the business, rents a workshop, buys tools and hires two staff to repair bicycles. Which statement best describes her role as an entrepreneur?
Mission 3

Why start a business?

  • Financial motive: profit, and the chance to build wealth.
  • Non-financial motives:
  • Independence: being your own boss; choosing how and when to work.
  • Ethics: running a business in line with your values, e.g. fair pay for suppliers, low environmental impact.
  • Social enterprise: a business whose main aim is social; it trades, and reinvests most profit in that aim.
  • Real case: The Big Issue (founded 1991 by John Bird and Gordon Roddick). People facing homelessness or poverty buy the magazine at a discount and sell it on, earning an income.
  • Say: "a social enterprise trades to meet a social aim" — not: "a social enterprise is a charity that gives things away".
Mission 3

Objectives beyond profit

  • Market share: the firm's sales as a % of total market sales.
  • Cost efficiency: producing at the lowest cost per unit.
  • Return on investment (ROI) = profit ÷ capital invested × 100.
  • Illustrative data: 50,000 invested; annual profit 6,000. ROI = 6,000 ÷ 50,000 × 100 = 12%.
  • Employee welfare and customer satisfaction: keeping staff and customers loyal.
  • Social objectives: aims for society or the environment, e.g. cutting emissions.
  • Satisficing: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit while keeping other groups content, rather than the maximum.
Mission 3

Stakeholders

  • Stakeholder: any person or group with an interest in, or affected by, what a firm does.
StakeholderUsually wants
Shareholders (owners)Profit, dividends, a rising share price
ManagersPay, status, a growing firm
EmployeesGood pay, secure jobs, safe conditions
CustomersLow prices, quality, good service
SuppliersRegular orders, prompt payment
LendersInterest paid, loans repaid
Local community, governmentJobs, low pollution, taxes paid
  • Internal stakeholders are part of the firm (owners, managers, employees); external ones are outside it.
Mission 3

When stakeholders clash

  • Illustrative case: a factory plans to automate its packing line, cutting 40 jobs and raising profit.
  • Shareholders gain. Employees lose. Customers may gain from lower prices. The local community loses jobs and spending.
  • Common conflicts: pay vs dividends, prices vs profit, expansion vs the local environment.
  • Whose aims win depends on power:
  • owners vote at meetings and can replace directors
  • employees can strike or leave; unions bargain
  • customers can switch to rivals or boycott
  • lenders set loan terms; governments regulate and tax.
  • Not every aim conflicts: well-paid, secure staff may work better, which helps profit in the long run.
Quick check
?A supermarket chain tells its suppliers that it will now pay invoices after 90 days instead of 30 days, which leaves the chain with more cash in hand. Which stakeholder conflict does this show most directly?
Mission 3

Corporate social responsibility

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a firm takes account of its effects on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
  • Examples: cutting emissions, paying suppliers fairly, staff training, community projects.
  • The case for: it can attract customers and staff, protect the firm's reputation and so raise long-run profit.
  • The case against: Milton Friedman (1970) argued that a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules; owners, not managers, should decide what good causes to fund.
  • Greenwashing: claiming more CSR than the firm actually does. Claims are hard for customers to check.
  • Say: "CSR goes beyond the law" — not: "CSR means obeying the law".
Mission 3Evaluate

Can a firm keep every stakeholder happy?

It depends on …

  • Time horizon: in the long run, satisfied staff and customers can raise profit, so aims may line up.
  • Power: a firm with one dominant shareholder may follow that owner's aims; a firm with many small shareholders gives managers more room.
  • Competition: fierce competition leaves less room for anything but low costs.
  • Information: it is hard to measure CSR and stakeholder satisfaction, so trade-offs are often hidden.
  • The law: minimum standards on pay, safety and pollution remove some conflicts for every firm.
  • Judge: firms usually balance stakeholders rather than satisfy them all; who wins depends on who holds power.
Mission check

?A bus company owned by shareholders plans to cut its loss-making rural routes. The local council, which grants the company its contract for town routes, objects. So do passengers and the drivers' union. The company keeps the routes in return for a small council payment, even though cutting them would have raised profit. What best explains the decision?

A firm's objectives are not set by its owners alone: stakeholders with power can change them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Theory assumes profit maximisation (MC = MR), but firms may aim for growth, market share, survival, satisfactory profit or social goals.
  • Revenue maximisation is at MR = 0 and sales maximisation at AR = AC: more output, a lower price, less profit.
  • Objectives depend on which stakeholders hold power, and stakeholders' aims often conflict.

Mission 3

  • The entrepreneur organises land, labour and capital, bears the risk and innovates; the reward is profit. Innovation brings creative destruction.
  • People start businesses for profit and for independence, ethics or a social aim; added value = price − cost of bought-in inputs.
  • Stakeholders' aims often conflict; which aims win depends on power. CSR has a case for and against.
