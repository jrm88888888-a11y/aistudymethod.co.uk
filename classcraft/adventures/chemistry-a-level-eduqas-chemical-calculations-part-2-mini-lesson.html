Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Make a guess: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Mission 1

Three ways to moles

  • From a mass: n = m ÷ M.
  • From a solution: n = c × V, with V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000).
  • From a gas: pV = nRT (p in Pa, V in m³, T in K, R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).
  • Back again: use the same equation, rearranged.
Mission 1

Worked example: mass to gas volume

  • Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g), acid in excess. What volume of H2 forms from 1.30 g of zinc?
  • n(Zn) = 1.30 ÷ 65.4 = 0.01988 mol (keep an extra figure until the end). Ratio 1 : 1, so n(H2) = 0.01988 mol.
  • At 293 K and 101 kPa: V = nRT ÷ p = 0.01988 × 8.31 × 293 ÷ 101 000 = 4.79 × 10⁻⁴ m³ = 0.479 dm³.
  • Mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → the quantity you want.
Mission 1

Carrying out a titration

  • A pipette (with a filler) puts a fixed volume of one solution into a conical flask. Add a few drops of indicator.
  • A burette adds the other solution, with swirling, over a white tile. Go dropwise near the colour change.
  • Read the burette at the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level, to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
  • The volume added is the titre. Do a rough titre, then repeat until titres are concordant: within 0.10 cm³.
  • Rinse burette and pipette with their own solutions; the flask with distilled water only.
Mission 1

Equivalence point and end point

  • Equivalence point: exactly enough of one solution has been added to react with all of the other, in the ratio in the equation.
  • End point: the point where the indicator changes colour. A suitable indicator changes colour at (or very close to) the equivalence point.
  • So the colour change tells you when the moles match the equation. The burette tells you how much was needed.
Mission 1

Choosing an indicator

  • Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in alkali.
  • Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in alkali; orange at the end point.
  • Strong acid + strong base: either indicator.
  • Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no sharp colour change, so no indicator is suitable.
Quick check
?Vinegar (ethanoic acid, a weak acid) is in the conical flask. Sodium hydroxide is added from the burette. Which indicator should be used, and what is the colour change at the end point?
Mission 1

Making a standard solution

  • A standard solution has an accurately known concentration.
  • Weigh the solid; then reweigh the empty weighing boat (weighing by difference).
  • Dissolve in a little distilled water in a beaker.
  • Pour into a volumetric flask with all the rinsings from the beaker and glass rod.
  • Make up to the mark with distilled water (bottom of the meniscus on the line). Stopper and invert to mix.
  • Standardising an acid: titrate it against a standard solution of a base (e.g. sodium carbonate) to find its exact concentration.
Mission 1

Worked example: a titration calculation

  • 25.0 cm³ of NaOH(aq) needs a mean titre of 21.40 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ H2SO4.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • Step 1, known solution: n(H2SO4) = 0.0500 × 0.02140 = 1.070 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 2, ratio: n(NaOH) = 2 × 1.070 × 10⁻³ = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 3, concentration: c(NaOH) = 2.140 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0856 mol dm⁻³.
Quick check
?A student records these titres: 24.30 cm³ (rough), 23.85 cm³, 23.40 cm³ and 23.50 cm³. Which mean titre should be used in the calculation?
Mission 1

Uncertainty in a titre

  • A burette reading has an uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³.
  • A titre uses two readings (final − initial), so its uncertainty is ±0.10 cm³.
  • Percentage uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ titre × 100.
  • For a 23.45 cm³ titre: 0.10 ÷ 23.45 × 100 = 0.43%.
  • A larger titre gives a smaller percentage uncertainty.
Quick check
?Now the mission: vinegar.
10.0 cm³ of vinegar is titrated with 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, using phenolphthalein. The mean titre is 16.60 cm³. CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l). What is the concentration of ethanoic acid in the vinegar, in mol dm⁻³?

The indicator turns pink once all the acid has reacted. The titre gives n(NaOH); the 1 : 1 ratio gives n(acid). That is about 50 g of acid per litre: typical of 5% vinegar.

Mission 1

Making a salt by titration

  • Pipette NaOH(aq) into a flask; titrate with HCl(aq) using an indicator. Record the titre.
  • Repeat with the same volumes but no indicator, so the salt is not contaminated.
  • Evaporate some of the water, then leave to crystallise.
  • Filter, and dry the crystals between filter papers.
  • The titration finds the exact volumes for neutralisation: no excess acid or alkali in the salt.
Quick check
?A student titrates 25.0 cm³ of a weak acid, HA, with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The student then titrates an identical 25.0 cm³ sample of HA to which 50.0 cm³ of distilled water has been added. How does the volume of NaOH needed to reach the equivalence point compare with the first titration, and why?
Mission check

?Limewater is a solution of calcium hydroxide. Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
25.0 cm³ of limewater is titrated with 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl, using phenolphthalein. The mean titre is 18.40 cm³. What is the concentration of calcium hydroxide, in mol dm⁻³?

As with the vinegar: the colour change marks when the moles match the equation; the burette gives the volume; the ratio does the rest.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Make a guess: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Mission 2

When you can't titrate directly

  • Some samples can't go straight into a titration: an insoluble solid (CaCO3, MgO), a slow reaction, or no sharp end point.
  • Back titration: add a known excess of a standard acid. The solid reacts completely.
  • Then titrate the leftover acid with a standard alkali.
  • Acid that reacted = acid added − acid left over.
Mission 2

Worked example: an indigestion tablet

  • A 1.250 g tablet (active ingredient CaCO3) is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The leftover acid needs 22.40 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Assume nothing else in the tablet reacts with acid.
  • Acid added: 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.02500 mol.
  • Acid left: n(NaOH) = 0.400 × 0.02240 = 0.00896 mol (HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1).
  • Acid reacted: 0.02500 − 0.00896 = 0.01604 mol.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.01604 ÷ 2 = 0.00802 mol; mass = 0.00802 × 100.1 = 0.803 g = 64.2% of the tablet.
Quick check
?In a back titration, an insoluble carbonate is added to excess hydrochloric acid, and the leftover acid is titrated with sodium hydroxide. Which amount equals the moles of HCl that reacted with the carbonate?
Mission 2

Dilutions and portions

  • A solution is made up to 250.0 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A 25.0 cm³ portion is pipetted out and titrated.
  • The portion holds one tenth of the total moles. Scale back up: × 10.
  • Diluting changes concentration, not moles: c1V1 = c2V2.
  • Track moles through every step. Scale at the right moment, and say what each number is the moles of.
Quick check
?10.0 cm³ of vinegar is diluted to 100.0 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A 25.0 cm³ portion needs 20.75 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O. What was the concentration of ethanoic acid in the original vinegar, in mol dm⁻³?
Calculate

Limestone purity

?1.25 g of powdered limestone is added to 50.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). When the fizzing stops, the mixture is made up to 250.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean titre of 27.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Calculate the percentage by mass of CaCO3 in the limestone. (Aᵣ: C 12.0, O 16.0, Ca 40.1)
Mission 2

Double titration

  • A mixture of NaOH and Na2CO3 is titrated with HCl(aq), using two indicators.
  • Phenolphthalein end point (titre V1): OH⁻ + H⁺ → H2O, and CO3²⁻ + H⁺ → HCO3⁻.
  • Add methyl orange and carry on. Extra titre V2: HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2O + CO2.
  • n(Na2CO3) = c(HCl) × V2. n(NaOH) = c(HCl) × (V1 − V2).
  • Example, 25.0 cm³ of mixture, 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl: V1 = 22.40 cm³, V2 = 8.60 cm³.
  • n(Na2CO3) = 8.60 × 10⁻⁴ mol (0.0344 mol dm⁻³); n(NaOH) = 1.38 × 10⁻³ mol (0.0552 mol dm⁻³).
Mission check

?An impure sample of magnesium oxide, an insoluble base, is analysed by back titration. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l). Assume the impurities do not react with acid.
  • 0.400 g of the sample is added to 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). The leftover acid needs 14.30 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. What is the percentage by mass of MgO in the sample? (Aᵣ: O 16.0, Mg 24.3)

You never have to dissolve the solid in water. Dissolve it in a measured excess of acid, titrate what is left, and the difference is what the solid used.

Mission 3: Mix 10 g of hydrogen with 10 g of oxygen. Which runs out first?

Make a guess: Mix 10 g of hydrogen with 10 g of oxygen. Which runs out first?

Mission 3

Limiting and excess

  • The limiting reactant is used up first, and the reaction stops.
  • So it fixes the maximum amount of product.
  • The other reactant is in excess: some is left over.
  • 2CO + O2 → 2CO2, starting with 6 CO and 4 O2: 6 CO2 form, and 1 O2 is left over.
Mission 3

Finding the limiting reactant

  • Convert every reactant to moles. Masses alone tell you nothing.
  • Divide each amount by its coefficient in the equation. The smallest result is the limiting reactant.
  • Calculate all products from the limiting reactant only.
  • Excess left over = amount present − amount that reacted.
Mission 3

Worked example: burning methane

  • CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l), with 8.00 g of CH4 and 24.0 g of O2.
  • n(CH4) = 8.00 ÷ 16.0 = 0.500 mol; ÷ 1 = 0.500.
  • n(O2) = 24.0 ÷ 32.0 = 0.750 mol; ÷ 2 = 0.375. Smaller, so O2 limits.
  • n(CO2) = 0.750 ÷ 2 = 0.375 mol; mass = 0.375 × 44.0 = 16.5 g at most.
  • CH4 left = 0.500 − 0.375 = 0.125 mol = 2.00 g.
  • The smaller mass (methane) was the one in excess.
Quick check
?Nitrogen and hydrogen react: N2(g) + 3H2(g) → 2NH3(g). A mixture contains 5.60 g of N2 and 1.50 g of H2. Which statement is correct? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, N 14.0)
Mission 3

Theoretical and experimental yield

  • Theoretical yield: the maximum mass of product, calculated from the limiting reactant.
  • Experimental (actual) yield: the mass of product actually obtained. It is normally less.
  • Percentage yield = experimental yield ÷ theoretical yield × 100.
  • Why less: incomplete or reversible reaction, side reactions, product lost in transfer and purification.
  • Above 100%? The product is impure, for example still wet.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the theoretical yield of a product in a reaction?
Mission 3

Worked example: percentage yield

  • CuO(s) + H2SO4(aq) → CuSO4(aq) + H2O(l); crystallising gives CuSO4·5H2O.
  • 4.00 g CuO: n = 4.00 ÷ 79.5 = 0.0503 mol. 40.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ acid: n = 0.0400 mol.
  • Ratio 1 : 1, so H2SO4 limits. The excess CuO is filtered off.
  • Theoretical yield: 0.0400 mol × 249.6 g mol⁻¹ = 9.98 g of CuSO4·5H2O.
  • 7.50 g collected: percentage yield = 7.50 ÷ 9.984 × 100 = 75.1%.
Calculate
?Copper(II) oxide is reduced by hydrogen: CuO(s) + H2(g) → Cu(s) + H2O(g). With hydrogen in excess, 3.98 g of CuO gives 2.85 g of copper. Calculate the percentage yield. (Aᵣ: O 16.0, Cu 63.5)
Mission check

?Thermite is a mixture of aluminium and iron(III) oxide. 2Al(s) + Fe2O3(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). A mixture of 10.0 g of Al and 10.0 g of Fe2O3 reacts, and 5.60 g of iron is collected. What is the percentage yield of iron? (Molar masses: Al 27.0, Fe2O3 159.6, Fe 55.8 g mol⁻¹)

Now the hydrogen. 10 g of H2 is about 5 mol; 10 g of O2 is only about 0.31 mol, which needs just 0.63 mol of H2. Oxygen runs out first, and most of the hydrogen is left over.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does the same balance give a less trustworthy result when you weigh a tiny amount?

Make a guess: Why does the same balance give a less trustworthy result when you weigh a tiny amount?

Mission 4

Every reading has an uncertainty

  • No measurement is exact: the uncertainty is the range either side of the reading where the true value probably lies.
  • Scale with marks: usually ± half the smallest division. Burette marks every 0.10 cm³ → ±0.05 cm³ per reading.
  • Digital display: usually ± half the last digit (or the value printed on the instrument). Balance to 0.01 g → ±0.005 g.
  • Exam questions often give you the uncertainty: use it.
Mission 4

Percentage uncertainty

  • % uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100.
  • A mass "by difference" uses two readings: ±0.005 + ±0.005 = ±0.01 g.
  • Weigh out 5.00 g: 0.01 ÷ 5.00 × 100 = 0.2%.
  • Weigh out 0.10 g: 0.01 ÷ 0.10 × 100 = 10%.
  • Same balance, same ±0.01 g: the smaller the quantity, the bigger the % uncertainty.
  • Your board may call this the percentage error in a measurement.
Mission 4

Worked example: a titre

  • Burette: start 1.20 cm³, end 24.65 cm³.
  • Titre = 24.65 − 1.20 = 23.45 cm³.
  • Two readings, each ±0.05 cm³ → titre ±0.10 cm³.
  • % uncertainty = 0.10 ÷ 23.45 × 100 = 0.43%.
  • A 10 cm³ titre would carry 1.0%: bigger titres are better.
Mission 4

Combining and minimising uncertainty

  • Multiplying or dividing measurements? Add their % uncertainties.
  • Example: 1.25 g (±0.01 g) made up to 250.0 cm³ (flask ±0.3 cm³).
  • Mass: 0.01 ÷ 1.25 × 100 = 0.80%. Volume: 0.3 ÷ 250.0 × 100 = 0.12%.
  • Concentration: 0.80 + 0.12 = 0.92%.
  • To minimise % uncertainty: measure larger quantities (more mass, bigger titres, bigger temperature changes) or use more precise apparatus.
Quick check
?A burette reading has an uncertainty of ±0.05 cm³. A student's titre is 12.50 cm³, found from two readings. What is the percentage uncertainty in the titre?
Mission 4

Accuracy, precision and errors

  • Accurate: close to the true value.
  • Precise: repeat readings close to each other.
  • Repeatable: same method, same person, similar results. Reproducible: different people or methods, similar results.
  • Random error: readings scatter either way. Repeats and a mean reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error: every reading shifted the same way (e.g. heat lost to the air, reading the top of the meniscus). Repeats do not remove it: change the method.
Mission 4

How far from the true value?

  • Compare your result with the accepted (data-book) value.
  • % difference = |your value − accepted value| ÷ accepted value × 100.
  • Example: ΔcH⦵ of ethanol measured as −1020 kJ mol⁻¹; data value −1367 kJ mol⁻¹: 25% out.
  • The apparatus uncertainties add to only about 3%, so most of the gap is systematic: heat lost to the air, incomplete combustion.
Mission 4

Concordant titres, means and anomalies

  • Titres (cm³): rough 24.00 · 23.30 · 23.75 · 23.40 · 23.35.
  • Concordant: within 0.10 cm³ of each other: 23.30, 23.40, 23.35.
  • Leave out the rough titre and the anomaly (23.75).
  • Mean titre = (23.30 + 23.40 + 23.35) ÷ 3 = 23.35 cm³.
  • On a graph, an anomaly is a point far off the line: repeat it, or leave it out and say why.
Mission 4

Significant figures

  • × and ÷: give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the data value with the fewest.
  • Keep every digit until the final step: round only once.
  • Burette readings: 2 decimal places, last digit 0 or 5 (e.g. 23.45 cm³).
  • Too many figures claims precision you don't have; too few throws precision away.
Quick check
?A student dissolves 2.5 g of NaCl (M = 58.5 g mol⁻¹) in water and makes the solution up to 250.0 cm³. Which value of the concentration, in mol dm⁻³, is given to the appropriate number of significant figures?
Mission 4

Drawing and reading graphs

  • Independent variable on the x-axis; label each axis with quantity and unit.
  • Label style: quantity / unit, e.g. volume / cm³.
  • Scales: use most of the grid, in easy steps.
  • Line of best fit: straight or a smooth curve, points scattered either side; ignore anomalies.
  • Gradient = Δy ÷ Δx from a large triangle, with units. Intercept: where the line crosses the y-axis.
Quick check
?A student plots the volume of gas collected against time. The line of best fit is straight, but does not pass through the origin. It passes through (10 s, 14 cm³) and (50 s, 62 cm³). What is the gradient, in cm³ s⁻¹?
Mission check

?A student mixes two solutions in a polystyrene cup to find an enthalpy change. The thermometer has an uncertainty of ±0.5 °C per reading, and the temperature rises by only about 4 °C. Which change would most reduce the percentage uncertainty in the temperature change, ΔT?

Same balance or thermometer, same absolute uncertainty. Make the quantity you measure bigger, and the uncertainty becomes a smaller share of it.

Mission 5

Choosing glassware

  • Volumetric pipette (one mark): delivers one fixed volume with a very small uncertainty.
  • Volumetric flask (one mark): contains one exact volume: for making solutions.
  • Burette (marked all the way): delivers any volume, read to small divisions.
  • Measuring cylinder: quick and approximate.
  • One mark = one job, done precisely. Many marks = flexible, less precise per reading.
  • Typical uncertainties: 25 cm³ pipette ±0.06 cm³; 250 cm³ flask ±0.3 cm³; 100 cm³ measuring cylinder ±1.0 cm³ (class B).
Mission 5

Titration technique

  • Rinse the pipette and burette with the solutions they will hold (not water).
  • Pipette: use a pipette filler; fill until the bottom of the meniscus sits on the mark, at eye level. Let it drain; touch the tip to the liquid surface; don't blow out the last drop.
  • Burette: remove the funnel; run liquid through the tap to clear air bubbles; read the bottom of the meniscus at eye level.
  • Conical flask on a white tile; add a few drops of indicator; swirl.
  • Add dropwise near the end point. Do a rough titration, then repeat until titres are concordant.
  • Record burette readings to 2 decimal places (ending in 0 or 5).
Mission 5

Making a standard solution

  • Weigh the solid by difference: weigh the container full, tip the solid into a beaker, reweigh the container.
  • Dissolve in a little distilled water in the beaker.
  • Pour into a volumetric flask through a funnel; rinse the beaker and funnel into the flask.
  • Add distilled water until the bottom of the meniscus is on the mark: the last part dropwise, at eye level.
  • Stopper and invert several times to mix.
Quick check
?A student needs to add exactly the same volume of sodium hydroxide solution to each of four conical flasks, ready for titration. Which piece of apparatus should the student use?
Mission 5

Heating safely and evenly

  • Water bath: gentle, even heating below 100 °C; a thermostatic bath holds a set temperature.
  • Electric heater (heating mantle or hotplate): no flame, so use it for flammable liquids.
  • Sand bath: even heating to higher temperatures.
  • Bunsen burner: never near flammable organic liquids.
  • Heating for a long time without losing volatile liquids: reflux, with a condenser.
  • Filtration: gravity filtration through filter paper keeps the solid back and lets the filtrate through; filtration under reduced pressure (Büchner funnel) is faster for collecting a solid.
Mission 5

Measuring gas, mass, time and temperature

  • Gas syringe: collects gas directly; good for gases that dissolve in water.
  • Over water: gas pushes water out of an upturned burette or measuring cylinder. Not for very soluble gases (e.g. NH3, HCl).
  • Mass loss: flask on a balance; the gas escapes; a cotton wool plug stops spray.
  • Time with a stopwatch; temperature with a thermometer or a temperature probe.
Mission 5

pH meter and pH probe

  • Calibrate first with buffer solutions of known pH (e.g. pH 4.00 and 7.00).
  • Rinse the electrode with distilled water between solutions.
  • Stir, wait for a steady reading, record it.
  • pH-probe titration: add one solution in small portions; record pH after each; plot pH against volume added.
  • Weak acid + weak base (e.g. ethanoic acid + ammonia): the pH changes only gradually at the end point, so no indicator changes sharply. The probe records the whole curve; the end point is where pH changes fastest.
Mission 5

Hazards and control measures

  • Hazard: what could cause harm. Risk: the chance of harm, given how you use it.
  • Corrosive / irritant: eye protection, gloves; clean up spills at once.
  • Flammable: no naked flames; water bath or electric heater.
  • Toxic (or harmful vapour): smallest amounts, fume cupboard.
  • Read the hazard data first (e.g. a safety data sheet), then choose a control measure for each hazard.
Quick check
?A student must heat a reaction mixture containing ethanol (highly flammable, boiling point 78 °C) for 30 minutes. Which plan best controls the risk?
Mission 5

Planning an investigation

  • Independent variable: what you change. Dependent variable: what you measure.
  • Control variables: what you keep the same, and how (e.g. a water bath for temperature).
  • Choose a sensible range and number of values; plan repeats.
  • Write numbered steps, with apparatus chosen for the precision needed.
  • Identifying an organic compound: plan tests in a logical order, each one narrowing the options (e.g. 2,4-DNPH to show C=O, then Tollens' to tell an aldehyde from a ketone).
Mission 5

Evaluating a method

  • Find the limitation: which step causes error, and in which direction?
  • Systematic? Fix the method; repeats won't help.
  • Random? Repeat and average, or use more precise apparatus.
  • Gas lost before the bung goes in → keep the reactants apart until sealed (e.g. a small tube of acid inside the flask, then tip).
  • Heat lost from a cup → lid and insulation, or extrapolate a cooling curve.
  • Small readings → measure larger quantities, to cut % uncertainty (see Mission 4).
  • Each improvement must match a specific limitation.
Quick check
?A student reacts pure calcium carbonate chips with excess hydrochloric acid and collects the carbon dioxide over water in an upturned measuring cylinder. The acid is tipped in from a small tube only after the bung is fitted. In every repeat, the volume of gas is lower than calculated. Which improvement deals with the most likely cause?
Mission check

?Before a redox titration, a student must add about 10 cm³ of dilute sulfuric acid to each conical flask. The acid is in excess, and its exact volume does not affect the result. Which apparatus is the best choice?

Match the apparatus to the job. One-mark glassware for exact volumes that matter; a cylinder when "about" is enough.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Moles from a mass, a solution or a gas volume, then the mole ratio, then back.
  • The indicator changes colour at the end point, which a suitable indicator makes match the equivalence point.
  • Use the mean of concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³); never the rough titre.

Mission 2

  • Back titration: acid reacted = acid added − acid left over; then use the equation's ratio.
  • With dilutions and portions, track moles step by step and scale (× 10 for a 25 of 250 portion).
  • Double titration: V2 (methyl orange stage) gives Na2CO3; V1 − V2 gives NaOH.

Mission 3

  • Moles ÷ coefficient: the smallest is the limiting reactant. It fixes the theoretical yield.
  • Excess left = amount present − amount reacted.
  • Percentage yield = experimental ÷ theoretical × 100.

Mission 4

  • % uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ value × 100; two readings → double the uncertainty; multiply or divide → add % uncertainties.
  • Repeats reduce random error; only a better method removes systematic error.

Mission 5

  • Volumetric pipette and flask: one exact volume. Burette: precise variable volume. Cylinder: quick and approximate.
  • Control each hazard with a measure that fits it; fix systematic errors by changing the method.
