Coordinate geometry · straight lines

Gradients and equations of lines

  • Gradient between (x1​,y1​)(x₁, y₁) and (x2​,y2​)(x₂, y₂): m=x2​−x1​y2​−y1​​m=(y₂−y₁)/(x₂−x₁)
  • Line with gradient mm through (x1​,y1​)(x₁, y₁): y−y1​=m(x−x1​)y−y₁=m(x−x₁)
  • General form ax+by+c=0ax+by+c=0, usually with integers aa, bb, cc. Rearrange to y=mx+cy=mx+c to read off the gradient.
  • Points AA, BB, CC are collinear (on one line) exactly when the gradient of ABAB equals the gradient of BCBC.

Parallel, perpendicular and models

  • Parallel lines have equal gradients: m1​=m2​m₁=m₂.
  • Perpendicular lines: m1​m2​=−1m₁m₂=−1, so m2​=−m1​1​m₂=−1/m₁.
  • Midpoint: (2x1​+x2​​,2y1​+y2​​)((x₁+x₂)/2, (y₁+y₂)/2). Distance: (x2​−x1​)2+(y2​−y1​)2​√((x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²). Where two lines meet: solve their equations simultaneously.

Coordinate geometry · circles

The equation of a circle

  • A point (x,y)(x, y) is on the circle with centre (a,b)(a, b) and radius rr exactly when its distance from the centre is rr.
  • Pythagoras on the horizontal and vertical gaps gives
(x−a)2+(y−b)2=r2(x−a)²+(y−b)²=r²
  • Read the centre with the signs reversed: (x−2)2+(y+1)2=9(x−2)²+(y+1)²=9 has centre (2,−1)(2, −1) and radius 9​=3√9=3.
  • The right-hand side is r2r², so take its square root for the radius.
Coordinate geometry · circles

Completing the square to find the centre

  • Expanded, a circle looks like x2+y2+Dx+Ey+C=0x²+y²+Dx+Ey+C=0, with equal coefficients of x2x² and y2y² and no xyxy term.
  • Complete the square in xx and in yy separately: x2+Dx=(x+2D​)2−4D2​x²+Dx=(x+D/2)²−D²/4.
  • Move the constants to the right; the right-hand side is r2r². It must be positive for a real circle.
  • You should also be familiar with the general form x2+y2+2fx+2gy+c=0x²+y²+2fx+2gy+c=0: centre (−f,−g)(−f, −g), radius f2+g2−c​√(f²+g²−c). (Edexcel's letters; many textbooks swap them: 2gx+2fy2gx+2fy, centre (−g,−f)(−g, −f).)

Centre and radius from the expanded form

Question
Find the centre and radius of the circle x2+y2+8x−10y+5=0x²+y²+8x−10y+5=0.
  1. 1
    Group the xx terms and the yy terms: (x2+8x)+(y2−10y)+5=0(x²+8x)+(y²−10y)+5=0
  2. 2
    Complete each square: (x+4)2−16+(y−5)2−25+5=0(x+4)²−16+(y−5)²−25+5=0
  3. 3
    Move the constants across: (x+4)2+(y−5)2=36(x+4)²+(y−5)²=36
  4. 4
    Read off, reversing the signs: centre (−4,5)(−4, 5), r=36​=6r=√36=6
Answer
centre (−4,5)(−4, 5), radius 6
⚠️ Watch out
subtract the square of half the coefficient (16 and 25) inside the bracket step, then move every constant to the right before reading r2r².

Coordinate geometry · circles

Three circle facts in coordinates

  • Tangent ⟂ radius: if the radius to a point has gradient mm, the tangent there has gradient −m1​−1/m.
  • The perpendicular from the centre to a chord bisects the chord, so the perpendicular bisector of any chord passes through the centre.
  • The angle in a semicircle is a right angle: if ABAB is a diameter and CC is on the circle, AC⊥BCAC ⊥ BC. Conversely, a right angle at CC makes ABAB a diameter of the circumcircle of triangle ABCABC.

Coordinate geometry · circles

Does a line meet a circle?

  • Substitute the line into the circle equation to get a quadratic in one variable.
  • Use the discriminant b2−4acb²−4ac of that quadratic: >0>0 two intersection points, =0=0 the line is a tangent, <0<0 no intersection.
  • Solve the quadratic to find the actual points when they exist.

Coordinate geometry · parametric equations

Curves drawn by a parameter (A-level only)

  • In parametric equations both xx and yy are given in terms of a third variable, the parameter (often tt or θθ).
  • Each value of tt gives one point. For x=t+1x=t+1, y=t2y=t²:
tt−2−2−1−1001122
(x,y)(x, y)(−1,4)(−1, 4)(0,1)(0, 1)(1,0)(1, 0)(2,1)(2, 1)(3,4)(3, 4)
  • Plot the points in order of tt and join them; an arrow shows the direction of increasing tt.
  • A restricted domain of tt draws only part of a curve.
Coordinate geometry · parametric equations

Converting to a Cartesian equation

  • Eliminate the parameter to get one equation in xx and yy.
  • Algebraic parameters: make tt the subject of the simpler equation and substitute. From x=t+1x=t+1, t=x−1t=x−1, so y=(x−1)2y=(x−1)².
  • Trigonometric parameters: use an identity such as sin2t+cos2t=1sin² t+cos² t=1 or the double-angle formula cos2t=1−2sin2tcos 2t=1−2sin² t (see the Trigonometry lesson).
  • Carry the domain across: if t≥0t ≥ 0 and x=t+1x=t+1, the Cartesian curve only applies for x≥1x ≥ 1.

Circles from trig parameters

Recap

Big ideas

  • Gradient =change in xchange in y​=(change in y)/(change in x); a line is y−y1​=m(x−x1​)y−y₁=m(x−x₁) or ax+by+c=0ax+by+c=0.
  • Parallel: equal gradients. Perpendicular: m1​m2​=−1m₁m₂=−1. Collinear: equal gradients through a shared point.
  • In a straight-line model, the gradient is the rate of change and the intercept is the starting value.
  • A circle with centre (a,b)(a, b) and radius rr is (x−a)2+(y−b)2=r2(x−a)²+(y−b)²=r²; complete the square to get there from the expanded form.
  • Tangent ⟂ radius, the perpendicular from the centre bisects a chord, and the angle in a semicircle is 90∘90°: in coordinates these are gradient facts (m1​m2​=−1m₁m₂=−1) and midpoints.
  • Substitute a line into a circle and use the discriminant: two points, tangent, or no meeting.
  • Parametric equations give xx and yy separately in terms of a parameter; each value of tt is one point, and the domain of tt fixes which part of the curve is drawn.
  • Eliminate the parameter by substitution or with a trig identity to get the Cartesian equation, and carry the domain across.
  • In models the parameter is usually time, so parametric form keeps "when" as well as "where".
