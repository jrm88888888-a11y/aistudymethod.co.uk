Paper 2: 19th-Century Novel & Poetry since 1789

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of Pearson Edexcel GCSE English Literature Paper 2 (1ET0/02): how to analyse a printed extract and link it to your whole 19th-century novel, how to compare an anthology poem with a poem you choose, how to compare two unseen poems, and how to hit the Assessment Objectives AO1, AO2 and AO3. It drills terminology, quotations, comparison and technique — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Two sections: a novel and poetry

Paper 2 is a 2 hour 15 minute exam worth 80 marks — that's 50% of your GCSE. It is closed book, and there are no marks for spelling, punctuation and grammar on this paper. You answer across two sections:

  • Section A — 19th-century Novel (40 marks): TWO parts on ONE studied novel. Part (a): analyse a printed extract. Part (b): write about the whole novel on the same theme, from memory. Rewards AO1 and AO2 (context is not assessed here).
  • Section B — Poetry since 1789 (40 marks): TWO parts. Part 1 — compare a named anthology poem (printed) with a second anthology poem you choose (AO2 + AO3). Part 2 — compare two unseen poems, both printed (AO1 + AO2).

Set texts differ by school: your 19th-century novel might be Jane Eyre, Great Expectations, Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, A Christmas Carol, Pride and Prejudice, Silas Marner or Frankenstein. Your anthology poems come from one collection — Relationships, Conflict or Time and Place. The skills here work for any of them.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark is tied to an Assessment Objective (AO). On Paper 2 the mix changes by task — Section A rewards AO1 and AO2; the anthology poetry task rewards AO2 and AO3; the unseen task rewards AO1 and AO2 (no context):

Watch the AO map: the novel section has no AO3 (context) marks. The anthology poetry comparison does reward AO3. The unseen poetry comparison rewards AO1 and AO2 — your comparative response and the methods — but no context. So know which AOs are "live" before you write.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader:

method + evidence → effect on the readere.g. "the enjambment across the line (method) makes the emotion spill over uncontrollably (effect)"

Useful methods to spot in prose & poetry: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, symbolism, pathetic fallacy, semantic field, enjambment, caesura, volta, sibilance, alliteration, rhyme, rhythm, dramatic monologue, narrative voice, structure and form.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a stanza

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not a set poem) annotated the way you'd mark up a real poem in the exam:

The river slips its silver leash and runs,
restless, restless, past the sleeping town;
it will not stop — it cannot — for the sea
that swallows every name we ever knew.
  • "slips its silver leash" — metaphor: the river is a freed animal, an image of uncontrollable release.
  • "restless, restless" — repetition + sibilance: the soft 's' hush mimics ceaseless flowing motion.
  • "it cannot" — caesura (the dash) forces a jolt, stressing the river's helplessness.
  • "swallows every name" — personification: time/the sea erases memory, a note of loss.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your poetry paragraphs need.

Section B skill · comparison

Comparing poems well

Both poetry tasks are comparisons. Marks come from comparing ideas and methods across the poems, not from writing about one then the other. Compare across theme, language, form & structure and tone, and signpost with comparative connectives:

  • Similarity: similarly, likewise, both, in the same way.
  • Difference: whereas, however, by contrast, unlike, while.

Integrated beats block: an integrated comparison weaves both poems together in each paragraph ("Poem A does X, whereas Poem B…"). A block answer (all of A, then all of B) makes it hard to compare and usually scores lower. Aim to compare in every paragraph.

Section A structure

The novel: extract → whole

Section A has two linked parts on the same theme. Part (a) analyses the printed extract; part (b) ranges across the whole novel from memory:

Argument skill

Answer with a thesis, not a plot summary

Open with a thesis — a clear line of argument that answers the exact question about the writer's method and purpose. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "How does the writer present fear in this extract and the novel as a whole?"

Thesis: "The writer presents fear as something that spreads and distorts perception, using unsettling imagery and a claustrophobic setting to trap both character and reader in a mounting sense of dread."

Why it works: it takes a position (fear spreads and distorts), names methods (imagery, setting) and points to a purpose/effect (dread). Now every paragraph proves one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Because Paper 2 is closed book (only the extract and the printed poems are given), learn a bank of short, flexible quotations for your novel and anthology poems. Weave them into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The mood is dark. The quote is: 'the shadows gathered thick and cold across the silent, empty, waiting room.'"

✔ "The cluster of adjectives — 'thick', 'cold', 'waiting' — makes the shadows feel like a physical, threatening presence."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3

Context serves the analysis

Context (AO3) is rewarded in the anthology poetry comparison — but only if it explains a choice in the poem. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture. (Remember: no AO3 on the novel or the unseen poems.)

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "The First World War happened between 1914 and 1918. Many soldiers died. Now back to the poem."

✔ "Written amid the horrors of trench warfare, the poem's shattered, broken rhythm mirrors the collapse of order the poet witnessed — sharpening its protest."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or idea beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

Split your 2h 15m: roughly 55 minutes on the novel (both parts), 35 on the anthology comparison and 35 on the unseen comparison, leaving a little reading time. Spend the first few minutes of each planning:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the theme + the command word "how").
  • Read the extract/poems twice, annotating 3–4 methods and their effects.
  • For poetry, choose your comparison points — theme, language, form/structure — before you start.
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. No SpaG marks on this paper, so spend the time on analysis.

Closed book: only the extract and the printed poems are given — the rest of the novel is not, and you choose the second anthology poem from memory. Learn a bank of short, flexible quotations in advance.

Worked model · Section B

A comparison paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two poems, integrating method and effect with a comparative connective:

Model paragraph (generic)

"Both poems explore how the natural world outlasts us, but they strike very different tones. The first poem's gentle enjambment lets the image of the river 'spill' calmly across the lines, so the reader feels a soothing sense of continuity. By contrast, the second poem fractures its lines with abrupt caesurae, and the harsh alliteration of 'cold, cracking cliffs' makes nature feel indifferent and threatening rather than comforting. Where the first finds peace in permanence, the second finds only our smallness."

Spot the moves: shared idea named, then method + effect compared in each poem, linked by a connective ("by contrast") — an integrated AO2 comparison.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Not comparing: writing about one poem then the other (block) instead of integrating the comparison.
  • Wrong AOs: pasting context onto the unseen poems or the novel, where AO3 earns nothing.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • Plot/poem retelling: paraphrasing the story instead of arguing an answer.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should either make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and in poetry, keep comparing the two poems as you go.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Paper 2: A 19th-century novel (40, extract + whole) · B Poetry since 1789 (40) — closed book, 50% of GCSE, no SpaG marks

AO map: novel = AO1+AO2 · anthology poetry = AO2+AO3 · unseen poetry = AO1+AO2

AO1: argue a thesis, support with short quotes

AO2: method + terminology + evidence → effect on reader

AO3: weave context into the anthology comparison only
