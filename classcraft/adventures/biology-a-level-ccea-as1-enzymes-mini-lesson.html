Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Make a guess: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Mission 1

Enzymes are catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst.
  • A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
  • It is not used up: it leaves the reaction unchanged, so one molecule catalyses the same reaction again and again.
  • The reaction can happen without the enzyme, but at body temperature it would be far too slow.
  • Benefit: reactions in cells run fast enough to sustain life.
  • Say: "speeds up a reaction" — not: "makes a reaction possible".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how an enzyme acts as a catalyst in a cell?
Mission 1

Metabolism: a web of reactions

  • Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism — a network of linked pathways.
  • Almost every step is catalysed by its own enzyme. So an organism needs thousands of different enzymes.
  • Control the enzyme → control the step. That is how cells regulate metabolism.
Mission 1

Lowering the energy barrier

  • To react, bonds in the substrate must first be broken. That needs energy: the activation energy.
  • An enzyme gives the reaction a route with a lower activation energy.
  • At the same temperature, more substrate molecules now have enough energy to react. So the rate rises.
  • The energy of the substrate and of the products is unchanged. So is the energy released overall.
  • The enzyme supplies no energy.
  • Say: "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" — not: "the enzyme provides the activation energy".
Mission 1

Worked example: reading an energy diagram

Illustrative values, read from an energy diagram:

LevelWithout enzymeWith enzyme
Substrate5050
Peak11075
Products2020
  • Energies in kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Activation energy = peak − substrate level. Not the peak value read from the axis.
  • Without enzyme: 110 − 50 = 60. With enzyme: 75 − 50 = 25.
  • Energy released = substrate − products = 50 − 20 = 30, with or without the enzyme.
Quick check
?In an energy diagram for a reaction, the substrate is at 40 kJ mol⁻¹ and the products are at 15 kJ mol⁻¹. The highest energy reached during the reaction is 90 kJ mol⁻¹ without an enzyme and 60 kJ mol⁻¹ with the enzyme. For the enzyme-catalysed reaction, what are the activation energy and the overall energy yield?
Mission 1

The active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: one or more polypeptide chains folded into a compact 3D shape (tertiary structure).
  • The active site is a small pocket or groove where the substrate binds.
  • Hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges and hydrophobic interactions between R groups fix the shape of the active site.
  • The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site; its charged groups are attracted to opposite charges there.
  • Substrate + active site → enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme free again.
  • Say: "complementary shape" — not: "the same shape".
Mission 1

Specificity

  • Each enzyme catalyses one reaction, or a few very similar ones: specificity.
  • Reason: only a molecule whose shape and charges suit the active site can bind.
  • Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Amylase hydrolyses starch but not cellulose.
  • The glucose units are linked differently (α-glucose in starch, β-glucose in cellulose), so cellulose does not fit amylase's active site.
  • Size alone does not decide: many small molecules do not bind.
  • Say: "the active site is complementary to one substrate" — not: "any molecule small enough fits".
Quick check
?Lipase catalyses the hydrolysis of triglycerides but has no effect on starch. What is the reason?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical bacterium, enzyme E catalyses the conversion of compound A to compound B. The conversion of A to B releases energy. A mutation stops the bacterium from making enzyme E. No other enzyme in the bacterium acts on compound A. Which prediction about compound B in the mutant bacterium is best supported?

An enzyme changes the route, not the destination: a lower barrier, the same start and finish, and the enzyme itself unchanged.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Make a guess: Is an enzyme a rigid lock waiting for one key, or does it change shape as its target arrives?

Mission 2

Meeting by chance

  • Molecules in solution are in constant, random motion.
  • A reaction can only happen when a substrate collides with the active site, the right way round.
  • Enzymes do not seek, pull or grab their substrates.
  • More collisions per second → more enzyme–substrate complexes → faster rate (used again in Mission 3).
  • Say: "substrate and active site collide by random motion" — not: "the enzyme attracts its substrate".
Mission 2

Model 1: lock and key

  • Proposed by Emil Fischer, 1894.
  • The active site is a rigid shape. Only a substrate of exactly complementary shape fits, like one key in one lock.
  • Strength: explains specificity.
  • Limits: a rigid site does not explain how bound substrate is strained into reacting; and later evidence showed many enzymes change shape when they bind.
Mission 2

Model 2: induced fit

  • Proposed by Daniel Koshland, 1958.
  • The active site is flexible. As the substrate binds, the active site changes shape and moulds closely around it.
  • Both the enzyme and the substrate change shape as they bind.
  • The tight fit strains bonds in the substrate, which lowers the activation energy.
  • Enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme returns to its original shape.
  • Say: "the active site changes shape as the substrate binds" — not: "the active site is a rigid exact match".
Quick check
?According to the induced-fit model, what happens when a substrate binds to the active site of an enzyme?
Mission check

?Hexokinase is the enzyme that adds a phosphate group to glucose. X-ray structures show that the two halves of the enzyme are further apart when no glucose is bound. When glucose binds, the two halves close around it. What does this evidence show?

Not a rigid lock: the enzyme and its substrate adjust to each other, and that close, strained fit is part of what speeds the reaction.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does warming an enzyme speed it up, until suddenly it hardly works at all?

Make a guess: Why does warming an enzyme speed it up, until suddenly it hardly works at all?

Mission 3

Measuring the rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time.
  • Measure product appearing (e.g. oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearing (e.g. starch, with iodine).
  • A product–time curve is steep at first, then flattens as substrate is used up.
  • Initial rate: the gradient at time zero, before the substrate runs down. The fairest way to compare conditions.
  • Timing a fixed end point (a colour change, a disc rising)? Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
Mission 3

Worked example: initial rate from a tangent

Illustrative data: oxygen released by catalase, plotted against time.

1. Draw a straight line touching the curve at t = 0: the tangent.

2. Pick two points far apart on the tangent: (0 s, 0) and (40 s, 36).

3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 36 ÷ 40.

  • Initial rate = 0.90 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Same method at any point on a curve: tangent there, then gradient.
  • Fixed end point: a disc took 25 s to rise. Rate = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was recorded: 0 s, 0 cm³; 20 s, 12 cm³; 40 s, 20 cm³; 60 s, 24 cm³ (illustrative data). What is the best estimate of the initial rate of reaction from these data?
Mission 3

Temperature

  • Warmer → molecules have more kinetic energy → they move faster.
  • More frequent collisions between substrate and active sites, and more with enough energy → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second → faster rate.
  • Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure.
  • The active site changes shape; the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.
  • Denaturation is usually permanent: cooling does not restore the shape.
  • In the cold, enzymes are slow, not denatured.
  • Optima differ: many human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; DNA polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium ≈ 75 °C.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?A student measured the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at six temperatures. The rates, in arbitrary units, were: 10 °C, 2; 20 °C, 4; 30 °C, 8; 40 °C, 15; 50 °C, 9; 60 °C, 1 (illustrative data). Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission 3

pH

  • pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺).
  • H⁺ ions change the charges on R groups (e.g. –COO⁻ + H⁺ → –COOH).
  • Ionic and hydrogen bonds change → the active site's shape and charges change → substrate binds less well.
  • Far from the optimum the enzyme is denatured.
  • Optima differ: pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.
  • Say: "pH changes the charges on R groups" — not: "pH slows the molecules down".
Quick check
?Pepsin has an optimum pH of about 2. When it passes with food into the small intestine, where the pH is about 8, its activity falls almost to zero. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

Substrate and enzyme concentration

  • More substrate: more collisions with active sites → faster rate.
  • At high substrate concentration, every active site is occupied almost all the time: the enzyme is saturated. The rate levels off; enzyme concentration now limits it.
  • More enzyme: more active sites → rate rises in proportion, as long as substrate is in excess.
  • A rate–concentration plateau (saturation) is not the same as a product–time curve flattening (substrate used up).
  • Say: "the active sites are saturated" — not: "the enzyme is used up".
Quick check
?At high substrate concentrations, the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction levels off even when more substrate is added. Why?
Mission 3Practical

Rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction

  • Assessed practical: up to two enzyme experiments.
  • Method (catalase and hydrogen peroxide): put substrate and enzyme in separate tubes in a water bath to reach the set temperature.
  • Mix; collect oxygen in a gas syringe; read the volume every 10 s.
  • Repeat at five or more values of the independent variable, three times each.
  • Plot volume against time; tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate.
  • Control: temperature (water bath), pH (buffer), volumes and concentrations not being tested. Control tube: boiled enzyme.
  • Serial dilution for concentrations: 5.0 cm³ of 2.0% solution + 5.0 cm³ water → 1.0%; repeat → 0.50%, 0.25%, 0.125%.
Mission 3Practical

Colorimeter: starch and amylase

  • Iodine turns starch blue-black; as amylase hydrolyses starch, the colour fades.
  • Calibration curve: serial dilutions of starch of known concentration + iodine → absorbance (red filter) → plot absorbance against concentration.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank: iodine solution with no starch.
  • Mix starch and amylase at a set temperature and pH. Every 30 s, add a sample to iodine; read absorbance.
  • Calibration curve converts absorbance → starch concentration. Gradient of concentration against time = rate.
Mission check

?An enzyme from a cold-water fish has an optimum temperature of about 15 °C. A sample was kept at 45 °C for 10 minutes and then cooled back to 15 °C. Its activity at 15 °C was only 5% of that of an unheated sample tested at the same time (illustrative data). Which explanation is correct?

Heat speeds enzymes by speeding collisions, until it shakes apart the bonds that shape the active site. Then the rate falls, and usually stays down.

Mission 4

Competitive inhibitors

  • Similar in shape (and charge) to the substrate.
  • Binds reversibly to the active site. While it is there, the substrate cannot bind.
  • Inhibitor and substrate compete: whichever collides with a free active site binds.
  • More substrate → substrate wins more often → rate climbs back towards the uninhibited maximum.
  • More inhibitor → more active sites blocked → lower rate.
Mission 4

Non-competitive inhibitors

  • Bind at a site away from the active site.
  • Binding changes the enzyme's tertiary structure → the active site changes shape → substrate cannot bind, or is not converted.
  • No competition: extra substrate does not restore the rate. The maximum rate is lowered.
  • Say: "changes the shape of the active site" — not: "blocks the active site".
  • Say: "binds away from the active site" — not: "non-competitive means irreversible".

  • Test: does adding substrate bring the rate back towards the uninhibited maximum?
Quick check
?The rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction was measured at six increasing substrate concentrations, without and with inhibitor X. Without X the rates were 20, 35, 50, 58, 60 and 60 units. With X they were 8, 18, 33, 48, 57 and 60 units (illustrative data). What type of inhibitor is X?
Mission 4

Statins: a competitive drug

  • Liver cells make cholesterol by a pathway. One step is catalysed by HMG-CoA reductase.
  • Statins resemble its substrate and bind reversibly to its active site: competitive inhibition.
  • Less cholesterol is made → blood LDL cholesterol falls.
  • Binding is reversible: as the body clears the drug, the enzyme works again. So statins are taken every day.
  • An active-site-directed inhibitor used as a therapeutic drug.
Mission 4

Cofactors, coenzymes, prosthetic groups

  • Cofactor: a non-protein substance some enzymes need to work. It helps the substrate bind or takes part in the reaction.
  • Inorganic ion: e.g. Cl⁻ is a cofactor for amylase.
  • Prosthetic group: a cofactor permanently bound to the enzyme: e.g. Zn²⁺ in carbonic anhydrase.
  • Coenzyme: an organic cofactor. It carries atoms or groups between enzymes and is recycled.
  • Many coenzymes are made from vitamins: NAD from niacin (B3); FAD from riboflavin (B2); coenzyme A from pantothenic acid (B5).
Mission check

?Methanol is not very toxic itself, but the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase converts it into toxic products. Ethanol is also a substrate of alcohol dehydrogenase. Doctors have treated methanol poisoning by giving ethanol. Why does this reduce the harm?

One enzyme, one step: a molecule that occupies or reshapes that enzyme's active site slows that reaction and leaves the rest of metabolism running.

Mission 5

Enzymes that signal disease

  • Some enzymes are present, or active, only during a disease process. They are biomarkers.
  • In respiratory infections, white blood cells release elastase.
  • Elastase hydrolyses elastin, the stretchy protein in the walls of the alveoli and airways.
  • Less elastin → lungs recoil less → reduced lung function.
  • Detecting such enzymes in blood, urine or sputum helps doctors diagnose a disease and monitor treatment.
  • Another example: a raised level of the liver enzyme ALT in blood signals damaged liver cells.
Quick check
?A patient with a long-term chest infection has the elastase activity of their sputum measured each month during treatment. It falls from high to almost zero. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 5

Blocking a disease enzyme

  • If an enzyme drives a disease, a drug can inhibit it.
  • Active-site-directed inhibitors are designed to fit one enzyme's active site, so the substrate cannot bind.
  • HIV protease inhibitors: block the viral enzyme that cuts long viral proteins into working parts. New viruses cannot mature.
  • Sivelestat: inhibits neutrophil elastase; approved in Japan for acute lung injury.
  • Statins (see Mission 4): inhibit an enzyme that makes cholesterol.
  • Designed for one active site → few other enzymes are affected.
Quick check
?A disease is caused by over-activity of enzyme E. Researchers design drug D, an active-site-directed inhibitor of E. Which property must D have?
Mission 5

Immobilised enzymes

  • Enzymes fixed on or inside an insoluble support: fibres, gels or plastic beads.
  • Physical methods: adsorption onto a surface; entrapment in a gel (e.g. alginate beads).
  • Chemical method: covalent bonding to the support.
  • Benefits: more stable (tolerate a wider range of temperature and pH); allow continuous flow processes; product is enzyme-free; enzyme reused → cost-effective.
  • Drawback: reduced activity. Some active sites are hidden or hard for substrate to reach.
Mission 5Practical

Immobilising lactase

  • Mix lactase with sodium alginate solution.
  • Drip it from a syringe into calcium chloride solution: beads form, trapping the enzyme.
  • Rinse the beads; pack them into a column (a syringe barrel with a tap).
  • Run milk through. Test the product with a glucose test strip: lactose → glucose + galactose.
  • Variables: flow rate (slower → longer contact → more glucose), bead size, temperature. Control: beads with no enzyme.

Mission 5

Biosensor strips

  • A biosensor uses a biological molecule, such as an enzyme, to detect one substance.
  • Glucose strip: glucose oxidase and peroxidase are immobilised on the pad with a colourless dye.
  • Glucose oxidase: glucose + oxygen → gluconic acid + hydrogen peroxide.
  • Peroxidase: hydrogen peroxide + colourless dye → coloured product.
  • More glucose → deeper colour → read against a chart. Electronic meters measure a current instead.
  • Glucose oxidase is specific: other common sugars give little or no colour.
  • Pregnancy test strips use antibodies to detect the hormone hCG in urine; some designs signal with an enzyme.
Mission 5

Why use strips?

  • Fast: a result in seconds or minutes.
  • Specific: one enzyme, one substance.
  • Small samples: a drop of blood or urine.
  • Usable at home: people with diabetes monitor their own blood glucose.
  • Results are semi-quantitative (colour chart) unless read by a meter.
Mission check

?Saliva alcohol test strips contain alcohol oxidase, peroxidase and a colourless dye. Alcohol oxidase converts ethanol and oxygen into a product and hydrogen peroxide. A strip turns coloured with saliva from a person who has drunk alcohol, but not with a glucose solution. Why?

A test strip is enzyme specificity put to work: one active site, one substance, and a colour you can read.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why would a factory lock its enzymes in place when free enzymes work faster?

Make a guess: Why would a factory lock its enzymes in place when free enzymes work faster?

Mission 6

Enzymes held in place

  • An immobilised enzyme is held on, or inside, an insoluble support.
  • The substrate solution flows past. Product flows out.
  • The enzyme stays behind, ready for the next batch.
  • It still works: substrate molecules move at random and collide with the fixed active site.
  • Say: "substrate molecules collide with the immobilised enzyme" — not: "a fixed enzyme cannot meet its substrate".
Mission 6

Four ways to immobilise

  • Adsorption: enzyme sticks to the surface of a support (e.g. clay, glass beads, porous carbon). Simple, but the enzyme can leak off.
  • Covalent bonding: enzyme bonded to a support (e.g. cellulose fibres). Little leakage; some active sites may be distorted.
  • Entrapment: enzyme trapped inside a gel bead (e.g. calcium alginate) or a fibre mesh. Substrate must diffuse in.
  • Membrane separation: enzyme kept on one side of a partially permeable membrane. Small substrate and product molecules pass through.
  • CCEA's wording: enzymes secured "on or inside insoluble support materials such as fibres, gels or plastic beads".
Quick check
?Lactase is mixed with sodium alginate solution. Drops of the mixture fall into calcium chloride solution and set as small beads with the enzyme inside. Which method of immobilisation is this?
Mission 6

Why industry immobilises

  • Reuse: the same enzyme works for many batches, or for weeks in a column. Enzymes are costly, so this cuts costs.
  • Enzyme-free product: no step is needed to remove the enzyme from the product.
  • Improved stability: held in shape by the support, the enzyme is less easily denatured. It works over a wider range of temperature and pH.
  • Continuous flow: substrate is pumped through a column of enzyme without stopping.
  • Higher temperatures become usable, so reactions run faster.
Mission 6

The trade-off

  • Reduced activity: some active sites face the support or sit deep inside a bead. Substrate cannot reach them.
  • Substrate must diffuse into the gel, so each run is slower.
  • Set-up cost is higher; adsorbed enzyme may leak off.
  • Still cost-effective when the enzyme is used many times.
  • Evaluate by weighing these: one long-lived column against a fresh dose of free enzyme for every batch.

Mission 6

Enzymes on a test strip

  • Diagnostic reagent strips are biosensors: an enzyme fixed on a pad detects one substance.
  • Glucose strip: glucose oxidase oxidises glucose, making hydrogen peroxide.
  • A second enzyme, peroxidase, uses the peroxide to turn a colourless dye coloured.
  • More glucose → darker colour. Match it to the chart. (Meters measure a current instead.)
  • The enzyme is specific: other sugars in urine or blood give no colour.
  • Your spec words this as pregnancy tests among the enzyme reagent strips. In fact a pregnancy test detects hCG with antibodies, not an enzyme (IM mission).
Mission 6Practical

Lactase in alginate beads

  • AS1 practical work and assessed task: "illustrating enzyme immobilisation".
  • Mix lactase with sodium alginate; drip into calcium chloride solution to set beads. Rinse.
  • Pack the beads into a syringe barrel with a tap. Run lactose solution (or milk) through.
  • Test the liquid that drips out with a glucose test strip.
  • Control: beads made without lactase. No glucose should appear.
  • Variable to test: flow rate (tap setting). Keep temperature and bead volume the same.
  • Calculation: % lactose hydrolysed = (glucose out ÷ lactose in) × 100. Example: 35 ÷ 50 mmol dm⁻³ → 70 %.
Mission check

?A juice company uses the enzyme pectinase to clear cloudy apple juice. It compares adding free pectinase to each tank of juice with pumping juice through a column of pectinase trapped in beads. Which statement is correct?

That answers the mission's question: fixed enzymes may work a little more slowly, but they are kept, reused and kept out of the product.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can a tank of tiny living cells make medicines, cheese and even clean up oil spills?

Make a guess: How can a tank of tiny living cells make medicines, cheese and even clean up oil spills?

Mission 7

Why microorganisms?

  • Short life cycle: bacteria can divide every 20–30 minutes in good conditions.
  • Simple needs: many grow on cheap nutrients, even waste.
  • Grow at lower temperatures than many chemical processes: less energy.
  • Grown all year, whatever the climate; little land needed.
  • Easy to genetically modify to make new products.
  • Some microorganisms break down pollutants: on an oil spill, adding fertiliser speeds up the oil-eating bacteria already there.
Mission 7

GM bacteria make human proteins

  • A human gene is inserted into bacteria. (How.)
  • The genetic code is universal: bacterial ribosomes read the human gene and make the human protein.
  • The gene goes in without introns: bacteria cannot remove them.
  • Products from GM microorganisms include:
  • Human insulin for diabetes (first approved 1982).
  • Human growth hormone.
  • Chymosin (rennet) for cheese, so no calf stomachs are needed.
  • Grown in fermenters: huge numbers of cells, each making the protein.
Quick check
?GM bacteria carrying the human insulin gene make insulin identical to human insulin. Why?
Mission 7

GM viruses as medicines

  • Against cancer: a herpes virus with some genes removed, so it multiplies in cancer cells far more than in normal cells.
  • A gene for an immune signal is added. The virus is injected into skin cancer (melanoma).
  • Infected cancer cells burst, releasing new viruses and cancer antigens. The immune system then attacks the tumour too.
  • Approved in the USA and Europe in 2015.
  • Against bacteria: phages are viruses that infect and burst bacteria. Each kind infects only certain bacteria.
  • 2019: a teenager with cystic fibrosis had a drug-resistant bacterial infection that spread through her body after a lung transplant. She was treated with three phages. Two had been genetically modified to kill that bacterium reliably.
Quick check
?A GM virus injected into a tumour multiplies in cancer cells but hardly at all in normal cells. How does the infection spread through the tumour?
Mission check

?A bacterium infecting a patient is resistant to every antibiotic tried. Doctors treat the infection with a phage genetically modified to infect and kill that bacterium. Which statement explains why the phage can work?

Microorganisms, even viruses, can be modified to do a job for us, here to destroy the bacteria that drugs could not.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts: they lower the activation energy, leave the energy released unchanged, and are not used up.
  • The active site, held in shape by the whole tertiary structure, is complementary in shape and charge to one substrate: hence the enzyme–substrate complex and specificity.

Mission 2

  • Substrate and active site meet by random collisions; in induced fit the active site changes shape as the substrate binds, straining its bonds.

Mission 3

  • Temperature and substrate concentration raise the rate by increasing collisions, until denaturation (temperature) or saturation (substrate) takes over; pH acts on the charges that hold the active site's shape.
  • Compare initial rates, found from the tangent at time zero.

Mission 4

  • Competitive inhibitors bind the active site and are outcompeted by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, change the active site and lower the maximum rate.

Mission 5

  • Enzymes in clinical samples can diagnose and monitor disease; active-site-directed inhibitors block disease enzymes.
  • Immobilised enzymes are stable, reusable and leave products enzyme-free, at the cost of some activity; on test strips they make biosensors.

Mission 6

  • Immobilised enzymes are held on or inside an insoluble support: reused, stable, and leave the product enzyme-free.
  • The price: lower activity, as some active sites are hidden and substrate must diffuse in; worth it when the enzyme is used many times.

Mission 7

  • GM bacteria make human proteins (insulin, growth hormone, chymosin) because the genetic code is universal.
  • GM viruses can kill cancer cells or drug-resistant bacteria.
