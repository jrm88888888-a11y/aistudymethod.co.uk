Mission 1

Mission 1: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Make a guess: Hindus say some scriptures were "heard" and others "remembered". Why does the difference matter?

Mission 1

Heard and remembered

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): texts Hindus believe were heard or seen by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation, not composed by them.
  • Shruti = the Vedas, each with its hymns, ritual texts and Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): texts composed and handed down by humans: the epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita), the Puranas, and law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • Many Hindus believe shruti is eternal and without a human author (apaurusheya).
  • Your specification spells these smrti and Manusmrti.
Mission 1

Which has more authority?

  • Traditionally, shruti has the highest authority: it is revealed, timeless knowledge.
  • Smriti is authoritative because it explains and applies shruti.
  • Classical rule (Mimamsa school): if a smriti text conflicts with shruti, shruti wins.
  • Manusmriti 2.6 (paraphrase) lists the sources of dharma in order: the Veda, then smriti, then the conduct of good people, then one's own conscience.
  • Hindu schools that accept Vedic authority are called astika ("orthodox"); Buddhists and Jains, who reject it, are nastika.
Mission 1

The Vedas in worship

  • The Vedas are mostly heard in ritual, not read: priests recite them in Sanskrit with exact sound and accent.
  • Samskaras (life-cycle rites): Vedic mantras are chanted at the sacred-thread ceremony, weddings and funerals.
  • The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is recited daily by many Hindus.
  • Havan and temple rites use Vedic verses alongside later ritual texts.
  • Traditionally only "twice-born" men learned the Vedas; reform movements such as the Arya Samaj teach them to everyone, including women.
Mission 1

The Purusha Sukta

  • Rig Veda 10.90, the hymn of the cosmic person (Purusha): the gods sacrifice Purusha, and the universe comes from his body.
  • Verse 12 (paraphrase): from his mouth came the Brahmin (priest), from his arms the warrior, from his thighs the Vaishya (trader, farmer), from his feet the Shudra (servant).
  • Traditional reading: the four varnas are part of cosmic order; each has its own duty.
  • Organic reading: the varnas are interdependent parts of one body; none can live alone.
  • Critics: B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), Who Were the Shudras? (1946), argued the verse was a late addition used to justify inequality. Many Hindus today reject birth-based hierarchy.
Mission 1

The Upanishads: the end of the Veda

  • The Upanishads are the last part of the Vedas, c. 800–200 BCE.
  • Distinctive: philosophical dialogues between teacher and pupil, not hymns or ritual rules.
  • Key teaching: Brahman (ultimate reality) and atman (the true self) and how they relate.
  • They are called Vedanta, "end of the Veda", and are the basis of the Vedanta schools (Shankara's Advaita, Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita, Madhva's Dvaita).
  • Detail in a later mission.

Mission 1

The great smriti texts

  • Ramayana (attributed to Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the rescue of Sita and his rule. Rama is the ideal of dharma.
  • Mahabharata (attributed to Vyasa): the war between cousins, the Pandavas and Kauravas; duty in impossible situations.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna, within the Mahabharata. Often treated almost as shruti.
  • Puranas: stories of the gods and avatars; the Manusmriti: rules for varna, stage of life, family and kingship.
  • Smriti is known and loved: through festivals, storytelling, temple art, drama and television.
Mission 1Read the source

The Gita on the Vedas

Bhagavad Gita 2:46–47 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885):

> "Look! like as when a tank pours water forth

> To suit all needs, so do these Brahmans draw

> Text for all wants from tank of Holy Writ.

> But thou, want not! ask not! Find full reward

> Of doing right in right! Let right deeds be

> Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."

  • Arnold translates freely. Most translations of 2:46 read: for one who knows, the Vedas are as much use as a well in a flood. The criticism of using the Vedas for rewards is in 2:42–45.

Q1 In Arnold's version, what do some Brahmins do with the Vedas ("Holy Writ")?

Show answer

Draw texts from them for every wish, using ritual to gain rewards.

Q2 What does he ask of Arjuna instead?

Show answer

To act rightly for its own sake, not for the fruits.

  • So a smriti text reinterprets how to use shruti: one reason the Gita is so influential.
Mission 1

The Manusmriti: authority and controversy

  • A dharmashastra (law book), c. 200 BCE–200 CE: duties of each varna and ashrama, marriage, kingship, purity, penance.
  • Some verses honour women (3.56, paraphrase: where women are honoured, the gods rejoice); others restrict them (9.3, paraphrase: a woman is protected by father, husband, then sons, and should not be independent).
  • It ranks varnas and gives harsh rules for Shudras.
  • Ambedkar publicly burned it in 1927 as a symbol of caste oppression.
  • Today: some Hindus treat it as a guide to dharma; many see it as historical, not binding. Indian law (the Hindu Code Bills, 1955–56) replaced it in family law.

Mission 1Evaluate

Does smriti matter more than shruti?

  • Claim: For Hindus today, smriti is more important than shruti.
  • For: The epics and Gita are widely known among Hindus; they shape festivals (Diwali, Ramnavami), ethics and devotion.
  • For: Stories teach dharma in situations people recognise.
  • Against: Shruti defines what counts as Hindu (astika) and is present in every samskara.
  • Against: Smriti's own authority rests on agreeing with shruti; the Gita itself is called an Upanishad by tradition.
  • Judgement criteria: "Important" as authority or as influence? For which Hindus (priests, Vedanta scholars, devotees, reformers)?

Mission 2

Mission 2: A boy asks Death what happens after we die. What does Death teach him about the self?

Make a guess: A boy asks Death what happens after we die. What does Death teach him about the self?

Mission 2

Sitting near the teacher

  • Upanishad is often explained as "sitting down near" a teacher to receive hidden teaching.
  • The final part of the Vedas (shruti), so also called Vedanta, "end of the Veda".
  • The oldest (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) date from c. 700–500 BCE; others are later. Tradition counts 108; about 13 are "principal".
  • Setting: teachers (gurus) with pupils, often in forest retreats. Teachers include Yajnavalkya and Uddalaka; women such as Gargi and Maitreyi question and debate.
  • A shift: from performing outer sacrifice to gaining inner knowledge (jnana) of reality.
Mission 2

Nachiketas and Yama

  • Katha Upanishad: Vajashravas gives away worn-out cows at a great sacrifice (yajna). His son Nachiketas asks to whom he himself will be given; in anger his father replies that he gives him to Death.
  • Nachiketas waits three days at the house of Yama, god of death. As amends Yama grants three boons.
  • (1) His father's peace of mind. (2) Knowledge of the fire-sacrifice that leads to heaven. (3) What happens to a person after death.
  • Yama offers wealth, long life and pleasures instead; Nachiketas refuses, because such things are fleeting.
  • Yama's answer to the third boon: "This Self is never born, nor does It die. ... It is not slain even though the body is slain." (Katha 2:18, Paramananda)
  • Yajna: the story moves from ritual (the second boon) to knowledge of the self (the third), which ritual cannot give.
Mission 2

Brahman and atman

  • Brahman: the ultimate reality, the ground of everything, beyond all names and forms.
  • Atman: the true self in every living being, beneath body, senses and mind.
  • The Upanishads' central teaching: atman is Brahman, or at least inseparable from it. To know this is liberation (moksha).
  • Kena Upanishad 1:4 (Paramananda translation, 1919): "That which speech does not illumine, but which illumines speech: know that alone to be the Brahman (the Supreme Being), not this which people worship here." Verses 1:3–8 repeat this for mind, eye, ear and breath: Brahman as nirguna, beyond what senses and thought can grasp.
Mission 2Read the source

The chariot

Katha Upanishad 3:3–4 (paraphrase of Paramananda): the atman is the lord of the chariot; the body is the chariot; the intellect is the driver; the mind is the reins; the senses are the horses; the objects of sense are the roads.

> "But he who is full of discrimination and whose mind is always controlled, his senses are manageable, like the good horses of a driver." (Katha 3:6)

> "This Atman (Self), hidden in all beings, does not shine forth; but It is seen by subtle seers through keen and subtle understanding." (Katha 3:12)

  • Katha 3:15 (paraphrase): knowing the soundless, formless, eternal reality, a person escapes "from the mouth of death".
  • Q: Who is the driver, and what does a good driver do? (The intellect; it uses discrimination to keep the senses under control.)
  • Your spec names this parable: Katha Upanishad 3. Advaita identifies the lord of the chariot, the atman, with Brahman; Samkhya (dualism) reads it as pure spirit (purusha), distinct from the matter (prakriti) of body, mind and senses.

Salt in water

Chandogya Upanishad 6:13:1–3 (paraphrase): The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes salty. "In the same way," says Uddalaka, "you do not see the Real here, but it is here. That finest essence is the self of all this world. That is the Real. That is the atman. You are that (tat tvam asi), Shvetaketu."

Q1 What does the salt stand for?

Show answer

Brahman: unseen but present everywhere.

Q2 What does "you are that" claim?

Show answer

Shvetaketu's true self is one with that essence.

Mission 2

Jnana and the Vedanta schools

  • Jnana (knowledge) is realisation, not information: "This Self cannot be attained by study of the Scriptures, nor by intellectual perception" (Katha 2:23, Paramananda).
  • The path: a guru, ethical life, control of the senses, meditation (e.g. on Om).
  • The Upanishads begin the philosophical traditions of India, especially Vedanta. Its schools read the same texts differently:
  • Advaita (Shankara, c. 8th century): atman and Brahman are identical; the world of difference is maya.
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja, 11th–12th century): selves are real and distinct, yet part of Brahman, a personal God.
  • Dvaita (Madhva, 13th century): God and selves are eternally different.

Mission 3: An ancient hymn pictures society as one body. Does it make caste sacred?

Make a guess: An ancient hymn pictures society as one body. Does it make caste sacred?

Mission 3

Varna + ashrama + dharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma that fits your varna (class) and your ashrama (stage of life).
  • Its defenders present it as how universal order becomes social order: each person has a place and a duty, and society is held together as the cosmos is.
  • It is presented in the classical texts as an ideal social order: everyone's needs met by everyone's work. Critics see it as a justification of inequality.
Mission 3Read the source

The Purusha Sukta

Rig Veda 10.90 (paraphrase): the gods offer the cosmic Purusha (Person) in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. Verses 11–12:

  • From his mouth: the brahmana (priest).
  • From his arms: the rajanya (ruler, later called kshatriya).
  • From his thighs: the vaishya (merchant, farmer).
  • From his feet: the shudra (servant, labourer).
  • In the next verses the moon comes from his mind, the sun from his eye, the sky from his head.

Q1 What does the hymn suggest about society?

Show answer

Society as one body, each part with its own function; readers disagree whether head-to-foot order also implies a ranking.

Q2 What does it not mention?

Show answer

Jatis, untouchability, or rules about marriage and food.

Mission 3

Four varnas, four kinds of duty

  • As the classical texts describe them:
  • Brahmanas (brahmins): study and teach the Veda, perform ritual.
  • Kshatriyas: protect and rule; fight just wars.
  • Vaishyas: trade, farming, cattle.
  • Shudras: service of the other three.
  • The first three are dvija, "twice-born": the sacred-thread ceremony (upanayana) marks a second, spiritual birth.
  • The Gita lists each varna's qualities (Gita 18:41–44): serenity and learning for brahmins; courage for kshatriyas; trade and farming for vaishyas; service for shudras (paraphrase).

Mission 3Diversity

By birth or by nature?

Krishna in the Gita:

> "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Gita 4:13, Arnold)

  • The Sanskrit says by guna (qualities) and karma (actions).
  • By birth: the Dharma Shastras (e.g. Manusmriti) assign varna by birth; duties are inherited.
  • By nature: many reformers read varna as a matter of qualities: Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj); Vivekananda; today ISKCON and others.
Mission 3

Varna and jati

  • Varna: the four-fold scheme of the texts; ideal, all-India.
  • Jati: "birth group": thousands of local communities, often linked to an occupation, usually marrying within the group (endogamy), ranked by ideas of ritual purity.
  • Overlap: jatis claim or are given a place within a varna; that place can be disputed and has shifted over time.
  • Difference: the texts know four varnas; daily social life in India has been organised far more by jati.
  • English "caste" (from Portuguese casta) is used for both, which causes confusion.
  • Communities placed outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable"; many now call themselves Dalits.
Mission 3Evaluate

Does the Purusha Sukta make caste sacred?

  • Claim: The Purusha Sukta gives religious authority to the caste system.
  • For: It is shruti (revealed scripture), the highest authority, and it places the classes in a body from head to foot.
  • For: Later law books (Manusmriti) build birth-based rules on it.
  • Against: It is a late hymn (Book 10) and one image among many; it may describe the unity of society, not a ranking.
  • Against: It names four functions, not thousands of jatis or untouchability.
  • Against: Other teachings point another way: the wise look equally on a brahmin, a cow and an outcaste (Gita 5:18); varna by qualities (4:13).
  • Judgement criteria: What did the hymn mean then? How has it been used? What weight do other texts carry?
  • Your spec: "the significance of the teaching in the Purusha Sukta ... about the origin of the caste system".

Mission 4

Four stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: the student. Learn from a guru; celibacy, discipline, study of dharma.
  • Grihastha: the householder. Marry, work, raise children, give, worship.
  • Vanaprastha: the "forest-dweller", retirement. Hand on responsibilities; turn to spiritual practice.
  • Sannyasa: the renouncer (a sannyasi). Give up home, property and name; seek moksha.
  • Traditionally for twice-born men, beginning with the sacred thread; often pictured as four spans of about 25 years.
Mission 4

The householder holds it all up

  • Manusmriti 3.77–78 (paraphrase): as all creatures live by the air, so all the other stages live by the householder; it is the most important stage.
  • Householders feed students, support renouncers, care for elders and children, and keep worship going.
  • Traditional five daily offerings (pancha mahayajna): to the gods, the sages (study), the ancestors, other people (hospitality) and all beings (food for animals).
  • So a full worldly life is not a lesser path: it is dharma.
Quick check
?Why does Manusmriti call the grihastha stage the most important?
Mission 4

The stages today

  • Few Hindus follow all four stages strictly. Student and householder stages are almost universal; vanaprastha is often a gradual shift in retirement towards prayer, pilgrimage and service.
  • Sannyasis are a small minority; some renounce young without marrying (Shankara, by tradition, as a boy).
  • Many orders once accepted men only; women renouncers exist (e.g. the Sri Sarada Math, a women's order founded 1954).
  • In Britain and elsewhere, the stages are often taught as an ideal of a balanced life rather than a rule.
Mission 4Read the source

Own duty and universal duty

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently." (Gita 18:47, Arnold)

  • Link: both are dharma; varnashrama dharma is how universal dharma becomes concrete for one person at one stage.
  • Difference: sanatana dharma (non-violence, truth, purity, self-control) binds everyone always; varnashrama dharma changes with class and stage.
  • Tension: a warrior's duty to fight against universal non-violence (Arjuna).

Q1 What does Gita 18:47 value more than doing well?

Show answer

Doing one's own dharma, even imperfectly.

Q2 Which kind of dharma is it talking about?

Show answer

Svadharma: varnashrama dharma.

Mission 4

Bhakti changes the rules

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to God. In bhakti, what counts most is love, not birth, stage or ritual rank.

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path" (Gita 9:32, Arnold)

  • Bhakti poet-saints came from every background, including communities treated as untouchable: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva saint), Chokhamela (14th-century Maharashtra), Ravidas (15th–16th-century north India, from a leather-working community).
  • Chaitanya (1486–1534) shared the chanting of Krishna's names with all, regardless of caste; Basava (12th century) founded a Shaiva movement that rejected caste.
  • Bhakti often relaxed the rules in worship more than in marriage or daily life: the change was real but uneven.
Mission 4Evaluate

Are the ashramas still relevant?

  • Claim: The four ashramas are no longer relevant to Hindu life today.
  • For: Few Hindus become forest-dwellers or renouncers; modern careers and pensions do not fit the scheme.
  • For: Traditionally the scheme was for twice-born men only; it leaves most people out.
  • Against: The ideal (learn, contribute, let go, seek God) fits any modern life and is widely taught.
  • Against: Vanaprastha is lived out as retirement given to prayer, pilgrimage and service; sannyasa still flourishes in orders and ashrams.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant as a rule, or as an ideal? For whom: India or the diaspora; men or women?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Shruti ("heard": the Vedas, including the Upanishads) has the highest authority; smriti ("remembered": epics, Gita, Puranas, Manusmriti) applies it.
  • The Vedas live mainly in ritual recitation; smriti is better known and shapes festivals and ethics.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90) links the varnas to the cosmic body; readings range from divine order to later addition.

Mission 2

  • The Upanishads are the end of the Vedas (Vedanta): teachings of gurus on Brahman and atman, moving from outer sacrifice to inner knowledge.
  • The Katha Upanishad: Nachiketas learns from Yama; the chariot image (Katha 3) shows the intellect controlling the senses.
  • "You are that" (Chandogya 6:13) is read as identity (Advaita), part and whole (Vishishtadvaita), or difference (Dvaita).
  • Kena 1:3–8: Brahman is what makes speech, mind and senses work, beyond them all.

Mission 3

  • Varnashramadharma: duty by class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama), cosmic order as social order.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.11–12): brahmana from the mouth, rajanya from the arms, vaishya from the thighs, shudra from the feet.
  • Varna is a four-fold textual scheme; jatis are thousands of local birth groups; Hindus debate birth versus qualities (Gita 4:13) and the place of jati today.

Mission 4

  • Four ashramas: brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder, the support of all), vanaprastha (retirement), sannyasa (renunciation).
  • Varnashrama dharma makes universal (sanatana) dharma concrete for each person; Gita 18:47 values one's own dharma over another's.
