Mission 1

Mission 1: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Make a guess: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Mission 1

A molecule with two ends

  • A phospholipid has a head and two tails.
  • Head: a charged phosphate group. It is polar: hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • Tails: two fatty acid chains. They are non-polar: hydrophobic.
Mission 1

The bilayer builds itself

  • In water, heads face the water. Tails are kept away from it.
  • A cell has water on both sides: cytoplasm inside, tissue fluid or surroundings outside.
  • So phospholipids form two layers. Heads form both surfaces; tails meet in the middle.
  • The sheet forms spontaneously: no enzyme or ATP is needed to arrange it. (Enzymes do make the phospholipids.)
  • No covalent bonds join the phospholipids. Each stays a separate molecule.
  • Say: "water molecules bond to each other and exclude the tails" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Why do phospholipids form a bilayer when they are mixed with water?
Mission 1

The middle is a barrier

  • The core of the bilayer is the hydrophobic tails.
  • Small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids: O2, CO2.
  • Ions and polar molecules (such as glucose) barely enter the core. Charge matters more than size.
  • Water is polar but very small: it crosses slowly.
  • So membranes are partially permeable.
  • How substances cross: Mission 4.
Mission 1

Proteins: the mosaic

  • Proteins are scattered irregularly among the phospholipids, like the pieces of a mosaic.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in one or both layers. Some span the whole membrane.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on one surface.
  • Roles vary: channels and carriers (transport), enzymes, receptors, cell recognition.
  • Say: "integral: embedded in the bilayer" — not: "integral: always spans it".
Mission 1

Sugar chains on the outside

  • Glycoprotein: a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • Glycolipid: a lipid with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • The carbohydrate is only on the outer (extracellular) face.
  • It works in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
  • Some act as receptors or as antigens, recognised by the immune system.
Quick check
?Which statement about glycoproteins in the plasma membrane is correct?
Mission 1

Cholesterol, and why "fluid"

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids in animal cell membranes.
  • Fluid: phospholipids and proteins move sideways within the layer. The sheet stays continuous.
  • The same basic structure surrounds the cell and its organelles (nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts).
  • Say: "fluid: parts move sideways within the membrane" — not: "fluid: it is leaky, with gaps".
Mission 1

Draw it

A two-dimensional drawing needs:

  • Two rows of phospholipids, heads out, tails in.
  • An integral protein spanning the bilayer; a peripheral protein on one surface.
  • A glycoprotein, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • A glycolipid, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • Cholesterol among the tails, not on the surface.
  • Labels: hydrophilic (both surfaces), hydrophobic (the core).
  • Proteins scattered unevenly, never in a regular row.
Quick check
?A student draws a two-dimensional representation of the fluid mosaic model. Which feature of the drawing is correct?
Mission check

?Liposomes are tiny hollow spheres made by shaking phospholipids in water; some are used to carry medicines in the blood. Each one is a single bilayer enclosing a droplet of water. A water-soluble drug is dissolved in the water before the liposomes form. Where will the drug be held, and why?

Like a cell membrane, a liposome assembles itself in water, and its hydrophobic core keeps water-soluble substances on one side.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Make a guess: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Mission 2

The cell-fusion test

  • Label the surface proteins of a mouse cell with one fluorescent dye, and a human cell's with another.
  • Fuse the two cells into one.
  • At first, each colour covers its own half.
  • At 37 °C, within about 40 minutes the colours are mixed all over.
  • The proteins moved sideways through the bilayer. The membrane is fluid.
  • This was Frye and Edidin's experiment (1970).
  • When they cooled the cells to about 15 °C or below, the colours did not mix within 40 minutes: in a cold membrane, molecules move far more slowly.

Mission 2

Cholesterol steadies the membrane

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids: its –OH by the heads, its rings among the tails.
  • It restricts the movement of the other molecules in the membrane.
  • This makes the membrane more stable and less fluid, and less leaky to water-soluble substances.
Quick check
?Which statement describes cholesterol in the plasma membrane of an animal cell?
Mission check

?A student claims that proteins in the plasma membrane can move sideways within the membrane. Which observation would best support this claim?

Mixing after fusion and a bleached patch filling in point the same way: membrane proteins drift sideways through a fluid bilayer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Make a guess: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Mission 3

Why the pigment stays in

  • Beetroot's red pigment (betalain) is stored in the cell vacuole.
  • To leave, it must cross two membranes: the tonoplast and the cell-surface membrane.
  • The cell wall is fully permeable: it does not hold the pigment in.
  • Betalain is large and polar. It cannot cross an intact phospholipid bilayer.
  • So pigment in the water = damaged membranes. More pigment = more permeable.
Mission 3

Temperature

  • Warmer: phospholipids gain kinetic energy and move more. The bilayer becomes more fluid; permeability rises slowly.
  • Above about 40 °C: membrane proteins denature. Their shape is lost, leaving gaps in the bilayer.
  • Permeability then rises steeply: pigment pours out.
  • Below 0 °C: ice crystals can pierce membranes; thawed tissue leaks.
  • Say: "membrane proteins denature" — not: "heat breaks the bonds in the fatty acid tails".
Mission 3

Solvents

  • Organic solvents such as ethanol (alcohol) dissolve in the bilayer and disrupt it: lipids dissolve in them.
  • They also denature membrane proteins.
  • Higher solvent concentration → more disruption → more pigment leaks.
Quick check
?Beetroot discs held at 70 °C release far more pigment than discs held at 30 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3Practical

Beetroot and membrane permeability

  • Specified practical: investigation into the permeability of cell membranes using beetroot.
  • Cut cylinders with a cork borer; trim to equal length. Same surface area each.
  • Rinse in distilled water until it stays clear: removes pigment from cells cut open.
  • Put each in the same volume of water at one temperature (or ethanol concentration) for the same time.
  • Remove the beetroot, shake the tube, measure the liquid's absorbance in a colorimeter.
  • Control: same beetroot, size, volume and time. Only the independent variable changes.
Mission 3

Using a colorimeter

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample in a cuvette.
  • Absorbance: how much light the sample absorbs. More pigment → higher absorbance.
  • Choose a green or blue-green filter: the red pigment absorbs these colours most.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank (distilled water) first.
  • Same filter and clean cuvette for every reading; light passes through the clear sides.
  • Some colorimeters give % transmission: more pigment → lower transmission.
Mission 3

Worked example: serial dilution and calibration

To turn absorbance into a pigment concentration, make a calibration curve.

  • Start with a pigment extract: 100%.
  • 1-in-2 dilution: 5.0 cm³ of extract + 5.0 cm³ distilled water → 50%.
  • Repeat from each new tube: 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%. Each step halves the concentration.
  • Measure each absorbance (illustrative data):
Relative concentration / %100502512.56.25
Absorbance0.940.490.250.120.06
  • Plot absorbance against concentration; draw the best-fit line.
  • A sample reads 0.36: between 0.25 (25%) and 0.49 (50%).
  • Read across to the line, then down: about 38% of the extract's concentration.

Mission check

?Beetroot cylinders are left for 30 min at 20 °C in 0%, 25% and 50% ethanol, then the liquid's absorbance is measured. Which prediction and explanation is correct?

The pigment never changed. In hot water and in ethanol alike, the membranes holding it in were damaged.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Make a guess: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Mission 4

Diffusion

  • Particles move randomly, all the time.
  • More particles cross from the crowded side than from the other: net movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • This is movement down a concentration gradient.
  • It is passive: no energy from ATP.
  • At equal concentrations, particles still cross both ways. Net movement is zero.
  • Say: "net movement stops" — not: "the molecules stop moving".
Mission 4

Straight through the bilayer

  • Simple diffusion: particles pass between the phospholipids.
  • Only small non-polar molecules do this readily: O2, CO2.
  • Permeability of the bilayer depends only on size and polarity or charge. It is not selective in any other way.

Quick check
?The table shows approximate relative rates at which five substances cross an artificial membrane made only of phospholipids, with no proteins. Each step of 10⁻³ is a thousandfold change. Which statement is supported by the data?
Mission 4

Channel proteins

  • Facilitated diffusion: diffusion through a protein. Still passive; still down the gradient.
  • A channel protein spans the membrane with a water-filled, hydrophilic pore. Ions pass through it, avoiding the hydrophobic core.
  • Selective: the pore's width and the charges lining it let only certain ions through. A K⁺ channel passes K⁺ but not the smaller Na⁺.
  • Many are gated: open or closed. No ions pass when closed.
  • Say: "a channel is specific" — not: "a channel is a hole any small particle can use".
Mission 4

Carrier proteins

  • A carrier protein binds a specific molecule, such as glucose.
  • Binding causes a change in shape. The molecule is released on the other side.
  • Large polar molecules cross this way.
  • Net movement is still down the gradient. No ATP is used.
  • (Carriers that use ATP to pump against a gradient.)
Quick check
?A potassium channel in a cell membrane is open. Which statement describes the movement of ions through it?
Mission 4

Which route?

  • O2, CO2 → between the phospholipids.
  • Water → between the phospholipids, slowly.
  • Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) → channel proteins.
  • Glucose and other large polar molecules → carrier proteins.
  • The bilayer sorts only by size and charge. Proteins make a membrane selective: each lets through particular substances.
Quick check
?Artificial vesicles were made from a pure phospholipid bilayer. Oxygen crossed the bilayer rapidly, glucose very slowly and potassium ions hardly at all. When a potassium channel protein was inserted into the bilayer, potassium crossed rapidly but permeability to sodium ions and to glucose did not change. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?A hypothetical toxin binds to and blocks all of the channel and transport proteins in the plasma membrane of a cell. Which prediction about the cell, immediately after exposure to the toxin, is best supported?

Two routes, two rules: the bilayer lets through what is small and non-polar; proteins let through specific substances, ions included.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, so in water they form a bilayer on their own; the hydrophobic core blocks ions and polar molecules.
  • Proteins, glycoproteins, glycolipids and cholesterol sit in or on this fluid sheet, and its parts move sideways.

Mission 2

  • Labelled proteins mix after cell fusion and refill a bleached patch: membrane components move sideways, faster when warmer.
  • Cholesterol sits among the phospholipids and restricts their movement, stabilising the membrane.

Mission 3

  • Pigment leaks only when membranes are damaged: heat (more fluid phospholipids, then denatured proteins above about 40 °C) and organic solvents such as ethanol both raise permeability.
  • Measure leakage with a colorimeter (blank first, green filter); convert absorbance to concentration with a calibration curve from a serial dilution.

Mission 4

  • Simple diffusion: small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids, down their gradient; ions and polar molecules are kept out by the hydrophobic core.
  • Facilitated diffusion: specific channel or carrier proteins carry them across, still passively and down the gradient.
