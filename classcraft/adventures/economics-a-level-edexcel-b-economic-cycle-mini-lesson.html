Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 1

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Also called the economic cycle or trade cycle: the same thing.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 1

The phases of the cycle

  • Boom: fast growth, actual output above trend. Peak: the top.
  • Downturn (slowdown): growth falls. Recession: real GDP falls.
  • Trough: the bottom. Recovery (upturn): growth returns.
  • Common definition of a recession: real GDP falls for two consecutive quarters.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".

Mission 1

Firms through the cycle

  • Boom: sales and profits rise; firms hire, invest, build stocks. Capacity becomes stretched; costs and wages rise.
  • Recession: sales and profits fall; firms cut stocks, staff and investment; some go out of business.
  • Firms selling income-elastic goods (new cars, holidays) see the biggest swings.
  • Firms selling inferior goods (discount stores) may gain in a recession.
  • Firms can respond: cut costs, change prices, keep cash reserves, sell more in growing markets abroad.

Mission 1

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Negative output gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Positive output gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
Mission 1

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a negative output gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is a positive output gap.
  • On a PPC, a negative gap is a point inside the curve: resources are unemployed.
  • A positive gap is output beyond its sustainable level. Resources are over-used (overtime, machines run without maintenance).
Mission 1

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A negative output gap of 2.5% of potential output.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.

Mission 1

Why gaps are hard to measure

  • Actual output is measured; potential output is not. It has to be estimated.
  • Estimates use past trends, business surveys of spare capacity, and unemployment data.
  • Different institutions get different answers, and revise them later.
  • GDP itself is revised. UK example: the fall in real GDP in the 2008–09 recession was estimated at 5.8% in earlier data, then revised to 6.3% (OBR, 2012).
  • So policymakers may not know in real time whether the gap is positive or negative.
Mission 1

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: UK real GDP fell for five quarters in a row and shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It took five years to regain its pre-recession size (ONS, 2018).
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1Evaluate

Should governments try to close every output gap?

It depends on:

  • Measurement: potential output is estimated, so the size and even the sign of the gap may be wrong.
  • Time lags: policy can take a year or more to work, so it may arrive when the gap has closed.
  • The cause: a demand shock suits demand-side policy; after a supply shock, boosting AD can add to inflation.
  • Size and length: a small, short gap may close on its own; a deep recession can leave lasting damage.
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 2: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Make a guess: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Mission 2

Households and firms

  • The simplest model has two groups:
    • Households: own the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and buy goods and services.
    • Firms: hire factors and produce goods and services.
  • Two flows go round in opposite directions:
    • Real flow: factor services → firms; goods and services → households.
    • Money flow: factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) → households; spending → firms.
  • Assumptions of this simple model: no saving, no government, no trade. Households spend all their income.
Mission 2

Income = output = expenditure

  • Illustrative data. In one year, firms produce final goods worth 500 billion.
  • Households buy them: expenditure = 500 billion.
  • Firms pay out what they receive:
    • wages 320 + rent 40 + interest 30 = 390 billion
    • the rest is profit: 500 − 390 = 110 billion, income to the firms' owners.
  • Income = 390 + 110 = 500 billion.
  • Because profit is whatever is left, total income must equal the value of output.
  • And every unit spent is someone's income. So:
    • national output = national income = national expenditure
  • Three ways to measure national income: output (value added), income (factor incomes), expenditure (spending on final output).
  • In real data, the three measures differ a little, because of measurement errors (a "statistical discrepancy").

Mission 2

Withdrawals and injections

  • Withdrawals (also called leakages): income not passed on as spending on domestic output.
  • Injections: spending on domestic output that does not come from households' consumption.
    • Investment (I) by firms, Government spending (G), Exports (X).
  • Withdrawals shrink the flow; injections enlarge it.
  • Say: "investment is spending by firms on capital goods" — not: buying shares, which is saving.
  • Say: "government spending (G) on goods and services" — not: transfer payments, which only move income between households.
Mission 2

The five sectors

  • Households: supply factors; spend, save, pay tax, buy imports.
  • Firms: produce; pay factor incomes; invest.
  • Government: collects taxes (T); buys goods and services (G); pays transfers.
  • Banks and the financial sector: take households' savings; lend to firms for investment.
  • Foreign sector: buys exports (X); sells imports (M).
  • Every sector depends on the others: one group's spending is another's income.
Quick check
?Which of these is an injection into the circular flow of Canada?
Mission 2

Equilibrium

  • National income is in equilibrium when total withdrawals (W) = total injections (J):
    • S + T + M = I + G + X
  • Injections > withdrawals: more is entering the flow than leaving → national income rises.
  • Withdrawals > injections: more is leaving than entering → national income falls.
  • As income changes, withdrawals change too (more income → more saving, tax, imports), until W = J again.
  • The pairs need not match: S can be above I, as long as the totals are equal.

Mission 2

Case: spring 2020

  • April 2020, United States, during COVID-19 lockdowns (first estimates):
    • Household spending fell 13.6% in one month.
    • Households saved 33% of disposable income (12.7% in March).
  • More saving = a bigger withdrawal. Less spending = less income for firms and their workers.
  • Personal income actually rose 10.5%, mainly from government benefit payments: a reminder that transfers move income but are not G.
  • Many countries saw the same pattern: saving up, spending and output down.

Mission 3

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level. The inflation rate is its % change, usually over 12 months.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Real data: UK CPI inflation was 11.1% in October 2022 and 2.0% in May 2024. That fall was disinflation: prices kept rising.
  • Say: "inflation fell, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "inflation fell, so prices fell".
Mission 3

Index numbers and the base year

  • An index number shows a value relative to a base year, which is set to 100.
  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a price is 2.00 in the base year, 2.50 a year later, 2.60 the year after.
  • Index: 100 → 125 → 130.
  • Change from the base year: read it straight off: 125 means 25% higher.
  • Change between two later years: % change, not points. 125 → 130 is a 5-point rise but (130 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 4%.
  • Index numbers have no units, so series in different units or currencies can be compared.
  • Say: "the index rose by 5 points, a 4% rise" — not: "the index rose by 5%".
Quick check
?A country's consumer price index (base year 2015 = 100) was 125 in 2023 and 130 in 2024 (illustrative data). What was the inflation rate in 2024?
Mission 3

Measuring inflation with a basket

  • Statisticians pick a basket of goods and services that typical households buy.
  • They record the prices of the basket's items every month, in many shops and places.
  • A household spending survey shows how much households spend on each item. These shares become the weights.
  • Big spending shares (e.g. housing, food) get big weights: their price changes move the index more.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of this basket over time.
  • In the UK, the ONS collects about 180,000 prices a month for about 760 items.
  • The government sets the Bank of England an inflation target of 2% on the CPI measure.

Mission 3

CPI, CPIH and RPI

  • The ONS publishes three main measures of consumer price inflation.
  • CPI: the Bank of England's target measure (2%). Uses mainly geometric averages within each item.
  • CPIH: CPI plus owner occupiers' housing costs and Council Tax. The ONS's lead measure since 2017.
  • RPI: the older index. It differs from the CPI in three ways:
    • Coverage: excludes the top 4% of households by income and pensioner households who get at least three-quarters of their income from benefits.
    • Housing: includes mortgage interest payments and Council Tax, which the CPI leaves out.
    • Formula: uses mainly arithmetic averages, which push it up relative to the CPI (the formula effect).
  • RPI inflation has usually been higher than CPI inflation.
  • The RPI lost its National Statistics status in 2013, but is still used in some contracts and index-linked payments.
  • Say: "the RPI includes mortgage interest payments; the CPI does not" — not: "the RPI and CPI measure the same thing".
Mission 3

Real versus nominal

  • Nominal (money) value: measured in the prices of the time (current prices).
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation, measured in the prices of one year (constant prices).
  • Real value = nominal value × (price index in base year ÷ price index in current year).
  • Illustrative data: weekly pay rises from 400 to 520 while the CPI rises from 100 to 135.
  • Real pay now, in base-year prices: 520 × 100 ÷ 135 = 385.19.
  • Nominal pay is up 30%. Real pay is down 3.7%: prices rose faster than pay.
  • Shortcut: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. It works well only for small changes (here: 30 − 35 = −5, against an exact −3.7).
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when nominal pay rose.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 540. Over the same period, the consumer price index rises from 120 to 132. What happened to the worker's real pay?
Mission 3

Limits of the CPI

  • Substitution bias: when one item's price jumps, people switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket misses this, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living.
  • New products: new goods enter the basket only after a delay.
  • Quality changes: a dearer phone may also be a much better phone. Not all of the price rise is inflation.
  • Not your basket: the CPI tracks an average household. Students, pensioners and renters spend differently, so face different inflation rates.
  • Weights lag behind: they come from past spending surveys and are updated only from time to time (often yearly).
  • The UK CPI leaves out owner occupiers' housing costs, a big part of many households' spending (hence CPIH).
  • Real data: in 1996 the US Boskin Commission estimated that the CPI overstated the rise in the cost of living by about 1.1 percentage points a year; about 0.4 points of this was substitution bias.
  • Say: "the CPI measures changes in the price of a basket" — not: "the CPI measures each person's cost of living".
Quick check
?The price of beef rises by 30% while the price of chicken does not change. Many households buy less beef and more chicken. The consumer price index uses a fixed basket bought in the base year. What is the most likely effect on the measured inflation rate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In one year, food prices rise by 10% and all other prices stay the same. Household X spends 40% of its budget on food. Household Y spends 10% on food. In the country's consumer price index, food has a weight of 20%. Which set of inflation rates is correct?

A price index is only as good as its weights. Each household has its own inflation rate, and the CPI is the average one.

Mission 4: If every price in a country goes up at once, why would people buy less of the country's output?

Make a guess: If every price in a country goes up at once, why would people buy less of the country's output?

Mission 4

Aggregate demand

  • Aggregate demand (AD): total planned spending on a country's goods and services at each price level, in a given time period.
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Who spends: households (C), firms (I), government (G), the rest of the world (X), minus spending that goes abroad (M).
  • Axes: "Price level" (vertical) and "Real national output (Y)" or "Real GDP" (horizontal).
  • The AD curve slopes down: at a lower price level, more real output is demanded.
  • Say: "price level" — not: "price", on a macro diagram.
Mission 4

The four components

  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Investment (I): firms' spending on capital goods: machines, buildings, equipment. (Plus new housing.)
  • Government spending (G): on goods and services, e.g. teachers' pay, roads. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not in G: they are spent later as C.
  • Net exports (X − M): foreigners' spending on our exports minus our spending on imports.
  • Saving is income not spent. Investment is spending on new capital. Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • C is by far the largest component. Real data: consumer spending was 67.5% of US GDP in 2023. It is also well over half of GDP in the UK.
  • Investment is smaller but swings far more over the business cycle.
Quick check
?Which of these is counted as investment (I) in aggregate demand?
Mission 4

Why the AD curve slopes down

  • AD is not a market demand curve. When the price level rises, there are no cheaper home-produced goods to switch to.
  • Instead, a higher price level cuts real output demanded through three effects:
  • Wealth (real balance) effect: money and savings buy less, so households feel poorer → C falls.
  • Interest rate effect: buying the same goods needs more money → demand for money rises → interest rates rise → borrowing costs more → C and I fall.
  • International trade effect: home goods become dearer relative to foreign goods → X falls, M rises.
  • A lower price level works the other way: real output demanded rises.
Mission 4

Movement along AD or shift of AD?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD.
  • Price level up: a contraction of real output demanded. Price level down: an extension.
  • A change in anything else that changes C, I, G or X − M → a shift of AD.
  • More spending at every price level: AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2). Less: AD shifts left.
  • The three effects of the previous screen explain the slope. They are never reasons for a shift.
  • Say: "a rise in the price level causes a contraction along AD" — not: "a rise in the price level shifts AD left".
Quick check
?A rise in world oil prices pushes up the general price level in a country. Nothing else that affects spending changes. How should this be shown on the country's AD diagram?
Mission 4

What shifts C and I

Consumption (C) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Disposable income rises (e.g. income taxes are cut).
  • Interest rates fall: borrowing is cheaper, saving earns less.
  • Wealth rises (house or share prices go up).
  • Consumer confidence improves.
  • Household debt is lower, or credit is easier to get.
  • People expect prices to rise, so they buy now.

Investment (I) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Interest rates fall: projects cost less to finance.
  • Business confidence improves: firms expect more sales and profit.
  • New technology makes new equipment worth buying.
  • Business taxes (e.g. on profits) are cut.
  • Corporate debt is lower, so firms can borrow more easily.

The reverse of each shifts AD left.

Mission 4

What shifts G and net exports

  • G: set by government priorities and fiscal policy, e.g. more spending on health or roads → AD right.
  • Exports (X) rise when:
    • Incomes abroad rise (trading partners grow; the world economy booms).
    • The currency depreciates: exports become cheaper abroad.
    • Other countries cut trade barriers (e.g. remove tariffs).
    • Non-price factors improve: quality, design, reliability, reputation.
  • Imports (M) rise, cutting X − M, when:
    • Real income at home rises: people buy more of everything, including imports.
    • The currency appreciates: imports become cheaper at home.
    • The country removes its own tariffs or quotas.
  • Say: "a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home" — not the other way round.

Mission 4Evaluate

How far will AD shift?

It depends on …

  • Which component changes: C is the largest part of spending in most economies (often well over half), so a small % change in C moves AD a lot. In very open economies, X − M matters more.
  • Confidence and debt: a cut in interest rates may barely lift spending if households and firms are pessimistic or heavily in debt.
  • Time lags: changes in interest rates or taxes take months to affect spending.
  • Offsetting changes: higher G paid for by higher taxes cuts C; extra spending may leak into imports.
  • Judge: the direction of a shift is usually clear; its size is uncertain. What it does to output and prices also depends on aggregate supply.
Mission check

?The general price level in a country falls by 3% (illustrative data). Nothing else that affects spending changes. What happens to real output (real GDP) demanded, and why?

AD slopes down because of what the price level does to real wealth, interest rates and trade, not because buyers swap one home good for another.

Make a guess: Why can a jump in oil prices make a whole economy produce less and charge more at once?

Mission 5

Aggregate supply in the short run

  • Aggregate supply (AS): the total real output that all firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level, in a given period.
  • Axes: Price level (vertical), Real national output (Y) (horizontal).
  • Short run: some costs, especially money wages (wages in cash terms, also called nominal wages), are fixed. Firms cannot change every cost straight away.
  • The SRAS curve slopes upwards: a higher price level goes with more real output.
  • Long run: all costs can adjust, so the long-run curve (LRAS) is drawn differently.
Mission 5

Why SRAS slopes upwards

  • Sticky wages: money wages are set in contracts and pay deals, often for a year. They do not rise straight away when prices rise.
  • So when the price level rises:
    • firms get more for each unit they sell
    • many costs per unit stay the same
    • profit per unit rises, so firms supply more
  • Rising costs near capacity: to produce more, firms pay overtime and use older, less efficient machines. Extra output costs more, so it needs a higher price level.
  • The reverse holds too: a lower price level squeezes profit per unit, so firms cut output.
  • Say: "a higher price level", "real output" — not: "a higher price", "quantity". This is the whole economy, not one market.
Mission 5

Worked example: a firm with a fixed wage

  • Illustrative data. A firm sells each unit for 20.
  • Costs per unit: wages 12 (fixed by contract for a year), materials 4.
  • Profit per unit: 20 − 12 − 4 = 4.
  • The price level rises by 10%. The firm's price and its materials both rise by 10%:
    • price: 20 × 1.1 = 22
    • materials: 4 × 1.1 = 4.4
    • wages: still 12
  • New profit per unit: 22 − 12 − 4.4 = 5.6, up 40%.
  • Producing more now pays, so output rises: a movement up along SRAS.
  • When wages are renegotiated up 10% (to 13.2), profit per unit is 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4: the same as 4 once prices are 10% higher. The extra reason to produce disappears in the long run.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells its product for 50. Per unit, it pays 30 in wages, fixed by contract for the year, and 10 for materials. The price level then rises by 10%, and the firm's price and materials cost rise by 10% too. What is the firm's new profit per unit?
Mission 5

Movement along SRAS or shift?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS.
    • Price level rises → output rises: an extension of SRAS.
    • Price level falls → output falls: a contraction of SRAS.
  • A change in firms' costs (anything other than the price level) → a shift of SRAS.
    • Costs rise → SRAS shifts left (up): less output at each price level.
    • Costs fall → SRAS shifts right (down).
  • Say: "SRAS shifts left" — not: "SRAS falls" or "SRAS moves up".

Mission 5

Real world: the 1973–74 oil shock

  • In October 1973, Arab oil producers began an embargo on oil exports to the United States and other countries.
  • The price of oil rose from 2.90 dollars a barrel before the embargo to 11.65 dollars a barrel in January 1974: about four times as much.
  • Oil is an input to transport, power, plastics and fertiliser, so costs rose across the economy: SRAS shifted left.
  • The US was in recession from November 1973 to March 1975, while prices rose fast.
  • Rising prices and falling output together: stagflation.
  • Caution: US inflation was already rising before the embargo. The oil shock added to it; it was not the only cause.
Mission 5Evaluate

How much damage does a cost shock do?

It depends on:

  • Size and duration. A brief price spike may barely move SRAS; a lasting rise shifts it for years.
  • How much firms use the input. Energy-heavy industries (steel, chemicals, transport) are hit far harder than services.
  • Second-round effects. If workers win higher money wages to keep up with prices, SRAS shifts left again.
  • Firms' margins. Firms with high profits may absorb some of the cost rather than raise prices.
  • Policy. Subsidies or tax cuts can offset some of the rise in costs, at a cost to public finances.
Mission check

?New software lets workers in most firms produce 10% more each hour. Money wages and AD do not change. What is the most likely short-run effect?

Anything that changes firms' costs per unit shifts SRAS; only a change in the price level moves the economy along it. Higher productivity also raises capacity in the long run: a separate, long-run effect.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: boom, peak, downturn, recession, trough, recovery.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 2

  • Spending by one sector is income to another, so national output = national income = national expenditure.
  • Withdrawals (S, T, M) leave the flow; injections (I, G, X) enter it. Equilibrium: S + T + M = I + G + X.

Mission 3

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise; deflation is a falling price level.
  • A price index compares the cost of a weighted basket with its cost in a base year (= 100). Inflation is the % change in the index, not the change in points.
  • Real values remove inflation: real = nominal × (base-year index ÷ current index). The CPI has limits, including substitution bias, quality change and its average-household basket.

Mission 4

  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M): planned spending on real output at each price level. Consumption is the largest component.
  • A change in the price level moves the economy along AD (wealth, interest rate and trade or exchange rate effects); a change in anything else that alters C, I, G or X − M shifts it.
  • The size of a shift depends on the component, confidence, time lags and offsetting changes.

Mission 5

  • SRAS slopes upwards because money wages and some other costs are sticky in the short run.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS. A change in costs (wages, raw materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, productivity) → a shift.
