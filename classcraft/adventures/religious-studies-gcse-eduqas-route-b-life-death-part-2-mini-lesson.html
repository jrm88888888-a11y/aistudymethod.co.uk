Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do Christians sing their prayers, and why do some still use chants over 1,000 years old?

Make a guess: Why do Christians sing their prayers, and why do some still use chants over 1,000 years old?

Mission 1

Why sing at all?

  • Christians have sung in worship from the start: "speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs; singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19).
  • Music does three jobs:
  • Praise: it gives words to joy and thanks to God.
  • Unity: many voices become one voice, one community.
  • Memory and teaching: sung words stick, and carry belief.
Mission 1

Psalms: the Bible's songbook

  • The Book of Psalms: 150 songs and prayers of ancient Israel, sung by Jesus and the first Christians.
  • They cover every mood: praise, thanks, sorrow, anger, trust.
  • "Oh come, let's sing to the LORD. Let's shout aloud to the rock of our salvation!" (Psalm 95:1)
  • At Mass: the Responsorial Psalm follows the first reading; a cantor sings verses, everyone sings the response.
  • Daily prayer: monks, nuns, priests and many lay Catholics pray the psalms in the Liturgy of the Hours.
Mission 1

Plainchant

  • Plainchant (plainsong): one unaccompanied melody line, sung in unison, with a free rhythm that follows the words.
  • Gregorian chant is the Latin plainchant of the Western Church, named after Pope Gregory I (pope 590–604). Most of it took shape later, in the 8th–9th centuries.
  • Written in neumes: square notes on a four-line stave.
  • Words come first: no harmony, no beat, nothing to distract from the prayer.
  • Significance: calm and prayerful; the same chants are sung across the world and down the centuries.
  • Used today in monasteries, cathedrals, Latin Masses and seasons such as Lent and Holy Week.

Mission 1

Traditional hymns

  • A hymn is a song of praise in verses (stanzas), sung by the whole congregation to a repeated tune, often with organ.
  • Hymns spread fast at the Reformation: Martin Luther's "A Mighty Fortress" (c. 1529).
  • English hymn writers: Isaac Watts, "When I survey the wondrous cross" (1707); Charles Wesley, "And can it be" (1738).
  • Catholic hymns: Thomas Aquinas's Pange lingua (1264); John Henry Newman's "Praise to the Holiest in the height" (1865).
  • At Mass, hymns are often sung at the entrance, the offertory, Communion and the end.
  • Significance: hymns teach doctrine in memorable words ("sung theology") and let everyone join in.
Mission 1

Music and eternal life: Fauré's Requiem

  • A Requiem is music for the Mass for the dead, named from its first words: Requiem aeternam dona eis, Domine ("Eternal rest grant them, O Lord").
  • Gabriel Fauré (1845–1924), French Catholic composer and organist; first performed at the Madeleine church, Paris, in January 1888, at a funeral; expanded to seven movements by 1893 (full orchestral version 1900).
  • Fauré left out most of the Dies irae ("Day of wrath"), the long poem about judgement used in many Requiems.
  • He kept only its gentle last lines in the Pie Jesu: "Merciful Lord Jesus, give them rest."
  • He added the In Paradisum from the burial rite: the dead are led into paradise.
  • Fauré said he saw death as a happy deliverance rather than a painful experience (paraphrase of a 1902 interview).
  • Judgement is not removed: the Libera me still asks to be freed from eternal death "on that dreadful day".
Mission 1Read the source

In Paradisum

From the Latin funeral rite, sung as the body is carried out (literal translation):

> "May the angels lead you into paradise; at your coming may the martyrs receive you and bring you into the holy city, Jerusalem. May the choir of angels receive you, and with Lazarus, who once was poor, may you have eternal rest."

  • Lazarus: the poor man carried by angels to Abraham's side (Luke 16:19–31).

Q1 Name two beliefs about eternal life in this prayer.

Show answer

Angels and saints welcome the dead; heaven as a holy city; eternal rest; the poor are honoured.

Q2 Why does Fauré end with this rather than with judgement?

Show answer

To end in hope: death as the way into God's peace.

  • Catholic beliefs it expresses: resurrection hope, prayer for the dead (they can be helped on their way: purgatory), the communion of saints.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Traditional music is the best music for worship."

  • Reasons for: tested by centuries; deep, biblical words; links worshippers to the whole Church, past and present; calm, not about performers.
  • Reasons against: Latin chant and old hymn language can be hard to understand; young people may find it remote; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1).
  • Another view: "best" depends on the occasion: chant at a requiem, a lively hymn at a school Mass.
  • Judge it by: Does it help everyone pray and take part? Do the words express the Church's beliefs? Does it suit the occasion?
Mission check

?A Catholic parish has families from Poland, Nigeria, the Philippines and England. For the Easter Vigil, the priest has everyone sing the "Holy, holy" (Sanctus) in a simple Latin chant that all can learn. Which reason best fits Catholic teaching on music?

Sung prayer unites voices and hearts; traditional music adds a link to the whole Church, past and present.

Mission 2

Before death: the "last rites"

  • When a Catholic is dying, a priest is called to give the last rites: the sacraments that prepare for the journey to God.
  • Reconciliation (confession): sins forgiven; peace with God and the Church.
  • Anointing of the Sick: the priest lays hands on the person and anoints the forehead and hands with the Oil of the Sick (James 5:14–15).
  • Viaticum (Latin, "food for the journey"): Holy Communion as the last Eucharist, with Christ's promise "I will raise him up at the last day" (John 6:54).
  • Family and friends pray with the dying person (prayers of commendation).
Mission 2

The funeral in three stations

The Catholic funeral rite (Order of Christian Funerals) follows the body from home to church to cemetery, like a journey.

  • 1. Vigil (home): the evening before; prayers, Scripture, perhaps the Rosary, at home, in a funeral home or in church. A time of quiet support.
  • 2. Funeral liturgy (church): usually a Requiem Mass; sometimes a Liturgy of the Word.
  • 3. Committal (cemetery or crematorium): the body is laid to rest.
  • Each stage expresses the belief that the person is passing from death to eternal life with Christ.
Mission 2

In church: symbols of baptism and resurrection

  • Holy water sprinkled on the coffin as it enters: recalls baptism, when the person first shared Christ's death and rising (Romans 6:4).
  • White pall (cloth) over the coffin: the white baptismal garment; "you … have put on Christ" (Galatians 3:27). All are equal in death.
  • Paschal candle beside the coffin: Christ, the light who has overcome death (see Mission 3).
  • Crucifix, Book of the Gospels or Bible on the coffin: life shaped by Christ's word.
  • Incense at the end: respect for the body, once a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19), and prayers rising to God.
  • Vestments: white (resurrection), violet or black (mourning, prayer for mercy).
Mission 2

Prayers, readings and the farewell

  • Readings often chosen: "The souls of the righteous are in the hand of God" (Wisdom 3:1); "In my Father's house are many homes" (John 14:2); "I am the resurrection and the life" (John 11:25).
  • A homily proclaims the hope of resurrection; it is not just a eulogy.
  • The Mass is offered for the person who has died.
  • Final commendation: the community says farewell and commends the person to God.
  • Traditional prayer: "Eternal rest grant unto them, O Lord, and let perpetual light shine upon them."
  • As the coffin leaves, the In paradisum asks that angels lead the person into paradise (paraphrase).
  • Committal: at the grave or crematorium, the body is entrusted to the earth "in sure and certain hope" of resurrection. Catholics may choose cremation (allowed since 1963), but ashes are kept in a sacred place, not scattered (2016 guidance).

Mission 2

Praying for the dead

  • Catholics believe in the communion of saints: the Church on earth, the souls in purgatory and the saints in heaven are united in Christ.
  • So the living can help the dead by prayer: "it would be superfluous and idle to pray for the dead" if they were not expected to rise (2 Maccabees 12:44).
  • Masses for the dead: a family asks a priest to offer Mass for a person (a Mass intention), often at the funeral, a month later, and on anniversaries.
  • All Souls' Day (2 November): the whole Church prays for all the dead. November is the month of the Holy Souls.
  • Significance: comfort for the bereaved, hope for the dead in purgatory, and a reminder that death does not break love.
Mission 2

Music of eternal life: Fauré's Requiem

  • Gabriel Fauré (1845–1924), French composer and church organist; Requiem in D minor, Op. 48; first performed in 1888 at the Église de la Madeleine, Paris.
  • Seven movements: Introit and Kyrie · Offertory · Sanctus · Pie Jesu · Agnus Dei · Libera me · In paradisum.
  • Leaves out most of the Dies irae ("day of wrath"), the fearful judgement poem; keeps only its last gentle line in the Pie Jesu ("Kind Lord Jesus, grant them rest").
  • Ends with In paradisum, sung in the funeral rite as the body leaves the church: angels lead the soul to paradise.
  • Mood: calm, consoling, full of light (harp, organ, high voices). Fauré said he saw death as a happy deliverance rather than a painful experience (1902, paraphrase).
  • So it expresses eternal life as rest, light and peace with God, more than fear of judgement.
  • Other forms in funerals: plainchant, hymns, organ music; all serve prayer and hope.
Mission 2Evaluate

"A Catholic funeral is more for the living than for the dead."

  • Reasons for: much of the rite consoles the bereaved: the vigil's "quiet support", readings of hope, the gathered community; the aims include supporting the mourners.
  • Reasons against: the Mass is offered for the dead person; prayers and the final commendation entrust them to God's mercy; Catholics believe prayer really helps those in purgatory (communion of saints), so the dead are its first concern.
  • Another view: for Catholics the distinction is false: the living and the dead form one communion in Christ; the rite serves both and above all worships God.
  • Judge it by: Who are the prayers addressed to and about? What would be lost if the dead were not prayed for? Do non-Catholic funerals share the same aims?
Mission check

A Catholic funeral completes what baptism began: water, white garment and light proclaim that the person who died with Christ will rise with him.

Mission 3

The Paschal candle: what you see

  • A large candle, new each year, blessed at the Easter Vigil ("Paschal" = of Easter, from the Hebrew Pesach, Passover).
  • A cross: Christ died and rose.
  • Alpha (Α) and Omega (Ω), first and last letters of the Greek alphabet: "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End" (Revelation 22:13).
  • The numbers of the current year in the angles of the cross: Christ is Lord of all time, including now ("Jesus Christ is the same yesterday, today, and forever", Hebrews 13:8).
  • Five grains of incense (often red "nails") in the cross: the five wounds of Christ (hands, feet, side), now glorious.
  • The flame: Christ, risen and alive, the Light of the world (John 8:12).
Mission 3

Light in the darkness: the Easter Vigil

  • The Vigil begins after nightfall, in a dark church.
  • Outside, a new fire is blessed; the priest may cut the cross, Alpha, Omega and year into the candle and insert the five grains, praying that Christ's wounds guard and protect us (paraphrase).
  • The candle is lit from the new fire and carried in: "The Light of Christ" — "Thanks be to God". Everyone's small candles are lit from it until the church glows.
  • The Exsultet is sung beside it: a hymn of praise for the night when Christ broke the chains of death (paraphrase).
  • At the blessing of the baptismal water, the candle may be lowered into the font: Christ's death and rising give baptism its power.
  • Belief: resurrection: light conquers darkness; "the darkness hasn't overcome it" (John 1:5).
Mission 3

The same light at baptism and at death

  • The candle stands in the sanctuary through the 50 days of Easter, then beside the font.
  • Baptism: a baptismal candle is lit from it and given to the newly baptised (or parents and godparents): "Receive the light of Christ" (Rite of Baptism). The new Christian shares Christ's risen life.
  • Funerals: it stands beside the coffin (see Mission 2): the light received at baptism is still burning; the person who died with Christ will rise with him (Romans 6:4).
  • So one artefact links Easter, baptism and death: the whole Christian life is lived in the light of the Resurrection.

Mission 3

Christian sarcophagi in Rome

  • A sarcophagus is a stone coffin, often carved. Wealthy Romans used them; so did wealthy early Christians.
  • Christians buried their dead rather than cremating them: the body would be raised (1 Thessalonians 4:14).
  • Many lie in the catacombs, underground burial places outside Rome.
  • Their carvings use Bible scenes of rescue and new life: Jonah coming out of the fish, the raising of Lazarus, Daniel among the lions, the Good Shepherd.
  • After Christianity became legal (313), carvings grew bolder: Christ in triumph, the Chi-Rho (☧, the first two Greek letters of Christos).
Mission 3

The Sarcophagus with Scenes of the Passion

  • Made in Rome, c. 350, from the catacomb of Domitilla; now in the Museo Pio Christiano, Vatican Museums.
  • Five niches divided by columns, read left to right:
  • 1. Simon of Cyrene carries the cross for Jesus.
  • 2. The crowning: a soldier places a crown on Jesus; the crown of thorns appears as a jewelled crown of honour: suffering shown as glory.
  • 3. Centre: an empty cross topped by the Chi-Rho in a laurel wreath (the Roman sign of victory), held by an eagle; two doves; below, two Roman soldiers, one asleep, one looking up: the guards at the tomb (Matthew 28:4). This is the Anastasis (Resurrection).
  • 4–5. Jesus before Pilate; Pilate washes his hands.
  • No crucified body, no scene of death: Jesus is calm and dignified throughout.
  • Meaning for eternal life: the Passion leads straight to victory over death; the person buried here hoped to share that victory.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"Objects and images teach faith better than words."

  • Reasons for: a candle or carving speaks to everyone, including those who cannot read (much of the Christian world in history); symbols engage the senses and memory.
  • The Passion sarcophagus still proclaims the Resurrection about 1,650 years after it was carved.
  • Reasons against: objects can be misunderstood or treated superstitiously; some Protestants argue faith comes by hearing the Word (Romans 10:17) and warn against images (Exodus 20:4); words can explain why, not just that.
  • Symbols need explaining: few visitors know what Alpha and Omega means.
  • Another view: Catholics see word and image as partners.
  • The Paschal candle is lit while the Exsultet is sung.
  • Judge it by: Who is the audience? What happens when no one explains the symbol? Do objects add something words cannot?

Catholic artefacts of death all point the same way: through Christ's Passion to the light of the Resurrection.

Mission 4: If God already knows what you need, why do Christians pray at all?

Make a guess: If God already knows what you need, why do Christians pray at all?

Mission 4

Raising the mind and heart

  • A classic Christian definition: prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God (St John of Damascus, 8th century).
  • For Christians, prayer is communication with God: speaking and listening.
  • Jesus prayed often, alone and with others (e.g. Luke 5:16; Mark 14:32–36).
  • Why pray, if God knows? "your Father knows what things you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). Christians say prayer is about relationship and trust, not giving God information.
Mission 4

Types of prayer

  • Adoration: praising God for who God is.
  • Confession: admitting sin and asking forgiveness.
  • Thanksgiving: thanking God for gifts and blessings.
  • Supplication: asking. Petition = asking for yourself; intercession = praying for others.
  • Memory aid: ACTS (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication).

Mission 4Read the source

The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13)

"Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy.

Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven.

Give us today our daily bread.

Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.

Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one."

  • Many churches add: "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen." (not in the earliest manuscripts).
  • In worship most Christians say a traditional wording ("Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name…").

Q1 Which lines are about God, and which are requests for people?

Show answer

The first three are about God's name, Kingdom and will; the rest ask for bread, forgiveness and protection.

Q2 What does "daily bread" suggest about how Christians should depend on God?

Show answer

Day by day, for what is needed.

Mission 4

What the Lord's Prayer means

  • "Our Father": God is close and loving, like a parent; "our" makes it a prayer of the whole community.
  • "May your name be kept holy": adoration; God is honoured above all.
  • "Your Kingdom come": asks for God's rule of justice and peace on earth.
  • "Your will be done": trust and obedience, as Jesus prayed in Gethsemane that his Father's will, not his own, be done (Luke 22:42, paraphrase).
  • "Daily bread": needs, physical and spiritual (Catholics also think of the Eucharist).
  • "Forgive us… as we also forgive": forgiveness received is to be passed on.
  • "Deliver us": protection from evil.
  • Significance: Jesus gave it as a model for all prayer; said at every Mass and most services, and by Christians of almost every denomination.
Quick check
?Which line of the Lord's Prayer shows most clearly that Christians link God's forgiveness to the way they treat other people?
Mission 4

Set and informal prayer

  • Set prayers: written words, said alone or together: the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Book of Common Prayer collects, the Jesus Prayer.
  • Informal prayer: in your own words, as you feel led.
  • Your board's terms: formulaic (set) and extempore (informal).
  • Why set prayers: shared and unifying; rich in belief; help when words fail; connect with Christians through history.
  • Why informal prayer: personal and sincere; fits the moment; open to the Spirit's leading.
  • When each is used: set prayers in liturgical services (Mass, Holy Communion, baptisms, funerals) and in private (e.g. the Lord's Prayer, the Jesus Prayer); informal prayer in private, at prayer meetings and in non-liturgical services.
  • Different views: Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans value set prayer highly; evangelicals and Pentecostals stress extempore prayer; most Christians use both.
Quick check
?Many evangelical Christians prefer extempore prayer in their own words. Which reason best explains this?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Set prayers are more valuable than informal prayer."

  • Reasons for: Jesus gave a set prayer (Matthew 6:9–13); set prayers unite Christians across the world and through history; their words are tested and full of belief; they help when people cannot find words (grief, illness).
  • Reasons against: Jesus warned against empty repeated phrases (Matthew 6:7); informal prayer is personal and sincere; the Spirit helps believers pray (Romans 8:26); Quakers find God in silent, wordless prayer.
  • Another view: both are needed: set prayer shapes informal prayer, and informal prayer brings set words to life; the Lord's Prayer can be a pattern as well as a script.
  • Judge it by: What makes prayer "valuable": sincerity, unity, or faithfulness to Jesus' words? Can set prayers be sincere? Does it depend on the situation?
Mission 4Worked answer

Two different ways to pray

Question: Explain two different ways in which Christians pray.

  • Way 1: set prayers, such as the Lord's Prayer said together at Mass. Explained: Jesus taught these words (Matthew 6:9–13), so saying them unites Christians everywhere.
  • Way 2: informal prayer in their own words, such as at an evangelical prayer meeting. Explained: it is personal and fits the need of the moment, like speaking to a friend.
Mission check

?A Catholic family sits at a grandparent's hospital bed. They say the Our Father together, then each prays a few words of their own for her, then sit in quiet prayer. Which statement best describes how they are praying?

Christians pray to be close to God, not to inform God: in set words, in their own words, and in silence.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians sing to praise God, unite as one body, and remember what they believe: psalms, plainchant and hymns are the traditional forms.
  • Fauré's Requiem expresses hope of eternal rest: it drops most of the Dies irae and ends with angels leading the dead into paradise.

Mission 2

  • The last rites (Reconciliation, Anointing, Viaticum) prepare the dying; the funeral follows the body from home to church to cemetery.
  • Holy water, white pall and Paschal candle link death to baptism and proclaim resurrection; the Mass and commendation pray for the dead.
  • Masses for the dead and Fauré's Requiem express hope of eternal rest and light.

Mission 3

  • The Paschal candle (cross, Alpha and Omega, year, five grains) proclaims the risen Christ at the Easter Vigil, at baptism and at funerals.
  • The Passion sarcophagus (c. 350) shows Christ's Passion as victory: an empty cross crowned with the Chi-Rho in a victor's wreath.

Mission 4

  • In Christian teaching, prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God; types: adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication (petition and intercession).
  • The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13) is Jesus' model prayer: God's name, Kingdom and will first; then bread, forgiveness and protection.
  • Set (formulaic, traditional) and informal (extempore, spontaneous) prayer are valued differently across denominations.
