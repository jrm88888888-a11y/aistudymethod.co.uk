The Great Gatsby · structure and Nick as narratorScreen 1

The story and who tells it

  • Nick Carraway tells the story in the first person, looking back: "After two years I remember the rest of that day" (Chapter IX).
  • He is a participant narrator: Daisy's cousin, Gatsby's neighbour, Jordan's lover.
  • The events cover one summer, 1922. Fitzgerald published the novel in April 1925.

The Great Gatsby · structure and Nick as narratorScreen 5

Can we rely on Nick?

  • He was drunk at Myrtle's party: "I have been drunk just twice in my life, and the second time was that afternoon" (Chapter II).
  • He calls himself honest just after excusing Jordan's lies (Chapter III).
  • He is inside and outside the action: "I was within and without" (Chapter II).
  • His verdicts swing: "I disapproved of him from beginning to end" (Chapter VIII).
  • Many readers call him unreliable: partial, not lying.
The Great Gatsby · structure and Nick as narratorScreen 6

Structure: the order of telling

  • Gatsby's past arrives out of order: Chapter IV (his version; Jordan's 1917 story), VI (James Gatz), VIII (Louisville).
  • Nick admits it: "He told me all this very much later, but I’ve put it down here" (Chapter VI).
  • Bruccoli's facsimile of the unrevised proofs (Trimalchio, 2000) shows that Fitzgerald rewrote and reordered Chapters VI–VIII in winter 1924–25.
  • Effect: myth first, man later, so we learn to doubt stories.

Romantic, crook, or both?

?Gatsby: a great romantic, or just a liar and a crook?

Both, and that tension is the point. James Gatz invents Jay Gatsby and pays for the dream with crime, yet Nick sets the guests who "guessed at his corruption" against "his incorruptible dream". Strong answers weigh the two rather than choosing one.

The Great Gatsby · Daisy, Tom and JordanScreen 1

Seeing Daisy through others

  • We see Daisy only through others: Nick (Chapters I, V, VII), Jordan's story of 1917–19 (IV), Gatsby's memories (VIII).
  • Her voice is everything: Gatsby says "Her voice is full of money" (Chapter VII).
  • On her wedding eve she wept over a letter, then married Tom (Chapter IV).
  • At the Plaza: "I did love him once—but I loved you too" (Chapter VII).
  • She was driving when Myrtle died; she leaves with Tom and sends no flower (Chapter IX).

The Great Gatsby · Daisy, Tom and JordanScreen 4

Tom: power and prejudice

  • Nick gives him "a cruel body" (Chapter I); he breaks Myrtle's nose (Chapter II).
  • He parrots race theory from "Goddard": "if we don’t look out the white race will be—will be utterly submerged" (Chapter I).
  • "Goddard" echoes Lothrop Stoddard's The Rising Tide of Color (1920). The scene mocks Tom, but similar theories helped shape the 1924 Immigration Act.
  • At the Plaza he fears "intermarriage between black and white"; Jordan replies, "We’re all white here" (Chapter VII).
The Great Gatsby · Daisy, Tom and JordanScreen 5

Jordan: the modern woman

  • A golf champion with her own money and freedom, often read as a New Woman of the 1920s.
  • Nick calls her "incurably dishonest" (Chapter III): she was suspected of moving her ball in a tournament, and lied about a borrowed car.
  • She drives carelessly: "It takes two to make an accident" (Chapter III), a line that looks ahead to Chapter VII.
  • At the end she calls Nick another bad driver, turning his charge of dishonesty back on him (Chapter IX).
The Great Gatsby · Daisy, Tom and JordanScreen 6

Villain or victim? Critics on Daisy

  • Many critics of the 1940s and 1950s treated Daisy as the villain who destroys Gatsby.
  • Leland S. Person (1978) recast her as a victim: Tom dominates her, Gatsby idealises her, and their contest turns her into a prize.
  • Judith Fetterley's The Resisting Reader (1978) urged women to resist the male viewpoint of American classics, Fitzgerald's among them.
  • Against that: Daisy drives on after hitting Myrtle and leaves with Tom.

Mission 4

Does the novel believe in the American Dream, or bury it?

Make a guess: Does the novel believe in the American Dream, or bury it?

The Great Gatsby · the American DreamScreen 1

What is the American Dream?

  • The phrase never appears in the novel. James Truslow Adams popularised it in The Epic of America (1931): a better life for anyone, whatever their birth.
  • Readers apply it to Gatsby: a poor farm boy remakes himself and grows rich.
  • The novel tests the dream: the money is criminal, and old money never accepts him.
  • Nick ends with the first Dutch sailors' "capacity for wonder" (Chapter IX).

A dream aimed at the past

Critics on the dream

  • Lionel Trilling (1945 introduction; The Liberal Imagination, 1950) argued that Gatsby, divided between power and dream, comes to stand for America itself.
  • Marius Bewley's essay 'Scott Fitzgerald's Criticism of America' (1954) reads the novel as a critique of the dream that Gatsby embodies.
  • Compare The Grapes of Wrath (1939), where the Joads' dream of California is also tested.
Write itScreen 8
?Short analysis, ungraded.

In 3–4 sentences, explain how the last page presents Gatsby's dream. Use the extract on screen 5 and at least two short quotations.

Model answer

Fitzgerald's ending makes the dream both moving and doomed. Nick recalls "Gatsby’s wonder" at the green light, then adds that the dream "was already behind him", so the future Gatsby chased lay in the past. The closing metaphor of "boats against the current" widens this to "we": everyone rows forward while being "borne back ceaselessly into the past". The elegiac tone honours the effort even as it admits defeat.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Does the novel believe in the American Dream, or bury it?

Both. Fitzgerald honours the dream's wonder, from young Gatz's schedule to the Dutch sailors' "capacity for wonder", but shows it corrupted by crime and aimed at a past that can't return. The last line makes that the fate of "we", not just Gatsby.

Old money, new money

  • Old money inherits wealth and name: Tom's family "were enormously wealthy" (Chapter I). New money is recent and showy.
  • Gatsby's fortune is new and criminal. Prohibition banned selling alcohol from January 1920 to December 1933; doctors could still prescribe it. Tom says Gatsby's drugstores "sold grain alcohol over the counter" (Chapter VII).
  • Tom calls him "Mr. Nobody from Nowhere" (Chapter VII); Daisy is "appalled by West Egg" (Chapter VI).

Mission 6

A green light, a pair of painted eyes. Why do they matter so much?

Make a guess: A green light, a pair of painted eyes. Why do they matter so much?

The Great Gatsby · symbols and colourScreen 1

How symbols work here

  • A symbol is an object that carries meanings beyond itself; a motif is a symbol or image that recurs.
  • In Gatsby, meanings are given by characters: Gatsby makes the light magic; Wilson makes the eyes God.
  • So meanings shift as the story moves: track who is looking, and when.
  • Strong answers say "suggests" and give more than one reading.

The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg

  • An oculist's faded billboard over the valley: "The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are blue and gigantic" (Chapter II).
  • Nick personifies them: "his eyes, dimmed a little by many paintless days, under sun and rain, brood on over the solemn dumping ground."

  • They watch the novel's crimes: "Doctor T. J. Eckleburg’s faded eyes" are there when the cars stop at Wilson's garage (Chapter VII).

Mission 7

It sold poorly in 1925. Now it's a classic. What changed?

Make a guess: It sold poorly in 1925. Now it's a classic. What changed?

The Great Gatsby · the Jazz Age and critical readingsScreen 1

The Jazz Age

  • Fitzgerald popularised the label (Tales of the Jazz Age, 1922) but didn't invent it.
  • The novel is set in summer 1922 and was published in April 1925.
  • Its world: jazz, cars, bootleg drink under Prohibition (1920–33), freer young women.
  • Wolfshiem "fixed the World’s Series back in 1919" (Chapter IV): eight Chicago White Sox players were banned for life over a plot to lose on purpose.

The Great Gatsby · the Jazz Age and critical readingsScreen 5

Readings of race and nativism

  • Tom's "Goddard" echoes Lothrop Stoddard's The Rising Tide of Color (1920), published by Scribner's, Fitzgerald's own publisher.
  • The 1924 Immigration Act's quotas cut arrivals from southern and eastern Europe.
  • Walter Benn Michaels (Our America, 1995) argues Tom's hostility to Gatsby is about race more than class.
  • The narration has prejudices too: Wolfshiem is an antisemitic caricature; Nick mocks Black passengers (Chapter IV). Critics debate how far the novel exposes or shares them.

Mission checkScreen 8

?It sold poorly in 1925. Now it's a classic. What changed?

Readers changed more than the book did. A 1925 Jazz Age novel that sold modestly reached a mass readership through the 1945 Armed Services Editions and post-war schools, and critics from Trilling to feminist and race critics have kept finding new meanings in the same text.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Nick is a first-person participant narrator looking back on summer 1922; the novel's nine chapters reveal Gatsby's past out of time order.
  • Nick undercuts his own claim to reserve judgement; many readers see him as partial rather than dishonest: "within and without".
  • Narrative viewpoint is a key AO2 tool for Task 1's unseen extract and for comparing Gatsby with your second text.
  • Fitzgerald delays Gatsby so that rumour and legend come before the man; the smile is a performance that "vanished".
  • James Gatz "sprang from his Platonic conception of himself": devoted self-making in the service of "a vast, vulgar, and meretricious beauty".
  • Compare Gatsby with ambition and self-making in your second American text.
  • Daisy reaches us only through Nick, Jordan and Gatsby; Nick judges her grief "basic insincerity".
  • Tom is physically and socially dominant; his race theory echoes Stoddard and the nativism behind the 1924 Act. Jordan is the independent, dishonest New Woman.
  • AO5: early villain readings, Person's victim reading and Fetterley's resisting reader.
  • "The American Dream" was popularised in 1931; readers apply it to Gatsby's self-making, which the novel both honours and tests.
  • Gatsby's dream points backwards: "Why of course you can!" repeat the past; Daisy "tumbled short of his dreams".
  • The ending's metaphor, "boats against the current", turns his fate into everyone's.
  • Compare the dream in your second American text.
  • Fitzgerald maps class onto places: East Egg (old money), West Egg (new money), the valley of ashes (the workers), New York (where classes collide).
  • Gatsby's fortune comes from Prohibition-era crime; old money uses that, and his origins, to keep him out.
  • The cost falls on the Wilsons; Tom and Daisy "retreated back into their money".
  • Symbols in Gatsby get their meaning from the characters who look at them, so meanings shift.
  • The green light depends on distance; once Daisy is near, "His count of enchanted objects had diminished by one."
  • The eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg are an empty advert that Wilson turns into God; colour gathers meanings but never fixes them.
  • Task 1's unseen extract will reward this kind of close reading of images.
  • Written in and about the Jazz Age: Prohibition, jazz, cars, the 1919 World Series fix, race theory and the 1924 Immigration Act.
  • It sold modestly in 1925; war editions (1945) and post-war schools made it a classic.
  • Critics read it differently over time: Trilling (America itself), feminist readings of Daisy, Michaels on race; the narration's own prejudices are part of the debate.
  • Reception is part of AO3; interpretations over time are AO5.
