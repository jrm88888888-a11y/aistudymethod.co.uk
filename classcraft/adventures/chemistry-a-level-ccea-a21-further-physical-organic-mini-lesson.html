Mission 1

Measuring absorbance

  • A colorimeter: lamp → coloured filter → sample in a cuvette → detector.
  • First, zero the instrument with a cuvette of the solvent alone (the blank).
  • The meter reads absorbance, A: how much light the sample absorbs. No units.
  • More light absorbed → less reaches the detector → higher absorbance.
Mission 1

The calibration graph

  • Make standard solutions of known concentration from one stock solution.
  • Measure each one's absorbance: same filter or wavelength, same cuvette.
  • Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x). Here it is a straight line through the origin.
  • Measure the unknown the same way and read its concentration off the graph.
  • Example: copper(II) standards 0.020–0.100 mol dm⁻³ give A = 0.12–0.60. An unknown with A = 0.33 reads 0.055 mol dm⁻³.
  • The graph does the work: you don't need to know ε.
Mission 1

Following a reaction rate

  • Use colorimetry when one species is coloured and its amount changes during the reaction.
  • Example: CH3COCH3 + I2 → CH3COCH2I + HI, with acid as catalyst. Only iodine is coloured (yellow-brown).
  • Put the mixture in the colorimeter (blue filter) and record absorbance every few seconds.
  • Convert absorbance to [I2] with a calibration graph. Plot [I2] against time; the gradient gives the rate.
  • Why choose it: continuous readings, no samples removed, the reaction is not disturbed.
Quick check
?Which reaction is best followed by colorimetry?
Mission check

?Bromine reacts with methanoic acid: Br2(aq) + HCOOH(aq) → 2Br⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) + CO2(g). Only bromine is coloured (orange). A student follows the rate in a colorimeter. What will the readings show, and why is the method a good choice?

The drink with more dye absorbs more light. The same idea lets a colorimeter watch a coloured reactant disappear, second by second.

Mission 2

What rate means

  • Rate of reaction: the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time.
  • Units: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
  • Reactant concentrations fall; product concentrations rise. Rate is quoted as a positive value.
  • Other measured quantities work too: gas volume per second, mass lost per second.
Quick check
?Hydrogen peroxide decomposes: 2H2O2(aq) → 2H2O(l) + O2(g). Which quantity is the rate of this reaction at a particular moment?
Mission 2

Following a reaction as it happens

  • Gas volume: collect the gas in a gas syringe; read the volume at set times.
  • Mass loss: a gas escapes from a flask on a balance, e.g. CO2 from marble chips and acid.
  • Colour: a coloured reactant or product is followed with a colorimeter.
  • These are continuous monitoring methods: many readings from one run.
  • Change one variable; keep everything else the same.
Mission 2

Colorimetry

  • Light passes through the solution; a coloured species absorbs some of it.
  • Choose a filter of the colour the species absorbs (the complementary colour).
  • Make a calibration curve: absorbance of solutions of known concentration.
  • Read each absorbance during the reaction; convert it to concentration from the curve.
  • Good for reactions of Br2, I2 or MnO4⁻: nothing is removed from the mixture.
Mission 2

Sampling and quenching

  • Remove a small sample with a pipette at timed intervals.
  • Quench it, to stop the reaction: dilute in ice-cold water, or remove the catalyst.
  • Example: for an acid-catalysed reaction of iodine, add NaHCO3 to neutralise the acid.
  • Titrate the sample: e.g. the iodine left, with sodium thiosulfate and starch.
  • Each titre gives a concentration at that time: plot concentration against time.
Mission 2

Clock reactions: the initial-rate method

  • Time how long a fixed, small amount of reaction takes: t.
  • Early on, the rate hardly changes, so initial rate ∝ 1/t.
  • Disappearing cross: thiosulfate + acid forms a sulfur precipitate; time until the cross is hidden.
  • Iodine clock: time until a sudden blue-black colour with starch.
  • Repeat with different starting concentrations: one run per concentration.
Quick check
?A student reacts 0.10 g of magnesium ribbon with excess dilute hydrochloric acid: Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g). Which method is least suitable for measuring the rate of this reaction?
Mission 2

Average rate or rate at an instant

  • Average rate = total change ÷ total time: the gradient of a straight line between two points.
  • Rate at an instant = gradient of the tangent to the curve at that time.
  • Initial rate: the tangent at t = 0, where the curve is steepest.
  • The curve flattens as reactants are used up: the rate falls.
Mission 2

Worked example: drawing a tangent

  • Marble chips + acid: mass of CO2 lost against time. At 60 s, 1.19 g has been lost.
  • Draw the tangent at 60 s. It passes through (0 s, 0.46 g) and (120 s, 1.92 g).
  • Rate = (1.92 − 0.46) g ÷ (120 − 0) s = 1.46 ÷ 120 = 0.0122 g s⁻¹.
  • Average over the first 60 s: 1.19 ÷ 60 = 0.0198: larger, because the reaction was faster early on.
  • Read two far-apart points on the tangent, not on the curve.

Mission 3

The rate equation

  • Rate equation: rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ.
  • Also written rate = k[A]ˣ[B]ʸ.
  • Order with respect to a reactant: the power of its concentration. Here m and n.
  • Overall order = m + n.
  • Rate constant, k: the proportionality constant in the rate equation.
  • Orders are found by experiment. They need not match the coefficients in the equation.
  • Each order will be 0, 1 or 2.

  • Two concentrations changed together: multiply their factors. Never add.
  • The hook: ×2 gives ×4, so second order; ×2 gives ×1, so zero order. Rate = k[A]².

Mission 3

Units of k

  • k = rate ÷ (concentration terms). Find its units by cancelling, not memorising.
  • Second order: (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)² = mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹.
  • Zero: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. First: s⁻¹. Third: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
  • k changes with temperature, not with concentration.

Mission 3

Finding orders in the lab

  • Initial-rate method: several runs; change one starting concentration; keep the others and the temperature the same.
  • Measure each initial rate: a tangent at t = 0, or a clock time (rate ∝ 1/t).

Make a guess: Why does a medicine's level in the blood halve every few hours, however high it starts?

Mission 4

Concentration–time graphs

  • Zero order: a straight line sloping down. The rate stays constant.
  • First order: a curve. [A] falls by the same fraction in equal times.
  • Second order: steeper at first, then a longer, flatter tail.
  • Rate at any moment = gradient of the tangent (made positive).
  • Initial rate: the tangent at t = 0.
Mission 4

The halving-time test

  • First order: rate = k[A]. Halve [A] and the rate halves too.
  • So losing half of what's left always takes the same time, whatever [A] you start with.
  • That constant time is the half-life, t½.
  • Zero order: each halving takes less time than the last.
  • Second order: each halving takes longer than the last.
  • The medicine: a fixed halving time means its removal is first order.
Mission 4

Rate–concentration graphs

  • Plot rate (y) against [A] (x). Data: initial rates of separate runs, or tangents to one curve.
  • Zero order: a horizontal line. Rate does not depend on [A].
  • First order: a straight line through the origin. Gradient = k, when A is the only species in the rate equation.
  • Second order: a curve rising more and more steeply from the origin.
  • Don't carry shapes across: a falling straight line on a concentration–time graph is zero order, but a straight line on a rate–concentration graph is first order.
Quick check
?A reaction is zero order with respect to reactant X. Which statement about a graph for this reaction is correct?
Mission 4

Zero order: k from the gradient

  • Zero order: rate = k, so the concentration–time line has gradient −k.
  • Example (illustrative): [A] falls from 0.800 to 0.200 mol dm⁻³ in 300 s.
  • Gradient = (0.200 − 0.800) ÷ 300 = −2.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
  • k = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Units of k = units of rate.
Mission 4

Two ways to measure rate

  • Continuous monitoring: follow one run over time; plot a concentration–time (or volume–time) graph.
  • Monitor gas volume, mass loss, or colour with a colorimeter.
  • Initial-rate method: several runs, changing one starting concentration at a time; find each initial rate.
  • Compare initial rates: double [A] → rate ×1 (order 0), ×2 (order 1), ×4 (order 2).
  • Plot initial rate against [A]: a rate–concentration graph.
Mission 4

Clock reactions

  • A clock reaction times how long a fixed, small amount of product takes to form.
  • Iodine clock: H2O2 oxidises I⁻ to I2. A little S2O3²⁻ turns the I2 back into I⁻ at once.
  • When the S2O3²⁻ runs out, I2 builds up and starch turns blue-black suddenly.
  • Same amount of product each run, so initial rate ∝ 1/t.
  • Valid only while little reactant is used: an approximation to the initial rate.
  • Repeat with a different [I⁻] (or [H2O2]); keep everything else the same.
Quick check
?In an iodine clock, the blue-black colour appears after 48 s. A second run uses double the concentration of I⁻, with everything else the same, and the colour appears after 24 s. What is the order with respect to I⁻?

Mission 5

Elementary steps

  • Most reactions happen in a series of elementary steps: single collisions or break-ups.
  • The sequence of steps is the mechanism.
  • The steps must add up to the overall equation.
  • They must also fit the rate equation found by experiment.
  • Rate-determining step (RDS), also called the rate-limiting step: the slowest step. It limits the rate of the whole reaction.
Mission 5

Intermediates and transition states

  • An intermediate forms in one step and is used up in a later one.
  • It cancels when the steps are added, so it is never in the overall equation.
  • It sits in an energy minimum, so in principle it can be detected.
  • A transition state sits at an energy maximum: bonds partly made, partly broken. It cannot be isolated.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes a reaction intermediate from a transition state in a multistep reaction?
Mission 5

Molecularity

  • Molecularity: the number of particles that collide (or break up) in one elementary step.
  • Unimolecular: one. (CH3)3CBr → (CH3)3C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Bimolecular: two. (CH3)3C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH. Also 2HI → H2 + I2: count particles, not species.
  • Termolecular: three at once. Rare: three particles seldom collide together.
  • It applies to elementary steps, never to an overall equation.
  • Background: "molecularity" is not a term in your specification.
Mission 5

How the mechanism sets the rate equation

  • The rate equation contains species in the RDS, and in any fast step before it.
  • Species that react only after the RDS are zero order.
  • If the RDS is the first step, the orders = the numbers of each particle in it.
  • Example: (CH3)3CBr + OH⁻ → (CH3)3COH + Br⁻ has rate = k[(CH3)3CBr].
  • So the RDS contains one (CH3)3CBr and no OH⁻. OH⁻ reacts in a later, fast step.
Quick check
?The overall equation for a reaction is 2X + Y → X2Y. The experimental rate equation is rate = k[X][Y]. Which mechanism is consistent with both?
Mission 5

SN1 or SN2? The rate equation decides

  • Alkaline hydrolysis: R–X + OH⁻ → R–OH + X⁻.
  • Primary (e.g. 1-bromobutane): rate = k[RBr][OH⁻]. Both are in the one slow step: SN2.
  • SN2: OH⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon as the C–Br bond breaks, in one step.
  • Tertiary (e.g. 2-bromo-2-methylpropane): rate = k[RBr]. Zero order in OH⁻: SN1.
  • SN1: slow step, C–Br breaks to give a carbocation (intermediate). Fast step: OH⁻ bonds to it.
  • The "1" and "2": how many species are in the rate-determining step.
Quick check
?2-chloro-2-methylpropane is hydrolysed by aqueous sodium hydroxide. Its rate equation is rate = k[(CH3)3CCl]. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

Energy profile of a multistep reaction

  • One maximum (transition state) per step; one minimum per intermediate.
  • Each step's Ea runs from the species entering that step up to its own maximum.
  • A later step starts from the intermediate's dip, not from the reactants.
  • The step with the largest Ea (usually also the highest peak) is the slow step.
  • Background: multistep profiles go beyond your specification.
Mission 5

Back to bromine

  • CH3COCH3 + Br2 → CH3COCH2Br + HBr (in acid).
  • Rate equation: rate = k[CH3COCH3][H⁺]. Zero order in Br2.
  • So the RDS (and any fast step before it) involves propanone and H⁺ only.
  • Accepted outline: fast protonation; slow change to an enol, CH2=C(OH)CH3; then fast reaction of the enol with Br2.
  • Br2 waits for the slow step to make the enol. More Br2 can't make that step faster.
  • H⁺ is a catalyst: used, then re-formed. It is in the rate equation.
Mission check

?Below about 500 K (225 °C), NO2(g) + CO(g) → NO(g) + CO2(g) has rate = k[NO2]². Doubling [CO] does not change the rate. Which statement explains this?

Same idea as bromine. A reactant that only joins in after the slow step can't speed the reaction up.

Mission 6

k rises with temperature

  • k is constant only at one temperature. Raise T and k rises.
  • Hotter: a much larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea.
Mission 6

Larger Ea, bigger effect of temperature

  • A larger Ea means a smaller fraction of collisions with enough energy, so a smaller k.
  • But a large Ea makes k more sensitive to T: a 10 K rise multiplies k by a bigger factor.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea, so k is larger at the same T.
Quick check
?For a reaction with Ea = 50 kJ mol⁻¹, raising the temperature from 300 K to 310 K almost doubles the rate. Which statement gives the main reason for this large increase?
Quick check
?At a fixed temperature, a catalyst is added that lowers the activation energy of a reaction. What happens to the rate constant, k?
Mission check

?Two hypothetical elementary reactions, X and Y, have equal rates at 300 K.
The activation energy of reaction X is 50 kJ mol⁻¹ and that of reaction Y is 100 kJ mol⁻¹. Both reaction mixtures are heated to 310 K with no change in concentrations. Which statement about the rates at 310 K is correct?

So yes, a cricket could do it. Time its chirps at several temperatures: the more its chirp rate rises with temperature, the larger the apparent activation energy of the processes behind it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coloured species absorb light; absorbance rises in step with concentration. A calibration graph turns absorbance into concentration.
  • Colorimetry follows a rate continuously when one species is coloured.

Mission 2

  • Rate = change in concentration (or volume, or mass) per unit time.
  • The rate at one instant is the gradient of the tangent at that time; the initial rate is the tangent at t = 0.
  • Two methods: continuous monitoring (one run, many readings) and initial rate (clock: rate ∝ 1/t).

Mission 3

  • Rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ; orders (0, 1 or 2) come from experiment, not from the equation.
  • Factor on rate = (factor on concentration)^order; units of k by cancelling.

Mission 4

  • Concentration–time: straight line = zero order; constant halving time = first order.
  • Rate–concentration: horizontal = 0; straight through origin = 1; upward curve = 2.
  • Clock reactions: rate ∝ 1/t.

Mission 5

  • The slowest step sets the rate equation. Species that react after it are zero order.
  • Orders show what is in the rate-determining step (or made in fast steps before it).

Mission 6

  • k rises with T because a larger fraction of collisions have E ≥ Ea. Larger Ea, more sensitive to T.
