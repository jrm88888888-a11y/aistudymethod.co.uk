Mission 1

Violence and the human condition

  • Violence: using force to hurt, damage or control. It can be physical, verbal, emotional, or online.
  • The Bible shows violence early in the human story: after the Fall, "Cain rose up against Abel, his brother, and killed him" (Genesis 4:8, WEBBE).
  • By Noah's time, "the earth was filled with violence" (Genesis 6:11, WEBBE).
  • Christians see this as a result of sin: human nature is damaged, so violence is part of the human condition.
  • Jesus traces violence to the heart: being angry with a brother "without a cause" puts a person in danger of judgement (Matthew 5:22, WEBBE).
Mission 1

Christian teaching on violence

  • "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE).
  • "Whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:39, WEBBE)
  • At his arrest: "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52, WEBBE).
  • "Repay no one evil for evil." (Romans 12:17, WEBBE)
  • Almost all Christians reject violence for personal revenge. They differ on force by the state: most accept it as a last resort in self-defence or war (see Mission 4); Quakers and some others refuse all violence.
  • So believers in violent conflicts have served as soldiers, chaplains, medics, peacemakers or conscientious objectors.

Mission 1

Righteous anger

  • Righteous anger: anger at injustice or wrong done to others, not at personal insult.
  • Jesus "made a whip of cords and drove all out of the temple" and "overthrew their tables" (John 2:15, WEBBE): anger at the Temple being used for trade.
  • "Be angry, and don't sin." (Ephesians 4:26, WEBBE). Christians read this as: anger itself is not sin; what we do with it matters.
  • Righteous anger aims at the wrong, not at destroying the person; many Christians say it should lead to action for justice, not violence.
  • Humanists also see anger at injustice as a healthy motive for change, if channelled peacefully.
Mission 1

Violent protest

  • Protest: a public show of disagreement. In the UK peaceful protest is legal; violent protest (riots, damage, attacks) is not.
  • Examples: some suffragettes smashed windows (1912); the ANC turned to armed struggle against apartheid (1961); Martin Luther King (1929–1968) led non-violent protest for civil rights.
  • Most Christians support non-violent protest, following King and the example of Jesus.
  • Some Christians joined resistance to tyranny: Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), a Lutheran pastor, was executed for links to the plot against Hitler.
  • Non-religious views vary too: most Humanists favour peaceful change; some argue violence may be justified against tyranny.
Mission 1Diversity

Other traditions on violence

  • Islam: "slay not the life which Allah hath forbidden save with right" (Surah 17:33, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: God will hear the humble, "To right the fatherless and the oppressed, that man who is of the earth may be terrible no more" (Psalm 10:18, JPS 1917). Violence is limited by law; self-defence is permitted.
  • Buddhism: the First Precept: not harming living beings. The Karaniya Metta Sutta (paraphrase): as a mother protects her only child, cultivate boundless love for all beings.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-harm) is a high ideal; yet in the Gita, Arjuna, a warrior, must fight in a righteous war.
  • Sikhism: violence for revenge or gain is wrong; force is allowed only to defend the oppressed, when all else fails.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Violence is never justified."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' teaching (Matthew 5:39, 5:44; Luke 6:27–31) and example; violence breeds more violence (Matthew 26:52); non-violent movements (Gandhi, King) achieved change.
  • Also for: ahimsa and the First Precept.
  • Reasons against: self-defence and protecting the innocent; the Temple shows anger at injustice can lead to action.
  • Also against: Sikh and Hindu teaching on defending the oppressed.
  • Also against: resistance to tyranny (Bonhoeffer).
  • Another view: force by police or a lawful state differs from personal violence; "never" may be too absolute.
  • Non-religious: Humanists generally oppose violence, but most accept self-defence and some just wars.
  • Judge it by: Who is using force, and why? Is it the last resort? Does it protect others or seek revenge?

Mission 2

Conflict and war

  • Conflict: a serious disagreement or struggle between people or groups. It may or may not become violent.
  • War: armed conflict between countries, or between groups within a country (civil war).
  • Wars almost never have one cause. Historians look for several causes working together.
  • Religions teach that war is a great evil, even when they accept it may sometimes be necessary (see Mission 4).
Mission 2

Greed, self-defence, retaliation

  • Greed: wanting more land, wealth, power or resources. "Where do wars and fightings amongst you come from? Don't they come from your pleasures that war in your members?" (James 4:1, WEBBE)
  • "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil." (1 Timothy 6:10, WEBBE)
  • Self-defence: protecting a country or its allies from attack. Most religions and international law (UN Charter, Article 51) accept it.
  • Retaliation: striking back after an attack, often for revenge. It can start a cycle of violence that is hard to break.
  • Religions generally reject greed and revenge as reasons for war; many accept self-defence.
Mission 2

Politics, resources, history, culture

  • Politics: struggles for power; clashing ideologies; leaders seeking support at home.
  • Resources: oil, water, farmland, minerals.
  • History: old grievances, past wars, borders drawn by empires.
  • Culture and identity: ethnic, national or language groups who feel threatened or excluded.
  • Religion: beliefs, holy places, or religious identity can be part of a conflict.
  • Usually these overlap: religion often marks who belongs to a side, even when the dispute is about land or power.

Mission 2

Holy war

  • Holy war: a war fought for religious reasons, believed to be commanded or approved by God.
  • The Crusades (1095–1291): popes called Christians to recapture Jerusalem and the Holy Land; Muslim, Jewish and Christian civilians suffered.
  • Some modern extremist groups claim religious reasons for violence; most believers of those faiths reject their claims.
  • Today most Christians reject holy war: no war is fought "for God"; a war can at most be just (see Mission 4).
  • In Muslim teaching, lesser jihad is struggle in defence of the faith under strict conditions, not a war to force conversion: "There is no compulsion in religion" (Surah 2:256, Pickthall).
Mission 2

Is religion to blame?

  • The case against religion (many atheists and Humanists): religion divides people into "us" and "them"; it can make violence feel sacred; leaders use it to win support. Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006), argues religion makes such divisions worse (paraphrase).
  • Religious replies: most wars have mainly political, economic or ethnic causes; the most destructive wars of the 20th century were not fought for religion; extremists misuse religion against its own teaching.
  • Religion also works for peace: religious leaders and groups mediate and help victims.
  • Humanists also note that non-religious ideologies (nationalism, fascism, communism) have caused terrible wars.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Religion is the main cause of war."

  • Reasons for: holy wars such as the Crusades; extremist violence in religion's name; religious identity can harden divisions; sacred places are fought over.
  • Reasons against: most wars have political, economic or ethnic causes; non-religious ideologies caused the deadliest wars; religion is often used as a label for sides formed for other reasons; religious teachings limit war.
  • For example, Jesus: "Put your sword back into its place" (Matthew 26:52).
  • And the Qur'an: "begin not hostilities" (Surah 2:190).
  • Another view: religion can be both a cause of conflict and a powerful force for peace.
  • Judge it by: Would the war happen without religion? Is religion the cause, or the badge of a side? Do the teachings support the war, or are they misused?

Mission 3

What terrorism is

  • Terrorism: the use or threat of violence, usually against civilians, to spread fear and so force a political, religious or ideological change.
  • Three marks: violence · fear as the aim · a cause beyond the act itself.
  • It targets people who are not fighting: commuters, shoppers, concert-goers.
  • That is what separates it from war between armies, which has rules about who may be targeted.
  • Terrorists are usually small groups, not states, and often call themselves soldiers or martyrs.
Mission 3

Why people turn to terror

  • Political grievance: a sense that a people is occupied, ruled unfairly or ignored.
  • Ideology: nationalist, far-right, far-left or religious-extremist ideas that divide the world into "us" and "them".
  • Perceived injustice and humiliation: poverty, discrimination, foreign intervention.
  • Belonging and identity: extremist groups offer purpose and a "family" to isolated people.
  • Religion, politics and terrorism: in many 21st-century groups (e.g. al-Qaeda, the so-called Islamic State), political aims are wrapped in religious language. The religion's mainstream scholars reject that use.
  • Not all terrorism is religious or Muslim: e.g. the IRA and ETA (nationalist), the 2011 Norway attacks and the 2019 Christchurch mosque attacks (far-right), and Aum Shinrikyo's 1995 Tokyo attack (a new religious movement).
Mission 3

Radicalisation

  • Radicalisation: the process by which a person comes to support, and perhaps join, violent extremism.
  • Often gradual: grievance → contact with an extremist message (increasingly online) → a closed group → violence presented as a duty.
  • Recruiters often use a distorted reading of scripture taken out of its context.
  • Many mosques, churches, synagogues and temples run projects to counter extremist messages.

Mission 3

Torture

  • Torture: deliberately causing severe pain to force information, a confession or obedience, or to punish.
  • Law: the UN Convention against Torture (1984) and the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 3) ban it absolutely.
  • "Ticking bomb" argument: a utilitarian claims torturing one suspect is right if it saves many lives.
  • Replies: torture often yields false information; it destroys the torturer's humanity too; once allowed "just this once", it spreads. Humanists and human-rights groups also reject it as an absolute violation.
Mission 3Diversity

Other religions on terrorism

  • Islam: "whosoever killeth a human being for other than manslaughter or corruption in the earth, it shall be as if he had killed all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall). In 2014 more than a hundred Muslim scholars signed an open letter rejecting the so-called Islamic State's claims.
  • "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)
  • Judaism: "Thou shalt not murder." (Exodus 20:13, JPS 1917). Life is of supreme value: pikuach nefesh (saving a life) overrides almost every other commandment.
  • Buddhism: the First Precept (not harming life); hatred feeds more hatred.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-harming); the same divine Self lives in every being.
  • Sikhism: protect the innocent; violence driven by the Five Thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride) is wrong.
  • Christianity: "love your enemies" (Luke 6:27); mainstream churches condemn all terrorism.
  • Humanists: terrorism violates human rights and dignity; reason and democratic means should settle disputes.
  • Martyrs: in Christianity a martyr (Greek martys, "witness") and in Islam a shahid ("witness") dies for faith; neither tradition's mainstream teaching counts killing innocents as martyrdom, and Islam forbids suicide.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Religion is the main cause of terrorism."

  • Reasons for: some groups use religious language and promise heavenly reward. Some atheists (e.g. Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion, 2006) argue faith that cannot be questioned can make violence seem holy.
  • Reasons against: many terrorist groups are nationalist, far-right or political. Research on suicide attacks (Robert Pape, Dying to Win, 2005) linked most to resisting occupation. Mainstream teaching in every religion condemns terrorism.
  • Another view: religion is often a label for conflicts about land, power or identity.
  • Judge it by: What do the groups themselves want? How do most believers act? Is a distorted religion still "religion"?

Mission 4

Where the idea came from

  • Just war theory: a set of conditions that, Christians in this tradition hold, must all be met for a war to be morally right, and limits on how it is fought.
  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430) asked how Christians, taught to love enemies, could serve as soldiers.
  • His answer: war may be a sad necessity to restore peace and stop wrongdoing, never for revenge or gain.
  • In City of God (XIX, 12) he writes that "peace is the end sought for by war".
  • St Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) set out three conditions in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 40, a. 1).
  • Later thinkers (e.g. Francisco de Vitoria, c. 1483–1546; Hugo Grotius, 1583–1645) added more, including rules for conduct in war.
Mission 4Read the source

Aquinas' three conditions

Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40, a. 1 (English Dominican translation):

> "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged."

  • Second: "a just cause": those attacked deserve it because of some wrong they have done.
  • Third: "a rightful intention": to advance good or avoid evil, not for cruelty or power.
  • Aquinas quotes Augustine: "we go to war that we may have peace."

Q1 Why can a private person not start a just war?

Show answer

Q2 Could a war with authority and a just cause still be unjust?

Show answer

Mission 4

The criteria today

Going to war (jus ad bellum):

  • Just cause: e.g. self-defence against attack, or protecting the innocent.
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a lawful government, not a private group.
  • Right intention: to restore peace and justice, not to gain land, wealth or revenge.
  • Last resort: every peaceful means (talks, sanctions) has been tried.
  • Reasonable chance of success: no pointless loss of life.
  • Proportionality: the good achieved must outweigh the harm caused.

Fighting the war (jus in bello):

  • Discrimination: civilians must never be deliberately targeted.
  • Proportional force: no more force than needed; prisoners and the wounded treated humanely.
  • These rules on behaviour in war are now in international law: the Geneva Conventions (1949).

  • Catholic and Anglican teaching, and many other Protestants, accept just war as a last resort.

Mission 4Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: lesser jihad (jihad means "striving"; the greater jihad is the inner struggle to live as God wills).
  • "Fight in the way of Allah against those who fight against you, but begin not hostilities. Lo! Allah loveth not aggressors." (Qur'an 2:190, Pickthall)
  • Conditions most scholars teach: declared by a legitimate leader; in self-defence or to protect the oppressed; a last resort; never to force conversion; civilians, crops, trees and places of worship spared; stop when the enemy seeks peace (Qur'an 8:61).
  • "Fight in the way of Allah, and know that Allah is Hearer, Knower." (Qur'an 2:244, Pickthall)
  • Judaism: milchemet mitzvah (obligatory war: commanded by God, or defence when attacked) and milchemet reshut (optional war: needed a king and the Sanhedrin, so most say it cannot happen today).
  • "When thou drawest nigh unto a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace unto it." (Deuteronomy 20:10, JPS 1917). Fruit trees must not be cut down (20:19).
Mission 4Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: Dharma Yuddha ("righteous war"). The Kshatriya (warrior varna) has a duty (dharma) to protect society.
  • Krishna tells Arjuna: "Nought better can betide a martial soul / Than lawful war" (Bhagavad Gita 2.31, Arnold).
  • Rules (Mahabharata; Manusmriti VII): fight only warriors; never the unarmed, the fleeing or those who surrender; no poisoned weapons.
  • Gandhi read the Gita's battle as the inner struggle between good and evil.
  • Sikhism: Dharam Yudh ("war in defence of righteousness"): a last resort; not for revenge, land or plunder; minimum force; civilians unharmed; end when its aim is met.
  • Guru Gobind Singh (Zafarnama 22): when all other means have failed, it is right to take up the sword (paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: most Buddhists say no war is "just": the First Precept forbids killing, and hatred breeds hatred.
  • Some Buddhists accept that defence may be the lesser harm, but still see killing as unskilful, with karmic results.

Mission 4Evaluate

"A just war is impossible today."

  • Reasons for: modern weapons (bombs on cities, nuclear weapons) cannot spare civilians, so discrimination and proportionality fail. Pope Francis (Fratelli Tutti, 2020, §258) argues it is now very hard to apply the old just war criteria (paraphrase). Pacifists say war is never the most loving way.
  • Reasons against: a country that is invaded still has a just cause to defend itself; the UN Charter (1945, Article 51) recognises this. Intervention may stop genocide. Precision weapons can reduce civilian deaths.
  • Another view: the theory still matters because it is used to judge and limit wars, even when no war fully meets it.
  • Judge it by: Can any modern war meet every condition? Is doing nothing ever worse? Who decides whether the conditions are met?

Mission 5

What a holy war is

  • Holy war: a war fought for religious reasons, believed to be commanded or approved by God or a religious leader.
  • Typical aims: to defend the faith, win back holy places, or spread a religion.
  • Fighters are often promised a spiritual reward (forgiveness of sins, paradise).
  • Compare just war (see Mission 4): fought for justice, judged by moral conditions; religion is not the cause.
  • Many believers say "holy war" is a contradiction: war cannot be holy, though it may sometimes be necessary.
Mission 5

War in the Old Testament

  • God is pictured as a warrior who rescues Israel: "The LORD is a man of war. The LORD is his name." (Exodus 15:3)
  • Conquest of Canaan: God commands Joshua to take the land (e.g. Jericho, Joshua 6; Ai, Joshua 8:1).
  • Some passages command the total destruction of Canaanite cities (Deuteronomy 20:16–17). Jews and Christians have long wrestled with them: many read them as tied to that time and never to be repeated.
  • Rules of war (Deuteronomy 20): offer peace first (20:10); do not cut down fruit trees (20:19).
  • The prophets look ahead to an age with no war (Isaiah 2:4; Micah 4:3).

Mission 5

Christianity: the Crusades and today

  • The Crusades (from 1095): Pope Urban II called Christians to win back Jerusalem; fighters were promised remission of the penance due for sins.
  • Jerusalem fell in 1099 with a massacre of its Muslim and Jewish inhabitants.
  • Today mainstream Churches reject holy war: Jesus' kingdom is not spread by force.
  • In 2000 Pope John Paul II publicly asked forgiveness for violence some Christians had used "in the service of the truth" (Day of Pardon, 12 March 2000).
  • "I didn't come to send peace, but a sword." (Matthew 10:34). Most Christians read the "sword" as the division following Jesus can cause, even in families (Matthew 10:35; Luke 12:51 says "division").

Mission 5Diversity

Islam

  • Harb al-Muqadas ("holy war").
  • Most Muslim scholars say Islam has no holy war in the crusade sense: war cannot be fought to spread Islam, because "There is no compulsion in religion" (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall).
  • What exists is lesser jihad: defensive war with strict conditions (see Mission 4).
  • "And if they incline to peace, incline thou also to it, and trust in Allah." (Qur'an 8:61, Pickthall)
  • Surah 2:244 commands fighting "in the way of Allah"; 8:17 teaches that victory comes from Allah, not from the fighters' own strength.
Mission 5Diversity

Judaism, Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Judaism: milchemet mitzvah, an obligatory war commanded by God (e.g. Joshua's conquest) or fought in self-defence. David praises God, "Who traineth my hands for war" (2 Samuel 22:35, JPS 1917). Most Jews today hold that only defensive war is permitted.
  • Hinduism: no war to spread a religion. Krishna urges Arjuna to fight because it is his duty (dharma) as a Kshatriya: shirking it "shall be sin" (Bhagavad Gita 2.33, Arnold). Many Hindus, like Gandhi, also read the battle as an inner struggle.
  • Sikhism: the saint-soldier (sant sipahi): devoted to God and ready to defend the oppressed of any faith. Guru Hargobind wore two swords, miri (worldly power) and piri (spiritual authority).
  • Buddhism: no holy war. The Sammaditthi Sutta counts killing as unskilful action, rooted in greed, hatred and delusion. In the Kakacupama Sutta (Simile of the Saw), the Buddha teaches that even if bandits cut you limb from limb, anyone who hates them is not following his teaching (paraphrase).

Mission 5Evaluate

"Religion can never justify going to war."

  • Reasons for: Jesus taught love of enemies; holy wars like the Crusades caused great suffering; religion should unite, not divide.
  • Buddhist teaching counts killing as unskilful, and many Hindus put ahimsa (non-harm) first.
  • Reasons against: the Old Testament describes wars God approves and sets rules for them (Deuteronomy 20); defending the right to worship freely may be a just cause.
  • Other scriptures and traditions allow fighting in defence: lesser jihad within strict limits (Qur'an 8:61); Arjuna's duty as a Kshatriya (Gita 2.33); Sikhs may fight to protect the oppressed of any faith.
  • Another view: religion may justify defensive war, but never war to spread a faith.
  • Judge it by: Is the war about religion or only described in religious terms? Does defending a people's freedom of religion count? Who has authority to interpret scripture?

Mission 6

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD: weapons that can kill huge numbers of people and cause great damage, without distinguishing soldiers from civilians.
  • Nuclear: blast, heat and radiation. Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945) are the only wartime uses.
  • Chemical: poison gases (e.g. nerve agents). Biological: deadly diseases spread deliberately.
  • Effects last for generations: radiation sickness, cancers, poisoned land and water.
  • International law: Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1993); Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968); Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017), which nuclear-armed states, including the UK, have not joined.
Mission 6

Nuclear deterrence

  • Deterrence: owning nuclear weapons so that no enemy dares attack, for fear of devastating retaliation.
  • "Mutually assured destruction": if both sides would be destroyed, neither strikes first.
  • The UK keeps a nuclear deterrent (Trident submarines).
  • For: no nuclear war between major powers since 1945; protects a nation against blackmail.
  • Against: accidents or misjudgement could cause catastrophe; enormous cost; encourages other states to seek weapons; terrorists cannot be deterred; it rests on a threat to kill civilians.
  • Unilateral disarmament: giving weapons up alone. Multilateral: all sides disarming together by agreement.

Mission 6

Catholic teaching

  • Second Vatican Council, Gaudium et Spes 80 (1965): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities and their people is "a crime against God and man himself" (also CCC 2314).
  • The arms race is not a safe way to keep peace (Gaudium et Spes 81).
  • John Paul II (1982): deterrence "may still be judged morally acceptable", but only as a step towards progressive disarmament.
  • Pope Francis (2017): the threat to use nuclear weapons, and even possessing them, is to be firmly condemned. At Hiroshima (2019) he called their use and possession immoral.
  • The Holy See was among the first states to ratify the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017).
  • Different Catholic views: some Catholics still accept deterrence for now, until all sides disarm together; others (e.g. Pax Christi) campaign for disarmament now.
Mission 6

Other Christian views

  • Many Anglicans and other Protestants have accepted deterrence as a temporary necessity while working for multilateral disarmament.
  • Quakers, Methodists and Christian CND oppose nuclear weapons outright: they cannot meet the just war rule of discrimination.
  • "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52).
  • Genesis 1–2: humans are stewards of creation; WMD can poison the earth for generations.
Mission 6

Utilitarian and non-religious arguments

  • Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, 1748–1832; John Stuart Mill, 1806–1873): actions are right "in proportion as they tend to promote happiness" (Mill, Utilitarianism, 1863).
  • Argument for acquiring WMD: if deterrence prevents a world war, owning them produces the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • Some argued the Hiroshima bomb saved more lives than it took by ending the war sooner; historians dispute this.
  • Utilitarian argument against: even a small risk of nuclear war risks suffering on an unimaginable scale; the money could relieve poverty.
  • Humanists and atheists judge by consequences and human rights; most support multilateral disarmament; some accept deterrence as the lesser evil for now.
  • Religious responses: some believers accept the deterrence argument; others say deliberately threatening civilians is wrong whatever the outcome.

Mission 6Diversity

Other religions

  • Islam: "whosoever killeth a human being … it shall be as if he had killed all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall). The rules of lesser jihad forbid killing civilians and destroying crops, so most scholars reject using WMD. Some accept owning them as a deterrent for defence.
  • Judaism: Deuteronomy 20 limits destruction in war.
  • Many Jews accept deterrence to protect life; others argue WMD can never be used lawfully.
  • Buddhism: "All men tremble at punishment, all men love life; remember that thou art like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 130, Müller). WMD break the First Precept on a vast scale.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa; "He sees indeed who sees in all alike / The living, lordly Soul" (Bhagavad Gita 13.27, Arnold): harming others harms the one Self in all. India has nuclear weapons, and some Hindus defend them as deterrence.
  • Sikhism: Dharam Yudh forbids harming civilians, so WMD fail its conditions.
Mission 6

Conventional, technological and nuclear warfare

  • Conventional warfare: armies, ships and aircraft with ordinary weapons; just war rules can be applied to it.
  • Technological warfare: drones and "surgical" strikes.
  • For: fewer of your own soldiers die; targets can be chosen precisely.
  • Against: civilians still die; killing from far away may feel easier, making war more likely; who is responsible when machines choose targets?
  • Pope Francis (G7, 2024) called for a ban on lethal autonomous weapons.
  • Nuclear ("apocalyptic") warfare: it could end civilisation; most religious traditions see it as impossible to justify.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Nuclear deterrence is morally justified."

  • Reasons for: no nuclear war between great powers since 1945; a nation has a duty to protect its people; utilitarian benefit if war is prevented; John Paul II (1982) accepted it as a temporary step.
  • Reasons against: it rests on a willingness to kill millions of civilians; one accident could be catastrophic; Francis (2017) condemned even possession; Qur'an 5:32 and the First Precept treat each life as precious; vast cost.
  • Another view: acceptable only temporarily, with real steps towards multilateral disarmament.
  • Judge it by: Is threatening an act as wrong as doing it? Has deterrence actually kept the peace? What happens if one side disarms alone?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Violence is part of the human condition in the Bible (Cain, Genesis 4:8); Jesus teaches love of enemies, turning the other cheek and the Golden Rule (Matthew 5:39, 5:44; Luke 6:27–31).
  • Righteous anger targets injustice, not people (John 2:15; Ephesians 4:26).
  • Most believers support non-violent protest; views differ on resisting tyranny.
  • Other traditions limit violence: Surah 17:33, ahimsa, the First Precept and metta, Sikh defence of the oppressed.

Mission 2

  • Wars usually have several causes: greed, self-defence, retaliation, politics, resources, history, culture and sometimes religion.
  • Holy war is war believed commanded by God (e.g. the Crusades); most religious believers today reject it, and their teachings limit war.
  • Teachings that limit war: Matthew 26:52; Surah 2:190.
  • Critics say religion divides and sanctifies violence; believers reply that most wars are political or economic and that religion is often misused.

Mission 3

  • Terrorism uses violence against civilians to spread fear for a cause; its causes are political, ideological and personal as well as religious.
  • Mainstream teaching in every major religion, and Humanism, condemns terrorism.

Mission 4

  • Just war theory (Augustine, Aquinas) sets conditions for going to war (just cause, authority, intention, last resort, success, proportionality) and rules for fighting (discrimination, proportional force).
  • Other traditions have their own conditions: lesser jihad, milchemet mitzvah and reshut, Dharma Yuddha, Dharam Yudh; most Buddhists reject the idea of a just war.

Mission 5

  • A holy war is fought for religious reasons, with claimed divine approval and spiritual reward.
  • The Old Testament contains holy war accounts, rules to limit war (Deuteronomy 20) and visions of peace (Micah 4:3); the Crusades are the Christian example, now rejected by mainstream Churches.
  • Lesser jihad, milchemet mitzvah, Kshatriya dharma and the Sikh saint-soldier are about defence and duty; Buddhism rejects war as unskilful.

Mission 6

  • WMD (nuclear, chemical, biological) kill indiscriminately; deterrence keeps them so they are never used.
  • Catholic teaching condemns indiscriminate destruction (Gaudium et Spes 80); popes have moved from tolerating deterrence (1982) to condemning possession (2017).
  • Utilitarians weigh consequences; most religious traditions reject using WMD, but believers differ about deterrence.
