Mission 1: Is the Torah God's own words, or human words inspired by God?

Make a guess: Is the Torah God's own words, or human words inspired by God?

Mission 1

Three parts: TaNaKh

  • Tenakh is an acronym: Torah + Nevi'im + Ketuvim. 24 books in the Jewish count, mostly in Hebrew.
  • Torah (teaching, instruction): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The heart of the Tenakh.
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): Joshua to Kings (history); Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
  • Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot (scrolls, e.g. Esther, Ruth), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
  • "Torah" can also mean all Jewish teaching, written and oral.
Mission 1

Given at Sinai

  • The Torah's account: after the Exodus, Israel camps at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19). God makes a covenant with the people.
  • God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); Moses receives the rest of the teaching.
  • The people promise to do all God has said (Exodus 24:7); the rabbis read the Hebrew order, "we will do" before "we will hear", as accepting before fully understanding.
  • Moses writes the Torah and gives it to the priests and elders (Deuteronomy 31:9).
  • Revelation is to a whole people, not one individual: the root of Jewish identity.
  • 613 mitzvot (commandments) are counted in the Torah.

> "Now therefore, if ye will hearken unto My voice indeed, and keep My covenant, then ye shall be Mine own treasure from among all peoples; for all the earth is Mine; and ye shall be unto Me a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation." (Exodus 19:5–6, JPS 1917)

> "And he took the book of the covenant, and read in the hearing of the people; and they said: 'All that the LORD hath spoken will we do, and obey.'" (Exodus 24:7, JPS 1917)

Mission 1Diversity

Word of God or inspired word?

  • Orthodox: Torah min ha-shamayim ("Torah from heaven"). God gave the whole Torah to Moses; every letter is divine, complete, unchangeable. Maimonides' 8th Principle.
  • "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans over centuries; it records Israel's encounter with God. Ethical teaching binds; ritual laws may change.
  • Modern scholarship (e.g. the theory of several sources behind the Torah) shaped this view.
  • Masorti (Conservative): divine in origin, passed on and shaped by humans; law still binding but developing.
  • All treat the Torah as holy and central.

Mission 1

The Tenakh in worship and life

  • Synagogue: the Torah is read on Shabbat, Mondays, Thursdays and festivals; the yearly cycle ends and restarts at Simchat Torah. Public reading needs a minyan.
  • A haftarah from the Nevi'im follows the Shabbat reading; the Megillot are read at festivals (Esther at Purim, Ruth at Shavuot).
  • Psalms fill daily prayer; the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9) is in the mezuzah and tefillin.
  • Home and life: study, Shabbat table discussion of the week's portion; guidance for ethics and law.
  • Beliefs shape use: Orthodox Jews follow the laws as divine command; some Reform and Masorti communities read a third of each portion a week (a three-year cycle) and choose which practices to keep.
Mission 1

The Sefer Torah: care and study

  • Sefer Torah: handwritten in Hebrew by a trained scribe (sofer) on parchment, with a quill; one error makes it unfit until corrected.
  • Kept in the Aron Hakodesh (ark), facing Jerusalem; dressed in a mantle; read with a yad (pointer) so hands do not touch the text.
  • People stand when the ark is opened; it is lifted and shown to the congregation. On return: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her" (Proverbs 3:18, JPS 1917).
  • A worn-out scroll is not thrown away but buried in a Jewish cemetery.
  • Yeshiva: a place of study of Tenakh and Talmud, often in pairs (chavruta), where the oral Torah opens the written one.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the Torah still the main authority?

  • Claim: The Torah remains the main authority in Judaism today.
  • For: it is read in every synagogue, holds the 613 mitzvot and is treated with unique honour.
  • For: both Orthodox and Reform Jews call it holy and base their identity on Sinai.
  • Against: in practice the Talmud and later codes decide how laws apply (see Mission 2); rabbis interpret.
  • Against: many secular and Progressive Jews give individual conscience and modern ethics the final say.
  • Judge it by: Does "authority" mean honour or decision-making? Can the written Torah be separated from its interpretation? Which Jews?

Mission 2

Mission 2: If the Torah was written down, why do Jews say Moses also received a Torah by word of mouth?

Make a guess: If the Torah was written down, why do Jews say Moses also received a Torah by word of mouth?

Mission 2

A text that needs explaining

  • The written Torah gives commands without details:
  • "thou shalt not do any manner of work" on the Sabbath (Exodus 20:10, JPS 1917): what counts as work? (The Mishnah later lists 39 categories of work, such as kindling a fire, writing and building: Shabbat 7:2.)
  • "thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thy hand" (Deuteronomy 6:8, JPS 1917): how? (the answer: tefillin).
  • Slaughter "as I have commanded thee" (Deuteronomy 12:21, JPS 1917): where was that commanded? Nowhere in writing.
  • Rabbinic Judaism teaches that God gave Moses an oral Torah with the written one: explanations, methods and laws passed on by word of mouth.
  • Together: Torah she-bikhtav (written) and Torah she-be'al peh (oral).
Mission 2Read the source

The chain of transmission

> "Write thou these words, for after the tenor of these words I have made a covenant with thee and with Israel." (Exodus 34:27, JPS 1917)

  • The Hebrew for "after the tenor of" is literally "by the mouth of": the Talmud reads this as a hint of an oral covenant too (Gittin 60b).
  • Pirkei Avot 1:1 (paraphrase): Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Then pairs of sages, down to Hillel and Shammai (1st century BCE): order gives authority.

Q1 According to Avot 1:1, who received the Torah from the prophets?

Show answer

The Men of the Great Assembly.

Q2 Why does the chain matter for the oral Torah's authority?

Show answer

It claims an unbroken line back to Moses at Sinai.

Mission 2

The Mishnah

  • Why written down: after the Temple's destruction (70 CE) and the failed Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135), sages feared the oral teaching would be lost.
  • Edited c. 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi in the land of Israel, in Hebrew.
  • Content: six orders (Shas): Zera'im (seeds, farming), Mo'ed (festivals), Nashim (women, marriage), Nezikin (damages, civil law), Kodashim (holy things), Tohorot (purity); 63 tractates.
  • Style: short, arranged by topic not by verse; records disputes and minority opinions by name.
  • The teachers of the Mishnah are the Tannaim.
  • Importance: the first authoritative code of the oral Torah; studied as the base text of the Talmud.
Mission 2

The Gemara

  • Gemara: the discussion of the Mishnah by later sages, the Amoraim (c. 200–500 CE), mostly in Aramaic.
  • Style: a debate on paper: question, answer, objection, proof from scripture, digressions. Often no final ruling.
  • Content: halakhah (law) and aggadah (stories, ethics, theology, history).
  • Talmud = Mishnah + Gemara.
  • A printed page: Mishnah and Gemara in the centre; Rashi's commentary on the inner side; Tosafot on the outer side.
  • Importance: it teaches how to reason about law, not only what the law is.

Mission 2

Two Talmuds

  • Jerusalem Talmud (Yerushalmi): from the academies of Galilee (e.g. Tiberias); completed c. 400 CE; Western Aramaic; shorter and less edited; covers the farming laws of the land of Israel.
  • Babylonian Talmud (Bavli): from the academies of Sura and Pumbedita; main editing c. 500 CE (Rav Ashi and Ravina), with later polishing; Eastern Aramaic; much longer, fuller aggadah.
  • The Bavli became the Talmud: Babylonia's academies led the Jewish world for centuries; later authorities ruled it decisive where the two disagree.
  • Origins and transmission: oral discussion over three centuries, edited, then copied by hand; first fully printed in Venice (1520–23).

Mission 2Read the source

"It is not in heaven"

> "It is not in heaven, that thou shouldest say: 'Who shall go up for us to heaven, and bring it unto us'" (Deuteronomy 30:12, JPS 1917)

  • Talmud, Bava Metzia 59b (paraphrase): Rabbi Eliezer proves his view with miracles and a heavenly voice. Rabbi Joshua answers: "It is not in heaven." The majority of the sages decides.
  • The story ends with God smiling: "My children have defeated Me."

Q1 Who decides the law in this story?

Show answer

The majority of the sages, using the Torah's methods.

Q2 What does this suggest about the oral Torah's authority?

Show answer

God gave the Torah to humans to interpret; their reasoned rulings carry authority.

Mission 2Diversity

Whose authority?

  • Orthodox: the oral Torah is divine revelation given at Sinai with the written Torah; the Talmud is binding as rabbis apply it. Many study a page a day (daf yomi), finishing the Talmud every seven and a half years.
  • Masorti (Conservative): authoritative, but developed over history; law can change by rabbinic process.
  • Reform and Liberal: human wisdom from inspired teachers; valuable, not binding; individual informed choice.
  • Karaites (from the 8th century): reject the oral Torah altogether, following the written text only.
  • Challenges: historians trace the Talmud's growth over centuries; the Talmud itself records disagreement. Orthodox reply: disagreement belongs inside one revealed tradition.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the oral Torah divine revelation?

  • Claim: The oral Torah has the same divine authority as the written Torah.
  • For: the written Torah cannot be kept without it (tefillin, Shabbat); the chain of transmission (Avot 1:1); Exodus 34:27.
  • For: Orthodox Jews hold both were given at Sinai.
  • Against: historians see it as developing over centuries; Karaites reject it; the Talmud records many human disagreements.
  • Against: Reform Jews treat it as human wisdom, not binding.
  • Judge it by: Does "divine" require dictation, or can inspired interpretation count (Bava Metzia 59b)? Which community's view?
Mission check

The oral Torah lets an ancient written text speak to new questions; how binding that process is divides Jewish movements.

Mission 3: Without the Temple, how did Jews keep worshipping, and who now decides Jewish law?

Make a guess: Without the Temple, how did Jews keep worshipping, and who now decides Jewish law?

Mission 3

Exile: worship without the Temple

  • 586 BCE: the Babylonians destroy the First Temple in Jerusalem; many Judeans are taken to Babylon.
  • "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." (Psalm 137:1, JPS 1917)
  • Sacrifice could happen only at the Temple. So how could exiles worship?
  • Jewish tradition answers: they gathered to pray, read Torah and study: the beginnings of the synagogue (Greek: "gathering together").
  • The Second Temple was rebuilt (516 BCE) and destroyed by Rome (70 CE). After 70 CE the synagogue became the centre of Jewish worship everywhere.
Mission 3Read the source

A "little sanctuary"

> "Although I have removed them far off among the nations, and although I have scattered them among the countries, yet have I been to them as a little sanctuary in the countries where they are come" (Ezekiel 11:16, JPS 1917)

> "And they read in the book, in the Law of God, distinctly; and they gave the sense, and caused them to understand the reading." (Nehemiah 8:8, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Megillah 29a, paraphrase) reads the "little sanctuary" as the synagogues and houses of study of Babylon.
  • Nehemiah 8: after the return, Ezra reads the Torah aloud to the people, with explanation: a model for synagogue Torah reading.

Q1 Where does Ezekiel say God is present for the exiles?

Show answer

Among them in exile, "as a little sanctuary": not only in the Jerusalem Temple.

Q2 Which two activities in Nehemiah 8:8 still happen in synagogues?

Show answer

Public reading of the Torah and explaining it, as in a sermon or study.

Mission 3

What historians say; after 70 CE

  • The Babylonian origin is the traditional view. Direct evidence from the exile itself has not been found.
  • The earliest firm evidence: inscriptions about Jewish "prayer houses" in Egypt (3rd century BCE) and synagogue buildings in the Land of Israel (1st century BCE–1st century CE).
  • After the Romans destroyed the Second Temple (70 CE), rabbis at Yavneh rebuilt Judaism around Torah study, prayer and the synagogue.
  • Prayer took the place of sacrifice: the three daily services match the Temple's daily offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b).
Mission 3

Three houses in one

  • Beit Knesset (house of meeting/assembly): the community gathers for social life, decisions, celebrations and help.
  • Beit Midrash (house of study): Torah reading and learning; the Yiddish name shul comes from the German for "school".
  • Beit Tefillah (house of prayer): daily and Shabbat services, festivals.
  • A synagogue is defined by these functions, not by its size: any room with a Torah scroll, an ark and a minyan can serve.
Mission 3

Inside: the ark and the Temple

  • Aron Hakodesh (holy ark): a cupboard holding the Sefer Torah scrolls, on the wall facing Jerusalem. It recalls the Ark of the Covenant: "thou shalt put into the ark the testimony which I shall give thee" (Exodus 25:16, JPS 1917).
  • Parochet (curtain) before the ark: recalls the veil before the Holy of Holies (Exodus 26:33).
  • Ner Tamid (eternal light) above it: recalls the lamp that burned "continually" in the Sanctuary (Exodus 27:20, JPS 1917).
  • Bimah: the raised platform from which the Torah is read.
  • Often the Ten Commandments above the ark.
  • So the synagogue recalls the Temple, but is not a replacement Temple: no altar, no sacrifice, no priests leading worship.

Mission 3Diversity

Synagogue worship

  • Orthodox: prayer mostly in Hebrew; men and women seated apart (mechitzah: a screen or gallery); a minyan of ten men for public prayer; no instruments or microphones on Shabbat; the bimah often in the centre; men lead services.
  • Masorti: traditional liturgy in Hebrew; many communities seat men and women together and count women in the minyan.
  • Reform and Liberal: mixed seating; women and men count equally and lead services; more prayers in English; some use instruments; the bimah usually at the front; women rabbis.
  • Hasidic shtiebels: small, informal prayer rooms, often tied to a rebbe's following.
Mission 3

Rabbis: role and authority

  • Rabbi means "my teacher". Rabbis are not priests: they offer no sacrifice and are not needed for prayer; any adult Jew may lead a service.
  • Trained and ordained (semikhah) after study of Talmud and halakhah.
  • Roles: teach; preach; answer questions of halakhah (a posek gives rulings); pastoral care; weddings, funerals, bar/bat mitzvah.
  • Authority: the Torah tells Israel to follow the judges of each generation, "thou shalt not turn aside from the sentence which they shall declare unto thee" (Deuteronomy 17:11, JPS 1917). The rabbis saw themselves as these judges' heirs.
  • Orthodox: a rabbi's halakhic rulings bind those who ask; many Haredi Jews also follow the guidance of leading sages on wider matters.
  • Progressive: the rabbi is a teacher and guide; individuals make informed choices. Women have been rabbis since the 20th century.
Mission 3

The Beth Din

  • Beth Din ("house of judgement"): a rabbinical court of three judges (dayanim); compare Moses appointing judges (Exodus 18:22).
  • Main work today:
  • Divorce: supervising the get (bill of divorce); the problem of agunot ("chained" women whose husbands refuse a get) in a later lesson.
  • Conversion and questions of Jewish status.
  • Kashrut supervision and licensing.
  • Arbitration of civil and business disputes between Jews who agree to it; in the UK such decisions can be binding under arbitration law, but never override UK criminal or family law.
  • Each movement has its own: e.g. the London Beth Din (United Synagogue, Orthodox) and the Reform and Masorti batei din. Orthodox courts do not recognise Progressive rulings on status.

Mission 3

The synagogue's changing role today

  • Community centre: kosher kitchens and halls; youth groups; care for the elderly; food banks; security teams.
  • Education: cheder (Hebrew classes for children), adult study, bar/bat mitzvah preparation.
  • Life cycle: weddings, bar/bat mitzvah, mourning prayers.
  • Changes: in Britain, membership of mainstream synagogues has fallen over recent decades while strictly Orthodox communities have grown; small communities merge; Jewish community centres and schools now share roles once held only by the synagogue.
  • Online: since 2020 many Progressive synagogues stream services; Orthodox synagogues do not use technology on Shabbat or festivals.
  • From exile to today: a portable institution that adapts to where Jews live.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the synagogue the heart of the Jewish community?

  • Claim: The synagogue is the heart of the Jewish community.
  • For: It joins prayer, study and meeting in one place; since the exile and 70 CE it has kept Jewish identity alive in every land (Ezekiel 11:16).
  • For: Public prayer needs a minyan; Torah reading, festivals and life-cycle events gather there.
  • Against: The home is central too: Shabbat, Pesach, kashrut and teaching children.
  • Against: Many Jews rarely attend; schools, community centres and online life now share its roles.
  • Judgement criteria: Main use (prayer? study? meeting?) or main purpose (keeping the people together)? Does the answer differ for Orthodox and secular Jews?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah tells of its own giving at Sinai through Moses, within a covenant.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the Torah is the complete, unchangeable Word of God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Reform and Liberal Jews see it as inspired but humanly written.
  • The Sefer Torah is read publicly, studied, and treated with great care: ark, mantle, yad, burial.

Mission 2

  • The oral Torah explains the written Torah; Pirkei Avot 1:1 traces its chain from Moses to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Mishnah (Hebrew, c. 200 CE, six orders) + Gemara (Aramaic discussion) = Talmud; the Babylonian Talmud became more authoritative than the Jerusalem Talmud.

Mission 3

  • Tradition traces the synagogue to the Babylonian exile ("a little sanctuary", Ezekiel 11:16); after 70 CE it replaced the Temple as the centre of worship.
  • Beit knesset, beit midrash, beit tefillah: meeting, study, prayer; the ark, parochet and ner tamid recall the Temple.
  • Rabbis teach and rule on halakhah (Deuteronomy 17:11); a beth din of three handles divorce, conversion, kashrut and disputes.
