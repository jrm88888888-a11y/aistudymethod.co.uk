Hinduism: Beliefs & Practices

This mini-lesson works through the Edexcel GCSE Hinduism option: the key beliefs — Brahman, atman, the deities, dharma, karma, samsara and moksha — and the key practices such as puja, festivals and pilgrimage, and how these beliefs shape how Hindus live.

The big idea

One reality, many forms

At the heart of Hinduism is Brahman — the one ultimate reality behind everything. Hinduism holds two ideas together that at first seem opposite:

  • There is one ultimate reality, Brahman, present in all things.
  • Yet Hindus also worship many deities — Brahma, Vishnu, Shiva, Rama, Krishna, the goddess Devi and more.
  • For many Hindus, these deities are not separate gods but forms or aspects of the one Brahman.

Spec focus: the specification asks you to explain the nature of Brahman and the deities, the belief in atman, and the key ideas of dharma, karma, samsara and moksha — plus how Hindus put belief into practice.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Brahman — the one ultimate reality, the source of everything.
  • Atman — the eternal self or soul within each living being, which is part of Brahman.
  • Dharma — duty and righteousness: living rightly according to your place in life.
  • Karma — action and its consequences: good and bad actions shape your future.
  • Samsara — the cycle of birth, death and rebirth.
  • Moksha — liberation: release from samsara, the atman reuniting with Brahman.

Common mix-up: Brahman is the ultimate reality; Brahma is the creator deity within the Trimurti; and a Brahmin is a member of the priestly varna. Three different words — don't confuse them.

Atman

The eternal self within

Hindus teach that within every living being is the atman — the eternal self or soul. The atman is not the body or the personality; it is unchanging and everlasting, and it is a part of Brahman. Because the same atman-Brahman reality is in all beings, Hindus see the divine within every person.

Scripture: "The atman is never born and never dies... it is unborn, eternal and everlasting." (Bhagavad Gita 2:20) — the self is not destroyed when the body dies.
The deities

The Trimurti and the avatars

The deities are the forms of Brahman that Hindus worship. Three of the most important make up the Trimurti — three deities with three roles in the life of the universe:

  • Brahma — the creator of the universe.
  • Vishnu — the preserver, who protects and sustains the world.
  • Shiva — the destroyer, whose destruction clears the way for new creation.

Vishnu is believed to come to earth in different forms called avatars — for example Rama and Krishna — to restore dharma. Hindus also worship goddesses, together known as Devi (for example Lakshmi, Saraswati and Durga), who represent the feminine divine and the power (shakti) of Brahman.

Remember: for many Hindus these deities are not separate gods but forms or aspects of the one Brahman — which is why Hinduism holds together "one reality" and the worship of many deities.

Dharma

Duty and righteousness

Hindus believe in dharma — duty, righteousness and living rightly. Doing your dharma means acting well and fulfilling your responsibilities according to your stage and place in life. One form of this is varnashrama-dharma: the duties that belong to your varna (social role or class) and your ashrama (stage of life, such as student, householder or retirement).

Scripture: "It is better to do one's own dharma imperfectly than to do another's dharma well." (Bhagavad Gita 3:35) — do your own duty faithfully.

Karma & samsara

Action and the cycle of rebirth

Karma means action and its consequences: good actions bring good results and bad actions bring bad results, in this life or a future one. Karma shapes samsara — the cycle of birth, death and rebirth (reincarnation) that the atman passes through.

Scripture: "As a person casts off worn-out clothes and puts on new ones, so the atman casts off worn-out bodies and enters new ones." (Bhagavad Gita 2:22) — a picture of rebirth.
Moksha

Liberation — the ultimate goal

The final goal of Hindu life is moksha — liberation, release from the cycle of samsara. In moksha the atman is reunited with Brahman and is free from rebirth. It is reached by living out dharma, building good karma, devotion (bhakti), knowledge and self-discipline over many lifetimes.

Careful: moksha is not a heaven you are simply sent to — it is release from rebirth, the atman becoming one with Brahman.

The purusharthas

The four aims of life

Hinduism teaches four aims of life — the purusharthas — that guide a balanced, well-lived life:

  • Dharma — living rightly; duty and moral order.
  • Artha — earning wealth and prosperity honestly.
  • Kama — enjoying life's pleasures within dharma.
  • Moksha — liberation, the highest and final aim.

The first three (dharma, artha, kama) support a good worldly life; moksha is the ultimate goal that the others prepare a person for.

Watch out: the purusharthas include both dharma and moksha — the same words appear elsewhere in the topic. Here they are two of the four aims of life.

Practices

Puja — worship at home and the mandir

Hindus express their beliefs through puja — worship — offered both at home and in the mandir (temple). Puja often includes offering light, flowers, food, incense and prayers before an image of a deity.

  • Murtis — images or statues of the deities — are used as an aid to worship, helping the worshipper focus on the divine (saguna Brahman).
  • Havan — a ritual in which offerings are made into a sacred fire, accompanied by mantras.
  • Many Hindus keep a home shrine for daily puja, and visit the mandir for communal worship and festivals.

Careful: Hindus do not worship the murti as if the statue itself were God. The murti is an aid that helps focus worship on the deity it represents.

Practices

Festivals and pilgrimage

Hindu belief is also expressed through festivals and pilgrimage:

  • Diwali — the festival of light, celebrating the victory of good over evil and light over darkness; homes are lit with lamps (diyas) and Lakshmi is worshipped for prosperity.
  • Holi — the festival of colours in spring, celebrating good over evil and the arrival of spring, with people throwing coloured powders.
  • Pilgrimage — many Hindus travel to sacred sites, especially the river Ganges and the holy city of Varanasi, to bathe in the sacred waters and seek spiritual benefit.

These practices express beliefs: Diwali and Holi celebrate the triumph of good (linked to dharma), and pilgrimage to the Ganges reflects the search for purity and progress toward moksha.

Memory hook: Diwali = diyas / lights; Holi = hues / colours.

Why it matters

How these beliefs shape lives

Exam questions often ask about the impact of Hindu beliefs. These beliefs are not just ideas — they shape how Hindus live:

  • Belief in atman in all beings encourages respect for all life and often a vegetarian diet and non-violence (ahimsa).
  • Belief in karma and samsara encourages good, moral actions, since actions shape future lives.
  • Belief in dharma encourages people to carry out their duties faithfully at each stage of life.
  • The goal of moksha motivates devotion (bhakti), worship and self-discipline.
  • Belief in saguna Brahman shapes worship through murtis, puja, havan and festivals such as Diwali and Holi.

Recap
