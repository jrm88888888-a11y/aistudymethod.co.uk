Mission 1: Can one ultimate reality be beyond all description and also a God you can love?

Make a guess: Can one ultimate reality be beyond all description and also a God you can love?

Mission 1

Brahman: ultimate reality

  • Most Hindus believe in one ultimate reality behind everything: Brahman.
  • Your spec's words: Brahman as spirit, ultimate reality and absolute truth.
  • Brahman is eternal and unchanging; the source, support and end of all that exists.
  • Many Hindus simply call Brahman God.
  • Do not confuse: Brahman (ultimate reality) · Brahma (one deity, the creator in the Trimurti) · Brahmin (the priestly class).
  • Hindus understand Brahman in two main ways: nirguna (without qualities) and saguna (with qualities).
Mission 1Read the source

Brahman in the Upanishads

"The Being who remains awake while all sleep, who grants all desires, That is pure, That is Brahman, That alone is said to be immortal. On That all the worlds rest." (Katha Upanishad 2.2.8, Paramananda)

  • Verses 6–7 just before: Yama promises to teach "the secret of the eternal Brahman", and says beings are reborn according to their deeds and knowledge (paraphrase).

Q1 Which words in the Katha passage show Brahman is ultimate?

Show answer

"That alone is said to be immortal"; "On That all the worlds rest".

Mission 1

Nirguna Brahman

  • Nirguna: "without qualities" (attributes).
  • Brahman is beyond every name, shape and description; no human idea can contain it.
  • The Upanishads describe it by negation: neti, neti, "not this, not this".
  • Impersonal: not a "someone" to pray to, but pure being, consciousness and bliss.
  • Taittiriya Upanishad 2.7.1–2 (paraphrase): before creation was "non-being"; from it being came forth; it made itself. When a person finds fearlessness in what is invisible, bodiless, indefinable and unsupported, they are free from fear.
  • "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind." (Kena Upanishad 1.3, Paramananda)
Mission 1

Saguna Brahman

  • Saguna: "with qualities".
  • Brahman known as a personal God with qualities such as love, power, beauty and mercy, who can be known, worshipped and loved.
  • Many Hindus know saguna Brahman as Vishnu (and his avatars Rama and Krishna), Shiva, or the Goddess (Devi, Shakti).
  • Rig Veda 1.154.1–2 (paraphrase): the poet praises the mighty deeds of Vishnu, who measured out the earthly regions in three wide strides; all living beings dwell within his strides.

Mission 1

One reality, two ways

  • Most Hindus see nirguna and saguna as one Brahman, understood in two ways, not two Gods.
  • An image often used: water is one substance, whether as formless vapour or as ice with a shape.
  • Humans need form to relate to the infinite: saguna Brahman lets people love and serve God.
  • In daily life (saguna): worship (puja) before a murti, singing devotional songs (bhajans), festivals, prayer to a chosen deity.
  • In daily life (nirguna): meditation, study of the Upanishads, seeking knowledge (jnana) that sees Brahman in everything.
  • Many Hindus do both: worship at the mandir and meditate on the formless.
Mission 1Diversity

Monist and monotheist views

  • Monist (Advaita Vedanta, "non-dual"; taught by Shankara, traditionally 8th century CE): only Brahman is ultimately real.
  • For Advaita, nirguna Brahman is the highest truth. A personal God (Ishvara) is real at the level of everyday experience, but not ultimate.
  • Monotheist (many Vaishnavas, e.g. followers of Ramanuja or Madhva; also many Shaivas and Shaktas): Brahman is supremely personal.
  • For them, saguna is the highest: Vishnu (or Krishna, Shiva, Devi) is the Supreme Being. "Without qualities" means without any imperfect qualities.
  • Both views hold that there is one ultimate reality; they differ on whether the personal or the impersonal is final.
Mission 1

Vaikuntha and spiritual worlds

  • Vaikuntha: the eternal spiritual world of Vishnu, beyond the material universe. Its name suggests "without anxiety".
  • There is no birth, death or decay; liberated souls live in Vishnu's presence and serve him with love.
  • Other traditions speak of Goloka (Krishna's realm) and Kailasa (Shiva's).
  • For many Vaishnavas, moksha (liberation) means reaching Vaikuntha. For Advaitins, moksha means realising oneness with Brahman; spiritual worlds are not the final goal.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Brahman is best understood as nirguna."

  • Reasons for: the Upanishads say Brahman is beyond eye, speech and mind (Kena 1.3). Any quality would limit the infinite. Advaita teachers (the school that holds only Brahman is ultimately real) hold nirguna as the highest truth. It avoids picturing God as a human.
  • Reasons against: in the Gita, Krishna reveals himself as the Supreme, and says the path to the unmanifest is harder for embodied beings (Bhagavad Gita 12.5, paraphrase). Love (bhakti) needs a personal God. Most Hindus worship Brahman in saguna form.
  • Another view: both are true: one reality seen from two sides, as the Gita and many teachers hold.
  • Judge it by: Which scriptures carry most weight? Can the infinite have qualities? Which view makes sense of Hindu worship?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Hindu beliefs about the nature of Brahman.

  • Belief 1: Brahman is nirguna, beyond all qualities. Source: "There the eye does not go, nor speech, nor mind" (Kena Upanishad 1.3). Development: so some Hindus seek Brahman through meditation and knowledge rather than images.
  • Belief 2: Brahman is also saguna, with qualities. Development: Brahman can be known as a personal God such as Vishnu or Shiva, so many Hindus worship murtis with love (bhakti).
  • (c) 5-mark tip: two beliefs, each developed, and one source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 2: If God is everywhere, why do Hindus also look for God inside their own heart?

Mission 2

One reality, three ways of knowing it

  • Many Hindus say the one ultimate reality can be known in three ways.
  • A well-known verse of the Bhagavata Purana (1.2.11) names them: Brahman, Paramatma and Bhagavan (paraphrase).
  • Everywhere: Brahman, present in all things, non-personal.
  • Within the heart: the divine living inside each being.
  • Beyond: a personal, loving God.
  • Your spec calls the second feature Antaryami ("inner controller"). Paramatma ("supreme self") names the same idea.
  • Not three gods: one divine, seen from three distances.
Mission 2

Brahman: everywhere

  • Brahman is the divine reality that pervades everything: every atom, every person, every animal.
  • Seen this way, Brahman is non-personal: like light or space, not someone you talk to.
  • "All this is Brahman" (Chandogya Upanishad 3.14.1, paraphrase).
  • Significance: if the divine is in everything, everything deserves respect.
Mission 2

Antaryami: within the heart

  • The same divine is present inside every living being, guiding it from within.
  • "The Purusha (Self), of the size of a thumb, resides in the middle of the body" (Katha Upanishad 4.12, Paramananda).
  • "There lives a Master in the hearts of men" (Bhagavad Gita 18.61, Arnold).
  • Careful: the atman is the individual's own eternal self. Antaryami is God dwelling beside it in the heart (in most theistic readings).
  • Significance: God is as close as your own heart; Hindus meditate to become aware of this inner presence.

Mission 2

Bhagavan: beyond, a personal loving God

  • Bhagavan means "the one who possesses all excellences": beauty, power, knowledge, fame, wealth, detachment.
  • God as a person who can be known, loved, served and spoken to.
  • Worshipped through forms such as Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • God returns love: "A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made" (Bhagavad Gita 9.26, Arnold).
Mission 2Read the source

Mundaka Upanishad 2.1

Mundaka Upanishad, part 2, section 1 (paraphrase):

  • As thousands of sparks fly from a blazing fire, so all beings come from the Imperishable and return to it (2.1.1).
  • The cosmic Person: fire is his head, the sun and moon his eyes, the wind his breath, the earth his feet; he is the inner self of all beings (2.1.4).
  • This Person is all this; whoever knows him hidden in the cave of the heart cuts the knot of ignorance (2.1.10).

Mission 2Diversity

Which feature comes first?

  • All three features are widely accepted. Hindu traditions differ on which is highest.
  • Advaita Vedanta (Shankara, c. 8th century CE): non-personal Brahman is the highest truth; the personal God is real for worship, but is Brahman seen through maya. Liberation: knowing that atman is Brahman.
  • Vaishnava traditions (e.g. Ramanuja; Gaudiya Vaishnavas and ISKCON): Bhagavan (Vishnu or Krishna) is the fullest truth. ISKCON teaches that the everywhere-Brahman is like the sun's light, and Bhagavan like the sun itself.
  • Shaiva traditions: Shiva is Bhagavan, and also pervades and indwells all.
  • Many Hindus combine all three in daily life without ranking them.

Mission 2

Why it matters in Hindu life

  • Everywhere → respect for all life: ahimsa (non-violence), care for animals and nature; "namaste" honours the divine in another person.
  • Within the heart → meditation, japa and quiet prayer: looking inward to find God.
  • Beyond → devotion (bhakti): puja to a murti, singing bhajans, offering food, a loving relationship with God.
  • One person may do all three in a day.
Mission 2

Many paths, one truth: religious pluralism

  • Religious pluralism: the view that different religions can be genuine paths to the same ultimate truth.
  • Rig Veda 1.164.46: ekam sat vipra bahudha vadanti: "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names" (paraphrase).
  • Bhagavad Gita 4.11: in whatever way people come to Krishna, he receives them (paraphrase).
  • The three features help: if the one divine is everywhere and in every heart, people of every faith may meet it.
  • Sri Ramakrishna (1836–1886) taught that many faiths are many paths to one God; Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) brought this message to the West (1893).
  • Not all Hindus agree to the same degree: many Vaishnavas respect other faiths but hold that devotion to Krishna or Vishnu is the fullest path.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Bhagavan is the most important feature of the divine."

  • Reasons for: a personal God can be loved and can love back (Gita 9.26). Most Hindu worship (puja, bhajans, festivals) is directed to personal forms. Vaishnavas see Bhagavan as the source of Brahman (Gita 14.27).
  • Reasons against: Advaitins hold that non-personal Brahman is the final truth; personal forms are a step towards it. The Upanishads stress Brahman beyond all form (Kena 1.4–1.8). Antaryami makes God closest of all: within the heart.
  • Another view: the three are one reality; ranking them misses the point. Different features suit different people and stages of life.
  • Judge it by: Which feature shapes most Hindus' daily practice? Which do the scriptures stress most? Can one reality have a "most important" feature?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Hindu beliefs about the features of the divine.

  • Belief 1: the divine is everywhere as Brahman. Source: "All this is Brahman" (Chandogya Upanishad 3.14.1). Development: so many Hindus practise ahimsa, because harming any living being harms something in which the divine is present.
  • Belief 2: the divine lives within the heart as Antaryami. Source: "There lives a Master in the hearts of men" (Gita 18.61). Development: so Hindus meditate and pray quietly, seeking the God who is already inside them.
  • (c) 5-mark tip: two developed beliefs plus one source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 3

One and many

  • Most Hindus believe in one Supreme reality (Brahman).
  • That reality can show itself in many forms: the deities (devas, male; devis, female).
  • Each deity shows a different quality: power to create, to protect, to transform, wisdom, wealth, strength.
  • A Hindu often has an ishta-devata: a chosen deity to whom they feel closest.
  • Analogy many Hindus use: one actor, many roles; or one sun, many rays.
Mission 3

The Trimurti

  • Trimurti = "three forms". Three deities linked to the cycle of the universe:
  • Brahma, the creator: four heads facing every direction; holds the Vedas; rides a swan (hamsa). Very few temples are dedicated to him.
  • Vishnu, the preserver: holds conch, discus, mace and lotus; often blue; rides the eagle Garuda; comes to earth as avatars to restore dharma.
  • Shiva, the destroyer: ends what is old so new can begin; trident, third eye, the bull Nandi; worshipped as the linga; as Nataraja he dances the universe in and out of being.
  • Creation, preservation and destruction are one continuous cycle.
Mission 3Read the source

Vishnu's three strides

Rig Veda 1.22.16–21 (paraphrase):

  • Vishnu strode through this whole world; three times he set down his foot (1.22.17).
  • With three steps he upholds the order of the world; the protector whom none can deceive (1.22.18).
  • The wise always see Vishnu's highest step, like an eye fixed in the sky (1.22.20).

Mission 3

Shakti and the Tridevi

  • Shakti = divine power or energy, understood as female. Without Shakti, many Hindus say, the gods cannot act.
  • Each god has a consort who is his Shakti: the Tridevi ("three goddesses"):
  • Saraswati (with Brahma): knowledge, music, the arts; white sari, veena, swan; honoured by students.
  • Lakshmi (with Vishnu): wealth, good fortune, beauty; lotus, gold coins; welcomed at Diwali.
  • Parvati (with Shiva): devotion, the loving mother; mother of Ganesha. Her fierce forms are Durga (who defeats the buffalo-demon Mahishasura) and Kali.
  • Shaktas worship the Goddess (Devi) as the Supreme herself.
Mission 3Diversity

Who is supreme?

  • Vaishnavas: Vishnu (or Krishna) is the Supreme. Brahma is created by him; Shiva is a great devotee or a lesser power. The Trimurti are not equal.
  • Shaivas: Shiva is Mahadeva, the great God. He alone does all the work of the Trimurti (creating, preserving, destroying), and more; Brahma and Vishnu act under him.
  • Shaktas: the Goddess (Devi) is the Supreme; the male gods depend on her power.
  • Smartas (often linked to Shankara): worship five deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Ganesha, Surya) as equal forms of one Brahman.
  • Many Hindus in Britain visit one mandir with shrines to several deities and see no contradiction.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Hindus who worship many deities are really monotheists."

  • Reasons for: most Hindus say the deities are forms of one Supreme (Brahman). Scripture teaches unity: the Kurma Purana (1.9) has Shiva say he and Vishnu are not separate; Rig Veda 1.164.46: "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names" (paraphrase). Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each worship one Supreme.
  • Reasons against: in practice people pray to different deities for different needs. Some Hindus in villages honour local gods and goddesses as real, distinct powers. Advaitins say the deepest truth is non-personal Brahman, so "monotheism" (one personal God) is not quite right either.
  • Other labels scholars use: henotheism (worshipping one god without denying others); monism (all is one reality).
  • Judge it by: What do Hindus themselves say? Does worship of a chosen deity exclude others? Does a Western word fit at all?

Mission 4

Avatar: God comes down

  • Avatara (avatar) = "descent": the Supreme comes down into the world in a physical form.
  • Most avatars are of Vishnu, the preserver.
  • Why? Krishna explains: "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape" (Bhagavad Gita 4.7–8, Arnold).
  • Avatars come again and again, in every age, to protect the good and restore dharma.
  • An avatar is God's free choice; humans are reborn because of karma.
Mission 4

The ten avatars of Vishnu

  • The best-known list (Dashavatara): Matsya (fish), Kurma (tortoise), Varaha (boar), Narasimha (man-lion), Vamana (dwarf), Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the Buddha (in some lists Balarama), Kalki (still to come).
  • Each appears when dharma is under threat: e.g. Varaha lifts the earth from the ocean; Vamana's three steps win back the worlds.
  • Vamana's three steps echo Vishnu's three strides in Rig Veda 1.22 (see Mission 3).
  • Kalki is expected at the end of the present age, the Kali Yuga.
  • The two most loved: Rama and Krishna.
Mission 4

Rama: the model of dharma

  • Hero of the Ramayana (traditionally by the sage Valmiki).
  • Prince of Ayodhya. To keep his father's promise, he accepts 14 years' exile with his wife Sita and brother Lakshmana.
  • The demon king Ravana kidnaps Sita; with Hanuman and an army of monkeys, Rama rescues her.
  • His return to Ayodhya is remembered at Diwali in many regions.
  • Called maryada purushottama: the ideal man who keeps every duty: as son, husband, brother and king.
Mission 4

Krishna: God of love and teacher of the Gita

  • Born in Mathura; grows up among cowherds in Vrindavan.
  • Stories of the child Krishna stealing butter, and the young Krishna playing his flute for the gopis (cowherd girls), especially Radha: pictures of the soul's love for God (bhakti).
  • As an adult, Krishna is Arjuna's charioteer and teaches him the Bhagavad Gita before the great battle of the Mahabharata.

Mission 4Diversity

Who counts as an avatar?

  • Vaishnavas: avatars are central; Rama and Krishna are fully God.
  • Shaivas: Shiva appears in many forms (e.g. Nataraja), but the avatar idea matters far less.
  • The Buddha: many Hindu lists make him Vishnu's ninth avatar. Buddhists do not accept this.
  • Jesus: some Hindus (e.g. many followers of Ramakrishna and Vivekananda) honour Jesus as an avatar. Most Christians reject this: they believe Jesus is God incarnate once, uniquely; avatars come many times.
  • Arya Samaj (founded by Dayananda Saraswati, 1875) rejects avatars and murti worship: God is formless.

Mission 4

Murti: the form of God in worship

  • Murti = "form" or "embodiment": a sacred image of a deity, of stone, metal, wood or clay.
  • In a mandir it is installed with a ceremony, prana pratishtha ("establishing the life-breath"): many Hindus believe the deity is then present in it.
  • It is treated as an honoured guest: woken, bathed, dressed, offered food, put to rest.
  • Darshan: seeing the deity and being seen by it, a blessing.
  • Food offered becomes prasad, blessed food shared with worshippers.
Mission 4Read the source

Q1 According to 12.5, why might worshipping God in a form be easier for most people?

Q2 What does 9.26 suggest God values in an offering?

Show answer

Mission 4Diversity

Presence or symbol?

  • Real presence: many Vaishnava and temple traditions hold that God truly dwells in the consecrated murti. Sri Vaishnavas call it archa avatara: God's descent into a worshipped image.
  • Aid to focus: many Hindus, including many Advaitins, see the murti as a symbol that steadies the mind on God; the goal is to see God everywhere.
  • Rejection: the Arya Samaj teaches that God is formless and should not be worshipped in images.
  • Many Hindus dislike the word "idol": they do not worship stone, but God through the form.

Mission 4Evaluate

"A murti is only a symbol of God."

  • Reasons for: God is beyond all form (nirguna Brahman); Gita 12.5 admits form is a help for those in a body. Many Hindus, including many Advaitins, say the murti points beyond itself. The Arya Samaj rejects image worship altogether.
  • Reasons against: prana pratishtha is believed to make the deity present; the murti is cared for as a living guest. Sri Vaishnavas call it an avatar (archa avatara). Gita 9.26: God accepts what is offered, as if really receiving it.
  • Judge it by: What do worshippers experience in darshan? What do the scriptures and temple rites assume? Can something be a symbol and a real presence at once?
Mission check

Avatar and murti answer the same longing: a God who comes close enough to be seen, loved and served.

Mission 5

The atman: the real you

  • Atman = the eternal self, or soul, in every living being: humans, animals, plants.
  • It is not the body and not the mind: both are part of prakriti (matter) and change; the atman does not.
  • "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Bhagavad Gita 2.20, Arnold).
Mission 5Read the source

Changing clothes

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / "These will I wear to-day!" / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh." (Bhagavad Gita 2.22, Arnold)

Q1 In the image, what are the clothes, and who is the wearer?

Show answer

The clothes are bodies; the wearer is the atman.

Q2 What does this suggest about how a Hindu might face death?

Show answer

Calmly: death is a change of clothes, not the end of the self.

Mission 5

Samsara: the wheel of rebirth

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth (reincarnation).
  • At death the atman leaves the body and takes a new one: human, animal or other, in this world or another loka.
  • What decides the next birth? Karma: the results of past actions.
  • Samsara is seen as full of suffering and impermanence. The goal is to escape it.
Mission 5Read the source

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 4.4 (paraphrase):

  • As a caterpillar at the end of a blade of grass draws itself onto the next, so the self, leaving this body, draws itself to a new one (4.4.3).
  • As a person acts, so they become: good deeds make one good, bad deeds bad (4.4.5).
  • A person is made of desire: as is the desire, so is the will; as is the will, so is the deed (4.4.5).
  • One who is free of desire, whose only desire is the Self, does not go anywhere: being Brahman, they go to Brahman (4.4.6).
  • Interpretations: Advaitins read 4.4.6 as realising that the atman is Brahman. Theistic Vedantins (e.g. Ramanuja) read it as the self reaching God, while staying distinct.

Q1 What carries the self from one body to the next?

Show answer

Desire, and the deeds that come from it.

Q2 Why might two Hindu schools read 4.4.6 differently?

Show answer

One sees identity with Brahman; the other, closeness to God.

Mission 5

Why we stay stuck: avidya, maya, attachment

  • Avidya = ignorance: not knowing who we really are; thinking "I am this body".
  • Maya = the power that makes the changing world seem to be all there is.
  • From ignorance come selfishness, greed and anger: we grab for things that cannot satisfy the atman.
  • Detachment (vairagya): acting and enjoying life without clinging. "The soul which is not moved ... / Takes sorrow and takes joy indifferently, / Lives in the life undying!" (Bhagavad Gita 2.15, Arnold)
Mission 5

Moksha: liberation

  • Moksha: release from samsara; the atman is free and is never reborn.
  • Reached through knowledge, devotion, selfless action or meditation.
Mission 5Diversity

Atman and Brahman: one or two?

  • Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual"; Shankara, c. 8th century CE): atman is Brahman. The sense of being separate is avidya. Moksha = realising this identity. A monist view.
  • Vishishtadvaita ("qualified non-dual"; Ramanuja, traditionally 1017–1137): atman is a real part of God, as a body belongs to a soul: one with God, yet distinct.
  • Dvaita ("dual"; Madhva, 1238–1317): atman and God (Vishnu) are eternally distinct. Moksha = eternal, blissful service to God. A monotheist view.
  • The sage Uddalaka teaches his son Svetaketu: as salt dissolved in water is everywhere in it though unseen, so is the Self; "tat tvam asi": "you are that" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.13, paraphrase). Advaitins read this as identity; Ramanuja and Madhva read it differently.

?A follower of Madhva's Dvaita Vedanta reads "tat tvam asi" ("you are that"). Which interpretation would they most likely give?
Mission 5

Why it matters today

  • Respect for life: the same kind of atman lives in every being, so many Hindus are vegetarian and practise ahimsa.
  • Facing death: funerals (antyesti) include cremation, which many Hindus believe helps release the atman; ashes are often scattered in the Ganges or another river.
  • Living well: every action shapes the next life; the long goal is moksha.
  • Equality: many Hindus stress that the atman has no caste, gender or race.
  • Compare: most Christians believe in one earthly life, then judgement and the resurrection of the body; Hindus believe in many lives and liberation of the atman.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The atman and Brahman are identical."

  • Reasons for: "tat tvam asi" (Chandogya 6.8.7 and following) read literally; the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (4.4.6) says one free of desire, being Brahman, goes to Brahman (paraphrase). Advaita explains why the self feels limited: avidya. Mystics describe experiences of unity.
  • Reasons against: if the atman were already Brahman, how could it be ignorant? (Ramanuja's objection). The Gita speaks of loving devotion to Krishna, which needs two. Dvaita: identity would leave no one to enjoy God's love.
  • Judge it by: Which reading fits the most scriptures? What happens to devotion (bhakti) on each view? Does the view explain suffering and ignorance?
Mission 5Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain Hindu teachings about samsara and moksha.

  • Teaching 1: the atman is reborn in body after body (samsara). Source: "So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2.22). Development: so Hindus see death as a change, not an end, and try to live well because their deeds shape their next life.
  • Teaching 2: the goal is moksha, release from samsara. Development: Advaitins see it as realising that atman is Brahman; Dvaita Hindus as eternal loving service to God; either way, it is the final end of suffering.
Mission check

?Anika's great-grandmother dies peacefully. At the funeral a Hindu priest says: "Her body has been laid aside, but she has not ended. May she be free and not return." Which beliefs lie behind his words?

For Hindus, death is a change of clothes for the atman; the real hope is not a better life, but no more rebirth.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Most Hindus believe Brahman is the one ultimate reality: spirit, absolute truth, the source and support of all.
  • For Hindus, nirguna Brahman is beyond all qualities; saguna Brahman is Brahman with qualities, known as a personal God.
  • Monists (Advaita) put nirguna first; monotheists (e.g. many Vaishnavas) put the personal God first.
  • Vaikuntha is Vishnu's eternal spiritual world.

Mission 2

  • Many Hindus know the one divine in three ways: Brahman (everywhere, non-personal), Antaryami or Paramatma (within the heart), Bhagavan (beyond, a personal loving God).
  • Traditions rank them differently: Advaitins put non-personal Brahman first; Vaishnavas put Bhagavan first; many Hindus hold all three.
  • If the divine is everywhere and in every heart, many Hindus conclude that other religions can be genuine paths to the same truth (pluralism).

Mission 3

  • Many Hindus see the deities as forms of one Supreme (saguna Brahman): Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva destroys; Saraswati, Lakshmi and Parvati are their Shakti.
  • Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each see a different form (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess) as Supreme; Smartas treat several forms as equal.

Mission 4

  • Hindus believe an avatar is a descent of the Supreme (mostly Vishnu) to restore dharma "from age to age" (Gita 4.7–8); Rama and Krishna are the best loved.
  • A murti is a sacred form; after prana pratishtha many Hindus believe the deity is present; others see it as an aid to focus; the Arya Samaj rejects image worship.

Mission 5

  • Hindus believe the atman is the eternal self, distinct from body and mind; it moves from body to body in samsara, shaped by karma.
  • In Hindu teaching, avidya (ignorance), maya and attachment keep the atman bound; moksha is release, possible even while alive (jivanmukti) for some schools.
  • Advaita: atman is Brahman. Vishishtadvaita: atman is part of God, yet distinct. Dvaita: atman is eternally distinct from God.
