AP Human Geography

  • PSO-5.A.1 agricultural practices are influenced by the physical environment and climatic conditions, such as the Mediterranean climate and tropical climates
  • PSO-5.A.2 intensive farming practices include market gardening, plantation agriculture and mixed crop-livestock systems
  • PSO-5.A.3 extensive farming practices include shifting cultivation, nomadic herding and ranching
Mission 1

The land sets the limits

  • Climate: temperature, rainfall, and the length of the growing season.
  • Soil: how fertile it is and how well it holds water.
  • Relief: flat land is easy to plow; steep slopes need terraces or are left to grazing or trees.
  • Farmers choose crops and methods that fit these conditions, or pay to change them (irrigation, fertilizer, greenhouses).
Mission 1

Mediterranean climate

  • Mild, rainy winters and warm, dry summers.
  • Found in only five small regions: around the Mediterranean Sea, central Chile, South Africa's Cape, southwestern and southern Australia, and southern California with northern Baja California.
  • Covers about 2% of Earth's land.
  • Farming fits the dry summer: drought-tolerant olives, grapes and citrus, winter-grown grains, and irrigation for summer crops.
Mission 1

Tropical climates

  • Hot all year. Some areas are wet all year (rainforest); others have a wet and a dry season.
  • Long growing seasons suit plantation crops: coffee, cocoa, bananas, sugarcane, oil palm.
  • Heavy rain washes nutrients out of many rainforest soils, so they lose fertility fast once cleared. This is one reason for shifting cultivation.

?A region has mild, rainy winters and hot, dry summers. Which farming pattern fits best?
Mission 1

Intensive and extensive

  • Intensive farming: high inputs of labor, money, machines or chemicals on small areas, high output per acre.
  • Extensive farming: low inputs spread over large areas, low output per acre.
  • Fertilizer use, 2024, kg per hectare of arable land: Netherlands 269.6; USA 127.8; Niger 0.8.
  • Cereal yield, 2023, kg per hectare: USA 8,330; Netherlands 8,080; Australia 2,691; Niger 489.
Mission 1

Three intensive systems

  • Market gardening (truck farming): fruit, vegetables and flowers grown intensively, often near cities or in greenhouses, sold fresh.
  • Plantation agriculture: large estates growing one cash crop for export, mostly in tropical climates, with many workers and processing on site.
  • Mixed crop-livestock: crops and animals on the same farm; crops feed the animals, manure feeds the soil. Common in humid mid-latitude regions such as the US Corn Belt and much of Europe.
Quick check
?A farm grows corn and soybeans, feeds most of them to its own hogs, and spreads the manure on its fields. Which system is this?
Mission 1

Three extensive systems

  • Shifting cultivation: clear and burn a forest plot, farm it a few years, then move on and let it regrow. Tropical rainforests with poor soils.
  • Nomadic herding (pastoral nomadism): herders move livestock between seasonal pastures in dry lands too arid for crops, such as Central Asia, the Sahel and the Middle East.
  • Ranching: raising livestock commercially on large areas of grassland, such as the US West, Argentina and Australia.
Mission 1

Ranching in the US West

  • The Bureau of Land Management manages livestock grazing on 155 million acres of public land.
  • It administers nearly 18,000 grazing permits and leases on more than 21,000 allotments, mostly for cattle and sheep.
  • Grazing each year depends on drought, wildfire and market conditions: physical limits again.
Quick check
?BLM grazing covers about 155 million acres under about 18,000 permits and leases. On average, about how many acres is that per permit or lease?
Mission 1

Why the land sets intensity

  • Dry, cold, steep or infertile land supports few animals or crops per acre → extensive systems.
  • Warm, well-watered, fertile land can repay heavy inputs → intensive systems.
  • But money and technology matter too: Australia's grain farms are extensive (low yield per hectare, very large farms) even in a rich country.
Mission check

?Region A: hot and wet all year, thin soils under forest; families clear small plots, farm them two or three years, then move on. Region B: semi-arid grassland, few people; a company raises cattle across thousands of acres and sells them to meatpackers. Which description is correct?

Physical geography (Mediterranean or tropical climate, soil, relief) shapes what farmers grow and how intensively: market gardening, plantations and mixed crop-livestock are intensive; shifting cultivation, nomadic herding and ranching are extensive.

Mission 2

Three rural settlement patterns

  • Clustered (nucleated): homes grouped together in a village; farmland lies around it.
  • Dispersed: farmhouses spread out, each on its own land, far from neighbors.
  • Linear: homes in a line along a road, river, canal or coast.
Mission 2

Farming shapes the pattern

  • Farmers want to live close to the land they work.
  • Large, separate farms → each family lives on its own land → dispersed.
  • Shared fields, shared water or the need for defense → homes group together → clustered.
  • Each farm needs a frontage on a river or road → homes line up along it → linear.
  • How land is divided (the survey method) sets the shape of the farms.
Quick check
?From the air you see about 30 houses grouped around a church and a market square, with fields stretching out on every side. Which settlement pattern is this?
Mission 2

Metes and bounds

  • Metes and bounds: a plot's boundary is described by distances and directions between natural features.
  • Used in most British colonies outside New England, which surveyed its towns first. Descriptions "were often vague and were tied to trees, rocks, creek junctions, or stone mounds".
  • Result: irregular plots of every shape and size.
  • Problem: it "led to many overlapping claims, boundary disputes, and clouded titles".
Mission 2

Township and range

  • On May 20, 1785, Congress passed the Land Ordinance to divide western public land before it was sold.
  • Land was cut into townships six miles square, each split into lots of one mile square, or 640 acres, numbered 1 to 36. (Later called sections.)
  • Lot 16 in each township was kept to support public schools.
  • Almost 1.5 billion acres of US land have since been surveyed into townships and sections.
Mission 2

The grid makes dispersed farms

  • The Homestead Act (May 20, 1862) offered 160 acres of surveyed public land.
  • To keep it, a claimant had to live on the land for five years and farm it.
  • 160 acres = one quarter of a 640-acre section (640 ÷ 4).
  • So families lived on their own square claims, far apart: a dispersed pattern on a grid of square fields.
Quick check
?A township has 36 sections of 640 acres each. How many 160-acre homesteads could fit in one township?
Mission 2

Long lots

  • In New France (today's Quebec), land was granted under the seigneurial system from 1627 until 1854.
  • Estates were divided into river lots: "long, rectangular strips" giving each farm access to the river, "the principal communication route".
  • A typical holding was about 3 × 30 arpents (an old French unit): narrow at the river, very long inland.
  • Homes sit at the river end of each strip → a linear pattern. Later generations got land in second and third rows behind.

Mission 3

Domestication

  • Domestication: humans breed wild plants and animals until they depend on people and differ from their wild relatives.
  • It "began globally 12,000–11,000 y ago at the end of the most recent ice age".
  • A hearth is a place where an innovation, such as farming a crop, began.
Mission 3

Several hearths, not one

  • Researchers map several independent centers of domestication.
  • AP names the Fertile Crescent, the Indus River Valley, Southeast Asia and Central America. Others include China, the Andes, New Guinea and parts of Africa.
  • Each hearth domesticated its own local plants and animals.
Mission 3

The Fertile Crescent

  • Domestication "is believed to have commenced around 9500 BCE in the Near East", the region that includes the Fertile Crescent.
  • Its "founder crops" include wheat and barley.
  • Taurine cattle were domesticated there about 10,500 years ago; "early Holocene animal domestication was restricted to the Near East".
Mission 3

The Indus River Valley

  • Zebu (humped) cattle were domesticated in the Indus Valley about 8,000 years ago.
  • Wheat and barley also reached South Asia from southwest Asia.
  • Dates are still debated: a 2025 study re-dated Neolithic burials at Mehrgarh (Pakistan) to 5200–4900 BCE, much later than the earlier estimate of about 8000 BCE.
Mission 3

East and Southeast Asia: rice, roots and fruit

  • Rice was domesticated in the Lower Yangtze region of China between 6,900 and 6,600 years ago.
  • In the highlands of New Guinea, people used taro early on, and bananas "were intensively cultivated by at least 6950 to 6440" years ago.
  • AP's Southeast Asia hearth stands for this region's root and tree crops. New Guinea, an island shared by Indonesia and Papua New Guinea, gives some of the best-dated early evidence.
  • Each region domesticated different local plants: grains in the Yangtze, roots and fruit in New Guinea.
Mission 3

The Americas

  • Maize came from a wild grass, teosinte, in "a single domestication in southern Mexico about 9,000 years ago". It was present in Mexico's Balsas Valley by 8,700 years ago, with domesticated squash.
  • The cultivated potato traces back to a single domestication in Peru.
Quick check
?Which crop–hearth pair is correct?
Mission 3

Crops spread from hearths

  • Crops spread mainly with people: farmers moved and took seeds and animals with them (relocation diffusion); neighbors copied them (expansion diffusion).
  • Wheat and barley spread from southwest Asia toward South Asia. The slowest stretch was across the Zagros Mountains, "possibly due to topography and climate".
Mission 3

The Columbian Exchange

  • Columbian Exchange: "the exchange of diseases, ideas, food crops, technologies, populations, and cultures between the New World and the Old World" after Columbus's voyage in 1492.
  • Americas → world: the potato "first appeared outside of South America in Europe in 1567 and rapidly diffused worldwide".
  • Old World → Americas: horses. North American horses "show strong Iberian genetic affinities", and spread to the Great Plains and northern Rockies by the first half of the 1600s.
Quick check
?Which movement belongs to the Columbian Exchange?
Mission 3

Agricultural revolutions spread crops too

  • First Agricultural Revolution: the start of farming in the hearths, and its spread outward.
  • Second Agricultural Revolution (from the 1700s): new rotations, tools and crops spread through Europe and its colonies.
  • Green Revolution (from the mid-1900s): high-yield seed varieties spread around the world.
Mission check

?Chili peppers were first domesticated in Mexico. Suppose a historian finds chili in a European recipe from the 1600s. Which process best explains how it got there?

Farming began in several hearths: the Fertile Crescent (wheat, barley, cattle), the Indus Valley (zebu), East and Southeast Asia (rice, taro, bananas) and the Americas (maize, squash, potatoes). Crops then spread with people, and after 1492 the Columbian Exchange moved them between hemispheres: the potato reached Europe in 1567 and then the world.

Mission 4

Three agricultural revolutions

  • First: people domesticated plants and animals and began farming.
  • Second: new tools, methods and machines, alongside the Industrial Revolution, starting in Britain.
  • Third, the Green Revolution: high-yield seeds and chemicals raised yields across Asia from the late 1960s, and spread to other developing regions.
Mission 4

The second agricultural revolution

  • Enclosure: open fields and common land were fenced into private farms. England's Parliament passed over 5,200 enclosure acts between 1604 and 1914.
  • Enclosure covered just over a fifth of England's land, and "greatly improved the agricultural productivity of farms from the late 18th century".
  • New crop rotations (turnips and clover in the cycle) fed animals in winter and restored soil.
  • New tools: the seed drill, iron plows, and later reapers and other machines.
Mission 4

Machines replace muscle

  • In 1900, US farms used 22 million work animals. By about 2000, they used 5 million tractors instead.
  • Mechanization: machines do work once done by people and animals.
  • Chemical fertilizers, better transport (railroads) and new markets in growing cities followed.
Quick check
?Which change is part of the second agricultural revolution?
Mission 4

Impacts: food, health and factory workers

  • More food: higher output per farm meant better, more reliable diets.
  • Longer lives: in England and Wales, life expectancy at birth was 40.2 (boys) and 42.2 (girls) in 1841; by 2011 it was 79.0 and 82.8. Better food was one part of this; clean water and medicine were others.
  • Workers for factories: share of the US workforce in farming: 41% (1900), 21.5% (1930), 4% (1970), 1.9% (2000).
  • Historians "remain divided" on how far enclosure itself forced the poorest rural workers off the land.
Quick check
?Why did the second agricultural revolution help factories grow?
Mission 4

Revolutions move plants around the world

  • Agricultural revolutions spread crops, and new varieties of them, worldwide.
  • A short-stemmed wheat bred in Japan went to the United States in the late 1940s, then to Mexico, where Norman Borlaug crossed it with local wheats from 1953.
  • In 1967, Pakistan imported about 42,000 tons and India 18,000 tons of the new wheat seed from Mexico.
  • For rice, the International Rice Research Institute in the Philippines bred semi-dwarf varieties, building on work in China, Japan and Taiwan.
Mission 4

The Green Revolution package

  • High-yield seeds of wheat and rice, with short, stiff stems that do not fall over under heavy grain.
  • More chemicals: across Asia, fertilizer use grew by 10.75% a year from 1967 to 1982. Pesticides were part of the package too.
  • Irrigation: Asia's irrigated area grew by 2.1% a year over the same period.
  • Mechanized farming: tractors, pumps and threshers replaced some hand labor.
Mission 4

Positive consequences

  • Asia's cereal production roughly doubled between 1970 and 1995, from 313 to 650 million tons a year.
  • Population rose 60%, but food rose faster: cereal and calorie supply per person rose by nearly 30%.
  • In Asia, the number of poor people fell from 1,149 million (1975) to 824 million (1995).
  • Wheat and rice became cheaper.
Mission 4

Negative consequences

  • Environment: fertilizers and pesticides "pollute waterways and kill beneficial insects and other wildlife"; irrigation can cause "salt buildup"; groundwater falls where pumping beats rainfall.
  • Diets: as cereals got cheaper, fruits and vegetables got relatively more expensive, so micronutrient deficiencies became more common than calorie shortages.
  • People: gains were smaller where land ownership was very unequal: in Bihar, India, "poverty reduction was more limited".
  • One expert's summary: green revolutions "are not necessarily pro poor or environmentally benign".
Quick check
?Which is a negative environmental consequence of the Green Revolution?
Mission check

?A country's farmers switch to high-yield seeds, fertilizer, irrigation pumps and tractors. Which outcome is most likely over the next 20 years?

Machines, seeds and chemicals let each farmer grow far more. That fed growing cities and freed workers for factories (US farm share: 41% in 1900, 1.9% in 2000). The Green Revolution doubled Asia's cereal output, but polluted water, salted soil and drained groundwater.

Mission 5

Two reasons to farm

  • Subsistence agriculture: food grown mainly to feed the farmer's own household.
  • Commercial agriculture: crops and animals grown mainly to sell for profit.
  • Many farms do some of each. Geographers sort production regions by which one dominates.
Mission 5

Most farms are small

  • The world has more than 608 million farms.
  • Farms under 2 hectares (about 5 acres) are 84% of all farms, but operate only about 12% of farmland.
  • The largest 1% of farms operate more than 70% of the world's farmland.
  • Subsistence farming is mostly found on small farms in less developed countries (LDCs); commercial farming dominates the big ones.
Mission 5

Commercial farms specialize

  • In 1900, a US farm produced on average about five different commodities; by 2000, about one.
  • Specializing earns more from one market, but ties the farm's income to one price.
  • Monocropping: growing the same crop on the same field year after year.
  • Monoculture: one crop (or one animal type) covering a whole farm or region.
Quick check
?A farm in Iowa grows corn on the same fields every year and sells all of it to an ethanol plant. Which terms fit best?
Mission 5

Why it matters

  • Monocropping drains the same soil nutrients each year and lets pests of that crop build up, so farmers buy more fertilizer and pesticide.
  • Monoculture regions depend on one price: if it falls, the whole region loses income.
  • Subsistence farmers grow a mix of crops, which spreads risk but earns little cash.
Mission 5

Bid-rent: who pays the most for land?

  • Bid-rent theory: each land user "bids" what it can afford for land at each distance from the market.
  • Close to the market (a city), land costs the most. Only uses that earn a lot per acre can pay.
  • Farther away, land is cheaper, so uses that earn little per acre can still pay.
Mission 5

Intensive and extensive

  • Intensive farming: lots of labor, money or inputs on a small area. Examples: vegetables, dairy, greenhouses.
  • Extensive farming: few inputs spread over a large area. Examples: ranching (grazing), large grain farms.
  • US farmland, 2025 averages: cropland $5,830 per acre; pasture $1,920 per acre.
  • Cropland is worth about 3 times as much as pasture (calculated: 5,830 ÷ 1,920 = 3.0).
Quick check
?A farmer owns land just outside a large city, where land is very expensive. Which use is most likely to pay?
Mission 5

The pattern in the USA

  • 2025 average farm real estate value per acre: Rhode Island $22,500; New Jersey $16,600; Wyoming $1,000; New Mexico $725.
  • New Jersey's value is about 23 times New Mexico's (calculated: 16,600 ÷ 725 = 22.9).
  • The high-value states sit close to huge urban markets on the East Coast. In the dry West, much farmland is grazed extensively.
  • Near cities, farmers also compete with house builders and other urban land users, which pushes prices up further.
  • Distance is not the only factor: climate, water and soil also limit what land can do.
Mission 5

Economic forces at work

  • Land cost: high near markets, so farming there is intensive.
  • Market demand and prices: commercial farmers grow what sells; prices push them to specialize.
  • Access to money: subsistence farmers lack the cash and credit to buy land, machines or inputs.
Mission check

?In a country, a capital city sits on a coast. Land near the city is the most expensive in the country. Which pattern of farming is most likely, moving inland away from the city?

Land near markets costs the most, partly because farmers compete with city land uses, so farmers there must farm intensively (New Jersey, $16,600 per acre). Far away, cheap land suits extensive uses (New Mexico, $725). That is bid-rent applied to farming.

Agribusiness

  • Agribusiness: farming run as a large commercial business, linked to the firms that supply seeds, feed and machinery and that process, ship and sell food.
  • Commercial farms grow for sale; family farms are owned and run by a family, and can be small or large.
  • The trend: large-scale commercial operations are replacing small family farms.
Mission 6

Fewer, bigger farms

US Census of Agriculture:

  • Farms: 2,042,220 in 2017, 1,900,487 in 2022 (−6.9%).
  • Average farm size: 441 acres in 2017, 463 acres in 2022.
  • Land in farms fell only 2.2%, so the land is being shared among fewer, larger farms.

Mission 7

A farmer's model

  • Johann Heinrich von Thünen (1783–1850), a German landowner, published The Isolated State in 1826.
  • He asked: if farmers sell in one city, what should each farm grow?
  • His answer was a model: a simplified picture used to explain a pattern.
Mission 7

The assumptions

  • One isolated city, the only market.
  • A flat, uniform plain: same soil and climate everywhere.
  • Farmers carry produce to market overland, and transport cost rises with distance.
  • Farmers grow whatever earns them the most profit after transport.
Mission 7

The rings

  • Result: land use falls into concentric rings around the city.
  • 1. Market gardening and dairy: vegetables and milk spoil fast and must be near market.
  • 2. Forest: wood for fuel and building is heavy and bulky, costly to haul.
  • 3. Field crops (grain): lasts longer and costs less per unit of value to transport.
  • 4. Ranching / livestock grazing: animals can walk to market.
  • Beyond: wilderness, where no farming pays.
Quick check
?In von Thünen's model, why is dairy farming in the ring closest to the city?
Mission 7

Location rent

  • Location rent = what a farmer earns per acre after production costs and transport costs.
  • Each extra mile adds transport cost, so location rent falls with distance and reaches zero at some point.
  • Crops whose rent falls steeply (perishable, bulky) win near the city; others win farther out.
  • This is the same logic as bid rent.
Quick check
?An imaginary wheat farm: each acre gives 1 ton, wheat sells for $100 a ton at the city, growing it costs $60 a ton, and transport costs $2 per ton per mile. How far from the city does wheat stop paying (location rent = 0)?
Mission 7

Bending the rings

  • Real places break the assumptions: hills, different soils, rivers, railroads and highways.
  • A cheap transport route (a navigable river, rail line or highway) stretches rings along it, because distance costs less there.
  • Refrigeration and fast transport let perishable foods travel far, so the inner ring no longer has to be next to the city.
Mission 7

Different scales

  • Local: dairies, greenhouses and vegetable farms still cluster on many city edges.
  • National: farm types change with distance from big urban markets.
  • Global: geographer Richard Peet (1969) used von Thünen's model to interpret how commercial farming spread outward in the 19th century. The "city" becomes a whole core region that imports food.
Quick check
?Which change would most weaken the inner ring of market gardening around a city?
Mission 7

Specialty farming breaks the rings

  • Specialty farming: crops that need a particular climate or soil, so they grow where conditions suit, whatever the distance to market.
  • Example: almonds need a Mediterranean climate (mild, rainy winters; warm, dry summers), found in only five small regions of the world.
  • In 2025/26 the USA produced 1,231,500 t of the world's 1,657,450 t of almonds, about 74% (our calculation).
  • US almond exports are forecast to reach a record in 2026/27; the EU and India are the top import markets.
Mission 7

What the model still explains

  • Good for: why perishable and bulky goods are grown near markets, and why land use changes with access.
  • Weak on: climate and soil differences, government policy, modern transport, and global markets.
  • AP move: explain a pattern with transport cost and distance, then name a factor (specialty climate, technology) that breaks it.
Mission check

?In an invented country, vegetable farms and dairies surround the capital; wheat fields lie farther out; cattle ranches lie farthest. But 400 miles from the capital, a sunny valley with dry summers grows wine grapes sold around the world. What explains this?

Von Thünen's rings come from transport cost rising with distance from the market. Specialty farming sits where climate and soil suit it, so it often ignores the rings.

Mission 9

Farming changes land cover

  • Land cover change: replacing forest, grassland or wetland with fields and pasture.
  • FAO: "Agricultural expansion drives almost 90 percent of global deforestation".
  • More than half of forest loss is conversion to cropland; livestock grazing causes almost 40%.
Mission 9

Slash and burn and shifting cultivation

  • Slash and burn: cut the vegetation, burn it, and plant in the ash-enriched soil.
  • Shifting cultivation: farm a plot for a few years, then move on and leave it fallow so the forest regrows.
  • With long fallows the land recovers. If plots are reused before they recover, soils degrade and forest cover shrinks.
  • Deforestation is permanent clearing for farms or pasture, without regrowth.
Mission 9

Terraces and irrigation

  • Terraces: steps cut into slopes that slow runoff and hold soil and water.
  • The Rice Terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras "have followed the contours of the mountains", harvesting "water from the forest clad mountain tops" (UNESCO).
  • Irrigation: bringing water to crops by canals, wells, sprinklers or drip lines. It turns dry land green, and diverts water from rivers and lakes.
Mission 9

Draining wetlands and pastoral nomadism

  • Draining wetlands: ditches and pumps lower the water so marsh becomes farmland.
  • In Florida's Everglades, "an extensive network of canals and levees" was built "to drain land for agricultural and residential development".
  • Pastoral nomadism: herders move livestock between seasonal pastures in dry lands.
  • IPCC: herders' resilience is higher where land tenure is flexible enough to let them move their herds.

Mission 9

Case study: the Aral Sea

  • In the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya rivers to grow cotton and other crops in the desert.
  • "Although irrigation made the desert bloom, it devastated the Aral Sea", once the fourth-largest lake in the world.
  • In 2014 the eastern lobe of the South Aral Sea disappeared completely.
  • Fisheries collapsed, and salty dust blew off the dry lakebed onto fields.
Mission 9

Soil salinization

  • All irrigation water carries some dissolved salt. In hot, dry air much of the water evaporates, and the salt stays in the soil.
  • Over-watering and poor drainage raise the water table. Water rises to the surface, evaporates, and leaves more salt.
  • This is soil salinization. Half of Central Asia's irrigated lands, especially in the Amu Darya and Syr Darya basins, were affected (Qadir et al., 2009, cited by the IPCC).
  • Worldwide, more than 424 million hectares of topsoil are salt-affected; more than two-thirds of salt-affected soils are in arid and semi-arid climates.
Quick check

Mission 9

Desertification and pollution

  • Desertification: "land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas … resulting from many factors, including human activities and climatic variations" (IPCC).
  • Major human drivers include the "expansion of croplands" and "unsustainable land management practices": overgrazing, overcultivation, salinization.
  • Pollution: fertilizer and manure wash nitrogen and phosphorus into water, feeding algae that use up oxygen and create dead zones.
  • In Chesapeake Bay, USA, agriculture supplied about 46% of nitrogen loads in 2024 modeling.
Mission 9

Conservation efforts

  • Soil: terraces and contour plowing hold soil on slopes. Secure land tenure helps farmers adopt soil conservation (IPCC, high confidence).
  • Salt: drip irrigation, drains to lower the water table, and salt-tolerant crops. In the Aral basin, fields had to be "flushed with larger and larger volumes of river water".
  • Water: Kazakhstan's Kok-Aral dike and dam (2005) let the North Aral and its fisheries rebound, but limited flow into the South Aral.
  • Pollution: in 2010 the US EPA set a "pollution diet" for Chesapeake Bay. By 2023, practices in place had achieved an estimated 57% of the nitrogen cuts needed.
Mission 9

Changing diets

  • As incomes rise, people tend to eat more meat, milk and eggs, which need feed crops.
  • Meat supply in China rose from 58.25 kg to 73.54 kg per person per year between 2010 and 2023.
  • China's soybean imports rose from 10.4 million metric tons (2000) to 103.4 million metric tons (2023), much of it crushed for livestock feed.
  • In South America, the area planted with soybeans more than doubled, from 26.4 to 55.1 million hectares, in 2000–2019: diets in one place changed land cover in another.
Mission 9

Economic purpose and women's role

  • Economic purpose: subsistence farming feeds the farm family; commercial farming grows crops to sell, often far away. The Aral Sea's cotton was a commercial crop.
  • Commercial goals can push farmers to maximize output now (more irrigation, more fertilizer) at a cost to soil and water later.
  • Women's role: in southern Asia, 71% of working women work in agrifood systems, against 47% of men.
  • Yet men have greater ownership or secure rights over farmland in 40 of 46 countries studied.

Why roles vary

  • Type of production: catching fish at sea against processing and selling it on shore; subsistence plots against commercial plantations.
  • Other jobs: where few non-farm jobs exist, food work dominates for everyone (sub-Saharan Africa, 66% and 60%).
  • Access: land rights, wages and credit shape who can farm, and on what terms.
  • FAO models that closing the gender gaps in farm productivity and wages would add nearly $1 trillion to world GDP and cut the number of food-insecure people by 45 million.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Climate, soil and relief shape farming: Mediterranean climates grow drought-tolerant tree crops and vines; tropical climates grow plantation crops or support shifting cultivation.
  • Intensive: market gardening, plantation agriculture, mixed crop-livestock. Extensive: shifting cultivation, nomadic herding, ranching.

Mission 2

  • Rural settlements are clustered, dispersed or linear; farming practices and land division explain which.
  • Metes and bounds (natural features, irregular), township and range (6-mile townships, 640-acre sections) and long lots (narrow strips from a river) leave lasting landscapes.

Mission 3

  • Domestication began about 12,000–11,000 years ago in several hearths, including the Fertile Crescent, the Indus Valley, Southeast Asia and Central America.
  • Crops and animals spread by diffusion, then globally through the Columbian Exchange and the agricultural revolutions.

Mission 4

  • Second agricultural revolution: enclosure, new rotations and machines raised output; better diets, longer lives and workers for factories followed.
  • Green Revolution: high-yield seeds, chemicals, irrigation and machines doubled Asia's cereal output, with costs to water, soil and diets.

Mission 5

  • Subsistence farming feeds the household; commercial farming sells, and often specializes (monocropping, monoculture).
  • Bid-rent: land costs most near markets, so farming there is intensive; cheaper land far away is farmed extensively.

Mission 6

  • Agribusiness: large commercial farms replace small ones; economies of scale and technology drive it, raising carrying capacity.
  • Commodity chains link farm to plate; processing location, distribution and government policy shape what is grown where.

Mission 7

  • Von Thünen (1826): on a uniform plain, transport cost with distance from market sorts farming into concentric rings: perishables and dairy, forest, grain, ranching.
  • The logic works at local, national and global scales, but specialty farming regions (like Mediterranean-climate almonds) do not follow the rings.

Mission 8

  • Food moves through global supply chains: a diet change in China changes land use in Brazil.
  • Commodity dependence, political relationships (tariffs, war) and infrastructure (ports) shape who gets food and who earns from it.

Mission 9

  • Farming practices alter landscapes (slash and burn, terraces, irrigation, draining wetlands, pastoral nomadism) and cause land cover change, salinization, desertification and pollution; conservation can reduce the damage.
  • Societal effects include changing diets, the economic purpose of farming, and women's role in production.

Mission 10

  • Food insecurity is mostly about access: poverty, distance (food deserts), distribution, weather and lost farmland.
  • GMOs and aquaculture raise supply but bring debates over herbicide-resistant weeds, pesticide use, biodiversity, water and pollution.

Mission 11

  • Food-choice movements (local food, urban farming, CSA, organic, value-added specialty crops, fair trade, dietary shifts) shape what farms produce.
  • Their benefits are real but specific: the evidence shows diet often matters more than distance.

Mission 12

  • Women's roles vary by region (agrifood is 71% of women's jobs in southern Asia, 36% worldwide) and by type and stage of production (fishing against processing and trade).
  • Unequal land rights and pay limit women's productivity; at home, women often carry responsibility for feeding the household.
