Mission 1

The question: how is a sinner put right with God?

  • Justification: being put right (made or declared righteous) before God. From Greek dikaioō, "to justify, acquit".
  • Christians agree: all have sinned (Romans 3:23), and God's grace saves through Christ.
  • They have disagreed on how: by faith alone? By faith and works? Who decides who is saved?
  • 1517: Martin Luther (1483–1546), an Augustinian friar and professor at Wittenberg, posts the 95 Theses against the sale of indulgences.
  • The quarrel over indulgences became a quarrel over justification: the heart of the Reformation.
Mission 1

Luther's discovery

  • Luther tried to earn peace with God through monastic discipline, fasting and confession. It failed.
  • He came to hate the phrase "God's righteousness" (Romans 1:17; Latin iustitia Dei), reading it as the standard by which God punishes sinners.
  • Breakthrough (he recalls it in his 1545 Preface to his Latin works, paraphrase): it means the righteousness God gives as a gift, by which the righteous "live by faith".
  • He said it was as if he had been born again and entered paradise through open gates (paraphrase).
  • So righteousness is not achieved by us (active) but received (passive).

Mission 1

Sola fide: faith alone

  • Sola fide: we are justified by faith alone, not by faith plus works.
  • Faith here is trust (fiducia) in Christ's promise, not only agreeing to doctrines.
  • Imputed (credited) righteousness: Christ's righteousness is counted as ours, like a debt paid into our account. Luther called it an alien righteousness: it comes from outside us.
  • So the believer is simul iustus et peccator: at the same time righteous (in God's verdict) and a sinner (in himself).
  • Luther's German New Testament (1522) translated Romans 3:28 "by faith alone" (allein); he defended the word in his Open Letter on Translating (1530).

Mission 1

Luther and James 2:24

> "You see then that by works a man is justified, and not only by faith." (James 2:24, WEBBE)

  • In his 1522 Preface to the New Testament, Luther called James "an epistle of straw" compared with John, Romans, Galatians and 1 Peter, because it has nothing of the gospel's character (paraphrase).
  • His reasons: James seems to contradict Paul; it hardly mentions Christ's death and resurrection; he doubted it was by an apostle.
  • He moved James (with Hebrews, Jude and Revelation) to the end of his New Testament. He did not remove it, and later editions dropped the "straw" remark.
  • Other readings: James attacks a dead faith that is mere belief (2:17, 2:26); "justified" may mean shown to be righteous before others. Many Protestants hold that Paul and James do not contradict.
Mission 1

Calvin: grace and predestination

  • John Calvin (1509–1564), French Reformer in Geneva; Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536; final edition 1559).
  • Agreed with Luther: justification is God's acceptance of us, by imputation of Christ's righteousness, received by faith (Institutes III.11.2).
  • Fallen humans cannot choose God by themselves, so salvation depends wholly on God's choice: election.
  • > "By predestination we mean the eternal decree of God, by which he determined with himself whatever he wished to happen with regard to every man" (Institutes III.21.5, trans. Beveridge, 1845)
  • Double predestination: some are elected to eternal life; others are left to condemnation (the reprobate). He called the decree decretum horribile, an "awe-inspiring" or "dreadful" decree (III.23.7).
  • Sources: Romans 8:29–30; Romans 9:16; Ephesians 1:4–5.
  • Calvin urged believers to find assurance by looking to Christ, "the mirror" of election, not by speculating about the decree (III.24.5, paraphrase).
Mission 1Diversity

Who is chosen?

  • Augustine (354–430) had already taught that God's grace chooses the elect; Calvin built on him.
  • Reformed (Synod of Dort, 1618–19): unconditional election, limited atonement, irresistible grace.
  • Arminians (after Jacobus Arminius, 1560–1609) and Methodists (John Wesley, 1703–1791): God elects those he foresees will believe; grace can be resisted; Christ died for all.
  • Lutherans: predestination to life by grace; no decree of damnation.
  • Catholic teaching: God predestines no one to hell (Catechism 1037, paraphrase); grace and free will cooperate. The Council of Orange (529) had already rejected predestination to evil.
  • Key text for the wider view: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4).

Mission 1

So why be good?

  • Reformers' answer: good works do not make us righteous; they follow from being made right, as fruit follows from a good tree (Matthew 7:17).
  • Luther, The Freedom of a Christian (1520): a Christian is free from the need to earn salvation, and so free to serve the neighbour in love (paraphrase).
  • "We are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10).
  • Calvin: faith alone justifies, yet the faith that justifies is never alone; it always brings a new life (sanctification) (Antidote to Trent, 1547, paraphrase).
  • The elect are chosen "that we would be holy" (Ephesians 1:4): predestination is to holiness, not to licence.
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the New Testament letters support faith alone?

  • Claim: The New Testament letters support justification by faith alone.
  • For: Romans 3:28; Galatians 2:16; Ephesians 2:8–9 contrast faith with works directly.
  • For: Paul's model is Abraham, counted righteous by believing before he was circumcised (Romans 4:3, 4:10).
  • Against: "Alone" is not in Paul's Greek; Luther added it (allein) when he translated Romans 3:28 into German.
  • Against: James 2:24 says "not only by faith"; Paul too says God will "pay back to everyone according to their works" (Romans 2:6).
  • Against: "Works of the law" may mean Jewish law-observance, not good deeds in general (see Mission 2).
  • Judge it by: What does Paul mean by "works"? Can James and Paul be reconciled? Is Luther's reading shaped by his own struggle?

Mission 2

Trent: the Catholic response

  • The Council of Trent (northern Italy, 1545–1563): the Catholic Church's response to the Reformation.
  • Session VI, the Decree on Justification (13 January 1547): 16 chapters of teaching and 33 canons (short rulings ending "let him be anathema", i.e. excluded from the Church).
  • It rejected both extremes:
  • Against Pelagius: no one can be justified by their own works without God's grace (canon 1).
  • Against the Reformers: justification is not by faith alone, and not by Christ's righteousness merely credited to us.
Mission 2

Justification as a gift that changes you

  • Grace comes first: God's "prevenient" grace calls the sinner, who has no merit (ch. 5).
  • Free will cooperates: the person can accept or reject that grace (ch. 5; canon 4).
  • Instrumental cause: the sacrament of baptism (ch. 7).
  • Formal cause: the justice of God "whereby he maketh us just": righteousness infused (poured in), not only imputed (ch. 7).
  • With forgiveness, the person receives faith, hope and charity (ch. 7): faith is the beginning, but it must be living, formed by love.
  • "Justified freely": nothing before justification, faith or works, merits it (ch. 8).
  • Justification is a process: it can grow, be lost through mortal sin, and be restored through the sacrament of penance (ch. 10, 14).
Mission 2Read the source

Trent's canons

Council of Trent, Session VI, canons (trans. J. Waterworth, 1848):

  • Canon 9 condemns anyone who says "by faith alone the impious is justified", meaning that nothing else is required to co-operate in receiving grace.
  • Canon 11 condemns the view that we are justified "by the sole imputation of the justice of Christ", without grace and charity poured into our hearts.
  • Canon 24 condemns the view that good works are only the fruits and signs of justification, and not a cause of its increase (paraphrase).

Show answer

Q2 Which kind of righteousness does canon 11 deny is enough on its own?

Show answer

Mission 2

Works and merit

  • Trent ch. 10 quotes James 2:24 for the increase of justification: the justified grow in righteousness by keeping God's commandments.
  • Merit (ch. 16): eternal life is both a grace promised through Christ and a reward for good works done in grace.
  • But God's gifts are the source: when God rewards merit, he "crowns his own gifts" (an Augustinian idea, paraphrase).
  • So Catholic "justification by works" means works done by grace grow and keep justification. It does not mean earning God's favour by unaided effort.
  • A common misreading is that Catholics believe you earn heaven by being good. Trent's view: grace makes good works possible and meritorious.

Mission 2Diversity

Where the churches stand now

  • 1999: the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (Augsburg, 31 October).
  • Core agreement: we are accepted "by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work", and the Spirit renews hearts and calls to good works (§15, short quotation).
  • The mutual condemnations of the 1500s are said not to apply to the teaching of the other side as set out in the Declaration.
  • Later joined by the World Methodist Council (2006), the World Communion of Reformed Churches (2017); the Anglican Communion welcomed and affirmed it (2016–17).
  • Differences remain: merit, the role of sacraments, how far a believer is "righteous and sinner" at once. Some Lutherans and evangelicals reject the Declaration.
  • Orthodox Christians frame salvation more as theosis (sharing God's life) than as a courtroom verdict.
Mission 2

Why good conduct matters in each view

  • Catholic (Trent): works done in grace grow and keep justification; mortal sin can lose it.
  • Lutheran: works are the fruit of faith and serve the neighbour; they add nothing to justification.
  • Reformed (Calvin): works are the evidence of a living faith and of election; they give a secondary assurance.
  • Methodist (Wesley): faith leads to growing holiness of heart and life.
  • In every view, good conduct is required of a Christian. The disagreement is about why.
Mission 2Worked answer

Linking belief, source and development

Question: "Examine Catholic teaching on justification by works."

  • Belief: works done in grace increase justification. Source: Trent, Session VI, ch. 10, citing James 2:24. Development: so the Christian life is growth, which is why Catholics value the sacraments and works of mercy.
  • Belief: grace comes first and cannot be earned. Source: ch. 8: nothing before justification merits it. Development: this answers the charge that Catholics believe in salvation by effort.
  • AO1 tip: show the difference between justification by works with grace (Catholic) and by works without grace (Pelagian).
Mission 2Evaluate

Are faith and works both aspects of justification?

  • Claim: Both faith and works are aspects of justification.
  • For: James 2:24; Romans 2:6; Matthew 25:31–46: judgement looks at deeds.
  • For: Trent and the 1999 Declaration both link faith to a changed life.
  • Against: Romans 3:28, Galatians 2:16, Ephesians 2:8–9: faith apart from works.
  • Against: If works count, assurance is lost: no one knows if they have done enough (Luther's experience).
  • Middle position: faith alone justifies; works follow necessarily. Does that make works an aspect, or only a result?
  • Judge it by: What does "works" mean (moral deeds or Torah observance)? Is justification a moment or a process? Does the answer fit Paul and James?

Mission 3

Sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: human life is sacred (holy) because it is created by God and belongs to God.
  • Made in God's image: "God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE). Every human has equal, built-in worth.
  • A gift held in trust: "You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19, WEBBE).
  • Protected by command: "You shall not murder" (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE); Genesis 9:6 links the ban on killing to the image of God.
  • Sanctity of life is a principle: Christians agree on it, but differ on when it applies (the embryo) and whether killing is ever justified (war).

Mission 3

When does a human life begin to matter?

  • From conception: a new human organism with its own DNA exists from fertilisation; it deserves the full respect due to a person.
  • Gradualist: the embryo deserves respect from the start, and its moral status grows with development (implantation, a functioning nervous system, viability, birth).
  • History: Aquinas, following Aristotle, held that the rational soul is given some weeks after conception ("delayed ensoulment"), yet the Church still condemned abortion at every stage (though church penalties sometimes differed by stage).
  • 14 days: the UK Warnock Report (1984) and the Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act (1990) set 14 days as the legal limit for embryo research: around then the primitive streak forms and twinning is no longer possible.
  • Quality of life views (religious and non-religious) weigh the effects of a life on the person and on others; critics say this makes some lives worth less.
Mission 3Diversity

The embryo and the unborn child

  • Catholic: the embryo is to be treated as a person from conception (Donum Vitae, 1987; Evangelium Vitae, 1995). Embryo research that destroys embryos is wrong; IVF is also judged wrong, because it separates conception from the married couple's act of love, and more gravely so when spare embryos are created or destroyed. Direct abortion is always wrong; treatment to save the mother's life that indirectly ends the pregnancy may be allowed (double effect).
  • Many evangelicals and Orthodox Christians: similar, life from conception; some accept IVF without creating spare embryos.
  • Church of England and some other Protestants: the embryo has a special status from the start; abortion is a grave matter, justified only in strictly limited cases (e.g. serious risk to the mother); views on research vary.
  • Liberal Christians: weigh the embryo's growing value against the mother's life, health and conscience; some support legal access to abortion as the loving choice in hard cases.
  • All appeal to sanctity of life; they differ on who counts fully and when.

Mission 3

Just war theory

  • Christians who reject pacifism ask: when, if ever, is war justified? Main sources: Augustine (354–430) and Aquinas (1225–1274).
  • Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.40 a.1: three conditions for going to war (jus ad bellum):
  • 1. Proper authority: only a legitimate ruler may declare war.
  • 2. Just cause: to right a real wrong.
  • 3. Right intention: to promote good and avoid evil; the aim is peace.
  • Later writers added: last resort, reasonable chance of success, proportionality (the good achieved must outweigh the harm).
  • Conduct in war (jus in bello): discrimination (never target civilians) and proportionality of means.
Mission 3Read the source

Aquinas on war

> "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged. … Secondly, a just cause is required … Thirdly, it is necessary that the belligerents should have a rightful intention, so that they intend the advancement of good, or the avoidance of evil."

> (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, Question 40, Article 1, English Dominican translation)

Q1 Why does Aquinas say a private individual may not declare war?

Show answer

He can seek justice through a court; care of the common good belongs to those in authority.

Q2 Can a war with a just cause still be unjust for Aquinas?

Show answer

Yes: if the intention is wicked, e.g. cruelty or greed.

Mission 3

Love your enemies: pacifism and its critics

  • AQA set text Matthew 5:38–48: "Don't resist him who is evil; but whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also" (Matthew 5:39, WEBBE); "love your enemies … pray for those who mistreat you" (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE).
  • Pacifists (e.g. Quakers, Mennonites): Jesus forbids killing even in self-defence or war; Christians should resist evil by non-violent means.
  • Just war reply: Jesus speaks to personal revenge; the State has a duty to protect the innocent, and "doesn't bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4, WEBBE).
  • Augustine: what Jesus forbids is hatred and revenge in the heart; a soldier may fight without hating the enemy.
  • Both sides claim to apply the love commanded in Matthew 5:44.
Mission 3

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD: nuclear, chemical and biological weapons, able to kill huge numbers indiscriminately. Nuclear weapons were used at Hiroshima and Nagasaki in August 1945.
  • Tested against just war criteria:
  • Discrimination: they cannot separate soldiers from civilians.
  • Proportionality: the harm (radiation, effects on future generations, possible "nuclear winter") outweighs any good.
  • Reasonable chance of success: an all-out nuclear war would leave nothing to win.
  • Second Vatican Council, Gaudium et Spes 80 (1965): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities and their people is a crime against God and humanity (paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Deterrence: keeping weapons you must never use?

  • Deterrence: holding WMD so that an enemy never dares to use theirs.
  • Accepted conditionally: Pope John Paul II (1982 message to the UN) judged deterrence morally acceptable for now, only as a step towards progressive disarmament (paraphrase); US Catholic bishops, The Challenge of Peace (1983), similar.
  • Possession itself condemned: Pope Francis (2017): not only the use but the threat of use and even the possession of nuclear weapons is to be firmly condemned (paraphrase). The Holy See ratified the UN Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017).
  • Deterrence defended: some Christians argue that it has prevented war between major powers since 1945, and that unilateral disarmament would leave the innocent unprotected.
  • Pacifists: all war and all WMD are wrong.

Mission 3Evaluate

Can just war theory ever justify WMD?

  • Claim: Just war theory can never justify the use of weapons of mass destruction.
  • For: WMD fail discrimination and proportionality by their nature (Gaudium et Spes 80).
  • For: Escalation risk: even limited use could lead to all-out war; no reasonable chance of success.
  • Against: Some argue a small weapon against an isolated military target might meet discrimination.
  • Against: Some argue that, in a "supreme emergency" (Michael Walzer, Just and Unjust Wars, 1977), a nation facing destruction may break the rules; Christian critics reply that intending to kill the innocent is always wrong.
  • Judgement criteria: Whether any real use could stay limited · the moral difference between threat and use · the weight given to future generations.
  • AO2 tip: weigh the strongest for and against against one clear criterion, then reach your own justified conclusion.

Mission 4

Two words from Genesis

  • Genesis 1 gives humans dominion: rule over fish, birds, livestock and "every creeping thing".
  • Genesis 2 places the first human in the garden to cultivate and keep it (WEBBE).
  • Christians read these together. The question is what kind of rule dominion is.
  • Dominion as domination: nature exists for human use.
  • Dominion as stewardship: humans rule on God's behalf and answer to God for how they rule.

Mission 4

Dominion over animals: the classical view

  • For most of Christian history, dominion meant animals exist for human use: food, work, clothing.
  • Genesis 9:3: after the Flood, every moving thing "will be food for you".
  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica: the life of animals is preserved "not for themselves but for man" (II-II, Q. 64, Art. 1, reply 1).
  • But cruelty is still wrong: the Law forbade it so that people would be "less inclined to be cruel to other men" (I-II, Q. 102, Art. 6, reply 1).
  • This is an indirect duty: kindness to animals matters mainly because of its effect on humans.
  • Even so, Christians also cite Proverbs 12:10: "A righteous man respects the life of his animal" (WEBBE).

Mission 4

Stewardship

  • A steward manages property that belongs to someone else and must give an account.
  • Owner: God ("the land is mine", Leviticus 25:23).
  • Steward: humanity, made in God's image to reflect God's care, not to exploit.
  • Accountability: stewards will be judged on faithfulness (1 Corinthians 4:2; the parable of the faithful steward, Luke 12:42–48).
  • Future generations: a steward hands the estate on in good order.
  • Creation has value because God made it and called it "very good" (Genesis 1:31), not only because humans use it.
Mission 4

Stewards of animals today

  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church, 1992, paragraphs 2415–2418, paraphrase):
  • Dominion over animals is not absolute; humans owe animals kindness.
  • Using animals for food and clothing is legitimate; medical experiments are acceptable within reasonable limits if they help save human lives.
  • Causing animals to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity.
  • Animals must not receive the love and money owed to people in need.
  • Andrew Linzey (b. 1952), Anglican priest, Animal Theology (1994): animals have rights grounded in God (theos-rights); humans should be the "servant species", serving creation as Christ served.
  • Some Christians are vegetarian on these grounds; most hold that eating meat is permitted (Genesis 9:3).
Mission 4

Changing understandings

  • 1962: Rachel Carson's Silent Spring shows pesticides harming wildlife; public awareness grows.
  • 1967: historian Lynn White Jr. (1907–1987), "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis" (Science): Western Christianity, with its idea of dominion, helped cause the crisis. He calls it "the most anthropocentric religion the world has seen".
  • From the 1980s: science on climate change, species loss and pollution shows humans can damage the whole planet, not just a field.
  • Effect on the Christian role: stewardship becomes global, long-term and political, not just kindness to one's own animals.
  • 1979: Pope John Paul II names Francis of Assisi patron saint of ecology.
  • 2015: Pope Francis, Laudato Si': care for "our common home"; dominion does not justify absolute domination; Genesis 2:15 implies a relationship of "mutual responsibility" between humans and nature (paragraph 67).
Mission 4Diversity

How Christians respond now

  • Catholic: Laudato Si' calls for integral ecology: care for the poor and for the planet belong together.
  • Orthodox: Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew (b. 1940) teaches that damaging the natural world is a sin; humans as "priests of creation" offer the world back to God (also John Zizioulas).
  • Evangelical: many speak of creation care as part of following Christ (Lausanne Movement, Cape Town Commitment, 2010); A Rocha's Eco Church scheme helps congregations act.
  • Church of England: General Synod set a net zero by 2030 target (2020); its national investing bodies announced they would sell holdings in oil and gas companies (2023).
  • Dissenting voices: some evangelicals (e.g. the Cornwall Alliance) accept stewardship but doubt the severity of climate change and stress human flourishing and the poor's need for cheap energy.
  • Beyond stewardship: some theologians call stewardship too managerial; they prefer kinship with creation (Francis of Assisi's "Brother Sun, Sister Moon").

Mission 4Evaluate

Is Christian dominion to blame for the environmental crisis?

  • Claim: Christian belief in dominion has contributed to environmental damage (White, 1967).
  • For: Genesis 1:28 ("subdue") was long read as permission to exploit; the classical view gave animals only indirect worth.
  • For: industrial exploitation grew first in Christian Europe.
  • Against: Genesis 2:15 and Psalm 24:1 point to care and accountability; Christianity has strong traditions of creation care (Francis; Celtic and Orthodox spirituality).
  • Against: environmental damage also occurs in non-Christian and officially atheist societies; economic and technological causes may matter more.
  • Against: today many churches lead on environmental action.
  • Judgement criteria: What do the texts mean in context? How have Christians actually behaved? Are other causes stronger? Is stewardship itself enough, or too human-centred?
  • A-level evaluation (AO2): weigh the strongest form of each side before reaching your own judgement.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Luther: justification by grace through faith alone; Christ's righteousness imputed; the believer is righteous and sinner at once (Romans 1:17; 5:1; Ephesians 2:8–9; Galatians 2:16).
  • Calvin: salvation rests on God's eternal election (double predestination); others (Arminians, Methodists, Catholics) reject unconditional reprobation.
  • Good works matter as the fruit and evidence of faith, not as a means of earning salvation.

Mission 2

  • Trent (1547): justification is God's free gift, received with cooperating free will; righteousness is infused and grows through works done in grace.
  • In every tradition good conduct is required; they differ on whether it causes, increases or only shows justification.

Mission 3

  • Sanctity of life: life is God's gift, made in his image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Embryo and unborn: from conception (Catholic) vs gradualist; double effect; different Protestant views.
  • Just war (Aquinas: authority, cause, intention; later: last resort, success, proportion, discrimination): WMD widely judged to fail; deterrence divides Christians.

Mission 4

  • Dominion (Genesis 1:28) has been read as domination or as stewardship (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 24:1).
  • Classical view: animals for human use; cruelty wrong indirectly (Aquinas). Modern views: Catechism limits; Linzey's theos-rights.
  • Changing knowledge (Carson, White, climate science) has made stewardship global; churches respond in different ways.
