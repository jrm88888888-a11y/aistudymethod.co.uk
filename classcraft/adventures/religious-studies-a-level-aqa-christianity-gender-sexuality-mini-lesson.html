Mission 1

Equal in God's image

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

  • Both sexes bear the image of God (imago Dei): equal in dignity.
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is a "helper comparable to him". The Hebrew ezer (helper) is used elsewhere of God himself (e.g. Psalm 121:2), so it does not mean inferior.
  • After the Fall, "he will rule over you" (Genesis 3:16): many Christians read male rule as a result of sin, not God's design.
  • In Christ "there is neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28).
Mission 1Diversity

Equal, but are roles different?

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals; Danvers Statement, 1987): men and women are equal in worth, with different, complementary roles; husbands lead in the home and men lead in the church.
  • Egalitarian (Christians for Biblical Equality, established 1988): equal in worth and in roles; leadership by gifting, not sex.
  • Catholic (John Paul II, Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988): equal dignity; a distinct "genius" of women (§30); mutual self-gift; all-male priesthood (see Mission 2).
  • Orthodox: equal in salvation; distinct roles; an all-male priesthood; great honour for Mary, the Theotokos.
  • Quakers: equal ministry from the 1650s (Margaret Fell, Women's Speaking Justified, 1666).

Mission 1

1 Timothy 2:8–15

> "But I don't permit a woman to teach, nor to exercise authority over a man, but to be in quietness." (1 Timothy 2:12, WEBBE)

  • Reasons given: Adam was formed first; Eve was deceived (2:13–14); a woman "will be saved through her childbearing" (2:15).
  • Traditional reading: a rule for all times, grounded in creation, not culture; so women may not lead or preach to men in church.
  • This passage was long used to keep women out of church leadership and out of public life.
  • One of your set references: 1 Timothy 2:8–15.
Mission 1

Biblical criticism opens the question

  • Biblical criticism studies the Bible as historical texts: who wrote them, when, why, for whom.
  • 19th century: German scholars such as Friedrich Schleiermacher (1807) and F. C. Baur (1835) argued that Paul did not write 1 Timothy. Many scholars now date it to c. 100 CE, written in Paul's name.
  • Context: the letter fights local false teaching (1 Timothy 1:3–7); the ban may target that situation, not all women for all time.
  • Elizabeth Cady Stanton, The Woman's Bible (1895–98): a critical commentary on texts used against women.
  • Result: the freedom to challenge traditional readings: a verse may express its author's culture rather than God's timeless will.
  • Conservative scholars still defend Paul's authorship and note that verse 13 appeals to creation, not culture.
Mission 1

Changing roles and secular rights

  • Rights from secular governments (UK): votes for some women over 30 (1918); equal franchise (1928); Equal Pay Act (1970); Sex Discrimination Act (1975); Equality Act (2010).
  • Changing roles: women in higher education and every profession; reliable contraception (from the 1960s); fathers sharing childcare.
  • Churches responded: some changed teaching and practice (women ministers; in the Church of England, marriage vows without "obey", first offered in the proposed 1928 Prayer Book and kept as the usual form in the Alternative Service Book, 1980, and Common Worship); others resisted, holding that scripture, not society, sets roles.
  • So Christian thinking about gender developed under both pressures: biblical criticism inside the Church and secular change outside it.

Mission 1Evaluate

Should Christian teaching resist secular views of gender?

  • Claim: Christian teaching should resist current secular views of gender roles.
  • For: Scripture and tradition, not changing culture, are the Church's authority; creation order (1 Timothy 2:13).
  • For: Equal dignity does not require identical roles: complementarity protects motherhood and family (Mulieris Dignitatem).
  • Against: Secular equality echoes Christian roots: imago Dei (Genesis 1:27) and Galatians 3:28.
  • Against: Biblical criticism shows that some "biblical roles" are ancient culture, like slavery rules.
  • Judgement criteria: Is a text cultural or timeless? Do roles express equal dignity or limit it? Who decides: scripture, tradition, reason or experience?

Mission 2

What is ordination?

  • Ordination: the setting apart of a person for ministry, by prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • Three orders in Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches: deacon, priest (presbyter), bishop.
  • For Catholics and Orthodox, ordination (Holy Orders) is a sacrament: the priest acts in persona Christi (in the person of Christ) at the Eucharist.
  • Many Protestant churches speak of ministers or pastors, called and recognised by the church.
  • Three broad positions: all orders open to women; some orders open; none.
Mission 2

The Church of England before 1994

  • 1944: Florence Li Tim-Oi ordained priest by the Bishop of Hong Kong, in wartime China, to serve Macau: the first Anglican woman priest.
  • 1975: General Synod finds "no fundamental objections" to women priests, but does not act.
  • 1979: the Movement for the Ordination of Women (MOW) is founded.
  • 1987: the first women ordained deacon in England.
  • 11 November 1992: General Synod votes to ordain women priests (a two-thirds majority needed in each house; the House of Laity passes it narrowly).
  • 1993: the Priests (Ordination of Women) Measure becomes law; an Act of Synod provides "flying bishops" for parishes that cannot accept women priests.
Mission 2

1994 and after

  • 12 March 1994: 32 women ordained priest in Bristol Cathedral.
  • Some clergy and lay people left; several hundred clergy, with many lay people, were received into the Catholic Church.
  • 2012: a measure for women bishops fails in the House of Laity. 2014: it passes, with Five Guiding Principles promising "mutual flourishing" for those who disagree.
  • 2015: Libby Lane, first woman bishop (Stockport). 2018: Sarah Mullally, Bishop of London.
  • 2026: Sarah Mullally installed as Archbishop of Canterbury (25 March), the first woman to lead the Church of England.
  • The continuing debate: traditional Catholic and conservative evangelical parishes still receive the ministry of male bishops; critics say this tolerates discrimination; supporters say it keeps the Church united.

Mission 2

Arguments against ordaining women

  • Jesus chose twelve men as apostles, though he had women disciples; the Church follows his choice.
  • Tradition: the Church has ordained only men for almost 2,000 years.
  • Sacramental sign: the priest represents Christ, the Bridegroom of the Church; a man represents him more naturally (Catholic: Inter Insigniores, 1976).
  • Headship: some evangelicals hold that 1 Timothy 2:12 and 1 Corinthians 14:34 reserve teaching authority over men to men.
  • Unity: ordaining women divides churches and harms relations with the Catholic and Orthodox Churches.
Mission 2

Arguments for ordaining women

  • Equal image of God (Genesis 1:27) and equality in Christ (Galatians 3:28).
  • Jesus' practice: women followed and funded his ministry; Mary Magdalene was the first witness of the resurrection, sent to tell the disciples (John 20:17–18): called "apostle to the apostles".
  • Early church: Phoebe a deacon; Junia "notable amongst the apostles"; the Spirit poured out so that "your sons and your daughters will prophesy" (Acts 2:17).
  • The Twelve were Jewish men representing Israel's twelve tribes; their maleness was symbolic of Israel, not a rule for priesthood.
  • Vocation and justice: women report a genuine call from God; refusing it seems unjust, and gifts are wasted.
Mission 2Diversity

Catholic and Orthodox positions

  • Catholic: Ordinatio Sacerdotalis (John Paul II, 22 May 1994): "the Church has no authority whatsoever to confer priestly ordination on women", a judgement to be held definitively.
  • This is presented as a matter of faith, not of discipline: the Church cannot change it, rather than will not.
  • Catholic teaching insists this is not a judgement on women's dignity: Mary, the greatest of the saints, was not an apostle.
  • Women deacons: studied by papal commissions (2016, 2020); no change to date.
  • Orthodox: priesthood for men only, following tradition and the icon of Christ; some Orthodox theologians (e.g. Elisabeth Behr-Sigel, 1907–2005) argued the question is open; the historic order of deaconesses is being discussed.
Mission 2Diversity

Protestant churches

  • Methodist Church of Great Britain: women presbyters since 1974.
  • Church of Scotland: women ministers since 1968.
  • Salvation Army: women officers and preachers from its foundation (1865; Catherine Booth).
  • Quakers: no ordained clergy; women and men equally free to minister.
  • Some evangelical churches (e.g. the Southern Baptist Convention, Baptist Faith and Message 2000) keep the office of pastor for men.
  • So progress is uneven: the disagreement runs within traditions as well as between them.

Mission 2

What the debates mean for individuals and communities

  • Individuals: women can now answer a call to priesthood in many churches; others, in Catholic and Orthodox Churches, live with a call they believe cannot be fulfilled. Some opponents left their churches.
  • Communities: parishes choose whether to accept a woman priest or bishop; separate oversight (e.g. The Society) keeps some within the Church of England.
  • Ecumenism: women bishops are an obstacle in Anglican–Catholic dialogue.
  • Equality in society: the Equality Act 2010 allows organised religions an exemption to limit some roles to one sex for doctrinal reasons. Is that freedom of religion or discrimination?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should churches follow society on equality in ministry?

  • Claim: Churches that do not ordain women are failing the standard of equality expected by society.
  • For: Equality in the image of God and in Christ (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28) supports equal access to ministry.
  • For: Society's progress (votes, equal pay, Equality Act) made the Church's practice look unjust and hurt its mission.
  • Against: The Church's authority is Christ and tradition, not society; Ordinatio Sacerdotalis.
  • Against: Equal dignity is not the same as identical roles; complementarians see different callings, not inequality.
  • Judgement criteria: Is priesthood a right or a calling? Is Christ's choice of men binding or cultural? Does the Church of England's settlement give genuine "mutual flourishing"?
  • Spec: debates "up to and after 1994, the continuing debate today".

Mission 3

What is feminist theology?

  • Patriarchy: a social system in which men hold power and male experience is treated as the norm.
  • Feminist theology asks how patriarchy has shaped Christian scripture, doctrine, worship and leadership, and what should follow.
  • Context: first-wave feminism (votes for women; Elizabeth Cady Stanton, The Woman's Bible, 1895–98); second-wave feminism of the 1960s–70s.
  • Key themes: women's experience as a source for theology; a hermeneutic of suspicion (reading texts alert to male bias: Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza, In Memory of Her, 1983); language for God (McFague).
  • Two paths: reform Christianity from within (Ruether, McFague) or leave it as beyond repair.
Mission 3

Rosemary Radford Ruether

  • Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022), American Catholic theologian; Sexism and God-Talk (1983).
  • Her critical principle: whatever denies or diminishes "the full humanity of women" is not redemptive; whatever promotes it reflects the divine (paraphrase, ch. 1).
  • She is a reformist: she stays within Christianity and recovers its prophetic-liberating tradition, in which God sides with the oppressed.
  • Chapter 5 asks the question of this mission: "Can a Male Saviour Save Women?"
Mission 3

The problem of a male Christ

  • Over centuries, Christ's maleness was used to rank men above women.
  • Aquinas, following Aristotle, wrote that as an individual, "woman is defective and misbegotten" (Summa Theologica I q.92 a.1 ad 1), while insisting that woman is part of God's plan for human nature.
  • Christ's maleness has been used to argue that only men can represent Christ as priests (see Mission 2).
  • An early Christian principle: what Christ did not take on, he did not heal (Gregory of Nazianzus, 4th century). If Christ's maleness is what saves, women seem left out.
  • Ruether: Christ's maleness has no ultimate significance; it matters only as a social symbol in patriarchal societies.
Mission 3

Jesus against the warrior-messiah

  • Many Jews of Jesus' time hoped for a Messiah like King David: a warrior who would defeat Israel's enemies and rule.
  • Ruether: Jesus rejected that model of power. He came as a servant, not a ruler:

> "whoever wants to become great amongst you shall be your servant." (Mark 10:43, WEBBE)

  • He sided with the marginalised: the poor, outcasts, women. Women followed him and were first witnesses of the resurrection (Luke 24:9–10).
  • So Jesus' life announces the "kenosis of patriarchy" (Ruether's phrase): the self-emptying of male power.
  • His maleness, read this way, is a critique of patriarchy, not a support for it.

Mission 3

The androgynous Christ and the Christlike female

  • Androgynous Christ: Christ unites "masculine" and "feminine" qualities (e.g. Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love, c. 1395, calls Jesus "our Mother").
  • Ruether values the aim but warns that many androgynous pictures still treat the male as the norm and the "feminine" as a mere side of him.
  • Her own answer: Christ is not confined to the historical Jesus. The Christian community continues Christ's identity, so Christ can be met "in the form of our sister" (Ruether's phrase).
  • The Christlike female: Jesus came to the last and least. Women, among the most marginalised, can show the liberated humanity Christ brings in a fuller way than men who keep power: the last become first (Matthew 20:16).
  • Your spec: Ruether's "ideas about the androgynous Christ and her view that the female nature is more Christlike than the male".
Mission 3Diversity

Responses to Ruether

  • Catholic and Orthodox teaching: Christ saves all humans because he took on human nature; maleness is not what saves. But his maleness still matters as a sign, e.g. for priesthood (see Mission 2).
  • Many evangelicals: Christ is defined by scripture and the creeds; the historical Jesus cannot be replaced by a community's Christ.
  • Other feminists: Elizabeth Johnson, She Who Is (1992), develops a fuller Sophia (Wisdom) Christology.
  • Daphne Hampson: Ruether picks only the parts she likes; a Christianity tied to a male past cannot be made feminist (see Mission 4).

Mission 3Evaluate

Can a male saviour save women?

  • Claim: A male saviour can save women.
  • For: The creeds stress that Christ became human (incarnation), not male; Galatians 3:28: in Christ "neither male nor female".
  • For: Ruether: Jesus' life overturns patriarchal power.
  • Against: Christ's maleness has been used for centuries to exclude women; symbols shape power (Daly).
  • Against: Hampson: the uniqueness of one historical man makes the male normative for ever.
  • Judgement criteria: What saves: Christ's humanity or his maleness? Can tradition be reinterpreted this far? What effects do the symbols have on real women?

Mission 4

Reform or leave?

  • All feminist theologians agree that Christianity has been shaped by patriarchy.
  • Reformists (Ruether, McFague, Johnson): the core of Christianity is liberating; remove the sexism and stay.
  • Post-Christian feminists (Daly, Hampson): the sexism is built in; Christianity cannot be repaired, so they left.
  • Post-Christian does not mean atheist: both kept a spirituality; Hampson still believes in God.
  • The question: is patriarchy a stain on Christianity, or part of its structure?
Mission 4

Daphne Hampson

  • Daphne Hampson (b. 1944), British theologian (St Andrews); Theology and Feminism (1990); After Christianity (1996). Once active in the campaign for women priests.
  • Christianity is "irredeemably sexist" because it is a historical religion: it claims a unique revelation of God in a particular past.
  • That past was patriarchal: a male Jesus, God as Father and Son, male apostles. Christians must always look back to it, so the bias can never be removed.
  • Not moral: feminism needs full equality now. Not true: after the Enlightenment, she argues, a unique divine act in one time and place is not credible.
  • She is a post-Christian theist: she believes in God, known through human experience, but not in Christ's uniqueness.
Mission 4Read the source

"Neither male nor female"

> "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28, WEBBE)

  • Reformists (Ruether): this is the liberating core of the Gospel; later sexism betrays it.
  • Hampson: one verse, written by a man in a patriarchal world, cannot outweigh the structure of a religion centred on a male saviour and a Father God.

Mission 4Diversity

Ruether, Daly and Hampson compared

  • Is Christianity patriarchal? All three: yes, deeply.
  • Can it be reformed? Ruether: yes, from its prophetic core. Daly and Hampson: no.
  • What about Jesus? Ruether: he empties male power. Daly: Christ-worship sanctifies maleness. Hampson: his uniqueness makes the male normative.
  • God? Ruether: God/ess, the primal Matrix (her terms). Daly: God as Verb, Be-ing. Hampson: God known in experience, beyond any tradition.
  • Where do they go? Ruether stays in the Catholic Church and helps shape the Women-Church movement. Daly and Hampson leave.
  • Your spec: compare Hampson and Ruether on the patriarchal nature of Christianity.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can Christianity be changed, or should it be abandoned?

  • Claim: Christianity is essentially sexist and should be abandoned.
  • For: Hampson: a historical religion is tied to a patriarchal past; Daly: Christ-worship sanctifies maleness.
  • For: Centuries of reform have left many churches still male-led.
  • Against: Ruether: the prophetic tradition and Jesus' practice contain the critique of patriarchy; Galatians 3:28.
  • Against: Real change has happened: women priests and bishops (see Mission 2); inclusive language; female images of God.
  • Against (traditional Christians): God has no sex; equal dignity with different roles is not sexism (see Mission 1).
  • Judgement criteria: Is sexism the structure or a distortion? Is a religion defined by its origins or by how it develops? Does reform answer Hampson's point about the past?

Mission 5

Same sources, different weights

  • Christians draw on four sources: Bible, tradition (the Church's teaching over time), reason (including science and natural law) and experience.
  • Catholic teaching: scripture and tradition, interpreted by the Magisterium (the teaching authority of the Pope and bishops).
  • Many Protestants: scripture is the supreme authority (sola scriptura); churches still differ on how to read it.
  • Anglicans often speak of scripture, tradition and reason together.
  • Disagreements on sex and gender are mostly about how to read texts and how much weight to give new knowledge and experience.
Mission 5

Celibacy as a calling

  • Celibacy: choosing not to marry, and so to abstain from sex, for the sake of God's kingdom.

> "there are eunuchs who made themselves eunuchs for the Kingdom of Heaven's sake. He who is able to receive it, let him receive it." (Matthew 19:12, WEBBE)

> "He who is unmarried is concerned for the things of the Lord, how he may please the Lord" (1 Corinthians 7:32, WEBBE)

  • Paul calls both marriage and singleness a gift (charisma): "each man has his own gift from God" (1 Corinthians 7:7, WEBBE).
  • Jesus himself was unmarried: many Christians see celibacy as a sign of the kingdom, where people "neither marry nor are given in marriage" (Mark 12:25).
  • Religious life: monks, nuns and friars take vows of poverty, chastity and obedience.
Mission 5Diversity

Who must be celibate?

  • Catholic (Latin Church): priests normally celibate. This is a discipline (church law, Canon 277), not a doctrine, so it could change. Married former Anglican clergy may be ordained as exceptions.
  • Eastern Catholic churches: married men may be ordained priests.
  • Orthodox: a married man may be ordained deacon or priest, but may not marry after ordination; bishops are chosen from celibate monks.
  • Protestant churches: the Reformers rejected compulsory celibacy (Augsburg Confession, 1530, Article 23). Martin Luther married Katharina von Bora in 1525.
  • Protestants still honour celibacy freely chosen as a gift, but not as a higher state.

Mission 5

Marriage

> "What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." (Mark 10:9, WEBBE)

  • Jesus links marriage to creation: "male and female" (Genesis 1:27) become "one flesh" (Genesis 2:24).
  • Catholic teaching: marriage is a sacrament, a lifelong union ordered to the good of the spouses and to having and raising children. It cannot be dissolved; an annulment declares that a valid marriage never existed.
  • Orthodox: a sacrament; a second (or third) marriage may be allowed after divorce, with a penitential rite, by economy (oikonomia, pastoral flexibility).
  • Church of England: marriage is "a gift of God in creation" (Common Worship); not one of the two "sacraments of the Gospel" (Article 25). Since 2002, divorced people may remarry in church at the minister's discretion.
  • Most Protestants: marriage is a covenant before God; remarriage after divorce is widely accepted.
  • Traditional teaching in all these churches: sex belongs within marriage. Many Christians in practice accept cohabitation before marriage; the churches' official teaching has not changed.

The texts in the debate

> "You shall not lie with a man as with a woman. That is detestable." (Leviticus 18:22, WEBBE)

> "men doing what is inappropriate with men, and receiving in themselves the due penalty of their error." (Romans 1:27, WEBBE)

  • Also cited: 1 Corinthians 6:9–10; 1 Timothy 1:10; Genesis 19 (Sodom; Ezekiel 16:49 names its sin as pride and neglect of the poor).
  • In Romans 1, Paul's argument is about humanity turning from the Creator to idols (Romans 1:23–25); verses 26–27 come within it.

Show answer

Mission 5Diversity

Two ways of reading

  • Traditional view (Catholic, Orthodox, most evangelical churches):
  • The texts are consistent across both Testaments; Romans 1 grounds its claim in creation ("against nature"), not in local custom.
  • Genesis 1–2 and Mark 10 give a male–female pattern for marriage.
  • People with same-sex attraction are loved by God; what is at issue is sexual acts outside marriage between a man and a woman. Many gay Christians who hold this view choose celibacy.
  • Affirming view (many liberal Protestants and some evangelicals):
  • The texts condemn exploitative or idolatrous acts (e.g. pederasty, temple practice, excess of lust), not faithful, lifelong partnerships, which the writers did not discuss.
  • This is a historical-critical argument: it asks what the text meant in its first-century setting.
  • Precedent: the early Church saw the Spirit given to Gentiles and changed its practice (Acts 10–15).
  • Paul says celibacy is a gift (1 Corinthians 7:7): it should not be imposed on everyone who is gay.
  • Both sides appeal to love (agape): one as faithful obedience to God's design, the other as welcome and justice.
Mission 5

Where churches stand today

  • Catholic: same-sex acts are "intrinsically disordered" (Catechism 2357), but gay people must be treated with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (2358). Fiducia Supplicans (2023): priests may give brief, non-liturgical blessings to couples in irregular situations, including same-sex couples, without approving the union.
  • Orthodox: marriage is only between a man and a woman.
  • Church of England: same-sex couples cannot marry in church (the 2013 Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act excludes it). Prayers of Love and Faith (blessings within ordinary services) have been used since December 2023. In February 2026 Synod closed the Living in Love and Faith process; separate services would need full canon-law authorisation, and a new working group continues the work.
  • Same-sex marriages permitted by: Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church in Britain (2021), Church of Scotland (2022, ministers may opt in), United Reformed Church (2016).
  • Disagreement also runs within denominations: e.g. the Anglican Communion (Lambeth 1998 Resolution I.10 rejected homosexual practice).

Mission 5

Transgender matters

  • Transgender: a person whose gender identity differs from the sex recorded at birth.
  • Traditional view: sex is a given part of creation, "male and female he created them" (Genesis 1:27); the body is good and part of who we are. Male and Female He Created Them (Catholic, 2019) critiques theories that separate gender from biological sex; Dignitas Infinita (2024) says sex-change interventions as a rule risk threatening human dignity (paraphrase).
  • Catholic guidance (2023): transgender people may be baptised and, in some conditions, be godparents.
  • Affirming view: God knows the inner self (Psalm 139:13); the welcome of eunuchs, who did not fit gender norms, in Isaiah 56:3–5 and Acts 8:26–40 shows God's inclusion; "neither male nor female" in Christ (Galatians 3:28).
  • Church of England (2018 guidance): the service of Affirmation of Baptismal Faith may be adapted to mark a person's transition; no re-baptism.
  • Many Christians also stress pastoral care and the experience of intersex people (variations in sex characteristics), which complicates a simple binary.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should Christian teaching on sexuality change?

  • Claim: Christian teaching on sexuality and gender should change to reflect modern knowledge and experience.
  • For: The Church has re-read texts before (slavery; Gentile inclusion; remarriage after divorce).
  • For: Experience of faithful same-sex couples and transgender Christians shows "good fruit" (Matthew 7:16–20).
  • Against: Tradition has been consistent for two thousand years; it is not just a cultural assumption.
  • Against: The creation pattern is reaffirmed by Jesus (Mark 10:6–9), not merely by the law of Moses.
  • Against: Change can divide churches worldwide (Anglican Communion strains).
  • Judgement criteria: What authority does scripture have, and how is it read? Can tradition develop? What counts as love? Whose experience counts?

Mission 6: Galileo, Darwin, the Big Bang: did each discovery push God out, or change how Christians read the Bible?

Make a guess: Galileo, Darwin, the Big Bang: did each discovery push God out, or change how Christians read the Bible?

Mission 6

Why science challenges belief

  • Modern science stresses evidence (observation, experiment) and reason (testable hypotheses, revisable theories).
  • Many beliefs were once linked to readings of the Bible and to ancient science: an earth-centred cosmos, a young earth, fixed species.
  • Three kinds of challenge:
  • Discoveries that clash with a literal reading of a text (Galileo, Darwin).
  • Explanations that make God seem unnecessary (the "God of the gaps", the later screen).
  • Replacement: the view that science can answer every real question, so a religious interpretation of the universe is no longer needed.
Mission 6

Galileo

  • Copernicus (On the Revolutions, 1543): the earth moves round the sun (heliocentrism).
  • Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) used the telescope (1609–10): moons of Jupiter, phases of Venus. Evidence for Copernicus, though not yet proof.
  • Church authorities read texts such as Joshua 10:12–13 and Psalm 93:1 as teaching a still earth and a moving sun. In 1616 Copernicanism was declared contrary to scripture.
  • Galileo's Dialogue (1632) → trial in 1633: found "vehemently suspected of heresy"; house arrest until death.
  • Galileo's reply (Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, 1615), quoting Cardinal Baronius: scripture teaches "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes".
  • 1992: John Paul II accepted that theologians had erred.
  • Historians note politics, personalities and incomplete evidence too: not simply science against religion.
Mission 6Read the source

A sun that stood still

> "The sun stood still, and the moon stayed, until the nation had avenged themselves of their enemies." (Joshua 10:13, WEBBE)

> "The world also is established. It can't be moved." (Psalm 93:1, WEBBE)

  • Before Galileo, many read these as facts about astronomy.
  • Galileo's approach (and Augustine's before him): scripture speaks in ordinary language about how things appear; its purpose is salvation, not astronomy.

It says the world "can't be moved".

Q2 How could a Christian read Psalm 93:1 without contradicting heliocentrism?

Show answer

As poetry about God's reign and the stability of creation, not as a claim about the earth's motion.

Mission 6

Darwin

  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859); The Descent of Man (1871).
  • Natural selection: variation + inheritance + struggle for survival → species change slowly over vast ages. Humans share common ancestors with apes.
  • Challenges:
  • To a literal reading of Genesis 1–2 (six days; separate creation of kinds; a young earth).
  • To design: William Paley (Natural Theology, 1802) saw an eye as proof of a designer; natural selection offers another explanation.
  • To human uniqueness (made in God's image, Genesis 1:27) and to God's goodness, given waste and suffering in nature.
  • Darwin's own closing words (1859): "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one". In the 1860 edition he added "by the Creator".
Mission 6

Nineteenth-century Christian responses

  • Rejection: Bishop Samuel Wilberforce reviewed the Origin harshly (1860) and debated T. H. Huxley at Oxford (reports of the exchange differ). American theologian Charles Hodge: "What is Darwinism? It is Atheism" (1874), because it excluded design.
  • Acceptance: Charles Kingsley wrote to Darwin (1859) that it is "just as noble a conception of Deity" to believe God made creatures able to develop themselves; Darwin quoted him in later editions.
  • Frederick Temple (Bampton Lectures, 1884; later Archbishop of Canterbury): God works through evolution.
  • Aubrey Moore (Lux Mundi, 1889): Darwinism, "under the disguise of a foe, did the work of a friend", by ending the picture of an absent God who only intervenes now and then.
  • Catholic Church: no formal condemnation; Pius XII (Humani Generis, 1950) allowed study of the body's evolution, holding that each soul is created directly by God.
Quick check
?Which nineteenth-century figure argued that evolution actually helped Christian belief by showing God at work throughout nature?
Mission 6

The Big Bang

  • Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, proposed an expanding universe (1927) from a "primeval atom" (1931). Edwin Hubble's observations (1929) supported expansion.
  • The universe had a beginning about 13.8 billion years ago (current estimate).
  • Contemporary Christian responses:
  • Welcome: Pius XII (1951) saw it as consistent with creation; many see a fit with creatio ex nihilo (creation out of nothing) and with cosmological arguments.
  • Caution: Lemaître himself warned against using physics to prove creation; theology should not depend on the latest theory.
  • Challenge: Stephen Hawking (A Brief History of Time, 1988): if the universe has no boundary, "What place, then, for a creator?"
  • Reply: creation is about why anything exists at all, not only about a first moment (Aquinas held this).
Mission 6Diversity

Creationist and other views

  • Young Earth creationism: Genesis 1 is literal history; six 24-hour days; earth about 6,000–10,000 years old; Noah's flood explains fossils (Whitcomb and Morris, The Genesis Flood, 1961; Answers in Genesis).
  • Old Earth creationism: earth is ancient; "days" are long ages (day-age view), but God created kinds directly.
  • Intelligent Design: some biological systems are too complex to have evolved step by step (Michael Behe, Darwin's Black Box, 1996). Supporters say it is not creationism; critics (and a US court, 2005) said it is not science.
  • Theistic evolution / evolutionary creation: God creates through evolution (Francis Collins, The Language of God, 2006). The position of most Catholic, Anglican and mainstream Protestant teaching.
  • Most mainstream churches read Genesis 1 as theological truth, not a scientific account.
Mission 6

The God of the gaps

  • God of the gaps: using God to explain what science cannot yet explain.
  • The problem: as science fills the gaps, God is pushed out. Newton thought God corrected planets' orbits; later physics showed this was unnecessary.
  • Christian critics of the idea: Henry Drummond (1894) warned against "gaps" theology; Dietrich Bonhoeffer (prison letters, 1944) said God should be found in what we know, not in what we don't know; C. A. Coulson (Science and Christian Belief, 1955) made the phrase well known.
  • Alternative: God as the ground of the whole natural order, including the parts science explains.
Mission 6Evaluate

Has science replaced a religious interpretation of the universe?

  • Claim: Science has replaced a religious interpretation of the universe.
  • For: Each discovery (Galileo, Darwin, the Big Bang) removed a job once given to God; the gaps keep shrinking.
  • For: Science's method (testable evidence) seems more reliable than revelation or tradition.
  • Against: Many leading scientists (Galileo, Newton, Lemaître) were believers; many argue that science asks "how", religion "why".
  • Against: Christian thought developed rather than disappeared: non-literal reading, theistic evolution, creatio ex nihilo.
  • Against: Creationism's persistence shows that for some Christians the conflict is real, but that is about readings of scripture, not belief in God as such.
  • Judgement criteria: Was God ever only an explanation? Can science answer "why is there anything?" Are the conflicts with God or with a reading of Genesis?

Mission 7

Four ways to relate science and religion

  • Ian Barbour (1923–2013), Religion in an Age of Science (1990), sorted views into four:
  • Conflict: one must win (Dawkins; also some creationists).
  • Independence: separate questions, no overlap (Stephen Jay Gould, "non-overlapping magisteria", 1997).
  • Dialogue: each raises questions for the other (McGrath).
  • Integration: science and theology form one account of reality (John Polkinghorne, the physicist who became an Anglican priest, leans here).
  • Keep asking: which model does each thinker hold?
Mission 7

John Polkinghorne

  • John Polkinghorne (1930–2021): Professor of Mathematical Physics at Cambridge; resigned in 1979 to train for the Anglican priesthood; ordained 1982. Works include Quarks, Chaos and Christianity (1994) and Belief in God in an Age of Science (1998).
  • Science and theology are both searches for truth about one reality: he called them intellectual cousins. Both are critical realist: they make claims about what is really there, open to revision.
  • They answer different questions: science asks how, theology asks why. (His well-known example: the kettle boils because gas heats the water; and because I want a cup of tea.)
  • Both explanations can be true at once.
Mission 7

Polkinghorne: science points beyond itself

  • Intelligibility: why can human mathematics describe the universe at all? Theism explains it: one rational Creator.
  • Fine-tuning: the constants of physics fall within narrow ranges that allow life (the anthropic principle). Not a proof, but fits creation.
  • Open future: chaos theory and quantum physics show a world that is not a rigid machine; this leaves room for God's action and human freedom without breaking laws.
  • Not a God of the gaps: God acts through the openness of nature, which science itself describes.
  • Evolution is creation "making itself": God gives creation freedom (a self-limiting, or kenotic, love).
Mission 7Read the source

Creation as witness

> "The heavens declare the glory of God. The expanse shows his handiwork." (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE)

> "For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made, even his everlasting power and divinity" (Romans 1:20, WEBBE)

  • Christians who see science as compatible with faith draw on texts like these: studying nature is studying God's work.

Mission 7

Richard Dawkins: conflict

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), evolutionary biologist: The Selfish Gene (1976), The God Delusion (2006).
  • The existence of God is a scientific hypothesis about the universe, testable like any other.
  • Faith is "blind trust, in the absence of evidence, even in the teeth of evidence" (The Selfish Gene, 1976).
  • Natural selection explains design without a designer. A God able to design a universe would be more complex, so even less probable (the "Ultimate Boeing 747" argument, The God Delusion, ch. 4).
  • Science is the best (and only reliable) route to knowledge; religion stops people asking questions.
Mission 7

Alister McGrath: dialogue

  • Alister McGrath (b. 1953): doctorate in molecular biophysics, then theologian at Oxford; once an atheist. Dawkins' God (2004); with Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? (2007).
  • Dawkins' definition of faith is not the Christian one: for Christians, faith is a reasoned commitment that goes beyond but does not go against evidence.
  • Science's method is neutral about God: it can neither prove nor disprove God. Many scientists are believers.
  • Christianity is an inference to the best explanation: it makes sense of the world as a whole (its order, its intelligibility), as scientific theories do.
  • Science and faith both rely on things that cannot be proved, e.g. that the universe is orderly.
Mission 7Diversity

The nature of proof

  • Proof in maths and logic: certain; conclusion follows necessarily.
  • Evidence in science: theories are well supported, never final; open to revision (an unexpected observation can overturn them).
  • Dawkins: belief should be proportioned to evidence; there is no good evidence for God, so belief in God is irrational.
  • McGrath: no worldview, atheism included, can be proved; all involve faith in something; the question is which best explains the evidence.
  • Agreement: neither claims a mathematical proof. Disagreement: what counts as evidence, and whether religious belief is a hypothesis at all.

Mission 7

Genetic engineering: Christian responses

  • Science stimulates Christian ethics: new powers raise new questions. Gene editing (e.g. CRISPR-Cas9, 2012) can change DNA.
  • Somatic therapy: changes cells of one patient only. Germline editing: changes are inherited by future generations.
  • Catholic teaching (Dignitas Personae, 2008): somatic gene therapy is in principle acceptable as healing; germline therapy is at present not morally permissible because of the risks to future people; enhancement to design "better" humans is rejected; embryo destruction in research is wrong (life begins at conception).
  • Many Protestants and Anglicans: support therapy as part of Jesus' healing ministry and human stewardship (Genesis 1:28); cautious about germline changes and inequality.
  • More liberal voices: humans are "created co-creators" (Philip Hefner); some germline therapy to prevent disease may be a duty of love.
  • "Playing God" objection: overriding God's design; reply: all medicine "interferes" with nature.

Mission 7Evaluate

Must a scientist be an atheist?

  • Claim: A consistent scientist must be an atheist.
  • For (Dawkins): science requires evidence; there is no evidence for God; faith is the opposite of scientific thinking.
  • For: natural selection removes the need for a designer.
  • Against (McGrath): scientific method cannot test God; it is neutral, not atheist.
  • Against (Polkinghorne): the intelligibility and fine-tuning of the universe point beyond science; many leading scientists are believers.
  • Against: atheism is itself a belief beyond what science can prove.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence? Is God a scientific hypothesis? Is "many scientists believe" relevant, or only the arguments?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Equal dignity (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28) is common ground; Christians differ on roles: complementarian, egalitarian, Catholic "equal dignity, distinct vocation".
  • Biblical criticism (19th century), secular rights and changing roles opened traditional readings of 1 Timothy 2:8–15 to challenge.

Mission 2

  • Church of England: women deacons 1987, priests 1994, bishops 2014–15; a woman Archbishop of Canterbury from 2026; "mutual flourishing" for those who disagree.
  • Against: Christ's choice of twelve men, tradition, sacramental sign, headship. For: equal image of God, Galatians 3:28, women in Jesus' ministry and the early church, vocation.
  • Catholic teaching (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994): the Church has no authority to ordain women priests; Orthodox likewise; Protestant practice varies.

Mission 3

  • Feminist theology examines patriarchy in Christianity; reformists stay to change it, post-Christians leave.
  • Ruether (Sexism and God-Talk, 1983): Jesus rejected the warrior-messiah; his maleness has no ultimate significance; Christ can be met in women.
  • The androgynous Christ and the claim that women, as the last who become first, can be more Christlike.

Mission 4

  • Daly: worshipping Christ sanctifies maleness, so she left Christianity.
  • Hampson (Theology and Feminism, 1990): Christianity is irredeemably sexist because it is tied to a unique, patriarchal past; she remains a theist.
  • Ruether reforms from within; Daly and Hampson leave.

Mission 5

  • Celibacy is a gift and calling; Latin Catholic priestly celibacy is a discipline, while Orthodox and Protestant clergy may marry.
  • Marriage: a sacrament (Catholic, Orthodox) or a covenant (most Protestants); churches differ on divorce and remarriage.
  • On same-sex relationships and transgender matters, traditional and affirming Christians read the same texts differently and weigh tradition and experience differently; denominations, and groups within them, disagree.

Mission 6

  • Galileo and Darwin challenged literal readings of scripture; many Christians came to read the Bible as teaching "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes".
  • Responses to Darwin ranged from rejection (Wilberforce, Hodge) to welcome (Kingsley, Temple, Moore); today creationism, Intelligent Design and theistic evolution coexist.
  • The Big Bang is welcomed by some as fitting creation; the "God of the gaps" is widely rejected by Christian thinkers.

Mission 7

  • Polkinghorne: science and theology are cousins seeking truth about one reality; science asks how, theology why; intelligibility and fine-tuning point beyond science.
  • Dawkins treats God as a failed scientific hypothesis; McGrath replies that science is neutral on God and Christian faith is reasoned, not blind.
  • Christian responses to genetic engineering range from cautious (Catholic: somatic yes, germline not for now) to open (created co-creators).
