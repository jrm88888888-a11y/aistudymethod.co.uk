Mission 1

The Buddhist side of the dialogue

  • The dialogue asks how Buddhist ethics answers, and is challenged by, the theories in Component 1.
  • Key Buddhist moral principles to bring to every essay:
  • Intention (cetana): the Buddha calls intention karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63).
  • Kusala / akusala: actions rooted in non-greed, non-hatred, non-delusion are healthy; those rooted in greed, hatred, delusion are unhealthy.
  • Five precepts: training rules, freely undertaken, not commands from a god.
  • Compassion (karuna), loving-kindness (metta), non-harming (ahimsa).
  • Mahayana: skilful means (upaya) and the bodhisattva's compassion for all beings.
  • The goal behind them all: the end of dukkha, nibbana.
Mission 1

Kant and Buddhism

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785): only a good will is good without qualification; act from duty, by the categorical imperative.
  • Common ground: both judge an act by what lies inside it (good will; cetana), not by luck in its outcome.
  • Common ground: the precepts are universal in scope; many Buddhists keep the first precept as close to exceptionless.
  • Buddhist response: the precepts are training rules chosen to reduce suffering and purify the mind, not duties binding on reason for their own sake.
  • Buddhist response: Kant grounds morality in a rational, autonomous self; Buddhism teaches anatta (no fixed self).
  • Buddhist response: for Kant, only rational beings are ends in themselves; animals matter only indirectly. Buddhist compassion extends to all sentient beings.
  • Kantian challenge: acting from compassion, or to gain good karma, is not acting from duty, so it lacks moral worth.
Mission 1Read the source

Two tests for an action

> "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." (Kant, Groundwork, Section II, Abbott translation 1873)

> "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129, Müller 1881)

  • Both set a limit on how others may be treated.
  • Kant's ground is reason: every rational being is an end in itself. The Dhammapada's ground is empathy: others fear harm just as you do.

Q1 Whom does each passage protect?

Show answer

Kant: "humanity", rational persons. Dhammapada: all who fear death; verse 130 adds that all "love life", and Buddhists apply it to every living being, not only humans.

Q2 What motive does the Dhammapada appeal to that Kant would not count as moral worth?

Show answer

Fellow feeling: you are "like unto them". Kant grounds duty in reason, not feeling.

Mission 1

Bentham and Buddhism

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789): right is what produces the greatest pleasure and least pain; the hedonic calculus.
  • Common ground: both start from suffering and its relief. Dukkha is the first Noble Truth.
  • Common ground: both include animals. Bentham asks of animals not whether they can reason or talk, but whether they can suffer (IPML ch. 17).
  • Buddhist response: pleasure is not the goal. Craving for pleasure (tanha) is the cause of dukkha (second Noble Truth); the goal is nibbana, beyond pleasure and pain.
  • Buddhist response: an act is kusala because of its root, not its sum of outcomes. A good result from a greedy motive is still akusala.
  • Common ground again: karma does have results; Buddhists ask whether an act harms self or others (Ambalatthika-Rahulovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 61, paraphrase).
  • Utilitarian challenge: strict precepts may produce worse outcomes than breaking them.

Mission 1

Virtue ethics and Buddhism

  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics: the goal is eudaimonia (flourishing); virtues are a mean between extremes, built by habit.
  • Damien Keown (b. 1951), The Nature of Buddhist Ethics (1992): Buddhist ethics is closest to Aristotle.
  • Parallels Keown draws: nibbana as the telos, like eudaimonia; the Middle Way between indulgence and asceticism, like the mean; bhavana (cultivation) and sila, like habituation; the arhat or the Buddha as the model of a perfected person.
  • Kusala acts are good because they are expressions of nibbanic virtue (generosity, compassion, wisdom), not only because they lead there.
  • Differences: Aristotle's virtues serve life in the city; Buddhist ideals include renunciation. Aristotle praises proper pride; Buddhist teaching aims to uproot the conceit "I am" (anatta).
  • Virtue-ethics challenge: with no lasting self, whose character is being formed?
Mission 1Diversity

How far is Buddhist ethics one kind of theory?

  • Character-based (Keown): kusala = virtue; the goal is the transformed person.
  • Consequentialist (Charles Goodman, Consequences of Compassion, 2009): especially in Mahayana, the bodhisattva acts for the welfare of all beings; Santideva allows rules to bend for others' benefit.
  • Deontological reading: the Vinaya for monks and nuns is a detailed rule code; the precepts are taken as fixed vows; many Theravada teachers stress keeping them without exception.
  • Particularist (Charles Hallisey, 1996): the Pali texts use different patterns in different places; no single theory fits.
  • Theravada and Mahayana differ: the arhat ideal fits virtue readings; skilful means fits consequentialist readings.
  • Underlying question: Western theories assume a self who chooses and a good to maximise; anatta and nibbana may not fit either.
Mission 1Read the source

Skilful means: a hard case

  • Upayakausalya Sutra ("Skill in Means"), Mahayana, paraphrase: in a past life the Buddha-to-be, a ship's captain, learns that a robber plans to kill 500 merchants on board.
  • He kills the robber, out of compassion for the merchants and for the robber, who would have suffered terribly as the karmic result of mass murder. He is ready to bear the bad karma himself.
  • Santideva, Bodhicaryavatara 5.84 (8th century, paraphrase): for one with compassion who sees the benefit, even what is otherwise forbidden is permitted.

Q1 Which features of the captain's act look consequentialist?

Show answer

He compares outcomes: one death prevents 500 and saves the robber from worse karma.

Q2 Which feature does not fit Bentham?

Show answer

The decisive factor is compassion and his willingness to bear the karma: the root of the act, not a pleasure sum.

  • Most Theravada teachers would not treat this as a rule to follow: killing remains akusala.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Buddhist ethics is best understood as virtue ethics."

  • Reasons for: intention and roots make the inner state central (Keown). The goal is a perfected person, the arhat or Buddha. Practice is training (sila, samadhi, panna), like habituation. The Middle Way echoes the mean.
  • Reasons against: Mahayana skilful means and Santideva weigh outcomes (Goodman). The Vinaya and precepts work as rules. Karma links acts to results. Aristotle's flourishing self clashes with anatta.
  • Another view: Western labels are a poor fit (Hallisey): Buddhist ethics is a path to the end of dukkha, using rules, results and character at different stages.
  • Judgement criteria: Which school's texts are you judging? Does "virtue" need a lasting self? Is a theory defined by what makes acts right or by what it aims at?
Mission 1

Impact of other ethical perspectives, past and present

  • Past: early Buddhism defined itself against rival ethics: Jain stress on bodily action (Majjhima Nikaya 56) and fatalist or ritual views.
  • Present: rights. Many modern Buddhists, including the 14th Dalai Lama, now speak of human rights and universal responsibility. Keown asks whether rights language fits a tradition built on duties and compassion ("Are There Human Rights in Buddhism?", 1995).
  • Present: utilitarian animal ethics. Peter Singer's Animal Liberation (1975) gives secular support to Buddhist concern for animal suffering.
  • Support: the dialogue shows Buddhist ethics can engage public debate without appealing to God.
  • Challenge: if Buddhist ethics can be read as any theory, critics ask whether it has a distinctive method, and whether the authority of the Vinaya and suttas is weakened when modern theories reinterpret them.
Mission 1Worked answer

Building a dialogue paragraph

Question: "Buddhist ethics has more in common with Kant than with Bentham." Evaluate this claim.

  • Point (Kant): both locate moral worth inside the act: Kant's good will; the Buddha's teaching that intention is karma (AN 6.63).
  • Analysis: but Buddhist intention is compassionate, while Kant demands acting from duty; and anatta undercuts Kant's autonomous rational self.
  • Point (Bentham): both begin from suffering and include animals; Mahayana skilful means weighs outcomes (Goodman).
  • Analysis: but craving for pleasure causes dukkha, so Bentham's hedonism conflicts with the second Noble Truth.
  • Judgement: reach a reasoned conclusion of your own, e.g. on whether inner motive or concern with suffering is the deeper link, naming the school (Theravada or Mahayana) your argument depends on.
  • Synoptic tip: in each paragraph, name a Component 1 thinker and a Buddhist source, then say how each challenges or supports the other.

Mission 2

The tools Buddhists bring to an issue

  • No sutta mentions IVF or gene editing. Buddhists apply principles:
  • First precept: abstain from taking life; ahimsa (non-harming) to all sentient beings.
  • Intention (cetana) and its roots: is the act driven by greed, hatred or delusion, or by generosity, compassion and wisdom?
  • Karma and rebirth: actions shape future experience; life is a precious chance to progress (a "precious human rebirth").
  • Compassion (karuna) and, in Mahayana, skilful means.
  • Middle Way: avoid extremes.
  • Different schools and teachers weigh these differently; there is rarely one "Buddhist view".
Mission 2

Beginnings of life: embryo and abortion

  • Traditional teaching: a new life begins when rebirth consciousness enters at conception (the Vinaya counts a monk's age from conception).
  • So many Buddhists see the embryo as a being in the human realm: destroying it breaks the first precept. For a monk, causing an abortion is an expulsion offence (Vinaya).
  • Embryo research, cloning, "designer" babies: destroying embryos raises the same concern. Selecting traits for status may come from craving; therapy to relieve suffering is judged differently by many.
  • Japan: abortion is legal and common; mizuko kuyo rites remember the lost child and acknowledge karmic weight. Critics call some temple practice commercial.
  • Many Western Buddhists stress compassion and intention: a hard decision made with care is not the same as killing in anger.
  • Dialogue: Keown's Buddhism and Abortion (1999) argues the tradition is close to a sanctity of life position; utilitarians weigh the woman's welfare and the embryo's capacity to suffer.
Mission 2

End of life: euthanasia and assisted suicide

  • Vinaya, Parajika 3: a monk who kills a human, provides the means, or praises death to someone so that they die is expelled. The rule arose after monks, disgusted with the body, sought death.
  • So most Theravada teachers oppose voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide: the intention is to end a life, and death may be met in a confused or fearful state of mind.
  • Hard cases in the texts: the monks Channa and Vakkali, gravely ill, took their own lives; the Buddha is reported as not blaming Channa (Samyutta Nikaya 35.87). Scholars debate whether this was because he was already awakened.
  • Withdrawing futile treatment is widely seen as different from killing: the intention is not death.
  • Hospice care and meditation on dying are the common Buddhist response.
  • Dialogue: Bentham's calculus may support ending unbearable pain; Kant also opposes suicide (it uses one's own person as a mere means), though for different reasons.
Mission 2

Capital punishment

  • Many Buddhists apply the first precept to states as well as individuals: "do not kill, nor cause slaughter" (Dhammapada 129, Müller 1881).
  • The ideal king rules by dhamma: the ten royal virtues include non-violence.
  • Ashoka, Pillar Edict 4 (3rd century BCE, paraphrase): prisoners condemned to death are given three days' respite so relatives can appeal and the prisoner can prepare for the next life.
  • Practice differs: Bhutan abolished the death penalty (2004); Thailand and Japan, both largely Buddhist countries, retain it; Sri Lanka has carried out no executions since 1976.
  • Buddhist reasoning against: killing in response to killing repeats hatred; the offender can still change.
  • Dialogue: Kant defended retributive execution for murder; Bentham judged punishment by its deterrent effect. Buddhism gives most weight to reform.

Mission 2

Animal life and death

  • First precept: animals are sentient beings in samsara; killing them is akusala.
  • Food: the Buddha allowed monks to accept meat if they had not seen, heard or suspected it was killed for them (Jivaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 55, paraphrase). Lay followers should not trade in meat or living beings (Anguttara Nikaya 5.177).
  • Mahayana texts such as the Lankavatara Sutra reject meat-eating; Chinese and Korean monastics are vegetarian; many Tibetans eat meat where crops are scarce.
  • Intensive farming: great suffering for profit; many Buddhists see it as rooted in greed.
  • Scientific procedures and animal cloning: harm is akusala; some accept research that relieves great suffering if done with care and minimal harm; others refuse.
  • Blood sports: killing for pleasure is clearly against the precept and wrong livelihood.
  • Organs from animals: debated; motive (saving life) and harm (killing the animal) are weighed.
  • Dialogue: Bentham and Singer support concern for animal suffering; Kant gives animals only indirect status.
Mission 2

Genetic engineering

  • No creator God has fixed the "nature" of species, so the objection "playing God" has no direct Buddhist form.
  • Everything is impermanent (anicca) and conditioned: changing living things is not wrong in itself.
  • Questions Buddhists ask: Is the intention compassion (curing disease, feeding the hungry) or greed (profit, status)? Does it harm beings? Is it done with wisdom about unknown effects?
  • The 14th Dalai Lama, The Universe in a Single Atom (2005), paraphrase: welcomes medical uses that relieve suffering but warns against enhancement driven by competition and against unknown harms.
  • Dialogue: a utilitarian weighs outcomes too; Buddhists add the root of the act. Natural law objections based on God's design do not transfer.
Mission 2

Theft and lying

  • Second precept: abstain from taking what is not given. Positive side: generosity (dana).
  • Poverty breeds theft: in the Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 26, paraphrase), a king fails to give to the needy; theft, violence and lying spread. Rulers share responsibility.
  • Fourth precept: abstain from false speech. The Buddha speaks what is true, beneficial and at the right time (Abhaya Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 58, paraphrase).
  • Kant's hard case (1797): a murderer asks where your friend is hiding. Kant: do not lie.
  • Buddhist responses vary: many hold the precept firmly but seek a skilful truthful reply (silence, refusing to answer); some Mahayana teachers allow a compassionate lie as skilful means.
  • Dialogue with Component 1: natural law forbids lying absolutely; situation ethics allows it for love; virtue ethics asks what an honest and kind person would do.

Mission 2

Marriage

  • In most Buddhist cultures marriage is a social contract, not a sacrament; monks traditionally do not officiate, but may chant blessings.
  • Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31, paraphrase): husband and wife each owe five duties, including respect, faithfulness and sharing responsibility for the household.
  • Third precept: abstain from misconduct in sensual pleasures; adultery breaks trust and causes suffering.
  • Divorce is permitted in most Buddhist societies; the aim is to reduce harm.
  • Japanese Buddhist priests have been allowed to marry since 1872; most Japanese temples are family-run.
  • Dialogue: virtue ethics fits the stress on faithfulness and kindness; Kant sees marriage as a contract of mutual rights.
Mission 2Diversity

Same-sex relationships and transgender matters

  • The third precept does not define misconduct in detail. Early texts focus on harm, deceit and breaking commitments.
  • Traditional commentaries, especially in Tibet, list "improper" partners, times and acts; the 14th Dalai Lama has repeated these for Buddhists while opposing discrimination against gay people, and has said same-sex marriage is for individuals and the law to decide (2014).
  • Many Western and East Asian Buddhists apply the harm and intention test: a loving, faithful relationship is not misconduct. A Taiwanese nun, Shih Chao-hwei, led a Buddhist same-sex wedding in 2012.
  • Transgender matters: the Vinaya records a monk whose sex changed being allowed to join the nuns, keeping seniority. Yet pandakas (meaning disputed) were barred from ordination; some traditional teachers explain gender variance by karma, which many Buddhists now reject as unkind.
  • In Thailand, kathoey (trans women) are visible in society, though not fully equal.
  • Dialogue: Bentham's harm-based test supports acceptance; natural law arguments from biological purpose have no Buddhist basis.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The precepts are too general to guide Buddhists on modern ethical issues."

  • Reasons for: no text addresses IVF, gene editing or intensive farming; Buddhists reach opposite conclusions (Japan and Sri Lanka on abortion; meat-eating; same-sex marriage).
  • Reasons for: applying "intention" is hard: motives are mixed and hidden.
  • Reasons against: generality is a strength: principles of non-harm and intention adapt to new cases where fixed rules would fail.
  • Reasons against: the precepts give firm limits (e.g. on killing) while compassion guides the rest: a combination of rule and character (see Mission 1).
  • Judgement criteria: Should a moral guide give answers or train judgement? Does variety mean weakness or proper context-sensitivity? Is the Western demand for a single method fair to Buddhism?

Mission 3

Wealth: not the problem, craving is

  • Wealth gained by right livelihood is not condemned. The Buddha's great lay supporter Anathapindika was a rich merchant.
  • Four kinds of happiness for a householder (Anana Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 4.62, paraphrase): owning wealth, enjoying it, being free of debt, and being blameless. Blamelessness is worth far more than the other three.
  • Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31, paraphrase): divide income into four: one part to enjoy, two to invest in work, one saved for need.
  • The danger is craving (tanha) and attachment: wealth is impermanent and cannot bring lasting happiness.
  • Dana (giving) turns wealth into merit and loosens attachment. Monks and nuns renounce personal wealth.
Mission 3

Wealth, poverty and society

  • Kutadanta Sutta (Digha Nikaya 5, paraphrase): a wise king ends crime not by punishment but by giving seed to farmers, capital to traders and fair wages to workers.
  • Cakkavatti Sihanada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 26): neglect of the poor leads to theft and violence (see Mission 2).
  • E. F. Schumacher (1911–1977), "Buddhist Economics" (1966; in Small Is Beautiful, 1973): work should develop people and meet needs, not maximise consumption.
  • Critics within Buddhism question temples that link large donations with merit, e.g. controversy over Wat Phra Dhammakaya in Thailand.
  • Dialogue: Bentham would judge wealth by the happiness it produces; Aristotle praises liberality (generous giving as a mean); Kant sees a duty of beneficence. Buddhism adds the inner question: does wealth feed craving?

Mission 3Read the source

Ashoka's Edict 12

Ashoka (ruled c. 268–232 BCE), Rock Edict 12, paraphrase:

  • The king honours all religions, monks and householders alike, with gifts and respect.
  • What matters most is the growth of the essentials in all religions.
  • The root of this is restraint in speech: not praising one's own religion and condemning others without good reason.
  • Whoever praises their own religion and condemns others out of excessive devotion harms their own religion.
  • So contact between religions is good: people should listen to and respect each other's teachings.

Q1 What reason does Ashoka give for not condemning other religions?

Show answer

It harms your own religion; and all religions share essentials that should grow.

Q2 Does the edict say all religions teach the same truth?

Show answer

No: it asks for respect and listening, not agreement.

Mission 3

Tolerance and its limits

  • Upali Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 56): when Upali, a Jain supporter, becomes a follower of the Buddha, the Buddha tells him to keep giving alms to Jain monks (paraphrase).
  • Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65): do not accept teachings only because of tradition or authority; test them by whether they lead to harm or welfare (paraphrase). Often read as support for open enquiry.
  • Holding views lightly: the parable of the blind men and the elephant (Udana 6.4): disputing teachers each grasp only one part.
  • Limits: the Buddha also teaches that full awakening is found only where the Noble Eightfold Path is found (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16).
  • Nichiren (1222–1282), Rissho Ankoku Ron (1260): only the Lotus Sutra leads to Buddhahood; other schools harm the nation. Many Nichiren groups (e.g. Soka Gakkai) now take part in interfaith dialogue.
Mission 3

Freedom of religious expression

  • Buddhists as minorities: Tibetan Buddhists face state controls on monasteries, teachers and the recognition of reincarnated lamas; the 14th Dalai Lama has lived in exile since 1959.
  • Buddhists as majorities: Sri Lanka's constitution (1978, Article 9) gives Buddhism the "foremost place" while guaranteeing other religions' rights.
  • Myanmar: some monks (the 969 and Ma Ba Tha movements) spread anti-Muslim preaching, and campaigned for the 2015 laws restricting interfaith marriage and conversion; other monks publicly condemned violence against Muslims.
  • Buddhist arguments for freedom: Ashoka's edicts; ahimsa; faith cannot be forced, since insight must be seen for oneself.
  • Dialogue: Kant's respect for autonomy and utilitarian concern for social peace both support freedom; critics ask whether a "foremost place" for one religion is compatible with equal freedom.

Mission 3

Free will: a middle way

  • Component 1 positions: hard determinism (all acts caused, so no free will), libertarianism (some choices are free and uncaused by prior events), compatibilism (free acts are caused, but by the agent's own desires and reasoning).
  • Buddhist teaching: the Buddha rejects three views that make effort pointless: all experience is caused by past karma, by a creator God, or by nothing (Anguttara Nikaya 3.61, paraphrase).
  • Not all suffering comes from karma: illness can come from bile, phlegm, wind, climate, careless living or assault (Sivaka Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 36.21).
  • Dependent origination: every mental event arises from conditions, so there is no uncaused choice. But intention is itself a condition shaping the future.
  • Many scholars therefore compare Buddhism with compatibilism; others see a distinct middle way.
  • Mindfulness widens the gap between impulse and response: freedom comes in degrees.
Mission 3

Moral responsibility without a self

  • AQA's conditions of responsibility: free will and knowing the difference between right and wrong.
  • Who is responsible? Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): a thief who steals mangoes is guilty, because the stolen fruit grew from the ones planted. The doer and the one who reaps are neither the same nor another: a continuous stream.
  • Ignorance does not excuse: in the Milindapanha (III.7.8, paraphrase), one who does evil unknowingly gains greater demerit, as a man who grabs a red-hot iron ball unknowingly is burned worse than one who knows.
  • Delusion (moha) is itself an unskilful root, so not knowing is part of the problem.
  • Reward and punishment: karma is not punishment from a judge, but a natural law of cause and effect; human punishment should aim at reform.

Mission 3

Conscience: shame, concern and wisdom

  • There is no single Pali word for conscience. Two qualities come closest:
  • Hiri: moral shame, a sense of self-respect that recoils from wrongdoing.
  • Ottappa: moral concern or dread, regard for others and for consequences.
  • These two "bright states" guard the world: without them, people would not respect any limits (Anguttara Nikaya 2.9, paraphrase).
  • They are trained alongside wisdom (panna) and mindfulness; they are not an infallible inner voice.
  • Worry and remorse (kukkucca) is part of a hindrance (restlessness and worry): brooding guilt is unhelpful; the healthy response is to see the fault and resolve to do better.
  • Dialogue: Aquinas' synderesis (innate grasp of first moral principles) and conscientia (applying them, which can err); Freud's superego (internalised parental and social rules, source of guilt). Buddhism shares Aquinas' view that conscience can be trained and can err, but it is not God-given; it would see much Freudian guilt as kukkucca.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Buddhism cannot give a coherent account of moral responsibility."

  • Reasons for: anatta: if there is no lasting self, it is unclear who deserves praise or blame. Dependent origination: every choice is conditioned, which looks like determinism. Critics say "stream" responsibility is only a label.
  • Reasons against: the Milinda's continuity view: responsibility follows causal connection, as psychological-continuity views of personal identity also suggest. The Buddha rejects fatalism (AN 3.61). Compatibilism shows caused choices can still be free.
  • Further point: Buddhist responsibility does not depend on a judge: karma works through cause and effect, so desert is not needed in the same way.
  • Judgement criteria: Does responsibility need a self or only continuity? Is conditioned the same as determined? Does the answer depend on the school (Theravada Abhidhamma or Mahayana emptiness)?
Mission 3Worked answer

One synoptic paragraph

Question: "Conscience is of little value in Buddhist moral decision-making." Evaluate this claim.

  • Point: Buddhism has no single word for conscience, and the Kalama Sutta tells people to test teachings against the wise, not only against feeling.
  • Link to Component 1: Freud's superego produces guilt that may be irrational; Buddhism agrees such brooding (kukkucca) is a hindrance.
  • Counter-point: hiri and ottappa "guard the world" (AN 2.9): an inner sense of shame and concern for others is essential to sila.
  • Link: like Aquinas' conscientia, they can be trained and can err, so they work with wisdom and the precepts, not alone.
  • Judgement: argue a conclusion of your own on whether "conscience" is the right word for hiri and ottappa, and whether that affects their value.
  • Synoptic tip: define the key term in Buddhist terms first, then test it against the Component 1 theory.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Key principles: intention (cetana), kusala/akusala roots, the precepts as training rules, compassion and non-harming, Mahayana skilful means.
  • Kant shares the focus on inner motive but grounds it in duty and a rational self; Bentham shares concern with suffering (and animals) but makes pleasure the goal; Keown links Buddhism with Aristotle's virtue ethics.
  • Readings differ: virtue (Keown), consequentialist (Goodman, Mahayana), deontological (Vinaya and precepts), particularist (Hallisey).

Mission 2

  • Buddhists apply the precepts, intention, karma and compassion to new issues; schools and cultures often reach different conclusions.
  • Life and death: most see the embryo as a life from conception and oppose euthanasia (Vinaya Parajika 3); practice on abortion and capital punishment varies by country.
  • Animals, theft, lying, marriage, sexuality and genetic engineering: harm and intention, not "nature", are the main tests; Kant, Bentham and virtue ethics each support some responses and challenge others.

Mission 3

  • Wealth rightly gained is not condemned; craving and attachment are. Giving, right livelihood and care for the poor matter (AN 4.62, DN 5, DN 26).
  • Tolerance: Ashoka's Edict 12, the Upali and Kalama Suttas; limits in claims about the Eightfold Path and Nichiren's exclusivism; Buddhists both defend and, at times, restrict religious freedom.
  • Free will is a middle way between fatalism and uncaused choice; responsibility follows a causal stream; hiri and ottappa are the closest Buddhist ideas to conscience.
