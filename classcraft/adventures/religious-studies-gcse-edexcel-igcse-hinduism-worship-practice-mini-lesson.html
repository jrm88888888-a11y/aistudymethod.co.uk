Mission 1

Worship anywhere

  • Hindus believe the divine is everywhere (Brahman) and within the heart (antaryami).
  • So worship can happen in many places: the mandir (temple), the home, outdoor shrines, rivers and hills, and the heart.
  • Each place helps in a different way: focus, community, daily habit, closeness to nature, inner peace.
  • Most Hindus' daily worship happens at home; the mandir is for special visits, festivals and community.
Mission 1

The mandir: a home for God

  • Mandir: a "dwelling"; the deity is treated as a royal guest.
  • Garbhagriha ("womb-chamber"): the inner shrine, small and dark, holding the main murti.
  • Murti: sacred image in which, after a consecration rite (prana pratishtha), the deity is believed present.
  • Shikhara ("peak"): the tower above the garbhagriha, like a sacred mountain linking earth and heaven.
  • Vahana ("vehicle"): the deity's animal, facing the shrine: Nandi the bull for Shiva, Garuda the eagle for Vishnu, a lion for Durga, a mouse for Ganesha.
  • Mandapa: hall for worshippers; a bell rung on entering; a path to walk clockwise round the shrine (pradakshina).
  • Your spec writes garbagriha and "vehicle of the deity".
Mission 1

Designs and history

  • Early Vedic worship used fire altars in the open, not temples.
  • Stone temples spread from about the 5th century CE.
  • Nagara style (north India): a curved, beehive-like shikhara.
  • Dravida style (south India): a stepped pyramid tower (vimana) over the shrine and huge gateway towers (gopurams).
  • Many temples are built by rules in texts on sacred architecture (Vastu Shastra, Agamas): facing east, laid out on a square grid.
Mission 1

Worship together in the mandir

  • Priests (pujaris) wake, bathe, dress and offer food to the murtis through the day.
  • Devotees come for darshan: to see, and be seen by, the deity.
  • Congregational worship: many gather for arti (lamp offering) with singing; prasad (blessed food) is shared.
  • Festivals, weddings and life rites bring the whole community together.
  • The mandir is also a community centre: classes in language, music and scripture; meals; care for elders.

Mission 1

The home shrine

  • Most Hindu homes have a shrine: a room, a corner or a cupboard.
  • It holds murtis or pictures of the family's chosen deities (ishta devata), a lamp (diya), incense, a bell, water, flowers.
  • Daily puja, morning and often evening: often led by a parent or grandparent, sometimes by every member.
  • Importance: makes God part of daily life; brings the family together; children learn by joining in.
  • Food may be offered before meals and eaten as prasad.
  • The shrine is kept clean; shoes are left outside.
Mission 1

Outdoors and shrines

  • Outdoor shrines: by roads, under trees, at village edges; a stone or murti marked with red powder, flowers and a lamp.
  • Village deities protect the local area; passers-by stop briefly to pray.
  • Sacred trees (e.g. the peepal) and the tulsi plant in a courtyard are worshipped.
  • Rivers and hills: bathing at the Ganges ghats; walking round a sacred hill.
  • Importance: God is present in nature; worship is woven into ordinary journeys.
Mission 1Read the source

Worship in the heart

  • The space of the heart: God within (antaryami); worship by meditation and inner prayer, anywhere.

Bhagavad Gita 9.13–14 (Edwin Arnold's translation, 1885):

> "But My Mahatmas, those of noble soul / Who tread the path celestial, worship Me / With hearts unwandering ... Unendingly / They glorify Me"

Q1 How do the "noble" worshippers of 9.13–14 worship?

Show answer

With "hearts unwandering": steady inner devotion.

Q2 Why might this matter for a Hindu who cannot reach a mandir?

Show answer

Mission 1Evaluate

"Worship at home is more important than worship in the mandir."

  • For: daily practice happens at home; family and children learn there; it needs no priest.
  • For: God is everywhere and in the heart; a building is not required.
  • Against: in the mandir, the deity is formally installed and served by trained priests; darshan there is special.
  • Against: congregational worship and festivals build community.
  • Another view: many Hindus see them as partners: home for daily devotion, mandir for community and special occasions.
  • Judge it by: What is worship for: personal devotion or community? What do different traditions stress?

Mission 2

What worship is for

  • Hindu worship is mostly bhakti (loving devotion): treating God as an honoured, beloved guest.
  • It uses all the senses: sight (murti, lamps), sound (bells, mantras, songs), smell (incense, flowers), touch, taste (prasad).
  • Purposes: to show love and thanks; to receive blessing; to focus the mind; to purify the heart.
  • Offerings are returned as prasad (grace): blessed food shared by everyone.
Mission 2

Puja: honouring God as a guest

  • Puja: worship with offerings to a murti, at home or in the mandir.
  • Typical steps: ring the bell; light a lamp and incense; offer water, flowers, rice, fruit or sweets; say or sing mantras; bow.
  • In the mandir, priests may bathe and dress the murti (a fuller puja of up to 16 offerings).
  • The puja tray (thali) holds lamp, incense, kumkum (red powder), rice, flowers, a small bell and a water pot.
  • At home: daily, often morning and evening, at the family shrine; short and personal.
  • Food offered is then taken back and eaten as prasad.
Mission 2

Arti and darshan

  • Arti: a tray with lamps of ghee (clarified butter) is waved in circles before the murti, while people sing (e.g. Om Jai Jagdish Hare) and bells ring.
  • Then worshippers pass their hands over the flame and draw them to their eyes and head: receiving the deity's blessing and light.
  • Darshan ("seeing"): to see the deity, and be seen by the deity. Eye contact with the murti is a moment of blessing.
  • Darshan can also be of a holy person or a holy place.
  • In the mandir, the curtain before the shrine is drawn back for darshan at set times.
Mission 2

Havan: the fire offering

  • Havan (or homa): offerings of ghee, grains and herbs (samagri) placed into a sacred fire.
  • It comes from the oldest Vedic worship: fire (Agni) carries offerings to the gods.
  • Led by a priest chanting Vedic mantras; worshippers add offerings with each "svaha".
  • Used at life rites (weddings are witnessed by the fire), new homes and festivals, and to purify.
  • The Arya Samaj makes havan central and does not use murtis.

Mission 2

Sacred sound: mantra, japa, bhajan, kirtan

  • Mantra: a sacred word or verse believed to carry spiritual power. Om (Aum) is the most sacred sound.
  • Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10), recited daily by many Hindus at dawn: "May we attain that excellent glory of Savitar the God: So may he stimulate our prayers." (Griffith's translation, 1896)
  • Japa: repeating a mantra or God's name, often on a mala (108 beads): e.g. Om Namah Shivaya; Hare Krishna.
  • Bhajan: devotional song. Kirtan: call-and-response singing of God's names, with drums, cymbals and harmonium.
  • Sound and visual imagery work together: bells and chants for the ear; lamps, flowers and the murti for the eye.
Mission 2

Meditation

  • Dhyana: stilling and focusing the mind on God, on Om, or on the true self.
  • The Gita describes the setting: "In a fair, still spot / Having his fixed abode,—not too much raised, / Nor yet too low" (Bhagavad Gita 6.11, Arnold), with the mind fixed on the One (6.12).
  • Can be done anywhere: at home, in the mandir, outdoors.
  • Valued especially in raja yoga and by Advaita Hindus; devotional Hindus meditate on a form of God.
Mission 2Read the source

Offer it with love

Krishna to Arjuna, Bhagavad Gita 9.26 (Edwin Arnold's translation, 1885):

> "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made / With pious will."

Mission 2Diversity

Vaishnava and Shaiva worship

  • Vaishnavas (devotees of Vishnu and his avatars, Krishna and Rama): murtis dressed, decorated and offered food many times a day; tulsi leaves; a vertical tilak mark on the forehead; kirtan of God's names (e.g. ISKCON's Hare Krishna).
  • Shaivas (devotees of Shiva): worship of the Shiva linga with abhisheka (pouring water, milk or honey over it); bilva leaves; three horizontal lines of sacred ash; Om Namah Shivaya; all-night worship at Maha Shivaratri.
  • Both are bhakti: loving devotion to a personal God.
Mission 2Diversity

How important is worship? What are its benefits?

  • Devotional Hindus: worship is the heart of religion; through it God's grace is received.
  • Advaita Hindus: worship of a form of God is a valuable step; it prepares the mind for knowledge of formless Brahman.
  • Arya Samaj: havan and Vedic prayer, but no murtis.
  • Some Hindus: service of others and right living matter more than ritual.
  • Benefits named by Hindus: peace and focus; closeness to God; good karma and purification; family and community bonds; passing on tradition to children.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Worship is essential for every Hindu."

  • For: bhakti is the most widely followed path; the Gita praises the one who worships with the inmost self (6.47).
  • For: daily puja shapes family life and passes on the faith.
  • Against: jnana and karma yoga are also paths to moksha; a Hindu may follow them with little ritual.
  • Against: Advaita treats worship of forms as a stage, not the goal.
  • Against: some Hindus say living by dharma matters more than ritual.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "worship" (ritual only, or service and meditation too)? Which tradition? What is the goal of worship?

Mission 3

What is a samskara?

  • Samskara: a rite that marks, blesses and "refines" a person at a turning point in life.
  • Tradition counts sixteen samskaras, from before birth to death.
  • They are described in ancient household manuals (the Grihya Sutras) and later law books.
  • Most are led by a priest (often a Brahmin), with a sacred fire (havan), mantras and the family present.
  • Purpose: to purify, to bless each new stage of life, and to help the person live by dharma.
  • Today most Hindus keep a few main ones: birth rites, the sacred thread (in some families), marriage and death.
Mission 3

Before and at birth

  • Rites before birth pray for a healthy child and protect the mother (e.g. simantonnayana, the parting of the mother's hair, in pregnancy).
  • Jatakarma (birth rite): the father touches the baby's lips with honey and ghee, and writes or whispers Om on the tongue, often with a gold ring or pen.
  • Prayers for a long life, strength and wisdom.
  • Many families have a horoscope drawn up from the time of birth.
  • The family is in a period of ritual impurity for some days; visitors may wait before coming.
Mission 3

Name, first food, first haircut

  • Namakarana (naming): around the 11th or 12th day. The name may begin with a syllable from the horoscope; it is whispered in the baby's ear.
  • Nishkramana: first outing, to see the sun.
  • Annaprashana: first solid food at about six months: often sweet rice (kheer) from a silver spoon.
  • Mundan (chudakarana): first head-shaving, often in the first or third year, sometimes at a temple or pilgrimage site; hair offered to the river.
  • Meaning: to remove impurity carried from past lives, and to thank God for the child.

Mission 3

The sacred thread (upanayana)

  • Upanayana = "taking near": the child is brought to a teacher (guru) to begin sacred study.
  • Traditionally for boys of the three "twice-born" varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya), roughly between ages 8 and 12.
  • The boy receives the sacred thread (yajnopavita, janeu): three strands worn over the left shoulder and under the right arm.
  • The guru teaches him the Gayatri Mantra; the boy begs alms (often first from his mother), a sign of humility as a student.
  • It marks a second birth into spiritual life and the start of the student stage (brahmacharya).
  • The three strands are often said to stand for debts to God, to the sages and to the ancestors.
Mission 3

Marriage (vivaha)

  • For most Hindus the most important samskara: it begins the householder stage (grihastha).
  • Kanyadan: the bride's father gives her hand to the groom.
  • The sacred fire (Agni) is the witness; the couple make offerings into it.
  • The ends of their clothes are tied together; the groom gives a mangalsutra necklace and puts red sindoor in the bride's hair parting.
  • Saptapadi ("seven steps"): seven steps or circles around the fire, each with a vow: food, strength, wealth, happiness, children, long life, friendship.
  • In Indian law the marriage is complete when the seventh step is taken.
  • Weddings vary by region and family: from a few hours to several days.
Mission 3Read the source

Changing clothes

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh." (Bhagavad Gita 2.22, Arnold 1885)

  • The atman (self) does not die; only the body is laid aside.
  • So Hindu funerals are serious but not without hope: the soul moves on to a new birth, or to moksha.

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 3

Death rites (antyeshti)

  • Antyeshti = "the last sacrifice": the body is offered to the fire.
  • At death: Ganges water and a tulsi leaf may be placed in the mouth; family chant God's name.
  • The body is washed, dressed and carried to cremation, usually within a day, as mourners chant, e.g. "Ram nam satya hai" ("Rama's name is truth").
  • The chief mourner, traditionally the eldest son, walks around the pyre and lights it.
  • Ashes are gathered and placed in a sacred river, ideally the Ganges.
  • About 10–13 days of mourning follow, ending with shraddha: offerings of rice balls (pinda) to feed and help the soul of the departed.
Mission 3

Why the Ganges at death?

  • Many Hindus believe Ganga purifies and carries the soul toward a good rebirth or moksha.
  • In Varanasi, cremations at Manikarnika Ghat go on day and night; many believe that to die or be cremated there brings liberation.
  • Ashes are also placed in the Ganges at Haridwar and Prayagraj.
  • Hindus outside India often fly ashes home, or use rivers near them: some British councils provide places where ashes can be placed in a river.
  • In Britain, cremations usually take place in a crematorium; the chief mourner may press the button to start the cremation.

Mission 3Diversity

Different attitudes

  • Traditional families may keep many samskaras in full, in Sanskrit, with a priest.
  • Most families today keep a few: birth rites, marriage, death; others are shortened or joined together.
  • Sacred thread: many families no longer hold it; some do it just before a wedding. Reform groups such as the Arya Samaj give it to girls and to people of any birth. ISKCON gives initiation (diksha) by devotion, not birth.
  • Women's roles: traditionally sons light the pyre; today daughters sometimes do, and some women attend cremations who once would not.
  • In Britain: legal marriage registration, crematorium rules and work schedules shape the rites.
  • Some see samskaras as cultural family traditions; others as sacred duties that purify and guide the soul.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Rites of passage are less important for Hindus today than in the past."

  • For: fewer samskaras are kept; the sacred thread is often skipped; rites are shortened to fit modern life.
  • For: some young Hindus see them as culture rather than religion.
  • Against: marriage and death rites are still kept by almost all families, often at great cost and effort.
  • Against: in Britain, rites help keep identity and pass on faith; families still fly ashes to India.
  • Another view: the rites are not weaker, just adapted: the meaning (blessing, purification, dharma) remains.
  • Judge it by: Is importance measured by the number of rites kept, or by what they mean to people? Does adapting a rite weaken it? Do attitudes differ between generations?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hindus worship in the mandir, the home, at outdoor shrines and rivers, and in the space of the heart.
  • Mandir features: garbhagriha with murti, shikhara, vahana facing the shrine, mandapa; congregational arti, darshan and prasad.

Mission 2

  • Forms of worship: puja (offerings), arti (lamps), darshan (seeing), havan (fire), japa (repetition), bhajan and kirtan (song), meditation; offered food returns as prasad.
  • Gita 9.26: a leaf, flower, fruit or water offered with love is accepted.
  • Vaishnava and Shaiva worship differ in deity, offerings and marks; Hindus differ on how important ritual worship is.

Mission 3

  • Samskaras (traditionally sixteen) bless and purify each stage of life; birth rites (jatakarma, namakarana, annaprashana, mundan), the sacred thread (upanayana), marriage (vivaha, saptapadi) and death (antyeshti).
  • At death the body is cremated and the ashes placed in the Ganges where possible, because Ganga is believed to purify and help the soul toward moksha (Gita 2.22: the self lays the body aside like old clothes).
  • Hindus differ: some keep many rites, most keep a few; reformers open the thread to all; British life shapes practice.
