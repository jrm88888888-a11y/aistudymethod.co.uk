Mission 1

Mission 1: Blood leaves the heart in surges but creeps past your cells in a steady trickle. How?

Make a guess: Blood leaves the heart in surges but creeps past your cells in a steady trickle. How?

Mission 1

Open or closed, single or double

  • Open: blood leaves vessels and bathes the organs directly.
  • Closed: blood stays inside vessels all the way round.
  • Single (bony fish): heart → gills → body → heart. One pass through the heart per circuit.
  • Blood loses much of its pressure in the gill capillaries. It reaches the body at low pressure.
  • Double (mammals): heart → lungs → heart → body → heart. Two passes per circuit.
  • Pulmonary circuit (lungs) and systemic circuit (body). After the lungs, blood is pumped again, so the body gets high-pressure blood.
  • Say: "double = blood passes through the heart twice per circuit" — not: "two capillary beds".
Mission 1

Three layers, five vessels

  • Walls are built from up to three layers:
    • inner: endothelium (squamous: one flat cell thick, smooth);
    • middle: smooth muscle and elastic tissue;
    • outer: fibrous (collagen) tissue.

Mission 1

Arteries and arterioles

  • Each ventricular contraction forces a surge of blood into the arteries.
  • Elastic tissue stretches as the surge arrives, then recoils between beats.
  • Recoil keeps pressure up between beats and smooths the flow.
  • Thick muscle and elastic tissue let the wall withstand high pressure; fibrous tissue stops it overstretching.
  • Smooth endothelium: little friction.
  • Arterioles: smooth muscle contracts (vasoconstriction) or relaxes (vasodilation).
  • This sets how much blood enters each capillary bed.
  • So blood is redistributed between organs as activity changes.
  • Asleep: muscle flow is minimal. Brain flow stays nearly constant in sleep, wakeful rest and exercise.
  • Say: "the artery wall stretches and recoils" — not: "the artery wall contracts to pump blood".

Mission 1

Veins: back to the heart at low pressure

  • By the veins, blood pressure is low and there is no pulse.
  • Wide lumen: little resistance to flow.
  • Thin, flexible wall: squeezed by surrounding skeletal muscles as they contract.
  • Valves: passive flaps that close if blood starts to flow backwards.
  • So every squeeze moves blood one way: towards the heart.
  • Say: "valves prevent backflow" — not: "valves push blood along".
Mission 1

Capillaries: built for exchange

  • Wall: endothelium only, one cell thick → short diffusion distance.
  • Narrow: red cells pass in single file, close to the wall.
  • Branching: huge numbers form capillary beds → huge total surface area.
  • Every cell lies close to a capillary.
  • Flow is slowest here: more time for exchange.
  • Endothelial cells are squamous (flat).
  • Fenestrations: pores in the wall of some capillaries, where exchange must be very rapid (e.g. kidney glomerulus). Too small for blood cells.
  • Say: "thin walls give a short diffusion distance" — not: "thin walls let blood flow faster".

Mission 1

From surge to trickle

  • Arteries: pressure surges with each beat; elastic recoil smooths the swings.
  • Arterioles: narrow and muscular: the biggest fall in pressure. The surges die away.
  • Capillaries: one aorta branches into billions of capillaries. Their total cross-sectional area is enormous.
  • The same volume of blood per second spread over far more area → flow hundreds of times slower.
  • Veins: pressure lowest; muscles and valves return the blood.
  • So: surges in, steady trickle past the cells.
Mission 1

Measuring pulse rate

  • Each ventricular contraction sends a pressure wave along the arteries: the pulse.
  • Feel it with two fingertips (not the thumb) where an artery lies near the skin:
    • radial pulse: wrist, thumb side;
    • carotid pulse: neck, beside the windpipe.
  • Count beats for 60 s, or for 30 s and double. Repeat and take a mean.
  • Short counts magnify errors: a miscount of 1 in 10 s is an error of 6 beats per minute.
  • Compare with a digital monitor (e.g. a fingertip sensor).
  • Veins have no pulse: the wave dies out in the arterioles and capillaries.
Quick check
?A student counts 12 beats in 10 seconds at the radial pulse. Immediately afterwards a digital heart rate monitor on the same person reads 74 beats per minute. What is the most reasonable conclusion?
Mission 1Practical

Arteries and veins in section

  • Skill: tell arteries from veins in micrographs.
  • Artery: wall thick relative to the lumen; round, narrow lumen; wavy elastic layer.
  • Vein: wall thin relative to the lumen; wide lumen, often flattened or irregular.
  • Capillary: a ring of one or two flat endothelial cells, about one red cell wide.

Mission check

?With age, the walls of large arteries lose elastic tissue and become stiffer. Blood pressure in an arm artery is measured at its highest (as the ventricle contracts) and its lowest (between beats). What change in these readings is expected?

Mission 2

Mission 2: Your heart has no switches, yet its valves open and close at exactly the right moments. How?

Make a guess: Your heart has no switches, yet its valves open and close at exactly the right moments. How?

Mission 2

Four chambers, two pumps

  • Right side: receives deoxygenated blood from the body; pumps it to the lungs.
  • Left side: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs; pumps it to the body.
  • The septum keeps the two sides apart.
  • Route: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and ventricle.
  • Semilunar valves lie at the base of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
  • Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself.
  • In diagrams the heart faces you: its right side is on your left.
Mission 2

Built to deliver pressure

  • Cardiac muscle: contracts rhythmically, without tiring, for a lifetime.
  • Pacemaker (sinoatrial node): starts each beat.
  • Atria: thin walls. They pump only into the ventricles below.
  • Ventricles: thick walls. They pump out into the arteries.
  • Left ventricle wall about three times thicker than the right: it must drive blood round the whole body.
  • Both ventricles pump the same volume per beat.
  • Valves stop backflow. Septum stops mixing. Coronary vessels bring O2 to the hard-working muscle.
Quick check
?The wall of the left ventricle is about three times thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which statement correctly explains this difference?
Mission 2

Valves move with pressure

  • A valve has no muscle of its own and no nerve. Blood pressure moves it.
  • Higher pressure behind a valve pushes it open.
  • Higher pressure in front pushes it shut.
  • AV valve: open when atrial pressure > ventricular; shut when ventricular > atrial.
  • Semilunar valve: open when ventricular pressure > arterial; shut when arterial > ventricular.
  • Say: "the valve shuts because pressure in the ventricle exceeds that in the atrium" — not: "muscles close the valve".
Mission 2

The cardiac cycle

  • One cycle at 75 beats per minute lasts 60 ÷ 75 = 0.8 s.
  • (1) Atrial systole (about 0.1 s): atria contract; ventricles relaxed; AV valves open. Blood tops up the ventricles.
  • (2) Ventricular systole (about 0.3 s): ventricles contract. Their pressure rises:
    • above atrial pressure → AV valves shut;
    • above arterial pressure → semilunar valves open; blood is forced out.
  • (3) Diastole (about 0.4 s): all chambers relax. Ventricular pressure falls:
    • below arterial pressure → semilunar valves shut;
    • below atrial pressure → AV valves open; blood flows in from the veins.
  • This is the sequence in the left side after the SAN fires.
Quick check
?The sinoatrial node fires. Which sequence of events then follows in the left side of the heart?
Mission 2

Reading the pressure graph

  • Three lines: left atrium, left ventricle, aorta. Find where two lines cross.
  • A: ventricle rises above atrium → AV valve shuts.
  • B: ventricle rises above aorta → semilunar valve opens. Ejection starts.
  • C: ventricle falls below aorta → semilunar valve shuts. A small notch in the aortic trace.
  • D: ventricle falls below atrium → AV valve opens. Filling starts.
  • A → B and C → D: both valves shut; the ventricle's volume stays the same.
  • Aortic pressure never falls to zero: elastic recoil (see Mission 1).

Mission 2

Systolic and diastolic pressure

  • Blood pressure is measured in an artery of the upper arm, written e.g. 120/80 mm Hg.
  • Systolic (120): the peak, as the left ventricle contracts.
  • Diastolic (80): the minimum, while the ventricle relaxes. Elastic recoil holds it up.
  • 1 kPa ≈ 7.5 mm Hg: 120/80 mm Hg ≈ 16.0/10.7 kPa.
  • Higher readings mean the arteries are under more pressure throughout the cycle.
Quick check

Mission check

?Pressures in the right side of a heart, early in ventricular systole: right atrium 0.4 kPa; right ventricle 1.0 kPa and rising; pulmonary artery 1.3 kPa. Which statement is correct?

No switches needed: every valve opens when the pressure behind it is higher, and shuts when the pressure in front is higher.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Make a guess: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Mission 3

The heart feeds itself

  • Heart muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose.
  • It gets them from the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.
  • Blood inside the chambers does not supply the heart wall.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): coronary arteries narrowed or blocked.
  • Say: "occlusion starves the heart muscle of oxygen" — not: "blood can no longer pass through the chambers".
Mission 3

Atherosclerosis, step by step

  • (1) Endothelial dysfunction: the inner lining is damaged, e.g. by high blood pressure, smoking or high LDL cholesterol.
  • (2) Inflammatory response: white blood cells enter the wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
  • (3) Plaque (atheroma) builds inside the wall: cholesterol, white cells, fibrous tissue, calcium salts. The lumen narrows; the wall stiffens.
  • (4) Narrower, stiffer arteries raise blood pressure, which damages more endothelium.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening and narrowing of arteries by atheroma.
  • Say: "atheroma forms within the artery wall" — not: "fat sticks to the inside like grease in a drainpipe".
Mission 3

From plaque to blockage

  • A plaque can rupture, exposing the tissue beneath.
  • A clot (thrombus) forms in the artery: thrombosis.
  • Plaque plus clot can occlude (block) the artery. Or the clot breaks off and blocks a smaller artery downstream.
Quick check
?A blood clot completely occludes a branch of a coronary artery. What is the direct consequence?
Mission 3

What blockage does

  • Narrowed coronary artery: too little oxygen during exercise → chest pain (angina).
  • Blocked coronary artery: muscle beyond dies → myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Dead muscle is replaced by scar tissue, which does not contract.
  • A clot in an artery to the brain → stroke.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Make a guess: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Mission 4

Correlation or cause?

  • Correlation: two variables change together.
  • Causal relationship: a change in one produces a change in the other.
  • A correlation can arise from a confounding variable (a third factor linked to both), from chance, or from cause running the other way.
  • Stronger evidence of cause: a mechanism; a controlled trial; more exposure, more disease (dose–response); many studies agreeing.
  • Say: "is correlated with" — not: "causes", until the evidence shows it.
Mission 4

Correlation coefficients

  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives direction: + both rise together; − one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives strength: near ±1 strong; near 0 little or none.
  • Sample size changes how far you can trust a coefficient, not how strong it is.
  • A low or zero coefficient can be evidence against a hypothesis. Even a strong one does not prove cause.

Mission 4

Conflicting evidence: the HRT story

  • HRT (hormone replacement therapy): hormones taken to ease symptoms of the menopause.
  • Early observational studies: women taking HRT had less coronary heart disease. This was read as cause and effect.
  • Later randomised controlled trials: HRT caused a small increase in CHD risk.
  • Why? Women who chose HRT had higher socioeconomic status, which itself lowers CHD risk: a confounding variable.
  • Random allocation spreads confounders evenly between groups, so a trial isolates the treatment's own effect.
  • Say: "the correlation was real but not causal" — not: "the early studies got the numbers wrong".
Quick check
?Early epidemiological studies found that women taking HRT had a lower incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD). Later randomised controlled trials found that HRT slightly increased CHD risk. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A study of 2000 adults found a correlation coefficient of −0.40 between hours of golf played per week and the incidence of coronary heart disease. Which conclusion is best supported?

Fat and heart disease, HRT and heart disease, golf and heart disease: a correlation is where the question starts, not where it ends.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Make a guess: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Mission 5

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then the null hypothesis is not rejected.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 5

Worked example: chi-squared beyond genetics

Daisies counted in two areas of a field of equal size, one trampled and one not (illustrative data). Null hypothesis: no difference, so equal numbers are expected.

TrampledUntrampledTotal
Observed, O3862100
Expected, E5050100
(O − E)² ÷ E2.882.88
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E = 2.88 + 2.88 = 5.76.
  • Degrees of freedom = categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 5.76 > 3.84: significant. Reject the null hypothesis: daisies are not spread evenly between the two areas.
  • The test does not say why. Trampling is one explanation to test further.
  • Expected values come from the null hypothesis: here, equal areas → equal numbers.

Mission 5

Chi-squared for association

  • Do two species occur together more or less often than chance predicts? Record presence or absence of each in many random quadrats.
  • Arrange the counts in a 2 × 2 contingency table.
  • Expected count for each cell = row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • Degrees of freedom = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • Significant, and together less than expected: a negative association. Together more than expected: positive.
  • A negative association is consistent with competition, or with different habitat needs. It indicates; it does not prove.
Quick check
?Two lichen species were recorded as present or absent in 80 random quadrats on a stone wall: both present 16, only A present 32, only B present 24, neither 8. If the two species were distributed independently of each other, how many quadrats would be expected to contain both species?
Mission 5

Correlation

  • Plot two measured variables on a scatter graph (scattergram).
  • Positive correlation: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. 0 means no correlation.
  • −0.85 is as strong as +0.85.
  • R² (coefficient of determination) = r². It is the proportion of variation in one variable explained by variation in the other. r = +0.90 → R² = 0.81: 81%.
  • Example: along a transect, plant cover can be correlated with an abiotic variable such as soil salinity or light intensity.
Quick check
?Across a range of animal species (illustrative data), the correlation coefficient between body size and nerve impulse conduction speed was r = −0.85. What does this value show?
Mission 5

Correlation is not cause

  • A correlation, even a strong one, can arise from:
    • chance (small samples);
    • a third variable linked to both (a confounding variable);
    • cause running the other way.
  • Evidence for cause: a mechanism; controlled experiments or randomised controlled trials; the effect grows with the dose; many studies agree.
  • Early observational studies linked HRT with less coronary heart disease. Randomised trials then showed a small increase in risk. Women taking HRT had higher socioeconomic status, which itself lowers risk.
  • Say: "there is a positive correlation between X and Y" — not: "X causes Y", unless there is evidence for cause.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Make a guess: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Mission 6

Sources and sinks

  • Phloem carries organic substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids: translocation.
  • Source: where sugars enter the phloem. A photosynthesising leaf; a storage root in spring.
  • Sink: where sugars leave the phloem to be used or stored. Roots, meristems, fruits, growing storage organs.
  • Sap flows from source to sink: up or down.
  • Different sieve tubes can carry sap in opposite directions at the same time.
  • Say: "from source to sink" — not: "always down from the leaves".
Mission 6

Sieve tubes and companion cells

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells, end to end, forming sieve tubes.
  • End walls are sieve plates, full of pores: sap flows from cell to cell.
  • No nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm: little blocks the flow.
  • Companion cells: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria: they make the ATP for loading.
  • Many plasmodesmata link each companion cell to its sieve tube element.
  • Walls of cellulose, not lignified.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".
Quick check
?Which feature of a sieve tube element helps sap to flow along the phloem?
Mission 6

Loading at the source

  • (1) The companion cell uses ATP to pump H⁺ out: active transport.
  • (2) H⁺ diffuses back in through a co-transporter protein, carrying sucrose in with it.
  • (3) Sucrose passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata.
  • At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used in respiration or converted to starch.
  • Many mitochondria supply the ATP; plasmodesmata link the two cells. Loading at sources and unloading at sinks keep sap moving between them.
Quick check
?In spring, sugars stored in the roots of a deciduous tree are converted to sucrose and carried in the phloem to the opening buds. Which statement about this transport is correct?
Mission check

?A young leaf near a shoot tip is still expanding. It imports sucrose through the phloem. Weeks later, fully grown, the same leaf exports sucrose. Which explanation is correct?

Up to the buds, down to the roots, in and then out of the same leaf: sap always flows from where sugar is loaded to where it is used.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Arteries stretch and recoil; arterioles control flow and drop pressure; capillaries' huge total area slows flow for exchange; veins return blood with valves and muscle squeeze.
  • Fish: single circulation (heart once per circuit); mammals: double circulation, so body blood is at high pressure.

Mission 2

  • Valves open and shut only because of pressure differences; atrial systole, then ventricular systole, then diastole.
  • Systolic pressure is the arterial peak during ventricular systole; diastolic is the minimum during diastole.

Mission 3

  • Atheroma builds inside a damaged artery wall; it narrows the lumen and raises blood pressure.
  • A ruptured plaque triggers a clot; occlusion of a coronary artery starves heart muscle of oxygen.

Mission 4

  • A correlation coefficient gives direction (sign) and strength (size); even a strong correlation does not prove cause.
  • Confounding variables explain conflicting evidence, as with HRT; randomised trials test cause.

Mission 5

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • A correlation coefficient measures direction and strength (−1 to +1); correlation alone never shows cause.

Mission 6

  • Phloem sieve tubes (living, no nucleus, sieve plates) and companion cells (many mitochondria) translocate sucrose from sources to sinks, up or down.
