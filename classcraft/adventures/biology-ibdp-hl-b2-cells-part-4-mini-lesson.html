Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Make a guess: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Mission 1

One cell becomes two

  • A parent cell (often called the mother cell) divides into two daughter cells.
  • The parent does not survive as a third cell: its contents are shared out.
  • First the nucleus divides: mitosis.
  • Then the cytoplasm divides: cytokinesis.
  • Nuclear division must come first, or one cell would be anucleate (no nucleus).
  • Not every cell keeps dividing: most mature nerve cells never do.
Mission 1

What mitosis is for

  • Growth: more cells.
  • Repair and replacement: cells lost by wounds or wear.
  • Growth: plant meristems, early-stage embryos. Replacement: cells beneath the skin surface replace those shed. Repair: the same cells divide to heal a wound.
  • Each daughter cell gets the same chromosome number and the same genes: genetic constancy.
  • Say: "growth needs cell division to add cells" — not: "organs grow just by their cells getting bigger".
Mission 1

Copy first: two chromatids

  • Before a cell divides, every DNA molecule is replicated.
  • This happens in S phase of interphase.
  • Each chromosome is then two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • They stay joined until anaphase.
  • One chromatid = one DNA molecule. Two joined chromatids = one chromosome.
  • Say: "replication gives each chromosome two sister chromatids" — not: "replication doubles the chromosome number".
Quick check
?A cell of an organism has 8 chromosomes before its DNA is replicated. How many chromosomes and how many DNA molecules are in its nucleus at metaphase of mitosis?
Mission 1

The cell cycle

  • Interphase (G1 → S → G2), then mitosis, then cytokinesis.
  • G1: the cell grows, makes proteins, duplicates organelles.
  • S: DNA is replicated.
  • G2: more growth and protein synthesis; the cell prepares to divide.
  • Interphase is metabolically active: biosynthesis of proteins and DNA; mitochondria and chloroplasts grow and divide.
  • Interphase takes most of the cycle. It is not a resting stage.
  • Many cells leave the cycle and stop dividing; some can be stimulated to divide again.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly gives the sequence of phases in the cell cycle?
Mission 1

Mitosis in four stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense (shorter, thicker, visible). The nuclear envelope breaks down. The spindle forms.
  • Condensation: DNA wraps around histones and is supercoiled.
  • The nuclear double membrane breaks into vesicles, so spindle microtubules can reach the chromosomes.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Spindle fibres from both poles attach at each centromere.
  • Anaphase: centromeres divide. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles; the spindle fibres shorten.
  • The pull: microtubule motors move chromatids along microtubules, which shorten.
  • Telophase: chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. A nuclear envelope forms round each set. The spindle breaks down.
  • Say: "sister chromatids separate" — not: "homologous chromosomes separate".
Quick check
?A body cell has 4 chromosomes. It is in anaphase of mitosis. What is moving towards each pole of the cell?
Mission 1

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
  • It usually begins during telophase.
  • Animal cell: the membrane is pulled in at the equator, forming a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two.
  • The pull comes from a ring of contractile actin and myosin proteins.
  • Plant cell: the rigid wall cannot be pinched. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse into a cell plate.
  • The cell plate becomes new membranes and a new wall. It grows outwards until it joins the old wall.
  • Say: "spindle microtubules move chromosomes" — not: "the spindle pinches the cell in two".
Quick check
?A dividing cell is examined after its two new nuclei have formed. Many small vesicles are seen gathering and fusing in a line across the equator of the cell, midway between the nuclei. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission check

?A population of a hypothetical freshwater plant reproduces only by fragmentation: pieces break off a parent plant and grow into new plants by mitosis. Which claim about the new plants is best supported?

Copy every chromosome, then pull the copies apart: each new cell gets a full, identical set. Four million times a second, or once in a broken-off fragment.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Make a guess: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Mission 2

Read the stage from the chromosomes

  • Interphase: intact nucleus; no separate chromosomes (DNA uncoiled). Most cells look like this.
  • Cells divide out of step, so the commonest stage is the one that lasts longest.
  • Prophase: thread-like chromosomes, scattered; nuclear envelope disappearing.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes in a single line across the equator.
  • Anaphase: two groups of V-shaped chromatids moving apart.
  • Telophase: two clusters at the poles; nuclear envelopes re-forming. In plants, a cell plate between them.
  • Why visible? Condensed chromosomes are short and thick, so they take up stain densely.
  • Say: "intact nucleus, no visible chromosomes = interphase" — not: "= prophase".
Quick check
?Two cells are seen in a stained micrograph of a root tip. Cell 1 has an intact nucleus in which no separate chromosomes can be distinguished. Cell 2 has no nuclear envelope, and its condensed chromosomes lie in a single line across the middle of the cell. Which identification is correct?
Mission 2Practical

Root-tip squash

  • Skill (D2.1.8): identify phases in cells under the microscope.
  • Cut the last 5 mm of a growing root (onion or garlic). Dividing cells are in the meristem, near the tip.
  • Warm in hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1 mol dm⁻³, about 60 °C, a few minutes). This breaks the middle lamella, so cells separate, and stops division.
  • Rinse; add a stain that binds DNA (e.g. toluidine blue or acetic orcein).
  • Cover with a coverslip and squash with firm, vertical pressure: no sideways slide. Aim for one layer of cells.
  • Find the meristem at low power, then identify stages at high power (×400).
  • Key controls: use only the tip; same acid time and temperature for every root; count every cell in a field, not just the dividing ones.
  • Supports: the mitotic index.
Quick check
?In a stained root-tip squash, one cell shows two groups of V-shaped chromatids, their points facing opposite ends of the cell. Which explains this appearance?
Mission 2

Counting cells measures time

  • Mitotic index = number of cells in mitosis ÷ total number of cells counted.
  • A higher index means a larger share of cells dividing.
  • Cells in a tissue divide out of step. So the fraction of cells in a stage ≈ the fraction of the cycle spent in it.
  • Time in a stage = (cells in that stage ÷ total cells) × length of one cycle.
  • Most cells are in interphase: it is the longest stage.
  • Say: "divide by the total number of cells" — not: "by the cells not in that stage".
Mission 2

Worked example: from counts to hours

Illustrative data: one field of a root-tip squash. One cell cycle takes 20 hours.

StageCells
Interphase440
Prophase30
Metaphase12
Anaphase8
Telophase10
Total500
  • Cells in mitosis = 30 + 12 + 8 + 10 = 60.
  • Mitotic index = 60 ÷ 500 = 0.12 (12%).
  • Time in mitosis = 0.12 × 20 h = 2.4 h.
  • Prophase: 30 ÷ 500 × 20 h = 1.2 h, the longest stage of mitosis here.
  • Assumes cells are out of step, and every stage was identified correctly.
Quick check
?A student examined 240 cells in a micrograph of a growing tissue. 204 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes, and the remaining 36 cells had condensed chromosomes visible. What is the mitotic index of the sample?
Mission 2

Equal and unequal cytokinesis

  • Cytokinesis is usually equal: two daughter cells of similar size.
  • Each daughter must get at least one mitochondrion (and, in plants, a plastid, e.g. a chloroplast). These form only by division of existing ones.
  • Unequal cytokinesis, example 1: human oogenesis. The egg cell keeps almost all the cytoplasm; a tiny polar body gets almost none.
  • Example 2: budding in yeast. A small bud receives a nucleus after nuclear division, then separates.
  • Chromosomes are still shared equally. Only the cytoplasm is unequal.
Quick check
?Which statement about the division of the cytoplasm between two daughter cells is correct?
Mission check

?A student counted 100 cells in a micrograph of a growing root tip. 85 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes; 6 had condensed chromosomes scattered through the cell; 3 had chromosomes aligned across the equator; 4 had two groups of chromatids moving apart; 2 had two groups of chromosomes at the poles with nuclear envelopes forming. Which conclusion is correct?

More cells in a stage means more time spent in it. One frozen moment, counted carefully, is a clock.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Make a guess: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Mission 3

The body plan problem

  • A zygote divides by mitosis into many unspecialised, genetically identical cells.
  • They must become different in the right places: head at one end, tail at the other.
  • Body plan: the arrangement of body parts along the body's axes, such as head to tail.
  • The genes that set up body plans code for transcription factors. Each one switches on many other genes.
Mission 3

Gradients tell cells where they are

  • (1) In a fruit fly egg, the mother places mRNA for the protein Bicoid at the head end.
  • (2) After fertilisation, Bicoid protein spreads out: a concentration gradient, high at the head end, low towards the tail.
  • At this stage the fly embryo is one large cell with many nuclei, so the protein can spread between them.
  • (3) Bicoid is a transcription factor. Different genes switch on above different threshold concentrations, so each region expresses its own set.
  • Every nucleus keeps all its genes; the gradient changes which are expressed.
  • Say: "cells respond to the concentration they receive" — not: "the signal is simply on or off".
Quick check
?Female fruit flies with extra copies of the bicoid gene lay eggs containing more Bicoid mRNA than normal at the head end. What is predicted for their embryos?
Mission check

?In a chick wing bud, cells at the rear edge release a signalling protein that spreads towards the front edge. Each digit has its own shape, and the digits lie in a set order from rear to front. When extra signalling cells are grafted onto the front edge, the wing grows an extra set of digits in mirror image. What is the best explanation?

Position is read as concentration. Move the source and the body plan moves with it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Make a guess: One early embryo cell can grow into a whole baby. Why can't a cell from your bone marrow?

Mission 4

What makes a stem cell?

  • A stem cell is undifferentiated. It can do two things:
  • (1) Divide again and again, renewing itself.
  • (2) Differentiate into specialised cell types.
  • Key phrase: a capacity to divide endlessly and to differentiate along different pathways.
  • Most specialised cells cannot do both.
  • Say: "able to divide endlessly" — not: "always dividing". Stem cells can rest between divisions.
Quick check
?Which pair of properties defines a stem cell?
Mission 4

Potency: how many cell types can it make?

  • Totipotent: any cell type, including the placenta and other extra-embryonic tissues. The zygote and the cells of the first few divisions.
  • Pluripotent: any body cell type, but not the placenta. Inner cell mass of the blastocyst: embryonic stem cells.
  • Multipotent: a limited range of types. Adult tissues, e.g. bone marrow → blood cells.
  • Say: "totipotent includes the placenta; pluripotent does not" — not: "both mean any cell".
Quick check
?A cell taken from a 4-cell human embryo can develop into a complete individual with its own placenta. A cell from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst can form any tissue of the body but not a placenta. A stem cell from adult bone marrow forms only blood cells. How should the three cells be classified?
Mission 4

Stem cells in the adult body

  • Bone marrow: stem cells make every type of blood cell.
  • Hair follicles: stem cells below the skin surface make a new hair each growth cycle.
  • A stem cell niche is the microenvironment around stem cells. It can maintain them (resting, undifferentiated) or promote their division and differentiation.
Quick check
?In a hair follicle, stem cells in a region below the skin surface stay inactive for months between hair growth cycles. At the start of a new cycle they begin to divide and produce the cells of a new hair. What does this show about the stem cell niche?
Mission check

?A cell is taken from adult bone marrow and grown in a dish. It divides many times. Some daughter cells stay like the parent; others become erythrocytes, neutrophils and other blood cells, but never nerve or muscle cells. Which statement is correct?

An early embryo cell can still become anything; a bone-marrow stem cell is limited to blood.

That limited range is what multipotent means.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Make a guess: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Mission 5

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 5

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side / µm1234
Surface area / µm²6245496
Volume / µm³182764
SA:V / µm⁻¹6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • Cubes are a simplified model. Any shape scales the same way: area × (scale factor)², volume × (scale factor)³.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A cube of side 1 mm has a surface area of 6 mm², a volume of 1 mm³ and a surface area-to-volume ratio of 6. The side length is increased to 4 mm. What is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?

  • Place all cubes in the same acid solution for the same time.
  • Acid diffuses in; the indicator changes colour where it has reached.

Mission 5

Cells that beat the limit

  • Shape, not just size, sets SA:V. Cells add surface without adding much volume.
  • Microvilli: finger-like folds of the cell-surface membrane, e.g. ileum epithelial cells.
  • Flattening: erythrocytes are biconcave discs: more surface, shorter diffusion distance.
  • Proximal convoluted tubule cells: microvilli facing the filtrate and invaginations of the basal membrane.
  • Say: "microvilli are folds of membrane that add surface" — not: "microvilli beat like cilia".
Quick check
?A human erythrocyte (red blood cell) has a volume of about 90 µm³ and a surface area of about 135 µm². A sphere with the same volume would have a surface area of about 97 µm². What do these figures show about the adaptation of the erythrocyte?
Mission check

?A spherical single-celled organism grows from a radius of 10 µm to a radius of 30 µm, keeping its shape. Which prediction is best supported?

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Make a guess: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Mission 6

The airway tree

  • Air path: trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveoli (air sacs).
  • Repeated branching ends in hundreds of millions of alveoli: a surface of tens of square metres inside the chest.
  • Gas exchange happens only across the alveoli. The airways conduct air to them.
  • The lungs sit in the thorax, enclosed by the ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.
Mission 6

The alveolus: built for diffusion

  • Alveolar wall: one layer of flattened (squamous) epithelial cells.
  • Capillary wall: one layer of endothelial cells. Over much of the surface, air to blood is under 1 µm.
  • A dense capillary network wraps each alveolus. Continuous blood flow keeps the gradient.
  • Ventilation keeps alveolar air high in O2 and low in CO2.
  • A moist lining lets gases dissolve. Surfactant in it lowers surface tension, so alveoli do not collapse.
  • The branched network of bronchioles delivers air to every alveolus.
  • Say: "thin alveolar epithelium, one cell thick" — not: "thin cell walls". Animal cells have no cell walls.
Mission 6

Two kinds of alveolar cell

  • Type I pneumocytes: extremely thin. They form most of the alveolar surface and keep the diffusion distance short.
  • Type II pneumocytes: rounded, with many lamellar bodies (secretory vesicles). These discharge surfactant into the alveolus.
  • One tissue, two cell types: thinness and a cytoplasm full of vesicles cannot be built into one cell.
Quick check
?How are type I and type II pneumocytes adapted to their functions in the alveolar epithelium?
Quick check
?Mammalian lungs contain a branched network of bronchioles ending in millions of alveoli. How does this branching contribute to gas exchange?
Mission 6

Breathing in

  • (1) The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  • (1) The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. The internal intercostals relax.
  • (1) The abdominal muscles relax, so the abdominal organs can move down.
  • (2) Thorax volume rises, so pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric.
  • (3) Air flows in, down the pressure gradient. The lungs are pulled open; they do no work themselves.
Mission 6

Breathing out

  • At rest, breathing out is mostly passive. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
  • The diaphragm domes upward; the ribs move down and in; stretched elastic fibres in the lungs recoil.
  • Thorax volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
  • Forced breathing out: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in.
  • In forced breathing out, the abdominal muscles contract, pushing the organs up against the relaxed diaphragm.
  • External and internal intercostals are antagonistic: they move the ribs in opposite directions.
  • Say: "the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward" — not: "the diaphragm contracts and pushes up".
Mission 6

Antagonistic intercostals and titin

  • The fibres of the external and internal intercostals run in different directions, so they move the ribcage in opposite directions.
  • When one layer contracts, it stretches the other.
  • The stretch stores potential energy in the sarcomere protein titin.
  • The stored energy is released as the stretched layer recoils.
Quick check
?During inhalation the external intercostal muscles contract and move the ribcage up and outwards. What happens to the internal intercostal muscles at the same time, and why?
Quick check
?Which sequence of events draws air into the lungs during inspiration?
Mission check

?During a rugby match, a player is tackled hard. A small tear in the surface of one lung lets air leak into the space around that lung. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles on that side still contract normally, but that lung does not inflate. Which explanation is best?

The muscles change the volume of the thorax; the lungs simply follow. Break that link and the lung stays collapsed.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every chromosome is replicated first (two sister chromatids); mitosis then sends one chromatid of each chromosome to each pole, so both daughter cells get an identical, complete set. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm (cleavage furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).
  • The cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → mitosis (prophase, metaphase, anaphase, telophase) → cytokinesis.

Mission 2

  • Stages are identified from the chromosomes: none visible (interphase), scattered (prophase), in a line (metaphase), V-shaped groups moving apart (anaphase), clusters at the poles (telophase).
  • Cells divide out of step, so the fraction of cells in a stage gives the fraction of the cycle spent in it.
  • Mitotic index = cells in mitosis ÷ total cells counted.
  • Cytokinesis is usually equal; oogenesis and yeast budding are unequal, but every daughter gets at least one mitochondrion.

Mission 3

  • Gradients of transcription factors in the early embryo switch on different genes at different concentrations, so cells in different places differentiate differently.

Mission 4

  • A stem cell can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cells.
  • Totipotent: any cell, including placenta. Pluripotent: any body cell.
  • Multipotent: a limited range, as in adult bone marrow.

Mission 5

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.
  • Cells raise SA:V by shape: microvilli, flattening, folds.

Mission 6

  • Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; thin, moist, ventilated alveoli wrapped in capillaries give a huge, short diffusion path.
  • Breathing in: diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thorax volume up → pressure down → air in. Breathing out reverses this.
  • Type I pneumocytes are thin for diffusion; type II secrete surfactant. Intercostal layers are antagonistic; titin stores the stretch energy.
