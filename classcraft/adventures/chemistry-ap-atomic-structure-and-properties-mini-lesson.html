Mission 1: Why is chlorine's relative atomic mass 35.45 when no chlorine atom weighs that?

Make a guess: Why is chlorine's relative atomic mass 35.45 when no chlorine atom weighs that?

Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Almost all the mass is in a tiny, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons surround the nucleus. The atom is about 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

Mission 1

Reading the nuclear symbol

  • In ᴬZ X, Z = atomic (proton) number = number of protons. It fixes the element.
  • A = mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.
  • Neutral atom: electrons = Z. Positive ion: Z − charge. Negative ion: Z + charge.
  • ⁵⁶26Fe³⁺: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
  • ¹⁶8O²⁻: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons. The number of protons never changes.
Calculate
aHow many neutrons are in one atom of ²⁷13Al?
bHow many electrons are in one ²⁷13Al³⁺ ion?
cHow many electrons are in one ³¹15P³⁻ ion?
Quick check
?An ion has a charge of 1−, contains 18 electrons and has a mass number of 37. How many neutrons are in its nucleus?
Mission 1

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different masses.
  • Chlorine-35: 17 p, 18 n. Chlorine-37: 17 p, 20 n.
  • Same electron configuration → same chemical properties.
  • Different mass → different physical properties: density, rate of diffusion.
Quick check
?The mass spectrum of a sample of neon, Ne, shows three peaks, at masses of 20 amu, 21 amu and 22 amu. Which statement about the atoms in the sample is correct?
Mission 1

Atomic mass units

  • 1 amu = ¹/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
  • Average atomic mass: the weighted average of an element's isotopic masses, in amu.
  • The average mass of one particle in amu = the molar mass in g/mol.
  • One average chlorine atom: 35.45 amu. One mole of chlorine atoms: 35.45 g.
  • So weighing grams tells you how many particles you have.
Mission 1

A weighted mean

  • Average atomic mass = Σ(isotopic mass × % abundance) ÷ 100.
  • Boron: ¹⁰B 19.9% (10.01), ¹¹B 80.1% (11.01). (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199.2 + 881.9) ÷ 100 = 10.81.
  • Chlorine: ³⁵Cl 75.8% (34.97), ³⁷Cl 24.2% (36.97). (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100 = 35.45.
  • So 35.45 is an average: no single atom has it. It sits nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is about 3 times as common.
Calculate
?Lithium is 7.59% ⁶Li (isotopic mass 6.015 amu) and 92.41% ⁷Li (7.016 amu).
  • Calculate the average atomic mass of lithium, in amu, to three significant figures.
Quick check
?Boron has two stable isotopes, ¹⁰B (10.01 amu) and ¹¹B (11.01 amu). A sample of boron is prepared in which 90.0% of the atoms are ¹¹B. Which value is closest to the average atomic mass of boron in this sample, in amu?
Mission 1

Where the abundances come from

  • A mass spectrometer gives the data. For an element, each peak from a 1+ ion is one isotope.
  • x-axis: m/z = the isotope's relative mass. Peak height = its abundance.
  • Neon: m/z 20 (90.48%), 21 (0.27%), 22 (9.25%).
  • Average atomic mass = (19.992 × 90.48 + 20.994 × 0.27 + 21.991 × 9.25) ÷ 100 = 20.18 amu.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, not the average atomic mass.
Mission check

?Bromine's average atomic mass is 79.90 amu, almost exactly halfway between 79 and 81. What does this show?

Same reason as chlorine: isotopes. The average atomic mass is a weighted mean of whole-ish isotopic masses, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2Topic 1.1

Mission 2: How many water molecules are in a single drop of water?

Make a guess: How many water molecules are in a single drop of water?

Mission 2

The mole is a count

  • You can't count particles one by one. You weigh them instead.
  • Amount of substance, n, is measured in moles (mol).
  • One mole contains 6.022 × 10²³ particles: Avogadro's number, NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  • Number of particles N = n × NA. Always name the particle: atom, molecule, ion, electron or formula unit.
  • 1 mol of CO2 = 1 mol of molecules, but 3 mol of atoms.
Quick check
?How many oxygen atoms are present in 4.10 g of calcium nitrate, Ca(NO3)2 (molar mass 164.10 g/mol)?
Mission 2

Atomic mass units

  • Particle masses are given in atomic mass units (amu): 1 amu = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • The average mass of one particle in amu is numerically equal to the molar mass in g/mol.
  • One H2O molecule: 18.02 amu. One mole of H2O: 18.02 g/mol.
  • For ionic substances, use one formula unit: NaCl, 58.44 amu; 58.44 g/mol.
  • This is the link between mass and number of particles.
Quick check
?What is the average mass, in grams, of one molecule of ethane, C2H6?
Mission 2

n = m/M: counting a drop

  • n = m / M. Units check: g ÷ g/mol = mol.
  • A drop of water: m = 0.050 g.
  • M(H2O) = 2(1.008) + 16.00 = 18.02 g/mol. n = 0.050 ÷ 18.02 = 0.00277 mol.
  • N = 0.00277 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.7 × 10²¹ molecules.
  • About 1,700 billion billion: far too many to count, but easy to weigh.
Calculate
?A sample of sulfur dioxide, SO2, contains 1.20 × 10²³ molecules. Calculate its mass in g.
  • Data: O 16.00, S 32.06; NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?A student claims that a 10.0 g sample of solid potassium, K, contains more atoms than a 10.0 g sample of argon gas, Ar. Which statement best evaluates the claim?
Mission check

?A one-carat diamond is pure carbon with a mass of 0.200 g. About how many carbon atoms does it contain?
  • Data: C 12.01; NA = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Same route as the water drop: mass → moles → particles. Weighing counts particles you could never count one by one.

Mission 3Topic 1.2

Mission 3: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Make a guess: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Mission 3

What a mass spectrometer tells you

  • It turns particles into positive ions and sorts them by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.
  • For a 1+ ion, m/z = the ion's relative mass.
  • For an element: one peak per isotope. Peak height = relative abundance.
  • So it gives each isotope's mass and abundance, and from these the element's mean atomic mass.
  • You only need to read spectra, not explain how the machine works.
Mission 3

Aᵣ from relative peak heights

  • Heights are often scaled so the tallest peak is 100. Then they don't add up to 100.
  • Average atomic mass = Σ(mass × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Boron: m/z 10 (height 24.8), m/z 11 (height 100).
  • (10 × 24.8 + 11 × 100) ÷ (24.8 + 100) = 1348 ÷ 124.8 = 10.8.
  • Sense check: the answer lies between the smallest and largest m/z, nearer the tallest peak.
Quick check
?The mass spectrum of a sample of a hypothetical element Z shows three peaks, with heights given relative to the tallest peak: 28 amu (100), 29 amu (8.0) and 30 amu (4.0). What is the average atomic mass of Z in the sample, in amu?
Quick check
?A laboratory prepares a sample of magnesium in which the proportions of the three isotopes of magnesium differ from those in natural magnesium. Which information is needed to calculate the average atomic mass of magnesium in this sample?
Mission check

?The mass spectrum of gallium shows two peaks from 1+ ions: m/z 69 (height 100) and m/z 71 (height 66.4).
What is the average atomic mass of gallium given by these data, in amu?

You never weigh one atom. The spectrometer sorts ions by m/z and counts them; masses × abundances give the average. (The data-table value, 69.72, uses precise isotopic masses rather than whole-number m/z.)

Mission 4Topics 1.3, 1.4

Mission 4: Methanal, acetic acid and glucose all boil down to CH2O. How can three different substances share one formula?

Make a guess: Methanal, acetic acid and glucose all boil down to CH2O. How can three different substances share one formula?

Mission 4

Empirical and molecular formulae

  • Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
  • Molecular formula: the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
  • Molecular formula = (empirical formula)x, x a whole number. C2H4 → CH2; H2O2 → HO.
  • Neither shows which atoms are bonded to which.
  • Ionic compounds have no molecules: their formula is a formula unit, already the lowest ratio (NaCl, CaCl2).
Mission 4

Percentage by mass

  • Percent composition by mass = (atoms × atomic mass) ÷ molar mass × 100.
  • NH4NO3: M = 2(14.01) + 4(1.008) + 3(16.00) = 80.05 g/mol. % N = 28.02 ÷ 80.05 × 100 = 35.0%.
  • Law of definite proportions: every pure sample of a compound has the same mass ratio of elements.
Quick check
?A compound contains 40.0% carbon, 6.7% hydrogen and 53.3% oxygen by mass.
Its molar mass is 60.05 g/mol. What is the empirical formula of the compound?
Mission 4

From percentages to empirical formula

  • Take a 100 g sample: percentages become grams. Divide each by its atomic mass.
  • Divide every result by the smallest.
  • Scale to whole numbers: × 2 for .50; × 3 for .33 or .67; × 4 for .25 or .75. Never round 1.50.
  • Example: 43.7% P, 56.3% O.
  • P: 43.7 ÷ 30.97 = 1.41. O: 56.3 ÷ 16.00 = 3.52. Ratio 1 : 2.50.
  • × 2 = 2 : 5. Empirical formula P2O5.
Quick check
?A compound of aluminum and sulfur contains 35.93% aluminum and 64.07% sulfur by mass. What is its empirical formula?
  • Data: Al 26.98, S 32.06.
Mission 4

From empirical to molecular formula

  • You also need the molar mass, from another measurement.
  • x = molar mass ÷ empirical formula mass.
  • P2O5: 141.94. Molar mass 283.88 g/mol, so x = 2: P4O10.
  • The hook: methanal CH2O (x = 1), acetic acid C2H4O2 (x = 2), glucose C6H12O6 (x = 6). Same ratio, different molecules.
Quick check
?A student has found the empirical formula of a molecular compound from its percent composition by mass. Which additional quantity does the student need in order to determine the compound's molecular formula?
Mission 4

Mixtures, purity and lab formulae

  • A pure substance has one kind of particle; a mixture has two or more, in proportions that can vary.
  • Elemental analysis of a mixture tests purity: 2.50 g of NaCl + sand contains 1.00 g Cl.
  • NaCl holding 1.00 g Cl = 1.00 × 58.44 ÷ 35.45 = 1.6485 g. Purity = 1.6485 ÷ 2.50 = 65.9% NaCl.
  • Lab formula: 1.27 g Cu heated with excess S gives 1.59 g product, so 0.32 g S.
  • Cu 1.27 ÷ 63.55 = 0.0200 mol; S 0.32 ÷ 32.06 = 0.010 mol: 2 : 1, Cu2S.
Mission check

?Ethyne, a welding gas, and benzene, a liquid, both contain 92.3% carbon and 7.7% hydrogen by mass. What is the molecular formula of benzene?
  • Data: H 1.008, C 12.01. Molar mass of benzene = 78.11 g/mol.

Ethyne is C2H2 and benzene C6H6: one empirical formula, CH, two molecules. Methanal, acetic acid and glucose share CH2O the same way: an empirical formula is only a ratio.

Mission 5Topics 1.5–1.7

Mission 5: How can shining light on atoms reveal how their electrons are arranged?

Make a guess: How can shining light on atoms reveal how their electrons are arranged?

Mission 5

Coulomb's law

  • Opposite charges attract. F ∝ q1q2 / r².
  • Bigger charges → stronger force.
  • Smaller distance → stronger force. Halve r and F is 4 times as big.
  • In an atom: the positive nucleus attracts each negative electron.
Quick check
?Two particles with charges of +2 and −2 are separated by a distance d and attract each other with a force of magnitude F. Two other particles, with charges of +2 and −1, are separated by a distance of d/2. What is the magnitude of the force between the second pair of particles?
Mission 5

Shielding and effective nuclear charge

  • Core electrons (inner shells) sit between the nucleus and the valence electrons.
  • They shield: they cancel part of the nuclear charge.
  • Effective nuclear charge, Zeff: the net positive charge a valence electron feels. Simple model: Zeff ≈ protons − core electrons.
  • Na: 11 − 10 = +1. Cl: 17 − 10 = +7.
  • Electrons in the same shell shield each other only a little.
Mission 5

Ionization energy from Coulomb's law

  • Ionization energy (IE): energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom or ion.
  • Greater Zeff, smaller distance → stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Across a period: Zeff rises, same shell, atoms shrink → IE generally increases.
  • Down a group: new shell, much further away, Zeff about the same → IE decreases.
  • Small dips (Be → B, N → O) come from subshell energies and electron pairing.
Quick check
?A ground-state aluminum atom has the electron configuration 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p¹. How does the energy required to remove a 2p electron from the atom compare with the energy required to remove the 3p electron?
Mission 5

Radius, electron affinity, electronegativity

  • Atomic radius: shrinks across a period (Zeff rises, same shell); grows down a group (more shells).
  • Ionic radius: cations are smaller than their atoms (fewer electrons for the same nuclear charge; often a whole shell is lost); anions are larger (same nuclear charge, more electrons repelling).
  • Electron affinity: energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron. Across a period, generally more energy is released (greater Zeff).
  • Electronegativity: how strongly an atom in a bond attracts the shared electrons. Rises across a period, falls down a group.
  • One idea explains all four: Coulombic attraction to the nucleus, set by Zeff and distance.
Quick check
?In a simple model, the force of attraction between the nucleus and a valence electron is proportional to q/r², where q is the net positive charge acting on the electron and r is the electron's average distance from the nucleus. For the valence electron of hypothetical atom Y, q is twice as great and r is twice as great as for the valence electron of hypothetical atom X. If the force on the valence electron of X is F, what is the force on the valence electron of Y?
Mission 5

Photoelectron spectroscopy (PES)

  • Fire high-energy photons (X-ray or UV) at gaseous atoms. Each photon can knock out one electron.
  • Measure the kinetic energy of the ejected electrons.
  • Binding energy = photon energy − kinetic energy: the energy needed to remove that electron.
  • Each atom loses one electron. Electrons from every subshell are ejected across the sample.
Mission 5

Reading a spectrum

  • One peak per occupied subshell.
  • Position: binding energy, usually plotted increasing to the left. Further left = held more tightly.
  • Height: (ideally) proportional to the number of electrons in that subshell.
  • Sodium, 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹: peaks at 104, 6.84, 3.67 and 0.50 MJ/mol, heights 2 : 2 : 6 : 1.
  • Huge gap from 1s to 2s: the 1s electrons are much closer and unshielded.
Quick check
?Which description of the complete photoelectron spectrum of a ground-state silicon atom is correct?
Mission 5

Comparing spectra

  • Same subshell, more protons → peak shifts to higher binding energy: Li 1s at 6.26, Na 1s at 104 MJ/mol.
  • The lowest-energy peak (far right) is the most loosely held electron.
  • Its position equals the first ionization energy: Na 3s at 0.496 MJ/mol (shown as 0.50) = 496 kJ/mol.
  • So PES is direct evidence for shells and subshells: the arrangement read straight from the peaks.
Mission check

?The complete photoelectron spectrum of an element has six peaks. From highest to lowest binding energy, their relative heights are 2, 2, 6, 2, 6 and 1. Which statement is correct?

Each peak is a subshell; its position shows how tightly those electrons are held, its height how many there are. That is how light reveals the arrangement.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Z = protons (fixes the element); A = protons + neutrons; ions differ only in electrons.
  • Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. Aᵣ is their abundance-weighted mean, so it is rarely a whole number.
  • Average atomic mass in amu = molar mass in g/mol.

Mission 2

  • n = m/M, and N = n × NA. Always name the particle.
  • Average particle mass in amu = molar mass in g/mol.

Mission 3

  • A mass spectrum of an element: one peak per isotope; m/z = isotopic mass; height = abundance.
  • Mean mass = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).

Mission 4

  • Empirical formula: % → ÷ Ar → ÷ smallest → whole numbers. Molecular formula = (empirical)x, x = Mr ÷ empirical formula mass.

Mission 5

  • Coulomb's law: attraction rises with charge and falls steeply with distance; shielding lowers Zeff.
  • PES: one peak per subshell; position = binding energy, height ∝ number of electrons.
