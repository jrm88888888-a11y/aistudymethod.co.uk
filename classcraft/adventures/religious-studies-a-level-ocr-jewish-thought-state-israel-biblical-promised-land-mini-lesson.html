Mission 1

A land in a covenant

  • The Land enters Jewish history as part of God's covenant with Abraham.
  • "Unto thy seed will I give this land" (Genesis 12:7, JPS 1917).
  • Hebrew: Eretz Yisrael, "the Land of Israel"; the Torah calls it Canaan before the Israelites enter it.
  • The Promised Land: promised to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, entered under Joshua.
  • The Land is holy: some commandments apply only there (e.g. tithes of produce, the sabbatical year): the mitzvot ha-teluyot ba-aretz.
  • So the Land is not only a homeland: it is a place where the covenant can be lived in full.

Promise and conditions

> "In that day the LORD made a covenant with Abram, saying: 'Unto thy seed have I given this land, from the river of Egypt unto the great river, the river Euphrates'" (Genesis 15:18, JPS 1917)

> "And the land shall not be sold in perpetuity; for the land is Mine; for ye are strangers and settlers with Me." (Leviticus 25:23, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who owns the Land, according to Leviticus?

Show answer

God: Israel lives on it as "strangers and settlers" with God.

Q2 What do the two verses show together?

Show answer

The Land is a gift under covenant: given to Abraham's descendants, but held on God's terms.

Mission 1

Exile to Babylon, and return

  • 586 BCE: the Babylonians under Nebuchadnezzar destroy Jerusalem and the First Temple; many are taken into exile (Hebrew: galut).
  • The Torah and the prophets explain exile as the result of breaking the covenant, and promise return (e.g. Deuteronomy 30:3–5; Jeremiah 29:10).
  • "By the rivers of Babylon, there we sat down, yea, we wept, when we remembered Zion." (Psalm 137:1, JPS 1917)
  • 538 BCE: Cyrus of Persia lets exiles return; the Second Temple is completed c. 515 BCE; Ezra and Nehemiah renew the covenant.
  • Many Jews stayed in Babylonia: a lasting community outside the Land begins.
Mission 1

70 CE: the great dispersion

  • 70 CE: Roman armies under Titus destroy Jerusalem and the Second Temple.
  • 132–135 CE: the Bar Kokhba revolt is crushed; Jews are barred from Jerusalem, renamed Aelia Capitolina.
  • Without Temple or sacrifice, the rabbis rebuild Judaism around Torah, prayer and synagogue.
  • The diaspora (dispersion) grows: Babylonia, then North Africa, Spain, Europe, the Middle East.
  • Mourning built into the year: Tisha B'Av fast for both Temples.

Mission 1

Still at the centre, 70–1948

  • Prayer: the Amidah asks for the ingathering of exiles and the rebuilding of Jerusalem; grace after meals blesses God "who builds Jerusalem".
  • Law: the calendar was long fixed from the Land; rabbis debated which mitzvot apply outside it.
  • Presence: Jewish communities never fully left (e.g. Tiberias, Safed, Jerusalem, Hebron).
  • Journeys: Judah Halevi (d. 1141) set out for Zion; Nachmanides (1267) rebuilt a community in Jerusalem; Spanish exiles (after 1492) made Safed a centre of learning.
  • Hope: the Messiah would gather the exiles; Jews were to wait, not force the end.
  • Babylonia and later centres led Jewish learning; the Land led Jewish longing.
Mission 1

Shemittah and Jubilee

  • "In the seventh year shall be a sabbath of solemn rest for the land" (Leviticus 25:4, JPS 1917).
  • Shemittah (release): every 7th year, fields in the Land lie fallow; debts are released (Deuteronomy 15).
  • Jubilee (Yovel): every 50th year, "proclaim liberty throughout the land"; land returns to its original family (Leviticus 25:10).
  • Ideas behind them: the Land belongs to God; no one may hold it for ever; the poor are protected.
  • Stewardship: humans care for the Land on God's behalf (see Mission 3).
  • Today: the Jubilee is not observed (it needs all tribes in the Land). Shemittah still applies in Israel by rabbinic law. Many rely on the heter mechirah (a sale of land to a non-Jew for the year, approved by Rav Kook, 1909); many Haredi authorities reject it and buy from non-Jewish farms or imports instead.
Mission 1

Kibbutzim and the Land

  • Labour Zionists spoke of "redeeming" the Land by Jewish labour: draining swamps, planting, farming.
  • Kibbutzim (from Degania, 1910) put this into practice on land bought by the Jewish National Fund.
  • For secular pioneers, the bond with the Land replaced religious law: the plough instead of the prayer book.
  • Religious kibbutzim (from the 1930s) joined farming with Torah: some keep shemittah with special methods (e.g. growing in detached containers).
  • Kibbutzim shaped borders and defence: many were founded at the edges of Jewish settlement.

Mission 1

Debates about covenant and promise

  • Unconditional? The promise to Abraham is "for ever"; many religious Zionists read the modern return as its fulfilment.
  • Conditional? Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28–30 tie life in the Land to keeping the covenant; exile follows sin, return follows repentance.
  • Who brings the return? God alone, in the Messiah's time (most Haredim); or God through human action (religious Zionists); or human action alone (secular Zionists).
  • Is exile only geography? Some Progressive Jews and diaspora thinkers say the covenant is lived wherever Jews keep it; the Land is precious but not required for a full Jewish life.
Mission 1

Promised Land and modern map

  • Scripture gives different boundaries: Genesis 15:18 (Egypt to Euphrates); Numbers 34 (a smaller "land of Canaan"); "from Dan even to Beer-sheba" (Judges 20:1).
  • The rabbis also distinguished areas settled by returning exiles (stricter land laws) from the wider promise.
  • So "the Promised Land" is not one fixed line on a map: Jews have read its borders differently.
  • Modern borders, the State of Israel and religious responses.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the Land central to Judaism?

  • Claim: The Land of Israel is central to Judaism.
  • For: covenant promise to Abraham; many mitzvot tied to the Land; daily prayer for Zion; festivals follow its harvests (Sukkot, Shavuot).
  • For: exile itself is defined by distance from the Land.
  • Against: for most of history most Jews lived outside it; rabbinic Judaism flourished in Babylonia and Europe.
  • Against: Torah and covenant can be kept anywhere; Jeremiah told the exiles to "seek the peace of the city" where they were (Jeremiah 29:7).
  • Judgement criteria: central to belief, to practice, or to identity? For which Jews: Orthodox, Progressive, secular?
  • Your spec asks whether the Land has been a central tenet of Jewish thought from biblical times onwards.

Mission 2: Is the modern State of Israel the Promised Land of the Bible? Why do Jews answer differently?

Make a guess: Is the modern State of Israel the Promised Land of the Bible? Why do Jews answer differently?

Mission 2

1945–1947: Britain, the Jews and the Arabs

  • Britain ruled Mandatory Palestine from 1920; the Balfour Declaration (1917) had promised a Jewish "national home".
  • 1939 White Paper: Britain strictly limited Jewish immigration; Arab leaders also opposed further immigration.
  • After the Holocaust (1945): Holocaust survivors in displaced-persons camps sought entry; Britain turned back ships (e.g. Exodus 1947).
  • Jewish–British conflict: Jewish underground groups (Haganah; the more radical Irgun and Lehi) attacked British targets; the Irgun bombed the King David Hotel in Jerusalem (July 1946), killing 91 people.
  • February 1947: Britain hands the problem to the United Nations.
Mission 2

Partition and 1948

  • UN Resolution 181 (29 November 1947): partition into a Jewish and an Arab state, with Jerusalem under international rule.
  • The Jewish Agency accepted; Arab leaders and states rejected it. Civil war followed.
  • 14 May 1948: David Ben-Gurion reads the Scroll of Independence in Tel Aviv, declaring the State of Israel.
  • Its last words trust in the "Rock of Israel" (Tzur Yisrael): religious signers heard God; secular signers did not have to.
  • Next day, armies of Egypt, Transjordan, Syria, Iraq and Lebanon invaded. Armistice lines (the "Green Line") were agreed in 1949; Jordan held East Jerusalem and the Western Wall.
  • Migration: the Law of Return (1950) gives any Jew the right to settle. Holocaust survivors and Jews from Arab lands and Iran arrived in great numbers.
  • For Palestinians, 1948 is the Nakba ("catastrophe"): hundreds of thousands fled or were expelled and became refugees.
Mission 2Read the source

An ancient hope in a modern document

> "And He will set up an ensign for the nations, and will assemble the dispersed of Israel, and gather together the scattered of Judah from the four corners of the earth." (Isaiah 11:12, JPS 1917)

  • The Declaration (paraphrase): the Land of Israel was the birthplace of the Jewish people; in exile they never ceased to pray and hope to return; the State will be open for Jewish immigration and the ingathering of the exiles, and will ensure equal rights for all its inhabitants, regardless of religion, race or sex.

Q1 Which phrase in the Declaration echoes Isaiah 11:12?

Show answer

The "ingathering of the exiles".

Q2 Does the Declaration say God founded the State?

Show answer

No: it speaks of history, prayer, rights and the "Rock of Israel", leaving the religious meaning open.

Mission 2

Wars of 1967 and 1973

  • Six-Day War (June 1967): after Egypt closed the Straits of Tiran and massed troops, Israel struck first. It took Sinai and Gaza (from Egypt), the West Bank and East Jerusalem (from Jordan) and the Golan Heights (from Syria).
  • Religious impact: Jews reached the Western Wall for the first time since 1948; Rabbi Shlomo Goren blew the shofar there. Many saw a miracle; it energised religious Zionism.
  • UN Resolution 242 (1967): withdrawal from territories occupied in exchange for secure, recognised borders: the idea of "land for peace".
  • Yom Kippur War (October 1973): Egypt and Syria attacked on the holiest day of the Jewish year; heavy losses and national shock.
  • Afterwards: peace with Egypt (1979; Sinai returned by 1982) and with Jordan (1994).
Mission 2

Intifadas and recent conflict

  • Intifada (Arabic: "shaking off"): Palestinian uprising against Israeli rule in the West Bank and Gaza.
  • First Intifada (1987–1993): mostly stones, strikes and protests; led to the Oslo Accords (1993): Israel and the PLO recognised each other; a Palestinian Authority was created.
  • Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin was murdered in 1995 by a Jewish religious extremist who opposed giving up land.
  • Second Intifada (2000–2005): suicide bombings in Israeli cities; Israeli military operations and a separation barrier.
  • 2005: Israel withdraws settlers and troops from Gaza; Hamas takes control there (2007); repeated wars.
  • 7 October 2023: a Hamas-led attack kills about 1,200 people in Israel and takes about 250 hostages; the war in Gaza that follows causes very heavy Palestinian loss of life and destruction.
  • Political tensions continue over settlements, Jerusalem, security and Palestinian statehood.
Mission 2

Which borders?

  • Biblical: several different boundaries (see Mission 1): Genesis 15 is widest; Numbers 34 narrower.
  • 1949: the Green Line (armistice lines).
  • Since 1967: Israel controls the West Bank (many Israelis call it by its biblical names, Judea and Samaria); it annexed East Jerusalem and the Golan, which most states do not recognise.
  • Settlements: Israeli communities in the West Bank. Most states and the UN regard them as illegal under international law; Israel disputes this.
  • Proposals: two states based on the 1967 lines with land swaps; others claim the whole land for Israel, or the whole land for Palestine.
  • So the Promised Land and the State do not coincide: the State is both smaller than the widest promise and disputed in its edges.
Mission 2Diversity

Two national claims

  • Israeli Jewish claims: an unbroken religious and historical bond (covenant, Jerusalem, prayer); continuous Jewish presence; a refuge after centuries of persecution and the Holocaust; UN Resolution 181; the right of a people to self-determination; security after repeated wars.
  • Palestinian claims: centuries of continuous Arab residence and ownership; the right of a people to self-determination; the loss of homes and land in 1948 (refugees and their descendants); occupation since 1967 and settlement; Jerusalem's holy places for Muslims and Christians.
  • Both peoples speak of home, history and justice. Many Israelis and Palestinians support compromise; many on each side reject the other's claim.
  • Your spec asks you to compare Israeli and Palestinian claims to the Land and to the creation of a Jewish State.

Mission 2Diversity

Religious and political responses

  • Religious Zionist / many Modern Orthodox: the State is "the first flowering of our redemption" (the Chief Rabbinate's Prayer for the State, 1948); Independence Day (Yom Ha'atzmaut) with festive prayers.
  • Non-Zionist Haredim: live in, vote in and benefit from the State but give it no religious meaning; many do not celebrate Independence Day.
  • Anti-Zionist Haredim (Satmar, Neturei Karta): the State is a sin against God.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal) and Masorti: strong support for Israel as homeland and refuge; most favour two states, equal rights and recognition of non-Orthodox Judaism in Israel.
  • Secular Israelis: the State is national and democratic; religion is a private choice.
  • Land for peace? Rabbi Ovadia Yosef ruled that saving lives (pikuach nefesh) can permit giving up land for real peace; others rule that settling the whole Land is a commandment that overrides this.
Mission 2

Gush Emunim and Neturei Karta

  • Gush Emunim ("Bloc of the Faithful"), founded 1974 after the Yom Kippur War, inspired by Rabbi Zvi Yehuda Kook (1891–1982), son of Rav Kook.
  • Belief: 1967 was a step in redemption; settling Judea and Samaria is a religious duty (Nachmanides counted settling the Land among the Torah's commandments).
  • Practice: built settlements in the West Bank (e.g. at Sebastia, 1975), often before government approval.
  • Critics, including many religious Jews, say it made land holier than life and peace; a few members formed an underground that attacked Palestinians in the 1980s and were convicted.
  • Neturei Karta (1938): no Jewish state before the Messiah; refuse to recognise Israel.
  • Both take the Promised Land seriously as religious reality, and reach opposite conclusions about the State.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the State of Israel the Promised Land?

  • Claim: The modern State of Israel is the fulfilment of the biblical promise of the Land.
  • For: the ingathering of exiles and the return to Jerusalem match prophecy (Isaiah 11:12; Amos 9:14–15); religious Zionists see God acting in history.
  • For: for the first time since 70 CE, Jews govern themselves in the Land.
  • Against: the State was founded by secular nationalists, not by the Messiah (Haredim, Neturei Karta).
  • Against: its borders differ from scripture's; it is a democracy that includes non-Jewish citizens and does not rule by halakhah.
  • Against: some Progressive and secular Jews value the State as a homeland and refuge without claiming a religious fulfilment.
  • Judgement criteria: is "promise" about territory, sovereignty or redemption? Can a human political act fulfil a divine promise?

Mission 3

The earth is God's

  • Jewish teaching starts from ownership: the world belongs to God, not to humans.
  • "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein." (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917)
  • Of the Land of Israel: "the land is Mine; for ye are strangers and settlers with Me." (Leviticus 25:23, JPS 1917)
  • So humans are tenants or trustees: they may use the world, but must answer to its owner.
  • Stewardship: caring for something on behalf of its owner, and being accountable for it.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 3Read the source

Dominion and keeping

> "have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that creepeth upon the earth." (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917)

> "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it." (Genesis 2:15, JPS 1917)

  • Hebrew of Genesis 2:15: le'ovdah u-leshomrah, "to work it and to guard it".
  • A midrash (Ecclesiastes Rabbah 7:13, paraphrase): God shows Adam the trees of Eden and warns him not to spoil the world, for there will be no one to repair it after him.

Q1 Which verse gives humans power, and which gives them a duty?

Show answer

Genesis 1:28 gives power, "dominion"; Genesis 2:15 gives a duty, "to keep it".

Q2 How does many Jews' reading of the two verses together limit dominion?

Show answer

Dominion is power held in trust: humans rule as keepers, answerable to God.

Mission 3

Bal tashchit: do not destroy

  • In war, Israel must not cut down an enemy city's fruit trees: "for thou mayest eat of them, but thou shalt not cut them down" (Deuteronomy 20:19, JPS 1917).
  • The verse asks: "is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged of thee?" The tree is not the enemy.
  • The rabbis widened this into a general principle: bal tashchit, "do not destroy" needlessly.
  • Talmud (Shabbat 129a; Kiddushin 32a): breaking vessels or tearing clothes for no reason is forbidden.
  • Maimonides (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings 6:10, paraphrase): the ban covers anyone who needlessly destroys vessels, clothes, buildings, springs or food.
  • Not a ban on all use: waste and pointless destruction are the wrong.
Mission 3

Shemittah and Jubilee

  • Shemittah (release): every seventh year the land of Israel rests. "In the seventh year shall be a sabbath of solemn rest for the land" (Leviticus 25:4, JPS 1917).
  • No sowing or pruning; what grows by itself is free for the poor and for animals (Exodus 23:10–11). Debts are released (Deuteronomy 15:1–2).
  • Jubilee (Yovel): every fiftieth year, liberty is proclaimed and land returns to its original family (Leviticus 25:10).
  • Message: the land is held in trust; no one owns it for ever; the poor and the soil both get relief.
  • Today: many rabbis hold shemittah binding only in the Land of Israel, and by rabbinic law while most Jews live outside it. Since the late 1800s some rabbis allow a temporary sale of land to a non-Jew (heter mechirah), supported by Rav Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935); many Haredi Jews reject it and buy produce grown elsewhere. The Jubilee is not observed today.
  • Kibbutzim (collective farms, first founded 1909–10): most were secular and socialist, working the land as a return to it. Religious kibbutzim keep Shemittah, e.g. with crops grown off the soil.

Mission 4

What is a rabbi?

  • Rabbi: "my teacher". A scholar of Torah and halakhah, not a priest: no sacrifices, no special holiness.
  • Roles today: teaching, deciding questions of halakhah, preaching, pastoral care, leading a community.
  • Semikhah: ordination, a certificate that the holder may teach and decide halakhah.
  • A knowledgeable adult Jew can lead prayers without being a rabbi (in Orthodox practice, a man leads); the question is who may hold authority.
  • So the debate is about authority, tradition and equality, not about holiness.
Mission 4

Regina Jonas

  • Regina Jonas (1902–1944), Berlin. Studied at the Liberal rabbinical college in Berlin.
  • Her thesis (1930) asked: can a woman hold rabbinic office according to halakhic sources? She argued yes.
  • Ordained privately in December 1935 by Rabbi Max Dienemann (Liberal): the first woman rabbi.
  • Taught, preached and gave pastoral care as Nazi persecution grew.
  • Deported to Theresienstadt in 1942, where she kept up rabbinic work; murdered at Auschwitz in 1944.
  • Largely forgotten until her papers were found in an archive in 1991.
Mission 4

From 1972: women rabbis today

  • 1972: Sally Priesand, ordained by the American Reform seminary (Hebrew Union College).
  • 1975: Jackie Tabick, the first British woman rabbi (Leo Baeck College, Reform).
  • 1977: Julia Neuberger (b. 1950), ordained at Leo Baeck College; rabbi of the South London Liberal Synagogue, the first British woman to lead her own congregation; later a member of the House of Lords (2004) and Senior Rabbi of West London Synagogue (2011–2020).
  • 1985: Amy Eilberg, first Conservative (Masorti) woman rabbi.
  • 2009: Sara Hurwitz, first graduate of the Orthodox women's programme later called Yeshivat Maharat (New York).
  • Today women serve as rabbis across Reform, Liberal, Reconstructionist and Masorti Judaism.

Mission 4

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • Second-wave feminism (1960s–70s) asked why women were shut out of public life; many leading secular feminists were Jewish (e.g. Betty Friedan).
  • Jewish women asked the same of Judaism: no minyan, no aliyah, no ordination, no get without the husband.
  • 1971–72: Ezrat Nashim, a group of Conservative Jewish women, issued a "Call for Change": count women in the minyan, let them be witnesses, rabbis and cantors.
  • Rachel Adler, "The Jew Who Wasn't There" (1971): halakhah treats women as outside the full community (paraphrase;).
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: rethinking God-language, Torah and history from women's experience (Plaskow).
  • Impact: new rituals (naming ceremonies for girls, bat mitzvah, women's Rosh Chodesh groups), women scholars, and equal-rights campaigns beyond the synagogue.
Mission 4Read the source

Judge and king

> "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, she judged Israel at that time." (Judges 4:4, JPS 1917)

> "one from among thy brethren shalt thou set king over thee" (Deuteronomy 17:15, JPS 1917)

  • Maimonides, Laws of Kings 1:5 (paraphrase): from "king" (not "queen"), the rabbis learned that only men are appointed to positions of authority (serarah) in Israel.

Q1 How do supporters of women rabbis use Judges 4:4?

Show answer

A woman led and judged Israel, so female authority has biblical precedent.

Q2 How do Orthodox opponents use Deuteronomy 17:15?

Show answer

Through Maimonides, it limits communal authority to men; Deborah is treated as an exception or as an adviser.

Mission 4

Orthodox objections

  • Authority: Maimonides' rule against women in serarah.
  • Obligation: women are exempt from time-bound mitzvot like fixed prayer, so they cannot fulfil men's obligation as prayer leaders.
  • Community rules: a woman does not count in a minyan, and traditionally does not serve as a witness.
  • Modesty: concerns about kol isha (a woman's singing voice) and tzniut.
  • Tradition: no precedent in rabbinic history; fear of copying Reform.
  • Many Orthodox women answer: the roles are different but of equal dignity; the home and teaching are already leadership.
Mission 4

Orthodox women leaders today

  • Yoatzot halakhah (halakhic advisers), trained from 1997 at Nishmat in Jerusalem: answer women's questions on family purity laws.
  • Maharat title (from 2009); some graduates use Rabba or Rabbi. Rabbi Avi Weiss led this.
  • Opposition: the Rabbinical Council of America (2010, 2015) and the Orthodox Union (2017) ruled against ordaining or appointing women as clergy.
  • UK: Dina Brawer became the first British Orthodox woman ordained (2018, through Yeshivat Maharat); the United Synagogue does not appoint women rabbis.
  • Open Orthodox supporters: the modern rabbi is mainly a teacher and pastor, not the ancient serarah.

Mission 4Diversity

Responses across movements

  • Haredi: reject women rabbis and feminist change as against Torah; strong honour for women's home role.
  • Modern Orthodox: many welcome women scholars and advisers but stop at ordination; a minority (Open Orthodox) ordain women.
  • Masorti / Conservative: ordain women since 1985 (JTS decision 1983), arguing from halakhic development.
  • Reform and Liberal: full equality; already in 1922 the American Reform rabbis (CCAR) said women could not justly be denied ordination.
  • Feminist critique of all: equal access to roles is only the start; the tradition itself must change.

Mission 4Evaluate

Development or break?

  • Claim: The ordination of women is a genuine development of Judaism, not a break with it.
  • For: precedents (Deborah; learned women like Beruriah); Jonas argued from halakhic sources.
  • For: the modern rabbinate is teaching and care; women already teach Torah and give rulings (yoatzot).
  • Against: Maimonides' rule and the minyan and witness laws are long-standing halakhah.
  • Against: many Orthodox see it as importing secular feminism; the RCA and OU reject it.
  • Judgement criteria: what is the source of authority (halakhah, ethics, community)? What counts as continuity? Whose voice decides?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In Jewish belief, the Land is part of God's covenant with Abraham: a holy land where the covenant can be lived in full.
  • Leviticus 25:23 says the Land belongs to God, and Israel lives there as "strangers and settlers".
  • Exile to Babylon (586 BCE) and the Roman destruction (70 CE) scattered the Jews; prayer, law and festivals kept the Land at the centre of Jewish life.
  • Shemittah, Jubilee and stewardship express God's ownership; kibbutzim joined Zionism to working the Land.

Mission 2

  • 1945–48: conflict with Britain, UN partition (1947), Ben-Gurion's Declaration (14 May 1948), war, mass migration; for Palestinians, the Nakba.
  • Wars (1967, 1973), intifadas (1987–93, 2000–05) and recent conflict shape borders that differ from the biblical promises.
  • Responses range from religious Zionism and Gush Emunim (the State as redemption) through non-Zionist Haredim and Progressive Jews to Neturei Karta (the State as sin).

Mission 3

  • The earth is God's (Psalm 24:1); humans are stewards, given dominion (Genesis 1:28) but told "to keep it" (Genesis 2:15).
  • Bal tashchit (do not destroy needlessly) and shemittah (the land's seventh-year rest) limit human use.

Mission 4

  • Regina Jonas (ordained 1935, murdered 1944) was the first woman rabbi; Reform ordained Sally Priesand in 1972, Britain's Jackie Tabick and Julia Neuberger followed (1975, 1977), Masorti in 1985, a small Orthodox stream from 2009.
  • Jewish feminism grew from second-wave feminism and Jewish women's exclusion from minyan, ordination and divorce rights, and has changed ritual, scholarship and leadership.
  • Orthodox objections rest on Maimonides' rule on authority, minyan and witness laws; Reform rests on equality as a Jewish value.
