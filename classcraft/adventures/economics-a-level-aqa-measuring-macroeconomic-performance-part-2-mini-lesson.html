Mission 1

Mission 1: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Make a guess: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Mission 1

Three groups, not two

  • Start with the working-age population.
  • UK: everyone aged 16 and over.
  • Split it three ways:
    • Employed: did any paid work in the survey week (even 1 hour), or has a job but is temporarily away.
    • Unemployed: no job, available to start, and actively looking for work.
    • Economically inactive: everyone else.
  • ILO definition of unemployed: without a job, looked for work in the last 4 weeks, can start in the next 2 weeks (or has found a job and is waiting to start it).
  • Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • The labour force is also called the economically active population.
Mission 1

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 28.5 m, unemployed 1.5 m, inactive 20 m.
  • Labour force = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • = 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.
  • Not 1.5 ÷ 50 (= 3%): the inactive are not in the labour force.
  • Economic inactivity rate = inactive ÷ working-age population × 100 = 20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40%.
  • UK practice: ONS quotes employment and inactivity rates for ages 16–64; the unemployment rate uses everyone 16 and over.
  • Say: "5% of the labour force" — not: "5% of the population".
Mission 1

Inactive is not unemployed

  • Economically inactive: not working and not both looking and available.
  • Who? Full-time students, retired people, people looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled.
  • And discouraged workers: they want a job but have stopped looking, because they think none is available.
  • Discouraged workers are not counted as unemployed. Joblessness is understated.
  • Say: "economically inactive" — not: "unemployed", for someone who is not looking for work.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for a country: working-age population 8.0 m, employed 4.6 m, unemployed 0.4 m, inactive 3.0 m. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 1

Two ways to count

  • Labour force survey (ask people):
    • interviewers question a large sample of households about the week before
    • anyone who meets the ILO definition is counted as unemployed
    • UK: the Labour Force Survey (LFS), run by the ONS, gives the ILO unemployment figure
  • Claimant count (count benefit claims):
    • counts people claiming benefits because they are unemployed
    • comes straight from benefit records, so no survey is needed
    • UK: people claiming Jobseeker's Allowance, plus Universal Credit claimants who are required to look for work
Mission 1

Which count is better?

  • So the two figures usually differ, and can move apart.
  • Before Universal Credit, the UK claimant count was usually well below ILO unemployment.
Quick check
?Which person would be counted as unemployed in a labour force survey using the ILO definition, but not in the claimant count?
Mission 1

The UK's two measures in practice

  • ONS publishes both every month.
  • Universal Credit (introduced from 2013) changed who must look for work to keep receiving benefit.
    • Some people in low-paid work must now look for more work and are included in the claimant count.
    • From spring 2020 until mid-2025 the claimant count was higher than LFS unemployment (ONS). Before 2020 it was lower.
  • The LFS has its own problem: fewer households answer.
    • Response fell sharply by 2023. ONS paused its usual LFS estimates in October 2023 and published adjusted figures instead.
    • Reweighted LFS estimates returned in February 2024, labelled "official statistics in development".
  • Lesson: every measure depends on its method. Check how the data were collected before comparing years.
Mission 1

What the rate misses

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers count as inactive, not unemployed.
  • Underemployment:
    • part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed
    • people in jobs well below their skills count as employed too
  • Real case: in 2013 Spain's unemployment rate for under-25s was above 50% (Eurostat: 55.5%). Many under-25s are full-time students, so the youth labour force is small: this is a share of the young people in the labour force, not of all young people.
  • So the unemployment rate understates how many people want work, or more work.
Mission 1Evaluate

How well does the unemployment rate measure joblessness?

It depends on:

  • Hidden unemployment: in a long slump, more people give up looking. The rate can fall with no new jobs.
  • Underemployment: when firms cut hours instead of jobs, the rate rises less than lost work.
  • The method: survey response, benefit rules and the size of the informal economy all change what is counted.
  • So use it alongside other data: the employment rate, inactivity, hours worked and vacancies.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When a country buys more from the world than it sells, how does it pay the difference?

Make a guess: When a country buys more from the world than it sells, how does it pay the difference?

Mission 2

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
  • The current account balance, often as a % of GDP, is a key macroeconomic indicator.
Mission 2

Inside the current account

PartCredit (+) exampleDebit (−) example
Trade in goodsCar exportedOil imported
Trade in servicesForeign tourist's hotel billInsurance bought from abroad
Primary incomeProfits and interest earned on assets abroadDividends paid to foreign shareholders
Secondary incomeMoney sent home by nationals working abroadForeign aid given
  • Balance of trade = exports − imports of goods and services.
  • Primary income: wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad.
  • Secondary income: transfers with nothing in return.
  • Credits > debits on an account: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" when debits exceed credits — not: "the country made a loss".

  • A goods deficit does not mean a current account deficit: services or income can offset it.

Mission 2

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 2

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • Current + capital + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.
  • Errors and omissions: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.
Quick check
?Illustrative data (billions): current account −60; capital account +2; net errors and omissions 0. What is the financial account balance?

Mission 3

Mission 3: When every number in a news story is true, how can the story still mislead you?

Make a guess: When every number in a news story is true, how can the story still mislead you?

Mission 3

Read the data before you use it

  • Before you calculate anything, check four things:
  • Units: %, billions, an index, per person? Money in which currency and which year's prices?
  • Level or rate of change? "GDP" is a level; "GDP growth" is a rate of change.
  • Time period: a month, a quarter, a year? Which years are compared?
  • Source: who collected it, and how? Is it a forecast or an outturn?
  • Then ask: nominal or real? Money values not adjusted for inflation are nominal.
  • Say: "the rate of inflation fell" — not: "prices fell", when inflation is still above zero.
Mission 3

Percentage change or percentage points?

  • Percentage change = (new value − old value) ÷ old value × 100.
  • When the data are already rates (%), the plain difference is in percentage points.
  • Illustrative data: an unemployment rate rises from 4.0% to 5.0%.
  • Difference: 5.0 − 4.0 = 1 percentage point.
  • Percentage change: 1 ÷ 4 × 100 = 25%.
  • Real case: UK Bank Rate was 0.1% before December 2021 and 5.25% from August 2023.
  • That is a rise of 5.15 percentage points, or a 5,150% rise. Both true; the second sounds far more dramatic.
  • Percentage changes do not cancel: 100 falls 20% to 80; 80 rises 20% to 96, not 100.
  • Say: "rose by 1 percentage point" — not: "rose by 1%".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's unemployment rate falls from 8.0% to 6.0%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Index numbers in a data extract

  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100 (method in an earlier lesson).
  • Index numbers compare growth of series in different units.
  • Illustrative data: house prices and earnings, both 100 in the base year. Ten years later: house prices 160, earnings 125.
  • 160 > 125 does not mean houses cost more than people earn. It means house prices grew faster since the base year.
  • House prices relative to earnings: 160 ÷ 125 = 1.28, so 28% higher than in the base year.
  • Rebasing: new index = old index ÷ old index in the new base year × 100.
  • Illustrative data: an index is 110 in 2020 and 132 in 2024. With 2020 = 100: 132 ÷ 110 × 100 = 120.
  • 110 → 132 is a rise of 22 points, but 20%.
  • In a weighted index, items with bigger spending shares move the index more.
  • Say: "the index rose by 22 points, a 20% rise" — not: "the index rose by 22%".
Mission 3

Worked example: nominal to real

  • Illustrative data. Average weekly pay: 600 in 2020, 690 in 2024.
  • Price index (2020 = 100): 120 in 2024.
  • Nominal pay rose: 690 ÷ 600 = 1.15, so +15%.
  • Real pay in 2024, at 2020 prices = 690 × (100 ÷ 120) = 575.
  • Real change: 575 ÷ 600 = 0.958, so −4.2%.
  • Shortcut: 15% − 20% = −5%. Close, but only an approximation; use the exact method for exam answers.
  • Say: "real pay fell by about 4%" — not: "pay fell".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Government spending on health rises from 150 billion to 180 billion. Over the same period, a price index for health services rises from 100 to 125. What happened to real spending on health?
Mission 3

Graphs: a falling rate is not a falling level

  • On a graph of a rate of change (inflation, growth), a falling line above zero means the level is still rising, only more slowly.
  • Real case: UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, its highest rate in over 40 years.
  • It fell to 2.0% in May 2024.
  • Prices did not fall. They kept rising, more slowly: disinflation.
  • Also check: does the vertical axis start at zero? A cut-off axis makes small changes look large.
  • On a chart with two vertical axes, check which line belongs to which axis.
  • Say: "disinflation" — not: "deflation", unless the price level itself falls.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. An economy's quarterly real GDP growth rates are: Q1 0.8%, Q2 0.5%, Q3 0.2%, Q4 0.1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Build an evaluation chain

  • Point: answer the question in one line.
  • Analysis: a chain of reasoning, step by step: "because … which leads to … so …".
  • Evidence: quote the data with units and dates, and calculate where it helps.
  • Evaluation: "it depends on …": how big? how likely? short run or long run? who gains, who loses? how reliable is the data?
  • Judgement: weigh the factors and say which matters most, and why.
  • Say: "this depends on the size of the multiplier, because …" — not just: "it depends".
Mission 3Evaluate

Did the policy work?

  • Real case: the Bank of England's target is 2% CPI inflation.
  • Bank Rate rose 14 times, from 0.1% to 5.25% (December 2021 to August 2023).
  • CPI inflation fell from 11.1% (October 2022) to 2.0% (May 2024).
  • Criteria for success: was the target met? How quickly? At what cost to growth and jobs?
  • It depends on …
  • The counterfactual: what would inflation have done anyway? Other causes, such as world energy prices, may have changed too.
  • Time lags: interest rates affect spending and prices only after a delay, so the timing of any effect is uncertain.
  • Side effects: higher mortgage and loan costs, weaker spending and output.
  • Other policies: fiscal policy was acting at the same time.
  • Judge: a target being reached shows the outcome, not on its own what caused it.
Quick check
?In an essay on whether higher interest rates brought UK inflation down after 2022, which sentence is evaluation?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In 2022 a government began paying firms to take on apprentices aged 16–24. The youth unemployment rate fell from 15.0% in 2022 to 12.0% in 2024. Over the same years, the overall unemployment rate fell from 6.0% to 4.0%. Which conclusion is best supported by the data?

Calculate first, in the right units; then ask what else could explain the change before you credit the policy.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Make a guess: Can a country's income tell you how well its people really live?

Mission 4

Income per person

  • Living standards: how well people live.
    • Material: the goods and services they can consume.
    • Non-material: health, education, safety, free time, a clean environment.
  • Total GDP shows the size of an economy, not how well-off its people are.
  • GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population: average output, and income, per person.
Mission 4

Comparing over time

  • Use real GDP per capita: "real" removes inflation; "per capita" removes population growth.
  • Illustrative data:
Real GDPPopulationReal GDP per capita
Year 1600 billion30 million20,000
Year 2612 billion30.3 million20,198
  • Real GDP grew 2%, but output per person grew only about 1%.
  • Over long periods, take care:
    • quality improves: a phone today does far more than one 20 years ago, so GDP may understate gains.
    • new products and changes in working hours are not fully captured.
Mission 4

Comparing countries: PPP

  • To compare, convert to one currency (usually US dollars).
  • Market exchange rates move with trade and capital flows, and ignore that prices differ between countries.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP): an exchange rate at which the same basket of goods and services costs the same in both countries.
  • Illustrative data. A basket costs 50 US dollars in the US and 1,000 zeds in Country Z. PPP rate = 20 zeds per dollar. Market rate = 50 zeds per dollar.
  • Z's GDP per capita is 150,000 zeds:
    • at the market rate: 3,000 dollars
    • at PPP: 7,500 international dollars
  • Services such as haircuts and rent are usually cheaper in lower-income countries, so PPP figures raise their incomes.
  • Say: "GDP per capita at PPP" for living standards — not: at market exchange rates.
Quick check
?Country K's GDP per capita is 2,000 US dollars at market exchange rates and 6,000 international dollars at PPP. What best explains the gap?
Mission 4

What GDP misses

  • Nonmarket output: cooking, cleaning, caring for children or relatives, home repairs, volunteering. It is produced but not sold, so it is not in GDP.
  • Hidden economy: work not reported, to avoid tax or rules.
  • Leisure: GDP can be the same whether people work 35 or 50 hours a week.
  • Environment: pollution and lost resources are not subtracted; spending to clean up adds to GDP.
  • Health, education, safety and freedom are not measured directly.
  • Distribution: per capita is an average; it does not show who gets the income.
  • Say: "GDP measures market output" — not: "GDP measures welfare".
Quick check
?A parent leaves a paid job to look after their own child at home. The family stops paying for childcare. Nothing else changes. What happens to measured GDP?
Mission 4

Average or typical? Mean and median

  • GDP per capita is a mean: total ÷ number of people.
  • The median is the middle income when incomes are ranked: the typical person.
  • Illustrative data. Five incomes (thousands): 10, 15, 20, 25, 80.
    • Mean = 30. Median = 20.
  • Next year only the top income rises, to 130.
    • Mean = 40 (up 33%). Median = 20 (unchanged).
  • So GDP per capita can rise while the typical person is no better off.
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful is GDP per capita for comparing living standards?

It depends on:

  • What you compare: between countries, use PPP; over long periods, quality and new products are hard to capture.
  • The nonmarket and hidden economy: larger in some countries, so their output is understated more.
  • Distribution: the same mean can hide very different median incomes.
  • What else differs: working hours, health, environment, freedom.
  • But: GDP data are regular, follow international rules (the UN System of National Accounts), and tend to go with better health and education.
  • So: use GDP per capita alongside other indicators, not alone.
Mission check

?Real GDP per capita in Country P is 20% higher than in Country Q, measured in the same prices. Which finding would most weaken the claim that people in P have a higher material living standard?

GDP counts only what is bought and sold, so compare like with like before judging who lives better.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Working-age population = employed + unemployed + inactive. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Survey (ILO) and claimant count measure different things and often differ.
  • Discouraged and underemployed workers are not counted as unemployed, so the rate understates joblessness.

Mission 2

  • Current account: goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions = 0.

Mission 3

  • Check units, level or rate, period, source, and nominal or real before you use any number.
  • Rates change in percentage points; a percentage change divides by the old value. A falling rate above zero is still a rise.
  • An evaluation chain runs Point → Analysis → Evidence → Evaluation → Judgement; a policy reaching its target does not on its own prove it caused the change.

Mission 4

  • Real GDP per capita is the usual starting point for comparing living standards.
  • Between countries, convert at PPP, not market exchange rates.
  • GDP leaves out nonmarket output.
  • It also misses the hidden economy, leisure, the environment and distribution.
