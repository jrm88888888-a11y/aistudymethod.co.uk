Mission 1

Four kinds of cold environment

  • Polar: high-latitude land. A polar desert has under 250 mm of precipitation a year and a warmest-month mean below 10 °C.
  • Alpine: high mountains at any latitude. Nearer the equator, glaciers need higher elevations.
  • Glacial: land covered by glacier ice, today about 10% of Earth's land.
  • Periglacial: cold, non-glacial land, often near ice, usually with permafrost (ground at or below 0 °C for two or more years).
Mission 1

Where the ice is now

  • NSIDC: glaciers "exist on all the world's continents except Australia".
  • Ice sheets (over 50,000 km²) exist only on Greenland and Antarctica.
  • Antarctica's sheet covers almost 14 million km²; Greenland's about 1.7 million km².
  • Smaller glaciers form "at high latitudes or high elevations", in places with heavy winter snow and cool summers: Alaska, Patagonia, the Himalaya.
  • Two controls, then: latitude and altitude, plus enough snowfall.
Mission 1

Where the frozen ground is

  • Permafrost underlies about 23 million km², about 25% of the Northern Hemisphere's exposed land. A 2019 model puts permafrost at 13.9 million km²; areas with any chance of it at about 21 million km².
  • Continuous permafrost: 90–100% of the ground frozen; discontinuous: 50–90%; sporadic: 10–50%.
  • It also lies beneath Arctic continental shelves (subsea permafrost).
  • Some form of permafrost covers about 85% of Alaska.

Mission 1

Climate: cold and dry

  • Utqiaġvik (Barrow), Alaska, 1991–2020 normals: February mean −24.4 °C; July mean 5.4 °C.
  • Monthly means are below freezing for 8 months of the year.
  • Yearly precipitation is only about 137 mm: polar-desert dry.
  • Tundra generally: means below 0 °C for six to ten months; 150–250 mm of precipitation a year.
Mission 1

Soils: frozen below, soggy above

  • In summer only the top layer, the active layer, thaws.
  • Beneath it, permafrost "acts like a waterproof barrier", so meltwater cannot drain: soils become waterlogged.
  • Once frozen, "the decay of organic matter stops", so dead plant matter builds up.
  • Result: the Arctic holds "nearly one-third of Earth's stored soil carbon".
Mission 1

Vegetation: low and slow

  • Tundra has almost no trees because of the short growing season and permafrost.
  • Mosses, sedges and lichens are common.
  • Plants that do grow "stay close to the ground so they are insulated by snow" in winter.
  • Tundra also occurs "at the tops of very high mountains": alpine tundra.
Mission 1

How climate, soil and plants interact

  • Cold keeps the ground frozen → permafrost blocks drainage → waterlogged soil.
  • Cold, wet soil → slow decay → organic matter accumulates.
  • Short season and shallow thaw → low, slow-growing plants; snow insulates them.
  • If permafrost thaws, microbes break down that store, releasing CO₂ (with oxygen) or methane (without).
Quick check

Mission 1

The Pleistocene: an age of ice

  • The Pleistocene epoch ran from 2.58 million to about 11,700 years ago; the Holocene follows.
  • Ice ages came and went about every 41,000 years between one and three million years ago.
  • About 800,000 years ago the cycle lengthened to about 100,000 years.
  • So the map of cold environments has expanded and shrunk many times.
Mission 1

Ice at the last glacial maximum

  • At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land, against about 10% today.
  • Ice sheets covered much of North America and Scandinavia.
  • In Ireland, Scottish and local ice merged into an Irish Ice Sheet early in the last glacial cycle.
  • Scotland's north-west Highlands hold "erratic boulders, dropped by retreating glaciers at the end of the ice age".
  • Beyond the ice, the cold froze the ground. Some of it is still frozen: relict permafrost.
  • Yedoma, a carbon-rich relict permafrost from the Pleistocene, lies mostly in Siberia; parts of today's Arctic seabed were dry land then, and froze.

Mission check

?Western New Guinea, almost on the equator, has glaciers today. North-west Scotland, far from the equator, has none, yet erratic boulders lie across its Highlands. Which explanation fits both places?

Where cold environments are found depends on latitude, altitude and snowfall, and on time: in the Pleistocene, glaciers covered about three times today's area, including Ireland and north-west Scotland.

Mission 2

A glacier as an open system

  • Inputs: snow, avalanched snow, rock debris; energy from the sun and from gravity.
  • Stores: ice, firn, meltwater, debris carried in the ice.
  • Outputs: meltwater, vapour, icebergs, sediment dropped at the snout.
  • Dynamic equilibrium: if inputs match outputs, the glacier keeps its size while ice flows through it.
  • A landform is one feature (a corrie). A landscape is the whole set of landforms the system makes (a glaciated upland).
Mission 2

The glacial budget

  • Mass balance (glacial budget): "whether a glacier loses or gains mass over the course of a year".
  • Budget = accumulation − ablation.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens; the snout may advance. Negative: it thins; the snout may retreat.
  • The snout responds late: it takes years for extra ice to flow down to the front.
Quick check
?In one year a glacier gains 3.2 million tonnes of ice and loses 4.0 million tonnes. What is its budget, and what is the likely result?
Mission 2

Advance and retreat through time

  • Last glacial maximum: glaciers covered about 32% of land; about 10% today.
  • Little Ice Age: glaciers advanced from about the early 14th to the mid-19th century.
  • Recent: Olympic National Park, USA, had 266 glaciers in 1982 and 184 in 2009.
  • Local exceptions: Switzerland's Birch Glacier front advanced about 50 m from 2019, partly because rock debris on it cut surface melt.
Mission 2

Warm-based and cold-based ice

  • Pressure melting point: the weight of ice above lowers the temperature at which ice melts.
  • Warm-based: the base reaches it, so meltwater forms at the bed. Meltwater "encourages basal sliding, and rapid ice velocities".
  • Cold-based: the base stays below it, frozen to the rock; flow is "very slow or no flow".
  • Dry Valleys, Antarctica: mean annual air temperature about −19.8 °C; glaciers' basal temperatures about −17 °C, "no free running water".
Mission 2

What sets a glacier's base temperature?

  • Air and ground temperature: colder climates give colder ice.
  • Geothermal heat warms some glaciers from below.
  • Ice thickness: thick ice presses harder, so the base reaches melting point more easily.
  • Polythermal glaciers are "partly frozen to their bed": thick ice may be warm-based while thin margins stay frozen.
  • So a glacier's type can develop: thickening or warming can thaw its bed; thinning or cooling can freeze it.
Quick check
?Glacier P sits in a mild, wet mountain range; its bed is at the melting point. Glacier Q sits in a polar desert; its base is −15 °C. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 2

How ice moves

  • Internal deformation (creep): "All glaciers flow by creep". Ice is plastic: under its own weight, ice crystals deform and slip.
  • Basal sliding: ice "melts under pressure, resulting in a film of water at the ice-bed interface", and slides on it.
  • Bed deformation: some glaciers sit on soft, wet sediment that itself deforms.
  • Speeds vary hugely: in 1986, Hubbard Glacier, Alaska, moved forward 10 m a day during a surge, a short burst of fast flow.
Mission 2

Flow inside a valley glacier

  • Ice moves fastest at the surface centre; friction slows it at the bed and sides.
  • Extensional flow: the bed steepens, ice speeds up and thins; crevasses open.
  • Compressional flow: the bed flattens, ice slows and thickens, often near the snout.
  • Rotational flow: ice in a hollow turns about a pivot, deepening it into a corrie.
Quick check
?Crevasses open across a glacier just where its bed steepens. Which flow is this?
Mission 2

Movement drives erosion and deposition

  • Frost action and nivation weather rock above and around the ice; debris falls onto it.
  • Sliding ice erodes by plucking (tearing out jointed blocks) and abrasion (debris scraping the bed).
  • Cold-based ice slides little, so there is "less abrasion at the ice-bed interface".
  • Debris is transported on, in and under the ice and deposited as till where the ice melts.
Mission check

?Two glaciers sit side by side in the same mountain range. Glacier X is 300 m thick; Glacier Y is 40 m thick. Surveys show X moves several times faster than Y. Which explanation fits best?

Thickness and climate decide whether the bed thaws. A warm-based glacier slides on meltwater; a cold-based one moves only by slow internal deformation. That is why some glaciers move metres a day while others barely move.

Mission 3

A glacier is a system

  • Inputs: snow, mainly in the accumulation zone, high on the glacier.
  • Store: ice, which flows downhill under its own weight.
  • Outputs: meltwater, vapour and icebergs, mainly from the ablation zone lower down.
  • Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where a year's gain equals a year's loss.
Mission 3

From snow to ice sheet

  • Buried snow compacts and recrystallises. After about a year it becomes firn, about two-thirds as dense as water; over time, firn becomes glacier ice.
  • An ice sheet is glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km². Only two exist today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • In the last ice age, ice sheets also covered much of North America and Scandinavia.
Mission 3

How ice is gained and lost

  • Accumulation: direct snowfall; avalanches from slopes above; snow blown in by wind.
  • Ablation: melting; sublimation, ice turning straight to vapour; evaporation of meltwater; calving of icebergs where ice meets water; avalanches from the glacier's own front.
  • Ablation dominates at low altitude and in summer; accumulation at high altitude and in winter.
Mission 3

Mass balance

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation, over a year.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens and its snout may advance. Negative: it thins and its snout may retreat. Glaciers grow "when winter snowfall exceeds summer melting" (USGS).
  • Measured in water equivalent (w.e.): the depth of water if the ice gained or lost were spread evenly over the glacier.
  • Ice still flows downhill whatever the balance.
Quick check
?In one year a glacier gains 2.1 m w.e. and loses 1.4 m w.e. What follows?
Mission 3

Cold environments, then and now

  • Glacial: today about 10% of land, mostly the Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets; about 32% at the last glacial maximum.
  • Alpine: glaciers in high mountains at any latitude. Polar: high latitudes. Periglacial: frozen ground beyond the ice, under about 25% of the Northern Hemisphere's land.
  • Little Ice Age: glaciers advanced from about the early 14th to the mid-19th century (NSIDC). Glacier National Park, Montana: 80 glaciers over 0.1 km² at about 1850; 32 in 2005, a fall of nearly 60%.
  • Advance and retreat follow mass balance, with a lag while the glacier adjusts.
Mission 3

Weathering, nivation, mass movement

  • Frost action: water freezes in cracks, expands and prises rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it.
  • Nivation: freeze–thaw, meltwater and slow mass movement under and around a snow patch deepen a hollow (a nivation hollow), which may later grow into a corrie.
  • Mass movement: rockfalls and avalanches drop debris onto the ice, which carries it away.
Mission 3

Glacial erosion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: debris frozen into the base scrapes the bed like sandpaper, smoothing it and leaving scratches (striations).
  • Warm-based ice erodes most, because it slides.
Mission 3

Transport and deposition

  • Debris travels on the ice (supraglacial), within it (englacial) and beneath it (subglacial).
  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Till is dropped directly by ice: unsorted and angular.
Quick check
?A cutting shows a deposit of angular stones of all sizes, mixed with clay and with no layers. What laid it down?
Mission check

?Stakes show a glacier's ice moving 80 m down-valley a year near its snout. Yet the snout ends 20 m further up-valley than a year ago. What explains this?

Retreat describes only where the snout is. NSIDC: a retreating glacier "simply melts faster than it flows". That is how Muir Glacier retreated 12 km while its ice kept moving towards the sea: its mass balance was negative.

Mission 4

Where meltwater flows

  • Meltwater flows supraglacially (on top of the ice), englacially (within the ice) and subglacially (below the ice).
  • Surface water reaches the bed "through the bases of crevasses and moulins".
  • Discharge peaks in summer and in the afternoon of each melt day, and is lowest in winter, so deposits record pulses of high and low flow.
Mission 4

Meltwater erodes and carries

  • Meltwater "is usually very rich in sediment, which increases its erosive power": it erodes by abrasion with its load, as rivers do.
  • Under the ice, water is under pressure. Its path is "controlled mostly by the slope of the ice surface, rather than the slope of the bed".
  • So subglacial water can flow uphill over obstacles for short distances, cutting channels a normal river could not.
  • It carries huge loads of sand and gravel when discharge is high, then drops them when it slows.
Quick check
?Which pathway carries meltwater formed on the glacier surface down to the glacier bed?
Mission 4

Fluvioglacial deposits are different

  • Till (ice-laid): unsorted, unlayered, angular to sub-angular clasts in a fine-grained matrix.
  • Fluvioglacial (water-laid): sorted by size, stratified (in layers), with rounded clasts.
  • Why: water drops the largest particles first as it slows, and each flood pulse lays a new bed.
Quick check
?A pit face shows rounded pebbles in distinct layers, coarse gravel beds alternating with sand beds. Which agent laid it down, and why?
Mission 4

Meltwater channels

  • Subglacial channels (Nye channels): cut into the bed by water under pressure, they "can flow uphill and therefore have undulating long profiles", and "will usually form at topographic low points or cols".
  • Ice-marginal (lateral) channels: cut along the ice edge, roughly along the contours; they "can terminate abruptly".
  • Proglacial channels: "In front of the glacier, water drains away downslope"; overflow channels drain ice-dammed lakes across cols.
  • UK: lateral channels in the Vale of Eden helped reconstruct an ice stream's surface; there "They record progressive lowering of the ice surface" as the ice retreated (BGS).
Mission 4

Eskers

  • An esker is a long ridge of sand and gravel, "deposited by glacial meltwater flowing through tunnels within and underneath glaciers".
  • The tunnel fills with sediment; "As the ice retreats, the sediments are left behind as a ridge".
  • Size: "usually metres to tens of metres high"; length "from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres".
  • Eskers "tend to be oriented parallel to ice flow", and can cross hills because the water was under pressure.
  • UK: near Carlisle, "Several parallel eskers were created by meltwaters that flowed subglacially towards an ice margin" (BGS). Ireland: an esker exposed at Kinnitty, Co. Offaly.
Mission 4

Kames and kame terraces

  • Kames: mounds or flat-topped hills "made up of stratified sand and gravel", laid down in hollows in or on stagnant ice, then let down as the ice melted.
  • Kame terraces: "marginal deposition between the glacier and sidewalls results in kame terraces": benches along a valley side.
  • Kames often sit among kettle holes, as kame and kettle topography; Charlesbreen, Svalbard, is an example.
  • UK: glaciofluvial sand and gravel in the Tyne Gap and Vale of Eden form "ice-marginal fans, flat-topped plateaux, mounds, ridge systems and terraces" (BGS).
Mission 4

Outwash plains (sandur)

  • Outwash plains, also called "'Sandur' (after the Icelandic term for sand)", are built by "braided, sediment-rich streams" flowing away from the ice.
  • Beyond the ice, meltwater velocity falls: "The largest sediment (usually gravel) is deposited first", with finer sand "carried further downstream".
  • So grain size fines with distance from the snout, and channels braid around gravel bars.
  • Iceland's Skeiðarársandur is a classic sandur; in 1996 an eruption beneath the ice "collected in a subglacial lake at a tremendous rate", then burst out as a jökulhlaup (glacial outburst flood) across it.
Quick check

Mission check

?A dry channel is cut across a col between two valleys. Its floor rises, then falls, along its length, and no stream flows in it today. How did it most likely form?

Beneath ice, meltwater flows under pressure, guided by the ice surface, not the land. That is why subglacial channels and eskers can run uphill.

Mission 5

Permafrost and the active layer

  • Periglacial areas are cold, unglaciated areas shaped by frost.
  • Permafrost: ground at or below 0 °C for at least two years. It is "defined solely by temperature".
  • Active layer: the top layer, which thaws each summer. Taliks: unfrozen ground within permafrost.
  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost.
Mission 5

Zones, now and in the past

  • Continuous (90–100% of the ground frozen), discontinuous (50–90%), sporadic (10–50%).
  • Permafrost underlies about 25% of the Northern Hemisphere's exposed land: about 65% of it in Eurasia, 35% in North America and Greenland.
  • Another estimate is about 15%, counting only ground modelled as frozen rather than whole zones.
Quick check

Mission 5

Frost processes

  • Freeze–thaw: water in cracks expands as it freezes and prises rock apart.
  • Frost heave: water drawn to the freezing front builds ice lenses, which lift the ground and push stones up. Under roads, "The expanding ice pushes up the road and creates a hump".
  • Blockfields: on flat, high ground, freeze–thaw shatters bedrock in place, leaving wide spreads of angular boulders.
Mission 5

The active layer on the move

  • Solifluction: permafrost acts "like a waterproof barrier", so the thawed active layer stays saturated and flows slowly downslope.
  • Solifluction lobes: tongue-shaped bulges where the flowing layer slows. Terracettes: small steps along slopes; soil creep is one explanation, trampling by livestock another.
Mission 5

Patterned ground

  • Circles, polygons and stripes that form "when water and ice in the ground move the rocky soil around". In polar deserts they can be up to 5 m across.
  • Frost heave pushes stones up and outwards, so they gather at the edges (sorted patterned ground).
  • On slopes, circles are drawn out into stripes.
Mission 5

Ice wedges

  • In winter, frozen ground contracts and cracks; in spring, meltwater enters, freezes and widens the crack.
  • Repeated each year, the crack grows into a vertical ice wedge.
  • Wedges join into ice-wedge polygons across the tundra.
Quick check
?Why do stones collect at the edges of the circles in sorted patterned ground?
Mission 5

Pingos

  • Pingos are ice-cored hills 3–70 m high and 30–1,000 m across, found only in permafrost.
  • Closed-system: under a lake lies unfrozen ground (a talik) within permafrost. When the lake drains, the bed freezes from the surface and from the sides, squeezing water to the centre under pressure; it lifts the ground and freezes into an ice core.
  • Ibyuk Pingo is 49 m high, the world's second-tallest. It is one of 1,350 pingos in the region, most of which grew in drained lake beds.
  • Open-system: groundwater under pressure rises through gaps in discontinuous permafrost and freezes near the surface.
Quick check
?On a valley floor in discontinuous permafrost, groundwater flowing down from the hills rises under pressure and freezes into a mound. What has formed?
Mission 5

Thermokarst

  • Thermokarst: thawing ground ice "can gradually cave in", leaving "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls".
  • Permafrost can contain up to 80% ice; when it melts, the ground can collapse.
  • Signs of thaw include thaw slumps, degraded ice-wedge polygons and thermokarst lakes.
Mission 5

Landforms from past climates

  • Pingos and patterned ground form only under permafrost, so finding them shows the climate when they formed.
  • When permafrost thaws, a pingo's ice core melts and it collapses. Mackay links such collapse features to "paleo pingos, in areas now without permafrost".
  • Process and time: frost heave works each winter; ice wedges grow a little each year over centuries; Mackay surveyed 11 growing pingos for 20–26 years to measure their growth.
Mission 5

Modification now and in future

  • From 2007 to 2016, permafrost temperature rose by 0.29 ± 0.12 °C globally. From 1979 to 2021, the Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • By 2100, near-surface permafrost area is projected to fall by 2–66% under a low-emissions pathway (RCP2.6) and 30–99% under a high one (RCP8.5) (IPCC).
  • Thaw deepens the active layer, degrades ice wedges into thermokarst troughs and can make pingos collapse.

Mission 6

What glaciers do to rock

  • Erosion: ice and its debris wear away the bed and valley sides.
  • Entrainment: loosened rock is picked up, often by freezing onto the base of the ice.
  • Transport: debris rides on, in and under the ice.
  • Deposition: debris is dropped where the ice melts.
Mission 6

How ice erodes

  • Plucking (quarrying): meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: rock fragments in the base scrape the bed like sandpaper, polishing it and cutting striations.

Mission 6

Corries

  • A corrie (cirque) is a steep-sided, armchair-shaped hollow at a glacier's head, "a rounded amphitheater" (NPS).
  • Ice turns in rotational flow. Plucking steepens the back wall; abrasion deepens the floor; a rock lip is left at the front.
  • After the ice melts, a small lake, a tarn, may fill the hollow. Piegan Glacier still sits in a cirque in Glacier National Park, Montana.
Mission 6

Arêtes and pyramidal peaks

  • Arête: a knife-edged ridge left where two glaciers carved either side, such as the Garden Wall, Glacier National Park.
  • Corries cut backwards (headward erosion) until only a ridge separates them.
  • Pyramidal peak (horn): a peak "scraped nearly vertical by glaciers on three or more sides", such as Reynolds Mountain.

Mission 6

Glacial troughs

  • Rivers cut V-shaped valleys; glaciers scour them into U-shaped troughs, wide-floored and steep-sided.
  • A trough glacier fills the whole valley, so it erodes the sides as well as the floor.
  • Truncated spurs: the glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs, leaving steep cliffs along the trough sides.
Mission 6

Hanging valleys and ribbon lakes

  • Hanging valley: a tributary glacier carved less deeply, so its valley ends high above the main trough. Bird Woman Falls pours from one.
  • Ribbon lakes: long, deep lakes in over-deepened troughs, such as Lake McDonald and St. Mary Lake. Ice erodes deepest where it is thickest or the rock is weakest.
Mission 6

Roches moutonnées

  • A roche moutonnée is an "asymmetrical bedrock dome sculpted by glaciers" (NPS).
  • Stoss (up-ice) side: abrasion grinds it into a gentle, smoothed slope.
  • Lee (down-ice) side: plucking tears out joint blocks, leaving a steep, jagged face.
  • So the shape shows which way the ice flowed: from the gentle side towards the steep side.
  • Lembert Dome, in Tuolumne Meadows, Yosemite, is a roche moutonnée: its "steep western face and gradual eastern slope indicate the direction of glacial flow" (NPS).
Mission 6

Process, time and landscape

  • Seconds: a rockfall drops debris onto the ice. Years: plucking and abrasion. Millennia: whole troughs and corries.
  • The last glacial maximum in the Sierra Nevada (the Tioga glaciation) was about 20,000 years ago; "Previous, larger glaciations occurred many times over the past 2–3 million years" (NPS).
  • So landforms are built over many glacial cycles. In Glacier National Park, corries, arêtes, horns and troughs together form one characteristic landscape.

Mission 7

Till: what ice leaves behind

  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Material dropped directly by ice is till (boulder clay).
  • Till is unsorted (clay to boulders, mixed) and angular, because ice does not sort or round what it carries.
Mission 7

Moraines along the glacier

  • Lateral moraine: a ridge of debris along the valley side, much of it from frost-shattered walls above.
  • Medial moraine: where two glaciers join, their lateral moraines merge into a stripe down the middle, as on Alaska's Gilkey Glacier.
  • All moraines are till, so they are unsorted and angular.
Mission 7

Moraines across the valley

  • Terminal moraine: a ridge at the ice's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot... the larger this moraine will be" (NPS).
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges behind it, where retreat paused. A glacier may stay "in a steady state for decades, or even longer" (NPS).
  • Moraines mark old ice limits: Glacier National Park's Little Ice Age maximum was reconstructed from them (USGS).

Drumlins

  • Drumlins: smooth, oval hills of till, shaped beneath moving ice.

  • A till plain (till sheet) is a broad, low, gently rolling surface of till laid down as an ice sheet melted.
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire, is a UK example: its glacial tills are "mostly late Devensian (18 000 to 13 000 years old)" (BGS).

Mission 8

What makes an environment fragile?

  • A fragile environment is easily disturbed and slow to recover.
  • Tundra: low temperatures, short growing seasons and permafrost hinder tree growth; there is only a thin layer of topsoil above the permafrost.
  • So plants grow back slowly after damage, and lost soil is not quickly replaced.
  • Permafrost can contain up to 80% ice; when the ice melts, the ground can collapse.
Mission 8

Thermokarst: why small disturbances grow

  • Thawing ground ice caves in, making thermokarst: irregular, marshy hollows.
  • Dust darkens snow and plants; ponded water stores heat. Both warm the ground beneath.
  • More thaw → more subsidence → more ponding → more thaw: a positive feedback.
  • At Prudhoe Bay, road dust and roadside flooding contribute to more extensive thermokarst.

Mission 10

Processes that made a cwm

  • Snow collects in a shaded hollow, compacts into firn, then glacier ice.
  • Freeze-thaw and plucking steepen the backwall; abrasion by debris in the ice scours the floor.
  • Rotational movement deepens the floor more than the lip, leaving a rock basin behind a rock threshold.
  • When the ice melts, the basin can hold a lake: a tarn.
Mission 10

Darwin's observations (1842):

  • Rocks "smoothed, mammillated and scored, all round the lake", even below the water.
  • Similar marks "at great heights on all sides", made when ice "poured in a vast stream over the rocky barrier" at the north end.
  • A boulder broken into four slabs, as if "dropped nearly vertically from a height", which he read as fallen through a crevasse.
  • Outcome: ice once filled the cwm and overflowed its lip.
Mission 10

Field evidence 2: moraine ridges

At the south end of the lake, Darwin found:

  • Mounds of "earth and detritus, with great blocks of rock on their summits".
  • They fall into three narrow linear ridges, "with traces of a fourth".
  • The outer ridge forms a trough with the mountainside 10–15 feet deep.
  • He read them as marking "the principal stages in the retreat of the glacier".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Polar, alpine, glacial and periglacial environments lie at high latitudes or high altitudes; ice sheets only on Greenland and Antarctica; permafrost under about a quarter of the Northern Hemisphere's exposed land; some is relict Pleistocene permafrost (yedoma in Siberia).
  • Cold, dry climate → permafrost → waterlogged, carbon-rich soils → low tundra plants; Pleistocene ice once covered about 32% of land, including much of North America and Scandinavia, Ireland and north-west Scotland.

Mission 2

  • Budget = accumulation − ablation; it sets whether a glacier grows or shrinks, and the snout follows after a lag.
  • Warm-based ice slides on meltwater and moves faster; cold-based ice is frozen to its bed and creeps slowly.

Mission 3

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation. A negative balance makes the snout retreat, even though the ice keeps flowing forward.
  • Ice erodes by plucking and abrasion, and deposits unsorted till.

Mission 4

  • Meltwater flows on, in and under ice; it erodes, carries and sorts sediment, so fluvioglacial deposits are sorted, stratified and rounded, unlike till.

Mission 5

  • Permafrost is defined by temperature; the active layer thaws each summer. On slopes the thawed layer flows downslope by solifluction.
  • Frost heave sorts patterned ground; contraction cracking builds ice wedges; freezing of a drained lake bed builds a closed-system pingo.
  • Thaw degrades these landforms into thermokarst; collapsed pingos record past permafrost.

Mission 6

  • Plucking makes steep, broken faces; abrasion makes smooth, striated slopes.
  • Corries, arêtes and troughs on high ground; roches moutonnées where ice sheets scoured lower ground.
  • Landforms record many glacial cycles, not one.

Mission 7

  • Ice drops unsorted till: moraines at its edges, drumlins and till plains beneath it, erratics far from their source.

Mission 8

  • Fragility: easily disturbed, slow to recover; tundra (short growing season, thin soil) and ice-rich permafrost (thermokarst feedback).
  • Impacts over time and scale: Southern Ocean whaling → North Slope oil (34% of mapped area by 2010) → Antarctic tourism (113,518 in 2025–26). Management: Protocol and tourism limits; ice roads and elevation; futures depend on protection holding and on warming.

Mission 9

  • Cold environments are warming fastest; permafrost and ice are retreating, with impacts on land, ecosystems and people.
  • Management combines global mitigation with local adaptation; the size of the challenge depends on the emissions pathway.

Mission 10

  • Cwm Idwal: plucking, abrasion and rotational flow made a cwm and tarn; ice overflowed the lip and scored the rock; a later small glacier left moraine ridges.
  • Field data (Darwin's maps of ridges, sediment description, pollen cores, morphology) date the ice and test hypotheses: the mounds fit englacial thrusting.

Mission 11

  • The Lötschental shows a valley-glacier system at work: rock, ice and debris link the landforms, and change runs from millions of years to seconds.
  • Monitoring and early evacuation turned a disaster that destroyed a village into one with a single death.
  • The Alps offer hydropower, tourism and farming land; hazards and scarce safe land are the costs. Responses mix mitigation, resilience and adaptation.
