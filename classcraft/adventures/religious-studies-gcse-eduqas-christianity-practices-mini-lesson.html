Mission 1

Mission 1: Silence, set words or a live band: why do Christians worship in such different ways?

Make a guess: Silence, set words or a live band: why do Christians worship in such different ways?

Mission 1

What worship is

  • Worship: showing love, praise and honour to God; the old English word means giving God his worth.
  • "God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." (John 4:24)
  • Purposes: to praise and thank God, confess sin, hear God's word, ask for help, and grow as a community.
  • Public worship: together, usually on Sunday. Private worship: alone or at home.
  • "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am in the middle of them." (Matthew 18:20)
Mission 1

Liturgical worship

  • Liturgical: a set order and set words, written down, often led by a priest.
  • Follows the liturgical year (Advent, Christmas, Lent, Easter, Pentecost) with set Bible readings.
  • Examples: the Catholic Mass, Orthodox Divine Liturgy, Anglican Holy Communion.
  • Why valued: unites Christians across the world and through history; words are tested and rich in belief; everyone can join in; dignified and reverent.
Mission 1

Non-liturgical worship

  • Non-liturgical: no fixed text; the minister plans the order and prays in his or her own words (extempore).
  • Bible reading and the sermon are at the centre.
  • Hymns and songs, prayers, offering, blessing.
  • Common in many Baptist, Presbyterian, evangelical and free churches.
  • Why valued: fits the needs of the day; sincere and personal; puts God's word first.
  • Evangelical worship: less structured; Bible preaching, personal faith, contemporary songs, invitation to respond.
Quick check
?A church service follows the same printed order every week, with prayers everyone says together and Bible readings set for each Sunday of the year. What type of worship is this?
Mission 1

Informal and charismatic worship

  • Informal: relaxed, spontaneous, led by the Spirit "in the moment".
  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship: a band leads songs; people clap, raise hands, sometimes dance.
  • Gifts of the Spirit: speaking in tongues, prophecy, prayer for healing.
  • Worship may last a long time; anyone may pray aloud or share a testimony.
  • Why valued: joyful; expresses a personal relationship with God; open to the Spirit (Romans 8:14).
Mission 1

A Quaker meeting

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): meet in a plain room, seats in a circle or square.
  • Worship is mostly silence, waiting on God, usually for about an hour.
  • Anyone who feels moved by the Spirit may stand and speak (ministry).
  • No clergy, no set words, no hymns, no outward sacraments.
  • Ends when two elders shake hands, then everyone shakes hands.
  • The clearest example of worship with no liturgy at all; Matthew 18:20: Christ is present in the gathered group.
Mission 1

Private worship

  • Personal prayer and Bible reading, often daily ("quiet time").
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, plans and apps that explain a passage and suggest a prayer.
  • Catholic and Orthodox aids: the rosary, icons, statues and candles (more in another lesson).
  • Family prayer at meals and bedtime; listening to worship music.
  • Why it matters: Jesus taught "enter into your inner room" to pray (Matthew 6:6); a daily relationship with God, not only on Sundays.
Mission 1

The Bible in worship

  • Almost every form of Christian worship uses the Bible (a silent Quaker meeting may have no reading): readings, psalms, sermons, and the words of hymns and prayers.
  • Catholics and Anglicans follow a lectionary: set readings for each day; the congregation stands for the Gospel.
  • Orthodox and Catholic clergy carry, kiss and incense the Gospel book.
  • Protestant worship centres on the sermon explaining the passage; many bring their own Bibles.
Quick check
?In which kind of Christian worship might a congregation sit in silence for most of an hour, until someone feels moved by the Spirit to speak?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Liturgical worship is the best way for Christians to worship."

  • Reasons for: unites Christians worldwide and across centuries; set words carry tested belief; everyone can join in; reverent; for Catholics and Orthodox, the Eucharist is the heart of worship.
  • Reasons against: set words can become routine (Jesus warned against "vain repetitions", Matthew 6:7); worship must be "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24), so sincerity matters more than form; the Spirit leads (Romans 8:14); Quakers and Pentecostals find God in silence or spontaneity.
  • Another view: different forms suit different people and occasions; many churches mix set prayers with free prayer and modern songs.
  • Judge it by: What is worship for: God's glory, community or personal growth? Does form help or hinder sincerity? Who decides what pleases God?
Mission check

?Each form of worship expresses a belief. Which pairing of a group's belief with its style of worship is accurate?

Liturgical, non-liturgical, charismatic and silent worship each express what a community believes about how God meets people.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If God already knows what you need, why do Christians pray at all?

Make a guess: If God already knows what you need, why do Christians pray at all?

Mission 2

Raising the mind and heart

  • A classic Christian definition: prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God (St John of Damascus, 8th century).
  • For Christians, prayer is communication with God: speaking and listening.
  • Jesus prayed often, alone and with others (e.g. Luke 5:16; Mark 14:32–36).
  • Why pray, if God knows? "your Father knows what things you need before you ask him" (Matthew 6:8). Christians say prayer is about relationship and trust, not giving God information.
Mission 2

Types of prayer

  • Adoration: praising God for who God is.
  • Confession: admitting sin and asking forgiveness.
  • Thanksgiving: thanking God for gifts and blessings.
  • Supplication: asking. Petition = asking for yourself; intercession = praying for others.
  • Memory aid: ACTS (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication).

Mission 2Read the source

The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13)

"Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy.

Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven.

Give us today our daily bread.

Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors.

Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one."

  • Many churches add: "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen." (not in the earliest manuscripts).
  • In worship most Christians say a traditional wording ("Our Father, who art in heaven, hallowed be thy name…").

Show answer

Q2 What does "daily bread" suggest about how Christians should depend on God?

Show answer

Mission 2

What the Lord's Prayer means

  • "Our Father": God is close and loving, like a parent; "our" makes it a prayer of the whole community.
  • "May your name be kept holy": adoration; God is honoured above all.
  • "Your Kingdom come": asks for God's rule of justice and peace on earth.
  • "Your will be done": trust and obedience, as Jesus prayed in Gethsemane that his Father's will, not his own, be done (Luke 22:42, paraphrase).
  • "Daily bread": needs, physical and spiritual (Catholics also think of the Eucharist).
  • "Forgive us… as we also forgive": forgiveness received is to be passed on.
  • "Deliver us": protection from evil.
  • Significance: Jesus gave it as a model for all prayer; said at every Mass and most services, and by Christians of almost every denomination.
Quick check
?Which line of the Lord's Prayer shows most clearly that Christians link God's forgiveness to the way they treat other people?
Mission 2

Set and informal prayer

  • Set prayers: written words, said alone or together: the Lord's Prayer, the Hail Mary, the Book of Common Prayer collects, the Jesus Prayer.
  • Informal prayer: in your own words, as you feel led.
  • Why set prayers: shared and unifying; rich in belief; help when words fail; connect with Christians through history.
  • Why informal prayer: personal and sincere; fits the moment; open to the Spirit's leading.
  • When each is used: set prayers in liturgical services (Mass, Holy Communion, baptisms, funerals) and in private (e.g. the Lord's Prayer, the Jesus Prayer); informal prayer in private, at prayer meetings and in non-liturgical services.
  • Evangelical prayer meetings: members pray aloud in their own words, one after another. Quakers (Society of Friends): mostly silent, waiting prayer, with no set words.
  • Different views: Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans value set prayer highly; evangelicals and Pentecostals stress extempore prayer; most Christians use both.
Quick check
?Many evangelical Christians prefer extempore prayer in their own words. Which reason best explains this?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Set prayers are more valuable than informal prayer."

  • Reasons for: Jesus gave a set prayer (Matthew 6:9–13); set prayers unite Christians across the world and through history; their words are tested and full of belief; they help when people cannot find words (grief, illness).
  • Reasons against: Jesus warned against empty repeated phrases (Matthew 6:7); informal prayer is personal and sincere; the Spirit helps believers pray (Romans 8:26); Quakers find God in silent, wordless prayer.
  • Another view: both are needed: set prayer shapes informal prayer, and informal prayer brings set words to life; the Lord's Prayer can be a pattern as well as a script.
  • Judge it by: What makes prayer "valuable": sincerity, unity, or faithfulness to Jesus' words? Can set prayers be sincere? Does it depend on the situation?
Mission 2Worked answer

Two different ways to pray

Question: Explain two different ways in which Christians pray.

  • Way 1: set prayers, such as the Lord's Prayer said together at Mass. Explained: Jesus taught these words (Matthew 6:9–13), so saying them unites Christians everywhere.
  • Way 2: informal prayer in their own words, such as at an evangelical prayer meeting. Explained: it is personal and fits the need of the moment, like speaking to a friend.
Mission check

?A Catholic family sits at a grandparent's hospital bed. They say the Our Father together, then each prays a few words of their own for her, then sit in quiet prayer. Which statement best describes how they are praying?

Christians pray to be close to God, not to inform God: in set words, in their own words, and in silence.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some Christians count seven sacraments, some two, and some none at all?

Make a guess: Why do some Christians count seven sacraments, some two, and some none at all?

Mission 3

What is a sacrament?

  • A sacrament is a religious ceremony in which an outward sign (something seen and done) shows, and many Christians believe gives, an inward grace (God's free gift of love and help).
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1662) Catechism: "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace".
  • Latin sacramentum: a sacred pledge. Greek mysterion: a mystery, God's hidden action made known.
  • Every sacrament has a sign (e.g. water, bread and wine, oil) and words (e.g. "I baptise you in the name of the Father…").
  • Christians believe the grace comes from God, not from the water or bread themselves.
Mission 3

The seven sacraments

  • The Catholic Church teaches that Christ gave seven sacraments. They touch every stage of life: birth, growing up, food for the journey, illness, vocation.
  • Initiation: Baptism, Confirmation, Eucharist.
  • Healing: Reconciliation (Penance), Anointing of the Sick.
  • Service of communion and mission: Holy Orders, Matrimony.
  • Catholics call them efficacious signs: they do not only point to grace, they give it, because Christ acts through them.
  • (Each sacrament's rite and effects: Catholic missions.)
Quick check
?Which statement best expresses the Catholic understanding of a sacrament?
Mission 3Read the source

Article XXV

The Church of England's Thirty-Nine Articles (1571, printed in the Book of Common Prayer, 1662):

> "There are two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel, that is to say, Baptism, and the Supper of the Lord."

  • The other five are "commonly called Sacraments" but are not "Sacraments of the Gospel", because they lack "any visible sign or ceremony ordained of God" (Article XXV).
  • The article still calls sacraments "effectual signs of grace": they are more than badges of membership.

Q1 Which two sacraments does Article XXV accept?

Show answer

Baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Q2 Why are the other five not counted as sacraments of the Gospel?

Show answer

Christ did not command a visible sign or ceremony for them.

Mission 3

Why two?

  • Most Protestants keep two: baptism and the Eucharist (Lord's Supper). They are called dominical sacraments: commanded by Jesus himself, with a sign.
  • Baptism: "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," (Matthew 28:19)
  • Eucharist: "Do this in memory of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24)
  • Catholics and Orthodox reply: the other five also come from Christ, through the apostles and the Church's Tradition (e.g. anointing the sick in James 5:14; forgiving sins in John 20:23).
  • Protestants still value confirmation, marriage and ordination as holy rites; they just do not call them sacraments.
Quick check

Mission 3Diversity

How many, and what do they do?

  • Catholic: seven sacraments; efficacious signs of grace.
  • Orthodox: the Holy Mysteries; usually seven are named, but the list is held less rigidly; chrismation and first Communion are given straight after baptism, even to babies.
  • Anglican: two "sacraments of the Gospel"; many Anglicans also treat the other five as sacramental rites.
  • Lutheran and Methodist: two sacraments, through which God really gives grace (some Lutherans also count confession and absolution).
  • Baptist and many Pentecostal and independent churches: two ordinances (practices Jesus ordered): acts of obedience and witness, not channels of grace in themselves.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward sacraments (next screen).
Mission 3Diversity

No outward sacraments

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends, from the 1650s): all of life can be sacramental. They seek the inward baptism of the Holy Spirit and inward communion with Christ in silent meeting for worship. Outward rituals can become empty habits.
  • The Salvation Army stopped celebrating the sacraments in 1883 under William Booth. Its reasons: sacraments are not necessary for salvation; they had caused bitter division between churches; holy living matters most.
  • Salvationists have their own ceremonies (dedication of children; enrolling soldiers under the flag), but do not call them sacraments.
  • Neither group condemns Christians who do use sacraments.
Quick check
?A Quaker says, "We do not use bread and wine, but we meet Christ in the silence." Which belief does this show?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Christians do not need sacraments to be close to God."

  • Reasons for: God is everywhere and can be met in prayer and silence (Quakers). Grace is God's free gift; no ritual can control it. Salvationists have worshipped and served others as Christians for over a century without sacraments.
  • Reasons against: Jesus commanded baptism and the Lord's Supper (Matthew 28:19; 1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics and Orthodox believe sacraments are the main way Christ gives grace. Signs suit human beings, who are body as well as soul (the sacramental nature of reality).
  • Another view: sacraments are needed for the Church as a community, even if God can reach individuals in other ways.
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is grace tied to signs, or free? What do the different traditions mean by "close to God"?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two reasons, developed

Question: Explain two reasons why Christians disagree about the number of sacraments.

  • Reason 1: Most Protestants accept only sacraments commanded by Jesus with a sign. Source: Article XXV: "There are two Sacraments ordained of Christ our Lord in the Gospel". Development: so baptism and the Lord's Supper count, but confirmation and marriage do not.
  • Reason 2: Catholics accept Tradition as well as Scripture. Source: CCC 1210 teaches that Christ instituted seven sacraments (paraphrase). Development: so the Church's handing on of practices like anointing the sick shows they come from Christ too.
Mission check

?At a healing service, a Methodist minister anoints a sick woman's forehead with oil and prays for her. Most Methodists would describe this rite as what?

Christians agree that God gives grace freely; they disagree about which signs Christ commanded and how grace comes through them.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do some Christians baptise babies, while others wait until people can choose for themselves?

Make a guess: Why do some Christians baptise babies, while others wait until people can choose for themselves?

Mission 4

What baptism is

  • Baptism is the ceremony of initiation: Christians believe it makes a person a member of the Church, the Body of Christ.
  • It uses water, with the words "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13–17), and he sent his followers out "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Most churches baptise babies (infant baptism). Some baptise only people old enough to say they believe (believers' baptism).
  • Most Christians call it a sacrament; Baptists and many Pentecostals call it an ordinance.
Mission 4Read the source

Born of water and Spirit

Jesus to Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher who came to him at night:

> "Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born of water and Spirit, he can't enter into God's Kingdom." (John 3:5)

  • John 3:3–6: Nicodemus asks how an adult can be born again; Jesus answers that flesh gives birth to flesh, but the Spirit gives birth to spirit (paraphrase of 3:4, 3:6).

> "We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)

Q1 According to John 3:5, what two things are needed to enter God's Kingdom?

Show answer

Being born of water and of the Spirit.

Q2 In Romans 6:4, what does going into and coming out of the water stand for?

Show answer

Dying and rising with Christ to a new life.

Mission 4

Into the life of the Trinity

  • Baptism is "in the name of" Father, Son and Holy Spirit: Christians believe the person is joined to God's own life.
  • Father: the baptised become God's adopted children.
  • Son: they share in Jesus' death and resurrection (Romans 6:4).
  • Holy Spirit: they receive the Spirit, who makes them holy.
  • Catholic teaching: baptism washes away original sin and all sin, gives grace, and marks the person for ever, so it is given only once.
  • It is the "door" to the other sacraments: no one receives Confirmation or the Eucharist without being baptised.
Mission 4

Infant baptism: the rite

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian churches baptise babies; usually at a parish service.
  • Welcome: the priest or minister traces the sign of the cross on the baby's forehead.
  • Promises: parents and godparents reject evil and profess the faith for the child; the congregation promises support.
  • Water: poured three times over the head (or the baby is dipped), with the Trinity words: a sign of washing from sin and new birth.
  • Catholic rite: anointing with chrism (perfumed oil: sharing in Christ's role as priest, prophet and king), a white garment (clothed with Christ, cleansed from sin), and a candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle (the light of the risen Christ).
Quick check
?At a Catholic infant baptism, a godparent is given a candle lit from the Easter candle. What does the candle symbolise?
Mission 4

Why baptise babies?

  • Grace is a gift: God's love comes first; a baby cannot earn it and does not need to.
  • Original sin: Catholics believe baptism frees from original sin, so it should not be delayed (Catholic teaching also entrusts unbaptised children to God's mercy).
  • Whole households were baptised in the early Church (Acts 16:15, 16:33).
  • A covenant sign: Presbyterian and other Reformed Christians compare it to circumcision, given to Jewish baby boys (Colossians 2:11–12).
  • Jesus welcomed children and said the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14).
  • The child later makes the promises personally, at confirmation.
Mission 4

Believers' baptism

  • Practised by Baptists, most Pentecostals and many independent churches, for people old enough to profess faith (often teenagers or adults).
  • Testimony: the candidate tells the church how they came to faith in Jesus.
  • Immersion: the minister and candidate stand in a baptistery (pool in the church floor), river or sea; the candidate is lowered backwards under the water and raised up.
  • Symbolism: dying to the old life, buried with Christ, rising to new life (Romans 6:4).
  • Reasons: Peter said, "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38): faith comes first; Jesus was baptised as an adult; the New Testament never clearly describes a baby being baptised.
  • For Baptists, baptism is an act of obedience and public witness; it does not itself save.
Mission 4Diversity

The debate over infant baptism

  • Catholic: baptism is necessary for salvation for those who know the Gospel, and frees from original sin; babies should be baptised soon after birth; unbaptised adults are baptised after preparation, usually at the Easter Vigil.
  • Orthodox: infants receive baptism, chrismation and Communion together: full members from the start.
  • Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian: infant baptism is a sign of God's grace and covenant; adults who were not baptised as babies are baptised on profession of faith.
  • Baptist, most Pentecostal: only believers should be baptised; many Baptist churches baptise people who were baptised as babies, seeing infant baptism as not yet a baptism of faith.
  • Catholic and Protestant agree baptism uses water and the Trinity, and joins a person to Christ; they differ on who may be baptised and what baptism does.
  • Common ground: almost all churches accept each other's baptism if water and the Trinity words were used (Baptists are the main exception for infant baptism; some Orthodox churches also baptise converts afresh).
Quick check
?Which reason would most Baptists give for not baptising babies?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Babies should not be baptised."

  • Reasons for: faith should be a free personal choice. The New Testament pattern is "repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38). Baptism by immersion pictures death and resurrection (Romans 6:4), which a baby cannot understand.
  • Reasons against: grace is God's gift, not a reward for understanding. Households were baptised (Acts 16:33). Original sin (Catholic view) means baptism should not wait. The child can confirm the promises later.
  • Judge it by: Is baptism mainly God's action or the believer's response? What does the New Testament show? Does the community's promise make up for the child's?
Mission 4Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs about infant baptism.

  • Belief 1: Catholics believe babies should be baptised. Develop: baptism frees a person from original sin and gives God's grace, which a baby can receive without earning it.
  • Belief 2: Baptists believe only believers should be baptised. Develop: baptism is a public sign of a person's own faith, so it must follow repentance, as in Acts 2:38.

Christians link baptism with belonging to Christ and the Church; the debate is whether God's gift or the person's faith should come first.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Is the bread at Communion really Jesus' body, a reminder of him, or something in between?

Make a guess: Is the bread at Communion really Jesus' body, a reminder of him, or something in between?

Mission 5

One meal, many names

  • Christians share bread and wine, as Jesus did at his Last Supper. Most churches call it a sacrament (Baptists: an ordinance).
  • Eucharist: Greek for "thanksgiving".
  • Mass: Catholic name (from the Latin dismissal, "Go, it is the sending").
  • Holy Communion: sharing with Christ and each other (Anglican, Methodist and many others).
  • Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread: many Protestant churches.
  • Divine Liturgy: the Orthodox service in which the Eucharist is celebrated.
Mission 5Read the source

The Last Supper

At the Passover meal, the night before he died:

> "As they were eating, Jesus took bread, gave thanks for it, and broke it. He gave to the disciples and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body.'" (Matthew 26:26)

> "for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins." (Matthew 26:28)

  • Paul's earlier account adds Jesus' command: "Do this in memory of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24)

Q1 What did Jesus say the bread and the cup were?

Show answer

His body; his blood of the new covenant.

Q2 What reason for the blood does Matthew 26:28 give?

Show answer

It is poured out for the forgiveness of sins.

Mission 5

The Catholic Mass

  • Liturgy of the Word: readings from the Bible and a homily (sermon).
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: the priest says Jesus' words over unleavened bread (hosts) and wine (the consecration).
  • Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look) of bread and wine remain (Council of Trent, 1551).
  • The Mass makes present Christ's one sacrifice on the cross; it does not repeat it.
  • Catholics must attend Mass on Sundays; those prepared and free from serious sin receive Communion.
Quick check
?What do Catholics mean by transubstantiation?
Mission 5

Anglican and Lutheran Holy Communion

  • Church of England: Holy Communion is the main Sunday service in many parishes; people kneel or stand at the altar rail and receive both bread and wine.
  • Anglican beliefs vary: some (Anglo-Catholics) believe in a real, objective presence; many evangelical Anglicans stress a spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Lutherans: Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine; the bread is not changed in substance.
Mission 5

Memorial and spiritual presence

  • Memorial view (Huldrych Zwingli, 1484–1531): "this is my body" means "this represents my body". The bread and wine are symbols that help believers remember Christ's death.
  • Baptist practice: the Lord's Supper, often monthly; bread and small individual cups of (often non-alcoholic) wine are passed along the rows; usually all believers are welcome.
  • Spiritual presence (John Calvin, 1509–1564; Presbyterian and Reformed): the bread stays bread, but by the Holy Spirit believers truly feed on Christ through faith.
  • All these churches agree: Jesus commanded it, it proclaims his death "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26), and it unites believers.
Quick check
?In one church, elders pass bread and tiny cups along the rows. The pastor says, "This bread does not change: we eat it to remember that Jesus died for us." Which tradition fits best?
Mission 5Diversity

A spectrum of belief

  • Catholic: transubstantiation; Real Presence remains, so consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Orthodox: a real change, called a mystery, not defined in philosophical terms; leavened bread and wine given together on a spoon; babies receive too.
  • Lutheran: real presence in, with and under the bread and wine.
  • Anglican: a range, from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Presbyterian (Reformed): spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Baptist and many evangelicals: memorial.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward Communion; inward communion with Christ.
Mission 5

Why it matters

  • Unity with Christ: Catholics and Orthodox believe they receive Christ himself; others meet him spiritually or remember him.
  • Unity with each other: "we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Forgiveness and strength: grace to live as Christians in the week ahead.
  • Remembering and hoping: looking back to the cross and forward to Christ's return.
  • Sent out: the Mass ends by sending people to serve others.
  • A sadness: Christians disagree about who may receive, so many cannot share Communion together.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship."

  • Reasons for: Jesus commanded it (1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics call it "source and summit"; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present. It unites the Church across the world.
  • Reasons against: many Protestants put preaching the Bible at the centre, with Communion monthly. Quakers and The Salvation Army worship without it. Jesus also stressed love in action (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is Christ present in it in a special way? Which practice most changes how Christians live?
Mission 5Worked answer

Catholic and Protestant interpretations

Question: Explain Catholic and Protestant interpretations of the Eucharist.

  • Catholic: at the consecration, bread and wine become Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation). Source: "this is my body" (Matthew 26:26), taken literally. Develop: so hosts are reserved in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Protestant (Baptist): the Lord's Supper is a memorial. Source: "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24). Develop: so the bread is shared as a symbol, and the focus is remembering and obeying.
  • Common ground: both obey Jesus' command and proclaim his death.
  • 8-mark (c) tip: explain both views, use specialist terms, refer to a source.

Mission 6

Shrines: holy places of prayer

  • A shrine is a holy place linked to a saint, a relic or a reported vision.
  • Many famous shrines are Marian: linked to Mary, the mother of Jesus.
  • Catholics honour (venerate) Mary and ask her to pray for them. They worship God alone.
  • Say: "Catholics honour Mary and ask her prayers" — not: "Catholics worship Mary".
  • Pilgrims go to pray, to ask for help, to give thanks, and to grow closer to God with others.
Mission 6

Lourdes: what happened in 1858

  • Lourdes: a small town in the Pyrenees, south-west France.
  • 11 February 1858: Bernadette Soubirous, a poor 14-year-old, says she sees a young lady in the Grotto (cave) of Massabielle by the river.
  • Over five months she reports 18 visions (apparitions).
  • She says the lady asks for prayer, penance, a chapel and processions.
  • Bernadette digs where she says she was told; a spring appears. It still flows.
  • 1862: the local bishop declares that the apparitions can be believed. Bernadette becomes a nun and is declared a saint (1933).
Mission 6

Lourdes: what pilgrims do

  • At the Grotto: pray before the statue of Mary; touch the rock; light a candle as a sign of continuing prayer.
  • The water: drink it, wash in it, or bathe in the baths. A sign of cleansing and new life.
  • Torchlight procession each evening: thousands sing the Ave Maria, candles in hand.
  • Eucharistic procession: the Blessed Sacrament is carried; the sick sit at the front and are blessed.
  • Mass, confession, the Stations of the Cross on the hillside.
  • The sick come first: volunteers push wheelchairs, carry stretchers and serve meals.
Mission 6

Healing and suffering

  • Some pilgrims report cures. Doctors at the Lourdes Medical Bureau examine claims.
  • Very few are declared miraculous: 72 by 2025. The Church is cautious.
  • Most pilgrims seek spiritual healing: peace, strength to cope, forgiveness, hope.
Mission 6

Walsingham: England's Nazareth

  • Walsingham: a village in Norfolk, England.
  • Tradition: in 1061 a noblewoman, Richeldis, had a vision of Mary and built a copy of the Holy House in Nazareth, where the angel told Mary she would bear Jesus (Luke 1:26–38).
  • A great medieval shrine. Destroyed in 1538 at the Reformation.
  • Revived in the 20th century: a Catholic National Shrine (at the Slipper Chapel) and an Anglican Shrine, with an Orthodox chapel. Members of many churches come.
  • Pilgrims: walk the Holy Mile (some barefoot); join processions with the statue of Our Lady of Walsingham; pray; receive the sacraments; at the Anglican shrine, are sprinkled with water from the holy well.
  • Many pilgrims pray for healing of body, mind and spirit; most go home seeking peace and strength, not a cure.
  • Significance: honours the Incarnation (God becoming human in Jesus) and Mary's "yes" to God.
Mission 6Diversity

Catholic and Protestant views

  • Catholic: pilgrimage is valuable but not compulsory; it renews prayer and pictures life as a journey to God.
  • Orthodox: shrines, relics and icons are important; pilgrimage is common.
  • Anglican: varied. Anglo-Catholics go to Walsingham; many others do not.
  • Many Protestants (e.g. evangelical, Baptist, Reformed):
    • God can be worshipped anywhere, "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24, paraphrase).
    • Christ is the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5): devotion to Mary or relics may distract from him.
    • Salvation is by faith, not by journeys.
  • Some Protestants still make pilgrimages to the Holy Land, or to communities like Iona and Taizé (see Mission 7).

Mission 6Evaluate

"Visiting a shrine brings a Christian closer to God than praying at home."

  • For: time set apart from daily life; a place soaked in centuries of prayer; praying with thousands of others; the sacraments; many pilgrims report lasting change.
  • Against: God is present everywhere; Jesus told his followers to pray in a private room (Matthew 6:6, paraphrase).
  • Against: cost and travel: the money could help the poor (Matthew 25:31–46); a trip can become tourism.
  • Against: many Protestants: focus on a place, statue or relic may distract from Christ.
  • Judge it by: Is God's presence tied to places? What the pilgrim does there, and how they live afterwards? Is "closer to God" a feeling or a change of life?
Mission check

Lourdes, Rome and Walsingham draw pilgrims for prayer, unity and spiritual healing; a cure is hoped for, but it is not the measure of the journey.

Mission 7

A different kind of pilgrimage

  • A pilgrimage is a journey made for a spiritual purpose.
  • Some pilgrims go to a holy site: a place of Jesus' life, a saint's tomb, a reported vision.
  • Others go to a living community: people who share prayer, work and meals.
  • Many of these communities are ecumenical: Christians of different denominations together.
  • Ecumenism: working for unity between Christian churches. It does not mean merging them into one.
Mission 7

Taizé: a parable of community

  • Taizé: a village in Burgundy, eastern France.
  • 1940: Brother Roger (1915–2005), a Swiss Protestant, settles there and shelters refugees from the war, including Jews.
  • From 1949 brothers commit for life to celibacy, simplicity and community.
  • Today: about a hundred brothers from Catholic and Protestant backgrounds, from many countries.
  • They accept no gifts for themselves; they live by their own work.
  • Brother Roger called the community a "parable of community": a small sign that divided Christians can live as one.
Mission 7

A week at Taizé

  • Every week, thousands of pilgrims, mostly young people, arrive from all over the world.
  • Prayer three times a day in the Church of Reconciliation:
    • short chants, repeated many times, in many languages;
    • a Bible reading;
    • a long silence.
  • Bible introduction by a brother, then small-group sharing across languages.
  • Practical work: cooking, cleaning, washing up. Simple food and simple living.
  • Friday: prayer around the cross; Saturday: a candlelit vigil of the resurrection.
  • Significance: trust in God, reconciliation, and taking prayer home to ordinary life.

Contrasting pilgrimages, differing views

  • Many Protestants who avoid shrines welcome communities like these: no relics or Marian devotion; Bible, prayer and service at the centre.
  • Catholics also go: popes have welcomed Taizé, and Catholic brothers belong to it.
  • Some Christians are wary:
    • some fear that a focus on unity blurs real differences in belief;
    • some evangelicals question repetitive chant (Matthew 6:7, paraphrase);
Mission 7Evaluate

"A week in a Christian community is worth more than a visit to a holy site."

  • For: a week of shared life can change habits, not just feelings.
  • For: meeting Christians of other churches lives out John 13:34–35.
  • For: the focus stays on Christ and on service, not on a place or object.
  • Against: holy sites link pilgrims to real events of faith: Jesus' life, the apostles, the saints.
  • Against: shrines offer the sacraments and a welcome for the sick that communities may not.
  • Against: people need different things; both kinds of pilgrimage are prayer, so ranking them may miss the point.
  • Judge it by: What is pilgrimage for? Does the place matter, or the people? What lasts after the journey home?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Liturgical (set order and words), non-liturgical (minister-led, sermon-centred), informal/charismatic (spontaneous, Spirit-led) and private worship; Quakers worship in silence.
  • The Bible is used in almost every form of worship; Christians disagree about which form best expresses "spirit and truth" (John 4:24).

Mission 2

  • In Christian teaching, prayer is the raising of the mind and heart to God; types: adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication (petition and intercession).
  • The Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13) is Jesus' model prayer: God's name, Kingdom and will first; then bread, forgiveness and protection.
  • Set (formulaic, traditional) and informal (extempore, spontaneous) prayer are valued differently across denominations.

Mission 3

  • A sacrament is an outward, visible sign of an inward, spiritual grace.
  • Catholics and Orthodox count seven; most Protestants count two (baptism and the Lord's Supper); Quakers and The Salvation Army use no outward sacraments.

Mission 4

  • Baptism is the sacrament (or ordinance) of initiation, with water and the name of the Trinity.
  • Infant baptism stresses God's grace, original sin (Catholic) and the covenant; believers' baptism stresses personal faith and the dying-and-rising picture of immersion.
  • Godparents promise to support a baptised child.

Mission 5

  • The Eucharist (Mass, Holy Communion, Lord's Supper) goes back to Jesus' Last Supper: "this is my body… my blood of the new covenant".
  • Catholics: transubstantiation and the Real Presence, the "source and summit"; Orthodox: a real change, a mystery; Lutherans: real presence; Anglicans: a range; Reformed: spiritual presence; Baptists: memorial.

Mission 6

  • Lourdes (1858, Bernadette, the Grotto and spring) is a place of prayer and healing where the sick come first; most healing sought is spiritual.
  • Walsingham: England's Nazareth, honouring the Incarnation; Catholics value pilgrimage, many Protestants see no need for it.

Mission 7

  • Taizé: Brother Roger's ecumenical community (1940); chants, silence, Bible sharing and work, mostly with young people.
