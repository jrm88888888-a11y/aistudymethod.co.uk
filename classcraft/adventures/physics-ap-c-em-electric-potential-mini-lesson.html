Mission 1

Mission 1: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Make a guess: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Mission 1

Electric potential

  • Electric potential at a point: the electric potential energy per unit charge for a charge placed there: V = UE/q.
  • Zero at infinity, by definition.
  • A scalar: it has a sign but no direction.
  • Unit: J/C = volt (V).
  • Set by the source charges, not by the test charge: any test charge at P, of any size or sign, sees the same V.
Mission 1

Potential of a point charge

  • V = kq/r, with k = 1/4πε0
  • Falls as 1/r, not 1/r² (field strength falls as 1/r²).
  • Positive charge: V positive, highest near the charge. Negative charge: V negative.
  • Example: +4.0 nC. At 0.20 m, V = 180 V.
Mission 1

Potentials add as numbers

  • Several charges: add the potentials, with their signs. No angles, no components.
  • V = kΣqᵢ/rᵢ
  • Two charges of +4.0 nC, 0.30 m apart. Midpoint M is 0.15 m from each.
  • Field at M: equal and opposite → E = 0.
  • Potential at M: 240 V + 240 V = 480 V. Not zero.
  • E = 0 does not mean V = 0. The field depends on how V changes, not on its value.
Mission 1

Work and potential difference

  • Change in electric potential energy: ΔUE = qΔV
  • Worked example: move +2.0 nC from 0.20 m to 0.10 m from the +4.0 nC charge.
  • ΔV = 360 − 180 = 180 V. Work done on it by an external force (moved slowly) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 180 = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J.
  • Released at 0.10 m, it is pushed outward. By 0.20 m it has gained 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J of kinetic energy.
  • A negative charge does the opposite: it speeds up moving toward higher V.
  • Only the start and end potentials matter, not the path.
Quick check
?An electron is released from rest at a location where the electric potential is 50 V. Moving under the electric force alone, it reaches a location where the electric potential is 250 V. How much kinetic energy has it gained? Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
Mission 1

Potential energy of two charges

  • UE = kq1q2/r
  • It is the work done by an external force to bring the charges together from infinity.
  • It belongs to the pair, not to one charge.
  • Like charges: positive. Work is needed to push them together.
  • Unlike charges: negative. Work is needed to pull them apart to infinity.
  • +4.0 nC and +2.0 nC, 0.10 m apart: +7.2 × 10⁻⁷ J.
Quick check
?System X consists of two protons, and system Y consists of a proton and an electron. In each system an external force moves the two particles farther apart. For which system or systems does the electric potential energy increase?
Mission 1

Equipotentials

  • Equipotential: a surface (or line on a 2D map) where V is the same everywhere.
  • On 2D maps these are often called isolines.
  • Moving along one: ΔV = 0, so no work is done.
  • Always perpendicular to field lines.
  • The field points toward lower potential.
  • Point charge: concentric spheres. Equal steps of V get closer together near the charge.
  • Uniform field between plates: parallel planes, evenly spaced.
Mission 1

How the field follows from the potential

  • Ex = −dV/dx
  • The minus sign: the field points down the potential slope.
  • The field depends on how V changes, not on its value.
  • Uniform field: |E| = ΔV/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → 2.0 × 10⁴ V/m.
  • Point charge: E at r = −(slope of the tangent) to the V–r curve. Its size is how steep the curve is.
  • Steep slope → strong field. Flat (maximum or minimum) → E = 0, whatever V is.
Quick check
?The graph shows the electric potential V as a function of position x along the x-axis, in a region where the electric field is directed along the x-axis. What is the x-component of the electric field at x = 0.20 m?
Mission check

?Particles with charges +Q and −Q are fixed a distance d apart. Point M is midway between them. Which statement about the electric potential at M is correct?

Potential and field strength are different things. Between two equal positive charges, E = 0 but V is not zero; between +Q and −Q, V = 0 but E is not zero.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Released from rest among equipotential lines, which way does an electron move, and does it speed up?

Make a guess: Released from rest among equipotential lines, which way does an electron move, and does it speed up?

Mission 2

Potential energy of a system

  • UE of a system = work done by an external force to assemble it from infinitely far apart, starting and ending at rest.
  • Add kqᵢqⱼ/rᵢⱼ over every pair, each pair once. A scalar sum: signs matter, directions do not.
  • Three particles on a line, 0.10 m apart: +2.0 μC, −2.0 μC, +2.0 μC.
  • Two neighboring pairs: (9.0 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)(−2.0 × 10⁻⁶)/0.10 = −0.36 J each.
  • End pair: (9.0 × 10⁹)(2.0 × 10⁻⁶)²/0.20 = +0.18 J.
  • UE = −0.54 J. To separate them completely, an external force must do +0.54 J.
  • Adding one more particle q at point P: Wext = qVP, where VP comes from the charges already there.
Quick check
?Four small particles, each with charge +q, are held at the corners of a square of side s. What is the total electric potential energy of the system of four particles? (k = 1/(4πε0).)
Mission 2

Isoline maps and field maps

  • Field → isolines: draw lines ⟂ to the field vectors everywhere.
  • Isolines → field: field vectors ⟂ to the isolines, pointing toward lower V.
  • Equal steps of V: isolines close together = strong field; far apart = weak.
  • Force on a charge: positive → along E, toward lower V. Negative → against E, toward higher V.
  • Released from rest: the charge starts moving ⟂ to the isoline it is on.
Quick check
?The figure shows four equipotential lines and three points A, B and C. How do the magnitudes of the electric field at A, B and C compare?
Mission 2

Energy as a charge crosses isolines

  • Only the electric force does work: ΔK = −ΔUE = −qΔV.
  • Positive charge speeds up moving to lower V.
  • Negative charge speeds up moving to higher V.
  • So an electron released from rest moves ⟂ to the isolines toward higher V, and speeds up.
  • Proton released from rest; it reaches a point 500 V lower:
  • K = −qΔV = −(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)(−500) = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • v = √(2K/m) = √(2 × 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁷ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷) = 3.1 × 10⁵ m/s.
Quick check
?A proton and an electron are each released from rest beside one plate of a pair of oppositely charged parallel plates in vacuum: the proton beside the positive plate and the electron beside the negative plate. Each moves under the electric force alone to the opposite plate. Which particle arrives with the greater speed, and why?
Mission 2

Potential and field with calculus

  • Ex = −dV/dx. Example: V(x) = 200x² (V, x in m). Ex = −400x. At x = 0.50 m: Ex = −200 V/m.
  • ΔV = VB − VA = −∫E·dr from A to B. Path does not matter.
  • Uniform E = 300 V/m along +x, from x = 0 to x = 0.30 m: ΔV = −300 × 0.30 = −90 V.
  • Continuous charge: V = k∫dq/r, a scalar integral (no components).
Quick check
?In a region of space the electric potential depends only on x and is given by V(x) = V0(x/a)³, where V0 and a are positive constants. What is the x-component of the electric field at x = a?
Mission check

?The figure shows four equally spaced equipotential lines and a proton at point P moving with velocity v parallel to the lines. Only the electric force acts on the proton. Which describes the proton's motion after it leaves P?

The force is ⟂ to the isolines: toward lower V for a positive charge, higher V for a negative one. Crossing them with the force, the particle gains kinetic energy |q||ΔV|.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bend a charged rod into a half circle. Why is the potential at its center so easy to find?

Make a guess: Bend a charged rod into a half circle. Why is the potential at its center so easy to find?

Mission 3

Potential by integration

  • Split the charge into pieces dq. Each gives dV = k dq/r. Add them: V = k∫dq/r, with k = 1/(4πε0).
  • V is a scalar: no components, nothing cancels by direction. Easier than ∫dE.
  • Rod: dq = λ dx. Arc of radius R: dq = λR dθ.
  • V = 0 infinitely far away (finite distributions).
  • In scope: finite rod (on its line, or on its perpendicular bisector), ring on its axis, arc at its center, long line.
Mission 3

Arc at its center

  • Every dq on an arc is the same distance R from the center.
  • V = k∫dq/R = kQ/R.
  • Any arc: a quarter, a half or a whole circle. Even if λ is not uniform.
  • Compare the field at the center of a semicircle: 2kQ/(πR²). Components had to be resolved.
Quick check
?Arc 1 is a semicircular arc of radius R with charge Q spread uniformly along it; the electric potential at its center is V1. Arc 2 is a quarter-circle arc of radius R/2 with charge Q/2 spread uniformly along it; the electric potential at its center is V2. What is V2/V1?
Mission 3

Rod, point on its line

  • Rod of length L, charge Q, λ = Q/L. P on the rod's line, distance d beyond one end.
  • Piece at distance s from P: dV = kλ ds/s, s from d to d + L.
  • V = kλ ln((d + L)/d).
  • Example: L = 0.30 m, Q = 4.0 nC, d = 0.10 m. λ = 1.33 × 10⁻⁸ C/m.
  • V = (9.0 × 10⁹)(1.33 × 10⁻⁸) ln 4 = 1.7 × 10² V.
  • All charge at the rod's center (0.25 m) would give 144 V. The near end counts for more.
Quick check
?The figure shows a thin rod with charge +6.0 nC spread uniformly along its length and a point P on the line of the rod, beyond its right end. What is the electric potential at P? (k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²; take V = 0 infinitely far away.)
Quick check
?A thin rod of length L carries a uniform linear charge density λ and lies along the x-axis from x = −L/2 to x = +L/2. Point P is on the rod's perpendicular bisector, a distance y from its midpoint. Which integral gives the electric potential at P? (k = 1/(4πε0).)
Mission 3

Rod, point on its perpendicular bisector

  • Rod from x = −L/2 to +L/2; P at distance y from the midpoint.
  • Piece at x: distance r = √(x² + y²).
  • V = ∫ kλ dx/√(x² + y²), from −L/2 to L/2 = 2kλ ln[(L/2 + √(L²/4 + y²))/y].
  • Same rod, y = 0.080 m: √(0.15² + 0.080²) = 0.17 m. V = 2kλ ln 4 = 3.3 × 10² V.
  • Far away (y ≫ L): V → kQ/y, a point charge. A good check.
Mission 3

Ring on its axis, then the field for free

  • Ring radius R, charge Q; P on the axis at distance x. Every dq is √(x² + R²) away.
  • V(x) = kQ/√(x² + R²). No integral needed beyond ∫dq = Q.
  • Now Ex = −dV/dx = kQx/(x² + R²)3/2. The same result as adding field components, with none of that work.
  • Q = 5.0 nC, R = 0.12 m, x = 0.16 m: distance 0.20 m. V = 2.3 × 10² V; Ex = 9.0 × 10² V/m, along +x.
  • This works only because V is known as a function of x. One value of V tells you nothing about E.
Quick check
?A rod of length L carries charge +Q spread uniformly. At a point on the rod's line, a distance x beyond one end, the potential is V(x) = (kQ/L) ln((x + L)/x). What is the magnitude of the electric field at that point?
Mission check

?A thin rod is bent into a semicircle of radius R and carries charge +Q spread uniformly along it. Point P is on the line through the semicircle's center, perpendicular to its plane, a distance √3R from the center. What is the electric potential at P? (k = 1/(4πε0); V = 0 infinitely far away.)

When every piece of charge is the same distance from the point, V = kQ/r at once, whatever the shape: the half circle at its center, a ring on its axis, or the half circle seen from its axis.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A charged wire runs on forever. Why can't its potential be zero infinitely far away?

Make a guess: A charged wire runs on forever. Why can't its potential be zero infinitely far away?

Mission 4

From field to potential

  • ΔV = Vb − Va = −∫E·dr, along any path from a to b.
  • Electrostatic fields: the path does not matter. Choose an easy one.
  • Moving along E: V falls. Moving across E: no change.
  • Recipe: find E (often by Gauss's law), then integrate along a field line.
  • Only differences in V are physical. The zero is a choice.
Quick check
?The graph shows the x-component Ex of the electric field as a function of position x along the x-axis; the field has no other components. What is the potential difference V(0.40 m) − V(0)?
Mission 4

Point charge: zero at infinity works

  • E = kQ/r². Start at infinity, where we set V = 0:
  • V(r) = −∫ kQ/r′² dr′ from ∞ to r = kQ/r.
  • The integral is finite because E falls as 1/r².
  • The same holds outside any spherically symmetric charge distribution: V = kQ/r.
Quick check
?A very long, straight line of charge has uniform linear charge density λ > 0. Point a is a perpendicular distance ra from the line and point b is a perpendicular distance rb from it, with rb > ra. What is the potential difference Vb − Va?
Mission 4

Long line of charge

  • Gauss's law: E = λ/(2πε0r), radially outward for λ > 0.
  • Vb − Va = −(λ/(2πε0)) ln(rb/ra).
  • From infinity: ln(∞) is infinite. E falls only as 1/r, so the integral never settles.
  • So choose V = 0 at some finite radius r0: V(r) = −(λ/(2πε0)) ln(r/r0).
  • Outside a long, uniformly charged cylinder: the same field, the same result.
  • Equal ratios of distance give equal potential differences.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Long wire, λ = 2.0 nC/m. Find V(5.0 cm) − V(1.0 cm).
  • λ/(2πε0) = 2kλ = 2 × 9.0 × 10⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ = 36 V.
  • ΔV = −36 × ln(5.0/1.0) = −36 × 1.61 = −58 V.
  • Negative: moving outward, along E, V falls.
  • From 5.0 cm to 25 cm: the same ratio, so also −58 V.
Mission 4

And back: field from potential

  • Er = −dV/dr (and Ex = −dV/dx in one dimension).
  • Check the line: V = −(λ/(2πε0)) ln(r/r0) → Er = λ/(2πε0r). ✓
  • Field points down the potential slope. Steeper slope, stronger field.
  • Integrating E gives V up to a constant; differentiating V gives E exactly. The constant drops out.
Quick check
?In a region of space, the electric potential depends only on the distance r from a fixed point: V(r) = β/r², where β is a positive constant. What is the electric field at distance r?
Quick check
?A very long, solid, insulating cylinder of radius R has a uniform positive volume charge density ρ. Vs is the electric potential at the cylinder's surface and V0 is the electric potential on its axis. What is the potential difference Vs − V0?
Mission check

?A very large, flat sheet carries a uniform positive surface charge, so its electric field has the same magnitude E at every distance from it. A student tries to set V = 0 infinitely far from the sheet. What is wrong with this choice?

ΔV = −∫E·dr always gives a definite difference. If E falls as 1/r² the integral out to infinity is finite; if it falls as 1/r (a wire) or not at all (a sheet), it never settles, so the zero of potential must be chosen at a finite distance.

Mission 5

Mission 5: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Make a guess: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Mission 5

The force on a charge in a field

  • F = qE, the same everywhere in a uniform field.
  • Positive charge: force along the field (toward the negative plate).
  • Negative charge: force against the field (toward the positive plate).
  • Between parallel plates: |E| = ΔV/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V/m.
  • Electron or proton there: |F| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N. Same size, opposite directions.
Mission 5

Same force, different acceleration

  • Constant force → constant acceleration: a = F/m.
  • Electron: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m/s².
  • Proton: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.9 × 10¹² m/s².
  • The electron's mass is about 1/1800 of the proton's, so its acceleration is about 1800 times greater.
  • Gravity is negligible: the electron's weight is about 10⁻²⁹ N, some 10¹⁴ times smaller than 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N.
Quick check
?Two horizontal parallel plates, 2.5 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 150 V between them. What is the magnitude of the acceleration of an electron between the plates? Gravity is negligible. Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission 5

Accelerating through a potential difference

  • Work done by the field = |q||ΔV| = gain in kinetic energy.
  • From rest: K = ½mv² = |q||ΔV|. The field does work; UE falls by the same amount.
  • Worked example: electron through 2000 V.
  • v = √(2e|ΔV|/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.65 × 10⁷ m/s.
  • That is about 9% of the speed of light, so ½mv² is still accurate.
Quick check
?A proton (charge +e, mass m) and an alpha particle (charge +2e, mass 4m) each start from rest and are accelerated by the electric force alone through the same potential difference. What is the ratio of the alpha particle's final kinetic energy Kα to the proton's final kinetic energy Kp?
Mission 5

Deflection: a projectile in an electric field

  • Particle enters at right angles to the field (parallel to the plates).
  • Along the plates: no force → constant velocity v.
  • Across the plates: constant force → constant acceleration a = F/m.
  • Path between the plates: a parabola, like a ball thrown horizontally.
  • Time between plates: t = L/v. Sideways shift: y = ½at².
  • After the plates: no force → straight line.
Mission 5

Worked example: deflection

  • Electron at 2.65 × 10⁷ m/s enters midway between plates 5.0 cm long, 2.0 cm apart, 400 V.
  • a = e|ΔV|/(md) = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m/s² (as for the electron on the earlier screen).
  • t = L/v = 0.050 / 2.65 × 10⁷ = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹ s.
  • y = ½at² = ½ × 3.5 × 10¹⁵ × (1.9 × 10⁻⁹)² = 6.3 mm. Less than 10 mm, so it misses the plate.
  • Sideways velocity at exit: vy = at = 6.6 × 10⁶ m/s.
  • A proton at the same speed: about 1800 times less deflection.
Quick check
?In the worked example, the electron left the plates deflected sideways by a distance y. The potential difference across the plates is now doubled, and the electron enters at twice the speed. What is its sideways deflection at exit now?
Quick check
?An electron (charge magnitude e, mass m) enters the region between two parallel plates with speed v0 parallel to the plates, midway between them, as shown in the diagram. The potential difference between the plates is ΔV, and the electron leaves the region without striking a plate. Gravitational effects are negligible. Which expression gives the magnitude of the electron's velocity component perpendicular to the plates as it leaves the region?
Mission check

?A proton and an alpha particle are placed, one at a time, at the same point in a uniform electric field. The alpha particle has twice the charge of the proton and about four times its mass. Gravity is negligible. What is the ratio (acceleration of the alpha particle) / (acceleration of the proton)?

Same field, acceleration = charge × E ÷ mass. The electron and proton feel equal forces, but the electron has about 1/1800 of the mass: about 1800 times the acceleration, so it swerves far more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • V = UE/q; V = kΣqᵢ/rᵢ, a scalar sum; ΔUE = qΔV.
  • The field is set by how fast V changes with position (the slope of V against r): equipotentials are ⟂ to field lines, and E = 0 does not mean V = 0.

Mission 2

  • System UE = sum of kqᵢqⱼ/rᵢⱼ over every pair; isolines are ⟂ to E, and E points to lower V.
  • ΔK = −qΔV: positive charges speed up going to lower V, negative charges going to higher V.
  • Ex = −dV/dx and ΔV = −∫E·dr, independent of path.

Mission 3

  • V = k∫dq/r, a scalar integral: arc center kQ/R; rod on its line kλ ln((d + L)/d); ring axis kQ/√(x² + R²).
  • With V(x) known, Ex = −dV/dx gives the field without components.

Mission 4

  • ΔV = −∫E·dr (any path); Er = −dV/dr. Only differences are physical.
  • Long line or outside a long cylinder: Vb − Va = −(λ/(2πε0)) ln(rb/ra); no zero at infinity.

Mission 5

  • In a uniform field F = qE (positive charges along E, negative against it), so a = qE/m is constant; light particles accelerate most.
  • Through a potential difference ΔV, a charge gains kinetic energy |q||ΔV|.
  • Entering at right angles: constant velocity along the plates, constant acceleration across them, so a parabola.
