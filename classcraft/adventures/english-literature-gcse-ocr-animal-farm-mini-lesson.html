Mission 1

The farm ends with the name it started with. So what changed?

Make a guess: The farm ends with the name it started with. So what changed?

Animal Farm · structure and allegoryScreen 1

A fable in ten chapters

This lesson doesn't quote the novel, which is still in copyright: learn your key quotations from your own copy.

  • Animal Farm (1945) is a short novel in ten chapters. Orwell subtitled it A Fairy Story.
  • It is a beast fable: animals act like people so readers can judge human behaviour.
  • In Chapter 2 the animals drive out Mr Jones and rename Manor Farm as Animal Farm. In Chapter 10 Napoleon restores the old name.
Animal Farm · structure and allegoryScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Component 01, Section A (closed book): one two-part question on Animal Farm, 40 marks in all.
  • Part (a): compare an extract printed from the novel with an unseen extract from another modern prose text on a linked theme.
  • Part (b): a related question on the same novel, usually on another moment in it.
  • Section A rewards AO1 (personal response and references), AO2 (language, form, structure) and AO3 (context). Essay practice is for class.
Animal Farm · structure and allegoryScreen 3

Allegory, used with care

  • An allegory is a story whose characters and events stand for something beyond themselves.
  • Orwell said the main events follow the Russian Revolution, but he compressed them and changed their order.
  • He also said the satire was meant to apply more widely: to how any revolution can be betrayed.
  • So don't force every animal to equal one person. Old Major, for example, draws on both Marx and Lenin.
Animal Farm · structure and allegoryScreen 4

Rise, turn and fall

ChaptersWhat happens
1–2Old Major's dream; the Rebellion; the Seven Commandments
3–4A good first harvest; the Battle of the Cowshed. Quietly, the pigs keep the milk and apples
5–9Snowball expelled; the windmill; executions; Boxer sold
10Pigs on two legs; the old name returns
  • Orwell named the milk and apples as the turning point. The ending is circular: it returns to where it began.

Orwell brings the story back to its starting point on purpose. The farm has the old name, the leaders walk on two legs, and the ordinary animals are no better off: the revolution has changed who the masters are, not how the animals live.

Mission 2

Snowball is chased away. Would he have ruled any better?

Make a guess: Snowball is chased away. Would he have ruled any better?

Animal Farm · Napoleon and SnowballScreen 1

Two leaders, two styles

  • Snowball is lively and inventive. He gives speeches, sets up committees and reading classes, and plans the windmill (Chapters 3–5).
  • He leads the defence at the Battle of the Cowshed and is wounded (Chapter 4).
  • Napoleon speaks little and works behind the scenes. He wins support between meetings and takes Jessie and Bluebell's puppies to raise in private (Chapter 3).
Animal Farm · Napoleon and SnowballScreen 2

How Snowball is removed

  • In Chapter 5 the two pigs clash over the windmill. Napoleon shows contempt for Snowball's plans.
  • As the vote nears, Napoleon gives a signal and nine huge dogs, the grown puppies, chase Snowball off the farm.
  • Napoleon then ends the Sunday debates. When four young pigs protest, the dogs growl and the sheep drown them out.
Animal Farm · Napoleon and SnowballScreen 3

Napoleon in power

  • Weeks later Napoleon announces the windmill will be built after all; Squealer claims it was Napoleon's idea from the start (Chapter 5).
  • Snowball becomes the scapegoat for every disaster, and is later recast as a traitor (Chapters 6–9).
  • Napoleon appears less often, guarded by his dogs, and gathers grand titles (Chapter 8).
  • By Chapter 10 he walks upright and hosts the human farmers.
Animal Farm · Napoleon and SnowballScreen 4

Is Snowball simply good?

  • In Chapter 3 Snowball, like Napoleon, backs the pigs' claim to the milk and apples.
  • After the battle he brushes aside Boxer's grief at seeming to kill a stable lad.
  • Many readers link Snowball to Trotsky (forced out in 1927, exiled 1929) and Napoleon to Stalin. Orwell, a democratic socialist, criticised both sides; Faber's reader T. S. Eliot thought the book leaned towards Trotsky.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Snowball is chased away. Would he have ruled any better?

Orwell gives no clear answer. Snowball is more open and inventive than Napoleon, but he also accepted the milk and apples and dismissed Boxer's grief, so the strongest answers weigh both and ask what Orwell suggests about leaders who put themselves above the other animals.

Mission 3

The animals saw it happen. How are they talked out of it?

Make a guess: The animals saw it happen. How are they talked out of it?

Animal Farm · Squealer and propagandaScreen 1

Napoleon's voice

  • Squealer is introduced in Chapter 2 as a plump little pig with a high voice and a gift for persuasion: he can make almost anything sound true.
  • He rarely decides anything. He explains Napoleon's decisions to the other animals.
  • Many readers link him to the Soviet state's newspapers and propaganda, which told citizens what to think.
Animal Farm · Squealer and propagandaScreen 2

Squealer's toolkit

MethodExample (paraphrased)
FearAsks whether they want Jones back (Ch 3, 5)
Pseudo-scienceThe pigs' brainwork needs milk and apples (Ch 3)
StatisticsReads out long lists of rising figures (Ch 8, 9)
Rewriting the pastSnowball was Jones's agent all along (Ch 7, 9)
EuphemismCalls a cut in rations a readjustment (Ch 9)
DenialSays no resolution against trade was ever passed (Ch 6)
Animal Farm · Squealer and propagandaScreen 3

He never works alone

  • The dogs stand by him when he explains that the windmill was Napoleon's idea all along (Chapter 5): his words come with a threat.
  • The sheep bleat their slogan to drown out doubters (Chapters 5, 9, 10).
  • The animals can't check: few can read well, and memory fades as years pass.
  • Orwell said Spain showed him how easily propaganda can mislead even people in free countries.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The animals saw it happen. How are they talked out of it?

Squealer uses fear of Jones, figures, euphemism and a rewritten past, and the dogs and sheep make doubt dangerous or impossible to hear. Orwell shows that propaganda works best on people who can't read the record and are afraid to speak.

Mission 4

Boxer is the strongest animal on the farm. Why doesn't he resist?

Make a guess: Boxer is the strongest animal on the farm. Why doesn't he resist?

Animal Farm · Boxer and the working animalsScreen 1

The farm's hardest worker

  • Boxer is an enormous, gentle carthorse, far stronger than any other animal and much admired, but slow to learn: he never gets past the first letters of the alphabet.
  • At the first harvest (Chapter 3) he adopts a personal motto: he will simply work harder.
  • After Snowball's expulsion (Chapter 5) he adds a second: Napoleon can never be wrong.
Animal Farm · Boxer and the working animalsScreen 2

Trust under strain

  • Ch 4: he fights bravely, then grieves when he thinks he has killed a stable lad.
  • Ch 7: he doubts that Snowball was a traitor, until Squealer says Napoleon has declared it. When the dogs attack him he pins one down, then looks to Napoleon and lets it go.
  • Ch 8–9: injured in the Battle of the Windmill, he works on, hoping to retire.
Animal Farm · Boxer and the working animalsScreen 3

The other working animals

  • Clover, a motherly mare, senses things have gone wrong (Chapter 7) but can't find the words, and can't read the Commandments well enough to check them.
  • Benjamin, the donkey, can read but refuses to, and stays cynical.
  • The hens resist when their eggs are sold (Chapter 7), and nine die.
  • Mollie abandons the farm for ribbons and sugar (Chapter 5).
Animal Farm · Boxer and the working animalsScreen 4

What Boxer stands for

  • In Chapter 1 Old Major warns Boxer that Jones would one day sell him to the knacker. In Chapter 9 the pigs do exactly that: foreshadowing turned into bitter irony.
  • Many readers see Boxer as the Soviet working class: loyal, strong and exploited.
  • Orwell said revolutions only help ordinary people if they stay alert enough to remove their leaders.

Write itScreen 7
?Why might Orwell make his strongest character the most trusting?

In 2–4 sentences, explain why Orwell makes Boxer both the strongest and the most trusting animal. Refer to at least two chapters.

Model answer

Orwell makes Boxer stronger than any other animal, yet he never turns that strength on the pigs. In Chapter 7 he could crush one of Napoleon's dogs but lets it go when Napoleon signals, and he settles his doubts by trusting his second motto. Orwell suggests that the workers' strength is wasted when they trust leaders blindly, which makes his sale to the knacker in Chapter 9 all the more bitter.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 8

?Boxer is the strongest animal on the farm. Why doesn't he resist?

Boxer has the strength to resist but not the means to question: he can't read, he trusts his mottoes, and he settles every doubt by deferring to Napoleon. Orwell uses him to show how a loyal working class can be exploited by leaders it never learns to challenge.

Mission 5

The pigs start as equals. When do they stop being equal?

Make a guess: The pigs start as equals. When do they stop being equal?

Animal Farm · power and corruptionScreen 1

Old Major's warnings

  • In Chapter 1 Old Major tells the animals never to copy humans' habits: living in houses, sleeping in beds, wearing clothes, drinking alcohol, smoking, using money or trading.
  • No animal must ever tyrannise over another, and all animals are equal.
  • These warnings become a checklist. The reader can tick off each one as the pigs break it.
Animal Farm · power and corruptionScreen 2

One privilege at a time

ChapterWhat the pigs take
2The milk quietly disappears
3Milk and windfall apples, explained by Squealer
5Debate ended; Napoleon rules with his dogs
6Trade with humans; beds in the farmhouse
8Whisky
9Extra rations; ribbons on Sundays
10Walking upright, whips, Jones's clothes
  • Orwell called the milk and apples the turning point.
Animal Farm · power and corruptionScreen 3

How power is held

  • Fear: the dogs, and the executions in Chapter 7.
  • Knowledge: the pigs read and write; most animals can't check the record.
  • Propaganda: Squealer, the sheep, and poems praising Napoleon.
  • A scapegoat: Snowball is blamed for every failure.
  • Ritual: songs, parades and Spontaneous Demonstrations keep the animals busy and proud.
Animal Farm · power and corruptionScreen 4

Who is to blame?

  • One reading: power corrupts whoever holds it, so the pigs were bound to become like Jones.
  • Another: Orwell blames a power-hungry few and the animals who didn't object. He said things would have gone better if the others had put a stop to it when the pigs kept the milk.
  • Strong answers weigh both. Orwell denied that the book defends things as they were.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The pigs start as equals. When do they stop being equal?

The inequality begins almost at once: the milk vanishes in Chapter 2, and in Chapter 3 the pigs claim the milk and apples. Orwell builds the rest one step at a time, so each new privilege seems small, and by Chapter 10 the pigs are indistinguishable from the humans.

Animal Farm · the Russian Revolution and StalinScreen 3

Orwell's politics

  • Orwell was a democratic socialist. He wrote that his serious work from 1936 was against totalitarianism and for democratic socialism.
  • Fighting in Spain (1936–37), he saw Soviet-backed Communists persecute other left-wing groups.
  • He believed the myth that Stalin's USSR was socialist was harming socialism itself. The novel attacks a betrayed revolution, not the idea of equality.
Animal Farm · the Russian Revolution and StalinScreen 4

A hard book to publish

  • Written from late 1943 to early 1944, when the USSR was Britain's ally.
  • Gollancz rejected it. Cape withdrew after advice from a Ministry of Information official, very probably Peter Smollett, later exposed as a Soviet agent.
  • Faber rejected it in July 1944. Secker & Warburg accepted it, and it appeared on 17 August 1945, after the war in Europe ended.
  • Orwell said the quarrel at the end was deliberate: he doubted the Tehran friendship would last.

Mission 7

Seven rules on a barn wall. Who keeps changing them?

Make a guess: Seven rules on a barn wall. Who keeps changing them?

Animal Farm · satire, irony and the CommandmentsScreen 1

Satire and three kinds of irony

  • Satire mocks people or ideas to expose them. Orwell's target is a revolution betrayed by its leaders.
  • Verbal irony: saying one thing and meaning another.
  • Situational irony: the outcome is the opposite of what was intended.
  • Dramatic irony: the reader understands more than the characters.
  • Calling a political satire A Fairy Story is itself ironic.
Animal Farm · satire, irony and the CommandmentsScreen 2

The Commandments, changed one by one

Commandment (Ch 2, paraphrased)What it becomes
1–2. Two legs: enemy. Four legs or wings: friendGone by Ch 10; the pigs walk upright
3. No clothesGone; pigs wear Jones's clothes (Ch 10)
4. No bedsOnly sleeping between sheets is banned (Ch 6)
5. No alcoholOnly drinking too much (Ch 8)
6. No killing other animalsOnly killing for no reason (Ch 8)
7. All animals equalKept, but with a sly exception that ranks some animals above the rest (Ch 10)
Animal Farm · satire, irony and the CommandmentsScreen 3

Seeing what the animals can't

  • In Chapter 8 the animals find Squealer sprawled at night beside a broken ladder and a pot of white paint. They are puzzled; only Benjamin seems to understand, and he says nothing.
  • The reader works it out at once: dramatic irony.
  • Each change keeps the rule's shape but adds a get-out, so the animals assume their memories are wrong.
  • The reader becomes the alert animal Orwell wished the farm had.

?Seven rules on a barn wall. Who keeps changing them?

The pigs change the rules, as the ladder and paint pot in Chapter 8 show. The changes go unnoticed because few animals can read and memories fade, but the reader sees what the animals can't: Orwell's dramatic irony turns the reader into the watchful animal the farm lacked.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Animal Farm is a beast fable in ten chapters, subtitled A Fairy Story; it is also an allegory of the Russian Revolution with a wider warning about power.
  • Use allegory with care: Orwell compressed and reordered history, and some characters blend several real figures.
  • The structure rises (Ch 1–4), turns on the pigs keeping the milk and apples, falls (Ch 5–10) and ends where it began.
  • Snowball leads through speeches, plans and committees; Napoleon through secrecy, loyal supporters and force.
  • Napoleon expels Snowball with the dogs in Chapter 5, then ends debate and uses him as a scapegoat.
  • Snowball is not simply good: he shares the pigs' first privileges. Many readers link him to Trotsky and Napoleon to Stalin.
  • Squealer explains Napoleon's decisions; many readers link him to Soviet state propaganda.
  • His methods: fear of Jones, pseudo-science, statistics, rewriting the past, euphemism and denial.
  • The dogs and sheep back him up, and the animals' poor reading and fading memories let his versions win.
  • Boxer is the strongest and most loyal animal; his two mottoes replace thinking with work and trust.
  • Clover senses wrong but lacks words; Benjamin can read but stays silent; the hens resist and die; Mollie leaves.
  • Old Major's warning in Chapter 1 comes true in Chapter 9: many readers see Boxer as the exploited working class.
  • Old Major's warnings in Chapter 1 work as a checklist that the pigs break one by one.
  • Privilege grows gradually: milk and apples (Ch 3), force (Ch 5), farmhouse and trade (Ch 6), whisky (Ch 8), two legs (Ch 10).
  • Power is held by fear, knowledge, propaganda, a scapegoat and ritual; readers debate whether Orwell blames power itself or the leaders and those who didn't object.
  • Key dates: 1917 revolution; 1924 Lenin dies; Trotsky out 1927–29; Five-Year Plans from 1928; purges 1936–38; pact 1939; invasion 1941; Tehran 1943.
  • The novel maps loosely onto this history; Orwell compressed and reordered it.
  • Orwell was a democratic socialist attacking Stalin's betrayal of socialism; publishers rejected the book because it attacked an ally, and it appeared in August 1945.
  • Animal Farm is a satire; it uses verbal, situational and dramatic irony, starting with its subtitle A Fairy Story.
  • The Commandments (Ch 2) are bent one by one (Ch 6, 8) until a single rule on equality, with a sly qualification, is left (Ch 10).
  • The ladder in Chapter 8 lets the reader see what the animals can't: dramatic irony makes the reader the alert observer.
