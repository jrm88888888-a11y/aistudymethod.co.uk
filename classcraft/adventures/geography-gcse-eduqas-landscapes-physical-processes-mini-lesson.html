Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Uplands and lowlands

  • Uplands (high, steep, rugged): mainly in the north and west: the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, the Pennines, Wales; plus Dartmoor, Bodmin Moor and Exmoor in the south-west.
  • Lowlands (low, gentle): mainly in the south and east: clay vales such as the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury, broken by hills of more resistant chalk and limestone (the Downs; the Cotswolds, "hard limestones rising above mudstones").
  • England's highest mountain, Scafell Pike (978 m), is in the Lake District.
Mission 1

The link to geology

  • Relief (differences in height) "is greatly affected by the underlying geology".
  • Hard rocks resist erosion → uplands: granite (Dartmoor), slate (Lake District, Wales), gneiss (Western Highlands), hard sandstone (Exmoor).
  • Soft rocks wear away → lowlands: clays of the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury are soft and impermeable, so streams erode them fast.
  • Chalk is not very hard, but rain soaks straight through it, so it forms chains of hills like the Downs.
Mission 1

Major rivers

  • Most UK rivers rise in the uplands (high rainfall) and flow to the sea across the lowlands.
  • The Severn is the UK's longest river; it rises in the Welsh uplands and reaches the sea in the Bristol Channel.
  • The Thames flows east through the lowlands of southern England to London and the North Sea.
  • The Trent drains the Midlands towards the Humber; rivers on the east side of the Pennines flow to the North Sea.

Mission 1

Major coastal landscapes

  • Holderness (East Yorkshire): soft till cliffs about 20 m high, eroded by waves at high tide.
  • Old Harry Rocks (Dorset): stacks at the end of Ballard Down, a chalk headland.
  • Hard rock coasts (e.g. granite and slate in the west) erode slowly and form cliffs and headlands; soft rock coasts (clay, till) retreat fast.
  • Coasts mirror the land: geology again decides the shape.
Mission 1

Human activity shapes the land

  • Agriculture: farmland covers 69% of the UK's land; in England 63.1% of land is agricultural.
  • Forestry: woodland covers 14% of the UK (20% of Scotland, 10% of England).
  • Settlement: 8.7% of England's land is in developed use (homes, roads, industry).
  • Over centuries, grazing, tree planting and felling, and town growth have changed vegetation, drainage and the look of the land.
Mission 1

What makes a landscape distinctive?

  • Geology: slate fells in the Lake District; chalk downs in the south.
  • Land use and culture: the Lake District is an "upland agro-pastoral system", based on breeds such as the Herdwick sheep.
  • Vegetation: on Ballard Down, grazing is allowed "to prevent it converting to natural beech woodland".
  • Climate: wet western uplands v. dry eastern lowlands.
Quick check

Mission check

?In south-east England, a ridge of chalk stands above a clay vale. Chalk is not a very hard rock. Why does the chalk form the higher ground?

Relief follows geology: hard rocks of the north and west stand up as uplands; soft, impermeable clays of the south and east are worn down, while permeable chalk survives as low hills.

Mission 2

A glaciated upland that people use

  • The Lake District National Park in Cumbria covers 2,362 km². Ice carved its corries, troughs and ribbon lakes.
  • 38,993 people live there (Census 2021).
  • 17.45 million tourists visited in 2025.
  • It is a UNESCO World Heritage Site: "an unrivalled example of a northern European upland agro-pastoral system" (sheep and cattle farming).
Mission 2

Four ways to earn a living

  • Tourism: visitors spent £1.784 billion directly in 2025.
  • Farming: centuries of upland sheep farming, including the Herdwick breed. Thin soils, steep slopes and heavy rain suit grazing, not crops.
  • Forestry: woodland covers 13% of the park; 95 km² is owned or managed by the Forestry Commission.
  • Quarrying: slate has long been dug here. Honister Slate Mine sells slate and now also mine tours, a via ferrata and a zip wire.

When land uses clash

  • Tourism v. local people: demand for holiday homes pushes up house prices. In 2011, 24% of homes were holiday or second homes.
  • Tourism v. farming: walkers, dogs and cars share narrow lanes and grazing land with sheep and farm traffic.
  • Tourism v. jobs: tourism jobs are often seasonal and low-paid.
  • Traffic: visitors bring congestion, parking problems and litter.
Mission 2

Development v. conservation

  • UNESCO lists the risks: "development pressures, changing agricultural practices and diseases, and tourism".

  • Honister: new adventure attractions bring jobs, but add structures and visitors high on the fells.
  • The question is always the same: does new development (jobs, homes, attractions) harm what makes the place worth conserving?

Honeypots and carrying capacity

  • Honeypot site: a place that draws very large numbers of visitors, like bees to honey.
  • Windermere is one: Windermere Lake Cruises had over 1.3 million users in 2022.
  • Carrying capacity: the maximum number of visitors a place can take before it is damaged or the visit is spoiled.
  • Past that point paths erode, car parks overflow and the place stops feeling special.
Mission 2

Visitor pressure: good and bad

  • Positive: £1.784 billion of direct spending in 2025; money keeps shops, cafés and bus routes open.
  • Negative: footpath erosion, traffic congestion, litter.
  • Negative for locals: shops cater for tourists, and prices rise.
Mission 2

A changing rural economy

  • Holiday homes: 24% of homes in 2011; they push up prices for local buyers.
  • Jobs: tourism work is often seasonal and low-paid.
  • Old industries adapt: Honister Slate Mine now sells adventure experiences as well as slate.
  • Farming: UNESCO lists "changing agricultural practices" as a risk to the landscape.
Mission 2

Managing visitors

  • Rules: the Windermere 10 mph speed limit (2005) cut noise and wash from fast boats.
  • Protecting habitats: some lakeshore has been cordoned off so plants can regrow.
  • Spreading visitors out: car parks, buses and lake boats steer people away from overloaded spots.
  • Education: signs and rangers explain why sheep, walls and paths need care.
Mission 2

Repairing damage: footpaths

  • Fix the Fells (National Trust rangers and volunteers) looks after 344 upland paths, covering 661 km.
  • The causes of erosion: "millions of pairs of walking boots, the weather and gradient".
  • In 2025, 147 volunteers spent 3,206 days maintaining paths.
  • Repair keeps walkers on one hard, well-drained line, so the vegetation beside it can recover.

Mission 3

Erosion

  • A river's load is the rock and soil it carries.
  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, pushing air into cracks in the bed and banks.
  • Abrasion: the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
  • Attrition: stones hit each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: water dissolves soluble rock, such as limestone.
  • Vertical erosion deepens the channel; lateral erosion widens it.
Mission 3

Transportation

  • Traction: large boulders roll along the bed.
  • Saltation: smaller stones and sand bounce along the bed.
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried in the water, making it look muddy.
  • Solution: dissolved minerals are carried, invisible.
  • Big boulders "may only be moved during rapid flows under flood conditions".
Quick check
?A river carries sand grains that bounce along its bed. Which process is this?
Mission 3

Why rivers deposit

A river deposits (drops) its load when it loses energy:

  • velocity falls, e.g. on the inside of a bend or where it enters a lake or the sea;
  • discharge falls after a flood or in a dry spell;
  • the water becomes shallow;
  • it has more load than it can carry.
  • The largest, heaviest particles are dropped first.
Mission 3

Why fine grains are hard to pick up

  • One textbook reading of a velocity graph: a 1 mm sand grain is picked up at about 20 cm/s; a 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s. Versions of the graph differ.
  • Fine grains, especially clay, "stick together": they are cohesive.
  • They also lie in a thin layer of slow water against the bed, which shelters them.
  • Once lifted, fine grains stay in suspension even in slow water, so they are deposited last.
Quick check
?A river slows from 30 cm/s to 5 cm/s as it enters a lake. What is most likely?
Mission 3

Processes change downstream

  • Upper course: steep gradient, large angular load: vertical erosion dominates.
  • Middle course: more discharge: lateral erosion on the outside of bends, deposition on the inside.
  • Lower course: most discharge, fine load: deposition dominates, especially in floods and near the mouth.
Mission check

?In a flood, water runs at 30 cm/s past a sandy bar and a bank of firm clay. The sand is being picked up, but the clay bank is barely eroded. Why?

Fine grains are cohesive and sheltered at the bed, so silt needs about 60 cm/s to be picked up, against about 20 cm/s for 1 mm sand. Once lifted, they are the last to be deposited.

Mission 4

V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs

  • In the upper course the river cuts down: vertical erosion.
  • Weathering and mass movement wear the valley sides back, giving a V-shaped valley.
  • The river winds around ridges of harder rock. Seen from upstream, the ridges overlap: interlocking spurs.
Mission 4

How a waterfall forms

  • A band of hard (resistant) rock lies over soft rock.
  • The soft rock erodes faster, so the hard rock is left overhanging.
  • Falling water and swirling rocks erode a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The soft rock is undercut; the unsupported overhang collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall back upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
Mission 4

Niagara Falls

  • Hard dolostones and limestones lie over weaker shales and limestones.
  • About 12,000 years ago the river took its present course; since then the falls have moved about 11 km (7 miles) upstream.
  • Recent retreat: about 0.9–1.5 m (3–5 feet) a year over the last 500+ years; now 0.3 m (1 foot) a year, largely because water is taken for hydroelectric power.
  • So a waterfall is not fixed: it changes over thousands of years, and people can change the rate.
Quick check

Mission 4

A UK waterfall: High Force

  • High Force, on the River Tees, "plunges over the Whin Sill", a hard rock called dolerite.
  • "Below the Whin Sill you can see layers of sandstone and limestone": softer rocks that are undercut.
  • "Torrential meltwaters carved gorges like those at Low Force and High Force".
Mission 4

Meanders

  • On a bend the fastest water swings to the outside.
  • Outside bank: lateral erosion cuts a steep river cliff.
  • Inside bank: slow water deposits sand and gravel: a gentle slip-off slope, built of a point bar.
  • In cross-section the channel is deep on the outside and shallow on the inside.
  • Learn the term slip-off slope.
Quick check
?In a cross-section across a meander, where is the river deepest?
Mission 4

Floodplains and levees

  • Floodplain: the wide, flat valley floor that floods. It is widened as meanders migrate and built up by layers of alluvium (silt) left by floods.
  • When the river floods, water slows sharply as it leaves the channel, so the coarsest sediment is dropped first, beside the channel.
  • Flood after flood, this builds raised banks: natural levees.
  • Finer silt and clay settle further out on the floodplain.
Quick check
?A river floods and spreads across its floodplain. Where is the coarsest sediment deposited?
Mission 4

Landforms change over time, at different scales

  • Large scale: valleys and gorges form over thousands of years (Niagara: about 11 km in about 12,000 years).
  • Small scale: plunge pools and slip-off slopes change in years or even in one flood.
  • Meanders migrate across the floodplain; necks are cut through, leaving oxbow lakes.
  • Why: changes in discharge (floods and droughts), rock type, and human action (e.g. taking water from Niagara has slowed its retreat).
Mission check

?An upland river flows over a band of hard sandstone lying on top of soft shale. Over thousands of years its waterfall moves upstream. Which explanation is correct?

Hard rock over soft rock, undercutting in the plunge pool, and collapse move a waterfall upstream. Niagara has moved about 11 km in about 12,000 years, leaving a gorge.

Mission 5

Where waves get their energy

  • Waves are made by wind blowing over the sea.
  • Bigger waves come from stronger winds, winds that blow for longer, and a longer fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • When a wave breaks, water rushes up the beach (swash) and drains back (backwash).
Mission 5

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive waves: low and gentle. Strong swash, weak backwash, so they add sediment and build beaches.
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, often in storms. Weak swash, strong backwash, so they remove sediment and attack cliffs.
Quick check

Mission 5

Four ways the sea erodes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water, and air forced into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: sea water dissolves some rocks, such as chalk and limestone.
Mission 5

Transport, longshore drift and deposition

  • Four ways sediment moves: traction (big pebbles roll), saltation (small pebbles and sand bounce), suspension (fine particles carried in the water), solution (dissolved).
  • Longshore drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash pushes sediment up the beach at that angle, and the backwash drags it straight down. Sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped where waves and currents lose energy, such as in sheltered bays.
Quick check
?A beach faces south. The prevailing waves arrive from the south-west. Which way does longshore drift move the sand?
Mission 5

Weathering: breaking rock where it stands

  • Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion carries it away.
  • Mechanical: freeze-thaw (water in a crack freezes, expands and widens it) and salt weathering (salt crystals grow in tiny gaps and split the rock).
  • Chemical: rainwater is a weak acid. It slowly dissolves chalk and limestone (carbonation).
Mission 5

Mass movement: cliffs that fall and slump

  • Mass movement: rock and soil move downhill under gravity.
  • Rock fall: blocks break off a steep cliff, often after waves undercut it or freeze-thaw loosens them.
  • Sliding: material slides down a straight slip surface.
  • Slumping: soft, wet rock such as clay slips along a curved surface, rotating as it goes.
  • Landslide is the general name for these slides and slumps.
Mission 5

Holderness: soft clay against hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly 18,000–13,000 years old, and about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • Above the waves, the till fails mainly by rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls. More rainfall could make the till more likely to fail.
  • To the north, Flamborough Head is chalk, which "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004: about 114 m in total (2.16 × 53, calculated).
Mission 5

Where the eroded clay goes

  • The eroded cliffs are a sediment supply: up to 2 million m³ a year was removed from the cliffs, according to data going back to 1786.
  • Longshore drift carries it south. Spurn, at the southern end, formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast".
  • One stretch's erosion is the next stretch's deposition: link the processes in your answer.
Quick check
?Why does Holderness retreat so much faster than Flamborough Head, just to the north?
Mission check

?Two cliffs face the same North Sea storms. Cliff X is resistant chalk. Cliff Y is soft glacial clay, soaked after a wet winter. Which retreats faster, and why?

Aldbrough's cliffs are soft till. Waves attack the foot at high tide, the wet clay slumps from above, and longshore drift carries the debris south. That is why they retreated 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 6

Headlands and bays

  • Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles, the sea erodes the soft rock faster.
  • Soft rock is worn back into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • Bays are sheltered, so waves there lose energy and drop sand: bay-head beaches.
Mission 6

Cliffs, notches and platforms

  • Waves attack the foot of a cliff between high and low tide, cutting a wave-cut notch by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The rock above is left overhanging; it collapses and the cliff retreats.
  • A gently sloping wave-cut platform is left, exposed at low tide.
  • Water trapped in hollows on the platform at low tide forms rock pools. Waves also exploit bedding planes, the boundaries between rock layers.
Mission 6

Cave, arch, stack, stump: Old Harry Rocks

  • At Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, the sea exploits "a crack in the chalk joints", slowly creating a cave.
  • Erosion along joints and bedding planes turns the cave into an arch; when the arch collapses, a stack is left.
  • Old Harry's Wife "was still a stack until her eventual collapse in 1896 to render her a stump": a stump is a stack worn down until it is covered at high tide.
  • Exam move: name the process at each stage (hydraulic action, abrasion) and the weakness it exploits (joints, faults, bedding planes).
Quick check
?What is the correct order as a headland is eroded?
Mission 6

Why the sea drops sediment: beaches

  • The sea deposits sediment where it loses energy: in sheltered bays, in shallow water, behind obstacles, and where constructive waves dominate.
  • Deposition also needs a supply of sediment, from eroding cliffs, rivers and the seabed.
  • A beach builds up where more sediment arrives than is removed.
  • Sandy beaches have gentle slopes; shingle beaches (pebbles) are steeper. Slapton Sands, Devon, is shingle: "flint, chert and quartz pebbles".
Mission 6

Spits

  • Where the coast changes direction, sediment carried along the coast keeps going and is dropped in the calmer water beyond: a spit grows out from the land.
  • The end often curves round as waves bend into the calmer water behind it: a hooked (recurved) spit. Saltmarsh forms in the shelter behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Quick check

Mission check

?Shingle drifts east along a straight coast. Then the coastline turns sharply north into a river estuary. What will most likely form, and why?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment carried south along the Holderness coast by longshore drift keeps going where the coast turns at the Humber mouth, and is dropped in the calmer water. Its end hooks into the estuary, with saltmarsh behind.

Mission 7

Two coasts, two speeds

  • Holderness (East Yorkshire): cliffs erode at 1 to 4.5 m a year; the coast has retreated about 2 km in 1,000 years, losing 26 villages listed in the Domesday Book.
  • Swanage Bay and Durlston Bay (Dorset, on the Jurassic Coast): erosion maps use average rates of about 0.2 to 0.6 m a year.
  • Same country, same sea level, very different speeds. Three groups of factors explain it: geology, climate, people.
Mission 7

Geology: how resistant is the rock?

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till, left by ice 18,000–13,000 years ago: soft, about 20 m high, and they fail by rotational slumping.
  • Purbeck: Swanage Bay lies between "two geologically resistant control points", Handfast (chalk) and Peveril Points; the cliffs of the bay itself are "softer".
  • Resistant rock erodes slowly; weak, unconsolidated sediment erodes fast.
Mission 7

Geology: structure

  • Discordant coast: rock bands meet the sea at right angles, so weak bands are cut into bays and resistant bands stand out as headlands (Studland Bay, Ballard Point, Swanage Bay, Durlston Head).
  • Concordant coast: bands run parallel to the coast; a resistant outer band protects the weak rock behind, until it is breached, as at Lulworth Cove.
  • Joints and bedding planes are lines of weakness that the sea exploits.
Quick check
?At Aldbrough on the Holderness coast, cliffs retreated 2.16 m a year between 1951 and 2004. At that rate, how far would they retreat in 50 years?
Mission 7

Climate: wind and fetch

  • The UK's prevailing winds are west to south-westerly.
  • Wave energy grows with wind speed, duration and fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows across).
  • Winter is the windiest time: the jet stream tracks further south, so more Atlantic storms reach the UK.
  • A coast facing strong winds across a long fetch receives bigger, more destructive waves, and erodes faster.
Mission 7

Rivers: seasonal discharge

  • River Tees at Darlington (upland, rainy catchment): mean flow in December 34.5 m³/s, in July 9.1 m³/s (1991–2020 averages, our calculation).
  • River Lambourn at Shaw (chalk, fed by groundwater): 2.7 m³/s in February, 1.1 m³/s in September and October (1991–2019, our calculation).
  • The Lambourn's base flow index is 0.97, so almost all its water comes steadily from groundwater; the Tees's is 0.35.
  • More water means more energy to erode and transport: most river change happens in the wet season.
Mission 7

Extreme events: change in a day

  • Rivers: the Tees's highest recorded flow, 439 m³/s on 5 December 2015 (Storm Desmond), was about 24 times its mean flow. The Lambourn's highest was only about 5.5 times its mean. Large gravel and boulders on a river bed may only move "during rapid flows under flood conditions".
  • Coasts: the winter of 2013/14 was the stormiest on record for the British Isles. Spurn spit was breached by a tidal surge in December 2013, and one storm in 2014 removed up to 10 m of dunes.
  • Extreme events can do years of change in hours.

Mission 7

People manage erosion

  • Coasts: sea walls, rock armour and groynes (barriers across the beach that trap sand moving by longshore drift).
  • Rivers: embankments, straightened channels and bank protection.
  • In England, Shoreline Management Plans decide for each stretch whether to hold the line, advance, realign or take no active intervention.
Mission 7

Unintended consequences

  • Groynes trap sand on one side, so beaches further along the coast are starved and erode faster.
  • At Pagham (West Sussex), groynes at the Hinge "contributed to erosion of East Head".
  • In North Norfolk, the Shoreline Management Plan warns that defending one point would make it an "artificial promontory" that "would deflect much of the active sediment offshore", starving beaches downdrift.
  • On rivers, faster flow in a straightened channel can pass the flood and the erosion downstream.

Rates of change depend on geology (Holderness till erodes at 1–4.5 m a year; even the softer cliffs of Swanage Bay, sheltered between resistant chalk and limestone points, retreat only about 0.2–0.6 m a year), climate (fetch, winter storms, seasonal and extreme river flows) and people (defences that can starve other places of sediment).

Mission 8

The drainage basin

  • A drainage basin is the area of land drained by a river and its tributaries.
  • Watershed: the boundary of a basin, usually along high ground. It separates one basin from the next.
  • Source: where the river starts. Mouth: where it ends, in the sea or a lake.
  • Confluence: where two rivers join. A tributary is a smaller river that joins a larger one.
Quick check
?Two streams meet and flow on as one river. What is the meeting point called?
Mission 8

Inputs, stores and outputs

  • Input: precipitation (rain, snow, hail).
  • Stores: interception (on leaves), surface water (puddles, lakes), soil moisture, groundwater (in rock), the river channel.
  • Outputs: river discharge at the mouth, and evapotranspiration.
  • Evapotranspiration = evaporation from surfaces + transpiration from plants.
Mission 8

Transfers (flows)

  • Infiltration: water soaks into the soil.
  • Overland flow (surface runoff): water flows over the ground.
  • Throughflow: water flows downslope through the soil.
  • Percolation: water seeps down from the soil into the rock.
  • Groundwater flow: water moves slowly through rock to the river.
  • Channel flow: water flows along the river.
Quick check
?Which transfer usually takes longest to carry rainwater to the river?
Mission 8

How the flows depend on each other

  • Interception slows rain down, so more of it can infiltrate.
  • If rain falls faster than the soil can absorb it, the extra becomes overland flow.
  • If the soil is already full (saturated), new rain cannot infiltrate: it runs off as overland flow.
  • Water that does infiltrate moves as throughflow, or percolates deeper.
  • So: less interception or infiltration → more overland flow → water reaches the river faster.
Quick check
?Which conditions would produce the most overland flow?
Mission 8

Where the fresh water is

  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh.
  • Of that fresh water: 68.7% is ice and snow; 30.1% is groundwater.
  • Rivers hold about 0.006%: a tiny store, refilled by much bigger, slower ones.
  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt gave 40.6% of total river flow (1998–2007).
Mission 8

Groundwater keeps rivers flowing

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater gave on average 52% of the flow.
  • UK basin: the River Lambourn, Berkshire, drains chalk, a rock that stores water.
  • About 97% of its long-term flow comes from stored water such as groundwater (base flow index 0.97).
  • So in dry weeks, groundwater flow keeps the river running.
Quick check
?River Lambourn, 1962–2019: rainfall averaged 768 mm a year. River flow equalled 235 mm a year spread over the basin. About what share of the rain left as river flow?
Mission check

?In a long dry summer, a water company pumps much more water out of the rock beneath a river basin. What is most likely to happen to the river?

A river is the smallest, fastest store. Between rains it is fed by slow stores: groundwater flow (52% of flow on average in 54 US streams) and, in mountains, meltwater.

Mission 9

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph shows a river's discharge after a rainfall event. Rainfall is drawn as bars.
  • Rising limb: discharge increases. Peak discharge: the highest flow.
  • Falling limb: discharge drops back.
  • Lag time: the time from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow: the normal, slow flow from groundwater, below the storm water.
Quick check
?What is lag time?
Mission 9

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: basin 277 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234 km².
  • Rain on 23 December 2013: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow jumped from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, its highest daily mean since records began in 1958 (the record has some gaps).
  • Lambourn: 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. After about 317 mm more rain, it peaked at 9.53 m³/s on 15 February 2014.
Quick check
?The Beult's mean flow is 2.126 m³/s. Its peak on 24 December 2013 was 79.4 m³/s. How many times its mean flow was the peak?
Mission 9

Rock type: chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain soaks in, is stored as groundwater, and reaches the river slowly.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Little water can soak in, so it runs quickly to the river over and through the soil.
  • So geology controls the shape of the hydrograph: impermeable rock gives a short lag time and a high peak.
Mission 9

Other physical factors

  • Precipitation: intense or long-lasting rain gives more runoff.
  • Antecedent conditions: rain on already wet ground runs off faster. The Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed water to the river.
  • Basin shape and size: in a small, round basin water arrives together, so the peak is earlier.
  • Snow: a sudden thaw releases stored water quickly; frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission 9

Land use

  • Urban areas: roads, roofs and car parks are impermeable; drains carry water straight to rivers: shorter lag time, higher peak.
  • Forest: leaves intercept rain and roots help water soak in: longer lag time, lower peak.
  • Farmland: bare or compacted soil and field drains speed runoff; crops and grass slow it.
Quick check
?Which basin would have the shortest lag time and highest peak after the same storm?
Mission 9

River regimes

  • A river regime is the pattern of a river's discharge through the year.
  • Beult: highest in January (5.53 m³/s), lowest in July (0.22).
  • Lambourn: highest in March (2.59), lowest in autumn (1.11 in September, 1.10 in October).
  • Monthly rainfall varies far less: 47–84 mm on the Beult; 53–79 mm on the Lambourn.
Mission 9

What shapes a regime

  • Climate: rain is spread through the year in southern England, but higher temperatures in summer mean more evapotranspiration, so summer flows fall.
  • Geology: the chalk aquifer fills in winter and drains slowly, so the Lambourn peaks late, in spring. Clay gives a quick winter peak and very low summer flow.
  • Vegetation: plants intercept and transpire more water in summer, lowering flows.
Quick check
?Why is the Lambourn's highest monthly flow in March, not in winter?

  • Explain using named factors: geology, relief, soils, land use, rainfall.
  • Compare: here rainfall and basin size were similar, so geology made the difference.
Mission check

?Two UK basins of the same size get the same 40 mm storm. Basin X is on granite with thin soils. Basin Y is on sandstone that stores water. Which describes their hydrographs?

Geology. The Beult's clay sent the rain straight to the river (79.4 m³/s, about 37 times its mean). The Lambourn's chalk stored it and released it slowly; its February peak was a seasonal groundwater peak, not a storm lag time.

Mission 10

Why rivers flood: physical causes

  • A flood happens when a river's discharge is more than its channel can hold, so water spills onto land that is usually dry.
  • Intense rain: falls faster than the ground can absorb. Long rain: soaks the ground until it is saturated.
  • Relief: steep slopes send water to the river quickly.
  • Impermeable rock (e.g. clay) stops water soaking in.
Quick check
?Which situation is most likely to cause a river flood without any human cause?
Mission 10

Urbanisation

  • Impermeable surfaces (roofs, roads, car parks) stop rain infiltrating, so more becomes surface runoff.
  • Drains and sewers carry the water straight to the river.
  • Result: shorter lag time, steeper rising limb, higher peak discharge.
  • Building on floodplains also puts more people and property in the way.
Mission 10

Houston, 2017

  • Hurricane Harvey stalled over or near the Texas coast for four days; the highest storm rainfall, in south-east Texas, was about 1,540 mm (60.58 inches).
  • A study in Nature found that the city's growth made the flood response and the rainfall worse.
  • It estimated that urbanisation made such extreme floods in the basins studied about 21 times as likely (25–30 August 2017).
  • One study of one event: strong evidence, but not proof for every city.
Quick check
?A new housing estate is planned upstream of a town. Which design would best stop it raising flood peaks downstream?
Mission 10

Towns in the UK

  • In England, about 4.6 million properties are in areas at risk of flooding from surface water (rain that cannot drain away).
  • The Environment Agency notes that "in some places urban drainage systems do not have the capacity to manage surface water".
  • Paving over gardens and building new estates add impermeable surfaces; building on floodplains raises exposure.
Mission 10

Farming, forests and industry

  • Deforestation: fewer leaves to intercept rain and fewer roots to open the soil, so more runoff and soil erosion. Eroded soil can fill river channels.
  • A review by FAO and CIFOR found that forests can reduce flooding from "frequent, low-intensity, short-duration storms".
  • But "forests cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods": in long rain, forest soils become saturated too.
  • Agriculture: heavy machinery and grazing compact soil; ploughing downslope and field drains speed runoff.
Quick check
?Which statement about forests and floods is best supported by the evidence on the last screen?
Mission check

?Fields upstream of a town are replaced by houses, roads and storm drains that empty into a stream. The same storm now gives a bigger flood. How has the stream's hydrograph changed, and why?

Impermeable surfaces and drains cut infiltration and lag time and raise peak discharge. In Houston, urbanisation also increased the rainfall, making Harvey-scale floods about 21 times as likely.

Mission 11

Where: the Somerset Levels and Moors

  • South-west England. Rivers Parrett and Tone flow across it to the Bristol Channel.
  • Over 60,000 hectares, "very flat", and 18% of Somerset lies below average tide level.
  • Records of flooding go back to the 1600s. Much of the land was drained long ago for farming.
  • When: flooding began in mid-December 2013 and lasted into spring 2014.
Mission 11

Physical cause 1: a winter of storms

  • Mid-December 2013 to mid-February 2014: at least 12 major storms, the stormiest period for at least 20 years.
  • The UK's winter was the wettest on record, with 165% of average rainfall.
  • Each storm alone was not exceptional: "it was the overall totals accumulated … that led to the extensive flooding".
  • So the ground was saturated and rivers stayed full for weeks.
Mission 11

Physical cause 2: flat, low land and the tide

  • On flat land, water flows away slowly.
  • Water leaves through sluices into the tidal river. At high tide the sluice gates must close: water is tide-locked and cannot drain out.
  • Tides also carry silt from the sea up the Parrett and Tone.
  • Pumps cannot help when the river is already full: "Pumping into a full river will cause overtopping".
Quick check
?Why could floodwater on the Levels not simply drain away to the sea at every moment?
Mission 11

Human causes

  • Drained wetland: people farm and live on land that was once marsh.
  • Smaller river channels: silt had built up. Dredging "can restore the system to the channel capacity put in place in the 1960s".
  • Land use upstream: farming affects how fast water and soil reach rivers, though changed land management "would not have lessened the impact" of floods on this scale.
  • Hard surfaces: in towns, rain on roads and roofs "runs off fast and cannot infiltrate".
Mission 11

Impacts on people

  • 172 properties flooded; about 100 million m³ of water covered 65 km².
  • Over 200 homes were cut off, some for more than two months. Residents of Northmoor had to leave their homes.
  • Villages reached only by boat; the South West main line railway was flooded.
  • Transport disruption: impacts estimated "in the region of £100m".
  • Loss of life: no deaths were linked directly to flooding in England's winter 2013–14 floods; storms and high seas did cost lives on the coast.
  • Each flooded property in Somerset cost about £100,000, compared with £23,000 on average nationally, because floods lasted so long.
Mission 11

Impacts on the environment

  • 11,000 hectares of farmland still under water in early 2014, when the Flood Action Plan was written. In Somerset, 57% of the flooded farmland was under water for more than 15 days.
  • Wildlife sites: some 7,000 hectares of wildlife sites in Somerset were flooded or had high water levels.
  • Livestock had to be moved; crops were under water.
  • Pollution risk: sewage could not be treated in some cut-off communities.
Quick check
?Which impact of the 2014 Somerset floods is environmental?
Mission check

?In 2014 more than 100 pumps worked on the Levels, yet some homes were cut off for over two months. Why could pumping not clear the water faster?

The floods lasted so long because a record wet winter fell on flat, low land. Water drains out slowly through silted rivers whose sluices are tide-locked twice a day, so even 100 pumps had to wait for room in full rivers.

Mission 12

Two ways to manage floods

  • Hard engineering: building structures to control the river.
  • Soft engineering: working with natural processes, or changing how people use the land and prepare.
  • Hard schemes usually cost more to build and maintain; soft schemes usually cost less but may protect less.
Mission 12

Hard engineering

  • Dams and reservoirs: store floodwater upstream and release it slowly. Cost: very expensive; they flood land and trap sediment.
  • Straightening (channelisation): water leaves faster. Cost: it can send the flood downstream sooner and harms habitats.
  • Embankments or artificial levees (raised banks) and flood walls: hold more water in the channel. Cost: if they fail or are overtopped, flooding is sudden.
  • Deepening (dredging): more water fits in the channel, but sediment soon refills it.
  • Flood relief channels: carry extra water round a town. Cost: expensive to dig, and they protect only the town they bypass.
Mission 12

A UK flood relief channel: the Jubilee River

  • Built by the Environment Agency and opened in 2002: an 11.6 km man-made channel taking Thames floodwater round Maidenhead, Windsor, Eton and Cookham.
  • Benefit: it reduces flood risk to about 3,000 properties.
  • Limit: it "is not designed to reduce flood risk to communities upstream or downstream".
Quick check
?A council straightens a meandering river through its town. What is the most likely cost?
Mission 12

Soft engineering

  • Flood warnings and preparation (flood plans, flood doors, moving valuables upstairs): cheap; they save lives and some property, but the water still comes.
  • Flood plain zoning (land-use zoning): keep houses off the riskiest land; use it for fields, parks or playing fields. Hard where towns are already built.
  • Planting trees (afforestation): trees intercept rain and soils absorb more. Cost: they take years to grow and the land is lost to farming. Most useful for smaller floods: forests "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Washlands: parts of the floodplain left to flood on purpose, storing water. Cost: that land floods, so it cannot be built on or farmed all year.
  • River restoration: return a straightened river to a natural course, so it floods safe land and slows down. Cost: land beside the river floods more often.
Mission 12

Flood warnings in England

  • Three levels from the Environment Agency:
  • Flood alert: "flooding is possible so you should prepare now".
  • Flood warning: "flooding is expected so you should act now".
  • Severe flood warning: flooding "could cause danger to life".
  • People can sign up to get warnings by phone call, text or email.
Mission 12

A UK soft scheme: Slowing the Flow, Pickering

  • In Pickering, North Yorkshire, the scheme planted 40,000 trees, restored heather moorland and built over 300 leaky dams in streams and drains.
  • It also built an upstream flood storage area (a small reservoir).
  • Over Christmas 2015 it cut the flood flow by 15–20%, about half from the land management and half from the storage area.
  • It "prevented the flooding of a number" of homes and the town's museum.
Mission 12

Zoning and new homes

  • In England, about 6.3 million properties are in areas at risk of flooding; this could reach about 8 million by the middle of the century.
  • Planning rules use a sequential test "to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding".
  • An exception test allows some building at risk only if, among other things, it is safe for its lifetime and does not increase flood risk elsewhere.
  • This is land-use zoning in practice.
Mission 12

Who wants what on a floodplain?

  • Housebuilders and councils: homes are needed, and floodplains are flat and near towns.
  • People already living downstream: worry that new roofs and roads speed runoff towards them.
  • Insurers: Flood Re, the government–insurer scheme, covers homes built before 1 January 2009, so it does not cover newer homes.
  • Wildlife groups: want floodplains kept as wetland.
  • Alternative futures: build with defences · build elsewhere · leave the land as a washland or wetland.
Quick check
?Which measure is soft engineering?
Mission 12

The Dutch answer: Room for the River

  • The floods of 1993 and 1995, when more than 250,000 people had to leave, were "the direct reason" for the Room for the River programme.
  • Aim: "give rivers space again", not only raise dikes.
  • Works at 34 places along the Rhine branches; total cost €2.3 billion.
  • Methods: move dikes back, lower floodplains, dig high-water side channels, return reclaimed land to the river.
Mission check

?A town's river floods every few years. Planners want lower flood levels, no extra flooding downstream and more wildlife habitat. Which plan matches the Dutch approach?

The Dutch gave the rivers room. Dikes were moved back, floodplains lowered and side channels dug at 34 places.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Uplands north and west on hard rocks; lowlands south and east on soft rocks; chalk forms low hills because it is permeable.
  • Major rivers rise in the uplands; the Severn is the UK's longest.
  • Farming (69% of UK land), forestry (14%) and settlement make landscapes distinctive.

Mission 2

  • Glaciated uplands support tourism, farming, forestry and quarrying; these uses conflict with each other and with conservation.
  • Honeypots attract crowds; beyond the carrying capacity, damage follows, so visitors are managed and paths repaired (Fix the Fells).

Mission 3

  • Four erosion and four transport processes; rivers deposit when they lose energy; fine cohesive grains resist erosion.

Mission 4

  • Erosion landforms: V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges; erosion and deposition: meanders with slip-off slopes; deposition: floodplains and levees.

Mission 5

  • Destructive waves erode and constructive waves build. Erosion and slumping wear cliffs back; longshore drift moves the sediment along the coast.
  • Rock type sets the pace: weak till retreats fast; chalk resists.

Mission 6

  • Hard rock makes headlands; soft rock makes bays. Headlands are eroded along joints: cave, arch, stack, stump.
  • Where waves lose energy, sediment builds beaches; where the coast turns, it builds spits.

Mission 7

  • Geology: weak, unconsolidated rock (Holderness till) erodes far faster than resistant chalk and limestone (Purbeck); structure gives discordant headlands and bays or concordant straight coasts.
  • Climate: prevailing wind, fetch, winter storms, seasonal discharge and extreme events (Storm Desmond, the 2013 surge) speed change.
  • People: defences can have unintended consequences, e.g. groynes interrupt longshore drift.

Mission 8

  • A basin is an open system: precipitation in; stores and transfers; discharge and evapotranspiration out.
  • Slow stores (groundwater, ice) keep rivers flowing when it does not rain.

Mission 9

  • Short lag time and high peak: impermeable rock, steep slopes, wet soil, round basins, urban land.
  • Regimes reflect climate, geology and vegetation.

Mission 10

  • Physical causes: intense or long rain, saturated ground, steep relief, impermeable rock.
  • Human causes: urbanisation, deforestation, farming; they shorten lag time and raise peaks.

Mission 11

  • Somerset Levels, winter 2013–14 (Rivers Parrett and Tone). Physical: at least 12 storms, wettest winter on record, flat land below tide level, tide-locking. Human: drained wetland, silted channels, land use upstream.
  • Impacts: 172 properties flooded, over 200 homes cut off for weeks, railway and roads closed, about £100,000 per flooded property; farmland and 7,000 ha of wildlife sites under water; sewage risk.

Mission 12

  • Hard engineering controls the river with structures; soft engineering works with nature and changes land use, warnings and preparation.
