Mission 1

Mission 1: Why did a Galilean teacher keep clashing with Pharisees, priests and Romans?

Make a guess: Why did a Galilean teacher keep clashing with Pharisees, priests and Romans?

Mission 1

A land of client kings

  • 63 BCE: the Roman general Pompey takes Jerusalem. Judea comes under Roman power.
  • Herod the Great rules as a client king for Rome (37–4 BCE); he rebuilds the Temple on a grand scale.
  • After his death the kingdom is divided:
  • Archelaus: Judea and Samaria; deposed in 6 CE, when Judea is put under Roman prefects (Pontius Pilate, 26–36 CE).
  • Herod Antipas: Galilee and Perea (4 BCE–39 CE). Jesus grows up and teaches mostly in his territory; Jesus calls him "that fox" (Luke 13:32).
  • Philip: the north-east.
  • Luke dates John the Baptist's ministry by these rulers (Luke 3:1–2).
Mission 1

Living under Roman occupation

  • Taxes: tribute to Rome, tolls and local taxes, collected through tax collectors (e.g. Levi, Luke 5:27; Zacchaeus, Luke 19:2), who were widely despised.
  • The census of 6 CE (Quirinius) sparks a revolt led by Judas the Galilean (Acts 5:37): paying tribute to Caesar seen as betraying God as king.
  • Temple and high priest: the Romans appoint the high priest (Caiaphas, c. 18–36 CE). The Sanhedrin (council) handles Jewish affairs; Rome keeps power over capital punishment.
  • Crucifixion: a Roman punishment for rebels and slaves, a public warning.
  • Hopes of freedom grow; the Jewish War (66–70 CE) ends with the Temple destroyed in 70 CE, around the time the Gospels were being written.
Mission 1

Hellenism

  • Hellenism: the spread of Greek language, culture and ideas after Alexander the Great conquered the region (332 BCE).
  • Greek (koine) became the common language of the eastern Mediterranean: the New Testament is written in it, and Jews used a Greek translation of scripture, the Septuagint.
  • Greek-style cities: the Decapolis; Sepphoris (rebuilt by Antipas, a few miles from Nazareth); Tiberias.
  • 167 BCE: the Seleucid king Antiochus IV bans Jewish practice and defiles the Temple. The Maccabean revolt follows; the Temple is rededicated in 164 BCE (the origin of Hanukkah).
  • Jews responded to Hellenism along a spectrum: some embraced it, others resisted it fiercely. Several religious groups took shape in that struggle.

Mission 1

The Pharisees

  • A lay movement (not priests), respected by ordinary people; teachers in the synagogues.
  • Kept the written Torah and the oral tradition ("the tradition of the elders", Mark 7:3), applying the law to everyday life.
  • Extended purity rules (washing, tithing, food) beyond the Temple into the home.
  • Believed in the resurrection of the dead, angels and God's providence alongside human freedom.
  • Rabbinic Judaism grew mainly from the Pharisees after 70 CE.
  • In Luke, Pharisees invite Jesus to meals (Luke 7:36; 11:37; 14:1), warn him of danger (13:31) and criticise him (15:2). Paul was a Pharisee (Philippians 3:5).
Mission 1Read the source

The Sadducees

> "For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit; but the Pharisees confess all of these." (Acts 23:8, WEBBE)

  • Linked to the priestly aristocracy and the Temple; wealthy and powerful.
  • Accepted only the written Torah as binding, and rejected the Pharisees' oral tradition.
  • Worked with Rome to keep order and their position; disappeared after the Temple fell in 70 CE.
  • In Luke 20:27–40 they test Jesus with a question about resurrection; Jesus answers from the Torah (Exodus 3:6).

Q1 On which beliefs did Pharisees and Sadducees disagree, according to Acts 23:8?

Show answer

Resurrection, angels and spirits.

Q2 Why would Jesus answer Sadducees from the Torah rather than from the prophets?

Show answer

They accepted only the written Torah as authoritative.

Mission 1

Other groups

  • Scribes: experts in the law; many were Pharisees.
  • Essenes: described by Josephus and Philo; strict purity, community of goods, expectation of the end; rejected the Temple priesthood as corrupt. Most scholars link them with Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls (found from 1947). Not named in the New Testament.
  • "Fourth philosophy" (Josephus): followers of Judas the Galilean, "no king but God". The Zealots as an organised party appear in the Jewish War (66 CE); one disciple is Simon "who was called the Zealot" (Luke 6:15).
  • Herodians: supporters of Herod's family (Mark 3:6; 12:13).
  • Samaritans: worshipped on Mount Gerizim, not in Jerusalem; accepted only their own Torah; long hostility with Jews.
  • "Sinners" and the people of the land: those seen as careless of the law, or outcasts by occupation.

Mission 1

Law, ethics and Jesus

  • Sabbath: debates on what counts as work; Jesus' disciples pick grain and he heals on the Sabbath. "The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath." (Luke 6:5, WEBBE)
  • Purity and table fellowship: "This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them." (Luke 15:2, WEBBE)
  • Rome and tax: "give to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's." (Luke 20:25, WEBBE)
  • Temple: Jesus drives out traders (Luke 19:45–46), a challenge to the priestly authorities.
  • Enemies: "love your enemies" (Luke 6:27) in a land under occupation.
  • Jesus argues within Judaism: he attends synagogue (Luke 4:16), quotes Torah and prophets, and shares the Pharisees' belief in resurrection.
Quick check
?Why was the question "Is it lawful for us to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (Luke 20:22) a trap for Jesus?
Mission 1Diversity

Seeing the groups fairly

  • Most scholars date the Gospels to around or after 70 CE (c. 65–100), when the Church and the synagogue were separating: their portrait of the Pharisees reflects later conflict too.
  • "Pharisee" as a by-word for hypocrite is a Christian caricature; Jewish tradition remembers them as faithful teachers who made the law livable.
  • E. P. Sanders (Paul and Palestinian Judaism, 1977): first-century Judaism was a religion of grace and covenant, not of earning salvation by works (paraphrase).
  • Jesus' sharpest disputes are often with those closest to him in belief.
  • Groups disagreed fiercely but shared the one God, the Torah and the hope of God's kingdom.
Mission 1Evaluate

Religious divisions: problem or enrichment?

  • Claim: Religious divisions in first-century Palestine did more harm than good.
  • Problem: Divisions fed suspicion, violence and the alliance of Temple elite with Rome that led to Jesus' death.
  • Problem: Jewish–Samaritan hostility and contempt for "sinners" excluded many from community.
  • Enrichment: Debate sharpened thinking about the law (Sabbath, purity, love of neighbour); Jesus' teaching is shaped by it.
  • Enrichment: Different hopes (resurrection, the kingdom, purity) gave Christianity its language.
  • Today: Do divisions between and within religions harm, or force deeper ethical thought?
  • Judgement criteria: short- and long-term effects; whose perspective (Jewish, Christian, Roman); whether disagreement can be respectful.
Mission check

?In Luke 10:30–37 a priest and a Levite pass by a wounded man, and a Samaritan helps him. Why would the first Jewish hearers have been surprised by the hero?

Every clash in the Gospels sits on top of older divisions: Rome, Greek culture, the Temple and the law. Knowing them changes how the stories read.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Many Jews hoped for a king like David. Why do the Gospels show a Messiah who suffers and hides?

Make a guess: Many Jews hoped for a king like David. Why do the Gospels show a Messiah who suffers and hides?

Mission 2

"Anointed"

  • Messiah (Hebrew mashiach) and Christ (Greek christos) both mean "anointed".
  • In the Hebrew Bible kings and priests were anointed with oil: King Saul is "the LORD's anointed" (1 Samuel 24:6).
  • So "messiah" first meant someone appointed by God for a task, not a divine figure.
  • By the first century, "the Messiah" could mean a future figure who would bring God's rule. But there was no single expectation.
Mission 2

Many hopes, not one

  • A royal son of David: a king who would drive out foreign rulers and restore Israel (Psalms of Solomon 17, 1st century BCE).
  • A priestly messiah: the Qumran community expected "messiahs of Aaron and Israel", a priest and a king (Community Rule, 1QS IX.11).
  • A prophet like Moses: "The LORD your God will raise up to you a prophet from amongst you" (Deuteronomy 18:15, WEBBE).
  • A heavenly figure: "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds (Daniel 7:13, WEBBE).
  • Some Jewish texts expect God to act directly, with no messiah at all.
  • A suffering and dying Messiah is not clearly expected in pre-Christian Jewish texts.
Mission 2Read the source

The line of David

> "Your house and your kingdom will be made sure forever before you. Your throne will be established forever." (2 Samuel 7:16, WEBBE)

> "A shoot will come out of the stock of Jesse, and a branch out of his roots will bear fruit." (Isaiah 11:1, WEBBE)

  • 2 Samuel 7: the prophet Nathan's promise to David: a dynasty for ever.
  • Isaiah 11: after the dynasty is cut down like a tree, a new king will grow from the stump of Jesse, David's father.
  • Micah 5:2 links the future ruler with Bethlehem, David's town.

Q1 Why did the promise in 2 Samuel 7:16 create hope after 586 BCE, when there was no Davidic king?

Show answer

God had promised the throne "forever", so a future son of David was expected.

Q2 What does the image of a shoot from a stump suggest?

Show answer

New life after the dynasty seemed dead.

Mission 2

Son of David in the Gospels

  • Matthew opens: "Jesus Christ, the son of David" (Matthew 1:1, WEBBE); Matthew and Luke both give genealogies through David (they differ: Matthew 1:1–17; Luke 3:23–38).
  • Both place Jesus' birth in Bethlehem; Joseph is "of the house of David".
  • Blind Bartimaeus cries "Jesus, you son of David" (Mark 10:47, WEBBE); crowds hail Jesus as he enters Jerusalem.
  • But Jesus also questions the title: David calls the Messiah "Lord", so how can he be merely his son? (Mark 12:35–37).
  • Significance: the Gospels claim the Davidic promise, but redefine the kind of king: no army, no throne, a cross.

Mission 2Read the source

The Suffering Servant

  • Bernhard Duhm (1892) identified four Servant Songs in Isaiah: 42:1–4; 49:1–6; 50:4–9; 52:13–53:12.

> "But he was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities. The punishment that brought our peace was on him; and by his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5, WEBBE)

> "He said to me, 'You are my servant, Israel, in whom I will be glorified.'" (Isaiah 49:3, WEBBE)

  • The Servant is rejected, suffers for others and is vindicated by God.

Q1 Who does Isaiah 49:3 name as the servant?

Show answer

Israel.

Q2 What is new about the suffering in Isaiah 53:5 compared with ordinary punishment?

Show answer

It is borne on behalf of others: vicarious suffering that brings healing.

Mission 2Diversity

Who is the Servant?

  • Jewish interpretation: most often Israel, the faithful people who suffer among the nations (e.g. Rashi, 1040–1105); others proposed the prophet himself. An Aramaic paraphrase (the Targum) applies 52:13 to the Messiah, but turns the suffering onto Israel's enemies.
  • Christian interpretation: Jesus. Philip explains Isaiah 53 to the Ethiopian official as being about Jesus (Acts 8:32–35); at the Last Supper Jesus quotes Isaiah 53:12, "He was counted with transgressors" (Luke 22:37, WEBBE).
  • Morna Hooker (b. 1931), Jesus and the Servant (1959): the link between Jesus' own words and Isaiah 53 is much weaker than often claimed; sayings such as Mark 10:45 do not clearly echo it. The identification was developed mainly by the early Church (paraphrase).
  • Others (e.g. Joachim Jeremias) argued that Jesus himself saw his death in the light of Isaiah 53.

Mission 2

The messianic secret

> "He commanded them that they should tell no one about him." (Mark 8:30, WEBBE)

  • In Mark, Jesus silences demons (1:34; 3:12), the healed (1:44) and disciples (8:30; 9:9); parables hide the kingdom from outsiders (4:11–12).
  • William Wrede, The Messianic Secret (1901): this is a theological device, not history. The Church came to believe Jesus was Messiah after the resurrection; the "secret" explains why he was not recognised before (note Mark 9:9: "until after the Son of Man had risen").
  • Historical reading: Jesus avoided the title because people would hear "warrior king" (compare John 6:15).
  • Hooker (The Gospel According to St Mark, 1991): the secret belongs to Mark's theology: who Jesus is cannot be understood until the cross and resurrection; the reader is told from 1:1, but the characters must learn (paraphrase).
  • The secret keeps "leaking": the healed man spreads the news (1:45).
Mission 2

Matthew's proof texts

  • Matthew 1:18–2:23 (anthology extract 1) has five fulfilment quotations, e.g. "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet" (Matthew 1:22, WEBBE):
  • 1:23 ← Isaiah 7:14 (virgin, Immanuel) · 2:6 ← Micah 5:2 (Bethlehem) · 2:15 ← Hosea 11:1 (out of Egypt) · 2:18 ← Jeremiah 31:15 (Rachel weeping) · 2:23 ← "he will be called a Nazarene" (no exact source).
  • Raymond E. Brown (1928–1998), The Birth of the Messiah (1977; updated 1993): Matthew probably added these citations to stories he received, to show that Jesus' identity and origins fulfil scripture (paraphrase).
  • Isaiah 7:14: the Hebrew 'almah means "young woman"; the Greek Septuagint has parthenos ("virgin"), which Matthew follows. In context it is a sign to King Ahaz (c. 735 BCE). Brown: belief in the virginal conception led Matthew to the text, rather than the text creating the belief.
  • Hosea 11:1 originally recalls Israel's Exodus; Matthew reads Jesus as reliving Israel's story (and Herod as a new Pharaoh).

Mission 2Evaluate

Did expectations shape the story, or the story reshape expectations?

  • Claim: The Gospel writers shaped their accounts of Jesus to fit messianic expectations.
  • For: Matthew's proof texts sometimes use verses against their original sense (Hosea 11:1; "Nazarene").
  • For: Wrede: the messianic secret is a later theological construct.
  • Against: No one expected a crucified Messiah: "Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews" (1 Corinthians 1:23, WEBBE). A story built to fit expectations would not end this way.
  • Against: The Gospels redefine messiahship (Davidic king + suffering Servant + Son of Man) rather than simply matching it.
  • Scholars: Brown (Matthew interprets traditions he received); Hooker (secret and Servant as the early Church's and Mark's interpretation).
  • Judgement criteria: the original meaning of the texts; the date and purpose of each Gospel; whether interpretation is the same as invention.
Mission check

?In Luke 24:25–27 two disappointed disciples meet the risen Jesus, who asks: "Didn't the Christ have to suffer these things and to enter into his glory?" (Luke 24:26, WEBBE). He then explains "Moses and all the prophets" to them. What does this scene suggest about how the first Christians came to speak of a suffering Messiah?

A crucified Messiah was not what most people expected. The Gospel writers drew on old hopes (the son of David, the Servant, the Son of Man) and reshaped them around the cross.

Mission 3: Matthew says Jesus' birth "fulfilled" five old prophecies. What did he mean by fulfilled?

Make a guess: Matthew says Jesus' birth "fulfilled" five old prophecies. What did he mean by fulfilled?

Mission 3

Matthew's story in five scenes

  • Matthew opens with a genealogy (1:1–17): Jesus is "the son of David, the son of Abraham".
  • Then five scenes (1:18–2:23), told from Joseph's side:
  • 1. Joseph's dream: Mary's child is "of the Holy Spirit"; name him Jesus (1:18–25).
  • 2. Magi from the east follow a star, visit Herod, worship the child in a house in Bethlehem (2:1–12).
  • 3. Flight to Egypt (2:13–15).
  • 4. Herod kills the boys of Bethlehem (2:16–18).
  • 5. Return after Herod's death; the family settles in Nazareth (2:19–23).
  • Each scene is tied to an Old Testament text.
Mission 3

The fulfilment formula

  • Matthew uses a set phrase: "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet".
  • Scholars call these formula quotations (or fulfilment citations); in textbooks, proof texts.
  • About ten in the Gospel; five in chapters 1–2.
  • They are the narrator's comment, not words of Jesus or of the characters (except 2:6, spoken by the chief priests and scribes).
  • Purpose: to show that Jesus' life completes Israel's scripture.
  • This is the impact of messianic expectation on the text: Matthew presents Jesus as the expected son of David and as the one in whom scripture is fulfilled.
Mission 3Read the source

"God with us"

> "Now all this has happened that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet, saying," (Matthew 1:22, WEBBE)

> "Behold, the virgin shall be with child, and shall give birth to a son. They shall call his name Immanuel" (Matthew 1:23, WEBBE), "which is, being interpreted, God with us."

> "Therefore the Lord himself will give you a sign. Behold, the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." (Isaiah 7:14, WEBBE)

> "behold, the young woman shall conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." (Isaiah 7:14, JPS 1917, translated from the Hebrew)

  • Isaiah spoke to King Ahaz (c. 735 BC), as a sign that his enemies would soon fall.
  • The Hebrew word is 'almah (young woman); the Greek Septuagint has parthenos (virgin). Matthew quotes the Greek. Christian translations such as WEBBE keep "virgin" in Isaiah; the Jewish JPS translation follows the Hebrew.

Q1 Which word in Matthew's version links the birth to the idea of incarnation?

Show answer

"Immanuel", "God with us".

Q2 Why do scholars say Isaiah 7:14 had a meaning of its own before Matthew?

Show answer

It was a sign for Ahaz in his own crisis.

Mission 3

The five proof texts

  • 1:23 ← Isaiah 7:14: born of a virgin; Immanuel.
  • 2:6 ← Micah 5:2: a ruler from Bethlehem, David's town.
  • 2:15 ← Hosea 11:1: "Out of Egypt I called my son". In Hosea, the son is Israel at the Exodus.
  • 2:18 ← Jeremiah 31:15: Rachel weeping for her children; in Jeremiah, Israel's exiles.
  • 2:23 ← "he will be called a Nazarene": no Old Testament verse says this. Suggested sources: netser ("branch", Isaiah 11:1) or nazir (Nazirite, Judges 13:5).
  • Pattern: most texts in chapter 2 are about places (Bethlehem, Egypt, Ramah, Nazareth).

Mission 3

Why Matthew uses them

  • Audience: most scholars think Matthew wrote for a largely Jewish-Christian community, debating with the synagogue: scripture was the shared authority.
  • Jesus as son of David: genealogy, Bethlehem (Micah 5:2).
  • Jesus as a new Moses / new Israel: a wicked king kills baby boys (Pharaoh, Exodus 1:22); the child escapes and returns "for those who sought the young child's life are dead" (Matthew 2:20), echoing Exodus 4:19.
  • Gentiles worship: the magi look ahead to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Raymond E. Brown (1928–1998), The Birth of the Messiah (1977; 2nd ed. 1993): the citations are Matthew's own additions to stories he received. Chapter 1 answers who Jesus is; chapter 2 where he comes from (an idea from Krister Stendahl, 1960). Brown is one of your named scholars for 1.1; Hooker's work on the Servant is in Mission 2.
  • Matthew 1:18–2:23 is your set extract (1) for this topic: an exam question may print part of it.
Mission 3Evaluate

Do the proof texts show prophecy fulfilled?

  • Claim: Matthew's proof texts show that Jesus' birth fulfilled Old Testament prophecy.
  • For: "Fulfil" can mean completing a pattern, not only predicting: Israel's story (Egypt, exile) is relived in Jesus. On this reading Hosea and Jeremiah fit.
  • For: Christians read all scripture as pointing to Christ (Luke 24:27); Jewish interpreters of the time also applied texts to their own day (e.g. the pesher commentaries at Qumran).
  • Against: Read in context, the texts had other first meanings (Ahaz's sign; the Exodus; the exile).
  • Against: 2:23 has no clear source; Isaiah 7:14 depends on the Greek parthenos.
  • Against: Some argue texts may have shaped the stories; Brown thinks the fit is often too awkward for that, so the stories came first.
  • Judgement criteria: What does "fulfil" mean (prediction or pattern)? Should Matthew be judged by modern or first-century methods of reading scripture? Does the theology depend on the history?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Under Rome: Herod's family as client rulers; prefects such as Pilate in Judea; taxes, a Rome-appointed high priest and the threat of revolt.
  • Hellenism brought Greek language and cities, and a long struggle over Jewish identity.
  • Pharisees (oral law, resurrection, popular) and Sadducees (Temple, written Torah only, no resurrection) differed sharply; Essenes, Zealots, Herodians and Samaritans add to a divided landscape in which Jesus taught.

Mission 2

  • "Messiah" means "anointed"; first-century hopes varied: Davidic king, priest, prophet, heavenly figure.
  • The Gospels claim the line of David, read Isaiah's Suffering Servant as Jesus, and (in Mark) show a messianic secret (Wrede: a theological device; Hooker: understood only through the cross).
  • Matthew's fulfilment quotations (Brown) show Jesus' identity and origins fulfilling scripture, often by typology rather than prediction.

Mission 3

  • Matthew 1:18–2:23 is told from Joseph's side; each scene is tied to an Old Testament text, four of them by Matthew's fulfilment formula.
  • The five proof texts (Isaiah 7:14, Micah 5:2, Hosea 11:1, Jeremiah 31:15, "Nazarene") show Jesus as Immanuel, son of David and new Israel; "fulfil" can mean completing a pattern, not only predicting.
