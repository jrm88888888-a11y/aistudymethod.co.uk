Mission 1

Mission 1: Is a cold, wet week in July weather or climate, and how would you measure it?

Make a guess: Is a cold, wet week in July weather or climate, and how would you measure it?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.1 distinguish between weather and climate
  • 1C.1 measuring weather: temperature (digital thermometer, °C), precipitation (rain gauge, mm), wind direction (wind vane, eight compass points), wind speed (anemometer, knots), pressure (barometer, mb), cloud types (stratus, cumulus, cumulonimbus, cirrus), cloud cover (observation, oktas)
Mission 1

Weather or climate?

  • Weather: the state of the atmosphere over hours or days.
  • Climate: "the general weather over a long period", usually 30 years.
  • Climate includes averages, extremes and how often things happen.
  • "Climate is what we expect, weather is what we get".
Quick check
?Which statement describes climate?
Mission 1

Temperature: thermometer, °C

  • Measured with a digital thermometer in degrees Celsius (°C). The Met Office uses an electronic (platinum resistance) thermometer.
  • Kept in a Stevenson screen: white, louvred, door facing north, so no direct sun reaches it; the thermometer sits 1.25 m above the ground.
  • Site in the open, away from buildings and trees, which warm or shade the readings.
  • Daily maximum and minimum are read for 0900 to 0900.
Mission 1

Precipitation: rain gauge, mm

  • A rain gauge is a funnel over a container; collected water is measured in millimetres (mm).
  • The standard manual gauge has its rim 30 cm above the ground and is read daily at 0900.
  • Automatic gauges record each 0.2 mm of rain.
  • Rain gauges catch rain, not snow: snow blocks the funnel.
Mission 1

Wind: wind vane and anemometer

  • Wind vane → direction, given as one of eight compass points (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW).
  • Wind is named from where it blows: a westerly blows from the west.
  • Anemometer → speed, in knots (1 knot = 1.15 mph). Spinning cups turn faster in stronger wind.
  • Both sit on a 10 m mast, away from obstacles.
  • A gale is a mean wind of 34–40 knots.
Quick check
?Air is moving from the south-west towards the north-east. What does the weather station record?
Mission 1

Pressure: barometer, mb

  • A barometer measures atmospheric pressure in millibars (mb).
  • UK average at sea level: about 1013 mb; rarely above 1050 or below 950.
  • Low pressure: rising air cools, forming cloud and rain. High pressure: sinking air, settled weather.
Mission 1

Mission 1

Cloud cover: oktas

  • Cloud cover is observed by eye, as the eighths of sky covered: oktas.
  • 0 oktas = no cloud; 8 oktas = completely covered; 9 = sky hidden by fog.
  • Cloud type and total amount still need a human observer: no automatic sensor can identify cloud type.
Quick check
?A tall, towering cloud with a flat, spreading top brings hail and thunder. Which type is it?
Quick check
?Which instrument is matched to the correct unit?
Mission check

?A student's rain gauge collects 40 mm this March, more than in any other month so far this year. She writes: "March is my town's wettest month." Which correction is best?

A cold, wet week in July is weather; you would record it with a thermometer (°C), rain gauge (mm), wind vane and anemometer (knots), barometer (mb) and cloud observations (type and oktas). Thirty years of those records make climate.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can two places the same distance from the equator have completely different climates?

Make a guess: Why can two places the same distance from the equator have completely different climates?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.2 factors affecting climate: latitude, prevailing winds, distance from the sea, altitude
Mission 2

Weather or climate?

  • Weather: the state of the atmosphere at one time and place (today's rain).
  • Climate: the average weather of a place over a long period, usually 30 years.
  • CCEA's four factors that affect climate:
    • latitude
    • prevailing winds
    • distance from the sea
    • altitude.
Mission 2

Latitude

  • Latitude: distance north or south of the equator, in degrees.
  • The equator receives more direct sunlight; the poles receive less.
  • Near the equator the Sun is high, so its energy is concentrated on a small area.
  • Near the poles the Sun is low, so the same energy is spread over a larger area and passes through more atmosphere.
  • So temperatures generally fall with distance from the equator.
Quick check
?Why is it colder near the poles than at the equator?
Mission 2

Prevailing winds

  • The prevailing wind is the direction the wind blows from most often.
  • Air takes on the temperature and moisture of the surface it crosses.
  • Over the UK and Ireland, surface winds "generally" come "from the west or southwest".
  • These winds cross the Atlantic, so they bring mild, moist air and rain-bearing low-pressure systems.
Mission 2

Winds carry the ocean's warmth

  • The Gulf Stream is "the main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic".
  • The ocean warms the air above it; westerly winds then carry that warmth onto the land.
  • Researchers found that winds are the main reason winters are milder on the European side of the Atlantic than at the same latitude in eastern North America; the ocean's heat transport adds "a few degC" to winters on both sides.
  • Places where the prevailing wind blows from a cold land mass instead get cold, dry winters.
Mission 2

Distance from the sea

  • Water heats up and cools down more slowly than land.
  • Coastal (maritime) climate: the sea keeps summers cooler and winters milder, so the annual temperature range is small. Often wetter.
  • Inland (continental) climate: far from the sea, land heats fast in summer and cools fast in winter, so the range is large. Often drier.
  • This is called the continentality effect.
Quick check
?Two towns are at the same latitude and height. Town P is on a coast; town Q is 2,000 km inland. Which is most likely true?
Mission 2

Altitude

  • Altitude: height above sea level.
  • In the lowest layer of the atmosphere, temperature "decreases linearly" with height.
  • A standard model of the atmosphere uses a fall of about 6.5 °C for every 1,000 m (0.00649 °C per metre).
  • Why: the air is heated mainly from the ground below, and air that rises expands in the lower pressure higher up, which cools it.
  • Mountains also force air to rise, cool and form cloud, so uplands are usually wetter (relief rainfall).
Quick check
?A town at sea level is 15 °C. Using a fall of 6.5 °C per 1,000 m, what is the likely temperature on a summit 1,200 m above it?
Mission 2

Putting it together: Northern Ireland

  • Latitude: in the mid-latitudes, so cool summers and no extreme heat.
  • Prevailing winds: westerly and south-westerly winds off the Atlantic bring mild, moist air and frequent rain.
  • Distance from the sea: no place in Northern Ireland is far from the coast, so winters are mild and the annual range is small: a maritime climate.
  • Altitude: upland areas such as the Mournes and Sperrins are cooler and wetter than the lowlands around Lough Neagh.
Mission 2

Explaining a climate

  • Exam move: for each factor give factor → process → effect.
  • Example: "The prevailing south-westerly wind (factor) crosses the warm Atlantic (process), so winters are mild and wet (effect)."
  • Name the factor, explain why it works, then say what it does to temperature or rainfall.
Mission check

?Two places lie at the same latitude. Place X is a coastal lowland where the prevailing wind blows in from a warm ocean. Place Y is far inland at 1,000 m above sea level. Which is the most likely difference?

Latitude sets the basic energy a place gets; prevailing winds, distance from the sea and altitude then change it. That is why two places at the same latitude can have very different climates.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can an easterly wind bring snow to Britain in February but a heatwave in July?

Make a guess: Why can an easterly wind bring snow to Britain in February but a heatwave in July?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.3 temperature, moisture characteristics and seasonal variation of tropical maritime, tropical continental, polar maritime and polar continental air masses affecting the British Isles
Mission 3

What is an air mass?

  • A huge body of air with nearly the same temperature and humidity throughout.
  • It can cover several million km².
  • It gets its character from its source region, where it sits still for days: warming air by 10 °C can take "a week or more".
  • When it moves, it carries that weather to us.
Mission 3

Reading the name

  • Polar (P) = from cold regions → cold. Tropical (T) = from warm regions → warm.
  • Maritime (m) = over the sea → moist. Continental (c) = over land → dry.
  • Air crossing the sea picks up moisture from evaporation; air crossing land stays dry.
  • So: Tm, Tc, Pm, Pc. (Arctic maritime and returning polar maritime also reach us; you do not need them here.)
Quick check
?An air mass is labelled Pc. What does the label tell you about it?
Mission 3

Tropical maritime (Tm)

  • Source: warm Atlantic between the Azores and Bermuda; arrives on south-westerly winds.
  • Warm (near sea temperature) and very moist.
  • Cooled from below over colder sea → low cloud, drizzle, and fog on windward coasts and hills.
  • Winter: mild, raising temperatures "several degrees above the average". Summer: warm; can be sunny in the shelter of hills.
Mission 3

Polar maritime (Pm)

  • Source: northern Canada and Greenland; arrives on north-westerly winds.
  • The most common air mass over the British Isles.
  • Starts very cold and dry, but warms and gains moisture crossing the Atlantic → cool and moist.
  • Showers at any time of year.
  • Winter: rather cold; heavy showers, hail and thunder in the west and north. Summer: cool; heaviest showers over eastern England.
Quick check
?Which air mass most often affects the British Isles?
Mission 3

Tropical continental (Tc)

  • Source: North Africa and the Sahara; arrives on south-easterly winds.
  • Summer (most common June–August): very warm or hot, relatively dry; often over 30 °C by day.
  • Haze: visibility moderate or poor from pollution and Saharan dust.
  • The record temperatures of August 2003 came in Tc air.
  • Winter: harder to identify; average temperatures, rather moist and usually cloudy.
Mission 3

Polar continental (Pc)

  • Source: snowfields of Eastern Europe and Russia; arrives on easterly winds, with high pressure over Scandinavia.
  • A winter air mass only (November to April). In summer, that land is warm, so the air would be classed as tropical continental.
  • Very cold and dry: often below −10 °C at night.
  • Short crossing of the English Channel → clear skies, severe frost. Long crossing of the North Sea → moisture added, snow showers near the east coast.
Mission 3

Seasons at a glance

Quick check
?It is a mild January day in Northern Ireland. A south-westerly wind brings low cloud, drizzle and fog over the hills. Which air mass is this?
Mission check

?In July, the wind over Belfast swings from south-east to north-west. How is the weather most likely to change?

An easterly in February brings polar continental air from frozen Eastern Europe. In summer that land is warm, so air from the east counts as tropical continental, like the hot air that arrives from North Africa on south-easterly winds. Winds from the east can bring very different air in different seasons.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why is British weather so changeable, yet sometimes settled for weeks on end?

Make a guess: Why is British weather so changeable, yet sometimes settled for weeks on end?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.3 weather sequence of a frontal depression crossing the British Isles (warm front, warm sector, cold front); winter and summer anticyclone weather
Mission 4

High and low pressure

  • Air pressure is measured in millibars (mb) or hectopascals; standard sea-level pressure is 1013.
  • Isobars join places of equal pressure on a weather chart.
  • Low pressure (depression): air rises, cools and forms cloud and rain → unsettled weather.
  • High pressure (anticyclone): air sinks, so few clouds form → settled weather.
  • Winds blow anticlockwise around a low and clockwise around a high in the northern hemisphere. Close isobars mean strong winds.
Mission 4

Weather fronts

  • A front is the boundary between two air masses with different temperature and moisture.
  • Warm front: red line with semicircles. Warm air rises over colder air.
  • Cold front: blue line with triangles. Cold air pushes under warmer air.
  • Occluded front: purple line; forms when the cold front catches up with the warm front.
  • Most depressions reaching the UK form over the Atlantic and move west to east.
Quick check
?On a weather chart, a blue line with triangles is moving east across Northern Ireland. What is happening?
Mission 4

Inside a frontal depression

  • Low pressure at the centre; air spirals in anticlockwise and rises.
  • Warm front in front; cold front behind.
  • Between them is the warm sector: a wedge of warm, moist air.
  • The whole system moves from west to east, so a place in the UK gets the warm front first, then the warm sector, then the cold front.
Mission 4

1. The warm front arrives

  • High, wispy cirrus cloud "often form[s] in advance of a warm front".
  • Cloud thickens and lowers: cirrostratus, then altostratus, which "deepens and bulks out to become nimbostratus".
  • Steady, persistent rain for several hours "until the associated front passes over": the warm air rises gradually over a wide area.
  • Pressure falls as the depression approaches.
  • After the front passes, it turns milder: "warm air follows a warm front".
Mission 4

2. The warm sector

  • Mild air and often low cloud.
  • "The area between these fronts, called the 'warm sector', often brings low cloud and patchy light rain".
  • Rain may ease to drizzle; winds are steady.
Mission 4

3. The cold front, then clearer

  • Warm air is forced up quickly over the incoming cold air, forming tall cumulonimbus clouds.
  • Heavy, short-lived showers, strong gusty winds, sometimes thunder.
  • After the front: colder air, as "cold air follows a cold front"; skies clear, with scattered showers.
  • Pressure rises again after the cold front.
  • Exam move: describe each zone in order (warm front → warm sector → cold front) using cloud, precipitation, temperature and wind.
Quick check
?Which order of weather would someone in Belfast most likely see as a depression passes?
Mission 4

Winter depressions: 2013/14

  • In winter the jet stream is further south, so "unsettled conditions affect more of the UK".
  • Winter 2013/14: a "large number of low pressure centres" reached the UK, often "in rapid succession".
  • It was the wettest winter in the records: 165% of average precipitation for the UK.
  • It was mild (about 1.5 °C above average) and possibly the stormiest on record for the British Isles.
  • Flooding included the Somerset Levels.
Mission 4

Anticyclones

  • Sinking air → little cloud → dry, settled weather.
  • Light winds, blowing clockwise; isobars are far apart.
  • A high can become stationary and "keep weather fronts at bay": a block.
  • So high pressure can last for days or weeks.
Mission 4

Summer anticyclones

  • "Fine, warm weather with long sunny days and dry spells".
  • Hazards: heatwaves, droughts, poor air quality, high pollen counts.
  • Summer 2003: a "long-lasting high pressure system" reduced rainfall across Europe.
  • In the UK there were about 2,000 heat-related deaths, and hosepipe bans were brought in.
Mission 4

Winter anticyclones

  • "Cold, dry days with light winds" and "severe nighttime frosts".
  • Frost and fog are common hazards: clear skies let heat escape at night.
  • Sometimes a layer of stratocumulus cloud traps the gloom and keeps days cold.
  • December 1952, London: an anticyclone trapped smoke near the ground under an inversion. About 4,000 people were known to have died.
Quick check
?In January, a high-pressure system sits over Ireland for a week. Which weather is most likely?
Mission check

?A weather station in Belfast records this over one day: high thin cloud that thickens; steady rain for six hours; mild air with drizzle; then a heavy shower with gusty wind; then colder, clearer air. The next week a high settles over the UK. Which explains both parts?

British weather is changeable because Atlantic depressions, with their warm and cold fronts, cross the country from west to east. It turns settled when an anticyclone (sinking air) sits over the UK and blocks the fronts, sometimes for weeks.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If no one can see the future, how can a forecaster say it will rain tomorrow but not next month?

Make a guess: If no one can see the future, how can a forecaster say it will rain tomorrow but not next month?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.1 sources of data for a weather forecast: on land (land-based stations, rainfall radar), in the air (geostationary and polar satellites), at sea (buoys)
  • 1C.3 interpret synoptic charts and satellite images; limitations of forecasting: range and accuracy
Mission 5

Every forecast starts with now

  • "Accurate forecasts begin with knowing what the weather is doing right now".
  • Weather centres collect millions of observations every day: temperature, rain, pressure, wind, humidity.
  • Data come from land, air and sea, then go into supercomputers that calculate how the weather will change.
Mission 5

On land: weather stations

  • The Met Office runs about 300 land observing stations across the UK.
  • Main (synoptic) stations are about 40 km apart and report every hour, mostly automatically.
  • They measure pressure, temperature, humidity, wind, rainfall, cloud and visibility.
  • An even larger network of rain gauges helps with flood forecasting.
Mission 5

On land: rainfall radar

  • About 15 weather radars cover the UK.
  • A radar sends out pulses and listens for echoes from raindrops.
  • Each radar scans every 5 minutes: good rainfall data to about 75 km, useful data to 255 km.
  • Images show where it is raining, how hard, and which way the rain is moving, at 1 km detail.
Quick check
?A forecaster needs to know, right now, how fast a band of heavy rain is moving across Northern Ireland. Which source is best?
Mission 5

In the air: satellites

  • Geostationary satellites sit 35,786 km above the Equator and stay over the same spot.
  • They image the whole visible disc of Earth about every 10–15 minutes: ideal for watching systems move.
  • Polar satellites orbit about 800 km up, passing near both poles.
  • They are lower, so they give more detail, and cover the whole globe, but see each place only about twice a day.
Mission 5

At sea: buoys and ships

  • Most UK weather systems form over the Atlantic and move west to east.
  • Few people live out there, so instruments do: weather buoys moored in the deep North Atlantic.
  • They send air and sea temperature, wind speed and wave height to the Met Office every hour.
  • Ships and moored light vessels carry weather stations too.
Quick check
?Why do forecasters need both geostationary and polar satellites?
Mission 5

Reading a synoptic chart

  • Isobars join places of equal pressure, in millibars (mb).
  • Closer isobars = stronger winds; wind blows almost along the isobars.
  • In the northern hemisphere, wind circles anticlockwise round a low and clockwise round a high.
  • Fronts: semicircles = warm front, triangles = cold front; the symbols point the way the front is moving.
  • Fronts bring cloud and rain; the warm sector between them "often brings low cloud and patchy light rain".
Mission 5

Reading a satellite image

  • On a satellite image, cloud shows white or grey; land and sea show darker.
  • A swirl of cloud marks a depression; a long band marks a front; speckled cloud behind it means showers.
  • Clear skies over a wide area suggest high pressure: sinking air "reduces cloud formation".
  • Limits: high cloud can hide low cloud; fog and low cloud look alike; satellites cannot measure cloud base.
Quick check
?A chart shows closely spaced isobars over Scotland and a cold front crossing Ireland towards the east. What should the forecast for Scotland say?
Mission 5

Limitation 1: range

  • The atmosphere is chaotic: "very small errors in its initial state can lead to large errors in the forecast".
  • We can never measure every detail now, so there is "always a limit to how far ahead we can predict any detail".
  • Today's computer forecasts are skilful about a week ahead.
  • Skill has grown by about one extra day per decade.
  • Ensembles: the model is run many times from slightly different starts; if the runs agree, confidence is high; if they spread out, it is low.
Mission 5

Limitation 2: accuracy

  • Gaps in observations, such as remote oceans, must be filled by estimates from the model.
  • Radar accuracy falls with distance from the radar.
  • Local detail is hard: a station on a mountain or in a city measures only very local conditions.
  • Small, short-lived features, such as a single shower, can be missed or put in the wrong place.
Quick check
?Why is a forecast for 30 days ahead much less reliable than one for 2 days ahead?
Mission check

?A forecaster runs the computer model 18 times, each from slightly different starting measurements. For Thursday, every run shows heavy rain. For ten days ahead, half the runs show rain and half show sunshine. What should the forecast say?

Tomorrow's rain is already forming upwind, where stations, radar, satellites and buoys can measure it; further ahead, tiny errors in that picture grow, the runs spread out, and forecasts lose skill after about a week.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Make a guess: Hurricane Ian hit Florida with 130-knot winds in 2022. Why was water, not wind, its deadliest hazard?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 1C.4 impacts of extreme weather on people and property, case study of a hurricane outside the British Isles
Mission 6

One storm, three names

  • A tropical cyclone is "a rapidly rotating storm that begins over tropical oceans".
  • It is called a hurricane in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, a typhoon in the north-west Pacific, and a cyclone in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific.
Mission 6

Where and when

  • They form over tropical oceans, between about 25°S and 25°N.
  • They do not form within about 480 km (300 miles) of the equator: there is too little spin there.
  • Main seasons: July–October in the Northern Hemisphere, December–March in the Southern; globally, September is the most active month.
  • The north-west Pacific has them all year round. Over cool water or land, they weaken.
Quick check
?Why do tropical storms almost never form right on the equator, even where the sea is warm?
Mission 6

Why tropical storms are hazards

  • High winds destroy buildings, trees and power lines and turn debris into missiles.
  • Intense rain causes river and flash flooding inland.
  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm" above the normal tide, driven mainly by strong onshore winds. It is highest where the sea floor slopes gently. It causes coastal flooding.
  • Landslides: rain soaks steep slopes. Hurricane Maria (2017) triggered more than 40,000 landslides in Puerto Rico.
Mission 6

Hurricane Ian, 2022: Florida

  • Ian intensified rapidly over water of 30 °C and above, with low wind shear (winds at different heights were similar).
  • 28 September: it peaked at 140 kt (category 5), then hit Cayo Costa, Florida, at 130 kt (category 4).
  • Storm surge (sea water piled up by onshore winds) flooded Fort Myers Beach to 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground.
  • In Lee County, at least 52,514 buildings were damaged, 5,369 of them destroyed.
  • At least 156 people died in the US, 66 directly from the storm: surge killed 41, freshwater flooding 12 and wind 4.
Mission 6

Hurricane Ian: Cuba

  • 27 September: Ian hit western Cuba as a category 3 hurricane (110 kt).
  • Cuba reported 5 deaths, 3 of them caused directly by the storm.
  • More than 100,000 homes were damaged or destroyed, and the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.
  • The US damage was estimated at $112.9 billion; there is no dollar estimate for Cuba, so losses cannot be compared in money.
  • Exam move: describe impacts on people (deaths, homes, power) and on property, and say where data are missing.
Quick check
?Of Ian's 66 direct deaths in the US, 41 were caused by storm surge. What percentage is that, to the nearest whole number?
Mission check

?A category 3 hurricane is heading for a low-lying coast with a wide, gently sloping sea floor and a bay that narrows inland. Which hazard is likely to kill most people?

Ian's onshore winds drove a storm surge 3–4.6 m (10–15 feet) above ground at Fort Myers Beach. Surge caused 41 of the 66 direct deaths in the US (62%); wind caused 4.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Weather = hours to days; climate = average weather over about 30 years.
  • Thermometer °C · rain gauge mm · wind vane 8 compass points · anemometer knots · barometer mb · cloud type (cirrus, cumulus, cumulonimbus, stratus) · cloud cover oktas.

Mission 2

  • Latitude: lower sun angle towards the poles → colder. Altitude: about 6.5 °C cooler per 1,000 m.
  • Prevailing winds bring the temperature and moisture of where they come from; distance from the sea decides maritime (small range) or continental (large range) climates.

Mission 3

  • Air masses carry the temperature (polar/tropical) and moisture (maritime/continental) of their source region.
  • Tm mild and moist (south-west); Pm cool, showery, most common (north-west); Tc hot and dry in summer (south-east); Pc very cold and dry, winter only (east).

Mission 4

  • Low pressure (rising air) = cloud, rain, wind; high pressure (sinking air) = settled, few clouds.
  • A frontal depression brings: warm front (thickening cloud, steady rain) → warm sector (mild, low cloud, drizzle) → cold front (heavy showers, gusts, then colder).
  • Summer highs: heat, sunshine, drought (2003). Winter highs: cold, frost, fog (1952 smog).

Mission 5

  • Data sources: land (about 300 stations, about 15 rainfall radars), air (geostationary and polar satellites), sea (buoys, ships).
  • Charts: close isobars = strong wind; fronts bring cloud and rain. Satellite images: swirl = depression, band = front. Limits: range (chaos; skill about a week ahead) and accuracy (gaps, local detail).

Mission 6

  • Tropical storms form over warm tropical oceans between about 25°S and 25°N, but not near the equator, where there is too little spin.
  • On low coasts, storm surge is usually the deadliest hazard: in Ian, 41 of 66 direct US deaths.
