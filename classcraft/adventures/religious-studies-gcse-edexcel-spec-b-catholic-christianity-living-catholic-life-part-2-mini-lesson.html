Mission 1

What popular piety is

  • Popular piety: forms of prayer and devotion that grow from ordinary believers' faith and culture.
  • Examples: the Rosary, the Stations of the Cross, Eucharistic adoration, pilgrimages, processions, candles, medals.
  • They sit alongside the liturgy (the Mass and sacraments), not instead of it.
  • They engage the whole person: hands (beads), feet (walking), eyes (images), voice (repeated prayer).
  • They can be prayed anywhere: at home, in hospital, on a bus, in a group.
Mission 1Read the source

CCC 1674–1676

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1674–1676 (paraphrase):

  • 1674: the faith of Christians shows itself in many devotions, e.g. visits to shrines, pilgrimages, processions, the Stations of the Cross, the Rosary, medals.
  • 1675: these extend the Church's liturgical life but do not replace it; they should fit the liturgical seasons, flow from the liturgy and lead people back to it.
  • 1676: the Church's pastors should support and guide them, and correct them where needed; they carry real wisdom from the faith of ordinary people.

Q1 What must popular piety never do, according to CCC 1675?

Show answer

Replace the liturgy.

Q2 Why might the Church need to "correct" a devotion?

Show answer

Mission 1

The Rosary

  • A string of beads with a crucifix, used to count prayers.
  • Start: Apostles' Creed, Our Father, three Hail Marys, Glory Be.
  • Then five decades. Each: one Our Father, ten Hail Marys, one Glory Be.
  • The Hail Mary begins with Gabriel's and Elizabeth's words: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28); "Blessed are you amongst women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!" (Luke 1:42)
  • During each decade the person thinks about one mystery: an event in the life of Jesus and Mary.
  • Four sets: Joyful, Luminous (added by Pope John Paul II in 2002), Sorrowful, Glorious.
  • Christ-centred: Pope John Paul II called it a summary of the whole Gospel, prayed "with Mary". The repetition calms the mind for meditation.
Mission 1

The Sorrowful Mysteries

  • Prayed especially on Tuesdays and Fridays, and in Lent.
  • 1. The Agony in the Garden: "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death." (Matthew 26:38)
  • 2. The Scourging at the Pillar (John 19:1). 3. The Crowning with Thorns (Matthew 27:29).
  • 4. The Carrying of the Cross (John 19:17). 5. The Crucifixion and Death (Luke 23:46).

Mission 1

The Stations of the Cross

  • Fourteen images around the walls of most Catholic churches, showing Jesus' last journey.
  • 1 condemned to death · 2 takes up his cross · 3 falls the first time · 4 meets his mother · 5 Simon of Cyrene helps · 6 Veronica wipes his face · 7 falls the second time
  • 8 meets the women of Jerusalem · 9 falls the third time · 10 stripped of his garments · 11 nailed to the cross · 12 dies · 13 taken down · 14 laid in the tomb
  • Many churches add a 15th: the Resurrection.
  • At each station: "We adore you, O Christ, and we praise you, because by your holy cross you have redeemed the world." Then a reading, a reflection and a prayer.
  • Origin: pilgrims in Jerusalem walked the Via Dolorosa; Franciscans spread the practice to Europe, so others could make the journey at home.
  • Prayed especially on Fridays in Lent and on Good Friday.

Mission 1

Eucharistic adoration

  • Catholics believe the consecrated host remains the Body of Christ after Mass.
  • So they adore (worship) Christ present in it, outside Mass too.
  • Exposition: the host is placed in a monstrance (a gold or silver stand with a glass centre) on the altar; people pray in silence.
  • Benediction: hymns (e.g. Tantum Ergo, by Thomas Aquinas), incense, and the priest blesses the people with the monstrance.
  • Holy Hour: one hour of prayer, recalling Jesus in Gethsemane: "What, couldn't you watch with me for one hour?" (Matthew 26:40)
  • Some parishes have perpetual adoration: someone is always praying, day and night. The feast of Corpus Christi often has a procession.

Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox and Protestant views

  • Orthodox: icons are central; many pray the Jesus Prayer ("Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, a sinner") on a prayer rope. The Rosary and Stations are not part of their tradition.
  • Orthodox churches keep the Eucharist for the sick but do not expose it for adoration.
  • Many Protestants avoid repeated set prayers: "don't use vain repetitions" (Matthew 6:7). They pray to God alone: "one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • Most reject Eucharistic adoration: the Church of England's Article XXVIII says Christ did not command the sacrament to be reserved, carried in procession or worshipped (paraphrase).
  • Some Anglicans (Anglo-Catholics) use the Rosary, Stations and Benediction; many Protestant churches hold Good Friday walks and reflections on the cross.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Popular piety is as important for Catholics as the Mass."

  • Claim: devotions like the Rosary matter as much as the Mass.
  • For: they can be prayed daily, anywhere, by anyone, including the sick. The Rosary summarises the Gospel. They make faith personal and part of everyday life. The Church encourages them (CCC 1674).
  • Against: they extend the liturgy but do not replace it (CCC 1675). The Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life. Devotions can drift into superstition and need guiding (CCC 1676). Many Protestants see some of them as unbiblical.
  • Judge it by: Does "as important" mean "the same kind of thing"? Which leads to which? What does the person praying actually gain?

Mission 2

What is a pilgrimage?

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place for a religious purpose. The traveller is a pilgrim.
  • It is both an outer journey (travel, walking, effort) and an inner journey (prayer, change of heart).
  • Christians often describe the whole of life as a pilgrimage towards God (Hebrews 11:13–16 calls believers strangers and pilgrims on earth).
  • No Christian is obliged to go on pilgrimage. Unlike Hajj in Islam, it is a free choice.
Mission 2

Goals, challenges, effects

  • Spiritual goals: to grow closer to God; to pray where Jesus lived, died and rose; to repent or give thanks; to ask for healing or guidance; to deepen faith.
  • Physical goals: to reach and see the holy place; to walk the route; sometimes to seek physical healing.
  • Challenges: cost, distance, heat, crowds, tiredness; in the Holy Land, travel can be affected by conflict and checkpoints. Spiritual challenges: doubt, disappointment, staying prayerful amid tourists.
  • Effects: a renewed faith, a sense of the Bible "coming alive", friendships with other pilgrims, a changed life back home.
Mission 2

A short history

  • Jewish roots: Jesus' family went up to Jerusalem for festivals such as Passover (Luke 2:41).
  • 4th century: Emperor Constantine's mother Helena visited the Holy Land (c. 326–328); churches were built at the sites of Jesus' birth and tomb.
  • c. 381–384: Egeria, a woman pilgrim, kept a diary of worship in Jerusalem.
  • Middle Ages: pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome and shrines of the saints were very popular.
  • 16th century: the Reformation criticised pilgrimage as trying to earn salvation and a cause of superstition about relics; many shrines in England were closed.
  • 19th–21st centuries: pilgrimage revived among Catholics and Orthodox, and in new forms among Anglicans and other Protestants.
Mission 2Read the source

Jesus at the Passover

> "His parents went every year to Jerusalem at the feast of the Passover." (Luke 2:41)

  • Luke 2:42–43 (paraphrase): when Jesus was twelve, they went up as usual for the feast. When it ended and they set off home, the boy Jesus stayed behind in Jerusalem without his parents knowing. (They find him in the Temple: "I must be in my Father's house", 2:49.)
  • Catholic reading: the Holy Family models pilgrimage; Christians follow their example.
  • Protestant reading: the family was obeying Jewish law; this is a story about who Jesus is, not a command for Christians to go on pilgrimage.

Mission 2

Jerusalem

  • Via Dolorosa ("Way of Sorrows"): the route Jesus is believed to have carried his cross; 14 Stations of the Cross; Franciscan friars lead a procession every Friday.
  • Garden of Gethsemane: ancient olive trees and the Church of All Nations, where Christians remember Jesus praying before his arrest.
  • Church of the Holy Sepulchre: built over the places long honoured as Golgotha (Calvary), where Jesus was crucified, and the tomb from which Christians believe he rose. Pilgrims climb to the chapel of Calvary, kneel at the Stone of Anointing and queue to enter the tomb.
  • The church is shared by several Christian communities: Greek Orthodox, Catholic (Franciscans) and Armenian, with Coptic, Ethiopian and Syriac Christians.
  • Some Protestants also pray at the Garden Tomb, a quieter alternative site outside the old city walls.

Mission 2

Catholic teaching on pilgrimage

  • Catholics value pilgrimage as a form of prayer, not a duty.
  • Catechism 2691 (paraphrase): the parish church is the proper place for the community's liturgical prayer, but other places also help prayer: a prayer corner at home, monasteries and pilgrimages. Pilgrimages call to mind our earthly journey towards heaven and are special times for renewal in prayer; shrines are special places for this. (2692–2696 summarise the teaching on prayer, ending with the places best suited to it.)
  • The Church calls itself a pilgrim people, journeying through history towards God's Kingdom (Second Vatican Council, Lumen Gentium, chapter 7).
  • Popes have declared Jubilee years in which Catholics are invited to make pilgrimage to Rome and to local churches.
  • Orthodox Christians share this love of holy places, relics and icons.
Mission 2Diversity

Is pilgrimage important today?

  • Catholic and Orthodox: valued, encouraged, never compulsory; holy places and relics help prayer.
  • Anglican: views vary; many Anglicans make pilgrimages (e.g. the Anglican shrine at Walsingham, the Holy Land) after the practice revived in the 19th and 20th centuries.
  • Many Protestants: not needed, because God is everywhere. Jesus told the Samaritan woman that true worship is "in spirit and truth", not tied to one mountain or city (John 4:21–24, paraphrase).
  • Reformation concerns: pilgrimage can become an attempt to earn salvation, or superstition about relics.
  • Still, many Protestants visit the Holy Land on study tours to see the places of the Bible, and find the experience deepens faith.
  • Quakers: the journey that matters is the inner one.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Pilgrimage is still important for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' own family went on pilgrimage (Luke 2:41). Walking where Jesus walked makes faith vivid. The journey brings time for prayer and change. Catholic teaching sees it as a sign of life's journey to heaven (CCC 2691).
  • Reasons against: God is everywhere (John 4:21–24). Money could be given to the poor instead. Pilgrimage can become tourism, or an attempt to earn favour with God.
  • Another view: an "inner pilgrimage" of prayer, or a local walk, can have the same purpose.
  • Judge it by: Is a holy place really closer to God? Does the journey change people? Is it the best use of time and money?
Mission 2Worked answer

Two reasons, with a source

Mission 3

Shrines: holy places of prayer

  • A shrine is a holy place linked to a saint, a relic or a reported vision.
  • Many famous shrines are Marian: linked to Mary, the mother of Jesus.
  • Catholics honour (venerate) Mary and ask her to pray for them. They worship God alone.
  • Say: "Catholics honour Mary and ask her prayers" — not: "Catholics worship Mary".
  • Pilgrims go to pray, to ask for help, to give thanks, and to grow closer to God with others.
  • The Catechism names places of pilgrimage among the places that help prayer, and says pilgrimages remind Christians of their journey towards heaven (CCC 2691, 2696, paraphrase).
Mission 3

Lourdes: what happened in 1858

  • Lourdes: a small town in the Pyrenees, south-west France.
  • 11 February 1858: Bernadette Soubirous, a poor 14-year-old, says she sees a young lady in the Grotto (cave) of Massabielle by the river.
  • Over five months she reports 18 visions (apparitions).
  • She says the lady asks for prayer, penance, a chapel and processions.
  • Bernadette digs where she says she was told; a spring appears. It still flows.
  • 1862: the local bishop declares that the apparitions can be believed. Bernadette becomes a nun and is declared a saint (1933).
Mission 3

Lourdes: what pilgrims do

  • At the Grotto: pray before the statue of Mary; touch the rock; light a candle as a sign of continuing prayer.
  • The water: drink it, wash in it, or bathe in the baths. A sign of cleansing and new life.
  • Torchlight procession each evening: thousands sing the Ave Maria, candles in hand.
  • Eucharistic procession: the Blessed Sacrament is carried; the sick sit at the front and are blessed.
  • Mass, confession, the Stations of the Cross on the hillside.
  • The sick come first: volunteers push wheelchairs, carry stretchers and serve meals.

Mission 3Read the source

Bernadette's account

Words Bernadette reported the lady saying (1858, translated from the local dialect):

> "I do not promise to make you happy in this world, but in the other." (18 February)

> "I am the Immaculate Conception." (25 March)

  • Immaculate Conception: the Catholic belief that Mary was kept free from original sin from the moment she was conceived (defined in 1854).
  • Say: "the Immaculate Conception is about Mary's own conception" — not: "it is the virgin birth of Jesus".

Q1 What does the first saying suggest pilgrims should expect from Lourdes?

Show answer

Not a promise of earthly happiness or cure; hope of joy with God after this life.

Q2 In the second saying, who does the lady say she is?

Show answer

Mary, named by a Catholic title: the one conceived without original sin. Bernadette, with little schooling, did not know the phrase; this helped convince the parish priest.

Mission 3

Healing and suffering

  • Some pilgrims report cures. Doctors at the Lourdes Medical Bureau examine claims.
  • Very few are declared miraculous: 72 by 2025. The Church is cautious.
  • Most pilgrims seek spiritual healing: peace, strength to cope, forgiveness, hope.
Mission 3

Rome: the city of Peter

  • Jesus calls Peter the rock on which he will build his Church (Matthew 16:18, paraphrase).
  • Catholics believe Peter became the first Bishop of Rome; each pope is his successor.
  • Tradition: Peter was martyred in Rome (c. AD 64) and buried on the Vatican Hill.
  • St Peter's Basilica (completed 1626) stands over the site; Catholics believe his tomb lies beneath the high altar.
  • The Pope lives and works in Vatican City, beside the basilica.
  • St Paul was also martyred in Rome. Pilgrims visit his basilica and the catacombs, early Christian burial places.
Mission 3

Rome: what pilgrims do

  • Pray at the tomb of Peter; many say the Creed there, professing the faith of the apostles.
  • Papal audience (usually Wednesdays): the Pope teaches, prays and greets pilgrims.
  • Papal appearances: on Sundays at noon he prays the Angelus from a window above St Peter's Square.
  • At Christmas and Easter he gives the blessing Urbi et Orbi ("to the city and the world").
  • In Jubilee years (every 25 years; the latest in 2025) pilgrims pass through the Holy Door, a sign of turning to Christ.
  • Significance: unity with the worldwide Catholic Church and the Pope.

Mission 3

Walsingham: England's Nazareth

  • Walsingham: a village in Norfolk, England.
  • Tradition: in 1061 a noblewoman, Richeldis, had a vision of Mary and built a copy of the Holy House in Nazareth, where the angel told Mary she would bear Jesus (Luke 1:26–38).
  • A great medieval shrine. Destroyed in 1538 at the Reformation.
  • Revived in the 20th century: a Catholic National Shrine (at the Slipper Chapel) and an Anglican Shrine, with an Orthodox chapel. Members of many churches come.
  • Pilgrims: walk the Holy Mile (some barefoot); join processions with the statue of Our Lady of Walsingham; pray; receive the sacraments; at the Anglican shrine, are sprinkled with water from the holy well.
  • Many pilgrims pray for healing of body, mind and spirit; most go home seeking peace and strength, not a cure.
  • Significance: honours the Incarnation (God becoming human in Jesus) and Mary's "yes" to God.
Mission 3Diversity

Catholic and Protestant views

  • Catholic: pilgrimage is valuable but not compulsory; it renews prayer and pictures life as a journey to God.
  • Orthodox: shrines, relics and icons are important; pilgrimage is common.
  • Anglican: varied. Anglo-Catholics go to Walsingham; many others do not.
  • Many Protestants (e.g. evangelical, Baptist, Reformed):
    • God can be worshipped anywhere, "in spirit and truth" (John 4:21–24, paraphrase).
    • Christ is the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5): devotion to Mary or relics may distract from him.
    • Salvation is by faith, not by journeys.
  • Some Protestants still make pilgrimages to the Holy Land, or to communities like Iona and Taizé.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Visiting a shrine brings a Christian closer to God than praying at home."

  • For: time set apart from daily life; a place soaked in centuries of prayer; praying with thousands of others; the sacraments; many pilgrims report lasting change.
  • For: the Catechism calls pilgrimages strong occasions for renewing prayer (CCC 2691, paraphrase).
  • Against: God is present everywhere; Jesus told his followers to pray in a private room (Matthew 6:6, paraphrase).
  • Against: cost and travel: the money could help the poor (Matthew 25:31–46); a trip can become tourism.
  • Against: many Protestants: focus on a place, statue or relic may distract from Christ.
  • Judge it by: Is God's presence tied to places? What the pilgrim does there, and how they live afterwards? Is "closer to God" a feeling or a change of life?
  • 12-mark (d): give arguments on both sides, refer to a source of authority such as CCC 2691, and reach a justified conclusion.

Mission 4

Love your neighbour, in concrete ways

  • Jesus joins two commands: love God, and "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31, quoting Leviticus 19:18).
  • The Good Samaritan: the neighbour is "he who showed mercy", and "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:37).
  • Love must be concrete: "if a brother or sister is naked and in lack of daily food" and you only wish them well, "what good is it?" (James 2:15–16).
  • Catholic Social Teaching (CST): the Church's teaching on how society should be organised so that every person can flourish.
  • Its modern form begins with Pope Leo XIII's letter on workers, Rerum Novarum (1891).
Mission 4

Every person in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image … male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Imago Dei (Latin, "image of God"): every human being has dignity: worth that does not depend on wealth, ability, age or usefulness.
  • This is the root of all CST: if every person bears God's image, harming or ignoring them wrongs God.

Mission 4

Key principles of CST

  • Dignity of the human person: every life matters, from conception to natural death.
  • Common good: society should give everyone what they need to flourish, not just the majority.
  • Solidarity: we are one human family; others' suffering is our concern.
  • Preferential option for the poor: the needs of the poorest come first.
  • Subsidiarity: decisions taken as close as possible to the people affected.
  • Care for creation: the earth is a gift for all, including future generations.
  • Peace: built on justice, not just the absence of war.
  • CST asks for charity (meeting today's need) and justice (changing what causes the need).
Mission 4

Evangelii Gaudium 182–237: the poor in society

  • Pope Francis, Evangelii Gaudium ("The Joy of the Gospel", 2013), chapter 4.
  • Faith cannot be kept private: it has consequences for society (EG 182–183, paraphrase).
  • Every Christian and community is called to help free and raise up the poor, and to hear their cry (EG 187, paraphrase).
  • Francis wants "a Church which is poor and for the poor": the poor have much to teach (EG 198).
  • Tackle the structural causes of poverty, not just give help (EG 202, paraphrase).
  • Special care for the most vulnerable: the homeless, addicts, refugees, migrants, victims of trafficking, the elderly, the unborn, and creation itself (EG 209–215, paraphrase).
  • Peace in society needs justice and dialogue, built day by day (EG 217–237, paraphrase).
Mission 4Read the source

The Sheep and the Goats

"For I was hungry and you gave me food to eat. I was thirsty and you gave me drink. I was a stranger and you took me in." (Matthew 25:35)

"Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)

  • People are judged on concrete acts of love: feeding, welcoming, clothing, visiting the sick and prisoners (25:35–36).
  • Catholics see Christ in the poor: serving them is serving him.
  • Lives of individual Catholics: volunteering at a food bank, visiting a care home, supporting refugees, fair-trade buying, campaigning on debt.
  • Q: In 25:40, who does the King say was really helped? (The King himself: "you did it to me".)
Mission 4

Justice, peace and reconciliation

  • Justice: giving each person what is due to them as a child of God: "let justice roll on like rivers" (Amos 5:24).
  • Peace: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9).
  • Gaudium et Spes 78: peace is not merely the absence of war or a balance of power; it is the work of justice (Isaiah 32:17) and the fruit of love, and must be built up constantly (paraphrase).
  • Reconciliation: God "reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18): healing broken relationships between people and with God.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Giving to charity is enough: changing society is not the Church's job."

  • Reasons for: Jesus fed and healed individuals; he did not run a political campaign. Charity is direct, personal and non-divisive. Some Christians argue the Church should stay out of politics and focus on salvation.
  • Reasons against: CST says faith cannot be kept private (EG 183) and that structural causes of poverty must be tackled (EG 202). The prophets denounced unjust rulers (Amos 5:24). If laws keep people poor, charity alone can never be enough.
  • Another view: both together: charity meets today's need; justice changes tomorrow. Catholics may disagree about which policies best serve the common good.
  • Judge it by: Does helping individuals change the causes of poverty? Where is the line between Gospel justice and party politics? What did Jesus' example ask of his followers?
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two ways Catholic Social Teaching shows love of neighbour.

  • Way 1: Catholics tackle the structural causes of poverty. Source: Evangelii Gaudium 202 asks Christians to change the causes of poverty, not just give help (paraphrase). Development: so Catholics campaign for fair wages, housing and education for all, not just their own community.
  • Way 2: Catholics serve Christ in the poor. Source: "you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40). Development: helping a homeless person is treated as serving Jesus himself, so it is an act of worship as well as kindness.
  • (c) 5-mark tip: two developed points; refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 5

Faith in action

  • "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Catholic Social Teaching (see Mission 4) is lived out by individuals, parishes and agencies.
  • Agencies work at different levels: local (next door), national, global.
  • Each one puts a CST principle to work: dignity, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • This mission starts with CAFOD, the agency every course studies.
Mission 5

CAFOD: what it does

  • CAFOD: the Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, the official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962.
  • Part of Caritas Internationalis, the Church's worldwide network of charities.
  • Works through local partners (often local churches) rather than sending its own staff to run projects.
  • Emergency relief: food, water and shelter after disasters and wars.
  • Long-term development: farming, clean water, health, education, livelihoods.
  • Campaigning: asking UK governments and businesses to change unjust policies on debt, trade and climate.
  • Parishes and schools raise money on Family Fast Days (Lent and harvest).
Mission 5

CAFOD: why it does it

  • Imago Dei: every person has dignity, so no one should be left in poverty.
  • Matthew 25:40: "you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me."
  • Solidarity: one human family; distance does not make someone less a neighbour (Luke 10:37).
  • Option for the poor and justice: charity for today, campaigns to change causes.
  • Partnership: local people decide and lead; this respects their dignity and subsidiarity.
  • Help is given to anyone in need, regardless of religion.

Mission 5

SVP: love next door

  • Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP), founded in Paris in 1833 by a student, Blessed Frédéric Ozanam (1813–1853), and friends.
  • Named after St Vincent de Paul (1581–1660), a French priest who served the poor.
  • Mostly lay volunteers in local groups called conferences, often in a parish.
  • Aims: person-to-person help: visiting the lonely, sick, prisoners and families in need at home; food, furniture and friendship; seeking to tackle the causes of poverty.
  • Help is given to anyone, regardless of faith; the volunteer sees Christ in the person (Matthew 25:36).
Mission 5

Love of neighbour in everyday Catholic lives

  • Give: Family Fast Day, Lenten giving, a share of income.
  • Act: volunteer at an SVP conference, food bank, night shelter or refugee drop-in.
  • Speak: sign a CAFOD campaign, write to an MP.
  • Live simply: buy fair-trade, waste less, think about the effect of choices on the poor.
  • Pray: for people in need and for peace; Mass ends by sending Catholics out to glorify God by the way they live.
  • These match the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:35–36): feeding, welcoming, visiting.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Supporting a charity is the best way for a Catholic to love their neighbour."

  • Reasons for: agencies such as CAFOD have expertise and reach that one person lacks; they tackle causes as well as symptoms; giving through a parish unites a whole community in solidarity.
  • Reasons against: Matthew 25 asks for personal acts (visiting the sick and prisoners), which money cannot replace; SVP's founders insisted on person-to-person contact; love of neighbour starts at home, with family and the people next door; prayer and peacemaking matter too.
  • Another view: different ways suit different people and stages of life; CST asks for both charity and justice.
  • Judge it by: Does the way of loving change the causes of need? Does it involve real relationship? Is it open to everyone, whatever their circumstances?

Mission 6

Mission and evangelism

  • Mission (Latin mittere, "to send"): the whole task the Church is sent to do: sharing God's love in word and action.
  • Evangelism (Greek euangelion, "good news"): sharing the Gospel so that others may come to faith in Jesus.
  • So all evangelism is mission, but not all mission is evangelism: a food bank is mission; explaining the Gospel is evangelism.
  • Church growth: more people joining churches, and new churches being started.
  • Catholics often say evangelisation: bringing the Gospel into every part of life and society.
  • Catholic teaching counts the witness of a Christian life as the first step of evangelisation, completed by words.

Mission 6

Mark 16 and John 20

  • Mark 16:9–20 (paraphrase): the risen Jesus appears to Mary Magdalene and others; sends the eleven to "go into all the world" and preach the Good News to all creation (16:15); says signs will follow believers: driving out demons, new tongues, healing the sick, protection from snakes and poison (16:17–18); is taken up; the disciples go out, and the Lord works with them (16:20).
  • These verses are missing from the oldest manuscripts. The Catholic Church accepts them as Scripture; many Bibles print them with a note.
  • Interpretations of the signs: Pentecostal and charismatic Christians expect healing and tongues today; most others read the snakes and poison as promises of God's protection, not as practices.
  • John 20:21–22 (paraphrase): "As the Father has sent me, even so I send you"; Jesus breathes on them: "Receive the Holy Spirit!".
  • Significance: mission comes from God (Father sends Son, Son sends Church) and is empowered by the Spirit.

Mission 6Diversity

Salvation and the urgency of mission

  • How Christians understand salvation shapes how they see mission.
  • Exclusivist (many evangelical Christians): salvation only through conscious faith in Jesus (John 14:6; Acts 4:12). So evangelism is urgent.
  • Catholic teaching: salvation comes through Christ; the Church is his instrument; God's grace can reach people who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ. But the Church is still sent to proclaim him to all.
  • Liberal or pluralist Christians: God is at work in many faiths; mission stresses dialogue and justice more than conversion.
  • Most Christians agree that mission includes both words and loving action; they differ on the balance.
Mission 6

Pope Francis: a Church that goes out

Evangelii Gaudium ("The Joy of the Gospel", 2013), paraphrased:

  • Chapter 5, "Spirit-filled evangelisers": evangelisers are open to the Holy Spirit, who gives courage; they pray and work together; they share a personal experience of Jesus' love; Mary is the model.
  • Significance: every Catholic is a missionary disciple, not only priests or religious.
Mission 6

The Spirit sends

  • Mission is Trinitarian: the Father sends the Son; the Father and Son send the Spirit; the Spirit sends the Church (John 20:21–22).
  • Pope Francis: without the Spirit, mission is just a programme; with the Spirit, it becomes joyful witness.
Mission 6

Locally, nationally, globally

  • Locally: welcoming newcomers; courses for adults exploring faith or wanting to be baptised or received; youth work; food banks and street pastors; inviting friends.
  • Nationally: Christian radio and online media; national youth festivals and pilgrimages; church leaders speaking on public issues; joint campaigns by Churches Together.
  • Globally: missionary societies and orders (e.g. the Church Mission Society; for Catholics, Missio, the Pope's mission charity) support churches, schools and clinics worldwide.
  • Reverse mission: many churches in Britain are now led or planted by Christians from Africa, Asia, Latin America and Eastern Europe.
  • For Catholics, all three levels are linked to the Pope and bishops and to Pope Francis' call (in his 2013 letter Evangelii Gaudium, "The Joy of the Gospel") for the Church to "go out".

Mission 6

Evangelism today and new forms of church

  • Evangelism in practice: courses exploring faith (e.g. Alpha, started at Holy Trinity Brompton, London); sharing faith with friends; school and university Christian Unions; street preaching; online videos and podcasts.
  • New forms of church:
    • Messy Church: crafts, a short celebration and a meal for families (from 2004);
    • café church: worship and discussion over coffee;
    • church plants: new congregations in schools, pubs or warehouses;
    • online church.
  • The Church of England and the Methodist Church call these Fresh Expressions (from the report Mission-shaped Church, 2004).
  • Reasons: to reach people who would never come to a traditional service; changing Sunday habits; to fit the needs of families and young adults.
  • Church growth worldwide is strongest in Africa, Asia and Latin America; in Britain, Pentecostal, Black-majority and migrant churches are among those growing.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Showing love in action is a better way to spread Christianity than preaching."

  • For: Jesus served the sick and poor; "faith without works is dead" (James 2:17, paraphrase); actions build trust where words may be resented.
  • Against: the Great Commission commands teaching (Matthew 28:19–20); "how will they believe in him whom they have not heard?" (Romans 10:14, paraphrase).
  • Against: good works alone may be seen as simple kindness; people need to hear why Christians act.
  • Another view: Pope Francis: the love of Jesus moves Christians both to serve and to speak of him (Evangelii Gaudium 264, paraphrase).
  • Judge it by: What does "spread" mean: more believers, or more love? Can word and action be separated? Which reaches people in Britain today?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Catholic teaching is that popular piety (Rosary, Stations, adoration) extends the liturgy but never replaces it (CCC 1674–1676).
  • The Rosary: five decades, each a meditation on one mystery.
  • The Stations retrace Jesus' way to the cross.
  • Eucharistic adoration follows from belief in the Real Presence.

Mission 2

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place for a religious purpose: an outer and an inner journey, never compulsory.
  • Holy Land sites: Bethlehem (Church of the Nativity), Nazareth (Annunciation, St Joseph), Jerusalem (Via Dolorosa, Gethsemane, Holy Sepulchre at Golgotha).
  • Catholics and Orthodox value pilgrimage (CCC 2691: a sign of the journey to heaven); many Protestants say God can be worshipped anywhere (John 4:21–24), though many still go.

Mission 3

  • Lourdes (1858, Bernadette, the Grotto and spring) is a place of prayer and healing where the sick come first; most healing sought is spiritual.
  • Rome: tomb of Peter, St Peter's Basilica, the Pope, audiences and blessings; unity with the worldwide Church.
  • Walsingham: England's Nazareth, honouring the Incarnation; Catholics value pilgrimage, many Protestants see no need for it.

Mission 4

  • CST applies love of neighbour to society: dignity (imago Dei), common good, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • EG 182–237 and Matthew 25: serving Christ in the poor and tackling the causes of poverty.

Mission 5

  • CAFOD (1962) puts CST into action globally through partners: emergencies, development, campaigning; help regardless of religion.

Mission 6

  • Mission is the Church being sent to share God's love in word and action; evangelism is sharing the Gospel (Matthew 28:19–20).
  • Mission happens locally, nationally and globally, including new forms of church; Christians differ on how urgent conversion is.
  • Catholic sources (Evangelii Gaudium; Deus Caritas Est 19) call every Catholic to go out, moved by the Spirit and the love of Christ.
