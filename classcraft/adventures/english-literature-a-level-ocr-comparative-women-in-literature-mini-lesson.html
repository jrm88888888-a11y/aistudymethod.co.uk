Marriage, money and the plots women were given

  • Sense and Sensibility: Norland passes to John, and his wife Fanny argues his half-sisters need little help: "If they marry, they will be sure of doing well" (Chapter II). Marriage must provide.
  • Woolf argued in A Room of One's Own (1929) that a woman needs money and a room of her own to write fiction.
  • Many novels end in marriage or death: ask whether each one accepts that plot or exposes its cost.

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Before 1870 a wife's property passed to her husband's control; reforms in 1870, 1882, 1918 and 1928 frame the novels you compare.
  • Woolf's free indirect discourse lets Clarissa feel herself become "Mrs. Richard Dalloway"; Hardy exposes the double standard as "The Woman Pays".
  • Compare a core text (Sense and Sensibility or Mrs Dalloway) with your second text; Section 1 is an unseen extract.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • Both OCR exam components end in a comparative essay; NEA Task 2 compares too.

Key quotations to learn

  • not even Clarissa any more; this being Mrs. Richard Dalloway. (Mrs Dalloway, opening section) Identity absorbed into the husband's name.
  • invisible; unseen; unknown (Mrs Dalloway, opening section) A middle-aged woman's sense of disappearing.
  • You were one person; now you are another. (Tess, Chapter XXXV (Angel)) The double standard in a single antithesis.
  • Once victim, always victim—that’s the law! (Tess, Chapter XLVII (Tess to Alec)) Tess names the social "law" that traps her.
  • It would degrade me to marry Heathcliff now (Wuthering Heights, Chapter IX (Catherine)) Marriage chosen for status, not love.
