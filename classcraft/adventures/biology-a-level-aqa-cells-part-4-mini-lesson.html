Mission 1

Mission 1: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Make a guess: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Mission 1

Active transport

  • Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient: from low to high concentration.
  • It needs energy from ATP, made in respiration.
  • It needs a membrane protein that binds the particle.
  • The proteins are carrier proteins. Channel proteins cannot do it.
  • Each carrier binds only specific particles: active transport is selective.
  • Say: "active transport moves particles against their gradient" — not: "active transport is faster diffusion".
Mission 1

How a carrier pumps

  • (1) The particle binds to a specific site on the carrier, on one side of the membrane.
  • (2) ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pᵢ. The phosphate attaches to the carrier, which changes shape.
  • (3) The particle is released on the other side. The phosphate leaves; the carrier returns to its first shape.
  • No ATP, no shape change, no transport.
Quick check
?Root cells absorb nitrate ions from soil water in which the nitrate concentration is much lower than in the cell. When the roots are deprived of oxygen, nitrate uptake falls sharply. What explains these observations?
Mission 1

How do you know it is active?

  • Sign 1: the substance builds up above the outside concentration.
  • Sign 2: uptake falls sharply when the cell's ATP supply falls: no oxygen, or a respiratory inhibitor.
  • Diffusion is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. That is the test.
  • Ions leak back down their gradient. So a gradient needs a continuing energy supply, not a one-off.

Mission 1

The sodium–potassium pump

  • One carrier, two ions, both moved against their gradients.
  • Each cycle, one ATP: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in.
  • Result: Na⁺ is low inside the cell; K⁺ is high.
  • The low Na⁺ inside drives co-transport (next screen).
  • Say: "3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in" — not: "Na⁺ in, K⁺ out", nor "one for one".
Mission 1

Co-transport: glucose rides in with sodium

  • (1) Na⁺/K⁺ pumps on the blood side of each epithelial cell move Na⁺ out into the blood. Na⁺ inside the cell stays low.
  • (2) In the membrane facing the lumen, a co-transporter protein binds Na⁺ and glucose together. Na⁺ moves in down its gradient and carries glucose in against its gradient.
  • (3) Glucose builds up in the cell, then leaves into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • Amino acids are absorbed by co-transport with Na⁺ in the same way.
  • Say: "the co-transporter uses the Na⁺ gradient" — not: "the co-transporter hydrolyses ATP".
Quick check
?Epithelial cells from the small intestine were treated with a substance that inhibits sodium–potassium pumps. Uptake of glucose from the lumen by the cells fell sharply, although the sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters (co-transporter proteins) themselves were not affected by the substance. What explains this result?
Mission check

?Red blood cells hold K⁺ at a much higher concentration than the plasma around them. In donated blood stored at 4 °C, the cells slowly lose K⁺. When the cells are warmed to 37 °C with glucose, they take K⁺ back up to the original high level. Which explanation fits these observations?

A gradient against the odds is never free. Stop the energy supply and it runs down; restore it and the pumps rebuild it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Make a guess: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Mission 2

A tube through the body

  • Holozoic nutrition: food is taken in and digested inside the body.
  • Four stages: ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation. Undigested waste is egested.
  • The gut is a tube open at both ends. Its lumen is still outside the body's cells.
  • Digestion: enzymes hydrolyse large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones.
  • Absorption: those small molecules cross the gut lining into the blood or lymph. Only now are they inside.
  • Assimilation: cells build the absorbed molecules into the animal's own molecules and tissues.

Mission 2

Carbohydrates and proteins

  • Amylases hydrolyse glycosidic bonds in starch → maltose.
  • Membrane-bound disaccharidases (maltase, sucrase, lactase) finish the job: disaccharides → monosaccharides.
  • Endopeptidases hydrolyse peptide bonds within a polypeptide → many shorter chains.
  • Exopeptidases hydrolyse the terminal peptide bonds → single amino acids and dipeptides.
  • Membrane-bound dipeptidases hydrolyse dipeptides → amino acids.
  • Endopeptidases make more ends, so exopeptidases work faster.
  • Membrane-bound enzymes release monosaccharides and amino acids right beside the transporters that absorb them.
Mission 2

Lipids: bile salts and lipase

  • Fat forms large droplets in water. Lipase can act only at a droplet's surface.
  • Bile salts emulsify fat: large droplets → many tiny droplets → much larger surface area for lipase.
  • Emulsification breaks no bonds. It is not digestion.
  • Lipase hydrolyses ester bonds: triglyceride → monoglycerides + fatty acids.
  • Bile salts, monoglycerides and fatty acids cluster into micelles.
  • Say: "bile salts emulsify lipids" — not: "bile salts digest lipids".
Quick check
?Which statement describes the role of bile salts in the digestion of lipids?
Mission 2

Absorbing glucose and amino acids

  • Recap (see Mission 1), in an epithelial cell of the ileum:
  • (1) The Na⁺/K⁺ pump on the blood side uses ATP to keep Na⁺ low inside the cell.
  • (2) A co-transporter in the microvillus membrane binds Na⁺ and glucose. Na⁺ moves in down its gradient and carries glucose in against its gradient.
  • (3) Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • Amino acids and galactose are absorbed by co-transport with Na⁺ in the same way.
Quick check
?How do epithelial cells of the ileum absorb glucose from the lumen when the glucose concentration inside the cells is already higher than in the lumen?
Mission 2

Lipids: micelles to lacteals

  • Micelles carry monoglycerides and fatty acids to the epithelial cell surface.
  • They keep releasing them there, so a steep concentration gradient is kept up.
  • Monoglycerides and fatty acids are lipid-soluble: they diffuse through the phospholipid bilayer.
  • Inside, the ER rejoins them into triglycerides. The Golgi packs them with proteins into chylomicrons.
  • Chylomicrons leave by exocytosis and enter a lacteal (a lymph vessel), not the capillaries.
Mission check

?A person eats celery. Humans make no enzyme that digests cellulose. One cellulose fibre from the celery passes along the whole gut and leaves in the faeces. Which statement about this fibre is correct?

A meal gets inside you only when its molecules cross the gut lining. Before that, it is still in a tube that runs through you.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Make a guess: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Mission 3

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 3

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side / µm1234
Surface area / µm²6245496
Volume / µm³182764
SA:V / µm⁻¹6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A cube of side 1 mm has a surface area of 6 mm², a volume of 1 mm³ and a surface area-to-volume ratio of 6. The side length is increased to 4 mm. What is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?
Mission 3

Worked example: other shapes

  • Sphere: SA = 4πr²; V = (4/3)πr³.
  • Cylinder: SA = 2πrh + 2πr²; V = πr²h.
  • Cuboid (rectangular solid): SA = 2lh + 2lw + 2wh; V = lwh.
  • Sphere, r = 5 µm:
  • SA = 4 × π × 5² = 314 µm².
  • V = (4/3) × π × 5³ = 524 µm³.
  • SA:V = 314 ÷ 524 = 0.60 µm⁻¹.
  • Count every face: hidden faces too.
Quick check
?A hypothetical single-celled organism has the shape of a rectangular solid, 20 μm long, 10 μm high and 10 μm wide. What is the cell's surface area-to-volume ratio?

Mission 3

Large organisms

  • As an organism grows, SA:V falls. Many cells are far from the surface.
  • Diffusion is too slow over long distances.
  • So large organisms have specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, gut, leaves) and mass transport systems (blood).
  • Or a body shape that keeps SA:V high: flattened or long and thin.
  • Small animals: high SA:V, so they lose heat faster per gram, and have a higher metabolic rate per unit mass.
Quick check
?Shrews are among the smallest mammals. Like larger mammals, they keep their body temperature nearly constant. A shrew must eat much more food per gram of its body mass each day than a deer does. Which statement best explains this difference?
Mission 3

Cells that beat the limit

  • Shape, not just size, sets SA:V. Cells add surface without adding much volume.
  • Microvilli: finger-like folds of the cell-surface membrane, e.g. ileum epithelial cells.
  • Cells also speed transport with more channel and carrier proteins in their membranes.
  • Say: "microvilli are folds of membrane that add surface" — not: "microvilli beat like cilia".
Quick check
?A human erythrocyte (red blood cell) has a volume of about 90 µm³ and a surface area of about 135 µm². A sphere with the same volume would have a surface area of about 97 µm². What do these figures show about the adaptation of the erythrocyte?
Mission check

?A spherical single-celled organism grows from a radius of 10 µm to a radius of 30 µm, keeping its shape. Which prediction is best supported?

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Make a guess: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Mission 4

Why the pigment stays in

  • Beetroot's red pigment (betalain) is stored in the cell vacuole.
  • To leave, it must cross two membranes: the tonoplast and the cell-surface membrane.
  • The cell wall is fully permeable: it does not hold the pigment in.
  • Betalain is large and polar. It cannot cross an intact phospholipid bilayer.
  • So pigment in the water = damaged membranes. More pigment = more permeable.
Mission 4

Temperature

  • Warmer: phospholipids gain kinetic energy and move more. The bilayer becomes more fluid; permeability rises slowly.
  • Above about 40 °C: membrane proteins denature. Their shape is lost, leaving gaps in the bilayer.
  • Permeability then rises steeply: pigment pours out.
  • Below 0 °C: ice crystals can pierce membranes; thawed tissue leaks.
  • Say: "membrane proteins denature" — not: "heat breaks the bonds in the fatty acid tails".
Mission 4

Solvents

  • Organic solvents such as ethanol (alcohol) dissolve in the bilayer and disrupt it: lipids dissolve in them.
  • They also denature membrane proteins.
  • Higher solvent concentration → more disruption → more pigment leaks.
Quick check
?Beetroot discs held at 70 °C release far more pigment than discs held at 30 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 4Practical

Beetroot and membrane permeability

  • Required practical 4: the effect of a named variable on the permeability of cell-surface membranes.
  • Cut cylinders with a cork borer; trim to equal length. Same surface area each.
  • Rinse in distilled water until it stays clear: removes pigment from cells cut open.
  • Put each in the same volume of water at one temperature (or ethanol concentration) for the same time.
  • Remove the beetroot, shake the tube, measure the liquid's absorbance in a colorimeter.
  • Control: same beetroot, size, volume and time. Only the independent variable changes.
Mission 4

Using a colorimeter

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample in a cuvette.
  • Absorbance: how much light the sample absorbs. More pigment → higher absorbance.
  • Choose a green or blue-green filter: the red pigment absorbs these colours most.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank (distilled water) first.
  • Same filter and clean cuvette for every reading; light passes through the clear sides.
  • Some colorimeters give % transmission: more pigment → lower transmission.
Mission 4

Worked example: serial dilution and calibration

To turn absorbance into a pigment concentration, make a calibration curve.

  • Start with a pigment extract: 100%.
  • 1-in-2 dilution: 5.0 cm³ of extract + 5.0 cm³ distilled water → 50%.
  • Repeat from each new tube: 25%, 12.5%, 6.25%. Each step halves the concentration.
  • Measure each absorbance (illustrative data):

  • Plot absorbance against concentration; draw the best-fit line.
  • A sample reads 0.36: between 0.25 (25%) and 0.49 (50%).
  • Read across to the line, then down: about 38% of the extract's concentration.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Make a guess: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Mission 5

Self, non-self and antigens

  • Every cell carries surface molecules, mostly proteins and glycoproteins, that identify it.
  • The immune system ignores self molecules and responds to non-self ones.
  • This lets it identify pathogens, cells from other individuals of the same species, abnormal body cells and toxins.
  • An antigen is a molecule that triggers an immune response, including antibody production.
  • Say: "the antigen is recognised; the antibody is made against it" — not the other way round.
Quick check
?Which statement about antigens is correct?
Mission 5

One lymphocyte, one shape

  • Lymphocytes drive the adaptive (specific) response. They circulate in the blood and gather in lymph nodes.
  • Two kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells).
  • Each lymphocyte has receptors of one shape. Each B cell can make one type of antibody.
  • You have millions of different lymphocytes, made before any infection.
  • An antigen binds only the few with a complementary receptor. It selects them: clonal selection.
  • Say: "the receptor is complementary to the antigen" — not: "the same shape as the antigen".
  • Say: "the antigen selects a cell that already fits" — not: "the cell designs an antibody to fit".
Quick check
?Which statement about B-lymphocytes and antibodies is correct?
Mission 5

Antigen presentation wakes T helper cells

  • A macrophage engulfs a pathogen and displays its antigens on its surface: an antigen-presenting cell (APC).
  • A T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds the presented antigen and is activated.
  • AQA calls them TH cells.
  • It divides by mitosis into a clone and releases chemical signals (cytokines).
  • TH cells stimulate cytotoxic T cells (TC cells), B cells and phagocytes.
Mission 5

T killer cells: the cell-mediated response

  • AQA calls this the cellular response.
  • Stimulated by T helper cells, T killer cells (cytotoxic T cells) divide into a clone.
  • They bind body cells that display the antigen: virus-infected cells, cancer cells, transplanted cells.
  • By direct contact, they release proteins that make pores in the target cell's membrane. The cell dies.
Mission 5

B cells: the antibody response

  • This is the humoral response.
  • A B cell's receptor binds its complementary antigen. The B cell takes it in and displays it.
  • A T helper cell activated by the same antigen binds the B cell. Only now is the B cell activated.
  • The activated B cell divides by mitosis into a clone: clonal expansion.
  • Most become plasma cells: they secrete antibody. Every cell in the clone makes the same antibody.
  • AQA: these are monoclonal antibodies.
  • Some become memory cells: long-lived and ready for next time.
Quick check
?In an experiment, B-lymphocytes specific to an antigen were cultured with that antigen and no other cells. No antibody was produced. When helper T-lymphocytes were added to the culture, antibody production began. What does this show?
Mission 5

Why the first response takes days

  • Only a few B cells fit any one antigen.
  • They must be activated, then divide many times, before there are enough plasma cells.
  • This takes days. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, and you feel ill.
  • Memory cells survive for years. Next time they divide at once: the pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms.
Quick check
?After a person is exposed to a new pathogen for the first time, several days pass before antibodies against it can be detected in the blood. Which statement best explains this delay?
Mission check

?A child is born with no T lymphocytes. Her B lymphocytes are normal in number and type. Why does she make very little antibody against most pathogens?

The right cells already exist before infection. The antigen selects them, T helper cells switch them on, and mitosis builds the army.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Mission 6

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 6

Herd immunity

  • A pathogen spreads only when an infected person passes it to someone susceptible.
  • Vaccines protect the individual and, at high coverage, the population.
  • When a high enough percentage is immune, most contacts are immune: chains of transmission break.
  • This protects people who cannot be vaccinated: newborns, people with weakened immune systems.
  • More infectious diseases need higher coverage: about 95% for measles.
  • If coverage falls below that level, outbreaks return.
Quick check
?For measles, transmission in a population is greatly impeded once about 95% of people are immune. A community's vaccination coverage falls from 96% to 85%. Which statement correctly describes a consequence?
Mission 6

When antigens change

  • Memory cells bind only the antigen they were made against.
  • Antigen variability: mutations change a pathogen's surface antigens. Old memory cells no longer bind, so you can fall ill again.
  • Influenza antigens change often, so a new vaccine is made each year.
  • The common cold is caused by over 100 types of rhinovirus, each with different antigens: no single vaccine can cover them.
Quick check
?A person had influenza last winter and recovered fully. This winter they catch influenza again. Which is the best explanation?

  • Strengths: large sample, random allocation, placebo control, blinding.
  • Limits: about two months of follow-up after dose 2; symptomatic disease only; says nothing yet about long-term protection.

Mission 6

Ethics: the arguments

  • Ethics questions have arguments on several sides. Know them; weigh them.
  • Testing: vaccines are tested on animals, then on volunteers. How much risk is acceptable, and is consent fully informed?
  • Placebos: is it right to give a placebo when a vaccine might protect?
  • Side effects: serious ones are rare but not nil. The disease usually carries far greater risk. How should a small individual risk be weighed against benefit to the population?
  • Choice: should vaccination be compulsory to reach herd immunity, or an individual decision?
  • Fairness: who is vaccinated first when supply is short? Should richer countries fund vaccines for poorer ones?
Quick check
?A news article reports a newly published study claiming that a vaccine causes a rare side effect, before other scientists have evaluated the study. Which response shows the best understanding of how science works?
Mission 6

From genome to vaccine

  • Sequencing a simple organism's genome lets scientists work out its proteome: every protein it can make.
  • Search that proteome for proteins likely to sit on the pathogen's surface: candidate antigens.
  • Make those proteins and test which trigger protective antibodies.
  • Example: the meningitis B vaccine was developed this way from the genome of Neisseria meningitidis group B.
  • Simple organisms suit this: they have little non-coding DNA, so genome → proteome is predictable.
Mission check

?A measles vaccine gives long-lasting protection. Over 100 types of rhinovirus cause the common cold, each with different surface antigens. Why would a vaccine made from one rhinovirus type give little protection against colds?

Long-lasting protection needs an antigen that stays the same. When antigens change or come in many types, memory cells miss.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Active transport uses carrier (pump) proteins and ATP to move specific particles against their concentration gradient; it stops when the ATP supply stops.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump keeps Na⁺ low inside ileum cells; Na⁺ then flows in through a co-transporter, carrying glucose in against its gradient.

Mission 2

  • Holozoic nutrition: ingest, digest (extracellular hydrolysis in the lumen), absorb across the gut lining, assimilate.
  • Membrane-bound enzymes finish digestion; glucose and amino acids enter by co-transport with Na⁺; lipids enter via micelles.

Mission 3

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.
  • Large organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems; small animals lose heat faster per gram.
  • Cells raise SA:V by shape: microvilli, flattening, folds.

Mission 4

  • Pigment leaks only when membranes are damaged: heat (more fluid phospholipids, then denatured proteins above about 40 °C) and organic solvents such as ethanol both raise permeability.
  • Measure leakage with a colorimeter (blank first, green filter); convert absorbance to concentration with a calibration curve from a serial dilution.

Mission 5

  • Antigens select the few lymphocytes with complementary receptors; activated B cells form clones of plasma cells that all make one antibody, plus memory cells.
  • T helper cells, activated by antigen-presenting cells, switch on B cells and T killer cells, which destroy infected and abnormal cells.

Mission 6

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • High coverage gives herd immunity, protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.
  • Changing antigens defeat old memory cells: why the flu vaccine changes yearly.
