Mission 1

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system: electrical impulses along neurones to specific cells. Fast (milliseconds), short-lived, localized.
  • Endocrine system: hormones secreted into the blood. Slower to start, longer-lasting, often widespread.
  • Hormones are signals that travel long distances in the blood to target cells of another type.
Mission 1

Glands, blood, target cells

  • Endocrine glands are ductless: they secrete hormones directly into the blood.
  • The blood carries each hormone to almost every cell.
  • Only target cells respond: they have receptors with a shape complementary to that hormone.
  • Cells without the receptor are unaffected, however much hormone reaches them.
  • AP examples: insulin, human growth hormone, thyroid hormones, testosterone, estrogen.
  • Say: "the hormone binds to complementary receptors on target cells" — not: "the blood takes the hormone to its target".
Quick check
?A hormone released into the blood by a gland affects only some of the body's cell types. Which statement best explains why only these cell types respond to it?
Mission check

?In humans, cells of the pituitary gland at the base of the brain release human growth hormone, and target cells in many tissues respond, leading to growth. Which flowchart best represents how this signal reaches and affects its target cells?

Every hormone is broadcast to the whole body; the receptor decides who listens.

Make a guess: A hormone in your blood reaches almost every cell, yet only a few respond. How do cells pick their messages?

Mission 2

Why cells must communicate

  • In a multicellular organism, cells are specialized into tissues and organs.
  • Organs must coordinate their activities.
  • The organism must respond to changes in its internal and external environment.
  • Both animals and plants need this.
  • Animals use two systems: nervous (fast, electrical and chemical) and hormonal (chemicals carried in the blood).
Mission 2

Ligand meets receptor

  • Cell signaling: one cell releases a chemical messenger; another cell detects it and responds.
  • The messenger is a ligand.
  • It binds a receptor: a protein with a binding site complementary in shape and chemistry to that ligand.
  • Only target cells, which carry that receptor, respond.
  • Binding is reversible and leaves the ligand unchanged. It is the receptor that changes shape.
  • The receptor's ligand-binding domain recognizes one specific messenger: a peptide (protein) or a small molecule.
  • Say: "a complementary binding site" — not: "the same shape as the ligand".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the relationship between a ligand and its receptor?
Mission 2

Near or far

  • Direct contact: cells touch; a molecule on one cell's surface binds a receptor on the other.
  • Example: an antigen-presenting cell displays pathogen fragments; a helper T cell that touches it and binds a fragment is activated.
  • Short distance: the messenger diffuses to nearby cells. Neurotransmitters cross a synaptic gap of about 20 nm in under a millisecond.
  • Short-distance messengers are local regulators (e.g. neurotransmitters, quorum-sensing signals, morphogens).
  • Long distance: hormones travel in the blood, reach almost every cell, and act on target cells over minutes or longer.
  • Examples: insulin, human growth hormone, thyroid hormones, testosterone, estrogen.
Quick check
?At a synapse between a motor neuron and a muscle cell, the neuron releases the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, which causes the muscle cell to contract. Which statement correctly describes how this signal reaches the muscle cell?
Mission 2

Quorum sensing

  • 1: Each Vibrio fischeri cell secretes a signal, an autoinducer. At low density in open seawater it diffuses away, so its concentration stays low.
  • 2: At high density, inside a squid's light organ, autoinducer builds up past a threshold.
  • 3: Autoinducer binds a receptor protein in each cell → transcription of the lux genes → luciferase → light. The whole population switches on together.
  • A population-wide change in gene expression, set by cell density.
  • The squid uses the glow to mask its shadow from predators below.
  • Quorum sensing: microbes use a short-distance chemical signal to regulate pathways in response to population density.
Quick check
?Vibrio fischeri produces light when packed at high density inside the light organ of a squid, but not when living at low density in seawater. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 2

Receptor outside or inside?

  • Transmembrane receptors: for hydrophilic ligands (e.g. insulin, a protein; epinephrine (adrenaline), an amine) that cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer. The ligand stays outside.
  • Intracellular receptors: in the cytoplasm or nucleus, for hydrophobic ligands (steroid hormones, e.g. estrogen, testosterone) that diffuse through the bilayer.
  • The steroid–receptor complex often binds DNA and changes gene expression.
Mission 2

Reception, transduction, response

  • Reception: the ligand binds; the receptor changes shape.
  • Transduction: the shape change sets off a sequence of molecular changes inside the cell: a signal transduction pathway.
  • Response: e.g. an enzyme activated, a gene switched on, a molecule secreted.
  • The same ligand can cause different responses in different cells: it depends on the receptor, relay and target proteins each cell contains.
  • Pathways can amplify the signal.
Quick check
?In humans, the hormone epinephrine causes liver cells to break down glycogen and release glucose, and it causes heart muscle cells to contract more strongly. Which statement best explains how the same hormone produces different responses in these two cell types?
Mission check

?Cells of type P respond to hormone X, and cells of type Q do not. Both cell types are exposed to the same concentration of X. When Q cells are given a gene that makes them produce the receptor protein for X found in P cells, the Q cells respond to X. Which claim is best supported by these observations?

Every cell hears the hormone. Only cells with the matching receptor listen.

Mission 3

Receptors that are channels

  • Some receptors are themselves ion channels: ligand-gated channels.
  • Acetylcholine (ACh) receptor on a muscle fiber: ACh binds outside → the channel opens.
  • Na⁺ diffuses in down its concentration gradient → inside becomes less negative: depolarization.
  • This may trigger an action potential and contraction.
  • Fast (milliseconds): no second messenger.
  • Ligand binding can open or close a ligand-gated channel.
Quick check
?A muscle fiber has a resting membrane potential of −70 mV. When acetylcholine is applied, the potential changes to −50 mV within 1 ms. If sodium ions are first removed from the fluid outside the fiber, acetylcholine still binds but the potential stays at −70 mV. What do these results show?
Mission 3

G protein-coupled receptors

  • A GPCR is one protein chain that crosses the membrane seven times.
  • 1: The ligand binds outside → the receptor changes shape.
  • 2: The shape change activates a G protein on the inner face: it releases GDP and binds GTP.
  • 3: The active G protein switches on an effector enzyme, e.g. adenylyl cyclase.
  • The G protein switches itself off by hydrolyzing GTP to GDP.
  • Humans have hundreds of different GPCRs: for smell, light, many hormones and neurotransmitters.
Mission 3

Epinephrine (adrenaline) and cAMP

  • 1: Epinephrine binds a GPCR on a liver cell. It stays outside.
  • 2: The G protein activates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • 3: cAMP activates protein kinase A. Kinases add phosphate from ATP to the next protein, changing its shape and activity: a phosphorylation cascade.
  • 4: The last enzyme, glycogen phosphorylase, breaks down glycogen → glucose leaves the cell.
  • Phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP, switching the signal off when epinephrine leaves.
  • Say: "cAMP relays the signal inside the cell" — not: "cAMP is a second hormone sent to other cells".
Mission 3Worked example

Amplification

Illustrative data: suppose each active molecule activates or makes this many of the next.

1. 1 receptor → 10 active adenylyl cyclase (via G proteins).

2. Each adenylyl cyclase → 100 cAMP. Total: 10 × 100 = 1 000 cAMP.

3. Each cAMP → 1 active kinase. Total: 1 000.

4. Each kinase → 10 active phosphorylase. Total: 10 000.

5. Each phosphorylase → 1 000 glucose. Total: 10 000 × 1 000 = 10 000 000.

  • Overall amplification = 10 × 100 × 1 × 10 × 1 000 = 10⁷.
  • Multiply the step factors; never add them. A ×1 step adds nothing.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events after epinephrine (adrenaline) binds to its receptor on a liver cell?
Mission 3

What the response can be

  • Signaling cascades can lead to: changed enzyme activity, secretion of molecules, cell growth and division, changes in gene expression.
  • Changes in gene expression can alter phenotype: e.g. yeast mating pheromones switch on mating genes; ethylene switches on enzymes that ripen fruit; cytokines switch on genes for cell division; HOX genes set animal body plans.
  • Some pathways trigger apoptosis: programmed cell death. E.g. cells between developing fingers die, separating them.
  • Apoptosis is a normal, controlled process, not damage.
Mission 3

When the pathway changes

  • A mutation in any part of the receptor, or in any relay protein, can change what happens downstream.
  • Ligand-binding domain altered → ligand cannot bind → no response.
  • Intracellular domain or relay protein stuck "on" → response with no ligand. Some cancers involve relay proteins that stay active.
  • Chemicals can activate or inhibit any component:
  • Cholera toxin locks a G protein in its active form → cAMP stays high in gut cells → Cl⁻ and water pour out → severe diarrhea.
  • Beta-blocker drugs bind epinephrine receptors in the heart without activating them → heart rate and force do not rise.
Quick check
?A mutation changes the intracellular domain of a G protein-coupled receptor so that it activates its G protein whether or not ligand is bound. The ligand-binding domain is unchanged. What is predicted?
Mission check

?Drug Z inhibits phosphodiesterase, the enzyme that breaks down cAMP. Liver cells treated with Z are given a short pulse of epinephrine, which is then washed away. Compared with untreated cells, what is predicted?

Each step multiplies the signal, and each step can be switched on, off, or jammed. That is why so many drugs and toxins act on pathways.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Make a guess: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Mission 4

Where is the receptor?

  • A hormone acts only by binding a receptor with a complementary shape.
  • Polar hormones (proteins, peptides, adrenaline) cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer.
  • So their receptors are on the cell-surface membrane. The hormone stays outside.
  • Steroid hormones (e.g. estradiol, testosterone) are non-polar.
  • They diffuse through the bilayer and bind receptors inside: in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
  • AP: the receptor's ligand-binding domain recognizes a specific ligand, a peptide or a small molecule.
Quick check
?Estrogen is a small, nonpolar steroid hormone that binds a receptor inside its target cells. Insulin is a peptide hormone of 51 amino acids whose receptor is in the plasma membrane. Which statement best explains this difference in where the receptors are located?
Mission 4

The second messenger model

  • Adrenaline is the first messenger. It binds a receptor on the surface of a liver cell.
  • The receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor: binding changes its shape and activates a G protein inside the cell.
  • This activates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase.
  • The enzyme converts ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • cAMP activates protein kinase, which phosphorylates enzymes, switching on glycogen → glucose.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next: the signal is amplified.
  • Some receptors are ligand-gated channels instead: binding opens or closes the channel directly.
Quick check
?A few epinephrine (adrenaline) molecules binding to receptors on a liver cell can lead to the release of millions of glucose molecules. Which statement best explains this amplification?
Mission 4

Steroids switch genes on and off

  • The steroid diffuses into the cell.
  • It binds its receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus: a hormone–receptor complex.
  • The complex acts as a transcription factor: it binds a specific DNA sequence near the target gene.
  • RNA polymerase transcribes the gene → more mRNA → more of the protein.
  • New mRNA and protein take time: steroid responses take minutes to hours, not seconds.
Quick check
?Cells respond to testosterone only after about 30 minutes, and the response is abolished by a drug that blocks transcription. The same cells respond to epinephrine (adrenaline) within seconds, and this response is unaffected by the drug. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Inside liver cells, the enzyme phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to an inactive product. Caffeine inhibits phosphodiesterase. Liver cells are given caffeine and then a low dose of adrenaline. Compared with cells given adrenaline alone, what is predicted?

The second messenger is the amplifier: anything that keeps cAMP around turns the volume up.

Mission 5

One genome, many cell types

  • Almost every cell in a body carries the same genome.
  • Each cell uses only some of its genes. Which ones decides what the cell becomes.
  • Gene expression: transcription → translation → the protein does its job.
  • Transcription is the main switch. No transcription: no mRNA, no protein.
  • Constitutive genes: expressed all the time. Inducible genes: expressed only when a signal is present.
  • Which genes are expressed, and how much, sets the phenotype: cell specialization.
Quick check
?Developing red blood cells produce large amounts of the beta chain of hemoglobin, but liver cells produce none, although both cell types contain the gene. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 5

Transcription factors: the switches

  • Transcription factors: proteins that bind specific base sequences in DNA and control transcription.
  • In eukaryotes, RNA polymerase cannot start alone. Transcription factors bind the promoter first; then RNA polymerase binds.
  • Activators stimulate transcription. Repressors inhibit it.
  • Repressors are negative regulatory molecules: they bind DNA and block transcription.
  • Activators also bind enhancers: sequences that may lie thousands of base pairs away, upstream or downstream of the gene.
  • Genes that share one regulatory sequence respond to the same transcription factor: a group is switched on together.
  • Each cell type has its own mix of transcription factors, so its own set of genes is on.
  • Say: "the promoter is a DNA sequence; transcription factors are proteins that bind it" — not: "the promoter is a protein".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of yeast, protein P represses gene G by binding to a DNA sequence next to the promoter of gene G. A mutation changes this DNA sequence so that protein P can no longer bind to it. Which prediction about cells with this mutation is correct?
Mission 5

Estrogen switches genes on

  • Estrogen is a steroid hormone: lipid-soluble, so it diffuses through the phospholipid bilayer.
  • (1) Inside the cell it binds a receptor protein.
  • (2) In the nucleus, the hormone–receptor complex binds a specific base sequence near its target genes.
  • (3) The complex acts as a transcription factor: RNA polymerase binds; the target genes are transcribed.
  • A signal reaching a cell can end in a change in gene expression: new proteins, new cell function.
  • Say: "the estrogen–receptor complex binds the DNA" — not: "estrogen binds the DNA".
Quick check
?Estrogen is a steroid hormone that passes through the plasma membrane of its target cells. How does estrogen alter the pattern of gene expression in these cells?
Mission 5

Epigenetics: switching without rewriting

  • Epigenetics: heritable changes in gene expression without any change to the base sequence of DNA.
  • Chemical tags on the DNA, and on the histone proteins it wraps around, turn genes down or up.
  • Tags can stay in place through mitosis: daughter cells keep the same genes switched off.
  • Epigenetic changes are reversible modifications of DNA or histones.
Mission 5

Methyl groups and histones

  • (1) DNA methylation: methyl groups (–CH3) are added to cytosine bases, often in a gene's promoter.
  • Methylated promoter → transcription repressed → gene silenced.
  • Transcription factors and RNA polymerase can no longer bind the promoter.
  • (2) Loosely wound DNA can be transcribed; tightly wound DNA cannot. Acetyl groups on histones loosen the winding.
  • Decreased acetylation of histones → DNA packed tightly → transcription inhibited.
  • Both kinds of modification can be removed again, switching the gene back on.
  • Say: "methylation silences the gene; its base sequence is unchanged" — not: "methylation mutates the gene".
Quick check
?Cytosine bases in the DNA of a gene's promoter become methylated. What is the effect on the gene?
Quick check
?In a skin cell, the promoter of gene G is methylated and the gene is not expressed. The cell divides by mitosis. What is the most likely state of gene G in the daughter cells, and why?
Mission check

?In some plants, gene F codes for a protein that stops the plant flowering. After several weeks of cold, the histones around gene F are modified and gene F stops being transcribed. When the weather warms, the new cells made by mitosis in the growing shoot also do not transcribe gene F, and the plant flowers. The base sequence of gene F is unchanged. Which statement explains these observations?

A few weeks of cold, a gene switched off, and a memory carried through every new cell. No gene was changed.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hormones are secreted into the blood and reach almost every cell; only target cells with complementary receptors respond.

Mission 2

  • A ligand binds a receptor with a complementary binding site; only target cells with that receptor respond.
  • Signals act by direct contact, over short distances, or over long distances in the blood.
  • Hydrophilic ligands use transmembrane receptors; hydrophobic steroids use intracellular receptors. Binding starts a signal transduction pathway.

Mission 3

  • Ligand-gated channels change membrane potential in milliseconds; GPCRs act through G proteins, enzymes and second messengers such as cAMP.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next, so a few ligand molecules cause a large response; multiply the step factors.
  • Mutations and chemicals that change any component can switch a pathway on, off, or leave it stuck.

Mission 4

  • Polar hormones bind cell-surface receptors and stay outside the cell.
  • Adrenaline's message is relayed and amplified inside by cAMP and protein kinase.
  • Non-polar steroids diffuse through the bilayer to intracellular receptors.

Mission 5

  • Every cell carries the same genome; cell types differ in which genes are transcribed.
  • Transcription factors bind specific DNA sequences to switch genes on or off.
  • Estrogen works through a receptor that becomes a transcription factor.
  • Epigenetic tags (DNA methylation, histone modification) change expression without changing the sequence, and pass through mitosis.
