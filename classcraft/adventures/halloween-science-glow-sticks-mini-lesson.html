Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a glow stick glow without getting hot?

Make a guess: How can a glow stick glow without getting hot?

Mission 1

What's inside a glow stick

  • A glow stick holds two solutions, kept apart.
  • One is hydrogen peroxide, sealed in a thin glass tube.
  • Around it, in the plastic tube, is a second solution: an oxalate ester and a dye.
  • Bend the stick: the glass snaps and the two solutions mix.
Mission 1

No mixing, no reaction

  • Particles can only react when they collide.
  • Before you bend the stick, the two solutions can't meet: no reaction, no glow.
  • Once they mix, their particles collide and react, and the glow starts.
Quick check
?Why doesn't a glow stick glow before you bend it?
Mission 1

Where the light comes from

  • The chemicals store energy. The reaction releases some of it.
  • Some is passed to the dye molecules, which give it out as light. The rest warms the surroundings very slightly.
  • Light made directly by a chemical reaction is called chemiluminescence.
  • The dye sets the colour: different dyes give different colours.
Mission 1

Hot light and cold light

  • A filament bulb glows because its filament is extremely hot.
  • A glow stick's light doesn't come from heat. It comes straight from the reaction.
  • The energy comes out slowly, over hours. The heat escapes as fast as it is made, so the stick barely warms up.
Quick check
?A glow stick barely warms up while it glows. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?How can a glow stick glow without getting hot?

The light comes from a reaction, not from heat: this is chemiluminescence.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a glow stick last longer in the freezer?

Make a guess: Why does a glow stick last longer in the freezer?

Mission 2

Collision theory

  • Particles can only react when they collide.
  • Not every collision works. Particles must collide with at least a minimum amount of energy: the activation energy.
  • The rate of reaction is how fast reactants are used up, or products are made.
Mission 2

What temperature does

  • Heating makes particles move faster.
  • They collide more often, and more collisions have at least the activation energy.
  • More successful collisions each second: the rate increases.
  • Cooling does the opposite: the rate decreases.
Mission 2

Brightness shows the rate

  • The more particles react each second, the more light the stick gives out each second.
  • Faster reaction: brighter, but the chemicals are used up sooner, so it fades sooner.
Quick check
?Two identical glow sticks are bent at the same moment. One goes into iced water, the other into warm water. What happens?
Mission 2

The freezer trick

  • In a freezer, the particles still move, but more slowly.
  • Fewer collisions, and fewer with enough energy: the reaction slows right down.
  • The stick is dimmer, but uses its chemicals slowly, so it lasts longer.
  • Take it out and it brightens as it warms. The cold slowed the reaction; it didn't stop it.
Quick check
?Which statement about a glow stick in a freezer is correct?
Mission 2

Same light, different speed

  • The amount of chemical in the stick sets roughly how much light it can give out in total.
  • Temperature changes how fast that light comes out.
  • Warm: bright, but short. Cold: dim, but long.
Mission check

?Why does a glow stick last longer in the freezer?

The cold slows the reaction. The chemicals are used up more slowly, so the glow is dimmer but lasts longer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: What's the fairest way to test the freezer trick?

Make a guess: What's the fairest way to test the freezer trick?

Mission 3

Three kinds of variable

  • Independent variable: the one you change. Here, temperature.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure. Here, how fast the glow fades.
  • Control variables: everything you keep the same, so only temperature can cause a difference.
Quick check
?In this test, which of these is a control variable?
Mission 3

Plan it, then try it at home

  • Three water baths: iced, room temperature, warm (warm enough to hold your hand in comfortably; an adult checks it). Check each with a thermometer and top it up to keep it steady.
  • Glow sticks of the same brand, size and colour, bent at the same moment.
  • At set times, take each stick out for the same few seconds and read it with the same phone and app, propped at the same marked distance, in a dark room. Take the first reading straight after bending.
  • Record the time for each stick's reading to fall to half its first reading.
Quick check
?A student puts a green glow stick in iced water and a pink one in warm water, then compares how fast each fades. What is the main problem with her method?
Mission 3

Measuring well

  • "When I can't see it any more" depends on your eyes and the room. It's subjective.
  • A light-meter app gives a number. Phone readings are estimates, but that's fine here: each stick is only compared with its own first reading.
  • Repeat each temperature. Repeats show whether results are repeatable and help you spot anomalous ones.
  • Leave out any result that is clearly anomalous, then calculate a mean of the rest.
Mission 3

Stay safe at home

  • Try it with an adult. Never cut or break open a glow stick: inside are sharp glass and chemicals that irritate skin and eyes.
  • Warm bath: comfortable to hold your hand in, never hot. Never use boiling water or a microwave.
  • On skin: wash with soap and water. In eyes: rinse with water for at least 15 minutes, and tell an adult.

🧪 Try it at home, with an adult. Keep every stick sealed.

Quick check
?Which plan is safe?
Mission check

?What's the fairest way to test the freezer trick?

Change only the temperature, and time the fade with an instrument, not by eye.

Finale

Nature's glow sticks

  • Fireflies make their light with a chemical reaction too.
  • An enzyme called luciferase speeds that reaction up and isn't used up: it's a catalyst.
  • Many deep-sea anglerfish have a glowing lure, lit by bacteria that live inside it.
Quick check
?Luciferase speeds up the firefly's light reaction and is not used up. What is luciferase?
Recap

The big ideas

  • Bending mixes two solutions, so their particles can collide and react.
  • The reaction passes some energy to a dye, which gives it out as light: chemiluminescence.
  • Warmer particles collide more often and with more energy: a faster reaction.
  • Warm: bright but short. Cold: dim but long. Roughly the same light in total.
  • A fair test changes only the independent variable. An instrument replaces a judgement with a number.
