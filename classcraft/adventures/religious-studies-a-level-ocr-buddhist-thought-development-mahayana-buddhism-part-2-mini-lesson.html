Mission 1

From anicca to emptiness

  • Anicca: impermanence. Every conditioned thing arises, changes and passes away.
  • Early teaching: because things change and depend on conditions, there is no fixed self in a person (anatta).
  • Early analysis (Abhidharma) broke the world into tiny elements, dharmas, each with its own fixed nature.
  • Mahayana takes the next step: the dharmas too are empty. Nothing at all has a fixed, independent nature.
  • Sunyata: emptiness. Not "nothing exists" but "nothing exists by itself".
  • Your spec gives both forms: sunyata/sunnata.
Mission 1Read the source

Impermanent, not-self

> "'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller 1881)

> "'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity." (Dhammapada 279, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "forms are unreal" renders sabbe dhamma anatta: all things are not-self.

Q1 Which mark of existence does verse 277 teach?

Show answer

Anicca, impermanence.

Q2 How could verse 279 be read as a seed of emptiness?

Show answer

If all things, not just persons, lack self, nothing has a fixed nature of its own.

Mission 1

The Perfection of Wisdom sutras

  • Prajnaparamita: "perfection of wisdom": the sixth paramita, and a family of Mahayana sutras.
  • Earliest: the Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines, from about the 1st century BCE–1st century CE.
  • Longer versions (25,000; 100,000 lines) and very short ones: the Diamond Sutra and the Heart Sutra.
  • A copy of the Diamond Sutra from Dunhuang, printed in 868 CE, is the world's oldest dated printed book.
  • Their message: the bodhisattva sees all things as empty, and so acts with compassion free of clinging.
  • Importance: the philosophical root of Madhyamaka (see Mission 2) and of much of Mahayana practice.

Mission 1

The Heart Sutra: setting

  • Very short: in Xuanzang's Chinese version (7th century) about 260 characters.
  • Chanted daily in Zen, Tibetan and many other Mahayana communities.
  • Avalokitesvara, bodhisattva of compassion, deep in the perfection of wisdom, sees that the five skandhas are empty, and is freed from all suffering.
  • He explains this to Sariputra, the disciple famed in early texts for wisdom (and for Abhidharma).
  • So compassion speaks, wisdom is the message, and the early tradition is the listener.
  • Scholars debate its origin: Jan Nattier (1992) argued it was put together in China and later rendered into Sanskrit; others disagree.
Mission 1Read the source

Form and emptiness

Heart Sutra (paraphrase of the core lines):

  • Form is emptiness; emptiness is form.
  • Emptiness is not other than form; form is not other than emptiness.
  • The same is true of feeling, perception, mental formations and consciousness.

Q1 Why does the sutra say both "form is emptiness" and "emptiness is form"?

Show answer

Form lacks fixed nature; and emptiness is not a separate thing behind forms, it is how forms are.

Q2 Which earlier teaching do the five listed items come from?

Show answer

The five skandhas, the aggregates that make up a person.

Mission 1

Empty of what?

  • Empty of svabhava: own-being, a nature that exists by itself, unchanged and independent.
  • A cup is empty of own-being: it depends on clay, a potter, heat, and a use; break it and "cup" is gone.
  • But the cup still holds tea: emptiness does not deny that things appear and work.
  • So form and emptiness are not two things: emptiness is just the way forms exist.

Mission 1

"No suffering, no path"

  • The sutra goes on: in emptiness there is no eye, ear or mind; no ignorance and no ending of ignorance; no suffering, no origin, no cessation, no path; no wisdom and no attainment (paraphrase).
  • Not a rejection of the Buddha's teaching: the teachings are not things with own-being either.
  • The four noble truths are true and useful (like a raft); clinging to them as fixed objects is another trap.
  • Because the bodhisattva grasps nothing, the mind has no obstruction and no fear.
  • Links to skilful means: teachings are tools, not idols.
Mission 1

The mantra and practice

  • The sutra ends with a mantra: gate gate paragate parasamgate bodhi svaha.
  • Meaning, roughly: gone, gone, gone beyond, gone completely beyond, awakening, hail!
  • Chanting: in Zen temples, Tibetan monasteries, funerals and daily practice.
  • Fearlessness: nothing fixed to lose.
  • Compassion: if self and other are empty of fixed boundaries, others' suffering is not "theirs only".
  • Non-attachment in daily life: even to one's views, achievements and religious identity.
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the Perfection of Wisdom teachings represent reality?

  • Claim: The Mahayana teaching of emptiness represents reality as it truly is.
  • For: It follows from anicca and dependent arising: if all things depend on conditions, none can have own-being.
  • For: It frees from clinging, which early Buddhism names as the cause of suffering.
  • Against: Theravadins do not accept these sutras as the Buddha's word; the Pali canon does not deny the dharmas so radically.
  • Against: "No path, no attainment" may sound like nihilism and confuse practitioners.
  • Judgement criteria: Continuity with early teaching? Coherence? Does it help practice?

Mission 2

Nagarjuna and the Middle Way

  • Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), Indian monk-philosopher, probably from south India.
  • Main work: Mulamadhyamakakarika, the Fundamental Verses on the Middle Way: 27 chapters of short verses.
  • Founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school.
  • He gives the Perfection of Wisdom's emptiness (see Mission 1) a rigorous philosophical argument.
  • Honoured in Tibet and East Asia as a "second Buddha" and patriarch of many schools.
  • The "middle way": between eternalism (things exist permanently in themselves) and annihilationism (things are nothing at all).
Mission 2

The target: svabhava

  • Svabhava (Pali sabhava), own-being: a nature a thing would have by itself, independent of causes and parts, unchanging.
  • Some Abhidharma schools (e.g. the Sarvastivada) held that the basic elements, the dharmas, each have svabhava.
  • Nagarjuna's claim: nothing has svabhava. Everything is empty (sunyata/sunnata) of own-being.
  • Why it matters: clinging assumes things are solid and fixed. Show they are not, and clinging loses its object.
Mission 2

The argument from dependence

  • Premise 1: If something had own-being, it would exist by itself: not made by causes, not changing.
  • Premise 2: But things arise from causes and change: the Buddha's teaching of dependent arising (pratityasamutpada).
  • Conclusion: So nothing has own-being. To be dependently arisen is to be empty.
  • His method: show that each view, taken as fixed, leads to absurdity (a reductio).
  • Example (verse 1.1, paraphrase): nothing arises from itself, from something else, from both, or from no cause: four options, each examined and rejected when "arising" means arising with own-being.
Mission 2Read the source

Emptiness and dependent arising

Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18 (paraphrase):

  • Whatever arises dependently, that we call emptiness. That is a name given in dependence (a designation). That itself is the Middle Way.

Q1 Which two ideas does Nagarjuna say are the same?

Show answer

Dependent arising and emptiness.

Q2 Why is this called the Middle Way?

Show answer

Dependent things are not nothing, so not annihilation; they lack own-being, so not eternalism.

Mission 2

Emptiness is not nothing

  • Critics called Madhyamaka nihilism: if all is empty, nothing exists, so nothing matters.
  • Nagarjuna's answer: emptiness makes change, cause and the path possible. If things had fixed natures, nothing could change and no one could be freed (24.14, 24.20, paraphrase).
  • Emptiness of emptiness: emptiness is not a new thing or final theory. Taken as a "view", it becomes a disease (13.8, paraphrase).
  • Misunderstood, emptiness is like a snake grabbed by the wrong end (24.11, paraphrase): it bites.
  • So emptiness is a medicine for clinging, not a picture of a void.
Mission 2

Two truths

  • Conventional (relative) truth, samvrti-satya: the everyday world of persons, cups, causes, teachings: true as they function, in language and use.
  • Ultimate truth, paramartha-satya: all those things are empty of own-being.
  • Not two worlds: one world, seen two ways.
  • Example: "I am a student" is conventionally true; ultimately there is no fixed "I" with own-being.
  • The second truth does not cancel the first: both are needed.
Mission 2Read the source

Why both truths?

Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.8–10 (paraphrase):

  • The Buddhas teach by relying on two truths: conventional truth and ultimate truth.
  • Those who do not understand the difference do not understand the deep point of the Buddha's teaching.
  • Without relying on the conventional, the ultimate cannot be taught; without understanding the ultimate, nirvana is not reached.

Q1 Why can the ultimate not be taught without the conventional?

Show answer

Teaching uses words, concepts and examples, which are conventional.

Q2 Why is the distinction important for liberation?

Show answer

Seeing only the conventional, we cling; seeing the ultimate through the conventional, clinging ends.

Mission 2

Samsara and nirvana

  • Mulamadhyamakakarika 25.19–20 (paraphrase): there is not the slightest difference between samsara and nirvana; the limit of one is the limit of the other.
  • Not: "life as it is now, with craving, is already nirvana."
  • But: neither samsara nor nirvana has own-being. Nirvana is not another place or thing to escape to.
  • Nirvana is samsara seen without grasping: the same world, with clinging ended.
  • Your spec's wording: nirvana/nibbana; samsara and nirvana "should not be understood as different things".
Mission 2Diversity

Interpretations of emptiness

  • Madhyamaka (Nagarjuna; later Chandrakirti, 7th century): emptiness is the absence of own-being; no positive Absolute behind it.
  • Yogacara (4th century, Asanga, Vasubandhu): stresses the mind; emptiness as absence of the split between perceiver and perceived.
  • Buddha nature traditions and some Tibetan schools (shentong, "empty of other"): ultimate reality is empty of defilements but full of the qualities of awakening.
  • Theravada (suññata): emptiness of self in persons and things; does not accept the Mahayana sutras' wider claims.
  • Critics: Hindu and some Buddhist thinkers charged Madhyamaka with nihilism or self-refutation.
  • Scholars: Peter Harvey, An Introduction to Buddhism (2nd ed.), ch. 5; Peter Della Santina, Causality and Emptiness (2002).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Madhyamaka continuous with the Buddha?

  • Claim: Nagarjuna's teaching continues the historical Buddha's teaching, not breaks with it.
  • For: He builds on dependent arising and the middle way, and cites the Buddha's advice to his disciple Katyayana to avoid "exists" and "does not exist" (15.7; cf. Samyutta Nikaya 12.15).
  • For: Like the Buddha, he refuses speculative views and aims at ending clinging.
  • Against: He denies own-being to the dharmas themselves, going beyond what the Pali texts teach.
  • Against: Critics ask: is "all views are empty" itself a view? Nagarjuna replies that he asserts no thesis (Vigrahavyavartani, paraphrase); critics doubt that this is coherent.
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to early texts; coherence; value for practice. If words are merely conventional, do teachings keep their value? (the earlier screen: yes, as the means to the ultimate.)

Mission 3

From Chan to Zen

  • Zen is the Japanese form of the Chinese Chan school; both words come from Sanskrit dhyana, "meditation".
  • Tradition traces Chan to Bodhidharma (5th–6th century), an Indian monk said to have sat facing a wall for nine years.
  • Huineng (638–713), the "Sixth Patriarch", taught sudden awakening: our Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly.
  • Two lineages reached Japan: Rinzai (brought by Eisai, 1141–1215) and Soto (brought by Dōgen, 1200–1253).
  • Zen is a Mahayana school: it builds on Buddha nature and emptiness.
Mission 3Read the source

A transmission outside the scriptures

A four-line verse, attributed to Bodhidharma but first found in Song-dynasty China (11th–12th centuries), sums up Zen's self-understanding (rendering of the Chinese):

> A special transmission outside the scriptures;

> not founded on words and letters;

> pointing directly to the human mind;

> seeing into one's nature and becoming Buddha.

  • The Flower Sermon (paraphrase; also first recorded in Song China): the Buddha silently holds up a flower; only Mahakashyapa smiles. The Buddha says he has passed the true Dharma to him, beyond words.
  • So Zen claims a mind-to-mind transmission, teacher to student, in an unbroken lineage.

Q1 What does "pointing directly" leave out?

Show answer

Study of doctrine and long explanation: the teacher points to the student's own mind.

Q2 Why does the Flower Sermon need Mahakashyapa?

Show answer

Transmission is confirmed by a student's awakening, not by a text.

Mission 3

Zazen: seated meditation

  • Zazen = "seated meditation", the heart of Zen practice.
  • Posture: cross-legged (full or half lotus) or kneeling, back upright, eyes half-open, hands in the cosmic mudra (an oval below the navel).
  • Attention rests on posture and breath; thoughts are let come and go without being followed.
  • Done in a zendo (meditation hall), often for long periods; walking meditation (kinhin) between sittings.
  • Intensive retreats (sesshin) may involve many hours of zazen a day for a week.
Mission 3

Koan: a puzzle reason cannot solve

  • A koan is a story, question or saying from an earlier master, set by a teacher.
  • Famous examples: a monk asks Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha nature; he answers "Mu" ("no" / "nothing"). Hakuin (1686–1769): "What is the sound of one hand?"
  • Collections: the Blue Cliff Record (1125) and the Gateless Gate (Mumonkan, 1228; "Mu" is its first case).
  • The student holds the koan in zazen until ordinary reasoning is exhausted; in private interview (dokusan) the teacher tests the response.
  • The answer is not a clever explanation but a direct expression of insight.

Mission 3

Satori: awakening

  • Satori = "awakening"; kensho = "seeing one's nature". Many writers use them together; in Rinzai, kensho often names a first glimpse and satori a deeper awakening.
  • What is seen is not a new object but one's own Buddha nature: the mind free of the false split between self and world.
  • Zen stresses that it can come suddenly, in a moment (the sound of a stone striking bamboo, a shout, a word).
  • It is not the end of practice: awakening is deepened and lived in daily life.
  • Continuity: the earlier tradition's goal of nirvana is seen not as a distant place but as realised here and now.
Mission 3Diversity

Rinzai and Soto

  • Rinzai (from the Chinese master Linji, d. 866): koan practice, often in a graded curriculum (systematised in the line of Hakuin); vigorous effort; aims at a breakthrough to satori (awakening); sitters face into the hall.
  • Soto (from the Chinese Caodong line): shikantaza, "just sitting", with no object or goal; sitters face the wall.
  • Dōgen: practice and realisation are one. Zazen is not a means to a later awakening; sitting is itself the expression of Buddha nature.
  • Both use both: Dōgen commented on many koans; Rinzai monks sit for long hours.
  • Shared: lineage, the teacher's authority, monastic discipline, daily work and chanting.
  • Spec issue: differences of emphasis, not two different goals.

Mission 3

Zen and scripture in practice

  • "Not founded on words" is not "no words". Zen monasteries chant the Heart Sutra daily; Huineng is said to have awakened on hearing the Diamond Sutra.
  • The image often used: a sutra is a finger pointing at the moon. Do not mistake the finger for the moon.
  • Stories of Zen iconoclasm: a master burns his sutra commentaries; Linji says "If you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha" (paraphrase): cling to no image, not even of the Buddha.
  • Authority lies in the lineage of awakened teachers, who confirm a student's insight (dharma transmission).
  • Zen has a large literature of its own: koan collections, records of masters, Dōgen's Shōbōgenzō.
  • Suggested reading in your spec: D. Cush, Buddhism (1994), chapter 4.
Mission 3

What changed from earlier Buddhism?

  • Continuity: meditation at the centre; the goal is awakening; monastic life and precepts; the Buddha's warning that reciting texts is not enough:

> "The thoughtless man, even if he can recite a large portion (of the law), but is not a doer of it, has no share in the priesthood…" (Dhammapada 19, Müller 1881)

  • Changes: sudden rather than gradual awakening; Buddha nature already present; authority in a teacher lineage more than in texts; paradox and the koan; manual work as practice (a Chan saying: a day without work, a day without food).
  • Chinese culture shaped Chan: Daoist love of naturalness and paradox is often noted.
  • Spec issue: how and if Siddhartha's original teachings can be seen in Zen.
Mission 3Evaluate

Can awakening be taught without theory, ritual and words?

  • Claim: Zen shows that the truth of Buddhism lies beyond theory, ritual and language.
  • For: the Flower Sermon and the transmission verse place truth in direct seeing.
  • For: koans break down conceptual thinking; satori is an experience, not a doctrine learned.
  • For: the Buddha himself refused to answer some speculative questions as not leading to awakening.
  • Against: Zen is highly ritualised: bowing, chanting, formal meals, robes, ceremonies of transmission.
  • Against: Zen has a huge literature, and its "rejection of words" is itself taught in words.
  • Against: without doctrine, how can a student tell real insight from self-deception? Zen's answer is the teacher, which shifts authority to people.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the "rejection" literal or a teaching device? Does Zen practice match its rhetoric? Is a teacher's confirmation a reliable authority?

Mission 4

Amitabha and his vow

  • Amitabha ("Infinite Light"; also Amitayus, "Infinite Life"); Japanese Amida.
  • The Larger Sukhavativyuha Sutra (paraphrase): long ago a monk, Dharmakara, made 48 vows to create a perfect land for all beings, and practised for aeons to fulfil them.
  • He became the Buddha Amitabha: so, Pure Land Buddhists believe, the vows are now in force.
  • The 18th vow ("Primal Vow"), paraphrase: anyone who sincerely trusts, longs to be born in his land and calls his name, even ten times, will be born there (except those guilty of the gravest offences).
  • Attendants: the bodhisattvas Avalokitesvara (compassion) and Mahasthamaprapta (wisdom).
Mission 4

Sukhavati: the Pure Land

  • Sukhavati, the "Land of Bliss", lies in the west.
  • Three core texts: the Larger and Smaller Sukhavativyuha Sutras and the Contemplation Sutra (visualising Amitabha and his land).
  • Described as jewelled, fragrant, filled with the sound of the Dhamma; no hells or animal births.
  • Purpose: perfect conditions to hear the Dhamma and reach enlightenment easily, from where one returns to help others.
  • Readings differ: a real place after death; a symbol of the awakened mind; or (for Shinran) birth there is itself awakening.
  • The nembutsu: "Namu Amida Butsu", "I take refuge in Amida Buddha" (namu = homage to, or refuge in).
Mission 4

Pure Land in China

  • Huiyuan (334–416): a society at Mount Lu (402) meditated on Amitabha, aiming at birth in the Pure Land.
  • Tanluan (476–542): stressed other-power: birth depends on Amitabha's vow, not one's own strength.
  • Daochuo (562–645): we live in the age of the decline of the Dharma (Chinese mofa, Japanese mappō), when few can awaken by their own effort.
  • Shandao (613–681): vocal recitation of the name is the main practice.
  • Nianfo (Chinese), nembutsu (Japanese): "mindfulness of the Buddha", which came to mean calling his name.
  • Pure Land practice spread across many Chinese schools; it was rarely a separate school.

Mission 4

Honen and Jodo-shu

  • Honen (1133–1212), a Tendai monk, despaired of awakening by his own effort.
  • In 1175, reading the Chinese master Shandao, he turned to the exclusive nembutsu (senju nembutsu); this marks the founding of Jodo-shu (Pure Land school).
  • Work: Senchakushu (1198): in the age of mappō (the age of the Dharma's decline, when few can awaken by their own effort), choose the one practice Amida chose in his vow: reciting his name.
  • Practice: recite the nembutsu constantly, with faith in the vow, until death; tradition says he recited it tens of thousands of times a day.
  • Honen himself kept monastic precepts; his school spread to all classes, including women and the poor.
  • Jōdo-shū (OCR spelling).
Mission 4

Shinran and Jodo Shinshu

  • Shinran (1173–1263), Honen's disciple; exiled in 1207; married and called himself "neither monk nor layman".
  • Jodo Shinshu ("True Pure Land school"), today one of Japan's largest Buddhist denominations.
  • Key idea: shinjin, true entrusting, is given by Amida's other-power (tariki), not produced by the believer.
  • At the moment of shinjin, birth in the Pure Land is settled, in this life.
  • So the nembutsu is an expression of gratitude, not a practice that earns birth.
  • Tannisho ch. 3 (paraphrase): if even a good person is born in the Pure Land, how much more an evil person.
  • No monastic precepts; clergy marry; no rites for worldly benefits.
  • Jōdo Shinshū (OCR spelling).
Mission 4Diversity

Jodo-shu and Jodo Shinshu compared

  • Founder: Honen · Shinran.
  • Role of the nembutsu: the practice of reliance, repeated constantly · thanksgiving after shinjin.
  • When birth is settled: at death, met by Amida (deathbed practice matters) · at the moment of shinjin, in this life.
  • Self-power: recitation is still the believer's act, in reliance on the vow · all self-power given up; even faith is Amida's gift.
  • Clergy: monastic ordination and precepts (though most priests now marry) · married clergy from the start, no precepts.
  • Head temples: Chion-in, Kyoto · Nishi and Higashi Hongwanji, Kyoto.
  • Both: refuge in Amida alone; both rely on the Primal Vow.

Mission 4

Continuity and change

  • Roots in earlier teaching: recollection of the Buddha (buddhanussati) is in the Pali canon; Mahayana taught many buddhas, bodhisattva vows and the transfer of merit.
  • Changes:
  • Reliance on another's power, set against early texts:

"Self is the lord of self, who else could be the lord?" (Dhammapada 160, Müller)

"You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller)

  • Goal: birth in a Pure Land first, rather than Nibbana in this life.
  • Community: married clergy and lay-centred temples (Shinshu).
  • Modernity: Jodo Shinshu temples in America (from 1899) adopted Sunday services and hymns.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Pure Land Buddhism is an 'easy' path."

  • For: a classic image (attributed to Nagarjuna): the difficult path is walking overland; the easy path is sailing in a boat.
  • For: no need for long meditation, scholarship or monastic life: open to farmers, women, the poor.
  • For: in the age of mappō, it suits people as they really are.
  • Against: Shinran: true entrusting is the hardest thing of all, because it means giving up every trace of self-power.
  • Against: Honen's constant recitation, kept up for life, is demanding.
  • Against: "easy" describes Amida's provision, not the believer's effort: Pure Land Buddhists see it as compassion meeting people's limits, not laziness.
  • Judgement criteria: Easy to do, or easy to believe? Does "easy" mean less Buddhist? Does it fit the Buddha's teaching on effort?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Anicca (impermanence) and anatta (not-self) develop in Mahayana into sunyata: nothing has own-being (svabhava).
  • The Perfection of Wisdom sutras teach emptiness; the Heart Sutra: "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (paraphrase).
  • Even the four noble truths and the path are empty: useful, not to be clung to; the sutra is chanted daily and ends with the mantra "gate gate paragate…".

Mission 2

  • Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), Mulamadhyamakakarika: nothing has svabhava (own-being), because everything arises dependently (24.18).
  • Two truths: conventional and ultimate; the ultimate is taught through the conventional, and nirvana needs both (24.8–10).
  • Samsara and nirvana are not different things: both are empty; nirvana is samsara without grasping (25.19–20).

Mission 3

  • Zen (Chinese Chan) teaches that Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly in a sudden awakening (satori), passed on mind to mind in a teacher lineage.
  • Rinzai stresses koans and breakthrough; Soto stresses shikantaza, with practice and realisation one (Dōgen).
  • Scripture, ritual and words remain, but as pointers: a "special transmission outside the scriptures".

Mission 4

  • Amitabha's Primal Vow promises birth in the Pure Land (Sukhavati) to all who entrust themselves and call his name; the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu", is going for refuge in Amida.
  • Honen (Jodo-shu): constant recitation in reliance on the vow. Shinran (Jodo Shinshu): shinjin is Amida's gift; nembutsu is gratitude.
  • Debates: is it an "easy" path, and is it a gradual development or a radical change from the Buddha's teaching of self-effort?
