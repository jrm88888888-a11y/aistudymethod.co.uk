Mission 1

Mission 1: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Make a guess: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Mission 1

Diffusion

  • Particles move randomly, all the time.
  • More particles cross from the crowded side than from the other: net movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • This is movement down a concentration gradient.
  • It is passive: no energy from ATP.
  • At equal concentrations, particles still cross both ways. Net movement is zero.
  • Say: "net movement stops" — not: "the molecules stop moving".
Mission 1

Straight through the bilayer

  • Simple diffusion: particles pass between the phospholipids.
  • Only small non-polar molecules do this readily: O2, CO2.
  • Permeability of the bilayer depends only on size and polarity or charge. It is not selective in any other way.

Mission 1

Channel proteins

  • Facilitated diffusion: diffusion through a protein. Still passive; still down the gradient.
  • A channel protein spans the membrane with a water-filled, hydrophilic pore. Ions pass through it, avoiding the hydrophobic core.
  • Selective: the pore's width and the charges lining it let only certain ions through. A K⁺ channel passes K⁺ but not the smaller Na⁺.
  • Many are gated: open or closed. No ions pass when closed.
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts of water cross quickly.
  • Say: "a channel is specific" — not: "a channel is a hole any small particle can use".
Quick check
?A potassium channel in a cell membrane is open. Which statement describes the movement of ions through it?
Mission 1

Which route?

  • O2, CO2 → between the phospholipids.
  • Water → between the phospholipids, slowly.
  • Water → aquaporins, fast.
  • Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) → channel proteins.
  • The bilayer sorts only by size and charge. Proteins make a membrane selective: each lets through particular substances.
Quick check
?Artificial vesicles were made from a pure phospholipid bilayer. Oxygen crossed the bilayer rapidly, glucose very slowly and potassium ions hardly at all. When a potassium channel protein was inserted into the bilayer, potassium crossed rapidly but permeability to sodium ions and to glucose did not change. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?A hypothetical toxin binds to and blocks all of the channel and transport proteins in the plasma membrane of a cell. Which prediction about the cell, immediately after exposure to the toxin, is best supported?

Two routes, two rules: the bilayer lets through what is small and non-polar; proteins let through specific substances, ions included.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Make a guess: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Mission 2

Osmosis

  • Osmosis: the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.
  • The membrane lets water through but not the solute.
  • Water molecules cross randomly both ways; more cross towards the more concentrated solution.
  • Water moves from the lower solute concentration to the higher solute concentration.
  • Compared with another solution, one is hypotonic (lower solute concentration), hypertonic (higher) or isotonic (equal).
  • Say: "lower solute concentration" — not: "higher water concentration".
Quick check
?Two sucrose solutions, 0.2 mol dm⁻³ and 0.6 mol dm⁻³, are separated by a membrane that is permeable to water but not to sucrose. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Cells without a wall

  • Outside more dilute than the cytoplasm: water enters. The cell swells and can burst: lysis.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves. The cell shrinks and its surface puckers: crenation.
  • These are hypotonic and hypertonic surroundings.
  • Equal concentrations: water still crosses both ways, with no net movement.
  • This is a dynamic equilibrium.
  • Freshwater single-celled organisms, such as Paramecium, gain water all the time. A contractile vacuole expels it.
  • Multicellular animals keep their tissue fluid isotonic with their cells.
Mission 2

Cells with a wall

  • Outside more dilute: water enters. The cell contents push on the wall: turgor pressure. The cell is turgid.
  • The wall resists, so the cell does not burst.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves; the contents shrink; the membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis.
  • The wall is freely permeable: the gap fills with the outside solution.
  • No turgor pressure: the cell is flaccid.
  • Say: "the wall is freely permeable; the membrane is partially permeable" — not: "the wall keeps water out".
Quick check
?A strip of onion epidermis is mounted in a concentrated sucrose solution and viewed under a microscope. Which statement describes what happens to its cells?
Mission 2

Aquaporins

  • Water crosses the bilayer, but slowly.
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts cross quickly.
  • Evidence: Preston, Agre and colleagues (1992) made frog egg cells produce an aquaporin. In dilute solution these eggs swelled fast and burst; ordinary eggs barely swelled.
  • Cells with many aquaporins: kidney collecting-duct cells, root cells.
Mission 2

Isotonic fluids in medicine

  • A common drip fluid has about the same solute concentration as blood plasma: 0.9% sodium chloride.
  • It is isotonic with plasma.
  • Pure water would enter red blood cells by osmosis. They would swell and burst.
  • A concentrated solution would draw water out of them. They would shrink.
  • Organs for transplant are stored in an isotonic solution, so their cells neither swell nor shrink.
Quick check
?A kidney removed for transplantation is bathed in a solution while it is stored. Which type of solution is used, and why?
Mission check

?Jam contains so much sugar that most bacteria landing on it cannot grow. Which statement explains this?

Water follows solute, whether it is entering red blood cells from pure water or leaving bacteria for the jam.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Potato pieces gain mass in some sugar solutions and lose it in others. What does the point in between reveal?

Make a guess: Potato pieces gain mass in some sugar solutions and lose it in others. What does the point in between reveal?

Mission 3Practical

Water potential of plant tissue

  • Measure changes in length and mass; deduce the isotonic solute concentration.
  • Make a dilution series of sucrose from a stock solution: water and 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8.
  • Concentrations in mol dm⁻³.
  • Cut equal cylinders with a cork borer; blot; weigh each (or measure its length).
  • Leave in each solution for the same time at the same temperature. Blot the same way; reweigh.
  • % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. This allows for different starting masses.
  • Repeat at each concentration; use the mean.
Mission 3Read the data

Finding the zero point

Potato cylinders after 24 hours in sucrose. Illustrative data:

Sucrose (mol dm⁻³)0.00.20.40.60.8
Mean change in mass (%)+8.0+2.5−3.0−8.5−12.0
  • Example: 2.50 g → 2.70 g: (2.70 − 2.50) ÷ 2.50 × 100 = +8.0%.
  • Gain at 0.2, loss at 0.4: the line crosses zero between them.
  • Read from the graph, or interpolate: 0.2 + 0.2 × (2.5 ÷ 5.5) = 0.29 mol dm⁻³.
  • There, no net water movement: the solution matches the tissue.
  • 0.29 mol dm⁻³ is the isotonic concentration.
Quick check
?Potato cylinders were bathed for 24 hours in sucrose solutions and their mean percentage change in mass recorded (illustrative data): 0.0 mol dm⁻³, +8.0%; 0.2 mol dm⁻³, +2.5%; 0.4 mol dm⁻³, −3.0%; 0.6 mol dm⁻³, −8.5%; 0.8 mol dm⁻³, −12.0%. Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission 3

How reliable is each mean?

  • Repeat each concentration, e.g. five cylinders.
  • Standard deviation (SD): the spread of individual results around the mean.
  • Standard error (SE) = SD ÷ √n: how precisely the mean is known. More repeats, smaller SE.
  • Example: SD = 1.0%, n = 5: SE = 1.0 ÷ √5 = 0.45%.
  • Show SE as error bars on the graph of means.
  • Formulae need not be memorised; a calculator or spreadsheet does them.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose. Their percentage changes in mass had a mean of −3.0%, a standard deviation of 1.0% and a standard error of 0.45%. What does the standard error indicate?
Mission check

?Potato cylinders were left uncovered on a bench for two hours, so they lost some water by evaporation. They were then used in a mass-change experiment in sucrose solutions. Fresh cylinders from the same potato crossed zero change in mass at 0.29 mol dm⁻³. Where would the line for the dried cylinders cross zero?

The zero point marks the solution that matches the cells; change the cells and the zero point moves.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Make a guess: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Mission 4

Active transport

  • Active transport moves particles against a concentration gradient: from low to high concentration.
  • It needs energy from ATP, made in respiration.
  • It needs a membrane protein that binds the particle.
  • The proteins are pump proteins.
  • Each carrier binds only specific particles: active transport is selective.
  • Say: "active transport moves particles against their gradient" — not: "active transport is faster diffusion".
Mission 4

How a carrier pumps

  • (1) The particle binds to a specific site on the carrier, on one side of the membrane.
  • (2) ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pᵢ. The phosphate attaches to the carrier, which changes shape.
  • (3) The particle is released on the other side. The phosphate leaves; the carrier returns to its first shape.
  • No ATP, no shape change, no transport.
Quick check
?Root cells absorb nitrate ions from soil water in which the nitrate concentration is much lower than in the cell. When the roots are deprived of oxygen, nitrate uptake falls sharply. What explains these observations?
Mission 4

How do you know it is active?

  • Sign 1: the substance builds up above the outside concentration.
  • Sign 2: uptake falls sharply when the cell's ATP supply falls: no oxygen, or a respiratory inhibitor.
  • Diffusion is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. That is the test.
  • Ions leak back down their gradient. So a gradient needs a continuing energy supply, not a one-off.
Mission 4Read the data

  • A steady level is not zero movement: uptake was balancing leakage.
Quick check
?At 30 minutes, a drug that stops the cells from making ATP was added. Which claim is best supported by the data in the table?
Mission check

?Red blood cells hold K⁺ at a much higher concentration than the plasma around them. In donated blood stored at 4 °C, the cells slowly lose K⁺. When the cells are warmed to 37 °C with glucose, they take K⁺ back up to the original high level. Which explanation fits these observations?

A gradient against the odds is never free. Stop the energy supply and it runs down; restore it and the pumps rebuild it.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Make a guess: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Mission 5

A motor neurone

  • Cell body: nucleus and most of the cytoplasm.
  • Dendrites: many short fibres; carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: one long fibre; carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Axon terminals end at an effector (a muscle or gland).
Mission 5

Myelin

  • Many axons are wrapped in a myelin sheath.
  • Myelin is lipid-rich: an electrical insulator.
  • Gaps between sheath sections: nodes of Ranvier.
  • Non-myelinated axons have no sheath and no nodes.
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a neurone is correct?
Mission 5

A nerve is not a neuron

  • A nerve is a bundle of nerve fibres from many neurons.
  • Most nerves carry fibres of both sensory and motor neurons.
  • Transverse section: outer protective sheath of connective tissue.
  • Inside: myelinated fibres (axon ringed by pale myelin) and unmyelinated fibres (bare axons).
  • Say: "a nerve contains many neurons' fibres" — not: "a nerve is a long neuron".
Quick check
?A transverse section of a nerve shows an outer layer of connective tissue enclosing many small circular profiles. Some profiles have a thick pale ring around a central dot; others have no ring. What does the section show?
Mission 5

The resting potential

  • At rest, the inside of the axon is negative relative to the outside.
  • Typical value: about −70 mV. This is the resting potential.
  • A membrane with a charge difference across it is polarised.
  • Membrane potential = voltage inside relative to outside.
  • The neurone is not "off": it is held ready to fire.
Mission 5

How the resting potential is made

  • Sodium–potassium pump: uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
  • Result: Na⁺ high outside; K⁺ high inside. Two concentration gradients.
  • The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺: many K⁺ channels are open; most Na⁺ channels are closed.
  • So K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ diffuses in → more positive charge outside.
  • Large negatively charged proteins stay inside.
  • Say: "Na⁺ is actively transported out" — not: "Na⁺ diffuses out".
Quick check
?Measurements on a resting neurone show that potassium ions are about 30 times more concentrated inside than outside and sodium ions about 10 times more concentrated outside than inside, yet the inside is at −70 mV relative to the outside. Which explanation of the negative resting potential is consistent with these measurements?
Mission check

?A neurone is treated with a chemical that stops it producing ATP. Which prediction about its resting potential is correct?

Leaks never stop, so the pump never can: that is the energy cost of being ready.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Make a guess: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Mission 6

An action potential

  • A nerve impulse is an action potential: a brief reversal of the membrane potential, lasting about 1–2 ms.
  • Sodium ions move in: the inside briefly becomes positive. Potassium ions then move out: the inside becomes negative again.
  • The impulse is electrical because positive ions move. Each region of membrane triggers the next, so it travels along the fibre.
Mission 6

Myelin speeds things up

  • Myelinated fibres conduct much faster than non-myelinated fibres of similar diameter.
Mission 6

What sets the speed?

  • Myelination: the biggest effect.
  • Axon diameter: wider axon → less resistance to current flow inside it → faster.
  • Squid giant axon: non-myelinated but about 0.5 mm wide. Fast enough for its escape response.
  • Small non-myelinated fibres are slow: often about 1 m/s or less.

Speed is built into the fibre: insulation and width set how fast every impulse travels, however strong the stimulus.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Make a guess: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Mission 7

Inside a synapse

  • A synapse is a junction between a neurone and another cell.
  • Presynaptic side: the axon terminal, the synaptic knob.
  • It holds vesicles of neurotransmitter and many mitochondria.
  • The synaptic cleft: a gap of about 20 nm.
  • Postsynaptic membrane: carries specific receptor proteins.
  • At a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
Mission 7

Transmission, step by step

  • (1) An action potential depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
  • (2) Calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • (3) Ca²⁺ is a signal inside the neurone: vesicles move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. ACh is released by exocytosis.
  • (4) ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to its complementary receptors.
  • (5) The receptors open Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation: an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  • A large enough depolarisation starts an action potential in the postsynaptic neurone.
  • Say: "ACh binds to receptors with a complementary shape" — not: "ACh enters the next neurone".
Quick check
?What is the role of calcium ions in the release of neurotransmitter from a presynaptic neurone?
Mission 7

Clearing the cleft

  • The signal must stop, or the postsynaptic cell would keep firing.
  • Neurotransmitter is removed: broken down, or taken back into the presynaptic neurone (reuptake).
Mission 7

One-way traffic

  • Signals cross a synapse in one direction only: presynaptic → postsynaptic.
  • Why: vesicles of neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side.
  • And receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
Quick check
?Why can a signal pass in only one direction across a synapse?
Mission 7

Neurone to muscle

  • A synapse can link a neurone to an effector cell, such as a muscle fibre.
  • Motor neurone → skeletal muscle fibre: the neuromuscular junction.
  • Its neurotransmitter is acetylcholine: the same one used at many synapses between neurones.
  • ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fibre membrane → depolarisation → the fibre contracts.
Quick check
?A motor neurone forms a neuromuscular junction with a skeletal muscle fibre. Which statement about acetylcholine at this junction is correct?
Mission check

?Calcium ions were removed from the fluid around a synapse. When the presynaptic neurone was stimulated, action potentials still reached the presynaptic terminal, but no response was recorded in the postsynaptic neurone. What does this result show?

The gap is crossed by a chemical, and the chemical is released only when calcium tells the vesicles to go.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Simple diffusion: small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids, down their gradient; ions and polar molecules are kept out by the hydrophobic core.
  • Facilitated diffusion: specific channel or carrier proteins carry them across, still passively and down the gradient.

Mission 2

  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane into the more concentrated solution; cells without walls burst or shrink, cells with walls become turgid or plasmolysed.

Mission 3

  • Tissue in a sucrose series: the concentration giving zero change in mass matches the tissue.

Mission 4

  • Active transport uses carrier (pump) proteins and ATP to move specific particles against their concentration gradient; it stops when the ATP supply stops.

Mission 5

  • A neurone: cell body, many short dendrites, one long axon; myelin insulates the axon except at nodes of Ranvier.
  • At rest the inside is about −70 mV: the membrane is polarised.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, ATP) builds gradients; K⁺ leaks out faster than Na⁺ leaks in, so the inside stays negative.

Mission 6

  • An action potential is a brief reversal of membrane potential caused by positive ions crossing the membrane; it is propagated along the fibre.
  • Myelinated and wider fibres conduct faster.

Mission 7

  • Action potential → Ca²⁺ enters the presynaptic terminal → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → diffusion across the cleft → binding to complementary receptors → Na⁺ in → EPSP → action potential if threshold is reached.
  • One-way, because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic.
