Mission 1

Varna + ashrama + dharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma that fits your varna (class) and your ashrama (stage of life).
  • Its defenders present it as how universal order becomes social order: each person has a place and a duty, and society is held together as the cosmos is.
  • It is presented in the classical texts as an ideal social order: everyone's needs met by everyone's work. Critics see it as a justification of inequality.
  • Your spec: "cosmic order giving norms to society ... Varna (birth); ashrama (stage in life); dharma (appropriate duty)".
Mission 1Read the source

The Purusha Sukta

Rig Veda 10.90 (paraphrase): the gods offer the cosmic Purusha (Person) in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. Verses 11–12:

  • From his mouth: the brahmana (priest).
  • From his arms: the rajanya (ruler, later called kshatriya).
  • From his thighs: the vaishya (merchant, farmer).
  • From his feet: the shudra (servant, labourer).
  • In the next verses the moon comes from his mind, the sun from his eye, the sky from his head.

Q1 What does the hymn suggest about society?

Show answer

Society as one body, each part with its own function; readers disagree whether head-to-foot order also implies a ranking.

Q2 What does it not mention?

Show answer

Jatis, untouchability, or rules about marriage and food.

Mission 1

Four varnas, four kinds of duty

  • As the classical texts describe them:
  • Brahmanas (brahmins): study and teach the Veda, perform ritual.
  • Kshatriyas: protect and rule; fight just wars.
  • Vaishyas: trade, farming, cattle.
  • Shudras: service of the other three.
  • The first three are dvija, "twice-born": the sacred-thread ceremony (upanayana) marks a second, spiritual birth.
  • The Gita lists each varna's qualities (Gita 18:41–44): serenity and learning for brahmins; courage for kshatriyas; trade and farming for vaishyas; service for shudras (paraphrase).

Mission 1Diversity

By birth or by nature?

Krishna in the Gita:

> "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Gita 4:13, Arnold)

  • The Sanskrit says by guna (qualities) and karma (actions).
  • By birth: the Dharma Shastras (e.g. Manusmriti) assign varna by birth; duties are inherited.
  • By nature: many reformers read varna as a matter of qualities: Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj); Vivekananda; today ISKCON and others.
  • Your spec writes "Varna (birth)": the traditional view; the debate is part of your topic.
Mission 1

Varna and jati

  • Varna: the four-fold scheme of the texts; ideal, all-India.
  • Jati: "birth group": thousands of local communities, often linked to an occupation, usually marrying within the group (endogamy), ranked by ideas of ritual purity.
  • Overlap: jatis claim or are given a place within a varna; that place can be disputed and has shifted over time.
  • Difference: the texts know four varnas; daily social life in India has been organised far more by jati.
  • English "caste" (from Portuguese casta) is used for both, which causes confusion.
  • Communities placed outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable"; many now call themselves Dalits (see Mission 3).

Mission 1

Jati in modern India: the debate

  • The Constitution of India (1950) abolished "untouchability" (Article 17) and banned discrimination by caste (Article 15); reserved places in education and jobs help disadvantaged groups.
  • Jati still shapes marriage, social networks and politics for many Indians; caste discrimination continues and is contested.
  • Views differ:
  • Reform: varna as an ideal of interdependent duties; jati ranking and untouchability as corruption (Gandhi's position for much of his life).
  • Abolition: the whole system is unjust and must go (B. R. Ambedkar, 1891–1956, who left Hinduism for Buddhism in 1956).
  • Identity: some value jati as community and heritage, without hierarchy.
  • Your spec: "debates about the status of jati in modern Hinduism".
Mission 1Evaluate

Does the Purusha Sukta make caste sacred?

  • Claim: The Purusha Sukta gives religious authority to the caste system.
  • For: It is shruti (revealed scripture), the highest authority, and it places the classes in a body from head to foot.
  • For: Later law books (Manusmriti) build birth-based rules on it.
  • Against: It is a late hymn (Book 10) and one image among many; it may describe the unity of society, not a ranking.
  • Against: It names four functions, not thousands of jatis or untouchability.
  • Against: Other teachings point another way: the wise look equally on a brahmin, a cow and an outcaste (Gita 5:18); varna by qualities (4:13).
  • Judgement criteria: What did the hymn mean then? How has it been used? What weight do other texts carry?

Mission 1

Varnashramadharma and righteous living

  • Doing one's svadharma well is righteous living: it earns punya (merit) and good karma.
  • Good karma leads to a better rebirth; on the traditional view, a better birth may be into another varna (Chandogya V.10.7).
  • The purusharthas are pursued within varnashramadharma: what counts as right artha for a kshatriya is not the same as for a brahmin.
  • The Gita adds: duty done without desire for its fruit leads beyond karma towards moksha.

Mission 2

Four stages of life

  • Brahmacharya: the student. Learn from a guru; celibacy, discipline, study of dharma.
  • Grihastha: the householder. Marry, work, raise children, give, worship.
  • Vanaprastha: the "forest-dweller", retirement. Hand on responsibilities; turn to spiritual practice.
  • Sannyasa: the renouncer (a sannyasi). Give up home, property and name; seek moksha.
  • Traditionally for twice-born men, beginning with the sacred thread; often pictured as four spans of about 25 years.
Mission 2

The householder holds it all up

  • Manusmriti 3.77–78 (paraphrase): as all creatures live by the air, so all the other stages live by the householder; it is the most important stage.
  • Householders feed students, support renouncers, care for elders and children, and keep worship going.
  • Traditional five daily offerings (pancha mahayajna): to the gods, the sages (study), the ancestors, other people (hospitality) and all beings (food for animals).
  • So a full worldly life is not a lesser path: it is dharma.
Quick check
?Why does Manusmriti call the grihastha stage the most important?
Mission 2

The stages today

  • Few Hindus follow all four stages strictly. Student and householder stages are almost universal; vanaprastha is often a gradual shift in retirement towards prayer, pilgrimage and service.
  • Sannyasis are a small minority; some renounce young without marrying (Shankara, by tradition, as a boy).
  • Many orders once accepted men only; women renouncers exist (e.g. the Sri Sarada Math, a women's order founded 1954).
  • In Britain and elsewhere, the stages are often taught as an ideal of a balanced life rather than a rule.
Mission 2Read the source

Own duty and universal duty

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently." (Gita 18:47, Arnold)

  • Link: both are dharma; varnashrama dharma is how universal dharma becomes concrete for one person at one stage.
  • Difference: sanatana dharma (non-violence, truth, purity, self-control) binds everyone always; varnashrama dharma changes with class and stage.
  • Tension: a warrior's duty to fight against universal non-violence (Arjuna).

Mission 2

Bhakti changes the rules

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to God. In bhakti, what counts most is love, not birth, stage or ritual rank.

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path" (Gita 9:32, Arnold)

  • Bhakti poet-saints came from every background, including communities treated as untouchable: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva saint), Chokhamela (14th-century Maharashtra), Ravidas (15th–16th-century north India, from a leather-working community).
  • Chaitanya (1486–1534) shared the chanting of Krishna's names with all, regardless of caste; Basava (12th century) founded a Shaiva movement that rejected caste.
  • Bhakti often relaxed the rules in worship more than in marriage or daily life: the change was real but uneven.
  • Your spec: "ways in which the rules of varnashrama-dharma are transformed by bhakti".

Mission 2

Community and the meaning of life

  • Community of believers: family (where most practice happens), jati networks, sampradayas (traditions), gurus and satsang (gatherings of devotees), the mandir.
  • The community passes on and interprets dharma, supports each stage (feeding sannyasis, teaching students) and, in the diaspora, keeps identity alive.
  • Meaning and purpose of life: to fulfil one's dharma through every stage, pay the three debts (rinas) to the gods (worship), the sages (study) and the ancestors (children), and finally reach moksha.
  • Your spec: "role of the community of believers"; "beliefs about the meaning and purpose of life".
Mission 2

Stages, aims, karma

  • Each ashrama gives a different weight to the four aims: dharma (student), artha and kama within dharma (householder), detachment (retiree), moksha (renouncer).
  • Hindus believe that doing each stage's duty well earns punya and good karma, leading to a better rebirth.
  • Renunciation aims beyond karma: the sannyasi seeks to act without desire and so to end samsara.
  • So varnashramadharma links social life to liberation: righteous living prepares the way.
Mission 2Evaluate

Are the ashramas still relevant?

  • Claim: The four ashramas are no longer relevant to Hindu life today.
  • For: Few Hindus become forest-dwellers or renouncers; modern careers and pensions do not fit the scheme.
  • For: Traditionally the scheme was for twice-born men only; it leaves most people out.
  • Against: The ideal (learn, contribute, let go, seek God) fits any modern life and is widely taught.
  • Against: Vanaprastha is lived out as retirement given to prayer, pilgrimage and service; sannyasa still flourishes in orders and ashrams.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant as a rule, or as an ideal? For whom: India or the diaspora; men or women?

Mission 3

Varna and jati: two different things

  • Varna ("colour", "class"): four classes named in scripture: Brahmins (priests, teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers, warriors), Vaishyas (merchants, farmers), Shudras (labourers, servants).
  • Earliest account: the Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.12): the four come from the mouth, arms, thighs and feet of the cosmic person (paraphrase).
  • Jati ("birth"): thousands of local groups, each traditionally with its own occupation, marrying within the group (endogamy) and ranked by region.
  • "Caste" is a Portuguese word (casta) and is used for both: a source of confusion.
  • Overlap: jatis often claim a varna; varna gives the scriptural frame, jati the lived reality.
  • Spec wording: "overlap and differences between varna and jati".
Mission 3Read the source

Varna by nature, devotion for all

Bhagavad Gita 18.41 (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "The work of Brahmans, Kshatriyas, Vaisyas, / And Sudras, O thou Slayer of thy Foes! / Is fixed by reason of the Qualities / Planted in each"

Bhagavad Gita 9.32 (Arnold):

> "whoso will turn to Me, / Though they be born from the very womb of Sin, / Woman or man; sprung of the Vaisya caste / Or lowly disregarded Sudra,--all / Plant foot upon the highest path"

Q1 In 18.41, what fixes each varna's work: birth or qualities?

Show answer

The text says "the Qualities" (gunas): reformers stress this; traditionalists say qualities come with birth.

Q2 What does 9.32 offer that varna rules did not?

Show answer

The highest path is open to all who turn to Krishna, whatever their birth or sex.

  • Arnold's word "caste" translates varna, not jati.
  • Commentators differ on whether "born from the very womb of Sin" describes the groups named after it or a separate group. Critics who read it as describing them say the verse opens the highest path to all while keeping the ranking.
Mission 3

Untouchability

  • Some groups were placed outside the four varnas (avarna): treated as permanently polluting.
  • The idea rests on ritual purity and pollution: work with death, leather, waste or cremation was seen as impure, and the impurity as inherited.
  • Practices: separate wells and settlements; no entry to many temples; no shared food; violence against those who broke the rules.
  • Texts used to support it: e.g. Manusmriti 10.51–56 sets the lowest groups to live outside the village (paraphrase).
  • India's Constitution (1950) bans untouchability (Article 17), yet the practices have not disappeared.
  • Social issues today: discrimination in jobs, housing and marriage; violence; manual scavenging (cleaning waste by hand), still done mostly by Dalits despite being banned.

Mission 3

Whose name? Dalit, Harijan, Scheduled Castes

  • Untouchable: the old outsider word; now offensive.
  • Harijan ("people of Hari", i.e. of God): Gandhi's term from the 1930s. Meant as honour; many Dalits reject it as patronising.
  • Dalit ("broken", "crushed"): a name chosen by the community itself; often traced to the 19th-century reformer Jyotirao Phule, used by Ambedkar, and made popular by the Dalit Panthers (1972).
  • Scheduled Castes (SC): the legal term, from the lists ("schedules") in the Constitution.
  • The choice of name is itself the question: who names whom?
  • Your spec uses harijans: know the term and the criticism of it.
Mission 3

Secular India and the law

  • India's Constitution (1950) is secular and guarantees equality. Article 17 abolishes untouchability and makes its practice an offence.
  • Reservations: quotas for Scheduled Castes in education, government jobs and legislatures (Articles 15(4), 16(4), 330, 332).
  • Laws: Untouchability (Offences) Act 1955 (renamed Protection of Civil Rights Act, 1976); SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989.
  • Religion and the law: SC status is limited to Hindus, Sikhs (from 1956) and Buddhists (from 1990). Dalit Christians and Muslims are excluded: a live debate.
  • Temple entry campaigns: the Vaikom satyagraha (1924–25); Travancore's Temple Entry Proclamation (1936).
Mission 3

Bhakti transforms the rules

  • Varnashrama-dharma ties duty to birth (varna) and life stage (ashrama).
  • Bhakti (loving devotion) asks only for love of God, not birth or Sanskrit learning.
  • Low-caste and Dalit saints were honoured: Nandanar (Tamil Shaiva, Dalit), Chokhamela (14th century, Maharashtra, Mahar), Ravidas (15th–16th century, a leatherworker; North India).
  • Saints sang in local languages, so devotion did not need Brahmin priests.
  • Limits: bhakti often changed who is close to God more than who marries whom: caste rules in society largely stayed. Chokhamela was still barred from the Pandharpur temple.
  • Spec wording: "ways in which the rules of varnashrama-dharma are transformed by bhakti".
Mission 3

Brahminism and the status of jati today

  • Brahminism: critics' term for the ideology that places Brahmins and their purity rules at the top of society.
  • Louis Dumont (1911–1998), Homo Hierarchicus (1966): caste is ordered by the opposition of pure and impure.
  • Views on jati today:
  • Traditionalists: jati and its rules are part of dharma and keep social order.
  • Reformers (e.g. Vivekananda, Gandhi): varna as duty is valid; untouchability and hierarchy by birth are corruptions.
  • Abolitionists (e.g. Ambedkar): caste is rooted in the scriptures themselves and must go.
  • Modern life (cities, jobs, education) has weakened some rules; caste remains strong in marriage and politics.
Mission 3Diversity

Who speaks for Dalits?

  • Others speaking for them: Gandhi called himself a voice for "Harijans", and fasted to oppose separate electorates for them (1932).
  • Dalits speaking for themselves: B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956); the Dalit Panthers; Dalit literature, e.g. Omprakash Valmiki, Joothan (1997); Bama, Karukku (1992).
  • Many Dalit voices reject Hindu identity (Ambedkar became a Buddhist in 1956); others stay Hindu and reclaim saints such as Ravidas and Valmiki.
Mission 3

Hardy and Flood on equality

  • Friedhelm Hardy (1943–2004), The Religious Culture of India (1994): Indian religion as many strands; in emotional bhakti (his Viraha-bhakti, 1983, on the Tamil saints), love of God can matter more than caste or gender (paraphrase).
  • Gavin Flood (b. 1954), An Introduction to Hinduism (1996): varna is an ideal model in the texts; jati is the social reality; both are ordered by purity and pollution (paraphrase).
  • Both help to separate teaching from social practice, and both note how far bhakti and modern reform have, and have not, changed that practice.

Is caste part of Hindu teaching, or only culture?

  • Claim: Caste discrimination is a cultural corruption, not Hindu teaching.
  • For: the Gita links varna to qualities, not birth (18.41); bhakti saints taught equality before God (9.32).

Mission 4

What "yoga" means

  • Yoga from Sanskrit yuj: "to yoke", "to join". A discipline that unites the self with the ultimate.
  • Also called a marga (path): a way to moksha, liberation from samsara (the cycle of rebirth).
  • The Bhagavad Gita teaches several yogas side by side; each chapter is titled a yoga.
  • Not mainly postures: the postures belong to hatha yoga (see Mission 6).
  • Key belief shared by all paths: karma and ignorance bind the atman; the right discipline frees it.
Mission 4

Different paths for different people

  • Hindus believe people differ in temperament, so different paths suit different characters:
  • Jnana yoga: path of knowledge, for the reflective mind.
  • Karma yoga: path of action, for the active person.
  • Bhakti yoga: path of devotion, for the emotional heart.
  • Raja yoga: path of meditation, for the contemplative (see Mission 6).
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) popularised this fourfold scheme in his lectures Karma Yoga, Bhakti Yoga, Jnana Yoga and Raja Yoga (1896). The Gita itself mainly names three.
Mission 4

Karma yoga: selfless action

  • Karma yoga: doing one's dharma (duty) without attachment to results (nishkama karma).
  • Action itself does not bind; desire for its fruits does.
  • Offer every action to God; this purifies the mind (chitta-shuddhi) of selfish desire.
  • Aim: identification with the transcendent, the self no longer acting for itself.

> "Therefore, thy task prescribed / With spirit unattached gladly perform, / Since in performance of plain duty man / Mounts to his highest bliss." (Bhagavad Gita 3:19, Arnold)

Mission 4Read the source

No one can stop acting

> "No man shall 'scape from act / By shunning action; nay, and none shall come / By mere renouncements unto perfectness. / Nay, and no jot of time, at any time, / Rests any actionless; his nature's law / Compels him, even unwilling, into act" (Bhagavad Gita 3:4–5, Arnold)

  • Krishna answers Arjuna's wish to escape karma by not acting.

Q1 Why is "shunning action" impossible, according to Krishna?

Show answer

Q2 What, then, is the way to escape karma?

Show answer

Mission 4

Jnana yoga: the path of wisdom

  • Jnana: knowledge, not facts but direct realisation that atman is Brahman.
  • Method: viveka (discrimination between real and unreal), vairagya (renunciation), study of the Upanishads with a guru: hearing, reflecting, meditating.
  • Aim: identification with Brahman; ignorance (avidya) ends, and with it rebirth.
  • Classically linked to Advaita Vedanta and Shankara.
  • Seen as suited to certain groups such as Brahmins: it needs Vedic study (traditionally only for the twice-born) and often the renounced life of a sannyasi.
  • Links with raja yoga: deep meditation (dhyana) is how knowledge becomes realisation.

Mission 4

Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

  • Bhakti: loving devotion to a personal God, often Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • In the Gita, Krishna is identified with Brahman: loving him leads to moksha.
  • Open to everyone, whatever caste or learning (Gita 9:32;).
  • Practices: puja, chanting the divine name, singing (kirtan, bhajan), pilgrimage.
  • Liberation is often by God's grace, and may be eternal loving union rather than merging (Ramanuja).

> "Only by fullest service, perfect faith, / And uttermost surrender am I known / And seen, and entered into, Indian Prince!" (Bhagavad Gita 11:54, Arnold)

Mission 4

The Samkhya link

  • Samkhya: an ancient dualist school (see Mission 7). Two realities: purusha (pure consciousness) and prakriti (matter and mind, made of the three gunas).
  • Bondage: purusha wrongly identifies with prakriti. Liberation: kaivalya ("isolation"), knowing the difference.
  • The Gita uses Samkhya ideas: chapter 2's "wisdom of Samkhya" teaches that the atman is eternal and the body passes (2:11–30).
  • Implications: the paths can be read within dualist (Samkhya-Yoga) or non-dualist (Advaita) frameworks, and the goal of liberation changes with the framework.

Mission 4Evaluate

Are the paths equal?

  • Claim: All paths of yoga lead equally to moksha.
  • For: The Gita teaches all of them; Vivekananda: different people need different roads.
  • For: In practice they overlap: devotion includes action; knowledge needs meditation.
  • Against: Shankara: only knowledge removes ignorance; other paths are preparation.
  • Against: Gita 12:5: the path to the unmanifest is harder for embodied beings; many bhakti traditions see devotion as higher. Jnana was historically closed to many.
  • Judgement criteria: What is moksha (merging, or loving union)? Is "equal" about the goal or the difficulty? Who could actually take each path?

Mission 5

Bhakti marga: the way of loving devotion

  • Bhakti (from bhaj, "to share in", "to worship"): loving, personal devotion to God.
  • Bhakti marga: the way of devotion, one of the paths to moksha (see Mission 4).
  • God is approached as saguna, with qualities: Vishnu (often as Krishna or Rama), Shiva, or the Goddess.
  • The devotee relates to God as servant, friend, parent, child or beloved.
  • The heart of it: love and surrender; liberation through God's grace.
  • Liberation is often seen as eternal loving closeness to God, not loss of self.

Offered with love, open to all

Mission 5

Origins: the Tamil saints

  • The movement began in South India, in Tamil, c. 6th–9th centuries CE.
  • Alvars: 12 Vaishnava poet-saints devoted to Vishnu (e.g. Nammalvar; Andal, a woman). Their hymns were gathered as the Four Thousand Divine Hymns.
  • Nayanars: 63 Shaiva saints devoted to Shiva (e.g. Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar; Karaikkal Ammaiyar, a woman). Their hymns form the Tevaram.
  • They sang in Tamil, not Sanskrit, travelling from temple to temple.
  • Background: the Gita's teaching on devotion; temple worship growing; competition with Jain and Buddhist communities in South India.
Mission 5

Vaishnava bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Vishnu and his avatars, especially Krishna and Rama.
  • Krishna as child, cowherd and lover; the love of the gopis (cowherd women) in the Bhagavata Purana, and of Radha in later poetry such as Jayadeva's Gita Govinda (12th century), as the model of the soul's longing for God.
  • Rama as the ideal king and the object of devotion (Tulsidas; Hanuman as the perfect devotee).
  • Key ideas: prapatti (surrender) and God's grace.
  • Today: ISKCON, Swaminarayan and many other movements.
Mission 5

Shaiva bhakti

  • Loving devotion to Shiva, often worshipped as the lingam.
  • Shiva is both fearsome ascetic and loving lord who chooses his devotees and saves them by grace.
  • The Nayanars' hymns (Tevaram) are still sung in Tamil Shiva temples; they shaped Shaiva Siddhanta theology.
  • Lingayats (Virashaivas), from Basava: each devotee wears a small lingam on the body, rejects temple priests and caste ranking, and worships Shiva directly.
  • Shaiva bhakti shows the same pattern as Vaishnava: local language, personal love, grace.
Mission 5

Bhakti and the rules of caste

  • Varna: the four ideal classes of scripture. Jati: the thousands of birth-based groups people actually belong to, often tied to occupation and marriage.
  • Bhakti transforms varnashrama dharma: devotion, not birth, makes a person dear to God.
  • Saints came from low jatis: Nandanar (a Nayanar of an "untouchable" community), Tiruppan Alvar, Chokhamela, Ravidas.
  • Women saints (Andal, Akka Mahadevi, Mirabai) defied family and social roles.
  • Songs in local languages took religion out of the hands of Sanskrit-trained priests.
  • But: in many stories low-caste saints still face exclusion (Nandanar must pass through fire before entering the temple). Spiritual equality did not always end social caste.
Mission 5Evaluate

Did bhakti make Hinduism open to all?

  • Claim: The bhakti movement made Hindu religious life open to everyone.
  • For: Gita 9:32 and the saints: women and low-caste devotees reach God directly.
  • For: Worship moved into local languages, homes, songs and pilgrim roads, needing no priest.
  • Against: Caste ranking survived; temples often stayed closed to Dalits until 20th-century laws and campaigns.
  • Against: Devotion was often made "inner": equal before God, but not equal in society.
  • Judgement criteria: Open spiritually or socially? Which region and century? Whose voices tell the story: saints, priests or later reformers?

Mission 6

Raja yoga: the path of meditation

  • Raja yoga, the "royal" path: control of the mind through meditation.
  • Main text: the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali (about 195 short sutras; dating debated, many scholars place them around 400 CE).
  • Definition in Yoga Sutra 1.2 (paraphrase): yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind (chitta vritti nirodha).
  • When the mind is still, the self rests in its own true nature (1.3).
  • The name "raja yoga" for Patanjali's system was popularised by Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1896).
  • Links: jnana yoga needs meditation to turn knowledge into realisation (see Mission 4).
Mission 6

The eight limbs (ashtanga)

Yoga Sutra 2.29 lists eight limbs, from outer to inner:

  • 1 Yama: restraints (ahimsa, truthfulness, non-stealing, brahmacharya, non-possessiveness).
  • 2 Niyama: observances (purity, contentment, austerity, self-study, surrender to Ishvara).
  • 3 Asana: a steady, comfortable seat (2.46). 4 Pranayama: breath control.
  • 5 Pratyahara: withdrawing the senses. 6 Dharana: concentration. 7 Dhyana: meditation.
  • 8 Samadhi: absorption.
  • Goal: kaivalya, the self freed from entanglement with matter.
  • Ethics first: postures and breath come only after yama and niyama.
Mission 6

Samkhya beneath the Yoga Sutras

  • Patanjali builds on Samkhya dualism: purusha (consciousness) and prakriti (matter and mind, made of the three gunas).
  • Suffering comes from confusing the seer with what is seen (Yoga Sutra 2.17, paraphrase).
  • Difference: Samkhya does not need God; Patanjali adds Ishvara, a special purusha untouched by karma, as a focus for devotion (1.23–24).
  • Implications: classical yoga's goal is isolation of the self, not merging with Brahman (Advaita) or union with a loving God (bhakti). One word, "yoga", covers different goals.

Mission 6

Hatha yoga

  • Developed c. 11th–15th centuries, especially among the Nath yogis (traditionally founded by Gorakshanath).
  • Key text: Hatha Yoga Pradipika (Svatmarama, 15th century).
  • Practices: asanas, pranayama, cleansing techniques (shatkarma), locks (bandha) and seals (mudra).
  • Aim: purify the body and its subtle channels, awaken kundalini energy, and realise the body's divine nature; also strength and health.
  • Hatha means "force": a forceful, physical method.
  • The Hatha Yoga Pradipika presents hatha as a stairway to raja yoga (1.1–2, paraphrase).
Mission 6Read the source

Hatha serves raja

Hatha Yoga Pradipika 1.1–2 (paraphrase):

  • The author bows to Shiva, who taught hatha yoga as a stairway for those who wish to climb to raja yoga.
  • He teaches hatha only for the sake of raja yoga.

Q1 How does the text see the relationship between hatha and raja yoga?

Show answer

Hatha is a means; raja yoga, the stilled mind, is the goal.

Q2 What would this text say about postures practised only for fitness?

Show answer

They miss the point: the body is trained to serve meditation and liberation.

Mission 6

How yoga came West

  • 1893: Vivekananda speaks at the World's Parliament of Religions, Chicago; Raja Yoga (1896) presents yoga as meditation.
  • 1920: Paramahansa Yogananda arrives in the USA; Autobiography of a Yogi (1946).
  • 1930s–60s: T. Krishnamacharya (1888–1989) in Mysore develops dynamic posture sequences; students B. K. S. Iyengar (Light on Yoga, 1966), K. Pattabhi Jois, Indra Devi.
  • 1936: Swami Sivananda founds the Divine Life Society, Rishikesh; Swami Vishnudevananda opens Sivananda centres in the West from 1959.
  • 1960s–70s: Maharishi Mahesh Yogi; the Beatles in Rishikesh (1968).
  • 2015: first International Day of Yoga (21 June), set by the UN in 2014.
Mission 6

New purposes

  • Much Western yoga focuses on fitness, flexibility and stress relief, often with no religious aim.
  • Scholar Mark Singleton, Yoga Body (2010), paraphrase: modern posture yoga blends hatha with Indian and Western physical culture (gymnastics, body-building) of the early 20th century.
  • Some Western teachers keep mantra, meditation and ethics; others teach postures alone.
  • Back in India, posture yoga is now also promoted for health and as national heritage.
  • Purpose changes significance: for liberation (moksha), health, or both.

Mission 6Evaluate

Is yoga without moksha still yoga?

  • Claim: Postural yoga practised only for fitness is no longer really yoga.
  • For: Patanjali and the Hatha Yoga Pradipika make liberation the goal; postures serve meditation.
  • For: Ethics (yama, niyama) come first in the eight limbs; a gym class usually leaves them out.
  • Against: Hatha itself values health and the body; traditions have always changed.
  • Against: Many teachers hold that the practice works on mind and body whatever the student believes.
  • Judgement criteria: Is yoga defined by its goal, its practices or its tradition? Who has authority to decide: texts, gurus or practitioners?

Mission 7

Samkhya: counting what is real

  • Hindu thought has six classical darshanas ("views": schools of philosophy) that accept the authority of the Vedas.
  • Samkhya ("enumeration", counting) is one of the oldest. It lists the basic principles (tattvas) of reality: 25 in all.
  • Its legendary founder is the sage Kapila. Its classic text is the Samkhya Karika of Ishvarakrishna (c. 4th–5th century CE).
  • Samkhya ideas appear earlier in the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, often in a form that includes God.
  • Yoga (Patanjali) is its sister school: Samkhya gives the theory, yoga the practice.
Mission 7

Two realities: purusha and prakriti

  • Samkhya is dualist: reality has two eternal, separate kinds.
  • Purusha: pure consciousness. It only witnesses; it does not act or change. There are many purushas, one for each living being.
  • Prakriti: nature or matter. It is one, unconscious, always active and changing.
  • Key point: the mind, ego and intellect belong to prakriti, not to purusha. Thinking is a material process; awareness of it is purusha.
  • Suffering begins when purusha mistakes the activity of prakriti for itself: "I am angry", "I am this body".
Mission 7

The three gunas

  • Prakriti is made of three gunas ("strands", qualities), mixed in different proportions in everything:
  • Sattva: light, purity, calm, goodness.
  • Rajas: energy, passion, restlessness, desire.
  • Tamas: heaviness, darkness, inertia, dullness.
  • Before creation the gunas are in balance; when the balance is disturbed, the world unfolds.

> "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature, "Soothfastness," / "Passion," and "Ignorance." These three bind down / The changeless Spirit in the changeful flesh." (Bhagavad Gita 14:5, Arnold)

  • Hindus apply the gunas to food, character and actions: e.g. sattvic food is fresh and vegetarian; many aim to increase sattva.

Mission 7

Liberation: kaivalya

  • The goal is kaivalya: "aloneness" or isolation of purusha from prakriti.
  • It comes through viveka: discriminating knowledge that "I am not this body, mind or ego".
  • When purusha is no longer confused with prakriti, the dance of prakriti stops for it. No more rebirth.
  • Liberation is not union with God: each liberated purusha remains itself, separate and pure.
  • Classical Samkhya is non-theistic: it does not need Ishvara (a Lord) to explain the world. Many Hindus who use its ideas do believe in God.
Mission 7

Patanjali and the eight limbs

  • Patanjali, Yoga Sutras (date disputed, often placed c. 2nd century BCE–5th century CE).
  • He accepts Samkhya's purusha, prakriti and gunas. Yoga is the stilling of the movements of the mind (Yoga Sutra 1:2, paraphrase).
  • Ashtanga (eight-limbed) yoga: yama (restraints, e.g. ahimsa), niyama (observances), asana (posture), pranayama (breath), pratyahara (withdrawal of senses), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), samadhi (absorption).
  • Patanjali adds Ishvara: a special purusha, untouched by karma; devotion to Ishvara is one way to samadhi.
  • The goal is still kaivalya: the seer resting in its own nature.

Mission 7

Samkhya behind yoga today

  • Across yogic traditions, the shared aim is moksha: liberation from samsara. Patanjali's form of it (kaivalya) is Samkhya's.
  • Implication: "yoga" is often translated "union", but in Patanjali the goal is separation of the self from nature.
  • Different purposes: liberation (for sannyasis and serious practitioners), devotion (where yoga serves bhakti), health and calm (much modern postural yoga).
  • Across cultures: in the West, yoga is often taught as exercise, with Samkhya's metaphysics left out. Some Hindus welcome its spread; others say it has been cut off from its roots.
  • Paths of yoga in more depth.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Varnashramadharma: duty by class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama), cosmic order as social order.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.11–12): brahmana from the mouth, rajanya from the arms, vaishya from the thighs, shudra from the feet.
  • Varna is a four-fold textual scheme; jatis are thousands of local birth groups; Hindus debate birth versus qualities (Gita 4:13) and the place of jati today.

Mission 2

  • Four ashramas: brahmacharya (student), grihastha (householder, the support of all), vanaprastha (retirement), sannyasa (renunciation).
  • Varnashrama dharma makes universal (sanatana) dharma concrete for each person; Gita 18:47 values one's own dharma over another's.
  • Bhakti made devotion, not birth, the measure in worship (Gita 9:32), though caste often persisted in social life.

Mission 3

  • Varna is the four-class scriptural frame; jati is the lived system of birth groups; Dalits were placed outside the four varnas and treated as "untouchable".
  • India's Constitution abolishes untouchability and reserves places for Scheduled Castes; bhakti opened devotion to all but left much of social caste in place.
  • The debate includes who speaks: others (Gandhi's "Harijans") or Dalits themselves (Ambedkar, Dalit writers).

Mission 4

  • Yoga ("joining") is a disciplined path (marga) to moksha; different paths suit different temperaments.
  • Karma yoga: duty without attachment; jnana yoga: knowledge that atman is Brahman; bhakti yoga: loving devotion to a personal God.
  • Shankara puts knowledge highest, Ramanuja devotion, Vivekananda treats all as valid; Samkhya shapes how liberation is understood.

Mission 5

  • Bhakti is loving devotion to a personal God; liberation comes by grace and surrender.
  • It began with the Tamil Alvars (Vishnu) and Nayanars (Shiva), c. 6th–9th centuries, and spread north through saints of every caste, singing in local languages.
  • Bhakti made devotion, not birth, the measure of a devotee, though social caste often survived.

Mission 6

  • Raja yoga (Patanjali) stills the mind through eight limbs, built on Samkhya dualism, aiming at kaivalya.
  • Hatha yoga purifies the body as a stairway to raja yoga; modern posture yoga grew from it and from physical culture.
  • In the West yoga's purposes changed (health, fitness); views differ on ownership and on whether yoga without moksha is still yoga.

Mission 7

  • Samkhya is dualist: many purushas (pure consciousness) and one prakriti (nature, made of sattva, rajas and tamas); mind and intellect belong to prakriti.
  • Liberation (kaivalya) comes through knowing the self is not prakriti; classical Samkhya needs no God, but Patanjali adds Ishvara and the eight limbs of yoga.
