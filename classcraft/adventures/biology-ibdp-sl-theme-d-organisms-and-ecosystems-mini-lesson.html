Mission 1

Mission 1: Most flowers have both male and female parts. Why do so many work hard to avoid pollinating themselves?

Make a guess: Most flowers have both male and female parts. Why do so many work hard to avoid pollinating themselves?

Mission 1

Flowers are for sexual reproduction

  • Flowering plants reproduce sexually: meiosis makes haploid cells, these give rise to gametes, and gametes fuse at fertilisation.
  • Male gametes are made inside pollen grains, in the anthers.
  • Female gametes are made inside ovules, in the ovary.
  • A pollen grain is not a gamete: it carries the male gametes.
  • Pollination: transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.
  • Pollination is not fertilisation. Fertilisation comes later, inside the ovule.
  • A flower with both male and female parts is still reproducing sexually.
Mission 1

An insect-pollinated flower

  • Sepal: protects the flower in bud.
  • Petal: large, coloured, scented → attracts insects.
  • Nectary: makes sugary nectar, the insect's reward.
  • Stamen (male) = anther (makes pollen) + filament (holds the anther up).
  • Carpel (female) = stigma + style + ovary.
  • Stigma: sticky; pollen lands and germinates here.
  • Style: holds up the stigma; the pollen tube grows down it.
  • Ovary: contains the ovules.
  • Draw it: clear single lines, labels with functions, no shading.
  • Say: "the stigma receives pollen" — not: "the stigma makes pollen".
Quick check
?Which structure of an insect-pollinated flower is correctly paired with its function?
Mission 1

Self- or cross-pollination?

  • Self-pollination: pollen reaches a stigma on the same plant (same flower or another flower).
  • Cross-pollination: pollen reaches a stigma on another plant of the same species.
  • Pollen is carried by animals (insects, birds, bats) or by wind.
  • Self-pollination → self-fertilisation → inbreeding.
  • Inbreeding: offspring more homozygous → harmful recessive alleles already present are expressed more often.
  • Result: lower genetic diversity and lower vigour.
  • Say: "inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles" — not: "inbreeding causes mutations".
Mission 1

Ways to promote cross-pollination

  • Different maturation times: anthers shed pollen before the stigma is ready, or the other way round.
  • Separate male and female flowers on one plant (e.g. maize, hazel): no flower can pollinate itself.
  • Separate male and female plants (e.g. holly): every pollination is a cross.
  • Self-incompatibility: genetic; the plant's own pollen fails (next screen).
  • Animals and wind carry pollen between plants.
Quick check
?In one flower species the anthers shed their pollen and wither several days before the stigma of the same flower becomes receptive. How does this promote cross-pollination?
Mission 1

Self-incompatibility: rejecting your own pollen

  • Controlled by a gene with many alleles (S alleles).
  • Pollen lands on a stigma of the same plant.
  • Its S allele matches one in the stigma and style → it is recognised.
  • The pollen fails to germinate, or its pollen tube stops growing.
  • Pollen with a different S allele grows normally.
  • So the gametes that fuse come from different plants → more genetic variation.
  • Say: "genetic recognition after pollen lands" — not: "a barrier that keeps pollen off".
Quick check
?In a self-incompatible plant species, pollen from a flower lands on the stigma of the same flower and germinates, but no seed is set. Which statement about self-incompatibility explains this?
Mission 1

Crossing plants by hand

  • Pea flowers normally self-pollinate before they open.
  • So to cross two plants (the P generation):
  • Remove the anthers from a flower of one plant before they shed pollen.
  • Cover the flower with a bag.
  • Brush pollen from the other plant onto its stigma. Bag and label it.
  • Seeds from this flower → the F1 generation.
  • Let F1 plants self-pollinate → the F2 generation. Predict ratios with a Punnett grid (see Mission 3).
  • Breeders use crosses like this to make new crop and garden varieties.
Quick check
?A breeder wants to cross a tall pea plant with a dwarf pea plant to obtain an F1 generation. Which procedure is required, and why?
Mission check

?A rare wildflower survives as a few plants in one valley. They are self-compatible and mostly self-pollinate. Seeds from self-pollination give many more weak seedlings than seeds from crosses with plants from another valley. What is the best explanation?

That is why so many flowers avoid their own pollen: crossing keeps diversity and vigour high.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A dry bean can sit in a jar for years, then sprout within days of getting wet. What powers that first growth?

Make a guess: A dry bean can sit in a jar for years, then sprout within days of getting wet. What powers that first growth?

Mission 2

From stigma to embryo

  • Inside the anther, meiosis makes haploid pollen grains. Each makes male gametes by mitosis.
  • A compatible pollen grain on the stigma germinates.
  • A pollen tube grows down the style, secreting enzymes to digest a path.
  • It enters the ovule through the micropyle, a small gap in the ovule's coat.
  • It delivers the male gametes.
  • A male gamete fuses with the egg cell (female gamete): fertilisation.
  • The diploid zygote divides by mitosis → embryo.
Mission 2

Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit

  • Zygote → embryo: radicle (young root), plumule (young shoot), cotyledons (seed leaves).
  • Integuments → testa (seed coat). The micropyle stays as a tiny pore.
  • Ovule → seed.
  • Ovary wall → fruit wall (pericarp). Ovary → fruit.
  • The seed dries out and becomes dormant.
  • Say: "the ovule becomes the seed" — not: "the ovary already contains seeds".
Mission 2

Dispersal is not pollination

  • Pollination moves pollen, before fertilisation.
  • Seed dispersal moves seeds, after fertilisation.
  • Why disperse? Less competition with the parent and with each other; new areas colonised.
  • Wind: wings or plumes (e.g. sycamore, dandelion).
  • Animals: fleshy fruits eaten, seeds pass out in faeces; or hooks that catch on fur.
  • Water (e.g. coconut) or explosive pods.
Quick check
?What is the difference between pollination and seed dispersal?
Mission 2

Germination: living on stored food

  • Needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
  • Seed absorbs water through the micropyle; testa softens and splits. Enzymes become active.
  • Embryo releases gibberellin → amylase made.
  • Food reserves mobilised: starch → maltose → glucose; proteins → amino acids; lipids → fatty acids and glycerol.
  • Glucose → aerobic respiration → ATP. Glucose and amino acids → new cells (cellulose, proteins).
  • Radicle emerges first (anchors, absorbs water), then the plumule.
  • Dry mass falls until the first leaves photosynthesise.
Quick check
?A barley grain germinating in dark, moist soil grows a root and a shoot for several days. Where do the energy and materials for this growth come from?
Mission check

?Pea seeds were soaked and grown in complete darkness for ten days. Each day some seedlings were weighed fresh, then dried and weighed again. Fresh mass rose every day; dry mass fell every day. What explains this?

A seed carries its own packed lunch: until its leaves reach the light, every new cell is built from stored food.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Make a guess: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Mission 3

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA). Its position on a chromosome is its locus.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene, at the same locus.
  • Body cells are diploid: two copies of each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has, e.g. Tt.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable traits. It results from the genotype and the environment.
  • Gametes are haploid: one allele of each gene. Fertilisation makes a diploid zygote with one allele from each parent.
  • Say: "two alleles of the gene for height" — not: "a gene for tall and a gene for short".
Mission 3

Dominant and recessive

  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present. Capital letter: T.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when homozygous (tt). Lower-case letter: t.
  • So TT and Tt plants are equally tall.
  • Why? T codes for a functional protein; one copy makes enough of it.
  • t usually codes for a non-functional or absent product. It is still there, and still passed on.
  • Say: "the recessive allele has no effect in the heterozygote" — not: "the dominant allele switches it off".
Quick check
?In peas the allele T for tall stems is dominant to the allele t for dwarf stems, and TT and Tt plants are equally tall. What is the reason that the heterozygote is not shorter than the homozygous dominant plant?
Mission 3

The monohybrid cross

  • P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf). Gametes: all T; all t. F1: all Tt, all tall.
  • F2: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes T and t gametes in equal numbers.
  • A Punnett grid gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
  • The two alleles separate into different gametes. That is the law of segregation.
  • The hook: both tall parents were Tt. The short plant is the ¼ that got t from each.
Mission 3

Making a cross in flowering plants

  • Pollen contains the male gametes. The female gametes are in the ovary.
  • Peas make both on the same flower, so they normally self-pollinate and self-fertilise.
  • To cross two plants: remove the anthers from an unopened flower of one plant.
  • Brush pollen from the other plant onto its stigma. Cover the flower with a bag.
  • Plant the seeds: that is the F1. Let F1 plants self-pollinate to get the F2.
  • Breeders use crosses like this to produce new crop and ornamental varieties.

Mission 3

A recessive disorder in a family

  • Some human disorders are caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene.
  • Heterozygotes are unaffected carriers. Two carriers can have an affected child.
  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): the gene codes for the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine.
  • In pp people the enzyme is missing or does not work, so phenylalanine builds up. Treatment: a low-phenylalanine diet.
  • Carrier × carrier: each child has a ¼ chance of being affected.
  • Each fertilisation is independent. Earlier children do not change the odds.
Quick check
?A man and a woman are both unaffected carriers of the PKU allele. They already have one child with PKU. What is the probability that their next child will have PKU?
Mission 3

The test cross

  • A tall plant could be TT or Tt. You cannot tell by looking.
  • Test cross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt) plant.
  • tt passes on only t, which masks nothing. So each offspring shows the allele it got from the tall plant.
  • All tall offspring: the plant is probably TT.
  • Any dwarf offspring: the plant must be Tt. Expect about 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
Quick check
?To find out whether a purple-flowered pea plant is homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp), a breeder crosses it with a white-flowered plant (pp). Why is a white-flowered plant used for this test cross?
Mission check

?In mice, the allele B for black fur is dominant to the allele b for brown fur. A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse. Of the 9 offspring, 5 are black and 4 are brown. Which conclusion is correct?

Like the short pea plant in the hook, the brown pups reveal a recessive allele that was there all along, hidden in a heterozygote.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Make a guess: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Mission 4

Sex chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 22 pairs of autosomes + one pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Female: XX. Male: XY.
  • Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries X or Y. So the sperm decides the sex of the zygote.
  • The X carries far more genes than the much smaller Y.
  • A gene on a sex chromosome is sex-linked. Most are on the X: X-linked.
  • A male has one copy of each X-linked gene. A female has two.
Mission 4

Writing an X-linked cross

  • Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting), Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y carries no allele.
  • Haemophilia: a recessive X-linked allele; a clotting factor is missing, so blood clots slowly.
  • Carrier mother × unaffected father: XᴴXʰ × XᴴY.
  • Gametes: Xᴴ or Xʰ · Xᴴ or Y.
  • Offspring: XᴴXᴴ, XᴴXʰ (carrier), XᴴY, XʰY (affected).
  • Daughters: none affected, ½ carriers. Sons: ½ affected.
Quick check
?A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia (XᴴXʰ) has children with a man who does not have haemophilia (XᴴY). Their first child is a boy. What is the probability that he has haemophilia?
Mission 4

Why sons are affected more often

  • A male's one X comes from his mother. One recessive allele is enough: XʰY is affected.
  • A female needs two: XʰXʰ. That needs an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
  • An affected father passes his X to every daughter (all carriers) and to no son.
  • So X-linked recessive traits never pass father → son.
  • Say: "a son inherits his X chromosome from his mother" — not: "haemophilia passes from father to son".
Mission 4

Reading a pedigree

  • Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected. Generations I, II, III; individuals numbered left to right.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is recessive; both parents carry it.
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → the allele is dominant.
  • Recessive allele + affected daughter of an unaffected father → not X-linked, so autosomal.
  • A father passes his X to no son. If a son can only have got the allele from his father, the gene is not X-linked.
  • Close relatives are more likely to carry the same recessive allele from a shared ancestor. That is the genetic basis for prohibiting marriage between close relatives in many societies.
  • Inferring the pattern from many cases is inductive; applying it to deduce one person's genotype is deductive.
Quick check
?A man and a woman who are both unaffected by a genetic disorder have three children: an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected daughter later has four children with an unaffected man, and all four are unaffected. What can be deduced about the disorder?
Mission 4Read the data

  • II: Edward VII (unaffected), Alice (carrier), Leopold (affected), Beatrice (carrier).
  • III: Alice's son Friedrich (affected) and daughter Alexandra (carrier); Leopold's son Charles Edward (unaffected) and daughter Alice (carrier); Beatrice's son Leopold (affected).
  • IV: Alexandra's son Alexei (affected); Leopold's daughter Alice's son Rupert (affected).

  • DNA from the Russian royal remains (2009) found the mutation: an X-linked clotting-factor gene, haemophilia B.
Quick check
?From the earlier screen: Prince Leopold had haemophilia; his wife did not carry the allele. Which statement about their two children, Charles Edward and Alice, is correct?
Mission 4

Predicting from a pedigree

  • Step 1: dominant or recessive? Look for unaffected parents with an affected child.
  • Step 2: autosomal or X-linked? Check affected daughters' fathers and affected fathers' sons.
  • Step 3: write the genotypes you know (affected recessives; carriers revealed by children).
  • Step 4: use a cross to give a probability for the next child.
Mission check

?In birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W: males are ZZ and females are ZW. In a hypothetical species of bird, a feather trait is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on the Z chromosome; the W chromosome carries no allele of this gene. In a population in which this allele is present, which sex is expected to show the trait more often, and why?

It is not about being male. It is about having only one copy of the chromosome that carries the gene.

Mission 5: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 5

Sexual selection

  • Sexual selection: selection by success in getting mates.
  • Mate choice: usually females choose males whose traits signal overall fitness.
  • Competition: males fight or hold territories for access to females.
  • Example: male birds of paradise: elaborate plumage and complex courtship displays; females are dull.
  • The plumage costs energy and attracts predators. It spreads because the gain in mating success outweighs the survival cost.
  • Say: "females prefer the trait, so its alleles rise in frequency" — not: "it is artificial selection because the female chooses".
Quick check
?Male birds of paradise have elaborate, brightly coloured plumage and perform complex courtship displays, while females are dull. The plumage is costly to grow and makes males easier for predators to see. Which explains how this plumage evolved?
Mission 5

Read the data: Endler's guppies

John Endler (1980) studied guppies from Trinidad streams. Males have coloured spots; spot pattern is heritable.

  • Two opposing pressures: females prefer more colourful males; predators catch conspicuous males.
  • Greenhouse ponds: same starting fish, three treatments: dangerous predator (Crenicichla), weak predator (Rivulus), no predator.
  • Result: spot number fell with Crenicichla and rose with Rivulus or no predator. With Crenicichla, spot size came to match the gravel.
  • Field transfer: guppies moved from a Crenicichla stream to a stream with only Rivulus. Two years later the males had more and larger spots.
  • Reading: control the selection pressure, and you control the direction of evolution.
Quick check
?Endler transferred guppies from a stream containing the dangerous predator Crenicichla to a stream that had no guppies and only the weak predator Rivulus, which rarely eats adult guppies. Two years later, the males in the new stream had on average more coloured spots, and larger spots, than the population they came from. Which is the best interpretation?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical fish species, females prefer males with long tail fins, but long-finned males are caught more often by a predatory bird. Fin length is heritable. A sample of the fish is moved to a pond the bird cannot reach. What is most likely to happen to fin length over the following generations?

Two pressures were pulling in opposite directions. Remove one, and the population moves the other way.

Mission 6

Stable means lasting, not unchanging

  • Stability: an ecosystem keeps the same type of community over long periods.
  • Populations still fluctuate; individuals die and are replaced.
  • Evidence: dated sediment cores with fossil pollen, leaves and animals show the same community, layer after layer.
  • Parts of the Amazon rainforest have persisted for tens of millions of years; the Namib Desert has been arid for about 55 million years.
  • Say: "the community persists while populations fluctuate" — not: "nothing changes".
Mission 6

What keeps an ecosystem stable

  • A continuous supply of energy: usually sunlight. Energy is lost as heat, so it cannot be recycled.
  • Recycling of nutrients: their supply is finite.
  • Genetic diversity: some individuals survive a new disease or a change in conditions.
  • Climate (temperature, rainfall) staying within the tolerance of the species present. It may vary, but not beyond those limits.
Quick check
?Which set of conditions is required for an ecosystem to remain stable?
Mission 6

Tipping points

  • An ecosystem can absorb change, up to a point.
  • Tipping point: a threshold. Past it, a small extra change shifts the ecosystem into a different state.
  • Change speeds itself up: positive feedback.
  • The new state can persist even if the original cause is reduced: it may not reverse.
  • So change is often not proportional to the cause: little happens, then a lot happens quickly.
Mission 6

The Amazon makes much of its own rain

  • Trees draw up soil water and transpire it: water vapour enters the air.
  • The vapour cools the forest, drives air flows and falls again as rain further inland.
  • Roughly half of the Amazon's rain comes from water recycled by the forest itself.
  • Clear enough forest: less vapour, less rain, longer dry seasons, more fire. The rest of the forest may die back to drier, open vegetation.
  • How much clearing is too much? Uncertain. Early models: about 40%. Later estimates including climate change and fire: about 20–25% (Lovejoy and Nobre, 2018).
  • About 17% of the original forest had been cleared by 2021, and at least as much again degraded.
Mission 6

Worked example: percentage change

Illustrative data: a region had 520 000 km² of forest in 1985 and 437 000 km² in 2020.

  • Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
  • Change = 437 000 − 520 000 = −83 000 km².
  • −83 000 ÷ 520 000 × 100 = −16.0%.
  • The minus sign shows a decrease.
  • Divide by the original value, not the new one.
Quick check
?In 1970 the area of a rainforest was 4 000 000 km². By 2020 the area was 3 320 000 km². What is the percentage change in the area of forest?
Mission 6

Two more ecosystems near the edge

  • Taiga (boreal forest): warmer temperatures and less winter snow → drought → lower primary production, browning of trees → more frequent, more intense fires → old legacy carbon in the soil burns. The forest switches from storing carbon to releasing it.
  • Coral reefs: more CO2 → ocean acidification → less carbonate, so corals build skeletons more slowly. Warmer water → corals expel their zooxanthellae: bleaching; repeated or long bleaching kills corals.
  • Lose the corals and the whole reef ecosystem collapses.
Mission 6

Testing stability with a mesocosm

  • Mesocosm: a small model ecosystem used to test what affects stability.
  • Best: a sealed glass vessel. Matter cannot enter or leave, so nutrients must recycle; energy still flows: light in, heat out.
  • Aquatic or microbial mesocosms work better than terrestrial ones: e.g. pond water, sediment, water plants, snails, microorganisms.
  • Change one variable (e.g. light intensity); keep others constant; set up repeats.
  • Measure: dissolved oxygen, water clarity, survival of organisms over weeks.
  • Follow IB experimental guidelines: no harm to vertebrates; release or care for organisms properly afterwards.
Quick check
?Why is a sealed glass vessel preferable to an open tank for a mesocosm used to investigate ecosystem stability?
Mission 6

Keystone species

  • Keystone species: has an effect on community structure far larger than its numbers or biomass suggest.
  • Remove it and the community can collapse.
  • Classic evidence (Paine, 1966): on a rocky shore, the starfish Pisaster was removed from test plots. Mussels it used to eat took over the rock, and the number of species fell from 15 to 8.
  • Say: "a keystone species has a disproportionate effect" — not: "the most common species".
Quick check
?Sea otters make up a tiny fraction of the biomass of a kelp forest. Where otters were hunted to extinction, sea urchins multiplied and grazed the kelp down to bare rock, and the fish, seals and seabirds that depended on the kelp disappeared. What does this show about sea otters?
Mission check

?For decades, the phosphate entering a shallow lake rose steadily. The water stayed clear, with dense water plants, until one summer it turned green with algae and the plants died. Later, phosphate was cut back to the level of years before, but the water stayed green. What does this show?

Like the Amazon, the lake absorbed change until it crossed a threshold, then shifted to a new state that did not easily reverse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gametes are made inside pollen grains and ovules; pollination carries pollen to a stigma, and fertilisation follows later.
  • Cross-pollination is promoted by different maturation times, separate male and female flowers or plants, and self-incompatibility; inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles and lowers diversity and vigour.

Mission 2

  • A pollen tube delivers male gametes to the ovule; fertilisation gives a zygote that grows into the embryo; the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary the fruit.
  • Pollination moves pollen before fertilisation; dispersal moves seeds after it.
  • Germination mobilises stored food: starch → sugars for respiration and growth, so dry mass falls until leaves photosynthesise.

Mission 3

  • Alleles are forms of one gene; genotype + environment → phenotype; one dominant allele is enough for the dominant phenotype.
  • Heterozygote × heterozygote → 3 : 1; a test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals a hidden recessive allele.

Mission 4

  • X-linked recessive alleles show in any male who carries one (one X), but in females only when homozygous; sons get their X from their mother.
  • Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; then an affected daughter with an unaffected father → autosomal.

Mission 5

  • Sexual selection favours traits that win mates, even at a cost to survival.

Mission 6

  • Stable ecosystems persist for long periods; they need energy input, nutrient recycling, genetic diversity and climate within tolerance.
  • Past a tipping point, an ecosystem shifts to a new state that may not reverse; the Amazon threshold is uncertain, perhaps 20–40% deforestation. Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
