Mission 1

Mission 1: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Make a guess: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Mission 1

Competing for the same savings

  • To finance a deficit, the government borrows: it sells bonds to savers, banks and pension funds.
  • Firms also borrow to invest. Both compete for the same pool of saving.
  • More government borrowing can push up interest rates and so reduce private investment.
  • Crowding out: an increase in government spending or borrowing that reduces private sector spending.
  • Two kinds: financial crowding out works through higher interest rates. Resource crowding out happens near full employment: workers and materials used by the state are not available to private firms.
Mission 1

The loanable funds market

  • Supply of loanable funds: from saving. Higher real interest rates encourage more saving.
  • Demand for loanable funds: from borrowers: firms' investment plus government borrowing.
  • Illustrative data (billion): at a real interest rate of 4%, saving = private investment = 500.
  • The government borrows an extra 100. Demand shifts right by 100: D1 → D2.
  • New equilibrium: real interest rate 5%, total lending 550.
  • At 5%, private investment falls to 450. 50 is crowded out.
  • The other 50 comes from extra saving drawn out by the higher rate.
  • Say: "private investment falls" — not: "the government takes firms' money". The higher price of borrowing does the work.

Mission 1

Short run and long run

Short run:

  • A higher real interest rate cuts interest-sensitive spending: business investment, new homes, cars bought on credit.
  • This offsets part of the rise in AD from the extra government spending.

Long run:

  • Less private investment → the capital stock grows more slowly.
  • So productive capacity grows more slowly: a smaller rightward shift of LRAS (the PPC grows less).
  • Exception: if the government borrows to build public capital (roads, broadband, training), total capital may still grow.
Quick check
?At full employment, a government increases its deficit spending, financed by borrowing. Saving responds only slightly to interest rates. Which is the most likely long-run effect?
Mission 1

Debt, interest and credit ratings

  • A large debt means large interest payments every year. That money cannot pay for schools, health or lower taxes: debt interest crowds out other public spending.
  • If lenders worry about repayment, they demand a higher yield on government bonds. Agencies may downgrade the credit rating.
  • Government bond yields are a benchmark: when they rise, firms' and households' borrowing costs tend to rise too, deepening crowding out.
  • Example: Greece's rating was cut to "junk" in April 2010, and its borrowing costs soared.
Mission 1

When crowding out is small, or turns to crowding in

Crowding out is smaller when:

  • The economy is in a deep recession: spare capacity, high saving, weak private demand for loans. Extra borrowing raises interest rates very little.
  • The central bank holds interest rates down (rate cuts, QE).
  • Foreign lenders supply funds at little extra cost.

Crowding in (a Keynesian view): government spending raises incomes and sales. Firms then invest more to meet demand (the accelerator).

  • Public capital (roads, research, broadband) can make private investment more profitable.
  • Example: Japan's gross government debt is over 200% of GDP, yet its 10-year bond yield was held near 0% for several years from 2016. The Bank of Japan's bond buying explains part of this.
Mission 1Evaluate

How big is crowding out?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: large near full employment; small in a deep recession.
  • Saving and investment responses: if saving barely responds to interest rates, or investment is very sensitive to them, crowding out is larger.
  • Monetary policy and openness: a central bank holding rates down, or inflows of foreign saving, reduce it.
  • What the money buys: public capital can crowd in private investment; spending on current items is less likely to.
  • Confidence: if high debt makes lenders nervous, yields rise and crowding out grows.
  • The evidence is contested. Monetarist and new classical economists stress crowding out; Keynesian economists stress crowding in during recessions.
Quick check
?In which situation is crowding out likely to be smallest?
Mission check

?An economy is at full employment. The government cuts its budget deficit, so it borrows 100 billion less each year. The supply of loanable funds slopes upward. What happens in the loanable funds market?

Crowding out works in reverse: less government borrowing lowers the real interest rate and lets private investment rise, by less than the cut.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a pay rise leave a low-income family barely any better off?

Make a guess: Why can a pay rise leave a low-income family barely any better off?

Mission 2

Equity is not equality

  • Equality: everyone gets the same (income, wealth or opportunity).
  • Equity: a fair distribution. What is fair is a value judgement (normative).
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same (same income → same tax).
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently (higher income → pay more).
  • Say: "an equitable distribution" (fair) — not: "an equal distribution", unless you mean identical shares.
Mission 2

Why redistribute?

  • In a free market, income depends on the factors you own and what they earn: wages, rent, interest, profit.
  • Some own little: low skills, no property, unable to work. Their market income may be very low.
  • Many economists call a very unequal outcome a market failure: markets can be efficient yet produce an inequitable result.
  • Redistribution: government moves income or wealth from richer to poorer households, using:
    • taxes, transfers and benefits, state-provided services, and laws on pay.
Mission 2

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Compare the average tax rate (tax ÷ income) at different incomes:
    • Progressive: average rate rises as income rises.
    • Proportional: average rate stays the same.
    • Regressive: average rate falls as income rises.
  • Marginal tax rate: the rate on the next unit of income.
  • Illustrative data. Tax bands: 0% on the first 12,000; 20% from 12,000 to 50,000; 40% above 50,000.
    • Income 30,000: tax = 20% × 18,000 = 3,600. Average rate = 3,600 ÷ 30,000 = 12%. Marginal rate 20%.
    • Income 80,000: tax = 20% × 38,000 + 40% × 30,000 = 7,600 + 12,000 = 19,600. Average rate = 24.5%. Marginal rate 40%.
  • Average rate rises with income: progressive.
  • Taxes on spending (e.g. VAT) are usually regressive relative to income: poorer households spend a bigger share of income.
  • Higher rates can weaken incentives to work or invest, so the revenue raised is not simply rate × income.
Quick check
?Using the bands in the previous screen (0% on the first 12,000; 20% from 12,000 to 50,000; 40% above 50,000), what is the average tax rate on an income of 60,000?
Mission 2

Transfers: universal or means-tested?

  • Transfer payments: money paid by government with nothing produced in return: pensions, unemployment and disability benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers are part of public spending, but not spending on output: they move income between households.
  • Universal benefit: paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income (e.g. a state pension for all over an age).
  • Means-tested benefit: paid only to those with low income or wealth (e.g. the UK's Universal Credit).

Mission 2

The poverty trap

  • Poverty trap: as a low earner earns more, benefits are withdrawn and taxes start, so they keep little of the rise.
  • Effective marginal tax rate (EMTR): share of an extra unit of earnings lost to tax and benefit withdrawal.
  • Case: UK, Universal Credit. For each extra £1 of net earnings, benefit falls by 55p (the taper rate, cut from 63% in November 2021).
  • Worked example: a basic-rate taxpayer on Universal Credit earns £100 more.
    • Income tax 20% + National Insurance 8% = £28. Net earnings rise by £72.
    • Benefit withdrawn: 55% × £72 = £39.60.
    • Kept: £72 − £39.60 = £32.40.
    • EMTR = 1 − 0.72 × 0.45 = 67.6%.
  • Say: combine the rates by multiplying what is kept (0.72 × 0.45) — not: by adding them (28% + 55% = 83%), since the taper applies to net earnings.
Mission 2Graph lab

Change the taper

Line: net income (after tax and benefits) against gross earnings. Flat line = nothing kept; 45° line = everything kept.

  • Takeaway: a higher taper flattens the line (stronger trap) but ends benefit sooner (cheaper). A lower taper helps work incentives but stretches benefit to higher earners.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker pays income tax at 20% on extra earnings. A means-tested benefit is withdrawn at 60% of each extra unit of net (after-tax) earnings. What is the worker's effective marginal tax rate?
Mission 2Evaluate

Equity or efficiency?

  • Redistribution often involves a trade-off: more equity, but weaker incentives and some lost output.
  • It depends on:
    • rates: high tax rates and high withdrawal rates weaken incentives more
    • how people respond: if labour supply hardly changes with pay, little output is lost
    • design: universal vs targeted benefits
    • use of the money: spending on education and health can raise productivity, so equity and efficiency go together
  • Moral and political judgement: how much inequality is fair, and how much output to give up for it, is normative. Economics measures the trade-off; it cannot settle it.
  • Higher rates do not always raise more revenue, if people work, invest or declare less (the Laffer curve idea).
Mission check

?A government wants to weaken the poverty trap for low-income working families on a means-tested benefit. Which change would do this most directly?

The poverty trap is about the share of each extra unit that is lost, not the level of support. Lower the tax and withdrawal rates on the next unit, and work pays more, though usually at a higher cost to government.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Make a guess: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Mission 3

Two ways the budget changes

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending. E.g. a budget that cuts income tax or funds new roads.
  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and transfer spending that happen on their own as the economy moves through the trade cycle (also called the business cycle). No new decision is needed.
  • Tax rates and benefit rules stay the same. What changes is how much income, spending and unemployment there is.
Mission 3

How stabilisers work

In a recession:

  • Incomes fall → income tax paid falls. Spending falls → sales taxes (VAT) collected fall.
  • With a progressive tax, tax falls proportionally more than income.
  • Unemployment rises → unemployment benefit payments rise.
  • So disposable income falls by less than income. Consumption, and AD, fall by less.

Illustrative data:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first 10,000 · 20% from 10,000 to 50,000 · 40% above 50,000.
  • Income falls from 60,000 to 40,000 (−33%). Tax falls from 12,000 to 6,000 (−50%).
  • In a boom, the reverse: tax rises faster than income and benefit spending falls. This slows the rise in AD.
  • Say: "automatic stabilisers reduce the size of fluctuations" — not: "they stop recessions".

Quick check
?Illustrative data: income tax is 0% on the first 10,000, 20% from 10,000 to 50,000, and 40% above 50,000. A worker's income falls from 70,000 to 45,000. By how much does the worker's income after tax fall?
Mission 3

Stabilisers and the deficit

  • Lower tax revenue + higher benefit spending = a bigger deficit in a recession, with no new decision.
  • In a boom the deficit shrinks (or a surplus grows) automatically.
  • This automatic part is the cyclical deficit. Discretionary choices change the structural deficit.
  • 2008–09 example: UK borrowing rose to about 10% of GDP in 2009–10. Part was automatic (falling tax receipts, rising benefit claims). Part was discretionary, e.g. a temporary VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% (December 2008 to December 2009).
Mission 3

Automatic vs discretionary: strengths and limits

Automatic stabilisers:

  • Fast: no time lag for recognising the problem or passing a law.
  • Work in both directions: they cushion recessions and cool booms.
  • But partial: they reduce fluctuations; they do not remove them.

Discretionary policy:

  • Can be larger and targeted (e.g. at infrastructure or at low-income households).
  • But faces time lags: recognising the problem, deciding, then putting it into effect. It may arrive after recovery has begun.
  • Can face political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; reversing them in a boom is not.
Mission 3

Fiscal rules

  • A fiscal rule is a lasting limit on borrowing or debt, set to build lenders' trust and resist political pressure to overspend.
  • Examples:
  • EU rules: deficit no more than 3% of GDP; debt no more than 60% of GDP.
  • UK (2024): balance the current budget (borrow only to invest) and have a debt measure falling as a share of GDP, both by 2029–30.
  • Design problem: a rule on the actual deficit can force tax rises or cuts in a recession, which works against the automatic stabilisers.
  • So many rules target the structural (cyclically adjusted) balance, a forecast year, or have escape clauses. The EU suspended its deficit limits from 2020 to 2023.
  • Trade-off: flexible rules let stabilisers work; strict rules are more credible.
Quick check
?A country has a rule that its actual deficit must not exceed 3% of GDP. A recession pushes the deficit from 2% to 4% of GDP. To obey the rule at once, the government raises tax rates. What is the likely effect?
Mission 3Evaluate

How much can stabilisers do?

It depends on:

  • Progressivity and benefits: steeper tax rates and fuller unemployment benefits give a bigger cushion. Countries with larger public sectors tend to have stronger stabilisers.
  • Size of the shock: in a deep recession stabilisers cushion only part of the fall; discretionary policy may be needed too.
  • Debt and rules: high debt or strict rules on the actual deficit can force governments to work against the stabilisers.
  • Incentives: high marginal tax rates and generous benefits may reduce incentives to work, a long-run cost to weigh.
  • Confidence: if households save rather than spend, even well-designed support raises AD less.
Mission check

?Country A has a progressive income tax and pays unemployment benefits. Country B charges every adult the same fixed amount of tax and pays no unemployment benefits. Both suffer the same fall in exports. Neither government takes any new decision. Which statement is correct?

Automatic stabilisers cushion AD in a downturn, and the price is a larger cyclical deficit.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a global company builds a factory in a poorer country, who really gains?

Make a guess: When a global company builds a factory in a poorer country, who really gains?

Mission 4

Multinationals and FDI

  • A multinational corporation (MNC): a firm that produces or provides services in more than one country.
  • Edexcel also says transnational corporation (TNC).
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm in one country invests in productive assets in another that it controls.
    • Greenfield: building a new factory, mine or office.
    • Brownfield / acquisition: buying or merging with a foreign firm.
  • Statistical benchmark: owning 10% or more of the voting shares counts as a lasting interest, so FDI.
  • Inward FDI: foreign firms investing here. Outward FDI: our firms investing abroad.
  • Say: "FDI" for investment by firms — not: aid, which is a transfer from governments or charities.

Mission 4

Why firms go multinational

  • Market access: produce inside a big market, avoiding tariffs and transport costs (e.g. inside a trading bloc).
  • Lower costs: cheaper labour, land or energy.
  • Resources: oil, minerals, farmland only found in certain places.
  • Economies of scale: spread research, brands and management over global sales.
  • Spreading risk across many economies.
  • Lower tax: some countries offer low tax rates or tax breaks to attract investment.
  • Host governments' incentives (grants, tax holidays, special economic zones) pull FDI too.
Mission 4

Impact on the host country

Possible benefits:

  • Jobs and higher wages than local alternatives.
  • Capital a low-saving economy could not raise itself.
  • Technology transfer and skills: training, management methods, new processes.
  • Linkages: local suppliers win orders and learn standards.
  • Tax revenue, exports and foreign currency.

Possible costs:

  • Profit repatriation: profits flow abroad; GNI rises less than GDP.
  • Low pay, poor conditions, pollution if rules are weak.
  • Local firms crowded out by stronger rivals.
  • Footloose: plants can relocate when costs rise.
  • Tax avoidance and political influence.
  • Real case: inward FDI into Vietnam rose from US$1.3bn in 2000 to US$20.2bn in 2024.
Mission 4

Impact on the home country

  • Profits flow home: income credits in the current account (primary income).
  • Cheaper goods for home consumers; firms more competitive worldwide.
  • Jobs may move abroad (offshoring): structural unemployment in home regions.
  • Home investment may fall if firms build abroad instead.
  • Home tax base shrinks if profits are booked abroad.
Quick check
?A foreign-owned car plant in Country H produces output worth $500m a year (value added). It sends $200m of profit to its parent company abroad each year (illustrative data). Other things equal, how does the plant change Country H's GDP and GNI?
Mission 4

Transfer pricing

  • Transfer price: the price one part of an MNC charges another part for goods, services, brands or loans.
  • MNCs can set transfer prices to book profit where tax is low.
  • Worked example (illustrative data). Plant in Country H (profits tax 30%) makes a part for $40. A sister firm in Country L (tax 10%) sells the finished good for $100.

  • Country H loses tax revenue it could spend on public services.
  • Rule used by tax authorities: the arm's length principle: charge what two independent firms would charge.

Mission 4

Ethics and controlling MNCs

Ways to control MNCs:

  • Law: minimum wages, health and safety, environmental and competition law.
  • Tax rules: arm's length transfer pricing, sharing tax data between countries.
  • Real case: a global minimum tax rate of 15% on large MNCs, agreed through the OECD, now applies in many countries.
Mission 4

Evaluate: can MNCs be controlled?

It depends on…

  • Bargaining power. A small host competing for jobs may not risk losing the investment; large markets can set tougher terms.
  • International co-operation. One country acting alone can be avoided by moving profits or plants; agreed rules (minimum tax, data sharing) are harder to dodge.
  • Information. Transfer prices for brands and know-how are hard to check: no market price exists to compare.
  • Enforcement. Laws matter only if inspectors and courts work.
  • Consumers. Pressure and self-regulation work best where buyers notice and care.
Mission 4

Evaluate: inward FDI as a development strategy

How governments attract FDI: tax breaks, special economic zones, infrastructure, education and stable rules.

Whether it works depends on…

  • Linkages. FDI that buys from local suppliers and trains local staff spreads skills and demand. An enclave (imported inputs, foreign managers) does little.
  • Type of FDI. Resource-seeking (mining) often creates few jobs; manufacturing and services usually create more.
  • Terms. Large tax breaks cost revenue; countries competing to offer them risk a "race to the bottom".
  • Profit outflows. Much of the income may leave: watch GNI, not just GDP.
  • Absorptive capacity. Skills and infrastructure let local firms learn from foreign ones.
  • Time. Jobs come at once; productivity spillovers build over years.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and the real interest rate; private investment falls.
  • Crowding out is usually partial: higher rates also draw out extra saving.
  • Short run: interest-sensitive spending falls. Long run: slower capital accumulation and growth.
  • It is smaller in a deep recession, and public capital can crowd in private investment.

Mission 2

  • Equity is fairness, a value judgement; equality means identical shares.
  • Progressive taxes: the average tax rate rises with income.
  • Means-tested benefits target the poor but create a poverty trap: a high effective marginal tax rate.

Mission 3

  • Discretionary policy = a deliberate decision. Automatic stabilisers = tax and transfers that change with the cycle, with no decision.
  • In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: AD falls by less, the deficit rises.
  • Fiscal rules must leave room for stabilisers to work, or they can deepen a recession.

Mission 4

  • MNCs produce in more than one country; FDI buys control of productive assets abroad.
  • FDI brings control and production; portfolio investment brings money only and can leave fast.
  • Host countries can gain jobs, capital, technology and tax, but face profit outflows, footloose plants and weak standards.
  • Transfer pricing shifts profit to low-tax countries; control needs law, tax co-operation and pressure from buyers.
