Make a guess: Muslims call their key duties "pillars". What would fall down if one were missing?

Mission 1

Why "pillars"?

  • The Prophet is reported to have said that Islam is built on five: the shahadah, prayer, zakah, Hajj and fasting in Ramadan (Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase).
  • A pillar (rukn, plural arkan) holds up a building: the pillars are what Islam stands on.
  • Purpose: to live in submission to God. Each pillar is ibadah (worship): "I have only created Jinns and men, that they may serve Me." (Qur'an 51:56, Yusuf Ali)
  • In the Hadith of Jibril, the five pillars define islam (practice), beside iman (belief: the six articles) and ihsan (worshipping God as if you see Him).
  • The pillars are fard (obligatory) for every adult Muslim who is able.
Mission 1

The five and their origins

  • Shahadah (witness): the declaration of faith (47:19; 48:29).
  • Salah (prayer) five times daily (4:103); the number five fixed, in hadith, on the Night Journey (c. 621).
  • Zakah (purifying alms) for the needy (2:43; 9:60); organised in Madinah.
  • Sawm (fasting) in Ramadan (2:183–185); prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Hajj (pilgrimage) to Makkah for all who can (3:97); the Prophet's Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632) is the model.
  • CCEA spelling: Shahada, Salat, Zakat, Sawm, Hajj.
Mission 1Read the source

The shahadah

  • Ashhadu an la ilaha illa'llah, wa ashhadu anna Muhammadan rasulu'llah.
  • "I bear witness that there is no god but God, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the messenger of God."

> "Know, therefore, that there is no god but Allah" (Qur'an 47:19, Yusuf Ali)

> "Muhammad is the messenger of Allah" (Qur'an 48:29, Yusuf Ali)

  • Part one affirms tawhid and rejects shirk. Part two accepts Muhammad's message, so the Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Heard at birth (the adhan whispered to a baby), said in every salah, hoped for as a Muslim's last words.

Q1 Which part of the shahadah denies something, and what?

Show answer

The first: it denies any god but God.

Q2 Why does accepting Muhammad as messenger commit a Muslim to more than belief?

Show answer

It accepts his message and example as guidance for life.

Mission 1

Witness, public and private

  • Shahadah means witness: to declare what one knows to be true.
  • Conversion: a person becomes Muslim by saying the shahadah sincerely, with understanding. Often said before witnesses in a mosque; no further ceremony is required.
  • Public: a statement of truth to the world, and entry into the ummah.
  • Private: a personal commitment; without sincere intention the words alone are empty (63:1 calls hypocrites' testimony false).

Mission 1Diversity

Sunni pillars, Shi'a roots and branches

  • Sunni: the Five Pillars, as in the hadith.
  • Twelver Shi'a: five roots of faith (usul al-din): tawhid, adl (justice), nubuwwa (prophethood), imama (leadership of the Imams), ma'ad (resurrection).
  • And ten branches (furu' al-din): salah, sawm, zakah, khums, Hajj, jihad, enjoining good, forbidding evil, tawalla (loving God's friends) and tabarra (dissociating from their enemies).
  • Many Shi'a add to the shahadah "and Ali is the friend (wali) of God"; most Twelver jurists say it is not part of the obligatory words.
  • Isma'ilis list seven pillars, beginning with walaya (devotion to the Imam) and tahara (purity).
  • So Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the core practices; they group them differently, and Shi'a add duties such as khums.
Mission 1

One building, many dimensions

  • The pillars are a unity: each trains one part of life for God.
  • Word: shahadah. Time: salah shapes each day. Wealth: zakah. Body: sawm. Place and lifetime: Hajj.
  • Individual and ummah: each pillar is personal, yet shared (prayer lines, Ramadan, Hajj).
Mission 1

Intention and action

  • "Actions are only by intentions; each person will have what they intended" (Bukhari, no. 1; paraphrase).
  • Niyyah (intention): each act of worship begins with it.
  • Outward act without inward sincerity is condemned: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali); charity "to be seen of men" is void (2:264).
  • But intention does not replace action: the pillars have conditions and set forms.
  • Motivation, words and deeds: the reward is for the act done sincerely (2:110).

Mission 1

Duty, reward and different contexts

  • Religious duty: the pillars are fard; neglecting them is sin; doing them earns reward (2:110).
  • Within ability: "On no soul doth Allah Place a burden greater than it can bear." (Qur'an 2:286, Yusuf Ali)
  • Exemptions: the sick and travellers make up fasts (2:184); zakah only above a minimum (nisab); Hajj only "those who can afford the journey" (3:97).
  • Contexts (ritual, moral, legal): Muslims in Britain negotiate prayer at work or school, fasting in long summer days, and halal finance.
  • Scholars differ: e.g. for very long northern days, some follow local dawn and sunset, others nearer timings.
  • Eternal reward: Muslims believe sincere observance is rewarded in the hereafter.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are the pillars the whole of Islam?

  • Claim: Keeping the five pillars is all it takes to be a good Muslim.
  • For: The hadith says Islam is "built on" them; they are the duties every Muslim shares.
  • For: Done sincerely, they shape the whole of life: prayer "restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (29:45).
  • Against: "It is not righteousness that ye turn your faces Towards east or West" (Qur'an 2:177, Yusuf Ali): righteousness includes belief, kindness and keeping promises.
  • Against: The Hadith of Jibril adds iman and ihsan; ethics and the wider shari'ah go beyond the five.
  • Criteria: What does "good Muslim" mean: practice, belief, character? Can ritual be sincere without moral change? Do Sunni and Shi'a lists change the answer?

Mission 2

Salah within ibadah

  • Ibadah (worship, service): any lawful act done for God's sake. "Truly, my prayer and my service of sacrifice, my life and my death, are (all) for Allah" (Qur'an 6:162, Yusuf Ali).
  • Salah: the fixed ritual prayer, five times a day; the second pillar.
  • Other prayer surrounds it: nafila (extra), tahajjud (night prayer), du'a (personal calling on God), tasbih (glorifying), wird (Sufi litany), dhikr (remembrance).
  • CCEA spelling: Salat; Friday prayer Salat-ul-Jumu'ah.
Mission 2

Preparing to pray

  • Wudu (washing): "wash your faces, and your hands (and arms) to the elbows; Rub your heads (with water); and (wash) your feet to the ankles" (Qur'an 5:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ghusl (full washing) after major impurity; tayammum (dry earth) when there is no water (5:6).
  • Niyyah (intention) in the heart: this prayer, for God.
  • Face the qiblah, the Ka'bah in Makkah (2:144); a clean place; modest covering.
  • The adhan (call) and iqamah (call to stand) begin congregational prayer.
  • Purity of body points to purity of heart.
Mission 2Read the source

Fixed times

> "set up Regular Prayers: For such prayers are enjoined on believers at stated times." (Qur'an 4:103, Yusuf Ali)

> "Establish regular prayers - at the sun's decline till the darkness of the night, and the morning prayer and reading" (Qur'an 17:78, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Qur'an gives times in outline (also 11:114; 20:130). The number five, and their forms, come from the Sunnah: hadith say they were fixed on the Night Journey (Mi'raj).
  • Fajr (dawn, 2 rak'ahs) · Zuhr (after midday, 4) · Asr (afternoon, 4) · Maghrib (after sunset, 3) · Isha (night, 4).

Mission 2

One rak'ah

  • Rak'ah: one cycle of set words and positions.
  • Takbir ("Allahu akbar": God is greatest) opens the prayer.
  • Qiyam (standing): al-Fatiha in Arabic; in the first two rak'ahs, another passage of the Qur'an follows.
  • Ruku' (bowing), then standing again.
  • Sujud (prostration) twice: "bow down in adoration, and bring thyself the closer (to Allah)!" (Qur'an 96:19, Yusuf Ali). A hadith says a servant is closest to God in prostration (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Tashahhud (sitting, with the shahadah) and taslim ("peace be on you") to right and left end the prayer.

Mission 2Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a salah

  • Same pillar, same five prayers, same core words and movements.
  • Times: many Shi'a combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at three times a day; Sunnis combine mainly when travelling.
  • Hands: most Sunnis fold them (Hanafi, Shafi'i, Hanbali); Shi'a, and many Maliki Sunnis, pray with arms at their sides.
  • Prostration: Shi'a place the forehead on earth or something natural, often a small clay turbah (frequently from Karbala). It is prostrated on, not to.
  • Wudu: Shi'a wipe the feet; Sunnis wash them. Both cite 5:6.
  • Adhan: Shi'a add "Come to the best of deeds" and often a witness to Ali.
Mission 2

Jumu'ah, the Friday prayer

> "O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday (the Day of Assembly), hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business (and traffic)" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

  • Replaces Zuhr on Friday: a khutbah (sermon, in two parts) and two rak'ahs in congregation.
  • After it: "disperse through the land, and seek of the Bounty of Allah" (62:10): Friday is not a day of rest.
  • Sunni law: a duty for adult men who are resident and well; women may attend.
  • Twelver Shi'a: in the Imam's absence (occultation), many jurists hold Friday prayer may be prayed instead of Zuhr rather than required; Iran revived it publicly after 1979.
  • Purpose: unity of the ummah, teaching, news of the community.
Mission 2

The imam

  • The imam leads congregational prayer: chosen for knowledge of the Qur'an and good character (a hadith in Muslim gives this order).
  • Any qualified Muslim may lead; women may lead other women (the majority view).
  • Not a priest: no sacraments, no go-between. Every Muslim prays directly to God.
  • In Britain many mosques employ an imam to lead prayers, give the khutbah, teach, conduct marriages and funerals, and act as chaplain.
  • Persian- and Urdu-speaking Shi'a often call a prayer leader pish-namaz; Imam with a capital (the twelve Imams) means something different.
Mission 2

Other prayer

  • Du'a: "When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" (Qur'an 2:186, Yusuf Ali). Any language, any time.
  • Tahajjud: voluntary night prayer, "an additional prayer (or spiritual profit) for thee" (Qur'an 17:79, Yusuf Ali).
  • Nafila: extra prayers, many linked to the five (sunnah prayers before or after them).
  • Tasbih: glorifying God, e.g. after salah, often with prayer beads: SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu akbar (Shi'a call it the tasbih of Fatimah).
  • Wird: a daily litany given by a Sufi shaykh to his followers.

Mission 2

Outward act, inward state

  • "The believers must (eventually) win through,- Those who humble themselves in their prayers" (Qur'an 23:1–2, Yusuf Ali). Khushu': humble attention.
  • Warning: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ihsan: worship God as if you see Him; if you do not see Him, He sees you (Hadith of Jibril, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Effect on life: "Prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (Qur'an 29:45, Yusuf Ali).
  • The set form supports the heart: the body bows so the self learns to bow.
Mission 2Evaluate

Ritual, personal, collective?

  • Claim: Salah is simply a ritualistic act of piety.
  • For: fixed words in Arabic, many worshippers do not understand them; the form can become habit.
  • For: Muslims who pray mechanically are warned in 107:4–5, which shows the risk is real.
  • Against: niyyah and khushu' are required; the form trains attention.
  • Against: salah changes conduct (29:45); du'a and tahajjud give room for personal words.
  • Personal or collective? Prayer in congregation is worth many times prayer alone (Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase); yet du'a and night prayer are deeply personal.
  • Criteria: What makes an act "only" ritual? Is collective worship the setting for personal experience or a substitute for it?

Mission 3

Wealth as a trust

  • All things belong to God; humans hold wealth on trust. "Believe in Allah and His messenger, and spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Qur'an 57:7, Pickthall).
  • Wealth is good when used well: "Wealth and sons are allurements of the life of this world: But the things that endure, good deeds, are best" (Qur'an 18:46, Yusuf Ali).
  • It is also a test (64:15).
  • Hoarding is condemned (9:34); so is waste: "eat and drink: But waste not by excess" (Qur'an 7:31, Yusuf Ali).
  • Riba (interest, usury) is forbidden: "Allah hath permitted trade and forbidden usury" (Qur'an 2:275, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 3

Zakah: the pillar of giving

  • Zakah: "purification" and "growth". Giving purifies the rest of one's wealth and the giver's heart.
  • "Of their goods, take alms, that so thou mightest purify and sanctify them" (Qur'an 9:103, Yusuf Ali).
  • 2.5% of savings and trade goods held for a lunar year above the nisab (minimum; usually the value of 85 g of gold or 595 g of silver). Crops and livestock have their own rates.
  • Origins: commanded with prayer in the Qur'an (2:43); organised and collected under the Prophet in Madinah.
  • Zakat al-fitr: a small gift of food per person at the end of Ramadan, so the poor can celebrate Eid.
  • Spelling: Zakat.
Mission 3Read the source

Who receives zakah?

> "Alms are for the poor and the needy, and those employed to administer the (funds); for those whose hearts have been (recently) reconciled (to Truth); for those in bondage and in debt; in the cause of Allah; and for the wayfarer" (Qur'an 9:60, Yusuf Ali)

  • Eight categories: the poor, the needy, collectors, new Muslims (or those whose hearts are to be won), freeing captives, debtors, "the cause of Allah", travellers in need.

Mission 3

Sadaqah

  • Sadaqah (from "truth, sincerity"): voluntary giving, any amount, at any time, to anyone in need.
  • Hadith widen it beyond money: a smile, removing harm from a path, a kind word (Tirmidhi; Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Sadaqah jariyah (ongoing charity), e.g. a well: its good continues after death. A hadith names it with useful knowledge and a righteous child as deeds that go on benefiting the dead (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Giving in secret is praised: "if ye conceal them, and make them reach those (really) in need, that is best for you" (Qur'an 2:271, Yusuf Ali).
  • Never spoiled by boasting: "cancel not your charity by reminders of your generosity or by injury" (Qur'an 2:264, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 3

Khums

  • Khums: "a fifth". "know that out of all the booty that ye may acquire (in war), a fifth share is assigned to Allah,- and to the Messenger, and to near relatives, orphans, the needy, and the wayfarer" (Qur'an 8:41, Yusuf Ali).
  • Sunni reading: khums applies to spoils of war and buried treasure.
  • Twelver Shi'a reading: the word for "acquire" covers all gain; so 20% of each year's surplus (what is left after the year's living costs).
  • Half, the share of the Imam, goes to a senior scholar (marja') for religious and charitable work; half, the share of the sayyids, to needy descendants of the Prophet.
  • Twelver Shi'a also pay zakah, on nine named kinds of wealth (crops, livestock, gold and silver coin).

Mission 3

Why give?

  • Obedience: zakah is commanded with prayer (2:43).
  • Compassion: "they feed, for the love of Allah, the indigent, the orphan, and the captive" (Qur'an 76:8, Yusuf Ali).
  • Personal sacrifice: "By no means shall ye attain righteousness unless ye give (freely) of that which ye love" (Qur'an 3:92, Yusuf Ali).
  • Benefits gained: purification, reward multiplied like a grain growing "seven ears" (2:261), protection from greed.
  • Impact on the ummah: support for the poor, debtors and travellers; less envy; bonds of brotherhood.

Mission 4

Sawm: the duty

  • Sawm (fasting): the fourth pillar, kept through the month of Ramadan, the ninth month of the lunar calendar.
  • "O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed to you as it was prescribed to those before you, that ye may (learn) self-restraint" (Qur'an 2:183, Yusuf Ali).
  • From dawn to sunset: no food, drink or sexual relations (2:187: "until the white thread of dawn appear to you distinct from its black thread").
  • Niyyah (intention) for the fast; suhur (pre-dawn meal); iftar (breaking the fast), often with dates and water, as the Prophet did.
  • Origins: prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Because the lunar year is shorter, Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year: sometimes winter, sometimes summer.
Mission 4Read the source

Why Ramadan?

> "Ramadhan is the (month) in which was sent down the Qur'an, as a guide to mankind, also clear (Signs) for guidance and judgment" (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali)

> "Allah intends every facility for you; He does not want to put to difficulties." (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Why is Ramadan the month of fasting, according to 2:185?

Show answer

Mission 4

Who fasts?

  • Required of adult Muslims who are sane, healthy and not travelling.
  • Make up later: the sick and travellers (2:184–185); women who are menstruating or recovering from childbirth (from hadith).
  • Fidya: those who can never fast (e.g. long-term illness, frailty in old age) feed a needy person for each day missed (2:184).
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women may not fast if they fear harm; the schools differ on whether they make up, pay fidya, or both.
  • Children often practise with part-days.
  • Fasting that harms health is not required: the rule is God's ease (2:185).

Mission 4

The nights of Ramadan

  • Many Muslims recite the whole Qur'an during the month.
  • Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power), when the Qur'an's revelation began: "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Yusuf Ali). Sought in the last ten nights.
  • Sunnis often mark the 27th night; Shi'a give special weight to the 19th, 21st and 23rd, which also recall Ali's wounding and death in Ramadan (40 AH).
  • Tarawih: Sunnis pray these extra night prayers in congregation at the mosque.
  • Twelver Shi'a pray extra Ramadan night prayers individually, regarding congregational tarawih as a later practice (introduced under the caliph Umar).
  • I'tikaf: retreat in the mosque in the last ten days.
Mission 4

What the fast is for

  • Relationship to God: "Fasting is for Me, and I will reward it" (hadith qudsi, Bukhari; Muslim; paraphrase). Only God sees a fast kept in secret.
  • Spirituality: taqwa (God-consciousness, 2:183) and control of the self (nafs).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Ihya Ulum al-Din, Book 6: three levels of fasting: the stomach and body; the eyes, tongue, hands and feet kept from sin; the heart kept from everything but God (paraphrase).
  • Morality: whoever does not give up false speech and acting on it, God has no need of their giving up food and drink (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Community: shared iftars; empathy with the hungry; more giving (Ramadan is a common time to pay zakah).
Mission 4Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a fasting

  • Same fast, same exemptions, same month.
  • Ending the fast: Sunnis break it at sunset; many Shi'a wait until the redness in the eastern sky has gone, a few minutes later.
  • Night prayer: congregational tarawih (Sunni) or individual night prayers (Shi'a).
  • Special nights: 27th (often, Sunni); 19th, 21st, 23rd (Shi'a), with mourning for Ali on the 21st.
  • Moon sighting: within both, some follow local sighting, others a national or Saudi announcement or calculation; so mosques in one city may begin or end Ramadan on different days.
Mission 4

Eid-ul-Fitr

  • 1 Shawwal: the "festival of breaking the fast". Fasting on Eid day is forbidden.
  • Zakat al-fitr paid before the Eid prayer: a purification for the one who fasted and food for the poor (Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • Eid prayer in congregation, often in a park or large hall: two rak'ahs and a sermon; takbir on the way.
  • New clothes, gifts for children, visiting family, graves and neighbours; "Eid Mubarak".
  • Social importance: the poor are included; unity; brotherhood of the ummah.
  • Origins: the Prophet set the two Eids in Madinah in place of two earlier festival days (Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • CCEA spelling: Eid-ul-Fitr.
Mission 4

Eid-ul-Adha

  • 10 Dhu'l-Hijjah, during the Hajj: the "festival of sacrifice", celebrated by all Muslims, not only pilgrims.
  • Recalls Ibrahim's readiness to sacrifice his son: "O my son! I see in vision that I offer thee in sacrifice" (Qur'an 37:102, Yusuf Ali); "We ransomed him with a momentous sacrifice" (Qur'an 37:107, Yusuf Ali).
  • The Qur'an does not name the son; most Muslims say Isma'il.
  • Qurbani (udhiyah): a sheep, goat, cow or camel sacrificed; the meat is often shared in thirds: family, friends, the poor. Many British Muslims pay charities to sacrifice abroad.
  • "It is not their meat nor their blood, that reaches Allah: it is your piety that reaches Him" (Qur'an 22:37, Yusuf Ali).
  • CCEA spelling: Eid-ul-Adha.

Mission 4Evaluate

Who benefits more from fasting?

  • Claim: Fasting benefits the individual more than the community.
  • Individual: taqwa and self-control (2:183); the hadith qudsi makes the fast a secret between the person and God.
  • Individual: Al-Ghazali's levels are all about the inner life.
  • Community: shared iftars, tarawih and Eid build unity; zakat al-fitr and Ramadan giving feed the poor.
  • Community: a person who fasts learns empathy, and empathy is for others.
  • Criteria: Can the two be separated? Which benefits does the Qur'an name? Is a fast without changed conduct of any benefit?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Islam is "built on five": shahadah, salah, zakah, sawm, Hajj; each is worship and each is fard for those able.
  • The shahadah witnesses to tawhid and to Muhammad as messenger; saying it sincerely makes a person Muslim.
  • The pillars form a unity of outward act and inward intention; Twelver Shi'a group the same practices as roots and branches.

Mission 2

  • Salah: five fixed prayers, with wudu, niyyah and qiblah; each rak'ah moves from standing to prostration.
  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the pillar but differ in timing, hand position, prostration surface and wudu.
  • Other prayer (du'a, tahajjud, nafila, tasbih, wird) gives room for personal devotion beside salah and Jumu'ah.

Mission 3

  • Wealth belongs to God and is held on trust; hoarding, waste and riba are condemned.
  • Zakah (2.5% above the nisab, eight groups), sadaqah (voluntary, any good deed) and, for Twelver Shi'a, khums (a fifth of yearly surplus).

Mission 4

  • Sawm: dawn to sunset in Ramadan, for taqwa (2:183), with exemptions for illness, travel and frailty.
  • Ramadan's benefits: nearness to God, spiritual discipline, moral change, unity and care for the poor.
  • Eid-ul-Fitr ends the fast with zakat al-fitr and shared joy.
  • Eid-ul-Adha recalls Ibrahim's sacrifice (37:102–107; 22:37).
