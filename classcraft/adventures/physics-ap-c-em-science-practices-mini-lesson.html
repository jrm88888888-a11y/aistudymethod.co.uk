Mission 1

Mission 1: A newton, a joule, a watt: what are they actually made of?

Make a guess: A newton, a joule, a watt: what are they actually made of?

Mission 1

Number × unit

  • A physical quantity = number × unit. 3.0 m means 3.0 × (1 meter).
  • No unit, no meaning: "the length is 3.0" could be 3.0 mm or 3.0 km.
  • Units obey algebra: m ÷ s = m/s; m × m = m².
  • Table headings and axis labels give the quantity and its unit, e.g. speed (m/s).
Mission 1

The base units you need

  • Every SI unit is built from base units.
  • Mass: kg · length: m · time: s · current: A · temperature: K · amount of substance: mol.
  • Every unit you will use is built from these six. (The SI has a seventh, the candela, for luminous intensity; you will not need it.)
Mission 1

Derived units

  • A derived unit comes from a defining equation. Replace each quantity by its unit.
  • Velocity = displacement ÷ time → m/s. Acceleration → m/s².
  • Density = mass ÷ volume → kg/m³. Momentum = mass × velocity → kg·m/s.
  • Force = mass × acceleration → kg·m/s² = N (newton).
  • Work = force × distance → N·m = kg·m²/s² = J (joule).
  • Charge = current × time → A·s = C (coulomb).
Mission 1

Worked example: the watt

  • Power = energy ÷ time.
  • Unit: J/s.
  • J = kg·m²/s² (from work = force × distance).
  • W = kg·m²/s² ÷ s = kg·m²/s³.
  • Method: write the defining equation, then substitute units you already know, down to base units.
Quick check
?A spring's stiffness is its spring constant: the force needed per unit change in its length. What is the unit of the spring constant in base units?
Mission 1

Prefixes and powers of ten

  • T tera 10¹² · G giga 10⁹ · M mega 10⁶ · k kilo 10³
  • c centi 10⁻² · m milli 10⁻³ · µ micro 10⁻⁶ · n nano 10⁻⁹ · p pico 10⁻¹²
  • Scientific notation: 0.000 52 m = 5.2 × 10⁻⁴ m. One nonzero digit before the decimal point.
  • Prefix on a squared unit: square the factor too. 1 mm = 10⁻³ m, so 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m².
Quick check
?The cross-sectional area of a wire is 0.50 mm². What is this area in m²?
Mission 1

Converting units

  • Multiply by a fraction equal to 1, and cancel units.
  • 72 km/h = 72 × 1000 m ÷ 3600 s = 20 m/s.
Mission check

?Pressure is force ÷ area, and its unit is the pascal (Pa). What is the pascal in base units?

Newton = kg·m/s². Joule = kg·m²/s². Watt = kg·m²/s³. Every one is built from base units, by the equation that defines it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a straight line expose a law that is hiding inside a curve?

Make a guess: How can a straight line expose a law that is hiding inside a curve?

Mission 2

Plotting a good graph

  • What you change (independent variable): x-axis. What you measure: y-axis.
  • Label: quantity and unit, e.g. time (s).
  • Scale: points fill more than half the grid; steps of 1, 2 or 5.
  • Plot points as small, sharp crosses.
  • Line of best fit: a straight line or smooth curve, points scattered evenly either side. Never join the dots.
Mission 2

Slope, intercept, area

  • Slope = Δy ÷ Δx. Use a large triangle, at least half the line.
  • Slope has units: y-unit ÷ x-unit.
  • y-intercept: the value of y where x = 0.
  • Area under the line: its units are y-unit × x-unit. On a velocity–time graph it is displacement.
  • On a curve: draw a tangent, then take its slope.
Quick check
?A graph shows the force F needed to stretch a spring against how far it is stretched, x. F is in N and x in cm. The line passes through the origin and through (8.0 cm, 2.0 N). What is the spring constant in N/m?
Mission 2

Resistance from an I–ΔV graph

  • Ohm's law: I = ΔV/R. Plot I (vertical) against ΔV (horizontal).
  • Ohmic element: a straight line through the origin. I ∝ ΔV, so R is constant.
  • Slope = I ÷ ΔV = 1/R. So R = 1 ÷ slope.
  • Curved graph: not ohmic. R = ΔV/I at a point, not 1 ÷ the tangent slope.
Quick check
?The graph shows the current I in each of three circuit elements, X, Y and Z, as a function of the potential difference ΔV across it. Which of the elements are ohmic, and why?
Mission 2

Making a straight line

  • Match the equation to y = mx + b.
  • Object starting from rest, constant acceleration:
  • x − x0 = ½ax t² (a kinematics equation)
  • (x − x0) against t²: straight line through the origin, slope ½ax.
  • Displacement against t: a curve. The law was hiding in it.
  • Rule: rearrange the law into y = mx + b. Transform the measured variable (y) or the changed variable (x), whichever the law needs. Constants end up in the slope and intercept.
Mission 2

Worked example: slope and intercept

  • A ball rolls down a long ramp past photogates at different distances from a start line. Plot v² against distance.
  • vx² = vx0² + 2ax(x − x0) (a kinematics equation) → vertical axis vx², horizontal axis (x − x0), slope = 2ax, intercept = vx0².
  • The best-fit line has slope 1.6 m/s² → acceleration = 1.6 ÷ 2 = 0.80 m/s².
  • Intercept: 4.0 m²/s² → initial speed = √4.0 = 2.0 m/s.
Quick check
?The intensity I at a distance r from a small source radiating power P equally in all directions is I = P/(4πr²). Which graph is a straight line through the origin, and what is its slope?
Mission check

?The period of a mass on a spring is T = 2π√(m/k), where k is the spring constant. A student varies m and measures T. Which graph gives a straight line through the origin, and what is its slope?

Rearrange the law into a straight-line form, plot what it tells you to plot, and the curve becomes a line whose slope and intercept hold the constants.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Roughly how many times does a heart beat in a lifetime: 10⁶, 10⁹ or 10¹²?

Make a guess: Roughly how many times does a heart beat in a lifetime: 10⁶, 10⁹ or 10¹²?

Mission 3

Order of magnitude

  • Order of magnitude: the nearest power of ten.
  • 3 × 10⁴ → 10⁴. 7 × 10⁴ → 10⁵.
  • The cut-off is √10 ≈ 3.2, the midpoint on a log scale: below it round down, above it round up.
  • "Two orders of magnitude bigger": about 100 times bigger.
Mission 3

Numbers worth knowing

  • Adult: mass 70 kg, height 1.7 m, walking 1.5 m/s, reaction time 0.2 s.
  • Car on a fast road: about 30 m/s. One story of a building: 3 m.
  • Water: 1000 kg/m³. Air: about 1.2 kg/m³.
  • Sound in air: 340 m/s. Light: 3.0 × 10⁸ m/s.
  • Atom: 10⁻¹⁰ m across. Nucleus: 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Visible light: wavelengths 400–700 nm. Earth's radius: 6.4 × 10⁶ m.
Quick check
?Kinetic energy = ½ × mass × speed². What is the order of magnitude of the kinetic energy of an adult walking?
Mission 3

The Fermi method

  • Break the unknown into factors you can estimate.
  • Round each factor to one significant figure.
  • State each assumption.
  • Multiply. Give the answer as an order of magnitude or to 1 s.f.
  • Errors in different factors partly cancel, so the result is usually within a factor of ten.
Mission 3

Worked example: heartbeats

  • Resting heart rate: about 70 beats per minute.
  • Minutes in a year: 60 × 24 × 365 ≈ 5 × 10⁵.
  • Lifetime: about 80 years.
  • Beats ≈ 70 × 5 × 10⁵ × 80 ≈ 3 × 10⁹.
  • Order of magnitude: 10⁹.
Quick check
?What is the best estimate of the volume of water in a full bath?
Mission 3

How quantities scale

  • Read the equation as a proportion. Ask: what happens to Y if X doubles?
  • Area of a square of side L: A = L². Double L → A × 4.
  • Pendulum period, T ∝ √L. Make L 4 × longer → T × 2.
  • Gravity between two masses, F ∝ 1/r². Triple r → F × 1/9.
  • Hold everything else constant; change one factor at a time.
Mission 3

Estimates catch mistakes

  • Before you accept an answer, ask: is this size sensible?
  • A walker at 15 m/s? Ten times too fast: look for a unit error (km/h, cm, ms).
  • A 70 kg person's weight is about 700 N. An answer of 7 N or 70 000 N signals a slip.
  • Write the expected order of magnitude first, then calculate.
Quick check
?A solid cylinder stands on one end on a table. A second cylinder of the same material is twice as tall and has twice the diameter. How does the pressure it exerts on the table compare with the first cylinder's? (Pressure = force ÷ area.)
Mission check

?A classroom is about 8 m long, 6 m wide and 3 m high. What is the order of magnitude of the mass of air in it?

Break it into factors you know, round them, multiply. A heart beats about 3 × 10⁹ times in a lifetime: order 10⁹.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do physicists time 20 swings of a pendulum instead of just one?

Make a guess: Why do physicists time 20 swings of a pendulum instead of just one?

Mission 4

Planning an experiment

  • Independent variable: the one you change.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure.
  • Control variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them fixed.
  • Take several values (6 or more is good practice), spread over a wide range.
  • Repeat each reading and average.
  • Plan the graph first: what will you plot to get a straight line?
Mission 4

Measure many, then divide

  • One swing of a pendulum: about 1.4 s. Hand timing: about ± 0.2 s. That is 14%.
  • Time 20 swings: about 28 s, still ± 0.2 s. That is 0.7%.
  • T = 28.0 ÷ 20 = 1.40 s; uncertainty 0.2 ÷ 20 = ± 0.01 s.
  • The same reading uncertainty is shared out over many cycles.
  • Same idea: weigh 50 ball bearings and divide by 50.
  • Start and stop timing as the bob passes a marker at the center of the swing, where it moves fastest.
Mission 4

Conclusions and evaluation

  • Conclusion: state what the data show, only within the range tested. Refer to the graph.
  • Compare with theory, allowing for the uncertainties.
  • Evaluate: find the largest source of uncertainty and say why.
  • Improve with a specific change and the error it reduces: "photogates remove reaction time", not "be more careful".
  • Systematic error → change the method or correct it. Random error → repeat, or measure a larger quantity.
Mission 4

The experimental design question

  • Describe a procedure someone else could follow: equipment, what you measure, with what.
  • Name what you change, what you measure, and what you keep the same.
  • Several values over a wide range; repeated trials.
  • Say what to graph so the result is linear, and how the slope or intercept gives the answer.
  • Say how you reduce uncertainty: many trials, larger measured quantities.
Quick check
?A student investigates how the time t for a ball to fall from rest depends on the drop height h. (h = ½gt², with g constant.) Which plan is best?
Mission check

?A student has a ruler with 1 mm divisions and wants the thickness of one sheet of paper, about 0.1 mm. Which choice and reason are both correct?

The reading uncertainty stays about the same however much you measure. Measure 20 swings, or 200 sheets, and it becomes a tiny fraction of the total.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every derived unit reduces to base units (kg, m, s, A, K, mol) through its defining equation: N = kg·m/s², J = kg·m²/s², W = kg·m²/s³.
  • Prefixes: square or cube the factor with the unit (1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m²).

Mission 2

  • Slope = Δy/Δx with units; area = y × x; tangent for a curve. Linearize by rearranging the law into a straight-line form.

Mission 3

  • Order of magnitude = nearest power of ten. Fermi method: factors, 1 s.f., stated assumptions.
  • Know typical values (70 kg, 1.5 m/s, 1.2 kg/m³ for air, 10⁻¹⁰ m for an atom) and use them to sanity-check answers.
  • Predict factor changes from the proportion in the equation.

Mission 4

  • Plan: independent, dependent and control variables; at least 6 values over a wide range; repeats; a straight-line graph.
  • Measure many cycles or items and divide: the same reading uncertainty becomes a smaller percentage.
