Mission 1

What makes a gurdwara

  • Gurdwara means "door (or gateway) of the Guru".
  • What makes a building a gurdwara is the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib, the scripture Sikhs treat as their living Guru since 1708.
  • A converted church, a warehouse, a house: each becomes a gurdwara when the Guru Granth Sahib is installed with respect.
  • Outside: the Nishan Sahib, a saffron or blue flag with the Khanda emblem, shows that a gurdwara is here.
  • No ordained priesthood: a granthi reads and cares for the scripture, but any competent Sikh may do so.
Mission 1

Inside the prayer hall (darbar)

  • The Guru Granth Sahib rests on a raised platform (takht or palki) under a canopy (chanani), wrapped in cloths (rumalas).
  • A reader waves a chaur (whisk) over it: an old sign of royal honour, here given to the Guru.
  • Everyone covers their head, removes shoes and sits on the floor, lower than the scripture: no special seats for the rich or powerful.
  • Men and women often sit on different sides by custom; the Rahit Maryada does not require it.
  • No tobacco or alcohol may be brought in.
  • At night the scripture is carried to a resting room (sachkhand or sukhasan room) and opened again at dawn.
Mission 1

The diwan: what happens in worship

  • Kirtan: singing hymns (shabads) from the Guru Granth Sahib, usually by ragis with harmonium and tabla.
  • Katha: explanation of the hymns.
  • Ardas: the standing prayer that remembers the Gurus, the martyrs and the Khalsa, and asks for the good of all (Sarbat da bhala).
  • Hukamnama (vak lao): the scripture is opened at random and the first hymn on the left-hand page is read as the Guru's guidance for the day.
  • Karah parshad: a sweet offering of flour, sugar and ghee, shared equally with everyone present.
Mission 1

Freedom of worship

  • Who may lead? Any Sikh, man or woman, of any caste, who is competent: able to read Gurmukhi correctly and sing or explain the hymns.
  • Who may attend? Anyone, Sikh or not, of any religion, caste or sex, if they follow the gurdwara's customs.
  • This expresses Guru Nanak's teaching that all people share the one divine light, and his rejection of caste and priestly privilege.
  • Freedom of worship also reflects Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom (1675), remembered as defending other people's freedom to worship.
  • Worship as identity: gathering round the Guru Granth Sahib, in Punjabi and Gurmukhi, marks the sangat (congregation) as Sikh.

Mission 1

Where langar comes from

  • Langar: the free community kitchen, and the meal served in it. Every gurdwara has one.
  • Guru Nanak: at Kartarpur (c. 1522) his community farmed, worshipped and ate together.
  • Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, ran the langar; the Guru Granth Sahib praises her care (Guru Granth Sahib 967).
  • Guru Amar Das: "pehle pangat, pichhe sangat": first sit in line (pangat) for the meal, then join the congregation. Sikh tradition says even the Mughal emperor Akbar ate on the floor before meeting him.
  • Your spec names two sources for langar: the B40 Janam Sakhi (a life of Guru Nanak, manuscript dated 1733) and Adi Granth 1068.
Mission 1

Langar as sewa and equality

  • Sewa: selfless service. Volunteers cook, serve and wash up: physical sewa. Donating food or money: material sewa.
  • Everyone sits together in a row (pangat), usually on the floor, and is served the same simple vegetarian food.
  • Vegetarian, so that no guest is excluded by what they may or may not eat.
  • In Guru Nanak's Punjab, caste rules forbade eating with or from lower castes: eating together was a public rejection of caste.
  • Spiritual and material needs: the hungry are fed; those who serve learn humility.
Mission 1Read the source

The Rahit Maryada on langar

The Sikh Rahit Maryada (Code of Conduct, approved 1945), section on langar (paraphrase):

  • The langar teaches sewa and helps root out the distinction between high and low castes.
  • Anyone, of any caste or creed, may sit in the pangat; no distinction is to be made among those eating.
  • Everyone eats sitting together in a row (pangat), with no seating by rank.

Show answer

Q2 Why would a langar that seated castes separately fail the code?

Show answer

It would bring back the distinction of high and low the langar is meant to remove.

Mission 1Diversity

Ideal and practice

  • Chairs or floor? In the 1990s some gurdwaras in Canada used tables and chairs in the langar. The Akal Takht (1998) ruled that langar be eaten seated on the floor. Many gurdwaras now offer chairs for elderly or disabled people.
  • Caste-named gurdwaras: some British gurdwaras grew out of caste-based communities (e.g. Ramgarhia). Critics say this contradicts the langar's message; supporters say anyone may still attend.
  • Women's roles: women lead kirtan and serve as granthis in many gurdwaras, but women do not perform kirtan inside the sanctum at Harmandir Sahib, Amritsar; an SGPC announcement in 2005 that they could has not been put into practice. Many Sikhs campaign for change.
  • Language: some diaspora gurdwaras project English translations or give katha in English.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The gurdwara is an essential part of the Sikh religion."

  • For: the Guru Granth Sahib, the living Guru, is installed and honoured there. The sangat is where Sikhs grow spiritually (Guru Nanak praised the company of the holy). Langar, sewa, festivals, amrit initiation, weddings (Anand Karaj) and teaching all centre on it.
  • Against: Sikhs pray at home (Japji, Rehras, Kirtan Sohila); nam simran can be done anywhere. Many Sikh homes have a Guru Granth Sahib or a gutka (prayer book). Sikh teaching stresses the inner life over buildings and ritual.
  • Comparing practices: is kirtan, langar or hukamnama most central? Kirtan centres the Guru's word; langar shows faith in action.
  • Judge it by: Does "essential" mean necessary for salvation or for community life? What would Sikhism lose without it? Diaspora experience.
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, practice, significance

Question: Explain how the langar expresses Sikh beliefs.

  • Belief: all people share the one divine light, so caste and status are meaningless before God.
  • Practice: everyone sits in the pangat and receives the same food, served by volunteers.
  • Source: Guru Amar Das's "pehle pangat, pichhe sangat"; the Rahit Maryada's rule against distinctions of high and low.
  • Development: serving without reward is sewa, which works against ego (haumai) and is part of a Sikh's spiritual path.
  • AO1 tip: link each practice to a belief and a source, then say why it matters to Sikh identity.

Mission 2

Two kinds of Sikh festival

  • Gurpurbs: anniversaries of the Gurus' births and deaths (e.g. Guru Nanak's birth; Guru Arjan's martyrdom).
  • Melas ("fairs"): gatherings on dates shared with the wider Punjabi calendar, re-shaped by Sikh history: Vaisakhi, Bandi Chhor Divas (Diwali), Hola Mohalla.
  • Sikh tradition says Guru Amar Das first asked Sikhs to gather before the Guru at Vaisakhi and Diwali, giving old festivals a Sikh focus.
  • A festival in the gurdwara usually includes an akhand path (unbroken 48-hour reading of the Guru Granth Sahib), kirtan, Ardas and langar.
Mission 2

Vaisakhi 1699

  • Vaisakhi: Punjabi New Year in the solar calendar and the start of the wheat harvest, around 13–14 April.
  • At Anandpur in 1699, Guru Gobind Singh asked the gathered Sikhs: who will give his head for the Guru?
  • Five men came forward one by one and went into a tent. They returned dressed like the Guru: the Panj Piare (five beloved ones).
  • He prepared amrit (sugar-water stirred with a double-edged sword, the khanda) and initiated them into the Khalsa; then asked them to initiate him.
  • Men took the name Singh, women later Kaur; all were to keep the 5Ks.
  • The five came from different castes and regions: the Khalsa was one family, without caste.
Mission 2

Vaisakhi today

  • Akhand path ending on Vaisakhi morning; special kirtan and Ardas.
  • The Nishan Sahib is taken down, its pole washed, and a new cover (chola) and flag raised.
  • Amrit sanchar: many gurdwaras hold Khalsa initiation on or near Vaisakhi.
  • Nagar kirtan: a procession through the streets, led by five Sikhs representing the Panj Piare, with the Guru Granth Sahib carried on a float; free food for passers-by.
  • In Punjab, also harvest fairs and bhangra: cultural celebration alongside religious meaning.
  • Vaisakhi 1919: the Jallianwala Bagh massacre in Amritsar took place on this day.
Mission 2

What Vaisakhi means for Sikhs

  • Identity: the birth of the Khalsa; Singh, Kaur and the 5Ks make Sikhs visible.
  • Sacrifice: the five offered their heads; Sikhs remember the readiness to give everything for the Guru.
  • Virtues of Sikh living: courage, discipline, equality, devotion.
  • Aims and duties: protecting truth and standing up for the oppressed, the duties of the sant sipahi (saint-soldier).
  • Belonging: the Khalsa is a community; the Panj Piare at the head of processions show it.
  • The community's role: sangats plan the akhand path, cook langar, organise nagar kirtan and prepare amrit sanchar. Without them, the festival does not happen.

Mission 2

The release of Guru Hargobind

  • Guru Hargobind (Guru 1606–1644), sixth Guru, was held in Gwalior Fort on the orders of the Mughal emperor Jahangir.
  • Sikh tradition tells that when he was to be freed, he refused to leave without the 52 Hindu rulers (rajas) imprisoned with him.
  • Jahangir said only those who could hold on to the Guru's cloak could go. The Guru had a cloak made with 52 tassels, and all 52 walked out.
  • He reached Amritsar at Diwali; Sikhs lit lamps round the Harmandir Sahib to welcome him.
  • He became known as Bandi Chhor: "the one who frees prisoners".
  • Tradition usually dates the release to 1619; sources differ.
Mission 2

Diwali / Bandi Chhor Divas today

  • Harmandir Sahib and gurdwaras worldwide are lit with lamps, candles and lights; fireworks in Amritsar.
  • Akhand path, kirtan, Ardas and langar.
  • Many Sikhs call the day Bandi Chhor Divas (Day of Liberation) to mark its Sikh meaning; others still say Diwali, and many families celebrate alongside Hindu neighbours.
  • Sikhs also remember Bhai Mani Singh, granthi of Harmandir Sahib, executed in the 1730s after a Diwali gathering at Amritsar.
  • The date follows the lunar month, so it moves each year with Diwali (October or November).
Mission 2

What Bandi Chhor means for Sikhs

  • Freedom and deliverance: the Guru frees others, not just himself; spiritually, the Guru frees the soul from maya and haumai.
  • Good overcomes evil; light overcomes darkness: lamps express the Guru's light (gur = darkness-dispeller, as Sikhs often explain the word).
  • Aims and duties: to work for others' freedom and to fight injustice, like the Guru.
  • Unity: the sangat gathers in one place, often at Amritsar.
  • Community role: sangats light the gurdwara, cook langar, organise kirtan and keep stories alive for children.
Mission 2Read the source

Diwali in the Guru Granth Sahib?

  • The Bandi Chhor story is not in the Guru Granth Sahib: the scripture is hymns, not a history of the Gurus, and its first form, the Adi Granth (1604), predates the release. The story is kept by Sikh tradition and historians.
  • Bhai Gurdas (c. 1551–1636) uses Diwali as an image (Var 19:6, paraphrase): on the night of Diwali lamps are lit, stars appear in the sky, flowers bloom in gardens; yet all of these fade, while the light of the Guru's word endures.

Q1 What contrast does Bhai Gurdas draw?

Show answer

Brief, passing lights against the lasting light of the Guru's word.

Q2 Why might this matter for how Sikhs celebrate?

Show answer

The outward lights point to an inner, spiritual light.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Vaisakhi is more important than Bandi Chhor Divas."

  • For: it marks the birth of the Khalsa and the shape of Sikh identity today (5Ks, Singh/Kaur, amrit). Many choose it for initiation. It is distinctively Sikh, not shared with Hindu Diwali.
  • Against: Bandi Chhor's message (freedom for others, light over darkness) applies to every Sikh, including non-Khalsa (sahajdhari) Sikhs. It shows the Guru as liberator, at the heart of Sikh teaching on mukti.
  • Festivals in general: are they necessary? Sikh teaching stresses daily nam simran over special days; yet festivals bind the sangat and teach children. Is celebrating history meaningful? Remembering sacrifice can renew commitment; or it can become routine or merely cultural.
  • Judge it by: which festival shapes belief or practice more? Which unites Sikhs more? Does importance depend on whether a Sikh is amritdhari?

Festival, story, meaning

  • Practice today: amrit sanchar, nagar kirtan led by five Sikhs, a new Nishan Sahib.
  • Significance: Sikh identity and belonging; the duty to protect the oppressed as saint-soldiers; equality (five castes, one Khalsa).

Mission 3

Why Sikhs felt a need for reform

  • 1849: the British annex Punjab after the fall of Ranjit Singh's kingdom.
  • Many Sikhs share customs with Hindu neighbours: Brahmin priests at weddings, images of Hindu deities kept in the precincts of the Harmandir Sahib, caste practices.
  • Gurdwaras are run by hereditary custodians (mahants), some corrupt, backed by the British.
  • Pressure from outside: Christian missions (Punjab's last Maharaja, Duleep Singh, became a Christian in 1853) and the Hindu reform movement Arya Samaj (founded 1875), which criticised the Gurus and sought to bring Sikhs back into Hinduism.
  • Many Sikh leaders fear the community may dissolve.
  • Response: the Singh Sabha movements (from 1873); their Tat Khalsa wing defined Sikhism as a religion distinct from Hinduism, and its work led to the Rahit Maryada.
Mission 3

The Rahit Namas

  • Rahit: the Sikh code of conduct; namas: manuals. So Rahit Namas are 18th- and early 19th-century codes of Khalsa discipline.
  • Examples: the Tankhah-nama (attributed to Bhai Nand Lal), and rahit namas associated with Bhai Chaupa Singh, Bhai Desa Singh and Bhai Prahlad Singh.
  • They record the 5Ks, daily prayers, the ban on tobacco and other rules, often attributed to Guru Gobind Singh.
  • Problems: they differ from one another; some contain rules that reflect their own era (e.g. conflict with Mughals) or Brahmin influence.
Mission 3

The Sikh Rehat Maryada

  • SGPC committee work on one agreed code begins in 1931; approved 1945; published 1950.
  • Drafted after consulting scholars and the main Sikh institutions, drawing on the Guru Granth Sahib, the Gurus' example and the Rahit Namas.
  • Two parts:
  • Personal life: nam simran and daily banis (prayers), the gurdwara, ceremonies (birth, Anand Karaj, death), honest living, sewa.
  • Panthic (community) life: amrit initiation, the 5Ks, the four kurahits (cardinal prohibitions: cutting hair; eating ritually slaughtered meat; sexual relations outside marriage; tobacco), and tankhah (penance).
  • Your spec spells it Rahit Maryada. Relationship: the Rahit Maryada sifts the older Rahit Namas into one standard.
Mission 3Read the source

Who is a Sikh?

Sikh Rehat Maryada, Article I (paraphrase): a Sikh is any person who believes in one Immortal Being; the ten Gurus, from Guru Nanak to Guru Gobind Singh; the Guru Granth Sahib; the words and teachings of the ten Gurus; and the initiation (amrit) given by the tenth Guru; and who owes allegiance to no other religion.

Q1 Does this definition require every Sikh to have taken amrit?

Show answer

No: it requires belief in the amrit given by the tenth Guru; amrit is the ideal, taken when ready.

Q2 Why does "no other religion" matter for the Singh Sabha's aims?

Show answer

It draws the boundary between Sikh and Hindu that the reformers sought.

Mission 3

Impact: uniformity and its limits

  • Uniformity worldwide: the same daily banis, amrit ceremony, Anand Karaj and gurdwara order from Amritsar to Vancouver.
  • Identity: clear definitions of Sikh and non-Sikh; the 5Ks and kurahits as shared markers.
  • Self-discipline: a daily rule of life; tankhah allows wrongs to be confessed and put right.
  • Gurdwara practice: how the Guru Granth Sahib is installed, read and closed; Ardas; langar.
  • Limits: some groups keep their own maryada (e.g. the Damdami Taksal, Nihangs, the Akhand Kirtani Jatha), and the Namdharis follow a living Guru; many sahajdhari Sikhs are not fully covered by its panthic rules.
  • Interpretation and reform continue: e.g. debates over women's roles in the Harmandir Sahib's kirtan, or electronic copies of gurbani.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Rehat Maryada is an effective guide to Sikh living."

  • For: clear and practical; agreed by the main Sikh representative body; gives every Sikh a shared rule of life.
  • For: grounded in the Guru Granth Sahib and the Gurus' example; it removed contradictions found in the Rahit Namas.
  • Against: it reflects one variant (Tat Khalsa) and sidelines other Sikh ways of life (as some historians argue).
  • Against: it is not accepted in full by every group, and many Sikhs do not keep all of it.
  • Against: it says little on many modern issues (e.g. bioethics, online worship), so interpretation is still needed.
  • Judgement criteria: fidelity to the Gurus; acceptance across the Panth; usefulness in daily life today.

Mission 4

Nam and simran

  • Nam ("Name"): not just a word for God, but God's presence and nature running through all creation.
  • Simran: remembrance: keeping God constantly in mind.
  • Nam simran: remembering God through the Name; nam japna is its active form: repeating the Name.
  • Your spec calls it Naam Japo.
  • Aim: to turn the self from haumai (ego) to God, moving from manmukh (self-centred) to gurmukh (God-centred).
  • Nam is also a controlling force: the divine order (hukam) that sustains everything.
Mission 4

Names of God

  • Waheguru: "Wonderful Lord / Wonderful Enlightener", the name most used by Sikhs.
  • Satnam: "whose Name is Truth" (from the Mool Mantar).
  • Akal Purakh: "Timeless Being"; Nirankar: "the Formless"; Kartar: "Creator".
  • Gurbani also uses Hindu and Muslim names: Hari, Ram, Gobind, Allah, Rahim: God is beyond any one tradition.
  • Guru Arjan: the tongue recites names drawn from God's deeds; Satnam is God's primal Name (Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 1083; paraphrase).
  • Each name shows an attribute; none captures God fully: God is nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (known through qualities).
Mission 4Read the source

Japji, pauri 20

Guru Nanak (paraphrase): when hands, feet and body are dirty, water washes them; when clothes are soiled, soap cleans them; but when the mind is stained by wrongdoing, only the love of the Name can cleanse it (Japji, pauri 20, Guru Granth Sahib, Ang 4).

Q1 What can the Name do that water and soap cannot?

Show answer

Cleanse the mind of evil thoughts and wrongdoing.

Q2 Why does the verse say love of the Name, not just repetition?

Show answer

The cleansing comes from devotion and attention, not from sound alone.

  • Spec link (Japji): evil thoughts can only be cleansed through God's Name.
Mission 4

From repetition to experience

  • Gurbani describes stages of deepening:
  • Repetition with the tongue → with the mind → ajapa jaap, "unrecited repetition": the Name repeats itself, without effort.
  • Vismad: awe and wonder at God's greatness.
  • Sahaj: calm, natural poise; the mind at peace in God.
  • Anahad shabad: the "unstruck sound": a divine music heard within. Gurbani speaks of the panch shabad, the "five sounds" (pictured as five instruments), resounding in the attuned soul (e.g. Anand Sahib, Ang 917).
  • Your spec calls these the 5 Shabads (God as Word).
  • All by grace (nadar): effort prepares; God gives.
Mission 4

The 5 Khands

  • Japji (pauris 34–37) describes five khands (realms) of spiritual progress:
  • Dharam khand (duty) → Gian khand (knowledge) → Saram khand (spiritual effort) → Karam khand (grace) → Sach khand (truth: union with the Formless).
  • Nam simran is the practice that moves the soul through them.
  • Detail in an earlier lesson.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Naam Japo is evidence of a personal mystical union with God."

  • For: Gurbani describes ajapa jaap, sahaj, anahad shabad and Sach khand: union with the Formless.
  • For: many Sikhs report a felt sense of God's presence through simran, in line with descriptions of mystical experience.
  • Against: union depends on grace, not technique: repeating the Name does not guarantee union.
  • Against: inner experiences are private and cannot be checked; they may have psychological explanations.
  • Against: for many Sikhs, simran is ethical remembrance (living as if God is always present) more than mystical experience.
  • Judgement criteria: what counts as evidence; how Gurbani describes union; whether the effects on conduct (humility, sewa) are a better test than feelings.
Mission check

For Sikhs, the Name is not a spell but a relationship: remembered with love, it reshapes the self, and grace does the rest.

Mission 5

What assisted reproduction involves

  • Infertility: a couple cannot conceive after trying for a long time (often a year or more).
  • Assisted Reproductive Technology (ART): medical help to conceive.
  • IVF (in vitro fertilisation): eggs are fertilised by sperm in a laboratory; an embryo is placed in the womb (embryo transfer).
  • AIH: artificial insemination using the husband's sperm.
  • Donor gametes: sperm (AID, artificial insemination by donor) or eggs from someone outside the marriage.
  • Spare embryos: IVF often creates more embryos than are transferred; they may be frozen, donated, used in research or allowed to perish.
  • Surrogacy: another woman carries the pregnancy.
Mission 5

Sikh concepts that bear on infertility

  • Hukam: God's will or order, running through all of life. Many Sikhs accept childlessness as hukam; others see medicine as part of God's gifts.
  • Life as God's gift: Sikhs widely believe life begins at conception, when, by God's command, the soul enters the womb.
  • Grihasth: family life is the Sikh ideal (no celibate priesthood); children are a blessing.
  • Marriage (Anand Karaj): a spiritual union of two souls; faithfulness is part of the Rahit.
  • Sewa and compassion (daya): doctors who relieve suffering serve others.
  • Bibek budh: discerning intellect; the Gurus rejected superstitious remedies (charms, pilgrimages for sons).
  • Spellings on your papers: sewa, Rahit Maryada.
Mission 5Read the source

Conception and marriage in the Guru Granth Sahib

  • Sri Rag, Pehre (Guru Nanak, Adi Granth 74, paraphrase): in the first watch of the night, by God's command you entered the womb; you dwelt there in meditation on God.
  • Suhi ki Var (Guru Amar Das, Adi Granth 788, paraphrase): they are not husband and wife who merely sit together; they alone are husband and wife who have one light in two bodies.

Q1 According to Adi Granth 74, how does life begin?

Show answer

By God's command, when the soul enters the womb.

Q2 Why might Adi Granth 788 lead some Sikhs to question donor sperm or eggs?

Show answer

Marriage is the union of two in one light; a third person's gametes may seem to enter that union.

Mission 5

No direct ruling

  • The Gurus lived centuries before ART; the Guru Granth Sahib gives no direct ruling on IVF.
  • The Rahit Maryada says nothing about ART. It does forbid killing infant daughters, and Sikh bodies strongly condemn sex selection and female foeticide.
  • So Sikhs apply principles (hukam, gift of life, marriage, sewa, discernment) and consult conscience, family and doctors.

Common Sikh positions

  • IVF / AIH with the couple's own egg and sperm: widely accepted as medicine within marriage, like any other treatment.
  • Donor sperm or eggs: many Sikhs object, since a third person enters the marriage and the child's lineage is unclear; some compare it to adultery. Others accept it with both partners' consent, as a loving act.
  • Spare embryos: concern if life begins at conception; some couples limit the number of embryos created.
  • Surrogacy: views are mixed.
  • Sex selection: rejected by Sikh bodies as against the Gurus' teaching on women's equality.
  • Culture: pressure (often for sons) and stigma on wives in some Punjabi families; many Sikhs say these are cultural, not religious, and adoption within the family is a traditional alternative.
Mission 5

D. S. Chahal: reason and benefit

  • Devinder Singh Chahal: scientist and founder of the Institute for Understanding Sikhism (Canada); chapter "Sikh Perspectives on Bioethics" in Religious Perspectives in Bioethics (2004).
  • Presents Sikhism as one of the most scientific and logical religions.
  • Method (paraphrase): judge each act or technology by whether it is good for the individual and for humanity; use discriminating intellect (bibek budh); study and research before forming an ethical rule.
  • Applied to ART, this method weighs each treatment by its benefits and harms, case by case, rather than ruling it out as against God's will.
Mission 5

Dr Jodh Singh: Gurbani as the guide

  • Dr Jodh Singh: Sikh scholar of Sikh philosophy and translator of Sikh scripture, writing from within the tradition.
  • Approach (paraphrase): begins from Gurbani (the Gurus' word) and Sikh concepts: life as God's gift by hukam; the unity of husband and wife; living within the Gurus' ethical discipline.
  • So he stresses where ART meets those concepts: marriage, the status of the embryo, and the motive for treatment.
Mission 5

W. O. Cole: a view from outside

  • W. Owen Cole (1931–2013): British scholar of Sikhism, author with Piara Singh Sambhi of The Sikhs: Their Religious Beliefs and Practices (1978); writes as an outsider.
  • Approach (paraphrase): describes how Sikhs actually decide: Sikh ethics rests on principles rather than detailed rules; on new issues such as ART, decisions are shaped by family, community and Punjabi culture as well as by scripture.
  • Value of an outsider: notices the gap between teaching and cultural pressure (e.g. the wish for sons) that insiders may take for granted.
  • Limit of an outsider: may underplay the spiritual weight Sikhs give to hukam and Gurbani.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is IVF compatible with Sikh teaching?

  • Claim: IVF is compatible with Sikh teaching.
  • For: no ruling forbids it; Sikhs accept medicine generally; family life is the Sikh ideal; doctors' work is sewa; Chahal's benefit test supports it.
  • For: using the couple's own gametes keeps the marriage union intact.
  • Against: childlessness may be hukam to be accepted; spare embryos raise the question of life from conception; donor gametes bring a third party into marriage.
  • Against: IVF may feed cultural pressure for children (and sons) that the Gurus' teaching on equality challenges.
  • Judgement criteria: Does compatibility depend on the kind of IVF (own gametes, donor, spare embryos)? Is hukam accepted or co-operated with? Are the objections religious or cultural?
  • Issue in your spec, verbatim: "The extent to which IVF is compatible with Sikh teaching".
Mission check

?A Sikh couple are offered IVF using the wife's eggs and an anonymous donor's sperm. The husband says: "The Guru gave us medicine to use, so we should accept anything the doctor offers." His mother says: "A child must come from our own marriage." Which response best reflects mainstream Sikh discussion?

The question is rarely "IVF: yes or no?" It is which kind of treatment, for what motive, within what marriage.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A gurdwara is wherever the Guru Granth Sahib is installed; worship includes kirtan, Ardas, hukamnama and karah parshad.
  • Langar, from Guru Nanak, Mata Khivi and Guru Amar Das, is free, vegetarian and shared in a line: sewa and equality in action.
  • Any competent Sikh, man or woman, of any caste, may lead worship; practice does not always match the ideal.

Mission 2

  • Vaisakhi: Punjabi New Year and the founding of the Khalsa (Anandpur, 1699): identity, sacrifice, belonging, protecting the oppressed.
  • Bandi Chhor Divas: Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior with 52 rulers and return to Amritsar at Diwali: freedom, light over darkness.
  • The sangat keeps both festivals alive through akhand path, langar, nagar kirtan and amrit sanchar.

Mission 3

  • The Rahit Namas are varied 18th-century codes; the Rehat Maryada (Rahit Maryada; 1931–1950) sifts them into one standard: definition of a Sikh, personal and panthic life.

Mission 4

  • Nam is God's presence and nature; nam simran is loving remembrance; nam japna (Naam Japo) repeats the Name.
  • Repetition deepens to ajapa jaap, sahaj and the "unstruck sound" (panch shabad); Sikhs differ on methods and on whether simran is mystical union or ethical remembrance.

Mission 5

  • No direct Sikh ruling on ART exists; Sikhs apply hukam, the gift of life from conception, the unity of marriage, sewa and discernment.
  • Most Sikhs accept IVF with a couple's own gametes; donor gametes, spare embryos and sex selection raise the main objections.
  • Chahal judges by benefit and reason; Jodh Singh starts from Gurbani's concepts; Cole, an outsider, stresses how culture and community shape Sikh decisions.
