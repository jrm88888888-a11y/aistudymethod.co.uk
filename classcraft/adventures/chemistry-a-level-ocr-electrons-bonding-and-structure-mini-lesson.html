Mission 1: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Make a guess: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Mission 1

Shells split into sub-shells

  • Each shell (main energy level, n) splits into sub-shells: s, p, d, f.
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4: 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5, 7 orbitals, so hold 2, 6, 10, 14 electrons.
  • So the first four shells hold 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • Up to Z = 36 no f orbitals are filled; 4f only matters for the 32.
Mission 1

Orbitals: where an electron is

  • An orbital is a region around the nucleus where an electron is likely to be found. Not a path.
  • Each orbital holds up to two electrons, with opposite spins.
  • s orbital: a sphere centred on the nucleus.
  • p orbitals: three dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
Quick check
?What is the maximum number of electrons that a 3d sub-shell can hold, and why?
Mission 1

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau ("building up"): fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • Electrons-in-boxes: one box per orbital; arrows ↑↓ for spins. Oxygen 2p⁴: [↑↓][↑ ][↑ ].
Mission 1

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², shortened to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is fine: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.

Quick check

Mission 1

Configurations of ions

  • s- and p-block ions: add or remove electrons in the outer sub-shell.
  • Na⁺: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. S²⁻: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ (same as Ar).
  • d-block ions lose their 4s electrons first.
  • Fe²⁺: [Ar] 3d⁶. Fe³⁺: [Ar] 3d⁵. Cu²⁺ (from [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹): [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first too.
Quick check
?An iron atom (Z = 26) has the condensed electron configuration [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s². How is an Fe²⁺ ion formed from this atom?
Mission 1

Configurations and the periodic table

  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block (groups 1–2), p-block (13–18), d-block.
  • Across Periods 2 and 3: two s electrons are added, then six p electrons.
  • Down a group the outer configuration repeats: Mg 3s², Ca 4s². So their chemistry is similar (both form 2+ ions).
Mission check

?A ground-state phosphorus atom is [Ne] 3s² 3p³. Where are its three 3p electrons?

An electron isn't on a track. It is in an orbital: a region where it is likely to be. The configuration tells you which orbitals are occupied.

Mission 2: Salt is made of charged particles. So why doesn't a salt crystal conduct electricity?

Make a guess: Salt is made of charged particles. So why doesn't a salt crystal conduct electricity?

Mission 2

Which ions atoms form

  • Metal atoms lose electrons: positive ions, cations.
  • Non-metal atoms gain electrons: negative ions, anions.
  • Groups 1, 2 and 13 (3 in older numbering) lose all outer electrons: 1+, 2+, 3+.
  • Groups 15, 16 and 17 (5, 6, 7) gain enough to fill the outer shell: 3−, 2−, 1−.
  • Mg (2,8,2) loses two: Mg²⁺. N (2,5) gains three: N³⁻. Both now have neon's arrangement.
  • Charge = protons − electrons. The number of protons never changes.
Mission 2

Dot-and-cross diagrams of ions

  • Show one atom's electrons as dots, the other's as crosses.
  • Sodium oxide: two Na atoms each give one electron to one O atom.
  • Na⁺: its one outer electron has gone. Square brackets, charge + outside.
  • O²⁻: [2,8], six dots and two crosses, in square brackets, charge 2− outside.
  • Ratio of ions: 2 Na⁺ : 1 O²⁻, so Na2O.
Quick check
?An atom of element X has the electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁴. Which ion does X form in its ionic compounds?
Mission 2

Ionic bonds and formulae

  • Ionic bonding: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • It acts in all directions: each ion attracts every oppositely charged neighbour.
  • Formula: total charge zero. Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg3N2.
  • Name: cation first, then the anion ending -ide: magnesium nitride.
  • Roman numerals give a metal's charge: Fe³⁺ with O²⁻ is iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3.

Mission 2

The giant ionic lattice

  • Ionic compounds form a giant lattice: a regular 3-D array of alternating cations and anions.
  • The array maximises attractions between opposite charges and minimises repulsions between like ones.
  • There are no molecules. Al2O3 means 2 Al³⁺ for every 3 O²⁻.
  • In sodium chloride each Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻, and each Cl⁻ by 6 Na⁺.
Mission 2

Properties from the lattice

  • High melting point, very low volatility: every ion is held by many strong attractions.
  • Brittle: a blow shifts a layer, like charges meet, they repel, and the crystal splits.
  • Conducts only when molten or in solution: then the ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Solid: no conduction. The ions are fixed in the lattice, and there are no free electrons.
  • Often soluble in water: polar water molecules attract the ions out of the lattice.
Quick check
?Sodium chloride has a very low volatility: it does not evaporate at room temperature and melts only at 801 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 2

How strongly a lattice holds together

  • The attraction is stronger when ions have larger charges and smaller radii (centres closer).
  • Same charges, size decides: NaF melts at 993 °C, NaCl 801 °C, KCl 770 °C.
  • Double both charges, with ions about the size of NaF's: MgO (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) melts at about 2850 °C.
Quick check
?Ionic radii (pm): Na⁺ 102, K⁺ 138, F⁻ 133, Cl⁻ 181. Which shows the strength of the ionic bonding in NaF, NaCl and KCl in decreasing order?
Mission check

?In a kettle's heating element, the hot metal wire is packed in powdered magnesium oxide, MgO, inside a metal tube. MgO is ionic, yet it is used here as an electrical insulator. Why doesn't it conduct?

Salt is the same. Its Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions can't move in the crystal, so no charge flows. Melt it or dissolve it, the ions move, and the bulb lights.

Mission 3

What a covalent bond is

  • Covalent bond: the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the two bonded atoms.
  • The pair sits between the nuclei; both pull on it, holding the atoms together.
  • Too close, and the two nuclei repel: the bond length is where attraction and repulsion balance.
  • Lone pair: a pair of outer electrons not used in bonding.
  • Octet rule: atoms tend to end up with eight outer electrons. A tendency, not a law.
Quick check
?What holds the hydrogen atom and the chlorine atom together in a molecule of hydrogen chloride, HCl?
Mission 3

Drawing the electrons

  • A dot-and-cross diagram shows every outer electron: one atom's as dots, the other's as crosses.
  • 1. Count the outer electrons. Add one per negative charge; remove one per positive charge.
  • 2. Join the atoms by single bonds. 3. Complete the outer atoms' octets with lone pairs.
  • 4. Spare electrons go on the central atom. Too few? Share more pairs: multiple bonds.
  • CO2: 16 outer electrons. O=C=O, two lone pairs on each O.
  • N2: 10 outer electrons. N≡N, one lone pair on each N.
  • An ion goes in square brackets, charge outside: [O–H]⁻, three lone pairs on O.
  • Draw a covalent bond as a line once you know the pairs.
Quick check
?Which describes a correct dot-and-cross diagram for the cyanide ion, CN⁻?
Mission 3

Single, double, triple

  • Single, double and triple bonds share one, two and three pairs.
  • More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: the bond is shorter and stronger.

Mission 3

One atom supplies both electrons

  • Co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond: a shared pair with both electrons from one atom.
  • The donor has a lone pair. The acceptor has room for a pair.
  • H2O + H⁺ → H3O⁺: an O lone pair goes to H⁺. NH3 + H⁺ → NH4⁺ forms the same way.
  • Draw it as an arrow from donor to acceptor: O→H.
  • Once formed, it is identical to the others: all three O–H bonds in H3O⁺ match.
Quick check
?Ammonia, NH3, reacts with a hydrogen ion, H⁺, to form the ammonium ion, NH4⁺. Which statement about the bonds in NH4⁺ is correct?
Mission check

?Carbon monoxide, CO, has 10 outer electrons, the same as N2. Like N2, it has a triple bond and one lone pair on each atom. Which describes the three shared pairs?

Nitrogen is N≡N, three shared pairs like CO, but all ordinary covalent bonds. Breaking N≡N takes about 945 kJ mol⁻¹, against about 158 kJ mol⁻¹ for N–N. So very few substances react with N2 under ordinary conditions.

Mission 4

Electron pairs repel

  • Count the electron pairs round the central atom: bonding pairs and lone pairs.
  • A double or triple bond counts as one region, like one pair.
  • They repel, so they get as far apart as possible: VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion).
  • 2 regions: linear, 180°. 3: trigonal planar, 120°. 4: tetrahedral, 109.5°.
  • BeCl2 is linear, BCl3 trigonal planar, CH4 tetrahedral.

Mission 4

What sets the angles

  • Lone pairs sit closer to the central atom, so they repel more than bonding pairs.
  • Repulsion: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair.
  • Each lone pair squeezes the bond angles by about 2.5°.
  • CH4 109.5° → NH3 about 107° → H2O about 104.5°.
  • NH4⁺ has four bonding pairs, no lone pair: 109.5°, like CH4.
  • In alkanes, each C is tetrahedral (109.5°). Each C of a C=C is trigonal planar (120°).

Mission 4

Five and six pairs

  • From period 3 on, a central atom can hold more than eight outer electrons: an expanded octet.
  • Period 2 atoms (B, C, N, O, F) can't.
  • 5 pairs: trigonal bipyramidal. PCl5: three equatorial Cl at 120°, two axial Cl at 90° to them.
  • 6 pairs: octahedral. SF6: every F–S–F angle 90°.
Mission 4

Lone pairs with five or six pairs

  • Five pairs: lone pairs go equatorial, where they have fewer neighbours at 90°.
  • Six pairs: two lone pairs sit opposite each other, 180° apart.

Mission 5

Polar bonds

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond. Pauling scale.
  • Rises across a period (more protons, same shielding, smaller atom) and falls down a group (bigger atom, more shielding).
  • Different electronegativities: the pair is shared unequally. A polar bond: δ+ and δ−.
  • H–Cl: H 2.2, Cl 3.2. δ− on Cl. C–H (2.6, 2.2) is treated as non-polar.
  • Bigger difference, more polar. Very large difference: ionic. Bonding is a continuum.
Quick check
?Electronegativity values: H 2.2, C 2.6, O 3.4, F 4.0, P 2.2. Which bond is the most polar?
Mission 5

Polar molecules

  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles leave a permanent dipole overall.
  • Equal dipoles arranged symmetrically cancel: CO2, BF3 and CCl4 are non-polar.
  • In H2O, NH3 and CH2Cl2 they don't cancel: polar.
  • Check both: are the bonds polar, and does the shape cancel them?
Quick check
?Water, H2O, is a polar molecule with a permanent dipole (electronegativity: H 2.2, O 3.4). Which statement explains this?
Mission 5

Forces between molecules

  • London forces (induced dipole–dipole) act between all molecules.
  • Electrons move: a temporary dipole forms and induces a dipole in a neighbour. They attract.
  • More electrons and more surface contact: stronger London forces.
  • Permanent dipole–dipole: δ+ of one polar molecule attracts δ− of another (two HCl molecules).
  • London forces and permanent dipole–dipole forces together are van der Waals' forces.
  • All are much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds.
Mission 5

Hydrogen bonding

  • Hydrogen bond: an H atom bonded to N, O or F (δ+) attracts a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule.
  • Drawn as a dotted line, O–H⋯O roughly straight.
  • The O–H bond inside water is covalent; the hydrogen bond is between molecules.
  • Water forms up to four per molecule. Ice: an open lattice, so ice is less dense than water.
  • Anomalously high boiling points: H2O, NH3 and HF boil far above the trend of their groups.
Quick check
?Liquid methanol, CH3OH, contains hydrogen bonds. What is a hydrogen bond in methanol?
Mission 5

Ranking the forces

  • At similar molar mass: London < permanent dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  • O2, H2S and CH3OH: Mᵣ 32.0, 34.1 and 32.0.
  • O2, London only: boils at −183 °C.
  • H2S, polar, adds dipole–dipole: −60 °C.
  • CH3OH adds hydrogen bonds: 65 °C.
  • Very different masses? London forces can win: octane, C8H18, non-polar but far heavier, boils at 126 °C.
  • Alkanes: longer chain, higher bp. Branched isomers: less contact, lower bp.
  • Halogens: F2 → I2, more electrons, stronger London forces, higher bp.
Quick check
?Propane, CH3CH2CH3 (Mᵣ 44.11), ethanal, CH3CHO (Mᵣ 44.06), and ethanol, CH3CH2OH (Mᵣ 46.08), have comparable molar masses. Which lists them in order of increasing boiling point?
Mission 5

Simple molecular substances

  • Melting and boiling overcome forces between molecules. Covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Weak forces: low melting and boiling points. No ions or free electrons: no conduction.
  • Iodine: a lattice of I2 molecules held by London forces. It sublimes on gentle warming.
  • Ice: each H2O hydrogen bonded to four others in an open, tetrahedral lattice.
Mission 5

Solubility: like dissolves like

  • A solute dissolves if new solute–solvent attractions make up for those broken.
  • Ionic compounds in water: polar water molecules surround the ions (hydration).
  • Alcohols: the O–H hydrogen bonds to water. Short chains mix; longer chains dissolve less.
  • Polar molecules that can't hydrogen bond (haloalkanes) barely dissolve in water.
  • Non-polar solutes dissolve in non-polar solvents: I2 in hexane.
Quick check
?Ethanol, CH3CH2OH, mixes with water in all proportions, but hexane, C6H14, does not dissolve in water. Which statement explains this difference?
Mission 5

Separating by attractions: chromatography

  • You'll meet chromatography in detail later; here is why it works.
  • A spot of mixture sits on a stationary phase (paper, or a TLC plate). A solvent, the mobile phase, moves up through it.
  • Each component is attracted to both phases. More attraction to the stationary phase: it moves less.
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent, both from the baseline.
  • Spot 2.4 cm, solvent 8.0 cm: Rf = 0.30. Same conditions, same substance, same Rf.
Mission check

?Ammonia, NH3 (Mᵣ 17.0), boils at −33 °C. Phosphine, PH3 (Mᵣ 34.0), boils at −88 °C. Electronegativity: H 2.2, N 3.0, P 2.2. Why does the lighter molecule boil higher?

Water does the same, only more: each H2O can make up to four hydrogen bonds. Methane, with only weak London forces, boils at −162 °C; water, hydrogen bonded, at 100 °C.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sub-shells s, p, d, f hold 2, 6, 10, 14; orbitals hold two electrons of opposite spin.
  • Fill 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p; singly before pairing. Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s empties first in ions.

Mission 2

  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons; charges balance to zero in the formula.
  • A giant lattice of fixed ions: high melting point, brittle, conducts only when molten or dissolved.
  • Higher charges and smaller ions → stronger attraction.

Mission 3

  • A covalent bond is the attraction of two nuclei for a shared pair; in a co-ordinate bond one atom supplies both electrons.
  • More shared pairs: shorter, stronger bond. N≡N is why nitrogen is so unreactive.

Mission 4

  • Count every pair (or domain) round the central atom, lone pairs included; they spread as far apart as possible.
  • The shape names only the atoms. Lone pairs repel most and close up the bond angles.

Mission 5

  • Unequal sharing gives polar bonds; a molecule is polar only if its shape stops the dipoles cancelling.
  • Similar mass: London < permanent dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonds. Boiling overcomes these, not covalent bonds.
