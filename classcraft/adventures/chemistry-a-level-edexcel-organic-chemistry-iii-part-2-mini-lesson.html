Mission 1

Mission 1: Benzene needs a catalyst to react with bromine. Why does phenol react at once, with no catalyst at all?

Make a guess: Benzene needs a catalyst to react with bromine. Why does phenol react at once, with no catalyst at all?

Mission 1

Phenol: an OH on the ring

  • Phenol, C6H5OH: an –OH bonded directly to a benzene ring.
  • The O atom has lone pairs. One is in a p orbital, parallel to the ring's p orbitals.
  • It overlaps with the ring's π system: that lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring.
  • This changes the ring: it gains electron density.
Mission 1

Phenol and bromine water

  • Shake phenol solution with bromine water at room temperature. No catalyst.
  • The bromine is decolourised (orange → colourless). A white precipitate forms.
  • C6H5OH + 3Br2 → C6H2Br3OH + 3HBr
  • Product: 2,4,6-tribromophenol. Three H atoms are substituted at once.
  • Benzene, by contrast, needs pure bromine and a halogen carrier, and swaps just one H.
Mission 1

Why phenol reacts so readily

  • The O lone pair, from its p orbital, is donated into the π system.
  • The ring's π electron density increases, especially at C2, C4 and C6.
  • That denser ring polarises Br2 strongly enough on its own.
  • So the ring is far more open to attack by electrophiles than benzene's.
  • Back to the opening question: phenol's ring has extra electrons, supplied by the oxygen.
Quick check
?Phenol reacts with bromine water at room temperature, but benzene reacts with bromine only when a halogen carrier is added. Which explains the difference?
Quick check
?0.470 g of phenol reacts completely with excess bromine water. What is the maximum mass of 2,4,6-tribromophenol, C6H2Br3OH, that can form? (Ar: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0, Br 79.9)
Mission check

?Phenylamine, C6H5NH2, is shaken with bromine water at room temperature. The bromine is decolourised at once and a white precipitate forms. No catalyst is needed. Which explains this?

An O or N lone pair next to the ring feeds it electrons. A richer ring reacts with bromine with no catalyst.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Make a guess: How can one small molecule react with both acids and bases?

Mission 2

Amino acids

  • An α-amino acid (2-amino acid): RCH(NH2)COOH.
  • Both groups sit on one carbon, the α-carbon. R is the side chain.
  • Glycine: R = H, H2NCH2COOH. Alanine: R = CH3, H2NCH(CH3)COOH.
  • About 20 amino acids build proteins. They differ only in R.
Mission 2

Two groups, two jobs

  • –COOH is an acid group. –NH2 is a base group, like ammonia.
  • With alkali: H2NCH2COOH + NaOH → H2NCH2COO⁻Na⁺ + H2O.
  • With acid: H2NCH2COOH + HCl → ⁺H3NCH2COOH Cl⁻.
  • Each group reacts with its opposite: the molecule is amphoteric.
Quick check
?Glycine, H2NCH2COOH, is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid at room temperature. Which species of glycine is present?
Mission 2

The zwitterion

  • In the solid and in water, the –COOH passes H⁺ to the –NH2 of the same molecule.
  • Result: ⁺H3NCH(R)COO⁻, a zwitterion (dipolar ion): one ion with a + and a − end. Overall neutral.
  • Zwitterions attract each other strongly: an ionic lattice.
Mission 2

pH sets the form

  • In acid: the –COO⁻ end gains H⁺ → ⁺H3NCH(R)COOH (a cation).
  • In alkali: the –NH3⁺ end loses H⁺ → H2NCH(R)COO⁻ (an anion).
  • In between: mainly the zwitterion.
  • Draw all three for alanine: ⁺H3NCH(CH3)COOH · ⁺H3NCH(CH3)COO⁻ · H2NCH(CH3)COO⁻.
Mission 2

Twisting polarised light

  • Every α-amino acid except glycine has a chiral α-carbon: four different groups (H, R, NH2, COOH).
  • So it has two optical isomers (enantiomers).
  • Natural amino acids are a single enantiomer. Their solutions rotate the plane of plane-polarised light.
  • Glycine (R = H) is not chiral: no rotation.
  • A synthetic sample made as a 50 : 50 (racemic) mixture also gives no overall rotation.
Quick check
?Alanine is added to excess sodium hydroxide solution. Which species of alanine is present?
Mission 2

The peptide link

  • –COOH of one amino acid + –NH2 of another → peptide link, –CONH–, + H2O.
  • This is condensation, the same amide link as in polyamides.
  • Glycine + alanine gives two dipeptides: Gly-Ala, H2NCH2CONHCH(CH3)COOH, and Ala-Gly, H2NCH(CH3)CONHCH2COOH.
  • Many amino acids → a polypeptide. One or more polypeptides → a protein.
Mission 2

Hydrolysis and TLC

  • Hydrolysis: heat the peptide with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl for several hours. Water breaks each peptide link.
  • The amino acids come back (as cations, ⁺H3N–, in the acid).
  • Separate them by thin-layer chromatography (TLC).
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent front, both from the baseline.
  • Compare Rf with known amino acids run in the same solvent.
Quick check
?On a TLC plate the baseline is drawn 1.0 cm above the bottom edge. After running, the solvent front is 8.5 cm above the bottom edge and an amino acid spot is 3.7 cm above the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of the amino acid?
Mission check

?The dipeptide Gly-Ala, H2NCH2CONHCH(CH3)COOH, is dissolved in excess cold dilute hydrochloric acid. No hydrolysis happens. Which species is present?

A peptide still has an acid end and a base end, so it reacts with acids and bases just like a single amino acid.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Make a guess: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Mission 3

A strategy

  • 1. Molecular formula: % composition → empirical formula; Mᵣ from the M⁺ peak.
  • 2. IR: which bonds are present (O–H? C=O? both? neither?).
  • 3. MS fragments: mass lost → pieces such as CH3 (15), C2H5 (29), OH (17).
  • 4. ¹H NMR: signals = environments; areas = H ratio; splitting = neighbouring H; shift = what is nearby.
  • 5. ¹³C NMR: signals = carbon environments; shift = type of carbon (e.g. aldehyde/ketone C=O near 200; ester/acid C=O near 170).
  • 6. Chemical tests: a quick reaction can confirm a functional group.
  • Finally: propose a structure, then check it against every piece of data.
Mission 3

Worked example: compound W (step 1, the formula)

  • W contains C 54.5%, H 9.1%, O 36.4% by mass.
  • Divide by Ar: C 54.5 ÷ 12.0 = 4.54 · H 9.1 ÷ 1.0 = 9.1 · O 36.4 ÷ 16.0 = 2.28.
  • Divide by the smallest (2.28): C 2.0 : H 4.0 : O 1.0.
  • Empirical formula C2H4O (empirical formula mass 44.0).
  • MS: M⁺ at m/z 44, so the molecular formula is also C2H4O.
Mission 3

Same formula from combustion analysis

  • Burning 0.220 g of W gives 0.440 g CO2 and 0.180 g H2O.
  • Mass of C = 0.440 × 12.0 ÷ 44.0 = 0.120 g.
  • Mass of H = 0.180 × 2.0 ÷ 18.0 = 0.020 g.
  • Mass of O = 0.220 − 0.120 − 0.020 = 0.080 g (by difference).
  • Moles: C 0.0100 · H 0.0200 · O 0.00500 → ratio 2 : 4 : 1 → C2H4O.
Mission 3

Worked example: W (step 2, MS and IR)

  • MS: a peak at m/z 29: mass lost 44 − 29 = 15, a CH3.
  • IR: strong band at 1730 cm⁻¹: C=O.
  • IR: no broad band at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹: no O–H.
  • So far: a C=O and a CH3, with only two carbons.
  • Exact IR ranges: use the data sheet you get in your exam.
Mission 3

Worked example: W (step 3, NMR and confirming)

  • ¹H NMR: δ 9.8, 1H, quartet: an aldehyde H (–CHO), next to a CH3.
  • δ 2.2, 3H, doublet: CH3 next to C=O, next to one H.
  • ¹³C NMR: two signals, near δ 200 (C=O) and δ 31 (CH3).
  • W is ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Confirm: Mᵣ 44 ✓ · C=O, no O–H ✓ · CHO⁺ at 29 ✓ · 1H quartet, 3H doublet ✓.
  • ¹³C: two carbon environments ✓.
Quick check
?Compound Z, C3H8O, gives these data. IR: a strong, broad absorption centred near 3350 cm⁻¹. MS: a small M⁺ peak at m/z 60 and the base peak at m/z 45. Low-resolution ¹H NMR: three signals, areas 6 : 1 : 1. Which identification is consistent with all the data?
Quick check
?Back to the two bottles. Propanal, CH3CH2CHO, and propanone, CH3COCH3, are both C3H6O. Which technique tells them apart most directly, and how?
Mission 3

Propanal vs propanone: every technique

  • M⁺: both at m/z 58. No help.
  • IR: both have a strong C=O band, no O–H. No help.
  • MS fragments: propanone's base peak is m/z 43 (CH3CO⁺); propanal gives a strong peak at 29 (C2H5⁺ or CHO⁺). A clue, not a glance.
  • ¹H NMR: propanone 1 signal (singlet); propanal 3 signals, including the aldehyde H near δ 9–10. The glance answer.
  • ¹³C NMR: propanone 2 signals; propanal 3. Also decisive.
  • Tollens' reagent: silver mirror with propanal (an aldehyde); no change with propanone.
  • Rule: pick the technique that "sees" the difference: here, the environments.
Quick check
?Compound X, C4H8O2 (Mᵣ = 88), gives these data. IR: sharp absorptions at 2850–3000 cm⁻¹, a strong absorption at 1740 cm⁻¹ and no broad absorption (ranges: C–H 2850–3090 cm⁻¹; C=O 1700–1750 cm⁻¹; O–H in carboxylic acids 2500–3000 cm⁻¹, very broad). MS: M⁺ at m/z 88 and a peak at m/z 29 (CHO⁺ or C2H5⁺). ¹H NMR: δ 1.3 (triplet, 3H), δ 2.0 (singlet, 3H), δ 4.1 (quartet, 2H); H on a carbon bonded to the singly bonded O of an ester, –C(=O)–O–CH2–, appears at 3.7–4.8 ppm. Which identification is consistent with all the data?
Quick check
?Compound T, C4H8O, gives these data. IR: strong absorption at 1730 cm⁻¹; no broad absorption at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹. ¹³C NMR: three signals, one near δ 205. Low-resolution ¹H NMR: three signals, areas 6 : 1 : 1, one of them near δ 9.6. Which structure fits all the data?
Mission check

?Methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3, and ethyl methanoate, HCOOCH2CH3, are both C3H6O2. Which evidence tells them apart most directly?

Same formula, same M⁺, same IR bands: look for the technique that sees the different environments. Then check your structure against every piece of data.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Make a guess: Why do chemists plan the route to a new molecule by starting from the finished product?

Mission 4

Work backwards

  • Start from the target. Name its functional groups and count its carbons.
  • Ask: which one known reaction makes this group? That gives the compound one step back.
  • Repeat until you reach the starting material you were given.
  • Carbon count goes up? You need a C–C bond-forming step.
  • Then write the route forwards, with reagents and conditions for every step.
Mission 4

Your reaction map

  • Alkene → halogenoalkane (HBr) · → alcohol (steam, acid catalyst).
  • Halogenoalkane → alcohol (NaOH(aq)) · → amine (NH3) · → nitrile (KCN, +1 C) · → alkene (KOH in ethanol).
  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid (oxidation). Secondary alcohol → ketone. Acid + alcohol → ester.
  • Aldehyde or ketone → hydroxynitrile (cyanide, +1 C) · → alcohol (reduction).
  • Nitrile → amine (reduction).
  • Nitrile → carboxylic acid (hydrolysis).
  • Halogenoalkane → Grignard reagent → acid or alcohol (+ a whole alkyl group).
Mission 4

Worked example: three steps

  • Target: propylamine, CH3CH2CH2NH2 (3 C). Start: ethene (2 C).
  • Back 1: an amine from a nitrile by reduction → propanenitrile, CH3CH2CN.
  • Back 2: a nitrile from a halogenoalkane, adding 1 C → bromoethane, CH3CH2Br.
  • Back 3: bromoethane from ethene + HBr. Reached the start.
  • Forwards: (1) HBr, room temperature. (2) KCN in ethanol, reflux. (3) Reduce the nitrile.
  • Step 3 reagent: LiAlH4 in dry ether.
Quick check
?Which compound reacts with KCN in ethanol to give butanenitrile, CH3CH2CH2CN?
Mission 4

Molecules with several groups

  • In a multifunctional molecule, each group reacts as it normally would.
  • Example: HOCH2CH=CHCHO has a primary alcohol, an alkene and an aldehyde.
  • Bromine water: adds across the C=C. Decolourised.
  • Tollens' reagent: oxidises the aldehyde only. Silver mirror.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI), reflux: oxidises both –CH2OH and –CHO to –COOH.
  • For an unfamiliar target, still work backwards one known reaction at a time.
Mission 4

Two routes, one target

  • Target: 2-methylpropanoic acid, (CH3)2CHCOOH. Start: propene.
  • Back 1: the –COOH carbon is new, so a C–C step makes it. Precursor: 2-bromopropane.
  • Route 1: propene + HBr → 2-bromopropane → Mg in dry ether → (CH3)2CHMgBr → + CO2, then dilute acid.
  • Route 2: 2-bromopropane → KCN in ethanol → (CH3)2CHCN → reflux with dilute HCl.
  • Both are three steps. Grignard avoids KCN; the nitrile route avoids the need for dry conditions.
Quick check
?Which sequence converts bromoethane into 2-methylbutan-2-ol, (CH3)2C(OH)CH2CH3?
Mission check

?A chemist needs 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, CH3CH2CH(OH)CN, and has propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH. Which two-step plan works?

Start from the target, find the one reaction that makes it, then step back again. The route writes itself.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phenol's O lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring: the ring's electron density rises.
  • Bromine water, no catalyst: 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate) + 3HBr.

Mission 2

  • An amino acid has an acid group (–COOH) and a base group (–NH2), so it reacts with both acids and bases.
  • It exists as a zwitterion, ⁺H3NCH(R)COO⁻; acid gives the cation, alkali the anion.
  • Amino acids join by peptide links (condensation); hydrolysis reverses it.

Mission 3

  • Formula from % composition (and M⁺) → bonds from IR → pieces from MS → environments, ratios and neighbours from ¹H NMR → check against everything.
  • ¹³C NMR counts carbon environments; it is simpler to read than ¹H NMR.
  • Isomers: M⁺ and IR often match; NMR usually tells them apart.

Mission 4

  • Work backwards from the target, one known reaction per step; count carbons to spot a C–C step.
