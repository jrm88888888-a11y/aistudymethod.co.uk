Mission 1

Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Make a guess: Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Language · purpose, audience and formScreen 1

Purpose, audience, form

  • Purpose: what the text wants to do: inform, explain, argue, persuade, advise, entertain. Many texts have more than one.
  • Audience: who it's for: age, knowledge, interests, attitude, and how the text speaks to them.
  • Form: the type of text (article, advert, leaflet, letter, speech, blog, report, review) and its conventions.
  • Ask: who is this for, what does it want them to think, feel or do, and how does its form shape it?

Mission checkScreen 9

?Same photo, charity advert and news report. Why such different words?

Because they want different things. The news report's purpose is to inform a general reader, so it uses facts, figures, the past tense and an official quotation. The charity advert's purpose is to persuade readers to give, so it tells one dog's story, speaks to you directly and ends with a clear way to act.

Mission 2

Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Make a guess: Slim, skinny, scrawny: same body. Why three different verdicts?

Reading skills · word choiceScreen 1

Meaning and associations

  • Denotation: a word's dictionary meaning. Slim, skinny and scrawny all denote "thin".
  • Connotation: the associations and feelings a word carries. Slim sounds approving; skinny is more casual and can criticise; scrawny suggests weakness or poor health.
  • Writers choose words for their connotations, so a single word can steer your judgement.
Reading skills · word choiceScreen 2

Where the judgement hides

  • Verbs show how something acts: a waiter who slaps down a cup is ruder than one who puts it down.
  • Adjectives and adverbs describe; nouns name, and can judge too (a shack vs a cottage).
  • A semantic field is a group of words linked by one topic, such as decay or war.
  • Zoom in: name the word or its class, give its connotations, then say how it shapes the reader's view.

Mission 3

One word. Full stop. Why does a sentence that short land so hard?

Make a guess: One word. Full stop. Why does a sentence that short land so hard?

Reading skills · sentence formsScreen 1

Four sentence forms

  • Simple: one main clause. The tide turned.
  • Compound: two or more main clauses joined by and, but or or. The tide turned and the wind dropped.
  • Complex: a main clause plus a subordinate clause that can't stand alone. When the tide turned, we ran.
  • Minor: no main verb, but it works as a sentence. Not a sound.
  • Name the form only if you then say what it does.
Reading skills · sentence formsScreen 2

Function, length and contrast

  • Four functions: statement, question (including a rhetorical question), command (imperative) and exclamation.
  • Length works by contrast: a short sentence after long ones stands out.
  • Long sentences can pile up detail, build pace or overwhelm the reader.
  • Avoid the stock claim short sentences create tension. Say what this sentence does, here, and why.

?One word. Full stop. Why does a sentence that short land so hard?

It lands because of contrast and position. After longer sentences, a one-word minor sentence isolates a single idea, so the reader stops on it; strong answers explain what that word holds and why it matters at that point.

Mission 4

One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

Make a guess: One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

Language · viewpoint, attitude and toneScreen 1

Viewpoint, attitude, tone

  • Viewpoint: the writer's position or opinion on the subject.
  • Attitude: how the writer feels about it (admiring, uneasy, scornful…).
  • Tone: how the writing sounds as a result (warm, weary, mocking…).
  • Perspective: where the writer stands: their time, place and experience.
  • Writers rarely state an attitude. You infer it from evidence, then name it precisely: uneasy beats negative.

?One city, two writers, no feelings stated. How do you spot attitude?

You spot it in the writer's choices. Dickens notices furtive strangers and endless streets, so his tone is uneasy and weary; the modern writer notices friendly faces and glittering bridges, so hers is warm. Name the attitude precisely, then prove it with the words and patterns that create it.

Mission 5

Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Make a guess: Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Language · summary and synthesisScreen 1

Summary and synthesis

  • Summary: pick out the main points that answer the question, from one text.
  • Synthesis: bring two texts together on one focus, showing what they share or how they differ.
  • Both reward selection (only what fits the focus) and inference (what the details suggest).
  • Use your own words, with brief quotations as evidence. This is not language analysis.

Extract Language · summary and synthesisScreen 3

B — A hiker at a mountain lake, today

Language · summary and synthesisScreen 4

Select, infer, reword

Task: What do both writers find at the lake?

  • Select: underline only details about what they find: beauty, quiet, air.
  • Infer: what do the details suggest? a dream of beauty → Bird is overwhelmed.
  • Reword: quiet could feel physical → the stillness is almost a shock.
  • Link: Both…, whereas…, with one or two brief quotations.

Write itScreen 8
?Synthesise in a few lines.

Using both extracts, write 2–4 sentences on how the two writers' experiences of the lake differ. Use your own words and at most two short quotations.

Model answer

The writers differ most in how they share the lake. Bird seems to have it to herself: the only sign of other people is the distant ring of a woodcutter's axe, which adds to the peace. Hale's quiet is broken by a hundred tourists, and he has to walk away from the car park to feel the lake is yours again. Bird also delights in the facts, while Hale thinks they miss what matters.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two texts, a few lines. How do you sum them up without copying?

Select, infer, reword. Pick only the details that answer the focus, say what they suggest, and write them in your own words, linking the two texts with words like both and whereas. Brief quotations are evidence; long copied lines are not a summary.

Mission 6

Two writers, two centuries, one school day. Compare what, or how?

Make a guess: Two writers, two centuries, one school day. Compare what, or how?

Language · comparing two textsScreen 1

What comparing means

  • Compare ideas and perspectives (what each writer thinks and feels) and how they are conveyed (methods and their effects).
  • Look for similarities and differences: often the same topic, seen differently.
  • Weave the texts together point by point: Both…, but whereas…, similarly…, in contrast…
  • Avoid two separate essays with the other text bolted on at the end.

Mission 7

Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Make a guess: Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 1

An argument is a line, not a list

  • Make your viewpoint clear early, in one sentence.
  • Each paragraph adds a reason that builds on the last, not a random new point.
  • Fit tone and register to the reader and form: a letter to a council is not a speech to Year 9.
  • End by turning to the reader: what should they now think or do?
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Q8 or Q9: transactional writing (article, letter, review, speech, or part of a guide or leaflet), linked to the theme of the reading. 40 marks: AO5 24, AO6 16.
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 3

Concede, counter, return

  • Concede: state the strongest objection fairly, in its best form.
  • Counter (rebut): answer it with a reason or an example.
  • Return: bring the reader back to your view.
  • Why it works: a reader who disagrees feels heard and keeps reading; your view looks tested, not naive.
  • Pick the objection your reader really holds. Knocking down a silly version (a straw man) convinces nobody.
Pick the strongestScreen 4
?Task: write an article arguing that the school day should start at 10am. Which counter-argument is strongest?
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 5

Evidence that convinces

  • A specific, believable example beats an invented statistic: a made-up "99% of experts" makes readers doubt you.
  • Use the reader's world: their street, their school, their Saturday.
  • Techniques such as a rhetorical question or a pattern of three only work when they serve a point.
  • Save your strongest reason for last, or for just after the counter-argument.

Write itScreen 8
?Concede and counter.

Statement: 'Homework should be banned for students under 14.' You disagree. Write a counter-argument paragraph in three to five sentences: concede the strongest point for a ban, answer it, then return to your view.

Model answer

Those who want a ban have a point: a ten-year-old who spends every evening on worksheets is losing time to play, read and rest. Too much homework is a real problem. But the answer to too much is not none. Twenty minutes of reading, or practising the times tables that confused them in class, is the difference between a skill that fades by Friday and one that sticks. Cut the pile, by all means, but keep the one habit that teaches children to learn on their own.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Because you then answer it. Conceding the strongest objection, fairly, and then countering it shows your view survives the best case against it, so a doubtful reader feels heard and is more willing to be persuaded.

Mission 8

One opinion, a letter or a speech. What has to change?

Make a guess: One opinion, a letter or a speech. What has to change?

Writing · transactional formsScreen 1

Read the task for form, audience and purpose

  • Every transactional task names a form (letter, article, speech…), an audience and a purpose (to argue, persuade, advise, inform or explain).
  • Underline all three before you plan. Together they decide your register (how formal you are) and your conventions (the features readers expect from that form).
  • Your viewpoint can stay the same from form to form. What changes is how you open, address the reader, organise and close.
Writing · transactional formsScreen 2

Letters

PartFormal letter
Greeting‘Dear Ms Okafor,’ if you know the name; ‘Dear Sir or Madam,’ if you don't
OpeningWho you are and why you are writing
MiddleOne main point per paragraph: polite, firm, specific
CloseWhat you want to happen next, then ‘Yours sincerely,’ (named) or ‘Yours faithfully,’ (Sir or Madam)
  • A letter speaks to one reader, so ‘you’ means the person you addressed.
  • In an exam, spend your time on content: you don't need to invent full postal addresses unless the task asks.
Writing · transactional formsScreen 3

Articles and reviews

  • Article: a headline, often a standfirst (one sentence under it giving the angle), then paragraphs; subheadings can guide a longer piece.
  • An article speaks to many readers at once: write for that publication's readers. A school magazine allows a lighter tone than a national newspaper.
  • Review: name what you're reviewing, give a clear verdict, back it with specific details, and end with a recommendation.
Writing · transactional formsScreen 4

Speeches and talks

  • Open by greeting the audience in the room: ‘Good evening, everyone.’
  • Use direct address (you, we, us) and signposting so listeners can follow without a page to look back at: ‘My first reason…’, ‘Finally…’.
  • Choose devices that work aloud: rhetorical questions, repetition, lists of three, short punchy sentences.
  • End memorably and thank the audience. A speech never ends ‘Yours sincerely’.
Writing · transactional formsScreen 5

Leaflets and guides

  • Leaflet text: a title, subheadings, short sections, a few bullet points and a call to action. Write the words; don't draw pictures.
  • Guide: advice or steps in a sensible order, in the second person, with subheadings.

Mission checkScreen 9

?One opinion, a letter or a speech. What has to change?

The opinion can stay the same; the way you deliver it changes. A letter speaks to one reader, with a formal greeting, a clear request and the right sign-off. A speech greets a live audience, signposts its points and uses devices that work aloud. Read the form, audience and purpose first, then choose your conventions and register.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Purpose (what a text wants to do), audience (who it's for) and form (its type and conventions) shape every choice a writer makes.
  • Link each choice to its effect on the reader and to the purpose: avoid lists of features.
  • The same skills work in reverse when you write for a set audience, purpose and form.
  • Denotation is a word's dictionary meaning; connotation is the associations it carries.
  • Verbs, nouns, adjectives and adverbs can all carry a writer's judgement; groups of linked words form a semantic field.
  • Strong answers zoom in on a word, give its connotations and link them to the effect on the reader.
  • Simple, compound, complex and minor sentences; statements, questions, commands and exclamations.
  • Length works by contrast: a short sentence after long ones isolates an idea; a long list can pile up detail or overwhelm.
  • Name the form, then explain what this sentence does in this place; avoid stock claims like short sentences create tension.
  • Viewpoint is the writer's position; attitude is how they feel; tone is how the writing sounds. Perspective is where they stand.
  • Infer attitude from what the writer notices, word choice, imagery and patterns such as repetition, then name it precisely.
  • Comparison questions reward explaining how each writer conveys their perspective, not just what it is.
  • Summary selects the main points from one text; synthesis brings two texts together on one focus.
  • Select only what fits, infer what the details suggest, reword, then link with both, whereas, unlike.
  • Synthesis questions test content and inference, not language analysis.
  • Comparing means similarities and differences in ideas and perspectives and in methods and effects.
  • Use a grid: same topic, perspective, methods, effect; then turn each row into a comparative paragraph.
  • Weave the texts with both, whereas, similarly, in contrast; give brief evidence from each.
  • An argument is a line: a clear viewpoint, reasons that build, and an ending that turns to the reader.
  • Concede the strongest objection fairly, counter it with a reason or example, and return to your view; avoid straw men.
  • Specific, believable examples from the reader's world persuade better than invented statistics or piled-up techniques.
  • Underline the form, audience and purpose in every task: they decide your register and conventions.
  • Letters: the right greeting and sign-off (sincerely for a name, faithfully for Sir or Madam). Speeches: greet the room, signpost, use devices that work aloud. Articles: headline, standfirst, paragraphs for many readers.
  • Your board names its forms: learn the conventions of each, including guides, reviews and leaflet text.
