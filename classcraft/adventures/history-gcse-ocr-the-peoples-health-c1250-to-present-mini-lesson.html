The People's Health c1250–present

This mini-lesson works through the whole OCR thematic study on public health and living conditions in Britain — across four periods: Medieval (c1250–c1500), Early Modern (c1500–c1750), Industrial (c1750–c1900) and Modern (c1900–present). You'll see how towns, water, waste, housing and government shaped whether ordinary people were healthy.

Answer the questions as you go (some multiple choice, some ask for a date or key term) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Medieval · c1250–1500

Health in the medieval town

Most medieval people lived in the countryside, but towns grew crowded and dirty. With no clean water supply and no sewers, health depended on how well a town coped with its own waste.

  • Water came from wells, rivers and conduits — often the same water people threw waste into.
  • Butchers, tanners and dyers dumped blood, guts and chemicals into streets and rivers.
  • Human waste went into cesspits and middens; "gong farmers" emptied cesspits by hand at night.

Who was responsible? Town councils passed rules against dumping waste and fouling water, and appointed men to keep streets clean — but there was no national government action on health.

Medieval · beliefs and disease

Monasteries and the Black Death

Monasteries were often the healthiest places: monks planned clean water supplies, drains, latrines and infirmaries — because they were wealthy and valued cleanliness for religious reasons.

In 1348–49 the Black Death (plague) killed perhaps a third to a half of England's population. People did not understand its cause and tried a mix of responses:

  • Religious explanations — a punishment from God; some prayed or joined processions of flagellants.
  • Miasma — the idea that "bad air" caused disease, so people burned herbs and cleaned streets.
  • Practical steps — some towns tried to isolate the sick and clear rubbish.

Misconception alert: medieval people had no idea about germs (bacteria were not seen until the 1600s and not linked to disease until the 1800s). Their responses were based on religion and the "bad air" theory.

Early Modern · c1500–1750

Growing towns, same problems

Towns — above all London — grew much larger. Living conditions stayed poor, and outbreaks of plague kept returning.

  • The Great Plague of 1665 killed roughly a fifth of London's population. Towns tried quarantine — shutting up infected houses (marked with a red cross) and killing cats and dogs.
  • Pest houses (isolation houses) were built to keep plague victims away from others.
  • People still believed in miasma; there was still no understanding of germs.

Continuity: across the Medieval and Early Modern periods, the key ideas about disease (God, miasma) and the lack of national government action stayed broadly the same. Change was slow.

Early Modern · small changes

Some progress by 1750

There were a few improvements before the Industrial Revolution:

  • Improved water supply in London, e.g. the New River (opened 1613) brought fresh water into the city.
  • The Great Fire of London (1666) cleared slums; rebuilding used more brick and stone and wider streets.
  • Growing trade brought new foods and, later, tea and coffee (boiled water, safer to drink).

These were local and accidental improvements, not a planned public-health system. Governments still believed health was a personal or local matter, not their job — a policy later called "laissez-faire" ("leave alone").

Industrial · c1750–1900

The crisis of the industrial town

The Industrial Revolution pulled huge numbers of people into fast-growing cities like Manchester, Leeds and Birmingham. Housing could not keep up, and conditions became a public-health disaster.

  • Overcrowding: cheap "back-to-back" houses with no gardens, shared toilets and no clean water.
  • Cholera epidemics (1831, 1848, 1854, 1866) killed tens of thousands — spread through sewage-contaminated water.
Industrial · the turning point

Chadwick and Snow

Two men changed how people understood and tackled the crisis:

  • Edwin Chadwick's Report on the Sanitary Condition of the Labouring Population (1842) proved that dirt, bad drainage and poor housing caused disease and cost the country money. He argued for clean water and proper sewers.
  • In 1854, Dr John Snow traced a Soho cholera outbreak to the Broad Street water pump. By removing its handle, he showed cholera spread through contaminated water, not "bad air".

Why change was still slow: many MPs believed in laissez-faire and hated spending taxpayers' money or interfering in people's lives — so reform came only when disease became impossible to ignore.

Industrial · government acts

Government finally acts

Under pressure from cholera and reformers, the government moved from doing nothing to taking responsibility:

  • Public Health Act 1848 — set up a Board of Health and allowed (but did not force) towns to improve water and sewers. Weak, and abolished in 1858.
  • The "Great Stink" of 1858 — the stench of sewage in the Thames pushed Parliament to fund Joseph Bazalgette's huge London sewer system.
  • Public Health Act 1875 — a compulsory Act forcing councils to provide clean water, drainage and sewers, and appoint medical officers. A real turning point.

Compare the two Acts: 1848 was voluntary and weak; 1875 was compulsory and effective. The shift shows the state accepting real responsibility for public health.

Modern · c1900–1948

The Liberal reforms and beyond

By 1900 poverty studies by Booth (London) and Rowntree (York) shocked the nation by showing how many people were too poor to stay healthy. The Liberal government (1906–14) acted:

  • Free school meals (1906) and school medical inspections (1907) for children.
  • Old Age Pensions (1908) for the elderly.
  • National Insurance Act (1911) — gave some workers sick pay and medical care.

Why it mattered: for the first time, national government took responsibility for people's welfare, not just sewers — a big step towards the modern welfare state.

Modern · 1948

The birth of the NHS

The Second World War and the Beveridge Report (1942) — which attacked the "five giants" of Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness — created huge support for change. In 1948, health minister Aneurin (Nye) Bevan launched the National Health Service (NHS).

  • Healthcare became free at the point of use — funded by taxation, not by whether you could pay.
  • It covered doctors, hospitals, dentists and glasses for everyone.

The NHS was the greatest single change in the story: the state now guaranteed everyone the right to healthcare.

Modern · today's challenges

New problems, new campaigns

With clean water, sewers and the NHS, infectious diseases like cholera are no longer the main killers. Today's health problems are more about lifestyle:

  • Smoking, poor diet, obesity and lack of exercise now cause much ill health.
  • Government uses health campaigns, laws (e.g. the smoking ban in enclosed public places, 2007) and taxes to change behaviour.
  • Debate continues over how far the state should tell people how to live ("nanny state").

The big change over time: government moved from doing nothing (laissez-faire) to providing water and sewers, then to guaranteeing healthcare, and now to shaping lifestyles.

Exam skill

Factors driving change

OCR wants you to explain why health changed (or stayed the same) across the whole period. Look for these recurring factors:

  • Government — from laissez-faire to compulsory Acts and the NHS.
  • Science & ideas — miasma giving way to germ theory (Pasteur & Koch, later 1800s).
  • Individuals — Chadwick, Snow, Bazalgette, Bevan.
  • War, crisis & the economy — cholera, the Great Stink and WWII all forced action.

Turning points to argue for: the 1875 Act (government accepts responsibility) and the 1948 NHS (healthcare for all) are the two strongest "biggest change" answers.

Exam skill

Using sources on health

You will handle sources — reports, cartoons, photographs, maps — about living conditions. Two habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A reformer's report (like Chadwick's) is designed to shock and persuade; a cartoon may exaggerate to make a point.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know about the period.

Interpretations differ: historians disagree about what mattered most — was it reformers like Chadwick, government Acts, or scientific ideas like germ theory? Being aware that interpretations vary is exactly what the exam rewards.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Medieval (c1250–1500): dirty towns, cesspits, healthy monasteries; Black Death 1348–49 blamed on God & miasma.

Early Modern (c1500–1750): growing London; Great Plague 1665, quarantine & pest houses; still no germ theory; laissez-faire.

Industrial (c1750–1900): overcrowded cities, cholera; Chadwick's Report 1842; Snow & the Broad Street pump 1854; Public Health Acts 1848 (weak) & 1875 (compulsory); Bazalgette's sewers.

Modern (1900–now): Liberal reforms 1906–11; Beveridge 1942; NHS 1948 (free at point of use); lifestyle campaigns & the 2007 smoking ban.
