Texts in shared contexts: WW1 and its aftermath

Paper 2: Texts in shared contexts — Option 2A, WW1 and its aftermath. The organising principle is the word shared: you are not studying three texts, you are studying one historical moment through three texts, and the exam will test whether you can read a text you have never seen against that moment.

  • Paper 2 structure: Section A — one essay on a set text (25). Section B — contextual linking: a compulsory question on an unseen extract (25) and an essay linking two texts (25).
  • Your texts: one prose, one poetry and one drama text, of which one must be written post-2000.
  • Remember the either/or: Paper 2 is WW1 and its aftermath OR Modern times: literature from 1945 to the present day. You sit one option, never both.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Named texts appear only as swappable exemplars — the skills are what the exam rewards, and set-text lists change. Press Start when you are ready.

The field · 1

The literature of the war: not one voice but an argument

The single most damaging cliché in this option is that WW1 literature is protest. It is not. The writing of the war is a quarrel, and the quarrel is the subject.

  • The patriotic and consolatory mode — early-war poetry that offers sacrifice as meaningful, death as transfiguration, and the nation as a body worth dying for. Rupert Brooke is the standard reference. It is easy to sneer at; it is much harder, and much more interesting, to explain why it was believed, and by whom.
  • The protest and trench mode — the poetry of the soldier-witness: Wilfred Owen, Siegfried Sassoon, Isaac Rosenberg. Its authority is experiential — I was there, you were not — and its characteristic weapon is the gap between the language of home and the fact of the body.
  • The women's war — nurses, munitions workers, mothers, the bereaved, the women accused of sending men to die. Vera Brittain's memoir is the standard example. A war literature that is only male is a war literature that has taken the soldiers' word for who suffered.
  • The aftermath — shell shock, the unemployed veteran, the mourning industry, the memorial, the missing. The war does not end in 1918, and the option's title says so.
  • Retrospective and post-2000 writing — writers looking back across a century, with access to what the participants could not know. Their methods are different because their relationship to the evidence is different.

Exemplar, not syllabus: writers named here only illustrate the field. AQA revises its set-text lists — work from the texts your class studies. The paper examines your grasp of the shared context, not a fixed reading list.

The field · 2

Method: how the war is written

AO2 in this option means the specific techniques by which the war was made sayable — or shown to be unsayable.

  • The gap between rhetoric and body. The characteristic move of protest poetry: set an inherited abstraction (glory, honour, sacrifice, the old lie) against a physical particular (a mouth, a lung, a boot). The abstraction cannot survive the contact, and that is the argument.
  • Inherited forms under strain. The sonnet, the elegy, the pastoral, the hymn — all pressed into service and all deformed. A pastoral image of a field is not innocent when the field is in Flanders.
  • The elegy without consolation. Traditional elegy moves through grief to comfort. Much war writing performs the elegy and then withholds the comfort, leaving the reader in the grief the form had promised to resolve.
  • Documentary and the found detail — orders, letters, casualty lists, medical language. Realism as testimony; the flat register that refuses to be poetry.
  • The unspeakable. Ellipsis, euphemism, the sentence that stops. What a text cannot say is a method, and trauma writing is largely made of it.
  • Memory and retrospect. Memoir and post-2000 fiction are structured by the return: an event narrated by a survivor who cannot stop narrating it. Look for repetition, flashback, fractured chronology — form imitating the psychology of trauma.

AO3 · context

The shared context: what you actually need to know

AO3 is heavily weighted in Paper 2 — the whole option is built on shared context. But context means context that changes readings, not a history essay.

  • Volunteering and conscription — the mass volunteer army of 1914-15, and conscription from 1916. The shift changes what a poem about willingness means.
  • Propaganda and the home front — recruitment, the press, the white feather, the language of duty. Protest poetry is very often addressed not to generals but to civilians, and that is a crucial and often-missed point.
  • Censorship — soldiers' letters were censored, and so was public discussion. A text's silences may not be aesthetic choices.
  • Class and empire — officers and men; the Empire's troops from India, Africa, the Caribbean, Australia, Canada, who appear rarely in the canonical poetry and whose absence is itself analysable.
  • Gender — women's war work, the franchise, the changed labour market, and the return of the men.
  • The aftermath — shell shock and its medical treatment, the Unknown Warrior and the memorial culture, the missing, and the arguments over how the dead should be remembered.
  • Reception across a century — the war was re-narrated as futile chiefly by the 1960s; that reading is itself a historical event, and it shapes how you were taught the poems.

The deletion test: if you could cut the context sentence and the reading would stand unchanged, it earned nothing. Weld it — because conscription had replaced volunteering by the date of this poem, its irony about willingness lands as an accusation rather than a lament.

AO5 · interpretations

Reading the war through theory

  • Feminist — whose war is this? The trench lyric grants authority to male experience and, in doing so, quietly makes women either mourners or accusers. Recovering women's writing about the war is not an add-on; it changes what the war was.
  • Marxist — the officer poets were mostly public-school educated, and the canonical voice of the war is therefore a class voice. Who wrote, who was written about, and who is only ever counted?
  • Post-colonial — the imperial armies, the labour corps, the men who fought for empires that did not regard them as citizens. Their near-absence from the canonical literature is a fact about the canon, not about the war.
  • Trauma theory — trauma as an event that cannot be narrated at the time and returns compulsively afterwards. It explains the fractured chronology of memoir and of post-2000 retrospective fiction with unusual precision.
  • Eco-critical — the war as an assault on land: the shelled field, the drowned wood, the poisoned soil. The pastoral tradition is not merely a source of imagery here; it is one of the war's casualties.

The rule: a lens must produce a claim about the text. A Marxist critic would discuss class earns nothing. The poem's compassion for the men is real and is also administered from above — the speaker pities them in a register they would not have used about themselves earns a great deal.

Section B

The unseen extract: contextual linking

Section B opens with a compulsory question on an unseen extract. You will be asked how it relates to your understanding of the shared context of WW1 and its aftermath.

  1. Triage (three minutes). Who is speaking, and from where — the front, the hospital, the home front, the years after? Is this contemporary with the war, or retrospective? What is the genre: poem, memoir, letter, fiction, drama?
  2. Locate it in the argument. Consolatory, protesting, documentary, elegiac, retrospective? Naming the mode is your thesis.
  3. Analyse the methods — voice, form, structure, imagery, register, silence — and connect each to what the extract reveals about how the war was understood at that moment, by that speaker.
  4. Link outward to the shared context, which is what the question is for: this extract's version of the war sits alongside, or against, the versions you have studied.
  5. Infer context; never invent it. Do not name an author or a date you are unsure of.

An observation that almost always pays: ask who the extract is addressed to. Front-line writing addressed to civilians is doing something quite different from front-line writing addressed to other soldiers, or from writing composed decades later for readers who were not born.

Section B

The linking essay: two texts, one context

The second Section B question asks you to link two texts through the shared context. AO4 and AO3 are both in play, and they must work together.

  1. Open on the context, not the text: The war produced two incompatible demands on writing — that it should console, and that it should tell the truth — and these two texts pull in opposite directions on that fault line.
  2. Argue in idea-paragraphs (the body; the addressee; the treatment of the dead; the possibility of meaning), each handling both texts.
  3. Use genre difference as evidence. Your texts are prose, poetry and drama, and one is post-2000: a poem written in 1917 and a novel written in 2005 have completely different relationships to the evidence, and that difference is an argument, not an inconvenience.
  4. Chase the productive difference. Two texts that both find the war terrible are not an essay. Two texts that disagree about whether the terror can be represented are.

Time discipline: Paper 2 carries three 25-mark tasks. The unseen must not eat the linking essay.

The trap

Against the sentimental essay

This option produces more sentimental writing than any other on the specification. Guard against it.

  • Do not narrate the war. The examiner knows what happened at the Somme. Every sentence must be about a text.
  • Do not moralise. This shows the terrible futility of war is not a literary judgement, and it is the sentence examiners see most often.
  • Do not treat the poets as saints. They were writers making choices, and some of those choices are worth arguing with.
  • Do not assume the modern text is wiser. Retrospect brings knowledge and it also brings distance; a novel written ninety years later has access to the archive and no access to the fear.
  • Handle the dead with precision, not piety. The best writing about this literature is analytically cold and morally serious at the same time — and the second is only possible because of the first.

The sentence that raises the level: The poem does not tell us that the war was terrible; it demonstrates that the language available for saying so had already been used up by the people who started it.
