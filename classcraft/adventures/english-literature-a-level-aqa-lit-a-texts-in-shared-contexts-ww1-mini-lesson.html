Why 1918 sounds different

  • Experience: Owen has seen gas and trenches; Pope writes at home, in a sports-chant rhythm.
  • Purpose: Owen's Preface: "All the poet can do to-day is to warn."
  • Form: he bends tradition: an elegy-sonnet with guns for bells; half-rhymes.
  • But: Hynes (A War Imagined, 1990) notes that Brooke's sonnets ("If I should die, think only this of me:") stayed popular late in the war; the clean 1914/1918 split is partly a later myth. Fussell (1975) traces modern irony to the war.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why did the poetry of 1914 sound so different from that of 1918?

Much early verse, like Pope's, was written at home to recruit; Owen wrote from the front line to warn that same home front, so the voice, purpose and forms changed. But the contrast isn't absolute: Hynes argues the clean split is partly a myth built after the war, which is why "writers looking back" are part of this option.

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.
  • AQA: even a single-text answer can place the text among others of its period or genre.

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature A: AO4 counts in every question (about 12%). Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section B each set one essay linking two texts (25 marks); the NEA compares two texts.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A connection scores when it goes beyond the shared topic. Compare how each text treats the idea, using precise evidence and methods from both, and explain the differences, often through context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Recruiting verse (Pope, c.1915) speaks for the home front; Owen (1917–18) speaks from the front line to the home front, bending old forms (the elegy-sonnet) to warn.
  • Context that counts: volunteers (1914), conscription (1916), the Somme (1916), Craiglockhart (1917), Owen's death (4 Nov 1918) and posthumous Poems (1920).
  • The option includes "writers looking back": the 1914/1918 contrast was partly made later (Hynes), so ask when and why each text was written.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • AQA marks AO4 in every question, as well as the linking essays.

Key quotations to learn

  • Who's for the game, the biggest that's played, (Pope, "Who's for the Game?") War as sport: recruiting rhythm for the home front.
  • If I should die, think only this of me: (Brooke, "The Soldier" (1914 and Other Poems, 1915)) The patriotic 1914 voice Owen is often set against.
  • All the poet can do to-day is to warn. (Owen, Preface) Poetry as warning, not consolation.
  • What passing-bells for these who die as cattle? (Owen, "Anthem for Doomed Youth") The old ritual of mourning can't cope.
  • My friend, you would not tell with such high zest (Owen, "Dulce et Decorum Est") Direct address to the home front.
  • The old Lie: Dulce et decorum est (Owen, "Dulce et Decorum Est") The classical slogan named as propaganda.
