Mission 1

One God or many?

  • Theism: belief in God or gods.
  • Monotheism: one God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism).
  • Polytheism: many gods, each with their own powers (e.g. ancient Greek and Norse religion).
Mission 1

The "omni" words

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing: past, present, future, and every thought.
  • Omnipresent: present everywhere at once. "If I ascend up into heaven, you are there." (Psalm 139:8)
  • Omnibenevolent: all-loving and all-good.
  • These are ways of saying God has no limits.
  • Believers find these qualities through revelation: scripture, prophets and experience.
Mission 1

Beyond the world, and within it

  • Transcendent: beyond and outside the universe, space and time; greater than anything humans can imagine. "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8).
  • Immanent: present and active within the world and in people's lives.
  • A transcendent God inspires awe and worship; an immanent God is close enough to pray to.
  • Most Jews, Christians and Muslims hold both together: God is not part of the world, yet is never absent from it.
  • The Qur'an: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall), yet "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall).
Mission 1

Personal or impersonal? Knowable or not?

  • Personal: God can be known as someone: who loves, speaks, listens and responds, e.g. "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9).
  • Impersonal: the divine is more like a force or ultimate reality than a person, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism (a later mission).
  • Many believers hold that God is personal but is more than a person: not a human being.

Mission 1

God in Christianity

  • One God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit (the Trinity).
  • "God is love." (1 John 4:8)
  • Luke 15:11–32, the Lost Son: the father "saw him and was moved with compassion, and ran" (Luke 15:20): God is forgiving and longs for people to return.
  • God is creator, judge, all-powerful and all-good.
  • Suffering: Psalm 22:1: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" Jesus cries these words on the cross (Mark 15:34). Christians believe God shares human suffering in Jesus and does not abandon those who suffer.
Mission 1

Allah in Islam

  • Tawhid: the oneness of Allah. "Say: He is Allah, the One! … He begetteth not nor was begotten." (Qur'an 112:1, 3, Pickthall)
  • So Muslims reject the Trinity and any partner with God (shirk).
  • The 99 names: "Allah's are the fairest names. Invoke Him by them." (Qur'an 7:180, Pickthall). E.g. the Creator, the Shaper, the Fashioner (Qur'an 59:24); the Merciful; the Just.
  • Every surah but one opens: "In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Pickthall).
  • Transcendent: nothing is like Allah; never pictured. Immanent: Allah knows and is close to every person.
  • Suffering is a test: "surely We shall try you with something of fear and hunger … but give glad tidings to the steadfast" (Qur'an 2:155, Pickthall).
Mission 1

God in Judaism

  • One God: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
  • The Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9) goes on: love God with all your heart, soul and might; teach these words to your children; bind them on your hand and write them on your doorposts (tefillin and mezuzah).
  • Creator: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • Lawgiver and judge: gave the Torah; judges with justice and mercy.
  • God has no body and cannot be pictured. Many Jews do not say God's name, and write "G-d".
  • Suffering: Job questions God and is answered by God's greatness, not an explanation. Jews keep faith, protest and question at once.
Mission 1

A good God and a good creation

  • "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • Catholic teaching: God is perfect goodness, and creates out of love, not need.
  • So everything God makes is good and reflects God's goodness, as a work shows its maker.
  • "The LORD is good to all. His tender mercies are over all his works." (Psalm 145:9)
  • The challenge: if God is all-good and all-powerful, why is there suffering? (Next missions.)
Mission 1Diversity

Contrasting beliefs about God

  • Trinity (Christian) vs tawhid (Muslim) vs Jewish oneness with no incarnation: all monotheist, but they disagree about how God is one.
  • Personal God (Abrahamic faiths; Sikh Waheguru; many Hindus' devotion to Vishnu or Shiva) vs impersonal ultimate reality (nirguna Brahman; Buddhism has no creator God).
  • Deism: God made the world but does not intervene in it.

Mission 1Evaluate

"God cannot be both transcendent and personal."

  • Reasons for: a being beyond space and time cannot listen and respond like a person; personal language makes God too human; Buddhists and many Hindus think ultimate reality is impersonal.
  • Reasons against: scripture presents both (Isaiah 55:8–9 and Matthew 6:9; Qur'an 42:11 and 50:16); an all-powerful God can be close to all without being limited; Christians: God became personal in Jesus.
  • Another view: "personal" is an analogy: true but not complete.
  • Judge it by: Can human words describe God at all? Is a God who cannot be related to worth worshipping? Do believers' experiences count as evidence?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two different ideas

Question: Explain two different ideas about the nature of God.

  • Idea 1: God is transcendent: beyond the universe. Development: so Muslims never picture Allah: "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11).
  • Idea 2: God is immanent: present in the world. Development: so Muslims pray to Allah directly, anywhere, believing Allah is "nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16).

Mission 2

Two kinds of evil

  • Evil: whatever is bad, harmful or wrong. Suffering: the pain, physical or mental, that it causes.
  • Moral evil: caused by human choices: murder, cruelty, war, theft, bullying.
  • Natural evil (natural suffering): caused by nature, not by humans: earthquakes, floods, disease, famine from drought.
  • Some cases are mixed: a quake kills more people where buildings were badly made.
  • Moral evil raises the question: why did God make people free to do this? Natural evil: why did God make a world that works like this?

Mission 2

The inconsistent triad

  • Theists (Christians, Jews, Muslims, Sikhs and many Hindus) believe God is omnipotent (all-powerful) and omnibenevolent (all-loving).
  • Most also say God is omniscient (all-knowing): God knows about every evil.
  • But evil exists.
  • The problem: these three statements seem not to fit together. If God knew, could stop it and wanted to, why does it happen?
  • This is the inconsistent triad. Many versions add omniscience, giving four statements; the name inconsistent triad is kept. Philosophers call it the logical problem of evil.
  • The evidential problem: even if some evil is needed, why so much, and why such pointless suffering?

Epicurus' old questions

David Hume (1711–1776), Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part X, has a character repeat questions credited to the Greek philosopher Epicurus (341–270 BCE):

> "Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"

Q1 Which quality of God does the first question challenge?

Show answer

Omnipotence: "not able" means "impotent", powerless.

Q2 Which does the second challenge?

Show answer

Omnibenevolence: "not willing" means "malevolent", ill-willed.

Mission 2

Innocent suffering and providence

  • Providence: God's loving care for the world, guiding it towards good.
  • Innocent suffering is the hardest case: babies with painful illnesses, children in war, animals in fires. They have done nothing to deserve it.
  • In Fyodor Dostoevsky's novel The Brothers Karamazov (1880), Ivan refuses any future harmony bought with the tears of a tortured child (paraphrase).
  • Even believers cry out: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1, WEBBE). Christians recall that Jesus prayed these words on the cross (Mark 15:34).
Mission 2

Reject, question or examine

  • Some reject belief: atheists argue that the triad shows an all-powerful, all-loving God does not exist.
  • Humanists: suffering has natural causes (evolution, plate movement, disease) and human ones; no God is needed to explain it; people should reduce it themselves.
  • Some question: personal tragedy can shake faith; doubt and lament are found in scripture (Psalm 22, Job).
  • Some examine: believers think harder about what God's power and love mean, and find answers (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Diversity

Where evil comes from: Abrahamic faiths

  • Christian: humans misused free will in the Fall (Genesis 3); "sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin" (Romans 5:12, WEBBE). Original Sin: a damaged nature passed to all.
  • Jewish: humans have a yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and a yetzer ha-ra (inclination to evil) and free choice: "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917).
  • Muslim: Iblis refused to bow to Adam "through pride" (Surah 2:34, Pickthall) and now tempts humans; hardship is a test: "We shall try you with something of fear and hunger" (Surah 2:155, Pickthall).
Mission 2Diversity

Where suffering comes from: Dharmic faiths

  • Hindu: suffering follows karma: as a person acts in this life, so they reap in the next (Bhagavata Purana 6.1.45, paraphrase). Its root is ignorance (avidya) and maya (illusion that hides Brahman).
  • Buddhist: suffering (dukkha) is a mark of all existence; its cause is craving (tanha) and the Three Poisons (greed, hatred, ignorance). Mara stands for temptation and death.
  • "By oneself the evil is done, by oneself one suffers" (Dhammapada 165, Müller).
  • Sikh: life is under God's hukam (will), but humans turn from God through haumai (ego). The manmukh (self-centred person) is ruled by the five evils: lust (kam), anger (krodh), greed (lobh), attachment (moh), pride (ahankar).

Mission 2Evaluate

"Evil and suffering prove that God does not exist."

  • Reasons for: the triad seems logically inconsistent; the amount of suffering seems pointless (evidential problem); innocent suffering seems unfair; natural causes explain suffering without God.
  • Reasons against: God may have good reasons we cannot see (Isaiah 45:9); free will needs the possibility of evil; suffering may have a purpose (see Mission 3); many who suffer most keep their faith.
  • Another view: the problem may show that God's power or love must be understood differently, not that God does not exist.
  • Non-theistic view: for most Buddhists there is no creator to blame; the question becomes how to end suffering.
  • Judge it by: Is "prove" too strong a word? Does free will explain natural evil? Can a finite mind judge God's reasons?

Mission 3

Three kinds of answer

  • A theodicy ("justifying God") tries to show why a good, all-powerful God allows evil. The word was coined by Gottfried Leibniz (1710).
  • Philosophical answers: free will, soul-making.
  • Scriptural answers: Job, the Psalms, life as a test, the suffering of Christ.
  • Practical answers: prayer, charity, care for those who suffer.
  • Most believers use all three. Many say: we may not fully explain suffering, but we can trust God and act.
Mission 3

Augustine: free will and privation

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions, City of God, Enchiridion.
  • Augustine teaches that God made a good world. Evil is not a thing God made: "evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name 'evil.'" (Augustine, City of God XI.9, Dods)
  • Angels and humans misused free will: the fall of the angels and of Adam and Eve.
  • Original Sin: all humans inherit a damaged nature; natural evil follows the disorder sin brought into creation.
  • So evil comes from creatures, not from God. God is just to allow its consequences, and merciful to save through Christ.
Mission 3

Irenaeus and Hick: soul-making

  • St Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies: humans are made in God's image, but must grow into his likeness (Genesis 1:26).
  • Humans are made immature on purpose, so they can grow freely into goodness.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966): the world is a "vale of soul-making" (a phrase from the poet Keats).
  • Courage, compassion and forgiveness can only grow where there is real danger and pain.
  • Epistemic distance: God keeps a distance, so that people are free to believe or not.
  • Hick: in the end all people will reach the likeness of God (universal salvation), so all suffering will prove worthwhile.

Mission 3

Do these answers work?

  • Against Augustine: science shows death and disease long before humans existed, so natural evil cannot all come from the Fall. How could perfect beings choose evil? Why punish all humans for Adam's sin?
  • Against soul-making: the amount of suffering is too great: the Holocaust destroyed people, it did not make souls. Babies and animals suffer without growing. Is it loving to use pain as a teacher?
  • For both: free will is real only if evil is possible; virtues need challenges; God's goodness may reach beyond this life (Romans 8:18).
  • Atheists and Humanists: the answers explain away real pain; a world with less suffering would still allow free will.

Job's losses

Job, a good man, loses his children, wealth and health:

"Naked I came out of my mother's womb, and naked will I return there. The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." (Job 1:21, WEBBE)

  • His friends insist he must have sinned. God rejects their view. God's answer is a question: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (Job 38:4, WEBBE)

Mission 3

Islam: life as a test

  • "Who hath created life and death that He may try you which of you is best in conduct" (Surah 67:2, Pickthall).
  • Surah 2:151–177: Allah tries believers with "fear and hunger, and loss of wealth and lives" (Surah 2:155, Pickthall). The steadfast say: "Lo! we are Allah's and lo! unto Him we are returning." (Surah 2:156, Pickthall)
  • Righteousness is faith shown in giving to "orphans and the needy", in prayer, zakah, and patience in hardship (Surah 2:177, paraphrase).
  • Al-Qadr: Allah knows and decrees all things, yet humans are responsible for their choices.
  • Sabr (patient endurance) and du'a (personal prayer) are the believer's response; many Muslims believe hardship patiently borne wipes away sins (hadith).
  • Divergent: Shi'a Muslims see in Imam Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala (680) the meaning of suffering for justice; some Muslims stress Allah's decree, others human free will.
Mission 3

Judaism: Job, Psalms and the Shoah

  • Life as a test: "God did prove Abraham" (Genesis 22:1, JPS 1917).
  • The Shoah (Holocaust, 1933–1945; six million Jews murdered, most between 1941 and 1945) brought very different Jewish responses:
  • Eliezer Berkovits (1908–1992), Faith after the Holocaust (1973): God hides his face so humans can be free; the evil was human (paraphrase).
  • Emil Fackenheim (1916–2003): Jews must not give Hitler "posthumous victories" by abandoning Judaism (paraphrase).
  • Richard Rubenstein (1924–2021), After Auschwitz (1966): belief in a God who controls history is no longer possible (paraphrase).
  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016): faith that argues with God, in the tradition of Job and Abraham.
Mission 3

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh responses

  • Hindu: suffering follows karma, so it is not unjust. Brahma Sutras 2.1.34–36 (paraphrase): God is not partial or cruel, because what beings receive depends on their own past actions, and the round of rebirth has no first beginning.
  • Hindus respond with detachment, devotion and seva; liberation (moksha) ends suffering.
  • Buddhist: there is no creator to justify. The task is to end suffering: the Noble Eightfold Path, and karuna (compassion) for all who suffer.
  • Sikh: all happens within God's hukam (will). Sikhs accept it with contentment and remember God's name; Guru Nanak taught that suffering can be a medicine and comfort a disease (paraphrase).
  • Sikhs respond with seva (service), for example the langar, open to all.
Mission 3

Christians: the suffering of Christ

  • Christians believe God does not stay distant from suffering: in Jesus, God shares it.
  • "He was despised and rejected by men, a man of suffering and acquainted with disease." (Isaiah 53:3, WEBBE)
  • "By his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5, WEBBE) Christians read this as pointing to Christ's death.
Mission 3

Practical responses

  • Prayer: comfort, strength and solidarity: God "comforts us in all our affliction, that we may be able to comfort those who are in any affliction" (2 Corinthians 1:4, WEBBE).
  • Charity: Christians through CAFOD or Christian Aid; Muslims through zakah and Islamic Relief; Jews through tzedakah; Sikhs through Khalsa Aid.
  • Hope: many believe suffering will end: God "will wipe away every tear from their eyes" (Revelation 21:4, WEBBE).
  • Humanists: use reason, science and compassion to reduce suffering now; this life is the only one, so it matters.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Religious responses solve the problem of evil."

  • Reasons for: free will explains moral evil; soul-making gives suffering a purpose; Job shows humans cannot judge God.
  • Also for: Christ's suffering shows God shares pain.
  • Also for: life as a test gives meaning.
  • Reasons against: natural evil existed before humans; the amount and pointlessness of suffering; innocent suffering of children and animals.
  • Also against: the Shoah led some Jewish thinkers (Rubenstein) to reject belief in a God who controls history.
  • Another view: responses may not solve the problem as a puzzle but help believers live with it; practical responses matter most.
  • Divergent views of success: many Catholics and Muslims say the answers, taken together, are enough for faith; others stress mystery.
  • Judge it by: Does it explain natural as well as moral evil? Does it fit the amount of suffering? Does it help people who suffer?

Mission 4

A chain of causes

  • Everything we see has a cause: you were caused by your parents, a tree by a seed, rain by clouds.
  • Each cause was itself caused by something earlier.
  • The First Cause argument asks: what started the chain?
  • Its answer: there must be a first cause that was not itself caused. Believers say this is God.
  • It is also called the cosmological argument (from kosmos, Greek for "ordered world"): it argues from the existence of the universe to God.
Mission 4

Aquinas' First Three Ways

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Catholic priest and philosopher, Summa Theologiae (1265–1274), Part I, Question 2, Article 3: "five ways" to show God exists.
  • 1. Motion (change): things change, and each change is caused by something else. There must be a first mover, unmoved by anything.
  • 2. Cause: nothing causes itself, and the chain of causes cannot go back forever. There must be a first cause.
  • 3. Contingency: everything in nature could have not existed (it is contingent). If everything were like that, at some time there would have been nothing, and nothing would exist now. So there must be a necessary being: one that cannot not exist.
  • Aquinas ends each Way: this is what everyone calls God.
  • The First Way and Second Way are first-cause arguments; the Third is the cosmological argument from the existence of the world.
Mission 4Read the source

The Second Way

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, Q. 2, Art. 3 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "There is no case known (neither is it, indeed, possible) in which a thing is found to be the efficient cause of itself; for so it would be prior to itself, which is impossible."

> "Therefore it is necessary to admit a first efficient cause, to which everyone gives the name of God."

  • An efficient cause is whatever makes something happen or exist.

Q1 Why can nothing cause itself, according to Aquinas?

Show answer

It would have to exist before itself, which is impossible.

Q2 What does Aquinas say "everyone" calls the first cause?

Show answer

God.

Mission 4

Kalam: al-Ghazali

  • Kalam (Arabic: "speech", then "theology by reasoned argument").
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Muslim theologian, Kitab al-Iqtisad fi'l-I'tiqad (The Moderation in Belief), c. 1095.
  • His argument (paraphrase):
  • 1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause.
  • 2. The universe began to exist: it cannot be infinitely old.
  • 3. So the universe has a cause, and that cause is God, who did not begin.
  • Note the wording: not "everything has a cause", but "everything that begins".

Mission 4Diversity

Buddhist and Hindu views of a first cause

  • Buddhists: the Buddha kept a Noble Silence on questions such as whether the world had a beginning. In the Poisoned Arrow parable (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta), a wounded man who demands to know who shot the arrow before he lets it be removed will die first: such questions do not help end suffering.
  • Buddhists teach dependent origination: everything arises from causes and conditions, with no first beginning and no creator God needed.
  • Hindus: many see the universe as caused by Brahman. The Brahmanda ("cosmic egg") pictures the universe emerging from one source; Aum is the sacred sound of that source.
  • The universe is cyclical: it is created, sustained and dissolved again and again (the Trimurti), so a "first" moment may not exist.
  • The Rig Veda's Hymn of Creation (10.129) asks where creation came from and wonders whether even the highest being knows (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Objections from atheists and Humanists

  • What caused God? If everything needs a cause, so does God. If God needs none, perhaps the universe needs none either.
  • The universe might just be there. Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), BBC radio debate with Frederick Copleston (1948): the universe is a brute fact that needs no explanation (paraphrase).
  • Parts and whole. Russell: every human has a mother, but the human race does not have a mother (paraphrase). A cause for each part does not prove a cause for the whole. A character in David Hume's (1711–1776) Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part IX, makes the same point.
  • The chain might go back forever. Why can't there be an infinite chain of causes?
  • Science: the Big Bang (about 13.8 billion years ago) may have a natural explanation. Humanists say "we don't yet know" is more honest than "God did it".
  • Which God? Even a first cause would not show it is loving, personal, or the God of any one religion.

Mission 4

Religious replies

  • God is not caused. The argument never says "everything has a cause". Aquinas: contingent things need a cause, but God is necessary. Kalam: what begins needs a cause, but God is eternal.
  • An infinite chain explains nothing. A train of carriages stretching back forever still needs an engine to move at all.
  • The Big Bang fits a beginning. It was first proposed (1927, 1931) by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Catholic priest and physicist. Many believers say science explains how; the argument asks why there is anything at all.
  • Which God? Believers agree the argument is limited. It shows a Creator; revelation and experience fill in the rest.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The First Cause argument proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: everything we observe has a cause; nothing causes itself (Aquinas); the universe had a beginning (Big Bang), as kalam claims; a necessary being explains why anything exists.
  • Reasons against: "what caused God?"; the universe may be a brute fact (Russell); parts-and-whole (Hume); an infinite chain may be possible; the cause need not be the God of religion.
  • Another view: many believers say it shows God is reasonable to believe in, not proved; faith completes it.
  • Buddhist view: the question may not need an answer at all (Noble Silence).
  • Judge it by: Is "uncaused God" special pleading? Does "proof" mean certainty or good reason? Does the Big Bang help or hurt?

Mission 5

Order that looks planned

  • The design argument says the world shows signs of being planned, so it must have a designer: God.
  • It is also called the teleological argument (from Greek telos, "end" or "purpose").
  • It points to two things:
  • Order: nature follows regular laws (planets orbit, seasons repeat).
  • Purpose: parts work together for a goal (the eye for seeing, wings for flying).
  • Believers say order and purpose like this do not happen by chance.
Mission 5

Paley's watch

  • William Paley (1743–1805), Church of England priest, Natural Theology (1802).
  • Crossing a heath, you kick a stone: you might think it had always been there.
  • You find a watch: its springs and wheels are shaped and fitted together to tell the time. You conclude it had a maker (paraphrase).
  • You would think this even if you had never seen a watch made, or if it sometimes went wrong.
  • Living things, such as the eye, are far more complex than any watch, with parts fitted for a purpose.
  • So nature, Paley argues, needs an even greater designer: God.
Mission 5Read the source

Aquinas' Fifth Way

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, Q. 2, Art. 3 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "Whatever lacks intelligence cannot move towards an end, unless it be directed by some being endowed with knowledge and intelligence; as the arrow is shot to its mark by the archer."

  • Aquinas' design argument is from regularity: things without minds (planets, plants) act in regular ways towards an end.

Q1 What does the archer stand for in Aquinas' picture?

Show answer

God, the intelligent being who directs nature.

Q2 Why can't the arrow reach its mark on its own?

Show answer

It has no intelligence; it must be directed.

Mission 5

A universe tuned for life

  • Physicists find that some basic numbers of nature (such as the strength of gravity) fall in a narrow range that allows stars, planets and life.
  • Change them slightly, and no life could exist. This is called fine-tuning.
  • The anthropic principle (from Greek anthropos, "human"; named by physicist Brandon Carter in 1973): the universe has the features that allow observers like us.
  • Many believers read this as design: the universe was formed for humanity.
  • This fits Genesis: humans are made last and given dominion over the earth (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Others read it differently: we could only exist to notice it in a universe that allows life.

Mission 5Diversity

What design shows about God

  • Catholics: God is a wise, powerful and good designer. Evolution can be the way God works: Pope Pius XII (Humani Generis, 1950) and Pope John Paul II (1996) accepted evolution of the body as compatible with faith.
  • Many Protestants agree. Some evangelical creationists read Genesis 1 literally and reject evolution; some support intelligent design, which says some features are too complex to have evolved.
  • Muslims: nature is full of ayat (signs) of Allah; the Qur'an repeatedly asks people to reflect on them.
  • Jews: Genesis 1 shows God's ordered, good creation. Some Orthodox Jews read it literally; many Jews read the six days non-literally.
  • Buddhists: no designer is needed: things arise through dependent origination. Hindus: many see the world as ordered by Brahman; the Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90) describes the ordered world coming from a cosmic being.
Mission 5

Objections from atheists and Humanists

  • Weak comparison. David Hume (1711–1776), Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779): the universe is not much like a machine, so we cannot be sure it had a maker like a watch's.
  • Which designer? Hume's character Philo says the world could be "the first rude essay of some infant deity" (Dialogues, Part V): perhaps a team of gods, or a careless one.
  • Evolution. Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859): natural selection produces living things fitted for their surroundings, step by small step, without a designer.
  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The Blind Watchmaker (1986): nature's only "watchmaker" is blind natural selection (paraphrase).
  • Evil and suffering. Diseases, parasites and earthquakes look like poor design for a loving God.
  • Fine-tuning by chance. If there are many universes, or we could only exist in one that allows life, fine-tuning needs no designer. Humanists say science explains order better than a designer does.

Mission 5

Religious replies

  • Evolution is how God designs. Many Christians, Jews and Muslims accept evolution and see the laws that make it possible as designed. F. R. Tennant (1866–1957), Philosophical Theology (1930), argued that the whole process, not single organs, shows design.
  • Fine-tuning comes before evolution. Natural selection cannot explain why the universe allows life at all.
  • One designer is simpler. One God explains order more simply than many gods (a rule of thumb called Ockham's razor).
  • Suffering may have purposes: free will, or the growth of souls (see Mission 3).
  • Creationists reply differently: they reject evolution as an account of origins.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The design argument shows that God exists."

  • Reasons for: order and purpose look planned (Paley, Aquinas); fine-tuning allows life; scripture says creation reveals God (Romans 1:20; for Muslims, Surah 2:164 calls nature signs of Allah).
  • Reasons against: evolution explains apparent design (Darwin); the analogy is weak (Hume); suffering suggests poor design; chance or many universes could explain fine-tuning.
  • Another view: the argument supports faith for those who already believe, but does not convince on its own.
  • Judge it by: Does evolution remove the need for a designer, or push design back to the laws of nature? Is the watch analogy strong? Can a designer's goodness be read from a world with suffering?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, omnibenevolent; transcendent and immanent; personal and impersonal; knowable and unknowable.
  • Christians: Trinity and love (1 John 4:8); Muslims: tawhid; Jews: one God (Deuteronomy 6:4).

Mission 2

  • Moral evil comes from human choices; natural evil from nature. Suffering raises the inconsistent triad (omnipotent, omnibenevolent God and evil) and the evidential problem; innocent suffering is the hardest case.
  • Traditions locate the origin of evil differently: Fall and Original Sin; yetzer ha-ra and choice; Iblis and testing; karma, ignorance and maya; craving; ego and the five evils.

Mission 3

  • Augustine: evil is a privation of good, caused by misused free will (the Fall). Irenaeus and Hick: the world is a vale of soul-making. Both face objections (evolution; the amount of suffering).
  • Job: trust God beyond understanding; practical responses: prayer and charity; Humanists act to reduce suffering.
  • Muslims see life as a test (Surah 2:155–156, 67:2) met with sabr, prayer and charity.
  • Jews respond through Job, the Psalms, tzedakah and, after the Shoah, very different theologies.

Mission 4

  • The First Cause (cosmological) argument reasons from the existence of the universe to an uncaused cause: God.
  • Aquinas' First Three Ways argue from motion, cause and contingency; al-Ghazali's kalam argues from the universe's beginning.
  • Objections: what caused God? the universe as brute fact; parts and whole; infinite chains; science; which God? Believers reply that God is necessary and uncaused.

Mission 5

  • The design (teleological) argument reasons from order and purpose in nature to a designer: Paley's watch, Aquinas' Fifth Way, fine-tuning and the anthropic principle.
  • Romans 1:20 teaches that creation shows God's power, and for Muslims Surah 2:164 calls nature signs of Allah; traditions differ on evolution and on how to read Genesis 1.
  • Objections: Hume (weak analogy, which designer?), Darwin's natural selection, evil and suffering, chance; replies: evolution as God's method, fine-tuning, simplicity.
