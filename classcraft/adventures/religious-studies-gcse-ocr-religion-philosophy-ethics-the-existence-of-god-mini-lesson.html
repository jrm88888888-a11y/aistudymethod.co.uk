The Existence of God

This mini-lesson works through the OCR GCSE topic The Existence of God: the arguments used to argue for God (the Design and Cosmological arguments, religious experience and revelation), the Problem of Evil that challenges belief, and how believers and non-believers respond to it.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (matching, definitions and short explanations) and collect ⭐ stars. Each argument is stated fairly, alongside the responses to it. Press Start when you're ready.

The big question

Does God exist?

This topic asks: can we give reasons for or against the existence of God? Believers offer arguments pointing to God, while others raise challenges. Nothing here is "proved" like a maths sum — these are reasons people weigh up.

  • The Design argument — the order and purpose in the universe points to a designer (God).
  • The Cosmological argument — everything that begins needs a cause, so there must be a first cause (God).
  • Religious experience & revelation — some say God is known through nature, conscience, scripture, visions and miracles.
  • The Problem of Evil — the strongest challenge: why does an all-good, all-powerful God allow suffering?

Spec focus: OCR asks you to explain the Design and Cosmological arguments, religious experience and revelation, the Problem of Evil and suffering, and both religious and non-religious responses — always weighing the argument against its counter-argument.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Design (Teleological) argument — the universe shows order and purpose, so it must have a designer (God).
  • Cosmological (First Cause) argument — everything that begins has a cause; the universe began, so there is a first uncaused cause (God).
  • Revelation — God making himself known: general (through nature and conscience) or special (through scripture, visions, miracles, Jesus).
  • Omnipotent / omniscient / omnibenevolent — all-powerful / all-knowing / all-loving.
  • Problem of Evil — the challenge that evil and suffering count against an all-good, all-powerful God.

Common mix-up: the Design argument is about order and purpose; the Cosmological argument is about cause and the beginning of the universe. Keep them separate in the exam.

The Design argument

Paley's watchmaker

William Paley argued that if you found a watch on the ground, its intricate parts working together for a purpose would tell you it had a designer — it could not have come together by chance. Paley said the universe is like that watch, only far more complex, so it too must have a designer: God.

Counter-argument: many say evolution by natural selection explains the appearance of design without a designer — apparent "purpose" is the result of gradual adaptation, not a mind. So the Design argument is disputed, not settled.

The Cosmological argument

Everything needs a first cause

The Cosmological (First Cause) argument — linked to Aquinas' Five Ways and the Kalam argument — reasons: everything that begins to exist has a cause; the universe began to exist; therefore the universe has a cause. To avoid an endless chain of causes, there must be a first, uncaused cause — and believers say this is God.

Counter-argument: critics ask, "if everything needs a cause, what caused God?" Believers reply that God is uncaused and eternal — but non-believers say we could equally treat the universe itself as the uncaused starting point, so the argument is not conclusive.
Revelation

General and special revelation

Believers say God reveals himself — makes himself known. There are two types:

  • General revelation — God known indirectly, through nature (the world's order and beauty) and through conscience (an inner sense of right and wrong), available to everyone.
  • Special revelation — God known directly through scripture, visions, miracles and, for Christians, the person of Jesus.

Counter-argument: non-believers say religious experiences and "revelations" may be explained by psychology, emotion or the brain, and that different faiths report conflicting revelations — so they are not reliable proof of God. Believers reply that many such experiences are life-changing and consistent within a tradition.

The Problem of Evil

The inconsistent triad

The Problem of Evil is the strongest challenge to belief. If God is omnipotent (all-powerful), omniscient (all-knowing) and omnibenevolent (all-loving), why does so much evil and suffering exist? These three ideas plus the reality of evil seem to clash — this is called the inconsistent triad.

Two kinds of evil: moral evil is suffering caused by human choices (e.g. cruelty, war); natural evil is suffering from natural events (e.g. earthquakes, disease) not caused by humans.

Religious responses

The free will defence

One key religious response is the free will defence. God gave humans free will so they could genuinely love and choose good. But real freedom means people can also choose to do wrong — and that is where moral evil comes from. On this view, evil is the price of a world where our choices truly matter, not a sign that God does not exist.

Augustinian view: St Augustine linked evil to the misuse of free will and the Fall (humans turning away from God). Evil is a "going wrong" of a good creation, not something God made.

So for many believers, the Problem of Evil does not disprove God: a loving God allows freedom, and freedom makes some evil possible.

Religious responses

Suffering as a test or "soul-making"

A second religious response says suffering can have a purpose:

  • Soul-making (an Irenaean-type view) — a world with challenges lets people grow in virtues like courage, patience and compassion. We become better souls by overcoming hardship.
  • A test of faith — some believers see suffering as a test that can deepen trust in God.
  • Trust in God's greater plan — believers may accept that God sees a bigger picture we cannot, so evil now may serve a good we don't yet understand.

Careful — be fair: not all believers accept these answers. Many find natural evil (like a child's disease) very hard to square with a loving God, and some think the free will defence does not explain natural evil at all. The responses are attempts, not knock-down proofs.

Non-religious responses

The atheist and scientific view

Many atheists and Humanists conclude that the arguments for God fail and that evil counts against God's existence. Two main lines of thought:

  • Evil as evidence against God — an all-good, all-powerful God would prevent pointless suffering; since it exists, such a God probably does not.
  • Science instead of design — evolution by natural selection explains life's apparent design, and cosmology explains the universe's origins, without needing a creator.

Be fair to both sides: believers reply that science explains how things happen, not why there is anything at all, and that suffering may serve purposes we cannot see. Atheists reply this is unfalsifiable. GCSE answers should present both views and reach a reasoned judgement.

Practical responses

How believers respond to suffering

Beyond arguments, believers respond to evil and suffering through action:

  • Prayer — asking for strength, comfort and guidance, and drawing closer to God in hard times.
  • Charity and service — helping those who suffer (giving to food banks, disaster relief, supporting the sick) as a way of doing good in the face of evil.
  • Community support — offering comfort and hope, following teachings such as "love your neighbour".
Why this matters: for many believers, the honest religious response is not a neat philosophical "answer" but trusting God and acting with compassion — turning the challenge of suffering into practical love for others.

Don't muddle them

Design vs Cosmological

Two arguments for God that are easy to confuse — keep them apart:

  • Design (Teleological) — starts from the order and purpose we see (Paley's watch): this points to a designer.
  • Cosmological (First Cause) — starts from the fact that things begin to exist: this points to a first cause.

Both conclude "God", but from different evidence: design from how the universe is arranged, cosmological from that it exists at all. Both also face counter-arguments (evolution; "what caused God?").

Memory hook: Design = Detail & purpose; Cosmological = Cause & the start.

Reaching a judgement

Weighing up the arguments

Top-mark answers do not just describe — they evaluate, weighing each argument against its counter and reaching a reasoned conclusion:

  • Design: points to order and purpose — but evolution offers a rival explanation, so it persuades some, not all.
  • Cosmological: a first cause feels needed — but "what caused God?" and treating the universe as uncaused weaken it.
  • Revelation & experience: powerful for those who have them — but hard to verify and sometimes conflicting.
  • Problem of Evil: the strongest challenge — but free will, soul-making and trust in God are serious replies.
  • Overall: for believers the arguments confirm faith; for atheists they fall short. Neither side gives absolute proof — it is about which reasons you find most convincing.

Recap

The arguments to know

Design (Teleological): order & purpose → a designer, God (Paley's watch). Counter: evolution.

Cosmological (First Cause): the universe began → a first uncaused cause, God (Aquinas / Kalam). Counter: "what caused God?"

Revelation: general (nature, conscience) and special (scripture, visions, miracles, Jesus).

Problem of Evil: can an omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent God allow moral & natural evil? (inconsistent triad)

Religious responses: free will defence; soul-making / test; Augustinian view (misuse of freedom, the Fall); trust; prayer & charity.

Non-religious responses: evil as evidence against God; evolution & science instead of design.
