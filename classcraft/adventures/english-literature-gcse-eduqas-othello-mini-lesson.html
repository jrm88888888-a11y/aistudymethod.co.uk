Othello · story and tragic shapeScreen 1

The play in five acts

Othello dates from c.1603–04 and was played at court in November 1604. Shakespeare adapted a story from Cinthio's Gli Hecatommithi (1565).

ActWhat happens
1Venice. Iago, passed over for promotion, wakes Brabantio: his daughter Desdemona has married Othello in secret. The senate backs Othello and sends him to defend Cyprus against the Turks.
2Cyprus. A storm drowns the Turkish fleet; Othello and Desdemona are reunited. Iago gets Cassio drunk; Cassio brawls and loses his post.
3Iago hints to Othello that Desdemona and Cassio are lovers. Emilia picks up Desdemona's handkerchief and gives it to Iago. Othello vows revenge.
4Othello falls into a fit, strikes Desdemona in front of Lodovico and calls her a whore.
5Iago and Roderigo ambush Cassio, who is wounded; Iago kills Roderigo. Othello smothers Desdemona. Emilia reveals the truth; Iago kills her. Othello kills himself.

Othello · story and tragic shapeScreen 5

The shape of a tragedy

  • A tragedy follows a great figure from high status to death, partly through his own actions.
  • Tragic hero: Othello begins admired, "Valiant Othello" to the Duke (Act 1 Scene 3).
  • Fatal flaw (hamartia): readers disagree: jealousy, too much trust in "honest" Iago, or insecurity as an outsider.
  • Turning point: Act 3 Scene 3, where Iago plants suspicion and Othello vows revenge.
  • Catastrophe and recognition: Emilia exposes Iago; Othello sees what he has done and dies. Lodovico restores order: "Cassio rules in Cyprus."
Othello · story and tragic shapeScreen 6

From Venice to Cyprus, in no time

  • Venice (Act 1) is a city of law: Brabantio's charge is heard by the Duke and senate, and Othello is cleared.
  • Cyprus (Acts 2–5) is a garrison on the edge of war. The storm removes the Turks, so the conflict turns inward.
  • Compressed time: in Cyprus the action seems to last about two days. Yet Bianca complains, "What, keep a week away?" Critics call this double time.
  • The speed matters: Othello never has time to check Iago's story.

?Blissful in Act 2, a murderer by Act 5. How did it happen so fast?

The play follows a tragic shape that turns in Act 3 Scene 3, when Iago works on Othello's trust and Othello vows revenge. Shakespeare squeezes the action in Cyprus into a short time, so Othello acts before he can check the lie. How far Othello's own nature shares the blame is the debate the next missions explore.

Othello · OthelloScreen 7

Two ways to read Othello

  • A. C. Bradley (1904) saw a noble, trusting hero destroyed by Iago, inviting pity.
  • F. R. Leavis (1937) argued the opposite: Iago only works on what is already in Othello, his self-idealising and readiness to be deceived.
  • The last speech supports both: real grief, but also a hero still shaping his own story, as he did before the senate. Is he "not easily jealous"?
  • Strong answers weigh evidence from both sides.

Mission 3

Iago tells us why he hates Othello. Should we believe him?

Make a guess: Iago tells us why he hates Othello. Should we believe him?

Othello · IagoScreen 1

Who Iago is

  • Othello's ancient (ensign, standard-bearer). Othello made Cassio lieutenant instead (Act 1 Scene 1).
  • He tells Roderigo: "I am not what I am." He serves Othello only "to serve my turn upon him".
  • Everyone calls him "honest": Othello, Cassio and Desdemona all trust him.
  • His soliloquies and asides tell the audience his plans, so we watch with dramatic irony, knowing what the victims don't.

Reasons that keep changing

  • Act 1 Scene 1: passed over for Cassio, "a great arithmetician".
  • Act 1 Scene 3: a rumour about Othello and Emilia; "sport and profit".
  • Act 2 Scene 1: "I do love her too" (Desdemona), to "diet my revenge"; and "I fear Cassio with my night-cap too".

Othello · IagoScreen 7

Devil or man?

  • The play links Iago to hell: "Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world’s light." (Act 1 Scene 3). Othello calls him a "demi-devil" (Act 5 Scene 2).
  • Some readers see a stage devil who enjoys evil for its own sake.
  • Others see a resentful soldier, envious of rank, beauty and love.
  • At the end he refuses to explain: "Demand me nothing. What you know, you know." The question stays open.
Mission checkScreen 8

?Iago tells us why he hates Othello. Should we believe him?

Not fully. Iago gives the audience a string of reasons (the promotion, rumours about his wife, love for Desdemona, Cassio's "daily beauty"), but he admits he doesn't know if the rumour is true and ends in silence. Strong answers quote the motives and explore why Shakespeare leaves his malice partly unexplained.

Othello · Desdemona and EmiliaScreen 1

Desdemona in Act 1

  • She marries Othello in secret, against her father's wishes. Brabantio calls her "A maiden never bold" (Act 1 Scene 3).
  • She speaks for herself before the senate and asks to go to Cyprus: "Let me go with him."
  • She loved Othello for who he is: "I saw Othello’s visage in his mind".
  • In this play's world, a daughter owed obedience to her father and a wife to her husband.

Othello · Desdemona and EmiliaScreen 4

Under accusation

  • She pleads for Cassio again and again (Act 3 Scene 3), which Iago turns into "proof".
  • Asked for the handkerchief, she says, "It is not lost" (Act 3 Scene 4): a small untruth from fear.
  • Struck and called a whore, she answers, "Your wife, my lord, your true and loyal wife." (Act 4 Scene 2).
  • Dying, she protects him: "Nobody; I myself. Farewell." (Act 5 Scene 2).
  • Readers disagree: a passive victim, or a woman who chooses loyalty to the end?
Othello · Desdemona and EmiliaScreen 5

Emilia

  • Iago's wife and Desdemona's attendant.
  • She picks up the handkerchief and gives it to Iago "to please his fantasy" (Act 3 Scene 3), then says nothing.
  • She is blunt about men: "They are all but stomachs and we all but food;" (Act 3 Scene 4).
  • In Act 5 she defies her husband: "I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak." Iago kills her.

?Desdemona defies her father, then won't defy her husband. Why?

Desdemona's love doesn't change: it made her defy her father and it keeps her loyal to Othello even when he accuses her. What changes is that the husband she chose turns against her, in a world that gives husbands power over wives. Emilia's frank speeches and final defiance give the play another woman's voice.

Mission 5

Make a guess: There is no affair. So where does Othello's jealousy come from?

Othello · jealousyScreen 1

Jealousy everywhere

  • Iago suspects Othello and Cassio with his wife; the thought gnaws him "like a poisonous mineral" (Act 2 Scene 1).
  • Roderigo is jealous of Othello, and Iago feeds it for money.
  • Bianca suspects Cassio when he gives her the handkerchief: "This is some token from a newer friend." (Act 3 Scene 4).
  • Othello has no jealousy in Acts 1–2. Iago must create it.

Othello · jealousyScreen 4

From doubt to "proof"

  • Iago warns: "beware, my lord, of jealousy; / It is the green-ey’d monster" (Act 3 Scene 3).
  • Othello insists, "I’ll see before I doubt; when I doubt, prove;" then demands "the ocular proof".
  • Iago offers only a dream he says Cassio had, and a handkerchief he says Cassio has.
  • No real proof exists: Desdemona is innocent, and the handkerchief Othello later sees with Cassio was planted.

Othello · jealousyScreen 7

A monster "Begot upon itself"

  • Emilia: jealous souls "are not ever jealous for the cause, / But jealous for they are jealous" (Act 3 Scene 4).
  • Othello's own fears help it grow: "Haply, for I am black," and "declin’d / Into the vale of years" (Act 3 Scene 3).
  • At the end he calls himself "one not easily jealous". Many readers doubt it: he vows revenge within one scene.
  • A. C. Bradley (1904) argued that such jealousy turns human nature to chaos and frees the beast in a man.

Venice needs Othello to fight. Does it ever accept him?

Make a guess: Venice needs Othello to fight. Does it ever accept him?

Othello · race and the outsiderScreen 1

The "Moor" and the source

  • To Shakespeare's audience (c.1603–04) "Moor" could mean a North African, a Muslim, or loosely any dark-skinned African. Othello calls himself "black".
  • African people lived in England then; a Moroccan embassy visited London in 1600.
  • In Cinthio's source tale the Moor has no name and the story warns against such marriages. Shakespeare gives him a name, eloquence and greatness.
  • Context isn't marked in Section A: use it only to sharpen a point.
Othello · race and the outsiderScreen 2

Venice: respect and its limits

  • Venice is a rich trading republic run by a Duke and senators, at war with the Ottoman Turks. It needs Othello: "Valiant Othello, we must straight employ you".
  • Othello says Brabantio "lov’d me, oft invited me", yet Brabantio can only explain the marriage as witchcraft: "To fall in love with what she fear’d to look on!"
  • Many readers note that even the Duke's praise, "Your son-in-law is far more fair than black.", treats blackness as a fault.

Othello · race and the outsiderScreen 5

Othello's view of himself

  • In Act 1 he is proud of his "royal siege" and "perfect soul".
  • In Act 3 he begins to see himself through Venice's prejudice: "Haply, for I am black, / And have not those soft parts of conversation".
  • He links his own colour with sin: Desdemona's name is "begrim’d and black / As mine own face." (Act 3 Scene 3).
  • In his last speech he casts himself as Venice's enemy, an outsider he strikes down as he stabs himself.

Othello · race and the outsiderScreen 7

Reading race in the play

  • Thomas Rymer (1693) read the play as a warning to daughters not to elope with black men.
  • For centuries white actors played Othello in dark make-up. Ira Aldridge, a black American actor, played him in London in 1825; Paul Robeson in 1930.
  • Today readers debate: does the play expose Venice's racism, or repeat it? Strong answers show both.

Othello · language and key quotationsScreen 1

Two voices

  • Othello speaks grand blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter): long sentences, far-off places and cosmic images ("hills of seas / Olympus-high").
  • G. Wilson Knight's essay 'The Othello Music' (1930) is named after this style.
  • Iago shifts voice to suit his listener: plain prose with Roderigo ("Put money in thy purse"), verse in soliloquy.
  • His images are coarse: animals, poison, disease.

Mission checkScreen 9

?By Act 4 Othello talks like Iago. How did Iago get into his words?

Iago's language gets inside Othello as his lies do. Iago's coarse images of animals and poison start appearing in Othello's mouth ("Goats and monkeys!"), and his grand verse breaks into prose and fragments in Act 4. In Act 5 the verse returns, and readers argue about what that return means.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Othello (c.1603–04) moves from Venice, a city of law, to Cyprus, a garrison where the war ends and the conflict turns inward.
  • The tragic shape: an admired hero (Act 1), a high point (the reunion, Act 2), a turning point (Act 3 Scene 3), and a catastrophe and recognition (Act 5).
  • Compressed ("double") time leaves Othello no time to check Iago's story.
  • In Section A you write about an extract and then the whole play; knowing the five acts helps you pick moments quickly. Context isn't marked here.
  • In Act 1 Othello is calm, eloquent and trusted: his "Rude am I in my speech" is itself polished verse.
  • Under Iago's pressure his speech breaks into prose and fragments, and he strikes and then kills Desdemona.
  • His last speech asks to be remembered as one "that loved not wisely, but too well"; readers disagree how far to believe his self-portrait.
  • Iago is Othello's ancient, trusted as "honest", who tells the audience "I am not what I am."
  • His soliloquies give shifting motives; he admits the rumour about Emilia may be false. Coleridge called it "motiveless malignity".
  • He uses Roderigo's money, Cassio's weakness for drink and Emilia's loyalty, and ends refusing to explain.
  • Desdemona defends her marriage in public ("a divided duty") and stays loyal, even in death ("Nobody; I myself").
  • Readers disagree whether she is a passive victim or a woman who chooses loyalty.
  • Emilia, cynical about men, argues that husbands teach wives their faults, and at the end speaks out against Iago at the cost of her life.
  • Othello isn't jealous until Iago makes him so, with echoes ("Indeed?"), warnings ("the green-ey’d monster") and false proof.
  • The handkerchief is a trifle that jealousy turns into "proofs of holy writ".
  • Emilia says jealousy breeds itself; readers disagree whether Othello is "not easily jealous", as he claims.
  • "Moor" could mean several things to Shakespeare's audience; the play calls Othello black, and Shakespeare makes his Moor a named, eloquent, great figure.
  • Venice respects the general in public, but racist language surrounds him; Iago uses it as a weapon from the first scene.
  • Under Iago's pressure Othello begins to see himself through Venice's prejudice. Readers debate whether the play exposes or repeats racism.
  • Context isn't marked in Section A: the marks come from close reading of how Shakespeare presents race.
  • Othello's grand blank verse (the subject of Wilson Knight's essay 'The Othello Music') meets Iago's coarse, shifting voice of animals, poison and hell.
  • In Act 4 Othello's speech collapses into prose fragments and borrows Iago's images ("Goats and monkeys!").
  • In Act 5 his verse returns; readers debate whether that shows nobility restored or self-deception.

Key quotations to learn

  • Valiant Othello, we must straight employ you (Act 1 Scene 3 (Duke)) The hero's high status: Venice needs him.
  • O my soul’s joy! (Act 2 Scene 1 (Othello)) The high point of happiness before the fall.
  • If it were now to die, / ’Twere now to be most happy (Act 2 Scene 1 (Othello)) Joy shadowed by death: foreshadowing.
  • Hell and night / Must bring this monstrous birth to the world’s light. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Iago)) The plot is "born" as Act 1 ends.
  • Look on the tragic loading of this bed. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Lodovico)) The ending fixes our eyes on the cost.
  • Keep up your bright swords, for the dew will rust them. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Othello)) Calm command: authority without force.
  • Rude am I in my speech (Act 1 Scene 3 (Othello)) A modest claim made in eloquent verse.
  • She lov’d me for the dangers I had pass’d, / And I lov’d her that she did pity them. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Othello)) Love built on his story.
  • Is this the noble Moor, whom our full senate / Call all in all sufficient? (Act 4 Scene 1 (Lodovico)) Venice sees how far he has changed.
  • Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) His last verdict on himself.
  • An honourable murderer, if you will. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Oxymoron: can a murderer be honourable?
  • I am not what I am. (Act 1 Scene 1 (Iago)) His deception, stated openly to Roderigo.
  • I hate the Moor, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Iago)) The hatred is clear; the reasons aren't.
  • I know not if ’t be true, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Iago)) He admits his motive may be false.
  • The Moor is of a free and open nature / That thinks men honest that but seem to be so, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Iago)) Othello's virtue becomes Iago's tool.
  • He hath a daily beauty in his life / That makes me ugly. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Iago)) Envy of Cassio's goodness.
  • Demand me nothing. What you know, you know. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Iago)) He ends in silence: no final explanation.
  • I do perceive here a divided duty: (Act 1 Scene 3 (Desdemona)) Bold, reasoned defence of her marriage.
  • I saw Othello’s visage in his mind, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Desdemona)) She loves him for his inner self.
  • Your wife, my lord, your true and loyal wife. (Act 4 Scene 2 (Desdemona)) Innocence under attack.
  • Nobody; I myself. Farewell. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Desdemona)) Dying, she protects Othello.
  • They are all but stomachs and we all but food; (Act 3 Scene 4 (Emilia)) Emilia's blunt view of men.
  • I will not charm my tongue; I am bound to speak. (Act 5 Scene 2 (Emilia)) She defies Iago to tell the truth.
  • It is the green-ey’d monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Iago)) Jealousy as a monster that torments its host.
  • Give me the ocular proof, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) He demands to see, but never does.
  • Trifles light as air / Are to the jealous confirmations strong / As proofs of holy writ. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Iago)) Tiny "evidence" feels like certainty.
  • But jealous for they are jealous: ’tis a monster / Begot upon itself, born on itself. (Act 3 Scene 4 (Emilia)) Jealousy needs no cause.
  • Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, (Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) His claim, to be tested against Act 3.
  • Valiant Othello, we must straight employ you (Act 1 Scene 3 (Duke)) Public honour: Venice needs him.
  • To fall in love with what she fear’d to look on! (Act 1 Scene 3 (Brabantio)) A father's prejudice as disbelief.
  • Your son-in-law is far more fair than black. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Duke)) Praise that still treats blackness as a fault.
  • Haply, for I am black, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Othello turns Venice's prejudice on himself.
  • I saw Othello’s visage in his mind, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Desdemona)) Love that looks beyond the "visage".
  • Like to the Pontic Sea, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Othello)) Grand simile: revenge as an unstoppable tide.
  • I’ll pour this pestilence into his ear, (Act 2 Scene 3 (Iago)) Iago's words as poison.
  • Were they as prime as goats, as hot as monkeys, (Act 3 Scene 3 (Iago)) Iago's animal images ...
