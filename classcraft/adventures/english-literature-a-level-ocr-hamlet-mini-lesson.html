Mission 1

Revenge plays had a recipe. Does Hamlet follow it or rewrite it?

Make a guess: Revenge plays had a recipe. Does Hamlet follow it or rewrite it?

Hamlet · structure and revenge tragedyScreen 1

The revenge-tragedy recipe

  • Revenge tragedy was a popular Elizabethan form. Thomas Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy (c.1587) set the pattern, drawing on the Roman tragedian Seneca.
  • Typical ingredients: a secret murder, a ghost demanding vengeance, a revenger who delays, real or feigned madness, a play-within-a-play, and a bloody ending that kills the revenger too.
  • Hamlet (c.1600–01) reworks an old Danish story (Saxo Grammaticus, Belleforest) and probably a lost earlier Hamlet play, mentioned in 1589 and 1596.
Hamlet · structure and revenge tragedyScreen 2

  • Essay practice is for class.
Hamlet · structure and revenge tragedyScreen 3

Five acts, one command

ActWhat happens
1The Ghost reveals the murder and demands revenge.
2Hamlet acts mad; travelling players arrive at court.
3The play scene exposes Claudius; Hamlet spares him at prayer, then kills Polonius.
4Hamlet is shipped to England; Ophelia goes mad; Laertes returns for revenge.
5The graveyard; the duel; Gertrude, Laertes, Claudius and Hamlet die; Fortinbras arrives.
  • Three sons lose fathers: Hamlet, Laertes and Fortinbras. Each is a different kind of avenger, so each is a foil to the others.
  • The play scene in Act 3 is the turning point: proof at last, and then the first killing goes wrong.

Hamlet · structure and revenge tragedyScreen 6

Where the recipe changes

  • The revenger thinks aloud: seven soliloquies question revenge, death and himself.
  • He tests the evidence: the Ghost "May be the devil", so the play scene becomes a trap for proof.
  • The revenge is not planned: Hamlet kills Claudius on impulse, inside Claudius's own plot, after accepting "The readiness is all."
  • Order returns from outside, with Fortinbras.
  • Audiences over time have read this changed recipe differently: as delay, as doubt, or as a sign of the play's modernity.

Mission 2

Hamlet says he'll fake madness. Does he stay in control of it?

Make a guess: Hamlet says he'll fake madness. Does he stay in control of it?

Hamlet · delay, madness and performanceScreen 1

An antic disposition

  • After the Ghost, Hamlet warns his friends he may choose "To put an antic disposition on" (1.5): to act strangely, like a clown.
  • He plays mad with Polonius, Rosencrantz and Guildenstern and Ophelia; alone or with Horatio he is lucid.
  • He tells Gertrude he is "not in madness, / But mad in craft" (3.4).
  • The court disagrees: Polonius finds "method in't"; Claudius sees danger; Ophelia mourns "a noble mind" overthrown.

Hamlet · delay, madness and performanceScreen 4

Why does he delay?

  • Thought: Coleridge saw in Hamlet "an almost enormous, intellectual activity, and a proportionate aversion to real action"; Hazlitt wrote, "His ruling passion is to think, not to act".
  • Doubt: the Ghost "May be the devil" (2.2), so he wants proof.
  • Melancholy: Bradley (1904) blamed "the moral shock" of discovering, through her hasty remarriage, his mother's "true nature".
  • Conscience: "conscience does make cowards of us all" (3.1).

Hamlet · delay, madness and performanceScreen 6

A play full of performances

  • Hamlet scorns "seems" (1.2), then performs madness himself.
  • He coaches the players to "Suit the action to the word" and adds lines to the play he stages for the King.
  • Others act too: Claudius has a "most painted word"; Polonius gives Ophelia a book to "colour" her waiting.
  • The First Player weeps for Hecuba in a fiction; Hamlet, with real cause, says he "can say nothing".
  • Part (b) propositions may test this, e.g. 'Hamlet's madness is nothing but an act.'

Mission 3

Claudius kneels to pray for forgiveness. Is he a monster?

Make a guess: Claudius kneels to pray for forgiveness. Is he a monster?

Hamlet · Claudius, Gertrude and the courtScreen 1

The new king's first speech

  • Claudius opens his court with polished public rhetoric: the royal "we", balanced clauses, and quick, efficient business with Norway and Laertes.
  • He calls Gertrude "our sometime sister, now our queen" and says the court approved the match.
  • The Church forbade marrying a dead brother's widow (Leviticus 18:16), so the Ghost and Hamlet call it incest.
  • Bradley: as king he is "courteous and never undignified".

Hamlet · Claudius, Gertrude and the courtScreen 4

The prayer scene

  • Alone, Claudius confesses: "O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven" (3.3). It has "the primal eldest curse" on it: Cain killed his brother.
  • He can't repent while he keeps "My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen."
  • Hamlet finds him kneeling and spares him, so that he won't go to heaven.
  • Then: "Words without thoughts never to heaven go." Dramatic irony: he couldn't pray at all.

Hamlet · Claudius, Gertrude and the courtScreen 6

Gertrude: what does she know?

  • She never admits knowing of the murder; "As kill a king?" (3.4) sounds like shock.
  • The Ghost says "leave her to heaven"; Hamlet sees "Frailty" in her.
  • She admits "black and grained spots" in her soul, but over what?
  • At the end she drinks against the King's order: "I will, my lord; I pray you pardon me."
  • Bradley called her "very dull and very shallow"; others note we see her through men's eyes.
  • Productions over time have played her as complicit, ignorant or torn.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Claudius kneels to pray for forgiveness. Is he a monster?

He is more disturbing than a monster: a murderer who knows exactly what he has done, tries to pray, and still keeps "My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen." Strong answers weigh his working conscience against his choices, and note how dramatic irony makes the prayer scene turn on what Hamlet can't see.

Mission 4

Polonius's family mirrors Hamlet's. What does the mirror show?

Make a guess: Polonius's family mirrors Hamlet's. What does the mirror show?

Hamlet · Ophelia, Polonius and LaertesScreen 1

A family of watchers

  • Polonius advises Laertes, "to thine own self be true", then sends Reynaldo to spy on him: "By indirections find directions out" (2.1).
  • He orders Ophelia to refuse Hamlet's letters, then uses her as bait: "At such a time I’ll loose my daughter to him" (2.2).
  • He dies spying behind the arras (3.4).

Hamlet · Ophelia, Polonius and LaertesScreen 4

Ophelia's madness and death

  • In 4.5 Ophelia sings of a dead father and a seduced girl, and gives out flowers: "There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance".
  • Laertes calls it "A document in madness".
  • Her drowning is reported (4.7); the Priest says "Her death was doubtful", so her rites are cut short.
  • Elaine Showalter (1985): Ophelia's story is the history of how she has been represented, in art, psychiatry and theatre.
  • Her stage Ophelias change by era: restrained (18th century), picturesque (Romantic), more disturbed (modern).

Hamlet · Ophelia, Polonius and LaertesScreen 6

Laertes, the other revenger

  • Laertes returns at once, raises a mob ("Laertes shall be king") and storms the palace.
  • Asked what he would do to Hamlet, he answers, "To cut his throat i’ th’ church": Hamlet wouldn't kill Claudius at prayer.
  • He agrees to an unbated, poisoned sword: honour turned to treachery.
  • Dying, he admits, "I am justly kill’d with mine own treachery", and exchanges forgiveness with Hamlet.
  • Hamlet sees "The portraiture of his" cause in his own (5.2).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Polonius's family mirrors Hamlet's. What does the mirror show?

It shows a second family broken by the same crimes. Laertes, a son with a murdered father, acts at once and ends in treachery; Ophelia, used by her father and rejected by Hamlet, goes mad and drowns. Set against Hamlet's story, they make us judge his delay, and the cost of the court's plots to the young.

Mission 5

A Wittenberg student meets a ghost from purgatory. Trust it?

Make a guess: A Wittenberg student meets a ghost from purgatory. Trust it?

Hamlet · death, the Ghost and the afterlifeScreen 1

A ghost from purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: souls not yet fit for heaven are cleansed in purgatory, helped by the prayers of the living.
  • The Church of England rejected purgatory (Article 22 of the Thirty-Nine Articles).
  • Hamlet studies at Wittenberg, Martin Luther's university.
  • Yet the Ghost speaks like a soul in purgatory. He died without the last rites: "Unhous’led, disappointed, unanel’d".

Hamlet · death, the Ghost and the afterlifeScreen 4

Spirit of health or goblin damned?

  • Hamlet's first words: "Be thou a spirit of health or goblin damn’d" (1.4).
  • Horatio fears it may lead him to madness and death.
  • Protestant writers such as Lavater (English 1572) taught that apparitions were angels or devils, not souls of the dead.
  • So Hamlet fears "The spirit that I have seen / May be the devil" (2.2) and tests it with the play.
Hamlet · death, the Ghost and the afterlifeScreen 5

Death in Act 5

  • In the graveyard Hamlet holds Yorick's skull and pictures great men as clay: "Imperious Caesar, dead and turn’d to clay".
  • Ophelia's "maimed rites" show the Church's rules for a "doubtful" death.
  • Hamlet moves from dread of "The undiscover’d country" (3.1) to "The readiness is all" (5.2); Bradley calls this "fatalism rather than that of faith in Providence".
  • Shakespeare's son Hamnet died in 1596, aged 11; Greenblatt suggests that grief lies behind the play.

Mission 6

Something is rotten in the state of Denmark. Where does it start?

Make a guess: Something is rotten in the state of Denmark. Where does it start?

Hamlet · corruption, surveillance and the stateScreen 1

Images of rot and poison

  • Disease, poison and decay form an image cluster across the play.
  • Hamlet calls the world "an unweeded garden / That grows to seed" (1.2).
  • Marcellus: "Something is rotten in the state of Denmark" (1.4).
  • The Ghost says a false story has poisoned "the whole ear of Denmark" (1.5).
  • Hamlet sees "th’imposthume of much wealth and peace" (an abscess) in a pointless war (4.4).
  • Caroline Spurgeon (1935) found illness and injury images dominant in Hamlet.

Hamlet · corruption, surveillance and the stateScreen 4

A court of watchers

  • Claudius and Polonius hide as "lawful espials", watching "seeing unseen" (3.1).
  • Rosencrantz and Guildenstern are sent for to spy on Hamlet; Polonius sends Reynaldo to spy on Laertes.
  • Polonius hides behind the arras twice; the second time he dies.
  • Claudius: "Madness in great ones must not unwatch’d go."
  • Jan Kott (Shakespeare Our Contemporary, English 1964) read Hamlet as political drama: crime, spying and fear, with someone behind every curtain.
  • Kott's reading came from 1950s Poland: one audience's Hamlet over time.
Hamlet · corruption, surveillance and the stateScreen 5

Crown, election and succession

  • Denmark's crown is elective: Claudius "Popp’d in between th’election and my hopes" (5.2).
  • Rosencrantz: a king's fall drags down all around him, "The cease of majesty" (3.3), loyal words to a murderer.
  • A mob cries "Laertes shall be king"; Fortinbras takes the throne with Hamlet's "dying voice".
  • c.1600–01: Elizabeth I was old and had named no heir; Essex's rising failed in February 1601. A seized crown and an uncertain succession would feel urgent.

Mission 7

Every age finds its own Hamlet. So which reading is right?

Make a guess: Every age finds its own Hamlet. So which reading is right?

Hamlet · critical readingsScreen 1

The Romantic thinker

  • Samuel Johnson (1765) found "no adequate cause" for the feigned madness, and called Hamlet "rather an instrument than an agent".
  • S. T. Coleridge (lectures, 1810s) saw "an almost enormous, intellectual activity, and a proportionate aversion to real action".
  • William Hazlitt (1817): "It is WE who are Hamlet": anyone who has thought too much and acted too little.
Hamlet · critical readingsScreen 2

Bradley's tragic hero (1904)

  • A. C. Bradley rejected the view that outside obstacles stop Hamlet: he "never makes the slightest reference to any external difficulty".
  • He blamed melancholy caused by "the moral shock of the sudden ghastly disclosure of his mother's true nature".
  • This is character criticism: Hamlet treated almost as a real person with a past. Later critics questioned it.
Hamlet · critical readingsScreen 3

Twentieth-century Hamlets

  • Psychoanalytic: Ernest Jones (1910; Hamlet and Oedipus, 1949), after Freud, argued that Hamlet can't punish a man who has done what he unconsciously wished: killed his father and married his mother.
  • Formalist: T. S. Eliot (1919) judged the play an artistic failure: Hamlet's disgust is too great for its cause, Gertrude, so it lacks an objective correlative.
  • Political: Jan Kott (1964) saw a drama of power, spying and fear.
Hamlet · critical readingsScreen 4

Feminist and historicist Hamlets

  • Feminist: Elaine Showalter (1985) turned attention to Ophelia and to how her madness has been represented. Others ask why Gertrude is seen through Hamlet's disgust.
  • New historicist: Stephen Greenblatt (Hamlet in Purgatory, 2001) read the Ghost through the abolished doctrine of purgatory and the lost rites for the dead.
  • Part (b) rewards interpretations across time: know who, when and why.

Bradley argues that Hamlet's melancholy was caused by the shock of his mother's hasty remarriage. In 3–4 sentences, test this view against Hamlet's first soliloquy (1.2).

Model answer

Bradley's view is well supported by 1.2. Before the Ghost appears, Hamlet already longs for death, wishing "this too too solid flesh would melt", and the speech dwells on Gertrude's speed: "within a month". "Frailty, thy name is woman!" shows the shock spreading from one woman to all women. Yet a reading built only on character can miss the play's design: the Ghost soon gives Hamlet a second, political cause.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Every age finds its own Hamlet. So which reading is right?

No reading wins by age or fame. Each age brings its own questions (thought, character, the unconscious, politics, gender, religion), and the play can sustain many of them. The strongest answers state a critic's view precisely, test it against moments in the play, and say how far it holds.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Revenge tragedy (Kyd, Seneca) had stock ingredients: secret murder, ghost, delay, madness, play-within-a-play, bloody ending. Hamlet uses them all.
  • Shakespeare reshapes them: soliloquies question revenge, the play scene tests the evidence, and the final killing is improvised.
  • Part (a) analyses an extract; part (b) tests a proposition about the whole play.
  • Hamlet announces his "antic disposition" in 1.5 and performs it in public; alone or with Horatio he is lucid.
  • Readers explain the delay as too much thought (Coleridge, Hazlitt), doubt about the Ghost, melancholy (Bradley) or conscience.
  • The play is full of acting: the players, the play scene, Claudius's "painted word", Polonius's staging of Ophelia.
  • Claudius's public rhetoric (1.2) smooths over a hasty marriage the Church would call incest; in private (3.3) he confesses fratricide but can't repent.
  • Gertrude's knowledge is never made clear: "As kill a king?" sounds like shock, and readers and productions differ.
  • Gertrude has been played over time as complicit, ignorant or torn.
  • Polonius advises, spies and uses his children; he dies spying behind the arras.
  • Ophelia is told what to think, rejected by Hamlet, and goes mad after her father's death; Showalter reads her as a figure shaped by how she has been represented.
  • Laertes is Hamlet's foil: instant revenge, a willingness to kill in church, and treachery that recoils on him.
  • The Ghost speaks like a Catholic soul in purgatory, in a Protestant England that had rejected purgatory; Hamlet is a Wittenberg student.
  • Hamlet fears it "May be the devil" and tests its story; the play confirms the murder but not the Ghost's nature.
  • In Act 5 death levels all (Yorick, Alexander, Caesar) and Hamlet moves from dread to "The readiness is all".
  • An image cluster of disease, poison and rot runs from "an unweeded garden" to the "ulcerous place" of 3.4.
  • Elsinore is a court of watchers: espials, spies, letters and an arras; Kott read the play as a drama of political surveillance.
  • The crown is elective and contested; in 1600–01 England's succession was uncertain.
  • Romantic critics (Coleridge, Hazlitt) saw a thinker paralysed by thought; Johnson had found the madness unexplained.
  • Bradley (1904) blamed melancholy caused by the moral shock of Gertrude's remarriage.
  • Jones (Oedipal), Eliot (artistic failure, objective correlative), Kott (political), Showalter (Ophelia) and Greenblatt (purgatory) each shift the focus.
  • Part (b): interpretations across time, judged against the text.

Key quotations to learn

  • Revenge his foul and most unnatural murder. (1.5, the Ghost) The command that starts the revenge plot.
  • Taint not thy mind, nor let thy soul contrive / Against thy mother aught (1.5, the Ghost) The condition that makes revenge a moral problem.
  • The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right. (1.5, Hamlet) The revenger as a reluctant setter-right; his speech closes on a rhyming couplet near the end of Act 1.
  • The play’s the thing / Wherein I’ll catch the conscience of the King. (2.2, Hamlet) The play scene as detection, not killing.
  • purposes mistook / Fall’n on the inventors’ heads (5.2, Horatio) Plots recoil on their makers: revenge tragedy's ironic justice.
  • To put an antic disposition on (1.5, Hamlet) Madness announced as a chosen performance.
  • Though this be madness, yet there is method in’t (2.2, Polonius (aside)) Others suspect the act.
  • Must, like a whore, unpack my heart with words (2.2, Hamlet) Self-contempt for talking, not acting.
  • Thus conscience does make cowards of us all (3.1, Hamlet) Thought (or conscience) blocks action.
  • essentially am not in madness, / But mad in craft (3.4, Hamlet) Hamlet claims control of his madness.
  • With mirth in funeral, and with dirge in marriage, (1.2, Claudius) Balanced rhetoric smooths over the scandal.
  • Frailty, thy name is woman! (1.2, Hamlet (soliloquy)) Hamlet's judgement of Gertrude spreads to all women.
  • O, my offence is rank, it smells to heaven; (3.3, Claudius) Private confession of a "rank" crime.
  • My crown, mine own ambition, and my queen. (3.3, Claudius) Why he can't repent.
  • Words without thoughts never to heaven go. (3.3, Claudius) The rhyming couplet reveals the prayer failed: dramatic irony.
  • Thou turn’st mine eyes into my very soul, (3.4, Gertrude) Gertrude's guilt, but for what?
  • This above all: to thine own self be true; (1.3, Polonius) Advice that his own spying contradicts.
  • By indirections find directions out. (2.1, Polonius) His method: spying and traps.
  • I do not know, my lord, what I should think. (1.3, Ophelia) Ophelia told what to think.
  • There’s rosemary, that’s for remembrance; (4.5, Ophelia) Madness that still means something.
  • To cut his throat i’ th’ church. (4.7, Laertes) Laertes's revenge knows no limits.
  • I am justly kill’d with mine own treachery. (5.2, Laertes) The plotter caught in his own plot.
  • Be thou a spirit of health or goblin damn’d, (1.4, Hamlet) The Ghost's nature is uncertain from the start.
  • Doom’d for a certain term to walk the night, (1.5, the Ghost) Purgatory: suffering for a fixed term.
  • May be the devil, and the devil hath power / T’assume a pleasing shape (2.2, Hamlet) Protestant doubt; the reason to test the Ghost.
  • The undiscover’d country, from whose bourn / No traveller returns, (3.1, Hamlet) Dread of death; ironic after the Ghost's return.
  • Imperious Caesar, dead and turn’d to clay, (5.1, Hamlet) Death levels the great.
  • The readiness is all. (5.2, Hamlet) Acceptance, or fatalism (Bradley).
  • Something is rotten in the state of Denmark. (1.4, Marcellus) The state itself is diseased.
  • ’tis an unweeded garden / That grows to seed (1.2, Hamlet) Decay and neglect before the Ghost speaks.
  • the whole ear of Denmark / Is by a forged process of my death / Rankly abus’d (1.5, the Ghost) Private poison becomes public deceit.
  • Whilst rank corruption, mining all within, / Infects unseen. (3.4, Hamlet) Corruption hidden under a healthy surface.
  • Madness in great ones must not unwatch’d go. (3.1, Claudius) Surveillance as policy.
  • Popp’d in between th’election and my hopes, (5.2, Hamlet) A stolen succession.
  • But I have that within which passeth show; (1.2, Hamlet) The inner life that critics try to read.
  • O that this too too solid flesh would melt, (1.2, Hamlet) Despair before the Ghost speaks (Bradley's evidence).
  • I have of late, but wherefore I know not, lost all my mirth, (2.2, Hamlet) Melancholy he can't explain.
  • Thou wretched, rash, intruding fool, farewell! (3.4, Hamlet) His harshness, which Bradley refuses to soften.
  • It is WE who are Hamlet. (Hazlitt (1817)) The Romantic reader identifies with Hamlet.
  • the moral shock of the sudden ghastly disclosure of his mother's true nature (Bradley (1904)) Bradley's explanation of the melancholy.
