Arguments for the Existence of God

Paper 1 asks whether reason can establish that God exists. This lesson covers the three great families of argument — the design (teleological), cosmological and ontological arguments — and the sharpest objections to each.

A key distinction runs through everything: a posteriori arguments reason from experience of the world (design, cosmological), while a priori arguments reason from concepts alone (ontological). Work through each screen, answer the questions, and collect ⭐.

Design · a posteriori

The design (teleological) argument

Design arguments infer a designer from apparent order, regularity and purpose in nature.

  • Aquinas' Fifth Way (Summa Theologica, I, Q2, a3): natural bodies without intelligence act towards an end, as an arrow is directed by an archer — so they are directed by an intelligent being "whom we call God."
  • William Paley (Natural Theology, 1802): finding a watch on a heath, its parts fitted to a purpose imply a watchmaker; the eye and the universe show the same contrivance, so imply a Designer.
  • F. R. Tennant (Philosophical Theology, 1928–30): the anthropic argument (the cosmos is fine-tuned for life) and the aesthetic argument (we appreciate beauty beyond survival needs).

Design · challenges

Challenges to design

  • David Hume (Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion, 1779): the analogy is weak (the universe is unique and more like a growing organism than a machine); the order could have many designers, an imperfect or apprentice god, or arise by chance (the Epicurean hypothesis).
  • Charles Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859): natural selection offers a non-purposive mechanism for apparent design.
  • J. S. Mill (Nature, 1874): nature's cruelty and waste count against a wholly good, wise designer.

Cosmological · a posteriori

The cosmological argument

Cosmological arguments reason from the existence of the universe to a cause or explanation of it.

  • Aquinas' first three Ways: from motion (an unmoved mover), efficient causation (a first cause) and contingency (things that need not exist must depend on a necessary being).
  • Leibniz: the Principle of Sufficient Reason — everything has an explanation; the whole contingent series still needs a sufficient reason outside itself, namely a necessary being.

Cosmological · challenges

Challenges to the cosmological argument

  • Hume: the "fallacy of composition" — what is true of each part (having a cause) need not be true of the whole; and we have no experience of universe-making to reason from.
  • Bertrand Russell (BBC radio debate with F. C. Copleston, 1948): the universe may be a brute fact — "the universe is just there, and that's all."
  • Kant: causation applies within experience; we cannot legitimately extend it beyond the world to a first cause.
Ontological · a priori

The ontological argument

A wholly a priori argument: God's existence follows from the concept of God alone.

  • Anselm (Proslogion, 1078): God is "that than which nothing greater can be conceived." A being that exists in reality is greater than one existing only in the mind — so God must exist in reality (ch. 2); and must exist necessarily (ch. 3).
  • Descartes (Meditation V): existence belongs to God's essence as three angles belong to a triangle, or a valley to a mountain — a supremely perfect being cannot lack existence.

Ontological · challenges

Challenges to the ontological argument

  • Gaunilo ("On Behalf of the Fool"): the same logic would prove a perfect island must exist — a reductio ad absurdum.
  • Kant: existence is not a real predicate — saying a thing exists adds nothing to its concept, so you cannot define God into existence.
