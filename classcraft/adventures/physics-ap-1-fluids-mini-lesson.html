Mission 1

Mission 1: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Make a guess: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Quick check
?A block of ice keeps its own shape, but the water it melts into takes the shape of whatever holds it. Which explanation, in terms of the water molecules, accounts for this difference?
Mission 1

Solids, liquids, gases

  • Same molecules in every state. What differs: how strongly they interact, and so how they are arranged and move.
  • Solid: interactions hold each particle near a fixed place; it vibrates. Fixed shape.
  • Liquid: particles still close, but free to slide past each other.
  • Gas: particles far apart, interacting only briefly in collisions.
  • Fluid = liquid or gas: no fixed shape; it takes the shape of its container.
Mission 1

Density

  • Density = mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V.
  • SI unit: kg/m³. Also common: g/cm³.
  • 1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³. Water: about 1.0 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³.
  • Density belongs to the material, not the object. Cut a block in half: half the mass, half the volume, same ρ.
  • Rearranged: m = ρV and V = m/ρ.
  • A graph of mass against volume for one material is a straight line; its slope = ρ.
Quick check
?The density of aluminum is 2.7 g/cm³. What is the volume, in m³, of an aluminum block of mass 0.081 kg?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A brass cylinder: radius 1.0 cm, height 5.0 cm, mass 133 g.
  • V = πr²h = π × 1.0² × 5.0 = 15.7 cm³.
  • ρ = m/V = 133 ÷ 15.7 = 8.5 g/cm³.
  • In SI: 8.5 g/cm³ × 1000 = 8.5 × 10³ kg/m³.
  • Convert at the end, or work in kg and m³ throughout. Never mix g with m³.
Mission 1

Measuring density

  • Mass: top-pan balance (zeroed).
  • Regular solid: measure dimensions with vernier calipers or a micrometer; repeat at several places; calculate V.
  • Irregular solid: sink it in a displacement can or graduated cylinder; V = volume of water displaced.
  • Liquid: mass of an empty graduated cylinder, then with liquid added; read V at the bottom of the meniscus, eye level.
  • Better: plot mass against volume for several readings. Slope = ρ.
Quick check
?A student pours a liquid into a beaker in steps. After each step she records the volume V of liquid in the beaker and the total mass m of the beaker and liquid. The graph shows her results. What is the mass of 400 cm³ of this liquid?
Mission check

?A coin that looks like silver has a mass of 26.9 g. Lowered into a measuring cylinder of water, it raises the reading by 3.0 cm³. The table gives densities in kg/m³: aluminum 2.70 × 10³; copper 8.96 × 10³; silver 10.5 × 10³; lead 11.3 × 10³. Which metal is the coin most likely made of?

Same idea for the crown: find its mass, find its volume by displacement, and compare ρ = m/V with gold's 19.3 × 10³ kg/m³. No damage needed.

Mission 2: Why can you lie safely on a bed of nails, when one nail on its own would pierce your skin?

Make a guess: Why can you lie safely on a bed of nails, when one nail on its own would pierce your skin?

Mission 2

Pressure

  • Pressure P = F⊥/A: force per unit area, with F⊥ the force perpendicular to the surface.
  • Unit: pascal, 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  • A force at an angle: use only its perpendicular component, F cos θ (θ measured from the normal).
  • Same force, larger area → lower pressure. Same pressure, larger area → larger force.
Quick check
?A force of 50 N acts on a flat plate of area 25 cm². The force is at 60° to the plate's surface. What pressure does the force exert on the plate?
Quick check
?A sealed container holds a gas at a pressure of 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Which statement about this pressure is correct?
Mission 2

Where gas pressure comes from

  • Gas particles move randomly and keep colliding with the walls.
  • Each collision changes a particle's momentum: the wall exerts a force on it, and it exerts an equal and opposite force on the wall, perpendicular to the wall.
  • An enormous number of collisions every second add up to a steady force. Pressure = total perpendicular force ÷ area.
  • Pressure exists at every point in the gas, not just at the walls, and a surface placed anywhere feels it.
  • At a point, the push is the same on a surface facing any way. Pressure has no direction: it is a scalar. The force it produces has a direction.
Quick check
?The diagram shows three open containers of water, with the volume of water in each labeled. Containers 1 and 2 have bases of area A, and container 3 has a base of area A/2. Which correctly compares the gauge pressures P1, P2 and P3 at the bottoms of the containers?
Mission 2

Pressure in a liquid

  • A column of liquid, height h, base area A: weight = ρ(Ah)g.
  • Pressure from the column = weight ÷ A:
  • Pgauge = ρgh
  • Depends only on depth and density. Not on the shape or width of the container.
  • A liquid is (nearly) incompressible: ρ is the same at every depth, so the pressure due to the liquid is proportional to depth.
  • At depth h: absolute pressure P = P0 + ρgh, where P0 is the pressure at the surface (often atmospheric).
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A diver is 12 m below the surface of sea water. ρ = 1030 kg/m³; atmospheric pressure 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa.
  • Gauge pressure: ρgh = 1030 × 10 × 12 = 1.24 × 10⁵ Pa.
  • Absolute pressure: P = 1.01 × 10⁵ + 1.24 × 10⁵ = 2.25 × 10⁵ Pa: more than twice atmospheric.
Quick check
?The graph shows the absolute pressure P measured at several depths h below the surface of a liquid in a tank that is open to the atmosphere. Which claim is supported by the graph?
Mission 2

Absolute and gauge pressure

  • Gauge pressure: pressure above the reference (usually atmospheric). A tire gauge reads this.
  • Absolute pressure = reference pressure + gauge pressure.
  • Graph of absolute P against depth h: straight line, intercept P0, slope ρg.
  • Doubling the depth doubles the gauge pressure, but not the absolute pressure.
Mission check

?A fluid at pressure P pushes on a circular piston of radius r and exerts a force of magnitude F on it, perpendicular to the piston. The same fluid pressure acts on a second circular piston of radius 2r. The magnitude of the force on the second piston is how many times F?

Force = pressure × area. On a bed of nails your weight is spread over hundreds of points, so the pressure at each point is hundreds of times smaller than on one nail.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Make a guess: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Mission 3

Archimedes' principle

  • A fluid pushes up on an object in it: the buoyant force, Fb, the net upward force from the fluid.
  • Archimedes' principle: it equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
  • Fb = ρVg: ρ = density of the fluid; V = volume of fluid displaced (the submerged volume).
Mission 3

Why the fluid pushes up

  • Pressure rises with depth. The bottom face of a submerged block is deeper than the top face.
  • Upward force on the bottom > downward force on the top. Side forces cancel.
  • Block of height h, face area A: net upward force = ρghA = ρVg, the weight of fluid displaced.
  • Every one of these forces is the sum of countless particle collisions: the buoyant force is their collective effect.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a solid cube held at rest under water with its top face horizontal. The arrows show the forces exerted by the water on the cube's faces, and the cube's dimensions and the gauge pressures in the water at its top and bottom faces are labeled. What is the magnitude of the buoyant force exerted on the cube?
Quick check
?Two solid balls of equal volume, one made of lead and one of aluminum, hang from strings so that each is completely under water at the same depth. Lead is denser than aluminum. How does the buoyant force on the lead ball compare with the buoyant force on the aluminum ball?
Mission 3

Float or sink?

  • Fully submerged object, volume V: upward force ρfluid Vg; weight ρobject Vg.
  • ρobject > ρfluid: weight wins. It sinks.
  • ρobject < ρfluid: it rises until only part is submerged, then floats.
  • Floating: upward force = weight. It displaces its own weight of fluid.
  • Fraction submerged = ρobject / ρfluid.
  • A steel ship is mostly air inside: its average density is less than water's.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A 2.0 kg stone, volume 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³, hangs from a spring scale, fully under water (ρ = 1000 kg/m³).
  • Fb = ρVg = 1000 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 10 = 8.0 N. Weight = 2.0 × 10 = 20 N.
  • Scale reading = 20 − 8.0 = 12 N.
  • The stone feels lighter in water, but its weight has not changed.
Quick check
?A foam ball of volume V would float, but it is held at rest completely under water by a light string tied to the bottom of a tank. The density of the foam is a quarter of the density ρ of water. Which expression gives the tension in the string?
Mission 3

Newton's laws inside a fluid

  • Treat a small parcel of fluid as an object. Newton's laws apply to it.
  • Higher pressure behind than in front → net force forward → the parcel speeds up.
  • All the fluid in a container can be treated as one system. Forces between its own particles are internal and cancel in pairs.
  • Its center of mass accelerates only if the external forces (Earth, container, air) do not balance.
Quick check
?Water flows steadily in the +x direction along a horizontal pipe that is completely filled. Treat the water as an ideal fluid. The graph shows the pressure P in the water as a function of position x along the pipe. In which interval does a small parcel of the water slow down as it moves along the pipe?
Mission check

?A wooden block floats in water (density 1000 kg/m³) with 60% of its volume below the surface. It is then placed in oil of density 800 kg/m³. What fraction of its volume is below the surface of the oil?

Floating or sinking is set by density: the ship's steel hull encloses so much air that its average density is below water's, so it floats once it has displaced its own weight of water. A solid bolt cannot.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Water speeds up where a pipe narrows. Does its pressure there go up or down?

Make a guess: Water speeds up where a pipe narrows. Does its pressure there go up or down?

Quick check
?Honey poured from a jar flows much more slowly than water. A student models the honey as an ideal fluid. Which property of real honey does this model leave out?
Mission 4

The ideal fluid

  • Real fluids are messy. We use a model: the ideal fluid.
  • Incompressible: its density and volume stay constant, whatever the pressure.
  • No viscosity: no internal friction between layers sliding past each other.
  • Pipes are always completely filled unless a question says otherwise.
Mission 4

What makes a fluid flow

  • A pressure difference. The net push is from higher toward lower pressure, so the fluid speeds up in that direction.
  • Volume flow rate: V/t = Av (cross-sectional area × speed), in m³/s.
  • In time t, the fluid moves a distance vt, sweeping out a volume A(vt).
  • In a full pipe, fluid cannot pile up: the rate in = the rate out.
Quick check
?Water leaves the end of hose 1 with speed v. Hose 2 has half the inner diameter of hose 1, and water leaves its end with speed 2v. Both hoses are completely filled, and the water can be treated as an ideal fluid. The volume of water leaving hose 2 each second is what multiple of the volume leaving hose 1 each second?
Mission 4

The continuity equation

  • Same volume per second through every section of a full pipe: A1v1 = A2v2.
  • Narrower section → faster flow.
  • Example: a pipe narrows from 6.0 cm² to 2.0 cm². Water enters at 1.5 m/s.
  • v2 = A1v1/A2 = 6.0 × 1.5 ÷ 2.0 = 4.5 m/s.
  • Area of a circle ∝ diameter²: halve the diameter, and the speed is 4 times greater.
Quick check
?Water flows steadily to the right through the completely filled horizontal pipe shown, which has three sections with the cross-sectional areas labeled. Treat the water as an ideal fluid. Which of the following correctly ranks the water's speeds v1, v2 and v3 at points 1, 2 and 3?
Mission 4

Bernoulli's equation

  • Conservation of energy for a flowing fluid, per unit volume:
  • P1 + ρgy1 + ½ρv1² = P2 + ρgy2 + ½ρv2²
  • ½ρv²: kinetic energy per volume. ρgy: gravitational potential energy per volume (y = height).
  • Pressure difference does work on the fluid. That work becomes kinetic and potential energy.
  • Where the fluid is faster (same height), the pressure is lower.
  • Where the fluid is higher (same speed), the pressure is lower.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • The 6.0 cm² → 2.0 cm² pipe also rises 2.0 m. P1 = 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa; v1 = 1.5 m/s; water ρ = 1000 kg/m³.
  • Continuity: v2 = 4.5 m/s.
  • P2 = P1 − ρg(y2 − y1) − ½ρ(v2² − v1²)
  • P2 = 2.0 × 10⁵ − (1000 × 10 × 2.0) − ½ × 1000 × (4.5² − 1.5²)
  • P2 = 2.0 × 10⁵ − 2.0 × 10⁴ − 9.0 × 10³ = 1.7 × 10⁵ Pa.
Quick check
?Water flows steadily through a completely filled horizontal pipe. In a wide section, where the pressure is P1, the cross-sectional area is A1 and the water's speed is v. The pipe then narrows to a section of area A1/2, where the pressure is P2. Treat the water as an ideal fluid of density ρ. Which expression gives P1 − P2?
Mission 4

Torricelli's theorem

  • An open tank with a small hole Δy below the water surface.
  • Surface and jet are both at atmospheric pressure: the P terms cancel.
  • Wide tank: the surface falls very slowly, so v at the surface ≈ 0.
  • Bernoulli gives ρgΔy = ½ρv², so v = √(2gΔy).
  • Same speed as an object dropped from the surface. Example: Δy = 1.8 m → v = √(2 × 10 × 1.8) = 6.0 m/s.
Quick check
?The diagram shows water squirting from three small holes, A, B and C, in the side of a tall open bottle standing on the floor. The bottle is kept topped up so that the water level stays constant. Treat the water as an ideal fluid. From which hole does the water leave with the greatest speed?
Mission check

?Wind blows at 30 m/s across a flat roof of area 100 m². The air in the attic below is still, and the air far from the house is at the same pressure as the attic. Treat the air as an ideal fluid of density 1.2 kg/m³ and ignore height differences. What is the net force of the air on the roof?

Down. Continuity makes the water faster in the narrow section. Bernoulli: faster flow (and greater height) means lower pressure. The pressure difference is what speeds the water up.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ρ = m/V, in kg/m³ (1 g/cm³ = 1000 kg/m³); density belongs to the material, not the object.

Mission 2

  • P = F⊥/A, a scalar; P = P0 + ρgh (absolute) and Pgauge = ρgh.

Mission 3

  • Fb = ρVg: the weight of fluid displaced (ρ of the fluid, V displaced).
  • An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid's; a floating object displaces its own weight.

Mission 4

  • Ideal fluid: incompressible, no viscosity. Volume flow rate Av is the same everywhere in a full pipe: A1v1 = A2v2.
  • Bernoulli, P + ρgy + ½ρv² = constant along the flow, is energy conservation; Torricelli: v = √(2gΔy).
