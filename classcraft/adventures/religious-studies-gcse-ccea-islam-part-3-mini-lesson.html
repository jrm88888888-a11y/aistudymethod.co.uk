Mission 1

The days of Hajj

  • 8 Dhul-Hijjah: in ihram, pilgrims go to Mina, a valley of tents outside Makkah, and pray there.
  • 9th: Arafat: the standing, from midday to sunset. At night, Muzdalifah.
  • 10th: back to Mina: stoning the largest pillar, sacrifice, shaving or cutting the hair. Then to Makkah for tawaf (and sa'y). Muslims worldwide celebrate Id-ul-Adha.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three pillars each day.
  • Before leaving Makkah: a farewell tawaf around the Ka'bah.
Mission 1

Arafat: the heart of Hajj

  • On the 9th, pilgrims stand (wuquf) on the plain of Arafat from midday until sunset: praying, reading the Qur'an, and asking Allah for forgiveness.
  • The Prophet said, "Hajj is Arafat" (hadith, paraphrase): a pilgrim who misses the standing has not completed Hajj.
  • The Mount of Mercy (Jabal al-Rahmah) rises from the plain. Near it the Prophet gave his Farewell Sermon in 632 CE: all people equal, no Arab better than a non-Arab except by piety; lives and property sacred; women to be treated kindly (paraphrase).
  • Some traditions say Adam and Hawwa (Eve) were reunited and forgiven here.
  • Significance: a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: millions standing before Allah, in plain white, waiting for mercy.
Mission 1

Muzdalifah: a night under the sky

  • After sunset pilgrims move to Muzdalifah, between Arafat and Mina.
  • They pray Maghrib and Isha together and sleep on the ground in the open air, rich and poor alike.
  • "when ye press on in the multitude from 'Arafat, remember Allah by the sacred monument" (Qur'an 2:198, Pickthall)
  • They collect small pebbles for the stoning at Mina.
  • Significance: simplicity, equality and trust in Allah after the day of standing.
Mission 1

Mina: stoning, sacrifice, shaving

  • Stoning (rami): pilgrims throw seven pebbles at the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah) on the 10th, and at all three on the following days. Today the pillars are tall walls reached by a multi-storey bridge.
  • Muslim tradition says Shaytan tempted Ibrahim three times here to disobey Allah's command to sacrifice his son, and Ibrahim drove him away with stones.
  • Significance: pilgrims reject evil and temptation in their own lives.
  • Sacrifice: a sheep, goat or camel is sacrificed (today usually arranged with a voucher) and the meat goes to the poor. This recalls the ram Allah gave Ibrahim in place of his son.
  • Hair: men shave the head or cut it short; women cut a small lock. Most ihram rules now end.
  • Tawaf al-ifadah: a further tawaf (and sa'y) at the Ka'bah, then back to Mina for the remaining days.

Mission 1

Benefits: on Hajj and for life

  • Forgiveness: the Prophet said a pilgrim whose Hajj is accepted returns free of sin, like a newborn (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Closeness to Allah: days given entirely to prayer and remembrance.
  • Equality and ummah: people of every nation, colour and class in the same white cloth. Malcolm X (1925–1965) wrote after his Hajj in 1964 that he had eaten and prayed with pilgrims of every colour, and saw true brotherhood (paraphrase).
  • Patience and self-control: heat, crowds and no arguing (2:197).
Mission 1

Challenges

  • Cost: travel, accommodation and approved packages are expensive; many save for years.
  • Physical: summer heat in recent years, long walks, illness, crowds. Deadly crushes have happened (e.g. at Mina in 2015); Saudi authorities have rebuilt the Jamarat bridge and use crowd control.
  • Quotas: Saudi Arabia limits pilgrims from each country, so many wait years.
  • Spiritual: keeping patience and focus while tired, crowded and uncomfortable.
  • Commercialisation: luxury hotels overlooking the Ka'bah; some Muslims fear this undermines equality.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Hajj loses its meaning when it becomes a luxury holiday."

  • Reasons for: ihram and Muzdalifah teach equality and simplicity; luxury hotels and VIP tents divide rich from poor; Hajj should involve effort and patience; money could go to the poor.

Mission 2

Zakah: the third pillar

  • Zakah means "purification" and "growth". It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
  • CCEA spelling: Zakat.
  • It is a compulsory payment of 2.5% of a Muslim's savings and wealth, once a year, to set groups of people in need.
  • It is not charity in the sense of a favour: Muslims believe the poor have a right to it. "In their wealth the beggar and the outcast had due share." (Qur'an 51:19, Pickthall)
  • The Qur'an often joins it to prayer: "Establish worship, pay the poor-due" (Qur'an 2:43, Pickthall).
  • Purification: Muslims believe giving cleans the remaining wealth, and the giver's heart, of greed (9:103).
Mission 2

Wealth is a trust from Allah

  • Muslims believe everything belongs to Allah; humans hold wealth for a time, as trustees.
  • "Believe in Allah and His messenger, and spend of that whereof He hath made you trustees" (Qur'an 57:7, Pickthall)
  • The Qur'an warns those who hoard gold and silver and do not spend it in Allah's way (9:34–35).
  • So zakah is an act of worship (ibadah), not only a social policy.
Mission 2

Origins

  • Earlier prophets taught giving: Isma'il "enjoined upon his people worship and almsgiving" (Qur'an 19:55, Pickthall).
  • In Makkah the Qur'an urged Muslims to feed the needy and care for orphans.
  • In Madinah, after the Hijrah, zakah was made a fixed duty with set rates and set recipients (9:60), collected by people the Prophet appointed.
  • After the Prophet's death (632 CE), the first caliph Abu Bakr insisted that zakah must still be paid, treating refusal as rebellion against the community.
Mission 2

Who pays, and how much

  • Paid by adult Muslims whose wealth is above the nisab (minimum amount) for a whole lunar year.
  • Nisab: the value of about 85 g of gold or about 595 g of silver (figures vary slightly between scholars).
  • 2.5% of savings, cash, gold and silver, shares and business stock.
  • Different rates apply to crops (5% or 10%) and to farm animals.
  • Not counted: your home, car, clothes, furniture and tools for work.
  • Example: savings of £10,000 held all year above nisab → zakah of £250.
  • Many Muslims pay in Ramadan, when rewards are believed to be greater.

Who receives zakah

"The alms are only for the poor and the needy, and those who collect them, and those whose hearts are to be reconciled, and to free the captives and the debtors, and for the cause of Allah, and (for) the wayfarer; a duty imposed by Allah." (Qur'an 9:60, Pickthall)

Q1 How many groups can receive zakah according to 9:60?

Show answer

Eight: poor, needy, collectors, hearts to be reconciled, captives, debtors, the cause of Allah, travellers.

Q2 What does "a duty imposed by Allah" show about zakah?

Show answer

It is compulsory, set by Allah, not left to the giver's choice.

Mission 2

How zakah is distributed

  • Directly to people in need, through the mosque, or through Muslim charities (e.g. the National Zakat Foundation in the UK, Islamic Relief).
  • In some Muslim countries (e.g. Saudi Arabia, Malaysia, Pakistan) the state collects some or all zakah.
  • Cannot be given to your own parents, children or spouse: you already have a duty to support them.
  • What it is for: the eight groups in 9:60. Most scholars say zakah should not pay for building mosques or general projects; voluntary sadaqah can.
  • Most scholars say zakah goes to Muslims in need; sadaqah can go to anyone.
  • Givers usually keep it discreet, to protect the dignity of those who receive.
Mission 2

Khums: the Shi'a "fifth"

  • Khums means "one-fifth". Shi'a Muslims pay 20% of their surplus income: what is left after a year's living costs.
  • Basis: "whatever ye take as spoils of war, lo! a fifth thereof is for Allah, and for the messenger and for the kinsman (who hath need) and orphans and the needy and the wayfarer" (Qur'an 8:41, Pickthall). Shi'a scholars read "spoils" as all gains, not only war booty.
  • Split in two:
  • Share of the Imam (half): given to a senior scholar (marja') or his representative for religious education, mosques and the poor, while the Twelfth Imam is hidden.
  • Share of the sayyids (half): to needy descendants of the Prophet, who may not receive zakah.
  • Sunni Muslims apply 8:41 to war spoils (and, for many, buried treasure and minerals), so they do not pay khums on income.
  • Shi'a Muslims also pay zakah, but on a fixed list of goods (e.g. grain, livestock, gold and silver coins), so for most Shi'a in Britain khums is the main yearly payment.

Mission 2

Benefits of giving and receiving

  • For the giver: obeys Allah; purifies wealth and soul from greed; "whoso is saved from his own greed, such are the successful" (Qur'an 64:16, Pickthall); reward on the Day of Judgement; gratitude for what Allah gives.
  • For the receiver: needs met (food, debts, housing) as a right, not as begging, so with dignity; freed from debt; able to take part in community life.
  • For society: narrows the gap between rich and poor; reduces envy and crime; keeps money circulating; strengthens the ummah.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Zakah does more good for the giver than for the receiver."

  • Reasons for: the name means purification: it cleans the giver's wealth and heart (9:103); it is an act of worship that earns reward; a person can receive help from many sources, but only giving builds the giver's character.
  • Reasons against: 9:60 names the poor first: the purpose is to meet real needs; the poor have a right to it (51:19); for someone facing hunger or debt, the benefit is immediate and life-changing.
  • Another view: the benefits are shared: zakah binds giver and receiver into one ummah, so neither gains without the other.
  • Judge it by: What does the word "zakah" itself suggest? Which benefits can be measured? What does the Qur'an put first?

Mission 3

Sufism: the inner path

  • Sufism (Arabic tasawwuf) is the inner, mystical dimension of Islam: seeking to know and love Allah directly, with a purified heart.
  • It is not a separate branch like Sunni or Shi'a: most Sufis are Sunni; some are Shi'a.
  • Sufis keep the Shari'ah (the Five Pillars and Islamic law) and add inner disciplines.
  • Their goal is ihsan, described by the Prophet in the Hadith of Jibril: to worship Allah as if you see Him, and if you do not see Him, to know that He sees you (paraphrase).
  • Sufis often describe it as Shari'ah (the road) and tariqah (the path) leading to haqiqah (the truth: nearness to Allah).
Mission 3

Origins

  • Sufis trace their path to the Prophet: his nights of prayer, his retreats in the cave of Hira, his simple life.
  • 8th century: as the Muslim empire grew rich, devout people in Iraq turned away from luxury to prayer and self-denial, e.g. Hasan al-Basri (d. 728).
  • Name: probably from suf (wool): the rough woollen cloaks worn by early ascetics.
  • 8th–9th centuries: the stress moved from fear to love of Allah (Rabi'a) and inner knowledge (Junayd of Baghdad, d. 910).
Mission 3

Orders and saints

  • From the 12th century Sufis formed orders (tariqahs), each led by a sheikh with a chain of teachers (silsilah) going back to the Prophet.
  • Qadiri (from Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani, d. 1166): worldwide. Naqshbandi: silent dhikr; strong in Turkey, Central Asia and Britain. Chishti: South Asia, known for music. Mevlevi: Turkey, followers of Rumi.
  • Saints (awliya, "friends of Allah", Qur'an 10:62): people close to Allah; some are believed to receive blessing (barakah) and wonders.
  • Their tombs (shrines, e.g. Ajmer in India) are visited, especially on the death anniversary (urs).
  • Today: orders meet in mosques, homes and lodges, including in Britain; many teach charity and service.

Hearts at rest

"Those who believe, and whose hearts find satisfaction in the remembrance of Allah: for without doubt in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find satisfaction." (Qur'an 13:28, Yusuf Ali)

  • Remembrance = dhikr. Sufis take this verse as a central reason for their practice.

Q1 According to the verse, where do hearts find rest?

Show answer

In the remembrance of Allah.

Q2 Why might a Sufi repeat a name of Allah many times?

Show answer

To keep the heart in constant remembrance, so that it finds the rest the verse promises.

Mission 3

Dhikr: remembrance

  • Dhikr: repeating short phrases or names of Allah: "La ilaha illa'llah" (there is no god but Allah), "Allah", "SubhanAllah".
  • Silent dhikr in the heart (Naqshbandi) or aloud in a circle (hadra), often with rhythmic breathing and movement.
  • Prayer beads (misbaha or tasbih) help to count.
  • Purpose: to polish the heart (qalb) so it reflects Allah; to replace forgetfulness (ghaflah) with constant awareness.
  • History: "O ye who believe! Remember Allah with much remembrance." (Qur'an 33:41, Pickthall); orders developed set forms from the 12th century.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Sufism is a separate tradition from Sunni and Shi'a Islam."

  • For: Sufis have their own orders, leaders, rituals (dhikr circles) and shrines. Some Muslims criticise Sufi practices as innovations and treat Sufis as a separate group.
  • Against: most Sufis are Sunni (some Shi'a) and keep the same Five Pillars and Shari'ah. Sufism describes how a Muslim lives the faith (the inner life), not a different creed.
  • Another view: Sufism is a dimension found across Islam; the real diversity is between ways of practising it.
  • Judge it by: Do Sufis hold different core beliefs? How do Sufis describe themselves? What makes a group a "tradition"?

Mission 4

Akhirah: one life, then the hereafter

  • Akhirah means "the hereafter" or "the last": life after death. It is an article of faith.
  • Muslims believe each person has one life; there is no rebirth. "Every soul shall have a taste of death: And only on the Day of Judgment shall you be paid your full recompense." (3:185, Yusuf Ali)
  • This life is a test and a preparation (2:155; 67:2): what a person believes and does now shapes eternity.
  • So the purpose of life is to worship Allah and live as He asks.
Mission 4

Death and barzakh

  • Muslims believe that, at the time Allah has set, the Angel of Death (Izra'il) takes the soul (32:11).
  • The soul enters barzakh, "barrier": the state between death and the Day of Resurrection. "Before them is a Partition till the Day they are raised up." (23:100, Yusuf Ali)
  • There is no going back: in 23:100 a wrongdoer begs to return and do good, and is refused.
  • Hadith teach that two angels, Munkar and Nakir, question the dead in the grave: Who is your Lord? What is your religion? Who is your prophet?
  • The grave becomes a foretaste: wide and peaceful for the faithful, narrow and painful for wrongdoers (hadith, paraphrase).
Mission 4

The Day of Judgement

  • Muslims believe Yawm al-Qiyamah (Day of Resurrection) begins when Israfil sounds the trumpet (39:68).
  • All the dead are raised, body and soul, and gathered before Allah.
  • Each person receives their book of deeds: in the right hand for the saved (69:19), the left for the lost (69:25).
  • Deeds are weighed on the scales (mizan): "We shall set up scales of justice … not a soul will be dealt with unjustly in the least" (21:47, Yusuf Ali).
  • Many Muslims believe all must cross a bridge, as-Sirat, over hell to reach Paradise (hadith).
  • Muslims believe the Prophet will intercede (shafa'ah) for believers, by Allah's permission; Shi'a Muslims believe the Imams will also intercede.

Mission 4

Responsible and accountable

  • Responsibility: humans have free will and know right from wrong, through the Qur'an and the prophets.
  • Accountability: every deed counts. "Anyone who has done an atom's weight of good, see it! And anyone who has done an atom's weight of evil, shall see it." (99:7–8, Yusuf Ali)
  • Each person answers for themselves: "no bearer of burdens can bear the burden of another" (6:164).
  • Allah is generous: a good deed is rewarded "ten times" (6:160), and no one should despair of His mercy (39:53).
  • A hadith says no one enters Paradise by deeds alone, but by Allah's mercy (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
Mission 4

Al-Jannah, Paradise

  • Jannah ("garden"): the reward for faith and good deeds.
  • Described as gardens "under which rivers flow", with "beautiful mansions" (9:72); rivers of water, milk and honey (47:15).
  • Yet "no person knows what delights of the eye are kept hidden (in reserve) for them" (32:17, Yusuf Ali).
  • The greatest joy is not physical: "the greatest bliss is the good pleasure of Allah" (9:72, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 4

Jahannam, hell

  • Muslims believe Jahannam is a place of fire and suffering, and of separation from Allah's mercy.
  • "To it are seven gates: for each of those gates is a (special) class (of sinners) assigned." (15:44, Yusuf Ali)
  • Many scholars teach that believers who sinned may be purified in hell and then leave, by Allah's mercy and the Prophet's intercession; those who knowingly rejected Allah remain.
  • Only Allah knows who will be where: Muslims are taught not to declare others destined for hell.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Belief in akhirah is the most important influence on how a Muslim lives."

  • For: every deed is recorded and weighed (99:7–8), so akhirah gives each choice eternal weight. It motivates prayer, charity, honesty and patience in suffering. The Qur'an returns to it constantly.
  • Against: Tawhid and love of Allah come first: many Muslims say they obey Allah because He is worthy of worship, not for reward. The Prophet's example (Sunnah) and the ummah shape daily life directly. Acting only from fear or hope of reward can be seen as a lesser motive.
  • Another view: the beliefs work together: Tawhid gives the reason, the Sunnah the pattern, akhirah the urgency.
  • Judge it by: What do Muslims themselves say motivates them? Is acting for reward a weaker motive? Which belief would change practice most if removed?

Mission 5

Masjid: a place of prostration

  • Mosque comes from Arabic masjid: "place of prostration" (sujud, bowing the forehead to the ground in salah).
  • Muslims may pray anywhere clean: the Prophet said the whole earth has been made a place of prayer for him (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • So a mosque is not needed for every prayer. It is where the community prays together.
  • The first mosques: Quba' near Madinah, built when the Prophet arrived in 622 CE; then the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah: a simple courtyard, walls of mud brick, a roof of palm leaves on palm trunks.
  • The mosque is a centre of worship and the centre of the ummah (the worldwide Muslim community) in each place.
  • A large mosque used for Friday prayer is a jami' masjid ("gathering mosque").
Mission 5

Outside: minaret and dome

  • Minaret: the tower from which the adhan (call to prayer) was traditionally called.
  • Dome: a symbol of the vault of heaven, of Allah's creation over all; it also carries the voice of the imam round the hall.
  • Neither is required. Mosques take the shape of their culture:
  • e.g. pagoda-style roofs at the Great Mosque of Xi'an (China); mud-brick walls at Djenné (Mali); terraced houses and former churches, chapels and shops in Britain.
  • What every mosque needs: a clean prayer space, a way to show the direction of Makkah, and somewhere for wudu (washing).
Mission 5

Inside: everything faces Makkah

  • Qiblah: the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah. The wall facing it is the qiblah wall.
  • Mihrab: a niche in the qiblah wall. It shows the direction and marks where the imam leads prayer.
  • Minbar: stepped platform beside the mihrab, from which the imam gives the khutbah (sermon) at Friday prayer. The Prophet preached from a minbar of three steps.
  • Musalla (prayer hall): an open carpeted space, no seats or images; lines on the carpet keep worshippers in straight rows shoulder to shoulder.
  • Wudu area (ablution area): taps, seats and drains for washing hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head and feet before salah.
  • Shoes are left at the entrance: the prayer space is kept clean.

Mission 5

Men's and women's spaces

  • Most mosques have separate areas for men and women: a balcony, a side room, or a screen.
  • Reasons given: modesty; concentration during bowing and prostration in close rows; it follows the order of rows in the Prophet's mosque, where men prayed in front and women behind.
  • Men are expected to attend Jumu'ah (Friday prayer); women may attend but are not required to (see Mission 6).
  • Different practice: in some mosques women pray behind the men in the same hall; at the Ka'bah men and women pray in the same space.
  • Some British Muslims, women and men, criticise mosques with small or no women's areas and campaign for better facilities.
Mission 5Read the source

Ibrahim and the idols

Ibrahim (Abraham) asks his father and his people: "What are these images unto which ye pay devotion?" (Qur'an 21:52, Pickthall)

"Then he reduced them to fragments, all save the chief of them, that haply they might have recourse to it." (Qur'an 21:58, Pickthall)

  • The people say they found their fathers worshipping the idols (21:53); Ibrahim replies that their Lord is the Creator of the heavens and the earth (21:56).
  • When questioned, he points them to the largest idol, which cannot speak (21:59–63, paraphrase).

Q1 What does Ibrahim reject in this passage?

Show answer

Worshipping images made by humans instead of the Creator.

Q2 How might this story explain the absence of pictures in a mosque?

Show answer

Images could become objects of worship, which is shirk; so the prayer space has none.

Mission 5

Calligraphy, pattern, no images

  • Mosques contain no pictures of people or animals: nothing that could be worshipped or distract from Allah. Hadith also warn against making images of living beings (paraphrase).
  • Shirk (associating anything with Allah) is the gravest sin; Ibrahim's breaking of the idols (21:58) is its model rejection.
  • Instead: calligraphy (beautiful writing) of Qur'an verses, the name Allah, the name Muhammad, and in Shi'a mosques often Ali, Hasan and Husayn.
  • Styles include angular Kufic and flowing Naskh and Thuluth.
  • Geometric patterns and arabesques (plant-like scrolls) repeat without end: many Muslims see them as pointing to Allah's infinity and the order of creation.
  • Purpose: to express belief (Tawhid), honour Allah's words, aid remembrance, and beautify worship without images.
Mission 5

Objects used in worship

  • Copies of the Qur'an: kept on high shelves and read on a stand (rehal or kursi); many make wudu before touching the Arabic text.
  • Prayer mat: a clean space for each person's salah; the arch woven into many mats is pointed towards Makkah.
  • Compass (qiblah compass, now often an app): finds the direction of Makkah when praying away from a mosque.
  • Turbah: a small tablet of clay, often from Karbala, used by most Shi'a Muslims: they hold that the forehead should touch earth or something natural that is not eaten or worn.
  • Misbahah (prayer beads; also tasbih): 33 or 99 beads to count dhikr, e.g. SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu Akbar after salah.

Mission 5

What happens in a mosque

  • Salah five times a day; Jumu'ah (Friday midday prayer) with the khutbah from the minbar; Eid prayers.
  • Madrasah: classes, often after school, where children learn to read the Qur'an in Arabic and learn about Islam.
  • Life events: marriages (nikah), funeral prayers (janazah).
  • Ramadan: shared iftar (meal to break the fast) and night prayers.
  • Community: food banks, advice, youth groups, libraries, and open days for visitors of other faiths and none.
  • Leaders: the imam leads prayer, preaches and teaches; the mu'azzin gives the adhan (both taught fully in a later lesson).
Mission 5Diversity

How important is the mosque?

  • Very important: a well-known hadith says prayer in congregation is worth twenty-seven times prayer alone (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). Jumu'ah must be prayed together.
  • Many say home matters as much: salah is valid anywhere clean; for women, many Sunni scholars teach that prayer at home is as rewarded as in the mosque.
  • Different communities, different uses: in Britain many mosques serve one ethnic or language group; others are deliberately mixed. Some Shi'a communities also use an imambargah or husayniyya for gatherings (e.g. Muharram).
  • For many young British Muslims, the mosque's role as community and youth centre matters as much as prayer.
Mission 5Evaluate

"A mosque is essential for a Muslim's worship."

  • Reasons for: Jumu'ah must be prayed in congregation; congregational prayer brings greater reward (hadith); the mosque teaches the Qur'an, unites the ummah and supports family life.
  • Reasons against: "the earth has been made a mosque": salah is valid anywhere clean; many Muslims pray most prayers at home, work or school; women are not required to attend Jumu'ah.
  • Another view: the mosque is essential for the community, while each person's worship depends on intention and remembrance wherever they are.
  • Judge it by: Which kinds of worship need a group? Does "essential" mean required by the Shari'ah or very important in practice? Does the answer differ for men and women, or for Muslims far from any mosque?

Mission 6

Jumu'ah: Friday prayer

  • Jumu'ah (AQA, OCR, EDXA, EDXB, EDQ: Jummah; CIE: Jumʿah; EDXI: Jum'a): the Friday congregational prayer, held in place of Zuhr.
  • Form: a sermon (khutbah), then two rak'ahs led by the imam.
  • Who must attend (Sunni teaching): adult Muslim men who are healthy and not travelling. Women, children, travellers and the sick need not attend, but may; if they do, it replaces their Zuhr.
  • Preparation: a full wash (ghusl), clean clothes, arriving early.
  • Shi'a practice: during the Imam's occultation, many Twelver scholars teach that Jumu'ah may be prayed instead of Zuhr, not that it is required; large Friday prayers are held in Iran and Iraq, and in some British Shi'a centres.

"And when the prayer is ended, then disperse in the land and seek of Allah's bounty, and remember Allah much, that ye may be successful." (Qur'an 62:10, Pickthall)

  • Friday is not a day of rest like a Sabbath: work stops for the prayer, then continues.

Mission 6

The khutbah

  • Khutbah (OCR: khutba): a sermon in two parts, given by the imam from the minbar (pulpit); he sits briefly between them.
  • Contains praise of Allah, blessings on the Prophet, verses of the Qur'an and a du'a for the ummah.
  • Language: traditionally Arabic; in Britain usually partly in English or another community language, so all can understand.
  • Topics: faith, character, family life, current events, how to live as Muslims in society.
  • Worshippers sit in silence and listen: the khutbah is part of the worship.
Mission 6

Praying together

  • Rows: shoulder to shoulder, feet together, facing the qiblah behind one imam.
  • Equality: rich and poor, of every race and nation, stand side by side: the ummah made visible.
  • Reward: the Prophet taught that prayer in congregation is twenty-seven times better than prayer alone (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Mosque practice: men usually pray in front; women behind or in their own area or balcony; some mosques have no women's space, which many Muslims criticise.
  • Many Muslims pray all five prayers in the mosque; others go mainly for Jumu'ah, Ramadan and Eid.

Mission 6

Prayer at home and elsewhere

  • The Prophet taught that the whole earth has been made a place of prayer: any clean place will do (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Home: many Muslims pray most daily prayers at home, often on a prayer mat in a quiet room; families may pray together, with a parent leading.
  • The Prophet urged believers to pray some prayers (especially voluntary ones) at home, so that homes are not like graves without worship (hadith, paraphrase).
  • The home as a place of worship: children learn the movements and words by copying; the Qur'an is recited; du'a is part of daily life.
  • Elsewhere: prayer rooms at school, work, airports, hospitals; outdoors on a coat or mat. Travellers may shorten four-rak'ah prayers to two.
  • Women: prayer at home is fully valid; many women also attend mosques.
Mission 6

Du'a: calling on Allah

  • Du'a ("call"): personal prayer or supplication: asking Allah for help, forgiveness and guidance, or thanking Him.
  • Any time, any place, any language, in a person's own words or in prayers from the Qur'an and the Prophet; often with hands raised, palms up.
  • Preparation (CIE): being clean, facing the qiblah, praising Allah and blessing the Prophet first, being sincere and patient.
  • Special times: after salah, the last part of the night, while fasting, on Friday.
  • Content: e.g. "Our Lord! Give unto us in the world that which is good and in the Hereafter that which is good" (Qur'an 2:201, Pickthall).

Mission 6

Dhikr: remembering Allah

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating praises of Allah, aloud or silently.
  • After salah: "SubhanAllah" (glory be to Allah) 33 times, "Alhamdulillah" (praise be to Allah) 33 times, "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is greatest) 33 or 34 times, often counted on prayer beads (misbaha or tasbih).
  • Shi'a Muslims especially know this as the Tasbih of Fatimah, which the Prophet taught his daughter, and begin with Allahu Akbar (34).
  • Significance: "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Qur'an 13:28, Pickthall); it keeps Allah in mind between prayers.
  • Sufis make dhikr a central practice (see Mission 3).

Mission 6Diversity

How important is prayer? Different views

  • All Muslims agree salah is a duty; many teach it is the first deed judged on the Day of Judgement.
  • Abandoning prayer: some scholars (e.g. many Hanbalis) teach that deliberately abandoning salah takes a person out of Islam; most teach it is a very grave sin, but not unbelief.
  • Inner and outer: some stress correct performance; others, especially Sufis, stress khushu' (humble attention): a prayer without the heart is empty.
  • Mosque and home: some Muslims see the mosque as the heart of the community and attend every prayer; others pray mostly at home and go to the mosque on Fridays.
  • In Britain: fixed times can clash with school or work; many Muslims combine or make up prayers, or use prayer rooms; some feel this weakens worship, others that it shows faith can fit any life.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Praying in the mosque is more important than praying at home."

  • For: congregational prayer is "twenty-seven times" better (hadith). Jumu'ah must be prayed in congregation. The khutbah teaches and guides. The ummah is strengthened when Muslims pray side by side.
  • Against: the whole earth is a place of prayer, and Allah is "close" to every caller (2:186). The Prophet encouraged prayer at home. Sincerity matters more than place. For many women, carers and people far from a mosque, home is where they can pray.
  • Another view: they do different jobs: the mosque builds the community; the home builds personal faith and passes it to children.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for? Does "more important" mean more reward, more duty or more effect on a person's life? Does it differ for men and women?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • 8–13 Dhul-Hijjah: Mina → Arafat (standing until sunset; Mount of Mercy) → Muzdalifah (night, pebbles) → Mina (stoning, sacrifice, hair) → tawaf.
  • Benefits: forgiveness, equality, ummah, a changed life; challenges: cost, crowds, heat, quotas, commercialisation.

Mission 2

  • Zakah (Zakat) is a pillar: 2.5% of savings above the nisab held for a year, given to the eight groups of 9:60. Muslims believe wealth is a trust from Allah.
  • Shi'a Muslims also pay khums: one-fifth of surplus income (8:41), half to the share of the Imam, half to needy sayyids.
  • Benefits: the giver is purified; the receiver is helped as a right, with dignity; the ummah is strengthened.

Mission 3

  • Sufism (tasawwuf) is the inner dimension of Islam, practised by Sunni and some Shi'a Muslims alongside the Shari'ah; its aim is ihsan.
  • Orders (tariqahs) led by sheikhs practise dhikr (Qur'an 13:28); saints (awliya) are honoured at shrines.

Mission 4

  • In Muslim belief, Akhirah means one life, then death, barzakh, resurrection and judgement; this life is a test and a preparation.
  • Muslims believe every deed is recorded and weighed; Allah is just and merciful; Jannah rewards, Jahannam brings justice.

Mission 5

  • A mosque (masjid, "place of prostration") is the centre of communal worship and of the local ummah; its key features face Makkah: qiblah wall, mihrab, minbar, musalla, wudu area.
  • No images of living beings; calligraphy and geometric patterns express Tawhid and reject idolatry (Ibrahim breaking the idols, Qur'an 21:58).
  • Objects: Qur'an on a stand, prayer mat, compass, turbah (Shi'a), misbahah.

Mission 6

  • Jumu'ah (Friday prayer) replaces Zuhr: a two-part khutbah then two rak'ahs in congregation (62:9–10); in Sunni teaching it is required of adult men.
  • Salah may be prayed at home or anywhere clean; congregational prayer brings unity and greater reward.
  • Du'a is personal prayer in any words or language (2:186); dhikr is remembrance of Allah, especially after salah.
