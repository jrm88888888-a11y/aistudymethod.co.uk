Mission 1

Mission 1: Stretched out, the DNA in one of your cells would be taller than you. How does it fit inside the nucleus?

Mission 1

Where cells keep their DNA

  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus: their DNA lies in the cytoplasm.
  • Prokaryotes typically have one circular chromosome.
  • Eukaryotes keep most of their DNA in the nucleus, as several linear chromosomes.
  • Each chromosome holds one very long DNA molecule, associated with histone proteins.
  • Say: "the circular DNA is the prokaryote's chromosome" — not: "prokaryotes have no chromosomes".
Quick check
?The diagram represents a prokaryotic cell and a eukaryotic cell, with some of the structures inside them labeled. Which statement correctly describes structure X?
Mission 1

Packing it in

  • Stretched out, the DNA in one human body cell is about 2 m long. The nucleus is about 6 µm across.
  • (1) DNA winds around histones.
  • (2) The wound DNA coils, and coils again.
  • (3) Before nuclear division, each chromosome condenses: short, thick, visible, and movable without tangling.
  • Between divisions, chromosomes are less condensed, so DNA can be copied and its genes used.
  • Packing changes how tightly DNA is folded. It never changes the base sequence.
  • Say: "DNA is wound around histones" — not: "histones wrap around the DNA".
Quick check
?A line of eukaryotic cells grown in a laboratory carries a mutation that greatly reduces the amount of histone protein the cells make. Which prediction about the DNA in the nuclei of these cells is best supported?
Mission check

?Each chromatid of human chromosome 1 contains one DNA molecule about 85 mm long. When the cell divides, the condensed chromosome is about 10 µm long (illustrative round value). Which statement is correct?

One molecule, thousands of times longer than the chromosome it forms. Histones, then coil upon coil, make it fit.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Make a guess: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Mission 2

One gene, a new host

  • Recombinant DNA: DNA joined from two sources, e.g. a human gene inside a bacterial plasmid.
  • An organism carrying a gene from another species is transgenic: a genetically modified organism (GMO).
  • The genetic code is universal. Transcription and translation work the same way in all cells.
  • So the host reads the human gene and makes the same polypeptide.
  • Plasmids: small circles of DNA outside the chromosomes, in prokaryotes and in some eukaryotes (e.g. yeast). They make good carriers.
Mission 2

Into a vector, into a host

  • Transformation: host cells take up the plasmid.
    • Bacterial transformation introduces foreign DNA into bacterial cells.
  • Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid.
Mission 2

Marker genes: which cells took it up?

  • The plasmid also carries a marker gene.
  • Antibiotic resistance marker: spread the cells on agar with the antibiotic. Only cells with the plasmid grow.
  • In the transformation lab, plates with and without the plasmid and the antibiotic are compared.

Mission 2

Grow them: one cell becomes a factory

  • Transformed cells divide. Every new cell copies the plasmid and the gene.
  • Gene cloning: growing transformed cells to propagate a DNA fragment.
  • Human insulin: DNA coding for human insulin (no introns) put into E. coli; grown in large fermenters; insulin extracted and purified. Approved for use in 1982.
Mission check

?Bacteria were transformed with a plasmid carrying a version of a human gene that contains no introns. The transformed bacteria made a polypeptide with the same amino acid sequence as the human polypeptide. Which explanation best relates this result to a larger biological concept?

Give a cell the gene, not the protein. A shared code does the rest.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 3

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Replication ensures the continuity of hereditary information from cell to cell.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
Quick check
?Before a eukaryotic cell divides by mitosis, all of its DNA is replicated. Which statement best explains how this replication maintains the continuity of hereditary information from the parent cell to its two daughter cells?
Mission 3

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Topoisomerase relaxes the supercoiling (overwinding) that builds up ahead of the replication fork.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug binds to the helicase of a hypothetical species of bacterium and stops it from working; the other replication enzymes are not affected. Which prediction about DNA replication in cells treated with the drug, and the reason for it, is correct?
Mission 3

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?In a cell of a hypothetical species of animal, DNA replication has produced a chromosome made of two sister chromatids. Which statement correctly describes the DNA of the two sister chromatids?
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Make a guess: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Mission 4

Three possible models

  • Conservative: the parent molecule stays whole; the copy is all new.
  • Semi-conservative: each new molecule has one parent strand + one new strand.
  • Dispersive: old and new DNA are mixed in pieces along every strand.
  • In 1958, Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment that gave each model a different prediction.
Mission 4

The experiment

  • Bases contain nitrogen. ¹⁵N is a heavy isotope; ¹⁴N is the normal, light one.
  • E. coli were grown for many generations on ¹⁵N: all their DNA became heavy.
  • They were moved to a ¹⁴N medium. Every new strand made after that is light.
  • Samples were taken after each generation (one round of replication).
  • DNA was extracted and spun in a cesium chloride density gradient.
  • DNA settles where its density matches the solution: heavier DNA settles lower.
Mission 4

Read the data

Generations in ¹⁴NBands seen
0one band: heavy
1one band: intermediate
2two bands, equal amounts: intermediate and light
  • Intermediate (hybrid) DNA = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand.
  • Light DNA = two ¹⁴N strands.
  • Read each row as a test. Which models survive it?
Mission 4

Which model fails, and when

  • Conservative predicts generation 1: two bands, heavy and light. Seen: one intermediate band. Refuted at generation 1.
  • Dispersive predicts generation 1: one intermediate band. That fits.
  • But dispersive predicts generation 2: still one band, lighter than intermediate. Seen: two bands. Refuted at generation 2.
  • Semi-conservative predicts both results. It is supported.
  • Say: "the results support the semi-conservative model" — not: "the results prove it".
Mission 4

Worked example: after n generations

Start: one ¹⁵N molecule. Grow it in ¹⁴N.

  • Molecules after n generations = 2ⁿ.
  • The two original ¹⁵N strands never break up. Each ends up in one hybrid molecule.
  • So there are always exactly 2 hybrid molecules; all the rest are light.

Mission 5

Mission 5: DNA can only be built in one direction, yet its two strands run opposite ways. How are both copied at once?

Make a guess: DNA can only be built in one direction, yet its two strands run opposite ways. How are both copied at once?

Mission 5

Every strand has two different ends

  • The carbons of each sugar are numbered 1′ to 5′.
  • The phosphate is on the 5′ carbon; an –OH is on the 3′ carbon.
  • In the backbone, each phosphate links the 3′ carbon of one sugar to the 5′ carbon of the next.
  • So a strand has a 5′ end (free phosphate) and a 3′ end (free –OH).
  • The two strands of a double helix are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions.

Mission 5

DNA polymerase adds only at the 3′ end

  • DNA polymerase links the 5′ phosphate of an incoming nucleotide to the free 3′ –OH at the end of the strand.
  • So a new strand grows only at its 3′ end: synthesis is 5′ to 3′.
  • The template is read 3′ to 5′ (the strands are antiparallel).
  • DNA polymerase cannot start a strand on a bare template. It needs a short RNA primer with a free 3′ end.
  • Say: "added at the 3′ end, so the strand grows 5′ to 3′" — not: "added at the 5′ end".

Mission 5

Leading and lagging strands

  • Helicase opens the fork in one direction. Both new strands must still grow 5′ to 3′.
  • Leading strand: its template runs 3′ to 5′ toward the fork. It is made continuously, following the fork.
  • Lagging strand: it must grow away from the fork. It is made discontinuously, in short fragments.
  • The leading strand needs an RNA primer once; the lagging strand needs one for every fragment.
  • Both strands are made at the same fork, at the same time.
  • Say: "both strands are made 5′ to 3′; the lagging strand is made in fragments" — not: "the lagging strand is made 3′ to 5′".
Quick check
?The diagram shows a replication fork moving to the right. The 5′ and 3′ ends of the two parental strands are labeled, and X and Y mark the template arms on which the two new strands, strand X and strand Y, are being made. Which statement correctly describes how strand Y is made?
Fork moving → (right). Unreplicated helix on the right: top strand ends 5′, bottom strand ends 3′.
Separated arms on the left: top arm (X) ends 3′; bottom arm (Y) ends 5′.
Mission 5

Finishing the lagging strand

  • A short RNA primer starts every new strand and every lagging-strand fragment.
  • DNA polymerase extends each primer 5′ to 3′.
  • Ligase joins the fragments on the lagging strand into one continuous strand.
  • Ligase forms a covalent bond in the backbone; base pairing already holds the strands together.
Quick check
?A test tube contains a DNA molecule, all the enzymes needed to replicate it, and both DNA and RNA nucleotides. A compound is added that prevents RNA nucleotides from being joined together; it has no direct effect on DNA polymerase. Which prediction about the synthesis of new DNA strands in the tube is correct?
Mission check

?A hypothetical species of bacterium has a mutation that makes its DNA ligase inactive at 40 °C but not at 30 °C. Cells are moved from 30 °C to 40 °C just before a round of DNA replication begins. Which prediction about the new DNA made at 40 °C is correct?

One direction, two antiparallel strands: one strand follows the fork smoothly, the other is built backward in pieces and stitched together.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Make a guess: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Mission 6

From gene to ribosome

  • DNA holds the genetic information. In eukaryotic cells it stays in the nucleus.
  • mRNA carries a copy of one gene's information to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Translation: the base sequence of mRNA is used to build the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
  • The mRNA is read in codons: three bases = one amino acid (or a stop signal).
  • Amino acids are not made from the bases. They already float in the cytoplasm.
  • Say: "translation makes a polypeptide on a ribosome" — not: "the ribosome converts bases into amino acids".
Mission 6

Two RNAs, two shapes

  • mRNA: a single, linear strand; hundreds to thousands of nucleotides; a run of codons. No folded clover-leaf.
  • tRNA: a single strand of about 80 nucleotides, folded into a clover-leaf shape.
  • The fold is held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in the same strand.
  • One loop carries the anticodon: three bases.
  • The 3′ end is the amino acid attachment site. Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid.
  • The base sequence decides how an RNA folds; the fold decides its function.
Mission 6

The ribosome

  • Made of rRNA and protein, in two subunits: small and large.
  • The small subunit binds the mRNA.
  • The large subunit holds two tRNAs at the same time.
  • rRNA gives the ribosome its shape and catalyzes peptide bond formation.
Quick check
?A cell contains many different kinds of tRNA molecule. What is the role of a tRNA during translation?
Mission 6

Codon meets anticodon

  • The anticodon is complementary to the codon: A–U, U–A, G–C, C–G.
  • RNA has uracil, not thymine. So codon AUG pairs with anticodon UAC.
  • Codon and anticodon are held by hydrogen bonds: weak, so the tRNA can leave later.
  • The ribosome checks only this pairing. It never checks the amino acid.
  • Say: "complementary to the codon" — not: "the same as the codon".
Mission 6

Elongation, one codon at a time

  • (1) Two tRNAs sit on the ribosome, each paired with its codon.
  • (2) A peptide bond forms between their amino acids, by condensation. The chain now hangs on the second tRNA.
  • (3) The ribosome moves one codon along the mRNA. The empty tRNA leaves.
  • (4) The next tRNA pairs with the newly exposed codon. Repeat.
  • The peptide bond forms by dehydration synthesis, adding the new amino acid to the growing chain.
Quick check
?An antibiotic stops the ribosome from moving along the mRNA but does not affect the binding of tRNAs or the formation of peptide bonds. What effect will this have on translation of an mRNA?
Mission 6

Start, stop and the energy bill

  • Start codon AUG: translation begins here. AUG also codes for methionine, the first amino acid.
  • Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA): no tRNA pairs with them. The finished polypeptide is released.
  • Recall only AUG; any other codon you need is given in a code chart.
  • Many ribosomes can translate one mRNA at once, so many copies of the polypeptide are made quickly.
Mission 6

Worked example: decoding a gene

  • mRNA (5′→3′): AUG UUC GGA AAG UGA.
  • Step 2. Read from AUG in threes. Use the table: AUG Met · UUC Phe · GGA Gly · AAG Lys · UGA stop.
  • Step 3. Polypeptide: Met–Phe–Gly–Lys. Four amino acids; the stop codon adds none.
  • Step 4. Anticodons used (each written 3′→5′): UAC, AAG, CCU, UUC. None for UGA.
Quick check
?The coding region of an mRNA, from the first nucleotide of the start codon to the last nucleotide of the stop codon, is 903 nucleotides long. How many amino acids does the polypeptide translated from this region contain?
Mission check

?In a test tube translation system, the tRNAs with the anticodon 3′-ACG-5′ normally carry the amino acid cysteine, and they are the only tRNAs in the system that pair with the codon 5′-UGC-3′. Before translation, the cysteine attached to these tRNAs is chemically changed into a different amino acid, alanine; the tRNAs themselves are unchanged. An mRNA containing the codon 5′-UGC-3′ is then translated. Which prediction about the polypeptide made is correct?

The ribosome reads codons, never amino acids. The tRNA is the adapter that links the two, so whatever it carries goes in.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Prokaryotes typically have a circular chromosome; eukaryotes have multiple linear chromosomes, condensed using histones.

Mission 2

  • A gene from one species is expressed in another because the genetic code, transcription and translation are universal.
  • Bacterial transformation puts foreign DNA, often on a plasmid, into bacteria; growing them clones the DNA.

Mission 3

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.
  • Topoisomerase relaxes supercoiling ahead of the fork.

Mission 4

  • Meselson and Stahl (1958): heavy (¹⁵N) DNA, then growth on ¹⁴N; one intermediate band after 1 generation, intermediate + light after 2.
  • Generation 1 refutes conservative; generation 2 refutes dispersive; semi-conservative predicts both.

Mission 5

  • DNA polymerase adds nucleotides only to a 3′ end, so every strand grows 5′ to 3′ and needs an RNA primer to start.
  • Leading strand: continuous, one primer. Lagging strand: fragments, one primer each, joined by ligase.

Mission 6

  • mRNA codons pair with complementary tRNA anticodons on the ribosome; each tRNA brings its amino acid, and peptide bonds join them one codon at a time from AUG to a stop codon.
