Mission 1

Systems: open and closed

  • A system has inputs, outputs, stores and flows (transfers) between stores.
  • Closed system: energy crosses the boundary, but matter does not. The global hydrological cycle is closed: Earth's water is neither gained nor lost.
  • Open system: energy and matter cross the boundary. A drainage basin is open: water enters as precipitation and leaves as river flow and evapotranspiration.
Mission 1

Where Earth's water is stored

  • Oceans hold 96.54% of all Earth's water. Ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow hold 1.74%; groundwater 1.69%, just under half of it fresh.
  • The atmosphere holds 0.001%, rivers 0.0002% and living things (biological water) 0.0001%.
  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh. Of that fresh water, 68.7% is ice and snow and 30.1% is groundwater.
  • Fresh lakes hold 0.26% of fresh water and soil moisture 0.05%.
Mission 1

Global fluxes and the energy that drives them

  • Solar energy drives evaporation and transpiration. Gravitational potential energy brings water back down as precipitation and moves it downhill to the sea.
  • Annual fluxes (thousand km³ a year): precipitation on land 112.6; evapotranspiration from land 72.6; runoff from land to ocean 40.0; precipitation on the ocean 372.8.
  • Land receives 40.0 more than it evaporates. The same 40.0 returns as runoff, so ocean evaporation must be about 413 (372.8 + 40.0, calculated, assuming the ocean store stays steady).
  • The difference is carried from ocean to land as water vapour in the atmosphere.
Mission 1

Residence time

  • Residence time: the average time water stays in a store.
  • Atmosphere 9–10 days; rivers 12–20 days; soil moisture about 280 days.
  • Lakes 1–100 years; groundwater about 300 years; ice caps, glaciers and permanent snow more than 1,000 years.
  • Residence time ≈ store ÷ flux through it. Small, fast stores (rivers, atmosphere) are refilled from big, slow ones.

Mission 1

Why water matters for life

  • Humans: worldwide, water withdrawals are 69% for agriculture, 19% for industry and 12% for municipal use, such as homes.
  • Flora: plants take up water through roots and lose it by transpiration; without it they cannot photosynthesise.
  • Fauna: animals need water to drink, and rivers, lakes and wetlands are habitats.
  • Water also links to the carbon cycle: photosynthesis needs it.
Mission 1

Condensation and the causes of precipitation

  • Evaporation: liquid water becomes vapour, using solar energy. Transpiration: water vapour lost from plant leaves.
  • Condensation: air rises and cools until it reaches its dew point; vapour condenses on tiny particles (condensation nuclei) to form clouds.
  • Air is forced to rise in three ways: convectional (surface heating), orographic or relief (air forced over mountains) and frontal (warm air rising over cold air).
  • Droplets or ice crystals grow until they are heavy enough to fall as precipitation.
Mission 1

Basin inputs and outputs

  • Input: precipitation. Its type (rain or snow), intensity and duration decide what happens next.
  • Snowmelt (ablation) releases water stored as snow into the basin, often weeks or months after it fell.
  • Outputs: evaporation and transpiration (together evapotranspiration), and channel flow (runoff) leaving at the mouth.
Mission 1

Stores

  • Interception: water held on leaves and stems.
  • Surface storage: puddles, ponds and lakes. Soil water: water held in the soil.
  • Groundwater: water in permeable rock (aquifers). Channel storage: water in the river itself.
  • Stores delay water on its way to the river.
Mission 1

Flows

  • Throughfall (drips through the canopy) and stemflow (runs down stems and trunks) carry intercepted water to the ground.
  • Infiltration: water enters the soil. Percolation: it moves down into the rock.
  • Overland flow: infiltration-excess flow when rain is more intense than the soil can absorb; saturated overland flow when the soil is already full.
  • Throughflow: downslope through the soil. Groundwater flow: slowly through rock to the channel (base flow). Channel flow: in the river.

Mission 1

Ablation and cryospheric processes

  • Accumulation: snow builds up a store of ice and snow.
  • Ablation: loss from that store by melting, sublimation (ice straight to vapour) and evaporation.
  • Frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface: meltwater and rain run off instead of infiltrating.
  • So in cold basins, water is stored for months and released in a meltwater surge in spring and summer.
Mission 1

The water balance

  • P = Q + E ± ΔS: precipitation = runoff + evapotranspiration ± change in storage.
  • Over many years storage changes little, so E ≈ P − Q.
  • River Lambourn (234.1 km², on chalk): mean catchment rainfall 768 mm a year, 1962–2019.
  • Its mean flow, 1.743 m³/s, equals 235 mm of runoff a year spread over the catchment (our calculation).

Mission 1

Groundwater feeds rivers

  • In a USGS study of 54 US streams, groundwater supplied on average 52% of streamflow, from 14% to 90%.
  • The Lambourn's chalk aquifer keeps it flowing: its flow is at least 0.77 m³/s for 95% of the time. For the Beult, a clay river in Kent, it is only 0.057 m³/s.
  • So how a basin stores water decides how its river behaves in dry spells.
Mission 1

Ice feeds rivers

  • In the upper Indus basin, glacier melt supplied 40.6% of total runoff (1998–2007); in the upper Ganges, 11.5%.
  • Meltwater arrives in the warm season, often when rain is scarce.
  • The river is the smallest, fastest store. Groundwater and ice are the big, slow stores that refill it.
Mission check

?After a dry month with almost no rain, the River Lambourn is still flowing. Which explanation is best?

The river is a tiny, fast store. It keeps flowing because big, slow stores drain into it: groundwater as base flow (on average 52% of flow in 54 US streams) and meltwater from ice and snow (40.6% of runoff in the upper Indus).

Mission 2

Carbon: the building block of life

  • "Carbon is the chemical backbone of life on Earth": every sugar, protein and cell is built on carbon chains.
  • Natural world: plants fix carbon into sugars; animals eat it and breathe it out; shells and skeletons store it as calcium carbonate.
  • Humans: fossil fuels "provide energy that fuels our global economy"; limestone is used to make concrete.
  • Carbon is never made or destroyed here: it moves between stores by fluxes.
Mission 2

Where the carbon is

  • Unit: 1 petagram (Pg) = 1 gigatonne (Gt) = 10¹⁵ g.
  • Atmosphere: about 589 PgC before industry; 828 PgC in 2011.
  • Vegetation: 450–650 PgC; litter and soils: 1,500–2,400 PgC; permafrost: about 1,700 PgC.
  • Ocean: dissolved inorganic carbon about 38,000 PgC; marine life only about 3 PgC. The ocean holds "about 50 times more carbon than the atmosphere".
  • Rocks: "Most of Earth's carbon" is stored in rocks.
  • OCR's list: atmosphere, oceans, cryosphere, soil, vegetation.
Quick check
?In 2025 atmospheric CO₂ was projected to average 425.7 ppm. One ppm of CO₂ holds 2.124 GtC. About how much carbon was in the atmosphere?
Mission 2

Photosynthesis and respiration

  • Photosynthesis: plants "move carbon from the atmosphere into the biosphere". Gross primary production is about 123 ± 8 PgC a year.
  • Respiration (plants, animals, microbes) returns much of it to the air.
  • The fast cycle moves 1,000 to 100,000 million tonnes of carbon a year.
  • Evidence: CO₂ at Mauna Loa falls each northern summer and rises each winter, "driven by Northern Hemisphere summer vegetation growth … and winter decay".
Mission 2

Decomposition and soil carbon

  • Dead leaves, roots and animals become litter and soil organic matter.
  • Decomposition by microbes releases carbon "back into the atmosphere or soil".
  • Cold or waterlogged ground slows decomposition, so carbon builds up as peat and in permafrost.
  • Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
Mission 2

Combustion

  • Combustion oxidises carbon to CO₂ quickly.
  • Natural: wildfires burn vegetation and peat.
  • Human: burning fossil fuels; projected at 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025.
  • Fossil burning takes carbon from a slow store and adds it to the fast cycle within hours.
Mission 2

Sequestration in the oceans and sediments

  • Solubility pump: CO₂ "is more than twice as soluble in cold polar waters than in warm equatorial waters"; cold, dense water sinks and carries it to depth.
  • Biological pump: plankton fix carbon near the surface; "perhaps 30%" of it sinks into deeper waters, and "only about 0.1% reaches the seafloor".
  • Calcifying plankton build calcium carbonate shells; these sink, but "below about 4 km" virtually none accumulates on the seafloor.
  • What reaches the seafloor can be buried in sediments and, over millions of years, become rock.
Quick check
?Why does much ocean uptake of CO₂ happen in cold high-latitude seas?
Mission 2

The slow cycle: weathering and outgassing

  • CO₂ dissolves in rain to form carbonic acid, which dissolves rock (chemical weathering) and releases calcium and other ions.
  • Rivers carry the ions to the ocean, where calcium combines with bicarbonate to form calcium carbonate: shells, then limestone.
  • Volcanic outgassing returns rock carbon to the air: volcanoes emit 130–380 million tonnes of CO₂ a year.
  • Scale: the slow cycle moves 10–100 million tonnes of carbon a year; rock weathering about 0.3 and volcanism about 0.1 PgC a year.
  • Timescale: carbon "takes between 100-200 million years" to move round the slow cycle.

Mission check

?A limestone hill could return its carbon to the air by two routes: (1) natural, as the rock is eventually carried down and heated beneath a volcano; (2) human, as it is quarried and heated to make cement. Which statement is best supported?

Rock carbon returns to the air mainly by volcanic outgassing, a slow-cycle flux of only about 0.1 PgC a year, balanced over millions of years by chemical weathering and carbonate formation. Human extraction and burning move rock carbon far faster.

Mission 3

The setting: Amazonia

  • The Amazon forest stores 150–200 Pg C (billion tonnes of carbon), "equivalent to 15–20 years of global CO₂ emissions".
  • It "contributes up to 50% of rainfall in the region".
  • It has lost 13% of its original forest area to deforestation (15% counting young secondary forest).
  • Our drainage basin: the Upper Xingu, Mato Grosso, on the southern deforestation frontier.
Mission 3

The water cycle: a forest that waters itself

  • Rain is intercepted by the canopy; some evaporates straight back.
  • Roots draw soil water; leaves transpire it.
  • Trees "recycle around 36% of precipitation through transpiration".
  • Locally, "up to 50% of the forest's precipitation is forest-generated from within the basin".
  • Late dry-season transpiration helps decide when the wet season starts.
Mission 3

The carbon cycle: store, sink, source

  • Stores: biomass (trunks, roots, leaves) and soil organic matter.
  • Flows: photosynthesis in; respiration, decomposition and fire out.
  • Monitoring of 321 plots: Amazon forests have been a long-term net biomass sink, but net biomass gain fell by one-third in the 2000s compared with the 1990s, as tree deaths rose.
  • Aircraft measurements, 2010–2018: "Southeastern Amazonia, in particular, acts as a net carbon source".
Mission 3

From one tree to the whole forest

  • Tall emergent canopy trees contribute "around 71% of transpired water", recycling about 26% of precipitation.
  • Water transpired upwind falls as rain downwind, then is transpired again: a chain across the basin.
  • So one large tree's loss is local; losing many breaks the chain far away.
  • Deforestation "has already reduced moisture recycling in the Amazon, particularly in the south".
Mission 3

Physical factors: rock, soil, relief, temperature

  • Forest structure varies on an east–west gradient "coincident with variations in soil fertility and geology".
  • West: sediment from the rising Andes; rivers there carry more nutrients, and soils near the Andes are relatively fertile, where steep relief erodes soil to expose fresh parent rock.
  • Centre and east: old Brazilian and Guiana shields carry highly weathered Ferralsols.
  • Soil phosphorus best predicted wood production; soil physical properties drove tree turnover.
  • Temperature: the dry season has warmed by about 0.27 °C per decade since the early 1980s.
Quick check
?Why can deforestation in the eastern Amazon reduce rainfall far to the west and south?
Mission 3

Why the forest is cleared: food, fuel, minerals

  • Food: soy area in the Brazilian Amazon grew more than 10-fold, from 0.4 to 4.6 Mha, since 2000; most expansion was onto pasture first cleared for cattle, so soy "may drive deforestation indirectly by displacing pasture".
  • Minerals: mining caused 11,670 km² of deforestation in 2005–2015, 9% of Amazon forest loss.
  • Fuel and energy: "Mineral and hydrocarbon extraction and infrastructure are increasingly significant drivers of forest loss" in Amazonia.
  • Trends contrast: Brazil's 2025–26 Amazon deforestation estimate, 3,335 km², is the lowest since records began in 1988.
Mission 3

The Upper Xingu basin: water flows change

  • Researchers gauged small forest and soy headwater streams (2.5–13.5 km²) for a year.
  • Soy watersheds: daily water yield about four times higher, with greater seasonal swings.
  • Why: crops transpire far less than forest, so more rain leaves as runoff and baseflow.
  • Stormflow stayed under 13% of annual flow and did not differ by land use.
  • By 2010, over 40% of catchments outside protected areas were dominated by farming, with about 10,000 farm reservoirs.
Mission 3

Carbon, soil and nutrients after clearance

  • Cutting and burning turns biomass carbon into CO₂ and smoke; the land becomes a carbon source.
  • Gatti et al.: a longer, harsher dry season plus more deforestation "seem to promote ecosystem stress, increase in fire occurrence, and higher carbon emissions".
  • Streams in pasture and soy were warmer: daily maxima over 4 °C (pasture) and 3 °C (soy) above forest streams.
  • On old, weathered soils, the living forest is a key nutrient store: clearing and burning remove it, and exposed soil can be eroded.
Quick check

Mission 3

Drought: a store under threat

  • 2005: drought reversed the forest's carbon sink; biomass carbon impact 1.2–1.6 Pg C.
  • 2010: 57% of Amazonia had low dry-season rainfall, against 37% in 2005; predicted impact 2.2 Pg C, largely from trees killed by drought.
  • 2023: Manaus port water level fell to 12.70 m, its lowest since 1902.
  • Cause in 2023: a shift from La Niña to El Niño plus warm oceans changed atmospheric circulation, cutting moisture flow into Amazonia.
  • By 2050, models project 10–30 more consecutive dry days and annual maximum temperatures 2–4 °C higher.
Quick check

Mission 3

Managing the forest: restoration in the Xingu

  • 2004: the Y Ikatu Xingu campaign, prompted by Indigenous peoples downstream, aimed to restore 300,000 ha of cleared riverside and spring land.
  • Direct seeding (muvuca): a mix of about 70 native species sown by machine.
  • Cost: about US$1,600 per ha, against about US$2,400 for planting nursery seedlings.
  • By the paper's count, 2,564 ha were under restoration: "far from" the 300,000 ha goal.
  • Restoring riverside buffers could cut predicted stream warming "by as much as fivefold"; restoration may recover moisture recycling faster than it recovers biodiversity.
Mission 3

Managing farming: intensify and protect

  • Improved farming: raising cattle per hectare could spare forest, but modelling found the land spared "far less than proportional to the productivity increased".
  • Supply chains: the Soy Moratorium (traders refuse soy from newly cleared land) prevented about 18,000 ± 9,000 km² of deforestation in 2006–2016.
  • Protection: protected areas held 54% of remaining Brazilian Amazon forest in 2010.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): weigh each strategy's effect on both cycles, its scale against the 13% already lost, and how long it lasts.
Mission check

?A government in another rainforest region plans to clear 30–50% of a large river basin for farms. It says: "Less forest means more water in the rivers for our hydropower dams." Which response is best supported?

Clearing lowers transpiration, so nearby streams run fuller. But the forest makes much of its own rain; at basin scale, recycled moisture falls, droughts bite harder and the carbon store becomes a source.

Mission 4

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Alaska's Arctic coastal plain, north of the Brooks Range.
  • Permafrost underlies about 85% of Alaska.
  • Utqiaġvik (1991–2020): annual mean −10.2 °C; 137 mm of precipitation (our conversions).
  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay in 1968; the Trans-Alaska Pipeline (TAPS) was completed in 1977.
Mission 4

Water stores

  • Snowpack: Utqiaġvik averages about 46 inches of snowfall a year (sum of monthly normals); it is held on the ground for most of the year.
  • Ground ice: in places permafrost is up to 80% ice. A large, long-term store.
  • Active layer: thaws each summer and holds soil water above the permafrost, which "acts like a waterproof barrier".
  • Lakes, ponds and wetlands: water trapped on flat ground above impermeable permafrost.
  • Atmosphere: very cold air holds little water vapour: a small store.
Mission 4

Water flows and seasons

  • Kuparuk River near Deadhorse, monthly mean discharge, 2015–2023 (USGS):
  • January–April: almost zero. The river is frozen and water is stored as snow and ice.
  • May–June: the snowmelt flood. These two months carry 54% of the sum of monthly mean flows (our calculation).
  • June averages about 268 m³/s (9,460 ft³/s) (our calculation).
  • July–September: lower flows fed by summer rain and thaw of the active layer.
  • Infiltration is blocked by permafrost; evaporation is low in a cool summer. So runoff is fast and seasonal.

Mission 4

Carbon stores

  • Permafrost soils: Arctic and boreal permafrost hold 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
  • NOAA: at least 1.4–1.6 trillion tonnes of carbon have accumulated in the region's soils and permafrost.
  • Why so much? Plants grow in summer; when they die, cold and frozen ground stop decay: "Once frozen, the decay of organic matter stops".
  • Vegetation: low mosses, sedges, lichens and shrubs: a small store compared with the soil.
Mission 4

Carbon flows and seasons

  • Summer (short growing season): photosynthesis takes CO₂ in. Warmer recent summers brought more uptake.
  • Non-growing season: microbes keep respiring in unfrozen soil; this CO₂ has "offset growing season uptake".
  • Result: the tundra region has shifted from a CO₂ sink "to a small CO₂ source".
  • Methane: the permafrost region releases 15–39 Tg CH₄-C a year (2000–20).
  • Rates: decay is slow when cold and fast when warm; most carbon flows happen in the few thawed months.
Mission 4

Physical factors

  • Temperature: controls freezing, thaw depth, snowmelt timing and decay rates.
  • Rock permeability and porosity: permafrost turns even porous sediment into an impermeable layer, so water stays near the surface.
  • Relief: a flat, low coastal plain drains slowly, so lakes and wetlands form.
  • Vegetation: low biomass gives low photosynthesis; shrubs are spreading.
  • Organic matter in soil: a huge frozen store that can decay if it thaws.
  • Mineral composition of rocks: chemical weathering of rock minerals draws down CO₂ slowly, and is slower still in the cold.
Quick check

Mission 4

Oil and gas: impacts on the cycles

  • Prudhoe Bay: by 2010, over 34% of the intensively mapped area was affected by oil development.
  • Water: roads and gravel pads block drainage, causing roadside flooding; ponded water and road dust warm the ground.
  • Thermokarst spread across 19% of the remaining natural landscape, with a sudden increase soon after 1990; road dust and roadside flooding contribute.
  • Carbon: thaw exposes stored organic matter to decay; warm oil would thaw a buried pipe's surroundings.
  • The oil itself moves carbon from a geological store to the atmosphere when burned: TAPS has carried more than 18 billion barrels.
Mission 4

Strategies to moderate impacts

  • Raise the pipe: TAPS is above ground for 420 of its 800 miles.
  • Keep the ground frozen: tubes of anhydrous ammonia in the supports carry heat out of the ground; refrigerated brine cools some buried sections.
  • Ice roads: built on frozen marshes and used only in winter; they melt away in spring.
  • Raise buildings; paint roads white to reflect heat.
  • Smaller footprint: in 2023 the US government approved the Willow project with three of five proposed drill sites, cutting about 11 miles of roads, 20 miles of pipelines and 133 acres of gravel; about 68,000 acres of leases were relinquished.
  • Limit: these protect permafrost locally, but not the carbon released when the oil is burned.
Quick check

Mission check

?At a Siberian tundra site, measured summer CO₂ uptake has risen each decade, yet the site's yearly carbon balance has turned into a net source. Which finding would best explain this?

Cold has kept the tundra's huge soil carbon store locked up. Warming lengthens the unfrozen season, so microbes release more CO₂ (and methane where soils are waterlogged) than the extra plant growth takes in. Oil and gas infrastructure adds local thaw.

Mission 5

Dynamic equilibrium

  • A store stays the same size when inputs = outputs. Flows can be large; the store is still stable.
  • Dynamic equilibrium: the balance is kept by constant flows, and returns after small disturbances.
  • Example: through the ice-age cycles of the last million years, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm"; before industry it was "280 ppm or less".
  • Humans disturb the balance by changing land, extracting water and burning fossil carbon.
Mission 5

Land-use change: urban growth

  • In forested catchments much rainfall "is absorbed into soils", stored as groundwater and released slowly to streams.
  • Urbanisation replaces vegetation with impervious surfaces: water "runs off into storm sewers" instead.
  • Effects: less infiltration and groundwater recharge; more, faster surface runoff; severe floods "happen more often".
  • Carbon: vegetation and soil stores are removed or sealed.
Mission 5

Land-use change: farming and forestry

  • Deforestation (2015–2024): about 4 billion tonnes of CO₂ a year; regrowth and afforestation offset "about half".
  • Drained peat for farming: about 12% of peatlands drained and degraded, giving 4% of annual human-caused emissions.
  • Afforestation builds the biomass and soil stores and increases interception and transpiration.
  • Every land-use change alters both cycles at once.

Mission 5

Extraction: rivers and aquifers

  • Surface extraction: from the 1960s the Aral Sea's rivers were diverted to irrigate cotton; the eastern lobe of the South Aral Sea had "completely disappeared" by 2014. The Indus loses about 129 km³ a year to abstraction.
  • Aquifers: the US High Plains aquifer is mostly unconfined.
  • Irrigated land: 2.1 million acres (1949) → 15.0 million acres (2012).
  • Predevelopment (about 1950) to 2015: water levels fell 15.8 feet on average; recoverable storage fell 273.2 million acre-feet.
Mission 5

Artesian basins

  • An artesian basin is a confined aquifer: water held under pressure between impermeable layers, so a bore can flow at the surface without pumping.
  • Great Artesian Basin, Australia: almost 1.7 million km², over a fifth of the continent; storage capacity 64,900 million ML.
  • "Uncontrolled water flow from bores and bore drains reduces water pressure and volume". Springs fed by the basin are affected too.
  • Capping and piping bores (the GAB Sustainability Initiative) "saves over 250 GL per year".

Mission 5

Fossil fuel combustion

  • Burning coal, oil and gas moves carbon from the slow (rock) store to the atmosphere.
  • 2025: 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ from fossil fuels (≈ 10.4 GtC, calculated).
  • 1750–2019: fossil fuels and land-use change together released about 700 ± 75 PgC.
  • Atmospheric CO₂ 2025: 425.7 ppm, 52% above pre-industrial. The fossil store shrinks; the atmospheric store grows.
Mission 5

Carbon sequestration

  • Natural sinks, 2015–2024, per year: ocean 3.2 GtC, land 2.4 GtC; the atmosphere still gained 5.6 GtC.
  • Carbon capture and storage (CCS): at Sleipner (Norway) CO₂ has been removed from produced gas since 1996.
  • By end 2020, more than 19 million tonnes of CO₂ were stored in the Utsira formation under the North Sea.
  • Sequestration builds stores (biomass, soils, rock) and slows the atmospheric rise.
Quick check

Mission 5

Using stores as resources

  • Fossil stores: built over millions of years, drawn down since about 1750.
  • Groundwater: High Plains recoverable storage down about 8.6% since predevelopment (our calculation). Artesian pressure falls where bores run free.
  • Terrestrial stores: forests and peat drawn down for land and fuel.
  • Using a store faster than it refills reduces its availability for the future.
Mission check

?A semi-arid region pumps a confined aquifer for irrigation and clears woodland for new fields. Which set of changes is correct?

Dynamic equilibrium holds when inputs match outputs. Urban growth, farming, forestry, extraction and fossil fuel use change flows faster than stores can refill, so stores shrink (aquifers, fossil carbon, forests, peat) or grow (atmosphere, new forests), and their availability as resources changes.

Mission 6: Since 1979 the Arctic has warmed nearly four times as fast as the planet as a whole. Why there?

Make a guess: Since 1979 the Arctic has warmed nearly four times as fast as the planet as a whole. Why there?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.2-3.a positive and negative feedback loops within and between the water and carbon cycles
  • 1.2-4.a how the cycles link and are interdependent via oceans, atmosphere, cryosphere and vegetation
  • 1.2-4.a impact of long-term climate change on the water and carbon cycles
Mission 6

Stores that keep Earth habitable

  • To emit the energy it receives, a surface with no greenhouse effect would sit at about −19 °C; Earth's actual mean is about 14 °C.
  • Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas; CO₂ is second.
  • So the water store in the air and the carbon store in the air together regulate climate and keep liquid water, and life, possible.
  • Plants link both cycles: photosynthesis takes in CO₂ and water; plants release water vapour by transpiration.
Mission 6

Four links between the cycles

  • Atmosphere: CO₂ warms the air; warmer air can hold more vapour, about 7% more per 1 °C of warming.
  • Ocean: took up 23% of human CO₂ in 2010–2019 and more than 90% of the excess heat since 1970.
  • Cryosphere: Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 billion tonnes of organic carbon, almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere.
  • Vegetation: land plants stored 31% of human CO₂ in 2010–2019.
Mission 6

Feedback loops

  • A feedback loop: a change triggers effects that alter the original change.
  • Positive feedback amplifies it; negative feedback dampens it.
  • Negative example: a warmer surface emits more longwave radiation to space.
  • Water vapour feedback: warmer air holds more vapour, a greenhouse gas. IPCC: "This is a positive feedback".
  • Global water vapour in the air has very likely increased since the 1980s.
Mission 6

Albedo

  • Albedo: the fraction of sunlight a surface reflects (0 = none, 1 = all).
  • Thick sea ice covered with snow: up to about 0.9.
  • Bare sea ice: about 0.5–0.7.
  • Open ocean: about 0.06.
Mission 6

The ice–albedo feedback

  • Warming melts snow and sea ice → darker ocean and land exposed → more sunlight absorbed → more warming and melting.
  • Melt ponds lower albedo further, speeding the melt.
  • Evidence: the Arctic sea-ice minimum on 12 September 2026 was 4.60 million km² (preliminary), tied 10th-lowest on record.
  • The minimum is shrinking by 11.8% per decade against the 1981–2010 average.
Quick check
?The 2026 minimum (4.60 million km²) was 1.62 million km² below the 1981–2010 average minimum. By about what percentage was it below the average?
Mission 6

Arctic amplification

  • Over 1979–2021 the Arctic warmed "nearly four times faster than the globe" (several observational datasets).
  • Earlier studies said two to three times; models rarely reproduce four-fold.
  • On land too: Arctic June snow cover shrank 13.4 ± 5.4% per decade (1967–2018).
  • IPCC: the causes of polar amplification "are well understood". Albedo is one; others include the vertical temperature profile and clouds.
Mission 6

Permafrost and methane

  • Permafrost temperatures have risen to record highs since the 1980s.
  • Thaw lets microbes break down stored carbon, releasing CO₂ and methane: a positive feedback between the cryosphere and the carbon cycle.
  • IPCC: natural methane sources will increase with warming, including wetlands and permafrost thaw.
  • Methane reached 1,942 ppb in 2024, 166% above pre-industrial.
Quick check
?Which of these is a negative feedback?
Mission 6

Between the cycles: sinks and high-carbon ecosystems

  • Dampening: since the 1980s, CO₂ fertilisation has strengthened the land carbon sink (medium confidence).
  • Weakening: with higher emissions, land and ocean take up a smaller proportion of each year's CO₂ (high confidence).
  • In high-carbon ecosystems IPCC observes more wildfires, tree deaths, drying peatlands and permafrost thaw, weakening sinks and releasing greenhouse gases (medium confidence).
  • Water drives carbon here: drought and fire turn a carbon store into a carbon source.
Mission 6

A wetter Arctic water cycle

  • Warmer air can hold more moisture (about 7% per 1 °C), so more evaporation can stay in the air as vapour.
  • Human influence likely contributed to more high-latitude precipitation in the Northern Hemisphere; more is projected.
  • Projected for the Arctic: more precipitation, evapotranspiration and river discharge to the Arctic Ocean; less snow and permafrost may still dry soils.
  • Stores change: earlier spring snowmelt; abrupt thaw projected to raise small-lake area by over 50% by 2100 (high emissions).
Mission check

?A high mountain plateau with seasonal snow cover and permafrost is warming faster than the lowlands around it. Which pair of processes would both amplify its warming, linked through the same store?

The same two positive feedbacks drive the Arctic: snow-covered ice reflects up to about 90% of sunlight, open ocean only about 6%, so retreating ice and snow darken the surface (ice–albedo feedback), while thawing permafrost adds carbon. Working with others, they make the Arctic warm fastest.

Mission 7

Mission 7: The air in a forest changes between day and night. How do scientists spot the slow change hidden underneath?

Make a guess: The air in a forest changes between day and night. How do scientists spot the slow change hidden underneath?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.2-3.b short-term (diurnal, seasonal) changes in the cycles and their significance
  • 1.2-3.b long-term (millions of years) changes in water and carbon stores and flows
  • 1.2-3.b research and monitoring techniques for recording change in global cycles; why data are gathered
Mission 7

Change on three time scales

  • Diurnal: over 24 hours, driven by sunlight and temperature.
  • Seasonal: over a year, driven by sunlight, temperature, rainfall, snow and foliage.
  • Long-term: over millions of years, driven by plate tectonics, weathering, burial and ice sheets.
  • Short-term swings are natural "noise" around the long-term signal.
Mission 7

Diurnal change

  • Carbon, day: "photosynthesis is stronger than respiration, causing net removal of CO₂".
  • Carbon, night: "At sunset, when photosynthesis shuts down, the CO₂ mole fraction shoots up, especially at the lowest levels".
  • Water: sunshine and warmth raise evaporation and transpiration by day; cooler nights favour condensation and dew.
  • Significance: a single reading near vegetation says little about the global store.
Mission 7

Seasonal change: carbon and foliage

  • The yearly CO₂ cycle is "driven by Northern Hemisphere summer vegetation growth, which reduces atmospheric carbon dioxide, and winter decay".
  • The Northern Hemisphere has more land and so more foliage than the south.
  • Deciduous leaves fall in autumn: photosynthesis stops; decomposition continues.
  • Significance: the biosphere "breathes" each year, but the yearly cycle sits on a rising trend.
Mission 7

Seasonal change: water

  • Snowfields act as "frozen reservoirs" that "store precipitation from the cool season".
  • In spring and summer snowpacks melt, "releasing water into rivers".
  • "As much as 75 percent of water supplies in some western states are derived from snowmelt".
  • North Fork American River, California: minimum mean daily flow 1,200 ft³/s in March 2000 vs 55–75 ft³/s in August.

Mission 7

Long-term change: how the slow cycle shifts

  • The slow carbon cycle moves only 10–100 million tonnes of carbon a year.
  • Over millions of years, imbalance between volcanic outgassing and long-term sinks (weathering, burial) can change CO₂.
  • Burial of plant matter over millions of years built the fossil fuel stores.
  • Water: ice sheets grow and shrink, shifting water between the cryosphere and the ocean: sea level changes by tens of metres.

Mission 7

Monitoring carbon

  • Mauna Loa: CO₂ measured since March 1958, begun by C. David Keeling; site chosen for air "representative of much of the Northern Hemisphere", on bare lava with no vegetation or soil.
  • September 2026 monthly mean: 426.07 ppm.
  • Ice cores: bubbles hold "a sample of the atmosphere"; the EPICA Dome C core "extends back 800,000 years".
  • Satellites: OCO-2's spectrometers measure "absorption of reflected sunlight by carbon dioxide and molecular oxygen", with near-global coverage of the sunlit Earth every 16 days.
Mission 7

Monitoring water

  • GRACE (2002–2017) and GRACE-FO (from May 2018): twin satellites about 220 km apart sense changes in gravity, which track groundwater, soil moisture and ice sheets.
  • Argo floats: dive to 2 km and rise every 10 days, recording temperature and salinity; about 13,000 profiles a month (2024).
  • River gauges: the Lambourn at Shaw (Berkshire chalk) recorded flow from 1962 to 2019; its base flow index of 0.97 shows flow fed almost entirely by groundwater.
Mission 7

Why gather the data?

  • Separate signal from noise: NOAA removes "the average seasonal cycle" with a moving average of seven seasonal cycles to show the trend.
  • Test models of stores and flows against observations.
  • Inform policy: the Paris Agreement "works on a five-year cycle" of review.
  • Manage resources: aquifers, river flows and snowmelt supplies.
Quick check
?Which technique could detect a fall in groundwater across a whole aquifer region?
Mission check

?A student finds that monthly CO₂ at Mauna Loa was lower in early autumn than in the spring of the same year. She says: "The long-term rise in CO₂ has reversed." Which response is best?

Diurnal and seasonal changes are large but reversible swings driven by sunlight, temperature and foliage. Long-term changes over millions of years shift stores between rock, atmosphere, ocean and ice. Monitoring at background sites, from ice cores and from satellites, floats and gauges lets scientists separate the trend from natural variation.

Mission 8: Forests, peat bogs and farm soils all lock up carbon. Can the whole world agree to protect them?

Make a guess: Forests, peat bogs and farm soils all lock up carbon. Can the whole world agree to protect them?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.2-4.b global strategies to protect the carbon cycle: afforestation, wetland restoration, better farming, emissions cuts, carbon trading, international agreements
Mission 8

Why protect the carbon cycle globally?

  • 2025: 38.1 billion tonnes of fossil CO₂; permanent deforestation about 4 billion tonnes a year (2015–2024).
  • About 8% of the CO₂ rise since 1960 comes from climate change weakening land and ocean sinks.
  • The remaining budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂, about four years at 2025 emissions.
  • Carbon released anywhere mixes through the whole atmosphere, so protection needs global strategies.
Mission 8

Afforestation and reforestation

  • Afforestation: planting trees where there was no forest. Reforestation: replanting cleared land.
  • Bonn Challenge: launched by Germany and IUCN in 2011; goals 150 million ha by 2020 and 350 million ha by 2030.
  • 257 million ha have been pledged.
  • Limits: "competing demands on land" and "the risk of reversal" through fire or felling.
Mission 8

Protecting forests that already exist: REDD+

  • REDD+: "Reducing emissions from deforestation and forest degradation in developing countries", plus conservation, sustainable management and enhancement of forest carbon stocks.
  • Developing countries "can receive results-based payments" when they reduce deforestation.
  • Rulebook: the Warsaw Framework, adopted at COP19 in December 2013.
  • A test of 26 forest-protection offset projects found "most projects have not significantly reduced deforestation".
Quick check
?The Bonn Challenge goal for 2030 is 350 million ha; 257 million ha have been pledged. What share of the 2030 goal has been pledged?
Mission 8

Wetland and peatland restoration

  • Peatlands cover about 3–4% of land but hold "twice the amount of carbon as found in the world's forests".
  • About 12% are drained and degraded, adding about 4% of annual human-induced emissions.
  • Waterlogging keeps decomposition slow. Draining lets air in, and the peat oxidises to CO₂.
  • Rewetting (blocking drains, raising the water table) slows the loss. The IPCC lists "coastal wetlands, peatlands" among ecosystems to restore.
Mission 8

Better farming practices

  • 4 per 1000: launched by France on 1 December 2015 at COP21.
  • Soils hold "2 à 3 fois plus de carbone que l'atmosphère" (two to three times more than the atmosphere).
  • Raising carbon in the top 30–40 cm of soil by 0.4% a year would substantially cut the yearly rise in CO₂; the rate is a guide, not a binding target.
  • Practices named by the IPCC: "soil carbon management in croplands and grasslands, agroforestry and biochar".
Quick check
?Why does rewetting a drained peatland cut carbon emissions?
Mission 8

International agreements and emissions cuts

  • UNFCCC: agreed 1992, in force 1994.
  • Kyoto Protocol (1997): binding targets for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition, averaging a 5% cut on 1990 levels in 2008–2012.
  • Paris Agreement (2015): hold warming "well below 2°C" and pursue 1.5 °C; national plans on a five-year cycle.
  • UNEP (2025): current policies alone "would lead to up to 2.8°C of warming".
Mission 8

Carbon trading

  • Cap and trade: a cap on total emissions; firms get or buy allowances; those that cut cheaply sell spare allowances; the cap falls over time.
  • EU ETS: "the world's first carbon market", launched 2005; covered emissions down about 47% on 2005 by 2023; target 62% by 2030.
  • One study estimates the EU ETS saved about 1.2 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2008–2016 compared with no carbon market.
  • China's national ETS (2021) covers more than 60% of China's CO₂ but is intensity-based: it limits emissions per unit of output.
Quick check
?China's ETS limits emissions per unit of output, not total emissions. What follows?
Mission 8

Can global strategies work?

  • IPCC: by 2020 carbon taxes and trading covered over 20% of global emissions, "although coverage and prices have been insufficient to achieve deep reductions".
  • "International cooperation is a critical enabler", "although gaps remain".
  • Problems: free riders, voluntary pledges, checking that credits are real (additionality), and reversal (fire, drainage, felling).
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): judge each strategy on scale, permanence, cost and who must act, then reach a supported conclusion.
Mission check

?A government drains 10,000 ha of peat swamp for plantations, then plants 10,000 ha of new forest on farmland elsewhere and claims "no net loss of carbon". Which judgement is best supported?

Global strategies work best when they protect existing stores (peat, standing forest) as well as building new ones, and when agreements, payments and carbon markets are checked for additionality and permanence.

Mission 9: Since 1970 the world has lost about 22% of its wetlands. Can a plan for a whole river basin bring a wetland back?

Make a guess: Since 1970 the world has lost about 22% of its wetlands. Can a plan for a whole river basin bring a wetland back?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.2-4.b global strategies to protect the water cycle: improving forestry techniques, water allocation, drainage basin planning (runoff, surface stores, groundwater)
Mission 9

Planning a whole basin

  • Integrated drainage basin management (drainage basin planning) plans land and water use across a whole basin, not river by river or sector by sector.
  • It balances water for the environment, farms, towns, industry and culture, and involves all the stakeholders.
  • It is part of SDG target 6.5: integrated water resources management "at all levels, including through transboundary cooperation".
Mission 9

Case study: the Murray–Darling Basin, Australia

  • The basin covers about 1 million km² of south-eastern Australia and is home to 2.4 million people.
  • "Over two-thirds of Australia's irrigation occurs in the Murray–Darling Basin".
  • It spans parts of New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia and the Australian Capital Territory, each with its own water plans.
Mission 9

The Basin Plan: allocating water

  • The Basin Plan 2012, made under the Water Act 2007, "sets the amount of water that can be taken from the Basin each year": a sustainable diversion limit.
  • Governments agreed to recover 2,750 GL a year for the environment (about a 20% cut in water for consumptive use) and an additional 450 GL a year.
  • This is water allocation: a cap on what domestic, industrial and farm users may take, with a share kept for the environment.
Mission 9

Outcomes so far

  • Recovered water has "improved river flows, connectivity, and ecosystem and biodiversity outcomes".
  • But the Plan "will not be fully implemented within the original timeframe or budget" (Productivity Commission, 2024).
  • Only 26 GL a year of the extra 450 GL had been recovered, and less than half of a 605 GL supply-measure offset.
  • 10 of 20 New South Wales water resource plans, due in 2019, were still not in place.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, what percentage of the extra 450 GL a year had been recovered?
Mission 9

Who bears the costs?

  • Water purchases from irrigators should be gradual "to avoid driving water market disruption and community adjustment pressures": farming towns bear costs.
  • Governments need to improve how they "partner and share decision making with First Nations people".
  • So environmental gains depend on who gives up water, and how they are helped.
Mission 9

Global strategies to protect the water cycle

  • Improving forestry techniques: keep or plant forest on headwater slopes, leave buffer strips along streams, and avoid clear-felling steep slopes. Forests can reduce flooding from "frequent, low-intensity, short-duration storms", but "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Water allocation: caps and licences that share water between domestic, industrial and agricultural users and the environment (as in the Basin Plan).
  • Drainage basin planning: manage runoff (sustainable drainage, keeping floodplains free of building), surface stores (protect lakes and wetlands) and groundwater (limit abstraction to recharge).
Mission 9

Wetlands: surface stores under pressure

  • Since 1970, about 411 million ha of wetlands, roughly 22% of the global total, have been lost; losses continue at 0.52% a year.
  • About 25% of the remaining wetlands are in poor condition. Pressures include farming, pollution, infrastructure, disrupted water flows and climate change.
  • The Ramsar Convention (adopted at Ramsar, Iran, on 2 February 1971) has 172 parties and over 2,530 listed sites, covering about 14–17% of the world's wetlands.
  • Listing a site does not control the water that reaches it from upstream.
Quick check
?Which is a drainage basin planning measure that protects groundwater?
Mission check

?In Florida, water once "flowed slowly from the Kissimmee River to Florida Bay", forming the Everglades. Canals and levees drained much of it for farms and towns. The restoration plan (CERP), authorised in 2000, has 68 components and aims to restore the "quantity, quality, timing, and distribution" of water. Can it bring the wetland back?

Wetlands depend on water from the whole basin, so basin-wide planning is the tool. The Murray–Darling Plan has improved river health but is behind schedule and costly for farm towns. Restoration is possible, but slow, costly and contested.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A drainage basin is an open system of inputs, stores, flows and outputs inside the closed global cycle; P = Q + E ± ΔS.
  • Stores differ hugely in size and residence time.

Mission 2

  • Stores (PgC): ocean ~38,000 ≫ soils 1,500–2,400 and permafrost ~1,700 > atmosphere ~828 (2011) > vegetation 450–650; most carbon of all is in rock.
  • Fast cycle: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, ocean exchange (≈123 PgC a year by photosynthesis). Slow cycle: weathering, carbonate formation, burial, outgassing (fractions of a PgC a year, over millions of years).

Mission 3

  • Amazonia stores 150–200 Pg C and recycles much of its own rain; clearing for food, minerals and energy turns local gains in streamflow into basin-wide losses of rainfall and carbon.
  • Droughts (2005, 2010, 2023) show the store is vulnerable; restoration, better farming and protection help both cycles, but at small scale so far.
  • Drainage basin: Upper Xingu, where soy streams carry about four times the water of forest streams.

Mission 4

  • Water: stored as snow, ground ice, active-layer water and lakes; released in a May–June snowmelt flood over impermeable permafrost. Carbon: huge frozen soil store, small vegetation store; summer uptake, non-growing-season release, methane where waterlogged.
  • Oil and gas: roads, pads, dust and flooding drive thermokarst (34% of mapped Prudhoe Bay area affected); moderated by elevated pipes, ground cooling, ice roads, smaller footprints.

Mission 5

  • Dynamic equilibrium: stable stores with constant flows. Land-use change alters infiltration, runoff and biomass; extraction lowers rivers, aquifers (High Plains) and artesian pressure (Great Artesian Basin).
  • Fossil combustion moves slow-store carbon to the atmosphere (38.1 GtCO₂ in 2025); sequestration (sinks, CCS at Sleipner) builds stores but is small beside emissions.

Mission 6

  • Water and carbon stores regulate climate; they link through the atmosphere (vapour, CO₂), ocean, cryosphere and vegetation.
  • Arctic warming is amplified by positive feedbacks (ice–albedo, water vapour, permafrost carbon); some dampen it (radiation to space, CO₂ fertilisation), but sinks become less effective.

Mission 7

  • Diurnal: photosynthesis by day, respiration at night; seasonal: Northern Hemisphere growth and decay, snowpack storage and melt. Long-term: Eocene (ice-free, CO₂ 1,150–2,500 ppm) to the Last Glacial Maximum (sea level about 130 m lower).
  • Monitoring: Mauna Loa, ice cores, OCO-2, GRACE-FO, Argo, river gauges; data separate trend from noise, test models and inform policy and resource management.

Mission 8

  • Strategies: afforestation (Bonn Challenge), forest protection (REDD+), wetland and peatland rewetting, soil carbon farming (4 per 1000), emissions cuts under the UNFCCC, Kyoto and Paris, and carbon trading (EU and China ETS).
  • They depend on cooperation and verification; pledges, offsets and intensity targets can fall short.

Mission 9

  • The Murray–Darling Basin Plan allocates water between irrigation and the environment: rivers are healthier, but recovery is late and its costs fall on farming communities.
  • Global strategies to protect the water cycle: better forestry, water allocation and drainage basin planning for runoff, surface stores and groundwater.
