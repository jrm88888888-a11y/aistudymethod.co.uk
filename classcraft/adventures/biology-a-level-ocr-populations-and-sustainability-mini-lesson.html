Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Make a guess: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Mission 1

Four ways numbers change

  • Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove them.
  • Change in population = (births + immigrants) − (deaths + emigrants).
Quick check
?A newt population starts the year at 500. During the year there are 120 births, 80 deaths, 15 immigrants and 35 emigrants. What is the population at the end of the year?
Mission 1

Exponential growth

  • Few individuals, plentiful resources, weak limiting factors (little competition, predation or disease).
  • Births exceed deaths by a constant proportion of the population.
  • So the number added grows each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … A J-shaped curve.
  • Each individual does not breed faster. There are simply more individuals breeding.
Mission 1

Sigmoid growth and carrying capacity

  • Real populations slow down and level off: an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
  • Carrying capacity: the largest population the environment's resources can support sustainably.
  • Limiting resources: food, water, light, space, nest sites, mineral ions.
  • As numbers rise, resources per individual fall. Births fall; deaths rise. At the plateau, births ≈ deaths.
  • Births and deaths do not stop at the plateau: they balance.
  • Carrying capacity varies with abiotic factors, competition and predation.
Quick check
?Yeast was grown in a sealed flask of glucose solution and cell counts were made every four hours. After 24 hours the count stopped rising. Which explanation for the plateau is correct?
Mission 1

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

  • Density-dependent: affects a larger proportion of the population as it gets denser.
    • Competition for limited resources, predation, disease and parasites spread faster when crowded.
  • Density-independent: affects a similar proportion whatever the density.
    • Frost, drought, flood, fire, storms.
  • Biotic factors are usually density-dependent; abiotic factors such as weather are usually density-independent.
  • Say: "a storm kills the same proportion" — not: "density-dependent because it killed so many".
Quick check
?A storm kills 30% of a seabird colony. Over the following years, the colony returns to its former size. Which explanation is correct?

Mission check

?In 1911, 25 reindeer were introduced to St Paul Island, Alaska, where they had almost no predators. By 1938 there were about 2000; by 1950 only 8 remained, after the lichens they ate in winter had been destroyed. How should this be interpreted in relation to the sigmoid growth model?

Growth stops because each individual's share of the resources shrinks. Destroy the resources and the carrying capacity itself falls.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Make a guess: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Mission 2

Two kinds of competition

  • Competition: individuals use the same resource, and it is in limited supply.
  • Intraspecific: between members of the same species. They need exactly the same food, light, water, space, nest sites and mates.
  • Interspecific: between different species whose needs overlap.
  • No fight is needed. Seedlings shading each other are competing.
  • Both help set the population a habitat can support: its carrying capacity.
  • Say: "competition for a limited resource" — not: "competition means fighting".
Quick check
?Why does competition occur between members of the same population?
Mission 2

What competition does to growth curves

  • Intraspecific competition rises with density. It is what bends the curve into a plateau at carrying capacity.
  • Interspecific, grown together: each species may level off lower than when alone.
  • Or one keeps rising while the other falls to zero: competitive exclusion (Gause's Paramecium).
  • Remove a competitor: the other population rises to a new, higher plateau. It does not grow for ever.

Mission check

?A forester plants beech seedlings very close together in a cleared plot with no other plant species. Over ten years, most of the seedlings die and the survivors are tall and spaced out. Which explanation is best?

Same species, same needs, same limited light: that is why competition within a species is so fierce.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Make a guess: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Mission 3

Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together

  • Prey plentiful → more predators survive and breed. Predator numbers rise, a little later.
  • More predators → more prey eaten. Prey numbers fall.
  • Less food → predators starve and breed less. Predator numbers fall.
  • Fewer predators → prey recover. The cycle repeats.
  • The predator peak lags the prey peak: breeding and starving take time.
  • Case study: snowshoe hare and Canada lynx, Canada. Fur-trade records show a cycle of about 10 years.
  • Effects pass down a food chain. Where sea otters were hunted out, the urchins they eat multiplied and grazed kelp forests to bare rock.
Quick check
?Fur records from the Hudson's Bay Company show snowshoe hare and Canada lynx numbers rising and falling in cycles of about ten years, with each peak in lynx numbers occurring one to two years after a peak in hares. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3Read the data

Food or foxes: what limits hares?

Real data: Krebs and colleagues (1995), Yukon, Canada. 1 km² blocks of forest, followed for eight years. Snowshoe hare density during the peak and decline of the cycle, compared with untreated control areas:

?Use the hare data above. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Illustrative scenario. In a woodland, owls eat voles, and voles eat tree seedlings. A disease kills most of the owls. What is the most likely effect on the tree seedlings over the next few years?

Remove a predator and the effect skips down the food chain: otters, urchins and kelp; owls, voles and seedlings.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 4

Take less than grows back

  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without reducing what future generations can take.
  • Harvesting is sustainable only if the rate of harvesting is below the rate of replacement.
  • Net replacement = growth + new young (recruitment) − natural deaths.
  • Renewable does not mean it cannot run out.
  • Conservation: active management for sustainable use. Preservation: protecting an area by leaving it untouched.
Quick check
?A stock of Atlantic cod has a biomass of 200 000 tonnes. Each year, growth of the fish and recruitment of young fish add 15% to the biomass, while natural mortality removes 5% of it. What is the annual rate of replacement of the stock, below which the catch must stay for harvesting to be sustainable?
Mission 4

Fishing without emptying the sea

  • Quotas: a maximum catch, set from estimates of the stock.
  • Minimum mesh size: young fish escape and live to breed.
  • Closed seasons and no-take zones: protect spawning fish and nursery areas.
  • Limits on fleet size or days at sea.
  • Warning signs: more effort for the same catch; the fish caught get younger and smaller.
  • Real case: Newfoundland's northern cod. Catches peaked at over 800 000 tonnes in 1968. By 1992 the spawning stock had fallen by about 99%, and the fishery was closed.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Records for a cod fishery show that the annual catch stayed close to 250 000 tonnes for 20 years. Over the same period the number of fishing days needed to land that catch doubled, and the mean age of the cod caught fell from 8 years to 4 years. What do these data indicate?
Mission 4

Timber and forest products

  • Coppicing: cut a tree near the ground; many new stems regrow; cut again years later.
  • Rotational felling: fell one area at a time; replant; return only when it has regrown.
  • Selective felling: take only some mature trees; the forest structure and habitats remain.
  • Large-scale clear-felling exposes soil to erosion and leaching and destroys habitats.
Mission 4

Balancing people and wildlife

  • Masai Mara (Kenya): wildlife, Maasai cattle grazing and tourism share the land. Conservancies lease land from Maasai families; tourism income goes to landowners; grazing is planned.
  • Terai (Nepal): lowland forest and grassland, home to tigers and rhinos, surrounded by farms. Forest corridors link reserves; community forest groups harvest grass and wood in buffer zones.
  • Peat bogs: store carbon; cut for fuel and compost, drained for farming. Restoration: block drains, re-wet, restore Sphagnum moss; peat-free compost.
Mission 4

Protecting fragile places

  • Galápagos: invasive goats and rats removed from many islands; quarantine checks on visitors' luggage and boats; visitors only at set sites, with trained guides.
  • Antarctica: Antarctic Treaty (1959); Madrid Protocol (1991) bans mining; fishing (krill, toothfish) under catch limits; strict rules for tourist landings.
  • Snowdonia (Eryri) and the Lake District: footpath erosion by walkers; overgrazing by sheep. Paths rebuilt with stone; grazing managed; walkers asked to keep to paths.
Mission check

?Illustrative data.
A seaweed bed with a biomass of 5000 tonnes is harvested for food. Each year, growth adds 30% to the biomass and natural losses remove 10%. The current harvest is 1400 tonnes per year. Which conclusion is correct?

Fish, timber or seaweed: take no more than the net amount that grows back, and nature can keep supplying.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plentiful resources: exponential growth. As resources per individual shrink, births fall and deaths rise until they balance at the carrying capacity.
  • Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease) push numbers back towards carrying capacity; density-independent factors (weather) knock them about.

Mission 2

  • Intraspecific competition: same species, identical needs; it levels a population off at carrying capacity and cuts each individual's breeding success.
  • Interspecific competition lowers both species' numbers or excludes one; removing a competitor raises the other to a new, higher plateau.

Mission 3

  • Predator and prey numbers cycle, with the predator peak lagging; predation is density-dependent and lowers the prey's numbers.

Mission 4

  • Harvesting is sustainable only if it stays below net replacement (growth + recruitment − natural deaths).
