Mission 1

Mission 1: All Christians follow Jesus. So why are there Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and Pentecostals?

Make a guess: All Christians follow Jesus. So why are there Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and Pentecostals?

Mission 1

One Church, many branches

  • For about 1,000 years most Christians belonged to one Church, though with growing differences between East and West.
  • 1054: the Great Schism splits it into the Orthodox Church (East) and the Catholic Church (West).
  • 1517 onwards: the Reformation splits the Western Church: Protestant Churches form.
  • 1906 onwards: Pentecostal Churches grow fast, and charismatic renewal spreads through older Churches from the 1960s.
  • Each family of Churches is called a denomination (or group of denominations).
  • Nearly all still believe in one God as Trinity, in Jesus as Son of God and Saviour, and in the Bible.
Mission 1

East and West drift apart

  • Catholic growth: from Rome across Western Europe; the Bishop of Rome (the pope) seen as successor of Peter, with authority over the whole Church.
  • Orthodox growth: from Constantinople across the Eastern Roman Empire, then to the Slavs (missionaries Cyril and Methodius, 9th century) and Kievan Rus' (988).
  • Differences grew: Latin vs Greek; the pope's authority vs the East's view of him as "first among equals"; bread for the Eucharist (unleavened vs leavened).
  • 1054: the pope's legate and the Patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated each other.
  • 1204: crusaders sacked Constantinople, deepening the split.
  • 1965: Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras lifted the excommunications; the Churches remain separate.
Mission 1

The Filioque

  • The Nicene Creed (325, expanded at Constantinople in 381) says the Holy Spirit "proceeds from the Father".
  • The West later added Filioque, Latin for "and the Son": first in Spain (Toledo, 589), in Rome's Mass by 1014.
  • Western reasons: the Spirit is "the Spirit of his Son" (Galatians 4:6); Jesus says he will send the Spirit (John 16:7).
  • Eastern objections: (1) the Father is the one source within the Trinity (John 15:26: the Spirit "proceeds from the Father"); (2) no one part of the Church may change the Creed agreed by the whole Church.
  • So it was about belief and about authority: who can speak for the whole Church.
  • Today the Western (Latin) Catholic Church still says it; Orthodox do not; some Anglicans have proposed dropping it.

Mission 1

Why the Reformation happened

  • Abuses: some clergy were poorly educated or corrupt; Church posts were bought and sold.
  • Indulgences: the Church taught they reduced the punishment still due for sins already forgiven (including in purgatory); in 1517 they were being sold to raise money for St Peter's in Rome.
  • Printing press (Gutenberg, about 1450): new ideas and Bibles spread fast and cheaply.
  • Renaissance scholars went back to the Bible in its original languages (Erasmus' Greek New Testament, 1516).
  • Rulers wanted more control over the Church in their lands.
  • Earlier reformers had been silenced: John Wycliffe in England; Jan Hus, burned in 1415.
Mission 1

Martin Luther

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): German monk and professor of the Bible at Wittenberg.
  • Studying Romans, he concluded that God makes sinners right with him as a free gift, received by faith (Romans 1:17).
  • 31 October 1517: his 95 Theses challenge the sale of indulgences.
  • 1521: excommunicated by the pope; at the Diet of Worms he refuses to take back his writings unless shown wrong from Scripture.
  • Translates the New Testament into German (1522), so ordinary people can read it.
  • Marries a former nun, Katharina von Bora (1525); writes hymns and catechisms for families.
Mission 1Read the source

What Luther taught

Three "alones" sum up the Reformers' teaching (paraphrase):

  • Scripture alone (sola scriptura): the Bible is the final authority, above popes and councils.
  • Grace alone (sola gratia): salvation is God's free gift, not earned.
  • Faith alone (sola fide): people are made right with God by trusting Christ, not by good works or indulgences. "The righteous shall live by faith" (Romans 1:17).
  • Priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly through Christ (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Most Reformers taught that Christ instituted only two sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Q1 Why did "faith alone" make selling indulgences pointless?

Show answer

If people are put right with God by faith as a free gift, nothing paid for can add to it.

Q2 Why did "Scripture alone" lead Luther to translate the Bible?

Show answer

If the Bible is the final authority, everyone should be able to read it.

Mission 1

Protestantism spreads

  • Switzerland: Zwingli in Zurich; John Calvin in Geneva (Institutes, 1536): God's sovereignty; churches led by ministers and elders.
  • England: Henry VIII breaks with Rome over his marriage (Act of Supremacy, 1534); the Church of England becomes Protestant under Edward VI and Elizabeth I, with Cranmer's Book of Common Prayer.
  • Scotland: John Knox; the Presbyterian Church of Scotland (1560).
  • Ireland: Henry VIII is made head of the Church in Ireland (1530s); the state Church of Ireland becomes Protestant, though most people stay Catholic; Scottish settlers bring Presbyterianism to Ulster in the 1600s.
  • Later: Baptists (1600s), Methodists (John Wesley, 1700s).
  • Catholic response: the Council of Trent (1545–1563) reforms abuses, ends the sale of indulgences, and reaffirms seven sacraments and Scripture with Tradition.

Mission 1

Pentecostal and charismatic growth

  • 1906, Azusa Street, Los Angeles: a revival led by William J. Seymour, a Black preacher; people of many races worshipped together.
  • Stress on baptism in the Holy Spirit, shown in speaking in tongues, healing and prophecy, as at Pentecost (Acts 2:4).
  • New denominations followed: e.g. Assemblies of God (1914); Elim (founded in Monaghan, Ireland, 1915).
  • From the 1960s, charismatic renewal brought the same gifts into Anglican, Catholic and other Churches.
  • Today Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity grows fastest in Africa, Latin America and Asia.
  • Worship is lively and informal.
Mission 1Diversity

What do so many denominations mean?

  • Paul: "that there be no divisions amongst you" (1 Corinthians 1:10); "there should be no division in the body" (12:25).
  • View 1, a wounded body: divisions break Christ's body; Christians must pray and work for unity, as Jesus prayed "that they may all be one" (John 17:21).
  • View 2, many members: like eyes and hands (1 Corinthians 12), denominations bring different gifts: liturgy, Bible preaching, the Spirit's gifts, social action.
  • Catholic: the Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church; other Christians are truly joined to Christ through baptism.
  • Orthodox: Orthodox Christians believe their Church has kept the undivided faith of the early Church.
  • Ecumenism: World Council of Churches (1948), shared prayer and joint action; in Ireland, Catholic and Protestant leaders working together.

Mission 2

Laity and clergy

  • Laity (from Greek laos, "people"): all Christians who are not ordained. Nearly every Christian is a lay person.
  • Clergy: Christians set apart, usually by ordination, to lead, teach and care for others.
  • Titles vary: priest (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican), vicar or rector (Anglican parish), minister (Presbyterian, Methodist), pastor (Baptist, Pentecostal).
  • Some Churches also have bishops; the Catholic Church has a pope.
  • Christians disagree about what ordination is and whether it is needed at all.
Mission 2

The Catholic hierarchy

Catholics believe:

  • Pope: the Bishop of Rome, successor of St Peter (Matthew 16:18–19); visible head of the Church on earth; final teaching authority.
  • Cardinals: senior clergy who advise the pope and elect a new one.
  • Bishops (archbishops lead groups of dioceses): successors of the apostles; each leads a diocese; together with the pope they form the college of bishops and teach with authority (the magisterium).
  • Priests: lead parishes on the bishop's behalf.
  • Deacons: serve, preach, baptise, witness marriages and lead funerals; permanent deacons may be married men.
  • Laity: the People of God in the world (Lumen Gentium chapter 4).
  • Apostolic succession: the Catholic belief (shared by the Orthodox and many Anglicans) that bishops stand in an unbroken line from the apostles, passed on by ordination.
Mission 2

Catholic ordination and the priest

  • Holy Orders is one of the seven sacraments, in three degrees: deacon, priest, bishop.
  • Only a bishop ordains, by the laying on of hands and a prayer of consecration; a new priest's hands are anointed with chrism.
  • Catholics believe ordination leaves a permanent spiritual mark: "once a priest, always a priest".
  • The priest acts in the person of Christ (in persona Christi) when he celebrates the sacraments.
  • Role: celebrate Mass, hear confessions, baptise, marry, anoint the sick, preach, care for the parish.
  • In the Latin (Western) Catholic Church, priests are normally celibate; Catholic priests are men.

Ephesians 4:11–12

"He gave some to be apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, shepherds and teachers; for the perfecting of the saints, to the work of serving, to the building up of the body of Christ" (Ephesians 4:11–12).

  • Christ gives the Church different leaders, as gifts.
  • Their purpose: to equip ("perfect") all believers for service and to build up the whole body.
  • Catholic reading: bishops and priests continue the apostles' ministry.
  • Many Protestants: leaders exist to equip every member to do ministry, not to do it all themselves.

Building up the body of Christ.

Mission 2

Protestant structures

  • Presbyterian (e.g. Presbyterian Church in Ireland): no bishops. Each congregation is led by a minister and elders (the Kirk Session); congregations form a Presbytery; the General Assembly meets each year, chaired by a Moderator elected for one year.
  • Anglican (Church of Ireland, Church of England): bishops, priests and deacons; archbishops (Armagh and Dublin in the Church of Ireland; Canterbury and York in England); a synod of bishops, clergy and laity.
  • Methodist: an annual Conference with an elected President; circuits of churches, with ministers and lay local preachers.
  • Baptist: each congregation governs itself and calls its own pastor.
  • Orthodox: bishops and patriarchs; no pope; priests may marry before ordination; bishops are chosen from monks.
Mission 2

Protestant ordination and the minister

  • Ordination means being set apart by the Church for ministry, after a call from God and from a congregation.
  • Presbyterian: after training, a minister is ordained by the Presbytery with prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • For most Protestants ordination is not a sacrament: there are two sacraments (baptism and the Lord's Supper).
  • The minister is not a different kind of Christian; he or she is a servant (Latin minister) of the congregation.
  • Role: preach and teach the Bible (central), lead worship, lead baptism and the Lord's Supper, pastoral care, weddings and funerals, lead with the elders.
  • Many Protestant Churches ordain women as ministers (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

The priesthood of all believers

  • Taught by Martin Luther (To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, 1520).
  • "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9): this is said of all believers.
  • Jesus is the one great high priest (Hebrews 4:14–16), so every Christian can come to God directly, without a human go-between.
  • Every Christian can pray, read the Bible, confess sins to God and serve; every job can be a calling.
  • Ministers are still needed for order and teaching, not because they are closer to God.
  • Catholics and Orthodox also pray directly to God; the priest's distinctive role is in the sacraments.
  • Catholic teaching also speaks of a "common priesthood" of all the baptised, but says it differs in kind from the ordained priesthood (Lumen Gentium 10).

Mission 2

Churches without clergy

  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): no clergy; meetings for worship in silence; anyone may speak if moved by the Spirit.
  • Brethren assemblies: no ordained ministers; members share preaching and leading.
  • Reasons:
  • Priesthood of all believers: every Christian has direct access to God.
  • Gifts of the Spirit are given to all (1 Corinthians 12; 14:26: "each one of you has a psalm, has a teaching…").
  • Jesus is the one mediator between God and people (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • "Where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am" (Matthew 18:20): no priest needed for Christ to be present.
  • Concern that a paid, separate clergy can create a hierarchy Jesus did not intend.
Mission 2

Leaders and laity together

  • Lumen Gentium chapter 4 (the laity): lay people share in Christ's work as priest, prophet and king; their special task is to make the world holy from within: family, work, politics (paraphrase, LG 31).
  • Lumen Gentium chapter 5: the universal call to holiness: every Christian, lay or ordained, is called to the fullness of Christian life and love (paraphrase, LG 40).
  • Pastors should listen to the laity and value their advice (LG 37).
  • Catholic practice: parish councils, lay readers and catechists; lay members at recent synods.
  • Anglican and Methodist: laity sit with clergy in synods and Conference.
  • Presbyterian: elders are lay people elected by the congregation who share leadership with the minister.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Christians need ordained leaders."

  • Reasons for: Christ gives leaders to the Church (Ephesians 4:11–12); for Catholics and Orthodox only a priest can celebrate the Eucharist and absolve sins; trained leaders guard against false teaching; worship should be "decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Reasons against: the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9); Jesus the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5); Quakers and Brethren (Churches with no ordained clergy) have worshipped for centuries without clergy; the Spirit gives gifts to everyone (1 Corinthians 12); clergy can be set too far above the laity.
  • Another view: leaders are needed, but as servants of the whole body (Mark 10:43–45), not as a separate class.
  • Judge it by: Needed for what: sacraments, teaching or organisation? Is Bible or Tradition the authority here? Does the answer change between denominations?

Mission 3

Mission 3: One Bible verse tells women to keep silent in church. Why do Christians read it so differently?

Make a guess: One Bible verse tells women to keep silent in church. Why do Christians read it so differently?

Mission 3

The question

  • All Christians believe women and men are equally made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and equally saved: "neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28).
  • They disagree about roles: can women lead, preach and be ordained?
  • Two broad views:
  • Complementarian: men and women are equal but given different roles; some leadership roles are for men.
  • Egalitarian: all roles, including ordained leadership, are open to women and men alike.
  • Both sides argue from the Bible, from tradition, and from the example of the early Church.
Mission 3Read the source

1 Corinthians 14:33–40

"Let the wives be quiet in the assemblies, for it has not been permitted for them to be talking except in submission, as the law also says" (1 Corinthians 14:34).

  • Verse 33: "God is not a God of confusion but of peace, as in all the assemblies of the saints."
  • Verse 35: if they want to learn something, "Let them ask their own husbands at home".
  • Verses 36–40: seek to prophesy, do not forbid tongues; "let all things be done decently and in order".
  • Context: Paul is dealing with disorderly worship in Corinth (tongues, prophecy, people speaking at once).

Q1 What is Paul's main concern in the whole passage?

Show answer

Order and peace in worship.

Q2 What does verse 35 suggest the women were doing?

Show answer

Asking questions during worship.

Mission 3

Ways of reading it

  • A rule for all churches: many complementarian Christians read it, with 1 Timothy 2:11–12, as reserving preaching and leading for men.
  • A local problem: Paul was stopping disruptive questions in one church, not banning women's voices for ever.
  • Paul himself assumes women speak: in 1 Corinthians 11:5 women pray and prophesy aloud in worship, so "be quiet" cannot mean total silence.
  • Culture: first-century customs about women in public; Christians today must ask what was cultural and what is lasting.
  • The text itself: in some ancient manuscripts verses 34–35 appear after verse 40; some scholars think they were added later.
  • Catholic teaching rests its male priesthood mainly on Jesus' choice of twelve men, not on this verse: women read the Scriptures and the bidding prayers at Mass.

Mission 3Read the source

Romans 16:1–16

"I commend to you Phoebe, our sister, who is a servant of the assembly that is at Cenchreae" (Romans 16:1).

  • Phoebe: "servant" translates Greek diakonos, also used for deacons and ministers; she has been "a helper of many" (16:2), probably a patron, and likely carried Paul's letter to Rome.
  • Prisca (Priscilla) and Aquila: "my fellow workers in Christ Jesus" (16:3); a church met in their house (16:5); Prisca is usually named first.
  • Junia (with Andronicus): "notable amongst the apostles" (16:7).
  • Mary, Tryphaena, Tryphosa and Persis are praised for working hard in the Lord (16:6, 12).

Q1 Which two words or phrases in this chapter suggest women held leading roles?

Show answer

"Servant/deacon" for Phoebe; "notable amongst the apostles" for Junia.

Q2 How do complementarians usually reply?

Show answer

These were real ministries, but not the roles of elder or overseer.

Mission 3

Women leaders through history

  • Jesus: women disciples travelled with him and supported him (Luke 8:1–3); Mary Magdalene was the first witness of the resurrection (John 20:11–18).
  • Early Church: deaconesses served in the East for centuries (e.g. Olympias, Constantinople, 4th century).
  • Middle Ages: abbesses led great monasteries (Hilda of Whitby, who hosted the Synod of Whitby, 664); Hildegard of Bingen and Catherine of Siena taught and advised popes.
  • 1600s–1800s: Quaker women preached (Margaret Fell, Women's Speaking Justified, 1666); Methodist women preached; Catherine Booth co-founded the Christian Mission (1865), later the Salvation Army.
  • 20th–21st centuries: many Protestant Churches open ordination to women; the Church of England ordains women priests (1994) and bishops (2015); Sarah Mullally became the first woman Archbishop of Canterbury (2026).
Mission 3Diversity

Denominations today

  • Catholic: only men are ordained as deacons, priests and bishops. Jesus chose twelve men as apostles; the Church teaches it "has no authority" to ordain women as priests (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994, paraphrase). Women serve as readers, catechists, chaplains, heads of schools and religious orders, and in senior Vatican posts.
  • Orthodox: priests and bishops are men; the ancient order of deaconesses is discussed.
  • Anglican (Church of England, Church of Ireland): women deacons, priests and bishops; some parishes, in good conscience, do not accept them, and receive other provision.
  • Methodist, Presbyterian (e.g. Presbyterian Church in Ireland), Baptist Union, Salvation Army, Quakers: women ministers, elders or officers.
  • Some evangelical, Baptist and Pentecostal Churches: women preach and lead ministries, but senior pastor or elder is for men (complementarian).

Mission 3Evaluate

"Women should be able to hold every leadership role in the Church."

  • Reasons for: equal in God's image and in Christ (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28); Phoebe, Prisca and Junia (Romans 16); Mary Magdalene as first witness of the resurrection; women have led communities throughout history; the gifts of the Spirit are given to all (1 Corinthians 12:7).
  • Reasons against: Jesus chose twelve men as apostles; 1 Corinthians 14:34 and 1 Timothy 2:12 read as lasting commands; 2,000 years of tradition in Catholic and Orthodox Churches; the priest represents Christ, who was a man (Catholic teaching); complementarians: equal worth does not require identical roles.
  • Another view: women can lead in many ways without ordination; or ordain women as deacons but not as priests.
  • Judge it by: How should the Bible be interpreted (timeless or cultural)? How much weight does tradition carry? Who has authority to decide: the Bible, the Church's teaching office, or each denomination?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two understandings, one source

Question: Explain two divergent Christian understandings of 1 Corinthians 14:33–40. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Understanding 1: some complementarian Christians read "Let the wives be quiet in the assemblies" (14:34) as a lasting rule. Development: so in their churches the senior pastor or elder is a man, while women lead other ministries.
  • Understanding 2: many Christians see a local problem of disorder in Corinth. Development: Paul expects women to pray and prophesy (1 Corinthians 11:5), so Churches such as the Church of England ordain women.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two developed points and one source of wisdom and authority, with its reference.

Mission 4

What is an icon?

  • An icon (Greek eikon, "image") is a sacred painting, usually on wood, of Christ, Mary, a saint, an angel or a Bible event.
  • Icons are central to Orthodox Christianity (e.g. Greek, Russian, Romanian, Serbian Orthodox Churches).
  • Orthodox Christians call them windows into heaven: through them, believers sense the presence of the one shown.
  • An icon is not a portrait. It shows a person transformed by God, as they are in heaven.
  • Icon painters often say they write an icon (Greek graphein: write, paint): an icon proclaims the Gospel as scripture does.
Mission 4

Why icons? The Incarnation

  • Israel "saw no kind of form" when God spoke at Horeb (Sinai), so they were told to make no image of God (Deuteronomy 4:15–16).
  • Christians believe that in Jesus God became human: "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us" (John 1:14).
  • Christ "is the image of the invisible God" (Colossians 1:15). If God has been seen in Christ, Christ can be painted.
  • St John of Damascus (c. 675–749), defending icons: he did not worship matter, but the Creator of matter, who became matter for our sake (paraphrase).
  • So for Orthodox Christians, refusing icons can seem like doubting that Christ was truly human.
Mission 4

How icons speak

  • Gold background: the light of heaven; the scene is outside ordinary time.
  • No shadows: the light comes from God, not from one side.
  • Flat, still figures and reverse perspective: lines open out towards the viewer, so the icon "looks at" you and draws you in.
  • Large eyes, small mouths: seeing spiritually, inner silence.
  • Halos and inscriptions name the figure (e.g. IC XC for Jesus Christ).
  • Colours: Christ often wears red (divine) under blue (human): two natures in one person.
  • Painters pray and often fast while they work; the finished icon is blessed.

Mission 4

Icons in an Orthodox church

  • Walls and domes are covered with icons: heaven and earth worship together; believers are "surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1).
  • The dome often shows Christ the Pantocrator ("ruler of all").
  • The iconostasis: a screen of icons between the nave (people) and the sanctuary (altar).
  • Its Royal Doors in the centre; usually Christ to the right of the doors and the Theotokos (Mary, "God-bearer") to the left; rows above show feasts, apostles and prophets.
  • The iconostasis does not shut God out: it shows the saints as the link between earth and heaven.
Mission 4

Icons as a focus for devotion

  • On entering church: make the sign of the cross, bow, kiss the icon, light a candle in front of it.
  • At home: an icon corner with a lamp, where the family prays.
  • Icons are carried in processions, given at baptisms and weddings, and kept in cars and pockets.
  • Veneration, not worship: the Second Council of Nicaea (787) taught that icons receive honour and veneration; worship belongs to God alone.
  • As St Basil put it, "the honour paid to the image passes on to the prototype": respect goes to the person shown, not to wood and paint.
  • Orthodox Christians celebrate the restoration of icons each year on the Sunday of Orthodoxy (first Sunday of Lent).
Mission 4Read the source

The second commandment

"You shall not make for yourselves an idol, nor any image of anything that is in the heavens above, or that is on the earth beneath, or that is in the water under the earth: you shall not bow yourself down to them, nor serve them" (Exodus 20:4–5)

  • Numbering differs: Orthodox and most Protestants count this as the second commandment; Catholics and Lutherans count it as part of the first.

Q1 What two things does the commandment forbid?

Show answer

Making an idol or image; bowing down to or serving them.

Q2 How might an Orthodox Christian reply that icons are not idols?

Show answer

An idol is treated as a god; an icon honours the person shown, and God is worshipped alone. In the same Law, God ordered images of cherubim, Exodus 25:18.

Mission 4

Iconoclasm then and now

  • Iconoclasm ("image-breaking"): destroying or rejecting religious images.
  • Byzantine iconoclasm (c. 730–787 and 815–843): emperors ordered icons removed; defenders were punished. Nicaea II (787) and the final restoration (843) upheld icons.
  • The Reformation (16th century): many Protestants removed images: e.g. Zwingli's Zurich (1524); in England, wall paintings were whitewashed and images removed (from 1547; again in the 1640s).
  • Reasons: the second commandment; fear that people were praying to images; the Bible and preaching should be the focus; God's image is in people (Genesis 1:27), who should be served instead.

Mission 4Diversity

Who uses icons today?

  • Orthodox: icons are essential to worship and home prayer.
  • Catholic: venerate images too; Eastern Catholic Churches use icons fully; many Western Catholics pray with icons (e.g. Our Lady of Perpetual Help).
  • Anglican and Lutheran: some churches and homes use icons for prayer; others do not. The ecumenical Taizé Community uses icons in its church.
  • Reformed (Calvinist), Baptist, many evangelical and Pentecostal churches: no icons in worship: worship should centre on the Word and the Spirit, and images risk idolatry.
  • Quakers: no images; God is met in silence and within people.
  • Some Protestants enjoy icons as art or teaching but do not venerate them.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Icons help Christians worship God rather than distract them."

  • Reasons for: the Incarnation made God visible (John 1:14; Colossians 1:15); icons focus the mind and teach the faith; veneration passes to the person shown (Nicaea II); the senses help prayer, as humans are body and soul.
  • Reasons against: the second commandment (Exodus 20:4–5); a holy object can be treated as magical (as Israel did with the bronze serpent, 2 Kings 18:4); the Word and the Spirit are enough; money could serve the poor.
  • Judge it by: Is there a real difference between veneration and worship in practice? Does the Incarnation change the second commandment? Do icons help everyone, or only those taught to use them?
Mission 4Worked answer

Explain two ways icons are used

Question (5 marks): Explain two ways icons are used in Orthodox Christianity. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Way 1: a focus for prayer at home. Development: families pray in the icon corner with a lamp lit, because the icon is a window into heaven: believers sense Christ or the saint present.
  • Way 2: veneration in church: believers kiss icons and light candles. Source: Nicaea II (787) taught that icons are venerated and God alone worshipped; St Basil: honour passes to the person shown. Development: so kissing an icon is honour to the saint, not idolatry.
  • (c) tip: two ways, each developed, plus one source.
Mission check

?A Baptist church member visits an Orthodox church with a friend. She admires the icons but will not kiss one. Which reason would most Baptists give?

The same picture can be a window into heaven for one Christian and a risk of idolatry for another: the difference lies in how each reads the Incarnation and the second commandment.

Mission 5

Why Christians write

  • Jesus taught in parables: short stories that carry truth about God (e.g. the Lost Son, Luke 15:11–32). Christians have told stories ever since.
  • Literature in Christian life has several purposes:
  • Express belief in a fresh, imaginative way.
  • Teach and explain the faith.
  • Defend it against objections (apologetics).
  • Inspire prayer and devotion.
  • Reach outsiders who would never open a Bible.
  • Three types: fiction (invented stories), non-fiction (explaining, arguing, telling true experience) and poetry.
Mission 5

C. S. Lewis

  • Clive Staples Lewis (1898–1963), born in Belfast; scholar of medieval and Renaissance literature at Oxford, then Cambridge.
  • An atheist as a young man; became a Christian in 1931, helped by conversations with his friend J. R. R. Tolkien. A member of the Church of England.
  • Wrote in every type:
  • Fiction: The Chronicles of Narnia (seven books, 1950–56); The Screwtape Letters (1942); The Great Divorce (1945).
  • Non-fiction: Mere Christianity (1952, from wartime BBC radio talks); The Problem of Pain (1940).
  • Read and quoted by Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants alike.
Mission 5

The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe (1950)

  • Four children (Peter, Susan, Edmund, Lucy) step through a wardrobe into Narnia.
  • The White Witch rules: it is always winter and never Christmas.
  • Edmund betrays his brother and sisters to the Witch for sweets and promises.
  • Aslan, a great lion, son of the Emperor-beyond-the-Sea, arrives; the snow begins to melt.
  • By the Deep Magic, the Witch claims Edmund's life as a traitor. Aslan offers his own life instead.
  • Aslan is killed on the Stone Table, returns to life at dawn, and the Witch is defeated.
  • Lewis said this was not a strict allegory (where every detail is a code) but a "supposal": suppose there were a world like Narnia, and the Son of God came to save it (paraphrase of his 1958 letter).
Mission 5Read the source

The Stone Table

Lewis's story, paraphrased: Susan and Lucy watch through the night where Aslan was killed. At sunrise the Table cracks and Aslan stands alive. He explains a "Deeper Magic from before the Dawn of Time": when a willing victim who had done no wrong dies in a traitor's place, death itself starts working backwards.

Compare Jesus' words: "For the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)

Q1 Whose place does Aslan take?

Show answer

Edmund's: the traitor.

Q2 Which two Christian beliefs does the scene echo?

Show answer

Jesus' sacrificial death for sinners (atonement); his resurrection at dawn, first seen by women.

  • Also: Aslan breathes on statues the Witch turned to stone, and they live again (new life, like the Spirit's breath).

Mission 5

The Great Divorce (1945)

  • A dream story. The narrator is in a dim grey town, where people keep moving further apart.
  • A bus takes ghosts up to the bright foothills of Heaven. There even the grass is too solid for them: it hurts their feet.
  • Shining Solid People, often people they knew, come to meet them and invite them to stay and go further in.
  • Most ghosts refuse and go back. Each will not let go of something: pride, resentment, a grudge, a possessive love, their own opinions.
  • One ghost carries a red lizard (lust) on his shoulder. He lets an angel kill it; he becomes solid, and the lizard becomes a stallion.
  • The narrator's guide is the Scottish writer George MacDonald (1824–1905).
  • Lewis says in his preface it is imaginative, not a guess at what the afterlife is really like.
Mission 5

What The Great Divorce says

  • Hell is chosen: no one is kept out of heaven against their will; people stay away from God because they will not give something up.
  • Free will matters: God does not force anyone into heaven.
  • Heaven is more real than earth: "solid", not misty.
  • Giving up sin feels like dying, but brings fuller life (the lizard becomes a stallion).
  • The title answers William Blake's The Marriage of Heaven and Hell: Lewis says the two cannot be "married".
  • Catholic link: the Catechism calls hell a "self-exclusion" from communion with God (CCC 1033). MacDonald says the grey town is hell for those who stay but purgatory for those who leave it.
Quick check
?In The Great Divorce, why do most of the ghosts go back to the grey town?
Mission 5

Non-fiction

  • Non-fiction explains, argues or witnesses to real experience.
  • Apologetics: Lewis, Mere Christianity: Jesus claimed to be God, so he was either mad, lying, or truly Lord; he argued that "a great moral teacher" is not an option (paraphrase).
  • Autobiography: Augustine, Confessions (c. 397–400): a life turned to God. "Our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." (Pusey translation, 1838)
  • Mystical writing: Julian of Norwich, Revelations of Divine Love (c. 1395): "all shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well." (ch. 27, Warrack translation, 1901)
  • Devotion: Thomas à Kempis, The Imitation of Christ (c. 1420s).
  • Used in study groups, sermons, retreats and private reading.
Mission 5Read the source

Poetry

"Love bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back,

Guilty of dust and sin."

(George Herbert, 1593–1633, "Love (III)", in The Temple, 1633)

"The world is charged with the grandeur of God."

(Gerard Manley Hopkins, 1844–1889, Jesuit priest, "God's Grandeur", written 1877)

  • Poetry packs belief into image and feeling: the Psalms are poems; many hymns began as poems.

Q1 In Herbert's lines, who is "Love", and why does the soul draw back?

Show answer

God, or Christ; the soul feels unworthy because of sin.

Q2 Which belief about creation does Hopkins express?

Show answer

God's glory fills and shines through the natural world.

Mission 5Diversity

Different uses, different views

  • In church: a poem read at a wedding or funeral; a Narnia scene in a sermon; Lewis in a study group; hymns from poems.
  • Catholic practice: "spiritual reading" of saints' writings; poems used for prayerful reading.
  • Other settings: schools, bedtime reading, films (Narnia films from 2005), theatre, outreach to people outside church.
  • Some Christians are wary: some conservative Christians dislike the magic and pagan myth in Narnia; Tolkien disliked Lewis mixing myths together.
  • Some non-religious critics object: the novelist Philip Pullman has called Narnia religious propaganda (paraphrase).
  • Some prefer scripture alone: fiction can distract from, or be confused with, the Bible.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Fiction is the best way to express Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: Jesus himself taught in stories (parables); stories reach the imagination and outsiders; Narnia has reached millions who never read theology; Lewis's "supposal" lets readers meet ideas afresh.
  • Reasons against: stories can be misread (is Aslan really Jesus?); non-fiction explains and defends belief clearly (Mere Christianity); poetry and hymns are used in worship itself; scripture remains the authority.
  • Another view: each type does a different job: fiction imagines, non-fiction explains, poetry prays.
  • Judge it by: Which type expresses belief most accurately? Which reaches the most people? Which helps people pray or change their lives?
Mission 5Worked answer

Two purposes of literature

Question: Explain two purposes of literature in Christian life. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Purpose 1: to express belief through story. Development: in The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe, Aslan dies in Edmund's place, so readers grasp the atonement through imagination.
  • Source: Jesus taught in parables (e.g. Luke 15:11–32), so Christians see stories as a way God's truth is shared.
  • Purpose 2: to explain and defend belief. Development: Mere Christianity gives reasons for faith, helping Christians answer doubts.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two purposes, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.
Mission check

?A modern novel tells of an older sister who takes the blame for a theft her younger brother committed. She is punished, and he is set free. Later she is cleared, and they are reunited. How would most Christian readers who enjoy Lewis use a story like this?

Christian fiction need not name Jesus: a story of self-giving love can carry belief about him.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • 1054: the Great Schism (Orthodox East, Catholic West), over papal authority and the Filioque ("and the Son").
  • 1517 onwards: Luther's Reformation (Scripture alone, grace alone, faith alone, priesthood of all believers) gave rise to Protestant Churches.
  • Catholics answered with reform at the Council of Trent (1545–1563).
  • From 1906, Pentecostal and charismatic Churches grew fast; Christians read 1 Corinthians 12 to see denominations as a wounded body or many members.

Mission 2

  • Catholic hierarchy: pope, bishops, priests, deacons, laity; Catholics believe ordination is a sacrament given by a bishop; the priest celebrates Mass and the sacraments.
  • Protestant Churches are organised differently (e.g. Presbyterian: minister and elders, Presbytery, General Assembly); ordination sets ministers apart to preach and lead but is not a sacrament.
  • Protestants teach the priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly.
  • Quakers and Brethren have no ordained clergy.

Mission 3

  • 1 Corinthians 14:33–40 is read as a lasting rule (complementarian) or as dealing with disorder in Corinth (many others); Paul assumes women pray and prophesy (1 Cor 11:5).
  • Romans 16 names women leaders: Phoebe (diakonos), Prisca, Junia ("notable amongst the apostles").
  • Catholic and Orthodox Churches ordain only men as priests; Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian and many other Churches ordain women; some evangelical Churches keep senior roles for men.

Mission 4

  • Icons are sacred images, central to Orthodox worship: windows into heaven, justified by the Incarnation (John 1:14; Colossians 1:15).
  • Orthodox Christians venerate icons (kiss, light candles, icon corner); Nicaea II (787) distinguished veneration from worship.
  • Many Protestants reject icons in worship because of the commandment against images (Exodus 20:4–5); Catholics, some Anglicans and Lutherans use them.

Mission 5

  • Christian literature (fiction, non-fiction, poetry) expresses, teaches and defends belief, inspires prayer and reaches outsiders.
  • The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe: Aslan's death and return echo the atonement and resurrection (a "supposal", not strict allegory).
  • The Great Divorce: hell is chosen; heaven is more real; letting go of sin brings fuller life.
