Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Make a guess: Why are almost all cells too small to see, even inside the largest animals?

Mission 1

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 1

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side / µm1234
Surface area / µm²6245496
Volume / µm³182764
SA:V / µm⁻¹6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A cube of side 1 mm has a surface area of 6 mm², a volume of 1 mm³ and a surface area-to-volume ratio of 6. The side length is increased to 4 mm. What is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?
Mission 1

Worked example: other shapes

  • Sphere: SA = 4πr²; V = (4/3)πr³.
  • Cylinder: SA = 2πrh + 2πr²; V = πr²h.
  • Cuboid (rectangular solid): SA = 2lh + 2lw + 2wh; V = lwh.
  • Sphere, r = 5 µm:
  • SA = 4 × π × 5² = 314 µm².
  • V = (4/3) × π × 5³ = 524 µm³.
  • SA:V = 314 ÷ 524 = 0.60 µm⁻¹.
  • Count every face: hidden faces too.
Quick check
?A hypothetical single-celled organism has the shape of a rectangular solid, 20 μm long, 10 μm high and 10 μm wide. What is the cell's surface area-to-volume ratio?
Mission 1

Large organisms

  • As an organism grows, SA:V falls. Many cells are far from the surface.
  • Diffusion is too slow over long distances.
  • So large organisms have specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, gut, leaves) and mass transport systems (blood).
  • A good gas exchange surface has a large SA:V, is thin, and keeps a steep concentration difference.
Quick check
?Shrews are among the smallest mammals. Like larger mammals, they keep their body temperature nearly constant. A shrew must eat much more food per gram of its body mass each day than a deer does. Which statement best explains this difference?
Mission check

?A spherical single-celled organism grows from a radius of 10 µm to a radius of 30 µm, keeping its shape. Which prediction is best supported?

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Make a guess: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Mission 2

The airway tree

  • Air path: trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveoli (air sacs).
  • Repeated branching ends in hundreds of millions of alveoli: a surface of tens of square metres inside the chest.
  • Gas exchange happens only across the alveoli. The airways conduct air to them.
  • The lungs sit in the thorax, enclosed by the ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.
Mission 2

The alveolus: built for diffusion

  • Alveolar wall: one layer of flattened (squamous) epithelial cells.
  • Capillary wall: one layer of endothelial cells. Over much of the surface, air to blood is under 1 µm.
  • A dense capillary network wraps each alveolus. Continuous blood flow keeps the gradient.
  • Ventilation keeps alveolar air high in O2 and low in CO2.
  • A moist lining lets gases dissolve. Surfactant in it lowers surface tension, so alveoli do not collapse.
  • Say: "thin alveolar epithelium, one cell thick" — not: "thin cell walls". Animal cells have no cell walls.
Quick check
?Mammalian lungs contain a branched network of bronchioles ending in millions of alveoli. How does this branching contribute to gas exchange?
Mission 2

Breathing in

  • (1) The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  • (1) The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. The internal intercostals relax.
  • (2) Thorax volume rises, so pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric.
  • (3) Air flows in, down the pressure gradient. The lungs are pulled open; they do no work themselves.
Mission 2

Breathing out

  • At rest, breathing out is mostly passive. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
  • The diaphragm domes upward; the ribs move down and in; stretched elastic fibres in the lungs recoil.
  • Thorax volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
  • Forced breathing out: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in.
  • External and internal intercostals are antagonistic: they move the ribs in opposite directions.
  • Say: "the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward" — not: "the diaphragm contracts and pushes up".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events draws air into the lungs during inspiration?
Mission check

?During a rugby match, a player is tackled hard. A small tear in the surface of one lung lets air leak into the space around that lung. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles on that side still contract normally, but that lung does not inflate. Which explanation is best?

The muscles change the volume of the thorax; the lungs simply follow. Break that link and the lung stays collapsed.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Make a guess: How can you tell from the outside how well a person's lungs are working?

Mission 3

Lung volumes on a spirometer trace

  • A spirometer records the volume of air breathed in and out against time.
  • Tidal volume: volume in or out in one normal breath (about 0.5 dm³ at rest).
  • Inspiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe in after a normal breath in.
  • Expiratory reserve volume: the extra you can breathe out after a normal breath out.
  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve: the most you can breathe out after the deepest breath in.
  • Residual volume: air left after the hardest breath out. A spirometer cannot measure it.
Quick check
?A student's spirometer readings are: tidal volume 0.5 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 3.0 dm³, expiratory reserve volume 1.2 dm³. What is the student's vital capacity?
Mission 3

Worked example: ventilation and oxygen uptake

  • Breathing rate: breaths per minute (peaks per minute on a trace).
  • Respiratory minute ventilation = tidal volume × breathing rate.
  • At rest: 0.50 dm³ × 12 min⁻¹ = 6.0 dm³ min⁻¹. Exercise: 1.6 dm³ × 25 min⁻¹ = 40 dm³ min⁻¹.
  • Oxygen uptake: soda lime absorbs the CO2 breathed out, so the trace drifts down as O2 is used.
  • Example (illustrative data): the trace falls 0.75 dm³ in 3.0 min, so O2 uptake = 0.75 ÷ 3.0 = 0.25 dm³ min⁻¹.
Quick check
?During gentle exercise, a student breathes 15 dm³ of air per minute at a breathing rate of 20 breaths per minute. What is her tidal volume?
Mission 3Practical

Spirometer and exercise

  • Core practical 17: investigate the effects of exercise on tidal volume, breathing rate, respiratory minute ventilation and oxygen consumption using data from spirometer traces.
  • Method: chamber filled with medical-grade oxygen; soda lime in the circuit; nose clip; record for 1–2 minutes at rest.
  • Exercise for a set time and intensity (e.g. 3 minutes of step-ups), then record again at once. Compare with the same person's resting trace.
  • Safety: disinfect the mouthpiece; soda lime is corrosive; stop if dizzy; no one with asthma or a heart or lung condition.
  • Calculate: tidal volume, breathing rate, ventilation, O2 consumption (slope).
Mission 3

What sets the breathing rate?

  • (1) Muscles respire faster → more CO2 enters the blood.
  • (2) CO2 forms carbonic acid → more H⁺ → blood pH falls.
  • (3) Chemoreceptors detect the fall in pH.
  • (3) Chemoreceptors in the medulla, and in the aorta and carotid arteries, send impulses to the ventilation centre in the medulla oblongata.
  • (4) More nerve impulses go to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles: breathing becomes faster and deeper.
  • (5) Extra CO2 is breathed out; pH returns towards normal: negative feedback.
  • Say: "rising CO2 (falling pH) drives faster breathing" — not: "breathing speeds up because the body detects low oxygen".
Quick check
?During a run, a student's blood carbon dioxide concentration rises, blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.32 and ventilation rate rises from 12 to 30 breaths per minute. Which statement correctly links these changes?
Mission check

?At rest, an athlete's tidal volume is 0.6 dm³, inspiratory reserve volume 2.8 dm³ and expiratory reserve volume 1.4 dm³. During hard exercise her tidal volume rises to 2.2 dm³. Which statement is correct?

Lung volumes, breathing rate and disease data all answer one question: how much air reaches a large, thin, well-supplied surface.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Make a guess: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Mission 4

A molecule with two ends

  • A phospholipid has a head and two tails.
  • Head: a charged phosphate group. It is polar: hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • Tails: two fatty acid chains. They are non-polar: hydrophobic.
Mission 4

The bilayer builds itself

  • In water, heads face the water. Tails are kept away from it.
  • A cell has water on both sides: cytoplasm inside, tissue fluid or surroundings outside.
  • So phospholipids form two layers. Heads form both surfaces; tails meet in the middle.
  • The sheet forms spontaneously: no enzyme or ATP is needed to arrange it. (Enzymes do make the phospholipids.)
  • No covalent bonds join the phospholipids. Each stays a separate molecule.
  • Say: "water molecules bond to each other and exclude the tails" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Why do phospholipids form a bilayer when they are mixed with water?
Mission 4

The middle is a barrier

  • The core of the bilayer is the hydrophobic tails.
  • Small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids: O2, CO2.
  • Ions and polar molecules (such as glucose) barely enter the core. Charge matters more than size.
  • Water is polar but very small: it crosses slowly.
  • So membranes are partially permeable.
  • How substances cross: Mission 5.
Mission 4

Proteins: the mosaic

  • Proteins are scattered irregularly among the phospholipids, like the pieces of a mosaic.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in one or both layers. Some span the whole membrane.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on one surface.
  • Roles vary: channels and carriers (transport), enzymes, receptors, cell recognition.
  • Say: "integral: embedded in the bilayer" — not: "integral: always spans it".
Mission 4

Sugar chains on the outside

  • Glycoprotein: a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • Glycolipid: a lipid with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • The carbohydrate is only on the outer (extracellular) face.
  • It works in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
  • Some act as receptors or as antigens, recognised by the immune system.
Quick check
?Which statement about glycoproteins in the plasma membrane is correct?
Mission 4

Cholesterol, and why "fluid"

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids in animal cell membranes.
  • Fluid: phospholipids and proteins move sideways within the layer. The sheet stays continuous.
  • Say: "fluid: parts move sideways within the membrane" — not: "fluid: it is leaky, with gaps".
Mission 4

Draw it

A two-dimensional drawing needs:

  • Two rows of phospholipids, heads out, tails in.
  • An integral protein spanning the bilayer; a peripheral protein on one surface.
  • A glycoprotein, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • A glycolipid, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • Cholesterol among the tails, not on the surface.
  • Labels: hydrophilic (both surfaces), hydrophobic (the core).
  • Proteins scattered unevenly, never in a regular row.
Quick check
?A student draws a two-dimensional representation of the fluid mosaic model. Which feature of the drawing is correct?
Mission check

?Liposomes are tiny hollow spheres made by shaking phospholipids in water; some are used to carry medicines in the blood. Each one is a single bilayer enclosing a droplet of water. A water-soluble drug is dissolved in the water before the liposomes form. Where will the drug be held, and why?

Like a cell membrane, a liposome assembles itself in water, and its hydrophobic core keeps water-soluble substances on one side.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Make a guess: Oxygen slips straight through a cell membrane, yet a smaller sodium ion is stopped. Why?

Mission 5

Diffusion

  • Particles move randomly, all the time.
  • More particles cross from the crowded side than from the other: net movement from higher to lower concentration.
  • This is movement down a concentration gradient.
  • It is passive: no energy from ATP.
  • At equal concentrations, particles still cross both ways. Net movement is zero.
  • Say: "net movement stops" — not: "the molecules stop moving".
Mission 5

Straight through the bilayer

  • Simple diffusion: particles pass between the phospholipids.
  • Only small non-polar molecules do this readily: O2, CO2.
  • Permeability of the bilayer depends only on size and polarity or charge. It is not selective in any other way.

Mission 5

Channel proteins

  • Facilitated diffusion: diffusion through a protein. Still passive; still down the gradient.
  • A channel protein spans the membrane with a water-filled, hydrophilic pore. Ions pass through it, avoiding the hydrophobic core.
  • Selective: the pore's width and the charges lining it let only certain ions through. A K⁺ channel passes K⁺ but not the smaller Na⁺.
  • Many are gated: open or closed. No ions pass when closed.
  • Say: "a channel is specific" — not: "a channel is a hole any small particle can use".
Mission 5

Carrier proteins

  • A carrier protein binds a specific molecule, such as glucose.
  • Binding causes a change in shape. The molecule is released on the other side.
  • Large polar molecules cross this way.
  • Net movement is still down the gradient. No ATP is used.
  • (Carriers that use ATP to pump against a gradient.)
Quick check
?A potassium channel in a cell membrane is open. Which statement describes the movement of ions through it?
Mission 5

Which route?

  • O2, CO2 → between the phospholipids.
  • Water → between the phospholipids, slowly.
  • Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻) → channel proteins.
  • Glucose and other large polar molecules → carrier proteins.
  • The bilayer sorts only by size and charge. Proteins make a membrane selective: each lets through particular substances.
Quick check
?Artificial vesicles were made from a pure phospholipid bilayer. Oxygen crossed the bilayer rapidly, glucose very slowly and potassium ions hardly at all. When a potassium channel protein was inserted into the bilayer, potassium crossed rapidly but permeability to sodium ions and to glucose did not change. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission check

?A hypothetical toxin binds to and blocks all of the channel and transport proteins in the plasma membrane of a cell. Which prediction about the cell, immediately after exposure to the toxin, is best supported?

Two routes, two rules: the bilayer lets through what is small and non-polar; proteins let through specific substances, ions included.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why are the cells lining the small intestine covered in thousands of tiny finger-like folds?

Make a guess: Why are the cells lining the small intestine covered in thousands of tiny finger-like folds?

Mission 6

What sets the rate

Diffusion across a surface is faster with:

  • a larger surface area;
  • a bigger difference in concentration (steeper gradient);
  • a shorter diffusion distance (thinner surface);
  • a higher temperature (particles move faster).
  • For facilitated diffusion: more channel or carrier proteins.
Mission 6

Worked example: putting factors together

rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of surface

  • This is Fick's law.
  • Example: a disease halves the surface area and doubles the thickness. The gradient is unchanged.
  • Step 1: area factor = ½.
  • Step 2: thickness factor: divide by 2.
  • Step 3: rate × (½ ÷ 2) = rate × ¼.
  • The rate falls to one quarter.
Quick check
?An exchange surface changes: its surface area becomes 3 times as large, the difference in concentration across it becomes 2 times as large, and its thickness becomes 1.5 times as large. By what factor does the rate of diffusion change?
Mission 6

Cells built for fast transport

  • Epithelial cells of the small intestine (ileum): microvilli fold the cell-surface membrane, so a much larger surface area. Their membranes are packed with carrier and channel proteins.
  • Alveolar epithelial cells: very thin, so a short diffusion distance; together a huge surface.
  • Root hair cells: a long extension gives a large surface area; many carrier proteins for ions.
  • More membrane area or more proteins: more transport per second.

Mission check

?Fructose enters the epithelial cells of the small intestine by facilitated diffusion through carrier proteins. In some gut diseases these cells' microvilli become shorter and fewer. The fructose concentration in the gut and the number of carrier proteins in each square micrometre of membrane stay the same. Why does each cell now take up fructose more slowly?

Folded membranes and extra carriers do the same job: they give more places for substances to cross at once.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.
  • Large organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems; small animals lose heat faster per gram.

Mission 2

  • Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; thin, moist, ventilated alveoli wrapped in capillaries give a huge, short diffusion path.
  • Breathing in: diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thorax volume up → pressure down → air in. Breathing out reverses this.

Mission 3

  • Vital capacity = tidal volume + inspiratory reserve + expiratory reserve; measured with a spirometer.
  • Ventilation per minute = tidal volume × breathing rate.
  • Rising CO2 lowers blood pH → chemoreceptors → medulla → faster, deeper breathing (negative feedback).

Mission 4

  • Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, so in water they form a bilayer on their own; the hydrophobic core blocks ions and polar molecules.
  • Proteins, glycoproteins, glycolipids and cholesterol sit in or on this fluid sheet, and its parts move sideways.

Mission 5

  • Simple diffusion: small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids, down their gradient; ions and polar molecules are kept out by the hydrophobic core.
  • Facilitated diffusion: specific channel or carrier proteins carry them across, still passively and down the gradient.

Mission 6

  • Rate of diffusion rises with surface area and concentration difference and falls with thickness.
  • This relationship is Fick's law.
  • Facilitated diffusion also depends on the number of channel or carrier proteins; when carriers are saturated, only more carriers or more membrane raise the rate.
