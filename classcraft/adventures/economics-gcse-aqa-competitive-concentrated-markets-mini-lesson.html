Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do prices fall when rival sellers arrive, and what happens when only one firm is left?

Make a guess: Several new sellers open up in a busy market, and the price falls. Why?

Mission 1

From many sellers to one

  • A market structure describes how competitive a market is. Look at three things:
    • Number of producers: many, a few, or one.
    • Product differentiation: are the products the same, or made to look different (brands)?
    • Ease of entry: can new firms join easily?
  • Competitive market: many sellers, similar products, easy to join.
  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, often branded products, hard to join.
  • Monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, very hard to join.
Mission 1

Competitive markets

  • Features:
    • many buyers and sellers, none big enough to control the price
    • similar products, so buyers can switch easily
    • easy entry and exit: new firms can join or leave
    • buyers can compare prices (often online)
  • Why firms compete: to win customers, sales and profit.
  • Why competition happens: low start-up costs, many buyers, no legal limits on new firms.
  • Examples: market stalls, takeaways, hairdressers, phone cases sold online.
Mission 1

What competition does

WhoEffects of competition
ConsumersLower prices · more choice · better quality and service
ProducersPressure to cut costs and innovate · lower profits · weak firms may close
WorkersMore employers to choose from · but pressure on pay and job security as firms cut costs
  • Say: "more sellers increase supply, so the price falls" — not: "more sellers lower demand".

More sellers join

  • Takeaway: more sellers shift supply right; the price falls and each seller sells less, so profits fall.
Quick check
?On a supply and demand diagram, many new firms join a competitive market. Which statement describes the change correctly?
Mission 1

Monopoly

  • Pure monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, with no close substitutes.
  • The firm is a price maker: it can choose its price (but if it charges more, it sells less).
  • Barriers to entry keep rivals out.
  • In practice, a firm with a large share has monopoly power. UK merger rules let the competition authority look closely at a merger that gives a firm 25% or more of supply.
  • Examples: the only water company in an area; a drug firm with a patent on a new medicine.
Mission 1

Barriers to entry

BarrierExample
LegalGovernment gives one firm a licence to run a service
PatentsA new invention protected, normally for up to 20 years
MarketingHuge advertising and strong brand loyalty
TechnologyRivals cannot copy secret know-how
Start-up costsBuilding a railway or an aircraft factory costs billions
Economies of scaleThe big firm's low average cost undercuts any newcomer
  • High barriers let a firm keep high profits for a long time: no new rivals arrive to compete them away.
Mission 1

Monopoly: good or bad?

  • Possible disadvantages:
    • higher prices and less output than a competitive market
    • less choice for consumers
    • little pressure to cut costs or improve quality
  • Possible advantages:
    • economies of scale can mean lower costs
    • high profits can pay for research (new medicines)
    • one network avoids wasteful copies (a natural monopoly: two sets of water pipes to every house would be a waste)
  • Whether consumers gain depends on whether lower costs are passed on and on regulation.
Quick check
?Which of these is NOT a barrier to entry into a market?
Mission 1

Oligopoly: a few big firms

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • Features: high barriers to entry · often branded products · firms are interdependent: each watches what its rivals do.
  • Concentration ratio: the combined market share of the largest firms.
  • Example: UK grocery shares, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025:
SupermarketShare
Tesco28.2%
Sainsbury's15.7%
Asda11.6%
Aldi10.6%
  • Four-firm concentration ratio = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
Mission 1

Compete or collude?

  • Price war: firms cut prices again and again to win share. Great for consumers for a while; weak firms may leave.
  • Non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty cards, quality, after-sales service. Firms avoid a price war.
  • Collusion: firms secretly agree prices or share out the market instead of competing.
  • Cartel: a group of firms that collude. Illegal in the UK, the EU and many other countries.
  • Example: from 1997 to 2011, five major European truck makers colluded on prices. In 2016 the European Commission imposed fines totalling €2.93 billion.
Oligopoly: possible advantagesOligopoly: possible disadvantages
Economies of scaleCollusion keeps prices high
Profits fund research and new productsHigh barriers keep new firms out
Non-price competition can raise qualitySpending on advertising adds to costs
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a market, Firm W has a 30% share, Firm X 25%, Firm Y 15% and Firm Z 10%. Fifty small firms share the rest. What is the three-firm concentration ratio?
Mission check

?A government gives one company the only licence to run trains between two cities. There is no other practical way to travel between them. Compared with several companies competing on the route, what is most likely?

Remove competition and a firm can charge more; that is why governments regulate monopolies.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What decides how much a job pays, and why can two jobs pay so differently?

Make a guess: Why are some jobs paid much more than others?

Mission 2

A market for workers

  • In the labour market, workers supply labour and employers demand it.
  • The wage is the price of labour.
  • Derived demand: firms want workers because people want the goods and services they make. More online shopping → more delivery drivers wanted.
  • Diagram: wage on the vertical axis, number of workers on the horizontal.
    • Demand for labour slopes down: at a lower wage, firms hire more.
    • Supply of labour slopes up: at a higher wage, more people want the job.
Mission 2

What changes the demand for labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the demand curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • demand for the product changes (a boom in home deliveries → more drivers wanted)
    • productivity changes (each worker produces more, so each is worth more to the firm)
    • the cost of machines that can replace workers changes (cheaper self-checkouts → fewer cashiers wanted)
    • the cost of other inputs changes
  • Say: "a higher wage causes a contraction in demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls". The curve has not moved.
Mission 2

What changes the supply of labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the supply curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • population grows or shrinks
    • migration: workers arrive from, or leave for, other areas or countries
    • age structure: more people of working age
    • retirement age or school-leaving age changes
    • female participation: more women in paid work (childcare, attitudes)
    • skills and qualifications: more people trained for a job
    • mobility: how easily people can change job or move
    • non-wage factors of a job: conditions, hours, holidays, training, status
Quick check
?Which of these would shift the supply curve of nurses to the right?
Mission 2

How the wage is set

  • Step 1: demand for labour D and supply of labour S.
  • Step 2: they cross at the equilibrium wage (We) and employment (Le).
  • Above We: more people want the job than firms want to hire (excess supply). The wage is pushed down.
  • Below We: firms cannot fill jobs (excess demand, a shortage). The wage is pushed up.
  • Step 3: a shift in D or S gives a new We and Le.
Mission 2

Worked example: shifts

  • Illustrative data. Warehouse workers in a town: We = £12 an hour, Le = 520 workers.
  • Demand rises (online orders boom): D shifts right.
    • New wage £14, new employment 640.
    • Wage ↑ and employment ↑.
  • Supply rises instead (100 more people want the work at each wage): S shifts right.
    • New wage £11, new employment 560.
    • Wage ↓ but employment ↑.
ChangeCurveWageEmployment
Demand risesD rightUpUp
Demand fallsD leftDownDown
Supply risesS rightDownUp
Supply fallsS leftUpDown
  • Two jobs pay differently when their demand and supply differ: strong demand for the workers, or few people able and willing to do the job, means a higher wage.
Mission 2Graph lab

Shift the labour market

  • Takeaway: a demand shift moves the wage and employment the same way; a supply shift moves them opposite ways.
Quick check
?Many people move into a town looking for warehouse work. Other things equal, what happens in the town's market for warehouse workers?
Mission 2

Why pay differs

  • UK, April 2025: the median full-time employee earned £766.60 a week. A full-time worker on the National Living Wage (£12.21 an hour, 37.5 hours) earned about £458 (£457.88).
  • Skills and training: few people can do highly skilled jobs, so supply is low and pay high.
  • Productivity and demand: workers whose output is worth more to employers are paid more.
  • Non-wage factors: unpleasant or risky jobs pay more; attractive ones can pay less.
  • Sector: public sector pay is often set nationally, with more job security and pensions; private sector pay varies more.
  • Discrimination: some groups are paid less for the same work. UK gender pay gap, April 2024: 7.0% for full-time employees; 13.1% for all employees. Part of the gap reflects hours and occupation, not only discrimination.
  • Say: "pay depends on the supply of and demand for workers" — not: "pay depends on how useful the job is".
Quick check
?Brain surgeons are paid far more than care workers, although both jobs matter to society. Which is the best explanation?
Mission check

?A country raises the age at which young people may leave education from 16 to 18. In the market for workers aged 16 to 17, what is the most likely effect?

Anything that changes how many people can or will do a job shifts supply, and moves pay and jobs in opposite ways.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why is the pay that lands in your bank account smaller than the pay you were offered?

Make a guess: Someone is offered a job paying 30,000 a year. Roughly how much reaches their bank account?

Mission 3

Gross pay

  • Gross pay: total pay before any deductions.
  • Wage: paid by the hour. Weekly gross pay = hourly rate × hours worked.
  • Salary: a fixed amount a year, usually paid monthly (annual salary ÷ 12).
  • Gross pay can also include:
    • overtime: extra hours, often at a higher rate (time-and-a-half = 1.5 × the hourly rate; double time = 2 ×)
    • bonus: an extra payment for good results
    • commission: a % of the value of sales made
  • From 1 April 2026 the UK National Living Wage (age 21 and over) is £12.71 an hour.
Mission 3

Worked example: a week's pay

  • Sam earns £12.71 an hour. Basic week: 40 hours. Plus 4 hours of overtime at time-and-a-half.
  • Basic pay: 40 × £12.71 = £508.40
  • Overtime rate: 1.5 × £12.71 = £19.065 an hour
  • Overtime pay: 4 × £19.065 = £76.26
  • Gross pay: £508.40 + £76.26 = £584.66
  • Tip: work out the overtime rate first, then multiply by the overtime hours.
Quick check
?Ana earns £12.71 an hour. One week she works 20 normal hours plus 4 hours of overtime at double time. What is her gross pay for the week?
Mission 3

From gross to net

  • Deductions: money taken from gross pay before you receive it.
  • Main deductions:
    • Income tax: paid to the government
    • National Insurance (NI): paid to the government; builds a right to the State Pension and some benefits
    • Pension contributions: saved for your own retirement
    • other deductions are possible (for example, student loan repayments)
  • Net pay (take-home pay) = gross pay − deductions.
  • A payslip shows gross pay, each deduction and net pay.
  • Say: "net pay is after deductions" — not: "net pay is before tax".
Mission 3

Income tax

  • UK rates for 6 April 2026 to 5 April 2027 (Scotland has different bands):
BandYearly taxable incomeRate
Personal AllowanceUp to £12,5700%
Basic rate£12,571 to £50,27020%
Higher rate£50,271 to £125,14040%
Additional rateOver £125,14045%
  • Each rate applies only to the income inside its band.
  • Above £100,000, the Personal Allowance shrinks, reaching zero at £125,140.
  • Income tax is progressive: higher earners pay a larger share of their income.
Mission 3

National Insurance and pensions

  • NI (employees), 2026 to 2027:
    • 8% on pay between £242 and £967 a week (£1,048 to £4,189 a month)
    • 2% on pay above £967 a week
  • For a steady salary, that is about 8% of pay between £12,570 and £50,270 a year.
  • Workplace pension: most employees are enrolled automatically.
    • Legal minimum: 8% of earnings between £6,240 and £50,270 a year.
    • At least 3% from the employer; the employee pays the rest (5%, including tax relief).
  • In the worked examples here the pension is a simple 5% of gross pay. Real schemes vary.
Mission 3

Worked example: £30,000 a year

  • Salary £30,000. Illustrative pension: 5% of gross pay (no tax relief shown).
  • Income tax: taxable income = £30,000 − £12,570 = £17,430. Tax = 20% × £17,430 = £3,486
  • NI: 8% × £17,430 = £1,394.40
  • Pension: 5% × £30,000 = £1,500
  • Net pay = £30,000 − £3,486 − £1,394.40 − £1,500 = £23,619.60 a year
  • That is about £1,968.30 a month, out of a gross £2,500.
Quick check
?Use the rates on the earlier screens and the method in the previous screen. Kai earns £20,000 a year and pays 5% of gross pay into a pension. What is his net pay for the year?
Mission 3

A pay rise: how much do you keep?

  • The marginal rate: the share of the next £1 earned that is deducted.
  • Basic-rate taxpayer: 20% tax + 8% NI + 5% pension: 33p of each extra £1 is deducted.
  • So a £1,000 rise on £30,000 adds £670 to net pay.
  • Higher-rate taxpayer (above £50,270): 40% tax + 2% NI: keeps 58p of each extra £1 before pension.
  • The average tax rate = income tax ÷ gross pay:
    • £20,000: £1,486 ÷ £20,000 = 7.4%
    • £30,000: £3,486 ÷ £30,000 = 11.6%
  • The average rate rises with income: that is what makes income tax progressive.
Quick check
?Raj earns £40,000 a year and gets a pay rise of £2,000. He stays in the basic-rate band. Ignoring his pension, how much of the rise does he keep after income tax and NI?
Mission check

?Mia works part-time and earns £12,000 a year. Her pay rises to £14,000 a year. Using the 2026 to 2027 rates, how much more income tax does she pay each year?

Income tax is charged only on income above the Personal Allowance, and only at the rate of each band.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why might a busy factory make more than is best for the people who live around it?

Make a guess: Why might a busy factory make more than is best for the people who live around it?

Mission 4

When markets get it wrong

  • Markets use prices to decide what gets made and how much.
  • Usually this works: if people want more of something, its price rises and firms make more.
  • Market failure: the market does not allocate resources efficiently.
  • This is a misallocation of resources: too much of some things is made, too little of others.
  • The main cause in this mission: externalities. Public goods are in another lesson.
Mission 4

Private, external, social

  • Private cost: paid by the person or firm making the decision. A factory pays for fuel, wages and machines.
  • External cost: falls on a third party, someone outside the deal, who is not paid for it. Smoke harms the health of people nearby.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. The full cost to society.
  • Private benefit: gained by the buyer. You gain from your own education.
  • External benefit: gained by a third party. Your employer and the wider economy gain from your skills.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • An externality is the gap between the social and the private cost or benefit.
  • Say: "an external cost falls on a third party" — not: "an external cost is a cost to the firm".
Mission 4

Worked example: adding up the costs

Illustrative data.

  • A factory makes a batch of paint. It pays 30 for materials, energy and wages.
  • Fumes from the batch cause breathing problems nearby, costing 12 in health care and lost work.
  • Private cost = 30. External cost = 12.
  • Social cost = 30 + 12 = 42.
  • The factory decides using 30. Society bears 42.
  • Benefits work the same way. A vaccination gives the person a private benefit of 20 (they don't get ill).
  • It also gives others an external benefit of 15 (fewer people catch the disease from them).
  • Social benefit = 20 + 15 = 35.
Quick check
?A farm uses a chemical spray that drains into a river and harms fishing downstream. For each tonne of crops, the farm pays 200 in costs, and the damage to fishing is 50. (Illustrative data.) What is the social cost of each tonne?
Mission 4

Four kinds of externality

Externalities come from making things (production) and from using them (consumption).

Negative (external cost)Positive (external benefit)
ProductionFactory smoke; noise from a building siteA firm trains workers, who later take their skills to other firms
ConsumptionDriving at rush hour adds to congestion; smoking near othersVaccination protects others too; education raises skills across the economy
  • Both production and consumption can cause negative externalities.
  • Ask two questions: Who makes the decision? Who else is affected, and are they harmed or helped?
Quick check
?A student plays loud music in their flat late at night. The neighbours cannot sleep. What kind of externality is this?
Mission 4Graph lab

The smoky factory

Illustrative data. A factory sells each batch of paint for 20: this is what buyers think a batch is worth. Each extra batch costs the factory more to make: 9, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21, 23. Each batch also causes an external cost: smoke damage to people nearby. The factory makes a batch if 20 covers its own cost. Society is better off with a batch only if 20 covers the social cost.

  • Takeaway: the factory ignores the external cost, so it makes more than is best for society. The bigger the external cost, the bigger the gap.
Mission 4

Too much and too little

  • Negative externality: the decision-maker ignores the external cost. The price is too low for the full cost to society.
    • Result: overproduction or overconsumption. Too many resources go into making it.
  • Positive externality: the decision-maker ignores the external benefit.
    • Result: underproduction or underconsumption. Too few resources go into it.
  • The amount that is best for society is called the social optimum.
  • Either way, resources are misallocated: society would be better off with a different mix of goods.
  • The costs of misallocation: damage to health and the environment, wasted resources, and benefits that society misses out on (e.g. a less skilled workforce).
  • Say: "overconsumption of the good compared with the social optimum" — not: "people buy too much because it is cheap".
Quick check
?A flu vaccination costs 40. It gives the person a private benefit worth 30 and other people an external benefit worth 20. (Illustrative data.) Each person decides for themselves. What is the most likely result?
Mission 4

Why governments step in

  • Market failure gives a reason for government intervention: to move output closer to what is best for society.
  • Make the decision-maker face the external cost or benefit:
    • tax goods with external costs; subsidise goods with external benefits
    • regulation and fines: limits on pollution, penalties for breaking them
    • information: health campaigns and labels
  • How each tool works, with diagrams where your course needs them.
Mission check

?Flights from an airport cause noise for people who live nearby. Each extra flight costs the airline 50 thousand to run and causes noise damage worth 20 thousand to residents. (Illustrative data.) Which statement is correct?

When a cost lands on a third party, the decision-maker doesn't count it, so the market makes too much.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a tax on sugary drinks make people healthier, or does it just raise money?

Make a guess: Can a tax on sugary drinks make people healthier, or does it just raise money?

Mission 5

Why governments step in

  • Market failure: too much of some goods (external costs), too little of others (external benefits).
  • The government's toolkit:
    • taxes and subsidies: change the price
    • rules: regulation, legislation, fines
    • pollution permits
    • state provision and information
Mission 5

An indirect tax

  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending, paid to the government by the seller. Example: a tax per can of sugary drink.
  • The tax adds to firms' costs, so supply shifts left: S1 → S2.
  • The vertical gap between S1 and S2 is the tax per unit.
  • Price rises: P1 → P2. Quantity falls: Q1 → Q2.
  • The tax makes the price closer to the full cost to society: the polluter pays.
  • Pros: less of the harmful good; tax revenue for the government.
  • Cons: the external cost is hard to measure, so the tax may be too high or too low; it takes a bigger share of poorer people's income; a very high tax can lead to smuggling.
  • Say: "the supply curve shifts left (decrease in supply)" — not: "demand falls because of the tax".
Mission 5

Real world: the UK sugar tax

  • The UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy started on 6 April 2018.
  • It is paid by the makers of soft drinks with added sugar, per litre:
    • 5 g to under 8 g of sugar per 100 ml: 18p per litre
    • 8 g or more per 100 ml: 24p per litre
    • under 5 g: no levy
  • Many makers cut the sugar in their drinks to pay less or no levy. When the levy began, the UK government said over half of makers had already done so.
  • So a tax can work in two ways: a higher price for buyers, and a reason for firms to change the product.
  • Other countries tax sugary drinks too, e.g. Mexico (from 2014).
Mission 5Graph lab

Tax and elasticity

Illustrative data. A sugary drink sells at 1.50 a can; 200 thousand cans a week. The government adds a tax of 0.30 a can. Two markets: in one, demand is price inelastic (few substitutes); in the other, demand is price elastic (many substitutes).

  • Takeaway: inelastic demand: sales fall a little, the price rises more, and the tax raises more money. Elastic demand: sales fall a lot.
Quick check
?A government raises the tax on cigarettes, but the number of cigarettes smoked hardly falls. What is the most likely reason?
Mission 5

A subsidy

  • Subsidy: a payment from the government to producers, to lower their costs.
  • Used for goods with external benefits: public transport, vaccinations, solar panels.
  • Firms' costs fall, so supply shifts right: S1 → S2.
  • Price falls: P1 → P2. Quantity rises: Q1 → Q2.
  • Pros: more of a good that benefits society; cheaper for consumers.
  • Cons: opportunity cost: the money could be spent on hospitals or schools, or taxes could be lower. Firms may come to rely on it. The right size is hard to judge.
Quick check
?The government gives bus companies a subsidy for each journey they run. On a demand and supply diagram for bus journeys, what happens?
Mission 5

Rules, fines, permits and provision

  • Regulation and legislation: laws that limit harm, e.g. limits on factory emissions, bans on dangerous chemicals, safety standards.
    • Pro: clear and fast. Con: costly to enforce; a limit gives no reason to cut pollution below it.
  • Fines: penalties for breaking the rules. They only work if firms expect to be caught.
  • Pollution permits: the government sets a cap on total pollution and issues permits. Firms can buy and sell them.
    • A firm that cuts pollution cheaply sells spare permits; a firm that finds cutting costly buys them.
    • Real world: the EU Emissions Trading System, since 2005.
    • Pro: total pollution is capped. Con: setting the cap is hard; checking is costly.
  • State (direct) provision: the government supplies the good, e.g. vaccinations, schools. Con: opportunity cost.
  • Information provision: campaigns and labels, e.g. calorie labels on menus.
Quick check
?A government pays a subsidy to firms that make solar panels. Which of these is an opportunity cost of the policy?
Mission 5Evaluate

Does intervention work?

It depends on:

  • Information: can the government measure the external cost or benefit? Guess wrong, and the tax or subsidy is the wrong size.
  • Elasticity: with inelastic demand, a tax raises money but changes behaviour little.
  • Enforcement: rules and fines only work if they are checked; that costs money.
  • Unintended effects: illegal markets, smuggling, firms moving abroad, shortages under a maximum price.
  • Opportunity cost: every subsidy or state service uses money that could go elsewhere.
  • If intervention makes things worse, that is government failure.
Mission check

?Two cities of similar size each put the same charge on driving into the city centre, to cut traffic. City X has fast, cheap buses and trains to the centre. City Y has almost none. What is most likely?

Whether a tax changes behaviour or mainly raises money depends on how easily buyers can switch.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Market structures run from many sellers to one, depending on numbers, differentiation and ease of entry.
  • Competition lowers prices and profits and widens choice; barriers to entry protect monopoly power.
  • In oligopoly a few firms may compete hard, use non-price competition, or collude.

Mission 2

  • The wage is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour.
  • Shifts in demand move wage and employment the same way; shifts in supply move them opposite ways.
  • Pay differs with skills, demand, non-wage factors, sector and discrimination.

Mission 3

  • Gross pay is pay before deductions; net pay is what you take home.
  • Income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions are the main deductions.
  • Tax is charged band by band on income above the Personal Allowance, so it is progressive.

Mission 4

  • Market failure: the market misallocates resources, making too much of some things and too little of others.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • Negative externalities lead to overproduction or overconsumption; positive externalities to underproduction or underconsumption.

Mission 5

  • Taxes shift supply left (price up, quantity down); subsidies shift supply right (price down, quantity up).
  • With inelastic demand, a tax raises revenue but cuts the quantity only a little.
  • Every policy has costs: information, enforcement, unintended effects and opportunity cost.
