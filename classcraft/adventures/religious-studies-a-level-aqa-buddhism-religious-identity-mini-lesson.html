Mission 1

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community"; the third of the Three Refuges (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha).
  • Monastic Sangha: ordained monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), living by the Vinaya.
  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): all who have reached at least stream-entry, monastic or lay. For Theravada, the refuge in its fullest sense.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen together.
Mission 1

The Sangha in Thailand

  • Thailand is a Theravada country; the Sangha has long been linked with the monarchy.
  • Two orders (nikayas): the large Mahanikai and the smaller, stricter Thammayut, founded in the 1830s by Prince Mongkut while a monk (later King Rama IV).
  • The Sangha Act (1902, later revised) organises it nationally under a Supreme Patriarch and a Supreme Sangha Council.
  • Novices (samanera) keep ten precepts; monks keep the 227 Patimokkha rules.
  • Temporary ordination: traditionally, young men ordain for one rains retreat (vassa, about three months), often before marriage.
  • This makes merit for their parents, especially the mother, who cannot herself ordain as a monk.
Mission 1

Traditional roles of monks

  • The wat (temple-monastery) was the centre of village life.
  • Education: before state schools spread (from the 1920s), boys learned to read at the wat; novices still study Pali and Dhamma.
  • Healing and counsel: herbal medicine, advice, settling disputes.
  • Ceremonies: chanting at funerals, house-blessings and new ventures; paritta chants for protection.
  • Keepers of the Dhamma: teaching, sermons on observance days.
  • Two vocations: village and town monks (study, teaching, ritual) and forest monks (meditation), e.g. Ajahn Mun (1870–1949) and Ajahn Chah (1918–1992).

Mission 1

The exchange

  • Monks may not farm, cook stored food or (by the Vinaya) handle money. They depend on lay people.
  • Lay people give material support: food on the dawn alms round, robes at Kathina (after the rains retreat), medicine, lodging.
  • Monks give the Dhamma: teaching, example, chanting, counsel.
  • Lay people gain merit: the Sangha is called the best field of merit, where good deeds grow most.
  • On the alms round the monk does not ask and does not thank: the giver is grateful for the chance to give.
  • On observance days (uposatha), lay people visit the wat and take extra precepts.
Mission 1Read the source

Mutual help

The Itivuttaka (107), paraphrase: the Buddha tells his monks that householders are very helpful to them, providing robes, food, lodging and medicine; and the monks are very helpful to householders, teaching them the Dhamma. So, supporting each other, both live the holy life.

"As the bee collects nectar and departs without injuring the flower, or its colour or scent, so let a sage dwell in his village." (Dhammapada 49, Müller)

Mission 1

The dasa sila: ten precepts

  • Lay people keep the five precepts: no harming life, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, intoxicants.
  • On observance days many keep eight.
  • Novices keep the dasa sila (ten precepts): the five (with full celibacy), plus no eating after midday; no dancing, music or shows; no garlands, perfumes or adornments; no high or luxurious beds; no accepting gold and silver (money).
  • Each is a training rule (sikkhapada): "I undertake the training rule to abstain from …". An aspiration, not a commandment from a god.

Mission 1Diversity

Many kinds of sangha

  • Thailand: forest monks and town monks; and mae chi: women in white robes who keep eight or ten precepts but are not recognised as ordained nuns by the Thai Sangha.
  • Japan: since 1872, most temple priests in most schools marry and run family temples; Jodo Shinshu priests never were celibate.
  • Tibet: large monasteries; lamas as teachers; lay devotees support them.
  • Triratna (UK): an order neither monastic nor lay; "sangha" means the whole community.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The relationship between monks and lay people is an equal exchange."

  • For: Itivuttaka 107: each side is "very helpful" to the other; neither could live the path alone.
  • For: lay people gain merit and teaching; monks gain the support to practise.
  • Against: the gift of the Dhamma "exceeds all gifts" (Dhammapada 354), so the exchange is not equal in value.
  • Against: monks are materially dependent; lay donors can hold power over a temple.
  • Against: in Theravada, the monastic life is usually seen as the surer path to Nibbana, though lay people can also awaken.
  • Judgement criteria: Equal in what: need, value or status? Is a field of merit "receiving" or "giving"? Do modern changes (see Mission 2) alter the balance?

Mission 2

Changing roles

  • Over the 20th century, the Thai state took over many monastic roles: schools (from the 1920s), hospitals, social welfare.
  • Cities grew; many people now meet monks mainly at ceremonies, funerals and on the alms round.
  • Fewer men make the monastic life lifelong; temporary ordination continues but often for shorter periods.
  • Money scandals and a trade in blessed amulets have damaged trust in some monks.
  • New roles have grown:
  • Development monks: village co-operatives, rice banks.
  • Ecology monks: "ordaining" trees with saffron cloth to protect forests.
  • Care: hospices, e.g. for people with HIV/AIDS (from the 1990s).
  • Media: Dhamma talks on radio, TV and social media.
Mission 2

Pressures and the state

  • Consumerism: a large trade in amulets blessed by famous monks; donations to "celebrity" monks.
  • Scandals: cases of monks breaking the Vinaya, and investigations into temple finances (2010s), have hurt trust.
  • State control: the Sangha Act was amended in 2016 so that the king appoints the Supreme Patriarch; a new Supreme Patriarch was appointed in 2017.
  • Politics: monks may not vote in Thailand, yet some have joined political protests; the state discourages this.
  • Debate: is the Sangha too close to the state, or not supervised enough?
Mission 2

Reforming voices

  • Buddhadasa Bhikkhu (1906–1993): founded Suan Mokkh forest monastery (1932); taught a rational Dhamma for everyday life, stripped of magic and merit-trading.
  • P. A. Payutto (b. 1938): scholar-monk; Buddhadhamma; Buddhist Economics (1992): consume wisely, for well-being, not craving.
  • Santi Asoke (from 1975, led by Bodhiraksa): strict, vegetarian, simple communities rejecting consumerism; expelled from the national Sangha in 1989 but still active.
  • Forest monasteries abroad (e.g. Amaravati, England, 1984) spread the Thai tradition to the West.
  • Each answers the same question: how to keep the Sangha true to the Vinaya in a modern economy.

Mission 2

Wat Phra Dhammakaya: roots and teaching

  • Roots: Luang Pu Sodh Candasaro (1884–1959), abbot of Wat Paknam, Bangkok, taught a Dhammakaya ("Dhamma-body") meditation.
  • His pupil, the nun Khun Yai Chandra Khonnokyoong (1909–2000), trained Phra Dhammajayo (b. 1944) and Phra Dattajivo.
  • Founded 1970 in Pathum Thani, north of Bangkok.
  • Meditation: the mind rests at the centre of the body (just above the navel), visualising a bright crystal sphere, often repeating "Samma Arahang".
  • Teaching: deeper in meditation one can realise the Dhammakaya, a luminous inner Buddha-body.
  • Its teachers describe Nibbana as a true self (atta). Critics, notably P. A. Payutto (1999), said this contradicts anatta; supporters reply it is a faithful reading of the canon's "Dhamma-body".
Mission 2

Wat Phra Dhammakaya: practice and growth

  • Mass ceremonies: huge, ordered gatherings in white, around the vast Dhammakaya Cetiya (stupa), covered in small Buddha images, completed around 2000.
  • Merit-making is central: donations to build and spread Buddhism are presented as very fruitful merit.
  • Modern methods: satellite TV channel (DMC), online meditation, centres abroad, careful management and branding.
  • Discipline: cleanliness, punctuality, quiet; appeal to the urban middle class and university students.
  • Ordination drives: mass temporary ordinations and novice programmes; large alms-giving events for thousands of monks.
  • Lay meditation is promoted for everyone, not left to forest monks.

Mission 2Diversity

Supporters and critics

  • Supporters (many members and some scholars): it brings young urban people back to the temple; teaches meditation to lay people; is orderly, generous and global.
  • Critics (other monks, reformers, journalists): commercialisation ("merit for money"); doctrine (true self vs anatta); what they see as a personality cult around the abbot; closeness to political power.
  • 1999: accusations about fundraising, land and teaching; charges against the abbot were later dropped (2006).
  • 2016–17: the abbot was sought over funds linked to a credit union fraud; the military government ordered a long police search of the temple (2017); he was not found.
  • Scholar Rachelle Scott, Nirvana for Sale? (2009), paraphrase: the controversy reflects wider Thai debates about wealth, merit and what "true" Buddhism is.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Wat Phra Dhammakaya shows the Thai Sangha adapting well to the modern world."

  • For: it reaches people that village wats lose: young, urban, educated.
  • For: it revives meditation for lay people and uses modern media skilfully (cf. skilful means).
  • For: its discipline and scale show Buddhism as confident, not in decline.
  • Against: merit-making through big donations may feed craving and turn merit into a product.
  • Against: "Nibbana as true self" seems to depart from anatta, the core Theravada teaching.
  • Against: the legal disputes, and what critics call a personality cult around the abbot, have damaged trust in the Sangha.
  • Judgement criteria: Adaptation of method, or change of teaching? Does wealth corrupt, or can it serve the Dhamma? Who judges orthodoxy: the state, the Sangha, scholars, or the laity?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Monastic life and the precepts are still relevant today."

  • For: monks keep the Dhamma alive and teach it, now online as well as in person.
  • For: a life of few possessions is a living challenge to consumerism.
  • For: new roles (hospices, ecology, media teaching) show the Sangha serving modern needs.
  • Against: the state now does what monks once did (schools, health care).
  • Against: scandals and commercialisation suggest the Vinaya is hard to keep in a money economy.
  • Against: lay meditation movements and Western groups such as Triratna show the path without traditional monasticism.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to whom: Thai villagers, city workers, Western converts? Is relevance measured by numbers, by service, or by faithfulness to the Dhamma?

Mission 3

Puja: acts of devotion

  • Puja (or vandana): devotion and respect, at a shrine at home or in a temple (vihara, wat).
  • Bowing three times: to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.
  • Offerings and their meaning:
  • Flowers: fresh now, wilted soon: a reminder of anicca.
  • Candles or lamps: the light of wisdom dispelling ignorance.
  • Incense: the "fragrance" of good conduct spreading.
  • Chanting: homage ("Namo tassa bhagavato arahato samma-sambuddhassa": homage to the Blessed One, the Worthy One, the Fully Awakened One), the refuges and the precepts.
  • Walking clockwise around a shrine or stupa, keeping it on one's right, as a mark of respect.
Mission 3

Buddha images

  • The earliest Buddhist art showed no figure of the Buddha: only symbols (footprints, an empty seat, the Bodhi tree, the wheel).
  • Images appear around the 1st century CE (Gandhara, Mathura).
  • Mudras (hand gestures) carry meaning: earth-touching (the awakening), meditation, turning the wheel (teaching), fearlessness.
  • An image is often consecrated before use, e.g. an "eye-opening" ceremony in Sri Lanka.
  • Role: a reminder of the Buddha's qualities and a focus for the mind; treated with respect (shoes off, feet never pointed at it).
  • Mahayana shrines add other buddhas and bodhisattvas (Amitabha, Avalokitesvara/Guanyin, Tara).
Mission 3

Stupas

  • A stupa is a mound that holds relics: after the Buddha's death, his remains were divided and enshrined (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, DN 16).
  • Emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) is said to have built many; Sanchi (India) is a famous early example.
  • Names by region: chedi (Thailand), dagoba (Sri Lanka), pagoda (East Asia).
  • Devotees walk around it clockwise, offer flowers and lamps, and recollect the Buddha.

Mission 3Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana views of worship

  • Theravada: the Buddha has attained parinibbana: he does not hear or respond.
  • So devotion is respect and recollection (buddhanussati): it calms the mind, inspires practice and makes merit.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): honours paid to the Buddha bear fruit, though he does not accept them, as a fire that has gone out still helps those who make their own fire.
  • Many lay Theravadins also seek blessing and protection, e.g. through paritta (protective chanting).
  • Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas are active now in many worlds and can respond to devotion.
  • E.g. Lotus Sutra ch. 25: calling on Avalokitesvara (Guanyin) brings help; Pure Land: reliance on Amitabha's vow.
  • Tibetan devotion: prostrations, mantras, prayer wheels; devotion also trains the mind.
  • Spec: "different perspectives of Theravada and Mahayana Buddhism about the significance of worship".
Mission 3

Dana: giving

  • Dana: generosity, giving. The first of the ten perfections (Theravada) and of the six perfections (Mahayana).
  • Three kinds of gift: material things; fearlessness (protection, safety); the Dhamma (teaching), counted highest.
  • Lay people give food to monks on the morning alms round, and robes at Kathina after the rains retreat.
  • Intention matters: giving with a glad mind before, during and after the gift.

"The gift of the law exceeds all gifts; the sweetness of the law exceeds all sweetness; the delight in the law exceeds all delights; the extinction of thirst overcomes all pain." (Dhammapada 354, Müller)

  • ("liberality" = generosity; "the law" = the Dhamma; "thirst" = craving)

Mission 3

Punya: merit

  • Punya (Sanskrit; Pali puñña): merit, the good kamma produced by wholesome actions.
  • Three bases of making merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Merit brings happiness, a better rebirth and good conditions for practice; it carries over into future lives.
  • Beyond merit: the awakened one is "above good and evil, above the bondage of both" (Dhammapada 412, Müller): no longer making kamma at all.
Mission 3

Sharing merit

  • Theravada: after giving, people share (dedicate) the merit, often to dead relatives, while monks chant; water is poured from a small jug into a bowl.
  • Source: the Tirokudda Sutta (Khuddakapatha 7, paraphrase): departed relatives wait outside the walls; gifts to the Sangha dedicated to them help them.
  • Kamma is personal: "by oneself one is purified … no one can purify another" (Dhammapada 165, Müller).
  • So many Theravada teachers explain: the dead (or living) person rejoices in the good deed (anumodana) and so makes merit of their own. Sharing merit is itself generous, so the giver loses nothing.
  • Mahayana: parinamana: bodhisattvas turn over their vast merit to all beings; practice ends with a dedication of merit.

Mission 3

Wealth and possessions

  • Wealth is not evil in itself; craving (tanha) for it is the problem.
  • "There is no satisfying lusts, even by a shower of gold pieces" (Dhammapada 186, Müller).
  • Advice to householders (Sigalovada Sutta, DN 31, paraphrase): earn by right livelihood; use a quarter for living, half for work, and save a quarter for hard times.
  • AN 4.62 (paraphrase): a householder's happiness comes from owning wealth, using it, being free of debt, and being blameless; blamelessness is worth far more than the rest.
  • "Contentedness the best riches" (Dhammapada 204, Müller).
  • Responses to secular materialism: simplicity and generosity; monks in Britain (e.g. Amaravati) live without money on alms; Schumacher's "Buddhist Economics" (1966) argued for enough rather than more.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Giving in order to gain merit is not truly generous."

  • For: seeking a better rebirth is still a kind of craving; the goal is to go beyond merit (Dhammapada 412).
  • For: some reformist Buddhists criticise merit-making as calculating; the anthropologist Melford Spiro (Buddhism and Society, 1970) shows the aims can come apart, distinguishing merit-focused ("kammatic") from Nibbana-focused ("nibbanic") Buddhism.
  • For: Mahayana stresses giving merit away, not storing it.
  • Against: the wish to do good is wholesome desire, not craving; the Buddha praised the giver (Dhammapada 177).
  • Against: dana is training in letting go: the first perfection, the start of the path.
  • Against: merit-making binds lay and monastic communities together and supports the Sangha (see Mission 1).
  • Judgement criteria: Does motive cancel the good of the gift? Is merit an end, or a stage? Are lay and monastic goals rightly different?

Mission 4

Meditation on the path

  • Pali bhavana: "cultivation" or "bringing into being". Meditation is mind-training, not just sitting.
  • The Eightfold Path falls into three trainings: sila (ethics), samadhi (meditation, concentration), panna (wisdom).
  • The samadhi group: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration (the 6th, 7th and 8th factors).
  • The trainings support each other: ethics steadies the mind; a steady mind sees clearly; clear seeing deepens ethics.
  • Purpose: to tame the mind so that craving and ignorance can end: the way to Nirvana.
Mission 4

Two kinds of meditation

  • Samatha: calm, tranquillity. The mind settles on one object and becomes still.
  • Vipassana: insight, clear seeing. The mind looks into how things really are (see Mission 5).
  • An early text pairs them (AN 2.30, paraphrase): calm develops the mind and lets go of passion; insight develops discernment and lets go of ignorance.
  • Many Theravada teachers, following Buddhaghosa: calm suppresses the defilements for a while; insight uproots them.
  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
Mission 4

How samatha is practised

  • Choose one object: the breath (anapanasati, mindfulness of breathing), a coloured disc (kasina), a recollection of the Buddha, or loving kindness.
  • Sit upright and still; return the mind each time it wanders.
  • Counting breaths (e.g. one to ten) is a common start.
  • Five hindrances block calm: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt.
  • As they fade, a steady mental image or sign (nimitta) may arise and absorption becomes possible.
  • Nature: training attention. Purpose: a calm, clear mind fit for wisdom.

Mission 4

Metta bhavana: loving kindness

  • Metta: loving kindness, a warm wish for the welfare of beings. Bhavana: cultivation.
  • Metta is one of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abodes"): loving kindness, compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita), equanimity (upekkha).
  • The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8, paraphrase): as a mother protects her only child with her life, cultivate a boundless heart towards all beings.
  • Method (Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga IX): begin with oneself, then a dear person, a neutral person, a hostile person, and break down the barriers between them.
  • Many Western groups (e.g. Triratna) teach five stages: self, good friend, neutral, difficult, all beings.
  • Metta is a samatha practice that can lead to jhana; it is also the antidote to ill will.

Mission 5

Vipassana: clear seeing

  • Vipassana (Pali; Sanskrit vipashyana): insight, "seeing clearly".
  • The meditator watches experience closely: breath, sensations, feelings, thoughts.
  • Aim: to see directly the three marks of existence:
  • Anicca: everything changes; nothing stays.
  • Dukkha: nothing conditioned can fully satisfy.
  • Anatta: no fixed, lasting self is found anywhere.
  • Seeing this weakens craving and ends ignorance: insight leads towards Nirvana (nibbana).
  • Samatha steadies the mind; vipassana uses that steadiness to see (see Mission 4).
Mission 5Read the source

Foundations of mindfulness

The Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10; also Digha Nikaya 22), paraphrase: a meditator is mindful of four things, seeing each arise and pass away:

  • Body (breathing, posture, parts of the body).
  • Feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral).
  • Mind (e.g. a mind with or without anger).
  • Dhammas (mental qualities and teachings: the hindrances, the Four Noble Truths).

"'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller)

"Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Q1 Which mark of existence does verse 277 ask the meditator to see?

Show answer

Anicca: all created things perish.

Q2 What does verse 372 say about meditation and wisdom?

Show answer

Each needs the other; together they bring one near Nirvana.

Mission 5

Right mindfulness

  • Sati: mindfulness; the word also carries the sense of remembering (keeping the Dhamma in mind).
  • Right mindfulness is the 7th factor of the Eightfold Path.
  • It runs through all practice:
  • In ethics: noticing anger before it becomes harsh speech; remembering the precepts.
  • In samatha: noticing when the mind wanders and bringing it back.
  • In vipassana: watching things arise and pass.
  • In daily life: walking meditation, eating, working with full attention.
  • "Right" matters: mindfulness joined to wisdom and good intention, not attention for any purpose (see Mission 6).

Mission 5

Meditation as therapy

  • Since 1979, meditation drawn from vipassana and Zen has been taught in clinics without Buddhist belief.
  • Examples: MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction, Jon Kabat-Zinn); MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy, for recurrent depression).
  • Buddhist responses vary:
  • Welcome: it relieves suffering; a skilful means that may lead some people to the Dhamma (e.g. Thich Nhat Hanh, the 14th Dalai Lama).
  • Caution: without ethics and wisdom it is not right mindfulness, and it may serve stress-relief only.

Mission 6

From sati to "mindfulness"

  • Sati (Pali): mindfulness, the 7th factor of the Eightfold Path (see Mission 5).
  • 1979: Jon Kabat-Zinn (b. 1944), who had trained with Zen and insight teachers, founded MBSR (Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction) at the University of Massachusetts Medical School.
  • He taught it without Buddhist words, so that anyone could use it.
  • His working definition (paraphrase): paying attention on purpose, in the present moment, without judging.
  • 2002: MBCT (Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy), by Segal, Williams and Teasdale, joined mindfulness with cognitive therapy for recurrent depression.
  • The Mindfulness Movement: the spread of these secular forms into health, education and business.
Mission 6

Mindfulness in health care

  • Pain management: MBSR began with patients in chronic pain; the aim is to relate differently to pain, not to remove it.
  • Stress and anxiety: eight-week courses teaching body scan, breathing practice and mindful movement.
  • Depression: NICE (England) recommends MBCT to help prevent relapse in people with recurrent depression.
  • Cancer care: programmes such as Mindfulness-Based Cancer Recovery support patients through treatment.
  • Addiction: Mindfulness-Based Relapse Prevention teaches people to notice cravings without acting on them.
  • This is "modern usage of Buddhist meditation as a form of therapy".

Mission 6

What is left out?

  • In Buddhism, mindfulness is right mindfulness: one factor of eight, joined to right view, right intention and ethics.
  • The suttas also speak of wrong mindfulness (MN 117): attention without the rest of the path.
  • Often left out of secular courses: refuge, the precepts, karma and rebirth, anatta, the goal of Nirvana.
  • Critics' example: a sniper or a fraudster can be very attentive. Is that mindfulness?
  • "McMindfulness": a critical term popularised by Ron Purser and David Loy (2013; Purser's book, 2019) for mindfulness sold as a product for self-improvement.
  • Replies: many teachers keep ethics in through kindness practices; and secular courses never claimed to be Buddhism.
Mission 6Diversity

Buddhist responses

  • Welcome: Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022), The Miracle of Mindfulness (1975), taught mindfulness to everyone, including business and political leaders: any reduction of suffering is good.
  • Welcome: the 14th Dalai Lama has worked with scientists (Mind and Life dialogues, from 1987) to study meditation's effects.
  • Skilful means: Kabat-Zinn calls MBSR an expression of the Dharma in a universal form; some Buddhists agree it can be a doorway to deeper practice.
  • Caution: Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944) has warned that, cut off from its ethical setting, mindfulness can end up supporting consumer capitalism.
  • Caution: Purser and Loy: without right intention, it may reduce stress yet leave greed untouched.
  • Spec: "how Buddhists have responded": a range, not one view.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The Mindfulness Movement is not really Buddhist."

  • For: it drops refuge, precepts, rebirth and Nirvana; what remains is a technique, not a path.
  • For: its aims (health, performance) differ from awakening; MN 117 warns of wrong mindfulness.
  • For: Purser and Loy: sold as a product for self-improvement, it may reduce stress yet leave greed untouched.
  • Against: the practice comes from Buddhist teachers; Kabat-Zinn sees it as universal Dharma.
  • Against: Thich Nhat Hanh and the Dalai Lama welcome it as a relief of suffering, the Buddha's own concern.
  • Against: Secular Buddhists argue the Dharma never needed metaphysics.
  • Judgement criteria: Is Buddhism defined by belief, practice or goal? Who decides what counts? Can a part carry the whole?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • "Sangha" means the monastic community, the noble ones (stream-entry and beyond), or the whole community of Buddhists.
  • In Thailand the Sangha (Mahanikai and Thammayut) traditionally taught, healed, counselled and led ceremonies; temporary ordination makes merit for parents.
  • Lay people give material support and make merit; monks give the Dhamma.
  • Novices keep the dasa sila (ten precepts) as training rules.

Mission 2

  • The Thai state has taken over schooling and health care; monks have found new roles (development, ecology, hospices, media); scandals have damaged trust in some monks.
  • The Sangha also faces state control, and movements such as Wat Phra Dhammakaya divide opinion.
  • Wat Phra Dhammakaya (1970): crystal-sphere meditation, mass ceremonies, merit-making and modern media; praised as renewal, criticised for commercialisation and its "true self" teaching.
  • Whether monastic life and the precepts are still relevant is debated: service and simplicity versus changed roles and scandal.

Mission 3

  • Puja, images and stupas focus devotion on the Buddha. Theravada: he does not respond; devotion trains the mind and makes merit. Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas can respond.
  • Dana makes punya (merit), which can be shared.
  • Wealth is valued when gained rightly, used well and held without craving.

Mission 4

  • Meditation (bhavana) is the samadhi training of the Eightfold Path: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • Samatha calms the mind (breath, kasinas, metta) and can lead to the jhanas; vipassana gives insight (see Mission 5).

Mission 5

  • Vipassana is insight into the three marks (anicca, dukkha, anatta); right mindfulness (sati) runs through all practice.
  • Meditation is now used as therapy; Buddhists both welcome and question this (see Mission 6).

Mission 6

  • Since 1979, secular mindfulness (MBSR, MBCT) has spread into health care, schools and business, drawing on Buddhist meditation without Buddhist belief.
  • Buddhist responses range from welcome (Thich Nhat Hanh, the Dalai Lama) to caution (Bhikkhu Bodhi; "McMindfulness").
