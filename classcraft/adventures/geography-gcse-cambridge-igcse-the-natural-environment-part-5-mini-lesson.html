Mission 1

Mission 1: How do scientists know what Earth's climate was like 800,000 years ago, long before thermometers?

Make a guess: How do scientists know what Earth's climate was like 800,000 years ago, long before thermometers?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 5.1.1 evidence of climate change: temperature records, ice cores, sea-ice positions, historic writing and paintings
Mission 1

The Quaternary period

  • The Quaternary is the most recent period of geological time. It began 2.58 million years ago.
  • We live in its latest part, the Holocene, which began about 11,700 years ago (0.0117 million years).
  • Through the Quaternary the climate has switched again and again between cold and warm.
Mission 1

Glacials and interglacials

  • Glacials: long cold periods when ice sheets grew. Interglacials: shorter warm periods, like today.
  • In the last 800,000 years, warm interglacials came "roughly every 100,000 years".
  • At the peak of the last glacial, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land; today about 10%.
  • In England, ice sheets dumped the boulder clay (till) that now forms the Holderness cliffs: tills about 18,000 to 13,000 years old.
  • Smaller swings happen too: the Little Ice Age (about the early 1300s to the mid-1800s; sources date its start differently), when glaciers advanced in many areas.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes climate in the Quaternary?
Mission 1

Ice cores: how they work

  • Snow builds up in layers on ice sheets and is pressed into ice.
  • As it does, "the ice encloses small bubbles of air that contain a sample of the atmosphere". Scientists measure the CO₂ in the bubbles.
  • The ratios of different water isotopes in the ice show past temperature.
  • The oldest continuous records reach 800,000 years in Antarctica and 123,000 years in Greenland.
Mission 1

What ice cores show

  • In the ice-age cycles of the last million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm". Before the Industrial Revolution it was 280 ppm or less.
  • CO₂ and temperature rose and fell together through glacials and interglacials.
  • Today CO₂ is "nearly 50% higher than it was before the industrial revolution".
Quick check
?In ice cores, the fastest natural CO₂ rise is about 15 ppm in 200 years. Today CO₂ rises 15 ppm every 6 years. About how many times faster is today's rise?
Mission 1

Temperature records

  • Central England Temperature: monthly data since 1659, "the longest instrumental record of temperature in the world". It covers a triangle from Lancashire to London to Bristol.
  • Global records using thermometers start around 1850. In 2024 the world was about 1.55 °C warmer than in 1850–1900.
  • Reliability: accurate and direct, but short; early records cover few places. Since 1974, Central England data "have been adjusted to allow for urban warming".
Mission 1

Sea-ice positions

  • Since 1979, satellites have mapped Arctic sea ice. September sea ice fell by about 40% between 1979–1988 and 2010–2019.
  • The lowest summer minimum on record is 3.39 million km² (2012); 2026's was 4.60 million km².
  • Older positions come from ships' logs and explorers' reports.
  • Reliability: satellite data are accurate and cover the whole Arctic, but only since 1979; older reports are patchy and only where ships went.
Mission 1

Historical sources: diaries and paintings

  • Diaries: in Edo (Tokyo), records of cherry blossom dates and blossom-viewing parties from 1601–1905 were used to estimate March temperatures.
  • They show two cold spells (late 1600s and early 1800s), matching cold periods found in Kyoto records.
  • Paintings: show frozen rivers, snowy winters or glacier fronts at a place and time.
  • Reliability: rich local detail, but one place, not measured, and artists may exaggerate. They must be checked against other evidence.
Mission 1

Weighing the evidence

  • Ice cores: longest and continuous; a direct sample of old air. Only polar ice, and "small artefacts can arise" where the ice holds many impurities.
  • Temperature records: most accurate; only about 175 to 370 years long.
  • Sea ice: satellite-accurate since 1979; patchy before.
  • Tree rings, pollen: long or yearly; local, and affected by more than temperature.
  • Diaries, paintings: local and descriptive; need checking against measurements.
  • Scientists trust a finding most when several types agree.
Quick check
?Which source is least reliable for working out the global average temperature of the 1600s?
Mission check

?A new Antarctic ice core is drilled. Ice from one layer, about 450,000 years old, has air bubbles with about 190 ppm of CO₂, and its water isotopes point to cold. What can scientists conclude?

Ice cores trap old air (CO₂) and record temperature in their isotopes, back 800,000 years. Scientists trust the record because ice cores, pollen, tree rings, temperature records and historical sources agree.

Mission 2

Mission 2: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Make a guess: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 5.1.2 natural climate change factors: Milankovitch cycles, sunspots, volcanic activity
Mission 2

Three natural causes

  • Earth's climate has always changed, through glacial and interglacial periods.
  • Three natural causes:
    • orbital changes (Milankovitch cycles)
    • solar output (sunspots and the solar cycle)
    • volcanic eruptions.
Mission 2

Milankovitch cycles

  • Eccentricity: Earth's orbit changes from more circular to more oval, in a cycle of about 100,000 years.
  • Axial tilt (obliquity): the tilt of Earth's axis changes, in about 41,000 years.
  • Precession: the axis wobbles, changing which season is nearest the Sun, about 23,000 years on average.
  • Together they change how much sunlight each latitude and season gets, pacing glacials and interglacials.
Quick check
?Why can Milankovitch cycles not explain the warming of the last 100 years?
Mission 2

Sunspots and solar output

  • Sunspots are "cooler", "darker" areas on the Sun's surface.
  • Their number rises and falls in a solar cycle of about 11 years. More sunspots go with a more active Sun.
  • Over a cycle the Sun's energy output varies by only about 0.1%.
  • The Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), "when there were few sunspots", fell within the cooler Little Ice Age, but that began centuries earlier.
  • But warming from human greenhouse gases since 1750 is "over 270 times greater" than extra warming from the Sun.
Mission 2

Volcanic eruptions: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere".
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight".
  • High winds spread the haze around the world, so less sunlight reached the surface.
Mission 2

How big, and how long?

  • Global average temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months.
  • There was "a measurable cooling of the Earth's surface for a period of almost two years".
  • The droplets slowly fall out of the stratosphere, so the cooling fades.
  • Ash falls out within days; it is the sulfur haze that cools the whole planet.
Quick check
?Why did Pinatubo's cooling last only about two years?
Mission 2

Natural or human? Weighing the causes

  • IPCC: from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019, human activity caused about 1.07 °C of warming (best estimate).
  • Natural drivers (the Sun and volcanoes) changed temperature by only −0.1 to +0.1 °C.
  • Natural causes are real but slow (orbits), small (the Sun) or short-lived (volcanoes).
  • Exam move: name each natural cause, give its timescale or size, then compare with the human effect.
Mission check

?Imagine a huge eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What would happen to global temperature?

Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere". The haze scattered sunlight and cooled the planet by about 0.6 °C for almost two years.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Make a guess: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 5.1.3 human causes of the enhanced greenhouse effect (fossil fuels, deforestation, agriculture)
Mission 3

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight (short-wave) passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground.
  • The warm ground gives off heat (long-wave radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb some of it and give out heat in all directions, including back down.
  • Without them, Earth's surface would be about −19 °C; it averages about 14 °C.
Mission 3

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • People add greenhouse gases faster than nature removes them.
  • More gas traps more long-wave heat, so the planet warms: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • 2024 levels: carbon dioxide 423.9 ppm, methane 1,942 ppb, nitrous oxide 338.0 ppb.
  • That is 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial levels (before 1750).
Quick check
?What makes the greenhouse effect enhanced?
Mission 3

Cause 1: burning fossil fuels

  • Coal, oil and gas release carbon dioxide when burned.
  • In 2025 burning fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ (projection).
Mission 3

Cause 2: farming

  • Cattle and sheep release methane as they digest grass. Flooded rice fields and manure release methane too.
  • Fertilisers release nitrous oxide.
  • Farming and waste together released about 228 million tonnes of methane a year (2010–2019), against 115 million tonnes from fossil fuels.
  • Methane is the second most important human-influenced greenhouse gas, after CO₂.
  • Your specification calls this agriculture.
Mission 3

Cause 3: deforestation

  • Trees store carbon as they grow. Cutting and burning them releases it as CO₂.
  • A cleared forest also stops absorbing CO₂: a lost carbon sink.
  • Land-use change, such as deforestation, released about 4.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025 (projection).
Quick check
?In 2025, fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ and land-use change (such as deforestation) about 4.1 billion tonnes. What percentage of this total came from fossil fuels, to the nearest whole number?
Mission 3

What is a carbon footprint?

  • A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emitted to supply what a person, organisation or country uses, measured in CO₂-equivalent.
  • A country's footprint counts emissions from making its imports, wherever they happen.
  • Its territorial emissions count only what is released inside its borders.
Mission 3

The UK's footprint

  • The UK's carbon footprint was about 699 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent in 2023.
  • Emissions from making its imports rose from 31% of the footprint in 1996 to 53% in 2023.
  • Imports from China make up 25% of those import emissions.
Mission 3

Two measures, two stories

  • Since 1996, the UK's territorial emissions fell by 50%; its carbon footprint by only 15%.
  • Defra links this to "a shift from manufacturing towards a service-based economy, alongside an increased reliance on imported goods and services".
  • Some cuts were real: the UK's last coal power station closed in September 2024.
Quick check
?Why has the UK's carbon footprint fallen much less than its territorial emissions?
Mission check

?A country's territorial emissions fell by 40% over 20 years. In the same years it closed its steelworks and began importing most of its steel and electronics. Has its part in climate change fallen by 40%?

The UK's territorial emissions halved, but its carbon footprint fell by only 15% (1996–2023), because 53% of the footprint now comes from imports. Part of the cut is real; part has moved abroad.

Mission 4: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Make a guess: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 5.2.1 present and predicted impacts at a range of scales: sea level, temperature, weather, food production
Mission 4

The sea is rising faster

  • Global sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The yearly rise sped up: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 1.9 mm (1971–2006), 3.7 mm (2006–2018).
  • Satellites: about 10 cm since 1993.
Mission 4

Where the extra water comes from

  • Thermal expansion: seawater takes up more space as it warms.
  • Melting land ice: water from glaciers and ice sheets runs into the sea.
  • Shares of the rise, 1971–2018: thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, other land water 8%.
  • Melting sea ice adds almost nothing: it is already floating, so it already displaces its own weight of water.
Quick check
?Which of these adds least to sea-level rise?
Mission 4

Retreating glaciers

  • Reference glaciers worldwide lost on average 214 mm of water-equivalent thickness per decade in the 1980s, and 896 mm per decade in the 2010s.
  • Glacier National Park, USA: about 80 glaciers larger than 0.1 km² around 1850; 32 in 2005.
  • People: in the upper Indus basin, glacier melt made up 40.6% of river runoff (1998–2007). Shrinking glaciers threaten that water supply in the long term.
Mission 4

Effects on the environment

  • About half of the species studied worldwide have moved towards the poles or uphill.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, heat stress able to bleach coral hit 84% of the world's reef area.
  • Climate zones are shifting: dry (arid) zones are expanding and polar zones shrinking.
Mission 4

Effects on people

  • Health: heat-related deaths average 546,000 a year, a rate 23% higher than in the 1990s.
  • Diseases carried by mosquitoes and ticks have spread to new areas.
  • Displacement: storms forced 17.9 million movements in 2025. Not every disaster is caused by climate change; scientists test each event.
  • About 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in places highly vulnerable to climate change.
Quick check
?Which is an effect of climate change on people, rather than on the environment?
Mission 4

Food: changing crop yields

  • Farm output keeps growing, but climate change "has slowed this growth over the past 50 years globally".
  • Losers: yields of some crops, such as maize and wheat, have fallen in many lower-latitude (tropical) regions.
  • Winners: some crops (maize, wheat, sugar beet) have gained in many higher-latitude regions.
  • In Africa, livestock on pasture grow more slowly.
Mission 4

Effects on the economy, jobs and settlements

  • Jobs: heat cost 640 billion potential working hours in 2024, especially in outdoor work such as farming and building.
  • Food prices (projection): climate change could raise cereal prices by a median 7.6% by 2050 (range 1–23%).
  • Settlements: by 2050, many low-lying megacities and small islands are projected to face, at least once a year, extreme sea levels that used to come once a century.
Mission 4

More extreme weather

  • Since the 1950s, heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense over most land regions.
  • Heavy rainfall has become more frequent and more intense over most land since the 1950s.
  • A heat extreme that used to come once in 10 years now comes 2.8 times per decade; at 2 °C of warming, 5.6 times (projection).
Mission 4

Predicted sea level

  • By 2100, compared with 1995–2014: a likely rise of 0.28–0.55 m if emissions are very low, and 0.63–1.01 m if they are very high.
  • These are projections: the range depends on how much people emit.
  • Impacts happen at every scale: global (sea level), national (food prices), local (a flooded street).
Quick check
?Which statement about climate change and crop yields is best supported?
Mission check

?Imagine two summers. In summer 1, 100 billion tonnes of floating Arctic sea ice melts. In summer 2, 100 billion tonnes of ice melts from Greenland's ice sheet on land, and the upper ocean warms. Which summer raises sea level more?

The extra water comes from melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets) and from thermal expansion of warming seawater, which explained half the rise in 1971–2018. Rising seas are one of many effects on people, places and the economy.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In June 2021 a Canadian village reached 49.6 °C, a national record. Could that have happened without climate change?

Make a guess: In June 2021 a Canadian village reached 49.6 °C, a national record. Could that have happened without climate change?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 5.2.1 present and predicted impacts at a range of scales: sea level, temperature, weather, food production
Mission 5

Heatwaves are changing

  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that heatwaves have become more frequent and more intense over most land regions since the 1950s.
  • A heat extreme that used to come once in 10 years now comes 2.8 times per decade.
  • At 2 °C of warming it is projected to come 5.6 times per decade.
Mission 5

Case study: the Pacific Northwest heatwave, June 2021

  • Where: western Canada (British Columbia) and the north-west USA.
  • Cause: "a slow-moving strong high pressure system", sometimes called a heat dome, which reached record strength.
  • Sinking air and winds blowing down the mountainsides heated the air further.
  • In British Columbia temperatures rose from 24 June to a peak on 28–29 June, passing 40 °C in many places.
  • Lytton set Canada's all-time record: 49.6 °C.
Mission 5

Consequences

  • Deaths: 619 deaths in British Columbia between 25 June and 1 July were identified as heat-related.
  • 98% died indoors, 67% were aged 70 or over, and 56% lived alone. Most were in homes without adequate cooling.
  • Services: 911 emergency calls doubled at the peak; some callers waited or were told no ambulance was available.
  • Property and environment: shortly after its record, Lytton was largely destroyed in a wildfire.
Quick check
?619 deaths in British Columbia were heat-related, and 67% of those who died were aged 70 or over. About how many people aged 70 or over died?
Mission 5

Could it happen without climate change?

  • Attribution science compares how likely an event is today with a modelled world without human-caused warming.
  • World Weather Attribution: the heatwave was "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change".
  • An event this rare would have been at least 150 times rarer without it, and the heat was about 2 °C hotter.
  • The heat dome itself is weather. Climate change made its heat more extreme.
Mission 5

Heat, drought and food

  • Heat and drought cut harvests. Farm output keeps growing, but climate change "has slowed this growth over the past 50 years globally".
  • Horn of Africa, 2020–2023: five failed rainy seasons in a row in southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya.
  • Most at risk: families who rely on rain-fed farming and herding.
  • Climate change made such farming droughts about 100 times more likely, mainly because heat raised evaporation.
  • Predicted: cereal prices could rise by a median 7.6% by 2050 because of climate change (projection).
Quick check
?According to the study, how did climate change mainly worsen the Horn of Africa drought?
Mission check

?A city has its hottest day ever, during a stalled high-pressure system. Which statement could an attribution study support?

For Lytton, World Weather Attribution found the heat "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change": about 2 °C hotter, and at least 150 times more likely. The heat dome was weather; warming made it deadlier.

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 6

Two kinds of response

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes of climate change, by cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewable energy, planting forests).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to the effects of climate change to reduce harm (sea walls, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Responses happen at international, national and local scales, and by individuals.
Mission 6

Before Paris: Kyoto

  • 1992: the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was adopted.
  • 1997: the Kyoto Protocol set binding cuts for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition. It came into force in 2005.
  • Limits: the United States signed but never ratified it; Canada withdrew from 15 December 2012. Developing countries had no binding targets.
Mission 6

The Paris Agreement, 2015

  • Adopted on 12 December 2015; in force from 4 November 2016.
  • Aim: hold warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels", and try to limit it to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), and updates it every five years.
  • 194 Parties. The United States' withdrawal took effect on 27 January 2026.
Mission 6

Who cuts, and who pays?

  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • The least developed countries produced less than 0.4% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
  • Finance: rich countries' goal of US$100 billion a year was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion). In 2024 a new goal was set: US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Fund for responding to Loss and Damage helps developing countries that are particularly vulnerable.
Quick check
?What is a nationally determined contribution (NDC)?
Mission 6

Is Paris working?

  • Strengths: almost every country has joined; every Party must set and update targets.
  • Limits: targets are self-chosen. UNEP (2025): if all NDCs are met, warming this century of 2.3–2.5 °C; current policies alone, up to 2.8 °C.
  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C). The Paris goal is judged over decades, not one year.
  • The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂; 2025 emissions were about 38.1 billion tonnes from fossil fuels plus 4.1 billion from land-use change.
Quick check
?The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂. In 2025, about 38.1 billion tonnes came from fossil fuels and 4.1 billion from land-use change. At 2025 levels, about how many years would the budget last?
Mission 6

A developed country: the UK

  • Government: the Climate Change Act 2008 made cuts a legal duty: at least 80% by 2050 (from 1990), raised to 100% (net zero) in 2019.
  • It set five-year carbon budgets and an independent Committee on Climate Change.
  • Results: 2025 emissions were 54% below 1990 (provisional); the last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Hardest to cut: domestic transport, 31% of net emissions in 2025.
Mission 6

An emerging country: India

  • India's updated NDC (2022): cut the emissions intensity of its GDP by 45% by 2030 from 2005.
  • About 50% of its installed electricity capacity to come from non-fossil sources by 2030; net zero by 2070.
  • It promotes "LIFE – 'Lifestyle for Environment'", a mass movement on individual lifestyles.
  • Emissions per person, 2025: India 3.11 t, UK 5.45 t CO₂-equivalent.
  • Evaluate: an intensity target cuts emissions per unit of GDP, so total emissions can still grow as the economy grows.
Mission 6

Local action, individuals and technology

  • Technology: from 2010 to 2019, the cost of solar energy fell by 85%, wind by 55% and lithium-ion batteries by 85%.
  • Local: in Germany, 220,000 members of 951 energy cooperatives have invested about €3.6 billion in renewable energy.
  • Individuals: choices about travel, home heating, diet and what to buy change a person's carbon footprint.
  • Government sets the rules that make the low-carbon choice easier: standards, taxes and grants.
Mission 6

Evaluating mitigation techniques

  • Renewables: costs have fallen fast, but they need storage and grids for windless or dark hours.
  • Removing CO₂ (forests, machines): the IPCC says removal is "unavoidable" to balance hard-to-cut emissions if net zero is to be reached; it does not replace cutting emissions.
  • International agreements: near-universal, but targets are voluntary and current pledges miss the goal.
Mission 6

Evaluating adaptation techniques

  • Sea walls: protect people in the short term but can "lock in" risk.
  • Irrigation: "effective in reducing drought risk" in many regions, but it uses scarce water.
  • Cost: the Netherlands' Delta Programme budget (about €29 billion) is below its estimated costs (about €38 billion).
  • Limits: above 1.5 °C, limited fresh water may set hard limits for small islands.
  • Exam move: mitigation tackles the cause and helps everyone, but slowly; adaptation helps one place quickly, but cannot remove the hazard.
Quick check
?Which is the best evaluation of adaptation compared with mitigation?
Mission check

?Country X is a low-income country with tiny historical emissions and worsening coastal floods. Under the Paris Agreement, what is expected of it, and from others?

The agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries take the lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still fall short: UNEP projects 2.3–2.8 °C.

Mission 7

Hazard, exposure, vulnerability

  • Hazard: the physical threat, such as sea-level rise or a storm surge.
  • Exposure: people and property in places that could be harmed.
  • Vulnerability: how likely people are to be harmed, and how able they are to cope and adapt.
  • Risk comes from the three together. The same hazard can bring very different risk.
Mission 7

Why some coasts face greater risk

  • Low land: little height above the sea, and no higher ground to move to.
  • Sinking land (subsidence) and soft coasts that erode easily.
  • People and money: dense populations and big cities on the coast; poor communities with little to spend on defences.
  • By 2050, many low-lying megacities and small islands are projected to face, at least once a year, extreme sea levels that used to come once a century.
Mission 7

The Netherlands: highly exposed

  • 26% of the Netherlands lies below sea level; with land at risk from rivers, 55% is at risk of flooding.
  • It is a high-income country: income (GNI) per person US$68,530 in 2025.
Mission 7

The Netherlands: the government responds

  • Since 2017, flood-protection standards have been written into the Water Act.
  • Target: by 2050, a yearly chance of dying in a flood of no more than 1 in 100,000 for everyone behind the main defences.
  • In the first check (2021), about 67% of dike sections met the standard.
  • The Delta Programme has a budget of about €29 billion for 2015–2050; costs are estimated at about €38 billion: a funding gap.
Quick check
?Which best explains why the Netherlands is less vulnerable than its height suggests?
Mission 7

Tuvalu: highly exposed

  • Tuvalu is a Pacific atoll nation; its land averages 2 m above sea level.
  • 9,492 people (2025); more than half live on the largest islet, in Funafuti atoll.
  • Sea level there was nearly 15 cm higher in 2023 than 30 years earlier.
  • By 2050, much of its land and key infrastructure is projected to sit below the average high-tide level.
Mission 7

Tuvalu: responses

  • Raising land: a project funded by the Green Climate Fund created 8 hectares of raised land on Fogafale, Funafuti's main islet (2025).
  • Mobility: the Falepili Union treaty with Australia (in force since 28 August 2024) lets Tuvaluans choose to live, work and study in Australia; the 2025 ballot offered up to 280 visa places.
  • Australia recognises that Tuvalu stays a state "notwithstanding the impact of climate-change related sea-level rise".
Quick check
?In 2025, income per person was US$68,530 in the Netherlands and US$9,780 in Tuvalu. How many times Tuvalu's is the Dutch figure, to 1 decimal place?
Mission 7

Small Island States: special challenges

  • Little land and no high ground: there is nowhere inland to retreat to.
  • Fresh water: above 1.5 °C of warming, limited fresh water may set hard limits to adapting on small islands.
  • Cost: defences for a whole nation of islands can cost more than a small economy can pay alone.
  • Fairness: small island developing states produced 0.5% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
Mission 7

Adapting farms and water supply

  • Farming: effective options include improved crop varieties, agroforestry (trees with crops) and community-based adaptation.
  • Storing water, conserving soil moisture and irrigation are among the most common ways to adapt; irrigation "is effective in reducing drought risk" in many regions.
  • Water supply: in Cape Town's drought that began in 2015, made about three times as likely by climate change, daily use was cut from over 1,000 million litres to just below 500 million, through limits such as 50 litres per person per day.
Mission 7

Reducing risk from rising seas

  • The IPCC's responses to sea-level rise include: protect (dikes, sea walls), accommodate (raise homes, flood-proof buildings), advance (build land out or up) and planned relocation.
  • Sustainable options: coastal wetlands protect against erosion and flooding.
  • Sea walls protect people in the short term but can "lock in" risk by encouraging building behind them.
  • The Netherlands mainly protects; Tuvalu advances (raised land) and prepares for relocation (Falepili).
Quick check
?Farmers in a region that is getting drier want to adapt. Which is an adaptation of their farming system?
Mission 7

Governments at different levels of development

  • Netherlands (high income): pays for its own defences, sets legal standards, plans to 2050.
  • Tuvalu (small island developing state): relies on international funds for raised land and a partner country for migration.
  • Trade-off: under the treaty, Tuvalu must agree with Australia any security or defence deal with another state.
  • Exam move: name the country, then give one impact, one response and one strategy, and say whether it is sustainable (for example, wetlands) or may lock in risk (sea walls).
Mission check

?Two coastal towns face the same rise in sea level. Town P is 1 m above sea level, with high incomes, sea walls built to a legal standard and a funded 30-year plan. Town Q is 3 m above sea level on a small island, with low incomes, no defences and no higher ground. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Both the Netherlands and Tuvalu are exposed. The Netherlands has about seven times Tuvalu's income per person, a €29 billion programme and legal flood standards. Tuvalu has little land and no high ground, so it relies on raised land and mobility to Australia. Tuvalu is more vulnerable.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Quaternary (2.58 million years to today) has cycled between glacials and interglacials; CO₂ now is about 50% above pre-industrial levels.
  • Evidence: ice cores (CO₂ and temperature, 800,000 years), temperature records (since 1659 in England), sea ice (satellites since 1979), tree rings, pollen, diaries and paintings. Each has limits; agreement between them gives confidence.

Mission 2

  • Natural causes: Milankovitch cycles (100,000, 41,000, 23,000 years), sunspots (11-year cycle, ~0.1% change), volcanoes (Pinatubo: −0.6 °C, about two years).

Mission 3

  • Fossil fuels, farming and deforestation add CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide faster than nature removes them: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • A carbon footprint counts emissions from everything consumed, including imports. The UK's fell far less than its territorial emissions.

Mission 4

  • Sea level rises because seawater expands as it warms and land ice melts.
  • Effects reach the environment (species, coral, glaciers) and people (heat, disease, displacement).

Mission 5

  • Sinking air in high pressure brings clear skies and little rain: heatwaves and droughts.
  • Pacific Northwest, June 2021: a heat dome; 619 deaths in British Columbia. Warming made it about 2 °C hotter.

Mission 6

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm.
  • Paris (2015): every Party sets an NDC; aim well below 2 °C, ideally 1.5 °C. Strength: nearly universal. Weakness: self-chosen targets that fall short.
  • UK: legal net-zero target; India: intensity target, 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030, net zero 2070.

Mission 7

  • Risk = hazard + exposure + vulnerability. Money, land, plans and partners change vulnerability.
  • Netherlands: protect (dikes, standards). Tuvalu: raise land and plan for migration.
