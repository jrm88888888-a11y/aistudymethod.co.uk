Mission 1: Why is chlorine's relative atomic mass 35.45 when no chlorine atom weighs that?

Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Almost all the mass is in a tiny, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons surround the nucleus. The atom is about 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

Mission 1

Reading the nuclear symbol

  • In ᴬZ X, Z = atomic (proton) number = number of protons. It fixes the element.
  • A = mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.
  • Neutral atom: electrons = Z. Positive ion: Z − charge. Negative ion: Z + charge.
  • ⁵⁶26Fe³⁺: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
  • ¹⁶8O²⁻: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons. The number of protons never changes.
Calculate
aHow many neutrons are in one atom of ²⁷13Al?
bHow many electrons are in one ²⁷13Al³⁺ ion?
cHow many electrons are in one ³¹15P³⁻ ion?
Quick check
?An ion X³⁺ contains 23 electrons and 30 neutrons. Which nuclear symbol represents this ion? (Each symbol is written with its superscript first and its subscript second.)
Mission 1

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different masses.
  • Chlorine-35: 17 p, 18 n. Chlorine-37: 17 p, 20 n.
  • Same electron configuration → same chemical properties.
  • Different mass → different physical properties: density, rate of diffusion.
Quick check
?Chlorine (atomic number 17) has isotopes with mass numbers 35 and 37. Pure samples of ³⁵Cl2 gas and ³⁷Cl2 gas are compared at the same temperature and pressure. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Masses on the carbon-12 scale

  • Atoms are too light to weigh in grams, so we compare them with ¹/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
  • Relative isotopic mass: the mass of an atom of an isotope compared with ¹/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ: the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with ¹/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • On this scale ¹²C is exactly 12. Relative masses have no units.
Mission 1

A weighted mean

  • Aᵣ = Σ(relative isotopic mass × % abundance) ÷ 100.
  • Boron: ¹⁰B 19.9% (10.01), ¹¹B 80.1% (11.01). (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199.2 + 881.9) ÷ 100 = 10.81.
  • Chlorine: ³⁵Cl 75.8% (34.97), ³⁷Cl 24.2% (36.97). (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100 = 35.45.
  • So 35.45 is an average: no single atom has it. It sits nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is about 3 times as common.
Calculate
?Lithium is 7.59% ⁶Li (relative isotopic mass 6.015) and 92.41% ⁷Li (7.016).
  • Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium to three significant figures.
Mission 1

Where the abundances come from

  • A mass spectrometer gives the data. For an element, each peak from a 1+ ion is one isotope.
  • x-axis: m/z = the isotope's relative mass. Peak height = its abundance.
  • Neon: m/z 20 (90.48%), 21 (0.27%), 22 (9.25%).
  • Aᵣ = (19.992 × 90.48 + 20.994 × 0.27 + 21.991 × 9.25) ÷ 100 = 20.18.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, not the Aᵣ.
Mission check

?Bromine's relative atomic mass is 79.90, almost exactly halfway between 79 and 81. What does this show?

Same reason as chlorine: isotopes. Aᵣ is a weighted mean of whole-ish isotopic masses, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Make a guess: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Mission 2

What a mass spectrometer tells you

  • It turns particles into positive ions and sorts them by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.
  • For a 1+ ion, m/z = the ion's relative mass.
  • For an element: one peak per isotope. Peak height = relative abundance.
  • So it gives each isotope's mass and abundance, and from these the element's mean atomic mass.
  • You only need to read spectra, not explain how the machine works.
Mission 2

Aᵣ from relative peak heights

  • Heights are often scaled so the tallest peak is 100. Then they don't add up to 100.
  • Aᵣ = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Boron: m/z 10 (height 24.8), m/z 11 (height 100).
  • (10 × 24.8 + 11 × 100) ÷ (24.8 + 100) = 1348 ÷ 124.8 = 10.8.
  • Sense check: the answer lies between the smallest and largest m/z, nearer the tallest peak.
Quick check
?The mass spectrum of element E shows three peaks from singly charged atomic ions: m/z 24 (relative abundance 100), m/z 25 (relative abundance 12.7) and m/z 26 (relative abundance 13.9). What is the relative atomic mass of E, calculated from these data?
Mission 2

Molecules: the molecular ion

  • A molecule that loses one electron is the molecular ion, M⁺.
  • The M⁺ peak is at the highest m/z (ignore a tiny peak one unit above it). Its m/z = Mᵣ.
  • That tiny M+1 peak comes from the 1.1% of carbon atoms that are ¹³C.
  • The other peaks are fragments (organic analysis lesson).
Quick check
?The electron-impact mass spectrum of a liquid has peaks at m/z 15, 29, 31, 45 and 46, plus a very small peak at 47. Which compound could it be? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)
Mission check

?The mass spectrum of gallium shows two peaks from 1+ ions: m/z 69 (height 100) and m/z 71 (height 66.4).
What is the relative atomic mass of gallium given by these data?

You never weigh one atom. The spectrometer sorts ions by m/z and counts them; masses × abundances give the average. (The data-table value, 69.72, uses precise isotopic masses rather than whole-number m/z.)

Mission 3: How many water molecules are in a single drop of water?

Make a guess: How many water molecules are in a single drop of water?

Mission 3

The mole is a count

  • You can't count particles one by one. You weigh them instead.
  • Amount of substance, n, is measured in moles (mol).
  • One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ particles: the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
  • Number of particles N = n × NA. Always name the particle: atom, molecule, ion, electron or formula unit.
  • 1 mol of CO2 = 1 mol of molecules, but 3 mol of atoms.
Mission 3

Three definitions

  • Mole: the amount of substance containing as many particles as there are atoms in exactly 12 g of carbon-12.
  • (Since 2019 the mole is defined as the amount of substance containing exactly 6.022 140 76 × 10²³ particles.)
  • OCR accepts either definition.
  • Avogadro constant: the number of particles in one mole.
  • Molar mass, M: the mass of one mole of a substance, in g mol⁻¹.
  • Say what the amount is of: "amount of NaCl", "amount of O2".
Quick check
?How many atoms are there in 0.500 mol of water, H2O? (NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)
Mission 3

Relative masses on the carbon-12 scale

  • Masses are compared with 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom (exactly 12).
  • Relative molecular mass, Mr: the average mass of a molecule ÷ 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • Relative formula mass: the same idea for compounds with giant (ionic) structures, such as NaCl.
  • Add up the Ar values. Brackets multiply everything inside: Mr of Mg(OH)2 = 24.3 + 2(16.0 + 1.0) = 58.3.
  • Mr and Ar are ratios: no units.
Quick check
?A student writes: 'Mr of carbon dioxide, CO2 = 44.0 g mol⁻¹'. Which comment on this statement is correct?
Mission 3

n = m/M: counting a drop

  • n = m / M. Units check: g ÷ g mol⁻¹ = mol.
  • A drop of water: m = 0.050 g.
  • M(H2O) = 2(1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0 g mol⁻¹. n = 0.050 ÷ 18.0 = 0.00278 mol.
  • N = 0.00278 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.7 × 10²¹ molecules.
  • About 1,700 billion billion: far too many to count, but easy to weigh.
Calculate
?A sample of sulfur dioxide, SO2, contains 1.20 × 10²³ molecules. Calculate its mass in g.
  • Data: Ar O 16.0, S 32.1; NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?How many ions are there in 5.55 g of calcium chloride, CaCl2? (NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹; Ar: Cl 35.5, Ca 40.1)
Mission check

?A one-carat diamond is pure carbon with a mass of 0.200 g. About how many carbon atoms does it contain?
  • Data: Ar C 12.0; NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹.

Same route as the water drop: mass → moles → particles. Weighing counts particles you could never count one by one.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A 500 cm³ hospital drip bag holds 9.00 g of salt per dm³. How many moles of salt are in the bag?

Make a guess: A 500 cm³ hospital drip bag holds 9.00 g of salt per dm³. How many moles of salt are in the bag?

Mission 4

What a concentration measures

  • Concentration = amount of solute ÷ volume of solution: c = n / V.
  • Units: mol dm⁻³. Also called molarity.
  • V is the whole solution, not the water added.
  • Square brackets mean concentration: [NaCl] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³.
Mission 4

n = cV and volume units

  • n = c × V, with V in dm³. 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³: divide cm³ by 1000.
  • 40.0 cm³ of 0.150 mol dm⁻³ KOH: n = 0.150 × 0.0400 = 0.00600 mol.
  • Rearranged: 0.0125 mol in 250 cm³: c = 0.0125 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³.
Quick check
?What amount of sodium hydroxide is present in 25.0 cm³ of NaOH(aq) in which [NaOH] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³?
Mission 4

Mass concentration and the drip bag

  • Concentration can be a mass per volume: g dm⁻³. g dm⁻³ ÷ M = mol dm⁻³.
  • The bag: 9.00 g dm⁻³ ÷ 58.5 g mol⁻¹ = 0.1538 mol dm⁻³. V = 500 cm³ = 0.500 dm³.
  • n = 0.1538 × 0.500 = 0.0769 mol of NaCl.
  • The video's 0.0770 mol uses M = 58.44; your data sheet gives 58.5, so 0.0769. Both are 0.077 mol to 2 s.f.: either is right.
  • Check another way: the bag holds 9.00 × 0.500 = 4.50 g; 4.50 ÷ M gives the same answer.
Calculate
?A solution contains 4.90 g of sulfuric acid, H2SO4, in 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate its concentration.
  • Data: Ar H 1.0, O 16.0, S 32.1. Answer in mol dm⁻³.
mol dm⁻³
Mission 4

Making a standard solution

  • A standard solution has an accurately known concentration.
  • Weigh the solid accurately; reweigh the empty container (weighing by difference).
  • Dissolve it in a little distilled water in a beaker.
  • Transfer to a volumetric flask with a funnel; rinse beaker, rod and funnel into the flask.
  • Add water to the mark: bottom of the meniscus on the line, at eye level. Last drops from a dropping pipette.
  • Stopper and invert several times to mix.
Quick check
?A student prepares 100.0 cm³ of 0.200 mol dm⁻³ CuSO4(aq) by placing 3.19 g of CuSO4(s) in a 100.0 cm³ volumetric flask, dissolving it in some distilled water, adding distilled water to the mark and mixing. The concentration is later found to be less than 0.200 mol dm⁻³. Which of the following could explain this result?
Mission 4

Dilution

  • Adding water changes the volume, not the amount of solute.
  • So c1V1 = c2V2.
  • 25.0 cm³ of 2.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl made up to 250.0 cm³: c2 = 2.00 × 25.0 ÷ 250.0 = 0.200 mol dm⁻³.
  • Method: pipette the stock solution into a volumetric flask, then make up to the mark and invert.
Quick check
?A student adds 600.0 cm³ of water to 200.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaCl(aq), giving 800.0 cm³ of solution. What is the concentration of NaCl in the diluted solution, in mol dm⁻³?
Mission 4

Concentrations in reactions

  • Use n = cV to get moles, then the equation ratio.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH reacts exactly with 20.0 cm³ of H2SO4(aq).
  • n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. Ratio 2 : 1, so n(H2SO4) = 0.00125 mol.
  • c(H2SO4) = 0.00125 ÷ 0.0200 = 0.0625 mol dm⁻³.
  • This is the calculation behind a titration: method and practice come in a later mission.
Mission check

?Seawater contains about 1.29 g of magnesium ions, Mg²⁺, per dm³. How many moles of Mg²⁺ are in 250 cm³ of seawater? Data: Ar Mg 24.3.

Same two steps as the drip bag: mass concentration ÷ M, then × volume in dm³. The bag holds about 0.077 mol of NaCl.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Make a guess: How can a few drops of indicator reveal how much acid is in vinegar?

Mission 5

Three ways to moles

  • From a mass: n = m ÷ M.
  • From a solution: n = c × V, with V in dm³ (cm³ ÷ 1000).
  • From a gas: pV = nRT (p in Pa, V in m³, T in K, R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹).
  • Gas shortcut at room temperature and pressure: n = V ÷ 24.0 dm³ mol⁻¹.
  • Back again: use the same equation, rearranged.
Mission 5

Worked example: mass to gas volume

  • Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) → ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g), acid in excess. What volume of H2 forms from 1.30 g of zinc?
  • n(Zn) = 1.30 ÷ 65.4 = 0.01988 mol (keep an extra figure until the end). Ratio 1 : 1, so n(H2) = 0.01988 mol.
  • At RTP: V = 0.01988 × 24.0 = 0.477 dm³.
  • Mass → moles → mole ratio → moles → the quantity you want.
Mission 5

Carrying out a titration

  • A pipette (with a filler) puts a fixed volume of one solution into a conical flask. Add a few drops of indicator.
  • A burette adds the other solution, with swirling, over a white tile. Go dropwise near the colour change.
  • Read the burette at the bottom of the meniscus, at eye level, to the nearest 0.05 cm³.
  • The volume added is the titre. Do a rough titre, then repeat until titres are concordant: within 0.10 cm³.
  • Rinse burette and pipette with their own solutions; the flask with distilled water only.
Mission 5

Equivalence point and end point

  • Equivalence point: exactly enough of one solution has been added to react with all of the other, in the ratio in the equation.
  • End point: the point where the indicator changes colour. A suitable indicator changes colour at (or very close to) the equivalence point.
  • So the colour change tells you when the moles match the equation. The burette tells you how much was needed.
Mission 5

Choosing an indicator

  • Phenolphthalein: colourless in acid, pink in alkali.
  • Methyl orange: red in acid, yellow in alkali; orange at the end point.
  • Strong acid + strong base: either indicator.
  • Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no sharp colour change, so no indicator is suitable.
Quick check
?Vinegar (ethanoic acid, a weak acid) is in the conical flask. Sodium hydroxide is added from the burette. Which indicator should be used, and what is the colour change at the end point?
Mission 5

Making a standard solution

  • A standard solution has an accurately known concentration.
  • Weigh the solid; then reweigh the empty weighing boat (weighing by difference).
  • Dissolve in a little distilled water in a beaker.
  • Pour into a volumetric flask with all the rinsings from the beaker and glass rod.
  • Make up to the mark with distilled water (bottom of the meniscus on the line). Stopper and invert to mix.
Mission 5

Worked example: a titration calculation

  • 25.0 cm³ of NaOH(aq) needs a mean titre of 21.40 cm³ of 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ H2SO4.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • Step 1, known solution: n(H2SO4) = 0.0500 × 0.02140 = 1.070 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 2, ratio: n(NaOH) = 2 × 1.070 × 10⁻³ = 2.140 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Step 3, concentration: c(NaOH) = 2.140 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0856 mol dm⁻³.
Quick check
?A student records these titres: 24.30 cm³ (rough), 23.85 cm³, 23.40 cm³ and 23.50 cm³. Which mean titre should be used in the calculation?
Quick check
?Now the mission: vinegar.
10.0 cm³ of vinegar is titrated with 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH, using phenolphthalein. The mean titre is 16.60 cm³. CH3COOH(aq) + NaOH(aq) → CH3COONa(aq) + H2O(l). What is the concentration of ethanoic acid in the vinegar, in mol dm⁻³?

The indicator turns pink once all the acid has reacted. The titre gives n(NaOH); the 1 : 1 ratio gives n(acid). That is about 50 g of acid per litre: typical of 5% vinegar.

Quick check
?A student titrates 25.0 cm³ of a weak acid, HA, with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. The student then titrates an identical 25.0 cm³ sample of HA to which 50.0 cm³ of distilled water has been added. How does the volume of NaOH needed to reach the equivalence point compare with the first titration, and why?
Mission check

?Limewater is a solution of calcium hydroxide. Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
25.0 cm³ of limewater is titrated with 0.0500 mol dm⁻³ HCl, using phenolphthalein. The mean titre is 18.40 cm³. What is the concentration of calcium hydroxide, in mol dm⁻³?

As with the vinegar: the colour change marks when the moles match the equation; the burette gives the volume; the ratio does the rest.

Mission 6: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Make a guess: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Mission 6

When you can't titrate directly

  • Some samples can't go straight into a titration: an insoluble solid (CaCO3, MgO), a slow reaction, or no sharp end point.
  • Back titration: add a known excess of a standard acid. The solid reacts completely.
  • Then titrate the leftover acid with a standard alkali.
  • Acid that reacted = acid added − acid left over.
Mission 6

Worked example: an indigestion tablet

  • A 1.250 g tablet (active ingredient CaCO3) is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The leftover acid needs 22.40 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Assume nothing else in the tablet reacts with acid.
  • Acid added: 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.02500 mol.
  • Acid left: n(NaOH) = 0.400 × 0.02240 = 0.00896 mol (HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1).
  • Acid reacted: 0.02500 − 0.00896 = 0.01604 mol.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.01604 ÷ 2 = 0.00802 mol; mass = 0.00802 × 100.1 = 0.803 g = 64.2% of the tablet.
Quick check
?In a back titration, an insoluble carbonate is added to excess hydrochloric acid, and the leftover acid is titrated with sodium hydroxide. Which amount equals the moles of HCl that reacted with the carbonate?
Mission 6

Dilutions and portions

  • A solution is made up to 250.0 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A 25.0 cm³ portion is pipetted out and titrated.
  • The portion holds one tenth of the total moles. Scale back up: × 10.
  • Diluting changes concentration, not moles: c1V1 = c2V2.
  • Track moles through every step. Scale at the right moment, and say what each number is the moles of.
Quick check
?10.0 cm³ of vinegar is diluted to 100.0 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A 25.0 cm³ portion needs 20.75 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O. What was the concentration of ethanoic acid in the original vinegar, in mol dm⁻³?

When the fizzing stops, the mixture is made up to 250.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need a mean titre of 27.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Calculate the percentage by mass of CaCO3 in the limestone. Mission check

?An impure sample of magnesium oxide, an insoluble base, is analysed by back titration. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l). Assume the impurities do not react with acid.
  • 0.400 g of the sample is added to 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). The leftover acid needs 14.30 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. What is the percentage by mass of MgO in the sample? (Aᵣ: O 16.0, Mg 24.3)

You never have to dissolve the solid in water. Dissolve it in a measured excess of acid, titrate what is left, and the difference is what the solid used.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Z = protons (fixes the element); A = protons + neutrons; ions differ only in electrons.
  • Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. Aᵣ is their abundance-weighted mean, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2

  • A mass spectrum of an element: one peak per isotope; m/z = isotopic mass; height = abundance.
  • Mean mass = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Molecules: M⁺ (highest m/z) gives Mᵣ.

Mission 3

  • n = m/M, and N = n × NA. Always name the particle.
  • Mr and Ar have no units; molar mass is in g mol⁻¹.

Mission 4

  • c = n/V, V in dm³; g dm⁻³ ÷ M = mol dm⁻³. Make standard solutions up to the mark in a volumetric flask.

Mission 5

  • Moles from a mass, a solution or a gas volume, then the mole ratio, then back.
  • The indicator changes colour at the end point, which a suitable indicator makes match the equivalence point.
  • Use the mean of concordant titres (within 0.10 cm³); never the rough titre.

Mission 6

  • Back titration: acid reacted = acid added − acid left over; then use the equation's ratio.
  • With dilutions and portions, track moles step by step and scale (× 10 for a 25 of 250 portion).
