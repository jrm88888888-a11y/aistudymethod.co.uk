Calculus · differentiation

The gradient of a curve as a limit

  • A curve has no single gradient, but a chord between two points does.
  • Fix the point at x=3x = 3 on y=x2y = x² and move a second point closer. The chord gradients approach a limit:

Calculus · differentiation

Differentiating axnaxⁿ

  • For integer powers: if f(x)=axnf(x) = axⁿ, then f′(x)=anxn−1f'(x) = anxⁿ⁻¹. Multiply by the power, then reduce the power by 1.
  • Differentiate a sum term by term. A constant term differentiates to 0, and axax differentiates to aa.
  • Rewrite fractions as negative powers first: x4​=4x−14/x = 4x⁻¹ and x23​=3x−23/x² = 3x⁻².
  • The derivative at x=ax = a, written f′(a)f'(a), is the gradient of the curve there.

Calculus · differentiation

Increasing, decreasing, tangents and normals

  • If f′(x)>0f'(x) > 0 the function is increasing; if f′(x)<0f'(x) < 0 it is decreasing; if f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0 the curve is momentarily flat.
  • The tangent at x=ax = a has gradient m=f′(a)m = f'(a) and passes through (a,f(a))(a, f(a)): y−f(a)=m(x−a)y − f(a) = m(x − a).
  • The normal is perpendicular to the tangent, so its gradient is −m1​−1/m.
  • On a GDC paper you can find f′(a)f'(a) numerically with your GDC, then write the line by hand.

Mission check

?A car's distance is s=2t2s = 2t² metres after tt seconds. How fast is it going at exactly 3 s?

The speed at an instant is the gradient of the distance–time graph at that point: the limit of chord gradients, which is the derivative. Here dtds​=4tds/dt = 4t, so at t=3t = 3 the car is moving at 12 m/s.

Calculus · integration

Anti-differentiation

  • Integration reverses differentiation. If dxdy​=f(x)dy/dx = f(x), then y=∫f(x)dxy = ∫ f(x) dx.
  • For integer n=−1n ≠ −1:
∫axndx=n+1axn+1​+C∫ axⁿ dx = (a xⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + C
  • Raise the power by 1, then divide by the new power. Integrate a sum term by term.
  • Add the constant of integration CC: every constant differentiates to 0, so the reverse step cannot know it.
  • A boundary condition (a known point on the curve) fixes the value of CC.

Calculus · integration

Definite integrals and area

  • The area between y=f(x)y = f(x) and the xx-axis from x=ax = a to x=bx = b, where f(x)>0f(x) > 0, is the definite integral
Area=∫ab​f(x)dxArea = ∫_a^b f(x) dx
  • Write the integral first, with its limits, then use your GDC to evaluate it. Examiners award a mark for the correct expression.
  • If ff is a rate of change, the area under its graph is the total change: the area under a velocity–time graph is distance, and under a flow-rate graph it is volume.

Calculus · integration

The trapezoidal rule

  • When you only have a table of values, split the area into nn strips of equal width h=nb−a​h = (b − a)/n and treat each strip as a trapezium.
∫ab​ydx≈2h​[y0​+yn​+2(y1​+y2​+…+yn−1​)]∫_a^b y dx ≈ h/2[y₀ + yₙ + 2(y₁ + y₂ + … + yₙ₋₁)]
  • First and last values once, every middle value twice.
  • If the curve is concave-down (bends like a hill), the trapezia sit under it and the rule underestimates; if it is concave-up, the rule overestimates.

Mission check

?You know a tap's flow rate (litres per minute) at every moment. How do you find how much water filled the bath?

Volume is rate × time, which is an area on a rate–time graph. When the rate changes, add up thin strips: the definite integral of the flow rate over the filling time gives the total volume. With only a table of readings, the trapezoidal rule estimates it.

Calculus · stationary points

Stationary points

  • A stationary point is where f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0: the tangent is horizontal.
  • Local maximum: f′f' changes from positive to negative. Local minimum: f′f' changes from negative to positive.
  • To find them, solve f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0, then test the sign of f′f' just either side.
  • On a GDC paper, your GDC can find a local maximum or minimum point directly.
  • A local maximum is not always the greatest value on a domain: check the endpoints too.

Finding and classifying stationary points

Question

Recap

Big ideas

  • The derivative dxdy​dy/dx or f′(x)f'(x) is the gradient of the curve (the limit of chord gradients) and the rate of change of yy with xx.
  • For integer nn, axnaxⁿ differentiates to anxn−1anxⁿ⁻¹; rewrite xna​a/xⁿ as ax−nax⁻ⁿ first. f′(x)>0f'(x) > 0 means increasing, f′(x)<0f'(x) < 0 decreasing.
  • Tangent at x=ax = a: gradient m=f′(a)m = f'(a) through (a,f(a))(a, f(a)). Normal: gradient −m1​−1/m through the same point.
  • ∫axndx=n+1axn+1​+C∫ axⁿ dx = (axⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + C for n=−1n ≠ −1; a boundary condition fixes CC.
  • The area under y=f(x)>0y = f(x) > 0 from aa to bb is ∫ab​f(x)dx∫_a^b f(x) dx: write the integral, then evaluate it with your GDC. The area under a rate graph is the total change.
  • Trapezoidal rule: 2h​[y0​+yn​+2(y1​+…+yn−1​)]h/2[y₀ + yₙ + 2(y₁ + … + yₙ₋₁)] with equal strip widths.
  • Stationary points: solve f′(x)=0f'(x) = 0. Classify by the sign of f′f' either side: ++ to −− is a maximum, −− to ++ a minimum.
  • Optimisation: use the constraint to get one variable, differentiate, set to zero, justify, answer in context. Check endpoints for the greatest or least value.
