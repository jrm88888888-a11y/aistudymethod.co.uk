Mission 1

Mission 1: Species have always gone extinct. So why are biologists so alarmed by the extinctions happening now?

Make a guess: Species have always gone extinct. So why are biologists so alarmed by the extinctions happening now?

Mission 1

Extinction, then and now

  • Species have always gone extinct, at a slow background rate.
  • Today the rate is far higher. Vertebrate species lost since 1900 would have taken 800–10,000 years to disappear at the background rate (depending on the group).

Mission 1

What is driving the losses?

  • Habitat loss: deforestation, clearance for farming, urbanisation, drainage of wetlands.
  • Over-exploitation: hunting, overfishing, collecting.
  • Pollution: pesticides, fertiliser run-off, plastics.
  • Invasive species, pests and diseases, spread by global transport.
  • Climate change: species' ranges shift; some cannot move.
Mission 1

Farming and biodiversity

  • Farming techniques that reduce biodiversity:
  • Monoculture: one crop over large areas; few habitats, few food sources.
  • Removing hedgerows, ponds and field margins; draining wetlands.
  • Pesticides and herbicides kill non-target species; fertilisers let a few fast-growing plants dominate.
  • Practices that promote biodiversity: polyculture; crop rotation; hedgerow conservation and maintenance; predator strips at field margins; integrated pest management and biological control in place of pesticides.
  • Intensive farming gives more food and income, at a cost to biodiversity.
  • The balance: payments to farmers for conservation (agri-environment schemes), hedgerows and wild margins kept.
Quick check
?A farmer removes the hedgerows between three small fields to make one large field of wheat, sprayed with herbicide. Which statement best explains the effect on biodiversity?
Mission 1

Climate change moves species

  • As climate warms, many species shift their ranges: towards the poles and uphill.
  • Real data (Chen et al. 2011, many species and regions combined): median shift 16.9 km per decade to higher latitude and 11.0 m per decade uphill.
  • Species that cannot move fast enough, or have nowhere to go (mountain tops, islands), suffer local extinctions.
  • Arriving species change communities; timing mismatches (e.g. flowering and pollinators) can follow.
  • Consequences for species diversity and ecosystem diversity: some habitats shrink or disappear.
Quick check
?Illustrative mountain. A plant grows between 1800 m and 2000 m altitude. The summit is at 2100 m. Assume its range moves uphill at 11.0 m per decade (the median rate above). About how long until the top of its range reaches the summit?
Mission check

?In the early 1800s the passenger pigeon was probably the most numerous bird in North America, with flocks of billions. Through the 1800s it was hunted on a commercial scale, and the eastern forests where it fed and nested were cleared for farming. The last known bird died in a zoo in 1914. Which explanation is best supported?

Extinction is natural; this pace is not. Human activity is removing species far faster than new ones arise.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Make a guess: If the last of a species survives safely in a zoo, has that species really been saved?

Mission 2

Why conserve biodiversity?

  • Ecological: species depend on each other. Losing a keystone species changes the whole community.
  • Genetic resource: wild relatives of crops carry alleles for disease resistance and drought tolerance.
  • Economic: food, timber, medicines, tourism; healthy soils (continuous monoculture depletes them).
  • Aesthetic: landscapes and wildlife people value and enjoy.
Mission 2

In situ conservation

  • In situ: protecting a species in its natural habitat.
  • Nature reserves and wildlife reserves.
  • Reserves need active management: control invasive species and poaching; keep habitats suitable.
  • Advantage: the species keeps its habitat, its interactions and its natural selection pressures.
  • The alternative is ex situ: keeping a species outside its natural habitat, in zoos, botanic gardens or seed banks. It can keep the species alive, but not its habitat or the species it depends on.
Mission 2

Conservation in Northern Ireland

  • ASSI (Area of Special Scientific Interest): land protected for its wildlife, habitats or geology.
  • SAC (Special Area of Conservation): protected habitats and species of European importance, e.g. Strangford Lough.
  • Biodiversity Action Plans: each district council lists the priority habitats and species of its area, with actions to conserve them.
  • DARD agri-environment schemes: payments to farmers to manage land for wildlife, e.g. hedgerows and river margins.
  • NI priority habitats and species lists (2010): the habitats and species most in need of action, e.g. the Irish hare.
  • Find your own council's Biodiversity Action Plan.
Quick check
?A farmer in Northern Ireland is paid to leave wide uncultivated strips along a river and to manage hedgerows for nesting birds. Which initiative is this?
Mission check

?A rare orchid survives in one wild meadow. Its seeds are now stored in a seed bank, and plants grow in a botanic garden. The meadow is due to be ploughed. Orchid seeds need a particular soil fungus to germinate. Which conclusion is best supported?

A species kept only in a zoo or a seed bank survives, but it is not truly saved. Conservation needs the habitat as well.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Make a guess: The carbon atoms in your body were once in the air. How do they keep being recycled?

Mission 3

Where carbon is stored

  • Carbon sits in stores (reservoirs):
  • the atmosphere, as CO2;
  • the oceans, as dissolved CO2 and hydrogencarbonate ions;
  • living organisms, as carbon compounds;
  • dead organic matter and soil;
  • fossil fuels, peat and rocks such as limestone.
  • Carbon atoms move between stores. None is made or destroyed.
Mission 3

The processes that move it

  • Photosynthesis removes CO2 from air or water and fixes it in producers.
  • Feeding passes carbon compounds along food chains.
  • Respiration in every organism, producers included, returns CO2.
  • Decomposition: decomposers respire the carbon in dead matter and faeces, returning CO2.
  • Combustion of fuels and biomass releases CO2.
  • Respiration, combustion and decomposition add CO2 to the air; photosynthesis removes it.
  • Say: "plants respire all the time" — not: "plants photosynthesise instead of respiring".
Quick check
?A student draws a carbon cycle diagram for a pond. Which set of labelled arrows is correct?
Mission 3

Slow stores: oceans, rocks and fuels

  • CO2 dissolves in the oceans. Marine organisms build calcium carbonate shells; their remains form limestone.
  • Where decomposition was incomplete (waterlogged, little O2), plant remains became peat and, over millions of years, coal.
  • Oil and natural gas formed from marine plankton buried in sediments with little O2.
  • Combustion returns that stored carbon to the air as CO2 very quickly.
  • Say: "fossil-fuel carbon was fixed by photosynthesis" — not: "fossil fuels are just minerals".
Quick check
?Which statement about the combustion of biomass, peat, coal, oil and natural gas is correct?
Mission check

?A peat bog has stored carbon for thousands of years in waterlogged soil. It is drained for farmland, and the peat dries out. What happens to much of the carbon in the peat?

Carbon only keeps moving while something moves it: photosynthesis takes it in; respiration, decomposition and combustion send it back.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Make a guess: Nearly four-fifths of the air is nitrogen. So why do crops so often run short of it?

Mission 4

Atoms go round; energy flows through

  • Every element an organism needs is recycled: carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and the rest.
  • Energy is not recycled. It leaves each trophic level as heat.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and wastes. They return elements as simple inorganic ions that plants can absorb.
Mission 4

How decomposers feed

  • Saprobiotic nutrition: feeding on dead or waste organic matter.
  • Step 1: secrete enzymes onto the food.
  • Step 2: extracellular digestion: large molecules hydrolysed outside the cells.
  • Step 3: absorb the small, soluble products.
  • Fungal hyphae spread through the food: large surface area for secretion and absorption.
  • Proteins and other nitrogen compounds end up as ammonium; phosphates are released as phosphate ions.
Quick check
?Which statement about the recycling of chemical elements in ecosystems is correct?
Mission 4

Nitrogen cycle 1: getting nitrogen in

  • The air is about 78% N2. Its triple bond is very strong: plants cannot use N2.
  • Nitrogen fixation: bacteria reduce N2 to ammonia (NH3).
  • N2-fixing bacteria use the ammonia to make amino acids.
  • Ammonification: saprobionts turn the nitrogen in proteins, nucleic acids and urea of dead matter and wastes into ammonia / ammonium ions (NH4⁺).
  • In other words, decomposers release ammonium compounds.
Mission 4

Nitrogen cycle 2: nitrate, and back to the air

  • Nitrification: ammonium → nitrite (NO2⁻) → nitrate (NO3⁻). An oxidation: needs oxygen.
  • Plants absorb nitrate (and ammonium) by active transport, then make amino acids and nucleotides.
  • Denitrification: bacteria convert nitrate back to N2. Happens in anaerobic soil, e.g. waterlogged.
  • Say: "nitrifying bacteria make nitrate from ammonium" — not: "nitrifying bacteria fix nitrogen".
Quick check
?A field is flooded for several weeks and the soil becomes waterlogged. Afterwards, the nitrate content of the soil is much lower. Which process best explains the loss?
Mission 4

Partners that feed plants

  • Mutualism: a relationship between two species in which both benefit.
  • Root nodules of legumes (peas, beans, clover: the Fabaceae): Rhizobium bacteria fix N2. Plant gets ammonia / amino acids; bacteria get sugars and shelter.
  • Say: "bacteria in the nodules fix nitrogen" — not: "legumes fix nitrogen themselves".
Quick check
?What are the benefits to each partner in the relationship between a legume and the bacteria in its root nodules?
Mission 4

Leaching and eutrophication

  • Leaching: rain washes soluble nitrate and phosphate out of the soil into rivers and lakes.
  • The nutrient no longer limits growth: algal bloom at the surface.
  • Algae block light; plants below die. Algae die too.
  • Aerobic bacteria (decomposers) multiply and decompose the dead matter.
  • Their respiration raises BOD (biological oxygen demand) and lowers dissolved oxygen.
  • Fish and other aerobic animals die.
  • Say: "BOD rises, so dissolved oxygen falls" — not: "BOD falls" or "the fertiliser poisons the fish".
  • BOD: the oxygen used by microorganisms in a water sample kept in the dark over a set time (usually 5 days at 20 °C).
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Water was sampled at three points on a river: A, upstream of a farm; B, 2 km downstream of the farm's drainage outlet; and C, 15 km downstream. Nitrate concentration (mg dm⁻³) was 1 at A, 12 at B and 3 at C. BOD (mg dm⁻³) was 2 at A, 14 at B and 4 at C. The number of fish species found was 8 at A, 1 at B and 6 at C. Which explanation fits these data?
Mission check

?A farmer grows clover, a legume, in a field for a year, then ploughs the clover into the soil. Wheat sown next needs much less nitrogen fertiliser than wheat sown after wheat. Which explanation is correct?

Air is full of nitrogen, but plants can use it only after microorganisms have converted it. That is why crops run short.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Make a guess: How can people keep taking food and materials from nature without wrecking it?

Mission 5

Farming for yield

  • Intensive farming: more food, more profit, but monoculture, hedgerow removal and pesticides cost biodiversity.
  • Higher yields from less land, but at a cost: fewer habitats, fewer species, polluted water.
  • Balance: keep hedgerows and field margins; agri-environment schemes pay farmers to manage land for wildlife.
Mission 5

Slurry and silage in rivers

  • Slurry (animal faeces and urine) and silage effluent leak or run off into waterways: organic pollution.
  • Bacteria decompose the organic matter by aerobic respiration: BOD rises, dissolved oxygen falls.
  • Clean-water animals die: stonefly and mayfly nymphs, fish such as trout.
  • Low-oxygen tolerant species increase: rat-tailed maggots, sludge worms (Tubifex), bloodworms.
  • Reduce it: sealed, leak-proof slurry and effluent tanks; no spreading on wet, frozen or sloping ground or in the winter closed period; buffer strips beside rivers; spread fertiliser at the rate and time crops need.
Quick check
?Below a farm where silage effluent has leaked into a stream, stonefly nymphs have disappeared and rat-tailed maggots have become common. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 5

Biological control

  • Pest: an organism that reduces crop or livestock yield, or harms people, causing economic loss.
  • Biological control: a predator, parasite or competitor of the pest keeps its numbers below the damaging level.
  • Example: the wasp Encarsia formosa lays eggs in glasshouse whitefly larvae.
  • Advantages over chemicals: specific to the pest; no residues or build-up in food chains; resistance rarely develops; agent breeds itself, so no repeated spraying.
  • An effective agent: specific to the pest; breeds fast; survives the local conditions; does not become a pest itself.
  • It controls, not eradicates: pest and agent settle at low numbers.
Quick check
?A grower controls glasshouse whitefly on tomatoes by releasing the wasp Encarsia formosa instead of spraying insecticide. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A dairy farm beside a river wants to increase its milk output without harming the river or the wildlife on its land. Which plan is most likely to achieve both?

Taking more from the land is sustainable only if what leaves the farm does not wreck the river and the wildlife around it.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Make a guess: Why can a warming of just a degree or two force species to move, or vanish from a place?

Mission 6

Species on the move

  • Each species survives only within a band of conditions: temperature, rainfall, season length.
  • As the climate warms, that band shifts towards the poles and up mountains.
  • New populations establish at the cool edge; old ones die out at the warm edge. The range shifts.
  • This happens over generations, through dispersal and the survival of young, not by individuals migrating.
  • No cooler place to go (mountain tops, poles), or barriers in the way? Local extinction, or extinction of the species.
  • Say: "the range shifts" — not: "the range grows" or "the animals move house".
Quick check
?The bird species of a mountain in New Guinea were surveyed in 1965 and again in 2012. For most species both the upper and the lower limits of the altitudinal range had moved upslope, by about 100 m on average, over a period in which the region warmed. What is the best interpretation?
Mission 6

Losers, winners and biodiversity

  • Likely losers: species with narrow tolerances, slow dispersal or specialist diets; mountain-top and polar species.
  • Roads, farms and towns can block movement, trapping species in shrinking ranges.
  • Local extinctions lower local biodiversity; some species may become extinct altogether.
  • Likely winners: species that spread easily, including some pests, pathogens and invasive species.
  • Species shift at different rates, so communities reassemble and interactions (predator–prey, pollinator–flower) change.
  • So climate change threatens biodiversity at the level of species and of whole ecosystems.
Quick check
?In a survey of forests in the eastern United States, seedlings of several northern tree species were found on average further north than mature trees of the same species, while at the southern edge of the range there were mature trees but few seedlings. What does this indicate?
Mission check

?A hypothetical small mammal lives only in cool meadows near the top of an isolated mountain. Over 40 years the region warms by 1.5 °C. Which outcome is most likely?

A degree or two moves the conditions a species needs. Species that can follow survive in new places; those with nowhere to go are lost.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Today's extinction rate is far above the background rate, driven by human activity: habitat loss, over-exploitation, pollution, invasive species and climate change.
  • Intensive farming raises food production but lowers biodiversity.

Mission 2

  • In situ conservation keeps species and their interactions in the wild; ex situ (zoos, botanic gardens, seed and tissue banks) is insurance. No single approach is enough.
  • NI uses ASSIs, SACs, council Biodiversity Action Plans, agri-environment schemes and priority lists.

Mission 3

  • Carbon moves between stores by photosynthesis (removes CO2), feeding, and respiration, decomposition and combustion (all add CO2).
  • Decomposers recycle carbon and every other element; without them, carbon piles up in dead matter such as peat.

Mission 4

  • Elements are recycled by microorganisms; energy is not. Plants cannot use N2 until bacteria fix it.
  • Fixation (N2 → NH3/NH4⁺), ammonification (organic N → NH4⁺), nitrification (NH4⁺ → NO2⁻ → NO3⁻, aerobic), denitrification (NO3⁻ → N2, anaerobic).
  • Leached nutrients → algal bloom → decomposition → BOD up, oxygen down → fish die.

Mission 5

  • Organic pollution raises BOD and removes oxygen; storage, timing and buffer strips cut it; biological control is specific and leaves no residues.

Mission 6

  • Warming shifts the conditions species need poleward and upslope; ranges follow over generations, and species with nowhere to go face local extinction.
