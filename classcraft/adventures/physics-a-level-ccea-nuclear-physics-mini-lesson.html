Mission 1

Mission 1: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Make a guess: If half a radioactive sample decays in an hour, why doesn't the rest decay in the next hour?

Quick check
?Predict first. Radioactive decay is described as random and spontaneous. Which statement correctly describes what this means?
Mission 1

Random and spontaneous

  • Random: you cannot predict which nucleus decays next, or when.
  • Spontaneous: nothing outside triggers it. Temperature, pressure and chemistry make no difference.
  • Every undecayed nucleus of an isotope has the same, constant chance of decaying per second. A nucleus does not "age".
  • With huge numbers of nuclei, the fraction that decays in a given time is predictable.
Mission 1

Half-life

  • Half-life: the time for the number of undecayed nuclei of an isotope to halve.
  • Same half-life whatever the starting amount: each nucleus has the same chance.
  • After n half-lives, the fraction left is (½)ⁿ: ½, ¼, ⅛, 1/16 …
  • The decay curve never quite reaches zero.
  • Half-lives range from far less than a second (polonium-214: 164 μs) to billions of years (uranium-238: 4.5 × 10⁹ years).
Mission 1

Worked example: whole half-lives

  • A sample has 6.4 × 10⁶ undecayed nuclei. Half-life 2.0 hours.
  • After 8.0 hours: 8.0 ÷ 2.0 = 4 half-lives.
  • Left: 6.4 × 10⁶ × (½)⁴ = 6.4 × 10⁶ ÷ 16 = 4.0 × 10⁵.
  • Decayed: 6.4 × 10⁶ − 4.0 × 10⁵ = 6.0 × 10⁶.
  • Read the question: "left" or "decayed"?
Quick check
?A hospital prepares a dose of technetium-99m (half-life 6.0 h) that has an activity of 800 MBq at 08:00. What will its activity be at 14:00 the next day?
Mission 1

Activity and count rate

  • Activity A: the number of nuclei that decay per second. Unit: becquerel, 1 Bq = 1 decay per second.
  • Count rate: the number of decays a detector records per second (or minute).
  • Count rate < activity: the detector catches only some of the radiation.
  • Both are proportional to the number of undecayed nuclei, so both halve every half-life.
Mission 1

Measuring a half-life

  • Measure the background count rate first.
  • Record the count in equal time intervals. Subtract the background from each: corrected count rate.
  • Plot corrected count rate against time.
  • Read the time to fall from any value to half of it. Repeat from several starting points; average.
  • If you forget to subtract the background, the curve levels off at the background, not zero, and the half-life you read off is too long.
Quick check
?The background count rate in a laboratory is 20 counts min⁻¹. A detector near a source records 340 counts min⁻¹ at t = 0 and 100 counts min⁻¹ at t = 6.0 h, background included both times. What is the half-life of the source?
Mission 1

The decay constant

  • Decay constant λ: the probability per unit time that a nucleus decays. Unit: s⁻¹ (or h⁻¹, yr⁻¹).
  • Larger λ → faster decay → shorter half-life.
  • Spec form A = −λN: the minus sign shows that N is falling. Activity is quoted as a positive number of decays per second, λN. λ is the fraction of the nuclei decaying per second.
Mission 1

The exponential decay law

  • N = N0e−λt: N0 nuclei at t = 0; N at time t.
  • A = A0e−λt.
  • Half-life: t½ = 0.693 / λ.
  • Works for any time, not just whole half-lives.
Mission 1

Worked example: carbon dating

  • Old wood has 0.40 of the carbon-14 per gram of carbon found in living wood. Half-life 5730 years.
  • λ = 0.693 ÷ 5730 = 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ yr⁻¹.
  • N/N0 = e−λt → t = ln(N0/N) ÷ λ = ln(1/0.40) ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴.
  • t = 0.916 ÷ 1.21 × 10⁻⁴ = 7.6 × 10³ years.
  • Same answer from activities: A/A0 = N/N0 = 0.40.
  • Assumes the carbon-14 fraction in living things has stayed the same.
Quick check
?A sample contains 8.0 × 10²⁰ nuclei of iodine-131, which has a half-life of 8.0 days. How many iodine-131 nuclei remain after 20 days?
Mission check

?A sample of radon-222 is sealed in a tube. What is the probability that a given nucleus in the original sample has decayed after three half-lives?

Each surviving nucleus keeps the same chance of decaying. So in the next hour, half of what is left decays: a quarter of the original sample, not the other half.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a straight-line graph reveal the half-life of a source whose count falls along a curve?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the half-life of a source whose count falls along a curve?

Mission 2

Making the curve straight

  • A = A0e−λt. Take natural logs of both sides:
  • ln A = ln A0 − λt.
  • Compare y = c + mx: plot ln A against t.
  • Straight line. Gradient = −λ. Intercept = ln A0.
  • Works for N, for activity, and for corrected count rate C.
  • A straight line also tests the model: random decay gives exponential fall.
Mission 2

Why half-life = ln 2 / λ

  • After one half-life, N = N0/2.
  • So ½ = e−λt½. Take logs: ln ½ = −λt½.
  • ln ½ = −ln 2. So λt½ = ln 2.
  • t½ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693 / λ.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • ln(C / s⁻¹) is 6.20 at t = 0 and 4.47 at t = 30 min (best-fit line).
  • Gradient = (4.47 − 6.20) ÷ 30 = −0.0577 min⁻¹. So λ = 0.0577 min⁻¹.
  • t½ = ln 2 ÷ 0.0577 = 12.0 min.
  • Use the line, not two data points: it averages out the random scatter.
Quick check
?To determine the half-life of a nuclide, a student plots a graph of ln(corrected count rate) against time. The graph is a straight line with a gradient of −0.0231 min⁻¹. What is the half-life of the nuclide?
Mission 2

Measuring a half-life: protactinium

  • A sealed bottle holds a uranium compound in water, with an organic solvent layer on top.
  • Shake: protactinium-234m dissolves into the solvent. Let the layers separate.
  • GM tube beside the top layer only. Count every 10 s for about 5 minutes.
  • Subtract the background (measured first). Plot ln(corrected count rate) against t.
  • Gradient = −λ; t½ = 0.693/λ. Expected: about 70 s.
Mission check

?A hospital technician checks a short-lived medical tracer. She plots ln(activity / MBq) against time t in hours. The best-fit straight line falls from 6.0 at t = 0 to 4.6 at t = 6.0 h. What is the half-life of the tracer?

Taking logs turns e−λt into a straight line. Its gradient is −λ, and the half-life is ln 2 ÷ λ.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Make a guess: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Mission 3

Mass is a form of energy

  • E = Δmc²: every mass m has a rest energy mc².
  • c² is huge: 1 kg ↔ 9.0 × 10¹⁶ J.
  • Every energy change changes mass, chemical ones too; there Δm is far too small to measure.
  • Nuclear reactions: Δm is big enough to measure. The energy released leaves as kinetic energy of the products and as photons.
Mission 3

Units for tiny masses

  • Atomic mass unit: 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J.
  • 1 u ↔ 931.5 MeV: so 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻².
  • A mass in MeV c⁻²: multiply by c² and you have the energy in MeV. No c needed.
Quick check
?Radium-226 decays by α emission: ²²⁶88Ra → ²²²86Rn + ⁴2α. The atomic masses are 226.025410 u for radium-226, 222.017578 u for radon-222 and 4.002603 u for helium-4 (the electron masses cancel). Take 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV c⁻², 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in the decay of one radium-226 nucleus?
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement correctly describes the binding energy of a nucleus?
Mission 3

Missing mass and binding energy

  • A nucleus has less mass than its separate protons and neutrons.
  • Mass defect: Δm = (total mass of separate nucleons) − (mass of nucleus).
  • Binding energy = Δm c²: the energy needed to separate the nucleus completely into its nucleons.
  • The same energy was released when the nucleus formed. That is where the "missing" mass went.
  • Binding energy is not stored in the nucleus. More binding = less mass.
Mission 3

Worked example: helium-4

  • Data: mp = 1.007276 u, mn = 1.008665 u, helium-4 nucleus 4.001506 u.
  • Nucleons: 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u.
  • Δm = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u.
  • Binding energy = 0.030376 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV.
  • Per nucleon: 28.30 ÷ 4 = 7.07 MeV.
  • In SI: 0.030376 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 5.042 × 10⁻²⁹ kg; × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.54 × 10⁻¹² J.
  • Use nuclear masses (or atomic masses on both sides, so the electrons cancel).
Mission 3

The binding energy per nucleon curve

  • Binding energy per nucleon = binding energy ÷ nucleon number A. Higher = more tightly bound = more stable.
  • Rises steeply for light nuclei (²H: 1.1 MeV), with a spike at ⁴He (7.1 MeV).
  • Peak near A ≈ 56–62 (iron, nickel): about 8.8 MeV.
  • Falls slowly for heavy nuclei: ²³⁸U about 7.6 MeV.
  • Energy is released when the products have more total binding energy than the reactants:
  • Fusion: light nuclei (left of the peak) join.
  • Fission: heavy nuclei (right of the peak) split.
  • Energy released = (total binding energy after) − (total binding energy before). Total = A × (per nucleon value).
Quick check
?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²³⁶92U 7.59 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.51 MeV; ¹⁴¹56Ba 8.33 MeV. A ²³⁶92U nucleus splits into a ⁹²36Kr nucleus, a ¹⁴¹56Ba nucleus and three free neutrons. What is the energy change?
Mission check

?In a fusion reaction, a helium-3 nucleus and a deuterium nucleus fuse: ³2He + ²1H → ⁴2He + ¹1H. The atomic masses are ³He: 3.016029 u, ²H: 2.014102 u, ⁴He: 4.002603 u and ¹H: 1.007825 u (the electron masses on the two sides cancel). How much energy is released in one reaction? Use 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

The missing mass left as energy when the nucleus formed. For helium-4, 0.0304 u went out as 28.3 MeV, and the same 28.3 MeV is needed to pull it apart again.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Splitting a uranium nucleus gives out energy. How can breaking something apart release energy?

Make a guess: Splitting a uranium nucleus gives out energy. How can breaking something apart release energy?

Mission 4

Nuclear fission

  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei of comparable mass, plus a few neutrons (2 or 3 for uranium-235).
  • Spontaneous fission: no trigger (e.g. californium-252). Rare for most nuclei.
  • Induced fission: the nucleus absorbs a neutron first.
  • ²³⁵92U + ¹0n → ²³⁶92U → ¹⁴¹56Ba + ⁹²36Kr + 3 ¹0n
  • Balance it: nucleon numbers 235 + 1 = 141 + 92 + 3(1) = 236; proton numbers 92 = 56 + 36.
Quick check
?Some very heavy nuclides, such as californium-252, can undergo spontaneous fission, whereas uranium-235 in a reactor undergoes neutron-induced fission. Which statement about spontaneous fission is correct?
Mission 4

Where the energy comes from

  • Heavy nuclei sit right of the peak on the binding energy per nucleon curve: about 7.6 MeV.
  • The fragments sit nearer the peak: about 8.5 MeV. More tightly bound.
  • More binding → less total mass → the mass difference is released: E = Δmc².
  • About 200 MeV per fission, mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments; the rest as neutrons, γ photons and later decays.
Mission 4

Worked example: energy from one fission

  • ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n. Masses (u): ²³⁵U 235.043928; ¹⁴¹Ba 140.914403; ⁹²Kr 91.926173; n 1.008665.
  • Before: 235.043928 + 1.008665 = 236.052593 u.
  • After: 140.914403 + 91.926173 + 3(1.008665) = 235.866571 u.
  • Δm = 0.186022 u → × 931.5 = 173 MeV (2.77 × 10⁻¹¹ J).
  • Count the neutrons on both sides. Atomic masses: electrons cancel.
  • 1 kg of uranium-235 (if every nucleus split, at about 200 MeV each): about 8 × 10¹³ J.
Quick check
?A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two fragments and 2 free neutrons. The fragments contain 234 nucleons in total. The binding energy per nucleon is 7.6 MeV for uranium-235 and about 8.5 MeV for the fragments. A free neutron has no binding energy. Roughly how much energy is released?
Mission 4

Chain reaction and critical mass

  • Each fission releases 2–3 neutrons. Each can cause another fission: a chain reaction.
  • On average more than one neutron per fission causes fission: rate grows (a bomb).
  • Exactly one: steady rate (a reactor).
  • Less than one: the chain dies away.
  • Neutrons are lost through the surface. A small lump loses too many.
  • Critical size: the minimum size (mass) of fuel that sustains a chain reaction.
Quick check
?In a reactor core, each fission of uranium-235 releases 2.5 neutrons on average. Of these, on average 0.7 escape from the core and 0.8 are absorbed by the control rods and other materials without causing fission. What happens to the rate of fission?
Quick check
?Predict first. In a gas-cooled reactor, the uranium fuel rods sit in channels through a large block of graphite, which is the moderator. Carbon dioxide gas carries thermal energy from the core. What is the role of the graphite?
Mission 4

Inside a thermal reactor

  • Fuel rods: uranium enriched in uranium-235.
  • Moderator (water, graphite): slows fast neutrons by collisions. Slow (thermal) neutrons are far more likely to cause fission of uranium-235.
  • Control rods (boron, cadmium): absorb neutrons. Push in → fewer fissions → less power.
  • Coolant (water, carbon dioxide gas): carries thermal energy out of the core.
  • Heat exchanger: the coolant heats a separate water circuit; its steam drives the turbine.
  • Shielding: thick steel and concrete absorb neutrons and γ rays from the core.
Quick check
?High-level waste from a reactor contains caesium-137, a fission product with a half-life of 30 years. The waste is kept in a store for 90 years. What percentage of the initial caesium-137 activity remains at the end of this time?
Mission check

?Plutonium-240 nuclei can split on their own into two fragments and two free neutrons. In one such split, the total binding energy rises from about 1810 MeV (the plutonium nucleus) to about 2010 MeV (the two fragments). How much energy is released, and why?

Breaking a heavy nucleus apart releases energy because the pieces are more tightly bound than the original. Their total mass is less, and the mass lost leaves as about 200 MeV per fission.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A neutron bounces off nuclei like a snooker ball. Which nuclei would slow it down fastest?

Make a guess: A neutron bounces off nuclei like a snooker ball. Which nuclei would slow it down fastest?

Mission 5

Moderation: neutron snooker

  • Fission makes fast neutrons (about 2 MeV). Uranium-235 captures slow (thermal) ones far more readily.
  • The moderator slows them by elastic collisions: kinetic energy is passed on to its nuclei.
  • Neutron hits a heavy nucleus: it bounces back at almost the same speed. Little energy lost.
  • Neutron hits a nucleus of similar mass: it can pass on almost all its kinetic energy.
  • So the best moderators have light nuclei: hydrogen, deuterium, carbon.
Quick check
?A reactor is running at a steady power. The operators want to raise its power output slightly and then hold it at the new level. What should they do with the control rods?
Mission 5

Safety by design

  • Fuel: uranium enriched to a few per cent uranium-235, as pellets sealed in metal cladding.
  • Shielding: steel pressure vessel inside thick concrete: absorbs neutrons and γ rays.
  • Remote handling: spent fuel is moved by machines, behind shielding or under water. Nobody gets close.
  • Emergency shut-down: control rods drop fully in, automatically, within seconds.
  • Fission stops, but fission products keep decaying: cooling must continue after shut-down.
Mission 5

Radioactive waste

  • High-level: spent fuel, fission products. Very active and hot. Cooled in ponds for years; may be vitrified (sealed in glass) in steel canisters; then deep geological disposal.
  • Intermediate-level: fuel cladding, reactor parts. Set in concrete in steel drums; stored.
  • Low-level: gloves, clothing, tools. Compacted, sealed, buried near the surface.
  • Handling matches activity: high-level only by remote handling behind shielding; low-level by hand with simple precautions.
  • Environmental impact: long-lived isotopes could reach groundwater over thousands of years; sites must stay stable and secure for that long.
Quick check
?Two radioactive isotopes in spent fuel start with the same activity. Isotope P has a half-life of 30 years; isotope Q has a half-life of 24 000 years. Which statement about storing them is correct?
Mission 5

Weighing nuclear power

  • Benefits: very low life-cycle CO2 (similar to wind); steady output in any weather; tiny fuel mass; fuel is easy to stockpile (years of supply in a small volume).
  • Risks: rare but severe accidents (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011); waste that stays dangerous for millennia.
  • Economic: high cost and long build times; costly decommissioning; uranium is finite.
  • Security: fissile material and enrichment know-how could be diverted to weapons; plants need protecting.
  • Physics supplies the evidence (energy per kg, emissions, risk data). Society weighs it to decide.
Mission check

?Fast neutrons must be slowed down before a shield can absorb them. Why is a block of hydrogen-rich plastic better at slowing them than a block of lead of the same thickness?

Light nuclei slow neutrons fastest: a neutron passes on the largest share of its kinetic energy to a nucleus close to its own mass. A heavy nucleus just bounces it back.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Mission 6

Nuclear fusion

  • Fusion: two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, often with a spare particle.
  • ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n + energy
  • Light nuclei sit on the steep left side of the binding energy per nucleon curve. Heavy nuclei sit on the gentle right side.
  • Fusion (from the left) and fission (from the right) both move nuclei towards the peak near iron: more tightly bound.
  • More binding → less total mass → energy released (E = Δmc²).
Quick check
?In the fusion reaction ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n, the binding energies per nucleon are 1.11 MeV for ²1H, 2.83 MeV for ³1H and 7.07 MeV for ⁴2He. A free neutron has no binding energy. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Mission 6

Worked example: deuterium–deuterium fusion

  • Per nucleon, D–T fusion gives more: 17.6 MeV ÷ 5 = 3.5 MeV, against about 0.85 MeV for uranium fission.
  • ²1H + ²1H → ³2He + ¹0n. Atomic masses (u): ²H 2.014102; ³He 3.016029; n 1.008665.
  • Before: 2 × 2.014102 = 4.028204 u.
  • After: 3.016029 + 1.008665 = 4.024694 u.
  • Δm = 0.003510 u → × 931.5 = 3.27 MeV.
  • Electrons: 2 on each side, so atomic masses are fine.
Quick check
?One reaction in the Sun's core is ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2 ¹H. The atomic masses are 3.016029 u for helium-3, 4.002603 u for helium-4 and 1.007825 u for hydrogen-1 (the electron masses balance). Take 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Quick check
?Predict first. Hydrogen fusion in a star needs a core temperature of the order of 10⁷ K. Why is such a high temperature needed?
Mission 6

The electric barrier

  • Nuclei are positive: they repel.
  • The strong force attracts only within about 10⁻¹⁵ m. The nuclei must get that close.
  • Two protons 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: electric potential energy about 0.7 MeV.
  • So the nuclei need very high kinetic energy: a very high temperature (about 10⁷ K in the Sun's core).
  • Only the fastest nuclei fuse, helped by a quantum effect (tunnelling). The rest just bounce off.
Quick check
?Predict first. Hydrogen makes up most of the Sun's outer layers as well as its core, yet hydrogen fusion happens only in the core. Why?
Mission 6

Fusion in stars

  • A cloud of gas and dust collapses under its own gravity.
  • Gravitational potential energy → thermal energy: the core gets hotter and denser.
  • Hot and dense enough: hydrogen fuses into helium. Overall: 4 ¹H → ⁴He + 2e⁺ + 2ν. Including the positrons' annihilation, about 26.7 MeV in total.
  • High density → frequent collisions → enough fusions per second to keep the star shining.
  • Outward gas pressure and radiation pressure balance inward gravity: a stable star.
  • This is gravitational confinement: the star's own gravity holds its hot plasma together.
Mission 6

ITER: fuel and plasma

  • ITER: an international experimental fusion reactor (a tokamak), being built in France.
  • D–T reaction: ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He (3.5 MeV) + ¹0n (14.1 MeV).
  • Deuterium: extracted from sea water. Tritium: radioactive and scarce; ITER uses existing stocks from fission reactors. A future power plant would breed it from lithium using the fusion neutrons.
  • Temperature: about 1.5 × 10⁸ K, ten times the Sun's core (much lower density, so it must be hotter).
  • At this temperature the fuel is a plasma: bare nuclei and free electrons. It must never touch the walls.
Quick check
?Predict first. In ITER, beams of fast particles are fired into the plasma to heat it. Why must the particles be neutral atoms rather than ions?
Mission 6

Three ways to heat the plasma

  • Ohmic heating: a large current is driven through the plasma, which heats it like a resistor. Less effective as it gets hotter, because its resistance falls.
  • Neutral beam injection: fast neutral deuterium atoms are fired in. Neutral, so the magnetic field does not deflect them. Collisions share out their kinetic energy.
  • Radio-frequency heating: electromagnetic waves at frequencies matched to the circling of ions or electrons in the field. They absorb the energy.
Mission 6

The tokamak

  • Vacuum vessel: a doughnut-shaped (toroidal) steel chamber holding the plasma in a vacuum.
  • Magnetic confinement: coils make a field around the doughnut; a current in the plasma adds a second field. Together: helical field lines.
  • Charged particles spiral along the field lines and stay off the walls.
  • Blanket: lines the vessel. Absorbs the neutrons' energy as heat and shields the magnets. In a power plant it would also breed tritium from lithium and supply heat for steam.
Quick check
?Predict first. In laser-driven fusion, laser pulses compress a tiny fuel pellet in about a billionth of a second. What holds the compressed fuel together long enough for fusion to happen?
Mission 6

Three ways to confine a plasma

  • Gravitational: a star's own gravity holds its plasma. Needs a star-sized mass.
  • Inertial: lasers crush and heat a tiny fuel pellet in billionths of a second. Its own inertia holds it together long enough to fuse before it flies apart.
  • Magnetic: magnetic fields hold the charged plasma away from the walls (tokamak). Lasts seconds to minutes.
Mission 6

Fusion power: for and against

  • Difficulties: reaching and holding 10⁸ K; unstable plasma; walls damaged by fast neutrons; breeding enough tritium; getting more energy out than in, continuously.
  • Advantages: plentiful fuel; no CO2 in operation; no chain reaction, so no runaway; no long-lived high-level waste.
  • Disadvantages: no power station yet; very high cost and decades of development; tritium is radioactive; neutrons make the structure radioactive (mostly shorter-lived).
Mission check

?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²1H 1.1 MeV; ⁴2He 7.1 MeV; ²⁰10Ne 8.0 MeV; ²⁸13Al 8.3 MeV; ⁴⁰20Ca 8.6 MeV; ⁵⁶26Fe 8.8 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.5 MeV; ¹⁴⁴56Ba 8.3 MeV; ²³⁶92U 7.6 MeV. Which pair of processes would both release energy?

In both fusion and fission the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less: E = Δmc². Both processes move nuclei towards the most tightly bound size, near iron. Light nuclei fuse up to it; heavy nuclei split down to it. Either way the total mass falls, and that mass leaves as energy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Decay is random and spontaneous; each nucleus has a constant chance of decaying.
  • Half-life: the time for N to halve. After n half-lives, (½)ⁿ is left.
  • Activity (Bq) and count rate halve each half-life; subtract the background before reading a half-life.
  • N = N0e−λt; A = A0e−λt; t½ = 0.693/λ.

Mission 2

  • ln A = ln A0 − λt: plot ln A against t; gradient −λ; half-life = ln 2/λ.

Mission 3

  • Every energy change changes mass: E = mc². Nuclear reactions release energy because the products have less mass.
  • Binding energy = mass defect × c², the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its nucleons; more binding means less mass.
  • Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron: fusing light nuclei and splitting heavy nuclei both release energy.

Mission 4

  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei plus neutrons; spontaneous or neutron-induced.
  • Energy is released because the fragments are more tightly bound: less total mass, E = Δmc², about 200 MeV per fission.
  • A steady chain reaction needs, on average, one neutron per fission to cause another; moderator slows neutrons, control rods absorb them, coolant carries energy away, shielding absorbs neutrons and γ.

Mission 5

  • Moderators use light nuclei: neutrons lose most kinetic energy in elastic collisions with nuclei of similar mass.
  • Safety: shielding, remote handling, automatic shut-down by control rods, continued cooling for decay heat.
  • Waste: high-, intermediate- and low-level, each stored to match its activity and heat.
  • The risks and benefits of nuclear power are weighed by society using the evidence.

Mission 6

  • Fusion: light nuclei join; the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less and energy is released.
  • Fusion (light nuclei) and fission (heavy nuclei) both move towards the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve near iron.
  • Fusion needs very high temperature (to overcome electric repulsion) and high density (for frequent collisions): conditions in a star's core.
  • ITER: D–T plasma at about 1.5 × 10⁸ K, heated ohmically, by neutral beams and by radio waves, held off the walls by magnetic fields; confinement can be gravitational, inertial or magnetic.
