Mission 1

What supply means

  • Supply: the amount of a good producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a period of time.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (other things staying the same).
  • A supply schedule for a strawberry farm. Illustrative data:

  • Why more at a higher price? Each kg earns more, so producing more becomes profitable.
  • Extra output usually costs more to produce (overtime, poorer land). A higher price covers those extra costs.

  • The curve slopes up from left to right: a higher price, a larger quantity supplied.

Mission 1

From one firm to the market

  • Individual supply: one firm's supply. Market supply: all firms' supply added together.
  • At each price, add up what every firm offers. Illustrative data: at a price of 4, three farms offer 300, 150 and 50 kg → market supply 500 kg.
  • More firms in the market → market supply is higher at every price.
Quick check
?At a price of 6, three firms are willing to sell 40, 25 and 15 units a week. What is market quantity supplied at a price of 6?
Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • Step 1: price rises → slide up S = extension of supply. Price falls → slide down = contraction.
  • Step 2: a change in anything else → the whole curve shifts. Right (S1 → S2) = increase in supply; left = decrease in supply.
  • Increase in supply: more is offered at every price.
  • Say: "costs rose, so supply decreased" — not: "supply contracted" (contraction is only for a price change).
Mission 1

  • Costs and productivity: if each worker makes more per hour, each unit costs less to make, so supply increases.

Mission 1

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • A natural factor → supply decreases: S shifts left.
  • No change in price caused this. The price rise that followed was a result of the shift (see Mission 3), not its cause.

Mission 2

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly the quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
  • Price up → firms supply more. So PES is normally a positive number.
  • Bigger number = stronger response.
  • Say: "the price rise caused an extension of supply" — not: "supply increased" (that means the whole curve shifts).
Mission 2

Five categories

PESNameWhat it means
0Perfectly inelasticQuantity does not change at all. Vertical curve
between 0 and 1InelasticQuantity changes by a smaller % than price
1Unitary elasticQuantity changes by the same % as price
greater than 1ElasticQuantity changes by a bigger % than price
infinitePerfectly elasticAny quantity at one price. Horizontal curve
  • The dividing line is 1.
Mission 2

Reading a straight supply curve

  • Look at where the line meets the axes, not just how steep it is.
  • Starts on the price axis → elastic (PES > 1) all along it.
  • Passes through the origin → unitary (PES = 1) all along it, however steep.
  • Starts on the quantity axis → inelastic (PES < 1) all along it.
Quick check
?A straight supply curve starts on the quantity axis, to the right of the origin, and slopes upwards. What can you say about its PES?
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A strawberry farm sells punnets at £2.00. The price rises to £2.50.
  • Quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,100 punnets a week.
  • % change in Qs = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 = +10%
  • % change in P = (2.50 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +25%
  • PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 → inelastic.
  • Working backwards: PES = 1.5 and price rises 5% → % change in Qs = 1.5 × 5 = +7.5%. A firm supplying 800 now supplies 860.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The price of a garden bench rises from £40 to £44. The quantity supplied rises from 500 to 600 a month. What is the PES?
Mission 2

What makes supply elastic?

  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise quickly.
  • Stocks: goods already made, or that can be stored, can be released fast.
  • Factors of production: if extra workers, machines and materials are easy to find, or can switch from other uses → more elastic.
  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply becomes.
  • Production time: goods that take a long time to make or grow → less elastic.
Mission 2

Time changes everything

  • Very short run: output is fixed (e.g. fish already caught today). PES = 0.
  • Short run: firms use overtime, spare machines and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build new capacity, and new firms join the market. Supply is more elastic.
  • So a rise in demand → short run: mostly a higher price; long run: more extra output.
Quick check
?Which change would make a firm's supply more price elastic?

Mission 2

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans (for chocolate) come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in 2023–24 cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa prices on the New York futures market (contracts to buy cocoa for later delivery) passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to produce beans.
  • So in the short run, cocoa supply was very inelastic. Growers in other countries (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand output in the long run.
Mission 2

Why PES matters

  • Producers: with elastic supply, a firm can sell much more when demand rises. Keeping stocks and spare capacity raises PES.
  • Consumers: when supply is inelastic, a rise in demand means a big price rise, with little extra to buy.
  • Government: a subsidy to raise output works best when supply is elastic. If supply is inelastic, output barely rises: most of the subsidy ends up as extra income for producers.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. The price of tomatoes rises by 20% and stays there. One week later, a greenhouse grower's quantity supplied is 4% higher than before. One year later, it is 30% higher than before. Which statement is correct?

The same product can have inelastic supply this week and elastic supply next year: time is the key factor.

Mission 3

Where buyers meet sellers

  • A market is wherever buyers and sellers meet to trade: a shop, an auction, a website.
  • Put the demand and supply schedules together. Illustrative data: phone chargers.

  • Equilibrium price: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Here: price 3, quantity 300.
  • On the diagram: where D crosses S. Label Pe and Qe.
  • At equilibrium the market clears: no unsold goods, no disappointed buyers.
Mission 3

Too high, too low: market forces

  • Step 1, price too high (4): supplied 400, demanded 200. Excess supply = 400 − 200 = 200 (a surplus). Unsold stock piles up → sellers cut the price.
  • Step 2, price too low (2): demanded 400, supplied 200. Excess demand = 400 − 200 = 200 (a shortage). Queues form → the price is bid up.
  • Step 3: both pressures stop only at Pe = 3. That is disequilibrium pushed back to equilibrium.
  • Excess demand or supply = the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied at that price.

Mission 3

Real case: cocoa, 2022–2024

  • About 70% of the world's cocoa comes from West Africa, mainly Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana.
  • Weak harvests there meant world output fell short of the cocoa used for three seasons in a row (2021/22 to 2023/24).
  • Real data (IMF, global price of cocoa, yearly average, US dollars per tonne): 2022: 2,369 (about 2,400) · 2024: 7,391 (about 7,400).
  • The price more than tripled in two years.
  • Late 2024: hot weather with less rain threatened crops again.
  • On the diagram: supply decreased (S shifts left) → price rises, quantity sold falls.
Mission 3

Knock-on effects in related markets

  • Markets are linked. A change in one spreads to others.
  • Inputs: cocoa is used to make chocolate. Dearer cocoa → higher costs → supply of chocolate decreases → price of chocolate tends to rise.
  • Substitutes: dearer chocolate → demand for other sweets and snacks increases → their price and sales rise.
  • Complements (used together): if petrol gets much dearer, demand for large, fuel-hungry cars decreases.
Quick check
?Poor weather cuts the supply of butter, and its price rises sharply. Many shoppers use margarine instead of butter. What happens in the market for margarine?
Mission 3

The three jobs of price

  • Signal: a rising price tells producers that buyers want more.
  • Incentive: a higher price makes producing more profitable, so firms expand and new firms join.
  • Rationing: when a good is scarce, the price rises until only those willing and able to pay buy it.
  • This price mechanism answers the three questions:
  • What to produce: goods buyers pay for. How: the lowest-cost methods earn the most profit. For whom: those who can afford the price.
  • Price is a measure of worth: it shows what buyers are prepared to give up for a good.
Quick check
?Tickets for a popular concert are scarce. The seller keeps raising the price until the number of people willing and able to pay equals the number of seats. Which job of price is this?

Mission 4

From many sellers to one

  • A market structure describes how competitive a market is. Look at three things:
    • Number of producers: many, a few, or one.
    • Product differentiation: are the products the same, or made to look different (brands)?
    • Ease of entry: can new firms join easily?
  • Competitive market: many sellers, similar products, easy to join.
  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, often branded products, hard to join.
  • Monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, very hard to join.
Mission 4

Competitive markets

  • Features:
    • many buyers and sellers, none big enough to control the price
    • similar products, so buyers can switch easily
    • easy entry and exit: new firms can join or leave
    • buyers can compare prices (often online)
  • Why firms compete: to win customers, sales and profit.
  • Why competition happens: low start-up costs, many buyers, no legal limits on new firms.
  • Examples: market stalls, takeaways, hairdressers, phone cases sold online.
Mission 4

What competition does

WhoEffects of competition
ConsumersLower prices · more choice · better quality and service
ProducersPressure to cut costs and innovate · lower profits · weak firms may close
WorkersMore employers to choose from · but pressure on pay and job security as firms cut costs
  • Say: "more sellers increase supply, so the price falls" — not: "more sellers lower demand".

Mission 4

Monopoly

  • Pure monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, with no close substitutes.
  • The firm is a price maker: it can choose its price (but if it charges more, it sells less).
  • Barriers to entry keep rivals out.
  • In practice, a firm with a large share has monopoly power. UK merger rules let the competition authority look closely at a merger that gives a firm 25% or more of supply.
  • Examples: the only water company in an area; a drug firm with a patent on a new medicine.
Mission 4

Barriers to entry

BarrierExample
LegalGovernment gives one firm a licence to run a service
PatentsA new invention protected, normally for up to 20 years
MarketingHuge advertising and strong brand loyalty

  • High barriers let a firm keep high profits for a long time: no new rivals arrive to compete them away.
Mission 4

Monopoly: good or bad?

  • Possible disadvantages:
    • higher prices and less output than a competitive market
    • less choice for consumers
    • little pressure to cut costs or improve quality
  • Possible advantages:
    • economies of scale can mean lower costs
    • high profits can pay for research (new medicines)
    • one network avoids wasteful copies (a natural monopoly: two sets of water pipes to every house would be a waste)
  • Whether consumers gain depends on whether lower costs are passed on and on regulation.

Mission 4

Oligopoly: a few big firms

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • Features: high barriers to entry · often branded products · firms are interdependent: each watches what its rivals do.
  • Concentration ratio: the combined market share of the largest firms.
  • Example: UK grocery shares, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025:
SupermarketShare
Tesco28.2%
Sainsbury's15.7%
Asda11.6%
Aldi10.6%
  • Four-firm concentration ratio = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
Mission 4

Compete or collude?

  • Price war: firms cut prices again and again to win share. Great for consumers for a while; weak firms may leave.
  • Non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty cards, quality, after-sales service. Firms avoid a price war.
  • Collusion: firms secretly agree prices or share out the market instead of competing.
  • Cartel: a group of firms that collude. Illegal in the UK, the EU and many other countries.
  • Example: from 1997 to 2011, five major European truck makers colluded on prices. In 2016 the European Commission imposed fines totalling €2.93 billion.

Mission check

?A government gives one company the only licence to run trains between two cities. There is no other practical way to travel between them. Compared with several companies competing on the route, what is most likely?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Supply slopes up: a higher price makes extra output profitable.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease in supply (shift).
  • A tax per unit shifts S left; a subsidy shifts S right.
  • Market supply = the total of all firms' supply at each price.

Mission 2

  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. Below 1: inelastic. Above 1: elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, easy-to-find factors and, above all, more time.

Mission 3

  • Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied; above it a surplus, below it a shortage, and market forces push back.
  • A shift moves the equilibrium: more demand raises price and quantity; more supply lowers price and raises quantity.
  • Price signals, gives incentives and rations, which answers what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 4

  • Market structures run from many sellers to one, depending on numbers, differentiation and ease of entry.
  • Competition lowers prices and profits and widens choice; barriers to entry protect monopoly power.
  • In oligopoly a few firms may compete hard, use non-price competition, or collude.
