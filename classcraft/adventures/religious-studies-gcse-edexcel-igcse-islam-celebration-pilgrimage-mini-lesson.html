Mission 1

Sawm: the fourth pillar

  • Sawm means fasting (literally "to abstain"). It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
  • Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins when the new crescent moon is sighted.
  • From dawn to sunset, adult Muslims give up: food, drink (including water), smoking and sexual relations.
  • They also try to avoid lying, anger, gossip and bad deeds: the Prophet said Allah has no need of someone giving up food and drink who does not give up false speech (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • The lunar year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, so Ramadan moves through the seasons over about 33 years.
Mission 1Read the source

Fasting is prescribed

"O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed for you, even as it was prescribed for those before you, that ye may ward off (evil)" (Qur'an 2:183, Pickthall)

"The month of Ramadan in which was revealed the Qur'an, a guidance for mankind" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall)

Q1 According to 2:183, what is the purpose of fasting?

Show answer

To ward off evil: to develop taqwa, awareness of Allah and self-restraint.

Q2 Why is Ramadan the month chosen for fasting?

Show answer

It is the month in which the Qur'an was revealed, 2:185.

Mission 1

Origins and the Prophet's example

  • 2:183 says fasting was given to earlier peoples too: Muslims see Sawm as continuing the practice of earlier prophets.
  • In Makkah the Prophet fasted and spent nights in prayer; the Qur'an was first revealed to him in Ramadan.
  • In Madinah, in the second year after the Hijrah (624 CE), fasting the whole month of Ramadan was made compulsory.
  • The Prophet's example: he ate suhur before dawn, saying there is blessing in it (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase); he broke the fast at once at sunset with fresh dates or water; he was most generous in Ramadan.
  • He did not allow followers to harm themselves: he forbade fasting day after day without breaking at night.
Mission 1

A day in Ramadan

  • Intention (niyyah): made each night or at the start of the month.
  • Suhur: a meal before dawn, then Fajr prayer.
  • The day: work, school and salah as usual; extra Qur'an reading. Many read the whole Qur'an over the month (in 30 parts).
  • Iftar at sunset (Maghrib): often dates and water, then Maghrib prayer and a meal, often shared with family, the mosque or neighbours.
  • Night: Sunni Muslims pray tarawih (extra night prayers) in the mosque. Shi'a Muslims do not pray tarawih in congregation but keep other night prayers and du'as.

Mission 1

Who is excused, and how they make up

  • Excused from fasting:
  • children before puberty (many practise with half-days);
  • the ill, and travellers on a long journey (2:184);
  • women who are pregnant, breastfeeding, menstruating or recovering from childbirth;
  • the elderly or anyone whose health would be harmed; people with serious mental illness.
  • Why: "Allah desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall).
  • Making up: missed days are fasted later (qada). Those who can never fast feed a poor person for each day (fidyah, 2:184).
Mission 1

Benefits for the self and the ummah

  • Taqwa: awareness of Allah and self-restraint (2:183). A person who can control hunger can control anger and greed.
  • Obedience and reward: the Prophet taught that whoever fasts Ramadan with faith, hoping for Allah's reward, has past sins forgiven (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Empathy: hunger helps Muslims feel what the poor feel, and leads to giving.
  • Gratitude for food and water usually taken for granted.
  • Ummah: Muslims worldwide fast in the same month; iftars bring families and communities together.
Mission 1

Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power

  • The night in 610 CE when, Muslims believe, the angel Jibril brought the first verses of the Qur'an to Muhammad in the cave of Hira:
  • "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot." (Qur'an 96:1–2, Pickthall)
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Pickthall)
  • It falls on one of the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan; many Sunni Muslims mark the 27th. Shi'a Muslims stress the 19th, 21st and 23rd (the 21st is also the anniversary of Imam Ali's death).
  • Today: Muslims stay up in prayer, Qur'an reading and du'a, asking forgiveness. It reminds them that the Qur'an is guidance from Allah.

Mission 1Diversity

Fasting in Britain and a changing world

  • Long days: when Ramadan falls in midsummer, the fast in Britain runs from before 3 a.m. to after 9 p.m.; in winter it is much shorter.
  • Different rulings: most British Muslims follow local dawn and sunset; some scholars allow those in very long days to follow the times of Makkah or of a nearer moderate city.
  • Start dates: some mosques follow a local moon sighting, others Saudi Arabia's announcement, others calculation: so Ramadan and Eid can begin on different days in one town.
  • School and work: exams, sport and long shifts while fasting; many schools and employers make adjustments.
  • In a Muslim country: working hours often shorten and public life runs late; in Britain, Muslims are a minority, which can feel isolating or can strengthen identity.
  • Changes: prayer-time apps, online Qur'an classes, public "open iftars" with non-Muslim guests, and commercial Ramadan products; some Muslims welcome these, others worry about losing simplicity.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Sawm benefits the ummah more than the individual."

  • Reasons for: the whole ummah fasts together; shared iftars and tarawih build community; Zakat al-Fitr and Ramadan giving help the poor; empathy with the hungry leads to action.
  • Reasons against: 2:183 names the purpose as taqwa, a quality of each heart; forgiveness of sins is personal; Allah judges each person's intention; someone fasting alone (e.g. a traveller making up days) gains the same spiritual benefit.
  • Another view: the two cannot be separated: individuals who grow in self-control and gratitude make a stronger ummah.
  • Judge it by: What does the Qur'an say fasting is for? Can a benefit to the ummah exist without benefits to individuals? How do Muslims who fast alone, e.g. new converts in Britain, experience it?
Mission check

Mission 2

Hajj: the fifth pillar

  • Hajj is the pilgrimage to Makkah, in the month of Dhul-Hijjah (8th–13th days), one of the Five Pillars.
  • Who must go: every adult Muslim, once in a lifetime, if able: healthy in body and mind, able to pay after debts and family needs, and able to travel safely.
  • "Pilgrimage to the House is a duty unto Allah for mankind, for him who can find a way thither." (Qur'an 3:97, Pickthall)
  • Schools differ on whether a woman must travel with a male relative (mahram); Saudi rules now let women travel in approved groups.
  • Your board's spelling: Ka'ba.
Mission 2

Origins: Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il

  • Muslims believe Allah told Ibrahim (Abraham) to leave his wife Hajar and their baby son Isma'il in the dry valley of Makkah.
  • Hajar asked whether Allah had commanded this; when Ibrahim said yes, she replied that then Allah would not abandon them (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • When the water ran out, she ran seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, searching.
  • Water burst from the ground near Isma'il: the spring of Zamzam. People came to settle by it.
  • Later Ibrahim and Isma'il built the Ka'bah as a house for the worship of the One God (2:127), and Ibrahim called people to pilgrimage (22:27).
  • Over centuries the Ka'bah filled with idols. In 630 CE the Prophet Muhammad cleared them; his Farewell Hajj in 632 set the pattern Muslims follow today.
Mission 2Read the source

The House and its builders

"And when We made the House (at Makka) a resort for mankind and sanctuary, (saying): Take as your place of worship the place where Abraham stood (to pray)." (Qur'an 2:125, Pickthall)

"And when Abraham and Ishmael were raising the foundations of the House, (Abraham prayed): Our Lord! Accept from us (this duty)." (Qur'an 2:127, Pickthall)

Q1 Who does 2:127 say built the House?

Show answer

Ibrahim and Isma'il.

Q2 What does "sanctuary" in 2:125 suggest about Makkah?

Show answer

It is a place of safety and peace, where fighting and harm are forbidden.

Mission 2

Ihram: purity and equality

  • Ihram is the sacred state a pilgrim enters before Hajj, at a boundary point (miqat) outside Makkah.
  • First a full bath (ghusl), then the intention (niyyah) and the talbiyah: "Labbayk Allahumma labbayk" ("Here I am, O Allah, here I am").
  • Men: two plain white unstitched cloths. Women: ordinary modest clothes, often white; face and hands uncovered.
  • Rules: no perfume; no cutting hair or nails; no sexual relations; no hunting; men keep their heads uncovered; no arguing: "no lewdness nor abuse nor angry conversation on the pilgrimage" (Qur'an 2:197, Pickthall).
  • Significance: rich and poor look the same: equality before Allah. The white cloths are like the burial shroud, a reminder of death and Judgement. The rules focus the mind on Allah.

Mission 2

Tawaf: circling the Ka'bah

  • The Ka'bah: a cube-shaped building in the centre of the Masjid al-Haram (Sacred Mosque), covered in a black cloth (kiswah) embroidered with Qur'an verses. It is the qiblah for every Muslim's prayer.
  • Tawaf: walking around the Ka'bah seven times anticlockwise, starting at the corner of the Black Stone, which pilgrims kiss, touch or point to.
  • After tawaf, two rak'ahs of prayer near the Station of Abraham (2:125).
  • Significance: Allah at the centre of life; the whole ummah moving as one; following Ibrahim and the Prophet.
  • Not worship of the building: Muslims worship only Allah. The Ka'bah is a place and a direction, not an object of worship.
Mission 2

Sa'y and Zamzam

  • Sa'y: walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, now inside a long covered gallery. Men hurry along one marked stretch, as Hajar ran.
  • "(the mountains) As-Safa and Al-Marwah are among the indications of Allah." (Qur'an 2:158, Pickthall)
  • Zamzam: pilgrims drink from the well, and many bring its water home as a gift.
  • Significance: Hajar's trust in Allah and her effort: Muslims learn to rely on Allah and still act. Allah provides for those who turn to Him.
  • A woman's search is at the heart of every pilgrim's Hajj.

Mission 2Diversity

Shi'a shrines: another kind of pilgrimage

  • Shi'a Muslims perform Hajj like other Muslims. Many also make ziyarah (visiting) to shrines of the Imams and their families.
  • Karbala (Iraq): the shrine of Imam Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, killed there in 680 CE. On Arbaeen, forty days after Ashura, huge crowds walk there, many from Najaf (shrine of Imam Ali), about 80 km away.
  • Others: Mashhad (Iran, Imam Reza), Kadhimiya and Samarra (Iraq), Qom (Iran, Fatima Masumah).
  • Purpose: love for the Prophet's family (Ahl al-Bayt), remembering Husayn's stand against injustice, prayer and asking the Imams to intercede with Allah.
  • Different views: ziyarah is voluntary and never replaces Hajj. Many Sunni Muslims, especially Salafis, reject travel to shrines and asking the dead to intercede; many Sufi Sunni Muslims visit saints' tombs.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Hajj is the most important of the Five Pillars."

  • Reasons for: it unites the whole ummah in one place; a well-known hadith says an accepted Hajj is rewarded with Paradise, and the pilgrim returns free of sin like a newborn; it brings together prayer, giving, sacrifice and self-control.
  • Reasons against: the Shahadah is the foundation: without it no other pillar counts; salah is five times a day and called the first thing a person is judged on; Hajj is only for those who are able (3:97), so Allah does not make it a test for everyone.
  • Another view: the pillars hold up one "house": each is necessary and none stands alone.
  • Judge it by: Is "important" about frequency, reward or effect on life? Can a duty that many Muslims never perform be the most important? What do Muslims themselves say?

Mission 3

The days of Hajj

  • 8 Dhul-Hijjah: in ihram, pilgrims go to Mina, a valley of tents outside Makkah, and pray there.
  • 9th: Arafat: the standing, from midday to sunset. At night, Muzdalifah.
  • 10th: back to Mina: stoning the largest pillar, sacrifice, shaving or cutting the hair. Then to Makkah for tawaf (and sa'y). Muslims worldwide celebrate Id-ul-Adha.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three pillars each day.
  • Before leaving Makkah: a farewell tawaf around the Ka'bah.
Mission 3

Arafat: the heart of Hajj

  • On the 9th, pilgrims stand (wuquf) on the plain of Arafat from midday until sunset: praying, reading the Qur'an, and asking Allah for forgiveness.
  • The Prophet said, "Hajj is Arafat" (hadith, paraphrase): a pilgrim who misses the standing has not completed Hajj.
  • The Mount of Mercy (Jabal al-Rahmah) rises from the plain. Near it the Prophet gave his Farewell Sermon in 632 CE: all people equal, no Arab better than a non-Arab except by piety; lives and property sacred; women to be treated kindly (paraphrase).
  • Some traditions say Adam and Hawwa (Eve) were reunited and forgiven here.
  • Significance: a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: millions standing before Allah, in plain white, waiting for mercy.
Mission 3

Muzdalifah: a night under the sky

  • After sunset pilgrims move to Muzdalifah, between Arafat and Mina.
  • They pray Maghrib and Isha together and sleep on the ground in the open air, rich and poor alike.
  • "when ye press on in the multitude from 'Arafat, remember Allah by the sacred monument" (Qur'an 2:198, Pickthall)
  • They collect small pebbles for the stoning at Mina.
  • Significance: simplicity, equality and trust in Allah after the day of standing.
Mission 3

Mina: stoning, sacrifice, shaving

  • Stoning (rami): pilgrims throw seven pebbles at the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah) on the 10th, and at all three on the following days. Today the pillars are tall walls reached by a multi-storey bridge.
  • Muslim tradition says Shaytan tempted Ibrahim three times here to disobey Allah's command to sacrifice his son, and Ibrahim drove him away with stones.
  • Significance: pilgrims reject evil and temptation in their own lives.
  • Sacrifice: a sheep, goat or camel is sacrificed (today usually arranged with a voucher) and the meat goes to the poor. This recalls the ram Allah gave Ibrahim in place of his son (see Mission 4).
  • Hair: men shave the head or cut it short; women cut a small lock. Most ihram rules now end.
  • Tawaf al-ifadah: a further tawaf (and sa'y) at the Ka'bah, then back to Mina for the remaining days.

Mission 3

Benefits: on Hajj and for life

  • Forgiveness: the Prophet said a pilgrim whose Hajj is accepted returns free of sin, like a newborn (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Closeness to Allah: days given entirely to prayer and remembrance.
  • Equality and ummah: people of every nation, colour and class in the same white cloth. Malcolm X (1925–1965) wrote after his Hajj in 1964 that he had eaten and prayed with pilgrims of every colour, and saw true brotherhood (paraphrase).
  • Patience and self-control: heat, crowds and no arguing (2:197).
Mission 3

Challenges

  • Cost: travel, accommodation and approved packages are expensive; many save for years.
  • Physical: summer heat in recent years, long walks, illness, crowds. Deadly crushes have happened (e.g. at Mina in 2015); Saudi authorities have rebuilt the Jamarat bridge and use crowd control.
  • Quotas: Saudi Arabia limits pilgrims from each country, so many wait years.
  • Spiritual: keeping patience and focus while tired, crowded and uncomfortable.
  • Commercialisation: luxury hotels overlooking the Ka'bah; some Muslims fear this undermines equality.

Mission 3

Umrah: the lesser pilgrimage

  • Umrah: a shorter pilgrimage to Makkah that can be made at any time of year.
  • Same as Hajj: ihram, tawaf, sa'y, then shaving or cutting the hair.
  • Different: no fixed dates; no Arafat, Muzdalifah, Mina, stoning or required sacrifice; takes hours, not days.
  • Status: Hajj is a pillar. Scholars differ on Umrah: Hanafi and Maliki scholars call it a strongly recommended Sunnah; Shafi'i, Hanbali and Shi'a scholars hold it obligatory once for those able.
  • Many pilgrims perform Umrah and then Hajj in one journey.
  • Different attitudes: many Muslims make Umrah often, especially in Ramadan, when the Prophet said its reward equals a Hajj (hadith, paraphrase). Others warn that repeated Umrahs cost money that could feed the poor.
Mission 3

Makkah and Madinah

  • Makkah: birthplace of the Prophet (c. 570 CE); where revelation began; where early Muslims were persecuted; left in the Hijrah of 622.
  • Madinah: where the Hijrah ended; the first Muslim community was set up; the first mosque, Quba', was built; the Prophet died in 632.
  • Visiting after Hajj: the Prophet's Mosque, where prayer is said to be worth a thousand prayers elsewhere, except in Makkah (hadith, paraphrase); his grave under the Green Dome, with the graves of Abu Bakr and Umar beside it; the Quba' mosque, where praying is said to bring the reward of an Umrah (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Different attitudes: visiting Madinah is not part of Hajj. Most Muslims see it as highly recommended. Many Salafi scholars teach that the journey should be made for the mosque, not the grave, and that pilgrims greet the Prophet but do not ask him for help. Shi'a pilgrims also visit the graves of four Imams in the al-Baqi' cemetery, whose shrines were demolished in the 1920s.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Hajj loses its meaning when it becomes a luxury holiday."

  • Reasons for: ihram and Muzdalifah teach equality and simplicity; luxury hotels and VIP tents divide rich from poor; Hajj should involve effort and patience; money could go to the poor.
  • Reasons against: the rituals stay the same for everyone: all stand at Arafat and throw the same pebbles; comfort helps the old and ill complete Hajj safely; Allah judges the heart and intention, not the hotel.

  • Judge it by: What does ihram show about equality? Does safety justify cost? Which matters more: the outward conditions or the intention?

Mission 4

Two Ids, one moon

  • Id (Arabic 'Id) means a festival: literally a day that returns every year.
  • Islam has two Ids that every Muslim group keeps:
  • Id-ul-Fitr: the festival of breaking the fast, at the end of Ramadan.
  • Id-ul-Adha: the festival of sacrifice, during the Hajj.
  • Both follow the lunar Islamic calendar, so they move about 11 days earlier each year in the solar calendar.
  • A new month starts when the new crescent moon is seen.
  • The Prophet set these two festivals in Madinah, in place of older festival days (hadith in Sunan Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • Your spec spells them Eid-ul-Fitr and Eid-ul-Adha.
Mission 4

Id-ul-Fitr: why Muslims celebrate

  • When: 1 Shawwal, the day after Ramadan ends.
  • Thanks: Muslims thank Allah for the strength to complete the fast.
  • The Qur'an asks fasting Muslims to "complete the period, and that ye should magnify Allah for having guided you, and that peradventure ye may be thankful" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall).
  • Remembering the Qur'an: Ramadan is the month the Qur'an was first revealed (see Mission 1).
  • Purpose: joy after self-discipline; forgiveness; the whole ummah celebrating together.
  • Zakat-ul-Fitr (fitrah): a fixed gift of food, or its value, paid for each family member before the Id prayer, so the poor can celebrate too.
Mission 4

Id-ul-Fitr: how Muslims celebrate

  • Wash (ghusl), wear new or best clothes, eat something sweet such as dates.
  • Recite the takbir on the way: "Allahu akbar" (God is the Greatest).
  • Id prayer in congregation: two rak'ahs with extra takbirs, then a sermon. Held in mosques, halls or parks.
  • Greet: "Id Mubarak" (blessed Id). Embrace and forgive.
  • Visit family and friends; share a festive meal and sweets.
  • Children receive gifts or money; cards are sent.
  • Some families visit the graves of relatives and pray for them.
  • Family celebrations, Id prayers and gifts make it a day of shared joy after the fast.

Mission 4

Id-ul-Adha: Ibrahim's test

  • When: 10 Dhu al-Hijjah, the day pilgrims on Hajj sacrifice at Mina. Muslims everywhere join them.
  • Origin: the Qur'an tells how Ibrahim (Abraham) dreamed that Allah commanded him to sacrifice his son.
  • The son agreed. Both submitted to Allah. Muslims believe that at the last moment Allah stopped Ibrahim and provided a ram instead.
  • The Qur'an does not name the son. Most Muslims believe he was Isma'il (Ishmael).
  • Muslim tradition adds that Shaytan tried three times to tempt Ibrahim to disobey; he drove Shaytan away with stones. Pilgrims stone the pillars at Mina in memory.
  • Hajj also remembers Hajar (Hagar), Isma'il's mother, searching for water, and the spring of Zamzam that Allah provided (see Mission 2).
  • Meaning: total submission to Allah, trust, and Allah's mercy.
Mission 4Read the source

"A clear test"

> "O my dear son, I have seen in a dream that I must sacrifice thee. So look, what thinkest thou? He said: O my father! Do that which thou art commanded. Allah willing, thou shalt find me of the steadfast." (Qur'an 37:102, Pickthall)

> "Lo! that verily was a clear test. Then We ransomed him with a tremendous victim." (Qur'an 37:106–107, Pickthall)

Q1 How does the son respond to his father?

Show answer

He accepts Allah's command and promises to be steadfast.

Q2 What does "ransomed" tell Muslims about Allah?

Show answer

Allah did not want the boy's death: He tested obedience, then showed mercy by providing an animal.

Mission 4

Id-ul-Adha: how Muslims celebrate

  • Id prayer in congregation in the morning, with takbir.
  • Qurbani (Arabic udhiyah): a sheep, goat, cow or camel is sacrificed after the prayer, in Allah's name. A healthy animal of a set minimum age.
  • The meat is usually shared in three parts: the family, relatives and friends, and the poor.
  • Many Muslims pay for qurbani abroad through a charity, so meat reaches people in need.
  • Family visits, a festive meal, new clothes, gifts.
  • The Qur'an stresses the inner meaning: "Their flesh and their food reach not Allah, but the devotion from you reacheth Him." (Qur'an 22:37, Pickthall)

Mission 4Diversity

One festival, different ways

  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both keep Id-ul-Fitr and Id-ul-Adha with Id prayer, charity and family meals.
  • Qurbani: the Hanafi school treats it as a duty (wajib) for every adult Muslim who can afford it; the other Sunni schools and Shi'a scholars call it a strongly recommended sunnah.
  • Moon-sighting: some communities wait to see the moon locally; others follow Saudi Arabia's announcement or astronomical calculation. So Id can fall on different days, even in one city.
  • Culture: sweet dishes and new clothes for Id-ul-Fitr in many places, such as Turkey and South Asia; long journeys home in Indonesia; animal markets before Id-ul-Adha in Pakistan.
  • Different groups share the same core meaning: thanks, submission to Allah, and care for the poor.
Mission 4

Id in Britain and worldwide

  • Muslim-majority countries: Id is usually a public holiday, often of several days; shops, schools and offices close.
  • Britain: not a public holiday. Workers may need to ask for leave; schools in England must authorise absence on the day of Id itself, and any extra days are for the head teacher to decide.
  • Large Id prayers in parks and sports grounds; some councils host Id festivals.
  • Qurbani in Britain must by law take place in a licensed slaughterhouse, so families order it through a butcher or pay a charity to sacrifice abroad.
  • Moon-sighting differences can mean British Muslims celebrate on different days.
  • Changes: Id cards, online greetings, shop displays and Id-themed products; families spread across countries celebrate by video call.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sawm is fasting from dawn to sunset in Ramadan (Qur'an 2:183–185); its aim is taqwa, with suhur before dawn and iftar at sunset.
  • The ill, travellers, children, pregnant women and the elderly are excused; missed days are made up (qada) or replaced by feeding the poor (fidyah).
  • Laylat al-Qadr, in the last ten nights, marks the first revelation (Qur'an 96:1–5) and is "better than a thousand months" (97:3).

Mission 2

  • Hajj (fifth pillar) is required once in a lifetime of every adult Muslim who is able (Qur'an 3:97).
  • Its origins: Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il; Hajar's search between Safa and Marwa; Zamzam; Ibrahim and Isma'il building the Ka'bah (2:125–127).
  • At Makkah: ihram (equality, purity), tawaf (seven circuits anticlockwise), sa'y (seven walks between Safa and Marwa), Zamzam.

Mission 3

  • 8–13 Dhul-Hijjah: Mina → Arafat (standing until sunset; Mount of Mercy) → Muzdalifah (night, pebbles) → Mina (stoning, sacrifice, hair) → tawaf.
  • Benefits: forgiveness, equality, ummah, a changed life; challenges: cost, crowds, heat, quotas, commercialisation.
  • Umrah can be made any time and has no Arafat or Mina; Madinah holds the Prophet's Mosque and grave and the Quba' mosque.

Mission 4

  • Id-ul-Fitr ends Ramadan: thanks to Allah, Zakat-ul-Fitr, Id prayer, new clothes, food, gifts and family.
  • Id-ul-Adha remembers Ibrahim's test (Qur'an 37:102–107) during Hajj: Id prayer, qurbani shared with family, friends and the poor; devotion, not meat, reaches Allah (22:37).
  • In Britain Id is not a public holiday; qurbani is done through licensed slaughterhouses or charities abroad, and moon-sighting differences shape the date.
