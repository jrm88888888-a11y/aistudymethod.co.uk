Mission 1

From Chan to Zen

  • Zen is the Japanese form of the Chinese Chan school; both words come from Sanskrit dhyana, "meditation".
  • Tradition traces Chan to Bodhidharma (5th–6th century), an Indian monk said to have sat facing a wall for nine years.
  • Huineng (638–713), the "Sixth Patriarch", taught sudden awakening: our Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly.
  • Two lineages reached Japan: Rinzai (brought by Eisai, 1141–1215) and Soto (brought by Dōgen, 1200–1253).
  • Zen is a Mahayana school: it builds on Buddha nature and emptiness.
Mission 1Read the source

A transmission outside the scriptures

A four-line verse, attributed to Bodhidharma but first found in Song-dynasty China (11th–12th centuries), sums up Zen's self-understanding (rendering of the Chinese):

> A special transmission outside the scriptures;

> not founded on words and letters;

> pointing directly to the human mind;

> seeing into one's nature and becoming Buddha.

  • The Flower Sermon (paraphrase; also first recorded in Song China): the Buddha silently holds up a flower; only Mahakashyapa smiles. The Buddha says he has passed the true Dharma to him, beyond words.
  • So Zen claims a mind-to-mind transmission, teacher to student, in an unbroken lineage.

Q1 What does "pointing directly" leave out?

Show answer

Study of doctrine and long explanation: the teacher points to the student's own mind.

Q2 Why does the Flower Sermon need Mahakashyapa?

Show answer

Transmission is confirmed by a student's awakening, not by a text.

Mission 1

Zazen: seated meditation

  • Zazen = "seated meditation", the heart of Zen practice.
  • Posture: cross-legged (full or half lotus) or kneeling, back upright, eyes half-open, hands in the cosmic mudra (an oval below the navel).
  • Attention rests on posture and breath; thoughts are let come and go without being followed.
  • Done in a zendo (meditation hall), often for long periods; walking meditation (kinhin) between sittings.
  • Intensive retreats (sesshin) may involve many hours of zazen a day for a week.
  • Your Theme 4 calls zazen "just sitting": that phrase belongs above all to Soto.
Mission 1

Koan: a puzzle reason cannot solve

  • Your spec: a koan is a "paradoxical statement used as a practice".
  • A koan is a story, question or saying from an earlier master, set by a teacher.
  • Famous examples: a monk asks Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha nature; he answers "Mu" ("no" / "nothing"). Hakuin (1686–1769): "What is the sound of one hand?"
  • Collections: the Blue Cliff Record (1125) and the Gateless Gate (Mumonkan, 1228; "Mu" is its first case).
  • The student holds the koan in zazen until ordinary reasoning is exhausted; in private interview (dokusan) the teacher tests the response.
  • The answer is not a clever explanation but a direct expression of insight.

Mission 1

Satori: awakening

  • Satori = "awakening"; kensho = "seeing one's nature". Many writers use them together; in Rinzai, kensho often names a first glimpse and satori a deeper awakening.
  • What is seen is not a new object but one's own Buddha nature: the mind free of the false split between self and world.
  • Zen stresses that it can come suddenly, in a moment (the sound of a stone striking bamboo, a shout, a word).
  • It is not the end of practice: awakening is deepened and lived in daily life.
  • Continuity: the earlier tradition's goal of nirvana is seen not as a distant place but as realised here and now.
Mission 1Diversity

Rinzai and Soto

  • Rinzai (from the Chinese master Linji, d. 866): koan practice, often in a graded curriculum (systematised in the line of Hakuin); vigorous effort; aims at a breakthrough to satori (awakening); sitters face into the hall.
  • Soto (from the Chinese Caodong line): shikantaza, "just sitting", with no object or goal; sitters face the wall.
  • Dōgen: practice and realisation are one. Zazen is not a means to a later awakening; sitting is itself the expression of Buddha nature.
  • Both use both: Dōgen commented on many koans; Rinzai monks sit for long hours.
  • Shared: lineage, the teacher's authority, monastic discipline, daily work and chanting.

Mission 1

What changed from earlier Buddhism?

  • Continuity: meditation at the centre; the goal is awakening; monastic life and precepts; the Buddha's warning that reciting texts is not enough:

> "The thoughtless man, even if he can recite a large portion (of the law), but is not a doer of it, has no share in the priesthood…" (Dhammapada 19, Müller 1881)

  • Changes: sudden rather than gradual awakening; Buddha nature already present; authority in a teacher lineage more than in texts; paradox and the koan; manual work as practice (a Chan saying: a day without work, a day without food).
  • Chinese culture shaped Chan: Daoist love of naturalness and paradox is often noted.
  • Spec issue: "The extent to which Japanese Buddhism is unique."
Mission 1Evaluate

Can awakening be taught without theory, ritual and words?

  • Claim: Zen shows that the truth of Buddhism lies beyond theory, ritual and language.
  • For: the Flower Sermon and the transmission verse place truth in direct seeing.
  • For: koans break down conceptual thinking; satori is an experience, not a doctrine learned.
  • For: the Buddha himself refused to answer some speculative questions as not leading to awakening.
  • Against: Zen is highly ritualised: bowing, chanting, formal meals, robes, ceremonies of transmission.
  • Against: Zen has a huge literature, and its "rejection of words" is itself taught in words.
  • Against: without doctrine, how can a student tell real insight from self-deception? Zen's answer is the teacher, which shifts authority to people.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the "rejection" literal or a teaching device? Does Zen practice match its rhetoric? Is a teacher's confirmation a reliable authority?

Mission 2

Amitabha and his vow

  • Amitabha ("Infinite Light"; also Amitayus, "Infinite Life"); Japanese Amida.
  • The Larger Sukhavativyuha Sutra (paraphrase): long ago a monk, Dharmakara, made 48 vows to create a perfect land for all beings, and practised for aeons to fulfil them.
  • He became the Buddha Amitabha: so, Pure Land Buddhists believe, the vows are now in force.
  • The 18th vow ("Primal Vow"), paraphrase: anyone who sincerely trusts, longs to be born in his land and calls his name, even ten times, will be born there (except those guilty of the gravest offences).
  • Attendants: the bodhisattvas Avalokitesvara (compassion) and Mahasthamaprapta (wisdom).
Mission 2

Sukhavati: the Pure Land

  • Sukhavati, the "Land of Bliss", lies in the west.
  • Three core texts: the Larger and Smaller Sukhavativyuha Sutras and the Contemplation Sutra (visualising Amitabha and his land).
  • Described as jewelled, fragrant, filled with the sound of the Dhamma; no hells or animal births.
  • Purpose: perfect conditions to hear the Dhamma and reach enlightenment easily, from where one returns to help others.
  • Readings differ: a real place after death; a symbol of the awakened mind; or (for Shinran) birth there is itself awakening.
  • The nembutsu: "Namu Amida Butsu", "I take refuge in Amida Buddha" (namu = homage to, or refuge in).
  • Eduqas wording: nembutsu is "going for refuge in Amida/Amitabha Buddha".

Mission 2

Honen and Jodo-shu

  • Honen (1133–1212), a Tendai monk, despaired of awakening by his own effort.
  • In 1175, reading the Chinese master Shandao, he turned to the exclusive nembutsu (senju nembutsu); this marks the founding of Jodo-shu (Pure Land school).
  • Work: Senchakushu (1198): in the age of mappō (the age of the Dharma's decline, when few can awaken by their own effort), choose the one practice Amida chose in his vow: reciting his name.
  • Practice: recite the nembutsu constantly, with faith in the vow, until death; tradition says he recited it tens of thousands of times a day.
  • Honen himself kept monastic precepts; his school spread to all classes, including women and the poor.
Mission 2

Shinran and Jodo Shinshu

  • Shinran (1173–1263), Honen's disciple; exiled in 1207; married and called himself "neither monk nor layman".
  • Jodo Shinshu ("True Pure Land school"), today one of Japan's largest Buddhist denominations.
  • Key idea: shinjin, true entrusting, is given by Amida's other-power (tariki), not produced by the believer.
  • At the moment of shinjin, birth in the Pure Land is settled, in this life.
  • So the nembutsu is an expression of gratitude, not a practice that earns birth.
  • Tannisho ch. 3 (paraphrase): if even a good person is born in the Pure Land, how much more an evil person.
  • No monastic precepts; clergy marry; no rites for worldly benefits.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Pure Land Buddhism is an 'easy' path."

  • For: a classic image (attributed to Nagarjuna): the difficult path is walking overland; the easy path is sailing in a boat.
  • For: no need for long meditation, scholarship or monastic life: open to farmers, women, the poor.
  • For: in the age of mappō, it suits people as they really are.
  • Against: Shinran: true entrusting is the hardest thing of all, because it means giving up every trace of self-power.
  • Against: Honen's constant recitation, kept up for life, is demanding.
  • Against: "easy" describes Amida's provision, not the believer's effort: Pure Land Buddhists see it as compassion meeting people's limits, not laziness.
  • Judgement criteria: Easy to do, or easy to believe? Does "easy" mean less Buddhist? Does it fit the Buddha's teaching on effort?
  • Spec issue: "the importance of koan, nembutsu and daimoku as expressions of Buddhist teachings".

Mission 3

Nichiren and the Latter Day

  • Nichiren (1222–1282): a Japanese monk trained in the Tendai school, which honoured the Lotus Sutra as the Buddha's highest teaching.
  • He believed Japan had entered mappō, the "Latter Day of the Dharma": an age of decline when older practices no longer lead people to awakening.
  • His conclusion: in this age, only the Lotus Sutra saves, and it is accessed by chanting its title.
  • In 1253 he first proclaimed the chant Nam(u)-myōhō-renge-kyō.
  • His treatise On Establishing the Correct Teaching for the Peace of the Land (Risshō Ankoku Ron, 1260) blamed earthquakes, famine and epidemics on the nation's turn from the Lotus Sutra.
  • He was exiled twice and nearly executed (1271); followers saw the Mongol invasions (1274, 1281) as his warnings fulfilled.
Mission 3Read the source

The daimoku

  • Your spec: the daimoku is the "mantra used in Nichiren traditions".
  • Daimoku = "title". The chant is the title of the Lotus Sutra with a word of devotion:
  • Namu / Nam: "devotion to", "I take refuge in".
  • Myōhō: the "wonderful (or mystic) Law (Dharma)".
  • Renge: "lotus flower": it blooms and seeds at once (cause and effect together) and grows pure from muddy water (awakening within ordinary life).
  • Kyō: "sutra", teaching.
  • Nichiren taught that the title holds the whole sutra, as a seed holds the plant. Chanting it with faith plants the seed of Buddhahood in the one who chants.

Q1 Why is "lotus" a fitting symbol for awakening in daily life?

Show answer

It grows clean out of mud: Buddhahood is found in ordinary, troubled lives.

Q2 How does this practice differ from studying the sutra chapter by chapter?

Show answer

It relies on faith in the title as containing the whole, so anyone can practise it.

Mission 3

Practice: chanting before the Gohonzon

  • The Gohonzon ("object of devotion"): a calligraphic mandala scroll first inscribed by Nichiren. The daimoku runs down the centre; names of buddhas, bodhisattvas and protectors surround it.
  • Members enshrine it in a home altar (butsudan) and chant to it morning and evening.
  • Gongyō: reciting parts of chapters 2 (skilful means) and 16 (the Buddha's eternal life span) of the Lotus Sutra, followed by chanting daimoku.
  • Many chant for concrete goals (health, work, relationships) as well as for awakening and world peace: changing one's own life is believed to change one's surroundings.
  • Contrast: nembutsu relies on Amida's power for rebirth in the Pure Land (see Mission 2); koan practice seeks insight beyond words (see Mission 1). The daimoku seeks Buddhahood in this life.

Mission 3

Nichiren's view of other forms of Buddhism

  • Building on Tendai, Nichiren ranked all other sutras as provisional (skilful means); the Lotus Sutra alone was final.
  • He attacked rival schools in four famous charges (paraphrase): nembutsu leads to hell; Zen is the work of devils; Shingon will ruin the nation; the Ritsu (precepts) school betrays the nation.
  • Shakubuku: "break and subdue": forceful refutation of wrong views. Shōju: "embrace and accept": gentle persuasion. Nichiren judged shakubuku right for the Latter Day.
  • His motive, as he saw it, was compassion: to warn people away from teachings that could not save them in mappō.
Mission 3Diversity

Nichiren movements today

  • Nichiren Shū: centred on Kuonji temple at Mount Minobu; reveres Nichiren as a great teacher and bodhisattva.
  • Nichiren Shōshū: priestly school at Taisekiji; teaches that Nichiren is the True Buddha of the Latter Day.
  • Sōka Gakkai (founded 1930; international body SGI 1975): lay movement, separated from Nichiren Shōshū in 1991. Strong on peace, culture and dialogue; in Britain at Taplow Court, Buckinghamshire.
  • Risshō Kōsei-kai (1938): lay movement and a founder of the interfaith body Religions for Peace (1970); reads the Lotus Sutra's "one vehicle" as including all paths.
  • Nipponzan Myōhōji: monks who walk and drum the daimoku for peace; built the Peace Pagoda in Battersea Park, London (1985).
Mission 3Evaluate

How well does the daimoku express Buddhist teaching?

  • Claim: The daimoku is a full expression of Buddhist teaching.
  • For: it rests on the Lotus Sutra's teaching that all can attain Buddhahood (the "one vehicle").
  • For: it brings awakening into everyday life for lay people, as the lotus grows from mud.
  • Against: it can look like reliance on a formula rather than on wisdom, ethics and meditation (the Eightfold Path).
  • Against: chanting for worldly goals may seem to feed craving, the cause of dukkha.
  • Reply: Nichiren Buddhists answer that desires are the raw material of awakening, transformed through practice.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit the Three Refuges and the Four Noble Truths? Does it change how people live?
  • Spec issue: "The importance of koan, nembutsu and daimoku as expressions of Buddhist teachings."

Mission 4

Why Buddhism and science keep meeting

  • From the late 19th century, Asian and Western writers present Buddhism as a "scientific religion": no creator God, no demand for blind faith, cause and effect at its centre.
  • Buddhist responses range from "science confirms the Dharma", to "science and Dharma answer different questions", to "drop what science disproves".
  • Your spec's tension: Buddhism presented as avoiding blind faith, while Asian Buddhist worldviews include many beings and realms science does not recognise.
Mission 4Read the source

The Kalama Sutta

Anguttara Nikaya 3.65 (paraphrase). The Kalamas are confused: visiting teachers each praise their own doctrine and attack others'. The Buddha replies:

  • Section 9: do not accept a teaching merely because of: repeated report; tradition; rumour; scripture; logic or inference; how things appear; agreement with a view you already hold; the speaker's apparent ability; or because "this monk is our teacher".
  • When you yourselves know that something is unwholesome, blameworthy, criticised by the wise, and leads to harm and suffering when done, abandon it.
  • Section 10: when you yourselves know that something is wholesome, blameless, praised by the wise, and leads to benefit and happiness, take it up and stay with it.
  • Earlier in the sutta (sections 5–8), he asks them: does greed (then hatred, then delusion) arise in a person for their benefit or harm? They answer: for harm.

Q1 What is the test the Buddha gives?

Show answer

Your own experience of whether something leads to harm or to well-being, checked against the wise.

Q2 Is this the same as a laboratory test?

Show answer

Not quite: it is a test of ethical and mental results in one's own life.

  • Your spec: Kalama Sutta v. 9 and 10.
Mission 4

Two readings of the Kalama Sutta

  • "Charter of free inquiry" (popular, especially in the West): accept nothing without evidence, as in science.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi ("A Look at the Kalama Sutta", 1988): a warning against that reading.
  • The Kalamas were not yet followers; the Buddha starts from what they can see now (greed, hatred and delusion cause harm).
  • Faith (saddha) still has a place: the Buddha later teaches what cannot be checked at once (e.g. rebirth).
  • The same sutta offers four assurances: if there is rebirth and karma, a good life leads to a good rebirth; if not, a good life is still happy here and now. Belief in rebirth is not demanded first.
  • So the sutta supports testing in experience, but it is not a claim that Buddhism is a laboratory science.
Mission 4

Worlds full of beings

  • Traditional Asian Buddhist cosmology: six realms of rebirth (gods, asuras / demigods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings), many heavens (31 planes of existence in the Theravada scheme), nagas, devas and a world-mountain, Mount Meru.
  • Science has no evidence of these realms, and modern cosmology replaces Meru.
  • Responses:
  • Literal: many Asian Buddhists honour devas and spirits and make offerings for the dead; science studies only the physical, not every realm.
  • Psychological: the realms are states of mind in this life (e.g. Thai monk Buddhadasa, 1906–1993: hell is anxiety, hungry ghosts are craving).
  • Revision: the Dalai Lama says the Abhidharma cosmology of Mount Meru can be set aside, since science has shown it wrong.
  • Your spec: "Asian Buddhist worldviews populated with a diversity of beings and realms".
Mission 4

The challenge to karma and rebirth

  • Challenge: science finds no mechanism by which moral actions shape future lives; most neuroscientists see consciousness as produced by the brain, ending at death.
  • Response 1, karma as intention: the Buddha says that intention is karma (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase). Intentions shape habits and character; psychology can observe this in one life.
  • Response 2, outside science's reach: the Dalai Lama: science has not disproved rebirth; it has not investigated it. Not finding something is different from showing it is false.
  • Response 3, evidence offered: some Buddhists cite the psychiatrist Ian Stevenson (1918–2007) and his studies of children who report past lives; critics dispute the method.
  • Response 4, agnostic / secular: Stephen Batchelor, Buddhism Without Beliefs (1997): practise without needing to believe in rebirth (see Mission 5).
  • Karma is not fate: present intention matters. And the Buddha listed the exact results of karma among four things not to be speculated about (Anguttara Nikaya 4.77).
Mission 4

Miracles

  • Pali texts report iddhi (psychic powers) from deep meditation: e.g. the Buddha's Twin Miracle at Savatthi (fire and water from his body).
  • Challenge: such events break known laws of nature; science treats the reports as legend.
  • The Buddha's own caution: in the Kevaddha Sutta (Digha Nikaya 11) he names three "wonders": psychic power, reading minds, and instruction. He says he dislikes and is ashamed of the first two, because a sceptic could explain them away as a mere charm; only instruction truly transforms people (paraphrase).
  • Vinaya: monks are forbidden to display psychic powers to lay people (Cullavagga V.8, the sandalwood bowl).
  • Responses: powers are real but irrelevant to liberation; or the stories are symbolic; or, like rebirth, outside current science. All agree they are not the basis of faith.

Mission 4

"Buddhism agrees with quantum physics"?

  • The claim: emptiness and dependent origination resemble quantum findings: particles exist only in relation; the observer affects what is observed; no solid, independent "thing".
  • Popularised by Fritjof Capra, The Tao of Physics (1975), and the dialogue The Quantum and the Lotus (Matthieu Ricard and Trinh Xuan Thuan, 2001).
  • Thich Nhat Hanh: interbeing echoes the physicists' discovery that the observer is not wholly separate from what is observed.
  • Criticisms: the parallels use words ("emptiness", "observer") in different senses; physics is mathematics tested by experiment, Buddhist emptiness is insight aimed at ending suffering. Donald Lopez, Buddhism and Science: A Guide for the Perplexed (2008), argues the "Buddhism is scientific" claim is a modern construction with a history.
  • The Dalai Lama welcomes dialogue but warns against claiming science proves Buddhist teaching.
Mission 4

Three Buddhist voices on science

  • 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935), The Universe in a Single Atom (2005); dialogues with scientists since 1987 (Mind and Life Institute).
  • Buddhism and science share investigation; if science conclusively disproves a Buddhist claim, Buddhists should give that claim up (paraphrase).
  • But science has limits: it cannot settle questions of value, and it is not the whole of knowledge (first-person experience matters too).
  • Science needs compassion to guide its use.
  • The Dalai Lama's assessment of the value of science is the named point for your spec.
Mission 4Evaluate

How close are Buddhism and science?

  • Claim: Buddhism and science are closely compatible.
  • For: no creator God; cause and effect; testing in experience (Kalama Sutta); meditation research; the Dalai Lama's readiness to revise.
  • For: interdependence and impermanence fit ecology, evolution and physics.
  • Against: karma, rebirth, devas and realms are not scientific claims; miracle stories remain.
  • Against: different aims: liberation from dukkha vs explanation of nature; the "scientific Buddhism" image is partly a modern construction (Lopez).
  • Criteria: Compatible with which Buddhism (modernist, traditional Asian, secular)? Does compatibility need agreement on facts, or only on method? Must Buddhists change beliefs, or can they set limits to science?
  • Issues for evaluation: how close the relationship is; ways in which the scientific worldview and Buddhism are incompatible.
Mission check

For many Buddhists, science is a partner in investigation, not the judge of everything. Which claims it can test is the real debate.

Mission 5

Buddhism meets a secular West

  • Secularisation: religion loses its place as the source of truth and morality, becomes a private matter, and is attacked as irrational.
  • Some Buddhists respond by preserving tradition (Amaravati) or adapting it (Triratna).
  • Secular Buddhism goes further: it presents the dharma as a way of life that needs no belief in rebirth, karma as a cosmic law, or other unseen realms.
  • Two contrasting voices: Stephen Batchelor and David Brazier.
Mission 5

Stephen Batchelor

  • Stephen Batchelor (b. 1953, Scotland): ordained as a monk in the Tibetan Gelug tradition (1974), then trained in Korean Seon (Zen); left monastic life in 1985 and became a lay teacher.
  • Key works: Buddhism Without Beliefs (1997); Confession of a Buddhist Atheist (2010); After Buddhism (2015); Secular Buddhism (2017).
  • He argues that the Buddha taught a practical, this-worldly path, later overlaid by Indian metaphysics and religious institutions.
  • Dharma practice is something you do, not something you believe.
  • Your spec calls him "a Buddhist Atheist", presenting Buddhism as "a rational philosophy and way of life".
Mission 5Read the source

Four truths as four tasks

In the Buddha's first sermon (the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta), each of the four noble truths comes with something to do (paraphrase):

  • Dukkha is to be fully understood.
  • Its arising (craving) is to be abandoned.
  • Its ceasing is to be realised.
  • The path is to be developed.

Batchelor reads this as the heart of the matter: not four truths to believe, but four tasks to carry out: embrace suffering, let go of reactive craving, see its stopping, cultivate the path (paraphrase of After Buddhism).

Q1 What changes if the truths are tasks?

Show answer

The test of a Buddhist becomes what they do and how they live, not what they assent to.

Q2 Which word in the sermon supports his reading?

Show answer

Each truth has a verb: understood, abandoned, realised, developed.

Mission 5

Agnostic about rebirth and karma

  • In Buddhism Without Beliefs, Batchelor calls for an agnostic stance: neither affirming nor denying rebirth, since it cannot be known.
  • Later works lean further: he treats rebirth and karma as a moral cosmos the Buddha inherited from his culture, not his distinctive teaching.
  • What remains central: dependent arising, mindfulness, the ethical path, and awakening understood as a way of living freely now.
  • He values the Kalama Sutta's call to test teachings in experience (see Mission 4).
  • Critics, including traditional teachers such as the Theravada scholar-monk Bhikkhu Bodhi, reply that rebirth and a transcendent nibbana run through the whole Pali Canon; removing them may reduce Buddhism to therapy.

Mission 5

David Brazier: Buddhism is a religion

  • David Brazier (Dharmavidya): British Buddhist teacher and psychotherapist; founded the Amida Trust (1996), a Pure Land-based community.
  • Key works: The Feeling Buddha (1997); The New Buddhism (2001).
  • He argues that Buddhism is a religion: at its heart is faith (shraddha) and refuge: a relationship with the Buddha (for him, Amida) as something beyond oneself.
  • Humans are limited, "foolish beings" (bombu); awakening is not a self-improvement project but a response to grace and to others.
  • Stripping out devotion, faith and the sacred risks turning Buddhism into a self-help technique serving the individual ego.
  • Your spec: Brazier "claims Buddhism is a religion".
Mission 5Diversity

Batchelor and Brazier compared

  • What is Buddhism? Batchelor: a rational philosophy and practical way of life. Brazier: a religion of faith, refuge and devotion.
  • The Buddha: Batchelor: a wise human teacher. Brazier: an object of faith and refuge, more than a teacher.
  • Rebirth and the unseen: Batchelor: agnostic or sceptical. Brazier: affirms a spiritual reality beyond the self.
  • Main practice: Batchelor: mindfulness and ethical living. Brazier: nembutsu, devotion and service.
  • Shared ground: both are British converts who adapt Buddhism for the West; both draw on the early texts to make their case.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is Buddhism a religion or a philosophy?

  • Claim: Buddhism is best understood as a rational philosophy and way of life, not a religion.
  • For: no creator God; the Buddha urged testing teachings (Kalama Sutta); the four truths can be read as tasks.
  • For: many Western Buddhists practise meditation and ethics with no belief in rebirth.
  • Against: in Asia, Buddhism is lived with refuge, devotion, merit, rebirth and ritual; most Buddhists treat it as a religion.
  • Against: "religion" need not mean God: faith, refuge and a transcendent goal (nibbana) fit wider definitions.
  • Against: the "rational" Buddhism of the West may be a recent construction, picking texts to suit modern tastes.
  • Spec issues: "The legitimacy of Western presentations of Buddhism as atheistic and secular"; "The question of whether Buddhism is a religion."
  • Judgement criteria: Which definition of religion? Early texts, or living tradition? Who has authority to say?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Zen (Chinese Chan) teaches that Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly in a sudden awakening (satori), passed on mind to mind in a teacher lineage.
  • Rinzai stresses koans and breakthrough; Soto stresses shikantaza, with practice and realisation one (Dōgen).
  • Scripture, ritual and words remain, but as pointers: a "special transmission outside the scriptures".

Mission 2

  • Amitabha's Primal Vow promises birth in the Pure Land (Sukhavati) to all who entrust themselves and call his name; the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu", is going for refuge in Amida.
  • Honen (Jodo-shu): constant recitation in reliance on the vow. Shinran (Jodo Shinshu): shinjin is Amida's gift; nembutsu is gratitude.

Mission 3

  • Nichiren (1222–1282) taught that in mappō only the Lotus Sutra saves; chanting its title, Nam(u)-myōhō-renge-kyō, before the Gohonzon reveals Buddhahood in this life.
  • He saw other sutras as provisional and condemned rival schools (shakubuku).

Mission 4

  • The Kalama Sutta encourages testing teachings in experience, though not as a laboratory science; Buddhists respond to challenges to karma, rebirth, realms and miracles by reinterpretation, by setting limits to science, or by revising.

Mission 5

  • Secular Buddhism (Stephen Batchelor) presents the dharma as a practical way of life: the four truths as tasks; agnostic about rebirth.
  • David Brazier replies that Buddhism is a religion: faith and refuge in what is beyond the self are its heart.
