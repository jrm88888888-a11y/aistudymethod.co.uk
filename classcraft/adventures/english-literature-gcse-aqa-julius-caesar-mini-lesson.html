Mission 1

Named after Caesar, he dies halfway through. Whose tragedy is it?

Make a guess: Named after Caesar, he dies halfway through. Whose tragedy is it?

Julius Caesar · plot and tragic shapeScreen 1

The play in five acts

Julius Caesar was first staged in 1599: a Swiss visitor, Thomas Platter, saw it on 21 September in a thatched playhouse across the Thames, probably the new Globe. It was first printed in 1623. Shakespeare's main source was Plutarch's Lives, in Thomas North's English translation of 1579.

ActWhat happens
1Rome celebrates Caesar's civil-war victory. A Soothsayer warns him of the Ides of March (15 March). Cassius works on Brutus; Casca reports that Caesar refused a crown three times.
2Brutus decides Caesar must die and joins the conspirators. Calphurnia dreams of Caesar's murder; Decius persuades him to go to the Senate.
3The conspirators stab Caesar (Scene 1). Brutus lets Antony speak at the funeral; Antony turns the crowd, who riot (Scene 2).
4Antony, Octavius and Lepidus take power and condemn their enemies. Brutus and Cassius quarrel, then make up. Caesar's ghost visits Brutus.
5At Philippi, Cassius and then Brutus take their own lives. Antony praises Brutus; Octavius orders an honourable burial.

Julius Caesar · plot and tragic shapeScreen 5

Whose tragedy?

  • A tragedy follows a great figure to death, partly through their own choices.
  • The murder sits at the centre (3.1); Acts 3–5 show what it unleashes.
  • Brutus: admired, "with himself at war" (1.2), he makes a fatal choice and dies. His flaw may be idealism: he trusts Antony and the crowd.
  • Caesar: the title role, blinded by pride. He is alive in only three scenes, yet his ghost and name drive Acts 4–5.
  • Strong answers weigh both.

Mission 2

Brutus loves Caesar, yet stabs him. How does he talk himself into it?

Make a guess: Brutus loves Caesar, yet stabs him. How does he talk himself into it?

Julius Caesar · BrutusScreen 1

Brutus before the plot

  • A respected senator, Brutus calls himself "with himself at war" (1.2).
  • He fears the people will "Choose Caesar for their king", yet says "I love him well" (1.2).
  • His ancestor drove out Rome's last king: "My ancestors did from the streets of Rome / The Tarquin drive, when he was call’d a king." (2.1).
  • Cassius works on Brutus's honour: he flatters him and has forged letters thrown in at his window (1.2–2.1).

Julius Caesar · BrutusScreen 6

Two readings of Brutus

  • Noble idealist: he acts "for the general", gains nothing and dies by his own hand. Antony calls him "the noblest Roman of them all" (5.5).
  • Self-deceiver: he admits Caesar's conduct gives no reason, then must "Fashion it thus"; he worries about how the killing will look; Cassius manipulates him easily.
  • His last words, "I kill’d not thee with half so good a will." (5.5), fit both: guilt, or calm acceptance.

Mission 3

A god to Rome, a 'sick girl' to Cassius. Which Caesar is real?

Make a guess: A god to Rome, a 'sick girl' to Cassius. Which Caesar is real?

Julius Caesar · Caesar and CassiusScreen 1

Caesar in public

  • Caesar often speaks of himself by name, in the third person: "for always I am Caesar." (1.2); "Caesar shall forth." (2.2).
  • He waves away the Soothsayer: "He is a dreamer; let us leave him. Pass." (1.2).
  • Yet Shakespeare shows weaknesses: "this ear is deaf" (1.2), and Casca reports that he "fell down in the market-place", and Brutus names it the "falling-sickness" (epilepsy).
  • Alive in only three scenes (1.2, 2.2, 3.1), he is mostly seen through others' eyes.

Julius Caesar · Caesar and CassiusScreen 5

Caesar reads Cassius

  • Caesar warns Antony: "Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look; / He thinks too much: such men are dangerous." (1.2)
  • He is right. Alone, Cassius plans to win Brutus with forged letters: "Thy honourable metal may be wrought / From that it is dispos’d" (1.2).
  • Cassius's motive is debated: love of liberty ("I was born free as Caesar; so were you;") or envy ("Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus.").

Shakespeare gives us two Caesars: the public figure who speaks of himself as "Caesar" and claims to be "constant as the northern star", and a man who is deaf in one ear and falls in a fit. Cassius, who sees through others, makes the most of the weakness for his own ends.

Mission 4

Everyone calls Caesar ambitious. Does the play ever prove it?

Make a guess: Everyone calls Caesar ambitious. Does the play ever prove it?

Julius Caesar · power and ambitionScreen 1

Rome without kings

  • Romans told how their ancestors drove out the last king, Tarquin, and set up a republic, governed by the Senate and elected officials.
  • Shortly before his death on 15 March 44 BC, the Ides of March, Caesar was named dictator for life after winning a civil war.
  • So "king" is an explosive word. Brutus's ancestor drove out "The Tarquin" (2.1), and Casca hears the senators "Mean to establish Caesar as a king;" (1.3).

Weighing the evidence

  • Caesar agrees to stay home, until Decius says "The Senate have concluded / To give this day a crown to mighty Caesar." Then he goes (2.2).
  • In the Senate he calls himself "constant as the northern star", fixed above all other men (3.1).

Ambition outlives Caesar

  • The killers cry "Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead!" (3.1), yet Rome gets riots and civil war.
  • Antony, Octavius and Lepidus condemn enemies to death, "their names are prick’d", and plan "How to cut off some charge in legacies." (4.1): cutting Caesar's gifts to the people.

  • At Philippi, Antony calls Octavius "Caesar" (5.1). The real Octavius became Rome's first emperor, Augustus.

?Everyone calls Caesar ambitious. Does the play ever prove it?

The play never shows Caesar grasping for power. We get Casca's opinion, Caesar's pride and the lure of a crown, set against Brutus's admission and Antony's reminders. The killers act on a fear of the future, and after the murder, ambition passes to Antony and Octavius.

Mission 5

Brutus wins the crowd. Minutes later, Antony turns it. How?

Make a guess: Brutus wins the crowd. Minutes later, Antony turns it. How?

Julius Caesar · the funeral speechesScreen 1

Two speeches, one crowd

  • Act 3 Scene 2, the Forum. Brutus speaks first and leaves; Antony has agreed not to "blame us" (3.1).
  • Brutus speaks in prose: no regular rhythm, plain and reasoned.
  • Antony speaks in blank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter (usually ten syllables, five stresses).
  • Rhetoric persuades through ethos (the speaker's character), logos (reasoning) and pathos (feeling).
  • Brutus opens "Romans, countrymen, and lovers"; Antony, "Friends, Romans, countrymen".

?Brutus wins the crowd. Minutes later, Antony turns it. How?

Brutus offers balanced reasons in prose and trusts the crowd to judge. Antony, in verse, never openly breaks his promise: he repeats "honourable" until it sounds ironic, weeps, shows the wounded body and teases the will, until the crowd turn on the conspirators themselves.

Julius Caesar · omens and fateScreen 5

Reading the signs

  • Signs need interpreting. Cicero warns: "But men may construe things after their fashion, / Clean from the purpose of the things themselves." (1.3)
  • Cassius reads the storm as a sign against Caesar (1.3); Decius re-reads the bloody dream as "a vision fair and fortunate." (2.2)
  • Cassius says "Men at some time are masters of their fates:" (1.2). Yet at Philippi: "Now I change my mind, / And partly credit things that do presage." (5.1)
Julius Caesar · omens and fateScreen 6

Caesar's spirit

  • The Ghost calls itself "Thy evil spirit, Brutus." and promises "thou shalt see me at Philippi." (4.3). Real, or guilt? Brutus first blames "the weakness of mine eyes".
  • In North's Plutarch the visitor calls itself Brutus's evil spirit and is not named as Caesar; Shakespeare's stage direction makes it the Ghost of Caesar.
  • Brutus feels its revenge: "O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! / Thy spirit walks abroad, and turns our swords / In our own proper entrails." (5.3)

?Storms, ghosts, a dream of blood. Was Caesar's death fated?

The play fills Rome with omens, and the Soothsayer's and the Ghost's predictions come true. But Caesar chooses to ignore warnings, Decius and Cassius twist signs to suit themselves, and Cassius dies by misreading what he sees. Shakespeare leaves fate and choice in tension.

Mission 7

Why stage a ruler's murder in 1599, under an old, heirless queen?

Make a guess: Why stage a ruler's murder in 1599, under an old, heirless queen?

Julius Caesar · succession and the 1599 audienceScreen 1

England in 1599

  • Elizabeth I, born in 1533, was in her mid-sixties, unmarried and childless, and had named no heir.
  • A 1571 law made it an offence to write that anyone except the Queen's own children was her rightful heir; public debate of the succession was banned, though pamphlets still argued about it in the 1590s.
  • After a northern rising in 1569, the Homily against Disobedience and Wilful Rebellion joined the sermons read in churches.

Julius Caesar · succession and the 1599 audienceScreen 4

After the killing

  • Antony prophesies "Domestic fury and fierce civil strife / Shall cumber all the parts of Italy;" (3.1).
  • The mob kills Cinna the poet for sharing a conspirator's name: "Tear him for his bad verses," (3.3).
  • Three new rulers condemn their enemies (4.1); Rome splits into armies (Acts 4–5).
  • At Philippi, Antony calls the young Octavius "Caesar" (5.1), and Octavius has the last word (5.5). Killing Caesar does not end Caesars.
Julius Caesar · succession and the 1599 audienceScreen 5

Two ways an audience might take it

  • A warning against rebellion: killing a ruler brings riot and civil war, as the church homily taught.
  • A defence of liberty: Brutus asks "Shall Rome stand under one man’s awe?" (2.1) and dies "the noblest Roman".
  • Shakespeare gives both sides weight. One reading: the Roman setting and this balance let the play explore a dangerous subject safely. That is an interpretation, not a recorded fact.

Model answer

After the murder, Antony prophesies "Domestic fury and fierce civil strife / Shall cumber all the parts of Italy;". The words "Domestic" and "civil" show Romans fighting Romans. For an audience whose queen was old and had named no heir, this picture of a country tearing itself apart once its ruler is gone would feel close to home, even set safely in ancient Rome.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why stage a ruler's murder in 1599, under an old, heirless queen?

In 1599 England's queen was old and had named no heir, and the succession could not be openly debated. A Roman play let Shakespeare stage the killing of a ruler and its aftermath, riots, purges and civil war, while giving both the case for liberty and the case for order their weight.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Julius Caesar (staged 1599, printed 1623; source: North's Plutarch) splits at its centre: Caesar is killed in Act 3 Scene 1, and Acts 3–5 show the consequences.
  • Many readers see Brutus as the tragic hero (inner conflict, fatal choice, fall, final praise); others argue for Caesar, whose spirit drives the second half.
  • Paper 1 Section A gives you an extract, then the whole play: knowing the five acts lets you choose moments from both halves quickly.
  • Brutus loves Caesar but fears a crown; his ancestor drove out Rome's last king, and Cassius plays on this sense of honour.
  • In the orchard soliloquy (2.1) he admits Caesar's present conduct is no reason, so he reasons from the future: "think him as a serpent’s egg".
  • His honourable choices (no oath, sparing Antony, letting him speak, marching to Philippi) lead to disaster; readers debate whether he is a noble idealist or a self-deceiver.
  • Caesar speaks of himself in the third person and dismisses warnings, but Shakespeare also shows his deaf ear and his fit; he is mostly seen through others' eyes.
  • Cassius's river story and the Colossus simile are persuasion aimed at Brutus: they shrink Caesar's body and inflate his power.
  • Caesar rightly fears Cassius, who "thinks too much"; Cassius's motive (liberty or envy) is open to debate, and he dies misreading the battle.
  • Rome had driven out its kings, so "king" was a dangerous word; Caesar had been made dictator for life before his death in 44 BC.
  • Evidence of Caesar's ambition is reported or suggestive (Casca's "to my thinking", the crown that lures him to the Senate, the northern star); Brutus and Antony offer evidence against.
  • Killing Caesar doesn't end ambition: the triumvirs condemn their enemies, and a new "Caesar", Octavius, wins.
  • Brutus speaks in prose, with antithesis and rhetorical questions: an appeal to reason and honour (logos, ethos).
  • Antony speaks in blank verse and uses verbal irony ("honourable man"), pauses, the mantle, the body and the will: an appeal to feeling (pathos).
  • The crowd's swing, from "Let him be Caesar" to riot, shows how easily the people can be moved, a danger the play takes seriously.
  • Omens run through the play (Soothsayer, storm, Calphurnia's dream, Artemidorus, the Ghost, the birds at Philippi); Shakespeare took several from Plutarch.
  • Caesar's "Cowards die many times before their deaths" makes pride sound like courage and carries dramatic irony.
  • Signs must be interpreted, and characters read them to suit themselves; the play keeps fate and free will in tension.
  • 1599: Elizabeth I was in her mid-sixties with no named heir; a 1571 statute restricted talk of the succession; church homilies preached against rebellion.
  • The play stages a ruler's murder at a Roman distance and shows its consequences: Antony's prophecy, the mob, the purges, civil war, and a new "Caesar".
  • It can be read as a warning against rebellion or a defence of liberty; strong answers link specific moments to the context and weigh both.

Key quotations to learn

  • Than that poor Brutus, with himself at war, (Act 1 Scene 2 (Brutus)) Inner conflict: a mark of the tragic hero.
  • But I am constant as the northern star, (Act 3 Scene 1 (Caesar)) Pride just before his fall.
  • Et tu, Brute?—Then fall, Caesar! (Act 3 Scene 1 (Caesar)) The turning point at the play's centre.
  • O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! (Act 5 Scene 3 (Brutus)) Caesar's spirit outlives his body.
  • This was the noblest Roman of them all. (Act 5 Scene 5 (Antony)) The final verdict on Brutus.
  • Than that poor Brutus, with himself at war, (Act 1 Scene 2 (Brutus)) His inner conflict.
  • I know no personal cause to spurn at him, / But for the general. (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) He claims a public motive only.
  • And therefore think him as a serpent’s egg (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) Killing Caesar for what he might become.
  • Let us be sacrificers, but not butchers, Caius. (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) Wanting the killing to look noble; spares Antony.
  • There is a tide in the affairs of men, (Act 4 Scene 3 (Brutus)) His fatal decision to march to Philippi.
  • Caesar, now be still: / I kill’d not thee with half so good a will. (Act 5 Scene 5 (Brutus)) His last words, spoken to Caesar.
  • for always I am Caesar. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Caesar)) The public self, in the third person.
  • Yond Cassius has a lean and hungry look; / He thinks too much: such men are dangerous. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Caesar)) Caesar reads Cassius accurately.
  • Why, man, he doth bestride the narrow world / Like a Colossus (Act 1 Scene 2 (Cassius)) Caesar's power, inflated to shame Brutus.
  • Caesar doth bear me hard, but he loves Brutus. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Cassius, alone)) A hint of envy in his motive.
  • But I am constant as the northern star, (Act 3 Scene 1 (Caesar)) Pride moments before the stabbing.
  • Caesar, thou art reveng’d, / Even with the sword that kill’d thee. (Act 5 Scene 3 (Cassius)) Cassius's death as Caesar's revenge.
  • he would fain have had it (Act 1 Scene 2 (Casca)) A biased report that he wanted the crown.
  • Th’ abuse of greatness is, when it disjoins / Remorse from power (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) Brutus's definition of a tyrant.
  • But ’tis a common proof, / That lowliness is young ambition’s ladder, (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) Ambition as a ladder climbed, then scorned.
  • Ambition should be made of sterner stuff: (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Antony's challenge to the charge.
  • Liberty! Freedom! Tyranny is dead! (Act 3 Scene 1 (Cinna)) The killers' slogan, soon undercut.
  • These many then shall die; their names are prick’d. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Antony)) The new rulers' ruthless power.
  • Not that I loved Caesar less, but that I loved Rome more. (Act 3 Scene 2 (Brutus)) Antithesis: reason and patriotism.
  • Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears; (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Opens with "Friends", not "Romans".
  • I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him. (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Says the opposite of what he does.
  • For Brutus is an honourable man, (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Repetition that turns ironic.
  • This was the most unkindest cut of all; (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Pathos: Brutus's betrayal shown on the mantle.
  • Mischief, thou art afoot, / Take thou what course thou wilt! (Act 3 Scene 2 (Antony)) Antony alone: the riot is what he wanted.
  • Beware the Ides of March. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Soothsayer)) The first warning, ignored.
  • The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings. (Act 1 Scene 2 (Cassius)) Choice, not fate.
  • When beggars die, there are no comets seen; / The heavens themselves blaze forth the death of princes. (Act 2 Scene 2 (Calphurnia)) Heaven marks a ruler's death.
  • Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once. (Act 2 Scene 2 (Caesar)) Pride dressed as courage.
  • Thy evil spirit, Brutus. (Act 4 Scene 3 (Ghost)) The supernatural, or guilt.
  • O Julius Caesar, thou art mighty yet! (Act 5 Scene 3 (Brutus)) Caesar's spirit seems to win.
  • Shall Rome stand under one man’s awe? (Act 2 Scene 1 (Brutus)) The case against one-man rule.
  • How many ages hence / Shall this our lofty scene be acted over / In States unborn, and accents yet unknown! (Act 3 Scene 1 (Cassius)) Metatheatre: the 1599 audience is one of those "ages".
  • Domestic fury and fierce civil strife / Shall cumber all the parts of Italy; (Act 3 Scene 1 (Antony)) Civil war follows the death of a ruler.
  • Cry havoc and let slip the dogs of war, (Act 3 Scene 1 (Antony)) Revenge unleashed.
  • Tear him for his bad verses, tear him for his bad verses. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Fourth Citizen)) The mob out of control.
  • So call the field to rest, and let’s away, / To part the glories of this happy day. (Act 5 Scene 5 (Octavius)) Order restored by a new ruler.
