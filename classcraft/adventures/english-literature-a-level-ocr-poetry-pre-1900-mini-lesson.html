Poetry Pre-1900

Movements · metaphysical and Augustan

Wit, conceit and the couplet

Two earlier traditions:

  • Metaphysical poetry (early 17th century, associated with Donne) — intellectual wit and the conceit, an extended, ingenious comparison yoking unlike ideas together. Samuel Johnson coined the label “metaphysical poets”, half in criticism; T.S. Eliot later praised their fusion of thought and feeling.
  • Augustan poetry (late 17th–18th century, associated with Pope) — order, balance and satire in the tightly closed heroic couplet (rhymed iambic pentameter pairs).

Form is argument: a metaphysical conceit performs restless thinking; a heroic couplet performs control and judgement.

Movements · Romantic and Victorian

Nature, imagination and the monologue

Two later traditions:

  • Romantic poetry (c.1789–1830) — the primacy of feeling, imagination and nature; the sublime. Wordsworth’s Preface to Lyrical Ballads defines poetry as “the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings” recollected in tranquillity.
  • Victorian poetry — faith and doubt after Darwin, the elegy, and the dramatic monologue (associated with Browning), in which a single speaker unwittingly reveals themselves to a silent listener.

Persona alert: in a dramatic monologue the speaker is emphatically not the poet — the gap between what the speaker says and what they reveal is the whole art.

AO2 · form and metre

How a pre-1900 poem is built

Pre-1900 poetry is usually metrical, so analyse the beat:

  • Iambic pentameter — five unstressed-stressed pairs; the staple line, and its variations carry meaning.
  • Fixed forms — the sonnet (with its volta), the ode, the elegy, the heroic couplet, the ballad stanza.
  • Rhyme and its patterns — full rhyme for closure, the couplet for epigram, enclosed rhyme for containment.
  • Enjambment and caesura — running-on and mid-line pause working with or against the metre.

Scan for the exception: a metrically regular poem tells you most where it breaks the pattern.

AO4 · comparing poem and play

Across the forms

You compare a poem with a play, so respect the difference of form:

  1. Lead with the shared theme — love, death, ambition — not with either text.
  2. Compare voicing: a dramatic monologue and a soliloquy both stage self-revelation, but one is on a page, the other on a stage before an audience.
  3. Interleave in theme-paragraphs across both texts.
  4. Prize the productive difference between lyric compression and dramatic externalisation.

Stem: “Where the play’s soliloquy is overheard by an audience, the poem’s monologue traps a silent listener inside the fiction.”

AO3 · context

The world behind the poem

Context for pre-1900 poetry tracks the movements’ concerns:

  • Metaphysical/Augustan — religious controversy, then Enlightenment order and satire.
  • Romantic — revolution, industrialisation and a new faith in nature and the individual imagination.
  • Victorian — scientific challenge to faith, empire and rapid social change.

Weld it: because the Victorian poem is written into a crisis of religious doubt, its elegy cannot simply console — a context that changes the reading.

AO5 · interpretation

Reading the poem through theory

Choose a lens that produces a textual claim:

  • Feminist — who speaks and who is spoken about; the silenced female subject of a male monologue.
  • Eco-critical — the Romantic construction of nature as refuge or as commodity.
  • Psychoanalytic — the desire the speaker cannot admit but the poem lets slip.

Discipline: not “this is patriarchal”, but “the monologue gives the man every line and the woman only her silence, so the poem’s form itself enacts the power it describes.”
