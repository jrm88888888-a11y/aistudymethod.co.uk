Mission 1

An island outside the Empire

  • Ireland (Latin Hibernia) was never part of the Roman Empire: no Roman towns, roads, coins in daily use or Roman law.
  • Power was local. Perhaps 100–150 small kingdoms, each a túath (people, territory) under a rí (king).
  • Kings owed tribute to over-kings, and over-kings to provincial kings (e.g. Ulaid, Laigin, Mumu).
  • New dynasties were rising in the fifth century, notably the Uí Néill in the north and midlands, later linked with Tara.
  • No capital and no single ruler: a missionary had to win many kings, one by one.
Mission 1

A society of rank and kin

  • Society was hierarchical: kings, nobles, free farmers, and the unfree (including slaves).
  • Each person's rank fixed their honour-price (the compensation due if they were wronged).
  • Kin-groups held land and were answerable for their members.
  • Learned classes had high status and could cross túath boundaries: druids, filid (poets, keepers of genealogy and praise), and brehons (judges of customary law).
  • Most of what we know comes from later law tracts (7th–8th centuries), so the fifth-century picture is partly reconstructed.
Mission 1

The old religion

  • Polytheism: many gods, known mainly from later stories written down by Christian monks (e.g. Lug, the Dagda, the goddess Brigit).
  • Druids: priests, seers and advisers to kings.
  • Four seasonal festivals: Samain (1 November), Imbolc (1 February), Beltaine (1 May), Lugnasad (1 August).
  • Sacred places: wells, trees, hills, burial mounds (e.g. Newgrange, much older), royal sites such as Tara and Emain Macha.
  • Caution: almost all evidence is filtered through Christian writers; pre-Christian belief is hard to reconstruct.
Mission 1

Contact with Roman Britain

  • Ireland was not cut off. Trade: Roman pottery, coins and metalwork have been found, e.g. at Newgrange and on the coast north of Dublin.
  • Raids: Irish raiders (Latin Scotti) attacked Britain in the late fourth and fifth centuries and took captives. Patrick was one.
  • Settlement: Irish groups settled in west Wales and western Scotland.
  • So Irish people met Christians in Britain and Gaul, and Christian slaves and traders lived in Ireland.
  • Christianity could arrive quietly, through contact, before any organised mission.
Mission 1

Evidence for Christians before Patrick

  • Prosper of Aquitaine says Palladius was sent in 431 to Irish people "believing in Christ": there were already Christians to lead.
  • Language: some early Irish church words were borrowed from Latin in an old form, e.g. Cáisc (Easter, from Pascha), cruimther (priest, from presbyter). Linguists date some to before Patrick's time; the dating is debated.
  • Southern saints: later Lives claim Ailbe (Emly), Declán (Ardmore), Ciarán (Saigir) and Ibar preached before Patrick. These Lives are late and partisan; most scholars doubt the claim, but it may preserve a memory of early southern Christianity.
  • Captives: Patrick's own story shows Christians being carried into Ireland.
  • Most early Christian evidence points to the south and east, nearest Britain and Gaul.

Mission 1Read the source

Prosper's Chronicle

Prosper of Aquitaine (c. 390–c. 455), a Gaulish writer close to Rome, Chronicle, entry for 431 (Latin original):

> "Ad Scottos in Christum credentes ordinatus a papa Caelestino Palladius primus episcopus mittitur." (translation: "Palladius, ordained by Pope Celestine, is sent to the Irish believing in Christ as their first bishop.")

  • Entry for 429: the deacon Palladius urged Pope Celestine to send Germanus of Auxerre to Britain to fight the Pelagian teaching there (that people can be good without God's grace).
  • Prosper's Against the Collator (c. 433–434), paraphrase: by ordaining a bishop for the Irish, Celestine kept the Roman island (Britain) Catholic and made the barbarian island (Ireland) Christian.

Q1 What does "first bishop" imply about the Church in Ireland before 431?

Show answer

There were believers but no bishop: no organised church.

Q2 Why might Rome care about Ireland in 431?

Show answer

Concern for orthodoxy against Pelagianism, close to Britain; care for existing believers.

Mission 1

What happened to Palladius?

  • Prosper says nothing more about him: no record of success or failure.
  • Muirchú (late seventh century), paraphrase: Palladius was sent, but God did not grant him success; the Irish would not accept his teaching; he left and soon died in Britain.
  • Leinster traditions link him with three churches in what is now Co. Wicklow and Co. Kildare (Cell Fine, Tech na Román, Domnach Arta).
  • Some later annals give the bishops Secundinus, Auxilius and Iserninus (arriving c. 439) as Patrick's helpers; some scholars think they may have belonged to Palladius' mission.
  • Muirchú wrote to glorify Patrick and Armagh: a failed Palladius makes Patrick the true apostle.
Mission 1

Why Palladius matters

  • First fixed date in Irish church history (431), from a near-contemporary source.
  • Proves an organised mission from Rome, sent to existing Christians: Ireland's Christianity did not begin with Patrick.
  • Shows Rome's interest in Ireland, linked to defending orthodoxy in Britain.
  • "Two Patricks": T. F. O'Rahilly (1882–1953), The Two Patricks (1942), argued that the work of Palladius (also called Patricius, in his view) was later credited to Patrick. D. A. Binchy (1899–1989), "Patrick and his biographers" (1962), criticised the theory but agreed Patrick's fame absorbed others' work.
  • Most historians now see two missions: Palladius among Christians in the south-east; Patrick, mostly further north and west, among people who had not heard the gospel.

Mission 1Evaluate

How significant was Palladius?

  • Claim: Palladius matters more to the coming of Christianity to Ireland than Patrick.
  • For: He came first, with papal authority, on a securely dated mission; Patrick's dates are uncertain.
  • For: His mission may have founded the earliest churches in the south-east; his work may have been credited to Patrick (O'Rahilly).
  • Against: We know almost nothing he did; there are no writings of his. Patrick left two first-hand texts.
  • Against: Patrick reached peoples without Christians; Palladius served those already believing.
  • Against: Christians were already present before either man: the faith spread through unnamed slaves and traders too.
  • Judgement criteria: Significance for founding, for spreading, or for later memory? How much weight to give silence in the sources?
Mission 1Evaluate

Religion and its context

  • Claim: The way Christianity arrived in Ireland was shaped more by its social and political context than by religious conviction.
  • For: Christianity came along routes made by trade, raiding and slavery; it spread fastest where contact with Britain was closest.
  • For: A land of many kings meant missionaries had to win kings and nobles first; the Church later fitted into the túath and kin system.
  • For: Rome's interest in 431 was partly political-religious: protecting Catholic Britain from Pelagianism.
  • Against: Captives, Palladius and Patrick acted from faith, often at great risk, not for gain.
  • Against: The Church changed its context too: writing in Latin, new laws, monasteries, and the end of druidic religion.
  • Judgement criteria: Does context explain how faith spread, or why people believed? Do religion and context shape each other?
  • Perennial issue: compare religion and context today, e.g. churches adapting to a secular culture.

Mission 2

Two letters, and only two

  • Patrick left two Latin writings, the earliest documents written in Ireland: the Confessio and the Letter to Coroticus (Latin Epistola).
  • Both survive in copies; the oldest (part of the Confessio) is in the Book of Armagh (c. 807).
  • Everything else comes later, not from Patrick: the contest at Tara from seventh-century Lives (see Mission 5), the snakes from a twelfth-century Life, the shamrock from much later folklore.
  • Dates are uncertain: the annals give a mission from 432 to death in 461; many historians prefer a later life, with death c. 490s. Most agree: active in the fifth century.
  • His Latin is plain and awkward; he calls himself "peccator rusticissimus" (a very unlearned sinner, Confessio 1).
Mission 2

Patrick's life, in his words

  • Born in Roman Britain to a Christian family: father Calpornius, a deacon (Confessio 1) and town councillor (Letter 10); grandfather Potitus, a priest (Confessio 1).
  • Home near a place he calls Bannavem Taburniae: its location is unknown.
  • At almost 16, seized by Irish raiders with "many thousands" and taken to Ireland (Confessio 1).
  • Six years a slave, herding flocks. He had not taken faith seriously; now he prayed constantly (Confessio 16).
  • A voice in sleep told him a ship was ready; he fled about 200 miles to the coast and escaped (Confessio 17).
  • Back in Britain, a dream: a man called Victoricus brought letters; one began "The voice of the Irish", begging the "holy youth" to come and walk among them again (Confessio 23, paraphrase).
  • Trained, ordained, he returned as a bishop (Letter 1).
Mission 2

The Confessio: content and purpose

  • Written late in life ("before I die", Confessio 62, paraphrase), in about 62 short chapters.
  • Content: his captivity, conversion, escape, call, mission, dangers, and a reply to critics.
  • Purpose 1: defence. Church leaders in Britain (his seniores, elders) opposed him; a friend revealed a sin Patrick had confessed from his youth, from before he was a deacon (Confessio 26–32). Some suspected he sought gain.
  • Purpose 2: thanksgiving. In Latin, confessio means praise of God as well as admitting sin. He writes to give thanks for God's grace.
  • Purpose 3: witness. To leave a record of what God did through an unworthy servant.
  • Opens with a short creed of the Trinity (Confessio 4).
Mission 2Read the source

"To the ends of the earth"

Patrick fills his Latin with the Bible. He sees his mission as the fulfilment of Jesus' command:

> "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," (Matthew 28:19, WEBBE)

He applies to himself (Confessio 38) the words Paul quotes:

> "I have set you as a light for the Gentiles, that you should bring salvation to the uttermost parts of the earth." (Acts 13:47, WEBBE)

  • For Patrick, Ireland lay at the edge of the known world: preaching there meant the gospel had reached the end of the earth, and the end of the age was near (Confessio 34, 40, paraphrase).

Q1 Why would "the uttermost parts of the earth" matter to a missionary in Ireland?

Show answer

Ireland was the last land westward; Patrick saw himself fulfilling the command to preach to all nations before the end.

Q2 What does his use of scripture suggest about where he finds authority?

Show answer

In God's word and call, not in his own learning or rank.

Mission 2

Themes of the Confessio

  • Grace to the unworthy: God chose a "stone lying in deep mud" and set it on the wall (Confessio 12, paraphrase).
  • Divine guidance: dreams, voices and visions direct his life (escape, call, a dream of being crushed by Satan and freed by calling "Elijah", Confessio 20).
  • Prayer: as a slave, up to a hundred prayers by day and as many by night, in snow, frost and rain (Confessio 16).
  • Humility and insecurity: ashamed of his poor education compared with trained clergy (Confessio 9–13).
  • Integrity: he took no payment for baptisms or ordinations, and returned gifts (Confessio 49–50).
  • Mission and end-time: urgency to preach to the last people before the end.
Mission 2

The Letter to Coroticus

  • Event: soldiers of a British ruler, Coroticus, raided Ireland and killed or carried off newly baptised converts, still in white garments, the chrism (anointing oil) still on their foreheads (Letter 3, paraphrase).
  • Patrick sent a priest to ask for the captives back; the soldiers mocked him.
  • Content: an open letter: he declares with his own hand that he is a bishop in Ireland; he calls the soldiers fellow citizens not of the holy Romans but of demons (Letter 2, Latin civibus daemoniorum).
  • He excommunicates them: no Christian should eat or drink with them until they repent and free the captives.
  • Themes: pastoral grief, justice for slaves, the unity of Irish and British Christians, his authority as bishop.
  • Who was Coroticus? Often identified with Ceretic, king of Dumbarton (Strathclyde); not certain.

Mission 2

The portrait of Patrick

  • Humble: "a sinner", "least of all the faithful", aware of his weaknesses.
  • Defensive and sensitive: hurt by criticism, anxious to prove his honesty.
  • Prayerful and visionary: he trusts dreams and the inner voice as God's guidance.
  • Courageous: imprisoned, chained, threatened with death, yet he stays (Confessio 52, 55).
  • Steeped in scripture: almost every chapter echoes the Bible, especially Paul.
  • Pastoral: grieves for slaves and converts, especially women who suffered for their faith.
  • Not the later miracle-worker who defeats druids by magic (see Mission 5): his own writings show no contests with kings.
Mission 2

The nature of his mission

  • Where: probably mostly the north and west (his texts name only the "wood of Foclut" near the western sea, Confessio 23); later tradition adds Armagh and Downpatrick.
  • To whom: places "where no one had ever come" to baptise (Confessio 51, paraphrase): people without Christians, unlike Palladius' mission.
  • How: preaching, baptising, confirming, ordaining clergy; gifts to kings for safe passage, and payments to brehons (Confessio 52–53).
  • New communities: sons and daughters of Irish kings became monks and virgins of Christ (Confessio 41–42), a seed of later monasticism (see Mission 3).
  • Style: a bishop working among peoples, not towns; travelling, not settled.
Mission 2

Successes and challenges

  • Successes: he says he baptised many thousands (Confessio 14, 50); clergy ordained "everywhere"; noble and enslaved converts; the faith took root beyond the south-east.
  • Challenges, outside: hostile kings; imprisonment for 14 days in chains (Confessio 52); constant danger of death; raids like Coroticus'.
  • Challenges, inside: suspicion from British church leaders; his own lack of learning; converts persecuted by their families, especially slave women (Confessio 42).
  • Limits: no evidence he converted all Ireland; paganism lasted well after him; his structure of bishops would change (see Mission 3).
  • How far his own claims can be checked: we have only his side.

Mission 2

Continuing significance

  • Patron saint of Ireland; feast day 17 March.
  • Pilgrimage: Croagh Patrick (Co. Mayo, last Sunday of July); Lough Derg (Co. Donegal); Downpatrick and Saul (Co. Down).
  • Shared figure: honoured by Catholics, the Church of Ireland and Presbyterians alike; a figure before later divisions.
  • His writings still speak to Christians about grace, calling and forgiveness, and to all readers about slavery and human trafficking.
  • Both Catholic and Church of Ireland archbishops of Armagh hold the title Primate of All Ireland as heirs of Patrick.
  • The famous hymn "St Patrick's Breastplate" is later (seventh or eighth century), attributed to him.
Mission 2Evaluate

How successful was Patrick?

  • Claim: Patrick deserves the title "Apostle of Ireland".
  • For: He reached peoples with no Christians, baptised thousands and ordained clergy, by his own account.
  • For: He left the only first-hand writings of the period; his faith and courage inspired the later Irish church.
  • Against: Palladius came first, and Christians were already present (see Mission 1).
  • Against: We have only Patrick's own account; the title grew through seventh-century writers promoting Armagh.
  • Against: Ireland was not wholly Christian at his death; later monastic founders did much of the work (see Mission 3).
  • Judgement criteria: Apostle as first, as most effective, or as founding figure? How far can self-testimony be trusted?
Mission 2Evaluate

Faith, prayer and religious authority

  • Claim: Personal faith and prayer give a religious leader stronger authority than church office.
  • For: Patrick's authority came from God's call in dreams and prayer; he went on even when church leaders in Britain doubted him.
  • For: His converts followed a man of prayer and integrity, not a learned official.
  • Against: Patrick himself appeals to his office: "I declare that I am a bishop" (Letter 1, paraphrase); excommunication needs church authority.
  • Against: Private visions can mislead; churches test them by scripture and by the community (1 John 4:1).
  • Today: compare charismatic leaders and ordained clergy; private prayer and shared worship.
  • Judgement criteria: Can authority be valid without both a call and recognition? Who tests the call?

Mission 3

Where monasticism came from

  • Egypt and Syria (late 3rd–4th centuries): Antony (c. 251–356) left everything for the desert; Pachomius (c. 292–348) founded communities living by a rule.
  • Gaul: Martin of Tours (d. 397) founded Ligugé and Marmoutier; John Cassian (c. 360–c. 435) brought desert teaching to the West (Institutes, Conferences).
  • Britain: monastic life grew in western Britain; Candida Casa (Whithorn), linked with Ninian, and Welsh houses were remembered as schools for Irish founders.
  • These ideals reached Ireland through Britain and Gaul, by the same routes as Christianity itself (see Mission 1).
Mission 3Read the source

The call to leave everything

Antony's call, as told by Athanasius (Life of Antony, c. 360), came when he heard this gospel in church:

> "If you want to be perfect, go, sell what you have, and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me." (Matthew 19:21, WEBBE)

  • Monks took this literally: give up property, marriage and home to follow Christ fully.
  • In Ireland, Patrick already writes of the sons and daughters of kings becoming monks and virgins of Christ (Confessio 41, paraphrase;).

Q1 What three things does the verse ask the follower to do?

Show answer

Sell possessions, give to the poor, follow Christ.

Q2 Why might Patrick's monks and virgins upset their families?

Show answer

Giving up marriage and inheritance broke kin plans and alliances; Patrick says some were persecuted.

Mission 3

The founders (1): Enda and Finnian

  • Enda of Aran (Éanna, d. c. 530): tradition says he trained at Candida Casa; founded Killeany on Inis Mór (Aran Islands).
  • Remembered as a father of Irish monasticism: a model of severe asceticism (fasting, hard labour, island remoteness). Ciarán of Clonmacnoise is said to have studied with him.
  • Finnian of Clonard (d. 549, in a plague): trained in Wales (traditions link him with David, Cadoc and Gildas); founded Clonard (Co. Meath) c. 520.
  • Called the "teacher of the saints of Ireland": tradition says the "Twelve Apostles of Ireland" studied with him (among them Ciarán of Clonmacnoise, Colum Cille and Brendan of Clonfert).
  • Impact: Clonard as a school; Finnian's pupils spread the model across Ireland.
Mission 3

The founders (2): Brigit, Brendan, Comgall

  • Brigit of Kildare (d. c. 524): founded Cill Dara ("church of the oak"), a double monastery of women and men, with Bishop Conlaed for the sacraments. Kildare became one of the greatest churches in Ireland (see Mission 5).
  • Brendan of Clonfert (c. 484–c. 577): founded Clonfert (Co. Galway) c. 557; famed for sea voyages, later told in the Voyage of Saint Brendan (Navigatio, eighth–tenth century).
  • Comgall of Bangor (d. c. 602): founded Bangor (Co. Down) c. 555–559; a strict rule; tradition of continual praise in choirs; teacher of Columbanus, who carried Irish monasticism to Gaul and Italy.
  • The Antiphonary of Bangor (late seventh century) preserves its hymns and prayers.
  • Impact: a network of great houses: west (Aran, Clonfert), midlands (Clonard), east (Kildare, Bangor).

Mission 3

Distinctive features

  • Abbot (Irish ab) rules; his successor is the coarb (comarba, heir) of the founder.
  • Paruchia (federation): a founder's houses, scattered across Ireland, owe loyalty to the mother house, not to a local bishop.
  • Bishops live within or alongside monasteries to ordain and confirm, under the abbot's rule of life.
  • Layout: an enclosure (vallum), small cells (stone clocháns in the west), church, guest-house, fields.
  • Asceticism: fasting, vigils, praying with arms outstretched (the cross-vigil), reciting the 150 psalms, sometimes standing in cold water.
  • Learning: Latin, scripture, copying books (e.g. the Cathach, an early psalter linked with Colum Cille).
  • Peregrinatio: exile for Christ; leaving homeland as the greatest sacrifice.
Mission 3

Three martyrdoms

  • An Irish homily (the Cambrai Homily, late seventh century) names three kinds of martyrdom (paraphrase):
  • Red: death for Christ.
  • White: leaving all one loves for God (exile, separation).
  • Glas (blue-green): fasting, labour and penance, by which one is freed from desires.
  • In a land with few martyrs by blood, monastic self-denial became the heroic way to follow Christ.
  • Link to Jesus' words: "If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me." (Luke 9:23, WEBBE)
Mission 3

Why monasticism appealed

  • Fit with society: no towns, many small kingdoms, strong kin-groups. A monastery worked like a kin-group with land, a head and heirs.
  • Royal support: kings and nobles gave land; abbots were often from the founder's or a royal family.
  • Holiness: monks offered a heroic form of faith; prayer for kings and people.
  • Security and opportunity: education, food, shelter; a path for younger sons and daughters.
  • For women: a recognised life of prayer and leadership (e.g. Brigit), outside marriage alliances.
  • Spiritual seriousness: a clear way to seek salvation.
Mission 3

Role for church and society

  • Church: monasteries became the main centres of worship, pastoral care and training clergy.
  • Learning: brought literacy: Latin, then writing in Irish (laws, annals, poetry, saints' Lives).
  • Economy: large estates; tenants called manaig (monastic clients) farmed the land.
  • Care: hospitality, help for the poor and the sick; sanctuary for those in danger.
  • Mission beyond Ireland: Colum Cille to Iona (563); Columbanus to Gaul (c. 590). Next: penitentials (see Mission 4).
  • Politics: monasteries were allied to dynasties, sometimes even fighting one another (annals record battles between monastic communities in later centuries).
Mission 3

From bishops to abbots

  • Patrick's model (fifth century): bishops leading Christian communities among the peoples (túatha), as in Britain and Gaul (see Mission 2).
  • Seventh century: great monastic federations (Armagh, Kildare, Iona, Clonmacnoise) dominate; abbots hold land and power.
  • Why? Few towns; land held by kin; founders' fame; royal gifts to monasteries; the appeal of monastic holiness.
  • Catalogue of the Saints of Ireland (eighth century or later): a first order of bishops (Patrick's time), a second of priests (the monastic founders), a third of hermits: a later picture of the change.
  • Kathleen Hughes (1926–1977), The Church in Early Irish Society (1966): the episcopal church was overtaken by a monastic one in the sixth–seventh centuries.
Mission 3Diversity

How complete was the change?

  • Traditional view (Hughes): a clear shift: abbots replaced bishops as leaders; the Irish church became monastic in structure.
  • Revisionist view: Richard Sharpe (1954–2020), "Some problems concerning the organization of the Church in early medieval Ireland" (Peritia, 1984), and Colmán Etchingham, Church Organisation in Ireland AD 650 to 1000 (1999):
  • Bishops kept real authority and appear in law tracts with high status.
  • "Monastery" (Latin monasterium, Irish cell) often meant any church, including local churches serving lay people.
  • Many local churches offered pastoral care; the "monastic church" is partly an illusion of the sources, written by monks.
  • Both views agree: abbots of great houses became very powerful by the seventh century.

Mission 3Evaluate

The integrity of the religious life

  • Claim: A religious life cannot keep its integrity once it gains wealth and power.
  • For: Great Irish monasteries grew rich; abbots came from ruling families; some abbots were laymen; communities fought one another.
  • For: The Céli Dé ("clients of God") reform of the late eighth century (e.g. Máel Ruain of Tallaght, d. 792) arose to restore strictness, suggesting decline.
  • Against: Wealth also paid for learning, hospitality and care of the poor; the founders' ideals kept being renewed.
  • Against: Integrity belongs to persons and communities, not structures: many monks stayed faithful in rich houses.
  • Today: monastic communities (e.g. Benedictine Glenstal Abbey), and ecumenical communities such as the Iona Community and Taizé, face the same pull between ideal and institution.
  • Judgement criteria: Is integrity about poverty, sincerity, or service? Can reform within an institution keep it true?
Mission 3Evaluate

Self-denial and asceticism

  • Claim: Self-denial is essential to a serious religious life.
  • For: Jesus calls followers to "deny himself" (Luke 9:23) and fasted (Matthew 4:2); the Irish saints saw discipline as freedom from desire (glas martyrdom).
  • For: Fasting and simplicity are shared by many traditions (Lent, Ramadan, Buddhist monastic rules); many find they sharpen prayer and compassion.
  • Against: Extreme practices can harm the body, which Christians call God's good creation; Jesus warns against showy fasting (Matthew 6:16).
  • Against: Many Protestants, after the Reformation, rejected the idea that self-denial earns merit: grace, not effort, saves.
  • Secular view: self-denial can be valued for health, discipline or ethics (e.g. living simply for the planet), without any religious meaning.
  • Judgement criteria: Is self-denial a means or an end? Does it serve love of God and neighbour? Who judges excess?

Mission 4

Penance before the Irish

  • Penance: turning from sin to God, shown in acts (prayer, fasting, almsgiving), leading to reconciliation.
  • Early church (third–fifth centuries): public penance for grave sins (denying the faith, murder, adultery).
  • The sinner joined an order of penitents, was excluded from communion, did penance publicly, and was reconciled by the bishop.
  • Usually allowed only once in a lifetime; penitents kept lifelong restrictions (e.g. on marriage, army service).
  • Result: many people delayed penance until their deathbed.
Mission 4

The Irish change

  • In Ireland and Britain (sixth century) a new practice grew: private confession to a priest, abbot or soul-friend (Irish anamchara).
  • Repeatable: as often as needed, not once in a lifetime.
  • For all sins, small and great, including sins of thought.
  • A fixed penance for each sin, set out in handbooks called penitentials: often called tariff penance.
  • Roots: monastic spiritual guidance (from John Cassian: open your thoughts to an elder) and British church rulings (e.g. texts linked with Gildas).
  • Monks' practice spread to lay people through the monasteries' pastoral work (see Mission 3).

Mission 4

The Penitential of Finnian

  • Mid-sixth century; the earliest Irish penitential, about 53 short rulings. The author Finnian (Vinnianus) may be Finnian of Clonard, Finnian of Movilla, or a British Uinniau; debated.
  • Sins of thought and of deed: a sin planned but not done gets a lighter penance than one carried out.
  • Different penances for clergy and laity: clergy, held to a higher standard, get heavier penances.
  • Repair to others: e.g. a layman who wounds or kills must make compensation to the victim's family as well as fast.
  • "Contraries cure contraries" (paraphrase): patience heals anger, generosity heals greed, kindness heals envy.
  • Ends with mercy: the aim is the sinner's healing and return.
Mission 4

Columbanus' Penitential

  • Columbanus (c. 543–615), monk of Bangor under Comgall (see Mission 3); left Ireland c. 590 as a peregrinus; founded Annegray, Luxeuil and Fontaine (Gaul), later Bobbio (Italy).
  • Penitential written in Gaul (c. 600), drawing on Finnian.
  • Three parts: penances for monks, for clergy, for lay people.
  • Image of the doctor: as physicians use different medicines for different wounds, spiritual physicians must give different penances for different sins (paraphrase of the preface).
  • Typical penances: fasting on bread and water, years of penance, exile, abstinence, restitution.
  • Impact: brought Irish private penance to Frankish Europe through his monasteries.
Mission 4

Cummean's Penitential

  • Cummean (Cummeanus), probably Cuimíne Fota (d. 662); c. 650.
  • The most systematic: arranged under the eight principal vices (from Cassian): gluttony, fornication, avarice, anger, dejection, sloth (acedia), vainglory, pride.
  • Prologue: twelve remedies for sin, among them baptism, love, almsgiving, tears, confession, amendment of life, forgiving others and converting others (paraphrase).
  • Considers age, status, intention and circumstances: e.g. special sections for children's faults and for careless handling of the Eucharist.
  • Widely copied in Britain and on the Continent.

Mission 4

Sin and forgiveness in the Celtic Church

  • Sin as sickness: a wound of the soul; the confessor is a physician; penance is medicine.
  • Contraries cure contraries: each vice is healed by its opposite virtue (Finnian, Columbanus, Cummean, after Cassian).
  • Forgiveness comes from God; penance shows true repentance and heals the habit of sin.
  • Satisfaction and repair: the sinner makes good the harm to God, to the community and to the victim.
  • Restoration: completing penance returns the sinner to communion and the community.
  • Note: the language of healing sits alongside the language of payment (fixed tariffs), which later critics disliked.
Mission 4

Role and importance

  • Celtic Church: a guide for confessors; helped monasteries give pastoral care to lay people; set standards for clergy.
  • Society: tackled violence, sexual wrongdoing, theft, magic and pagan practices; paralleled Irish law's compensation to victims.
  • Wider church: carried abroad by Columbanus and other peregrini; shaped Frankish and Anglo-Saxon penitentials (e.g. the Penitential of Theodore, c. 700).
  • Reaction: Frankish reform councils (e.g. Chalon, 813; Paris, 829) attacked penitentials of uncertain authorship and ordered some burned.
  • Long-term: private, repeatable confession became the norm in the Western church; Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required annual confession.
  • Many historians call this one of Ireland's most lasting gifts to Christian practice.
Mission 4

Value as historical sources

  • Strengths: among the earliest Irish texts; show what church leaders worried about: violence, sexual morality, diet and purity, magic, relations between clergy and laity.
  • Show a society of kin, honour and compensation, and of slaves and warfare.
  • Limits 1: prescriptive: they say what should happen, not what did. A rule against a sin does not prove it was common.
  • Limits 2: copying: authors borrowed from one another and from Cassian; a ruling may come from Gaul, not Ireland.
  • Limits 3: dates and authors are uncertain (which Finnian? which Cummean?).
  • Best used alongside law tracts, annals and saints' Lives.

Mission 4Evaluate

How valuable were the penitentials?

  • Claim: The penitentials were more helpful than harmful to the spiritual life.
  • For: Repeatable, private confession offered hope to ordinary sinners, not one chance in a lifetime.
  • For: Healing language: a personal guide (soul-friend) and remedies fitted to the person.
  • For: Concern for victims: compensation and repair, not only private sorrow.
  • Against: Tariffs could make forgiveness seem a matter of paying the right amount (and later commutations let penances be shortened or paid by others).
  • Against: Detailed lists of sins, especially sexual, could encourage scrupulosity (anxious fear of having sinned) and legalism; Carolingian councils condemned their inconsistency.
  • Judgement criteria: Judge by their aims (healing) or their use (tariffs)? By their time or ours?
Mission 4Evaluate

Penitential texts today

  • Claim: Penitential texts still have real value for religious believers today.
  • For: The Catholic Sacrament of Reconciliation (Rite of Penance, 1973) continues private, repeatable confession; the Orthodox keep confession with a spiritual father.
  • For: Penitential psalms (e.g. Psalm 51), the general confession (a prayer of confession said together) in Anglican worship (e.g. the Church of Ireland), and Lent keep repentance central.
  • For: The idea of a soul-friend is popular again in spiritual direction across churches.
  • Against: Many Protestants (e.g. Presbyterians) confess directly to God, not to a priest; set penances may seem to add to Christ's work.
  • Against: Fixed tariffs from the sixth century do not fit modern moral questions; some find detailed lists of sins oppressive.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the value in the rules, or in the practice of honest confession and healing?
Mission 4Evaluate

Sin and forgiveness: religious and secular

  • Claim: Forgiveness makes full sense only within a religious framework.
  • For (religious): Sin is against God as well as people; only God can forgive it fully (1 John 1:9). Christians forgive because they are forgiven: "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors." (Matthew 6:12, WEBBE)
  • For: Jesus' "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22) asks for forgiveness without limit, which needs grace.
  • Against (secular): Humanists value forgiveness for psychological health and social peace; it needs no God.
  • Against: Restorative justice and truth commissions (e.g. South Africa, 1996–2003) seek acknowledgement, repair and reconciliation through public processes open to people of any belief or none (though South Africa's drew on Christian and ubuntu ideas), like the penitentials' compensation.
  • Debate: Must forgiveness wait for repentance? May victims refuse to forgive? (Questions of the past in Northern Ireland.)
  • Judgement criteria: Is forgiveness about the offender's guilt, the victim's healing, or a restored relationship?

The penitentials joined private confession, healing and repair. Their questions about guilt and forgiveness are still asked, inside and outside religion.

Mission 5

What is hagiography?

  • Hagiography: writing about saints (Greek hagios, holy). A Life (Latin Vita) tells a saint's birth, deeds, miracles and death.
  • Aim: to show God at work in a holy person, and to inspire imitation and devotion.
  • Built from topoi (stock themes): a promised birth, a holy childhood, power over nature, contests with enemies, foreknowledge of death.
  • Models from abroad: Athanasius' Life of Antony (c. 360), Sulpicius Severus' Life of Martin (c. 397), Gregory the Great's Dialogues (593–594).
  • Irish Lives were read in church on the saint's feast day.
Mission 5

Cogitosus and Brigit

  • Cogitosus, a monk of Kildare, wrote the Life of Brigit c. 650–680: the earliest surviving Irish saint's Life.
  • Brigit (d. c. 524) founded Kildare (see Mission 3), more than a century before.
  • Content: almost entirely miracles, few dates or places:
  • As a girl she gives her mother's butter to the poor, and it is restored.
  • She milks a cow three times in one day for guests; changes water into beer for the sick.
  • She hangs her wet cloak on a sunbeam; she tames wild animals.
  • Her miracles show compassion, hospitality and care for the poor.
Mission 5

Kildare in Cogitosus

  • Preface (paraphrase): Kildare is the head of almost all Irish churches; its federation stretches across Ireland "from sea to sea".
  • Ruled jointly by an abbess (Brigit's heir) and a bishop (from Conlaed onwards).
  • The church (final chapter): a rare early description of an Irish church: tall, decorated, with painted panels; partitions separating men and women; the tombs of Brigit and Conlaed either side of the altar, adorned with gold, silver and jewels.
  • The "city": Kildare is a city of refuge, without walls, where the safety of fugitives is protected.
  • So the Life also advertises Kildare's status and wealth.
Mission 5

Muirchú's Life of Patrick

  • Muirchú moccu Machtheni, writing c. 680–700, at the request of Áed, bishop of Sléibte (Sleaty), an ally of Armagh. He calls Cogitosus his "father" in the genre.
  • Preserved in the Book of Armagh (c. 807).
  • Book I: captivity and escape (from the Confessio); study with Germanus at Auxerre; Palladius' failure (see Mission 1); landing; Dichu at Saul, the first convert and church; Patrick's old master Miliucc burns himself rather than submit.
  • Easter at Slane and Tara.
  • Macc Cuill, a robber, is converted and becomes a bishop on the Isle of Man.
  • Daire grants the hill of Armagh after a quarrel and a miracle.
  • Book II: Patrick's prayer, an angel's guidance, his death at Saul, and a fight between the Uí Néill and the Ulaid over his body.
Mission 5

Slane and Tara

  • On the night of a pagan feast at Tara, no fire may be lit before the king's. On Easter Eve, Patrick lights the paschal fire on the Hill of Slane.
  • King Lóegaire's druids warn that this fire, unless put out, will never be put out in Ireland.
  • Contests: the druid Lochru mocks the faith and is lifted up and dashed to death; snow and darkness brought by the druid Lucet Mael are undone by Patrick; in a fire ordeal the druid burns while Patrick's boy Benignus survives.
  • Lóegaire submits out of fear; others, like the poet Dubthach, truly believe.
  • Biblical models: Elijah and the prophets of Baal (1 Kings 18), the three young men in King Nebuchadnezzar's furnace (Daniel 3), Moses before Pharaoh. Tara is a new Babylon.

Mission 5

Why were the Lives written?

  • Devotion: to honour the saint and lead readers to praise God.
  • Example: to give monks and lay people a model of holiness to imitate.
  • Liturgy: readings for the saint's feast.
  • Status and property: Cogitosus promotes Kildare; Muirchú promotes Armagh, Patrick as apostle of all Ireland, and Armagh's alliance with the Uí Néill kings.
  • Conversion story: to explain how a pagan land became Christian, as a victory of God over druids.
  • Literary: to show that Irish churches could produce Lives as fine as those of Gaul and Rome.
Mission 5

How reliable are they?

  • Distance: written well over a century (Brigit) to about two centuries (Patrick) after the saints died.
  • Genre: stock miracles borrowed from the Bible and continental Lives; stories that serve purpose, not record.
  • Contradictions: Muirchú's Patrick studies with Germanus in Gaul and confronts a king at Tara; Patrick's own writings mention neither.
  • Anachronism: Lóegaire is pictured as king of all Ireland at Tara, a seventh-century idea of kingship.
  • Value: excellent evidence for the seventh century: its politics, rivalries, buildings (Kildare), law and beliefs.
  • Some details may keep genuine memories (Saul, Dichu, Patrick's links with the north-east), but they cannot be proved.

Mission 5

Spiritual and devotional value

  • God's power and care: miracles show God acting through the saint for the poor, sick and threatened.
  • Virtues to copy: Brigit's generosity and hospitality; Patrick's courage, prayer and trust.
  • Belonging: saints root local Christians in a sacred story of their land.
  • Living devotion: St Brigid's Day (1 February; since 2023 a public holiday on or near that date in the Republic of Ireland) with Brigid's crosses; St Patrick's Day (17 March); pilgrimages to Kildare, Downpatrick, Slane.
  • Different Christians: Catholics and Orthodox venerate saints and ask their prayers; most Protestants honour them as examples only.

Mission 5Evaluate

Reliability and value

  • Claim: Celtic hagiography has little value because it is historically unreliable.
  • For: Late, partisan and full of borrowed miracles; it often contradicts first-hand sources (Patrick's writings).
  • For: It shaped popular pictures of saints (Patrick at Tara) that history cannot support.
  • Against: It is excellent evidence for the writers' own time: politics, church claims, buildings, beliefs.
  • Against: Its purpose was devotion, not modern biography; judging it as history misreads the genre.
  • Against: Its spiritual value (example, hope, belonging) does not depend on every detail being literal.
  • Judgement criteria: Value for whom: historians, believers, or both? Value as evidence of the saint or of the writer's world?
Mission 5Evaluate

The example of the saints

  • Claim: The lives of the saints still give a real moral and spiritual example today.
  • For: Virtues such as care for the poor (Brigit), courage and justice for the enslaved (Patrick) speak to every age.
  • For: Saints show holiness is possible for real people; Hebrews 13:7 calls for imitation.
  • Against: Legendary miracles make saints seem unlike ordinary people, hard to imitate.
  • Against: Some values (extreme asceticism, triumph over pagans by destruction) may seem harmful or intolerant now.
  • Diversity: Catholic and Orthodox Christians ask the saints' intercession (prayer on their behalf); Protestants honour them as examples only; secular readers may admire them as moral exemplars (models of good living) without belief.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the example in the historical person or in the ideal the Life presents?
Mission 5Evaluate

Devotional value of religious writings

  • Claim: Religious writings from long ago keep their spiritual value in any age.
  • For: Patrick's Confessio, Augustine's Confessions (c. 397–400) and Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) are still read for prayer and guidance.
  • For: Their honesty about sin, doubt and grace speaks across centuries.
  • Against: Their worldview (miracles, druids, demons) can seem distant; readers may need interpretation.
  • Against: Modern writings (or none at all) may speak more directly to today's believers.
  • Judgement criteria: Is value found in historical truth, in spiritual insight, or in how readers use the text in prayer?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ireland was outside the Empire: many small kingdoms, rank and kin, druids and poets, seasonal festivals and sacred places.
  • Trade, raids and settlement brought Christians to Ireland before 431; the best evidence is Prosper's "believing in Christ".
  • Palladius (431) was the first bishop sent; later Patrick traditions made him a failure, and his work may have been credited to Patrick.

Mission 2

  • Patrick's Confessio (defence, thanksgiving, witness) and Letter to Coroticus (protest and excommunication) are the earliest writings from Ireland.
  • They show a humble, prayerful, scripture-soaked bishop who trusted God's call and faced danger and criticism.
  • His mission reached peoples without Christians; its scale is known only from his own account.

Mission 3

  • Irish monasticism grew from desert, Gaulish and British roots; founders Enda, Finnian, Brigit, Brendan and Comgall built a network of great houses.
  • Features: abbot and coarb, paruchia, bishops within monasteries, asceticism, learning, peregrinatio; appeal through fit with a rural, kin-based society.
  • The shift from bishops to abbots is real but debated: Hughes (monastic takeover) vs Sharpe and Etchingham (bishops kept authority).

Mission 4

  • Early public, once-only penance gave way in Ireland to private, repeatable confession with a soul-friend and fixed penances.
  • Finnian (thought and deed, compensation), Columbanus (doctor image; took it to Gaul), Cummean (eight vices, twelve remedies); "contraries cure contraries".
  • Valuable but prescriptive sources; they shaped Western confession; their ideas still meet modern religious and secular views of forgiveness.

Mission 5

  • Hagiography shows God at work in a saint, using stock themes; Cogitosus' Brigit (c. 650–680) and Muirchú's Patrick (c. 680–700) are the earliest Irish Lives.
  • Purposes: devotion, example, liturgy, promoting Kildare and Armagh; reliability is low for the saints' lifetimes, high for the writers' own time.
  • Their spiritual value (example, hope, belonging) is judged differently by Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and secular readers.
