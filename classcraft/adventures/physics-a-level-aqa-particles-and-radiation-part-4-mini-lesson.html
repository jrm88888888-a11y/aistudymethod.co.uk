Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Make a guess: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Mission 1

Energy levels

  • An electron bound in an atom can have only certain energies: energy levels.
  • Lowest level: the ground state (n = 1). Higher levels: excited states.
  • Zero energy = electron just free of the atom (ionised).
  • So every bound level is negative: energy must be supplied to free the electron.
  • Hydrogen: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV, … crowding towards 0.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Levels may be given in eV or J.
Mission 1

Emitting a photon

  • An electron drops from a higher level to a lower one. The atom emits one photon.
  • Photon energy = energy lost: hf = E1 − E2.
  • With c = fλ: λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Bigger drop → higher frequency → shorter wavelength.
  • Each transition gives one frequency: one spectral line.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • Hydrogen: n = 2 (−3.40 eV) → n = 1 (−13.6 eV).
  • ΔE = −3.40 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV.
  • In joules: 10.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • f = ΔE/h = 1.632 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.46 × 10¹⁵ Hz.
  • λ = c/f = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m (122 nm, ultraviolet).
  • Always use the difference between levels, never one level's value.
Quick check
?A hypothetical atom has only four energy levels below its ionisation energy: −8.00 eV, −5.00 eV, −3.50 eV and −1.00 eV. In a hot gas of these atoms, collisions put atoms into all four levels, and none of the atoms is ionised. What is the longest wavelength of the photons that the gas emits in transitions among these levels? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 1

How many lines?

  • An excited electron can drop straight down or in steps.
  • Every pair of levels gives one possible line.
  • 3 levels → 3 lines: 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  • Different gaps → different wavelengths. The largest gap gives the shortest wavelength.
Quick check
?An atom has only four energy levels: −1.50 eV, −2.50 eV, −4.00 eV and −6.00 eV. What is the energy of the emitted photon that has the SHORTEST wavelength?
Mission 1

Absorbing a photon; ionisation

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap from the electron's level. All or nothing.
  • Any other photon passes straight through.
  • Ionisation energy: energy to free the electron from the ground state = 0 − E1. Hydrogen: 13.6 eV (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J).
  • Any photon with at least this energy can ionise; the extra becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron.
  • From an excited level, less is needed: the ground state needs the most.
Mission 1

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous: all wavelengths. Hot, dense source: a lamp filament, a star's surface.
  • Emission line: bright lines on a dark background. Hot gas at low pressure.
  • Absorption line: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines. White light through a cooler gas.
  • The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
  • They look dark because the absorbed energy is re-emitted in all directions, so little continues forward.
Quick check
?Predict first. Two discharge tubes, P and Q, each contain a single gas. The emission spectrum of each gas shows exactly four bright lines in the visible region, but none of the wavelengths of the lines from P matches any of the wavelengths of the lines from Q. A student claims that P and Q contain the same element. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Evidence and fingerprints

  • Sharp lines, not a smear: atoms change energy only by fixed amounts. Evidence for discrete levels.
  • Each element has its own set of levels, so its own set of lines.
  • Match the lines with laboratory spectra → identify the element.
  • Works in a lamp, a gas cloud or a star's outer layers.
  • Helium was discovered in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that matched no known element.
Mission check

?An atom has energy levels at −10.0 eV (the ground state), −5.0 eV and −3.0 eV. An atom in its ground state is struck by a photon of energy 3.0 eV. What happens?

An atom can only take in or give out energy equal to a gap between its levels. Neon's gaps are fixed, so its photons, and its colours, are too.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Starlight has dark gaps in its rainbow. What do the gaps reveal about the star?

Make a guess: Starlight has dark gaps in its rainbow. What do the gaps reveal about the star?

Mission 2

Excitation by collision

  • Atoms can also be excited by colliding electrons, not only by photons.
  • A photon is all or nothing. A colliding electron can give part of its kinetic energy.
  • Electron's energy ≥ a gap → the atom can be excited. The electron moves on with the rest.
  • Electron's energy ≥ ionisation energy → the atom can be ionised.
  • The excited atom then de-excites, emitting photons.
Mission 2

The fluorescent tube

  • Tube: mercury vapour at low pressure. Inside of the glass: a phosphor coating.
  • Electrons, accelerated by the p.d., collide with mercury atoms and excite (or ionise) them.
  • Mercury de-excites, emitting mostly ultraviolet photons.
  • The phosphor absorbs the UV. Its atoms de-excite in several smaller steps.
  • Smaller steps → lower-energy photons → visible light.
Quick check
?Hydrogen atoms in the ground state are bombarded by electrons, each with kinetic energy 12.5 eV. The lowest levels of hydrogen are −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV and −0.85 eV. Which can happen in a single collision?
Mission 2

Making line spectra

  • Emission: a discharge tube (low-pressure gas, high p.d.), or a salt in a flame.
  • The gas is excited, then emits photons only at its own wavelengths: bright lines.
  • Absorption: shine white light through a cooler gas (for example sodium vapour in a flame).
  • The gas absorbs its own wavelengths: dark lines on a continuous spectrum.
Mission 2

Starlight

  • A star's hot, dense surface emits a continuous spectrum.
  • The light then passes through the star's cooler, tenuous (thin) outer atmosphere.
  • Atoms there absorb their own wavelengths → dark absorption lines.
  • Match each set of lines with laboratory spectra → the elements in the star's atmosphere.
  • Every element's lines are different, so several elements can be identified in one spectrum.
Mission check

?A cool cloud of gas lies in front of a hot star, and the star's spectrum seen through the cloud has dark lines. A second telescope views the same cloud from the side, against dark sky, with no star behind it. What does the second telescope see?

One gas absorbs and emits the same wavelengths. Seen against a hot source: dark lines. Seen against dark sky: bright lines. In a star, the dark lines list the elements in its cooler atmosphere.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 3

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
Mission 3

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • λ = h/mv, where mv is the momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: Eₖ = p²/2m, so p = √(2mEₖ) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the electron as non-relativistic. What is the de Broglie wavelength of the electron?
Quick check
?Predict first. In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a potential difference of a few kilovolts pass through a thin film of graphite and strike a fluorescent screen, where bright concentric rings are seen. How does this experiment provide evidence for the wave nature of matter?
Mission 3

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Diffraction: waves spread out as they pass through a gap or around an obstacle; it is strongest when the gap is about one wavelength.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 3

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√V. Four times the p.d. halves λ.
Mission 3

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A cricket ball at 30 m s⁻¹: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission 3

Testing a bold idea

  • 1924: de Broglie proposed that particles have wavelengths. No evidence yet.
  • 1927: Davisson and Germer (nickel crystal) and G. P. Thomson (thin films) independently observed electron diffraction.
  • Results were published, scrutinised by other physicists and repeated with other crystals and films before the idea was accepted.
  • Today this checking includes formal peer review before publication.
  • The accepted picture of matter changed because evidence was validated by the scientific community.
Mission check

?A beam of electrons and a beam of protons are each accelerated from rest through 100 V and aimed at the same crystal, whose rows of atoms are 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Which beam shows clear diffraction?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. They diffract when it is close to the size of the gap, as with the spacing of carbon atoms.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Levels are negative (0 = ionised). A transition emits or absorbs one photon: hf = E1 − E2, λ = hc/(E1 − E2).
  • Line spectra are evidence of discrete levels, and each element's lines identify it, in a lamp or a star.

Mission 2

  • A colliding electron can give part of its energy; the fluorescent tube turns mercury's UV into visible light via a phosphor.
  • A star's spectrum is continuous light from its surface with dark lines from its cooler atmosphere; the lines identify its elements.

Mission 3

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/mv; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).
