Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do some countries have far higher living standards than others, and how can we measure the gap?

Make a guess: One country produces nearly twice as much per person as another. What does that tell you about how well its people live?

Mission 1

Living standards and development

  • Living standards: how well people live: their income, health, education and the goods and services they can enjoy.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's living standards and well-being over time.
  • Developed countries: high incomes per person, long lives, most people well educated.
  • Developing (less developed) countries: lower incomes per person, and often shorter lives and less schooling.
  • Development is more than money: no single number captures it.
Mission 1

Worked example: real GDP per head

  • GDP per head (GDP per capita) = GDP ÷ population.
  • Use real GDP per head: GDP adjusted for inflation, so a rise means more output, not just higher prices.
  • To compare countries, convert to one currency (often US dollars).
  • Illustrative data:
CountryReal GDPPopulationGDP per head
A$600 billion50 million?
B$900 billion150 million?
  • A: 600,000,000,000 ÷ 50,000,000 = $12,000
  • B: 900,000,000,000 ÷ 150,000,000 = $6,000
  • B has the bigger economy, but A produces twice as much per person.
  • Say: "GDP per head" when comparing living standards — not: total GDP.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country C has a real GDP of $240 billion and a population of 80 million. What is its real GDP per head?
Mission 1

Beyond income: other indicators

  • Health: life expectancy at birth (average years a newborn can expect to live); infant deaths per 1,000 births.
  • Health care: doctors or hospital beds per 1,000 people; access to clean water and sanitation.
  • Education: share of adults who can read and write (literacy rate); average years of schooling.
  • Technology: share of people using the internet; mobile phone subscriptions per 100 people.
  • Each shows a different part of living standards. Using several gives a fuller picture.
Mission 1

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • A composite (combined) indicator, published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990.
  • It combines three things:
    • health: life expectancy at birth
    • education: expected years of schooling for children and average years of schooling for adults
    • income: real income per head (GNI per head, adjusted for what money buys in each country)
  • It runs from 0 to 1. The closer to 1, the more developed.
  • Say: "HDI measures health, education and income" — not: "HDI measures income".
Mission 1

Real case: richer per head, shorter lives

  • 2023, World Bank data (GDP per head in US dollars):
GDP per headLife expectancy
Equatorial Guineaabout $6,80063.7 years
Sri Lankaabout $3,80077.5 years
  • Equatorial Guinea produced about 1.8 times as much per person.
  • Yet people in Sri Lanka could expect to live about 14 years longer.
  • A large share of Equatorial Guinea's output comes from oil and gas. GDP per head is an average: it does not show who receives the income, or whether it pays for clinics and schools.
  • So: higher GDP per head does not always mean higher living standards.
Mission 1

Strengths and weaknesses of the indicators

  • Real GDP per head: strengths
    • Measured regularly in most countries, so easy to compare.
    • More output per person usually means more goods and services to go round.
  • Real GDP per head: weaknesses
    • An average: hides how unequally income is shared.
    • Leaves out unpaid work (housework, caring) and much of the informal economy.
    • Ignores pollution, working hours and leisure.
    • Prices differ: a dollar buys more in some countries than others.
  • HDI: strengths: includes health and education, not just income.
  • HDI: weaknesses: still averages; leaves out inequality, freedom and the environment; health, education and income count equally, which is a judgement.
  • Differences within a country: living standards can vary a lot between regions, cities and the countryside, men and women, and rich and poor households. A national average hides this.
Quick check
?Which is a weakness of using real GDP per head to compare living standards in two countries?
Mission 1

How sectors change as countries develop

  • Primary sector: farming, fishing, mining. Secondary: manufacturing and construction. Tertiary: services.
  • Share of jobs in farming, 2023 (World Bank, ILO estimates):
CountryJobs in farmingGDP per head
Niger74%about $650
Sri Lanka26%about $3,800
Japan3%about $35,200
UKunder 1%about $49,900
  • Developing economies: the primary sector is large; often most people work on farms.
  • As they develop, workers move into secondary (factory) jobs, where output per worker is usually higher.
  • Developed economies: the tertiary sector is largest. The secondary share often falls (deindustrialisation).
Mission 1

Why countries differ in development

  • Income: low income leaves little to spend on food, health and schooling.
  • Productivity: output per worker is low with little capital, technology or training.
  • Population growth: GDP must grow faster than population for GDP per head to rise.
    • Niger, 2023: population grew 3.3%. If real GDP grew 3%, real GDP per head would fall slightly.
  • Size of sectors: a large farming sector with low output per worker holds incomes down.
  • Saving and investment: low income → little saving → little investment → low productivity → low income: a vicious circle.
  • Education and health: skilled, healthy workers produce more.
  • Resources: land, minerals and climate matter, but resources alone do not guarantee development.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's real GDP grows by 4% a year. Its population grows by 3% a year. What happens to its real GDP per head?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Over ten years, Country X's real GDP per head rose by 20%. Life expectancy stayed at 60 years, the share of children in school did not change, and most of the extra income went to the richest 10% of people. Which conclusion is best supported?

GDP per head is a good start, but you need health, education and how income is shared to judge living standards.

Mission 2: Why do some families have far more than others, and can a government narrow the gap?

Make a guess: Why do some families have far more than others, and can a government narrow the gap?

Mission 2

Income and wealth

  • Income: money received over a period of time (a flow), e.g. per week or per year.
  • Types of income:
    • wages and salaries: from working
    • rent: from owning property or land
    • interest: from savings
    • profit and dividends: from owning a business or shares
    • benefits: payments from the government (a transfer, not earned)
  • Wealth: the value of what a person owns at a point in time (a stock): a house, savings, shares, a pension.
  • Net wealth = value of assets − debts (such as a mortgage or loan).
  • Wealth can create income (rent, interest, dividends), and saved income builds wealth.
Mission 2

Worked example: same income, different wealth

  • Illustrative data. Two families each have an income of 40,000 a year.
Family AFamily B
House300,000400,000
Second flat (rented out)0200,000
Savings5,00050,000
Mortgage (debt)250,0000
  • Family A: 300,000 + 5,000 − 250,000 = 55,000 net wealth.
  • Family B: 400,000 + 200,000 + 50,000 = 650,000 net wealth: almost 12 times as much.
  • B's flat also earns rent, so B can save more and grow its wealth faster.
  • Say: "income is a flow, wealth is a stock" — not: "a high income means you are wealthy".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A household owns a house worth 200,000 and has 20,000 in savings. It owes 150,000 on its mortgage and 10,000 on a car loan. What is its net wealth?
Mission 2

Why incomes and wealth differ

  • Skills and qualifications: scarce skills that firms value (doctors, engineers) earn more.
  • Type of job and hours: full-time or part-time; some jobs pay more for risk or responsibility.
  • Age: earnings usually rise with experience, then fall in retirement.
  • Where you live: wages and house prices differ between regions.
  • Inheritance: some people inherit houses, land or savings.
  • Owning assets: wealth earns rent, interest and dividends; the gap can widen over time.
  • Discrimination: unfair treatment by gender, ethnicity or disability can lower pay (illegal in many countries, but it still happens).
  • Bargaining power: workers in trade unions or with rare skills can win higher pay.
Mission 2

Consequences of inequality

  • Arguments that some inequality helps: higher pay rewards skill, effort and risk; it gives people an incentive to train, work and start businesses.
  • Arguments that large inequality harms:
    • relative poverty: some people cannot afford what most take for granted
    • gaps in health and education between rich and poor
    • less social mobility: children from poorer families find it harder to get on
    • possible social tension and crime
  • Economists disagree about how much inequality is too much. It depends on its causes and on what people value: fairness, freedom and rewards for effort.
Quick check
?Two friends earn exactly the same wage for 30 years. One inherits a house and rents it out; the other inherits nothing. What is most likely to happen to the gap in their wealth?
Mission 2

Absolute and relative poverty

  • Absolute poverty: too little income to buy the basic needs for survival: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • The World Bank's extreme poverty line: living on less than 3.00 US dollars a day (adjusted for prices in each country).
    • About 2.3 billion people lived in extreme poverty in 1990; around 811 million in 2026 (World Bank estimate).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income in your own country, e.g. below 60% of the median (middle) income.
    • You can be in relative poverty in a rich country even if you can buy food and shelter.
  • Say: "relative poverty compares with others in the same country" — not: "relative poverty means less poor".
Mission 2

Causes of poverty

  • Unemployment or low-paid, insecure work.
  • Low skills and little education.
  • Illness, disability and old age: less able to work.
  • Large families relative to income.
  • Discrimination.
  • In low-income countries: low productivity, little capital, weak health care, conflict, and natural disasters.
Quick check
?Over twenty years, the real income of every household in a country doubles. Nobody's share of total income changes. What happens to poverty?
Mission 2

Progressive taxes: worked example

  • Progressive tax: people with higher incomes pay a larger share of their income in tax.
  • Proportional: everyone pays the same share. Regressive: people with lower incomes pay a larger share.
  • Illustrative income tax:
    • first 10,000: no tax
    • income from 10,000 to 40,000: taxed at 20%
    • income above 40,000: taxed at 40%
  • Income 20,000: tax = 20% × 10,000 = 2,000. Share of income = 10%.
  • Income 60,000: tax = 20% × 30,000 + 40% × 20,000 = 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000. Share = 23.3%.
  • The higher earner pays a bigger share: the tax is progressive, so it narrows the gap after tax.
  • Taxes on spending, such as VAT, are often regressive: poorer households spend a larger share of their income.
Quick check
?Use the illustrative tax bands from the worked example (0% on the first 10,000; 20% from 10,000 to 40,000; 40% above 40,000). How much tax is paid on an income of 100,000?
Mission 2

Other ways to narrow the gap

  • Benefits: payments to people on low incomes, unemployed, disabled or retired.
  • Free or cheap education and health care: give poorer children and families the same chances; better skills raise future earnings.
  • National minimum wage (NMW): a legal lowest hourly wage, which raises the pay of the lowest paid.
  • Economic growth: creates jobs and raises incomes; it has cut absolute poverty in many countries.
  • Trade-offs:
    • high taxes and benefits may reduce the incentive to work, save or invest (economists disagree on how much)
    • an NMW set too high may cost some jobs; studies disagree on how much
    • education works slowly: the benefits take years to show
Mission 2Evaluate

How far should a government narrow the gap?

It depends on:

  • Incentives: how much higher taxes and benefits change people's choices to work and invest.
  • The cause: inherited wealth, low skills and discrimination need different policies.
  • What people value: fairness, freedom and rewards for effort are weighed differently.
Mission check

?A government needs to raise more tax revenue. It also wants to narrow the gap between rich and poor households. Which change does both?

A progressive tax takes a larger share from higher incomes, so it raises revenue and narrows the gap at the same time.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why might a country with too few young people worry as much as one with too many?

Make a guess: What could worry a country where few babies are born and people live long lives?

Mission 3

What makes a population grow or shrink

  • Birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase = birth rate − death rate. If negative, it is a natural decrease.
  • Net migration = people moving in (immigration) − people moving out (emigration).
  • Population change = natural increase + net migration.
  • Say: "birth rate" (per 1,000 people) — not: "number of births".
Mission 3

Worked example: Niger and Japan

  • 2023, World Bank data, per 1,000 people:
Birth rateDeath rateLife expectancy
Niger41.98.961.2 years
Japan6.013.084.0 years
  • Niger: 41.9 − 8.9 = 33.0 per 1,000 = 3.3% natural increase a year.
  • Japan: 6.0 − 13.0 = −7.0 per 1,000 = −0.7%: a natural decrease.
  • Surprise: Japan's death rate is higher, even though its people live about 23 years longer.
  • Why? Japan has far more older people, and a death rate counts deaths among the whole population.
  • Japan's actual population fell by about 0.5% in 2023, less than 0.7%, mainly because more people moved in than left.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country has a birth rate of 25 per 1,000, a death rate of 10 per 1,000 and net migration of −5 per 1,000 (more people leave than arrive). By what percentage does its population change in a year?
Mission 3

Why birth and death rates change

  • Birth rates fall as countries develop:
    • more girls and women in education and paid work
    • wider access to contraception
    • fewer babies die, so families need fewer births to raise the children they want
    • children cost more (longer schooling) and are less needed for farm work or for support in old age
  • Culture and religion also affect family size.
  • Death rates fall with better health care, clean water, sanitation and diet.
  • But as the population ages, the death rate can rise again, even while life expectancy keeps rising (Japan, an earlier screen).
Mission 3

Migration: causes and effects

  • Push factors (leaving): low wages, few jobs, conflict, disasters.
  • Pull factors (arriving): higher wages, jobs, safety, family links.
  • Possible effects on the country people move to:
    • more workers, filling skill gaps; more taxes paid
    • if many arrive quickly: pressure on housing and public services in some areas
  • Possible effects on the country people leave:
    • loss of skilled workers (brain drain)
    • remittances: money migrants send home to their families
  • The size of each effect depends on migrants' skills and ages, and on how fast they arrive.
Quick check
?Many doctors and nurses emigrate from Country K to work abroad. Which is a likely effect on Country K?
Mission 3

The optimum population

  • Optimum population: the size of population that gives the highest output (real GDP) per head, given a country's resources and technology.
    • (1) Under-population: too few workers to make full use of land, capital and resources. More people raise output per head.
    • (2) Optimum: output per head is at its peak.
    • (3) Over-population: too many workers for the resources. Each extra worker adds less, so output per head falls.
  • The optimum can change: better technology, more capital or new resources raise it.
Quick check
?A country develops new farming technology and builds many new factories. What happens to its optimum population?
Mission 3

Population structure

  • Age structure: the shares of people in each age group.
  • Gender structure: the shares of males and females in each age group.
  • Shown on a population pyramid.
  • 2023, World Bank data:
Under 1515–6465 and over
Niger47.0%50.5%2.6%
Japan11.6%58.8%29.6% (about 3 in 10)
  • Niger: wide base, narrow top: a young population.
  • Japan: narrow base, wide top: an ageing population.
  • Women tend to live longer than men, so older age groups usually have more women.
Mission 3

Worked example: the dependency ratio

  • Dependants: people under 15 and 65 and over (most are not in paid work).
  • Dependency ratio = (under 15 + 65 and over) ÷ (15–64) × 100.
  • Niger: (47.0 + 2.6) ÷ 50.5 × 100 = 98 dependants per 100 people of working age.
  • Japan: (11.6 + 29.6) ÷ 58.8 × 100 = 70.
  • Both are high, for opposite reasons: Niger has many children; Japan has many older people.
Mission 3

Effects of a changing structure

  • Ageing population (more older people):
    • more spending on pensions and health care
    • fewer workers per older person, so higher taxes on each worker, or lower pensions
    • possible labour shortages; but new markets for goods and services that older people buy
    • responses: raise the retirement age, encourage immigration of workers, support families to have children
  • Young population (many children):
    • more spending on schools and child health
    • a big future labour force, if there are jobs and training for it
  • Say: "ageing population" for a rising share of older people — not: "a bigger population".
Mission 3

Effects of a changing size

  • A growing population:
    • more workers and a bigger market for firms
    • but pressure on land, water, housing and public services
    • real GDP per head falls if output grows more slowly than population (see Mission 1)
  • A shrinking population:
    • less pressure on resources
    • but fewer workers and customers; some firms and towns may decline
  • Whether a change helps or harms depends on whether the country is below or above its optimum.
Quick check
?A country's birth rate has fallen for 40 years and its people are living longer. Which problem is the government most likely to face?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Country A: 45% of people are under 15 and 3% are 65 and over. Country B: 13% are under 15 and 30% are 65 and over. Which statement is correct?

Too many children and too many older people both raise the number of dependants each worker supports, but they call for different spending.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Real GDP per head = real GDP ÷ population. It is useful, but an average that misses health, education, inequality and the environment.
  • HDI combines health, education and income on a 0 to 1 scale.
  • As countries develop, the primary sector shrinks and secondary, then tertiary, sectors grow.

Mission 2

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Net wealth = assets − debts.
  • Incomes differ with skills, jobs, age, region, inheritance and owned assets; wealth gaps can grow over time.
  • Absolute poverty: cannot meet basic needs. Relative poverty: far below the typical income in your country.
  • Progressive taxes, benefits and free education and health care narrow the gap, with trade-offs.

Mission 3

  • Population change = (birth rate − death rate) + net migration.
  • The optimum population gives the highest output per head; better technology and more capital raise it.
  • Age structure matters: young populations need schools; ageing populations need pensions and health care, and both raise the dependency ratio.
