Mission 1

An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

Make a guess: An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 1

What Paper 1 puts in front of you

  • Two unseen passages, each with a guiding question.
  • They are non-literary texts, each a different text type: an advert, a speech, an infographic, an opinion column, a web page.
  • At SL you choose one.
  • You write a guided analysis: an argued reading of how the text makes meaning, not a summary of it.
IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1, Guided textual analysis. SL: 1 hour 15 minutes, one of the two passages, 20 marks, 35% of your grade.
  • Each analysis is marked on four criteria, 5 marks each: A Understanding and interpretation, B Analysis and evaluation, C Focus and organization, D Language.
  • From May 2026 the passages carry no line numbers: point to the words themselves by quoting them.
IB Paper 1 · reading an unseen passageScreen 3

Three reads, then a plan

ReadAsk
1 · GistWhat is happening?
2 · The questionRe-read with the guiding question. Mark four to six moments that answer it.
3 · PatternsWhere does it shift? What repeats, builds or contrasts?
PlanOne sentence that answers the question (your claim), then three or four points in order.
  • At read 1, also ask: what text type is it, for which audience and purpose?
  • The criteria reward an argued, organised reading. That comes from the reading, not from writing faster.

Mission checkScreen 8

?An unseen passage and the clock running. Why not start writing at once?

Reading time is where the marks are made. Read once for the gist, once with the guiding question, once for shifts and patterns; then write one claim and three or four ordered points. The writing goes faster, and stays focused, because you know what you are arguing.

Mission 2

The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

Make a guess: The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

IB Paper 1 · the guiding question and a thesisScreen 1

What the guiding question does

  • Each passage has its own guiding question. It points you to a technical or formal aspect, e.g. layout, tone, the use of images, the way an argument is built.
  • It is optional. You may choose your own point of entry, as long as it is another technical or formal aspect and your analysis stays focused.
  • Most students use it: it gives you a way in when time is short.
  • Either way, your answer needs a thesis.
IB Paper 1 · the guiding question and a thesisScreen 2

From a topic to a thesis

  • A thesis answers the question in one or two arguable sentences: what the text does, how, and to what effect.
  • A topic names a subject; a thesis makes a claim someone could disagree with.
  • Topic: ‘The page is about a holiday resort.’ Thesis: ‘The page sells calm by first making the reader feel crowded: its list of city pressures sets up the empty sand as a cure.’
  • A good thesis is a plan: each part becomes a paragraph.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The paper gives you a guiding question. Do you have to use it?

No. The guiding question is a way in, not a rule. Use it, or choose another technical or formal aspect, but turn your focus into a thesis: one arguable claim about how the text works and to what effect, which every paragraph then develops.

Mission 3

An advert has fifteen words and one photo. Where is all the meaning?

Make a guess: An advert has fifteen words and one photo. Where is all the meaning?

IB Paper 1 · non-literary text typesScreen 1

Start with the text type

  • Paper 1 in this course sets non-literary texts. First ask: what text type is it?
  • Each type has conventions: the features readers expect, e.g. an advert's slogan, logo and call to action.
  • Then ask: who is the audience, what is the purpose, and what is the context?
  • The analysis is in how this text uses, bends or breaks its conventions for that audience, and how its parts work together.
IB Paper 1 · non-literary text typesScreen 2

Five text types and their conventions

Text typeConventions to look for
Advertimage, slogan, body copy, logo, call to action; a target audience's desires or fears
Speechdirect address, anaphora, tricolon, anecdote, inclusive we; an occasion and a listening audience
Opinion columnheadline, the writer's persona, claim and evidence, concession, a closing call
Cartooncaricature, exaggeration, symbol, labels, caption; a topical target
Infographicheadline, chart or icons, visual hierarchy, colour coding, a source line; selected data

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1, Guided textual analysis: two unseen non-literary texts, each a different text type, each with a guiding question.
  • SL: you analyse one, in 1 hour 15 minutes, for 20 marks (35%).
  • There is no set list of non-literary texts: any type may appear, so learn conventions, not particular texts.
  • No line numbers from May 2026: quote the words, or describe the visual feature exactly.
Mission checkScreen 9

?An advert has fifteen words and one photo. Where is all the meaning?

In the combination. A short advert packs meaning into every choice: a word like "outlive" carries threat and emotion, the photo makes the threat visible, and the layout and call to action turn fear into a purchase. Name the text type, its conventions and its audience, then show how the parts work together.

Mission 4

Move a photo from left to right. Why does the page's meaning change?

Make a guess: Move a photo from left to right. Why does the page's meaning change?

IB Paper 1 · visual and multimodal analysisScreen 1

Multimodal texts

  • A multimodal text makes meaning through more than one mode: words, images, layout, colour, typography.
  • Analyse each mode, then how they work together: does the image repeat the words, add to them or pull against them?
  • Words can anchor an image: a caption or headline fixes which of its possible meanings we take (Roland Barthes, 1964).
  • Describe visual features precisely: there are no line numbers to point to.
IB Paper 1 · visual and multimodal analysisScreen 2

Where things go on the page

  • Reading path: in left-to-right cultures the eye tends to move from top left to bottom right.
  • Given and new (Kress and van Leeuwen): left tends to read as already known, right as the new point.
  • Ideal and real: top tends to offer the promise; bottom gives practical detail.
  • Salience: what the eye meets first (size, colour, contrast, focus).
  • Framing: lines, boxes and white space join or separate elements.
  • Tendencies, not laws: they depend on reading culture.
IB Paper 1 · visual and multimodal analysisScreen 3

ChoiceAsk

AngleLow (power), high (vulnerability) or eye level (equality)?
GazeDoes a figure look at us (demand) or away (offer, for us to observe)?
VectorsWhich lines or looks lead the eye, and where to?
Colour and lightWarm or cold? Bright or dim? What mood?
TypographySize, weight, capitals: how loud is each piece of text?

Model answer

The photo looks straight down into the empty lunchbox, a high angle that puts the reader in a parent's position, looking at what a child has been given. Its cold grey light contrasts with the warm orange button on the right, so the eye travels from a bleak problem to a bright, simple action, which is what a charity needs its reader to do.

IB Paper 1 · visual and multimodal analysisScreen 8

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1, Guided textual analysis: unseen non-literary texts, often multimodal, each with a guiding question that may point to visual features or layout.
  • SL: one analysis, 1 hour 15 minutes, 20 marks (35%).
  • With no line numbers from May 2026, refer to visual features by place and detail: ‘the caption under the photo’.
  • Visual bodies of work (a photographer's series, an ad campaign) can also be chosen for your Individual Oral.
Mission checkScreen 9

?Move a photo from left to right. Why does the page's meaning change?

In left-to-right reading cultures, the left of a layout tends to read as given (what we already know) and the right as new (the point to notice). Move a photo across and it changes role, from background to message, so the same words and picture make a different argument. It is a tendency, so argue it from the text in front of you.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Paper 1 gives you two unseen passages with guiding questions; you analyse one at SL and both at HL.
  • The passages are non-literary, each a different text type.
  • Read three times (gist, the guiding question, patterns), then plan one claim and three or four ordered points.
  • From May 2026 there are no line numbers: quote the words you mean.
  • The guiding question points to a technical or formal aspect; it is optional, but your own way in must be one too.
  • A thesis is an arguable claim about how a text works and to what effect; a topic only names a subject.
  • In the invented column, a concession makes the writer's firm turn sound reasonable.
  • Start with the text type and its conventions, then audience, purpose and context.
  • Analyse how a text uses or bends its conventions, and how its words, images and layout work together.
  • Speeches build through patterns for listeners (anaphora, tricolon, anecdote); adverts compress meaning into slogan, image and call to action.
  • Multimodal texts make meaning through words, images and layout together; captions and headlines can anchor an image's meaning.
  • Layout: reading path, given and new (left/right), ideal and real (top/bottom), salience and framing.
  • Inside the image: shot distance, angle, gaze (demand or offer), vectors, colour and light, typography.
