Mission 1Topics 9.9, 9.10

Mission 1: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Make a guess: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Mission 1

Cell potential from two E° values

  • E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode), using the two standard reduction potentials. Reduction happens at the cathode.
  • Worked example: zinc and silver half-cells.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E° = +0.80 V. Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), E° = −0.76 V.
  • Ag⁺ is reduced; Zn is oxidized. E°cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.
  • Never multiply E° by a coefficient: 2Ag⁺ or Ag⁺, the value is +0.80 V.
Quick check
?The standard reduction potentials at 25°C are +0.80 V for Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ → Ag(s) and −0.14 V for Sn²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → Sn(s). What is E° for the reaction 2 Ag(s) + Sn²⁺(aq) → 2 Ag⁺(aq) + Sn(s)?
Mission 1

Will it react?

  • For an equation, find the species reduced: its E° goes first.
  • E° positive: the reaction is thermodynamically favored and gives a positive voltage. Negative: unfavored; it runs only with an externally applied potential (an electrolytic cell).
  • Cl2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2Cl⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +1.36 − (+0.77) = +0.59 V. Chlorine oxidizes Fe²⁺.
  • I2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2I⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +0.54 − (+0.77) = −0.23 V. No reaction; instead Fe³⁺ oxidizes I⁻.
Quick check
?For the reaction Ni(s) + Zn²⁺(aq) → Ni²⁺(aq) + Zn(s), E°cell = −0.51 V at 25°C. Which statement about this reaction under standard conditions is correct?
Mission 1

Why lithium gives a big voltage

  • Li⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Li(s): E° = −3.04 V, the most negative common value.
  • So Li gives up electrons most readily (background: the strongest reducing agent).
  • E°cell is the gap between two E° values. A very negative E° makes a big gap with almost any partner.
  • Zinc (−0.76 V) is far less negative. An alkaline cell, with a zinc anode, gives about 1.5 V.
  • A lithium coin cell reaches about 3 V with one cell, not two stacked.
  • Lithium reacts with water, so lithium cells use a non-aqueous electrolyte.
Mission 1

From E° to ΔG°

  • ΔG° = −nFE°, with F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻.
  • n = moles of electrons transferred in the balanced overall equation. Count them once.
  • Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag: each Zn loses 2e⁻, so n = 2.
  • ΔG° = −(2)(96,485 C/mol)(1.56 V) = −3.01 × 10⁵ J/molrxn = −301 kJ/molrxn. (1 V = 1 J/C.)
  • Positive E° ↔ negative ΔG° ↔ thermodynamically favored.
  • Double the equation: E° stays +1.56 V; n and ΔG° double.
Quick check
?The standard reduction potentials at 25°C are +0.34 V for Cu²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → Cu(s) and −0.74 V for Cr³⁺(aq) + 3 e⁻ → Cr(s). What is ΔG° for the reaction 2 Cr(s) + 3 Cu²⁺(aq) → 2 Cr³⁺(aq) + 3 Cu(s)? (F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻)
Mission 1

Measuring a cell's voltage

  • Clean each metal strip with emery paper to remove the oxide layer.
  • Dip each into a 1.0 M solution of its ions, at 25°C.
  • Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in saturated KNO3(aq). (KCl would precipitate AgCl in a silver half-cell.)
  • Use a high-resistance voltmeter: almost no current flows, so it reads the maximum voltage.
  • Compare with the prediction. Small differences come from non-standard concentrations or temperature and oxide films.
Mission check

?A cell uses a chlorine half-cell: Cl2(g) + 2e⁻ ⇌ 2Cl⁻(aq), E° = +1.36 V. Which metal half-cell, joined to it, gives the largest E°cell?
Mg²⁺/Mg −2.37 V · Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V · Cu²⁺/Cu +0.34 V · Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V

Same reason as lithium. The more negative the E° of the metal, the bigger the gap, and the bigger the voltage.

Mission 2Topic 9.8

Mission 2: Why can you recharge a phone battery but not a standard AA battery?

Make a guess: Why can you recharge a phone battery but not a standard AA battery?

Mission 2

Building a zinc–copper cell

  • A galvanic (voltaic) cell turns the energy of a thermodynamically favored redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Two half-cells: zinc in Zn²⁺(aq); copper in Cu²⁺(aq).
  • Joined by a wire (through a voltmeter or bulb) and a salt bridge, e.g. KNO3(aq).
  • Zinc is oxidized more readily: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻. The zinc electrode loses mass.
  • Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu. The copper electrode gains mass; the blue fades.
  • Oxidation happens at the anode (Zn); reduction at the cathode (Cu). E°cell = +0.34 − (−0.76) = +1.10 V.
Mission 2

Electrons in the wire, ions in the bridge

  • Electrons flow through the wire, from Zn to Cu. Never through the solutions or bridge.
  • Zn²⁺ builds up in the zinc half-cell: anions (NO3⁻) move in from the bridge.
  • Cu²⁺ is used up in the copper half-cell: cations (K⁺) move in.
  • This keeps each solution neutral, so current keeps flowing.
  • Lift the salt bridge out: the circuit breaks, and the reading falls to zero.
Quick check
?A galvanic cell is built from a Zn electrode in Zn(NO3)2(aq) and a Cu electrode in Cu(NO3)2(aq). The electrodes are connected by wires to a voltmeter, and the solutions are joined by a salt bridge containing KNO3(aq). The voltmeter shows a steady reading. The student then lifts the salt bridge out of both beakers. How does the voltmeter reading change, and why?
Mission 2

Three kinds of cell

  • Non-rechargeable (primary): e.g. alkaline AA. Used once; its reactions can't practically be reversed.
  • Rechargeable (secondary, storage): e.g. lithium-ion phone cell, lead–acid car battery.
  • Charging: an external current drives each electrode reaction backward, re-forming the reactants.
  • Fuel cell: fuel (e.g. H2) and O2 are fed in continuously. It never needs electrical recharging.
  • A cell stores chemical energy, not electricity.
  • Charging is electrolysis: external electrical energy drives the thermodynamically unfavored reverse reaction.
  • Oxidation is always at the anode. The electrode that was the anode during use becomes the cathode during charging.
Quick check
?Which statement about the electrodes of galvanic and electrolytic cells is correct?
Mission 2

Benefits and risks

Non-rechargeableRechargeable (e.g. lithium-ion)Hydrogen fuel cell
Forcheap; long shelf lifereused hundreds of times; less waste; light, high voltage (Li)runs while fuel is supplied; only product is water; efficient
Againstsingle use; waste; metals to recyclemore expensive; capacity falls; Li cells can catch fire if damaged or overcharged; mining of Li and CoH2 mostly made from fossil fuels; flammable, hard to store; costly Pt catalyst
  • Cells sent to landfill can leak toxic metals into soil and water: recycle them.
Quick check
?Which statement about fuel cells, non-rechargeable cells and rechargeable cells is correct?
Mission check

?A car's lead–acid battery is recharged by the engine many times. During use, solid lead(II) sulfate forms on both electrodes:
Pb(s) + SO4²⁻(aq) → PbSO4(s) + 2e⁻
PbO2(s) + 4H⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) + 2e⁻ → PbSO4(s) + 2H2O(l)
Why can this battery be recharged?

Same reason as your phone. A rechargeable cell's reactions can be driven backward; an alkaline AA's, in practice, can't.

Mission 3Topic 9.10

Mission 3: Can two strips of the same metal, dipped in the same kind of solution, ever make a working battery?

Make a guess: Can two strips of the same metal, dipped in the same kind of solution, ever make a working battery?

Mission 3

E° is a special case

  • E° is the cell potential at standard conditions: every solute 1 M, every gas 1 atm, so Q = 1.
  • A real cell is rarely at standard conditions.
  • Its actual potential, E, depends on the concentrations of the species in the cell reaction.
  • Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s): E° = +1.10 V, but E changes as [Zn²⁺] and [Cu²⁺] change.
Mission 3

E measures the distance from equilibrium

  • A galvanic cell is a reaction that has not reached equilibrium.
  • E is its driving force toward equilibrium.
  • Farther from equilibrium → larger |E|.
  • As the cell operates, reactants are used up and products build up: Q rises toward K.
  • So E falls gradually, reaching zero when Q = K.
  • A "dead" battery is a cell reaction at equilibrium.
Quick check
?A galvanic cell based on the reaction Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) is connected to a light bulb and allowed to operate at constant temperature. How does the cell potential change as the cell operates, and why?
Mission 3

Reading the Nernst equation

  • E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q. Use it to reason, not to crunch numbers.
  • Q < 1 → ln Q negative → E greater than E°.
  • Q = 1 → ln Q = 0 → E = E°.
  • Q > 1 → ln Q positive → E less than E°.
  • Q = K → E = 0: equilibrium, no net electron flow.
  • Example: lower [Cu²⁺] in the Zn–Cu cell → Q = [Zn²⁺]/[Cu²⁺] rises → E falls below 1.10 V.
Mission 3

Not Le Châtelier

  • Le Châtelier's principle describes how a system at equilibrium responds to a change.
  • An operating cell (E ≠ 0) is not at equilibrium, so don't use "the equilibrium shifts".
  • Instead, ask: did the change move Q toward K or away from it?
  • Away from K → |E| increases. Toward K → |E| decreases.
  • E° never changes with concentration: it is defined at Q = 1.
Quick check
?A galvanic cell based on Zn(s) + Cu²⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Cu(s) is operating at 25°C. Solid Cu(NO3)2 is dissolved in the copper half-cell, increasing [Cu²⁺], and the measured cell potential increases. Which explanation of the increase is correct?
Mission 3

Concentration cells

  • Two Cu electrodes: one in 0.010 M Cu²⁺, one in 1.0 M Cu²⁺, joined by a wire and a salt bridge.
  • Same half-reaction on both sides, so E° = 0.
  • But the concentrations differ, so the cell is not at equilibrium: E ≠ 0.
  • Equilibrium = equal concentrations. Electrons flow in the direction that gets there.
  • Anode (dilute side): Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ raises the dilute [Cu²⁺].
  • Cathode (concentrated side): Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s) lowers the concentrated [Cu²⁺].
  • Electrons flow from the dilute side to the concentrated side. E falls to zero as the concentrations become equal.
Mission 3

Answering the card

  • Yes: two strips of the same metal make a working cell if their solutions have different concentrations.
  • Overall: Cu²⁺(concentrated) → Cu²⁺(dilute). Q = [Cu²⁺]dilute / [Cu²⁺]concentrated = 0.010, so Q < 1 = K.
  • Q < 1 → E > E° = 0, so the cell has a positive potential.
  • It runs until the concentrations are equal (Q = 1 = K), then E = 0.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the potential, E, of a galvanic cell at 25°C depends on log Q for the cell reaction. Which statement is supported by the graph?
Mission check

?A concentration cell is made from two silver electrodes. The left electrode dips into 0.0010 M AgNO3(aq) and the right into 0.10 M AgNO3(aq), with a salt bridge between them. The cell is connected to a small motor and left to run. Which statement is correct?

A cell's potential is its distance from equilibrium. Different concentrations alone are enough to make a working cell.

Mission 4Topic 9.11

Mission 4: When a key is copper-plated in blue copper sulfate solution, why doesn't the blue fade?

Make a guess: When a key is copper-plated in blue copper sulfate solution, why doesn't the blue fade?

Mission 4

CuSO4(aq) with inert electrodes

  • Water competes at both electrodes. Background: you won't be asked to predict this.
  • Cathode (reduction): Cu²⁺ (E° = +0.34 V) is reduced more easily than H⁺ or water (0.00 V or below). Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s).
  • Anode (oxidation): sulfate isn't oxidized; water is. 2H2O(l) → O2(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻.
  • Cu²⁺ is used up and nothing replaces it: the blue fades.
Mission 4

Copper electrodes: electroplating

  • Swap the graphite anode for copper. Copper is oxidized before water: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻.
  • At the key (the cathode): Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s).
  • Every Cu²⁺ deposited on the key is replaced by one from the anode.
  • [Cu²⁺] stays constant, so the blue doesn't fade. The anode loses the mass the key gains.
  • Electroplating: object = cathode; plating metal = anode; electrolyte contains the plating metal's ions.
Quick check
?A steel spoon is nickel-plated, using a nickel anode in NiSO4(aq). Which pair of half-equations is correct?
Mission 4

From current to moles of electrons

  • I = q/t, so q = I × t. Current in amperes (C/s), time in seconds, charge in coulombs.
  • F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻: the charge on 1 mol of electrons.
  • mol e⁻ = q ÷ F.
  • Use the half-reaction for the mole ratio: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu, so mol Cu = mol e⁻ ÷ 2.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag: 1 mol e⁻ per mol Ag. Au³⁺ + 3e⁻ → Au: 3 mol e⁻ per mol Au.
  • Then mass = mol × molar mass.
Mission 4

Worked example: plating the key

  • A current of 0.500 A passes for 30.0 min. What mass of copper coats the key?
  • t = 30.0 × 60 = 1800 s. q = 0.500 × 1800 = 900. C.
  • mol e⁻ = 900. ÷ 96,485 = 9.33 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • mol Cu = 9.33 × 10⁻³ ÷ 2 = 4.66 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • mass Cu = 4.66 × 10⁻³ × 63.55 = 0.296 g.
  • The copper anode loses 0.296 g in the same time.
Quick check
?A student electroplates zinc onto a cathode from ZnSO4(aq). During the experiment the mass of the cathode increases by 0.327 g. What quantity of charge passed through the cell? (F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻.)
Quick check
?A student electroplates nickel onto a cathode from NiSO4(aq), using a constant current of 2.50 A. For how long must the current pass to increase the mass of the cathode by 2.348 g? (F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻.)
Quick check
?A constant current deposits 0.010 mol of Ag(s) from a solution containing Ag⁺(aq) in 30 minutes. The same current is then passed through a solution containing Au³⁺(aq). How long does it take to deposit 0.010 mol of Au(s)?
Mission check

?A spoon is silver-plated using a silver anode in AgNO3(aq). A current of 0.150 A passes for 20.0 min. What mass of silver is lost from the anode? (F = 96,485 C/mol e⁻; Ag = 107.87 g/mol)

The anode loses exactly what the spoon gains, so [Ag⁺] stays constant. Same reason the copper sulfate stays blue.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode); positive E° ↔ negative ΔG° = −nFE° ↔ thermodynamically favored.
  • Lithium's very negative E° makes a large gap, so lithium cells give a large voltage.

Mission 2

  • Electrons flow in the wire; ions flow in the salt bridge and solutions.
  • Rechargeable cells: a charging current reverses each electrode reaction. Fuel cells: fuel and O2 fed in continuously.
  • Oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode, in every cell, charging included.

Mission 3

  • E = E° only when Q = 1; Q < 1 gives E > E°, Q > 1 gives E < E°, and E = 0 when Q = K.
  • Reason with Q and K, not Le Châtelier: in a concentration cell, electrons flow from the dilute side to the concentrated side until the concentrations are equal.

Mission 4

  • Electroplating: object = cathode, plating-metal anode, plating-metal ions. The anode replaces the ions, so their concentration stays constant.
  • q = It; mol e⁻ = q/F; mol metal = mol e⁻ ÷ ion charge; mass = mol × molar mass.
