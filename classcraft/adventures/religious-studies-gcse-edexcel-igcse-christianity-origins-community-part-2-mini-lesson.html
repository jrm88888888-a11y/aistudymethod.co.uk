Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a Catholic Bible have more books than a Protestant one?

Make a guess: Why does a Catholic Bible have more books than a Protestant one?

Mission 1

A library, not a single book

  • "Bible" comes from the Greek ta biblia: "the books".
  • Written by many people over more than 1,000 years, in Hebrew, some Aramaic, and Greek.
  • Two parts: the Old Testament (before Jesus) and the New Testament (Jesus and the first Christians).
  • Testament means covenant: an agreement between God and people.
  • Christians believe it has one unity: the story of God and humanity, from creation to new creation.
  • How many books? 66 in Protestant Bibles, 73 in Catholic Bibles, more in Orthodox Bibles.
Mission 1

The Old Testament

  • Law (the Pentateuch: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy): creation, the patriarchs, Moses and the commandments.
  • History (e.g. Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings): Israel in the promised land, its kings and exile.
  • Writings (e.g. Psalms, Proverbs, Job): poetry, prayers and wisdom.
  • Prophets (e.g. Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos): God's messengers call people back to God and speak of hope to come.
  • The Jewish Tanakh has the same core books in a different order: Torah, Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).
Mission 1

The New Testament

  • Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John): the life, death and resurrection of Jesus. "Gospel" means "good news".
  • Acts: history of the early Church, from the Ascension to Paul in Rome.
  • Letters (epistles): 21 letters to churches and leaders, 13 under Paul's name; teaching on belief and life.
  • Revelation: visions of God's final victory, written in symbols (apocalyptic).
  • Almost all churches accept the same 27 New Testament books.
Quick check
?A student reads a book that contains the commandments given through Moses and laws for worship. In which section of the Bible is she reading?
Mission 1

How the Bible came together

  • Oral tradition first: stories, laws and songs were passed on by word of mouth, then written down and edited over centuries.
  • The Septuagint: a Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures (from the 3rd century BCE), used by many early Christians.
  • New Testament: Paul's letters come first (c. 50–60 CE); the Gospels c. 65–100 CE.
  • Canon (Greek "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books.
  • Tests used: link to an apostle, use in worship across the churches, agreement with the faith handed down.
  • 367 CE: Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, lists the 27 New Testament books; councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) confirm them.
  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit guided this whole process.
  • Catholic Bibles have 7 more Old Testament books (e.g. Tobit, 1 and 2 Maccabees). They were in the Greek Septuagint but not in the Hebrew scriptures; Protestant Bibles follow the Hebrew list.
Mission check

?Ana's Bible contains Tobit and 1 Maccabees. Her friend Ben's Bible does not. Ana is Catholic and Ben is Protestant. What best explains the difference?

Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox Bibles share a core, but differ on the books found only in the Greek Septuagint.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Mission 2

Revelation: God makes himself known

  • Revelation: Christians believe God shows people what they could not find out alone: who God is and what God wants.
  • General revelation: through the world, nature and conscience: open to everyone.
  • Special revelation: through particular events and people: the prophets, the scriptures, and above all Jesus, the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
  • Christians believe the Bible records and passes on God's revelation: it is the Word of God.
Mission 2

Inspiration

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God guided the human writers.
  • Most Christians believe the writers used their own language, style and culture, while God worked through them.
  • So, for most Christians, the Bible is both fully human words and the Word of God.
  • Christians differ on what follows: is every detail without error, or the message God intended?
Mission 2Read the source

Catholic teaching

  • Dei Verbum (Second Vatican Council, 1965), chapter 3 (paraphrase):
  • God is the author of scripture; the human writers are true authors too, using their own powers (DV 11).
  • Scripture teaches without error the truth God wanted written for the sake of our salvation (DV 11).
  • To understand it, readers must look at what the human author meant, including the literary form (DV 12).
  • Many Catholics and Protestants hold this view (the middle one of the three views on the later screen).
  • Q: According to Dei Verbum, what kind of truth is scripture guaranteed to teach? (The truth God wanted written for our salvation.)
Quick check
?What does it mean to say the Bible is inspired?
Mission 2Diversity

Three views of the Bible's authority

  • The literal Word of God: every word is from God and true in every detail, including history and science (e.g. creation in six 24-hour days). Held by fundamentalist Christians and some evangelicals.
  • The inspired (revealed) Word of God: God's message comes through human writers; some parts are symbolic or reflect their time. Held by most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants.
  • A source of guidance and teaching: written by people inspired by their faith in God; a wise and treasured guide, open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Edexcel IGCSE's terms: literal (every word as it stands) and metaphorical (read for deeper meaning).
  • Implications: views on creation and science, on women's roles in church, on ethical questions.

Mission 2

The Bible as a moral guide: the Ten Commandments

  • Ten Commandments (Exodus 20:1–17): God's covenant laws given to Moses at Sinai.
  • Jesus sums them up as love of God and love of neighbour.
  • Most Christians keep Sunday as their day of rest and worship, the day of the resurrection.
Mission 2

The Bible in daily life

  • Formal worship: set readings (a lectionary) at Mass or Communion; psalms sung; a sermon explains the readings.
  • Informal worship: Bible study groups, house churches, preaching in free churches.
  • Home and private worship: daily reading and prayer. "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105)
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, reading plans and apps, commentaries.
  • Comfort and guidance: e.g. Psalm 23 at funerals; looking to the Bible when making decisions.
Quick check
?Josh uses an app that gives him a short Bible passage each morning with a few lines explaining it and a prayer. What is he using?
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Bible is the only authority Christians need."

  • Reasons for: the Bible is "God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16). Protestant sola scriptura: Church teaching and tradition can err, but God's Word does not. Every Christian can read it for themselves.
  • Reasons against: the Bible needs interpreting, and Christians disagree on what it means. The Church chose which books belong in it (see Mission 1). Catholics: Scripture, Tradition and Magisterium belong together. Modern questions (e.g. IVF, AI) are not addressed directly; reason and conscience are needed.
  • Another view: many Protestants hold scripture as the highest authority, but still value creeds, reason and experience.
  • Judge it by: Can a text interpret itself? Who decides between interpretations? How do Christians handle questions the Bible does not mention?
Mission check

?An architect plans a cathedral. Over many years, different stonemasons carve it, each in their own style. The finished building still follows the architect's plan. Which Christian belief about the Bible does this picture best?

Most Christians believe the Bible is God's Word through human words. They differ on how literally to read it and on who interprets it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If the Bible says nothing about a choice, how do Christians decide what is right?

Make a guess: If the Bible says nothing about a choice, how do Christians decide what is right?

Mission 3

Many sources, one decision

  • Christians make moral decisions using several sources together:
  • The Bible, especially Jesus' teaching and example.
  • Church teaching: for Catholics, Tradition and the Magisterium (the Church's teaching office).
  • Reason: thinking carefully about what is good for people.
  • Conscience: the inner sense of right and wrong.
  • The individual: in the end each person must decide and take responsibility.
  • Christians differ on which source carries most weight when they seem to clash.
Mission 3

Conscience

  • Conscience: the inner judgement that tells a person what is right or wrong in a particular situation.
  • Catholics call it the voice of God within: a law people did not make for themselves, which calls them to love good and avoid evil (Gaudium et Spes 16).
  • Catholic teaching also calls conscience a judgement of reason (CCC 1778).
  • Catholic teaching adds that conscience must be formed well: by scripture, Church teaching, prayer and advice.
  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890): conscience is "the aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk, 1875): Christ's first representative within each person.
Quick check
?Which statement matches Catholic teaching on conscience?
Mission 3Diversity

When Bible, Church and conscience seem to clash

  • Catholic: conscience must be followed, but formed by scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium; a conscience that ignores them may be in error.
  • Many evangelical Protestants: the Bible is the final authority; conscience must be "captive to the Word of God" (Luther at Worms, 1521, paraphrase). Some warn that conscience is affected by sin (Jeremiah 17:9).
  • Liberal Protestants: reason and conscience may lead a Christian to read some Bible passages as limited by their time.
  • Quakers: the Inner Light (God's Spirit in each person) guides decisions, tested together in the meeting.
  • Edexcel IGCSE: the Bible gives principles; conscience applies them to new situations. Christians differ on which leads when they seem to disagree.
Quick check
?Martin Luther said his conscience was "captive to the Word of God". What does this suggest about how many Protestants see conscience?
Mission 3Evaluate

"A Christian should always follow their conscience."

  • Reasons for: Gaudium et Spes 16: conscience is where God's voice is heard. Catholic teaching: a certain conscience must be obeyed. Newman would toast conscience first and the pope afterwards. Acting against conscience damages a person's integrity.
  • Reasons against: conscience can be wrong or badly formed (CCC 1790–1791); people have done terrible things "in conscience". Many Protestants say the Bible must judge conscience. Church teaching and community advice protect against self-deception.
  • Another view: follow conscience, but only after forming it carefully: the two belong together.
  • Judge it by: What happens when conscience is mistaken? Who checks a person's conscience? Is "following conscience" the same as doing what one wants?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two sources for a decision

Question: Explain two sources Christians use when making moral decisions. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Source 1: the Bible. Develop: Christians look to Jesus' teaching, e.g. the Two Great Commandments, love God and love your neighbour (Matthew 22:37–39), to find the principle of love.
  • Source 2: conscience. Source: CCC 1778 calls conscience a judgement of reason. Develop: so a Catholic thinks through the situation and follows a well-formed conscience, even when the Bible gives no direct rule.
Mission check

?A Catholic doctor is asked to give a treatment that would deliberately end a patient's life. No Bible verse mentions this treatment. She says: "I have prayed and studied what my Church teaches, and I cannot do it." What is she mainly relying on?

Christians weigh the Bible, Church teaching, reason and conscience together; Catholics hold that a well-formed conscience must be followed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: All Christians follow Jesus. So why are there Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and Pentecostals?

Make a guess: All Christians follow Jesus. So why are there Catholics, Orthodox, Protestants and Pentecostals?

Mission 4

One Church, many branches

  • For about 1,000 years most Christians belonged to one Church, though with growing differences between East and West.
  • 1054: the Great Schism splits it into the Orthodox Church (East) and the Catholic Church (West).
  • 1517 onwards: the Reformation splits the Western Church: Protestant Churches form.
  • 1906 onwards: Pentecostal Churches grow fast, and charismatic renewal spreads through older Churches from the 1960s.
  • Each family of Churches is called a denomination (or group of denominations).
  • Nearly all still believe in one God as Trinity, in Jesus as Son of God and Saviour, and in the Bible.
Mission 4

Why the Reformation happened

  • Abuses: some clergy were poorly educated or corrupt; Church posts were bought and sold.
  • Indulgences: the Church taught they reduced the punishment still due for sins already forgiven (including in purgatory); in 1517 they were being sold to raise money for St Peter's in Rome.
  • Printing press (Gutenberg, about 1450): new ideas and Bibles spread fast and cheaply.
  • Renaissance scholars went back to the Bible in its original languages (Erasmus' Greek New Testament, 1516).
  • Rulers wanted more control over the Church in their lands.
  • Earlier reformers had been silenced: John Wycliffe in England; Jan Hus, burned in 1415.
Mission 4

Martin Luther

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): German monk and professor of the Bible at Wittenberg.
  • Studying Romans, he concluded that God makes sinners right with him as a free gift, received by faith (Romans 1:17).
  • 31 October 1517: his 95 Theses challenge the sale of indulgences.
  • 1521: excommunicated by the pope; at the Diet of Worms he refuses to take back his writings unless shown wrong from Scripture.
  • Translates the New Testament into German (1522), so ordinary people can read it.
  • Marries a former nun, Katharina von Bora (1525); writes hymns and catechisms for families.
Mission 4Read the source

What Luther taught

Three "alones" sum up the Reformers' teaching (paraphrase):

  • Scripture alone (sola scriptura): the Bible is the final authority, above popes and councils.
  • Grace alone (sola gratia): salvation is God's free gift, not earned.
  • Faith alone (sola fide): people are made right with God by trusting Christ, not by good works or indulgences. "The righteous shall live by faith" (Romans 1:17).
  • Priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly through Christ (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Most Reformers taught that Christ instituted only two sacraments: baptism and the Lord's Supper.

Q1 Why did "faith alone" make selling indulgences pointless?

Show answer

If people are put right with God by faith as a free gift, nothing paid for can add to it.

Q2 Why did "Scripture alone" lead Luther to translate the Bible?

Show answer

If the Bible is the final authority, everyone should be able to read it.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Luther's Reformation still matters to Christians today."

  • Reasons for: Bibles in everyday languages; "faith alone" is central to Protestant preaching worldwide; the priesthood of all believers shaped lay leadership; his hymns and catechism are still used; Protestant and Pentecostal Churches worldwide grew from the Reformation.
  • Reasons against: the sale of indulgences ended long ago (Council of Trent); in 1999 Catholics and Lutherans signed a Joint Declaration on Justification, agreeing on its basics; many Lutheran Churches have formally rejected his later writings against Jews.
  • Another view: his questions still matter (authority, grace, the Bible), even where his answers or his tone do not.
  • Judge it by: Relevant to which Christians? The man or the ideas? Could reform have happened without division?
  • Luther is one option for "an important Christian other than Jesus"; give his teaching, his contribution and how far he is relevant today.
Mission check

?A church member says: "I read the Bible for myself, I pray to God directly without a priest, and I trust God's grace, not my good deeds, to save me." Which historical development do these ideas most clearly come from?

The Church divided at key moments over belief and authority; many Christians today see their denominations as one family that should seek unity.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Do Christians need a priest to reach God, or is every believer a priest?

Make a guess: Do Christians need a priest to reach God, or is every believer a priest?

Mission 5

Laity and clergy

  • Laity (from Greek laos, "people"): all Christians who are not ordained. Nearly every Christian is a lay person.
  • Clergy: Christians set apart, usually by ordination, to lead, teach and care for others.
  • Titles vary: priest (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican), vicar or rector (Anglican parish), minister (Presbyterian, Methodist), pastor (Baptist, Pentecostal).
  • Some Churches also have bishops; the Catholic Church has a pope.
  • Christians disagree about what ordination is and whether it is needed at all.
Mission 5

The Catholic hierarchy

Catholics believe:

  • Pope: the Bishop of Rome, successor of St Peter (Matthew 16:18–19); visible head of the Church on earth; final teaching authority.
  • Cardinals: senior clergy who advise the pope and elect a new one.
  • Bishops (archbishops lead groups of dioceses): successors of the apostles; each leads a diocese; together with the pope they form the college of bishops and teach with authority (the magisterium).
  • Priests: lead parishes on the bishop's behalf.
  • Deacons: serve, preach, baptise, witness marriages and lead funerals; permanent deacons may be married men.
  • Laity: the People of God in the world (Lumen Gentium chapter 4).
  • Apostolic succession: the Catholic belief (shared by the Orthodox and many Anglicans) that bishops stand in an unbroken line from the apostles, passed on by ordination.
Mission 5

Catholic ordination and the priest

  • Holy Orders is one of the seven sacraments, in three degrees: deacon, priest, bishop.
  • Only a bishop ordains, by the laying on of hands and a prayer of consecration; a new priest's hands are anointed with chrism.
  • Catholics believe ordination leaves a permanent spiritual mark: "once a priest, always a priest".
  • The priest acts in the person of Christ (in persona Christi) when he celebrates the sacraments.
  • Role: celebrate Mass, hear confessions, baptise, marry, anoint the sick, preach, care for the parish.
  • In the Latin (Western) Catholic Church, priests are normally celibate; Catholic priests are men.

Mission 5Read the source

Ephesians 4:11–12

"He gave some to be apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, shepherds and teachers; for the perfecting of the saints, to the work of serving, to the building up of the body of Christ" (Ephesians 4:11–12).

  • Christ gives the Church different leaders, as gifts.
  • Their purpose: to equip ("perfect") all believers for service and to build up the whole body.
  • Catholic reading: bishops and priests continue the apostles' ministry.
  • Many Protestants: leaders exist to equip every member to do ministry, not to do it all themselves.

Q1 Who does the "work of serving", according to verse 12?

Show answer

The saints: all believers, equipped by their leaders.

Q2 What is the aim of all leadership here?

Show answer

Building up the body of Christ.

Mission 5

Protestant structures

  • Presbyterian (e.g. Presbyterian Church in Ireland): no bishops. Each congregation is led by a minister and elders (the Kirk Session); congregations form a Presbytery; the General Assembly meets each year, chaired by a Moderator elected for one year.
  • Anglican (Church of Ireland, Church of England): bishops, priests and deacons; archbishops (Armagh and Dublin in the Church of Ireland; Canterbury and York in England); a synod of bishops, clergy and laity.
  • Methodist: an annual Conference with an elected President; circuits of churches, with ministers and lay local preachers.
  • Baptist: each congregation governs itself and calls its own pastor.
  • Orthodox: bishops and patriarchs; no pope; priests may marry before ordination; bishops are chosen from monks.
Mission 5

Protestant ordination and the minister

  • Ordination means being set apart by the Church for ministry, after a call from God and from a congregation.
  • Presbyterian: after training, a minister is ordained by the Presbytery with prayer and the laying on of hands.
  • For most Protestants ordination is not a sacrament: there are two sacraments (baptism and the Lord's Supper).
  • The minister is not a different kind of Christian; he or she is a servant (Latin minister) of the congregation.
  • Role: preach and teach the Bible (central), lead worship, lead baptism and the Lord's Supper, pastoral care, weddings and funerals, lead with the elders.
  • Many Protestant Churches ordain women as ministers.
Mission 5

The priesthood of all believers

  • Taught by Martin Luther (To the Christian Nobility of the German Nation, 1520).
  • "You are a chosen race, a royal priesthood" (1 Peter 2:9): this is said of all believers.
  • Jesus is the one great high priest (Hebrews 4:14–16), so every Christian can come to God directly, without a human go-between.
  • Every Christian can pray, read the Bible, confess sins to God and serve; every job can be a calling.
  • Ministers are still needed for order and teaching, not because they are closer to God.
  • Catholics and Orthodox also pray directly to God; the priest's distinctive role is in the sacraments.

Mission 5

Leaders and laity together

  • Lumen Gentium chapter 4 (the laity): lay people share in Christ's work as priest, prophet and king; their special task is to make the world holy from within: family, work, politics (paraphrase, LG 31).
  • Lumen Gentium chapter 5: the universal call to holiness: every Christian, lay or ordained, is called to the fullness of Christian life and love (paraphrase, LG 40).
  • Pastors should listen to the laity and value their advice (LG 37).
  • Catholic practice: parish councils, lay readers and catechists; lay members at recent synods.
  • Anglican and Methodist: laity sit with clergy in synods and Conference.
  • Presbyterian: elders are lay people elected by the congregation who share leadership with the minister.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Christians need ordained leaders."

  • Reasons for: Christ gives leaders to the Church (Ephesians 4:11–12); for Catholics and Orthodox only a priest can celebrate the Eucharist and absolve sins; trained leaders guard against false teaching; worship should be "decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Reasons against: the priesthood of all believers (1 Peter 2:9); Jesus the one mediator (1 Timothy 2:5); Quakers and Brethren (Churches with no ordained clergy) have worshipped for centuries without clergy; the Spirit gives gifts to everyone (1 Corinthians 12); clergy can be set too far above the laity.
  • Another view: leaders are needed, but as servants of the whole body (Mark 10:43–45), not as a separate class.
  • Judge it by: Needed for what: sacraments, teaching or organisation? Is Bible or Tradition the authority here? Does the answer change between denominations?
Mission check

?A group of Christians from different Churches spends six months at a remote research station with no priest or minister. Which statement about their Sunday worship is most accurate?

Catholics believe ordained priests are needed for the sacraments; Protestants stress the priesthood of all believers; Quakers and Brethren have no ordained clergy.

Mission 6

Agape: love that gives

  • Agape (Greek): selfless, unconditional love that seeks the good of others, expecting nothing back.
  • Christians see it in Jesus: healing, feeding, forgiving, and giving his life.
  • The Two Greatest Commandments: love God "with all your heart… soul… mind, and… strength", and "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:30–31)
  • The Good Samaritan shows who my neighbour is: anyone in need, even an enemy. "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37)
  • Christian charity work begins here: love of God shown as love of neighbour.
Mission 6Read the source

"Most certainly I tell you, because you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)

  • Context: Jesus describes the King judging "all the nations" (Matthew 25:31–46); those who ignored the needy are sent away (25:45–46).

Mission 6

St Teresa of Calcutta (1910–1997)

  • Born Anjezë Gonxha Bojaxhiu in Skopje; joined the Loreto Sisters; taught in Calcutta (Kolkata), India.
  • 1946: felt a "call within a call" to serve the poorest of the poor.
  • 1950: founded the Missionaries of Charity (vows of poverty, chastity, obedience, and wholehearted free service to the poor).
  • Opened a home for the dying (1952), then homes for people with leprosy, orphans, and later people with HIV/AIDS, worldwide.
  • Teaching: Christ is met in the poor (Matthew 25:40); she described the suffering as Jesus "in distressing disguise". Prayer comes first, then service. Do small things with great love.
  • Nobel Peace Prize 1979; made a saint by the Catholic Church in 2016.
Mission 6Evaluate

"St Teresa of Calcutta is still relevant to Christians today."

  • Reasons for: the Missionaries of Charity still serve in many countries; her example inspires volunteers of every denomination; her letters show she felt God absent for decades yet kept serving, which many believers find honest and encouraging.
  • Reasons against: critics (e.g. Christopher Hitchens, The Missionary Position, 1995) said her homes gave poor medical care and that she did not challenge the causes of poverty; her strong opposition to abortion and contraception divides opinion today.
  • Judge it by: Is relevance about her ideas, her example, or her organisation? Does charity without campaigning for justice still matter? Do criticisms of her methods undo her teaching?
Mission check

?After an earthquake, a Christian student says: "We should send help only through agencies that also preach the Gospel. Otherwise it isn't Christian work." Which reply best fits the Christian teaching in this mission?

Christians differ on whether to share faith in words alongside help, but most see practical love for anyone in need as serving Christ.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Bible is a library: Old Testament (law, history, writings, prophets) and New Testament (gospels, Acts, letters, Revelation).
  • The canon formed over centuries; the 27 New Testament books were agreed by the late 4th century.
  • Catholic Bibles have 73 books; Protestant Bibles have 66; Orthodox Bibles more.

Mission 2

  • Christians believe God reveals himself, and the Bible is the inspired ("God-breathed") record of that revelation (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Views of authority: literal Word, inspired Word (most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants), or a human guide inspired by faith.
  • The Bible is used in worship, at home, through reading aids and groups, for comfort and moral guidance.

Mission 3

  • Conscience is a judgement of reason where Catholics believe God's voice is heard (GS 16; CCC 1777–1781); it must be formed and followed.
  • Christians differ on the weight of Bible, Church, reason and conscience.

Mission 4

  • 1517 onwards: Luther's Reformation (Scripture alone, grace alone, faith alone, priesthood of all believers) gave rise to Protestant Churches.

Mission 5

  • Catholic hierarchy: pope, bishops, priests, deacons, laity; Catholics believe ordination is a sacrament given by a bishop; the priest celebrates Mass and the sacraments.
  • Protestant Churches are organised differently (e.g. Presbyterian: minister and elders, Presbytery, General Assembly); ordination sets ministers apart to preach and lead but is not a sacrament.
  • Protestants teach the priesthood of all believers: every Christian can come to God directly.

Mission 6

  • Agape and the Two Greatest Commandments (Mark 12:30–31) motivate Christian charity.
  • So does the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46): Christians read it as teaching that serving people in need is serving Christ.
  • St Teresa of Calcutta saw Christ in the poorest; her relevance today is debated.
