Mission 1

Mission 1: What four kinds of resources go into making even a simple loaf of bread?

Make a guess: What four kinds of resources go into making even a simple loaf of bread?

Mission 1

The four factors of production

  • Factors of production: the resources used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Enterprise: organising the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of enterprise (entrepreneurship) as a factor of production?
Mission 1

Capital goods and consumer goods

  • Capital goods: used to produce other goods and services (a delivery van, an oven in a bakery).
  • Consumer (consumption) goods: used up to satisfy wants directly (a family car, a loaf of bread).
  • The same good can be either. It depends on use, not on what it is.
  • Making more capital goods now means fewer consumer goods now, but more output later: an opportunity cost.
Mission 1

Economic agents and what they want

  • Households: own the factors of production and sell their services; buy goods and services. Usual aim: maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms: hire factors to produce goods and services. Usual aim: maximise profit; others include growth, market share, survival.
  • Government: taxes, spends, provides public services, makes laws. Aims include growth, jobs, stable prices and a fairer distribution of income.
  • Aims can conflict: a firm's profit vs the local environment; buyers' low prices vs workers' high wages.
Quick check
?Which statement about economic agents is correct?
Mission check

?A farmer uses her savings to buy a tractor. She then decides to risk planting a new crop in her fields. Enterprise is also called entrepreneurship. Which list correctly classifies (1) the tractor, (2) her decision to take the risk of the new crop, and (3) the soil of her fields?

Money is not a factor of production. What counts is what each resource does: natural resources, human effort, man-made tools, and the organising and risk-taking.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When economists disagree, is it about the evidence or about values?

Make a guess: Two economists study the same data and still disagree about what the government should do. Why?

Mission 2

Positive and normative

  • A positive statement is about what is, was or will be. It can be tested against evidence.
  • A normative statement is about what ought to be. It rests on a value judgement, so evidence alone cannot settle it.
  • A positive statement can be false and still be positive: "Inflation was 90% last year" (illustrative) can be checked and refuted.
  • A prediction is positive: it can be tested once the future arrives.
  • Clue words for normative: should, ought, fair, too high, better. But check the meaning, not just the word.
  • Say: "positive = testable" — not: "positive = true" or "positive = optimistic".
Quick check
?Which of these statements is normative?
Mission 2

Value judgements in policy

  • A value judgement is a view based on what someone believes is right, fair or important.
  • Policy needs both: positive analysis (what will happen?) and normative choices (is that outcome good?).
  • Example: economists may agree a tax will cut pollution and raise prices for poorer households. Whether that trade-off is worth it is a value judgement.
  • So economists can agree on the evidence and still disagree on the policy.
Mission check

?Two economists discuss fuel duty (illustrative figures).
Economist 1: "A 10% rise in fuel duty would cut fuel use by 3% in the long run."
Economist 2: "Fuel duty should not rise, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income on fuel."
Which classification is correct?

A fact inside a statement doesn't make its conclusion positive; the word "should" carries a value.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Water keeps us alive and diamonds do not, so why do diamonds cost so much more?

Make a guess: Water keeps us alive and diamonds do not, so why do diamonds cost so much more?

Mission 3

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
  • Governments aim to maximise social welfare (the well-being of society as a whole).
Mission 3

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.
  • Slices of pizza at one meal (illustrative data, in "utils"):

  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: as more units are consumed, each extra unit adds less utility.
  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

?Glasses of juice drunk on a hot afternoon give these total utilities (illustrative data): 1 glass 12 utils, 2 glasses 22, 3 glasses 29, 4 glasses 33, 5 glasses 34. What is the marginal utility of the 4th glass?
Mission 3

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".
Quick check
?Which statement best explains, using utility, why a demand curve usually slopes downward?
Mission 3

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 3Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: a later lesson.
Mission check

?A hiker has had no water all day on a hot trail and would pay a large amount for one bottle. At home, after drinking plenty, she would not pay even a small amount for one more bottle. Which explanation is best?

What we pay depends on how much one more unit adds, not on how much the good matters in total.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does each extra unit of one thing cost a country more and more of another?

Make a guess: A country moves workers from farms to factories. Each extra batch of machines costs it more food than the last. What is the most likely reason?

Mission 4

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources and technology.
  • Your course calls it the production possibility frontier (PPF). Use "PPF" in answers.
  • Assumptions: only two goods; a fixed quantity and quality of resources; fixed technology; on the curve, all resources are fully and efficiently used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice and trade-offs.
Mission 4

Worked example: opportunity cost on the curve

  • Illustrative data: an economy makes food and machines.
PointMachinesFood (tonnes)
A0150
B10140
C20120
D3090
E4050
F500
  • Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up.
  • Step 1: B → C adds 10 machines and loses 140 − 120 = 20 tonnes of food.
  • Step 2: per machine: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 tonnes of food.
  • Step 3: repeat along the curve: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 5 tonnes per machine (E → F).
  • Say: "the opportunity cost of 1 machine is 2 tonnes of food" — not: a money price, or "costs 20 machines".
Quick check
?Use the table on the previous screen (illustrative data). The economy moves from point D (30 machines, 90 tonnes of food) to point E (40 machines, 50 tonnes of food). What is the opportunity cost of each extra machine?
Mission 4

Shape: why the curve bows outward

  • Increasing opportunity cost → the curve is concave to the origin (bowed outward).
  • Why: resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first workers moved to machines are those best at machines and worst at farming. Later ones are expert farmers.
  • Constant opportunity cost → a straight line. Resources are equally good at both goods.
  • The slope of the curve at any point shows the opportunity cost there.
Mission 4Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. The more food is made, the more machines each extra tonne costs. On a straight line the cost is constant.
Mission 4

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources used → productively efficient.
  • Inside (point U): some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
  • Why an economy can be inside: a recession, unemployed workers, idle factories, strikes, resources stuck in the wrong uses.
  • Outside (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Allocative efficiency: the one point on the curve that best matches what society wants. The curve alone can't show which point that is.
  • From U, more of both goods is possible with no opportunity cost: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?On a PPC for food and machines, an economy is at a point inside the curve. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are reallocated between the goods. The curve stays put.
  • Shift outward: more or better resources, or better technology or productivity (education, training, investment, new discoveries).
  • Shift inward: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, depleted resources.
  • Skewed (non-parallel) shift: the change affects one good only. A better machine-making method moves the machines end only; the food end stays put.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government estimated the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" (reallocation) — not: "a shift" for a change of mix.
Mission 4Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: a skewed outward shift. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 4

More output: two routes

  • Actual growth: output rises, e.g. from U (inside) to a point on the curve. Uses spare resources. Short run.
  • Potential growth: the curve shifts outward. The economy's capacity rises. Long run.
  • Both together: the economy reaches a point beyond today's curve.
  • Say: "actual growth" (more output) vs "potential growth" (more capacity) — not: "growth" for both.
Mission 4

Capital goods today, more of everything tomorrow

  • Capital goods: goods used to make other goods (machines, factories, roads).
  • Consumer goods: goods used up by households now (food, clothes, phones).
  • Your course calls these consumption goods.
  • Choose more capital goods now → fewer consumer goods now (the opportunity cost).
  • But more capital → a bigger outward shift later.
  • A country choosing mostly consumer goods now may see its curve shift out more slowly.
Quick check
?A country's farms and factories are unchanged, but thousands of skilled workers emigrate. At the same time, the government moves some workers from farming into building machines. How should this be shown on a PPC for food and machines?
Mission 4Evaluate

What the PPC can and can't tell you

  • It shows what is possible, not what is best. Choosing a point needs a value judgement about what society wants.
  • It depends on assumptions: two goods, fixed resources and technology. Real economies make millions of goods and change constantly.
  • Growth from an outward shift takes time. Investment, training and research pay off years later.
  • The curve shows output, not who gets it or the environmental cost of producing it.
  • It depends on why an economy is inside the curve: a short recession and a long-run problem (e.g. skills in the wrong places) need different fixes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a change in one market move prices in a completely different one?

Make a guess: The price of fuel jumps. What happens to sales of big cars that use a lot of fuel?

Mission 5

Product markets and factor markets

  • Product markets: goods and services. Households buy, firms sell.
  • Factor markets: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Firms buy, households sell.
  • The two are linked: firms hire factors because they want to make products.
  • So a change in a product market spreads to the factor markets that serve it, and back again through incomes.
Mission 5

Joint demand: goods used together

  • Joint demand: goods bought together, such as games consoles and games, or printers and ink. Also called complements.
  • Step 1: price of printers falls → extension of demand for printers.
  • Step 2: more printers in use → demand for ink shifts right.
  • Step 3: ink price and quantity rise.
  • The price of a complement is a shift factor of demand.
  • Say: "demand for ink increases (shifts)" — not: "the demand for printers shifts" (a change in a good's own price is a movement).
Mission 5

Competitive demand: goods that replace each other

  • Competitive demand: goods that are substitutes, such as coffee and tea, or train and coach travel.
  • Price of coffee rises → contraction of demand for coffee → demand for tea shifts right.
  • The closer the substitutes, the bigger the shift.
Quick check
?The price of printers falls sharply. What happens in the market for printer ink?
Mission 5

Composite demand: one good, many uses

  • Composite demand: a good wanted for several different uses.
  • Milk → drinking milk, cheese, butter, cream.
  • If demand for cheese rises, more milk goes to cheese makers.
  • Less milk is left for butter: the supply of butter shifts left and its price rises.
  • Other examples: steel (cars, buildings, cans); land (homes, farms, shops).
  • Say: "composite demand: one good, many uses" — not: "joint demand" (goods used together).
Mission 5

Derived demand

  • Derived demand: demand for something because of what it helps to produce.
  • Demand for factors is derived from demand for products.
  • More homes wanted → more demand for bricklayers, bricks and building land.
  • So a rise in demand in a product market shifts demand in a factor market.
  • Labour demand is derived demand: the basis of wage theory.
Quick check
?More people want to eat in restaurants, so the demand for chefs rises. What kind of demand for chefs is this?
Mission 5

Joint supply: by-products

  • Joint supply: making one good automatically makes another.
  • Cattle → beef and leather. Crude oil refining → petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
  • Step 1: demand for beef rises → more cattle are reared and slaughtered.
  • Step 2: more hides → supply of leather shifts right.
  • Step 3: leather price falls, quantity rises.
  • The two goods move in opposite price directions here.
Mission 5

Case: carbon dioxide, 2021

  • In the UK, food-grade CO₂ is a by-product of making fertiliser from natural gas.
  • In 2021, gas prices nearly quadrupled. In September, the fertiliser firm CF Industries halted its two UK plants.
  • Those plants made about 60% of the UK's food-grade CO₂.
  • CO₂ supply fell sharply. Poultry processors (which use it to stun birds) and drinks makers (for the fizz) warned of disruption. The government told food firms to expect CO₂ prices to rise about 400%.
  • The government agreed short-term financial support to restart one plant, to give the market time to adjust.
  • A gas market shock cut the supply of CO₂ and raised its price, in a market that seems to have nothing to do with gas.
Quick check
?Petrol and diesel are both produced when crude oil is refined. Demand for petrol falls a lot, and refiners process less crude oil. What happens in the market for diesel?

Mission 5

Knock-on effects

  • One change can ripple through several markets. Illustrative chain:
  • More people buy electric cars.
  • Joint demand: demand for home chargers rises.
  • Derived demand: demand for electricians who fit them rises; their wages rise.
  • Competitive demand: demand for petrol cars falls.
  • Composite demand: more lithium for car batteries leaves less for other batteries.
  • How big is each knock-on? It depends on how closely the goods are linked (cross elasticity, a later lesson) and on the elasticity of supply in each market.
Mission check

?Crushing sunflower seeds produces sunflower oil and sunflower meal, which is sold as animal feed. Demand for sunflower oil rises sharply. What is the most likely effect in the market for sunflower meal?

When goods come from the same process, a boom in one makes the other more plentiful.

Make a guess: With no one in charge, how do millions of buyers and sellers end up producing what people want?

Mission 6

No one is in charge

  • Every day, millions of buyers and sellers decide what to buy, make and sell.
  • No one gives the orders. Yet shops are mostly stocked with things people want.
  • The price mechanism: changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, decide how scarce resources are used.
  • It answers the basic questions: what to produce, how, and for whom.
  • Prices do three jobs: signalling, incentive and rationing.
Mission 6

Signalling

  • A price change is a signal: it carries information.
  • A rising price says: buyers want more than is on offer here. The good has become scarcer.
  • A falling price says: there is too much on offer. Use resources elsewhere.
  • Buyers and sellers need not know why the price changed (a drought, a fashion, a new use). The price alone tells them how to react.
Mission 6

Incentive (and the role of profit)

  • A higher price makes producing the good more profitable.
  • Firms already in the market produce more: quantity supplied rises (a movement along S).
  • Call this an extension of supply.
  • In time, new firms enter (market supply shifts right), and resources (workers, land, machines) move into the industry.
  • A falling price cuts profit. Firms produce less, and resources move out to better-paid uses.
  • Profit is the reward that drives this. Losses are the warning to leave.
  • So profit is the engine of resource allocation in a free market: it pulls resources to where buyers value them most.
  • Say: "the higher price gives existing firms an incentive to extend supply" — not: "the higher price shifts the supply curve".
Mission 6

Rationing

  • Scarce goods must be shared out somehow.
  • A higher price cuts quantity demanded: some buyers buy less, or stop buying.
  • Call this a contraction of demand.
  • The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price.
  • Price rationing removes a shortage without queues, lotteries or ration books.
  • But "able to pay" depends on income. Price rationing is not the same as giving goods to those who need them most.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls", for a movement along D.

Mission 6

One shock, three jobs: Europe's gas, 2022

  • 2022: Russia cut pipeline gas supplies to Europe. Wholesale gas prices soared.
  • Signal: the price told every user and supplier that gas was now very scarce.
  • Incentive: suppliers shipped in more liquefied natural gas from other countries, by sea.
  • Rationing: factories and households cut their use.
  • Real data: EU gas use, August 2022 to March 2023, was 17.7% lower than the average for those months in 2017–2022 (Eurostat, 2023). The EU had set a voluntary target of 15%.
  • Caution: a mild winter and government campaigns to save energy also played a part. Price was not the only cause.
Mission 6

Product markets and factor markets

  • Product market: households buy goods and services; firms sell.
  • Factor market: firms buy (hire) land, labour and capital; households sell.
  • They are linked. Demand for a factor is derived demand: it comes from demand for what the factor makes.
  • Example: demand for electric cars rises → car prices and profits rise → firms want more battery engineers → engineers' wages rise.
  • Higher wages signal workers to train for and move into that job.
  • Income earned in factor markets is what households spend in product markets.
Quick check

Mission 6Evaluate

How well does the price mechanism allocate resources?

Strengths

  • Uses information spread across millions of people. No planner could gather it all (Hayek, 1945).
  • Automatic: signals and incentives work without orders.
  • Rewards efficiency and new ideas through profit.

Limits. It depends on …

  • Income distribution: price rations by ability to pay, so the poorest can be priced out of essentials.
  • Market failure: prices miss external costs and benefits (pollution), and public goods may not be provided at all.
  • Information and competition: monopoly power or poor information distort the signals.
  • Speed of response: factors move slowly (new skills, new mines), so prices can swing sharply.
  • Judge: a powerful way to coordinate choices, but its results are only as good as the prices it works with.
Mission check

?A new type of battery greatly increases demand for lithium. The price of lithium rises sharply. Which statement matches each function of price correctly?

One price change does three jobs at once: it informs, it rewards, and it shares out what is scarce.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Four factors: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.
  • Households, firms and government depend on each other.

Mission 2

  • Positive statements can be tested (even if false); normative statements rest on value judgements.

Mission 3

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.

Mission 4

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods; on it = productively efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable.
  • Bowed out = increasing opportunity cost (resources not equally suited); straight line = constant.
  • Reallocation = movement along; more or better resources or technology = outward shift (skewed if one good only).

Mission 5

  • Joint demand (complements) move together; competitive demand (substitutes) move in opposite directions.
  • Joint supply: more of one product means more of its by-product.
  • Derived demand links product markets to factor markets.

Mission 6

  • The price mechanism allocates scarce resources with no one in charge: prices signal, give incentives and ration.
  • Product and factor markets are linked: demand for factors is derived from demand for goods.
  • It works well when prices reflect all costs and benefits; it struggles with market failure and unequal incomes.
