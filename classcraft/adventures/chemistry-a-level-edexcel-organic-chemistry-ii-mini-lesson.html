Mission 1

Mission 1: Spearmint and caraway get their smells from mirror-image molecules. How can your nose tell them apart?

Make a guess: Spearmint and caraway get their smells from mirror-image molecules. How can your nose tell them apart?

Mission 1

Stereoisomers and restricted rotation

  • Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space.
  • A C=C is a σ bond plus a π bond. The π bond is a sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the plane.
  • Twisting about the C=C would break the π bond: a large energy barrier. So there is no rotation.
  • Groups are locked on the same side or on opposite sides of the C=C.
  • This needs two different groups on each C=C carbon. Propene, CH2=CHCH3, has none: one carbon carries two H.
Mission 1

Naming E and Z: CIP priority rules

  • On each C=C carbon, rank its two groups by the atomic number of the atom attached. Higher = priority.
  • A tie? Compare the atoms attached next: –CH2CH3 (C, H, H) beats –CH3 (H, H, H).
  • Z (zusammen, together): the two priority groups on the same side.
  • E (entgegen, opposite): on opposite sides.
  • ClCH=CHCl: Cl > H on each carbon. Both Cl on the same side: (Z)-1,2-dichloroethene.
  • BrClC=CHCH3: Br > Cl on C1; CH3 > H on C2. Br and CH3 on opposite sides: E.
Mission 1

cis–trans: a special case of E/Z

  • cis–trans applies when one group on each C=C carbon is the same.
  • With an H on each carbon: cis = Z, trans = E.
  • Without that H, they can differ. 2-Bromobut-2-ene, CH3CBr=CHCH3: methyls on the same side = cis.
  • But on C2, Br outranks CH3. So the priority groups (Br on C2, CH3 on C3) are opposite: E.
  • No shared group (BrClC=CHCH3)? Only E/Z can name it.
Quick check
?In one stereoisomer of 2-chlorobut-2-ene, CH3CCl=CHCH3, the Cl atom and the H atom are on the same side of the C=C. Which is correct?
Quick check
?Which compound can exist as E and Z stereoisomers about its C=C bond?
Mission 1

Chiral centres and enantiomers

  • A chiral centre (an asymmetric carbon) is bonded to four different groups.
  • It gives two optical isomers, or enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • Optical isomers are an object and its non-superimposable mirror image.
  • Example: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH3CH(OH)COOH. C2 carries H, OH, CH3 and COOH.
  • Draw it in 3D: two bonds in the plane, a wedge towards you, a dash away (tetrahedral, 109.5°).
  • Draw the other enantiomer as the mirror image. No rotation makes the two match.
Mission 1

Spotting a chiral centre

  • Only a carbon with four single bonds can be chiral: CH3, CH2 and C=C carbons never are.
  • Compare whole groups, not just the atom attached.
  • 1-Chloro-2-methylbutane, CH2ClCH(CH3)CH2CH3: C2 carries H, CH3, C2H5 and CH2Cl: chiral.
  • Pentan-3-ol, CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3: C3 carries two C2H5 groups: not chiral.
  • Mark a chiral centre with an asterisk: CH3C*H(OH)COOH.
Quick check
?Which carbon atom in 3-methylhexane, CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH3, is a chiral centre?
Mission 1

Optical activity and racemic mixtures

  • Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light: by equal angles, in opposite directions. It is optically active.
  • The test uses plane-polarised monochromatic light.
  • A racemic mixture (racemate): equal amounts of both enantiomers. The rotations cancel: optically inactive.
  • Enantiomers share melting point, boiling point, density and most reactions.
  • They behave differently only in a chiral environment: an enzyme, a receptor.
  • Your smell receptors are chiral. Each carvone enantiomer fits them differently: spearmint or caraway.
Mission 1

How racemic mixtures form

  • A planar reaction site can be attacked equally from either face. If the product carbon is chiral, you get a 50 : 50 mixture.
  • SN1: the planar carbocation is attacked from either face. One enantiomer in → racemic product out: optically inactive.
  • SN2: the nucleophile attacks from the side opposite the leaving group (inversion). One enantiomer in → one enantiomer out: still optically active.
Mission 1

Where chirality matters

  • Amino acids H2NCH(R)COOH: C2 carries H, NH2, COOH and R. Every 2-amino acid except glycine (R = H) is chiral and optically active.
Quick check
?2-Hydroxypropanoic acid made in the laboratory from non-chiral starting materials is a racemic mixture. Which statement about this sample is correct?
Mission check

?Butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, exists as two stereoisomers with identical boiling points. Which explains why there are two?

Same as carvone. Mirror-image molecules share atoms, bonds and boiling point; only a chiral setting, like your nose, tells them apart.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C4H9Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Make a guess: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C4H9Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Mission 2

Same formula, different carbon

  • 1-bromobutane, CH3CH2CH2CH2Br: primary.
  • 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CBr: tertiary.
  • Both + OH⁻(aq) → an alcohol + Br⁻.
  • Rate experiments show they react by different mechanisms: SN2 and SN1.
  • S = substitution · N = nucleophilic · the number = species in the rate-determining step.
Mission 2

SN2: one step

  • Typical of primary halogenoalkanes.
  • OH⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite Br.
  • Arrows: O lone pair → C; C–Br bond → Br. Both happen at once.
  • Halfway: a transition state [HO···C···Br]⁻, partial bonds, an energy maximum.
  • Two species in the one step: rate = k[RBr][OH⁻].
Mission 2

SN1: two steps

  • Typical of tertiary halogenoalkanes.
  • Step 1 (slow): C–Br breaks heterolytically → (CH3)3C⁺ + Br⁻. Arrow: C–Br bond → Br.
  • (CH3)3C⁺ is a carbocation intermediate, planar around C⁺.
  • Step 2 (fast): lone pair on O of OH⁻ → C⁺ → (CH3)3COH.
  • Only RBr is in the slow step: rate = k[RBr]. Zero order in OH⁻.
Quick check
?2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CBr, reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions by the SN1 mechanism. Which describes the first step?
Mission 2

Evidence from rate equations

  • Change one concentration at a time and measure the initial rate.
  • Primary: double [OH⁻] → rate doubles. OH⁻ is in the slow step → SN2.
  • Tertiary: double [OH⁻] → rate unchanged. OH⁻ is not in the slow step → SN1.
  • In both, doubling [RBr] doubles the rate.
  • The rate equation tells you which species are in the rate-determining step.
Quick check
?A halogenoalkane, RX, is hydrolysed by OH⁻(aq). Illustrative initial-rate data:
Which rate equation and mechanism fit?
  • Exp 1: [RX] 0.010, [OH⁻] 0.10 mol dm⁻³ → rate 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  • Exp 2: [RX] 0.020, [OH⁻] 0.10 mol dm⁻³ → rate 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  • Exp 3: [RX] 0.010, [OH⁻] 0.20 mol dm⁻³ → rate 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
Mission 2

Why the routes differ

  • Tertiary: three alkyl groups push electrons towards C⁺, stabilising the carbocation.
  • They also crowd the back of the carbon, blocking attack. → SN1.
  • Primary: the carbocation would be too unstable, and the back is open. → SN2.
  • Secondary: both mechanisms can occur.
  • Hydrolysis rate (e.g. AgNO3 in ethanol): tertiary > secondary > primary.
Mission 2

SN2 turns the molecule inside out

  • OH⁻ bonds at the back as Br leaves at the front.
  • The other three groups flip over, like an umbrella in a gale.
  • This is inversion of configuration.
  • SN1 is different: OH⁻ can attack the planar C⁺ from either side equally.
Mission 2

Evidence from optical activity

  • Start with one enantiomer of a chiral halogenoalkane: it rotates plane-polarised light.
  • SN2: one enantiomer → one enantiomer of product (inverted). Product is optically active.
  • SN1: planar carbocation, attack on both faces equally → racemic mixture (50 : 50).
  • A racemic mixture causes no net rotation: optically inactive.
  • So: active product → SN2. Inactive product → SN1.
Quick check
?A single enantiomer of 3-bromo-3-methylhexane rotates plane-polarised light. After hydrolysis with OH⁻(aq), the 3-methylhexan-3-ol formed does not rotate the light. What does this show?
Mission 2

Energy profiles

  • SN2: one hump. The top is the transition state. No intermediate.
  • SN1: two humps. The carbocation intermediate sits in the dip between them.
  • The dip lies above the reactants and below both humps.
  • The first hump (the slow step) is the higher one.
Mission 2

Why iodoalkanes react fastest

  • In both mechanisms the C–X bond breaks.
  • C–Cl 346 · C–Br 290 · C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Weaker bond → breaks faster: RI > RBr > RCl, same R and conditions.
  • In SN1, C–X breaking is the slow step.
Quick check
?Which statement about the mechanisms of nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes is correct?
Mission check

?Two isomers of C5H11Cl are each hydrolysed by OH⁻(aq). Doubling [OH⁻] doubles the rate for 1-chloropentane, but leaves the rate unchanged for 2-chloro-2-methylbutane. Which statement is correct?

The tertiary carbon forms a stable carbocation and is crowded at the back → SN1. The primary carbon's back is open and its carbocation unstable → SN2. That's why the two C4H9Br isomers take different routes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Make a guess: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Mission 3

The carbonyl group

  • Carbonyl: C=O.
  • Aldehyde: C=O at the end of the chain, with an H on it. R–CHO. Name ends -al: ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Ketone: C=O inside the chain. R–CO–R′. Name ends -one: propanone, CH3COCH3.
  • The C=O carbon is trigonal planar (bond angles about 120°).
Mission 3

Boiling points and solubility

  • C=O is polar, so molecules attract by permanent dipole–dipole forces.
  • No H on O, so no hydrogen bonds between carbonyl molecules.
  • Boiling points (Mr about 58–60): butane −1 °C · propanal 48 °C · propanone 56 °C · propan-1-ol 97 °C.
  • The O lone pairs accept hydrogen bonds from water: small aldehydes and ketones dissolve.
  • Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain grows.
Mission 3

Why nucleophiles choose C=O

  • O is more electronegative: Cδ+=Oδ−.
  • A nucleophile (lone-pair donor) is attracted to the δ+ carbon.
  • Alkene C=C: non-polar and electron-rich. It repels nucleophiles and attracts electrophiles.
  • So C=O undergoes nucleophilic addition: nucleophile to C, then H to O.
Mission 3

Reduction with LiAlH4

  • LiAlH4 (lithium tetrahydridoaluminate) in dry ether.
  • Dry: LiAlH4 reacts violently with water. Water or dilute acid is added only afterwards.
  • Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH
  • Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3
  • [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • The nucleophile is the hydride ion, H⁻.
Quick check
?Butanone, CH3COCH2CH3, is reduced.
What is the organic product?
  • Reagent: LiAlH4 in dry ether, then dilute acid.
Mission 3

Adding cyanide: hydroxynitriles

  • Ethanal + HCN → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN
  • Propanone + HCN → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH3)2C(OH)CN
  • The chain gains one carbon.
  • Reagent: HCN in the presence of KCN.
  • HCN is a weak acid: little CN⁻ on its own. The cyanide salt supplies the CN⁻ nucleophile.
Mission 3

Mechanism: cyanide

  • Show Cδ+=Oδ− dipole.
  • Arrow 1: from the lone pair on the C of CN⁻ to the carbonyl C.
  • Arrow 2: from the C=O π bond to the O. An alkoxide intermediate, C–O⁻, forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on O⁻ to the H of HCN; arrow 4: from the H–C bond of HCN to its C. CN⁻ is regenerated.
Quick check
?In the mechanism for the reaction of propanone with cyanide, where does the first curly arrow start and end?
Mission 3

Why a racemic mixture forms

  • The C=O group is planar.
  • CN⁻ attacks from above or below with equal probability.
  • If the carbon ends up with four different groups, it is a chiral centre.
  • So the product is a 50:50 mixture of enantiomers: a racemic mixture.
  • It does not rotate plane-polarised light.
  • Evidence: the product mixture is optically inactive, as expected for attack on a planar group.
  • Aldehydes (except methanal) and unsymmetrical ketones give racemates. Propanone does not: two CH3 groups.
Quick check
?Which carbonyl compound gives a racemic mixture when it reacts with HCN?
Mission check

?Hex-5-en-2-one, CH2=CHCH2CH2COCH3, has a C=C bond and a C=O bond.
What is the organic product?
  • It is treated with LiAlH4 in dry ether, then dilute acid.

Nucleophiles (H⁻, CN⁻) are drawn to the δ+ carbon of C=O. The C=C bond has no δ+ end, so they pass it by.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Make a guess: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Mission 4

Why aldehydes are easy to oxidise

  • Aldehyde: an H on the carbonyl carbon. Ketone: two carbons on it.
  • Mild oxidising agents turn that C–H into C–OH: aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
  • RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
  • Ketones would need a C–C bond broken: mild oxidising agents cannot do it.
  • The oxidising agent is reduced, and changes colour. That is the test.
Mission 4

Tollens' reagent: the silver mirror

  • Make it fresh: AgNO3(aq) + a drop of NaOH(aq) → brown precipitate; add dilute NH3(aq) until it just dissolves. This gives [Ag(NH3)2]⁺.
  • Warm in a water bath, not over a flame.
  • Aldehyde: silver mirror (or grey-black precipitate). Ketone: no change.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag: silver ions are reduced; the aldehyde is oxidised.
  • Pour away straight after use: stored reagent can form explosive solids.
Mission 4

Fehling's and Benedict's solutions

  • Blue solution of a copper(II) complex, in alkali.
  • Warm with an aldehyde: brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu2O.
  • Cu²⁺ + e⁻ → Cu⁺
  • Ketone: stays blue.
  • Benedict's solution gives the same colour change.
  • Aromatic aldehydes such as benzaldehyde give little or no change with Fehling's: use Tollens'.
Mission 4

Acidified dichromate

  • K2Cr2O7 with dilute H2SO4, warm.
  • Aldehyde: orange → green (Cr2O7²⁻ reduced to Cr³⁺).
  • Ketone: stays orange.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols also turn it green, so it is not a test for aldehydes alone.
Quick check
?Propanal is warmed with Tollens' reagent and a silver mirror forms. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

2,4-DNPH: is there a C=O?

  • 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNPH). Also called Brady's reagent.
  • Add a few drops of the compound: yellow / orange precipitate with aldehydes and ketones.
  • No precipitate with the C=O of carboxylic acids or esters.
  • It tells you C=O is present, not which kind.
Mission 4

Which carbonyl? Melting point of the derivative

  • 1. Filter off the precipitate.
  • 2. Recrystallise: dissolve in the minimum hot solvent (e.g. ethanol), cool, filter, wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
  • 3. Measure the melting point.
  • 4. Compare with a data table of derivative melting points.
  • Impurities lower the melting point and make it melt over a wider range.
Quick check
?A compound gives an orange precipitate with 2,4-DNPH. After recrystallising, the derivative melts at 154–156 °C. The compound gives no silver mirror with Tollens' reagent. Melting points of 2,4-DNPH derivatives: propanal 155 °C · propanone 126 °C · butanal 123 °C · pentan-3-one 156 °C. What is the compound?
Mission 4

The iodoform test

  • Reagent: iodine in the presence of alkali (I2 + NaOH(aq)), warm.
  • Positive: pale yellow precipitate of CHI3, antiseptic smell.
  • Given by CH3CO– compounds: ethanal (the only aldehyde) and methyl ketones.
  • Also by CH3CH(OH)– alcohols (ethanol, propan-2-ol): the reagent oxidises them to CH3CO– first.
  • CH3COCH3 + 3I2 + 4NaOH → CHI3 + CH3COONa + 3NaI + 3H2O
Quick check
?Which compound gives a pale yellow precipitate when warmed with iodine and sodium hydroxide solution?
Mission 4

Planning a test sequence

  • Step 1: 2,4-DNPH. Precipitate → aldehyde or ketone.
  • Step 2: Tollens' or Fehling's. Positive → aldehyde; no change → ketone.
  • Or warm acidified dichromate: green → aldehyde (if no alcohol is present).
  • Step 3: iodine + alkali. Pale yellow precipitate → CH3CO– group.
  • Step 4: melting point of the 2,4-DNPH derivative → which compound.
  • One test rarely identifies a compound. Each test narrows the list.
Quick check
?Propanal and propanone are each warmed with Fehling's solution. What is seen?
Mission check

?4-Oxopentanal, CH3COCH2CH2CHO, has an aldehyde group and a ketone group. It is warmed with Tollens' reagent. What happens?

The H on an aldehyde's carbonyl carbon is what makes it easy to oxidise. That is why only aldehydes give a silver mirror.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Swap one hydrogen for a carbon chain: why does a sour acid become a sweet-smelling liquid?

Make a guess: Swap one hydrogen for a carbon chain: why does a sour acid become a sweet-smelling liquid?

Mission 5

Acids and esters: the structures

  • Carboxylic acid: –COOH. Ethanoic acid, CH3COOH.
  • Ester: –COO– with a carbon on both sides. RCOOR′.
  • An ester is an acid with the O–H hydrogen replaced by an alkyl group.
  • Name: alkyl (from the alcohol) + -oate (from the acid).
  • Methyl ethanoate, CH3COOCH3. Ethyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH2CH3.
  • Acyl chloride: –COCl. Ethanoyl chloride, CH3COCl (see Mission 6).
Mission 5

Hydrogen bonding: boiling point and solubility

  • Acids have O–H and C=O: strong hydrogen bonds between molecules.
  • Boiling points (Mr 60): ethanoic acid 118 °C · propan-1-ol 97 °C · methyl methanoate 32 °C.
  • Small acids mix with water: they hydrogen bond to water molecules.
  • Solubility falls as the hydrocarbon chain grows.
Mission 5

Weak acids that still make salts

  • CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺: only partly dissociates. A weak acid.
  • Alkali: CH3COOH + NaOH → CH3COONa + H2O
  • Carbonate: 2CH3COOH + Na2CO3 → 2CH3COONa + CO2 + H2O
  • Salts are named -oate: sodium ethanoate.
Mission 5

The carbonate test

  • Add sodium carbonate: effervescence.
  • The gas turns limewater milky: CO2.
  • Alcohols, aldehydes, ketones and esters: no fizzing.
Quick check
?0.010 mol of ethanoic acid reacts with excess sodium carbonate. What amount of carbon dioxide forms?
Mission 5

Making carboxylic acids

  • Oxidise a primary alcohol or an aldehyde: reflux with excess acidified K2Cr2O7 (orange → green).
  • CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O
  • Hydrolyse a nitrile: reflux with dilute acid.
  • CH3CN + 2H2O + HCl → CH3COOH + NH4Cl
Mission 5

Reducing and chlorinating acids

  • LiAlH4 in dry ether reduces the acid to a primary alcohol.
  • CH3COOH + 4[H] → CH3CH2OH + H2O
  • PCl5: CH3COOH + PCl5 → CH3COCl + POCl3 + HCl (steamy fumes).
Quick check
?Propanoic acid, CH3CH2COOH, is treated with LiAlH4 in dry ether, followed by dilute acid. What is the organic product?
Mission 5

Esterification

  • Carboxylic acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water
  • CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH ⇌ CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O
  • Catalyst: concentrated H2SO4. Warm or reflux.
  • Reversible: an excess of one reactant gives a higher yield.
  • The water forms from the acid's –OH and the alcohol's H.
Quick check
?Propanoic acid is warmed with methanol and a few drops of concentrated sulfuric acid. What ester forms?
Mission 5

Hydrolysis: splitting the ester

  • Acid: reflux with dilute H2SO4(aq). Reversible; does not go to completion.
  • CH3COOCH2CH3 + H2O ⇌ CH3COOH + CH3CH2OH
  • Alkali: reflux with NaOH(aq). Goes to completion.
  • CH3COOCH2CH3 + NaOH → CH3COONa + CH3CH2OH
  • The carboxylate ion cannot react with the alcohol, so it does not reverse.
  • Add strong acid afterwards to get the free carboxylic acid.
Quick check
?Methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3, is heated under reflux with sodium hydroxide solution. What are the products?
Mission check

?Two bottles, X and Y, each hold a liquid of formula C3H6O2. One is propanoic acid; the other is methyl ethanoate.
X: boils at 141 °C, fizzes with sodium carbonate.
Y: boils at 57 °C, smells fruity, no fizzing.
Which statement is correct?

Replacing the acid's O–H hydrogen with a carbon group removes both its acidity and its hydrogen bonding. What is left is a volatile, fruity ester.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does ethanoyl chloride fume in moist air, when ethanoic acid just sits in the bottle?

Make a guess: Why does ethanoyl chloride fume in moist air, when ethanoic acid just sits in the bottle?

Mission 6

Meet the acid derivatives

  • Swap the –OH of a carboxylic acid for another group: an acid derivative.
  • Acyl chloride: –COCl. Ethanoyl chloride, CH3COCl. Name ends -oyl chloride.
  • Acid anhydride: two acyl groups joined by O. Ethanoic anhydride, (CH3CO)2O.
  • Amide: –CONH2. Ethanamide, CH3CONH2 (a primary amide).
  • Secondary (N-substituted) amide: –CONHR, e.g. N-methylethanamide, CH3CONHCH3.
Mission 6

Making an acyl chloride

  • Start from the carboxylic acid. Dry conditions: water destroys the product.
  • PCl5 (from Mission 5): CH3COOH + PCl5 → CH3COCl + POCl3 + HCl
  • Steamy fumes of HCl. The acyl chloride is separated by distillation.
Mission 6

Why so reactive?

  • The carbonyl carbon is bonded to two electronegative atoms: O and Cl.
  • So it is strongly δ+: a target for any nucleophile with a lone pair.
  • Chlorine then leaves easily as Cl⁻: a good leaving group.
  • So acyl chlorides react at room temperature, often violently. No catalyst.
  • In a carboxylic acid the C is less δ+, and –OH is a poor leaving group.
  • So, the opening question: water vapour in the air hydrolyses it. The "fumes" are HCl.
Quick check
?Ethanoyl chloride reacts with cold water within seconds. Ethanoic acid can stay in water for years without changing. Which explains the difference?
Mission 6

Four nucleophiles, one pattern

  • The nucleophile swaps in for Cl. HCl is the other product.
  • Water → carboxylic acid: CH3COCl + H2O → CH3COOH + HCl
  • Alcohol → ester: CH3COCl + CH3CH2OH → CH3COOCH2CH3 + HCl. Fast, and not reversible.
  • Concentrated ammonia → primary amide:
  • CH3COCl + 2NH3 → CH3CONH2 + NH4Cl. The second NH3 takes up the HCl.
  • Primary amine → N-substituted amide: CH3COCl + 2CH3NH2 → CH3CONHCH3 + CH3NH3⁺Cl⁻
Quick check
?Ethanoyl chloride is added dropwise to an excess of ethylamine, CH3CH2NH2. Which two products form?
Mission 6

Acid anhydrides: the gentler cousin

  • Same reactions, but the leaving group is ethanoate, not Cl⁻.
  • By-product: ethanoic acid, not HCl. Slower: warming is usually needed.
  • You meet it in one key reaction: making aspirin.
Mission 6

Making aspirin

  • Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) has a phenolic –OH and a –COOH.
  • Ethanoic anhydride ethanoylates the –OH, making an ester: aspirin.
  • C7H6O3 + (CH3CO)2O → C9H8O4 + CH3COOH
  • Method: warm with a few drops of concentrated acid catalyst (H3PO4 or H2SO4).
  • Add cold water; collect crystals by filtration under reduced pressure.
  • Recrystallise from the minimum of hot solvent; dry; check the melting point (pure: about 136 °C, sharp).
Quick check
?Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid) is warmed with ethanoic anhydride to make aspirin. What happens?
Mission check

?Chloroethane, CH3CH2Cl, and ethanoyl chloride, CH3COCl, are each added to cold water. What do you see, and why?

Two electronegative atoms on one carbon make acyl chlorides the most reactive acid derivatives. Even water, a weak nucleophile, attacks at once.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Make a guess: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Mission 7

Building the skeleton

  • Most organic reactions only swap functional groups. The carbon chain stays the same length.
  • To make a longer chain, you need a reaction that forms a new C–C bond.
  • The trick: a carbon nucleophile, a carbon atom with a lone pair or a δ− charge.
  • Cyanide, ⁻:C≡N, is the simplest: it adds one carbon.
  • Grignard reagents add a whole alkyl group (later in this mission).
Mission 7

Cyanide + halogenoalkane

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻
  • Product: propanenitrile. The chain grows from 2 C to 3 C.
  • Naming: count the C≡N carbon in the chain. CH3CH2CN is propanenitrile.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic substitution. Lone pair on the C of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ C; the C–Br electrons go to Br.
  • KCN is highly toxic. Acid turns cyanide into HCN gas, so cyanide and acid only ever meet in a fume cupboard, in small amounts, under controlled conditions.
Mission 7

Cyanide + carbonyl

  • Aldehydes and ketones add cyanide across C=O: hydroxynitriles (see Mission 3).
  • Reagent: HCN in the presence of KCN.
  • CH3CHO → CH3CH(OH)CN, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. Chain: 2 C → 3 C.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic addition. Product is racemic when the new C is chiral.
  • Hazards: HCN is a very toxic gas, and cyanide salts are toxic too. Fume cupboard, gloves, small amounts.

Mission 7

Nitrile → carboxylic acid

  • Acid hydrolysis: heat under reflux with dilute HCl.
  • CH3CH2CN + 2H2O + HCl → CH3CH2COOH + NH4Cl
  • The C≡N carbon becomes the –COOH carbon.
  • From a hydroxynitrile: CH3CH(OH)CN → CH3CH(OH)COOH, 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid).
Mission 7

Nitrile → amine

  • Reduction with LiAlH4 in dry ether: CH3CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2NH2
  • Product: propylamine (propan-1-amine), one carbon longer than bromoethane.
  • Unlike ammonia + halogenoalkane, this gives only the primary amine: no further substitution.
Quick check
?2-Hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN, is heated under reflux with dilute hydrochloric acid. What is the organic product?
Mission 7

Grignard reagents

  • Warm a halogenoalkane with magnesium in dry ether: CH3CH2Br + Mg → CH3CH2MgBr
  • CH3CH2MgBr is a Grignard reagent, RMgX.
  • Mg is much less electronegative than C, so the C–Mg carbon is δ−: a carbon nucleophile.
  • It must be kept dry. Water destroys it: CH3CH2MgBr + H2O → CH3CH3 + Mg(OH)Br
Mission 7

Grignard + CO2 and carbonyls

  • The δ− carbon attacks the δ+ carbon of C=O, forming a new C–C bond. Then add dilute acid.
  • + CO2 (dry ice) → carboxylic acid, one C longer: CH3CH2MgBr → CH3CH2COOH
  • + methanal → primary alcohol: CH3CH2MgBr + HCHO → CH3CH2CH2OH
  • + other aldehyde → secondary alcohol: CH3CH2MgBr + CH3CHO → CH3CH2CH(OH)CH3
  • + ketone → tertiary alcohol: CH3MgBr + CH3COCH3 → (CH3)3COH
  • All in dry ether, then dilute acid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • E/Z: restricted rotation about C=C plus two different groups on each carbon; name by CIP priorities.
  • A chiral centre (four different groups) gives enantiomers: equal and opposite rotation of plane-polarised light; a racemate is inactive; they differ only in chiral environments.

Mission 2

  • SN2 (primary): one step, OH⁻ attacks opposite X, rate = k[RX][OH⁻].
  • SN1 (tertiary): slow C–X break to a carbocation, then fast attack; rate = k[RX].
  • SN2 gives one inverted enantiomer (optically active); SN1 gives a racemic mixture (inactive).

Mission 3

  • C=O is polar: nucleophiles add to the δ+ carbon, then the O gains H. C=C is not polar: no nucleophilic addition.
  • Attack from either face of the planar C=O gives a racemic mixture when a chiral centre forms.

Mission 4

  • Aldehydes (H on the C=O carbon) are oxidised to carboxylic acids; ketones are not. The reagent is reduced and changes colour.
  • 2,4-DNPH shows a C=O is present; the derivative's melting point identifies the compound.

Mission 5

  • Acid → ester: the O–H hydrogen is replaced by an alkyl group. No acidity, no hydrogen bonding between molecules.
  • Esters form with an acid catalyst (reversible) and hydrolyse with acid (reversible) or alkali (complete, gives the salt).

Mission 6

  • Acyl chlorides: δ+ carbonyl C and Cl⁻ leaving group. Water → acid, alcohol → ester, NH3 → amide, amine → N-substituted amide; HCl formed.
  • Ethanoic anhydride reacts the same way, more gently, giving ethanoic acid: used to make aspirin.

Mission 7

  • CN⁻ adds one carbon: with halogenoalkanes (substitution) or carbonyls (addition).
  • Nitriles can be reduced to amines.
  • Nitriles can be hydrolysed to carboxylic acids.
  • Grignard reagents (RMgX, dry ether) add a whole alkyl group to CO2 or a carbonyl.
