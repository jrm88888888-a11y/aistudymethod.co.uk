The Vietnam Conflict 1945-75

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole Edexcel 4HI1 depth study: the origins in French Indochina, the escalation of US involvement, the Tet Offensive and its turning point, opposition and My Lai, Vietnamisation and withdrawal, the fall of Saigon, and why the USA failed.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some are multiple-choice, some ask you to explain), and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Origins · French Indochina

A colonial war begins

Before the war, Vietnam was part of French Indochina. During WWII Japan occupied it; when Japan surrendered in 1945, the communist-nationalist Ho Chi Minh declared independence and led the Vietminh against the returning French.

  • Ho Chi Minh — nationalist and communist leader who wanted a united, independent Vietnam.
  • The Vietminh — his guerrilla army, fighting the French from 1946.
  • The USA funded France, fearing the spread of communism in Asia.

Causation: the conflict began as a war against colonial rule, not against America. This origin explains why so many Vietnamese saw later US involvement as another foreign occupier.

1954 · The French defeat

Dien Bien Phu & Geneva

In 1954, the Vietminh general Vo Nguyen Giap surrounded the French base at Dien Bien Phu, dragging heavy artillery up the surrounding hills. After a long siege the French were forced to surrender — ending French rule.

The Geneva Agreement (1954) then divided Vietnam along the 17th parallel:

  • North Vietnam — communist, led by Ho Chi Minh.
  • South Vietnam — anti-communist, soon led by Ngo Dinh Diem.
  • Nationwide elections were promised for 1956 — but never held.
The South, the Vietcong & the domino theory

Why the USA got drawn in

In the South, Ngo Dinh Diem's government was corrupt, unpopular and persecuted Buddhists. Opposition grew into the communist-backed Vietcong (NLF), supplied from the North.

US thinking was shaped by the domino theory: if South Vietnam fell to communism, neighbouring countries would topple one after another like dominoes.

Misconception buster: the USA did not intervene simply to defend democracy — Diem's regime was undemocratic. The real driver was the domino theory and Cold-War containment.

Escalation · 1961-64

From advisers to open war

President Kennedy sent thousands of military advisers and backed the Strategic Hamlet Programme (moving villagers into fortified hamlets to cut them off from the Vietcong). Diem was overthrown and killed in 1963.

The turning point came under President Lyndon B. Johnson. In August 1964 the Gulf of Tonkin incident — a disputed attack on US ships — led Congress to pass the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, giving Johnson the power to wage full-scale war.

Source awareness: historians still debate whether the second Gulf of Tonkin "attack" really happened. Johnson used the incident to justify escalation — a reminder to question why and when a source was produced.

US tactics

Johnson's war machine

From 1965 Johnson escalated massively. US tactics relied on firepower and technology:

  • Operation Rolling Thunder — a sustained bombing campaign of North Vietnam from 1965.
  • Search and destroy — patrols hunted Vietcong; success measured by "body counts".
  • Agent Orange — a chemical defoliant stripping jungle cover.
  • Napalm — a burning jelly used against troops and villages.
Vietcong tactics

Guerrilla warfare

The Vietcong could not match US firepower, so they fought a guerrilla war: ambushes, booby traps, tunnels, and blending in with civilians. Supplies came south along the Ho Chi Minh Trail through Laos and Cambodia.

  • Hit-and-run ambushes — then vanish into the jungle or villages.
  • Booby traps & tunnels — cheap defences that wore down US morale.
  • The Ho Chi Minh Trail — a supply route the USA could never fully cut.
  • "Hearts and minds" — the Vietcong won civilian support the USA struggled to gain.

Causation link: US high-tech tactics were designed for open battle; guerrilla warfare denied them a clear enemy to fight — a key reason the USA could not win.

1968 · The turning point

The Tet Offensive

In January 1968, during the Tet (New Year) holiday, the Vietcong and North Vietnamese launched surprise attacks on over 100 towns and cities across the South — even reaching the US embassy in Saigon and the city of Hue.

Militarily, the offensive failed: the Vietcong suffered huge losses and were driven back. But it was shown live on American television, shattering claims that the war was nearly won.

Misconception buster: Tet was a military victory for the USA but a political disaster. It turned US public opinion against the war and is the key turning point of the conflict.

Opposition & My Lai

The war loses support

As casualties mounted and the draft continued, opposition to the war grew — protests, marches and campus demonstrations spread across the USA.

In March 1968, US soldiers massacred hundreds of unarmed civilians — mostly women, children and the elderly — in the village of My Lai. When the story broke in 1969, it convinced many Americans that the war had become immoral.

Nixon · Vietnamisation

Getting America out

President Richard Nixon (from 1969) introduced Vietnamisation: building up the South Vietnamese army (ARVN) so US troops could gradually withdraw, while continuing heavy bombing and secretly expanding the war into Cambodia and Laos.

His adviser Henry Kissinger negotiated with the North. The Paris Peace Accords (ceasefire) of January 1973 allowed the USA to pull out its combat troops.

1975 · The end

The fall of Saigon

After the US withdrawal, the weakened South could not hold on. North Vietnamese forces launched a final offensive in early 1975. On 30 April 1975, tanks smashed through the gates of the presidential palace and Saigon fell.

Vietnam was reunited under communist control. The last Americans were evacuated by helicopter — an image of defeat seen around the world.

Misconception buster: the USA was never defeated in a major open battle. It lost the war politically and strategically — through cost, casualties, lost public support and an enemy it could not pin down.

Why the USA failed

Reasons for defeat

Exam questions often ask you to explain the reasons for US failure. Group them:

  • Military: guerrilla tactics, tunnels and the Ho Chi Minh Trail neutralised US firepower.
  • Political: Tet and My Lai destroyed US public support; opposition grew.
  • Vietnamese strength: Vietcong morale, local support and Northern determination.
  • US weakness: low morale, "body count" tactics, corrupt Southern governments, huge cost.

Working with sources

Thinking like a historian

Edexcel rewards source and interpretation skills. When you meet a source or an interpretation, ask:

  • Who produced it, and why? (A US government report vs a protest poster.)
  • When? A source from 1965 differs from one after Tet or My Lai.
  • What is missing? TV showed the fighting in cities, not the Vietcong's heavy losses.
  • Interpretations differ: some stress US failure; others stress how close Tet came to breaking the North.

Tip: use provenance (nature, origin, purpose) plus your own knowledge to judge how useful or reliable a source is — never dismiss a source just because it is biased; ask what its bias reveals.
