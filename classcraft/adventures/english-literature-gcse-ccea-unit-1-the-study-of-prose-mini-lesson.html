Mission 1

A page from a novel you'll never read. Where do you even start?

Make a guess: A page from a novel you'll never read. Where do you even start?

Prose skills · unseen proseScreen 1

Everything you need is on the page

  • You won't know the book, and you don't need to. The passage and its short introduction give you all you need.
  • You're rewarded for understanding what happens and what it suggests, analysing how language and structure create effects, and a personal response backed by evidence.
  • Write about it as a made thing: "Hardy presents…", not just "the girls feel…".
  • Don't invent what happens before or after. Stay with the page.

Prose skills · unseen proseScreen 6

Follow how the passage moves

  • Unseen answers gain marks for structure as well as words. Track where the passage starts, where it moves, and where it ends.
  • Hardy begins close up with the task (turnips, the hacker), widens to the whole field, lifts to the sky, then sets the two blank faces against each other.
  • The girls come last, at the end of the longest sentence, after earth and sky: the sentence's shape makes them small too.
  • Write tentatively where meaning is open: "perhaps", "this may suggest".

Mission 2

'This is a metaphor' earns almost nothing. What makes it analysis?

Make a guess: 'This is a metaphor' earns almost nothing. What makes it analysis?

Literature skills · method and effectScreen 1

Spotting is not analysing

  • Feature-spotting names a technique: 'This is a simile.' It shows you know a term, not that you understand the writing.
  • Examiners reward analysis: how a writer's language, form and structure create meanings and effects (AO2).
  • Analysis answers three questions: What has the writer done? How does it shape what we see or feel? Why does the writer want that here?
  • The term is a tool. The effect is the point.

Mission 3

Is Scrooge a person? Why do examiners care about the answer?

Make a guess: Is Scrooge a person? Why do examiners care about the answer?

Literature skills · characters as constructsScreen 1

A character is made of words

  • Scrooge has no life outside the book. Dickens chose every detail: his name, his similes, the childhood the Ghost shows.
  • So write about the maker: 'Dickens presents Scrooge as…', 'Stevenson uses Hyde to…'.
  • Characters do jobs: they carry a theme, contrast with another character (a foil), stand for an idea, or move the plot.
  • Ask of every character: what is this character for?
Literature skills · characters as constructsScreen 2

Person-talk and construct-talk

Person-talkConstruct-talk
'Scrooge is mean because he was lonely at school.''Dickens gives Scrooge a lonely childhood in Stave 2, so readers can pity him and believe he can change.'
'Hyde is scary because he looks evil.''Stevenson never lets anyone describe Hyde clearly, so readers' fear fills the gap.'
  • Person-talk guesses at feelings and causes outside the text. Construct-talk explains the writer's choices and their effect on the reader.

Literature skills · characters as constructsScreen 5

Built across the whole text

  • Stave 1: cold, hard similes: "solitary as an oyster".
  • Stave 2: the shell cracks: he "sobbed" and "wept".
  • Stave 5: new similes: "I am as light as a feather, I am as happy as an angel".
  • The change is designed: Dickens rebuilds Scrooge so readers believe redemption is possible.
  • Stevenson's Enfield finds "something down-right detestable" in Hyde but can't describe him: Hyde is built to be felt, not seen.

How your exam asks this

Mission checkScreen 9

?Is Scrooge a person? Why do examiners care about the answer?

Scrooge is not a person: he is made of Dickens's word choices, and he does a job in the story. Examiners care because writing 'Dickens presents Scrooge as…' leads you to methods, effects and purposes, while person-talk leads to guesses about feelings the text never gives.

Mission 4

Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

Make a guess: Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 1

An essay is an argument

  • Marks follow the argument, not how many quotations you use.
  • A thesis is your answer to the question in one or two sentences: how the writer presents something and why (what the text suggests).
  • Each paragraph proves one part of the thesis; quotations are its evidence.
  • This mission teaches the thesis and the plan. Full essay practice is for class.
Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 2

Unpack the question first

Example: How does Dickens present Scrooge's change in A Christmas Carol?

  • Circle the focus (Scrooge's change) and the command: how does Dickens present means methods and purpose, not just what happens.
  • Check the scope: an extract, the whole text, or both? If the question starts from an extract, plan how you'll move out to the whole text.
  • Ask so what? Why might Dickens want readers to watch this change?

Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 5

From thesis to plan

  • Break the thesis into 3–4 steps; each step becomes a paragraph.
  • Write each step as a topic sentence that argues, not a plot point.
  • Under each: a short quotation or two, the method, and context only where it explains the point.
  • Order the steps so the argument builds.
StepTopic sentenceEvidence
1Dickens makes Scrooge's coldness seem total, so change looks impossible."solitary as an oyster" (simile)
2The ghosts force Scrooge to hear his own cruelty, so change begins with recognition."decrease the surplus population", repeated by the Spirit in Stave 3
3The ending makes redemption joyful rather than grim."as merry as a schoolboy" (repeated similes)
4Through Scrooge, Dickens challenges readers who shared his view of the poor.Scrooge's support for "the Union workhouses", set up under the 1834 Poor Law

Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 7

How your exam asks this

  • Unit 1 Section A (novel) and Unit 2 Section A (drama) are exam answers; Unit 3 is a controlled-assessment essay on a whole Shakespeare play, on a theme CCEA sets for your year.
Write itScreen 8
?Plan, don't write the essay.

For the question How does Shakespeare present ambition in Macbeth?, write a thesis (one or two sentences) and three topic sentences, one for each step of your argument.

Model answer

Thesis: Shakespeare presents ambition as a desire that promises greatness but destroys whoever acts on it, warning his audience against breaking the natural order. Step 1: At first Macbeth's ambition is a secret wish he can barely admit, even to himself. Step 2: Lady Macbeth turns that wish into action by attacking his sense of manhood. Step 3: Once king, Macbeth finds his reward is fear and sleeplessness, so ambition proves empty.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

The difference is the argument. The stronger essay starts with a thesis that answers how and why, plans 3–4 steps that prove it, and uses each quotation as evidence for a step. The weaker one uses the same quotations to retell the story or list techniques.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Everything you need is on the page and in its introduction: don't guess at the rest of the book.
  • Read three times: gist, focus, zoom. Choose a few rich details and explain what each suggests.
  • Track the passage's structure too: where it starts, moves and ends.
  • In Unit 1 you answer every bullet.
  • Naming a technique earns little on its own: explain what it makes the reader see or feel, and why the writer wants that (AO2).
  • Zoom in on one precise word or phrase, embed short quotations, and offer a second reading with 'perhaps' or 'could'.
  • Write about the writer: "Dickens presents…", "Dickens uses…".
  • A character is a construct: write 'Dickens presents…', 'Stevenson uses…', and ask what each character is for.
  • Construct-talk explains the writer's choices and their effect on the reader; person-talk guesses at feelings and causes outside the text.
  • Track how a writer builds or changes a character across the whole text, as Dickens does from Stave 1 to Stave 5.
  • A thesis answers the question in one or two sentences: how the writer presents something, and why.
  • A plan breaks the thesis into 3–4 steps, each with a topic sentence that argues and a quotation as evidence.
  • Unpack the question first (focus, command, scope), and use context only where it explains a point. Essay writing itself is practised in class.
