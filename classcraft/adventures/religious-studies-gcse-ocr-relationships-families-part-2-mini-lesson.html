Mission 1

Ending a marriage

  • Divorce: the legal ending of a marriage. Separation: living apart while still married.
  • Annulment: a declaration that a marriage was never valid in the first place.
  • Remarriage: marrying again while a former spouse is still alive.
  • Law in England and Wales: since April 2022, no-fault divorce (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020). The only ground: the marriage has broken down irretrievably; no one has to be blamed.
  • Common reasons: adultery, abuse, addiction, money worries, growing apart.
Mission 1

What Jesus taught

  • Marriage is God's work: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; Genesis 2:24).
  • In Matthew, one exception: sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9; 5:32). Mark has no exception (Mark 10:11–12).
  • Paul: a wife should not leave her husband; if she does, she should stay unmarried or be reconciled (1 Corinthians 7:10–11, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Catholic teaching: annulment, not divorce

  • A valid, consummated sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved by any human power (CCC 2382, paraphrase).
  • Divorce is a grave offence against the natural law (CCC 2384, paraphrase), but civil divorce may be tolerated to protect legal rights or children (CCC 2383); separation is allowed.
  • A spouse unjustly abandoned is not to blame (CCC 2386).
  • Remarriage while a former spouse lives is not accepted; the person cannot usually receive Holy Communion.
  • Annulment (declaration of nullity): a Church tribunal finds the marriage was never valid, e.g. no free consent, force or fear, no intention of a lifelong faithful union open to children. Both may then marry in church.
  • By contrast, the Church of England has allowed divorced people to remarry in church, at the priest's discretion, since 2002.

Mission 1Diversity

Islam

  • Divorce is allowed as a last resort; a hadith calls it the most disliked of permitted things (Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, try to reconcile: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk." (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall)
  • Talaq (divorce by the husband), then iddah: a waiting time of three monthly cycles, to allow reconciliation and check for pregnancy (Qur'an 2:228).
  • "Divorce must be pronounced twice and then (a woman) must be retained in honour or released in kindness." (Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall)
  • A wife can seek divorce by khul' (returning her dowry) or through a judge or Shari'ah council.
Mission 1Diversity

Judaism

  • Divorce is allowed, though sad. Deuteronomy 24:1: the husband "writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • The get (bill of divorce) is given by the husband before a Beth Din (rabbinic court). The wife must accept it freely.
  • Orthodox: a civil divorce is not enough; without a get neither may remarry in Orthodox Judaism. A woman whose husband refuses is an agunah ("chained" woman).
  • Reform: divorce and remarriage accepted; a get is encouraged but the movement's own procedures help where a husband refuses.
Mission 1Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: marriage is a sacred samskara, ideally binding beyond this life; divorce was not part of traditional Hindu law.
  • Buddhists: marriage is a social contract, not a sacrament; no religious ban on divorce. Reduce suffering; act with compassion (karuna).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Equal but different, or equal and the same: who should do what in a family?

Mission 2

Roles then and now

  • Traditional roles: man as breadwinner and head; woman as homemaker and main carer.
  • Today: most couples both work; more fathers share childcare or stay at home; roles are negotiated.
  • Gender equality: men and women have equal worth, rights and opportunities.
  • Two religious approaches:
  • Complementarian: equal in worth, different and complementary roles.
  • Egalitarian: equal in worth, roles shared or interchangeable, decided by gifts and circumstances.
Mission 2

Genesis: equal in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Both share God's image and the same blessing and task (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is "a helper comparable to him". The Hebrew word (ezer) is also used of God helping Israel, so it does not mean inferior.
  • Genesis 3:16: after the Fall, "he will rule over you". Many Christians and Jews read this as the result of sin, not God's original plan.
Mission 2Read the source

Ephesians 5

"subjecting yourselves to one another in the fear of Christ." (Ephesians 5:21)

"Wives, be subject to your own husbands, as to the Lord." (Ephesians 5:22)

"Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25)

  • Compare Colossians 3:18–19: wives be in subjection; husbands love your wives "and don't be bitter against them".

Mission 2Diversity

Christian views on roles

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals, some traditional Christians): the husband is a loving, serving head; the wife supports and respects him (Ephesians 5:22–25).
  • Egalitarian (many Anglicans, Methodists, liberal Christians): 5:21 means mutual submission; "neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28); roles by gifts.
  • Catholic: man and woman are equal in dignity and complementary. John Paul II (Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988) read Ephesians 5 as mutual submission.

Mission 2Diversity

Islam

  • Equal before Allah: "Whosoever doeth right, whether male or female, and is a believer..." (Qur'an 16:97, Pickthall); both created "from a single soul" (Qur'an 4:1).
  • Different responsibilities: the husband must provide for the family (Qur'an 4:34); a wife's own earnings and property remain hers; mahr (dowry) is paid to her.
  • "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them." (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall) Scholars differ on what the "degree" means (e.g. extra responsibility).
  • Surah 4:3 limits polygyny by the demand of justice; 2:228–232 protect women in divorce.
  • Diversity: traditional Muslims stress distinct roles; many British Muslims share work and childcare.
Mission 2Diversity

Judaism

  • "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917) This poem (31:10–31) is sung in many Jewish homes on Friday night, often by the husband; the woman runs a home, trades and buys a field (31:16).
  • Orthodox Jews: equal worth, different roles. Women are exempt from many time-bound commandments, partly to allow for family duties; the home is a woman's special sphere of holiness (e.g. Shabbat candles).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews: egalitarian: roles in home and synagogue are shared.
  • Genesis 2:18 ("helper") is read by many Jews as partnership.
Mission 2Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: Manusmriti 3.55–60 (paraphrase): women must be honoured by fathers, brothers and husbands; where women are honoured the gods rejoice, and where they are not, rites bring no reward.
  • The wife is ardhangini ("half of the body") of her husband; stridharma (women's duty) traditionally centred on home and family. Many Hindus today share roles; others keep traditional ones.
  • Buddhists: Sigalovada Sutta: the husband honours her, does not look down on her, is faithful, gives her authority in the home and gives her gifts (ornaments); the wife manages the home well, is hospitable to relatives, faithful, protects the family's wealth and is skilful.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Men and women should have different roles in the family."

  • Reasons for: complementarian reading of Ephesians 5:22–25 and Qur'an 4:34; Orthodox Jewish and traditional Hindu teaching; biological differences (pregnancy, breastfeeding).
  • Reasons against: Genesis 1:27 and Galatians 3:28; Ephesians 5:21 mutual submission; GGS 788 "one light in two bodies"; Humanist equality; modern families need flexibility.
  • Another view: equal worth is essential; whether roles differ should be the couple's free choice.
  • Judge it by: Does "different" mean "unequal"? Are scripture's roles timeless or cultural? Who decides: tradition or the couple?
Mission check

?In a family, the mother is a surgeon and the father works part-time so he can do the school run and cook. Which statement best describes how the main religious traditions would see this?

The real disagreement is not about whether women and men are equal in worth, but whether equal means the same roles.

Mission 3

Prejudice, discrimination, equality

  • Prejudice: judging someone unfairly before knowing them (an attitude). Sexism: prejudice based on sex.
  • Discrimination: treating someone unfairly because of a prejudice (an action).
  • Gender equality: equal rights, status and opportunities for women and men.
  • UK law: Equal Pay Act 1970; Sex Discrimination Act 1975; now the Equality Act 2010.
  • Examples: unequal pay for the same work; women passed over for promotion; girls denied schooling (Malala Yousafzai, shot in 2012 for campaigning for girls' education).
Mission 3

Christian teaching on equality

  • Both sexes are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)
  • Jesus treated women as disciples and witnesses: Mary Magdalene first told the others he had risen (John 20:18).
Mission 3Diversity

Women's ministry in the churches

  • Catholic: only men can be ordained priests. John Paul II (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994): the Church has no authority to ordain women. Reasons: Jesus chose twelve men as apostles; the priest acts in the person of Christ.
  • Catholics stress this is about roles, not worth; women lead as religious sisters, teachers, theologians; since 2021 they can be formally installed as readers and acolytes.
  • Orthodox: male priesthood, for similar reasons of tradition.
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994, bishops since 2014 (first, Libby Lane, 2015); in 2025 Sarah Mullally was chosen as the first woman Archbishop of Canterbury.
  • Methodists, Salvation Army, URC, many Baptists: women ministers; the Salvation Army from its start (Catherine Booth).
  • Some evangelical churches: women lead in many ways but not as senior pastors (1 Timothy 2:12).

?A firm refuses to promote a woman because the manager believes "mothers aren't committed to their jobs". Which statement is accurate?
Mission 3Diversity

Islam

  • Equal reward for faith and good deeds: Qur'an 33:35 lists "men who surrender unto Allah, and women who surrender" through ten pairs (Qur'an 33:35, Pickthall).
  • Worship: men and women pray in separate rows or rooms; women may lead other women; most scholars teach only men lead mixed congregational prayer. Attending the mosque is a duty for men at Jumu'ah, optional for women.
  • Equality in action: Khadijah, successful businesswoman; Aisha, a leading scholar; Fatima al-Fihri founded al-Qarawiyyin in Fez (859), often called the oldest university still working.
  • Many Muslims say discrimination against women comes from culture, not Islam; groups such as Muslim Women's Network UK campaign for women's rights.
Mission 3Diversity

Judaism

  • Both made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Orthodox: separate seating (mechitzah); only men count for a minyan (prayer quorum of ten) and lead public prayer; rabbis are men. Women's learning and leadership roles are growing.
  • Reform and Liberal: mixed seating; women rabbis and cantors. First woman rabbi: Regina Jonas (Germany, 1935); in Britain, Jackie Tabick (1975).
Mission 3Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: the same atman (self) is in all, so all deserve equal regard. The Gita's true yogi "sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living" (Bhagavad Gita 6.29, Arnold).
  • God is worshipped as female too (Durga, Lakshmi, Saraswati). Women increasingly train as priests; some temples still limit women's roles.
  • Buddhists: women and men can both reach enlightenment; the Buddha founded an order of nuns.

Mission 4

What a religious upbringing involves

  • Rites at birth: baptism (Christian), the adhan whispered and aqiqah (Muslim), brit milah or a baby-naming (Jewish), naming in the gurdwara (Sikh), namakarana (Hindu).
  • At home: prayer, scripture stories, food rules, festivals.
  • In the community: worship, classes (Sunday school, madrassah, cheder), faith schools.
  • Coming of age: confirmation, bar/bat mitzvah; later, for Sikhs, taking Amrit.
  • Values: honesty, compassion, service.
  • Roles: in many traditions mothers lead faith at home (e.g. lighting Shabbat candles), while fathers often lead public worship; in others, both share the role.
  • Christian: both parents, with the godparents, make the baptism promises; most churches teach that mothers and fathers share the task.
  • Jewish: in Orthodox Judaism Jewish identity passes through the mother, who traditionally leads much home observance; the Talmud gives the father a duty to teach his children Torah (Kiddushin 29a, paraphrase).
  • Muslim: both parents are responsible; mothers often lead early teaching of prayer and the Qur'an at home, and fathers often take children to Friday prayer at the mosque, which is required of men.
  • Hindu and Buddhist: mothers often lead daily puja at the home shrine; the Buddhist Sigalovada Sutta gives mother and father the same five duties to their children.

Mission 4

A Christian upbringing and belief in God

  • At baptism, parents and godparents promise to bring the child up in the faith.
  • Children learn to pray, hear Bible stories, join worship, attend Sunday school or a church school.
  • First Communion and confirmation: the young person affirms the faith for themselves.

The ummah supports families

  • Worship: Jumu'ah prayer, Ramadan iftars, Eid.
  • Rites of passage: adhan at birth, aqiqah, nikah (marriage), funeral prayers.
  • Classes and groups: madrassah (Qur'an and Arabic), youth groups, parenting and marriage courses.

Mission 4Diversity

Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish and Sikh communities

  • Buddhists: the sangha is one of the Three Refuges: Buddhists go for refuge to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha (Khuddakapatha 1, paraphrase). Support: vihara worship, Wesak, blessing ceremonies, dhamma school, counselling.
  • Hindus: samskaras (namakarana, upanayana), mandir worship, festivals (Diwali), Bal Vihar classes, help from elders.
  • Jews: brit milah or naming, cheder, bar/bat mitzvah, Jewish schools, Shabbat hospitality, Jewish care charities.

Mission 5

Prejudice and discrimination

  • Prejudice: judging a person or group unfairly, usually before knowing them. It is an attitude (in the mind).
  • Discrimination: treating a person or group unfairly because of a prejudice. It is an action.
  • Equality: every person has the same worth and should have the same rights and chances.
  • Common types: race, religion, gender, sexuality, disability, age, class.
  • UK law: the Equality Act 2010 makes discrimination unlawful on grounds such as race, religion or belief, sex, sexual orientation and disability.
  • Most religions teach that every person has equal worth. Believers disagree over whether equal worth means identical roles, for example in who may lead worship.

Mission 5

Christian teaching and the Churches

  • "God doesn't show favouritism" (Acts 10:34). Showing partiality is a sin (James 2:9).
  • The Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37): a despised outsider is the true neighbour.
  • "Love one another. Just as I have loved you" (John 13:34): love that excludes no one.

Mission 5

Equality in Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Surah 49:13, Pickthall)
  • Men and women receive the same reward for faith and good deeds (Surah 33:35; 40:40).
  • Judaism: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
Mission 5

Equality in Buddhism, Hinduism and Sikhism

  • Buddhism: all beings can become enlightened. The Buddha taught that a person is noble or low by their deeds, not their birth (Vasala Sutta, Sutta Nipata 1.7, paraphrase).
  • Queen Mallika: when King Pasenadi was disappointed that she had a daughter, the Buddha said a daughter may prove even better than a son (Samyutta Nikaya 3.16, paraphrase).
  • Soma Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 5.2): the nun Soma answers Mara that being a woman is no obstacle to insight for one whose mind is well concentrated (paraphrase).
  • Hinduism: the same atman (eternal self) is in every being.
  • Rig Veda 10.90 (the Purusha Sukta): the four varnas come from one cosmic being. Some read this as a ranking; many Hindus read it as one body whose parts all need each other.
  • Sikhism: Guru Nanak taught: recognise God's light in everyone and do not ask about caste (Guru Granth Sahib 349, paraphrase). Langar: all sit and eat together; women may lead any part of worship.

Mission 5

The status of women

  • Most religions teach men and women have equal worth. They differ on whether that means the same roles.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Churches: only men are ordained priests, because Jesus chose twelve men as apostles. Women lead in many other roles (religious sisters, teachers, theologians).
  • Church of England: women priests since 1994; women bishops allowed since 2014.
  • Some evangelical churches teach male headship in church and family (1 Timothy 2:11–14); others, and Methodists, Baptists and Pentecostals in Britain, ordain women.
  • Islam: men and women are equal before God (Surah 33:35). Men and women usually pray in separate rows or rooms; women may lead other women in prayer.
  • Judaism: Orthodox Jews separate men and women in the synagogue and count only men in a minyan. Reform and Liberal Judaism have women rabbis.
  • Genesis 3:16 ("he shall rule over thee", JPS 1917) is read by many Jews as describing life after the first sin, not God's ideal; others use it to support different roles.
  • Buddhism: the Buddha founded an order of nuns (bhikkhunis), though with extra rules for nuns. Full nuns' ordination continues in many Mahayana lands; in Theravada it died out and its revival is debated.
  • Hinduism: the divine feminine (Shakti) is worshipped, and Shiva is shown as Ardhanarishvara, half male and half female. Manusmriti 5.147–148 places women under the protection of father, husband or son; many Hindus say this reflects ancient culture, and some groups (e.g. Arya Samaj) train women as priests.
  • Culture shapes practice: some inequalities come from local custom rather than scripture, and believers argue over which is which.
Mission 5Diversity

Difference or discrimination?

  • "Equal but different" (complementarian): men and women have equal worth but different God-given roles. Supporters say this is faithfulness to scripture and tradition, not prejudice.
  • "Equal and the same" (egalitarian): Galatians 3:28 means no role should be closed by gender. Supporters say limits on roles are discrimination.
  • On sexuality: some say the Church can welcome people while teaching that same-sex sexual acts are wrong; others say this still harms people.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists judge rules by their effect on human well-being and equal rights.
Quick check

Mission 5Evaluate

"Religion does more to fight prejudice than to cause it."

  • Reasons for: core teachings of equal worth (Genesis 1:27; Surah 49:13; Guru Granth Sahib 349); believers led campaigns against slavery, racism and caste; churches, mosques and gurdwaras serve everyone.
  • Reasons against: scripture has been used to justify slavery, caste and unequal treatment of women; some religious groups exclude women from leadership or reject same-sex relationships; religious prejudice (antisemitism, Islamophobia, sectarianism) still causes harm.
  • Archbishop Desmond Tutu fought apartheid and chaired South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–98), seeing every person as made in God's image.
  • Another view: prejudice comes from people, not from religion; the same is true of non-religious ideologies.
  • Judge it by: Should we judge a religion by its teachings or by its followers' actions? Is "different roles" a form of prejudice?
Mission check

?A religious community teaches that women and men have equal worth, but only men may lead its main act of worship. Two members disagree about whether this is discrimination. What is their disagreement mainly about?

Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination is an action. Religions teach equal worth, but they differ on whether equal worth means identical roles.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Divorce ends a marriage in law; annulment declares it was never valid. Catholics accept annulment, not remarriage after divorce; most other Christians allow remarriage.
  • Islam (talaq, iddah, khul'), Judaism (get, agunah) and, reluctantly, Dharmic traditions allow divorce as a last resort.

Mission 2

  • Equal in God's image (Genesis 1:27); Ephesians 5 read as headship (complementarian) or mutual submission (egalitarian).
  • Other faiths: Qur'an 2:228 and 4:34; Proverbs 31; Manusmriti 3.55–60; Sigalovada Sutta.

Mission 3

  • Prejudice is an attitude; discrimination an action; sex discrimination is illegal under the Equality Act 2010.
  • Christians: Genesis 1:27, Galatians 3:28; Catholic and Orthodox churches ordain only men; Anglicans and most Protestants ordain women.
  • Other faiths: Qur'an 33:35; Orthodox vs Reform Judaism; Gita 6.29; Buddhist nuns; practice does not always match teaching.

Mission 4

  • A religious upbringing: rites at birth, prayer at home, classes, worship, coming-of-age rites (Proverbs 22:6; Matthew 19:14).

Mission 5

  • Prejudice is an unfair attitude; discrimination is an unfair action. Religions teach the equal worth of all (imago Dei; Surah 49:13; God's light in all).
  • Religions differ over women's roles and same-sex relationships: "equal but different" or "equal and the same".
