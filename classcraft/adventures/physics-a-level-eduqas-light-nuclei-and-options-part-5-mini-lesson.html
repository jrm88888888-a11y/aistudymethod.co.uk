Mission 1

Mission 1: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Make a guess: An electron meets its antimatter twin and both vanish. What comes out, and why never just one thing?

Mission 1

Annihilation

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: same mass, opposite charge.
  • When a particle meets its antiparticle, they annihilate.
  • Their rest energy (mass) becomes the energy of photons: usually two gamma-ray photons.
  • e⁻ + e⁺ → γ + γ
  • Charge: −1 + 1 = 0 ✓ (photons are neutral).
  • Momentum: almost at rest, total momentum ≈ 0. One photon would carry momentum away, so there must be two, travelling in opposite directions.
  • Energy: total energy before = total photon energy after.
Quick check
?An electron and a positron annihilate, producing two photons. Which quantity is different after the annihilation from before it?
Mission 1

How much energy?

  • Slow electron and positron: each photon carries the rest energy of one electron: 0.511 MeV.
  • If the particles were moving, their kinetic energy adds to the photon energy.
Mission check

?An electron and a positron, each with 0.30 MeV of kinetic energy, collide head-on and annihilate into two photons. The rest energy of an electron is 0.511 MeV. What is the energy of each photon?

An electron and a positron vanish into two gamma photons that fly apart in opposite directions. One photon alone could not conserve momentum. Any kinetic energy the pair had goes into the photons too.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Make a guess: Every particle can be pushed and pulled by four kinds of force. How do you know which ones it feels?

Mission 2

Four fundamental interactions

  • Strong: acts on quarks, so on all hadrons. Range about 10⁻¹⁵ m, roughly a nucleon's diameter. Strongest.
  • Electromagnetic: acts on charged particles. Infinite range.
  • Weak: acts on all quarks and all leptons. Range about 10⁻¹⁸ m.
  • Gravitational: acts on all mass. Infinite range. By far the weakest.
  • Strength order: strong > electromagnetic > weak > gravitational.
  • Gravity matters only between large objects such as planets. The weak interaction is noticed only where the strong and electromagnetic ones cannot act (e.g. β decay).
Mission 2

Who feels what

  • Ask two questions: made of quarks? Charged?
  • Hadrons (made of quarks): strong and weak, plus gravity, and electromagnetic: even neutral hadrons, as their quarks are charged.
  • Leptons: weak, never strong, plus gravity. Electromagnetic too if charged.
  • Electron: gravity, electromagnetic, weak.
  • Neutrino: weak and gravity only. That is why neutrinos pass through the Earth.
Quick check
?Which of these particles never feels the strong interaction?
Mission check

?Which interactions does a negative pion, π⁻ (quark content dū), experience?

Two questions decide it. Made of quarks? Then it feels the strong interaction. Charged? Then it feels the electromagnetic one. All quarks and leptons feel the weak interaction and gravity.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Make a guess: A neutron left on its own soon decays. What changes inside it when it does?

Mission 3

The weak interaction changes quarks

  • Quarks come in types, called flavours, such as up and down.
  • Only the weak interaction can change a quark's flavour.
  • Only the weak interaction involves neutrinos.
  • So β decay is a weak interaction: a quark changes flavour and a neutrino or antineutrino is emitted.
Mission 3

β⁻ decay at quark level

  • In a nucleus, or on its own: a neutron becomes a proton.
  • n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • udd → uud: one down quark becomes an up quark.
  • d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Charge check: −⅓ = +⅔ + (−1) + 0 ✓
Mission 3

β⁺ decay at quark level

  • Only inside a nucleus: a proton becomes a neutron.
  • p → n + e⁺ + νₑ
  • uud → udd: one up quark becomes a down quark.
  • u → d + e⁺ + νₑ
  • Charge check: +⅔ = −⅓ + (+1) + 0 ✓
  • A free proton cannot do this: the products have more mass. The nucleus supplies the energy.
Quick check
?A carbon-11 nucleus (6 protons, 5 neutrons) holds 17 up quarks and 16 down quarks. It decays by β⁺ emission to boron-11. How many up and down quarks does the boron-11 nucleus hold?
Mission check

?In the Sun, the first step of fusion joins two protons into a deuterium nucleus (one proton and one neutron), and a positron and a neutrino are emitted. What happens at quark level, and which interaction is responsible?

One proton becomes a neutron (uud → udd) by the weak interaction, the same change as in β⁺ decay. The neutrino is the giveaway. This slow weak step is why the Sun burns for billions of years.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Make a guess: Charge and energy would both balance if a proton turned into a positron and light. So what forbids it?

Mission 4

The bookkeeping rules

  • Some quantities are the same before and after every interaction and decay.
  • Charge (in units of e): always conserved.
  • Also baryon number and lepton number.
  • Check each quantity separately: add up the left side, add up the right side.
Quick check
?Two protons collide at high energy: p + p → p + n + X, where X stands for all the other particles produced. What must the total charge of X be?
Mission 4

Baryon number, B

  • Quark +⅓, antiquark −⅓.
  • Baryons (qqq), e.g. p, n: B = +1. Antibaryons: B = −1.
  • Mesons (q q̄): B = 0. Leptons and photons: B = 0.
  • Total B is the same before and after.
  • Counting quarks works too: quark number = 3 × baryon number.
Mission 4

Lepton number, L

  • Leptons (e⁻, νₑ …): L = +1. Antileptons (e⁺, ν̄ₑ …): L = −1. Everything else: L = 0.
  • Total L is the same before and after.
Quick check
?A student proposes that a free neutron decays into a proton and an electron and nothing else: n → p + e⁻. Which conservation law that holds in all nuclear decays would this proposal break?
Mission 4

Worked example: allowed or forbidden?

  • Free neutron decay: n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ
  • Q: 0 = (+1) + (−1) + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 = 1 + 0 + 0 ✓
  • L: 0 = 0 + 1 + (−1) ✓ → allowed.
  • Proposed: p → e⁺ + γ
  • Q: +1 = +1 + 0 ✓
  • B: 1 → 0 ✗ · L: 0 → −1 ✗ → forbidden.
  • One broken law is enough to rule an interaction out.
Mission check

?Which of these proposed interactions is forbidden by a conservation law?

A proton turning into a positron and light keeps charge and energy balanced, but breaks baryon number (1 → 0) and lepton number (0 → −1). No one has ever seen it.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Make a guess: A helium nucleus is lighter than the protons and neutrons that make it. Where did the missing mass go?

Mission 5

Mass is a form of energy

  • E = mc²: every mass m has a rest energy mc².
  • c² is huge: 1 kg ↔ 9.0 × 10¹⁶ J.
  • Every energy change changes mass, chemical ones too; there Δm is far too small to measure.
  • Nuclear reactions: Δm is big enough to measure. The energy released leaves as kinetic energy of the products and as photons.
Mission 5

Units for tiny masses

  • Atomic mass unit: 1 u = 1/12 of the mass of a carbon-12 atom.
  • 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg.
  • 1 MeV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹³ J.
  • 1 u ↔ 931.5 MeV: so 1 u = 931.5 MeV c⁻².
  • A mass in MeV c⁻²: multiply by c² and you have the energy in MeV. No c needed.
Quick check
?Radium-226 decays by α emission: ²²⁶88Ra → ²²²86Rn + ⁴2α. The atomic masses are 226.025410 u for radium-226, 222.017578 u for radon-222 and 4.002603 u for helium-4 (the electron masses cancel). Take 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg = 931.5 MeV c⁻², 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in the decay of one radium-226 nucleus?
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement correctly describes the binding energy of a nucleus?
Mission 5

Missing mass and binding energy

  • A nucleus has less mass than its separate protons and neutrons.
  • Mass defect: Δm = (total mass of separate nucleons) − (mass of nucleus).
  • Binding energy = Δm c²: the energy needed to separate the nucleus completely into its nucleons.
  • The same energy was released when the nucleus formed. That is where the "missing" mass went.
  • Binding energy is not stored in the nucleus. More binding = less mass.
Mission 5

Worked example: helium-4

  • Data: mp = 1.007276 u, mn = 1.008665 u, helium-4 nucleus 4.001506 u.
  • Nucleons: 2(1.007276) + 2(1.008665) = 4.031882 u.
  • Δm = 4.031882 − 4.001506 = 0.030376 u.
  • Binding energy = 0.030376 × 931.5 = 28.30 MeV.
  • Per nucleon: 28.30 ÷ 4 = 7.07 MeV.
  • In SI: 0.030376 × 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ = 5.042 × 10⁻²⁹ kg; × (3.00 × 10⁸)² = 4.54 × 10⁻¹² J.
  • Use nuclear masses (or atomic masses on both sides, so the electrons cancel).
Mission 5

The binding energy per nucleon curve

  • Binding energy per nucleon = binding energy ÷ nucleon number A. Higher = more tightly bound = more stable.
  • Rises steeply for light nuclei (²H: 1.1 MeV), with a spike at ⁴He (7.1 MeV).
  • Peak near A ≈ 56–62 (iron, nickel): about 8.8 MeV.
  • Falls slowly for heavy nuclei: ²³⁸U about 7.6 MeV.
  • Energy is released when the products have more total binding energy than the reactants:
  • Fusion: light nuclei (left of the peak) join.
  • Fission: heavy nuclei (right of the peak) split.
  • Energy released = (total binding energy after) − (total binding energy before). Total = A × (per nucleon value).
Quick check
?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²³⁶92U 7.59 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.51 MeV; ¹⁴¹56Ba 8.33 MeV. A ²³⁶92U nucleus splits into a ⁹²36Kr nucleus, a ¹⁴¹56Ba nucleus and three free neutrons. What is the energy change?
Mission check

?In a fusion reaction, a helium-3 nucleus and a deuterium nucleus fuse: ³2He + ²1H → ⁴2He + ¹1H. The atomic masses are ³He: 3.016029 u, ²H: 2.014102 u, ⁴He: 4.002603 u and ¹H: 1.007825 u (the electron masses on the two sides cancel). How much energy is released in one reaction? Use 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.

The missing mass left as energy when the nucleus formed. For helium-4, 0.0304 u went out as 28.3 MeV, and the same 28.3 MeV is needed to pull it apart again.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Splitting a uranium nucleus gives out energy. How can breaking something apart release energy?

Make a guess: Splitting a uranium nucleus gives out energy. How can breaking something apart release energy?

Mission 6

Nuclear fission

  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei of comparable mass, plus a few neutrons (2 or 3 for uranium-235).
  • Spontaneous fission: no trigger (e.g. californium-252). Rare for most nuclei.
  • Induced fission: the nucleus absorbs a neutron first.
  • ²³⁵92U + ¹0n → ²³⁶92U → ¹⁴¹56Ba + ⁹²36Kr + 3 ¹0n
  • Balance it: nucleon numbers 235 + 1 = 141 + 92 + 3(1) = 236; proton numbers 92 = 56 + 36.
Quick check
?Some very heavy nuclides, such as californium-252, can undergo spontaneous fission, whereas uranium-235 in a reactor undergoes neutron-induced fission. Which statement about spontaneous fission is correct?
Mission 6

Where the energy comes from

  • Heavy nuclei sit right of the peak on the binding energy per nucleon curve: about 7.6 MeV.
  • The fragments sit nearer the peak: about 8.5 MeV. More tightly bound.
  • More binding → less total mass → the mass difference is released: E = Δmc².
  • About 200 MeV per fission, mostly as kinetic energy of the fragments; the rest as neutrons, γ photons and later decays.
Mission 6

Worked example: energy from one fission

  • ²³⁵U + n → ¹⁴¹Ba + ⁹²Kr + 3n. Masses (u): ²³⁵U 235.043928; ¹⁴¹Ba 140.914403; ⁹²Kr 91.926173; n 1.008665.
  • Before: 235.043928 + 1.008665 = 236.052593 u.
  • After: 140.914403 + 91.926173 + 3(1.008665) = 235.866571 u.
  • Δm = 0.186022 u → × 931.5 = 173 MeV (2.77 × 10⁻¹¹ J).
  • Count the neutrons on both sides. Atomic masses: electrons cancel.
  • 1 kg of uranium-235 (if every nucleus split, at about 200 MeV each): about 8 × 10¹³ J.
Quick check
?A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two fragments and 2 free neutrons. The fragments contain 234 nucleons in total. The binding energy per nucleon is 7.6 MeV for uranium-235 and about 8.5 MeV for the fragments. A free neutron has no binding energy. Roughly how much energy is released?
Mission check

?Plutonium-240 nuclei can split on their own into two fragments and two free neutrons. In one such split, the total binding energy rises from about 1810 MeV (the plutonium nucleus) to about 2010 MeV (the two fragments). How much energy is released, and why?

Breaking a heavy nucleus apart releases energy because the pieces are more tightly bound than the original. Their total mass is less, and the mass lost leaves as about 200 MeV per fission.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Joining nuclei releases energy, and so does splitting them. How can both be true?

Mission 7

Nuclear fusion

  • Fusion: two light nuclei join to form a heavier nucleus, often with a spare particle.
  • ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n + energy
  • Light nuclei sit on the steep left side of the binding energy per nucleon curve. Heavy nuclei sit on the gentle right side.
  • Fusion (from the left) and fission (from the right) both move nuclei towards the peak near iron: more tightly bound.
  • More binding → less total mass → energy released (E = Δmc²).
Quick check
?In the fusion reaction ²1H + ³1H → ⁴2He + ¹0n, the binding energies per nucleon are 1.11 MeV for ²1H, 2.83 MeV for ³1H and 7.07 MeV for ⁴2He. A free neutron has no binding energy. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Mission 7

Worked example: deuterium–deuterium fusion

  • Per nucleon, D–T fusion gives more: 17.6 MeV ÷ 5 = 3.5 MeV, against about 0.85 MeV for uranium fission.
  • ²1H + ²1H → ³2He + ¹0n. Atomic masses (u): ²H 2.014102; ³He 3.016029; n 1.008665.
  • Before: 2 × 2.014102 = 4.028204 u.
  • After: 3.016029 + 1.008665 = 4.024694 u.
  • Δm = 0.003510 u → × 931.5 = 3.27 MeV.
  • Electrons: 2 on each side, so atomic masses are fine.
Quick check
?One reaction in the Sun's core is ³He + ³He → ⁴He + 2 ¹H. The atomic masses are 3.016029 u for helium-3, 4.002603 u for helium-4 and 1.007825 u for hydrogen-1 (the electron masses balance). Take 1 u = 1.66 × 10⁻²⁷ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. How much energy is released in one reaction?
Mission check

?Approximate binding energies per nucleon are: ²1H 1.1 MeV; ⁴2He 7.1 MeV; ²⁰10Ne 8.0 MeV; ²⁸13Al 8.3 MeV; ⁴⁰20Ca 8.6 MeV; ⁵⁶26Fe 8.8 MeV; ⁹²36Kr 8.5 MeV; ¹⁴⁴56Ba 8.3 MeV; ²³⁶92U 7.6 MeV. Which pair of processes would both release energy?

In both fusion and fission the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less: E = Δmc². Both processes move nuclei towards the most tightly bound size, near iron. Light nuclei fuse up to it; heavy nuclei split down to it. Either way the total mass falls, and that mass leaves as energy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Annihilation: particle + antiparticle → two photons; charge, energy and momentum are conserved.

Mission 2

  • Four interactions: strong (quarks, hadrons; ~10⁻¹⁵ m), electromagnetic (charges; infinite), weak (all quarks and leptons; ~10⁻¹⁸ m), gravitational (all mass; infinite).
  • Hadrons feel strong and weak; leptons feel weak but never strong.

Mission 3

  • Only the weak interaction changes quark flavour or involves neutrinos.
  • β⁻: d → u + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (a neutron becomes a proton). β⁺: u → d + e⁺ + νₑ (a proton becomes a neutron, only in a nucleus).

Mission 4

  • Check each conserved quantity separately; one failure forbids the interaction.
  • Charge, baryon number and lepton number are conserved in every interaction.

Mission 5

  • Every energy change changes mass: E = mc². Nuclear reactions release energy because the products have less mass.
  • Binding energy = mass defect × c², the energy needed to separate a nucleus into its nucleons; more binding means less mass.
  • Binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron: fusing light nuclei and splitting heavy nuclei both release energy.

Mission 6

  • Fission: a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei plus neutrons; spontaneous or neutron-induced.
  • Energy is released because the fragments are more tightly bound: less total mass, E = Δmc², about 200 MeV per fission.

Mission 7

  • Fusion: light nuclei join; the products are more tightly bound, so their total mass is less and energy is released.
  • Fusion (light nuclei) and fission (heavy nuclei) both move towards the peak of the binding energy per nucleon curve near iron.
