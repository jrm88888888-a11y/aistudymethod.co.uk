Measuring population

Birth rate, death rate & natural change

Population change is measured with rates per 1,000 people per year so countries of different sizes can be compared:

  • Crude birth rate — the number of live births per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Crude death rate — the number of deaths per 1,000 people in a year.
  • Natural change — birth rate minus death rate. If births exceed deaths there is natural increase; if deaths exceed births there is natural decrease.

Natural change ignores migration — it only counts births and deaths already in a country.

Demographic Transition Model

The 5 stages of the DTM

The Demographic Transition Model (DTM) shows how birth and death rates — and therefore total population — change as a country develops through five stages:

  • Stage 1 — High fluctuating: high birth rate, high death rate, population low and stable.
  • Stage 2 — Early expanding: death rate falls (better food, water, medicine) while birth rate stays high — population grows rapidly.
  • Stage 3 — Late expanding: birth rate falls (contraception, women working, fewer children needed) — growth slows.
  • Stage 4 — Low fluctuating: low birth rate, low death rate, population high and stable.
  • Stage 5 — Declining: birth rate drops below death rate — natural decrease and an ageing population.

Population pyramids

Reading a population pyramid

A population pyramid shows the number of males and females in each age group. Its shape tells you about a country's stage of development:

  • LEDC pyramid — wide base (high birth rate, many children) and narrow top (short life expectancy). This creates youth dependency.
  • MEDC pyramid — narrow base (low birth rate) and a wide top (long life expectancy). This creates aged dependency.
Dependency & implications

What dependency means

The dependent population is under 15 or over 64 — too young or too old to work. The working population (15–64) supports them. The dependency ratio compares the two.

  • Aged dependency (MEDC): higher pension and healthcare costs, pressure on the working population, but skilled experienced workers and possible labour shortages.
  • Youth dependency (LEDC): high spending on schools, healthcare and food for children; but a large future workforce once they grow up.

Formula: dependency ratio = (dependent population ÷ working-age population) × 100.

Consequences of migration

Effects on both areas

Migration affects the place people leave (the source) and the place they arrive (the host):

  • Source area: loses young workers (a "brain drain"), but gains money sent home (remittances) and less pressure on jobs.
  • Host area: gains workers to fill labour shortages and a richer mix of cultures, but may face pressure on housing, schools and services.
Migration

Push & pull factors

Migration is the movement of people to live in a new place. It is driven by two forces:

  • Push factors drive people away from an area: war, unemployment, poverty, natural disasters, persecution, poor services.
  • Pull factors attract people to an area: jobs, higher wages, safety, better schools and hospitals, joining family.

Barriers can block migration: physical barriers such as mountains, deserts or seas, and human barriers such as visa rules, border controls, cost and language.

Types of migrant

Economic migrant vs refugee

The reason for moving decides the legal category:

  • Economic migrant — moves by choice, mainly to find work or a higher standard of living. This is voluntary migration.
  • Refugee — forced to flee their country to escape war, persecution or disaster and cannot safely return. An asylum seeker is someone who has applied for refugee status but is awaiting a decision.

Case study flag: CCEA uses an example such as Syrian refugees arriving in Greece. Check any specific numbers of arrivals, dates or routes against your class notes before quoting them — do not rely on memory for the figures.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Rates: birth & death rate per 1,000; natural change = births − deaths

DTM: 5 stages — death rate falls first (Stage 2), then birth rate (Stage 3)

Pyramids: LEDC wide base = youth dependency; MEDC wide top = aged dependency

Dependency ratio: (dependents ÷ working age) × 100
