Mission 1

Mission 1: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Make a guess: At today's rate, Niger's population would double in about 21 years. Japan's is shrinking. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2A.1 define crude birth rate, crude death rate and natural change (increase and decrease)
  • 2A.1 five stages of the DTM: changing birth rates, death rates and population change
  • 2A.1 compare pyramids of an MEDC (aged dependent) and an LEDC (youth dependent)
Mission 1

Births, deaths and natural change

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths. Natural decrease: deaths exceed births.
  • Natural increase rate (% a year) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10.
  • Together these are natural change.

Quick check
?Use Niger's birth and death rates. What is its natural increase rate?
Mission 1

Fertility and replacement

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's rates.
  • Replacement level is about 2.1: "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
  • World TFR, 2024: 2.25. Niger 5.94; Japan 1.22.
  • At 3.27% a year, Niger doubles in about 70 ÷ 3.27 ≈ 21 years (the "rule of 70").
Mission 1

The demographic transition model (DTM)

  • Stage 1: high birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 2: death rates fall (food, clean water, vaccination); births stay high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3: birth rates fall (fewer child deaths, education, contraception, cities); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 5: births below deaths; the population falls.
Mission 1

The DTM and development

  • Stage tends to rise with development: low-income countries in stages 2–3, high-income countries in stages 4–5.
  • Niger (low income) fits late stage 2: deaths low, births still very high.
  • Japan (high income) fits stage 5: deaths (12.4) exceed births (6.1).
Quick check
?A country has a birth rate of 28 per 1,000, a death rate of 6, and its birth rate has fallen for 20 years. Which stage fits best?
Mission 1

Population pyramids

  • Bars show % of the population in each 5-year age group; males left, females right.
  • Niger: wide base. 46.6% under 15; 2.6% aged 65+. Youth dependent.
  • Japan: narrow base, wide top. 11.4% under 15; 29.8% aged 65+. Aged dependent.
Quick check
?A pyramid has a narrow base and wide bars above age 65. What does it show?
Mission 1

Putting it together

  • Niger: many births, a young population, few deaths → rapid natural increase.
  • Japan: few births, many older people, more deaths → natural decrease.
  • Age structure matters: a young population has few deaths even where health care is weaker.
Mission check

?Bangladesh: birth rate 48.2 and death rate 17.1 in 1972; 20.0 and 5.1 in 2024. Why has its growth slowed?

(48.2 − 17.1) ÷ 10 = 3.11% in 1972; (20.0 − 5.1) ÷ 10 = 1.49% in 2024. The gap between births and deaths, shaped by fertility and age structure, decides growth.

Mission 2

Mission 2: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Make a guess: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2A.1 assess social and economic implications of aged and youth dependency
Mission 2

Key terms

  • Anti-natalist policy: tries to lower the birth rate.
  • Pro-natalist policy: tries to raise the birth rate.
  • Youth dependency: many children (0–14) relying on people of working age (15–64).
  • Aged dependency: many older people (65+) relying on people of working age.
  • Dependency ratio = (people aged 0–14 + 65+) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100.
Mission 2

China's anti-natalist policy

  • A public letter of 25 September 1980 is often cited as the one-child policy's "official" start.
  • But fertility had already fallen: 6.09 children per woman (1970) → 2.75 (1979).
  • By 2000 it was 1.63, well below replacement (about 2.1).
  • The 1970s "Longer, Later, Fewer" campaign (later marriage, longer gaps, fewer children) came first: researchers estimate it cut fertility by about 0.88 children per woman.
  • So the one-child policy added to a fall already under way.
Mission 2

Impacts of the one-child policy

  • Fewer births, faster: fertility stayed below 2.1 from the 1990s.
  • More boys than girls: China's sex ratio at birth reached 117 boys per 100 girls in 2000; the world figure is about 105.
  • Ageing: people aged 65+ rose from 4.4% (1980) to 14.7% (2024) of China's population, and are projected to reach 30.9% by 2050.
  • Fewer young workers to support each older person.
Quick check
?Which evidence shows that China's fertility was falling before the one-child policy?
Mission 2

The U-turn: pro-natalist China

  • 2016: two children for all couples; the law was revised in 2015 "when China's two-child policy was fully implemented".
  • 2021: the law changed "to permit all couples to have three children".
  • Why: "the fertility rate in 2020 dropped to 1.3 children per woman"; the shift "addresses the challenges of a graying populace and declining fertility rates".
Mission 2

Did the U-turn work?

  • Births rose from 17.5 million (2015) to 18.3 million (2016), then fell to 8.8 million (2024).
  • 2025: 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths; population 1,404.89 million, down 3.39 million in a year.
  • Fertility was 1.01 in 2024.
  • So the two-child rule gave a brief rise; it did not reverse the fall.
Mission 2

Youth dependency: implications

  • Niger, 2024: 46.6% of people are under 15: about 12.6 million children (our calculation).
  • Social: many more school places, teachers, vaccinations and maternity care are needed.
  • Economic: about 92 children for every 100 people of working age (our calculation); little left to save or invest; jobs needed as they grow up.
  • Opportunity: if fertility falls and these children get skills and jobs, a large workforce can boost growth.
Mission 2

Aged dependency: implications

  • Japan, 2024: 29.8% of people are aged 65+; about 51 older people for every 100 of working age (our calculation).
  • Social: more demand for health and long-term care; WHO says "All countries face major challenges to ensure that their health and social systems are ready".
  • Economic: more spent on pensions and care; fewer workers and taxpayers.
  • Opportunity: older people work, volunteer, care for grandchildren and spend.
Quick check
?Which is an economic implication of aged dependency?
Mission check

?A country's fertility rate is 0.8. Its government offers cash for each new baby. What is most likely after five years?

China turned pro-natalist because fertility fell far below replacement (1.3 in 2020) and the population aged; deaths now exceed births. Policies can nudge the timing of births, but rarely reverse very low fertility.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Some move abroad for a better job; others flee for their lives. What pushes people, and what pulls them?

Make a guess: Some move abroad for a better job; others flee for their lives. What pushes people, and what pulls them?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2A.2 push and pull factors leading to migration
  • 2A.2 barriers to migration: human (e.g. visas) and physical (e.g. topography)
  • 2A.2 distinguish between an economic migrant and a refugee
Mission 3

Moving across borders

  • International migration: moving away from where you usually live "and across an international border".
  • Emigration: leaving a country (seen from the country left behind).
  • Immigration: arriving in a new country to live (seen from the country of arrival).
  • The country people arrive in is the destination country.
Mission 3

Who is who?

  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or higher pay. This is voluntary: the person chooses to go.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for their race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion. This is forced migration.
Quick check
?Amira leaves her country because she is threatened with prison for her political views. She is granted protection in another country. What is she?
Mission 3

Push and pull

  • Push factors drive people away: few jobs, low wages, poor schools or healthcare, conflict, persecution, natural disasters.
  • Pull factors draw people towards a place: jobs, higher wages, safety, family already there, education, healthcare.
Mission 3

Example: Indians working abroad

  • In 2024 the UK was home to about 1.04 million people born in India.
  • Indian migrants abroad send money home (remittances): about $0.43 billion in 1975, about $150.7 billion in 2025. That is roughly 350 times as much in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation (our calculation).
  • Pull: jobs and higher pay abroad. Push: fewer well-paid jobs at home. Family networks make the move easier.
Quick check
?Which of these is a pull factor?
Mission 3

Human barriers

  • A visa is official permission written into a passport letting a non-citizen enter or stay.
  • Since 1 January 2021 the UK has used a points-based system for work visas, treating EU and non-EU citizens equally.
  • In December 2023 the government announced that from spring 2024 the salary needed by overseas workers would rise by nearly 50%, from £26,200 to £38,700.
  • Other human barriers: cost of travel, language, leaving family behind.
Mission 3

Physical barriers

  • Topography: high mountains, wide deserts and seas make journeys long, costly and dangerous.
  • The Mediterranean Sea is a dangerous crossing into Europe: IOM's records, to October 2024, count 30,522 migrants dead or missing there.
  • Distance and harsh climate are physical barriers too.
Quick check
?Which is a physical barrier to migration?
Mission check

?Maria, a nurse, leaves the Philippines for a hospital job in the UK at higher pay. She sends money home every month. Which statement is correct?

Economic migrants move by choice, pulled by jobs and pay; refugees are forced out by persecution. Both change their origin and destination countries.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Make a guess: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2A.2 challenges faced by refugees and by the destination country, using one case study
Mission 4

Who is who

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but has "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
Quick check
?A family flees fighting and crosses into a neighbouring country, where they ask for protection. Their claim has not yet been decided. What are they?
Mission 4

The Rohingya: long-term causes

  • Origin: northern Rakhine State, Myanmar. Destination: Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh.
  • A UN fact-finding mission found: "Most Rohingya have become de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • Their travel "has long been restricted", affecting rights "to food, health and education".
  • These conditions left them vulnerable before 2017.
Mission 4

The trigger

  • 25 August 2017: an armed group (ARSA) attacked a military base and "up to 30 security force outposts".
  • Security forces responded with what the authorities called "clearance operations".
  • The UN mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
  • Push: persecution and violence. Pull: safety across the nearest border.
Mission 4

Challenges for the refugees

  • 31 October 2026: 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh.
  • Most live in "33 highly congested camps in Cox's Bazar District".
  • They depend on aid: the 2025 response plan required US$455.6 million.
  • Safe from violence, but crowded camps, dependence on aid and no clear way home.
Mission 4

Challenges for Bangladesh, the destination

  • Scale and time: about 1.2 million refugees at the end of 2025; the UN found returns "not possible" in 2018.
  • Cost and land: aid must be raised every year, and camps are so crowded that "some 35,000 refugees relocated to Bhasan Char", an island.
  • Host communities: the 2025–26 response plan covers the needs of "Rohingya refugees and host communities".
  • Host area (Cox's Bazar), UNDP 2018:
    • about 4,818 acres of forest reserve destroyed (Forest Department estimate)
    • wages for unskilled work in Teknaf and Ukhiya depressed by about 20%, because Rohingya work for less
    • so poverty rose by 2.73 (Teknaf) and 2.63 (Ukhiya) percentage points.
  • Law: Bangladesh is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention (146 states are).
Quick check
?Which is a challenge for Bangladesh, the destination country, rather than for the refugees themselves?
Mission check

?By the end of 2025, 2.8 million people had fled Sudan to other countries, and 9.1 million were displaced inside Sudan. Which statement is correct?

The Rohingya fled persecution and violence (a political push) to the nearest safety. Bangladesh now hosts 1,208,018 refugees (31 Oct 2026), mostly in crowded camps funded by aid: a long-term challenge for refugees and host alike.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Natural change = births − deaths. Niger: 3.27% a year, doubling in about 21 years. Japan: −0.63%.
  • DTM: Niger late stage 2 (low income); Japan stage 5 (high income).
  • Pyramids: wide base (youth dependent) vs narrow base and wide top (aged dependent).

Mission 2

  • China: anti-natalist (1980) → two children (2016) → three (2021). Fertility had fallen before 1980; now 1.01 (2024).
  • Youth dependency (Niger): schools, health, jobs. Aged dependency (Japan): care, pensions, fewer workers.

Mission 3

  • Migration is caused by push and pull factors; economic migrants choose to move, refugees are forced to flee persecution.
  • Barriers: human (visas, salary rules, cost) and physical (mountains, deserts, seas).

Mission 4

  • Refugees cross a border fleeing persecution; IDPs do not. Rohingya: statelessness, then the 2017 "clearance operations".
  • Challenges: congested, aid-dependent camps (refugees); land, money and time (Bangladesh).
