Mission 1

Mission 1: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Make a guess: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Mission 1

Inside a synapse

  • A synapse is a junction between a neurone and another cell.
  • Presynaptic side: the axon terminal, the synaptic knob.
  • It holds vesicles of neurotransmitter and many mitochondria.
  • The synaptic cleft: a gap of about 20 nm.
  • Postsynaptic membrane: carries specific receptor proteins.
  • At a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
Mission 1

Transmission, step by step

  • (1) An action potential depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
  • (2) Calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • (3) Ca²⁺ is a signal inside the neurone: vesicles move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. ACh is released by exocytosis.
  • (4) ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to its complementary receptors.
  • (5) The receptors open Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation: an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  • If the depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential starts in the postsynaptic neurone.
  • Say: "ACh binds to receptors with a complementary shape" — not: "ACh enters the next neurone".
Quick check
?What is the role of calcium ions in the release of neurotransmitter from a presynaptic neurone?
Mission 1

Clearing the cleft

  • The signal must stop, or the postsynaptic cell would keep firing.
  • Neurotransmitter is removed: broken down, or taken back into the presynaptic neurone (reuptake).
Mission 1

One-way traffic

  • Signals cross a synapse in one direction only: presynaptic → postsynaptic.
  • Why: vesicles of neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side.
  • And receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
Quick check
?Why can a signal pass in only one direction across a synapse?
Mission 1

A receptor that is also a channel

  • The nicotinic acetylcholine receptor is a transmembrane protein with a pore.
  • ACh binds → the receptor changes shape → the pore opens.
  • Positive ions (mainly Na⁺) diffuse in → membrane potential becomes less negative.
  • It is a neurotransmitter-gated ion channel.
  • Compare: voltage-gated Na⁺ and K⁺ channels open in response to a change in membrane potential.
  • ACh stays outside the cell; only ions pass through.
Quick check
?A muscle fibre has a resting membrane potential of -70 mV. When acetylcholine is applied, the potential changes to -50 mV within 1 ms. If sodium ions are first removed from the fluid outside the fibre, acetylcholine still binds but the potential stays at -70 mV (illustrative data). What do these results show?
Mission 1

Neurone to muscle

  • A synapse can link a neurone to an effector cell, such as a muscle fibre.
  • Motor neurone → skeletal muscle fibre: the neuromuscular junction.
  • Its neurotransmitter is acetylcholine: the same one used at many synapses between neurones.
  • ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fibre membrane → depolarisation → the fibre contracts.
Quick check
?A motor neurone forms a neuromuscular junction with a skeletal muscle fibre. Which statement about acetylcholine at this junction is correct?
Mission check

?Calcium ions were removed from the fluid around a synapse. When the presynaptic neurone was stimulated, action potentials still reached the presynaptic terminal, but no response was recorded in the postsynaptic neurone. What does this result show?

The gap is crossed by a chemical, and the chemical is released only when calcium tells the vesicles to go.

Mission 2

Mission 2: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Make a guess: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Mission 2

Adding up: summation

  • One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
  • Spatial summation: several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter at the same time → their EPSPs add.
  • Total reaches threshold → action potential. Below threshold → none (all-or-nothing).
Mission 2

Brakes: inhibitory synapses

  • An inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g. GABA) opens channels that let Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out.
  • The inside becomes more negative than at rest: hyperpolarised.
  • This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).
  • The membrane is now further from threshold: an action potential is much less likely.
  • EPSPs and IPSPs act on the same membrane, so they sum: excitation minus inhibition.
  • Say: "an IPSP hyperpolarises the membrane" — not: "an inhibitory synapse is just a weaker excitatory one".
Quick check
?A postsynaptic neurone has a resting potential of −70 mV and a threshold of −55 mV. At the same moment it receives two excitatory inputs, each producing a +8 mV change, and one inhibitory input producing a −6 mV change. What is the result?
Mission 2

Motor units

  • A motor unit: one motor neuron + all the muscle fibres its branching axon supplies.
  • Each branch ends at a neuromuscular junction.
  • When the neuron fires, all its fibres contract together.
  • More force: recruit more motor units.
  • Small motor units (few fibres each) → fine control. Large units → big, powerful steps.
Quick check
?What is a motor unit in skeletal muscle?
Mission 2

Drugs at synapses: find the step

  • Ask: which step does the drug change? Release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
  • Same shape as the neurotransmitter → binds and activates receptors → more stimulation.
  • Binds and blocks receptors → less stimulation.
  • Inhibits the breakdown enzyme or blocks reuptake → neurotransmitter stays in the cleft → stimulation lasts longer.
  • Cocaine blocks reuptake of dopamine.
  • Neonicotinoids (insecticides) bind to insect ACh receptors and are not broken down: the receptors stop responding, transmission is blocked, the insect is paralysed.
Quick check
?Cocaine binds to the transporter proteins that return dopamine to the presynaptic neuron. What effect does this have at a dopamine synapse?
Mission 2

Many kinds of neurotransmitter

  • Amino acids: glutamate, GABA, glycine.
  • Amines: dopamine, serotonin, noradrenaline.
  • Peptides: e.g. endorphins.
  • Also acetylcholine, and a gas, nitric oxide.
  • Your guide words this as "nitrous oxide". The gas neurotransmitter is nitric oxide (NO); nitrous oxide (N2O) is an anaesthetic.
  • Why so many? Each binds only to its own specific receptors, so many signals can work side by side.
  • Different chemicals suit different speeds, durations and effects.
Quick check
?Why is it advantageous for animals to use a wide range of chemically different substances as signalling chemicals rather than a single one?
Mission check

?A drug binds to GABA receptors on a postsynaptic neurone. When GABA is also bound, the drug makes the receptor's chloride ion channel open more often. What is the effect on the postsynaptic neurone?

A neurone fires only when its accelerators outweigh its brakes; a drug that strengthens the brakes quietens it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Make a guess: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Mission 3

A motor neurone

  • Cell body: nucleus and most of the cytoplasm.
  • Dendrites: many short fibres; carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: one long fibre; carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Axon terminals end at an effector (a muscle or gland).
Mission 3

Myelin

  • Many axons are wrapped in a myelin sheath.
  • Myelin is lipid-rich: an electrical insulator.
  • Gaps between sheath sections: nodes of Ranvier.
  • Non-myelinated axons have no sheath and no nodes.
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a neurone is correct?
Mission 3

A nerve is not a neuron

  • A nerve is a bundle of nerve fibres from many neurons.
  • Most nerves carry fibres of both sensory and motor neurons.
  • Transverse section: outer protective sheath of connective tissue.
  • Inside: myelinated fibres (axon ringed by pale myelin) and unmyelinated fibres (bare axons).
  • Say: "a nerve contains many neurons' fibres" — not: "a nerve is a long neuron".
Quick check
?A transverse section of a nerve shows an outer layer of connective tissue enclosing many small circular profiles. Some profiles have a thick pale ring around a central dot; others have no ring. What does the section show?
Mission 3

The resting potential

  • At rest, the inside of the axon is negative relative to the outside.
  • Typical value: about −70 mV. This is the resting potential.
  • A membrane with a charge difference across it is polarised.
  • Membrane potential = voltage inside relative to outside.
  • The neurone is not "off": it is held ready to fire.
Mission 3

How the resting potential is made

  • Sodium–potassium pump: uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
  • Result: Na⁺ high outside; K⁺ high inside. Two concentration gradients.
  • The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺: many K⁺ channels are open; most Na⁺ channels are closed.
  • So K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ diffuses in → more positive charge outside.
  • Large negatively charged proteins stay inside.
  • Say: "Na⁺ is actively transported out" — not: "Na⁺ diffuses out".
Mission 3

The pump: an exchange transporter

  • (1) 3 Na⁺ bind on the cytoplasm side.
  • (2) ATP phosphorylates the pump → shape change → 3 Na⁺ released outside.
  • (3) 2 K⁺ bind on the outside → phosphate released.
  • (4) Pump returns to its original shape → 2 K⁺ released inside.
  • Net: one positive charge out per cycle. With the gradients it builds, this generates the membrane potential.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes one cycle of the sodium–potassium pump?
Quick check
?Measurements on a resting neurone show that potassium ions are about 30 times more concentrated inside than outside and sodium ions about 10 times more concentrated outside than inside, yet the inside is at −70 mV relative to the outside. Which explanation of the negative resting potential is consistent with these measurements?
Mission check

?A neurone is treated with a chemical that stops it producing ATP. Which prediction about its resting potential is correct?

Leaks never stop, so the pump never can: that is the energy cost of being ready.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Make a guess: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Mission 4

A tube through the body

  • Animals are heterotrophs: they get carbon compounds from other organisms.
  • Holozoic nutrition: food is taken in and digested inside the body.
  • Four stages: ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation. Undigested waste is egested.
  • The gut is a tube open at both ends. Its lumen is still outside the body's cells.
  • Digestion: enzymes hydrolyse large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones.
  • Digestion in the gut is extracellular: enzymes are secreted and act in the lumen. Glycolysis and the Krebs cycle are intracellular.
  • Absorption: those small molecules cross the gut lining into the blood or lymph. Only now are they inside.
  • Assimilation: cells build the absorbed molecules into the animal's own molecules and tissues.
Quick check
?A dog eats meat. At which stage are the amino acids from the meat first inside the dog's body?
Quick check
?Which statement about intracellular and extracellular enzyme-catalysed reactions is correct?
Mission 4

Absorbing glucose and amino acids

  • Recap, in an epithelial cell of the ileum:
  • (1) The Na⁺/K⁺ pump on the blood side uses ATP to keep Na⁺ low inside the cell.
  • (2) A co-transporter in the microvillus membrane binds Na⁺ and glucose. Na⁺ moves in down its gradient and carries glucose in against its gradient.
  • (3) Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • The protein is a sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter: indirect active transport. ATP is used by the pump, not the cotransporter.
  • Sodium-dependent glucose cotransporters also reabsorb glucose in the nephron.
Quick check
?How do epithelial cells of the ileum absorb glucose from the lumen when the glucose concentration inside the cells is already higher than in the lumen?
Mission 4

Moving food along

  • Peristalsis: a wave of contraction of the circular muscle behind the food, relaxation in front. It pushes food one way.
  • It works without gravity: you can swallow upside down.
  • Starting a swallow is voluntary: controlled by the CNS (brain and spinal cord).
  • Egestion of faeces is also under voluntary CNS control.
  • Between these points, peristalsis is involuntary: the enteric nervous system (ENS) in the gut wall coordinates it.
Quick check
?A person swallows a mouthful of food. Which statement correctly describes how the movement of this food through the digestive system is controlled?
Mission check

?A person eats celery. Humans make no enzyme that digests cellulose. One cellulose fibre from the celery passes along the whole gut and leaves in the faeces. Which statement about this fibre is correct?

A meal gets inside you only when its molecules cross the gut lining. Before that, it is still in a tube that runs through you.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Make a guess: Your kidneys filter all of your blood plasma about sixty times a day. Why don't you run dry?

Mission 5

Two jobs

  • Excretion: removing the waste products of metabolism, such as urea (made in the liver from excess amino acids).
  • Osmoregulation: controlling the osmotic concentration of the blood, in osmol L⁻¹ (plasma: about 0.3 osmol L⁻¹).
  • The kidney does both, but they are separate jobs.
  • Faeces are not excreted: undigested food never entered metabolism. Removing it is egestion.
  • Say: "excretion removes metabolic waste" — not: "excretion removes anything the body doesn't need".
Mission 5

The kidney, outside and in

  • Blood in: renal artery. Blood out: renal vein.
  • Outer cortex. Inner medulla, divided into cone-shaped pyramids.
  • Urine drains into the pelvis, then down the ureter to the bladder, and out through the urethra.
  • Each kidney holds about a million nephrons: the working units.
Mission 5

One nephron

  • Bowman's capsule cups a knot of capillaries, the glomerulus. Both sit in the cortex.
  • Then: proximal convoluted tubule (PCT) → loop of Henle (dips into the medulla) → distal convoluted tubule (DCT) → collecting duct.
  • Blood: afferent arteriole → glomerulus → efferent arteriole → capillaries around the tubules.
  • Fluid entering the capsule is glomerular filtrate. What reaches the end of the collecting duct is urine.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes osmoregulation from excretion?
Mission 5

Ultrafiltration

  • The afferent arteriole is wider than the efferent arteriole → high hydrostatic pressure in the glomerulus.
  • This pressure forces fluid out through a three-layer filter: capillary endothelium (with pores), basement membrane, and podocytes in the capsule wall.
  • The filter sorts by size. Through: water, glucose, amino acids, urea, ions.
  • Stay in the blood: blood cells, platelets and plasma proteins (too large).
  • So filtrate = plasma minus its proteins. An adult makes about 180 dm³ a day.
  • Say: "filtered by size, under pressure" — not: "the glomerulus picks out the waste".
Quick check
?Which statement about ultrafiltration in the glomerulus is correct?
Mission 5

Selective reabsorption in the PCT

  • 1: Na⁺–K⁺ pumps in the cell membrane facing the blood use ATP to pump Na⁺ out of the cell. Na⁺ inside stays low.
  • 2: Na⁺ diffuses into the cell from the filtrate through co-transporter proteins, carrying glucose (or amino acids) with it, against glucose's own gradient.
  • 3: Glucose leaves the cell into the blood by facilitated diffusion.
  • Solutes moving into the blood lower its water potential, so water follows by osmosis.
  • In the PCT: all glucose and amino acids, and about two-thirds of the water and Na⁺, are reabsorbed.
  • This is indirect active transport: ATP is used by the pump, not by the sodium-dependent glucose cotransporter.
  • Say: "glucose is co-transported with sodium ions" — not: "glucose diffuses back into the blood".
Quick check
?A drug stops the sodium–potassium pumps in the cells lining the proximal convoluted tubule. What happens to the reabsorption of glucose from the filtrate?
Mission 5

PCT cells: built for reabsorption

  • Microvilli (a brush border) on the side facing the filtrate.
  • Basal invaginations (infoldings) on the side facing the blood.
  • Both add membrane without adding volume → high surface area to volume ratio and room for many co-transporters and pumps.
  • Many mitochondria: ATP for the Na⁺–K⁺ pumps.
  • Close to capillaries: short diffusion distance to the blood.

Mission check

?Fluid was sampled from Bowman's capsule and from the end of the proximal convoluted tubule of a healthy kidney. Glucose was 5 mmol L⁻¹ in the capsule and undetectable at the end of the tubule. Urea was 5 mmol L⁻¹ in the capsule and higher at the end of the tubule. Which explanation is correct?

Filter everything small, then take back what is useful. That is how 180 dm³ of filtrate a day becomes about 1.5 dm³ of urine.

Mission 6

Mission 6: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Make a guess: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Mission 6

Why take cells apart?

  • A microscope shows what an organelle looks like. To find out what it does, you need it on its own, in quantity.
  • Cell fractionation: break cells open, then separate their organelles into fractions.
  • Isolated organelles keep working: e.g. isolated mitochondria use oxygen and make ATP.
  • An organelle is a discrete subunit of a cell, adapted to a function. Nuclei, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane count; the cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm do not.
  • Science often advances when a new technique appears: organelle function could be studied only once ultracentrifuges and fractionation methods existed.
Mission 6

Step 2: ultracentrifugation

  • First, cells are broken open in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution: enzymes are slowed so they cannot digest the organelles, organelles do not burst or shrink by osmosis, and pH stays constant so proteins are not denatured. The homogenate is filtered.
  • Spin the filtered homogenate in an ultracentrifuge.
  • The largest, densest organelles settle first, into a pellet at the bottom.
  • The liquid above, the supernatant, is poured into a fresh tube and spun faster and longer.
  • Repeat. Each spin brings down smaller, less dense organelles.
  • This is differential centrifugation.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly applies the term organelle as it is used in IB Biology?
Mission check

?Liver cells were broken open in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution and the homogenate was spun in an ultracentrifuge at a series of increasing speeds. Only the pellet formed at the second speed consumed oxygen and produced ATP when supplied with substrates. What does this investigation illustrate about how progress in science is made?

Keep the organelles cold, isotonic and buffered, then spin at rising speeds: each fraction can then be tested on its own.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Action potential → Ca²⁺ enters the presynaptic terminal → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → diffusion across the cleft → binding to complementary receptors → Na⁺ in → EPSP → action potential if threshold is reached.
  • One-way, because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic.

Mission 2

  • EPSPs and IPSPs sum on one membrane; an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold. IPSPs hyperpolarise.
  • Predict a drug's effect from the step it changes: release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.

Mission 3

  • A neurone: cell body, many short dendrites, one long axon; myelin insulates the axon except at nodes of Ranvier.
  • At rest the inside is about −70 mV: the membrane is polarised.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, ATP) builds gradients; K⁺ leaks out faster than Na⁺ leaks in, so the inside stays negative.

Mission 4

  • Holozoic nutrition: ingest, digest (extracellular hydrolysis in the lumen), absorb across the gut lining, assimilate.

Mission 5

  • Ultrafiltration: blood pressure forces water and small solutes through the glomerular filter; cells and plasma proteins stay behind.
  • Selective reabsorption in the PCT: Na⁺ gradient drives glucose co-transport; water follows by osmosis; microvilli, infoldings and mitochondria make it fast.

Mission 6

  • Isolating one type of organelle lets its function be tested directly: a new technique opened a new field.
