Mission 1

Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Firm: money spent on a new machine cannot also pay for an advert campaign.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

?On Saturday, Kai can work a café shift that pays 60 or a tutoring session that pays 45 (same currency, same hours, and he enjoys both equally). Instead, he goes to a festival with free entry. Measured in money, what is the opportunity cost of going to the festival?

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. Mission 3 compares market, planned and mixed economies.
Quick check
?A government decides that hospital treatment will be given according to medical need, not ability to pay. Which basic economic question is this decision mainly answering?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is opportunity cost in practice?

It depends on:

  • Information: the next best alternative may be unknown or uncertain when the choice is made.
  • Valuation: many alternatives have no price (time, health, a clean environment), so they are hard to compare.
  • Time: some costs fall on future generations, as when a resource is over-used.
  • Whose cost: a firm's choice may impose costs on others (pollution) that it does not count.
  • Judgement: the idea always applies, but measuring it can be hard.
Mission check

?A city makes its buses free to ride. The buses are paid for from the city's tax revenue. Which statement about this choice is correct?

Scarce resources have other uses, so even a "free" service costs society the next best thing those resources could have made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How could ten workers make thousands of pins a day when one alone could barely make twenty?

Make a guess: How could ten workers make thousands of pins a day when one alone could barely make twenty?

Mission 2

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 2

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 2

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 2

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 2

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 2

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 2

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Who decides what gets made: prices, planners or a mix of the two?

Make a guess: In a country of millions, who decides how many loaves of bread are baked tomorrow?

Mission 3

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must decide:
  • What to produce (and how much)?
  • How to produce it (which resources, which methods)?
  • For whom to produce it (who gets the output)?
  • An economic system is the way a society answers these questions.
  • It has two parts: who owns the resources, and the coordinating mechanism that links millions of decisions.
Mission 3

Free market economy

  • Resources are privately owned.
  • The price mechanism coordinates: prices rise where goods are wanted and scarce, fall where they are not.
  • Firms seek profit; consumers choose what to buy. Spending acts like votes (consumer sovereignty).
  • The state's role is small: protect property rights, enforce contracts, keep law and order.
  • Strengths: incentives to work, invest and innovate; prices carry information quickly; wide choice.
  • Weaknesses: some goods under-provided (e.g. street lighting); pollution ignored; large inequality of income and wealth; booms and slumps.
Mission 3

Planned (command) economy

  • Most resources are owned by the state.
  • A central planning agency answers what, how and for whom: it sets output targets and fixes prices.
  • Strengths: resources can be directed to national priorities (e.g. heavy industry, defence, health); income can be kept more equal; full employment can be a target.
  • Weaknesses: planners lack the information to plan millions of goods; fixed prices bring shortages and queues or surpluses; weak incentives to cut costs or innovate; little consumer choice.
Quick check
?In a planned economy, a central agency sets each factory's output and fixes all prices. A shortage of winter coats appears. What is most likely to happen next under this system?
Mission 3

Mixed economy

  • Both the market (private sector) and the state (public sector) allocate resources.
  • Most goods are bought and sold in markets.
  • The state provides some goods (e.g. defence, roads, often schools and healthcare), taxes, regulates and redistributes income.
  • Every real economy is mixed. They differ in how much the state does.
  • Think of a spectrum: free market at one end, planned at the other. Countries sit in between, and move along it over time.
Mission 3

Real case: planning and reform

  • The Soviet Union ran its economy through central plans, with five-year plans from 1928 until the state broke up in 1991.
  • Planning built heavy industry fast, but consumer goods were often short and queues were common.
  • China began market reforms in 1978. Over the next few years farm households were allowed to keep or sell output above their state quota; private firms were later allowed.
  • The state still owns many large firms in China, alongside a large private sector: a mixed economy.
  • Both show economies moving along the spectrum, not fixed in one box.
Mission 3

The role of the state

  • Even market supporters give the state a role. Common roles:
  • Legal framework: property rights, contracts, competition law.
  • Public goods and services the market under-provides (e.g. defence, street lighting).
  • Correcting market failure: pollution, information gaps, monopoly.
  • Redistribution: taxes and benefits to reduce inequality.
  • Stabilising the economy in booms and slumps.
  • But governments can fail too: poor information, slow decisions, unintended effects (government failure).
Mission 3Evaluate

Which system allocates resources best?

  • It depends on information: prices pass on dispersed knowledge well; planners struggle with millions of goods.
  • It depends on goals: markets tend to score well on efficiency and choice; equality and national priorities may need the state.
  • It depends on market failures: where externalities or public goods matter, markets alone under- or over-provide.
  • It depends on the state's capacity: good intervention needs good information, honest institutions and incentives.
  • Economists disagree about where on the spectrum an economy should sit. Much of that is a value judgement.
Quick check
?In Country Y, most firms are privately owned and most prices are set in markets. The state funds schools and hospitals from taxes and sets a minimum wage. How is Country Y's economy best described?
Mission check

?A sudden frost destroys half of a country's coffee harvest. How would the response most likely differ between a free market economy and a planned economy with fixed prices?

Markets let a price change do the coordinating; plans need someone to notice and decide.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.

Mission 3

  • Every economy must answer what, how and for whom; systems differ in ownership and in their coordinating mechanism.
  • Free market: prices and private ownership. Planned: the state owns and plans. Mixed: both; every real economy is mixed.
