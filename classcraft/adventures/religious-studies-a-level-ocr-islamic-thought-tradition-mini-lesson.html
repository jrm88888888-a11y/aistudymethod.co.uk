Mission 1

Mission 1: A message says "the Prophet said…". How do Muslims decide whether he really said it?

Make a guess: A message says "the Prophet said…". How do Muslims decide whether he really said it?

Mission 1

Three words: Sunnah, Hadith, Sira

  • Sunnah ("trodden path", custom): the Prophet's example: what he said, did, and silently approved.
  • Hadith ("report", plural ahadith): a single report of one saying or action. Hadiths are how the Sunnah is known.
  • Sira ("way of going", biography): the story of the Prophet's life, told in order.
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as God's word; the Sunnah as the Prophet's lived explanation of it.
  • Muslims say or write "peace be upon him" (pbuh) after the Prophet's name.
Mission 1Read the source

Why follow the Prophet's example?

> "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct) for any one whose hope is in Allah and the Final Day" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali)

> "He who obeys the Messenger, obeys Allah" (Qur'an 4:80, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Qur'an tells the Prophet to "explain clearly to men" what was revealed (16:44, paraphrase): the Sunnah is that explanation.

Mission 1

Anatomy of a hadith

  • Every hadith has two parts.
  • Isnad (chain, "support"): the names of those who passed the report on, back to the Prophet: "A told me that B told him that C heard the Prophet say…".
  • Matn (text): the words or action reported.
  • Scholars test both: is every link in the chain known, trustworthy and able to have met the next? Does the text fit the Qur'an and well-established Sunnah?
  • The study of narrators ('ilm al-rijal, "knowledge of the men") produced huge biographical dictionaries.
  • Hadith qudsi ("sacred report"): God's words in the Prophet's phrasing. It is not Qur'an: it is not recited in salah as Qur'an.

Mission 1

Grading a hadith

  • Sahih (sound): unbroken chain of trustworthy, accurate narrators; no hidden defect; not contradicting stronger reports.
  • Hasan (good): as sahih, but a narrator's memory is slightly less exact. Still used in law.
  • Da'if (weak): a break in the chain or an unreliable narrator. Many allow it only for encouraging good deeds, not for law.
  • Mawdu' (fabricated): invented. Rejected.
  • By number of chains: mutawatir (mass-transmitted, by so many that error is ruled out) and ahad (single or few chains: most hadiths).
Mission 1

From memory to collections

  • 632 CE: the Prophet dies. Companions remember and teach his words; some write notes.
  • 8th century: reports are gathered; fabrication (for politics, sects, piety) makes scholars insist on chains.
  • 9th century: the great Sunni collections. Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870) chose only reports he judged sound: Sahih al-Bukhari. With Sahih Muslim (Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, d. 875) it is the most trusted Sunni book after the Qur'an.
  • Sunni Six Books add Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.
  • Shi'a Islam accepts reports from the Prophet and from the Imams of his family, mainly through narrators loyal to them. Its Four Books begin with al-Kafi of al-Kulayni (d. 941).

Mission 1

Sira: the life told as a story

  • Ibn Ishaq (c. 704–767) wrote the first full life of the Prophet. It survives mainly in the edition of Ibn Hisham (d. c. 833), who shortened it and cut material he judged unsuitable.
  • Composition: one continuous narrative, in order of events, with poetry, speeches and accounts of the campaigns (maghazi).
  • Transmission: chains are often given, but looser: several reports may be blended into one account; some passages have no chain or say "it is alleged".
  • Hadith, by contrast: separate short reports, each with its own chain, arranged by topic (prayer, fasting, trade) for law and practice.
  • Value: Sira gives the historical context of the revelation; Hadith gives tested guidance for religious practice. Muslims use both.
  • Your spec: "differences between Hadith and Sira in terms of composition and their approach to the transmission of prophetic reports".
Mission 1Read the source

The first revelation, told twice

Sahih al-Bukhari, Chapter 1 (the Book of Revelation), hadith 3, told by Aisha (paraphrase):

  • Revelation began with true dreams. Muhammad sought solitude in the cave of Hira. The angel came and said "Read!" He replied, "I am not a reader." The angel pressed him hard three times, then recited 96:1–3.
  • He went home trembling to Khadijah: "Cover me." She assured him God would never disgrace one who keeps ties of kinship and helps the weak, and took him to her cousin Waraqah, who said this was the same messenger God sent to Moses.

Sira Ibn Hisham (from Ibn Ishaq; paraphrase):

  • It is Ramadan, during his yearly retreat. Gabriel comes while he is asleep, holding a cloth with writing. "Read!" "What shall I read?" After 96:1–5, he wakes "as though these words were written on my heart".
  • Fearing he is possessed, he means to throw himself from the mountain; a voice says he is God's messenger, and he sees Gabriel filling the horizon. Khadijah consults Waraqah.

Mission 1

The Sunnah in daily life and law

  • The Qur'an commands salah; the Sunnah shows how: times, number of units, words, movements. Likewise zakah rates and Hajj rites.
  • So most Muslims treat the Qur'an and Sunnah as one guidance: the Qur'an supreme, the Sunnah explaining and applying it.
  • Everyday Sunnah: eating with the right hand, greeting with "salam", the call to prayer at a baby's birth, kindness to neighbours.
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820), al-Risala: the "wisdom" taught alongside the Book (2:151) is the Sunnah; a sound hadith binds, even if it has a single chain. This made the Sunnah the second source of law.
Mission 1Diversity

How much can hadiths be trusted?

  • Mainstream Sunni: hadiths graded sahih are authoritative; the Sunnah is revelation in meaning (53:3–4 is often cited).
  • Shi'a: hadiths are trusted when they come through reliable narrators, especially from the Imams; many Sunni-transmitted reports are treated with caution.
  • Qur'an-only Muslims (Qur'aniyyun), e.g. Ghulam Ahmad Parwez (1903–1985) in Pakistan: hadiths are human records and not binding; the Qur'an is enough. A small minority.
  • Western sceptics: Ignaz Goldziher (Muslim Studies, vol. 2, 1890) saw many hadiths as products of later debates; Joseph Schacht (The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence, 1950) argued that chains "grew backwards" to earlier authorities.
  • Replies: Harald Motzki (1948–2019) dated some legal material to the 7th century through study of early collections; Jonathan A. C. Brown (Hadith, 2009) shows that Muslim critics examined texts, not just chains.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Without the Hadith, Muslims could not live by the Qur'an."

  • Reasons for: the Qur'an gives commands without detail (salah, zakah); 16:44 and 4:80 give the Prophet's explanation authority; 14 centuries of law and worship depend on it.
  • Reasons against: the Qur'an calls itself clear and complete (Qur'an-only Muslims cite 6:38 and 16:89); some practice is known by continuous community practice (the Maliki stress on the practice of Madinah), not single reports; weak and fabricated hadiths have misled people.
  • Middle view: the Qur'an needs the Sunnah, but the Sunnah can be known through practice and mass transmission, so not every single hadith carries equal weight.
  • Judge it by: Could salah be performed from the Qur'an alone? Is the Sunnah the same as the written Hadith? How reliable is the grading system? Who has authority to decide?

Mission 2: How did one night in a mountain cave turn Makkah against its most trusted man?

Make a guess: How did one night in a mountain cave turn Makkah against its most trusted man?

Mission 2

The cave of Hira, 610 CE

  • Around the age of 40, Muhammad spent nights in a cave on Mount Hira near Makkah, in prayer and reflection.
  • The sources say this was in the month of Ramadan.
  • Muslims believe the angel Jibril (Gabriel) came to him with the command "Read!" or "Recite!" (Arabic iqra').
  • Muhammad answered that he could not read. Muslims call him ummi, usually understood as "unlettered".
  • The angel pressed him three times, then gave him the first verses of the Qur'an: Surah 96.
  • This is the start of wahy (revelation) and of his prophethood (nubuwwa).
Mission 2Read the source

Surahs 96 and 97

> "Proclaim! (or read!) in the name of thy Lord and Cherisher, Who created- Created man, out of a (mere) clot of congealed blood: Proclaim! And thy Lord is Most Bountiful,- He Who taught (the use of) the pen,- Taught man that which he knew not." (Qur'an 96:1–5, Yusuf Ali)

> "We have indeed revealed this (Message) in the Night of Power: … The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:1–3, Yusuf Ali)

  • Surah 97 describes the night itself: angels and the Spirit come down; it is "Peace!... This until the rise of morn!" (Qur'an 97:5, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims keep it in the last ten nights of Ramadan (often the 27th).

Mission 2

Khadijah and Waraqah

  • Shaken, Muhammad went home and asked Khadijah to cover him with a cloak.
  • Khadijah reassured him (paraphrase): God would never disgrace him, for he kept ties with family, helped the poor, honoured guests and stood by people in trouble.
  • She is honoured as the first person to believe in his message.
  • She took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, an old Christian who knew the earlier scriptures.
  • Waraqah said (paraphrase): this was the same angelic messenger (the Namus) who came to Musa; his people would one day drive him out.
  • Significance: a believer outside Makkah's idol worship confirmed the call fitted the pattern of earlier prophets.
  • Waraqah's warning came true: when Muhammad preached one God openly, Quraysh, whose wealth depended on the idols' shrine, persecuted the Muslims until the Hijrah (622).

Mission 2

Hadith and Sira: two kinds of source

  • Hadith: a short report of what the Prophet said or did. Each has an isnad (chain of named narrators) and a matn (the text).
  • Sahih al-Bukhari: collected by al-Bukhari (810–870), who kept only reports whose chains he judged sound. Arranged by topic; Book 1 is on how revelation began.
  • Sira: a biography, a continuous story in time order, mixing reports, background and poetry.
  • Ibn Ishaq (c. 704–767) wrote the first great Sira; it survives mainly in the edited version of Ibn Hisham (d. c. 833), who cut and annotated it.
  • Sira chains are often combined or incomplete, so Muslim scholars apply looser standards to it than to Sahih hadith.
  • Value: hadith are relied on for belief and law (religious wisdom); Sira supplies order and context (historical knowledge).
  • Your spec writes "the Prophet Muhammad (pbuh)": "peace be upon him", the blessing Muslims say after his name.

Mission 3: The Prophet died in 632. Who should lead next, and why does the answer still divide Muslims?

Make a guess: The Prophet died in 632. Who should lead next, and why does the answer still divide Muslims?

Mission 3

The question in 632

  • 632 CE: the Prophet Muhammad dies in Madinah. Muslims believe he is the last prophet: revelation has ended (33:40).
  • But the community still needs leadership: to lead prayer, keep unity, judge, defend.
  • Two questions are tangled together:
  • Who should lead?
  • What kind of authority does the leader have: political only, or religious guidance too?
  • Muslims answered differently. The answers became Sunni and Shi'a Islam.
  • "Muhammad is no more than a messenger: many Were the messenger that passed away before him." (Qur'an 3:144, Yusuf Ali) Abu Bakr recited this verse on the day of the Prophet's death.
Mission 3

The Sunni account: Abu Bakr

  • While the Prophet's family prepared his burial, the Ansar (Madinan helpers) met at the Saqifah (meeting hall) of Banu Sa'ida to choose a leader.
  • Abu Bakr, Umar and Abu Ubaydah joined them. Umar pledged loyalty (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr; the others followed; the wider community pledged next day in the mosque.
  • Why Abu Bakr? The Prophet's companion in the cave during the Hijrah, "the second of two" (9:40); an early believer; father of Aishah; and asked by the Prophet to lead the prayers in his last illness (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Title: Khalifat Rasul Allah, successor (caliph) of the Messenger of God: a political leader, not a prophet.
  • Sunni view: the Prophet named no successor; the community chose by consensus.
  • Your spec: "Sunni accounts of Abu Bakr's appointment through community consensus".
Mission 3

The Shi'a account: Ali

  • Ali ibn Abi Talib: the Prophet's cousin, raised in his house, among the first to believe, married to his daughter Fatimah; father of Hasan and Husain.
  • Ghadir Khumm, March 632, on the way back from the Farewell Pilgrimage: the Prophet took Ali's hand and said, "For whomever I am his mawla (master), Ali is his mawla" (paraphrase; reported in Shi'a and Sunni collections, e.g. Tirmidhi).
  • Shi'a: this was designation (nass): God's choice of Ali as leader, announced by the Prophet.
  • Hadiths of the family: the Two Weighty Things, the Qur'an and the Prophet's household (Muslim; paraphrase); "You are to me as Aaron was to Moses, except there is no prophet after me" (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • So for Shi'a Muslims, Ali was the rightful leader and first Imam; the Saqifah set aside God's choice.
  • Ali gave allegiance to Abu Bakr later (after Fatimah's death, in Sunni reports) and kept the community's unity.
  • Your spec: "Shi'a accounts of the prophet's designation of Ali as leader".
Mission 3Read the source

The Prophet's family

> "And Allah only wishes to remove all abomination from you, ye members of the Family, and to make you pure and spotless." (Qur'an 33:33, Yusuf Ali)

> "No reward do I ask of you for this except the love of those near of kin." (Qur'an 42:23, Yusuf Ali)

  • Ahl al-bayt: "people of the house", the Prophet's family.
  • Shi'a: 33:33 refers to the "people of the cloak": the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain (a hadith in Muslim tells how he covered them with his cloak). It points to their purity and their right to guide.
  • Sunnis: the verse sits among verses addressed to the Prophet's wives, who are included; Sunnis also love and honour the family.

Mission 3

The four Rightly Guided Caliphs

  • Rashidun ("rightly guided"): the Sunni name for the first four caliphs.
  • Abu Bakr (632–634): the Ridda wars bring rebel tribes back; the first gathering of the Qur'an begins.
  • Umar (634–644): named by Abu Bakr; great expansion; the diwan (register of pensions); the Hijri calendar; title Amir al-Mu'minin (Commander of the Believers). Before dying he named a council (shura) of six.
  • Uthman (644–656): chosen by the shura; one standard copy of the Qur'an sent to the cities; accused of favouring his Umayyad relatives; killed by rebels in Madinah.
  • Ali (656–661): chosen in Madinah after Uthman's murder; his rule is torn by civil war.
  • Sunni view: a golden age, the model of just rule by shura and the Sunnah.

Mission 3

Ali's caliphate and the first fitna

  • Fitna: trial, civil strife. 656–661 is the first fitna.
  • Battle of the Camel (656): Ali against Aishah, Talha and Zubayr, who demanded justice for Uthman.
  • Siffin (657): Ali against Mu'awiya, Uthman's Umayyad kinsman and governor of Syria. Ali accepts arbitration.
  • A group rejects the arbitration ("judgement belongs to God alone") and leaves: the Kharijites.
  • 661: a Kharijite kills Ali at prayer in Kufa. His son Hasan makes peace with Mu'awiya, who becomes caliph.
  • Sunnis: Ali is the fourth Rightly Guided Caliph. Shi'a: Ali is the first Imam; his short rule was the only time the rightful Imam also ruled (with Hasan's months).
  • Political and religious disagreement are now joined: who rules, and who guides.
Mission 3

Scholars on the succession

  • Wilferd Madelung (1930–2023), The Succession to Muhammad (1997), Chapter 1: the Qur'an's stories of earlier prophets give their families a special place; Abu Bakr's accession was less inevitable than later Sunni accounts suggest (paraphrase).
  • Muhammad Husayn Tabataba'i (1903–1981), Shi'ite Islam (tr. Nasr, 1975): the Shi'a hold that the Imam must be designated, because only one protected from error can guide the community in religion (paraphrase).
Mission 3Evaluate

Designation or consensus?

  • Claim: The Shi'a account of the succession is better supported than the Sunni account.
  • For: Ghadir Khumm is reported in Sunni sources too; the Qur'an honours prophets' families.
  • Madelung's reading of the Qur'an's prophetic families supports this side.
  • For: the hasty Saqifah meeting, without the Prophet's family present, looks less like a full consensus.
  • Against: the Prophet appointed Abu Bakr to lead prayer; most Companions accepted him; Ali himself pledged allegiance.
  • Against: "mawla" has several meanings; nothing in the Qur'an names Ali.
  • Criteria: Which sources count (Sunni or Shi'a hadith)? Does early acceptance prove legitimacy? Is leadership political, spiritual, or both?
  • Spec issue: "how the different narratives of succession relate to and explain the major differences".

Mission 4: For 1,300 years there was a caliph, then in 1924 there wasn't. What did a caliph actually do?

Make a guess: For 1,300 years there was a caliph, then in 1924 there wasn't. What did a caliph actually do?

Mission 4

From caliphate to kingship

  • Khalifah (caliph): "successor" or "deputy" of the Messenger, leading the ummah.
  • A well-known hadith: the caliphate would last thirty years, then kingship would follow (Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • Thirty years after 632 brings us to about 661: the end of Ali's rule and Hasan's peace with Mu'awiya.
  • Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), Muqaddimah (1377): the caliphate changed gradually into mulk (royal power), keeping religious forms for a time.
  • From 661 the title "caliph" continued, but it was passed down in dynasties.
Mission 4

What was a caliph for?

  • Al-Mawardi (974–1058), al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya (The Ordinances of Government): the classic Sunni theory. He calls the caliph "imam".
  • Duties: protect the faith; enforce the shari'ah and judge justly; defend the borders; collect zakah and taxes; appoint honest officials.
  • Qualifications: just character, knowledge of the law, sound body and mind, courage; descent from Quraysh (the Prophet's tribe).
  • How chosen: by those with authority to "loose and bind" (leading figures), or named by the previous caliph. Both follow the Rashidun (Saqifah; Umar).
  • Limits: the caliph is not a prophet; he cannot change revelation or define doctrine.
  • Sunni authority is thus split: the caliph rules; the ulama interpret.

Mission 4Evaluate

Was the caliphate after 661 a true caliphate?

  • Claim: After the Rightly Guided Caliphs, the caliphate was kingship in religious dress.
  • Context: in 680 Mu'awiya, the first Umayyad caliph, had his son Yazid succeed him; Yazid's army killed Husain, the Prophet's grandson, at Karbala.
  • For: hereditary succession (Yazid); the "thirty years" hadith; Ibn Khaldun's account of mulk.
  • For: Shi'a Muslims: rulers who killed Husain could not be God's representatives.
  • Against: later caliphs led prayers, defended borders, upheld the shari'ah courts and spread learning.
  • Against: Sunni jurists accepted imperfect rulers to avoid fitna (civil strife): order under the law mattered more than an ideal ruler.
  • Criteria: Is a caliphate defined by how the ruler is chosen, or by what he does? Can political and religious authority be separated?

Mission 5: Most Shi'a Muslims say a guide chosen by God has been hidden for over 1,000 years. Who leads them now?

Make a guess: Most Shi'a Muslims say a guide chosen by God has been hidden for over 1,000 years. Who leads them now?

Mission 5

The party of Ali

  • Shi'a comes from Shi'at Ali: "the party (followers) of Ali".
  • A minority of Muslims worldwide; the majority in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain; large communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India and Britain.
  • Shared with Sunnis: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet as the final messenger, salah, fasting, Hajj, the Day of Judgement.
  • The distinctive belief: Imamah (the Imamate): after the Prophet, God appointed Imams from his family to guide the community (see Mission 3).
  • Origins: Ali's designation at Ghadir Khumm (as Shi'a understand it); the martyrdom of Husain at Karbala in 680.
Mission 5

Who is an Imam?

  • Designated (nass): each Imam is named by the Prophet or the Imam before him, by God's command.
  • Ma'sum (infallible): protected by God from sin and error ('ismah).
  • Knowledge ('ilm): the Imam knows the outer and inner meaning of the Qur'an and the Prophet's teaching.
  • Hujjah (proof of God): the earth is never without one (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase).
  • So the Imams' words and actions are an authoritative source beside the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah. Their reported sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections such as al-Kafi (10th century).
  • The Imam is not a prophet: revelation ended with Muhammad. He explains and guards it.
  • Your spec: "the infallible Imams as sources of wisdom and divine guidance".
Mission 5Read the source

Verses Shi'a scholars cite

> "He said: 'I will make thee an Imam to the Nations.' He pleaded: 'And also (Imams) from my offspring!' He answered: 'But My Promise is not within the reach of evil-doers.'" (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali)

> "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you." (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali)

  • 2:124: God makes Abraham an Imam after testing him. Shi'a reading: Imamate is God's appointment, and no wrongdoer can hold it; so the Imam must be sinless.
  • 4:59: Shi'a reading: "those charged with authority" are the infallible Imams, since God would not command obedience to someone who might lead into error. Sunni reading: rulers and scholars, obeyed within the limits of God's law.
  • With 33:33 (which speaks of God purifying the Prophet's Family; Shi'a apply it to the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain), these verses ground belief in the Imams.

Q1 In 2:124, who cannot receive God's promise of leadership?

Show answer

Evil-doers, wrongdoers.

Q2 Why do Sunnis and Shi'a read 4:59 differently?

Show answer

They disagree on who holds authority: the Imams, or rulers and scholars.

Mission 5

Authority while the Imam is hidden

  • In the greater occultation, Twelvers hold that qualified jurists (mujtahids) represent the Imam's guidance in law.
  • Each believer follows (taqlid) a living marja' al-taqlid ("source of imitation"), e.g. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani (b. 1930) in Najaf; the marja' receives part of the khums.
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just jurist should also hold political rule. This became the basis of the Islamic Republic of Iran (1979).
  • Many Shi'a jurists, including al-Sistani, give jurists a narrower role in politics.
  • So authority flows: God → Prophet → Imams → (in occultation) jurists on the Imam's behalf, never replacing him.

Mission 6

Mission 6: The Qur'an doesn't just tell people God is One: it argues it. Do its arguments work?

Make a guess: The Qur'an doesn't just tell people God is One: it argues it. Do its arguments work?

Mission 6

Signs for people who think

  • Ayah means both "verse" and "sign": the Qur'an's verses and the features of nature both point to God.
  • The Qur'an repeatedly asks its hearers to reflect: on the sky, the seasons, their own bodies.
  • Later theologians (mutakallimun, practitioners of kalam, "speech", i.e. rational theology) built these hints into formal arguments.
  • Three are on the map here: from origination (3:190), from design (23:12–14), and from mutual hindrance (21:22).
  • Every argument aims at the God of tawhid: one, and unlike anything created.
Mission 6Read the source

What the arguments must reach

> "there is nothing whatever like unto Him, and He is the One that hears and sees (all things)" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali)

> "And there is none like unto Him." (Qur'an 112:4, Yusuf Ali)

  • Divine transcendence: God is not a very large thing inside the universe, nor its first link; God is beyond comparison.
  • So an argument that proves only "some cause" or "some designer" has not yet reached the God of the Qur'an.

Q1 What does 42:11 add after denying any likeness?

Show answer

That God hears and sees: transcendence does not mean God is unaware.

Q2 Why might 112:4 set a test for the other arguments?

Show answer

They must reach a being with no equal, not just a powerful cause.

Mission 6

3:190 and the kalam cosmological argument

> "Behold! in the creation of the heavens and the earth, and the alternation of night and day,- there are indeed Signs for men of understanding" (Qur'an 3:190, Yusuf Ali)

  • Kalam-style readings take constant change in the world (night into day) as a mark of things that begin to exist (huduth, origination).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Moderation in Belief (al-Iqtisad fi al-i'tiqad): whatever begins to exist has a cause of its beginning; the world began to exist; so it has a cause (paraphrase).
  • The world cannot have existed for an infinite past: an actually infinite series of past events could never have been completed.
  • The cause must be beyond the world, timeless and willing: God.
  • Revived by William Lane Craig (b. 1949), The Kalam Cosmological Argument (1979).
Mission 6

23:12–14 and the argument from design

> "Man We did create from a quintessence (of clay); Then We placed him as (a drop of) sperm in a place of rest, firmly fixed" (Qur'an 23:12–13, Yusuf Ali)

  • The passage traces ordered stages: clay, drop, clot, lump, bones, flesh, "another creature", ending "So blessed be Allah, the best to create!" (23:14).
  • Teleological reasoning: an ordered process that reaches a fitting goal points to a wise purpose, not chance.
  • 3:191 adds the believer's response: "not for naught Hast Thou created (all) this".
  • Ibn Rushd (1126–1198), Exposition of the Methods of Proof (al-Kashf), named this the Qur'an's argument from providence: everything in the world suits human life (paraphrase).
  • Some modern Muslims read the stages as advance scientific knowledge; others treat that reading as weak support.

Mission 6Read the source

21:22: no room for two gods

> "If there were, in the heavens and the earth, other gods besides Allah, there would have been confusion in both!" (Qur'an 21:22, Yusuf Ali)

  • Kalam name: the proof from mutual hindrance (burhan al-tamanu'), used by al-Ash'ari, al-Maturidi and al-Ghazali.
  • Suppose two all-powerful gods. One wills a body to move; the other wills it to stay still.
  • Both wills happen: impossible (moving and still at once). Neither: both are powerless. Only one: the other was not all-powerful.
  • So there can be only one omnipotent God; and the world's single, stable order fits that.
  • See also 17:42: other gods would have sought a way to the Lord of the Throne.

Q1 Why must the theologian only show that the gods could disagree?

Show answer

A god who could be blocked is not all-powerful.

Mission 6

How scholars place these arguments

  • Ayman Shihadeh, "The existence of God", in T. Winter (ed.), The Cambridge Companion to Classical Islamic Theology (2008): early kalam's main proof was from the origination of the world; after Ibn Sina (980–1037), many theologians also argued from contingency (whatever might not have existed depends on a Necessary Being) (paraphrase).
  • Scripture supplied the starting points and the conclusion; reason built the steps between.
  • Al-Ghazali, Deliverance from Error (c. 1100): kalam's task is to protect the creed from doubters, not to give believers their faith (paraphrase).
  • Ibn Rushd thought kalam proofs too complex for most people; the Qur'an's own arguments (providence, invention) suit everyone (paraphrase).
Mission 6Evaluate

"The Qur'an's arguments show that one God exists."

  • Strengths: each starts from what anyone can observe (change, order, unity); together they form a cumulative case; 21:22 gives a reason for oneness, which many Western arguments lack.
  • Against the design argument: Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859): natural selection can explain ordered development without a designer.
  • Against oneness: Hume's Philo asks, "why may not several deities combine in contriving and framing a world?" (Dialogues concerning Natural Religion, 1779, Part V).
  • Reply: Philo's gods are limited workers; 21:22 is about omnipotent beings, which cannot share power. Design itself may be how God works, not a rival to God.
  • Against kalam: an infinite past may be possible; or the universe may need no cause. Replies defend both premises.
  • Judge it by: Were these verses meant as proofs, or as signs for those already reflecting? Does showing a "first cause" reach the God of 112? Can a cumulative case succeed where single arguments fail?

Mission 7

Mission 7: The Qur'an says nothing is like God, yet speaks of God's hand and face. How do Muslims read it?

Make a guess: The Qur'an says nothing is like God, yet speaks of God's hand and face. How do Muslims read it?

Mission 7

The puzzle

  • The Qur'an insists: "there is nothing whatever like unto Him" (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali).
  • Yet it speaks of God's hand(s), face, eyes, and of God being established on the Throne.
  • These are anthropomorphic descriptions: speaking of God in human terms.
  • Two errors every school wanted to avoid:
  • Tashbih (likening): treating God as having a body like creatures.
  • Ta'til (stripping): emptying God of the attributes the Qur'an gives.
  • OCR spellings: Mu'tazilism, Ash'arism, Hanbalism.
Mission 7Read the source

Hand and face

> "(Allah) said: 'O Iblis! What prevents thee from prostrating thyself to one whom I have created with my hands?'" (Qur'an 38:75, Yusuf Ali)

> "But will abide (for ever) the Face of thy Lord,- full of Majesty, Bounty and Honour." (Qur'an 55:27, Yusuf Ali)

  • Also: "both His hands are widely outstretched" in generosity (5:64); "the Hand of Allah is over their hands" at a pledge of loyalty (48:10); "(Allah) Most Gracious is firmly established on the throne" (20:5).

Q1 In 55:26–27, what is the face of God contrasted with?

Show answer

All that is on earth, which will perish: God alone remains.

Q2 What does 38:75 suggest about Adam's creation?

Show answer

It was special: God made him "with my hands".

Mission 7

Clear verses and ambiguous verses

  • 3:7 divides the Qur'an into muhkamat (clear, decisive verses), "the foundation of the Book", and mutashabihat (ambiguous, allegorical ones).
  • It warns against those who chase the ambiguous verses "seeking discord".
  • Key dispute: the verse says no one knows their hidden meanings (ta'wil) "except Allah". Does the next phrase, "those who are firmly grounded in knowledge", begin a new sentence (only God knows) or continue it (God and the learned know)?
  • Reading 1 supports leaving the meaning to God. Reading 2 supports interpreting the verses by reason.
  • All three schools treat 42:11 as a clear verse that governs how the others are read; they disagree on what follows from it.
Mission 7

The Mu'tazila: interpret by reason

  • Rationalist theologians, 8th–10th centuries; traced to Wasil ibn 'Ata (d. 748); later systematised by Qadi 'Abd al-Jabbar (d. 1025).
  • First of their five principles: tawhid. A God with a body, or with eternal attributes distinct from Himself, would not be truly one.
  • So they practise ta'wil (figurative interpretation): hand = power or grace; face = God's own self (so 55:27 is read as: God Himself remains); established on the Throne = rules over creation.
  • Reason is a God-given test: a reading that makes God bodily must be wrong, since reason and the clear verses rule it out.
  • Also held that the Qur'an is created (not eternal). Caliph al-Ma'mun enforced this in the Mihna (inquisition), from 833.
Mission 7

The Hanbalis: affirm, without asking how

  • Followers of Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), who was imprisoned and flogged in the Mihna for refusing to call the Qur'an created.
  • Affirm the hand and face as real attributes of God because God affirms them; do not explain them away.
  • But they are not body parts: God's hand is not like a creature's hand (42:11).
  • Formula: bi-la kayfa, "without (asking) how". A saying attributed to Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) on the Throne: its meaning is known, its "how" is unknown, belief in it is required, and asking about it is innovation (paraphrase).
  • Later Hanbalis differ: many leave the meaning itself to God (tafwid); Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328) held the meaning is known, only the "how" is unknown.
  • Critics called them anthropomorphists; Hanbalis reject tashbih as firmly as their critics.

Mission 7

The Ash'aris: a middle way

  • Abu al-Hasan al-Ash'ari (873/4–935/6) was a Mu'tazili until about 912, then turned against them.
  • Uses reason (kalam) like the Mu'tazila, but to defend the creed of the Hadith scholars.
  • In al-Ibana: God has a hand and a face as attributes, bi-la kayfa; not power, not a body.
  • Attributes are "neither God nor other than God": real, eternal, yet not separate gods.
  • Later Ash'aris (al-Juwayni, d. 1085; al-Ghazali; Fakhr al-Din al-Razi, d. 1210) allowed either tafwid (leave the meaning to God) or ta'wil (interpret, e.g. hand as power) when the literal sense would imply a body.
  • The Ash'ari school became the most widespread Sunni theology, alongside the Maturidi.
Mission 7Diversity

Three readings side by side

  • Hand (38:75): Mu'tazili: power, grace. Hanbali: a real attribute, not a limb, how unknown. Ash'ari: an attribute bi-la kayfa; later, also "power" by ta'wil.
  • Face (55:27): Mu'tazili: God's self. Hanbali: a real attribute. Ash'ari: an attribute, or God's self.
  • Method: Mu'tazili: reason governs reading. Hanbali: text governs; reason may not reinterpret. Ash'ari: text first, reason to defend and sometimes interpret.
  • Main fear: Mu'tazili: tashbih. Hanbali: ta'til. Ash'ari: both.
  • Today, the debate continues: e.g. Salafi teachers broadly follow Ibn Taymiyya; many seminaries (e.g. al-Azhar) teach Ash'ari and Maturidi theology.

Mission 7Evaluate

"The Mu'tazili approach best protects tawhid."

  • For: ta'wil removes every hint of a body, so God is clearly one and incomparable (42:11); reason is honoured as God-given.
  • Against: it can slide into ta'til: if "hand" just means "power", why did God choose the word? Reason is put above revelation; 3:7 may mean only God knows these meanings.
  • For the Hanbali view: most faithful to the text and the early community. Against: "without how" may avoid the question; critics see a risk of tashbih.
  • For the Ash'ari view: keeps both text and reason; avoids both errors. Against: inconsistent: who decides which verses to interpret?
  • Judge it by: Which approach best fits both 42:11 and 38:75? What should guide reading: the text, reason, or the early community? Is "protecting tawhid" the only goal, or also faithfulness to revelation?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sunnah is the Prophet's example; Hadith are the reports that carry it, each with an isnad (chain) and matn (text); Sira is his biography.
  • Scholars grade hadiths (sahih, hasan, da'if, mawdu'); al-Bukhari and Muslim compiled the most trusted Sunni collections; Shi'a Muslims have their own Four Books.
  • Bukhari's chained report and Ibn Hisham's narrative tell the first revelation differently because of their different methods.

Mission 2

  • 610 CE, Mount Hira, Ramadan: Jibril, "Read!", Qur'an 96:1–5; Surah 97 names the Night of Power. Khadijah believed; Waraqah linked the call to Musa.
  • Quiet preaching (Khadijah, Ali, Zayd, Abu Bakr), then open preaching of tawhid, judgement and justice; Quraysh opposed it for religious, economic and political reasons; persecution, boycott, the Year of Sorrow.
  • Bukhari's hadith (topic-arranged, tested chains) and Ibn Hisham's Sira (continuous life story) tell the first revelation differently.

Mission 3

  • Sunnis: the Prophet named no successor; the community chose Abu Bakr by consensus; the four Rightly Guided Caliphs are a model of just rule.
  • Shi'a: the Prophet designated Ali at Ghadir Khumm; leadership belongs to the purified family, the ahl al-bayt, through the Imams.
  • The first fitna (656–661) joined political and religious disagreement and hardened the division.

Mission 4

  • From 661 the caliphate became dynastic: Umayyads (Damascus, 661–750), Abbasids (Baghdad, 750–1258), later rival and Ottoman caliphs until 1924.
  • Sunni theory (al-Mawardi): the caliph protects the faith and enforces the shari'ah; the ulama interpret it. The Shi'a Imam is designated and infallible.

Mission 5

  • Shi'a Muslims believe God appointed twelve (or, for Isma'ilis, a different line of) Imams from the Prophet's family: designated, infallible, sources of guidance.
  • Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam is hidden (in occultation); qualified jurists act for him meanwhile.

Mission 6

  • The Qur'an presents nature as signs (ayat); kalam theologians turned them into arguments.
  • 3:190: origination (kalam cosmological, al-Ghazali); 23:12–14: design and providence (Ibn Rushd); 21:22: mutual hindrance, for God's oneness.
  • Critics (Hume, Darwin) challenge the steps; defenders reply, and some read the verses as signs for reflection rather than formal proofs.

Mission 7

  • The Qur'an speaks of God's hand and face, yet says nothing is like God (42:11); schools steer between tashbih (likening) and ta'til (stripping).
  • Mu'tazila: ta'wil by reason (hand = power, face = God's self). Hanbalis: affirm as real attributes, bi-la kayfa. Ash'aris: affirm bi-la kayfa; later also tafwid or ta'wil.
  • 3:7 (clear and ambiguous verses) lies behind the dispute about who can know the meaning.
