Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Make a guess: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Mission 1

Supply against demand

  • Every living cell needs oxygen in and carbon dioxide out, plus nutrients in and wastes out.
  • Heat is exchanged with the surroundings across the surface too.
  • Supply depends on surface area: the area materials cross.
  • Demand depends on volume: the amount of living, respiring tissue.
  • So the key number is the surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume. Written SA:V or SA/V.
Mission 1

Worked example: bigger means less surface per volume

Cube sideSurface area = 6 × side²Volume = side³SA:V
1 mm6 mm²1 mm³6 mm⁻¹ (6 : 1)
3 mm54 mm²27 mm³2 mm⁻¹ (2 : 1)
10 mm600 mm²1000 mm³0.6 mm⁻¹ (0.6 : 1)
  • Side × 3 → area × 9, but volume × 27. SA:V ÷ 3.
  • Total surface area rises with size. SA:V falls.
  • Say: "a smaller surface area to volume ratio" — not: "a smaller surface area".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of single-celled protist, cells normally divide when they reach a certain size. A mutant cell keeps growing without dividing and keeps the same shape, until its volume is eight times the volume at which cells usually divide. Which prediction about the mutant cell at this size is best supported?
Mission 1

Small organisms: diffusion is enough

  • A single-celled organism, e.g. Amoeba, exchanges gases across its cell-surface membrane.
  • O2 diffuses in and CO2 diffuses out, each down its concentration gradient.
  • High SA:V, and no part of the cell is more than a few micrometres from the surface.
  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances. Ten times the distance takes about a hundred times as long.
  • Approximate times for O2 to diffuse through water: 0.01 mm (about one cell) 0.02 s · 1 mm 4 minutes · 1 cm 7 hours.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: change shape

Two shapes with the same volume, 8 mm³:

  • Cube, 2 × 2 × 2 mm: surface area 24 mm², SA:V = 3 mm⁻¹.
  • Flat sheet, 4 × 4 × 0.5 mm: surface area 40 mm², SA:V = 5 mm⁻¹.
  • Flattened or thin shapes add surface with little extra volume.
  • They also keep every cell close to the surface.
  • Examples: the flat body of a flatworm; thin, flat leaves; root hairs.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: specialised systems

  • In a large, active animal the body surface is too small, and most cells are too far from it.
  • Specialised exchange surfaces pack a huge area inside the body: lungs, gills.
  • Ventilation brings fresh air or water to that surface.
Quick check
?Why does gas exchange become a greater challenge as organisms increase in size?
Mission check

?Two small sea animals have no gills, lungs or blood vessels, so every cell gets its O2 by diffusion from the body surface. They have the same volume. One is a sphere 4 mm across; the other is a flat sheet 0.2 mm thick. Which statement best explains why the flat shape suits supply by diffusion alone?

A flat shape gives a high SA:V and short distances, so diffusion alone can supply every cell. A bulky body needs exchange surfaces and transport.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What do lungs, gills and leaves have in common that makes them so good at swapping gases?

Make a guess: What do lungs, gills and leaves have in common that makes them so good at swapping gases?

Mission 2

What makes an exchange surface efficient

  • Large surface area: more molecules cross at once.
  • Thin: a short diffusion distance, often one cell or less.
  • Steep concentration gradient, kept up by supply on one side and removal on the other.
  • Moist: O2 and CO2 dissolve in a film of water before they cross membranes.
  • Permeable: O2 and CO2 are small, non-polar molecules that cross the phospholipid bilayer.
Mission 2

Keeping the gradient steep

  • Diffusion evens out concentrations. If nothing moves, the gradient disappears and net diffusion stops.
  • Outside: ventilation replaces used air (lungs) or water (gills) with fresh.
  • Inside: continuous blood flow carries O2 away and brings CO2.
  • A dense network of capillaries puts blood next to every part of the surface.
  • Say: "blood flow carries oxygenated blood away, keeping the gradient" — not: "blood slows down to collect more oxygen".
Quick check
?Suppose that blood flow through the gill capillaries of an anaesthetised fish is stopped while water continues to flow over the gills, and that oxygen uptake across the gills falls almost to zero within a minute. Which explanation is best supported?
Quick check
?Why is the surface of a gas-exchange organ kept moist?
Mission check

?Lungless salamanders have no lungs and, as adults, no gills. They exchange gases across their skin and the lining of the mouth. Which set of features would best suit this way of life?

Lungs, gills, leaves and salamander skin follow the same rules: large area, short distance, a gradient kept steep.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How does a leaf take in the gas it needs without drying out?

Make a guess: How does a leaf take in the gas it needs without drying out?

Mission 3

Inside a leaf

Layer (top to bottom)Main role
Waxy cuticlewaterproof; cuts water loss
Upper epidermistransparent, tightly packed cells: lets light through, keeps water in
Palisade mesophylltall, packed cells with many chloroplasts: most light absorbed, most photosynthesis
Spongy mesophyllloose cells with air spaces: gases diffuse to every cell
Vein (vascular bundle)xylem brings water and ions; phloem carries sugars away
Lower epidermis + cuticlehas most of the stomata, each between two guard cells
  • The leaf is broad and thin: a large area for light, and a short path for gases.
Mission 3

How carbon dioxide gets in

  • The cuticle is almost impermeable. Gases enter and leave through stomata.
  • CO2 diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll to every mesophyll cell.
  • It dissolves in the moist cell surfaces, then diffuses into the chloroplasts.
  • The mesophyll gives a huge, moist internal surface. No cell is far from an air space.
  • Photosynthesis keeps using CO2, so the gradient into the leaf stays steep. No ventilation is needed.
  • Say: "CO2 diffuses in through the stomata and air spaces" — not: "the veins carry CO2 into the leaf".
Quick check
?Which adaptation of a leaf allows carbon dioxide to reach the photosynthesising cells inside it?
Mission 3

Opening and closing a stoma

  • (1) Guard cells take up water by osmosis and become turgid.
  • (1) Their inner wall, next to the pore, is thicker. Swelling bends each cell outward: the stoma opens.
  • (2) Guard cells lose water and become flaccid: the stoma closes.
  • Stomata usually open in the light, letting CO2 in for photosynthesis.
  • They close in the dark and when water is short, reducing water loss.
  • Say: "the guard cells become turgid" — not: "the guard cells contract".
Mission 3Practical

Stomatal density

  • Find stomatal density from micrographs or leaf casts.
  • Paint clear nail varnish on the lower epidermis; let it dry; lift it with clear tape; stick it on a slide (a leaf cast).
  • Count stomata in one field of view at high power. Repeat in at least five fields.
  • Area of field of view: measure its diameter with a stage micrometer; area = πr².
  • Worked example (illustrative data): counts 18, 24, 21, 17, 20 → mean 20. Diameter 0.40 mm → area = π × 0.20² = 0.126 mm².
  • Density = 20 ÷ 0.126 = 160 stomata per mm².
  • Counts vary between fields: leaves are variable. The mean of many fields is more reliable than one count.
Quick check
?A student counts the stomata visible in five different fields of view on the lower epidermis of a leaf: 22, 26, 19, 27 and 21. The area of one field of view at high power is 0.20 mm². What is the stomatal density?
Mission 3Practical

Drawing a low-power plan

  • A plan diagram shows tissue layers as outlined regions. No individual cells.
  • Sharp pencil; clear, continuous lines; no shading; layers in correct proportion; ruled label lines.
Mission check

?A water lily's leaves float flat on the surface of a pond. Where would you expect most of its stomata to be?

A leaf takes in CO2 only where its stomata meet air, and every open stoma also lets water vapour out. Where the stomata sit is part of that trade-off.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two sets of results share the same average but look nothing alike. What number captures the difference?

Make a guess: Two sets of results share the same average but look nothing alike. What number captures the difference?

Mission 4

Three averages and a range

  • Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  • Median = the middle value when the values are in order (for an even number, halfway between the middle two).
  • Mode = the most frequent value.
  • Range = largest − smallest.
  • One extreme value pulls the mean towards it. The median hardly moves.
  • Use mean, median and mode to describe continuous variables, such as height.
Quick check
?The heights in centimetres of nine students are: 150, 152, 152, 155, 158, 160, 163, 170 and 175. What is the median height?
Mission 4

Standard deviation: the typical spread

  • Standard deviation (SD): how far values typically lie from the mean.
  • Small SD: values cluster tightly. Large SD: values are widely spread.
  • Two samples can share a mean and differ hugely in SD: the hook.
  • For data with a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, about 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, and about 95% within ±2 SD.
  • SD has the same unit as the data.
  • A large SD is not a "mistake". In biology it often reflects real variation between individuals.
Mission 4

Worked example: calculating SD

Stomata counted in five fields of view on one leaf: 22, 26, 19, 27, 21.

  • Mean, x̄ = 115 ÷ 5 = 23.
  • Σ(x − x̄)² = 46.
  • SD, s = √[Σ(x − x̄)² ÷ (n − 1)] = √(46 ÷ 4) = √11.5 = 3.4.
  • Divide by n − 1 (here 4), not n: this estimates the SD of the whole population from a sample.
  • A calculator or spreadsheet does this; you need not memorise the formula.
Quick check
?Limpets were counted in twenty random quadrats on each of two shores. On both shores the mean was 8 limpets per quadrat. The standard deviation was 1.2 on shore A and 6.5 on shore B. What can be concluded?
Mission 4

Random samples and replicates

  • A mean is only as good as the sample behind it.
  • Random sampling: place quadrats at random coordinates (random number tables or a generator), never by choice.
  • Replicates: count stomata in several fields of view, not one field several times.
  • Biological material varies, so replicate counts differ. That spread is information, not failure.
  • Report the mean ± SD.
  • The SD of quadrat counts shows how evenly a population is spread: small SD, even; large SD, clumped.
Quick check
?Three students estimated the stomatal density of leaves from the same plant. Student A counted one field of view at high power and reported 90 per mm². Student B counted ten different fields, obtained values between 60 and 130 per mm², and reported the mean, 95 per mm². Student C counted the same single field as A five times, getting 90 per mm² each time. Which evaluation of these results is best?
Mission 4

Percentage change and percentage difference

  • Percentage change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Example: stomatal density rises from 120 to 150 per mm². (150 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = +25%.
  • A fall gives a negative value.
  • Percentage difference compares two groups with no "before": difference ÷ mean of the two × 100.
  • Example: 120 and 150 per mm² on two plants. 30 ÷ 135 × 100 = 22%.
  • Always divide by the initial value for a change.
Quick check
?In a country, the number of new COVID-19 cases recorded per day fell from 2000 in one week to 1600 in the following week. What is the percentage change in the daily number of cases?
Mission 4

Box-and-whisker plots

  • A box plot shows a continuous variable's distribution at a glance.
  • Median: line inside the box. Box: first quartile (Q1) to third quartile (Q3).
  • Interquartile range (IQR) = Q3 − Q1: the spread of the middle half.
  • Six aspects: minimum, Q1, median, Q3, maximum and outliers.
  • Outlier: more than 1.5 × IQR above Q3 or below Q1. Plot it as a separate point; whiskers stop at the furthest non-outlier.
  • Whiskers are not error bars.
Mission check

?The lengths of five seedling roots, in mm, are 14, 16, 15, 18 and 17. Which gives the mean and standard deviation correctly?

Same mean, different spread: the SD is the number that tells two such data sets apart.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Make a guess: Two bars on a graph have different heights. When does that difference actually mean something?

Mission 5

What an error bar can show

  • A bar or point shows a mean. The error bar shows uncertainty or spread.
  • Error bars can show different things. Read the legend first.
  • ±1 SD: the spread of the individual values.
  • ±1 SE or ±2 SE: how precisely the mean is known.
  • ±2 SE is roughly a 95% confidence interval.
  • Bars from the range (min to max) are sometimes drawn too. They depend on only two values.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose for 24 hours. The mean percentage change in mass was −3.0% with a standard deviation of 1.0%; the mean percentage change in length was −2.0% with a standard deviation of 2.5%. What do these data indicate?
Mission 5

Standard error of the mean

  • Standard error (SE) = s ÷ √n, where s is the SD and n the sample size.
  • SD describes the individuals. SE describes the mean: how close it is likely to be to the true population mean.
  • SE is always smaller than SD (for n > 1).
  • SE falls as n rises: four times as many samples halves the SE.
  • A calculator or spreadsheet gives SE; you need not memorise the formula.
  • Say: "standard error shows how precise the mean is" — not: "standard error is the size of the mistakes".
Mission 5

Worked example: SE error bars

Root lengths of 10 seedlings: mean = 48 mm, s = 6.0 mm.

  • SE = 6.0 ÷ √10 = 1.9 mm.
  • ±2 SE = ±3.8 mm: bars from 44.2 to 51.8 mm, about a 95% confidence interval.
  • Bars may show ±1 SE instead (46.1 to 49.9 mm): read the legend.
  • ±2 SE bars of two groups do not overlap: the difference is likely to be significant.
  • They overlap: a significant difference has not been shown. That does not prove the means are equal.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose. Their percentage changes in mass had a mean of −3.0%, a standard deviation of 1.0% and a standard error of 0.45%. What does the standard error indicate?
Mission check

?A student grew bean seedlings with fertiliser X or fertiliser Y, 10 plants each, and plotted mean root length with error bars:
X: mean 42 mm, bar from 39 to 45 mm. Y: mean 50 mm, bar from 47 to 53 mm. Which conclusion is best supported?
  • The bars show ±2 SE, about a 95% confidence interval.

Bar heights alone mean little. Bars that show the uncertainty in each mean tell you whether a difference is likely to be real.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Your blood passes every tissue all day. How does it know to drop more oxygen where muscles work hardest?

Make a guess: Your blood passes every tissue all day. How does it know to drop more oxygen where muscles work hardest?

Mission 6

Haemoglobin

  • Found in red blood cells. It carries almost all the O2 in blood.
  • A protein with quaternary structure: four polypeptide chains.
  • Each chain holds one haem group with an Fe²⁺ ion. Each haem binds one O2.
  • So one haemoglobin carries up to four O2: it becomes oxyhaemoglobin.
  • Binding is reversible: haemoglobin + 4O2 ⇌ oxyhaemoglobin.
Mission 6

Loading and unloading

  • Partial pressure of oxygen (pO2) measures how much O2 is present, in kPa.
  • Lungs: high pO2 (about 13 kPa) → haemoglobin loads O2.
  • Respiring tissues: low pO2 → haemoglobin unloads O2.
  • Affinity: how readily haemoglobin binds O2.
  • Percentage saturation: how full the haem groups are.
Mission 6

Read the data: the dissociation curve

Human blood at 37 °C, pH 7.4 (Severinghaus, 1979):

pO2 / kPa245.3813
Saturation / %1957759198
  • Plotted, these give an S-shaped curve.
  • Low pO2: the first O2 binds with difficulty. Curve flat.
  • Binding it changes the shape of haemoglobin. The other haem groups bind O2 more easily: cooperative binding. Curve steep.
  • High pO2: almost every haem is full. Curve levels off.
  • Say: "binding of the first O2 changes haemoglobin's shape" — not: "haemoglobin molecules cooperate with each other".
Quick check
?Adult haemoglobin is about 20% saturated with oxygen at a partial pressure of oxygen of 2 kPa, 60% at 4 kPa, 90% at 8 kPa and 98% at 13 kPa. Which explanation accounts for this pattern?
Mission 6

Steep where it matters

Use the dissociation-curve table above. Blood leaves the lungs 98% saturated.

  • Resting tissue, pO2 5.3 kPa → 75%. Unloaded: 98 − 75 = 23 percentage points.
  • Hard-working muscle, pO2 2 kPa → 19%. Unloaded: 98 − 19 = 79 percentage points.
  • In the steep part, a small fall in pO2 releases a lot of O2.
  • So the tissue using O2 fastest, with the lowest pO2, gets the most.
Mission 6

The Bohr effect

  • Respiring tissue makes CO2. CO2 forms carbonic acid → H⁺ → pH falls.
  • More CO2 lowers haemoglobin's affinity for O2.
  • The curve shifts right: at the same pO2, saturation is lower. More O2 is unloaded.
  • CO2 binds allosterically, to the polypeptide chains, not to the haem. This changes haemoglobin's shape.
  • In the lungs, CO2 diffuses out: the curve shifts left, so loading is easier.
  • The same blood at pH 7.2, calculated from the Severinghaus curve with the standard Bohr shift:
pO2 / kPa245.3813
Saturation at pH 7.4 / %1957759198
Saturation at pH 7.2 / %1343618495
  • Say: "CO2 lowers the affinity of haemoglobin for O2" — not: "CO2 pushes O2 off the haem".
Quick checkRead the data
?Blood leaves the lungs at pH 7.4, 98% saturated. In a working muscle, pO2 is 4 kPa and the extra CO2 has lowered the pH to 7.2. At 4 kPa, saturation is 57% at pH 7.4 and 43% at pH 7.2. By how many percentage points does the saturation of this blood fall in the muscle?
Mission 6

Left or right?

  • Curve to the left: higher affinity. Loads O2 at a lower pO2.
  • Curve to the right: lower affinity. Unloads O2 more readily.
  • Fetal haemoglobin: higher affinity than adult haemoglobin (different subunits).
  • In the placenta, at the same pO2, the mother's haemoglobin unloads and the fetus's haemoglobin loads.
  • Say: "left = higher affinity" — not: "left means lower".
Quick check
?In the placenta, maternal blood and foetal blood are separated by a thin barrier. How is oxygen transferred from maternal blood to foetal blood?
Mission check

?As blood flows through the lung capillaries, CO2 diffuses out of it into the alveoli. How does this help the blood load oxygen?

Haemoglobin responds to its surroundings: high CO2 makes it let go, low CO2 makes it hold on. That is how it delivers most where muscles work hardest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • As size increases, SA:V falls; demand (volume) outgrows supply (surface).
  • Diffusion alone is enough only over short distances; large organisms need flattened shapes, specialised exchange surfaces and ventilation.

Mission 2

  • Efficient exchange surfaces: large area, thin, steep gradient kept up by ventilation and blood flow.
  • They are also moist, so gases dissolve before crossing.

Mission 3

  • CO2 enters through stomata and diffuses through air spaces to a large, moist mesophyll surface; guard cells open (turgid) and close (flaccid) the stomata.

Mission 4

  • Mean, median and mode describe the centre; SD describes the typical spread about the mean, using every value.
  • Percentage change divides by the initial value; box plots show median, quartiles and spread.

Mission 5

  • Read the legend: SD bars show the spread of individuals; SE and confidence bars show how precisely each mean is known.
  • SE = s ÷ √n; non-overlapping 95% bars (±2 SE or 95% confidence limits) indicate a significant difference.

Mission 6

  • S-shaped curve from cooperative binding; the steep part lets small falls in pO2 release a lot of O2.
  • Bohr effect: more CO2 → lower affinity → curve right → more O2 unloaded in respiring tissue.
  • Fetal haemoglobin has higher affinity than adult haemoglobin: curve to the left.
