Number and algebra · matrices

Matrices and multiplication

  • A matrix with mm rows and nn columns has order m×nm × n. The element aij​a_ij is in row ii, column jj.
  • Add, subtract or compare same-order matrices element by element. kAkA multiplies every element by kk.
  • Multiply row by column: row ii of AA times column jj of BB, summed, gives element ijij of ABAB.
  • ABAB exists only if columns of AA = rows of BB: (m×n)(n×p)(m × n)(n × p) gives m×pm × p.
  • II is the identity matrix (AI=IA=AAI = IA = A); 00 is the zero matrix.

Number and algebra · matrices

Systems of equations as Ax=bAx = b

  • A system of linear equations can be written as Ax=bAx = b: AA holds the coefficients, xx the unknowns, bb the constants.
  • If AA is invertible, multiply both sides on the left by A−1A⁻¹: x=A−1bx = A⁻¹b.
  • Order matters: bA−1bA⁻¹ is not the same (and here is not even defined).
  • For three or more unknowns, enter AA and bb in your GDC and calculate A−1bA⁻¹b.

Number and algebra · matrices

Eigenvalues and eigenvectors

  • A non-zero vector vv is an eigenvector of MM if Mv=λvMv = λv: MM only scales it. The scale factor λλ is the eigenvalue.
  • Eigenvalues solve the characteristic polynomial: det(M−λI)=0det (M − λ I) = 0.
  • For M=(ac​bd​)M = ( a , b ; c , d ) this is λ2−(a+d)λ+(ad−bc)=0λ² − (a + d)λ + (ad − bc) = 0.
  • For each λλ, solve (M−λI)v=0(M − λ I)v = 0 to find an eigenvector (any non-zero multiple also works).

Number and algebra · matrices

Diagonalisation and powers

  • If MM has distinct real eigenvalues λ1​λ₁, λ2​λ₂ with eigenvectors v1​v₁, v2​v₂, then M=PDP−1M = PDP⁻¹.
  • P=(v1​​v2​​)P = ( v₁ , v₂ ) has the eigenvectors as columns; D=(λ1​0​0λ2​​)D = ( λ₁ , 0 ; 0 , λ₂ ), in the same order.
  • Powers: Mn=PDnP−1Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹, and Dn=(λ1n​0​0λ2n​​)Dⁿ = ( λ₁ⁿ , 0 ; 0 , λ₂ⁿ ). The inner P−1PP⁻¹P pairs cancel.
  • This gives a formula for MnMⁿ for every nn, not just one power.

Number and algebra · matrices

Transition matrices and steady states

  • In a transition matrix TT, the element Tij​T_ij is the probability of moving from state jj to state ii, so each column adds to 1.
  • With a column state matrix s0​s₀, the state after nn steps is sn​=Tns0​sₙ = Tⁿs₀.
  • A steady state ss satisfies Ts=sTs = s with entries adding to 1: the eigenvector for eigenvalue 1.
  • For a regular chain (some power of TT has all entries positive), sn​sₙ tends to this steady state from any starting state.

Graph theory · walks and adjacency matrices

Graph vocabulary

  • A graph is a set of vertices joined by edges. Two vertices joined by an edge are adjacent; two edges sharing a vertex are adjacent edges.
  • The degree of a vertex is the number of edges meeting it. Each edge adds 2 to the total, so the sum of degrees is 2×2 × the number of edges.
TermMeaning
simple graphno loops and no multiple edges between the same pair
complete graph Kn​Kₙsimple, every pair of the nn vertices adjacent
weighted grapheach edge carries a number (distance, cost, time)
directed graphedges have directions; each vertex has an in degree and an out degree
connecteda route exists between any two vertices (directed: strongly connected if you can follow the arrows from any vertex to any other)
subgraph / treepart of a graph / a connected graph with no cycles

Graph theory · walks and adjacency matrices

Walks, trails, paths, Euler and Hamilton

NameRuleClosed version
walkany sequence of adjacent edges
trailno edge repeatedcircuit
pathno vertex repeatedcycle
  • An Eulerian trail uses every edge exactly once; an Eulerian circuit does so and ends where it starts.
  • In a connected graph: all degrees even means an Eulerian circuit exists; exactly two odd vertices means an Eulerian trail exists, starting at one odd vertex and ending at the other. Otherwise, neither.
  • A Hamiltonian path visits every vertex exactly once; a Hamiltonian cycle also returns to the start. There is no simple degree test for these.

Graph theory · walks and adjacency matrices

Adjacency matrices

  • Adjacency matrix AA: entry aij​a_ij = number of edges from vertex ii to vertex jj.
  • The (i,j)(i, j)th entry of AkA^k = number of walks of length kk from ii to jj. For lengths up to kk, add the entries of A,A2,…,AkA, A², …, A^k.
  • A weighted adjacency table stores weights instead of 1s.
  • Transition matrix TT: Tij​T_ij = probability of moving from jj to ii, so column jj holds deg(j)1​1/(deg(j)) in each adjacent row and sums to 1.

Graph theory · trees and algorithms

Minimum spanning trees

  • A tree is a connected graph with no cycles; on nn vertices it has n−1n − 1 edges.
  • A spanning tree is a subgraph that is a tree containing every vertex. A minimum spanning tree (MST) has least total weight.
AlgorithmMethod
KruskalList edges by weight. Add the smallest edge that does not form a cycle. Stop at n−1n − 1 edges.
PrimStart at any vertex. Add the shortest edge from a vertex in the tree to one not yet in it.
Prim, table formCross out the new vertex's row; search the columns of all tree vertices for the smallest entry.

Graph theory · trees and algorithms

The Chinese postman problem

  • Goal: the shortest closed route using every edge at least once.
  • All vertices even: an Eulerian circuit exists; the answer is the total weight.
  • Two odd vertices: add the shortest path between them; those edges are walked twice.
  • Four odd vertices: list the 3 pairings, total the shortest paths for each, and repeat the least.
  • Why it works: repeating a path adds 1 to the degree of each end, so every vertex becomes even and an Eulerian circuit exists.

Graph theory · trees and algorithms

The travelling salesman problem

  • Goal: the least-weight Hamiltonian cycle in a weighted complete graph. First make a table of least distances.
  • Nearest neighbour (upper bound): always go to the nearest unvisited vertex, then return to the start.
  • Deleted vertex (lower bound): MST of the other vertices, plus the two shortest edges from the deleted one.
  • lower≤optimal≤upperlower ≤ optimal ≤ upper.
  • Choosing: every edge → Chinese postman; every vertex once and back → travelling salesman; connect all vertices cheaply → MST.

Mission check

?A postman must walk every street. What's the shortest route?

Find the junctions with odd degree. With none, the shortest route is just the total length. Otherwise pair the odd junctions up in the way that gives the least total shortest-path length, and walk those paths twice: every junction becomes even, so one closed route covers every street.

Recap

Big ideas

  • (m×n)(n×p)(m × n)(n × p) gives m×pm × p, row by column; matrix multiplication is associative and distributive but not commutative.
  • For a 2×22 × 2 matrix, detA=ad−bcdet A = ad − bc and A−1=ad−bc1​(d−c​−ba​)A⁻¹ = 1/(ad − bc)( d , −b ; −c , a ); if detA=0det A = 0 there is no inverse.
  • A linear system is Ax=bAx = b, solved by x=A−1bx = A⁻¹b (by hand for 2×22 × 2, GDC for larger).
  • Eigenvalues solve det(M−λI)=0det (M − λ I) = 0; an eigenvector satisfies Mv=λvMv = λv.
  • With distinct real eigenvalues, M=PDP−1M = PDP⁻¹ and Mn=PDnP−1Mⁿ = PDⁿP⁻¹.
  • In a transition matrix the columns are "from" and add to 1; sn​=Tns0​sₙ = Tⁿs₀, and the steady state solves Ts=sTs = s (eigenvalue 1).
  • Degree = edges at a vertex; the degree sum is twice the number of edges. Kn​Kₙ has 2n(n−1)​(n(n−1))/2 edges.
  • Connected graph: all degrees even means an Eulerian circuit; exactly two odd means an Eulerian trail between them; otherwise neither.
  • The (i,j)(i, j)th entry of AkA^k counts walks of length kk from ii to jj; in a transition matrix, Tij​T_ij is the probability of moving from jj to ii.
  • Kruskal: cheapest edges first, skip any that make a cycle. Prim: grow one tree, adding the cheapest edge to a new vertex. An MST on nn vertices has n−1n − 1 edges.
  • Chinese postman: total weight plus the least-weight pairing of odd vertices by shortest paths (up to four odd vertices).
  • Travelling salesman: nearest neighbour gives an upper bound; deleted vertex (MST of the rest plus the two shortest edges) gives a lower bound.
