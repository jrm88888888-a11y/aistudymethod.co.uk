Mission 1: Jesus' best friends argued over top seats and one swore he never knew him. Why does Mark tell us?

Make a guess: Jesus' best friends argued over top seats and one swore he never knew him. Why does Mark tell us?

Mission 1Read the source

Take up your cross

"Whoever wants to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me. For whoever wants to save his life will lose it; and whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it." (Mark 8:34–35)

  • Spoken just after Peter calls Jesus the Christ and is rebuked for rejecting the idea of a suffering Messiah (8:29–33).
  • Deny himself: put God's will before one's own wants.
  • Take up his cross: a condemned man carried his own cross: be ready for shame, suffering, even death.

Q1 What three things must a disciple do?

Show answer

Deny self, take up the cross, follow.

Q2 What is the paradox in 8:35?

Show answer

Lose your life and you save it; cling to it and you lose it.

Mission 1

Cost and rewards

  • "For what does it profit a man to gain the whole world and forfeit his life?" (Mark 8:36)
  • Whoever is ashamed of Jesus, the Son of Man will be ashamed of when he comes in glory (8:38).
  • Peter: "we have left all and have followed you" (10:28).
  • Jesus: those who leave home, family or land will receive "one hundred times more now in this time… with persecutions; and in the age to come eternal life." (Mark 10:30)
  • The new family of believers replaces what is lost; persecution comes too.
  • "Many who are first will be last, and the last first." (10:31)

Mission 1

Three predictions, three misunderstandings

  • On the journey to Jerusalem (8:27–10:52), Jesus predicts his death three times. Each time the disciples misunderstand, and he teaches about discipleship:
  • 8:31 → Peter rebukes him → "take up his cross" (8:34).
  • 9:31 → they argue who is greatest → "servant of all" (9:35).
  • 10:33–34 → James and John ask for top seats → "came not to be served but to serve" (10:45).
  • The third is the fullest: handed to the chief priests, condemned, given to the Gentiles, mocked, spat on, flogged, killed; "On the third day he will rise again." (Mark 10:34)
  • The disciples "were amazed; and those who followed were afraid" (10:32).
Mission 1

James and John's request

  • "Grant to us that we may sit, one at your right hand and one at your left hand, in your glory." (Mark 10:37)
  • They expect a glorious kingdom now; they want status.
  • Jesus: "Are you able to drink the cup that I drink, and to be baptised with the baptism that I am baptised with?" (10:38)
  • Cup and baptism are images of the suffering ahead.
  • They say "We are able". Jesus says they will share his suffering, but the seats are not his to give (10:39–40).
  • Irony: the places at his right and left at the cross are taken by two criminals (15:27).
Mission 1

Greatness is service

  • The other ten are angry with James and John (10:41).
  • Gentile rulers "lord it over" people. "But it shall not be so amongst you, but whoever wants to become great amongst you shall be your servant." (Mark 10:43)
  • "For the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)
  • Ransom: the price paid to free a slave or prisoner. Christians believe Jesus' death frees people from sin.
  • Today: leaders as servants (the Pope's title "Servant of the servants of God"); foot-washing on Maundy Thursday; service in food banks, hospitals, care work.

Mission 1

Peter's denials

  • After the Last Supper, on the way to the Mount of Olives, Jesus predicts that all will fall away. Peter: "Although all will be offended, yet I will not." (Mark 14:29)
  • Jesus: before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times (14:30). Peter: "If I must die with you, I will not deny you." (14:31)
  • In the high priest's courtyard, a servant girl and bystanders challenge Peter three times; his Galilean accent gives him away (14:66–70).
  • Third time he curses and swears: "I don't know this man of whom you speak!" (Mark 14:71)
  • The rooster crows a second time. Peter remembers, and weeps (14:72).
  • Jesus, meanwhile, inside, openly says "I am" (14:62). The contrast is deliberate.
Mission 1Diversity

Example and warning

  • Warning: even the leading disciple failed under pressure. Fear of others can silence any Christian.
  • Example: Peter repents (he weeps) and is restored: the angel says "tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7). He becomes a leader of the Church.
  • Catholics see Peter as the first Pope; his failure and restoration show that the Church's authority rests on God's grace, not human strength.
  • Many Protestants stress that every believer, like Peter, is saved by grace, not by never failing.
  • Then and now: the first disciples gave up home and work and faced death; most 21st-century Christians face pressure at school or work; some face prison or death today.

Mission 1Evaluate

"In Mark, the disciples are more a warning than an example for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: they misunderstand all three predictions; James and John seek status; all desert Jesus at his arrest (14:50); Peter denies him with an oath.
  • Reasons against: they leave everything at once (1:18; 10:28); they preach and heal (6:12–13); Peter repents and is restored (16:7); tradition says Peter, James and others died as martyrs.
  • Another view: Mark shows both on purpose: failure is real, but so is forgiveness, so ordinary Christians can identify with them.
  • Judge it by: Does Mark's story end with failure or restoration? Is a disciple defined by mistakes or by what follows them?

Mark's disciples fail, learn and are restored: greatness is service, and the cost is real.

Mission 2: Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God: why did Jesus' followers need so many names for him?

Make a guess: Messiah, Son of Man, Son of God: why did Jesus' followers need so many names for him?

Mission 2

What Jews hoped for

  • Messiah (Hebrew) and Christ (Greek) both mean "anointed one": someone set apart by God, as kings and priests were anointed with oil.
  • Under Roman rule, many Jews hoped God would send a Messiah. Hopes varied:
  • a king from David's family who would drive out the Romans and restore Israel;
  • a priestly leader (some groups, such as the Dead Sea Scrolls community);
  • a prophet like Moses (Deuteronomy 18:15).
  • Most expected a human leader sent by God, not a Messiah who would suffer and die.
Mission 2

Son of David

  • God promised King David: "I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever." (2 Samuel 7:13)
  • So "Son of David" meant the royal Messiah: a king of David's line.
  • Blind Bartimaeus calls out: "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" (Mark 10:47)
  • At the entry into Jerusalem the crowd shouts about "the kingdom of our father David" (11:10).
  • Jesus himself questions the title: David calls the Messiah "Lord", so he must be more than David's son (12:35–37).
  • Today: Christians see Jesus as fulfilling God's promises to Israel; but his kingdom is not political.
Mission 2

Son of Man

  • Jesus' favourite name for himself in Mark; others never use it of him.
  • In Hebrew idiom "son of man" can simply mean "a human being" (God addresses Ezekiel as "Son of man", Ezekiel 2:1).
  • In Daniel's vision it is a heavenly figure: "there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man" (Daniel 7:13), given everlasting dominion (7:14).
  • In Mark, Jesus uses it in three ways:
  • Authority now: "the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (Mark 2:10).
  • Suffering: he "must suffer many things… and be killed, and after three days rise again" (8:31).
  • Future glory: "coming with the clouds of the sky" (14:62).
Mission 2Read the source

The third passion prediction

"Behold, we are going up to Jerusalem. The Son of Man will be delivered to the chief priests and the scribes. They will condemn him to death, and will deliver him to the Gentiles. They will mock him, spit on him, scourge him, and kill him. On the third day he will rise again." (Mark 10:33–34)

  • This is the third prediction (after 8:31 and 9:31). The disciples were "amazed" and "afraid" (10:32).

Mission 2

The suffering servant (Isaiah 53)

  • Isaiah 52:13–53:12 describes God's servant who suffers for others:
  • "But he was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities. The punishment that brought our peace was on him; and by his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5)
  • Christians read this as a prophecy of Jesus' death for the sins of others; Mark echoes it: the Son of Man came "to give his life as a ransom for many" (10:45; compare Isaiah 53:12).
  • Jews usually read the servant as the people of Israel suffering among the nations ("You are my servant, Israel", Isaiah 49:3), and do not see a suffering Messiah there.
Mission 2

Son of God

  • 1st-century Jewish meaning: God's people (Israel is "my son", Exodus 4:22), the king ("You are my son", Psalm 2:7), or a righteous person close to God. Not usually a divine being.
  • In Mark the title frames the Gospel: the opening (1:1), the voice at the baptism (1:11) and transfiguration (9:7), the unclean spirits (3:11), the high priest's question (14:61–62) and the centurion: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39)
  • In Mark it is a Gentile soldier who says it at the cross: for Mark, Jesus is shown to be Son of God most clearly in his death.
  • Christians today: Jesus is the eternal Son, "true God from true God" (Nicene Creed, 325/381), the second person of the Trinity.
Quick check

Mission 2Diversity

What the titles mean today

  • Many Christians today read the titles like this:
  • Son of Man: Jesus shared fully in human life and suffering, and will return as judge (the creeds: "he will come again in glory").
  • Son of God: Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant Christians: Jesus is fully divine. Some liberal Christians read it as Jesus' unique closeness to God.
  • Christ / Messiah: Jesus as king; Catholics keep the feast of Christ the King. But his kingship is service (10:45), a model for Christian leaders.
  • Son of David: God keeps his promises; Christians read the Old Testament as pointing to Jesus.
  • Jesus' own understanding reshapes each title: power through suffering and service. This calls 21st-century Christians to serve rather than dominate.
  • Other views: Jews do not accept Jesus as Messiah (no age of peace has come); many non-religious scholars think the higher titles grew in the early Church.
Mission 2Evaluate

"'Son of Man' is the most important title for understanding Jesus."

  • Reasons for: it is the title Jesus chose for himself; it holds together his authority, suffering and future glory; it avoids political misunderstanding.
  • Reasons against: "Son of God" frames the whole Gospel (1:1; 15:39) and is central in the creeds; "Christ" is so important it became part of his name; for many Christians "Saviour" says most about what he does for them.
  • Another view: no title is enough alone; each corrects the others (e.g. Messiah redefined by Son of Man).
  • Judge it by: Whose understanding counts most: Jesus', Mark's, or the Church's? Which title best explains the cross?
Mission check

In Mark, Jesus reshapes the titles: the Messiah and Son of Man wins through suffering and service.

Mission 3: At his last meal Jesus said "This is my body". Why do Christians still disagree about what he meant?

Make a guess: At his last meal Jesus said "This is my body". Why do Christians still disagree about what he meant?

Mission 3

Entry into Jerusalem

  • Near the Mount of Olives, Jesus sends two disciples for a young donkey "on which no one has sat" (Mark 11:2).
  • He rides into Jerusalem. People spread cloaks and branches on the road.
  • The crowd shout: "Hosanna! Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord! Blessed is the kingdom of our father David that is coming in the name of the Lord! Hosanna in the highest!" (Mark 11:9–10)
  • Hosanna: Hebrew "save us, please!", by then also a shout of praise.
  • He enters the Temple, looks around at everything, and leaves for Bethany (11:11).
Mission 3

What the entry shows

  • Zechariah 9:9: "your King comes to you… lowly, and riding on a donkey".
  • A king of peace: conquerors rode warhorses; a donkey signals humility.
  • "Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord" is from Psalm 118:26, sung by pilgrims at the great festivals.
  • The Son of David title is acted out publicly: in Mark, the "Messianic secret" begins to break.
  • Many in the crowd hoped for a king who would drive out Rome; Jesus' kingship will be shown on the cross.
  • Today: Palm Sunday begins Holy Week; many churches give out palm crosses and hold processions.
Quick check
?Why do Christians think the choice of a donkey for the entry into Jerusalem was significant?
Mission 3

Passover

  • Passover (Pesach) remembers God freeing the Israelites from slavery in Egypt (Exodus 12).
  • The blood of a lamb on the doorposts: God passed over their homes when the firstborn of Egypt died (Exodus 12:13).
  • In Jesus' time, lambs were sacrificed at the Temple and eaten in Jerusalem by families and groups.
  • The meal: lamb, unleavened bread (no time for dough to rise), bitter herbs (slavery), cups of wine, the story retold, psalms of praise (the Hallel, Psalms 113–118).
  • Pilgrims flooded Jerusalem, and hopes of a new liberation ran high: a tense time for the Romans.
Mission 3

The meal and the betrayer

  • "On the first day of unleavened bread, when they sacrificed the Passover" (Mark 14:12), two disciples follow a man carrying a water jar to a furnished upper room.
  • At table, Jesus: "one of you will betray me—he who eats with me." (Mark 14:18)
  • Each asks, "Surely not I?" (14:19).
  • "The Son of Man goes as it is written about him, but woe to that man by whom the Son of Man is betrayed!" (14:21)
  • Betrayal by someone sharing your table was a deep breach of friendship.
  • Mark presents Jesus going knowingly: his death is part of God's purpose, yet Judas is still responsible.
Mission 3Read the source

Bread and cup

"As they were eating, Jesus took bread, and when he had blessed it, he broke it and gave to them, and said, 'Take, eat. This is my body.' He took the cup, and when he had given thanks, he gave to them. They all drank of it. He said to them, 'This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many.'" (Mark 14:22–24)

  • "I will no more drink of the fruit of the vine until that day when I drink it anew in God's Kingdom." (14:25)
  • They sing a hymn and go out to the Mount of Olives (14:26).

Q1 What four actions does Jesus do with the bread?

Show answer

Takes, blesses, breaks, gives.

Q2 What does Jesus call the cup?

Show answer

His blood of the new covenant, poured out for many.

Mission 3

What the words meant

  • Body broken: his death is coming; he gives himself.
  • Blood of the covenant: at Sinai Moses sprinkled "the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:8); Jeremiah promised a new covenant (Jeremiah 31:31).
  • Poured out for many: a sacrifice for others (compare Mark 10:45, "a ransom for many"; Isaiah 53:12).
  • New Passover: as the lamb freed Israel from slavery, Christians believe Jesus' death frees people from sin.
  • Future hope: he will drink wine again "in God's Kingdom": a coming banquet.
  • Paul's earlier account adds "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24): the basis of the Church's practice.
Quick check

Mission 3Diversity

What happens at the Eucharist?

  • Catholic: transubstantiation: at the consecration the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood in substance, while their appearance remains. The Mass makes present the one sacrifice of the cross.
  • Orthodox: a real change in the Divine Liturgy, called a holy mystery, without defining how.
  • Lutheran: Christ is truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine (sacramental union).
  • Anglican: a range: many affirm a real, spiritual presence; some hold views close to Catholic or to memorial.
  • Reformed (Calvin): Christ is spiritually present and received by faith.
  • Memorial (Zwingli; many Baptists and other free churches): the bread and wine are symbols; the meal remembers Christ's death ("in memory of me").
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army do not use bread and wine: they seek inward communion with Christ.
  • Catholics call the Eucharist "the source and summit of the Christian life" (Second Vatican Council, Lumen Gentium 11, 1964).

Mission 3Evaluate

"At the Last Supper Jesus meant the bread and wine only as symbols."

  • Reasons for: Jesus was physically present holding the bread, so "is" may mean "represents" (as "I am the door", John 10:9); Paul says "in memory of me"; at a Passover meal every food was symbolic.
  • Reasons against: the words are plain: "This is my body"; John 6:55, "my flesh is food indeed"; the early Church (e.g. Ignatius of Antioch, c. 110) spoke of the Eucharist as Christ's flesh; Paul warns against eating "in a way unworthy of the Lord" (1 Corinthians 11:27).
  • Another view: "symbol" and "real" are not opposites: in Jewish thought, remembering makes a past saving event present.
  • Judge it by: How should "is" be read? How much weight do the early Church and tradition carry? Does the setting (Passover) favour one reading?

Mission 4: Jesus says "I am" while Peter says "I don't know him". Why does Mark tell both at once?

Make a guess: Jesus says "I am" while Peter says "I don't know him". Why does Mark tell both at once?

Mission 4

"I will not deny you"

  • After the Last Supper, on the way to the Mount of Olives, Jesus warns: "All of you will be made to stumble because of me tonight" (Mark 14:27).
  • He quotes the prophet Zechariah: strike the shepherd and the sheep scatter (Zechariah 13:7).
  • He adds a promise: after he is raised, he will go ahead of them into Galilee (Mark 14:28).
  • Peter insists he will stay loyal, even if all the others fall away.
  • Jesus: "before the rooster crows twice, you will deny me three times." (Mark 14:30)
  • Peter: "If I must die with you, I will not deny you." The others all say the same (Mark 14:31).
  • Keep this boast in mind: the night tests it.
Mission 4Read the source

Gethsemane

Gethsemane (probably "oil press") is a garden on the Mount of Olives. Jesus takes Peter, James and John further in. He is "greatly troubled and distressed" (Mark 14:33).

"Abba, Father, all things are possible to you. Please remove this cup from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire." (Mark 14:36)

  • Three times he prays; three times he finds the disciples asleep. "The spirit indeed is willing, but the flesh is weak." (Mark 14:38)

Q1 What does Jesus ask the Father for, and what does he accept?

Show answer

To be spared the "cup" of suffering; he accepts God's will above his own.

Q2 What do the disciples do while he prays?

Show answer

They sleep, three times.

Mission 4

What Gethsemane shows

  • Fully human: Jesus feels fear and grief ("My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death", Mark 14:34).
  • Obedient Son: he calls God Abba (an Aramaic word for father, used with trust) and chooses the Father's will.
  • Purpose: he could run in the dark, but stays. Christians read this as Jesus freely accepting death for others (Mark 10:45).
  • The cup: in the Hebrew Bible, a picture of suffering and God's judgement. Not the cup of the Last Supper.
  • Failing disciples: "Couldn't you watch one hour?" (Mark 14:37).
  • Today: Christians pray "your will be done" in hard times; Catholics keep a watch on Maundy Thursday night and pray the Agony in the Garden as the first Sorrowful Mystery of the Rosary.
Mission 4

Betrayal and arrest

  • Judas, one of the Twelve, arrives with an armed crowd sent by the chief priests, scribes and elders (Mark 14:43).
  • His sign is a kiss, with the greeting "Rabbi!": a mark of respect used to betray (Mark 14:45).
  • Someone cuts off the ear of the high priest's servant. Jesus does not resist: "this is so that the Scriptures might be fulfilled" (Mark 14:49).
  • "They all left him, and fled." (Mark 14:50) The shepherd is struck; the sheep scatter.
  • A young man runs off naked, leaving his linen cloth (Mark 14:51–52). Some see a picture of total desertion; an old suggestion is that it was Mark himself.

Mission 4Read the source

Before the high priest

Jesus is taken to the high priest, with the chief priests, elders and scribes (the council, often called the Sanhedrin). Witnesses accuse him of saying he would destroy the Temple and build another in three days, but "their testimony didn't agree" (Mark 14:59). Jewish law needed two agreeing witnesses (Deuteronomy 19:15).

"Again the high priest asked him, 'Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?' Jesus said, 'I am. You will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of the sky.'" (Mark 14:61–62)

Q1 What does Jesus do when the false witnesses speak?

Show answer

Stays silent: "he stayed quiet, and answered nothing", 14:61.

Q2 What two titles does the high priest use?

Show answer

The Christ; the Son of the Blessed, a respectful way of saying Son of God.

Mission 4

Why "I am" changed everything

  • Until now Jesus has kept his identity quiet (the Messianic Secret). Here, on trial, he says it openly.
  • Son of Man "coming with the clouds" echoes Daniel 7:13; "right hand of Power" echoes Psalm 110:1. Christians read this as a claim to share God's authority as judge.
  • "I am" may also echo God's name in Exodus 3:14. Many Christians read it so; others see a plain "yes".
  • The high priest tears his clothes: blasphemy (Mark 14:63–64). The council condemns him as deserving death.
  • They spit, blindfold, beat him and mock: "Prophesy!" (Mark 14:65).
  • Irony: at that moment, in the courtyard below, his prophecy about Peter is coming true.
Mission 4

Three denials

  • Peter has followed "from a distance" and warms himself by the fire (Mark 14:54).
  • 1st: a servant girl says he was with Jesus. "I neither know nor understand what you are saying." (Mark 14:68)
  • 2nd: she tells the bystanders, "This is one of them." He denies again.
  • 3rd: bystanders notice his Galilean accent. He curses and swears: "I don't know this man of whom you speak!" (Mark 14:71)
  • The rooster crows a second time. Peter remembers, and weeps (Mark 14:72).
  • Mark tells the trial and the denial together: Jesus confesses who he is; Peter denies who he is with.
  • Not the end: the young man at the tomb says, "tell his disciples and Peter" (Mark 16:7). Peter goes on to lead the first Christians.
Quick check
?Mark tells Peter's denial and Jesus' trial side by side. What contrast does this create?
Mission 4

Before Pilate

  • At dawn the council hands Jesus to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor (Mark 15:1). The council, it seems, could not carry out an execution itself (compare John 18:31); historians debate its powers.
  • The charge changes from blasphemy to politics: "Are you the King of the Jews?" "So you say." (Mark 15:2)
  • Jesus stays silent; Pilate "marvelled" (Mark 15:5). Christians hear Isaiah 53:7: like a lamb, "he didn't open his mouth".
  • Pilate offers to release a prisoner at the feast. The crowd, stirred up by the chief priests, chooses Barabbas, a rebel who had committed murder.
  • Pilate sees the priests acted out of envy (15:10) and asks, "Why, what evil has he done?" (15:14), but hands Jesus over to please the crowd, after flogging.
Mission 4Diversity

Who was responsible?

  • Mark names several: Judas, the chief priests, the stirred-up crowd, Pilate, the Roman soldiers.
  • For centuries some Christians blamed "the Jews" as a whole, misusing Matthew 27:25. This fed hatred and persecution.
  • Catholic teaching rejects this: the Passion cannot be blamed on all Jews of that time or on Jews today (Second Vatican Council, Nostra Aetate 4, 1965, paraphrase).
  • The Catechism adds that all sinners bear responsibility for Christ's suffering (CCC 598, paraphrase). Most Protestant and Orthodox churches now teach the same rejection of collective blame.
  • Many Christians read Pilate as weak: he knows Jesus is innocent but protects his position. Historians note other sources describe Pilate as harsh.
  • Most Christians see the deepest cause as God's purpose: Jesus "came… to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45).

Mission 4Evaluate

"Peter's failure makes him a better example for Christians than a loyal disciple would be."

  • Reasons for: most Christians fail too; Peter shows honest weakness, real sorrow (he weeps) and forgiveness (16:7). Christians believe God uses imperfect people: Peter becomes a leader of the Church.
  • Reasons against: Jesus himself, praying and facing trial, is the model to follow. The women who stay at the cross (Mark 15:40–41) are a better example of loyalty. Peter's story could make failure seem acceptable.
  • Judge it by: Is an "example" someone to copy, or someone to learn from? Does Mark want readers to see themselves in Peter? Which matters more: never failing, or returning?
  • 12-mark tip: give reasoned arguments on both sides, use Mark's text as evidence, then reach a justified conclusion of your own.

Under pressure, Jesus says "I am" and Peter says "I don't know him". Mark tells both so readers see courage, failure and forgiveness together.

Mission 5

The road to Golgotha

  • Soldiers force a passer-by, Simon of Cyrene, to carry the cross (Mark 15:21). Mark names his sons, Alexander and Rufus, perhaps because Mark's first readers knew them.
  • They reach Golgotha, "The place of a skull" (Mark 15:22), outside the city walls.
  • Jesus refuses wine mixed with myrrh, a drink that dulled pain.
  • Soldiers divide his clothes by casting lots, as in Psalm 22:18.
  • Crucified at "the third hour" (about 9 a.m.), between two robbers.
  • The charge above him: "THE KING OF THE JEWS." (Mark 15:26) Meant as mockery; Christians read it as true.
  • Crucifixion: Rome's most shameful execution, used for slaves and rebels.
Mission 5

"He saved others"

  • Passers-by jeer: come down from the cross, you who would destroy the Temple (Mark 15:29–30).
  • The chief priests and scribes: "He saved others. He can't save himself." (Mark 15:31)
  • Even the men crucified beside him insult him.
  • Mark's irony: the mockers speak truer than they know. Christians believe that by not saving himself, Jesus saves others.
Mission 5Read the source

The cry from the cross

From the sixth to the ninth hour (noon to 3 p.m.), darkness covers the land (Mark 15:33).

"At the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, 'Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?' which is, being interpreted, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34)

  • These are the first words of Psalm 22, a psalm that begins in despair and ends in trust that God "heard" (Psalm 22:24).
  • Bystanders mishear "Eloi" as a call to Elijah (Mark 15:35).

Q1 Which words does Mark keep in Aramaic, and what do they mean?

Show answer

"Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?": "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?"

Q2 Where do the words come from?

Show answer

The opening of Psalm 22.

Mission 5

Torn veil, Roman witness

  • Readings of the cry: Jesus truly felt abandoned as he took on human sin; or he prayed the whole psalm, which ends in trust. Many Christians hold both: real anguish, real trust.
  • Jesus gives a loud cry and "gave up the spirit" (Mark 15:37).
  • The Temple veil is torn "from the top to the bottom" (Mark 15:38). Readings: God tears it, not people; the way to God is open to all (compare Hebrews 10:19–20); the old Temple order is ending.
  • The centurion, a Gentile in charge of the execution: "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39)
  • Mark opened with "Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1). Now, at the cross, a human being other than Jesus declares it for the first time in Mark. Before this only God's voice and evil spirits had said it; the high priest only asked it as a question.

?Why is it significant that the first person in Mark to call Jesus "Son of God" is the centurion at the cross?
Mission 5

Burial

  • Women watch from a distance: Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Joses, Salome. They had followed Jesus in Galilee (Mark 15:40–41). The men have fled; the women stay.
  • Joseph of Arimathea, "a prominent council member" looking for God's Kingdom, asks Pilate for the body (Mark 15:43). This took courage.
  • Pilate checks with the centurion that Jesus is already dead (Mark 15:44–45).
  • Joseph wraps the body in linen, lays it in a tomb cut from rock, and rolls a stone against the door. The two Marys see where he is laid (Mark 15:46–47).
  • Why it matters: the death is real and confirmed; the tomb is known; the stone is in place. All three matter on Easter morning.
Quick check
?Which detail of the burial in Mark do Christians most often use to show that Jesus really died?
Mission 5

Why did Jesus die? Christian answers

  • Ransom: "to give his life as a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45): Jesus frees people from sin.
  • Sacrifice: like the Passover lamb, his death seals a new covenant ("Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place", 1 Corinthians 5:7).
  • Substitution: he takes the punishment for others' sin: "by his wounds we are healed" (Isaiah 53:5).
  • Victory: by dying and rising, he defeats sin, death and evil.
  • Example: his death shows God's love and teaches self-giving love (Romans 5:8).
  • Atonement: all of these explain how Jesus makes humans "at one" with God again.
Mission 5Diversity

Different emphases

  • Every mainstream church affirms that Jesus died "for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3). They differ in emphasis.
  • Catholic: Christ's sacrifice on the cross is made present in every Mass; all are invited to share it. The crucifix keeps the suffering in view.
  • Orthodox: the cross as victory over death; Good Friday leads to Easter, whose icon shows Christ breaking open the gates of death.
  • Evangelical Protestants: often stress substitution: Christ took the punishment sinners deserved.
  • Liberal Protestants: often stress the example of love and justice, and question punishment language.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists see a good teacher executed by Rome. His example may inspire, but his death changes nothing between humans and God, who they do not believe exists.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Mark shows the crucifixion as a defeat."

  • Reasons for: Jesus is deserted, mocked and cries out in abandonment. No rescue comes. The powerful (priests, Rome) seem to win.
  • Reasons against: Jesus predicted it (Mark 8:31) and chose it in Gethsemane. The veil is torn. The centurion confesses him as Son of God. The mockers' words ("King", "saved others") turn out true. The story does not end at the tomb.
  • Another view: a historian might say a defeat for Jesus' movement, until his followers came to believe he was raised.
  • Judge it by: Defeat by whose standards: Rome's, or the story's? Does Mark mean the cross to be read without Easter? What does the centurion see?
Mission 5Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two things Mark's account of the crucifixion shows about Jesus. Refer to Mark's Gospel.

  • Point 1: Jesus is the Son of God. Source: the centurion says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39) Development: a Gentile sees it through how Jesus dies, so Christians believe the cross reveals who Jesus is, not just his miracles.
  • Point 2: Jesus dies to bring people to God. Source: the Temple veil is torn from top to bottom (15:38). Development: many Christians read this as God opening the way to himself for everyone, which is why they believe they can pray to God directly.
  • 5-mark tip: two points, each developed, plus a reference to Mark (a source of wisdom and authority).
Mission check

?At a Good Friday service, a Catholic church, an Orthodox church and an evangelical church each focus on Jesus' death. Which statement about them would be accurate?

The centurion sees in the way Jesus dies what Mark's readers are meant to see: the crucified Jesus is the Son of God.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Mark's Gospel ends with three women running from an empty tomb, too afraid to speak. Why end there?

Make a guess: Mark's Gospel ends with three women running from an empty tomb, too afraid to speak. Why end there?

Mission 6

Very early on the first day

  • The Sabbath is over. Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James and Salome buy spices to anoint the body (Mark 16:1).
  • They come at sunrise on "the first day of the week" (Sunday).
  • Their worry: "Who will roll away the stone from the door of the tomb for us?" (Mark 16:3)
  • The stone, "very big", is already rolled back.
  • Inside: a young man in a white robe, sitting on the right. They are amazed.
  • The same women saw Jesus die (15:40) and saw where he was laid (15:47). They are Mark's key witnesses.
Mission 6Read the source

"He has risen!"

"Don't be amazed. You seek Jesus, the Nazarene, who has been crucified. He has risen! He is not here. See the place where they laid him!" (Mark 16:6)

"But go, tell his disciples and Peter, 'He goes before you into Galilee. There you will see him, as he said to you.'" (Mark 16:7)

Q1 What evidence does the young man point to?

Show answer

The empty place where the body had been laid.

Q2 Who is named in the message, and why does it matter?

Show answer

Peter, who had denied Jesus: he is not left out.

  • "As he said to you": Jesus had promised to go ahead of them into Galilee (Mark 14:28).
Mission 6

An ending that stops short

  • "They went out, and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had come on them. They said nothing to anyone; for they were afraid." (Mark 16:8)
  • The oldest complete manuscripts of Mark end here. Verses 16:9–20 were added later to give a fuller ending; many Bibles print them in brackets.
  • Readings of 16:8:
  • The ending was lost or never finished.
  • Mark ends on purpose: the reader must decide how to respond. Will you go and tell?
  • Fear in Mark is the awe people feel before God's power, as at the calming of the storm (4:41).
  • Mark's first readers already knew the message had been told, or they would not be Christians.

Mission 6

Explaining the empty tomb

  • Bodily resurrection: God raised Jesus to a new, transformed bodily life. The belief of Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant Christians.
  • Spiritual resurrection: some liberal Christians hold that Jesus' followers truly met him alive in a spiritual sense; the tomb story expresses that faith.
  • Body stolen: already rumoured in Matthew's day: "his disciples came by night and stole him away" (Matthew 28:13).
  • Wrong tomb: the women went to the wrong grave (Kirsopp Lake, 1872–1946, The Historical Evidence for the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, 1907).
  • Swoon theory: Jesus did not die but revived in the cool tomb (some 18th–19th-century rationalist writers).
  • Christians reply that Pilate had the centurion confirm the death (Mark 15:44–45).
  • Visions or legend: the disciples had grief visions, or the story grew over time (e.g. D. F. Strauss, 1808–1874, The Life of Jesus, 1835).
Quick check
?A student argues that Jesus only fainted on the cross and later woke up in the tomb. Which detail in Mark do Christians use against this "swoon theory"?
Mission 6

Why the resurrection matters to Christians

For Christians:

  • Central to faith: "If Christ has not been raised, your faith is vain; you are still in your sins." (1 Corinthians 15:17)
  • Vindication: Christians believe God showed that Jesus' claims were true (the Son of Man "after three days rise again", Mark 8:31).
  • Victory: they believe sin and death are defeated: "Death is swallowed up in victory." (1 Corinthians 15:54)
  • Hope: Christians believe they too will be raised; Christian funerals speak of the resurrection.
  • New life now: they believe baptism shares in Christ's death and rising (Romans 6:4).
  • Worship: Sunday is kept as the day of resurrection; Easter is the greatest feast.
Mission 6Diversity

Differing beliefs today

  • Catholic: the resurrection is a real, historical event, and the heart of the Paschal Mystery (Christ's passion, death and rising), celebrated at the Easter Vigil and every Sunday Mass.
  • Orthodox: Easter (Pascha) is the "feast of feasts"; believers greet each other "Christ is risen!" "He is risen indeed!"
  • Many evangelicals: stress the empty tomb as historical evidence for faith.
  • Some liberal Christians: the resurrection is real but spiritual; Christ lives in the community of faith. Most churches reject this as incomplete.
  • Atheists and Humanists: dead people do not rise; natural explanations are more likely. Jesus' ethical teaching can still be valued without the resurrection.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The empty tomb proves that Jesus rose from the dead."

  • Reasons for: the burial site was known (15:47), so the body could have been produced. Women were the first witnesses, which an invented story of the time would be unlikely to choose. Even opponents in Matthew admit the tomb was empty (28:13). The disciples changed from frightened to bold, ready to die for their belief.
  • Reasons against: an empty tomb could have many causes (theft, wrong tomb). The Gospels differ in details. They were written decades later by believers. Hume-style argument: a natural explanation is always more probable than a miracle.
  • Another view: many Christians say the empty tomb is a sign, not a proof: faith rests on meeting the risen Christ (John 20:29).
  • Judge it by: What would count as proof of an event 2,000 years ago? Do Gospel differences weaken the core story or show independent witnesses? Is the question one of evidence, faith, or both?
Mission 6Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the resurrection is important to Christians. Refer to a source of Christian belief.

  • Reason 1: it shows Jesus defeated death. Source: "He has risen! He is not here." (Mark 16:6) Development: so Christians believe death is not the end, and funerals speak of hope.
  • Reason 2: it is the foundation of faith. Source: Paul: without it "your faith is vain" (1 Corinthians 15:17). Development: so Christians gather on Sunday, the day of resurrection, and Easter is the highest feast.
  • 5-mark tip: two reasons, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cost: deny yourself, take up your cross (8:34); rewards: a new family "with persecutions" and eternal life (10:28–31).
  • Three predictions, three misunderstandings; the answer to the third is service: "not to be served but to serve" (10:45).
  • Peter's three denials (14:66–72) are a warning and, with his restoration (16:7), an example.

Mission 2

  • Messiah/Christ means "anointed"; many 1st-century Jews hoped for a royal Son of David to free Israel.
  • Jesus calls himself the Son of Man (Daniel 7:13): authority, suffering (10:33–34) and future glory; Mark frames him as Son of God (1:1; 15:39).

Mission 3

  • Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey as a humble king (Zechariah 9:9); crowds shout "Hosanna" (Mark 11:1–11).
  • At a Passover meal he gives bread and wine as his body and his "blood of the new covenant" (Mark 14:22–24).
  • Catholics, Orthodox, Lutherans, Anglicans, Reformed and memorialist Christians understand his words in different ways.

Mission 4

  • Gethsemane: Jesus is distressed but accepts the Father's will ("not what I desire, but what you desire"); the disciples sleep, then flee at the arrest.
  • Before the high priest Jesus says "I am", is condemned for blasphemy; before Pilate the charge is "King of the Jews", and Pilate hands him over.
  • Peter denies Jesus three times at the same moment, weeps, and is later restored: an example and a warning for disciples.

Mission 5

  • Crucified as "King of the Jews", mocked as one who "can't save himself", Jesus cries the words of Psalm 22 and dies.
  • The veil is torn and a Roman centurion says, "Truly this man was the Son of God!"
  • Joseph buries the body in a rock tomb, watched by the women.
  • Christians explain the death as ransom, sacrifice, substitution, victory and example, with different churches stressing different meanings.

Mission 6

  • Mark 16:1–8: the women find the stone rolled back and the tomb empty; a young man says "He has risen!" and sends a message to the disciples "and Peter"; they flee in fear.
  • Explanations range from bodily resurrection (most Christians) to spiritual resurrection, theft, wrong tomb, swoon and visions.
  • Christians see the resurrection as central to their faith: victory over death, hope, Sunday and Easter worship.
