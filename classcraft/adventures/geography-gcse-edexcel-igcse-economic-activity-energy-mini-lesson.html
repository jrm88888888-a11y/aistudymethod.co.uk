Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do fewer than one in a hundred UK workers farm, while most Ethiopian workers do?

Make a guess: Why do fewer than one in a hundred UK workers farm, while most Ethiopian workers do?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.1a employment by sector (primary to quaternary); why structures differ with development (Clark-Fisher model)
  • 4.1c why sector employment changes: raw materials, globalisation, mechanisation, demographic change, government policy
Mission 1

Four sectors of work

  • Primary: taking raw materials from the land or sea: farming, fishing, mining, forestry.
  • Secondary: making things: manufacturing, construction.
  • Tertiary: providing services: shops, health care, teaching, transport, tourism.
  • Quaternary: knowledge and information: research and development, IT, consultancy.
  • Employment structure: the share of workers in each sector.
Quick check
?A scientist designs a new vaccine in a research laboratory. Which sector is this?
Mission 1

Three countries, three structures (2023)

Share of all workers:

  • UK (high income): agriculture 0.9%, industry 15.9%, services 83.2%.
  • India (lower-middle income): agriculture 43.5%, industry 25.0%, services 31.5%.
  • Ethiopia (low income): agriculture 62.2%, industry 6.4%, services 31.5%.
  • Income groups from the World Bank.
  • These are an HIC (high-income country), a MIC (middle-income country) and a LIC (low-income country).
  • The data group work into three sectors: quaternary jobs are counted inside services.
Mission 1

Structures change over time

1991 → 2023, share of workers:

  • UK: industry 30.8% → 15.9%; services 66.8% → 83.2%.
  • India: agriculture 63.1% → 43.5%; industry 14.6% → 25.0%.
  • Ethiopia: agriculture 77.2% → 62.2%; services 16.2% → 31.5%.
  • Pattern: as countries develop, workers move out of farming, then from industry into services.
Quick check
?India's share of workers in agriculture fell from 63.1% in 1991 to 43.5% in 2023. By how much did it fall?
Mission 1

The Clark-Fisher model

  • A model of how employment structure changes as a country develops.
  • Stage 1 (pre-industrial): most people work in the primary sector.
  • Stage 2 (industrial): secondary jobs rise as factories grow; primary jobs fall.
  • Stage 3 (post-industrial): tertiary (and quaternary) jobs dominate; secondary jobs fall.
  • Exam move: place a named country at a stage and use its figures as evidence.
Mission 1

The UK through the model

  • Primary share of workers: 14.3% in 1920 → 1.3% in 2016.
  • Tertiary share rose "from 1966 onwards" to 83.6% in 2016.
  • Manufacturing "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • So the UK is in stage 3; Ethiopia is still mostly in stage 1; India is in between.
Mission 1

Why jobs shift (1)

  • Mechanisation: machines do farm and factory work, so fewer workers are needed for the same output.
  • Availability of raw materials: when a resource runs out or stops being used, its jobs go. UK coal fell from about 40% of electricity in 2012 to 0%.
  • Exam move: for "reasons", name the factor, say which sector it changes, and give the direction (more or fewer jobs).
Mission 1

Why jobs shift (2)

  • Globalisation: firms move factory work to where costs are lower. Apple's manufacturing is done by "outsourcing partners located primarily in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam". Factory jobs grow there, while design and services stay in the HIC.
  • Demographic change: an ageing population needs more care and health workers (tertiary); a growing young population needs more schools and jobs.
  • Government policy: governments can close or support industries. The UK has moved to ban new coal mining; other governments offer support to attract factories.
  • Changing rules and technology also change who works where: the ONS points to "changes in technology, industrial structure, legislation and social change".
Quick check
?Combine harvesters let one farm worker do the work of many. Which cause of change is this, and which sector loses jobs?
Mission 1

The model does not fit every country

  • In Ethiopia, 1991–2023: agriculture fell by 15.0 points, services rose by 15.3, industry barely changed (6.7% → 6.4%).
  • So many workers moved straight from farming to services (shops, transport, small trades), skipping a big industrial stage.
  • The model is based on the history of HICs such as the UK; today's LICs face global competition for factory work.
  • Exam move: "evaluate the Clark-Fisher model": show where it fits (UK) and where it does not (Ethiopia).
Mission check

?China's employment structure: 1991: agriculture 59.6%, industry 21.4%, services 18.9%. 2023: agriculture 22.8%, industry 31.4%, services 45.8%. Which statement fits best?

As countries develop, mechanisation cuts farm jobs, factories grow (helped by globalisation and government policy), and later services and knowledge jobs take over. The UK went through this long ago, so under 1% of its workers farm; Ethiopia is early in the process, and is moving into services without a large industrial stage.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are some factories by the sea, some beside motorways and some next door to universities?

Make a guess: Why are some factories by the sea, some beside motorways and some next door to universities?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.1b factors affecting the location of economic activity in each sector, and how these factors change over time
Mission 2

Why location matters

  • A firm locates where it can keep costs low and reach its customers.
  • Different industries need different things, so the best site differs.
  • The factors to know: land, labour, raw materials, energy, transport, markets, political policies, technology, communications, containerisation.
Mission 2

Land, raw materials and energy

  • Land: big factories need flat, cheap land with room to grow.
  • Raw materials: if they are bulky and heavy and lose weight in processing (e.g. iron ore into steel), it pays to be near the source or a port where they arrive.
  • Energy: early factories clustered on coalfields; heavy industry still needs cheap, reliable power.
Mission 2

Labour, markets, transport and policy

  • Labour: enough workers with the right skills, at a cost the firm can pay.
  • Markets: near customers, especially if products are bulky or perishable.
  • Transport: motorways, railways, ports and airports.
  • Political policies: grants, tax breaks and planning rules can pull a firm to a place.
  • Example of labour needs: Toyota's UK plants (announced in 1989; cars at Burnaston, Derbyshire, engines at Deeside, north Wales) employ about 3,000 people. A plant that size needs a large local workforce.
Quick check
?A company turns heavy iron ore into steel, and the steel weighs much less than the ore. Where should it locate?
Mission 2

Containerisation

  • Containerisation: goods packed in standard steel boxes that move from ship to train to lorry without unpacking.
  • World container port traffic rose from 547 million to 913 million TEU (twenty-foot container units) between 2010 and 2024.
  • Research finds containerisation was a driver of 20th-century economic globalisation.
  • Effect on location: parts and products can come from far away cheaply, so factories need not sit beside raw materials or markets. Deep-water container ports attract warehouses and distribution centres.
  • Example: London Gateway opened a fourth berth; London's container traffic rose 38% in 2025.
Quick check
?How has containerisation changed where factories can locate?
Mission 2

Technology and communications

  • High-tech and research firms need skilled graduates, fast communications and a pleasant environment.
  • They cluster near universities.
  • Cambridge Science Park: in 1970 Trinity College turned farmland outside Cambridge into the UK's first science park.
  • It now has more than 7,000 members in software, life sciences, engineering and biotech.
Mission 2

Factors differ by sector

  • Primary (farming, mining, fishing): must go where the resource is: good soils and climate, the ore body, the fishing grounds.
  • Secondary (manufacturing): raw materials, energy, labour, transport, markets.
  • Tertiary (services, shops): near customers and easy to reach: town centres, retail parks by main roads.
  • Quaternary (research, IT): universities, skilled staff, communications.
Mission 2

Factors change: Port Talbot

  • The steelworks at Port Talbot, south Wales, was "the UK's largest single industrial carbon emitter".
  • The UK government offered up to £500 million towards a £1.25 billion plan to replace its blast furnaces with an electric arc furnace.
  • Construction began on 14 July 2025; the project is expected to cut the site's carbon emissions by about 90% and is said to secure 5,000 jobs.
  • Lesson: energy and policy (grants, climate targets) now shape where and how steel is made.
Mission 2

How location factors change over time

  • Energy: once factories had to be on coalfields; electricity now reaches almost anywhere, so many firms are footloose.
  • Transport: containers and motorways cut distance costs.
  • Communications: the internet lets offices and call centres locate far from customers.
  • The economy shifts: UK manufacturing jobs fell sharply from the 1960s; services reached 83.6% of UK jobs in 2016.
Quick check
?Which location factor matters much less to UK manufacturers today than it did 150 years ago?
Mission check

?A firm makes bottled drinks. Its main ingredient, water, can be found almost anywhere, but full bottles are heavy and are sold in every town. Where is it most likely to locate?

Each industry weighs the factors differently. Bulky products follow their markets; heavy industry follows raw materials, energy and ports; high-tech firms follow universities, skills and communications; and factors change over time with containerisation, technology and policy (e.g. Port Talbot's new furnace).

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Make a guess: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.2a positive and negative impacts of economic sector shifts in a named developed country (the UK)
Mission 3

From making things to services

  • UK workforce jobs in manufacturing (June each year):
  • 1978: 6.73 million of 26.99 million = 24.9% (our calculation)
  • 2001: 3.83 million of 30.14 million = 12.7%
  • 2026: 2.46 million of 36.73 million = 6.7%
  • Services rose to 84.8% of all jobs in 2026 (31.16 million).
  • This is deindustrialisation: the decline of the traditional industrial base.
Mission 3

A post-industrial economy

  • A post-industrial economy earns most of its income from services and knowledge, not factories.
  • Tertiary jobs provide a service (shops, health, transport); quaternary jobs are based on knowledge: research, IT, consultancy.
  • Professional, scientific and technical jobs: 1.85 million (2001) → 3.50 million (2026), up 90% (our calculation).
  • Information and communication (IT, media, telecoms): 1.21 million → 1.61 million.
  • Finance: UK financial services exports were worth £98.7 billion in 2025.
Quick check
?Which job is in the quaternary sector?
Mission 3

Why the shift happened

  • Manufacturing employment "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • Globalisation: many goods are now made more cheaply abroad and imported.
  • Technology: machines do more of the work in the factories that remain.
  • Rising incomes: people spend more on services such as health, travel and finance; tertiary jobs reached 83.6% of employment by 2016.
Mission 3

Impacts of the shift: good and bad

  • Positive: growth in high-value services and research; e.g. financial services exports of £98.7 billion in 2025.
  • Positive (environment): industry's greenhouse gas emissions fell 12% in 2025, mainly because blast furnaces closed.
  • Negative: job losses where factories close. After the Redcar steelworks closed in 2015, 2,150 former workers and supply-chain staff claimed benefits within a year.
  • Negative: regional inequality. In 2024 GDP per head was £72,201 in London but £29,957 in the North East.
Quick check
?Which is a negative impact of the UK's shift from manufacturing to services?
Mission check

?A northern town loses its steelworks. Ten years later most new jobs are in a hospital, a call centre and a software firm. Which description fits best?

In the UK, manufacturing fell from 24.9% of jobs in 1978 to 6.7% in 2026, and services rose to 84.8%. The shift brought high-value research and finance jobs, lower industrial emissions, but also job losses and regional inequality.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Income per person in India has risen about seven times since 1995. Who has gained, and at what cost?

Make a guess: Income per person in India has risen about seven times since 1995. Who has gained, and at what cost?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.2a positive and negative impacts of economic sector shifts in a named developing or emerging country
Mission 4

Rapid development in numbers

  • GDP per person: US$375 (1995) → US$2,702 (2025): about 7 times (calculated, current US$, not adjusted for prices).
  • Growth: 7.6% in 2025.
  • Not everyone gained equally: income data show the top 1% received 22.6% of national income in 2022–23, the highest in its series since 1922.
  • People below US$3.00 a day: 27% (2011) → 3% (2023).
  • Over the same years, jobs moved out of farming into industry and services.
Mission 4

Quality of life: big gains

  • Life expectancy: 61 (1995) → 72 (2024).
  • Children dying before age 5: 110 per 1,000 (1995) → 27 (2024).
  • Mothers dying in childbirth: 362 per 100,000 (2000) → 80 (2023).
  • Electricity: 60% of people (2000) → 99.9% (2024).
  • Secondary school enrolment (gross): 45% (1995) → 80% (2025).
Mission 4

Sector shifts: positive impacts

  • Jobs moved from farming (63% in 1991) to industry (26%) and services (33%) by 2025.
  • Factory and office jobs usually pay more than farm work; incomes rise.
  • Foreign investment: US$40.8 billion in 2025, bringing jobs and technology.
  • People move to cities: urban share 27% (1995) → 36% (2025).
Mission 4

Sector shifts: negative impacts

  • Too few new jobs: services give 49% of output but only 33% of jobs.
  • Farming left behind: 42% of workers produce 16% of output.
  • City pressure: in 2011, 41.8% of Mumbai's people lived in slums.
  • Waste: Mumbai's Deonar landfill still received about 4,500 tonnes of rubbish a day (2018).
  • Gains are uneven: the top 1%'s income share is the highest since 1922.
Mission 4

Environmental impacts

  • Air: average PM2.5 exposure 48 µg/m³ (1995) → 54 µg/m³ (2023).
  • WHO's first interim target is 35 µg/m³; its guidelines aim much lower.
  • Rubbish: Mumbai's Deonar landfill took about 4,500 tonnes a day (2018).
  • Water: 90% of water withdrawals go to farming (2022), so rivers and groundwater are under pressure.
  • Industry, traffic and city growth all add to air pollution.
Quick check
?India's average PM2.5 exposure was 54 µg/m³ in 2023. WHO's first interim target is 35 µg/m³. By how much does India exceed it, as a percentage of the target?
Mission check

?Country P has grown fast for 30 years. A minister says: "Growth has improved life for everyone equally, at no cost." Which pair of facts about P would best challenge this claim?

India shows this pattern: longer lives, more schooling, far less extreme poverty, but rising air pollution, crowded cities and a growing share of income for the richest.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.2b informal employment in a named megacity (Mumbai): causes (development, rural–urban migration), characteristics, advantages and disadvantages
Mission 5

Mumbai: a fast-growing city

  • Mumbai is on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea.
  • Census 2011: 12,442,373 people in Greater Mumbai.
  • The UN, counting the whole built-up city, gives 15.95 million in 2000 and 20.20 million in 2025: a rise of about 4.25 million in 25 years (our calculation).
  • In 2025 it was the most densely populated city of a million or more in the world: about 27,000 people per km².
  • India is an emerging country.
Mission 5

Why people move to Mumbai

  • Cities grow by natural increase (more births than deaths) and migration.
  • Push from rural areas: low farm incomes, few jobs, drought, poor services.
  • Pull to the city: jobs, wages, schools and hospitals.
  • Since the 1960s, M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
Mission 5

Slums: unplanned housing

  • A slum is crowded housing that lacks basic services such as water, toilets and drains; much of it is built without permission.
  • Census 2011: 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • Praja Foundation, citing the same census, gives 41.84%.
Mission 5

Where the slums are

  • Slums grow on land others do not want or cannot use: beside railways, on marshy or flood-prone ground, next to landfill, at the city edge.
  • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%).
  • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%).
  • M/East contains "Asia's second largest and oldest open land-fill".
  • Formal housing dominates where land is most valuable.
Quick check
?Two sources, both using the 2011 census, give Mumbai's slum share as 52.5% and 41.84%. What is the best explanation?
Mission 5

Life in M/East

Praja Foundation, citing a 2017 study by the charity Apnalaya:

  • The landfill still receives about 4,500 tonnes of garbage a day.
  • Infant mortality: 66 per 1,000 live births, against a national average of 41.
  • Average age at death: 39.
  • Air and groundwater are contaminated; basic amenities "are conspicuous by their absence".
  • Average family income in its slum clusters: Rs 7,802 a month.
Quick check
?Which M/East figure is the clearest sign of poor health and living conditions?
Mission 5

Informal work

  • Formal jobs: contract, regular pay, taxes paid, legal protection.
  • Informal jobs: no contract, sick pay or pension. Examples: street vending, waste picking, domestic work, small workshops, building-site day labour.
  • In 2024, informal jobs were 81.5% of all jobs in lower-middle-income countries such as India, against 12.9% in high-income countries.
  • Causes: migrants arrive faster than formal jobs are created; many lack qualifications; informal work is easy to start.
  • In M/East, "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed".
Mission 5

Informal work: for and against

  • For: income for people with no formal job; cheap goods and services; recycling of waste; skills learned on the job.
  • Against: low, irregular pay; no sick pay or pension; unsafe conditions; child labour risk.
  • For the city: few taxes are paid, so less money for services.
Quick check
?Which worker is in the informal sector?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, Ward A sits by the port and business district, where land is expensive. Ward B is at the edge, beside the city's landfill; people cleared from central slums were resettled there, and new migrants keep arriving. Which ward will have the higher slum share, and why?

Land values and history explain the gap: D ward 10.0% in slums, M/East 84.9%. Cheap marginal land beside a landfill, resettlement from inner-city slums and new migrants concentrated slums in M/East.

Mission 6

Mission 6: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Make a guess: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.2c Malthus and Boserup theories of population and resources
Mission 6

The numbers since 1961

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 → 4,229 kg per hectare.
  • Food supply per person: 2,196 kcal a day (1961) → 2,884 kcal a day (2013).
  • Yet in 2025, 645 million people were still chronically undernourished.
Quick check
?Food supply rose from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal per person per day (1961–2013). What was the percentage increase?
Mission 6

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Thomas Malthus (1798): "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Population doubles again and again (1, 2, 4, 8…); food grows by equal steps (1, 2, 3, 4…).
  • So population would outrun food, until checks cut it:
    • preventive checks lower births, e.g. marrying later
    • positive checks raise deaths, e.g. famine and disease.
Mission 6

Boserup: the optimistic view

  • Ester Boserup (1965) argued the reverse: "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • More people push farmers to find new methods, such as irrigation for two or three crops a year and Green Revolution seeds and fertiliser.
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.
Quick check
?"As villages grew, farmers dug irrigation channels and began growing two crops a year." Which view does this support?
Mission 6

Who was right?

  • Globally, yields roughly tripled while population grew about 2.6 times (1961–2023): closer to Boserup.
  • But 645 million people were undernourished in 2025: food is not shared evenly.
  • Malthus did not foresee new technology, trade in food, or birth rates falling as incomes rise.
  • Boserup assumed slow growth and time to adapt; fast growth or worn-out soils can outpace new methods.
  • One study of Bangladeshi villages found the answer "depends in a large part on temporal and spatial scale".
Mission check

?In an imaginary country, population doubled between 1990 and 2020, while food production tripled. What happened to food per person?

Since 1961, population grew about 2.6 times, but yields roughly tripled, so food per person rose (2,196 → 2,884 kcal a day, 1961–2013). Yet 645 million people are still undernourished: enough food on average does not mean food for all.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In 2025, solar power generation grew by about 30%. In the same year, the world used more fossil fuel than ever. How can both be true?

Make a guess: In 2025, solar power generation grew by about 30%. In the same year, the world used more fossil fuel than ever. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.3a global variation in energy demand and production; influence of population growth, wealth and technology
Mission 7

A century of energy

  • World energy use: about 18,000 TWh (1920) → about 172,000 TWh (2023): about 9.6 times as much.
  • World population: 1.90 billion → 8.09 billion: about 4.3 times.
  • In 1920, coal gave about 55% and traditional biomass (wood, dung) about 39% of energy.
  • In 2023, oil, gas and coal together gave about 82%.
Mission 7

Why use keeps rising

  • Rising population: more people need heat, light, food and transport.
  • Economic development (wealth): industry, cars, flights and appliances. Energy use per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Technology: new machines and devices use energy; new technology also unlocks new supplies, such as US oil from fracking.
Quick check
?From 1920 to 2023, world energy use grew about 9.6 times and population about 4.3 times. How did energy use per person change?
Mission 7

Who uses the energy?

  • Energy use per person, 2024: Qatar 214,520 kWh; USA 76,800; China 34,514; UK 28,016; world average 21,654; India 7,813.
  • Rwanda, 2023: about 530 kWh per person.
  • Pattern: highest in oil-rich Gulf states and high-income countries; lowest in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 7

Who produces it? Surplus and deficit

  • Top oil producers, 2023: USA 22%, Saudi Arabia 11%, Russia 11% of world output.
  • Fossil fuel produced compared with all energy used, 2024 (our calculation):
    • Qatar about 4.1 times; Norway 3.8; Australia 3.0; Saudi Arabia 2.2; Russia 1.7: energy surplus.
    • UK about 0.34; Germany 0.08; Japan almost 0: energy deficit, so they must import.
  • A country whose demand is greater than its own production has an energy gap.
Mission 7

Energy security

  • Energy security: "reliable, affordable access to all fuels and energy sources" (IEA).
  • A country that imports most of its energy is less secure.
  • Singapore makes about 95% of its electricity from imported natural gas.
  • Jordan imported energy equal to 95% of its supply in 2022.
Quick check
?Which country is most likely to face energy insecurity?
Mission 7

What affects supply?

  • Physical: where oil, gas, coal and uranium lie; sun, wind and steep rivers for renewables.
  • Cost of exploitation: deep-sea, Arctic or remote reserves cost more to extract and transport.
  • Technology: from 2009, "hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling" lifted US output to a record 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023.
  • Political: conflict or sanctions. In March–May 2026, oil through the Strait of Hormuz fell to about 2.7 million barrels a day, from about 20 million.
Mission 7

Adding, not replacing

  • 2025: total energy supply passed 600 exajoules, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".
  • But fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and still supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • So new solar adds to fossil fuels; it does not yet replace them.
Mission check

?A country's solar output doubles in five years. Over the same years its oil imports rise. Which explanation fits best?

World energy use keeps rising with population, wealth and technology. In 2025 solar grew about 30%, but total supply grew too, and fossil fuels still gave 86%: renewables are adding to, not yet replacing, fossil fuels.

Mission 8

Mission 8: If wind is free and never runs out, why don't we get all our electricity from it?

Make a guess: If wind is free and never runs out, why don't we get all our electricity from it?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.3b advantages and disadvantages of non-renewable (coal, oil, gas, uranium, shale) and renewable (solar, wind, HEP, geothermal, biomass) energy
Mission 8

Two kinds of energy

  • Renewable: will not run out on a human timescale, because nature keeps renewing it.
  • Non-renewable: a fixed stock in the ground that is not replaced on a human timescale, so it runs out as we use it.
  • Renewable: solar, wind, hydro (HEP), tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass.
  • Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium).
  • Shale gas and shale oil are fossil fuels too: just gas and oil trapped in a tight rock called shale.
Mission 8

What powers the world?

  • In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied 86% of the world's total energy.
  • Solar grew by 30% that year, and renewables were the largest source of growth.
  • So renewables are growing fast, but from a small base.
Quick check
?Which energy source is non-renewable?
Mission 8

Non-renewables: for and against

  • Coal, oil, gas: reliable on demand; supply chains exist. But burning them releases CO₂, and the stock runs out.
  • Gas is flexible: power stations can ramp up quickly to meet peaks, and it emits the least carbon of the fossil fuels.
  • Nuclear (uranium): very low carbon, steady output. But it makes radioactive waste and stations are expensive and slow to build.
  • Shale gas: a new home supply, but fracking in Lancashire triggered a magnitude 2.9 earthquake in 2019.
Mission 8

Renewables: for and against

  • No fuel to buy and very low emissions once built.
  • Wind and solar are variable: no output on calm days or at night.
  • HEP: steady and controllable, but dams flood valleys and block fish.
  • Geothermal: steady, but only where hot rocks are near the surface (e.g. Iceland).
  • Biomass: can be stored and burnt on demand, but needs land and must be regrown to be renewable.
  • Tidal: predictable, but costly and can change estuary habitats. Wave: varies with the weather and is costly.
Quick check
?Which is the main disadvantage of solar power?
Mission 8

Why not all wind?

  • Wind is free, but it varies: on calm days turbines make little.
  • Demand does not stop, so the grid needs sources it can turn up on demand (gas, HEP, biomass), storage, or imports.
  • The UK government calls gas "flexible and reliable", easy to increase for short peaks.
  • So countries build an energy mix: wind is a big part, not the whole.
Mission check

?A small island has plenty of sunshine and plans to get all its electricity from solar panels. What is the biggest problem it must solve?

Free energy is not the same as reliable energy. Wind and solar vary, so a grid also needs storage, imports or sources it can turn up on demand: that is why countries use a mix.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Britain ended coal power in 2024, but India still relies on coal. Which is managing energy sustainably?

Make a guess: Britain ended coal power in 2024, but India still relies on coal. Which is managing energy sustainably?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 4.3 energy resource management case studies in a developed country (UK) and a developing or emerging country (India)
  • 4.3c sustainable energy management through education, efficiency and conservation in industry, transport and the home
Mission 9

Two countries

  • UK: Human Development Index (HDI) 0.946, in the UN's "very high" group.
  • India: HDI 0.685, in the "medium" group.
  • The UK is a developed country; India is an emerging one.
Mission 9

India's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: coal 57%, oil 27%, gas 6%, hydro 3%, solar 3%, wind 2%, nuclear 1%.
  • So about 90% came from fossil fuels.
  • These figures leave out traditional fuels such as fuelwood and dung, which many rural homes still burn.
  • Electricity, 2025: coal 71%, solar 9%, hydro 9%, wind 5%, nuclear 3%, gas 2%.
Mission 9

The UK's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: oil 39%, gas 32%, wind 11%, nuclear 5%, coal 2%; other renewables make up the rest.
  • About 74% came from fossil fuels: oil mainly for transport, gas for heating and power.
  • Electricity, 2025: gas 31%, wind 29%, bioenergy 14%, nuclear 12%, solar 7%, coal 0.1%.
Quick check
?What is the biggest difference between the two countries' electricity?
Mission 9

Impacts in India

  • Emissions: India's CO₂ rose from 584 million tonnes (1990) to 3,176 million tonnes (2024), about 5.4 times.
  • But per person it is 2.2 tonnes, under half the UK's 4.6.
  • Imports: India produces only about 13% as much oil as it uses, so most must be imported.
  • Health: smoke from cooking on open fires and inefficient stoves causes household air pollution. The share of Indians cooking with clean fuels rose from 49% (2015) to 81% (2024).
Mission 9

Impacts in the UK

  • Emissions: UK CO₂ fell from 602 million tonnes (1990) to 318 million tonnes (2024), down 47%.
  • Coal ended: from about 40% of electricity in 2012 to 0% after Ratcliffe-on-Soar closed in 2024.
  • Imports: the UK produced about 50% of the gas it used in 2024, down from 112% in 2000.
Quick check
?India's total CO₂ is about ten times the UK's, but its CO₂ per person is lower. Why?
Mission 9

India: managing energy

  • Targets: by 2030, about 50% of power capacity from non-fossil sources, and a 45% cut in emissions per unit of GDP from 2005 levels.
  • Solar: from 0.5% of electricity (2015) to 9.4% (2025).
  • Clean cooking: the Ujjwala scheme has given poor households over 105 million LPG gas connections, with a free first refill and stove.
  • Industry: the PAT scheme sets energy-saving targets for big factories.
Mission 9

UK: managing energy

  • Law: the Climate Change Act requires UK emissions in 2050 to be at least 100% lower than in 1990: net zero.
  • Coal: coal power ended in 2024, and new coal mines are to be banned.
  • Offshore wind: a January 2026 auction secured a record 8.4 GW.
  • Homes: the £1 billion Great British Insulation Scheme upgraded 85,900 homes by October 2025.
Quick check
?Which strategy cuts demand for energy rather than adding new supply?
Mission 9

Education, efficiency, conservation

  • Education: India's NDC calls for a mass movement for "Lifestyle for Environment" (LiFE). In the UK in 2026, 80% said they give at least a fair amount of thought to saving energy at home.
  • Efficiency in industry: India's PAT scheme, first cycle, saved 8.67 million tonnes of oil equivalent against a target of 6.686. UK factories agree energy-efficiency targets in return for a tax discount.
  • Transport: the UK has committed to phasing out new cars that rely only on petrol or diesel engines by 2030.
  • Home: UK insulation; Indian LPG replacing wood smoke.
Mission 9

Carbon and ecological footprints

  • Carbon footprint: the greenhouse gases caused by a person, product or country.
  • Ecological footprint: the land and sea area needed to supply what people use and absorb their waste.
  • Calculate: CO₂ per person in 2024: UK 4.6 t, India 2.2 t. 4.6 ÷ 2.2 ≈ 2.1: the average UK resident emitted about twice as much.
  • These are emissions made inside each country; goods imported from abroad are counted where they are made.
Quick check
?A UK household fits loft insulation and turns its thermostat down by one degree. Which kind of sustainable energy management is this?
Mission 9

Judging sustainability

  • UK: emissions down 47%, coal gone, wind rising, net zero in law. But fossil fuels still give about 74% of energy, and half of gas is imported.
  • India: renewables and clean cooking rising fast, from a low base. But coal still gives 71% of electricity and emissions keep growing.
  • Different starting points: India's energy use per person is still rising; the UK's is falling.
Mission check

?Country Z gets most of its electricity from coal, its demand is rising fast, and it is adding solar faster than any other source. Which judgement fits best?

Like India, Country Z is moving towards sustainability while fossil fuels still dominate. The UK has gone further (coal gone, emissions down 47%) but still relies on oil and gas. Neither is finished: sustainability is judged by direction and by the mix today.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sectors: primary (extract), secondary (make), tertiary (serve), quaternary (knowledge).
  • HICs (UK) have most workers in services; LICs (Ethiopia) in farming; MICs (India) in between.
  • Clark-Fisher: primary → secondary → tertiary as countries develop.
  • Causes: raw materials, mechanisation, globalisation, demographic change, government policy.

Mission 2

  • Location factors: land, labour, raw materials, energy, transport, markets, policy, technology, communications, containerisation; the most important depends on the sector.
  • Factors change: containerisation and electricity free many firms from raw materials and coalfields; grants and climate policy reshape heavy industry. Such firms are footloose.

Mission 3

  • Manufacturing jobs: 24.9% → 6.7% (1978–2026); services 84.8%.
  • Positives: high-value services, lower emissions. Negatives: closures (Redcar), regional gaps.

Mission 4

  • Gains: life expectancy 61 → 72; under-5 deaths 110 → 27 per 1,000; electricity 99.9%; extreme poverty 27% → 3% (2011–23).
  • Costs: PM2.5 54 µg/m³ (above WHO's 35 interim target) and waste in cities. Slums and too few new jobs outside farming.

Mission 5

  • Mumbai grew fast through migration and natural increase. About half its people lived in slums in 2011, concentrated where land was cheap and marginal, as in M/East.
  • Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, services) and challenges (housing, water, waste, pollution, informal work).

Mission 6

  • Food per person has risen since 1961, but hunger remains.
  • Malthus: population outruns food until checks act. Boserup: population pressure drives new farming methods. Global data fit Boserup better, but scale matters.

Mission 7

  • Energy use rose about 9.6 times since 1920, driven by population, development and technology; it is very unequal per person.
  • Surplus countries produce more than they use; deficit countries import and are less energy secure.

Mission 8

  • Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are renewed by nature; non-renewable fossil fuels and uranium are fixed stocks.
  • Every source has pros and cons; wind and solar are low-carbon but variable, so they work best in a mix.

Mission 9

  • India (medium HDI): coal 57% of energy, 71% of electricity; emissions rising; managing through solar, clean cooking (Ujjwala) and industrial efficiency (PAT). UK (very high HDI): fossil fuels about 74% of energy, coal power ended 2024; emissions down 47% since 1990; net zero law, offshore wind, insulation.
  • Sustainable management uses education, efficiency and conservation in industry, transport and the home.
