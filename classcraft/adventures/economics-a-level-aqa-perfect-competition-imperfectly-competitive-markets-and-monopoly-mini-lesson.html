Mission 1

Mission 1: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Make a guess: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Mission 1

Thinking at the margin

  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Marginal revenue (MR): the extra revenue from selling one more unit.
  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of making one more unit.
  • MR > MC: one more unit adds to profit. Make more.
  • MR < MC: one more unit cuts profit. Make less.
  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR.
  • Say: "profit is maximised where MC = MR" — not: "where revenue is highest" or "where cost per unit is lowest".

?Illustrative data. A bakery sells batches of bread at a fixed price of 5 per batch, however many it sells. Its fixed costs are 4. The marginal cost of the 1st to 5th batches is 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8. How many batches maximise its profit?
Mission 1

MC = MR on the diagram

  • Find Q where MC cuts MR from below. That is the profit-maximising output.
  • Go up from that Q to the AR (demand) curve to read the price.
  • Go up to AC at the same Q to read cost per unit.
  • Profit = (AR − AC) × Q: the rectangle between price and AC.
  • If AR < AC at that output, MC = MR gives the smallest loss the firm can make while producing.
  • Say: "read the price off the demand (AR) curve" — not: "off the point where MC = MR".

Mission 1Evaluate

Do real firms set MC = MR?

It depends on …

  • Information: firms rarely know their exact MC and MR. Many use rules of thumb, such as adding a mark-up to cost.
  • Objectives: owners and managers may aim for growth, sales or a satisfactory profit instead (see Mission 2).
  • Time: a firm may hold prices below the short-run profit-maximising level to keep rivals out or win loyal buyers, aiming at long-run profit.
  • Competition: in fierce competition, firms that stray far from maximum profit may not survive.
  • Judge: MC = MR is a model of how profit-seeking firms behave, more accurate in competitive markets than as a description of how managers decide.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A water park makes a large profit each year. It is thinking of opening for one extra hour every evening. The extra hour would bring in 900 of ticket and food sales. It would add 1,100 to costs (staff, lighting, heating the pools). What should a profit-maximising water park do?

Profit rises only while each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Past that point, more sales mean less profit.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why might a business choose to earn less profit than it could?

Make a guess: Why might a business choose to earn less profit than it could?

Mission 2

Why aim for maximum profit?

  • Economic theory usually assumes firms maximise profit: output where MC = MR (see Mission 1).
  • Reasons a firm might:
  • Owners take the risk and want the reward (dividends, a higher share price).
  • Retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Profit builds a cushion for bad years and makes lenders and investors keen.
  • High profit signals success and pays for growth.
Mission 2

Three targets on one diagram

  • Profit maximisation: MC = MR.
  • Sales revenue maximisation: MR = 0. The last unit adds nothing to TR, so TR is at its peak.
  • Sales (volume) maximisation: sell as many units as possible while still making normal profit: AR = AC.
  • Moving from profit max to revenue max to sales max: more output, a lower price, less profit.
  • Say: "revenue is maximised where MR = 0" — not: "where MC = MR".
Mission 2

Worked example: three outputs

  • Illustrative data. AR = 24 − Q, so MR = 24 − 2Q. TC = 42 + 0.5Q², so MC = Q.
  • Profit max: 24 − 2Q = Q → Q = 8, P = 16. TR = 128, TC = 74, profit = 54.
  • Revenue max: 24 − 2Q = 0 → Q = 12, P = 12. TR = 144 (the highest), profit = 30.
  • Sales max: AR = AC at Q = 14, P = 10. TR = TC = 140: normal profit only.
  • Each target gives up some profit for size.

Change the target

Mission 2

Growth, market share, survival

  • Growth: a bigger firm can gain economies of scale and market power, and spreads its risks. Managers often gain pay and status from size.
  • Market share: a larger share of sales can bring power over price and a strong brand. Firms may cut prices now to win it.
  • Survival: the first aim for many new firms and in recessions. Cover costs, keep cash flowing.
  • Quality: a reputation for quality can justify higher prices.
  • Many of these can serve long-run profit while lowering profit today.
Mission 2

Satisficing

  • Satisficing: aiming for a satisfactory level of profit, not the maximum. The term comes from the economist Herbert Simon.
  • Why firms satisfice:
  • Owners and managers are different people. Managers need only enough profit to keep shareholders content.
  • Firms have several stakeholders to keep happy at once.
  • Information is limited. Finding the exact MC = MR point is costly.
  • Satisficing is a minimum target, not a lack of effort. Profit below it brings pressure for change.
Mission 2

CSR and social objectives

  • Corporate social responsibility (CSR): a firm takes account of its impact on society and the environment, beyond what the law requires.
  • Examples: cutting emissions, fair supplier contracts, community projects, staff welfare.
  • Social enterprises and not-for-profit organisations put a social aim first.
  • Real case: in 2022 the founder of the outdoor clothing firm Patagonia transferred its ownership to a trust and a non-profit body. Profit not reinvested in the firm goes to fighting climate change.
  • For CSR: it can attract customers and staff, and protect the brand: long-run profit.
  • Against: Milton Friedman (1970) argued a firm's social responsibility is to increase its profits within the rules; owners, not managers, should choose how to spend on good causes.
Quick check
?A new food-delivery firm sets its prices below its costs to sign up as many customers as possible. It expects to make losses for several years. Which objective best explains this?
Mission 2Evaluate

Do firms really maximise profit?

It depends on …

  • Who controls the firm: an owner-manager may maximise profit; managers of a large company with many small shareholders have more room to pursue other goals.
  • Competition: in fiercely competitive markets, firms that do not aim at maximum profit may not survive.
  • Time horizon: growth, market share and CSR can be routes to long-run profit, so they may not be alternatives at all.
  • Information: firms rarely know MC and MR exactly; rules of thumb can approximate maximum profit.
  • Judge: profit maximisation is a useful assumption for predicting behaviour, but real firms often balance several objectives.
Mission check

?The managers of a large hotel chain aim each year for a level of profit that keeps its shareholders content. Once they reach it, they do not push for more. They avoid price rises and cost cuts that would upset staff and regular guests, even though these would raise profit. Which objective best describes their behaviour?

A firm can earn less than the maximum on purpose when other goals, or other people, matter to whoever runs it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When the people who own a company are not the people who run it, whose goals win?

Make a guess: A large company is owned by thousands of shareholders but run by a team of hired managers. Whose goals usually win?

Mission 3

Owning versus controlling

  • In a small firm (a sole trader or a partnership) the owners usually run the business themselves.
  • A large public limited company (plc) may have thousands of shareholders. They own the company.
  • Shareholders elect a board of directors, who appoint managers to run the firm day to day. The managers control it.
  • This split is the divorce (separation) of ownership and control.
  • It lets a firm raise huge sums from many owners and hire expert managers.
  • But owners and managers may want different things.
  • Say: "a public limited company is privately owned by its shareholders" — not: "a plc is in the public sector".
Mission 3

The principal–agent problem

  • A principal hires an agent to act on the principal's behalf.
  • Here the shareholders are the principals; the managers are the agents.
  • The principal–agent problem: the agent may pursue their own goals rather than the principal's.
  • Two conditions make it a problem:
  • Different objectives: owners want profit (and a rising share price); managers may want pay, status, power or an easy life.
  • Asymmetric information: managers know far more about the business than scattered owners, so owners cannot easily check what managers do.
  • Other examples: voters (principals) and politicians (agents); a patient and a doctor; a homeowner and a builder.
Quick check
?Which situation is the clearest example of the principal–agent problem?
Mission 3

What might managers aim for instead?

  • If owners cannot fully control managers, the firm may not maximise profit (MC = MR).
  • Managerial utility maximisation (Williamson): managers pursue their own satisfaction: salary, perks, large staffs, status.
  • Sales revenue maximisation (Baumol): managers' pay and prestige often rise with the firm's size, so they push revenue (MR = 0) rather than profit.
  • Growth maximisation: a bigger firm brings more power and is harder to take over.
  • Satisficing: managers aim for just enough profit to keep shareholders content, then follow other goals.
  • These objectives are compared on diagrams in Mission 2.
Mission 3

Lining up the incentives

Owners try to make the agents' goals match their own:

  • Performance-related pay: bonuses linked to profit or the share price.
  • Share options: managers gain if the share price rises, so they think like owners.
  • Monitoring: independent (non-executive) directors, audited accounts, published reports.
  • Shareholder votes: owners can vote down pay deals or remove directors.
  • UK example: since 2013, shareholders in UK quoted companies have a binding vote on the directors' pay policy.
  • Threat of takeover: if managers waste profit, the share price falls, a rival may buy the firm cheaply, and the managers lose their jobs.
  • Each fix has limits: targets can be gamed, and bonuses tied to this year's results can encourage short-termism.
Quick check
?To align managers with shareholders, a company pays its chief executive a large bonus if the share price is higher at the end of this year. Which unintended effect is most likely?
Mission 3Evaluate

How much does the divorce matter?

  • The principal–agent problem is a strong reason firms may not maximise profit. How serious it is depends on:
  • How spread out ownership is: a few large shareholders (e.g. pension funds) can monitor managers far better than thousands of small ones.
  • Information: better accounts, audits and independent directors shrink the information gap.
  • Competition: in a highly competitive market, a firm that wastes money makes losses and may not survive, which limits managers' freedom.
  • Pay design: rewards tied to long-run value align goals better than one-year targets, but no scheme is perfect.
  • Takeover threat: works only if shares are traded and rivals can buy them.
Mission check

?Lena founded a chain of gyms and still owns all of it. She has moved abroad and hired a chief executive on a salary plus a bonus based on the number of members. Lena checks the accounts twice a year. Which outcome is most likely?

An agent works towards whatever the reward measures, so the reward must measure what the owner wants.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Make a guess: When a product is very profitable and anyone can start selling it, where do the profits go?

Mission 4

The long run

  • Long run: all factors can change. Firms can enter or leave the industry.
  • In perfect competition there are no barriers, so entry and exit are free.
  • Normal profit: the minimum return needed to keep the firm's resources in this industry. It is their opportunity cost.
  • Normal profit is counted inside AC. So at P = AC, the owners earn just enough to stay.
  • Say: "normal profit" — not: "no profit". The owners are still paid their opportunity cost.
Mission 4

Supernormal profit attracts entry

  • Build it in steps (two panels: industry and firm):
  • 1. Industry: D and S1 set price P1, above minimum AC. Firm: supernormal profit rectangle.
  • 2. Profit is a signal: new firms enter. Industry supply shifts right: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price falls to P2 = minimum AC. Each firm cuts output back to the lowest point of AC.
  • 4. Supernormal profit is gone: normal profit only. Entry stops.
Quick check
?A perfectly competitive industry is earning supernormal profit. New firms enter until the long run is reached. What happens to output?
Mission 4

Losses drive exit

  • The mirror image:
  • 1. Price below minimum AC: firms make losses.
  • 2. In the long run, loss-making firms leave. Industry supply shifts left: S1 → S2.
  • 3. Price rises back to minimum AC.
  • 4. The firms that remain make normal profit. Exit stops.
Mission 4

Long-run equilibrium

  • Entry and exit stop only when price = minimum AC.
  • Firm in long-run equilibrium: P = AR = MR = MC = AC (at its minimum).
  • Each firm makes normal profit: no reason to enter or leave.
  • Industry: D and S set this price; the number of firms adjusts to match demand.
Mission 4Graph lab

A demand boom

Illustrative data. 240 identical firms, each making normal profit at a price of 26. Assume each firm's costs do not change as the industry grows.

  • Takeaway: entry continues until price is back at minimum AC (26). More firms; same output each.
Mission 4

Worked example: the boom, step by step

  • Illustrative data. Each firm: minimum AC = 26 at 30 units.
  • Start: market demand at 26 is 7,200 units → 7,200 ÷ 30 = 240 firms.
  • Short run: demand rises. Still 240 firms. Price jumps to 43; each firm makes 40 units.
    • Industry output: 240 × 40 = 9,600.
    • Profit per firm: (43 − 28) × 40 = 600 (supernormal profit). AC at 40 units = 28.
  • Long run: firms enter until price = 26 again.
    • Market demand at 26 is now 14,700 units → 14,700 ÷ 30 = 490 firms.
  • Industry output doubles and more; each firm is back to 30 units and normal profit.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a perfectly competitive industry, each firm's minimum AC is 4 at an output of 500 units. Today 100 firms each produce 550 units and sell at 6. At a price of 4, market demand would be 60,000 units. Costs do not change as firms enter. How many firms will there be in the long run?
Mission 4

Why the long run is efficient

  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC. The value of the last unit to buyers equals its cost.
  • Productive efficiency: output at minimum AC, the lowest cost per unit.
  • Consumers pay the lowest price that firms can sustain: minimum AC.
Mission 4Evaluate

A benchmark, not a description

  • Few real markets meet every assumption: products differ, information is costly, entry takes time and money.
  • It depends on economies of scale: where minimum efficient scale is large, many small firms would each have high costs. A few big firms can produce more cheaply.
  • It depends on innovation: with only normal profit in the long run, and rivals able to copy any idea, firms have little to fund or reward R&D. Dynamic efficiency may be weak.
  • It depends on time: entry and exit can take years, so supernormal profits and losses can last.
  • Still useful: it is the benchmark against which monopoly and other structures are judged.
Mission check

?A city's market for bicycle repairs is perfectly competitive and in long-run equilibrium. The city then charges every repair firm a fixed yearly fee, whatever its output. Demand is unchanged. What happens in the long run?

Free entry and exit push price to minimum AC. Any change in costs or demand is eventually competed into the price.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Make a guess: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Mission 5

Many firms, different products

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a differentiated product.
  • Low barriers to entry and exit.
  • Each firm has a little price-setting power: its product is not identical to rivals'.
  • Examples: cafés, hairdressers, restaurants, takeaways, a new style of café such as bubble tea.
  • Firms are small, so each one acts independently: it does not plan around one rival's reaction.
Mission 5

Standing out: non-price competition

  • Product differentiation: real or perceived differences (quality, design, location, service, brand).
  • Non-price competition: competing on anything but price: branding, advertising, loyalty cards, opening hours, new menu items.
  • Aim 1: shift the firm's demand curve right (more customers at each price).
  • Aim 2: make demand less price elastic (customers less willing to switch).
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve slopes downward because its product is differentiated" — not: "because it has few rivals".
Quick check
?A hairdresser in a busy town centre raises its price slightly. It loses some customers but not all of them. Which best explains why?
Mission 5

The short run

  • Profit is maximised where MR = MC. Price is read up to the AR curve.
  • AR > AC: supernormal profit. AR < AC: a loss. AR = AC: normal profit.
  • In the short run, this looks just like the monopoly diagram. The difference is what happens next.
Mission 5

Worked example: short-run profit

  • Illustrative data. A new bubble tea shop.
  • Demand: AR = 30 − 0.2Q, so MR = 30 − 0.4Q.
  • Costs: MC = 0.2Q. AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 30 − 0.4Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 50.
  • Price from AR: 30 − 0.2 × 50 = 20.
  • AC at Q = 50: 360/50 + 0.1 × 50 = 7.2 + 5 = 12.2.
  • Supernormal profit = (AR − AC) × Q = (20 − 12.2) × 50 = 390.
Mission 5

From short run to long run

  • Profit above normal is a signal. Barriers are low, so new firms enter.
  • Each firm loses some customers to the newcomers: its demand curve shifts left.
  • More close substitutes: demand becomes more price elastic.
  • Entry stops when AR just touches (is tangent to) AC: normal profit.
  • Losses work the other way: firms leave, and each remaining firm's demand shifts right until AR touches AC again.
  • Say: "each firm's demand curve shifts left as firms enter" — not: "the market demand curve falls".

Mission 5

Worked example: the long run

  • Illustrative data. After entry, each shop's demand: AR = 18 − 0.125Q, so MR = 18 − 0.25Q.
  • Costs are unchanged: MC = 0.2Q; AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 18 − 0.25Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 40.
  • Price: 18 − 0.125 × 40 = 13.
  • AC at Q = 40: 360/40 + 0.1 × 40 = 9 + 4 = 13.
  • P = AC: profit per unit 0. Normal profit.
  • PED at the chosen price: SR −2; LR −2.6. Demand is more elastic after entry.
Mission 5

Excess capacity

  • The long-run tangency is on the falling part of AC.
  • So output is below the output at the lowest AC: excess capacity.
  • In the previous screen: output 40; lowest AC (12) at 60. Excess capacity = 60 − 40 = 20 units.
  • Not productively efficient: P = AC = 13 > lowest AC = 12.
  • Not allocatively efficient: P = 13 > MC = 8. Buyers value one more unit more than it costs to make.
  • Say: "excess capacity: output is below the level that minimises AC" — not: "the firm has too many workers".

Mission 5

Advertising

  • Advertising aims to shift the firm's demand right and make it less price elastic (brand loyalty).
  • It also raises costs: AC shifts up.
  • Short run: if extra sales outweigh the extra cost, profit rises.
  • Long run: rivals copy the idea, and new firms enter. Profit returns to normal.
  • Informative advertising (prices, opening hours, what is new) helps buyers choose.
  • Persuasive advertising (image, brand) may add to costs and prices without making the product better.
Quick check
?A nail salon launches a successful advertising campaign that makes its customers more loyal. In the short run, what happens to the salon's demand curve?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is excess capacity a waste?

  • It depends on how much buyers value variety: a slightly higher price than perfect competition may be the cost of choice.
  • It depends on how elastic demand is: with many close substitutes, demand is very elastic, the tangency is near the lowest AC, and the excess capacity is small.
  • It depends on the type of advertising: informative or persuasive.
  • It depends on dynamic efficiency: normal profit leaves little to fund research, but rivalry pushes firms to try new products.
  • It depends on how realistic the model is: some firms build brands, or win locations, that rivals cannot easily copy.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a town has a few gyms making large profits. Opening a gym is easy, and each gym differs a little in equipment and classes. New gyms open until the market settles. Which describes a typical gym in the new long-run equilibrium?

Low barriers mean profit above normal attracts entry until each firm's AR just touches AC, on the falling part of AC.

Mission 6

A few firms dominate

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • High barriers to entry (economies of scale, brands, patents, high start-up costs).
  • Products may be identical (cement, crude oil) or differentiated (cars, smartphones, supermarkets).
  • Much non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty schemes.
  • Structure (few firms, high concentration) is one way to spot it. Conduct (firms reacting to each other) is another. A market can be concentrated without firms behaving like oligopolists.

Mission 6

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: each firm's best choice depends on what its rivals do.
  • So every decision (price, output, advertising) brings uncertainty: how will rivals react?
  • If one firm cuts its price, rivals may match the cut to protect their sales. All end up with lower prices and little extra market share.
  • If one firm raises its price, rivals may not follow, and it loses customers to them.
  • Matching cuts can spiral into a price war: each firm undercuts the others, and profits fall for all.
  • Say: "oligopolists are interdependent" — not: "oligopolists ignore their rivals" or "oligopolists always collude".
Mission 6

Compete or collude?

  • Non-collusive oligopoly: firms compete; each decides alone.
  • Collusive oligopoly: firms cooperate to limit competition, e.g. fixing prices or sharing out markets.
  • Overt (explicit) collusion: a formal agreement. A cartel is a group of firms that agree on prices or output.
  • Tacit collusion: no agreement, but firms behave as if there were one, e.g. following a price leader.
  • Why collude? Less uncertainty, higher joint profit, keeping new firms out.
  • Why not? Cartels are illegal in most countries, there may be fines, and members may cheat.
Mission 6

When does a cartel work?

  • A cartel is more likely to hold when there are:
    • few firms (easier to agree and to watch each other)
    • similar costs and similar products (easier to agree one price)
    • high barriers to entry (no newcomers to undercut the cartel)
    • easy monitoring of rivals' prices and output (cheating gets spotted)
    • stable demand (less pressure to break ranks)
    • weak enforcement of competition law
  • Consequences: higher prices, lower output, high profit for members, less choice; buyers lose surplus.
Quick check
?Four firms in a market are thinking of forming a cartel. Which set of conditions makes the cartel most likely to last?
Mission 6

A cartel acts like a monopoly

  • The members act as one firm: they face the industry demand curve.
  • Joint profit is greatest where industry MR = MC. Price is read up to AR.
  • Members then share the output out as quotas.
  • Illustrative data: AR = 100 − Q; MC = AC = 20 for every member.
    • Cartel: MR = 100 − 2Q = 20, so Q = 40, P = 60.
    • Competitive benchmark: P = MC = 20, Q = 80.
Mission 6

Worked example: the pull to cheat

  • Illustrative data: two firms, A and B. AR = 100 − Q. MC = AC = 20.
  • Cartel quotas: 20 each. Q = 40, P = 60. Profit each: (60 − 20) × 20 = 800.
  • A secretly produces 25. Q = 45, so P = 100 − 45 = 55.
    • A: (55 − 20) × 25 = 875. A gains.
    • B: (55 − 20) × 20 = 700. B loses.
  • If both produce 25: Q = 50, P = 50. Each earns (50 − 20) × 25 = 750.
  • Each firm gains by cheating, whatever the other does. Together they lose. (Payoff matrix in another lesson.)

Mission 6

Price leadership and tacit collusion

  • Price leadership: one firm changes its price; the others follow.
    • Dominant-firm leadership: the largest firm leads.
    • Barometric leadership: a firm that reads market conditions well leads.
  • No meeting and no agreement: this is tacit collusion, which is hard to prove.
  • Result: prices that move together and stay stable for long periods.
Mission 6

The kinked demand curve

  • A model of price rigidity in non-collusive oligopoly.
  • Assumption: rivals do not follow a price rise, but do match a price cut.
  • Above the current price: demand is relatively price elastic (a rise loses many sales).
  • Below the current price: demand is relatively price inelastic (a cut wins few sales).
  • So the demand (AR) curve has a kink at the current price, and MR has a vertical gap below the kink.
  • MC can rise or fall within the gap with no change in profit-maximising price or output.
  • Limits: it does not explain how the starting price was set, and firms do change prices (e.g. when all face a cost rise).
Mission 6

Worked example: PED and revenue at the kink

  • Illustrative data. Current price 10; quantity 100. Total revenue = 10 × 100 = 1,000.
  • Rise to 11: rivals do not follow; Q falls to 80.
    • PED = −20% ÷ +10% = −2 (elastic). TR = 11 × 80 = 880. TR falls.
  • Cut to 9: rivals match; Q rises to only 104.
    • PED = +4% ÷ −10% = −0.4 (inelastic). TR = 9 × 104 = 936. TR falls.
  • Either change cuts revenue, so the firm keeps the price at 10.
  • MR just left of the kink = 5; just right of it, MR is below zero (−15). Any MC curve passing through this gap (here, MC of 5 or less at Q = 100) keeps output at 100.

Mission 6

Real cartels

  • OPEC: set up in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. Members agree production targets to influence the world oil price.
  • Truck makers (EU): in 2016 the European Commission fined truck makers a total of €2.93 billion for colluding on prices from 1997 to 2011.
  • One member, MAN, revealed the cartel and was not fined: the law rewards the first to confess.
  • In the UK, cartels are illegal under the Competition Act 1998. The CMA investigates them.
  • OPEC is made up of governments, not private firms. Competition laws on cartels do not apply to it in the same way.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is oligopoly good or bad?

  • Costs: prices above MC (allocative inefficiency); collusion transfers surplus to firms; heavy advertising may add to costs.
  • Benefits: economies of scale can mean low average costs; supernormal profit can fund research and new products (dynamic efficiency); non-price competition can raise quality and choice.
  • It depends on conduct: fierce rivalry (even price wars) helps buyers; collusion harms them.
  • It depends on barriers: if entry is possible, the threat of new firms limits high prices.
  • It depends on regulation: the chance of being caught and fined.

In oligopoly, every price decision depends on how rivals react. That is why prices often stay put or move together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR (MC cutting MR from below); the price is read off the AR (demand) curve.

Mission 2

  • Theory assumes profit maximisation (MC = MR), but firms may aim for growth, market share, survival, satisfactory profit or social goals.
  • Revenue maximisation is at MR = 0 and sales maximisation at AR = AC: more output, a lower price, less profit.

Mission 3

  • When owners (principals) hire managers (agents), different goals plus asymmetric information let managers pursue their own aims.
  • So firms may pursue sales, growth, managerial utility or satisficing instead of maximum profit.

Mission 4

  • Supernormal profit attracts entry; losses cause exit. Both stop at price = minimum AC.
  • Long-run equilibrium: P = MR = MC = minimum AC; normal profit (zero economic profit).
  • It is allocatively and productively efficient.

Mission 5

  • Many firms, differentiated products, low barriers: each firm has a downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand curve.
  • Short run: MR = MC; profit above normal or a loss is possible. Long run: entry or exit leaves normal profit (AR tangent to AC).
  • Long run: excess capacity and P > MC, but buyers get variety.

Mission 6

  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, high barriers, interdependence and uncertainty.
  • Firms can compete or collude: overtly (cartels) or tacitly (price leadership). Cartels act like a monopoly but each member is tempted to cheat.
  • Kinked demand curve: rivals match cuts but not rises, so prices are rigid; MC can move within the MR gap.
