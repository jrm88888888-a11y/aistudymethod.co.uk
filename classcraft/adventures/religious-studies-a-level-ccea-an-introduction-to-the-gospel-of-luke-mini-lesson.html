Learning focus

Background: the making of Luke's Gospel

Luke's Gospel opens a window on first-century Palestine under Roman rule. To read it well you need the religious landscape: the Pharisees, lay experts in the Law who valued the oral tradition and the resurrection, and the Sadducees, the priestly aristocracy who accepted only the written Torah and rejected resurrection.

The dominant scholarly view is that Luke and Acts form a single two-volume work by one author, addressed to Theophilus. Traditionally the author is identified as Luke, a physician and companion of Paul. Key exam questions are its authorship, date, purpose and characteristics, and the historical accuracy and reliability of the Gospel.

AS focus: Luke–Acts is often read as a Christian apology — a reasoned defence and explanation of the faith for a Graeco-Roman readership.

Learning focus

The Infancy Narrative (Luke 1:5–2:52)

Luke's first two chapters are unique to his Gospel. The Infancy Narrative weaves together:

  • the annunciations of the births of John and Jesus (1:5–38);
  • the birth stories of Jesus and John (1:57–2:7);
  • the visit of the shepherds (2:2–20);
  • the Presentation in the Temple (2:21–40); and
  • the boy Jesus at the Temple (2:41–52).

Scholars debate whether these chapters are history, theology, or a carefully crafted overture that announces Luke's great themes — salvation, the Spirit, the poor and the fulfilment of Israel's hope.

The early ministry of Jesus

Luke sets the ministry in motion with three theologically loaded episodes:

  • the baptism and temptation of Jesus (3:21–22, 4:1–13) — Jesus is declared God's Son and proves faithful where Israel failed;
  • the rejection at Nazareth (4:14–30) — Jesus reads from Isaiah in the synagogue and announces good news to the poor, a programmatic scene that also foreshadows rejection; and
  • controversy with the religious authorities (5:17–26, 6:1–11) over forgiveness and the Sabbath.

Why Nazareth matters: many scholars treat the Nazareth sermon as Luke's manifesto — it defines Jesus' mission and previews the movement of salvation beyond Israel.

The Kingdom of God in the parables

Luke uses parables to open up the Kingdom of God. The specification groups them into two clusters:

  • Parables of mercy: the Good Samaritan (10:25–37), the Forgiving Father (15:11–32) and the Rich Man and Lazarus (16:19–31);
  • Parables of the Kingdom: the Sower (8:4–15) and the Great Banquet (14:12–24).

A parable is a short comparison that both reveals and conceals: it invites insiders in and leaves the complacent puzzled. Luke's mercy parables especially stress forgiveness, reversal and welcome for the outsider.

Miracles in Luke

Luke records miracles that, by definition, run contrary to the laws of nature. The specification asks about their meaning, purpose, theology and credibility:

  • Healing: the Centurion's Servant (7:1–10) and the Ten Lepers (17:11–19);
  • Raising to life: the Widow's Son (7:11–17);
  • Nature miracles: the Calming of the Storm (8:22–25) and the Feeding of the Five Thousand (9:10–17).

The credibility debate: are miracles historical events, symbolic theology, or accounts shaped by faith? Candidates should weigh both believing and sceptical responses.

Discipleship in Luke

Luke traces a demanding path of discipleship:

  • the call to discipleship (5:1–11, 27–32; 6:12–16);
  • the nature of mission (9:1–6; 10:1–16);
  • the demands of discipleship (9:57–62; 14:25–33) — counting the cost; and
  • the role of Peter (22:31–34, 54–62), whose failure and restoration make him a model of grace.
Learning focus

Salvation History

Salvation History is the idea that God works out a saving purpose across time. The most influential reading is that of Hans Conzelmann, whose study The Theology of St Luke argued that Luke divides history into three epochs: the age of Israel, the age of Jesus, and the age of the Church.

On this view, the Gospel is not simply biography but a theology of how salvation reaches the whole world. Candidates should be able to evaluate Conzelmann's scheme, noting scholars who question whether Luke really thought in such tidy stages.
