Mission 1: Why did a Galilean teacher keep clashing with Pharisees, priests and Romans?

Make a guess: Why did a Galilean teacher keep clashing with Pharisees, priests and Romans?

Mission 1

A land of client kings

  • 63 BCE: the Roman general Pompey takes Jerusalem. Judea comes under Roman power.
  • Herod the Great rules as a client king for Rome (37–4 BCE); he rebuilds the Temple on a grand scale.
  • After his death the kingdom is divided:
  • Archelaus: Judea and Samaria; deposed in 6 CE, when Judea is put under Roman prefects (Pontius Pilate, 26–36 CE).
  • Herod Antipas: Galilee and Perea (4 BCE–39 CE). Jesus grows up and teaches mostly in his territory; Jesus calls him "that fox" (Luke 13:32).
  • Philip: the north-east.
  • Luke dates John the Baptist's ministry by these rulers (Luke 3:1–2).
Mission 1

Living under Roman occupation

  • Taxes: tribute to Rome, tolls and local taxes, collected through tax collectors (e.g. Levi, Luke 5:27; Zacchaeus, Luke 19:2), who were widely despised.
  • The census of 6 CE (Quirinius) sparks a revolt led by Judas the Galilean (Acts 5:37): paying tribute to Caesar seen as betraying God as king.
  • Temple and high priest: the Romans appoint the high priest (Caiaphas, c. 18–36 CE). The Sanhedrin (council) handles Jewish affairs; Rome keeps power over capital punishment.
  • Crucifixion: a Roman punishment for rebels and slaves, a public warning.
  • Hopes of freedom grow; the Jewish War (66–70 CE) ends with the Temple destroyed in 70 CE, around the time the Gospels were being written.
Mission 1

Hellenism

  • Hellenism: the spread of Greek language, culture and ideas after Alexander the Great conquered the region (332 BCE).
  • Greek (koine) became the common language of the eastern Mediterranean: the New Testament is written in it, and Jews used a Greek translation of scripture, the Septuagint.
  • Greek-style cities: the Decapolis; Sepphoris (rebuilt by Antipas, a few miles from Nazareth); Tiberias.
  • 167 BCE: the Seleucid king Antiochus IV bans Jewish practice and defiles the Temple. The Maccabean revolt follows; the Temple is rededicated in 164 BCE (the origin of Hanukkah).
  • Jews responded to Hellenism along a spectrum: some embraced it, others resisted it fiercely. Several religious groups took shape in that struggle.

Mission 1

The Pharisees

  • A lay movement (not priests), respected by ordinary people; teachers in the synagogues.
  • Kept the written Torah and the oral tradition ("the tradition of the elders", Mark 7:3), applying the law to everyday life.
  • Extended purity rules (washing, tithing, food) beyond the Temple into the home.
  • Believed in the resurrection of the dead, angels and God's providence alongside human freedom.
  • Rabbinic Judaism grew mainly from the Pharisees after 70 CE.
  • In Luke, Pharisees invite Jesus to meals (Luke 7:36; 11:37; 14:1), warn him of danger (13:31) and criticise him (15:2). Paul was a Pharisee (Philippians 3:5).
Mission 1Read the source

The Sadducees

> "For the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit; but the Pharisees confess all of these." (Acts 23:8, WEBBE)

  • Linked to the priestly aristocracy and the Temple; wealthy and powerful.
  • Accepted only the written Torah as binding, and rejected the Pharisees' oral tradition.
  • Worked with Rome to keep order and their position; disappeared after the Temple fell in 70 CE.
  • In Luke 20:27–40 they test Jesus with a question about resurrection; Jesus answers from the Torah (Exodus 3:6).

Q1 On which beliefs did Pharisees and Sadducees disagree, according to Acts 23:8?

Show answer

Resurrection, angels and spirits.

Q2 Why would Jesus answer Sadducees from the Torah rather than from the prophets?

Show answer

They accepted only the written Torah as authoritative.

Mission 1

Other groups

  • Scribes: experts in the law; many were Pharisees.
  • Essenes: described by Josephus and Philo; strict purity, community of goods, expectation of the end; rejected the Temple priesthood as corrupt. Most scholars link them with Qumran and the Dead Sea Scrolls (found from 1947). Not named in the New Testament.
  • "Fourth philosophy" (Josephus): followers of Judas the Galilean, "no king but God". The Zealots as an organised party appear in the Jewish War (66 CE); one disciple is Simon "who was called the Zealot" (Luke 6:15).
  • Herodians: supporters of Herod's family (Mark 3:6; 12:13).
  • Samaritans: worshipped on Mount Gerizim, not in Jerusalem; accepted only their own Torah; long hostility with Jews.
  • "Sinners" and the people of the land: those seen as careless of the law, or outcasts by occupation.

Mission 1

Law, ethics and Jesus

  • Sabbath: debates on what counts as work; Jesus' disciples pick grain and he heals on the Sabbath. "The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath." (Luke 6:5, WEBBE)
  • Purity and table fellowship: "This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them." (Luke 15:2, WEBBE)
  • Rome and tax: "give to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's." (Luke 20:25, WEBBE)
  • Temple: Jesus drives out traders (Luke 19:45–46), a challenge to the priestly authorities.
  • Enemies: "love your enemies" (Luke 6:27) in a land under occupation.
  • Jesus argues within Judaism: he attends synagogue (Luke 4:16), quotes Torah and prophets, and shares the Pharisees' belief in resurrection.

Mission 1Diversity

Seeing the groups fairly

  • Most scholars date the Gospels to around or after 70 CE (c. 65–100), when the Church and the synagogue were separating: their portrait of the Pharisees reflects later conflict too.
  • "Pharisee" as a by-word for hypocrite is a Christian caricature; Jewish tradition remembers them as faithful teachers who made the law livable.
  • E. P. Sanders (Paul and Palestinian Judaism, 1977): first-century Judaism was a religion of grace and covenant, not of earning salvation by works (paraphrase).
  • Jesus' sharpest disputes are often with those closest to him in belief.
  • Groups disagreed fiercely but shared the one God, the Torah and the hope of God's kingdom.
Mission 1Evaluate

Religious divisions: problem or enrichment?

  • Claim: Religious divisions in first-century Palestine did more harm than good.
  • Problem: Divisions fed suspicion, violence and the alliance of Temple elite with Rome that led to Jesus' death.
  • Problem: Jewish–Samaritan hostility and contempt for "sinners" excluded many from community.
  • Enrichment: Debate sharpened thinking about the law (Sabbath, purity, love of neighbour); Jesus' teaching is shaped by it.
  • Enrichment: Different hopes (resurrection, the kingdom, purity) gave Christianity its language.
  • Today: Do divisions between and within religions harm, or force deeper ethical thought?
  • Judgement criteria: short- and long-term effects; whose perspective (Jewish, Christian, Roman); whether disagreement can be respectful.
  • Spec: "critically evaluate the problems posed by religious divisions and the opportunity for religious and ethical enrichment".

Mission 2: Luke never signs his Gospel. So who wrote it, when, and who was "Theophilus" meant to convince?

Make a guess: Luke never signs his Gospel. So who wrote it, when, and who was "Theophilus" meant to convince?

Mission 2

One work in two volumes

  • Luke and Acts are addressed to the same person, Theophilus, and Acts calls the Gospel "the first book" (Acts 1:1).
  • Same style, vocabulary and themes (the Spirit, prayer, Jerusalem, salvation for all).
  • Volume 1: from Jerusalem's Temple to Jesus' death, resurrection and ascension. Volume 2: from Jerusalem "to the uttermost parts of the earth" (Acts 1:8), ending in Rome.
  • Henry J. Cadbury (1883–1974), The Making of Luke–Acts (1927), made "Luke–Acts" the standard name.
  • Together they are over a quarter of the New Testament.
Mission 2Read the source

Luke's preface

> "Since many have undertaken to set in order a narrative concerning those matters which have been fulfilled amongst us, even as those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and servants of the word delivered them to us, it seemed good to me also, having traced the course of all things accurately from the first, to write to you in order, most excellent Theophilus; that you might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed." (Luke 1:1–4, WEBBE)

  • One long Greek sentence in the style of Greek historians.

Q1 Is the author an eyewitness?

Show answer

No: he relies on "eyewitnesses and servants of the word" and on earlier written accounts by "many".

Q2 What purpose does he state?

Show answer

That Theophilus "might know the certainty" of what he was taught.

  • "Most excellent" is used in Acts for Roman governors (Acts 23:26, 26:25): Theophilus may be a person of rank, or a patron. His name means "lover of God", so some read him as every believing reader.
Mission 2

Who wrote it?

  • External evidence (from outside the text):
  • Irenaeus, Against Heresies III.1.1 (c. 180): Luke, Paul's companion, wrote the Gospel Paul preached.
  • The Muratorian Fragment (usually dated late 2nd century) and Tertullian (Against Marcion IV) agree.
  • P75 (papyrus, c. 175–225): the oldest manuscript with the title "Gospel according to Luke".
  • Internal evidence (from the text):
  • The 'we' passages in Acts suggest the author travelled with Paul (see Mission 3).
  • Letters bearing Paul's name mention "Luke the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14), Luke a fellow worker (Philemon 24) and "Only Luke is with me" (2 Timothy 4:11). Many scholars doubt Paul wrote Colossians and 2 Timothy; Philemon is undisputed.
  • An educated Greek writer who explains Jewish customs ("the feast of unleavened bread, which is called the Passover", Luke 22:1): probably a Gentile.

Mission 2

"The beloved physician"?

  • W. K. Hobart, The Medical Language of St. Luke (1882): Luke uses technical medical words, so the author was a doctor.
  • Examples often given: "a great fever" (Luke 4:38; Mark 1:30 has only "a fever"); Luke softens Mark's remark that a sick woman "had suffered many things by many physicians … but rather grew worse" (Mark 5:26; compare Luke 8:43).
  • Cadbury (The Style and Literary Method of Luke, 1919–20) showed the same words in non-medical Greek writers such as Josephus: the vocabulary is that of any educated Greek.
  • So: consistent with a physician, but not proof.
  • Doubts about Luke as Paul's companion come mainly from Acts (Paul's theology and travels): see Mission 3.
Mission 2

When was it written?

  • Early (before c. AD 62–64): Acts ends with Paul alive in Rome (Acts 28:30–31), so Luke came earlier. Held by Adolf von Harnack (later work) and J. A. T. Robinson, Redating the New Testament (1976).
  • Intermediate (c. AD 80–90): the majority view.
  • Luke uses Mark (usually dated c. 65–70).
  • Luke rewrites Mark's "abomination of desolation" (Mark 13:14) as "when you see Jerusalem surrounded by armies" (Luke 21:20): read as reflecting the Roman siege of AD 70.
  • "Many have undertaken" (1:1): several accounts already exist.
  • Late (c. AD 100–120): some find knowledge of Josephus (c. AD 93) or a response to Marcion (c. 140). Marcion's use of a form of Luke sets the latest limit.

Mission 2

Why did Luke write?

  • Certainty: to assure Theophilus that what he was taught is reliable (1:4).
  • Apology: a reasoned defence of Christianity: it is no threat to Rome, and it is the true heir of Israel's faith.
  • Salvation History: God's plan in stages: Israel, Jesus ("the middle of time"), the Church. Conzelmann (1954) saw this as Luke's answer to the delay of Christ's return.
  • Universal gospel: salvation for Jews and Gentiles, rich and poor, men and women.
  • Order: to write "in order" (1:3): an ordered, persuasive account, not necessarily strict chronology.
Mission 2Read the source

Innocent before Rome

> "I find no basis for a charge against this man." (Luke 23:4, WEBBE)

> "Certainly this was a righteous man." (Luke 23:47, WEBBE)

  • Pilate declares Jesus innocent three times (23:4, 14, 22); Herod Antipas also finds "nothing worthy of death" (23:15).
  • Mark's centurion says "Truly this man was the Son of God!" (Mark 15:39). Luke's word dikaios means righteous or innocent.
  • Acts continues the pattern: Gallio (18:14–15), Festus and Agrippa (26:31–32) find Paul guilty of no crime.

Q1 How does Luke 23:47 support the "apology" view?

Show answer

A Roman officer declares Jesus innocent: Christianity's founder broke no Roman law.

Q2 Why might a reader question this view?

Show answer

Luke also calls Jesus Son of God (1:35); "righteous" may be theological, recalling the innocent sufferer, not only political.

Mission 2

Characteristics of the Gospel

  • Universalism: the genealogy reaches back to Adam (3:38), not only to Abraham.
  • The poor and outcasts: shepherds, tax collectors, Samaritans, "good news to the poor" (4:18).
  • Women: Elizabeth, Mary, Anna, the women who supported Jesus (8:1–3).
  • Prayer and the Holy Spirit: Jesus prays at each turning point.
  • Joy and praise: songs at the start, praise in the Temple at the end.
  • Jerusalem: the goal of a long travel narrative (9:51–19:27).
  • Sources: Mark, Q and Luke's own material (L), including the Good Samaritan and the Lost Son.
Mission 2

How accurate is Luke?

  • For accuracy:
  • Luke 3:1–2 dates John's preaching by five rulers and the high priests (Tiberius' 15th year, Pilate, Herod, Philip, Lysanias; Annas and Caiaphas): the method of a historian.
  • Lysanias was once thought an error (a Lysanias died in 36 BC); an inscription from Abila names a later Lysanias the tetrarch.
  • The preface claims eyewitness sources and careful research.
  • Against:
  • The Quirinius census (2:2) conflicts with Herod's reign (see Mission 4).
  • Luke moves events for theology: the Nazareth sermon opens the ministry (4:16–30), but comes later in Mark (6:1–6).
  • Resurrection appearances only in and near Jerusalem (Luke 24); in Matthew 28 the disciples meet the risen Jesus in Galilee.
  • I. Howard Marshall (1934–2015), Luke: Historian and Theologian (1970): Luke is both, and his theology does not cancel his history.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Luke reliable history?

  • Claim: Luke's Gospel is historically reliable.
  • For: Historian's preface; sources close to eyewitnesses; accurate names and titles; Luke as Paul's companion would have met Jesus' followers.
  • For: Ancient historians also shaped speeches and order; judged by their standards, Luke does well.
  • Against: Theological aims (apology, Salvation History) shape the material; the census problem; changed order and settings.
  • Against: If written c. 80–90 by a Gentile, Luke is at least a generation from events.
  • Judgement criteria: Ancient or modern standards of history? Does accuracy on small details (Lysanias) show accuracy on large ones? Can a theological writer also be a reliable historian?

Mission 3: Halfway through Acts, the narrator suddenly says "we". Was the author really on board?

Make a guess: Halfway through Acts, the narrator suddenly says "we". Was the author really on board?

Mission 3

The second volume

  • "The first book I wrote, Theophilus, concerned all that Jesus began both to do and to teach" (Acts 1:1): Acts continues what Jesus began.
  • The plan: "You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth." (Acts 1:8)
  • Chapters 1–12: mostly Peter, Jerusalem, Samaria, the first Gentile (Cornelius).
  • Chapters 13–28: mostly Paul, his journeys, arrest and voyage to Rome.
  • Context: Roman roads and peace, Greek as a common language, Jewish synagogues across the Empire with Gentile "God-fearers" attached: routes for the message.
Mission 3

Evidence for the author

  • Internal:
  • Same author as Luke: preface to Theophilus, same style and themes.
  • The 'we' passages suggest a companion of Paul.
  • That companion is not among those named in the third person (e.g. Timothy, Silas, Aristarchus, Acts 20:4); letters bearing Paul's name place Luke with him in prison (Philemon 24; Colossians 4:14). By elimination, Luke fits.
  • Knowledge of sea travel, Roman law and local titles.
  • External:
  • Irenaeus, Against Heresies III.14.1 (c. 180): Luke was "inseparable" from Paul, and the 'we' passages show it.
  • The Muratorian Fragment says Luke wrote "the acts of all the apostles" in one book.
Mission 3Read the source

Where "we" begins

> "When he had seen the vision, immediately we sought to go out to Macedonia, concluding that the Lord had called us to preach the Good News to them." (Acts 16:10, WEBBE)

> "Setting sail therefore from Troas, we made a straight course to Samothrace" (Acts 16:11, WEBBE)

  • The four 'we' passages: 16:10–17 (Troas to Philippi); 20:5–15 and 21:1–18 (to Jerusalem); 27:1–28:16 (voyage and shipwreck to Rome).

Q1 What do all four passages have in common?

Show answer

Each includes travel by sea.

Q2 Why does this matter for the debate?

Show answer

It supports the view that "we" might be a sea-voyage convention, as well as an eyewitness's diary.

Mission 3

Three readings of "we"

  • 1. Eyewitness: the author joined Paul at these points. Irenaeus, William Ramsay, F. F. Bruce (The Acts of the Apostles, 1951). Detail and vividness, especially in chapter 27.
  • 2. Source: the author used a travel diary by another companion (e.g. Silas or Timothy) and kept its "we". But why keep "we" in a work so carefully edited?
  • 3. Literary convention: Vernon K. Robbins, "By Land and by Sea" (1978): ancient sea-voyage narratives often switch to "we". Critics reply that the examples are few and not all in voyages.
  • A fourth view: a later author uses "we" to claim authority as Paul's companion (e.g. Richard Pervo).

Mission 3

Early, intermediate or late?

  • Early (c. AD 62): Acts ends with Paul alive, under house arrest (28:30–31). No mention of his death (c. 64–67), Nero's persecution (64) or the fall of Jerusalem (70). Harnack; J. A. T. Robinson (1976).
  • Intermediate (c. AD 80–90): Acts follows Luke, which follows Mark and (for many) reflects AD 70. The ending is a literary choice: the gospel has reached Rome. Majority view.
  • Late (c. AD 100–130): possible use of Josephus (Antiquities, c. 93); Paul's letters known but not used; concerns of a settled Church. Pervo, Dating Acts (2006).
  • Each view must explain the abrupt ending.

Mission 3

Why was Acts written?

  • Spread of the gospel: from Jerusalem to Rome, as planned in 1:8.
  • Work of the Holy Spirit: some call it "the Acts of the Holy Spirit": Pentecost, guidance, Gentile inclusion.
  • Apology: Roman officials find Christians innocent: Gallio (18:14–15), Festus and Agrippa (26:31–32); Paul is a Roman citizen.
  • Defence of Paul: his mission to Gentiles is legitimate; his story parallels Peter's (healings, escapes, visions).
  • Unity: Peter and Paul agree (Acts 15). F. C. Baur (1792–1860, Tübingen) saw this as a later smoothing-over of real conflict.
  • Salvation History: the age of the Church after the age of Jesus (Conzelmann).
Mission 3

Characteristics of Acts

  • Speeches: around two dozen; about a third of the book (Peter at Pentecost, Stephen, Paul at Athens).
  • Summaries of church life: 2:42–47; 4:32–35; 5:12–16.
  • Miracles and visions at turning points.
  • Geography as structure: Jerusalem → Judea and Samaria → Antioch → Asia Minor → Greece → Rome.
  • Accurate local titles: "proconsul" in Cyprus (Sergius Paulus, 13:7) and Achaia (Gallio, 18:12); city "rulers" at Thessalonica (Greek politarchai, 17:6, 17:8), a title confirmed by inscriptions; "the chief man of the island" of Malta (28:7).
Mission 3

Challenges and defences

  • Paul of Acts and Paul of the letters (Philipp Vielhauer, "On the 'Paulinism' of Acts", 1950):
  • Athens speech (17:22–31) praises natural knowledge of God; Romans 1 uses it to show guilt.
  • Paul circumcises Timothy (16:3), yet writes: "if you receive circumcision, Christ will profit you nothing" (Galatians 5:2).
  • Acts calls Paul an apostle only at 14:4 and 14:14; Paul insists on the title.
  • Jerusalem visits: Galatians 1–2 gives two visits in fourteen or more years; Acts gives more (9, 11, 15). Which visit is Galatians 2?
  • Theudas: Gamaliel (Acts 5:36–37) mentions Theudas before Judas the Galilean; Josephus dates Theudas c. AD 44–46, after Gamaliel's speech.
  • Speeches: ancient historians wrote what fitted the occasion (Thucydides, History I.22).
  • Defences: Ramsay (St. Paul the Traveller and the Roman Citizen, 1895) and Colin Hemer (The Book of Acts in the Setting of Hellenistic History, 1989): local titles, places and sea details are exact; differences from the letters reflect a companion's view, not Paul's own.
Mission 3Evaluate

How accurate is Acts?

  • Claim: Acts is a historically accurate account of the early Church.
  • For: Exact titles, places and voyage detail; Gallio's dates fit the Delphi inscription (c. AD 51–52); possible eyewitness 'we'.
  • For: Speeches carry early-sounding material (e.g. simple Christology in Acts 2): see a later lesson.
  • Against: Tensions with Paul's letters (theology, visits); the Theudas order; composed speeches; apologetic and unifying aims.
  • Against: Accuracy in background (titles, routes) does not prove accuracy in events (miracles, speeches).
  • Judgement criteria: Ancient or modern standards? Where Acts and Paul differ, whose purpose explains the difference? How much weight can archaeology carry?

Mission 4

Why chapters 1–2 matter

  • After a polished Greek preface (1:1–4), Luke switches to a style like the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint): the story picks up where Israel's scriptures left off.
  • Two parallel panels: John and Jesus, each announced, born, named, praised. Jesus is always the greater.
  • Temple frame: the story opens in the Temple (1:9) and the Gospel ends there (24:53).
  • Themes the whole Gospel will develop: the Holy Spirit, prayer and praise (four songs), joy, the poor and lowly, women (Elizabeth, Mary, Anna), salvation for all peoples.
  • Titles: Son of the Most High, Son of God, Saviour, Christ, Lord.
  • Scholarship: Hans Conzelmann (1915–1989), The Theology of St Luke (1954; ET 1960), largely left chapters 1–2 out of Luke's Salvation History; Paul Minear (1966) argued they are its key.
Mission 4

Two annunciations

  • An annunciation is an angel's announcement of a birth. Both follow an Old Testament pattern: appearance → fear → "do not be afraid" → message → question → sign.
  • John (1:5–25): Gabriel appears to Zechariah, a priest, burning incense in the Temple. His wife Elizabeth is old and childless (like Abraham and Sarah). John will come "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (1:17).
  • Zechariah asks for proof and is struck silent "because you didn't believe my words" (Luke 1:20).
  • Jesus (1:26–38): Gabriel comes to Mary in Nazareth, a virgin engaged to Joseph of David's house. Her son will be "the Son of the Most High" and reign on David's throne (1:32–33).
  • Contrast: a priest in the holy place doubts; a young woman in a Galilean village believes.

  • Catholic and Orthodox tradition see Mary's "yes" (the fiat) as central to salvation; many Protestants stress it as a model of faith.

Mission 4

Two births

  • John (1:57–80): neighbours expect him to be named after his father; Elizabeth and Zechariah insist on John. Zechariah speaks again and sings the Benedictus ("Blessed be the Lord, the God of Israel", 1:68).
  • Between the annunciations and the births, Mary visits Elizabeth and sings the Magnificat (1:46–55): God "has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly" (Luke 1:52). It echoes Hannah's song (1 Samuel 2:1–10).
  • Jesus (2:1–7): Caesar Augustus orders a census; Joseph and Mary travel from Nazareth to Bethlehem, "David's city".
  • Mary lays her firstborn in a feeding trough "because there was no room for them in the inn" (Luke 2:7). The Greek katalyma may mean a guest room.
  • Luke's contrast: the child he calls Saviour and Lord is born in lowliness, while the emperor issues decrees.
Mission 4

The shepherds

  • An angel appears to shepherds at night near Bethlehem (2:8–20).
  • "I bring you good news of great joy which will be to all the people. For there is born to you today, in David's city, a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord." (Luke 2:10–11)
  • A heavenly host sings "Glory to God in the highest" (2:14).
  • Why shepherds? Ordinary working people, often poor: the first to hear are the lowly. Shepherds also recall David, the shepherd king of Bethlehem. (Some scholars add that shepherds had low status; others think this is overstated.)
  • Counter-imperial echo: Augustus was hailed as "saviour" and his birthday as "good news" (Priene inscription, 9 BC). Luke gives these words to Jesus.
  • "Mary kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19).
  • Your spec lists the visit as 2:2–20 (verses 8–20 tell it; 2–7 set the scene).

Mission 4

The Presentation in the Temple

  • Day 8: circumcision and naming (2:21): the family keeps the Law.
  • Day 40: Mary's purification (Leviticus 12) and the presentation of the firstborn to God (Exodus 13:2). Their offering, "a pair of turtledoves, or two young pigeons" (Luke 2:24), is the offering of those who cannot afford a lamb (Leviticus 12:8).
  • Simeon: righteous, waiting for "the consolation of Israel"; promised by the Spirit he would see "the Lord's Christ" (2:25–26). He sings the Nunc Dimittis.
  • He warns: the child is "for a sign which is spoken against" (Luke 2:34), and "a sword will pierce through your own soul" (Luke 2:35), often read as pointing to the cross.
  • Anna: an elderly prophetess of the tribe of Asher, who worships in the Temple night and day and speaks of the child "to all those who were looking for redemption in Jerusalem" (Luke 2:38).
  • A man and a woman, both faithful Jews, witness together: a Lukan pairing.

Mission 4

The boy Jesus in the Temple

  • At twelve, Jesus goes to Jerusalem for Passover (2:41–42). He stays behind; after three days his parents find him "in the temple, sitting in the middle of the teachers, both listening to them and asking them questions" (Luke 2:46).
  • His first words in the Gospel: "Didn't you know that I must be in my Father's house?" (Luke 2:49)
  • He calls God "my Father": the narrative's climax of identity, now in Jesus' own voice.
  • He returns home obedient; "Jesus increased in wisdom and stature, and in favour with God and men" (Luke 2:52): real human growth.
  • The only story of Jesus' boyhood in the canonical Gospels.
Mission 4

Luke's redaction and the history question

  • Redaction: Luke shapes the birth to show his themes: the lowly raised, the Spirit at work, faithful Israel welcoming its Messiah, salvation for Gentiles, Jesus greater than John.
  • Problem: Luke dates the birth to the census "when Quirinius was governor of Syria" (2:2). Josephus dates Quirinius' census to AD 6; Matthew and Luke both place the birth under Herod, who died c. 4 BC.
  • No other source records an empire-wide census under Augustus, or a rule to register in one's ancestral town.
  • Defences: Greek prōtē could mean "before Quirinius governed"; Quirinius may have held earlier office in the region; Luke claims careful research (1:3); Mary as an eyewitness source ("pondering", 2:19, 2:51).
  • Scholarly views: Raymond Brown (The Birth of the Messiah, 1977) judges the census a confusion and the narrative largely theological; conservative scholars such as I. Howard Marshall (The Gospel of Luke, 1978) keep the history open.
Mission 4Evaluate

History, theology, or both?

  • Claim: Luke's Infancy Narrative is valuable mainly for its theology, not as history.
  • For: Septuagint style, songs and angelic speeches suggest careful composition.
  • For: The census date conflicts with Herod's reign; the accounts of Matthew and Luke are hard to harmonise.
  • Against: Luke claims to write "in order" after careful research (1:3); the shared core with Matthew suggests older tradition.
  • Against: "Mainly theology" is a false choice for many believers: Catholic, Orthodox and most evangelical Christians hold that the events happened and carry meaning.
  • Judgement criteria: What did ancient readers expect of history? How much weight can the census problem bear? Does the faith claim depend on each detail?
  • Also weigh: how important are chapters 1–2 to the Gospel as a whole?

Mission 5

Luke's opening sequence

  • Before Jesus teaches anyone, Luke shows who he is, in four steps:
  • Baptism (3:21–22): God names him Son.
  • Genealogy (3:23–38): traced back to Adam, "the son of God".
  • Temptation (4:1–13): the Son is tested.
  • Nazareth (4:14–30): the Son announces his mission, and is rejected.
  • Mark puts the Nazareth visit much later (Mark 6:1–6). Luke moves it to the front and expands it: it works as a programme for the whole Gospel.
  • Thread through every step: the Holy Spirit (3:22; 4:1; 4:14; 4:18).
Mission 5

The baptism (3:21–22)

  • John has already been imprisoned in Luke's story (3:19–20), and Luke does not name who baptised Jesus. The focus moves from John to Jesus.
  • Jesus is praying when the sky opens: a Lukan theme (Jesus prays at each turning point).
  • The Spirit comes "in a bodily form like a dove": Luke stresses that it was real and visible.
  • The voice: "You are my beloved Son. In you I am well pleased." (Luke 3:22)
  • It echoes Psalm 2:7 (the king as God's son) and Isaiah 42:1 (the servant in whom God delights): king and servant in one person.
  • Theological significance: Jesus' identity is declared by God, and he is equipped by the Spirit for his mission.
Mission 5

Why was Jesus baptised?

  • John preached "the baptism of repentance for remission of sins" (Luke 3:3). Christians believe Jesus was without sin. So why?
  • Solidarity: Luke says Jesus was baptised "when all the people were baptised" (3:21). He stands with the people he came to save.
  • Commissioning: like the anointing of a king or prophet, it marks the start of his public work.
  • Model for his followers: Christian baptism looks back to this moment (with the command in Matthew 28:19 and the preaching in Acts 2:38).
  • Matthew alone gives Jesus' own reason: "to fulfil all righteousness" (Matthew 3:15).
Mission 5

The temptation (4:1–13)

  • "Full of the Holy Spirit", Jesus is led into the wilderness for forty days (an echo of Israel's forty years, and of Moses and Elijah).
  • Three tests, each answered from Deuteronomy (8:3; 6:13; 6:16):
  • Bread: "Man shall not live by bread alone" (Luke 4:4). He will not use power for himself.
  • Kingdoms: worship the devil for "all this authority" (4:6). He will not take power by the wrong route.
  • Temple: jump and let angels catch you. He will not force God's hand.
  • Luke's order differs from Matthew's (bread, Temple, mountain): Luke ends at Jerusalem, where his Gospel will climax.
  • "If you are the Son of God" (4:3, 9) tests the title just given at the baptism.
  • Where Israel failed in the wilderness, the Son stays faithful.

Mission 5

Why the rejection?

  • First reaction: admiration, then doubt: "Isn't this Joseph's son?" (4:22).
  • Jesus quotes a proverb: "no prophet is acceptable in his hometown" (4:24).
  • Then two examples from the prophets: Elijah was sent to a widow in Sidon; Elisha healed Naaman the Syrian (4:25–27). Both were non-Israelites.
  • The anger follows (4:28–29): God's favour is not owned by his home town, or by Israel alone.
  • He passes "through the middle of them" (4:30): his mission cannot be stopped.
  • Significance: the scene foreshadows the Gospel and Acts: rejection by many of his own, good news reaching Gentiles, and a death at Jerusalem.
Mission 5

The authorities in Luke

  • Pharisees: lay teachers devoted to keeping the Law in daily life, including oral traditions (see Mission 1). Scribes: experts in the Law.
  • Luke's picture is mixed, not simply hostile: Pharisees invite Jesus to meals (7:36; 14:1) and warn him of Herod (13:31); in Acts some Pharisees become believers (Acts 15:5).
  • The controversies are disputes within Judaism about how to honour God's Law, not Jesus against Judaism.
  • Many scholars warn that reading these stories as a verdict on Jews in general has fed Christian anti-Judaism.
Mission 5

Three controversies

  • Paralysed man (5:17–26): "Your sins are forgiven you." The scribes and Pharisees ask: "Who can forgive sins, but God alone?" (5:21). Jesus heals the man "that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (5:24). Issue: authority to forgive.
  • Grain fields (6:1–5): the disciples pluck grain on the Sabbath. Jesus cites David eating the holy bread (1 Samuel 21). Claim: "The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath." (6:5)
  • Withered hand (6:6–11): "Is it lawful on the Sabbath to do good, or to do harm? To save a life, or to kill?" (6:9). He heals; they are "filled with rage" (6:11).
  • Each story moves from an act to a claim about who Jesus is.

Mission 5Evaluate

Are initiation and ritual still relevant?

  • Claim: Initiation and ritual remain relevant to religious life today.
  • For: Rituals mark belonging and identity: baptism (Christianity); Bar and Bat Mitzvah (Judaism); Amrit Sanskar (Sikhism); upanayana, the sacred thread (Hinduism).
  • For: They make belief visible and shared: a community promises support (godparents, sponsors, the Sangat).
  • For: Christians see baptism as more than a symbol: for Catholic and Orthodox Christians a sacrament that brings grace; for many Protestants a public act of obedience and witness.
  • Against: Ritual can become empty form: many families seek infant baptism for custom, not faith. The prophets and Jesus both criticise religion that is outward only (e.g. Luke 11:42).
  • Against: Some Christians (e.g. Quakers, the Salvation Army) do not practise water baptism at all, holding that inner faith is what matters.
  • Against: Secular society offers non-religious naming and coming-of-age ceremonies, so ritual may not need religion.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the ritual change the person, or only mark a change? Can a ritual be meaningful without belief? Does Jesus' baptism make Christian baptism essential, or a model?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Under Rome: Herod's family as client rulers; prefects such as Pilate in Judea; taxes, a Rome-appointed high priest and the threat of revolt.
  • Hellenism brought Greek language and cities, and a long struggle over Jewish identity.
  • Pharisees (oral law, resurrection, popular) and Sadducees (Temple, written Torah only, no resurrection) differed sharply; Essenes, Zealots, Herodians and Samaritans add to a divided landscape in which Jesus taught.

Mission 2

  • Luke–Acts is one work in two volumes for Theophilus, written to give "certainty" and often read as an apology: Christianity is innocent before Rome and the heir of Israel.
  • Tradition (Irenaeus, c. 180) names Luke, Paul's companion and "beloved physician"; internal evidence fits an educated Gentile; most date the Gospel c. 80–90, others earlier or later.
  • Accuracy is debated: precise rulers and titles versus the census problem and theological shaping.

Mission 3

  • Acts is volume two of Luke–Acts; internal evidence ('we', style) and external evidence (Irenaeus, Muratorian Fragment) point to Luke, Paul's companion.
  • The 'we' passages (16:10–17; 20:5–15; 21:1–18; 27:1–28:16) are read as eyewitness, source or convention; dates range from c. 62 to c. 130, and each must explain the open ending.
  • Purposes include the spread of the gospel to Rome, the Spirit's work, apology and Paul's defence; accuracy is challenged (Vielhauer, Theudas, Jerusalem visits) and defended (Ramsay, Hemer, titles).

Mission 4

  • Luke 1–2 sets two births side by side (John and Jesus), in Septuagint style, framed by the Temple, and introduces Luke's themes: the Spirit, prayer and praise, joy, the lowly, women, salvation for all.
  • Annunciations (1:5–38), births (1:57–2:7), shepherds, Presentation (2:21–40) and the boy Jesus (2:41–52) build Jesus' identity: Son of God, Saviour, Christ, Lord; "my Father's house".
  • Redaction explains Luke's shaping; the Quirinius census raises the history question, answered differently by different scholars and believers.

Mission 5

  • Baptism (Son, servant, anointed by the Spirit), temptation (the Son stays faithful where Israel failed) and Nazareth (the programme: good news to the poor, fulfilled "today") set out who Jesus is and what he came to do.
  • Rejection at Nazareth and the controversies (forgiveness, the Sabbath) foreshadow the conflict and the reach to the Gentiles in Luke–Acts.
  • Initiation rituals mark belonging across religions; whether they remain relevant is debated.
