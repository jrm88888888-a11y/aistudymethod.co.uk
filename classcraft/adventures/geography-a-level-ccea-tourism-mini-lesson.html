Mission 1

Mission 1: UK residents made about three times as many trips abroad as Indian nationals did in 2025, yet India has about 21 times as many people. Why?

Make a guess: UK residents made about three times as many trips abroad as Indian nationals did in 2025, yet India has about 21 times as many people. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D1.i growth of mass tourism into a global industry: transport, disposable income, package holidays, internet
Mission 1

What mass tourism is

  • A tourist is a visitor whose trip includes an overnight stay, outside their usual environment, for less than a year.
  • Mass tourism: very large numbers of people taking similar, standardised holidays to the same destinations, sold at low prices.
  • Its roots: in 1841 Thomas Cook persuaded a railway company to run a special train from Leicester to Loughborough, "believed to have been the first publicly advertised excursion train in England".
  • Today: about 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals in 2025. Tourism is a global industry.
Mission 1

How fast it grew

  • UK residents' visits abroad: 17.5 million (1980) → 93.1 million (2019) → 99.9 million (2025).
  • Air passengers worldwide: 310 million (1970) → 4.46 billion (2019).
  • Growth stalls with shocks: air passengers fell by about 60% in 2020 (our calculation).
Quick check
?UK residents made 17.5 million visits abroad in 1980 and 93.1 million in 2019. By about how many times did the number grow?
Mission 1

Driver 1: disposable income

  • Disposable income: what a household has left after taxes, to spend or save.
  • Travel is a luxury: spending on it rises once essentials are paid for.
  • About 3.2 billion people were in the global middle class in 2016 (Brookings estimate), with growth expected to be "heavily concentrated in Asia".
  • Mass destinations serve this demand: Spain received 19.1 million visits from residents of Great Britain in 2025.
Mission 1

Driver 2: paid holidays

  • Income is not enough without time off.
  • Workers in Great Britain gained a legal right to three weeks' paid leave in 1998, rising to four weeks for leave years beginning after 23 November 1999.
  • Today most GB workers on a 5-day week must get at least 28 days (5.6 weeks) a year.
  • Paid leave turns free time into demand for holidays.
Mission 1

Driver 3: transport

  • Rail (1840s), then cars, then jet aircraft cut journey times to distant coasts.
  • In the EU, the single aviation market lets any EU airline fly any route within the EU.
  • The European Commission: "Prices have fallen dramatically, new business models appeared". These include the low-cost airlines.
  • Faster, cheaper flights widened the range of reachable destinations.
Mission 1

Driver 4: package holidays

  • A package combines at least two types of travel service (transport, accommodation, car hire, other tourist services) for the same trip.
  • A tour operator buys seats and beds in bulk, so each holiday costs less: economies of scale.
  • One price, one booking: low risk for first-time travellers abroad.
  • Standardised products for very large numbers: the core of mass tourism.
Quick check
?Under the UK's 2018 rules, which of these trips is a package?
Mission 1

Driver 5: the internet

  • Travellers compare prices, read reviews and book directly, without a high-street travel agent.
  • Dynamic packaging: the traveller builds a trip online. UK rules now count some linked online booking processes as packages.
  • Online platforms work at a vast scale: 1.2 billion room nights were booked across Booking Holdings' platforms in 2025.
  • The internet lowered search costs and opened niche and long-haul destinations to mass markets.
Mission 1

Same drivers, different stages

  • In 2025, UK residents made about 1.44 visits abroad per resident; Indian nationals about 0.022 departures per resident (our calculation).
  • The World Bank classes the UK as high income and India as lower-middle income.
  • Indian nationals' departures rose from 26.9 million (2019) to 32.8 million (2025): the drivers are spreading.
  • Brookings links middle-class growth to the largest economies, "notably China and India".
Quick check
?Indian nationals' departures rose 22% between 2019 and 2025. Which driver from this mission best explains a rise this fast?
Mission 1

Exam move: how the drivers combine

  • Demand: disposable income and paid leave make people able to travel.
  • Supply: transport, package holidays and the internet make travel cheaper and easier to buy.
  • They reinforce each other: cheap flights fill package seats; online booking fills flights.
  • Exam move: for "explain how mass tourism has developed", link each driver to a cause–effect chain and give a dated example.
Mission check

?UK residents made 17.5 million visits abroad in 1980 and 93.1 million in 2019. What best explains this growth?

The same drivers explain the hook. Per person, UK residents travel abroad about 64 times as often as Indian nationals (1.44 against 0.022 in 2025), because high incomes, paid leave and cheap transport arrived in the UK first; India's departures are rising as they spread.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Make a guess: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D1.ii positive social and economic impacts of tourism
Mission 2

Economic benefits

  • Foreign exchange: tourists spend currency earned abroad.
  • Government revenue: taxes and fees on visitors and businesses.
  • Jobs and incomes: hotels, transport, guiding, food, crafts.
  • Infrastructure: airports, roads and utilities that residents also use.
Mission 2

Case: the Maldives

  • Upper-middle income. 2,246,516 tourist arrivals in 2025.
  • Tourism: 20.3% of GDP (2025, constant prices); "the largest economic sector" (World Bank).
  • 179 resorts, 16 hotels, 973 guest houses and 168 safari vessels in 2025.
  • Investment arrives from outside: the IFC has invested over US$50 million in Maldivian tourism firms and funds.
Mission 2

Jobs: who is employed

  • Resorts employed 55,874 people at the end of 2022: 41% Maldivian (23,146) and 59% foreign workers.
  • Maldivian jobs bring wages to island households.
  • Foreign workers also gain: wages they can send home to their families.
Mission 2

The multiplier effect

  • Multiplier effect: money spent by tourists is re-spent locally, so total income rises by more than the first spend.
  • Round 1: a tourist pays a hotel. Round 2: the hotel pays wages and local suppliers. Round 3: workers and suppliers spend in local shops.
  • Each round is smaller: money leaks out when it buys imports or is sent abroad.
  • More local suppliers and local ownership mean a bigger multiplier.
Mission 2

The multiplier at a UK event

  • Glastonbury Festival, Somerset (2023, festival-commissioned study):
    • the festival itself spent £62 million; just under £12 million went to 258 Somerset companies;
    • it generated around £168 million of income for UK businesses, including £32 million in Somerset.
  • About 19% of the festival's own spending stayed in Somerset (our calculation).
  • Treat a commissioned study with care: ask who paid for it.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of the multiplier effect?
Mission 2

Closer to home: screen tourism in Northern Ireland

  • Filming Game of Thrones generated £251 million for the local economy.
  • In 2018 it helped attract about 350,000 out-of-state visitors (1 in 6), spending over £50 million.
  • New attractions followed, including a permanent studio tour at Linen Mill Studios, Banbridge.
Mission 2

Social benefits

  • Community projects: Rwanda shares 10% of park tourism revenue with neighbouring communities. Nearly 1,300 projects (schools, health care, water) have been funded with more than Rwf 23 billion.
  • Housing and facilities: the Glastonbury Festival has built 52 social homes in its host village, Pilton, and upgraded the playing fields.
  • Spreading income: small guest houses (973 in the Maldives in 2025) let more local families earn from visitors.
  • Skills: jobs bring training in languages, hospitality and management.
Quick check
?Which of these is a social rather than an economic benefit of tourism?
Mission check

?In 2018, Game of Thrones helped bring about 350,000 out-of-state visitors to Northern Ireland, spending over £50 million. Who gains from this spending?

In the Maldives, too, several groups gain: tourism is about a fifth of GDP, 41% of resort jobs (23,146) were Maldivian in 2022, and guest houses spread income. The benefits are real but uneven: 59% of resort jobs went to foreign workers.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D1.iii changing tourist demands and resorts over time: Pleasure Periphery and the Butler model
  • A2-2.D2.i challenges of mass tourism: pollution, overcrowding, honeypot sites, social sustainability, competition for resources
Mission 3

Carrying capacity

  • Carrying capacity: the most visitors a place can take before unacceptable damage occurs.
  • Physical: space. Ecological: damage to ecosystems. Perceptual (social): when residents or visitors feel it is too crowded.
  • Passing it creates the challenges of mass tourism: pollution, overcrowding and conflict over resources.
Mission 3

The Butler model (1980)

  • Richard Butler's tourist area life cycle: exploration → involvement → development → consolidation → stagnation, then decline or rejuvenation.
  • Numbers reach the critical range of capacity around stagnation. Without new investment, the resort declines.
  • Limits: real places rarely follow one smooth curve; shocks and management change the path.
Quick check
?A resort's visitor numbers have stopped rising, its hotels are ageing and it is no longer fashionable. Which Butler stage fits best?
Mission 3

The Pleasure Periphery

  • Turner and Ash (1975) described international tourism spreading from rich, industrial core regions to a Pleasure Periphery: warmer, cheaper places within reach of the core.
  • As transport got faster and cheaper, the periphery was pushed further out: first the Mediterranean, then long-haul tropical coasts and islands.
  • Today Spain is the most visited country for residents of Great Britain (19.1 million visits in 2025); in the Maldives the UK is the third-largest source market (9.0% of arrivals).
Mission 3

Changing demands, changing resorts

  • Then: standard sun-sea-sand packages in large hotels.
  • Now: more independent, experience-led and long-haul trips. Booking Holdings reports about 4.7 million properties, including 4.2 million alternative accommodations (homes, apartments) rather than hotels.
  • Older resorts in the inner periphery face stagnation; they must rejuvenate (new attractions, upgraded hotels) or decline.
  • New destinations further out enter exploration and development: Butler's cycle repeats across the Pleasure Periphery.
Quick check
?Which statement best links the Pleasure Periphery to the Butler model?
Mission 3

Honeypot sites and overcrowding: Maya Bay

  • A honeypot site draws very large numbers of visitors to one small place.
  • Maya Bay (Phi Phi Leh, Krabi, Thailand) became famous after featuring in the film The Beach.
  • At its peak in 2017, almost 5,000 people a day visited.
  • It closed in June 2018 so that coral and marine life damaged by thousands of daily visitors could recover.
Mission 3

Pollution and fragile environments

  • At Maya Bay, coral and marine life were damaged "by the thousands of daily tourists". Under the reopening plan, boats must use a new jetty on the other side of the island instead of entering the bay; daily numbers are controlled.
  • Cruise ships in Venice: UNESCO welcomed Italy's 2021 decision "to ban large cruise ships from the Venice Lagoon".
  • Other pollution from mass tourism: sewage, litter, noise and air pollution from transport.
Mission 3

Social sustainability: Venice

  • UNESCO: "The exceptionally high tourism pressure on the city of Venice has resulted in a partial functional transformation".
  • About 53,000 residents live in the historic centre (2018), against an average of 66,800 visitors and tourists a day; day-trippers number almost 22 million a year.
  • Social sustainability asks whether a community can keep living, working and belonging in its own place.
Mission 3

Competition for resources

  • Housing: in Venice, homes have been replaced by tourist accommodation and businesses. Residents compete with visitors for space.
  • Water, energy and land: hotels, pools and golf courses draw on the same supplies as residents and farms, peaking in the dry summer season.
  • Public space: streets, beaches and transport crowd at peak times.
Quick check
?Venice's historic centre has about 53,000 residents and an average of 66,800 visitors a day. How many visitors are there per resident on an average day?
Mission 3

Managing the pressure

  • Closure (Maya Bay): quick ecological recovery, but income from the site stops.
  • Quotas and routes (Maya Bay's controlled daily numbers): protect the site but limit access.
  • Pricing: Venice trialled an access fee on set dates in 2026. It raises money but may exclude poorer visitors.
  • Dispersal: while Maya Bay was shut, Thailand's tourism authority promoted Krabi's other islands.
Mission check

?In 2026 Venice charged an access fee to visitors on set dates. Why would a city charge people to enter a place that tourists love?

Maya Bay passed its ecological carrying capacity: almost 5,000 visitors a day in 2017 damaged its coral. Closing it from June 2018, and reopening with a jetty away from the reef and controlled numbers, let it recover.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Visitors to Mallorca pay a small tax for every day they stay. Has it eased the pressure of mass tourism?

Make a guess: Visitors to Mallorca pay a small tax for every day they stay. Has it eased the pressure of mass tourism?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D2.ii evaluate strategies used to reduce the negative social and environmental impacts of mass tourism, through one tourism management policy at national or regional scale (Balearic Islands' sustainable tourism tax, Mallorca)
Mission 4

The pressure

  • Balearic Islands (Mallorca, Menorca, Ibiza, Formentera): a regional government within Spain.
  • International tourists: 15.3 million (2024) and 15.7 million (2025).
  • Spain as a whole: 96.8 million in 2025; the Balearics received about one in six (our calculation).
  • Mass tourism brings income, but also crowding, pressure on water, land and heritage.
Mission 4

The policy: a regional law

  • Law 2/2016 of the Balearic Islands created a tax on tourist stays.
  • Paid per person, per day of stay, at the place of accommodation.
  • Its revenue "queda afecta íntegramente" (is wholly earmarked) to a fund to promote sustainable tourism.
  • Scale: regional policy, set by the Balearic government.
Mission 4

How the tax works (2016 law)

  • Rates by type of stay, per day: €0.50 (hostels, campsites) to €2 (top-rated hotels and apartments).
  • Tourist cruise ships: €1 per day.
  • Exempt: stays by children under 16.
  • 50% reduction in the low season (1 November to 30 April) and from the 9th day of a long stay.
Quick check
?Under the 2016 rates, two adults and their 12-year-old child stay 7 nights in August in a hotel charging €2 per person per day. How much tax do they pay?
Mission 4

Where the money goes

The 2016 law lists what the fund may pay for:

  • the natural, rural, agrarian and marine environment
  • low-season tourism
  • historic and cultural heritage
  • research and innovation
  • training and quality of employment
Mission 4

What the policy aims to do

  • Make visitors pay for some costs they create (the "user pays" idea).
  • Reinvest in the environment and heritage that attract them.
  • Spread visits through the year: the low-season reduction.
  • Not a cap: nothing in the tax sets a maximum number of visitors.
Quick check
?Which feature of the law is designed to reduce the seasonal peak of mass tourism?
Mission 4

Evaluation 1: did numbers fall?

  • International tourists still rose: 15.3 million (2024) → 15.7 million (2025), +2.6%.
  • The tax was never designed to cut numbers.
  • So judge it against its own aims: funding and seasonality, not volume.
Mission 4

Evaluation 2: strengths and limits

  • Strengths: revenue is earmarked in law; visitors, not residents, pay; the low-season reduction targets the peak; children are exempt.
  • Limits: the earmark was suspended for 2020 by later laws; a few euros per day is small beside the cost of a holiday; the tax does not cap visitor numbers; results depend on which projects the fund chooses.
  • Policy changes: the law has since been amended, including the articles on rates and the fund, so the 2016 rates are a starting point, not today's.
Quick check
?Which evaluation of the Balearic tourist tax is best supported by the evidence in this mission?
Mission 4

Exam move: evaluating a policy

  • Aims v outcomes: what was it for (fund, season), and what happened (numbers still rising)?
  • Stakeholders: residents, hotels, tourists, the regional government; who gains, who pays?
  • Scale and partners: a tax works with other tools (planning limits, caps, transport) for a full strategy.
  • Judgement: a sustainable-funding success; a partial strategy for reducing negative impacts.
Mission check

?Another tourist region suffers overcrowding at its peak season. It wants to cut summer crowding without losing tourist income. Which policy, based on this mission, is most likely to work?

The Balearic tax raises money for the environment and heritage and nudges visits into the low season, but international tourists still rose to 15.7 million in 2025. A tax funds sustainability; reducing pressure needs more than one tool.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Make a guess: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D3.i define ecotourism
  • A2-2.D3.ii ecotourism benefits and negative impacts: displacement, threats to indigenous cultures, leakage, greenwashing, damage to fragile environments (case study)
Mission 5

Defining ecotourism

  • Ecotourism (The International Ecotourism Society, 2015): "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education".
  • Its principles include providing "direct financial benefits for conservation".
  • Sustainable tourism is wider: tourism that "takes full account of its current and future economic, social and environmental impacts".
  • So ecotourism is one form of sustainable tourism; not every nature trip qualifies.
Quick check
?Which trip best fits the definition of ecotourism?
Mission 5

Case study: Rwanda

  • Rwanda is a low-income country.
  • Its mountain gorillas live in Volcanoes National Park, part of the Virunga Massif, shared with DR Congo and Uganda.
  • Tourism earned US$685 million in 2025, from 1.49 million visitor arrivals of all kinds.
Mission 5

High value, low volume

  • In May 2017 the permit price rose from US$750 to US$1,500.
  • The Rwanda Development Board (RDB) called it "a high-value, low – volume market segmentation".
  • Numbers are capped on the ground: at most 8 visitors per group, and one hour with the gorillas.
  • Fewer visitors, each paying more: less pressure on the forest, more revenue per visit.
Mission 5

Benefit 1: conservation

  • Virunga Massif gorillas: as few as 242 in 1981, 480 in 2010, 604 in 2016.
  • The world total is about 1,063 (surveys of 2015–16 and 2018).
  • Caution: these counts predate the 2017 price rise and span three countries. They show recovery under long-term protection, not the effect of one price.
Mission 5

Benefits 2: communities and the economy

  • Revenue sharing: with the price rise, the community share of park tourism revenue rose from 5% to 10%.
  • Nearly 1,300 community projects have been funded with more than Rwf 23 billion: schools, health care, water and livelihoods.
  • Economic: RDB reports permit revenue rose 4% in 2017 compared with 2016 (the agency's own account).
Quick check
?If 10% of one US$1,500 permit is shared with neighbouring communities, how much is that?
Mission 5

Costs 1: displacement and indigenous cultures

  • Displacement: Rwanda plans to expand Volcanoes National Park by about 3,740 ha. The first phase (about 450 ha) affects 547 households, being moved to a new village; compensation is ongoing.
  • Threats to indigenous cultures: the Twa (Batwa) were forest-dwellers. "In the late 1980s, all remaining forest-dwelling Twa were evicted from Volcanoes National Park" (Minority Rights Group).
  • "As a result of this land confiscation, Twa have lost much of their traditional forest knowledge". Conservation that later earns gorilla revenue began with their loss of land.
  • Culture can also become a product: dances and crafts staged for visitors (commodification).
Mission 5

Costs 2: damage to fragile environments

  • Gorillas can catch human diseases: "risk of infectious diseases" remains a threat.
  • Every visit means trails, vehicles and lodges in a small, fragile habitat.
  • That is why group size and viewing time are capped: ecotourism depends on limits.
Mission 5

Costs 3: leakage

  • Leakage: tourist spending that leaves the host country.
  • In ecotourism it leaks through foreign-owned lodges and tour operators, imported food and fittings, international flights and booking-platform commission.
  • A high price does not guarantee local gain: what matters is how much stays, through local ownership, local staff and revenue sharing.
Mission 5

Costs 4: greenwashing

  • Greenwashing: "misleading the public to believe that a company or other entity is doing more to protect the environment than it is" (UN).
  • In tourism: "eco" labels on lodges or tours that fund no conservation and bring little local benefit.
  • UK law: environmental claims "must be truthful and accurate", "must be clear and unambiguous" and "must be substantiated" (Green Claims Code).
  • Test a claim against the definition: does the fee fund conservation, and do local people benefit?
Quick check
?Which is the clearest sign that an "eco-lodge" may be greenwashing?
Mission 5

Access and perspectives

  • Access: foreign visitors pay US$1,500; Rwandan and East African citizens a promotional US$200. Rwanda's GNI per person was US$1,150 in 2025.
  • RDB: high prices fund conservation and communities.
  • Neighbouring households: projects arrive, but some must move for park expansion.
  • Exam move: judge sustainability on all three pillars (environmental, social, economic) and say for whom.
Mission check

?A tour company sells expensive "eco-safaris". None of the fee goes to conservation, its lodges import most supplies, and it sets no limit on group size. Is the high price making this tourism more sustainable?

In Rwanda, the higher price came with limits (8 per group, one hour) and revenue sharing (10%, US$150 per permit), and tourism earned US$685 million in 2025. The costs remain: access for poorer visitors, disease risk and displacement.

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-2.D3.iii challenges of establishing effective international regulation of ecotourism; how the Québec Declaration, the Global Ecotourism Conference 2007, the Green Globe scheme and UNESCO World Heritage Sites are used to regulate it
Mission 6

What would good regulation protect?

  • The Québec Declaration says ecotourism, unlike wider sustainable tourism:
    • contributes to conserving natural and cultural heritage;
    • includes local and indigenous communities in planning and benefits;
    • interprets heritage for visitors;
    • suits independent travellers and small groups.
  • Regulation must check all four, not just the "eco" label.
Mission 6

Why international regulation is hard

  • Tourists and firms cross borders, but laws are national.
  • Global agreements are advisory: the Québec Declaration "is not a negotiated document".
  • Certification is mostly voluntary; small firms need support to afford it.
  • Labels can be used without real change (greenwashing).
  • Checking needs monitoring, which needs money and local capacity.
Quick check
?Which is the main reason a global ecotourism standard is hard to enforce?
Mission 6

The Québec Declaration, 2002

  • From the World Ecotourism Summit, Québec City, Canada, 19–22 May 2002, in the UN International Year of Ecotourism.
  • Under UNEP and the World Tourism Organization; over 1,000 participants from 132 countries.
  • Built on 18 preparatory meetings involving over 3,000 representatives.
  • Output: principles plus recommendations to governments, the private sector, NGOs and communities.
Mission 6

How the Québec Declaration is used

  • It asks governments to:
    • set regulatory and monitoring mechanisms with sustainability indicators and impact assessments, and make results public;
    • make tourism pay its environmental costs, including international transport;
    • use zoning and land-use planning;
    • base certification on international guidelines but local criteria.
  • Use: a template for national policy. Limit: nothing forces a state to adopt it.
Mission 6

Global Ecotourism Conference, 2007

  • Held in Oslo, Norway, organised by The International Ecotourism Society (TIES).
  • TIES calls it "the first global gathering of ecotourism professionals after the IYE" (the 2002 International Year of Ecotourism).
  • Five years after Québec.
  • Use: industry networking and shared good practice. Like Québec, it guides; it cannot enforce.
Quick check
?How do the Québec Declaration and the 2007 Oslo conference regulate ecotourism?
Mission 6

Green Globe certification

  • Roots in the 1992 Rio Earth Summit and Agenda 21.
  • A membership programme by 1994; 350 members in 74 countries by 1995.
  • Separate from the World Travel & Tourism Council since 1999; auditors in more than 80 countries.
  • Standard: 44 criteria with over 400 compliance indicators, e.g. obey local law, employ local residents, keep marketing accurate.
  • Use: firms are audited and can show a recognised label.
Mission 6

Limits of certification

  • Voluntary: only firms that choose (and pay) take part.
  • Green Globe says its criteria are "not an alternative to government regulation".
  • Québec warns certification "should reflect regional and local criteria": one global checklist may miss local issues.
  • Many labels compete, so tourists struggle to judge them.
Mission 6

UNESCO World Heritage Sites: Galápagos

  • Galápagos Islands, Ecuador: a World Heritage Site.
  • UNESCO lists "increased tourism, demographic growth" among the main threats.
  • Ecuador's 1998 Special Regime Law regulates tourism "through a visitor management system, permits and quotas", and controls residency and migration to the islands.
  • Uninhabited islands have "carefully planned tourist itineraries limiting visitation"; tourists' entry fee funds conservation.
Mission 6

The Danger List as pressure

  • 2007: Galápagos placed on the List of World Heritage in Danger over "invasive species, unbridled tourism and over-fishing".
  • 2010: removed after Ecuador made "significant progress".
  • Use: international reputational pressure on a state.
  • Limit: UNESCO cannot enforce; the state must act.
Quick check
?In the Galápagos, which measure most directly limits visitor numbers?
Mission check

?A small island nation markets new "eco-lodges" to foreign visitors. Coral damage is rising. Which approach is most likely to regulate ecotourism effectively?

International measures guide (Québec, Oslo 2007), certify (Green Globe) or pressure (UNESCO's Danger List), but effective regulation still depends on national law with permits, quotas and monitoring.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mass tourism grew into a global industry (about 1.52 billion arrivals in 2025) through disposable income, paid holidays, transport, package holidays and the internet.
  • The drivers reached high-income countries first, which is why participation per person is still far higher there.

Mission 2

  • Tourism brings foreign exchange, jobs, government revenue and infrastructure; the multiplier effect spreads spending through local firms.
  • Social benefits include community projects, housing and skills; how widely they spread depends on local ownership and supply chains.

Mission 3

  • Butler: resorts rise, stagnate at capacity, then decline or rejuvenate. Pleasure Periphery: tourism spreads outward from the core as transport gets cheaper, so new resorts start new cycles.
  • Mass tourism's challenges: pollution, overcrowding at honeypot sites, weak social sustainability and competition for resources.

Mission 4

  • Balearic Law 2/2016: a per-day tax on tourist stays (€0.50–€2 in 2016), with under-16 exemption and 50% reductions in the low season and from day 9; revenue earmarked for a sustainable tourism fund.
  • Evaluation: it meets a funding aim and targets seasonality, but numbers kept rising (15.3 → 15.7 million, 2024–25); it is a partial strategy, not a cap.

Mission 5

  • Ecotourism: responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, benefits local people and educates.
  • Benefits (Rwanda): conservation funding, community projects, revenue. Costs: displacement, threats to indigenous cultures, leakage, greenwashing, damage to fragile environments.

Mission 6

  • International regulation is hard: laws are national, global texts are advisory, certification is voluntary and greenwashing is easy.
  • Québec (2002) and Oslo (2007) set principles and share practice; Green Globe audits firms against 44 criteria; World Heritage status and the Danger List add pressure, as in the Galápagos, where Ecuador's own law sets permits and quotas.
