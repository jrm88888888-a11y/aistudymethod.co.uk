Mission 1

Mission 1: Is water an acid or a base?

Make a guess: Is water an acid or a base?

Mission 1

Acids give protons, bases take them

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. It uses a lone pair to take the H⁺.
  • HNO3 + NH3 → NH4⁺ + NO3⁻: HNO3 donates H⁺; NH3 accepts it.
  • An acid–base reaction is a proton transfer.
  • H⁺(aq) and H3O⁺(aq) are two ways of writing the same hydrated proton.
Mission 1

Base or alkali?

  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, releasing OH⁻(aq): NaOH, KOH, NH3(aq).
  • Every alkali is a base; not every base is an alkali.
  • Iron(III) oxide neutralises acids, so it is a base. It is insoluble, so it is not an alkali.
  • A base need not contain OH: NH3, O²⁻ and CO3²⁻ all accept H⁺ with a lone pair.

Quick check
?Ammonia reacts with water: NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). Which species acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid in the forward reaction?
Mission 1

Conjugate acid–base pairs

  • A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton.
  • Conjugate base: remove one H⁺; the charge falls by one. HSO4⁻ → SO4²⁻.
  • Conjugate acid: add one H⁺; the charge rises by one. NH3 → NH4⁺.
  • Every proton transfer has two pairs: HNO3/NO3⁻ and NH4⁺/NH3.
  • The acid and base that react are not a pair: each one's partner is across the arrow.
Quick check
?Which pair of species is a conjugate acid–base pair?
Mission 1

Amphiprotic species

  • Amphiprotic: can act as a Brønsted–Lowry acid or a base, depending on its partner.
  • It needs an H it can lose and a lone pair to accept H⁺.
  • Examples: H2O, HSO4⁻, H2PO4⁻, HPO4²⁻.
  • In any one reaction it plays one role only.
Mission 1

Water both ways: writing the equations

  • Water as a base: HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻.
  • Water as an acid: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻.
  • HPO4²⁻ as a base: HPO4²⁻ + H3O⁺ ⇌ H2PO4⁻ + H2O.
  • HPO4²⁻ as an acid: HPO4²⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ PO4³⁻ + H2O.
  • Check: as a base it gains one H; as an acid it loses one. Charges balance.
Quick check
?Which equation shows the hydrogensulfate ion, HSO4⁻, acting as a Brønsted–Lowry acid?
Mission check

?Water takes part in two reactions: (1) CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3⁺ + OH⁻ and (2) HBr + H2O → H3O⁺ + Br⁻. What role does water play?

Water is both, but one at a time. Faced with an acid it acts as a base; faced with a base it acts as an acid.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Make a guess: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Mission 2

What pH measures

  • pH = −log10[H⁺], with [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³. pH has no units.
  • Rearranged: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
  • Lower pH means higher [H⁺].
  • One pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]; two units, a hundredfold.
  • Very concentrated solutions can have pH below 0 or above 14.
Mission 2

Non-integer pH: worked examples

  • [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³: pH = −log10(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.49.
  • pH 4.60: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Use the log key (base 10), not ln.
  • Sense check: pH 3.49 lies between 3 and 4, so [H⁺] lies between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³.
Calculate
?A sample of rainwater has [H⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Calculate its pH to two decimal places.
Quick check
?Hydrobromic acid, HBr, is a strong acid.
What is the pH of 4.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ HBr(aq)?
Mission 2

Measuring pH two ways

  • Universal indicator (paper or solution): match the colour to a chart to estimate pH, to about 1 unit.
  • pH meter or data-logger probe: a precise reading, usually to 0.01.
  • Calibrate the meter first with buffer solutions of known pH (for example pH 4, 7 and 10).
  • Rinse the probe with distilled water between solutions; let the reading settle before you record it.
Quick check
?A student needs the pH of a solution. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Water ionises, a little: Kw

  • Water dissociates slightly: H2O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • [H2O] is effectively constant, so it is folded into the constant: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻].
  • Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K. pKw = −log10Kw = 14.00.
  • Every aqueous solution contains both H⁺ and OH⁻. Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
  • At 298 K, neutral means both are 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³: pH 7.00.
  • Kw changes with temperature, so neutral is pH 7 only at 298 K (25 °C). More in a later mission.
Mission 2

Strong base: pH from Kw

  • The product is fixed, so [H⁺] up tenfold means [OH⁻] down tenfold.
  • 0.020 mol dm⁻³ KOH dissociates fully: [OH⁻] = 0.020 mol dm⁻³.
  • [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.020 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³; pH = 12.30.
  • Sense check: a base, so pH is above 7 at 298 K.
Quick check
?At 298 K, solution P has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ and solution Q has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. How does [OH⁻] in P compare with [OH⁻] in Q?
Calculate
?Lithium hydroxide is a strong base. Calculate the pH of 0.0050 mol dm⁻³ LiOH(aq) at 298 K. Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.
Mission check

?Two samples of rain are tested with a pH meter. Sample A has pH 4.2; sample B has pH 5.7. How does [H⁺] in sample A compare with [H⁺] in sample B?

Back to the video. Lemon juice (pH ≈ 2) and coffee (pH ≈ 5) differ by 3 units: 10³, so about 1000 times more H⁺ in the lemon juice.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Make a guess: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Mission 3

Strong and weak

  • Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water. Weak ones dissociate only slightly.
  • Strong acids: HCl, HNO3, H2SO4 (also HBr, HI). Strong bases: NaOH, KOH.
  • Weak: carboxylic acids (CH3COOH, HCOOH), NH3 and amines.
  • Strong: HNO3 → H⁺ + NO3⁻. Weak: CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ (lies far to the left).
  • Weak base: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻, so [OH⁻] is far below the NH3 concentration.
  • Later, Ka puts a number on how weak an acid is.
Mission 3

Strength is not concentration

  • Concentration: amount of acid per unit volume. Strength: the extent of dissociation.
  • Diluting a strong acid raises its pH, but it stays strong.
  • At equal concentration, a weak acid has a higher pH and lower conductivity than a strong one.
  • At equal concentration, a weak base has a lower pH than a strong base.
  • A weak acid still neutralises the same amount of base: as H⁺ is used up, more of the acid dissociates.
Quick check
?A solution of nitric acid, HNO3(aq), has a concentration of 0.0010 mol dm⁻³. Which statement about this solution is correct?
Quick check
?Solution X is 0.0010 mol dm⁻³ KOH(aq). Solution Y is 2.0 mol dm⁻³ NH3(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Which way does the equilibrium lie?

  • An acid–base equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base.
  • The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
  • HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻ is effectively complete: NO3⁻ barely accepts H⁺.
  • HCOOH + H2O ⇌ HCOO⁻ + H3O⁺ lies left: H3O⁺ is a stronger acid than HCOOH.
  • Background: this rule is not in your specification, but it explains why weak acids dissociate so little.
Mission 3

Amines are weak bases too

  • An amine's N lone pair accepts H⁺: CH3CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2NH3⁺ + OH⁻.
  • Base strength depends on how available that lone pair is.
  • Primary aliphatic amines (ethylamine): the alkyl group pushes electron density onto N. Lone pair more available: stronger than NH3.
  • Primary aromatic amines (phenylamine): the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring. Less available: weaker than NH3.
  • Order: phenylamine < ammonia < ethylamine.
Mission check

?Which statement about solutions P and Q is correct?
  • P is 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ HCl(aq). Q is 0.50 mol dm⁻³ CH3COOH(aq), measured pH 2.53.

Yes. A dilute enough strong acid has a higher pH than a concentrated weak one. Strength and concentration are different things.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Make a guess: What is the pH of boiling pure water?

Mission 4

pOH, and the four-way switch

  • pOH = −log[OH⁻], just as pH = −log[H⁺].
  • At 298 K, pH + pOH = 14.00, because Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴.
  • Worked: [OH⁻] = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ → pOH 3.60 → pH 10.40 → [H⁺] = 4.0 × 10⁻¹¹ mol dm⁻³.
  • Background: pOH is not in your specification. You can always go via [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] instead.
Mission 4

Kw changes with temperature

  • H2O ⇌ H⁺ + OH⁻ is endothermic. Heat it: equilibrium shifts right, so Kw rises.
  • Kw: 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K; about 5.4 × 10⁻¹³ at 373 K.
  • Boiling pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻], so [H⁺] = √Kw = 7.35 × 10⁻⁷. pH = 6.13.
  • Still neutral: neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. It means pH 7.00 only at 298 K.
Calculate
?At 273 K, Kw = 1.14 × 10⁻¹⁵ mol² dm⁻⁶. Calculate the pH of pure water at 273 K.
Mission 4

Ka and pKa

  • Weak acid: HA(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + A⁻(aq). Ka = [H⁺][A⁻] / [HA], units mol dm⁻³.
  • Ka is just the equilibrium constant for the dissociation. Water, the solvent, is left out.
  • pKa = −log10Ka, and Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴷᵃ. pKa has no units.
  • Larger Ka → smaller pKa → stronger weak acid (more dissociated).
  • Ethanoic acid: Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³, so pKa = 4.76.
Quick check
?Benzoic acid has pKa = 4.20. What is its Ka?
Quick check
?At 298 K the pKa of methanoic acid, HCOOH, is 3.75 and the pKa of ethanoic acid, CH3COOH, is 4.76.
Which statement about 0.10 mol dm⁻³ solutions of the two acids is correct?
Mission 4

Weak bases: Kb and pKb

  • Background: Kb and pKb are not in your specification. For bases you use Kw.
  • Weak base: B + H2O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻] / [B].
  • pKb = −log10Kb. Larger Kb, smaller pKb: stronger base.
  • Ammonia: pKb 4.75. Methylamine: pKb 3.34, a stronger base.
  • Also background: for a conjugate pair, Ka × Kb = Kw. The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
Mission check

?What is the pH of pure water at this temperature, and is it neutral?
  • At 323 K, Kw = 5.5 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.

Boiling pure water is about pH 6.1, and it is still neutral. Kw rises with temperature, so [H⁺] and [OH⁻] rise together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Acid = proton donor; base = proton acceptor (using a lone pair). Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺.
  • Water, like HSO4⁻ and HPO4²⁻, is amphiprotic: its partner decides its role.

Mission 2

  • pH = −log10[H⁺]; one unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
  • Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K: for a strong base, [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻].

Mission 3

  • Strong: completely dissociated; weak: only slightly. Strength is not concentration.
  • Equal concentrations: weak acid, higher pH. Different concentrations: anything can happen.
  • An acid–base equilibrium lies towards the weaker acid and weaker base.

Mission 4

  • Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]; pKa = −log10Ka. Smaller pKa: stronger acid.
  • Kw rises with temperature: pure water is always neutral, but pH 7.00 only at 298 K.
