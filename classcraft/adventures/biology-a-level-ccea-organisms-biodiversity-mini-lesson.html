Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Make a guess: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Mission 1

Supply against demand

  • Every living cell needs oxygen in and carbon dioxide out, plus nutrients in and wastes out.
  • Heat is exchanged with the surroundings across the surface too.
  • Supply depends on surface area: the area materials cross.
  • Demand depends on volume: the amount of living, respiring tissue.
  • So the key number is the surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume. Written SA:V or SA/V.
  • Surface area: the cells in direct contact with the surroundings. Volume: the metabolically active tissue that creates the demand.

Worked example: bigger means less surface per volume

  • Total surface area rises with size. SA:V falls.
  • Say: "a smaller surface area to volume ratio" — not: "a smaller surface area".

Mission 1

Small organisms: diffusion is enough

  • A single-celled organism, e.g. Amoeba, exchanges gases across its cell-surface membrane.
  • O2 diffuses in and CO2 diffuses out, each down its concentration gradient.
  • High SA:V, and no part of the cell is more than a few micrometres from the surface.
  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances. Ten times the distance takes about a hundred times as long.
  • Approximate times for O2 to diffuse through water: 0.01 mm (about one cell) 0.02 s · 1 mm 4 minutes · 1 cm 7 hours.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: change shape

Two shapes with the same volume, 8 mm³:

  • Cube, 2 × 2 × 2 mm: surface area 24 mm², SA:V = 3 mm⁻¹.
  • Flat sheet, 4 × 4 × 0.5 mm: surface area 40 mm², SA:V = 5 mm⁻¹.
  • Flattened or thin shapes add surface with little extra volume.
  • They also keep every cell close to the surface.
  • Examples: the flat body of a flatworm; thin, flat leaves; root hairs.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: specialised systems

  • In a large, active animal the body surface is too small, and most cells are too far from it.
  • Specialised exchange surfaces pack a huge area inside the body: lungs, gills.
  • Ventilation brings fresh air or water to that surface.
  • Mass transport: a fluid flows in bulk, carrying substances long distances quickly. Diffusion covers only the last micrometres.
  • Mammals: blood pumped round by the heart (circulation); air moved in and out by ventilation.
  • Flowering plants: water and mineral ions in xylem; sugars in phloem (translocation).
  • Plants need transport despite a lower metabolic rate: they are large, and roots and leaves are far apart.

Mission 2

What makes an exchange surface efficient

  • Large surface area: more molecules cross at once.
  • Thin: a short diffusion distance, often one cell or less.
  • Steep concentration gradient, kept up by supply on one side and removal on the other.
  • Moist: O2 and CO2 dissolve in a film of water before they cross membranes.
Mission 2

Fick's law

  • Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of surface.
  • Each feature on the last screen is one term in this relationship.
  • Area × 2 → rate × 2. Difference in concentration × 2 → rate × 2. Thickness × 2 → rate ÷ 2.
  • Area ÷ 2 and difference × 2 together → rate unchanged.
  • Thickness is the length of the diffusion path.
  • Use it to compare rates as ratios, not to find an absolute rate.
Quick check
?A model exchange surface is changed. Its surface area is doubled and its thickness is tripled. The difference in concentration does not change. By what factor does the rate of diffusion change?
Mission 2

Keeping the gradient steep

  • Diffusion evens out concentrations. If nothing moves, the gradient disappears and net diffusion stops.
  • Outside: ventilation replaces used air (lungs) or water (gills) with fresh.
  • Inside: continuous blood flow carries O2 away and brings CO2.
  • A dense network of capillaries puts blood next to every part of the surface.
  • Say: "blood flow carries oxygenated blood away, keeping the gradient" — not: "blood slows down to collect more oxygen".
Quick check

Mission 3

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • For an organism: surface area = cells in direct contact with the surroundings; volume = metabolically active tissue.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 3

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side / µm1234
Surface area / µm²6245496
Volume / µm³182764
SA:V / µm⁻¹6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A cube of side 1 mm has a surface area of 6 mm², a volume of 1 mm³ and a surface area-to-volume ratio of 6. The side length is increased to 4 mm. What is the new surface area-to-volume ratio?
Mission 3

Worked example: other shapes

  • Sphere: SA = 4πr²; V = (4/3)πr³.
  • Cylinder: SA = 2πrh + 2πr²; V = πr²h.
  • Cuboid (rectangular solid): SA = 2lh + 2lw + 2wh; V = lwh.
  • Sphere, r = 5 µm:
  • SA = 4 × π × 5² = 314 µm².
  • V = (4/3) × π × 5³ = 524 µm³.
  • SA:V = 314 ÷ 524 = 0.60 µm⁻¹.
  • Count every face: hidden faces too.
Quick check
?A hypothetical single-celled organism has the shape of a rectangular solid, 20 μm long, 10 μm high and 10 μm wide. What is the cell's surface area-to-volume ratio?
Mission 3

Large organisms

  • As an organism grows, SA:V falls. Many cells are far from the surface.
  • Diffusion is too slow over long distances.
  • So large organisms have specialised exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, gut, leaves) and mass transport systems (blood).
Quick check
?Shrews are among the smallest mammals. Like larger mammals, they keep their body temperature nearly constant. A shrew must eat much more food per gram of its body mass each day than a deer does. Which statement best explains this difference?
Mission check

?A spherical single-celled organism grows from a radius of 10 µm to a radius of 30 µm, keeping its shape. Which prediction is best supported?

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 4

Make a guess: Blood leaves the heart in surges but creeps past your cells in a steady trickle. How?

Mission 4

Why diffusion is not enough

  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances.
  • Large animals have a small SA:V and a high metabolic rate. Their surface cannot supply every cell.
  • Mass transport: the whole fluid flows, carrying everything in it. A pump (the heart) drives it.
  • It moves substances quickly over large distances, between exchange surfaces and cells.
Mission 4

Open or closed, single or double

  • Open: blood leaves vessels and bathes the organs directly.
  • Closed: blood stays inside vessels all the way round.
  • Single (bony fish): heart → gills → body → heart. One pass through the heart per circuit.
  • Blood loses much of its pressure in the gill capillaries. It reaches the body at low pressure.
  • Double (mammals): heart → lungs → heart → body → heart. Two passes per circuit.
  • Pulmonary circuit (lungs) and systemic circuit (body). After the lungs, blood is pumped again, so the body gets high-pressure blood.
  • Say: "double = blood passes through the heart twice per circuit" — not: "two capillary beds".
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) In a bony fish, mean blood pressure was measured at about 5 kPa in the artery leaving the heart and about 3.5 kPa in the vessel leaving the gills. In a mammal, mean pressure in the aorta was about 13 kPa. What do these data show about the two types of circulation?
Mission 4

The mammalian circuit: named vessels

  • Heart → lungs: pulmonary artery. Lungs → heart: pulmonary vein.
  • Heart → body: aorta. Body → heart: vena cava.
  • Heart muscle: coronary arteries (branch from the base of the aorta).
  • Kidneys: renal artery in, renal vein out.
  • Thorax: superior and inferior venae cavae.
  • Abdomen: mesenteric artery (to the gut), hepatic portal vein (gut → liver), hepatic artery (to the liver), hepatic vein (liver → vena cava).
  • Arteries carry blood away from the heart, whatever its oxygen content. The pulmonary artery carries deoxygenated blood.
Mission 4

Three layers, five vessels

  • Walls are built from up to three layers:
    • inner: endothelium (squamous: one flat cell thick, smooth);
    • middle: smooth muscle and elastic tissue;
    • outer: fibrous (collagen) tissue.

Mission 4

Arteries and arterioles

  • Each ventricular contraction forces a surge of blood into the arteries.
  • Elastic tissue stretches as the surge arrives, then recoils between beats.
  • Recoil keeps pressure up between beats and smooths the flow.
  • Thick muscle and elastic tissue let the wall withstand high pressure; fibrous tissue stops it overstretching.
  • Smooth endothelium: little friction.
  • Arterioles: smooth muscle contracts (vasoconstriction) or relaxes (vasodilation).
  • This sets how much blood enters each capillary bed.
  • Say: "the artery wall stretches and recoils" — not: "the artery wall contracts to pump blood".
Quick check
?What is the role of the elastic tissue in the wall of an artery?
Mission 4

Veins: back to the heart at low pressure

  • By the veins, blood pressure is low and there is no pulse.
  • Wide lumen: little resistance to flow.
  • Thin, flexible wall: squeezed by surrounding skeletal muscles as they contract.
  • Valves: passive flaps that close if blood starts to flow backwards.
  • So every squeeze moves blood one way: towards the heart.
  • Much fibrous tissue protects the wall.
  • Say: "valves prevent backflow" — not: "valves push blood along".
Mission 4

Capillaries: built for exchange

  • Wall: endothelium only, one cell thick → short diffusion distance.
  • Narrow: red cells pass in single file, close to the wall.
  • Branching: huge numbers form capillary beds → huge total surface area.
  • Every cell lies close to a capillary.
  • Flow is slowest here: more time for exchange.
  • Endothelial cells are squamous (flat).
  • Say: "thin walls give a short diffusion distance" — not: "thin walls let blood flow faster".
Quick check
?A muscle receives blood through a network of thousands of capillaries rather than through a single vessel of the same total volume. What is the advantage of this arrangement for exchange?
Mission 4

From surge to trickle

  • Arteries: pressure surges with each beat; elastic recoil smooths the swings.
  • Arterioles: narrow and muscular: the biggest fall in pressure. The surges die away.
  • Capillaries: one aorta branches into billions of capillaries. Their total cross-sectional area is enormous.
  • The same volume of blood per second spread over far more area → flow hundreds of times slower.
  • Veins: pressure lowest; muscles and valves return the blood.
  • So: surges in, steady trickle past the cells.
Mission 4Practical

Arteries and veins in section

  • Practical: identify arteries, veins and capillaries in sections or photographs.
  • Artery: wall thick relative to the lumen; round, narrow lumen; wavy elastic layer.
  • Vein: wall thin relative to the lumen; wide lumen, often flattened or irregular.
  • Capillary: a ring of one or two flat endothelial cells, about one red cell wide.
Quick check
?In a section, vessel P has a wall 0.30 mm thick around a round lumen 0.40 mm across. Vessel Q has a wall 0.10 mm thick around a flattened lumen 1.6 mm across. Which identification is correct?
Mission check

?With age, the walls of large arteries lose elastic tissue and become stiffer. Blood pressure in an arm artery is measured at its highest (as the ventricle contracts) and its lowest (between beats). What change in these readings is expected?

Stretch and recoil in elastic arteries turn the heart's surges into steady pressure; lose the elastic tissue and the surges come back.

Mission 5

Transpiration: the price of carbon dioxide

  • Stomata open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis.
  • Water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
  • Water vapour diffuses out through the open stomata: transpiration.
  • Transpiration is an unavoidable consequence of gas exchange.
  • Say: "water vapour diffuses out while stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens stomata to lose water".
Quick check
?Why is transpiration described as a consequence of gas exchange rather than as a process the leaf carries out for its own sake?
Mission 5

Xylem vessels: built for flow

  • Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to leaves.
  • Dead cells, no contents: nothing obstructs the flow.
  • End walls lost: cells join into one continuous tube.
  • Lignified walls: rigid, so the vessel does not collapse under tension; also waterproof, and support the plant.
  • Pits: gaps in the lignin. Water moves sideways between vessels and out to leaf cells.
  • Say: "lignin stops the vessel collapsing under tension" — not: "lignin is only for support".
Mission 5

Seeing the lignin

  • Protoxylem (first-formed, in growing regions): lignin in rings or spirals, so the vessel can still stretch.
  • Metaxylem (later, in mature regions): lignin over most of the wall, with pits; rigid and wider.
Quick check
?Why are the walls of xylem vessels thickened with lignin?
Mission 5

Cohesion–tension: pulled from the top

  • (1) Water evaporates from leaf cell walls. Water is drawn out of the xylem, through the cell walls, to replace it.
  • (2) This puts the xylem water under tension.
  • Tension is a negative pressure.
  • (3) Cohesion: hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, so the whole column is pulled up unbroken.
  • Adhesion: water is attracted to the hydrophilic vessel walls, which helps it creep up narrow spaces.
  • The flow from roots to leaves is the transpiration stream.
  • Passive: no ATP. The energy comes from the Sun evaporating water.
  • Evidence: tree trunks shrink slightly in diameter at midday; cut xylem draws air in, not water out.
  • Say: "water is pulled up under tension" — not: "the roots pump water up".
Quick check
?What generates the force that draws water up the xylem from the roots to the leaves?
Mission check

?Florists cut the stems of fresh flowers under water. A stem cut in air and then put in a vase often wilts, even though its cut end is in water. Which explanation is correct?

The tree and the vase work the same way: evaporation pulls, cohesion passes the pull down, and the column must stay unbroken.

Mission 6

Routes out, and the leaf's own features

  • Stomata: the main route of transpiration.
  • Cuticle: a minor route. Waxy, so very little water crosses it.
  • The epidermis secretes the cuticle and holds the guard cells.
  • Internal factors: more leaf surface area or higher stomatal density → faster; thicker cuticle → slower.
Mission 6

Four outside factors

  • Transpiration rate depends on the water-vapour gradient from air spaces to air, and on how open the stomata are.
Factor risesRateWhy
Light intensity↑stomata open wider
Temperature↑faster evaporation and diffusion; warm air holds more vapour, so the gradient is steeper
Humidity↓less steep gradient
Air movement↑humid air next to the leaf is blown away; gradient steeper
  • Soil water low → guard cells lose turgor, stomata close → rate falls.
  • To measure the rate, a potometer times how far an air bubble moves as a cut shoot takes up water: distance moved ÷ time gives the rate of uptake.
  • Say: "humid air reduces the water-vapour gradient" — not: "humid air makes the leaf wetter, so it loses more".

Mission check

?A grower's greenhouse plants wilt on hot afternoons. The grower wants to slow their transpiration. Which change would do this?

Warm, dry, moving air steepens the gradient; humid, still air flattens it. The plant does not decide: diffusion does.

Mission 7

The limiting factor

  • Rate of photosynthesis depends on several factors at once: light intensity, CO2 concentration, temperature.
  • The one in shortest supply sets the rate: the limiting factor.
  • Raise the limiting factor → rate rises.
  • Raise any other factor → no change.
  • Light: powers the light-dependent stage. CO2: substrate for rubisco. Temperature: speed of enzyme-catalysed steps.
Mission 7

Reading the curves

1. Rate rises as light rises: light is limiting.

2. The curve flattens: light is no longer limiting. Another factor is.

3. A curve at higher CO2 reaches a higher plateau: CO2 was limiting the first plateau.

  • On the rising part, curves at different CO2 overlap: there, only light matters.
  • Say: "the plateau shows another factor is limiting" — not: "the plant has reached its maximum rate".
Quick check
?Pondweed submerged in water at 25 °C with the carbon dioxide concentration of ordinary air was exposed to increasing light intensity. The rate of photosynthesis rose at first and then levelled off, staying constant however much the light intensity was increased. Which change is most likely to raise the rate at this plateau?
Mission 7

Temperature and water

  • Light-independent reactions are enzyme-catalysed: warmer → faster, up to an optimum.
  • Above the optimum: enzymes such as rubisco denature; rate falls.
  • Low temperature can limit the rate even in bright light and high CO2.
  • Say: "water stress limits photosynthesis by closing stomata" — not: "water is short as a reactant".
Mission 7

Gross, net and the compensation point

  • Leaves respire all the time, in light and dark.
  • Gross photosynthesis: total rate.
  • Net photosynthesis = gross − respiration. This is what gas exchange measures.
  • Rate measured as CO2 uptake or O2 production per unit time.
  • Compensation point: light intensity where photosynthesis = respiration. Net gas exchange is zero.
  • Midday: net CO2 uptake (mostly photosynthesis). Night: only respiration, so net CO2 release.
  • CO2 reaches mesophyll cells by diffusion: open stomata, air spaces in spongy mesophyll, thin leaf.
Mission 7

Worked example: finding the compensation point

Illustrative data: net O2 release by a leaf (arbitrary units).

Light intensity / arbitrary units010203040
Net O2 release / arbitrary units−1.0−0.20.61.42.2

1. Straight line. Gradient = (2.2 − (−1.0)) ÷ (40 − 0) = 0.08 per light unit.

2. y-intercept = −1.0: in the dark the leaf only respires, so respiration = 1.0.

3. Compensation point: net = 0. 0 = −1.0 + 0.08x → x = 1.0 ÷ 0.08 = 12.5 light units (the x-intercept).

4. Gross at 30 units = net + respiration = 1.4 + 1.0 = 2.4.

  • Assumes respiration stays the same in light and dark.
Mission 7

Practical: measuring the rate

  • Pondweed (e.g. Elodea or Cabomba) in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution (CO2 source).
  • Measure O2: volume collected in a photosynthometer capillary per minute (better than counting bubbles).
  • Vary light: lamp distance; intensity falls about as 1 ÷ d². Use an LED or a water heat shield.
  • Vary CO2: hydrogencarbonate concentration. Vary temperature: water bath.
  • Let the plant acclimatise at each setting; repeat; keep other factors constant.
  • Alternatives: algal beads in hydrogencarbonate indicator; floating leaf discs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • As size increases, SA:V falls; demand (volume) outgrows supply (surface).
  • Diffusion alone is enough only over short distances; large organisms need flattened shapes, specialised exchange surfaces and ventilation.
  • Mass transport (circulation, xylem, phloem) carries substances over long distances.

Mission 2

  • Efficient exchange surfaces: large area, thin, steep gradient kept up by ventilation and blood flow.
  • They are also moist, so gases dissolve before crossing.
  • Fick's law: rate ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness.

Mission 3

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.
  • Large organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems; small animals lose heat faster per gram.

Mission 4

  • Arteries stretch and recoil; arterioles control flow and drop pressure; capillaries' huge total area slows flow for exchange; veins return blood with valves and muscle squeeze.
  • Fish: single circulation (heart once per circuit); mammals: double circulation, so body blood is at high pressure.

Mission 5

  • Xylem vessels are dead, hollow, lignified tubes with pits: a continuous path that does not collapse under tension.
  • Evaporation from leaves puts the xylem under tension; cohesion pulls the whole column up. No ATP is spent.

Mission 6

  • Light, temperature and air movement raise transpiration; humidity lowers it. All act through stomatal opening or the water-vapour gradient.

Mission 7

  • The rate is set by the factor in shortest supply (light, CO2 or temperature). A plateau means another factor now limits.
  • Net photosynthesis = gross − respiration; at the compensation point, net gas exchange is zero.
