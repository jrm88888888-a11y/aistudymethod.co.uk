Arguments Based on Reason

The ontological argument is a priori and deductive: it tries to prove God exists from the very definition of God, using reason alone. We follow Anselm and Descartes, the parody of Gaunilo, the objection of Kant, and the modern defence of Norman Malcolm.

Anselm · Proslogion

Anselm: the classic argument

In the Proslogion, Anselm defines God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived. His reasoning:

  • Even 'the fool' who denies God has this idea in his understanding.
  • But a being that exists in reality is greater than one that exists in the mind alone.
  • So if the greatest conceivable being existed only in the mind, we could conceive a greater one (that also exists in reality) — a contradiction.
  • Therefore God must exist in reality.

Second form: Anselm adds that God cannot even be conceived not to exist — God has necessary existence, unlike contingent things.

Descartes

Descartes: the perfect being

Descartes gives his own version. God is a supremely perfect being. Existence is a perfection. A supremely perfect being that lacked existence would lack a perfection, and so would not be supremely perfect.

  • Therefore existence belongs to God's essence, as necessarily as three angles belong to a triangle, or a valley to a mountain.
  • We cannot conceive of God without existence any more than a triangle without its angles.
Challenge · Gaunilo

Gaunilo: the perfect island

A monk, Gaunilo, replied 'on behalf of the fool'. He parodies Anselm: imagine the most perfect island conceivable. By Anselm's logic, since a real island is greater than an imagined one, this perfect island must exist — which is absurd.

Gaunilo's point: you cannot define things into existence. Anselm replied that the argument only works for God, the necessary being, not for contingent things like islands.

Challenge · Kant

Kant: existence is not a predicate

Kant gives the most famous objection: existence is not a predicate — not a property that adds to the concept of a thing.

  • Saying a thing exists tells us it is instantiated in reality; it does not describe or enlarge the concept.
  • Kant's example: a hundred real thalers (coins) contain no more in concept than a hundred imagined ones — existence adds nothing to the idea.
  • So you cannot include 'exists' in the definition of God and thereby prove God real.

Defence · Malcolm

Malcolm: necessary existence

Norman Malcolm accepts Kant's point about the first form, but defends Anselm's second form. He argues God's existence is either impossible or necessary:

  • God, if God exists, cannot be contingent (dependent) — that would not be the greatest being.
  • God's existence is not impossible (the concept is not self-contradictory).
  • Therefore God's existence is necessary.

Evaluate: critics say Malcolm still moves illegitimately from the concept of a necessary being to its real existence — the same gap Kant identified.

Recap

The big ideas to take into the exam

Anselm: God = that than which nothing greater can be conceived; a real God is greater than one only in the mind; God has necessary existence

Descartes: existence is a perfection, so a supremely perfect being must exist (triangle analogy)

Gaunilo: the perfect island parody: you cannot define things into existence

Kant: existence is not a predicate; it adds nothing to a concept

Malcolm: God existence is impossible or necessary; not impossible, so necessary
