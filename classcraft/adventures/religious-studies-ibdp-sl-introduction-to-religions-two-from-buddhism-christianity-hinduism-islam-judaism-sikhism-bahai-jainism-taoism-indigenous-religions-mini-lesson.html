Mission 1

Nine religions, three groups

  • The course names nine living religions in three groups:
  • Group 1: Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism.
  • Group 2: Judaism, Christianity, Islam.
  • Group 3: Taoism, Jainism, the Bahá'í Faith.
  • Part 1 (Introduction to world religions): you study five of the nine, with at least one from each group.
  • So every course includes at least one of Taoism, Jainism or the Bahá'í Faith.
  • The groups are a course tool, not a theory: they make you sample very different traditions.
Mission 1

The shape of the course

  • Part 1: five religions, surveyed (about 50 teaching hours).
  • Part 2: two religions in depth, one from Group 1 and one from Group 2 (about 80 hours).
  • Part 2 is studied through five themes: rituals, sacred texts, doctrines/beliefs, religious experience, ethics and moral conduct.
  • Part 3: an investigative study of your own (internal assessment, about 20 hours).
  • Assessment: Paper 1 (Part 1, stimulus questions, 1¼ hours, 30%) · Paper 2 (Part 2, two essays, 1½ hours, 45%) · internal assessment (25%).
  • Paper 1 sets nine stimulus-based questions (one per religion) in three sections that match the groups; you answer five, at least one from each section.
Mission 1

Three questions for every religion

  • Part 1 asks the same three questions of each religion:
  • What is the human condition? What are humans like, and what has gone wrong (or is missing)?
  • Where are we going? What is the goal: liberation, salvation, union, harmony, a world restored?
  • How do we get there? Which beliefs, practices and ways of life lead to the goal?
  • Think of it as diagnosis → goal → path.
  • The questions let you compare religions without forcing them into one mould: each religion answers in its own terms.

Mission 1

Inquiring, analytical, empathetic

  • The course aims at an inquiring, analytical and empathetic approach to religions.
  • Inquiring: ask real questions; follow the evidence of what believers say and do.
  • Analytical: define concepts precisely; compare; notice tensions and diversity within each tradition.
  • Empathetic: try to grasp what it is like to belong to a religion and why its beliefs matter to the faith member.
  • It is not a course about which religion is true. You will not be asked for that verdict.
  • Respect is not agreement: you can describe a belief accurately and still think about it critically.
Mission 1

The phenomenological method

  • Phenomenology of religion studies religion as it appears to believers.
  • Epoché ("bracketing"): set aside your own assumptions and judgements of truth while you describe. An ancient Greek sceptics' term, made central to modern philosophy by Edmund Husserl (1859–1938).
  • Empathy: imaginatively enter the insider's point of view.
  • Classic phenomenologists of religion include Gerardus van der Leeuw (1890–1950), Religion in Essence and Manifestation (1933, English 1938).
  • Describe first; compare second; evaluate last, and only concepts and evidence, never someone's faith.
Mission 1

Seven dimensions of religion

  • Ninian Smart (1927–2001) proposed dimensions of religion: a checklist so you do not reduce a religion to beliefs only.
  • Ritual, experiential, narrative/mythic, doctrinal, ethical, social/institutional, material.
  • Six appear in The Religious Experience of Mankind (1969); material is added in The World's Religions (1989).
  • Use: a religion's answer to "how do we get there?" may stress the ritual (e.g. puja), ethical (e.g. sewa, selfless service), experiential (e.g. meditation) dimension, or several at once.

Mission 1Evaluate

Do three questions fit every religion fairly?

  • Claim: The three questions let you study every religion fairly.
  • For: They are open: "liberation", "salvation", "harmony" and "a world repaired" can all answer "where are we going?"
  • For: They make comparison possible without ranking, and push you beyond memorising lists.
  • Against: They may carry a salvation-shaped pattern (problem → goal → path) taken from some religions. Many Jews stress living by Torah now more than a destination; Taoists speak of harmony, not escape.
  • Against: Full epoché may be impossible: the questions you ask already shape what you find. Some insiders say understanding needs practice, not only empathy.
  • Judgement criteria: Do insiders recognise the account? Does it show diversity within the religion? Does it use the religion's own terms?
Mission 1Worked answer

Using a stimulus fairly

  • Stimulus: a photo of people sitting in silent meditation in a Buddhist vihara.
  • Weak: "Buddhists meditate because they are calm people."

  • Step 3 (diversity): Theravada and Zen practise it differently (e.g. vipassana insight; zazen sitting).
  • Skill tip: use the stimulus, name the key concept in the religion's own term, then link it to one of the three questions.

Mission 2

One name, many traditions

  • "Hinduism" is a modern name for a family of traditions from India, with no single founder or creed.
  • Many Hindus call it Sanatana Dharma, "the eternal way" or "eternal order".
  • Major traditions: Vaishnava (devotion to Vishnu, e.g. as Krishna or Rama), Shaiva (Shiva), Shakta (the Goddess, Devi), Smarta.
  • Key texts: the Vedas and Upanishads (shruti, "what is heard"); the Bhagavad Gita (within the epic Mahabharata).
  • Most Hindus share the ideas in this mission, but explain them differently. Say "many Hindus believe", not "Hindus believe".
Mission 2

Atman and Brahman

  • Atman: the true, eternal self in every living being; not the body or the mind.
  • Brahman: ultimate reality, the source and ground of everything.
  • How do atman and Brahman relate? The traditions disagree, and the answer shapes the goal.
  • Advaita ("non-dual"): atman is Brahman. The Upanishadic saying tat tvam asi, "that thou art" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7), is read this way.
  • Theistic schools: atman is distinct from God, but depends on God and belongs to God.
Mission 2

The condition: samsara and karma

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth. The atman passes through many lives (human, animal, divine and more).
  • Karma: action and its consequences. Every intentional action leaves effects that shape this life and future births.
  • Good karma can bring a better rebirth, but any rebirth is still inside samsara.
  • Karma is not fate: it is the result of chosen actions, and present choices shape what comes next.
  • The human condition: the atman is bound to samsara by karma.
Mission 2Read the source

Show answer

Q2 What does the image suggest dies at death, and what does not?

Show answer

Mission 2

The condition: maya and ignorance

  • Why does the atman stay bound? Because of avidya: ignorance of its true nature.
  • We identify with body, desires and ego ("I am this body; this is mine"). Desire drives action; action creates karma; karma brings rebirth.
  • Maya: often translated "illusion".
  • In Advaita, maya makes the one reality appear as many separate things; the world is not ultimately real as it appears.
  • In theistic schools, the world is real; maya is God's power, and the problem is attachment and forgetting God.
  • So "maya = the world is fake" is too simple: say how each school uses the word.

Mission 2Diversity

Where are we going? Moksha

  • Moksha: liberation from samsara, the end of rebirth. The highest of the four aims of life (purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama, moksha).
  • Advaita (associated with Shankara, c. 8th century CE): moksha is realising that atman is Brahman; separateness disappears, like a wave knowing it is ocean.
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja, traditionally 1017–1137): the soul is real and distinct, yet inseparable from God; moksha is loving union with God (Vishnu).
  • Dvaita (Madhva, c. 1238–1317): soul and God are eternally distinct; moksha is joyful life in God's presence.
  • Heaven (svarga) is not moksha: in most Hindu thought it is a temporary reward, still within samsara.
Mission 2

How do we get there? Three paths

  • Hindus speak of several margas or yogas (paths, disciplines) to moksha:
  • Jnana yoga: path of knowledge. Study, reflection and meditation that remove ignorance; central for Advaita.
  • Karma yoga: path of action. Do your duty (dharma) without attachment to reward.
  • Bhakti yoga: path of devotion. Loving worship of God (puja, singing, pilgrimage); the most widely practised path.
  • Many add raja (or dhyana) yoga, the path of meditation.
  • Most Hindus combine paths. Many theistic traditions add that liberation comes through God's grace.
Mission 2Read the source

> "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made / With pious will." (9:26)

Q1 Which path does each passage teach?

Q2 Why might acting without wanting "the fruit" stop new karma binding the self?

Show answer

Binding comes from selfish desire; action done as duty or offering does not feed attachment.

Mission 3

A diagnosis and a cure

  • Siddhartha Gautama (traditionally c. 5th century BCE, northern India) became the Buddha, "the awakened one". Buddhists see him as a teacher who found the way, not as a god.
  • His first teaching sets out the Four Noble Truths, often compared to a doctor's method:
  • Dukkha: there is suffering (the illness).
  • Samudaya: suffering has a cause, tanha (craving) (the cause).
  • Nirodha: suffering can end (the cure exists): nibbana.
  • Magga: the way to end it is the Noble Eightfold Path (the treatment).
  • The Truths map onto the three questions: condition (1–2), goal (3), path (4).
Mission 3

The condition: three marks of existence

  • Buddhists teach three marks (lakkhana) of all conditioned things (Pali terms; Sanskrit in brackets):
  • Anicca (anitya): impermanence. Everything arises, changes and passes away.
  • Dukkha (duhkha): suffering, or unsatisfactoriness: pain, but also the unease of clinging to what cannot last.
  • Anatta (anatman): not-self. There is no permanent, unchanging self or soul.
  • A person is a changing process of five khandhas (skandhas, "aggregates"): body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness.
  • Contrast: Hindu traditions teach an eternal atman; Buddhism denies one.
Mission 3Read the source

Dhammapada 277–279 (trans. F. Max Müller, 1881):

> "‘All created things perish,’ he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (277)

> "‘All created things are grief and pain,' … ‘All forms are unreal,’ he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity." (278–279)

Q1 Match each verse to a mark.

Show answer

Q2 What do the verses say happens to someone who sees these marks?

Show answer

Mission 3

Why suffering continues

  • Tanha (craving): thirst for pleasure, for existence and for non-existence. We cling to what cannot last.
  • Craving is rooted in ignorance (avijja): not seeing the three marks.
  • The three poisons, greed, hatred and delusion, keep the cycle turning (often pictured at the hub of the Tibetan wheel of life).
  • Kamma (karma): intentional actions shape future experience and rebirth.
  • Rebirth without a self: like a flame lit from another flame, a new life continues the old process; nothing permanent passes over.
  • So the human condition is samsara driven by craving, ignorance and kamma.
Mission 3

Where are we going? Nibbana

  • Nibbana (nirvana): literally "blowing out", the fires of greed, hatred and delusion going out.
  • It is the end of craving, and so the end of dukkha and of rebirth.
  • It can be reached in this life (the Buddha's awakening); at death the awakened person is not reborn.
  • Buddhists describe it as peace, freedom and the unconditioned, but often say it cannot be fully described: not a place, not annihilation of a self (there was no permanent self to destroy).
Mission 3Diversity

Arahant and bodhisattva

  • Theravada (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar): the ideal is the arahant, "worthy one", who reaches nibbana by following the Buddha's path, often as a monk or nun.
  • Mahayana (China, Japan, Korea, Tibet, Vietnam): the ideal is the bodhisattva, who aims at full Buddhahood and vows to help all beings to awakening, out of compassion (karuna).
  • Many Mahayana schools teach Buddha-nature: every being can become a Buddha.
  • Pure Land Buddhists rely on the vow of Amitabha Buddha for rebirth in his Pure Land, where awakening is easier.
  • Both traditions aim at awakening; they differ on the ideal and on who can help.

Mission 3

How do we get there? The Eightfold Path

  • The Noble Eightfold Path: eight "right" (samma, complete) ways, practised together, not in order.
  • Wisdom (panna): right view, right intention.
  • Ethical conduct (sila): right speech, right action, right livelihood.
  • Meditation (samadhi): right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • The Middle Way: between indulgence and harsh self-denial; the Buddha tried both before his awakening.
  • Lay Buddhists often keep the Five Precepts (e.g. not harming living beings, not taking what is not given).
Mission 3Read the source

Going for refuge

Dhammapada 190–191 (trans. Müller, 1881):

> "He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths:— Viz. pain, the origin of pain, the destruction of pain, and the eightfold holy way that leads to the quieting of pain"

  • Müller's "the Law" = the Dhamma (the teaching); "the Church" = the Sangha (the community). Together with the Buddha: the Three Refuges (Three Jewels).

Q1 Name the three refuges in Pali.

Show answer

Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha.

Q2 Which three questions do verses 190–191 touch?

Show answer

Pain and its origin: condition; destruction of pain: goal; the eightfold way: path.

Mission 4

Sikhism in brief

  • Founded by Guru Nanak (1469–1539) in the Punjab; followed by nine further human Gurus.
  • The tenth, Guru Gobind Singh (1666–1708), named the scripture as the final Guru: the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • The scripture opens with the Mool Mantar, beginning Ik Onkar: "There is one God" (or "one Creator").
  • Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord"): the name most Sikhs use for God.
  • Sikhs believe God is nirgun (beyond all qualities) and sargun (present in creation), and that God's light (jot) is in every being.
Mission 4

Hukam: living within God's order

  • Hukam: God's will or order, which runs through all creation.
  • Humans have a precious opportunity: a human birth is the chance to come close to God.
  • Sikhs believe in karma and rebirth: actions shape future births in the cycle of birth and death.
  • Problem: instead of living in tune with hukam, people live centred on themselves.
  • Guru Nanak's answer: not ritual or withdrawal from the world, but a God-centred life as a householder.
Mission 4

The condition: haumai

  • Haumai: literally "I-I" or "I-me": ego, self-centredness.
  • The self-centred person thinks of everything in terms of "I" and "mine", and forgets God.
  • Sikhs believe haumai separates the person from Waheguru, who is present within them,
  • and keeps the soul in the cycle of rebirth.
  • It is not just pride: it is a whole outlook in which the self, not God, is the centre.
Mission 4Read the source

A disease with its own cure

Guru Granth Sahib, Asa di Var, Ang (page) 466, a shalok of Guru Angad (paraphrase):

  • Haumai is a chronic disease, but its cure is also found within it.
  • If God grants grace, a person acts on the Guru's word, and the disease is healed.

Q1 What does the image of a "chronic disease" suggest about haumai?

Show answer

It is deep-rooted and long-lasting, not a single wrong act.

Q2 Where does the passage say the cure comes from?

Show answer

From God's grace, by which a person lives by the Guru's teaching.

Mission 4

Maya and the five thieves

  • Maya: the attraction of the passing world (wealth, status, possessions) when it is mistaken for what is ultimate.
  • The world is God's real creation; the problem is attachment to it.
  • Haumai works through five thieves (panj chor) that rob a person of virtue:
  • Kaam (lust) · krodh (anger) · lobh (greed) · moh (attachment) · ahankar (pride).
  • Manmukh: a self-willed person, ruled by the mind and its desires.
  • Gurmukh: a Guru-oriented person, whose life is centred on God through the Guru's teaching.

Mission 4

Where are we going? Mukti

  • Mukti: liberation from the cycle of rebirth.
  • It is union with Waheguru: many Sikhs describe the soul merging in God's light, like a drop returning to the ocean.
  • Jivan mukti: many Sikhs stress liberation while alive. A jivan mukt lives fully in the world, free of haumai, in constant remembrance of God.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib describes the soul's progress through spiritual stages (khands), ending in Sach Khand, the realm of truth (Japji Sahib, pauris 34–37).
  • Mukti is not a reward for rituals or pilgrimage.
Mission 4

How do we get there? Grace

  • Nadar (also kirpa or gurprasad): God's grace. The Mool Mantar ends: the One is known "by the Guru's grace".
  • Japji Sahib, pauri 4 (Ang 2) (paraphrase): by actions a person receives this body; by God's grace, the gate of liberation.
  • So human effort matters, but liberation is finally God's gift.
  • Grace and effort work together: the gurmukh's disciplined life opens the way to receive grace.
Mission 4

How do we get there? A God-centred life

  • Nam simran (also nam japna): remembering God's Name, through meditation, repetition of "Waheguru" and singing hymns (kirtan). It turns the mind from "I" to God.
  • Sewa: selfless service to others, e.g. in the langar (free community kitchen). It wears down haumai.
  • Guru Nanak's three duties: nam japna, kirat karni (earning an honest living) and vand chhakna (sharing with others).
  • Sangat (the congregation) supports the path.
  • Many Sikhs take amrit (initiation) and join the Khalsa; others (sahajdhari, keshdhari) follow the Gurus' teaching without initiation. Practice and identity vary.

Mission 5

The soul: a spark of the divine

  • Sikhs believe each person has a soul (atma, or jot: light).
  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light: O my mind, you are the embodiment of divine light; know your origin (Guru Granth Sahib 441, paraphrase).
  • Every person, of any religion, caste or sex, carries the same light.
  • The soul's goal: to return to and unite with its source.
Mission 5

Haumai: the self at the centre

  • Haumai = "I, I": ego, self-centredness.
  • It makes the self feel separate from God and others, and hides the divine light.
  • Scripture calls haumai a chronic disease, but says its cure lies within it too (Guru Granth Sahib 466, paraphrase): the same self that turns inward can turn to God.
  • Haumai is the root of the five thieves.
  • A short definition: haumai is self-centred pride that separates a person from God.
Mission 5

Maya: the passing show

  • Maya = attachment to the world as if it were the final reality.
  • Mainstream Sikh teaching does not call the world unreal or evil: it is God's creation and good.
  • When scripture calls the world "false" or a dream, it means passing, not non-existent.
  • The problem is clinging: to wealth, status, family, pleasure, as though they last.
  • Maya feeds haumai; haumai clings to maya.
  • Sikh image: the world is like a dream or a passing shade; enjoy it, but do not be owned by it.
Mission 5

Manmukh: facing the self

  • Manmukh = "facing one's own mind": self-willed, led by haumai.
  • Marked by the five thieves (panj chor): kaam (lust), krodh (anger), lobh (greed), moh (attachment), ahankar (pride).
  • The manmukh forgets the Name and stays in the cycle of rebirth.
  • Not a fixed class of people: anyone can live as a manmukh, and anyone can turn.
Mission 5

Gurmukh: facing the Guru

  • Gurmukh = "facing the Guru": God-centred, led by the Guru's word.
  • Marked by the five virtues: sat (truth), santokh (contentment), daya (compassion), nimrata (humility), pyaar (love).
  • Lives in the world: family, work, service, remembering the Name.
  • The goal of Sikh life is to move from manmukh to gurmukh, by effort and grace.

Mission 6

A people in covenant

  • Judaism is the religion of the Jewish people, rooted in the Tanakh (Hebrew Bible): Torah, Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).
  • Central idea: the covenant (brit), a binding relationship between God and the Jewish people.
  • God's covenant with Abraham (Genesis 17) and at Sinai through Moses, where Jews believe God gave the Torah.
  • "Chosen people" is understood by most Jews as chosen for responsibility: to live by the Torah, not as superior to others.
  • The Shema states the core belief: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
Mission 6

The condition: made in God's image

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917)
  • B'tselem Elohim ("in the image of God"): every human being has dignity and worth.
  • Humans have free will: they can choose good or evil, and are responsible for their choices.
  • Judaism has no doctrine of original sin: people are not born guilty. The human condition is choice, not a fallen state.
Mission 6

Two inclinations

  • The rabbis teach that every person has two inclinations (yetzer, "impulse"):
  • Yetzer hara: the inclination to evil; selfish desire, appetite, ambition.
  • Yetzer hatov: the inclination to good; conscience, the pull towards God's will.
  • The yetzer hara is not a devil or an outside force. It is part of being human, and it can be directed.
  • A famous midrash (Genesis Rabbah 9:7, paraphrase): without the yetzer hara, no one would build a house, marry, have children or do business.
  • So the task is not to destroy desire but to rule it through Torah.

> "sin coucheth at the door; and unto thee is its desire, but thou mayest rule over it." (Genesis 4:7, JPS 1917)

  • The Hebrew word behind "imagination" in Genesis 8:21 is yetzer.

Mission 6

Where are we going? This world first

  • Judaism puts great weight on this life: living by God's commandments now, in families and communities.
  • Tikkun olam ("repairing the world"): many Jews see acting for justice as sharing in God's work.
  • The Torah says little directly about life after death; later Jewish thought says more.
  • So for many Jews "where are we going?" is first a question about the world's future and the people's future, then about the individual after death.
Mission 6

The Messianic Age

  • Jews look forward to the Messianic Age: a time of peace, justice and knowledge of God for all nations.
  • Many Jews (especially Orthodox) expect a human Messiah (mashiach, "anointed one"), a descendant of King David, to bring it.
  • Many Reform and Liberal Jews hope for a Messianic Age brought about by human effort with God, without a personal Messiah.
  • Jews do not accept Jesus as the Messiah: for them, the age of peace has not yet come.

> "and they shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruninghooks; nation shall not lift up sword against nation, neither shall they learn war any more." (Isaiah 2:4, JPS 1917)

Mission 6Diversity

Olam ha-ba and resurrection

  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come", used both for life after death and for the age to come.
  • Traditional teaching: the soul is judged; Gan Eden (paradise) for the righteous; Gehinnom mainly as a place of temporary purification.
  • Resurrection of the dead (techiyat ha-metim): one of Maimonides' (1138–1204) Thirteen Principles of faith, and blessed daily in the Amidah prayer by Orthodox Jews.
  • Reform Judaism's Pittsburgh Platform (1885) rejected bodily resurrection and literal heaven and hell, keeping belief in the soul's immortality; many Reform Jews today leave these questions open.
  • The Mishnah (Sanhedrin 10:1, paraphrase) says all Israel have a share in the world to come, with a few named exceptions; the rabbis also taught that the righteous of all nations have a share.

Mission 6

How do we get there? Torah and mitzvot

  • Torah: "teaching" or "instruction". Narrowly, the five books; broadly, the Written Torah plus the Oral Torah (Mishnah and Talmud).
  • Mitzvot: commandments. Tradition counts 613 in the Torah (248 positive, 365 negative).
  • Mitzvot cover ritual (Shabbat, kashrut, prayer) and ethics (justice, charity, honesty).
  • Halakhah ("the way to walk"): Jewish law applying the mitzvot to daily life.
  • Teshuvah ("return"): repentance; turning back to God, especially at Yom Kippur.
  • Keeping mitzvot is a response to the covenant, not a way to earn God's love.

Choose life

> "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life, that thou mayest live, thou and thy seed" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917)

> "It hath been told thee, O man, what is good, and what the LORD doth require of thee: only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God." (Micah 6:8, JPS 1917)

Show answer

By choosing the way of God's commandments: justice, mercy, humility.

Q2 Which part of the human condition does Deuteronomy 30:19 assume?

Show answer

Free will: people really can choose.

Mission 6Diversity

Orthodox and Reform on the mitzvot

  • Orthodox Jews: the whole Torah, Written and Oral, was given by God at Sinai; all mitzvot remain binding, interpreted through halakhah.
  • Reform / Liberal Jews: the Torah is inspired but written by humans over time; ethical mitzvot are central, and individuals and communities make informed choices about ritual mitzvot.
  • Conservative / Masorti Jews: halakhah is binding but develops over history.
  • All agree: Judaism is lived out in deeds, not only belief.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Five religions from nine, at least one from each group (Hinduism · Buddhism · Sikhism / Judaism · Christianity · Islam / Taoism · Jainism · Bahá'í).
  • Three questions for each: the human condition (diagnosis), where are we going (goal), how do we get there (path).
  • Study inquiringly, analytically and empathetically: bracket your own verdict (epoché), see from inside (empathy), then compare without ranking.

Mission 2

  • Condition: the atman is bound in samsara by karma, through ignorance (avidya) and attachment; maya is understood differently by Advaita and theistic schools.
  • Goal: moksha, liberation from rebirth: realising atman is Brahman (Advaita) or loving union with a personal God (Vishishtadvaita, Dvaita).
  • Way: jnana (knowledge), karma (selfless action) and bhakti (devotion) yoga, often combined, and in many traditions God's grace.

Mission 3

  • Condition: dukkha, anicca and anatta; suffering continues through craving (tanha), ignorance and kamma, with rebirth but no permanent self.
  • Goal: nibbana, the blowing out of greed, hatred and delusion; arahant ideal (Theravada), bodhisattva ideal and Buddhahood for all beings (Mahayana).
  • Way: the Noble Eightfold Path (wisdom, ethics, meditation) and the Three Refuges: Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha.

Mission 4

  • Condition: haumai (ego) and attachment to maya, working through the five thieves; the manmukh lives centred on self and stays in rebirth.
  • Goal: mukti, union with Waheguru; many Sikhs stress jivan mukti, liberation while alive.
  • Way: grace (nadar) with nam simran, sewa, kirat karni and vand chhakna; the gurmukh lives centred on God.

Mission 5

  • The soul is a spark of Waheguru's light; haumai (ego) and maya (clinging) hide it.
  • Manmukh: self-facing, ruled by the five thieves. Gurmukh: Guru-facing, shaped by the five virtues, living in the world.

Mission 6

  • Condition: humans are made in God's image, with free will and two inclinations (yetzer hara, yetzer hatov); no original sin.
  • Goal: the Messianic Age of peace and olam ha-ba (with resurrection for most Orthodox Jews; Reform views differ); great weight on this world (tikkun olam).
  • Way: the covenant lived through Torah and mitzvot, with teshuvah; Orthodox, Conservative and Reform Jews differ on how binding ritual mitzvot are.
