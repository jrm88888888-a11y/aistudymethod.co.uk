Chemistry — Energetics

This mini-lesson walks you through energetics for KS3 Science: how energy changes when a substance changes state, what happens in exothermic and endothermic reactions, some everyday examples, and the big idea that energy is never made or destroyed.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Energy is always involved

Energy changes are everywhere

Whenever matter changes state or a chemical reaction happens, energy is either taken in from the surroundings or given out to them. We usually notice this as a change in the temperature of the surroundings.

  • Ice melting or water boiling — energy is taken in.
  • Steam condensing or water freezing — energy is given out.
  • Burning fuel, or an iron hand-warmer warming up — energy is given out.

Golden rule: energy is never made or destroyed — it only moves between the chemicals and their surroundings. This is called conservation of energy.

Changes of state

Energy and changes of state

To go from a more ordered state to a more spread-out one (solid → liquid → gas), particles need more energy, so energy is taken in. Going the other way (gas → liquid → solid) gives energy out.

Watch out: in a change of state no new substance is made — it is still water, just in a different form. Energy still moves though!

Exothermic reactions

Exothermic: gives out heat

An exothermic reaction gives out energy to the surroundings, usually as heat. Because energy leaves the reaction and warms everything around it, the temperature rises.

  • Burning fuels (combustion) — e.g. a candle, a Bunsen burner, a bonfire.
  • Neutralisation — an acid reacting with an alkali.
  • Iron hand-warmers — iron powder reacting with oxygen in the air.
  • Respiration in your cells.

Remember: exo = exit — heat exits the reaction, so it feels hot and the thermometer reading goes up.

Endothermic reactions

Endothermic: takes in energy

An endothermic reaction takes in energy from the surroundings. Some take it from the mixture around them, so the temperature falls. Others need energy supplied by heating or by light.

  • Citric acid with sodium hydrogencarbonate (bicarbonate of soda) — the mixture goes cold.
  • Thermal decomposition — a substance breaks down when heated. It takes in energy from the flame.
  • Photosynthesis — plants take in energy from light.

Remember: endo = into — energy goes into the reaction. A heated tube is hot because of the flame, not the reaction.

Starting a reaction

Needs a flame to start?

A candle needs a match to light it. Does that make burning endothermic? No.

Once lit, the candle keeps burning by itself. The energy comes from the wax and oxygen reacting, not from the match. So burning is exothermic.

Misconception buster: how a reaction starts does not decide its type. Ask: does it give out or take in energy overall?

Energy is conserved

Where does the "lost" energy go?

In an exothermic reaction it can look like energy is created — but it isn't. Energy is transferred from the reacting chemicals to the surroundings.

In an endothermic reaction it can look like energy disappears — but it doesn't. It is transferred from the surroundings into the reacting chemicals.

Misconception buster: energy is never made or destroyed. It only moves between the chemicals and their surroundings — this is conservation of energy.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Exothermic: gives out energy → temperature rises (burning, neutralisation, iron hand-warmers, respiration).

Endothermic: takes in energy → temperature falls (citric acid + bicarbonate of soda), or it needs heating or light (thermal decomposition, photosynthesis).

Changes of state: melting/boiling take energy in; freezing/condensing give energy out. They are physical changes, not reactions.

Starting a reaction: needing a flame to start does not make it endothermic. Burning is exothermic.

Conservation: energy is never made or destroyed — it only moves.
