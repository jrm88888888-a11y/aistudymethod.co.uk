Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are most of Britain's mountains in the north and west, and its flattest land in the south and east?

Make a guess: Why are most of Britain's mountains in the north and west, and its flattest land in the south and east?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.1 overview of the location of major upland/lowland areas and river systems
Mission 1

Uplands and lowlands

  • Uplands (high, steep, rugged): mainly in the north and west: the Scottish Highlands, the Lake District, the Pennines, Wales; plus Dartmoor, Bodmin Moor and Exmoor in the south-west.
  • Lowlands (low, gentle): mainly in the south and east: clay vales such as the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury, broken by hills of more resistant chalk and limestone (the Downs; the Cotswolds, "hard limestones rising above mudstones").
  • England's highest mountain, Scafell Pike (978 m), is in the Lake District.
Mission 1

The link to geology

  • Relief (differences in height) "is greatly affected by the underlying geology".
  • Hard rocks resist erosion → uplands: granite (Dartmoor), slate (Lake District, Wales), gneiss (Western Highlands), hard sandstone (Exmoor).
  • Soft rocks wear away → lowlands: clays of the Weald and the Vale of Aylesbury are soft and impermeable, so streams erode them fast.
  • Chalk is not very hard, but rain soaks straight through it, so it forms chains of hills like the Downs.
Mission 1

Major rivers

  • Most UK rivers rise in the uplands (high rainfall) and flow to the sea across the lowlands.
  • The Severn is the UK's longest river; it rises in the Welsh uplands and reaches the sea in the Bristol Channel.
  • The Thames flows east through the lowlands of southern England to London and the North Sea.
  • The Trent drains the Midlands towards the Humber; rivers on the east side of the Pennines flow to the North Sea.
Quick check
?A student marks four places on a UK map. Which one is in an upland area?
Quick check
?Why is Dartmoor upland while the Weald clay is lowland?
Mission check

?In south-east England, a ridge of chalk stands above a clay vale. Chalk is not a very hard rock. Why does the chalk form the higher ground?

Relief follows geology: hard rocks of the north and west stand up as uplands; soft, impermeable clays of the south and east are worn down, while permeable chalk survives as low hills.

Mission 2

Where waves get their energy

  • Waves are made by wind blowing over the sea.
  • Bigger waves come from stronger winds, winds that blow for longer, and a longer fetch (the distance of open water the wind blows over).
  • When a wave breaks, water rushes up the beach (swash) and drains back (backwash).
Mission 2

Constructive and destructive waves

  • Constructive waves: low and gentle. Strong swash, weak backwash, so they add sediment and build beaches.
  • Destructive waves: high and steep, often in storms. Weak swash, strong backwash, so they remove sediment and attack cliffs.
Quick check
?A beach is getting wider and higher over a calm summer. Which waves are most likely to be doing this?
Mission 2

Four ways the sea erodes

  • Hydraulic action: the force of the water, and air forced into cracks, breaks rock apart.
  • Abrasion: waves throw sand and pebbles at the cliff, wearing it away.
  • Attrition: pebbles knock together, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: sea water dissolves some rocks, such as chalk and limestone.
  • AQA calls hydraulic action hydraulic power. Learn abrasion, attrition and hydraulic power.
Mission 2

Transport, longshore drift and deposition

  • Longshore drift: waves arrive at an angle, so the swash pushes sediment up the beach at that angle, and the backwash drags it straight down. Sediment zig-zags along the coast.
  • Deposition: sediment is dropped where waves and currents lose energy, such as in sheltered bays.
Quick check
?A beach faces south. The prevailing waves arrive from the south-west. Which way does longshore drift move the sand?
Mission 2

Weathering: breaking rock where it stands

  • Weathering breaks rock down in place; erosion carries it away.
  • Mechanical: freeze-thaw (water in a crack freezes, expands and widens it) and salt weathering (salt crystals grow in tiny gaps and split the rock).
  • Chemical: rainwater is a weak acid. It slowly dissolves chalk and limestone (carbonation).
Mission 2

Mass movement: cliffs that fall and slump

  • Mass movement: rock and soil move downhill under gravity.
  • Rock fall: blocks break off a steep cliff, often after waves undercut it or freeze-thaw loosens them.
  • Sliding: material slides down a straight slip surface.
  • Slumping: soft, wet rock such as clay slips along a curved surface, rotating as it goes.
Mission 2

Holderness: soft clay against hard chalk

  • Holderness cliffs are glacial till (boulder clay), mostly 18,000–13,000 years old, and about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
  • At high tide, waves erode their lower and sometimes middle sections.
  • Above the waves, the till fails mainly by rotational slumping, with smaller topples and falls. More rainfall could make the till more likely to fail.
  • To the north, Flamborough Head is chalk, which "is more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Aldbrough retreated 2.16 m a year in 1951–2004: about 114 m in total (2.16 × 53, calculated).
Quick check
?Why does Holderness retreat so much faster than Flamborough Head, just to the north?
Mission check

?Two cliffs face the same North Sea storms. Cliff X is resistant chalk. Cliff Y is soft glacial clay, soaked after a wet winter. Which retreats faster, and why?

Aldbrough's cliffs are soft till. Waves attack the foot at high tide, the wet clay slumps from above, and longshore drift carries the debris south. That is why they retreated 2.16 m a year from 1951 to 2004, while chalk at Flamborough Head resists erosion.

Mission 3

Headlands and bays

  • Where bands of hard and soft rock meet the coast at right angles, the sea erodes the soft rock faster.
  • Soft rock is worn back into bays; hard rock is left sticking out as headlands.
  • This is a discordant coast: rock bands run across the coastline.
  • Bays are sheltered, so waves there lose energy and drop sand: bay-head beaches.
Mission 3

When the rocks run along the coast

  • On a concordant coast the rock bands run parallel to the coastline.
  • At Lulworth, Dorset, hard Portland Stone lies on the seaward side, with softer rocks behind it.
  • Lulworth Cove formed "after a break in the Portland and Purbeck rocks" let the sea erode the softer rocks behind: a small, round cove.
Mission 3

Cliffs, notches and platforms

  • Waves attack the foot of a cliff between high and low tide, cutting a wave-cut notch by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The rock above is left overhanging; it collapses and the cliff retreats.
  • A gently sloping wave-cut platform is left, exposed at low tide.
Mission 3

Cave, arch, stack, stump: Old Harry Rocks

  • At Old Harry Rocks, Dorset, the sea exploits "a crack in the chalk joints", slowly creating a cave.
  • Erosion along joints and bedding planes turns the cave into an arch; when the arch collapses, a stack is left.
  • Old Harry's Wife "was still a stack until her eventual collapse in 1896 to render her a stump": a stump is a stack worn down until it is covered at high tide.
  • Exam move: name the process at each stage (hydraulic action, abrasion) and the weakness it exploits (joints, faults, bedding planes).
Quick check
?What is the correct order as a headland is eroded?
Mission 3

Why the sea drops sediment: beaches

  • The sea deposits sediment where it loses energy: in sheltered bays, in shallow water, behind obstacles, and where constructive waves dominate.
  • Deposition also needs a supply of sediment, from eroding cliffs, rivers and the seabed.
  • A beach builds up where more sediment arrives than is removed.
  • Sandy beaches have gentle slopes; shingle beaches (pebbles) are steeper. Slapton Sands, Devon, is shingle: "flint, chert and quartz pebbles".
Mission 3

Spits

  • Where the coast changes direction, sediment carried along the coast keeps going and is dropped in the calmer water beyond: a spit grows out from the land.
  • The end often curves round as waves bend into the calmer water behind it: a hooked (recurved) spit. Saltmarsh forms in the shelter behind.
  • Spurn formed "as a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast carried by currents". It is over three miles long but as little as 50 m wide.
Mission 3

Bars

  • A bar is a ridge of sediment that joins two headlands, cutting off the water behind as a lagoon.
  • Slapton Sands, Devon, is a shingle bar linking Strete Gate with Torcross. It dammed an old estuary and formed a freshwater lagoon, Slapton Ley.
  • Its main formation probably began about 5,000 years ago, as the rising sea pushed shingle landward. Longshore drift still replenishes it.
Quick check
?What is the key difference between a spit and a bar?
Mission 3

Sand dunes

  • Onshore winds blow dry sand from a wide beach; it piles up behind obstacles at the top of the beach.
  • Embryo dunes: the first low dunes, where pioneer plants trap sand.
  • Marram grass binds the sand, so dunes grow higher (yellow or white dunes).
  • Further inland, sand gains organic matter and grassland replaces marram: grey dunes. Low, damp hollows between them are dune slacks.
Quick check
?Why do beaches often form in bays rather than on headlands?
Mission check

?Shingle drifts east along a straight coast. Then the coastline turns sharply north into a river estuary. What will most likely form, and why?

Spurn is a spit. Sediment carried south along the Holderness coast by longshore drift keeps going where the coast turns at the Humber mouth, and is dropped in the calmer water. Its end hooks into the estuary, with saltmarsh behind.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Over 1,000 years, one English coast has lost 26 villages to the sea. Where did all that land go?

Make a guess: Over 1,000 years, one English coast has lost 26 villages to the sea. Where did all that land go?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.2 example of a section of UK coastline, identifying its major erosional and depositional landforms
Mission 4

Holderness: where and why it matters

  • East Yorkshire's North Sea coast, from the chalk of Flamborough Head in the north to Spurn at the mouth of the Humber in the south.
  • The till cliffs retreat 1–4.5 m a year on average.
  • Over the last 1,000 years the coast has retreated about 2 km, losing 26 villages listed in the Domesday survey of 1086.
Mission 4

Two rocks, two speeds

  • Flamborough Head: chalk, "more resistant to erosion than the overlying till".
  • Holderness cliffs: glacial till (boulder clay), left by ice 18,000–13,000 years ago; about 20 m high at Aldbrough.
Mission 4

Erosional landforms 1: Flamborough Head

  • Headland: resistant chalk juts out into the sea.
  • Caves: more than 200 sea caves, mainly around the headland; the largest go back more than 50 m.
  • Waves exploit cracks (joints) in chalk: a crack → cave → arch → stack → stump, as at Old Harry Rocks in Dorset.
  • Storms "could impact the chalk at wave level, creating undercutting and cavities".
Mission 4

Erosional landforms 2: the till cliffs

  • At high tide, waves erode the foot of the cliff.
  • The wet clay above slips along curved surfaces: rotational slumping is the main way these cliffs fail.
  • Waves then carry the slumped debris away, and the cycle repeats.
  • In front of the cliffs, waves wear down a gently sloping shore platform.
Quick check
?Which landform would you expect at Flamborough Head but not on the clay cliffs at Aldbrough?
Mission 4

Where the land goes

  • Waves arrive at an angle, so longshore drift moves eroded sediment south along Holderness.
  • Net drift: about 200,000–350,000 m³ a year.
  • Using records back to 1786, up to 2 million m³ a year has been eroded from the cliffs.
  • Fine clay is carried off in suspension; sand and gravel move along the beach.
Mission 4

Depositional landforms: Spurn

  • Spurn is a spit: "a long, narrow spit from deposits moving along the coast".
  • Over three miles long, but in places only 50 m wide.
  • Made of shingle beach and tidal flat deposits.
  • It grows where the coast turns at the Humber mouth: drift carries sediment on into open water, where it is dropped.
Quick check
?Holderness's cliffs erode quickly, yet Spurn, at the southern end of the same coast, is a long ridge of sand and shingle. Why?
Mission 4

People: defend some places, not all

  • The shoreline management plan holds the line at Bridlington, Hornsea, Withernsea, Mappleton and the Easington gas terminals.
  • Elsewhere, the cliffs are left to erode, so beaches down-drift keep their supply.
  • Mappleton, 1991: two rock groynes and a rock revetment (rock armour laid as a sloping bank along the foot of the cliff), built "to prevent cliff recession", with a design life of 50 years.
Mission check

?On another coast, soft cliffs erode and longshore drift carries the sediment to a spit at the far end. A town halfway along builds groynes that trap the drifting sand. What is most likely to happen?

Holderness's lost land did not vanish: the sea eroded the till, longshore drift carried sand and gravel south, and Spurn grew from it. That is why the plan defends only a few places and lets the rest erode.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

Mission 5

Four policies for each stretch of coast

  • Hold the line: keep the defences where they are.
  • Advance the line: build new defences further out to sea.
  • Managed retreat (managed realignment): let the sea in, behind a new line of defence inland.
  • No active intervention: "Let nature take its course – no work will be carried out to repair defences".
Mission 5

Hard engineering

  • Sea walls: concrete walls that reflect waves. Strong, but very costly, and waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Rock armour (rip rap): big boulders that break up waves. Cheaper than a sea wall, but can look out of place and make the beach harder to reach.
  • Gabions: wire cages filled with rocks; cheaper than walls, but the wire rusts, so they do not last as long.
  • Groynes: barriers across the beach that trap longshore drift and keep the beach wide. Down-drift, the beach can be starved: groynes at one point on the Sussex coast "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
Mission 5

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment (replenishment): add sand or shingle to the beach, which absorbs wave energy. It looks natural, but must be repeated.
  • Reprofiling: reshape the beach. Dune regeneration: plant marram and fence dunes so they trap sand.
  • Managed retreat: give low land to the sea; it becomes saltmarsh, which absorbs wave energy.
Quick check
?Groynes keep one town's beach wide. What is their most likely cost?
Mission 5

Medmerry: the problem

  • Medmerry lies west of Selsey. A shingle bank there protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 hectares of land.
  • The flood risk was 1 in 1 in any given year, and the bank had about 1 year of life left.
  • The B2145 and the utilities that serve Selsey cross the low land behind the bank.
Mission 5

The options and their costs (2007)

  • No active intervention: £8–10 million. Without winter repairs, the bank was likely to breach within a year.
  • Hold the line: £80 million; judged "not practical".
  • Managed realignment: £10 million, for benefits valued at £40 million.
  • Costs are present values: today's value of the spending over the strategy's time span.
Quick check
?Managed realignment at Medmerry was estimated to cost £10 million and to bring benefits worth £40 million. How many pounds of benefit for each £1 spent?
Mission 5

Hold the line or let the sea in?

  • For holding the line: all property and land keep some protection, and losses are delayed.
  • Against: rising flood risk, more future funding, and "increasingly out of step with natural processes".
  • For managed realignment: it works with natural processes, cuts long-term spending, manages flood risk and may create a large area of wildlife habitat.
  • Against: flooding of land and property, possible loss of beach access, and a possible cut in shingle supply to the Witterings, along the coast.
Mission 5

What happened: effects and conflicts

  • Completed in 2014, with 7 km of new embankments inland, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey.
  • The £28 million scheme was then "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe"; it created a new wetland, with the RSPB's support.
  • The government also had a duty to create new intertidal habitat, to replace losses elsewhere.
  • Conflict: realignment meant "the flooding of a relatively large area of land".
  • Evaluate: £28 million spent is not directly comparable with the 2007 present-value estimate of £10 million.
Mission check

?On a low farmland coast, an old sea wall is failing. Rebuilding it would cost far more than the land behind it is worth. A town sits on higher ground nearby, but its only road crosses the low land. Which policy fits best?

At Medmerry, holding a failing bank was costed at £80 million against £10 million to realign (2007 estimates). New banks inland protect Selsey's road and services, and the flooded land became wetland. The price was a "relatively large area of land" given to the sea.

Mission 6

Long profile and cross profile

  • Long profile: the river's height from source to mouth. Steep near the source, gentle near the mouth.
  • Cross profile: a slice across the valley and channel.
  • Upper course: steep, narrow V-shaped valley; small, shallow channel.
  • Lower course: wide, flat valley floor; wide, deep channel.

Mission 6

Erosion

  • A river's load is the rock and soil it carries.
  • Hydraulic action: the force of water, pushing air into cracks in the bed and banks.
  • Abrasion: the load scrapes and grinds the bed and banks.
  • Attrition: stones hit each other, becoming smaller and rounder.
  • Solution: water dissolves soluble rock, such as limestone.
  • Vertical erosion deepens the channel; lateral erosion widens it.
Mission 6

Transportation

  • Traction: large boulders roll along the bed.
  • Saltation: smaller stones and sand bounce along the bed.
  • Suspension: fine silt and clay are carried in the water, making it look muddy.
  • Solution: dissolved minerals are carried, invisible.
  • Big boulders "may only be moved during rapid flows under flood conditions".
Quick check
?A river carries sand grains that bounce along its bed. Which process is this?
Mission 6

Why rivers deposit

A river deposits (drops) its load when it loses energy:

  • velocity falls, e.g. on the inside of a bend or where it enters a lake or the sea;
  • discharge falls after a flood or in a dry spell;
  • the water becomes shallow;
  • it has more load than it can carry.
  • The largest, heaviest particles are dropped first.
Mission 6

Why fine grains are hard to pick up

  • One textbook reading of a velocity graph: a 1 mm sand grain is picked up at about 20 cm/s; a 0.01 mm silt grain needs about 60 cm/s. Versions of the graph differ.
  • Fine grains, especially clay, "stick together": they are cohesive.
  • They also lie in a thin layer of slow water against the bed, which shelters them.
  • Once lifted, fine grains stay in suspension even in slow water, so they are deposited last.
Quick check
?A river slows from 30 cm/s to 5 cm/s as it enters a lake. What is most likely?
Mission 6

Processes change downstream

  • Upper course: steep gradient, large angular load: vertical erosion dominates.
  • Middle course: more discharge: lateral erosion on the outside of bends, deposition on the inside.
  • Lower course: most discharge, fine load: deposition dominates, especially in floods and near the mouth.
Quick check
?Which describes the upper course of a river such as the Tees near Middleton?
Mission check

?In a flood, water runs at 30 cm/s past a sandy bar and a bank of firm clay. The sand is being picked up, but the clay bank is barely eroded. Why?

Fine grains are cohesive and sheltered at the bed, so silt needs about 60 cm/s to be picked up, against about 20 cm/s for 1 mm sand. Once lifted, they are the last to be deposited.

Mission 7

V-shaped valleys and interlocking spurs

  • In the upper course the river cuts down: vertical erosion.
  • Weathering and mass movement wear the valley sides back, giving a V-shaped valley.
  • The river winds around ridges of harder rock. Seen from upstream, the ridges overlap: interlocking spurs.
Mission 7

How a waterfall forms

  • A band of hard (resistant) rock lies over soft rock.
  • The soft rock erodes faster, so the hard rock is left overhanging.
  • Falling water and swirling rocks erode a deep plunge pool by hydraulic action and abrasion.
  • The soft rock is undercut; the unsupported overhang collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall back upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
Mission 7

Niagara Falls

  • Hard dolostones and limestones lie over weaker shales and limestones.
  • About 12,000 years ago the river took its present course; since then the falls have moved about 11 km (7 miles) upstream.
  • Recent retreat: about 0.9–1.5 m (3–5 feet) a year over the last 500+ years; now 0.3 m (1 foot) a year, largely because water is taken for hydroelectric power.
Quick check
?Niagara has retreated about 7 miles in about 12,000 years. 1 mile = 1.609 km. What was its average rate of retreat?
Mission 7

A UK waterfall: High Force

  • High Force, on the River Tees, "plunges over the Whin Sill", a hard rock called dolerite.
  • "Below the Whin Sill you can see layers of sandstone and limestone": softer rocks that are undercut.
  • "Torrential meltwaters carved gorges like those at Low Force and High Force".
Mission 7

Meanders

  • On a bend the fastest water swings to the outside.
  • Outside bank: lateral erosion cuts a steep river cliff.
  • Inside bank: slow water deposits sand and gravel: a gentle slip-off slope, built of a point bar.
  • In cross-section the channel is deep on the outside and shallow on the inside.
Mission 7

Oxbow lakes

  • Erosion on the outside of two bends narrows the neck of land between them.
  • In a flood the river cuts through the neck and takes the shorter route.
  • Deposition seals off the old bend, leaving an oxbow lake.
Quick check
?In a cross-section across a meander, where is the river deepest?
Mission 7

Floodplains and levees

  • Floodplain: the wide, flat valley floor that floods. It is widened as meanders migrate and built up by layers of alluvium (silt) left by floods.
  • When the river floods, water slows sharply as it leaves the channel, so the coarsest sediment is dropped first, beside the channel.
  • Flood after flood, this builds raised banks: natural levees.
  • Finer silt and clay settle further out on the floodplain.
Quick check
?A river floods and spreads across its floodplain. Where is the coarsest sediment deposited?
Mission 7

Estuaries

  • An estuary is the tidal mouth of a river, where fresh water meets sea water.
  • Twice a day the tide comes in and the flow slows; fine silt and mud are deposited.
  • Over time, mudflats build up; plants colonise the higher mud to form salt marsh.
Mission check

?An upland river flows over a band of hard sandstone lying on top of soft shale. Over thousands of years its waterfall moves upstream. Which explanation is correct?

Hard rock over soft rock, undercutting in the plunge pool, and collapse move a waterfall upstream. Niagara has moved about 11 km in about 12,000 years, leaving a gorge.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Why does the River Tees plunge over a cliff in the Pennines but loop lazily near the sea?

Make a guess: Why does the River Tees plunge over a cliff in the Pennines but loop lazily near the sea?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.3 example of a UK river valley identifying its major erosional and depositional landforms
Mission 8

The River Tees: source to sea

  • Rises in the North Pennines and flows south-east to the North Sea at Teesside.
  • Gauges show the fall: 211 m above sea level at Middleton-in-Teesdale, 12 m at Low Moor, about 85 km further on.
  • Upland course: moorland and dales. Lowland course: a broad, low plain around Darlington, Yarm and Stockton.
Mission 8

The rock that makes the waterfalls

  • The Whin Sill is dolerite: molten rock that cooled underground about 295 million years ago.
  • It is hard, "resistant to erosion".
  • It sits above softer layers of sandstone, limestone and shale.
  • Where the Tees crosses it: High Force, Low Force and Cauldron Snout waterfalls.
Mission 8

Erosional landforms: waterfall and gorge

  • Water and the rocks it carries erode the softer rock beneath the dolerite faster (hydraulic action and abrasion).
  • A plunge pool forms; the hard cap is undercut, then collapses.
  • Repeated collapse moves the waterfall upstream, leaving a steep-sided gorge.
  • At Low Force, the Whin Sill "forms the rocky gorge beneath the bridge"; torrential meltwaters at the end of the ice age carved the gorges at Low Force and High Force.
Quick check
?Why does High Force exist where it does?
Mission 8

Lowland landforms: meanders

  • In the Tees Lowlands the "meandering, slow moving River Tees" crosses a broad plain.
  • Outside of each bend: faster, deeper flow erodes a river cliff.
  • The lower Tees winds between "steep, well-wooded banks".
  • Inside of each bend: slower flow deposits a slip-off slope (point bar).
  • Where it cuts through glacial deposits, the middle Tees forms "local bluffs with gorge like characteristics".
Mission 8

Depositional landforms: floodplain and estuary

  • Floodplain: in floods the river spreads out, slows and drops alluvium across the valley floor. The lowlands are covered in "glacial and alluvial drift material".
  • Estuary: where river meets sea, fine sediment settles out as mudflats, as at Seal Sands, with salt marsh and sand dunes nearby.
  • Upstream, Teesdale's grassy mounds are drumlins: clay and boulders shaped by moving ice, not by the river.

Mission check

?Another UK river rises on high, wet moorland made only of soft shale and sandstone, then crosses a wide, flat plain to the sea. Which landform is it least likely to have?

On the Tees, the hard Whin Sill over softer rock makes High Force and its gorge in the uplands; on the low plain, the slow river meanders, floods and drops its load in the estuary.

Make a guess: On 23 December 2013, 43–45 mm of rain fell on two river basins in southern England. Within a day, one river surged to one of its highest flows since records began; the other barely rose, and peaked only in mid-February after weeks more rain. Why did the same storm have such different effects?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.3 physical and human factors affecting flood risk (precipitation, geology, relief, land use)
  • 3.1.3.3 hydrographs showing precipitation and discharge
Mission 9

The storm hydrograph

  • A storm hydrograph shows a river's discharge after a rainfall event. Rainfall is drawn as bars.
  • Rising limb: discharge increases. Peak discharge: the highest flow.
  • Falling limb: discharge drops back.
  • Lag time: the time from peak rainfall to peak discharge.
  • Base flow: the normal, slow flow from groundwater, below the storm water.
Quick check
?What is lag time?
Mission 9

Two rivers, one storm

  • Beult, Kent: basin 277 km². Lambourn, Berkshire: 234 km².
  • Rain on 23 December 2013: Beult 42.6 mm; Lambourn 44.9 mm.
  • Beult: daily mean flow jumped from 32.0 to 79.4 m³/s on 24 December, its highest daily mean since records began in 1958 (the record has some gaps).
  • Lambourn: 1.42 to 2.39 m³/s. After about 317 mm more rain, it peaked at 9.53 m³/s on 15 February 2014.
Quick check
?The Beult's mean flow is 2.126 m³/s. Its peak on 24 December 2013 was 79.4 m³/s. How many times its mean flow was the peak?
Mission 9

Rock type: chalk and clay

  • The Lambourn drains chalk: about 97% of its bedrock is highly permeable. Rain soaks in, is stored as groundwater, and reaches the river slowly.
  • The Beult drains a "predominantly clay catchment": about 74% of its bedrock has low permeability. Little water can soak in, so it runs quickly to the river over and through the soil.
  • So geology controls the shape of the hydrograph: impermeable rock gives a short lag time and a high peak.
Mission 9

Other physical factors

  • Precipitation: intense or long-lasting rain gives more runoff.
  • Antecedent conditions: rain on already wet ground runs off faster. The Beult was already high after rain on 16–22 December.
  • Relief: steep slopes speed water to the river.
  • Basin shape and size: in a small, round basin water arrives together, so the peak is earlier.
  • Snow: a sudden thaw releases stored water quickly; frozen ground acts like an impermeable surface.
Mission 9

Land use

  • Urban areas: roads, roofs and car parks are impermeable; drains carry water straight to rivers: shorter lag time, higher peak.
  • Forest: leaves intercept rain and roots help water soak in: longer lag time, lower peak.
  • Farmland: bare or compacted soil and field drains speed runoff; crops and grass slow it.
Quick check
?Which basin would have the shortest lag time and highest peak after the same storm?
Mission 9

Exam move: describe, then explain

  • Describe using numbers from the graph: peak discharge, lag time, how steep the rising limb is.
  • Explain using named factors: geology, relief, soils, land use, rainfall.
  • Compare: here rainfall and basin size were similar, so geology made the difference.
Mission check

?Two UK basins of the same size get the same 40 mm storm. Basin X is on granite with thin soils. Basin Y is on sandstone that stores water. Which describes their hydrographs?

Geology. The Beult's clay sent the rain straight to the river (79.4 m³/s, about 37 times its mean). The Lambourn's chalk stored it and released it slowly; its February peak was a seasonal groundwater peak, not a storm lag time.

Mission 10

Mission 10: In 1995, more than 250,000 people in the Netherlands had to leave their homes as the rivers rose. The Dutch response was not simply higher dikes. What did they do?

Make a guess: In 1995, more than 250,000 people in the Netherlands had to leave their homes as the rivers rose. The Dutch response was not simply higher dikes. What did they do?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.3 costs and benefits of hard engineering (dams and reservoirs, straightening, embankments, flood relief channels)
  • 3.1.3.3 costs and benefits of soft engineering (flood warnings, preparation, flood plain zoning, planting trees, river restoration)
Mission 10

Two ways to manage floods

  • Hard engineering: building structures to control the river.
  • Soft engineering: working with natural processes, or changing how people use the land and prepare.
  • Hard schemes usually cost more to build and maintain; soft schemes usually cost less but may protect less.
Mission 10

Hard engineering

  • Dams and reservoirs: store floodwater upstream and release it slowly. Cost: very expensive; they flood land and trap sediment.
  • Straightening (channelisation): water leaves faster. Cost: it can send the flood downstream sooner and harms habitats.
  • Embankments or artificial levees (raised banks) and flood walls: hold more water in the channel. Cost: if they fail or are overtopped, flooding is sudden.
  • Deepening (dredging): more water fits in the channel, but sediment soon refills it.
  • Flood relief channels: carry extra water round a town. Cost: expensive to dig, and they protect only the town they bypass.
Mission 10

A UK flood relief channel: the Jubilee River

  • Built by the Environment Agency and opened in 2002: an 11.6 km man-made channel taking Thames floodwater round Maidenhead, Windsor, Eton and Cookham.
  • Benefit: it reduces flood risk to about 3,000 properties.
  • Limit: it "is not designed to reduce flood risk to communities upstream or downstream".
Quick check
?A council straightens a meandering river through its town. What is the most likely cost?
Mission 10

Soft engineering

  • Flood warnings and preparation (flood plans, flood doors, moving valuables upstairs): cheap; they save lives and some property, but the water still comes.
  • Flood plain zoning (land-use zoning): keep houses off the riskiest land; use it for fields, parks or playing fields. Hard where towns are already built.
  • Planting trees (afforestation): trees intercept rain and soils absorb more. Cost: they take years to grow and the land is lost to farming. Most useful for smaller floods: forests "cannot stop catastrophic large-scale floods".
  • Washlands: parts of the floodplain left to flood on purpose, storing water. Cost: that land floods, so it cannot be built on or farmed all year.
  • River restoration: return a straightened river to a natural course, so it floods safe land and slows down. Cost: land beside the river floods more often.
Mission 10

Flood warnings in England

  • Three levels from the Environment Agency:
  • Flood alert: "flooding is possible so you should prepare now".
  • Flood warning: "flooding is expected so you should act now".
  • Severe flood warning: flooding "could cause danger to life".
  • People can sign up to get warnings by phone call, text or email.
Mission 10

A UK soft scheme: Slowing the Flow, Pickering

  • In Pickering, North Yorkshire, the scheme planted 40,000 trees, restored heather moorland and built over 300 leaky dams in streams and drains.
  • It also built an upstream flood storage area (a small reservoir).
  • Over Christmas 2015 it cut the flood flow by 15–20%, about half from the land management and half from the storage area.
  • It "prevented the flooding of a number" of homes and the town's museum.
Mission 10

Zoning and new homes

  • In England, about 6.3 million properties are in areas at risk of flooding; this could reach about 8 million by the middle of the century.
  • Planning rules use a sequential test "to steer new development to areas with the lowest risk of flooding".
  • An exception test allows some building at risk only if, among other things, it is safe for its lifetime and does not increase flood risk elsewhere.
  • This is land-use zoning in practice.
Quick check
?Which measure is soft engineering?
Mission 10

The Dutch answer: Room for the River

  • The floods of 1993 and 1995, when more than 250,000 people had to leave, were "the direct reason" for the Room for the River programme.
  • Aim: "give rivers space again", not only raise dikes.
  • Works at 34 places along the Rhine branches; total cost €2.3 billion.
  • Methods: move dikes back, lower floodplains, dig high-water side channels, return reclaimed land to the river.
Mission check

?A town's river floods every few years. Planners want lower flood levels, no extra flooding downstream and more wildlife habitat. Which plan matches the Dutch approach?

The Dutch gave the rivers room. Dikes were moved back, floodplains lowered and side channels dug at 34 places.

Mission 11

Mission 11: Why did Leeds build river gates that fall flat just when a flood arrives?

Make a guess: Why did Leeds build river gates that fall flat just when a flood arrives?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.1.3.3 a UK flood management scheme: why it was required, the management strategy, and its social, economic and environmental issues
Mission 11

Why Leeds needed a scheme

  • Leeds city centre sits on the River Aire and the Hol Beck.
  • December 2015 (Storm Eva, Boxing Day): "some of the highest river levels ever recorded".
  • Nearly 3,000 homes (including indirect impacts on high-rise flats) and 700 business properties were affected.
  • The riverside is also where the city wants new homes and offices (South Bank regeneration).
Mission 11

Phase 1: what was built (2015–2017)

  • Opened 4 October 2017, led by Leeds City Council with the Environment Agency.
  • Moveable weirs at Crown Point and Knostrop: the first used for flood alleviation in the UK.
  • River and canal merged at Knostrop Cut, removing a man-made island that made a bottleneck.
  • 4.5 km of flood walls and embankments through the city centre.
Mission 11

How a lowered weir lowers a flood

  • A normal weir is a step across the river. It holds back water, keeping the river upstream deep.
  • In a flood the gates are lowered on inflatable bladders; this takes about two hours.
  • With the step gone, water flows away faster and the river level in the city falls.
  • So the flood walls could be kept low, "so as not to spoil views" of the waterfront.
Quick check
?Why does lowering the Knostrop and Crown Point weirs reduce flood risk in Leeds city centre?
Mission 11

Phase 2: upstream and the whole catchment

  • Cost £112.1 million; 14 km of the Aire upstream of the city centre, through Kirkstall to Apperley Bridge.
  • Aims for a 1 in 200 chance of flooding in any year, allowing for climate change, protecting 1,048 homes and 474 businesses.
  • Walls and embankments, a flood storage area (Cragg Wood), and a 2.4 ha wetland at Kirkstall Meadows.
  • Natural flood management across the catchment, right up to the river's source at Malham: e.g. 5,000 trees planted at the Brownlee Triathlon Centre.
Mission 11

Social issues

  • For: more than 3,000 homes and 500 businesses better protected by phase 1.
  • For: a new bridge on the Trans Pennine Trail and riverside views kept, as the walls are low.
  • Against: months of disruption: temporary traffic controls during building.
  • Against: phase 1 protected the city centre and Woodlesford, not upstream areas such as Kirkstall, badly hit in 2015; they had to wait for phase 2.
Mission 11

Economic issues

  • Phase 1 cost £50 million: £35 million from government, £10 million from Leeds City Council, the rest from partners.
  • It was expected to safeguard 22,000 jobs over 10 years, and created 150 jobs and apprenticeships.
  • It protects 300 acres of land for development.
  • Reusing 180,000 tonnes of dug-out material saved about £6 million.
  • In 2018 the council still had to "secure the remaining funding" for full phase 2 protection.
Mission 11

Environmental issues

  • The old Knostrop weir was about 3 m high: a barrier to fish and eels.
  • New fish and eel passes at both weirs; salmon had recently been seen in the Aire "for the first time in 200 years".
  • Otter ramps and holts; 700 trees along the Trans Pennine Trail.
  • Phase 2: wetland at Kirkstall Meadows with "kingfisher banks, otter holts"; new woodland to "create new habitats and store carbon".
Quick check
?Which is an environmental issue of the Leeds scheme?
Quick check
?Phase 1 cost £50 million, of which £35 million came from government grants. What percentage came from government?
Mission check

?A town centre floods because an old stone weir holds the river high. Planners can (1) raise the river walls by 2 m, or (2) replace the weir with gates that lower in floods, plus fish passes and lower walls. Which judgement best uses the Leeds evidence?

The gates fall flat in a flood so the river can drain away and the city-centre level drops. That let Leeds keep walls low and add fish passes, but £50 million protected only the centre; upstream areas needed a £112 million second phase.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Uplands north and west on hard rocks; lowlands south and east on soft rocks; chalk forms low hills because it is permeable.
  • Major rivers rise in the uplands; the Severn is the UK's longest.

Mission 2

  • Destructive waves erode and constructive waves build. Erosion and slumping wear cliffs back; longshore drift moves the sediment along the coast.
  • Rock type sets the pace: weak till retreats fast; chalk resists.

Mission 3

  • Hard rock makes headlands; soft rock makes bays. Headlands are eroded along joints: cave, arch, stack, stump.
  • Where waves lose energy, sediment builds beaches; where the coast turns, it builds spits.
  • A spit that crosses a bay becomes a bar.

Mission 4

  • Holderness: soft till cliffs retreat 1–4.5 m a year; resistant chalk forms Flamborough Head, with over 200 caves; Spurn spit is built from the eroded sediment.

Mission 5

  • Hard engineering holds the line but is costly and can harm the coast down-drift; soft engineering works with natural processes.
  • Medmerry chose managed retreat: cheaper and lower-risk than holding a failing bank, with habitat gained and land lost.

Mission 6

  • Downstream: discharge, width and depth rise; gradient and load size fall (Tees: 211 m to 12 m high; 9.1 to 21.0 m³/s).
  • Four erosion and four transport processes; rivers deposit when they lose energy; fine cohesive grains resist erosion.

Mission 7

  • Erosion landforms: V-shaped valleys, interlocking spurs, waterfalls, gorges; erosion and deposition: meanders and oxbow lakes; deposition: floodplains and levees.
  • Estuaries form where tides slow the river and mud is deposited.

Mission 8

  • Upland Tees: hard dolerite (Whin Sill) over softer rocks gives waterfalls (High Force) and gorges; lowland Tees: meanders, floodplain, estuary mudflats (Seal Sands).

Mission 9

  • Short lag time and high peak: impermeable rock, steep slopes, wet soil, round basins, urban land.

Mission 10

  • Hard engineering controls the river with structures; soft engineering works with nature and changes land use, warnings and preparation.

Mission 11

  • Why: Boxing Day 2015, nearly 3,000 homes and 700 businesses affected along the River Aire.
  • Strategy: phase 1 (2017) moveable weirs, river–canal merge, 4.5 km of walls; phase 2 upstream walls, flood storage, wetland and catchment-wide tree planting.
  • Issues: social (protection, views, disruption, who waits); economic (£50m, 22,000 jobs safeguarded, funding gaps); environmental (fish passes, salmon, otters, trees).
