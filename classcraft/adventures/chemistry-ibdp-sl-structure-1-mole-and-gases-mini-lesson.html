Mission 1

The mole is a count

  • Amount of substance, n, is measured in moles (mol).
  • One mole contains 6.02 × 10²³ entities: the Avogadro constant, NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • Number of entities: N = n × NA.
  • Always name the entity: atom, molecule, ion, electron or formula unit.
  • 1 mol CO₂ = 6.02 × 10²³ molecules, but 3 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.81 × 10²⁴ atoms.
  • 1 mol Al₂(SO₄)₃ contains 5 mol of ions: 2 Al³⁺ and 3 SO₄²⁻.

Mission 1

Relative masses on the carbon-12 scale

  • Atoms are compared with one twelfth of the mass of a ¹²C atom, so ¹²C = 12 exactly.
  • Relative atomic mass, Ar: use the two-decimal values in the data booklet.
  • Relative formula mass, Mr: add the Ar of every atom in the formula.
  • Ar and Mr are ratios, so they have no units.
  • A bracket multiplies all inside it: Mr(Mg(OH)₂) = 24.31 + 2(16.00 + 1.01) = 58.33.
  • Water of crystallization counts: Mr(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = 159.62 + 5 × 18.02 = 249.72.
Quick check

Quick check
?A student writes: 'Mr of carbon dioxide, CO₂ = 44.01 g mol⁻¹'. Which comment on this statement is correct?
Mission 1

Molar mass and n = m/M

  • Molar mass, M: the mass of one mole, in g mol⁻¹. Same number as Mr.
  • n = m/M (data booklet). Units check: g ÷ g mol⁻¹ = mol.
  • Use the formula of the real entity: nitrogen gas is N₂, M = 28.02 g mol⁻¹.
  • Worked example: 11.0 g of CO₂. n = 11.0 ÷ 44.01 = 0.250 mol.
  • Molecules = 0.250 × 6.02 × 10²³ = 1.50 × 10²³.
Quick check
?The relative molecular mass of methane, CH₄, is 16.05. Which statement about the molar mass, M, of methane is correct?

Mission 2

Empirical and molecular formulae

  • Empirical formula: the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element.
  • Molecular formula: how many atoms of each element one molecule really contains.
  • Molecular formula = (empirical formula)ₓ, where x is a whole number.
  • H₂O₂ → HO. C₂H₄ → CH₂. H₂O is already its own empirical formula.
  • Neither formula shows which atoms are bonded to which.
Mission 2

Percentage by mass

  • % by mass of an element = (number of its atoms × Ar) ÷ Mr × 100.
  • Worked example: ammonium nitrate, NH₄NO₃. Mr = 2(14.01) + 4(1.01) + 3(16.00) = 80.06.
  • % N = 2 × 14.01 ÷ 80.06 × 100 = 28.02 ÷ 80.06 × 100 = 35.00%.
  • Weight every atom by its Ar: a share of atoms is not a share of mass.
Quick check
?What is the percentage by mass of nitrogen in ammonium sulfate, (NH₄)₂SO₄? (Ar: H 1.01, N 14.01, O 16.00, S 32.07)
Mission 2

From percentages to empirical formula

  • Treat percentages as grams in a 100 g sample. Divide each by its Ar.
  • Divide every result by the smallest.
  • Scale to whole numbers: ×2 for .50, ×3 for .33 or .67, ×4 for .25 or .75. Never round 1.50.
  • Worked example: 43.64% P, 56.36% O. P: 43.64 ÷ 30.97 = 1.409; O: 56.36 ÷ 16.00 = 3.523.
  • Ratio 1 : 2.50, × 2 = 2 : 5. Empirical formula P₂O₅.
Quick check
?A compound of aluminium and sulfur contains 35.93% aluminium and 64.07% sulfur by mass. What is its empirical formula? (Ar: Al 26.98, S 32.07)
Mission 2

From empirical to molecular formula

  • You also need the molar mass, M, of the compound.
  • x = M ÷ mass of one empirical formula unit.
  • Worked example: P₂O₅ has empirical formula mass 2(30.97) + 5(16.00) = 141.94.
  • The compound's M is 283.88 g mol⁻¹, so x = 283.88 ÷ 141.94 = 2.
  • Molecular formula = (P₂O₅)₂ = P₄O₁₀.
Calculate
1A compound contains 30.45% nitrogen and 69.55% oxygen by mass. Its molar mass is 92.02 g mol⁻¹. How many atoms are there in one molecule? (Ar: N 14.01, O 16.00)
Mission check

?The empirical formula of ethanoic acid is CH₂O. What does this empirical formula tell you about ethanoic acid?

CH₂O is only the ratio of atoms. Methanal is CH₂O, ethanoic acid C₂H₄O₂ and glucose C₆H₁₂O₆: x = 1, 2 and 6. Their molar masses tell them apart.

Mission 3

Molar concentration

  • Molar concentration, C = n ÷ V: amount of solute per dm³ of solution, in mol dm⁻³.
  • Square brackets mean concentration in mol dm⁻³: [NaCl] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³.
  • [NaCl] is never an amount in moles.
  • V is the volume of the whole solution, not of the water added.
  • A standard solution is made up to the mark in a volumetric flask.
Mission 3

n = CV and volume units

  • n = CV (data booklet), with V in dm³.
  • 1 dm³ = 1000 cm³, so divide cm³ by 1000.
  • Worked example: 40.0 cm³ of KOH(aq), [KOH] = 0.150 mol dm⁻³.
  • n = 0.150 × 0.0400 = 0.00600 mol.
  • Rearranged: 0.0125 mol in 250 cm³ gives C = 0.0125 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0500 mol dm⁻³.
Quick check
?What amount of sodium hydroxide is present in 25.0 cm³ of NaOH(aq) in which [NaOH] = 0.100 mol dm⁻³?
Mission 3

g dm⁻³ and mol dm⁻³

  • Concentration can also be a mass per volume: g dm⁻³.
  • g dm⁻³ ÷ M = mol dm⁻³. Units check: g dm⁻³ ÷ g mol⁻¹ = mol dm⁻³.
  • mol dm⁻³ × M = g dm⁻³.
  • Worked example: [NaOH] = 0.250 mol dm⁻³, M = 40.00 g mol⁻¹: 0.250 × 40.00 = 10.0 g dm⁻³.
Calculate
1A solution contains 4.90 g of sulfuric acid, H₂SO₄, in 500 cm³ of solution. Calculate [H₂SO₄] in mol dm⁻³. (Ar: H 1.01, O 16.00, S 32.07)
Quick check

Mission 4

The ideal gas model

  • Particles are in constant, random motion.
  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the gas.
  • There are no intermolecular forces, attractive or repulsive.
  • All collisions are elastic: total kinetic energy is unchanged.
  • Pressure comes from particles colliding with the walls, equally in all directions.
Quick check
?Which statement is one of the key assumptions of the ideal gas model?
Mission 4

Why real gases deviate

  • Real particles have a finite volume and do attract each other.
  • High pressure: particles are close, so attractions act more and their own volume is no longer negligible.
  • Low temperature: slow particles are affected more by the attractions.
  • Stronger intermolecular forces mean larger deviation: NH₃ (hydrogen bonding) more than He.
  • Near room temperature and atmospheric pressure, most gases behave almost ideally.
Quick check
?A sample of nitrogen is held at a pressure of 2.0 × 10⁶ Pa, first at 150 K and then at 600 K. It is a gas at both temperatures. At which temperature does it deviate more from ideal gas behaviour, and why?
Mission 4

Seeing deviation on a graph

  • For an ideal gas, PV/nRT = 1 at every pressure.
  • Attractions soften and reduce wall collisions, so P is lower than ideal: PV/nRT below 1.
  • At very high pressure the particles' own volume cuts the free space, so PV/nRT rises above 1.
  • You explain these in words; no calculation is needed.
Quick check
?Under which conditions does a real gas behave most like an ideal gas?
Mission check

?Four gases are each at 300 K and 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa. Which gas deviates most from ideal gas behaviour?

Mission 5

Avogadro's law

  • Avogadro's law: at the same T and p, equal gas volumes hold equal numbers of molecules.
  • It works because particle volume is negligible: size doesn't change how many fit.
  • It counts molecules, not atoms.
  • Equal volumes hold equal amounts, so their masses are in the ratio of their molar masses.
Quick check
?Two identical sealed flasks are at the same temperature and pressure. One is filled with methane, CH₄, and the other with chlorine, Cl₂. The methane flask contains 5.00 × 10²² atoms in total. How many atoms does the chlorine flask contain? (Ar: H 1.01, C 12.01, Cl 35.45)
Mission 5

Reacting gas volumes

  • At the same T and p, gas volume ∝ amount in moles.
  • So gases react in the volume ratio of their coefficients.
  • Worked example: 2CO(g) + O₂(g) → 2CO₂(g).
  • 40 cm³ of CO reacts with 20 cm³ of O₂ to make 40 cm³ of CO₂.
  • Only gases count: a liquid or solid product has almost no volume.
Quick check
?Sulfur dioxide reacts with oxygen: 2SO₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2SO₃(g). What volume of oxygen reacts completely with 30.0 cm³ of sulfur dioxide? All volumes are measured at the same temperature and pressure.
Mission 5

Molar volume

  • Molar volume, Vm: the volume of one mole of gas.
  • It is the same for every ideal gas at a given T and p.
  • At STP (273.15 K, 100 kPa): Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • V = n × Vm, so n = V ÷ Vm.
  • Worked example: 7.00 g of N₂. n = 7.00 ÷ 28.02 = 0.250 mol; V = 0.250 × 22.7 = 5.68 dm³.
  • Only for gases, and only at STP. Other conditions: use PV = nRT (Mission 6).
Calculate
1What is the mass, in g, of 3.40 dm³ of carbon dioxide at STP? (Ar: C 12.01, O 16.00; Vm = 22.7 dm³ mol⁻¹ at STP)

Mission 6

Pressure and volume

  • Fixed amount of gas, constant temperature: p ∝ 1/V.
  • Halve the volume and the pressure doubles: particles hit the walls twice as often.
  • p against V: a falling curve.
  • p against 1/V: a straight line through the origin.
Calculate
1A fixed amount of an ideal gas at constant temperature occupies 2.40 × 10⁻⁴ m³ at 1.50 × 10⁵ Pa. It expands to 6.00 × 10⁻⁴ m³. Calculate its new pressure in Pa.
Mission 6

Temperature, in kelvin

  • Constant pressure: V ∝ T. Constant volume: p ∝ T. T must be in kelvin.
  • Hotter particles move faster and hit the walls harder and more often. They do not expand.
  • Plotted against T in K: straight lines through the origin.
  • Plotted against °C: straight lines that extend back to zero at −273 °C (0 K).
  • Investigate p–V: gas syringe with a pressure sensor, at constant T.
  • Investigate V–T: syringe in a water bath. p–T: sealed flask with a pressure sensor.
Quick check
?A fixed mass of an ideal gas is kept at constant pressure. Its volume is measured at several temperatures and plotted against temperature in °C. Which description of the graph is correct?
Quick check
?A fixed amount of an ideal gas is kept at constant volume. Its pressure is plotted against its temperature in kelvin. Which describes the graph?
Mission 6

The ideal gas equation

  • PV = nRT (data booklet). R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (data booklet).
  • SI units only: P in Pa, V in m³, T in K.
  • 1 kPa = 10³ Pa. 1 dm³ = 10⁻³ m³. 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³.
  • Convert first, then substitute.
Mission 6

Worked example: finding a molar mass

  • 0.880 g of a gas occupies 4.72 × 10⁻⁴ m³ at 1.05 × 10⁵ Pa and 25 °C (298 K).
  • n = PV ÷ RT = (1.05 × 10⁵ × 4.72 × 10⁻⁴) ÷ (8.31 × 298) = 49.6 ÷ 2476 = 0.0200 mol.
  • M = m ÷ n = 0.880 ÷ 0.0200 = 44.0 g mol⁻¹.
  • M ≈ 44 g mol⁻¹ is consistent with CO₂ (44.01 g mol⁻¹).
Quick check

Quick check
?A gas syringe contains 2.20 cm³ of an ideal gas at 20 °C and 1.20 × 10⁵ Pa. What amount of gas does it contain? R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Calculate
1Calculate the pressure, in kPa, of 0.0800 mol of an ideal gas in a 2.00 dm³ container at 35 °C. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
Mission 6

The combined gas law

  • For a fixed amount of gas: P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ (data booklet). T in kelvin.
  • Leave out any quantity that stays constant.
  • Worked example: 0.0150 m³ of gas at 1.01 × 10⁵ Pa and 290 K moves to 5.05 × 10⁴ Pa and 250 K.
  • V₂ = V₁ × (P₁/P₂) × (T₂/T₁) = 0.0150 × 2 × (250/290) = 0.0259 m³.
  • Check direction: lower pressure → bigger; cooler → smaller.
Calculate
1A fixed amount of an ideal gas occupies 3.60 × 10⁻⁴ m³ at 1.50 × 10⁵ Pa and 17 °C. What volume, in cm³, does it occupy at 1.00 × 10⁵ Pa and 100 °C?
Mission check

?The air in a car tyre has a pressure of 3.20 × 10⁵ Pa at 15 °C. After a long drive the air inside is at 45 °C. Assuming the volume of the tyre stays constant, what is the new pressure?
Recap

The big ideas

  • A mole is 6.02 × 10²³ of a named entity: N = n × NA.
  • Ar and Mr have no units; molar mass is in g mol⁻¹; n = m/M.
  • Empirical formula: simplest ratio. Molecular formula = (empirical)ₓ, with x = M ÷ empirical formula mass.
  • Concentration [X] is in mol dm⁻³; n = CV with V in dm³; divide g dm⁻³ by M to get mol dm⁻³.
  • Ideal gas: negligible particle volume, no forces, elastic collisions. Real gases deviate when cold or compressed.
  • Equal gas volumes (same T, p) hold equal numbers of molecules. PV = nRT in Pa, m³ and K.
