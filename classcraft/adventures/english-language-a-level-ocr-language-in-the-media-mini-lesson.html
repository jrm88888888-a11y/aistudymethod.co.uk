Language in the media · representation and agencyScreen 1

Representation and framing

  • News doesn't just report events: it represents them, choosing what to include, what to call it and what comes first.
  • Lexical choice: protesters or rioters; arrive or flood in. Water metaphors (flood, wave) present people as an uncontrolled mass and a danger.
  • Framing: Robert Entman (1993) defines it as selecting some aspects of a reality and making them more salient, to promote a particular definition of the problem, cause, moral judgement or remedy.
Language in the media · representation and agencyScreen 2

Transitivity: who does what to whom

Transitivity analysis (from Halliday) asks who is the Actor (the doer), what is the Process (the verb) and who is the Goal (the one the process is done to).

Language in the media · representation and agencyScreen 6

Bias, stance and the multimodal page

  • Voices: who is quoted (officials or witnesses) and the reporting verbs (said, claimed, admitted).
  • Evaluative lexis and modifiers (peaceful, ugly) and modality signal stance in a text that claims objectivity.
  • Synthetic personalisation (Fairclough, 1989): a mass audience is addressed as individuals, often with you.
  • Graphology: headline size, image, caption, layout; online, hyperlinks and comment threads.
  • OCR asks you to apply theories of language and power, gender or technology.

Mission checkScreen 9

?'Flood in' or 'arrive': same people, same event. What did the verb do?

The verb framed the story. 'Flood in' is a water metaphor that turns people into an uncontrolled mass and a danger; 'arrive' is neutral and keeps them as people. Like the choice between active and passive, it shapes whom readers see, and how, without changing the facts.

Language diversity · language and genderScreen 2

Lakoff and her critics

  • Language and Woman's Place (1973 article, 1975 book) listed features of 'women's language': hedges ('sort of'), tag questions ('isn't it?'), empty adjectives ('divine'), super-polite forms, rising intonation on statements.
  • Her method was mainly introspection: her own and acquaintances' speech, and TV.
  • Holmes (1984): women used more facilitative tags (inviting others in); men more modal tags (checking facts).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): in US trials these features appeared in low-status witnesses of both sexes: powerless language.
Language diversity · language and genderScreen 3

Testing the interruption claim

Zimmerman and West (1975)Recorded 31 conversations around a US university campus; in the 11 between a man and a woman, men made almost all of the 48 interruptions (46)
Their conclusionMen deny women equal status as speakers: dominance
ProblemsSmall campus sample; and does every cut-in count? A supportive overlap that agrees is not a grab for the floor
Later evidenceJames and Clarke's (1993) review found little consistent difference between the sexes; power and context matter more
  • To test the claim you must define an interruption, choose comparable contexts and code every cut-in the same way.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Do men interrupt more? How would you even test it?

The answer depends on what you count. Zimmerman and West found men made almost all the interruptions in their 1975 sample, but later studies found little consistent difference once supportive overlaps were separated from floor grabs and contexts were compared. A fair test defines an interruption, compares like settings and looks at power as well as gender.

What kind of power?

ConceptWhoMeaningInvented example

Language diversity · language and powerScreen 2

Face, politeness and indirectness

  • Face (Goffman, 1967): the public self-image we want to protect. Brown and Levinson (1978; 1987): positive face = the wish to be liked; negative face = the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Orders, requests and criticism are face-threatening acts (FTAs). Choice of strategy depends on power, social distance and size of imposition.
StrategyInvented example
Bald on record'Sit down.'
Positive politeness (stress closeness)'Shall we all grab a seat, guys?'
Negative politeness (avoid imposing)'Could you possibly sit down?'
Off record (hint)'Plenty of chairs free.'
  • Grice (1975): hearers assume we cooperate, so a hint creates an implicature they must work out.
Language diversity · language and powerScreen 3

Powerful and powerless speech

  • In unequal encounters the powerful speaker opens and closes the talk, sets topics, asks the questions, can phrase orders as questions (indirect directives), may interrupt, and chooses terms of address (first name downwards, title upwards).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): low-status witnesses in US trials, of both sexes, used more hedges and intensifiers: powerless language.
  • Synthetic personalisation (Fairclough, 1989): mass texts address everyone as one friendly 'you'.
  • In media texts, ask how power is represented: who is named, quoted and given agency.

Mission checkScreen 9

?How does a headteacher's 'Shall we sit down?' differ from a friend's?

From a friend, 'Shall we sit down?' is a real suggestion between equals that can be turned down. From a headteacher it is a directive: the question form and inclusive 'we' are politeness strategies that soften the face threat, but the head's instrumental power means the student can't really say no.

Language discourses · attitudes to languageScreen 1

What is a language discourse?

  • A discourse is a recurring way of talking about a topic, with its own words, images and assumptions.
  • Common discourses about language: decline (English is getting worse), golden age (it was better once), purity (outside influences spoil it), change as natural (the linguist's view).
  • OCR asks you to critically evaluate attitudes towards language and its users.

Language discourses · attitudes to languageScreen 6

Writing the linguist's reply

  • Write for non-specialists: explain any term in a phrase (descriptivists, linguists who study how people really speak).
  • Answer the column's claims with evidence: complaints about decline go back centuries (Swift proposed an academy to fix English in 1712), and meanings have always shifted (nice once meant "foolish").
  • Explain what the features do: abbreviations save time; like can introduce reported speech or soften a claim.
  • Keep the genre: headline, a hooking opening, a confident but fair tone.

Reply to the column's claim that teenagers are ruining English. Write for readers with no linguistics training, and use at least one piece of evidence about language change.

Model answer

Every generation thinks the young are wrecking English. In 1712 Jonathan Swift wanted an academy to stop English changing, and it carried on changing anyway. Linguists, who study how people actually speak, see words like innit not as grunts but as tools: they help a speaker sound friendly and show which group they belong to. Language isn't crumbling. It's doing what it has always done: adapting to the people who use it.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?A columnist says teens are ruining English. What would a linguist say?

A linguist would reply descriptively: show that complaints about decline are centuries old, that change is normal, and what features like innit actually do for speakers, all in clear language for non-specialist readers.

Recap

Big ideas

  • News represents events through choices of lexis, metaphor and order; framing (Entman, 1993) makes some aspects salient.
  • Transitivity: active voice shows the actor; agentless passives and nominalisation hide it (Fairclough, 1989: such choices are ideological).
  • Stance shows in voices, reporting verbs, evaluative lexis, modality and graphology.
  • Component 02 Section B: analyse a multimodal media text with theories of power, gender or technology.
  • Four approaches: deficit (Lakoff), dominance (Zimmerman and West, Spender), difference (Tannen, Coates), diversity (Cameron, Butler). Each has evidence and critics.
  • Lakoff's features were challenged by Holmes (functions of tags) and O'Barr and Atkins (powerless language, linked to status not sex).
  • Claims about interruption depend on definitions and context; later reviews found little consistent sex difference.
  • In media texts, ask how gender is represented and reproduced by the text's choices.
  • Power can be instrumental (from a role) or influential (from persuasion) (Wareing); Fairclough separates power in discourse from power behind it.
  • Brown and Levinson's face and politeness strategies, and Grice's implicature, explain how indirect forms can carry commands.
  • In unequal encounters the powerful control topics, turns and address; O'Barr and Atkins linked 'powerless' features to status.
  • In media texts, ask how power is represented and how texts position readers (synthetic personalisation).
  • A discourse is a recurring way of talking about language; decline, golden age and purity discourses are common in the media.
  • Evaluate the attitudes in a text, then shape your own writing to the purpose, form and audience you are given.
  • A linguist's reply is descriptive, evidence-based and written for non-specialists.
