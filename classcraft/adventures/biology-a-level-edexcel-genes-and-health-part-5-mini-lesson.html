Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Make a guess: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Mission 1

Copying is almost perfect

  • Every cell division copies billions of bases.
  • DNA polymerase sometimes pairs the wrong base. Proofreading and repair fix almost all errors.
  • The few that escape become permanent: spontaneous mutations.
  • Errors in DNA repair add more.
  • So mutations happen even with no harmful agent present. Each person carries dozens of new mutations neither parent had.
Mission 1

Random, not directed

  • A mutation can happen anywhere in the genome.
  • No natural mechanism makes a chosen change to a chosen base to produce a useful trait.
  • A mutagen raises the rate. It does not pick the gene.
  • The environment selects among variants that already exist. It does not create them.
  • Say: "a random mutation that happened to give resistance" — not: "the bacteria mutated to become resistant".
Mission 1

Read the data: chance or need?

  • 1943: Luria and Delbrück asked whether bacteria become resistant to a virus because they meet it.
  • Set-up: grow 20 separate small cultures of E. coli from a few cells each; also take 10 samples from one big culture. Spread each on plates with virus. Count resistant colonies.
  • Real data (Luria and Delbrück, 1943), resistant colonies per plate:

  • Separate cultures vary wildly. A mutation early in growth leaves many resistant descendants; a late one, few; often none.

Mission 1

The only source of new alleles

  • A new allele can arise only by mutation.
  • Meiosis and fertilisation reshuffle existing alleles into new combinations. They make no new alleles.
  • Most mutations are harmful or neutral to the individual. A few are beneficial.
  • Evolution is a change in allele frequency: mutation makes the new allele; natural selection changes its frequency.
Quick check
?Why is gene mutation described as the original source of all genetic variation?
Mission check

?When a new insecticide was first used on a crop, it killed nearly all individuals of a hypothetical pest insect. After several years of use, most of the pest population was resistant. Which statement best explains how resistance became common?

Mutation supplies variation by chance; the environment only chooses among what is already there.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can one faulty protein in cell membranes affect breathing, digestion and fertility?

Make a guess: How can one faulty protein in cell membranes affect breathing, digestion and fertility?

Mission 2

One gene, one channel

  • Gene: CFTR. Protein: the CFTR protein, a channel protein in the cell surface membrane of epithelial cells.
  • It lets chloride ions (Cl⁻) cross the membrane. Opening it needs ATP.
  • Found in cells lining the airways, pancreatic ducts, intestine, sperm ducts and sweat glands.
  • Cystic fibrosis (CF) is recessive: a person needs two faulty alleles.
  • One working allele makes enough CFTR. Carriers have no symptoms.
Mission 2

Many mutations, one disease

  • Over 2000 different variants of the CFTR gene have been recorded. Many of them cause CF.
  • Commonest: F508del (ΔF508). Three bases deleted → amino acid 508, phenylalanine, is missing.
  • The frame is kept, but the protein misfolds. The cell breaks it down; little reaches the membrane.
  • Other mutations: no protein made at all (e.g. an early stop codon), or a protein that reaches the membrane but rarely opens.
  • Different mutations, same result: too little chloride moves.
Quick check
?F508del removes three bases, one complete codon, from the CFTR gene. Which statement best explains why the protein fails?
Mission 2

From faulty channel to sticky mucus

  • (1) Healthy airway: CFTR moves Cl⁻ out of the cells into the mucus. Na⁺ follows. The solute concentration of the mucus rises.
  • Water moves out of the cells into the mucus by osmosis. Mucus stays thin; cilia sweep it away.
  • (2) CF airway: Cl⁻ is not secreted. More Na⁺ is absorbed into the cells from the mucus.
  • (3) Now the cells have the higher solute concentration. Water moves out of the mucus by osmosis.
  • Mucus becomes thick and sticky. Cilia cannot move it.
  • Say: "water moves by osmosis towards the higher solute concentration" — not: "the cells make more mucus".
Mission 2

Gas exchange

  • Sticky mucus blocks bronchioles. Less air reaches many alveoli.
  • Fewer ventilated alveoli: smaller surface area for gas exchange; smaller concentration gradient.
  • Trapped bacteria cause repeated infections. Inflammation scars and thickens the alveolar walls: longer diffusion distance.
  • Fick's law: rate ∝ (surface area × concentration difference) ÷ thickness. All three get worse.
  • Result: breathlessness, coughing, and lower oxygen uptake, especially during exercise.
Quick check
?Long-term infection in CF scars and thickens the walls of some alveoli. What happens to the rate of diffusion of oxygen across those walls?
Mission 2

Digestion

  • Sticky mucus blocks the pancreatic duct.
  • Pancreatic enzymes (amylase, lipase, protease) do not reach the small intestine.
  • Food is poorly digested, so fewer products are absorbed. Many people with CF struggle to gain weight.
  • Mucus lining the small intestine is also thicker, slowing absorption.
  • Enzymes trapped in the pancreas damage it: fibrous cysts form (hence the name). Damage to insulin-secreting cells can lead to diabetes.
  • Many people with CF take enzyme capsules with meals.
Mission 2

Reproduction

  • Males: the sperm ducts (vas deferens) are blocked or absent. Sperm are made but cannot reach the semen. Most men with CF are infertile.
  • Females: thick cervical mucus makes it harder for sperm to reach the egg. Fertility is reduced, not lost.
  • Same cause as the lungs and pancreas: too little Cl⁻ and water in the mucus.
Quick check
?Many people with CF are underweight even though they eat a high-energy diet. Which explanation is best?
Mission 2

Read the data: the sweat test

  • In sweat ducts, CFTR works the other way: it reabsorbs Cl⁻ from sweat, so sweat ends up only slightly salty.
  • In CF, Cl⁻ stays in the sweat: very salty sweat. This is the standard diagnostic test.
  • Cut-offs used in diagnosis (chloride in sweat, mmol dm⁻³): 60 or more: CF likely · 30–59: borderline; more tests · below 30: CF unlikely.

Mission check

?In some people with CF, the CFTR protein reaches the cell surface membrane but its channel rarely opens. A drug that holds these channels open for longer has been developed. For a person with this type of mutation, which prediction is best supported?

One channel protein, many tissues: wherever it fails, the mucus loses water. Fix the channel and every system benefits.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Make a guess: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Mission 3

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA). Its position on a chromosome is its locus.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene, at the same locus.
  • Body cells are diploid: two copies of each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has, e.g. Tt.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable traits. It results from the genotype and the environment.
  • Gametes are haploid: one allele of each gene. Fertilisation makes a diploid zygote with one allele from each parent.
  • Say: "two alleles of the gene for height" — not: "a gene for tall and a gene for short".
Mission 3

Dominant and recessive

  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present. Capital letter: T.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when homozygous (tt). Lower-case letter: t.
  • So TT and Tt plants are equally tall.
  • Why? T codes for a functional protein; one copy makes enough of it.
  • t usually codes for a non-functional or absent product. It is still there, and still passed on.
  • Say: "the recessive allele has no effect in the heterozygote" — not: "the dominant allele switches it off".
Quick check
?In peas the allele T for tall stems is dominant to the allele t for dwarf stems, and TT and Tt plants are equally tall. What is the reason that the heterozygote is not shorter than the homozygous dominant plant?
Mission 3

The monohybrid cross

  • Show every step in a fully labelled genetic diagram: parental phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, offspring genotypes, phenotypes, ratio.
  • P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf). Gametes: all T; all t. F1: all Tt, all tall.
  • F2: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes T and t gametes in equal numbers.
  • A Punnett square gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
  • The two alleles separate into different gametes. That is the law of segregation.
  • As probabilities: P(tt) = ½ × ½ = ¼. P(Tt) = (½ × ½) + (½ × ½) = ½.
  • The hook: both tall parents were Tt. The short plant is the ¼ that got t from each.

Mission 3

A recessive disorder in a family

  • Some human disorders are caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene.
  • Heterozygotes are unaffected carriers. Two carriers can have an affected child.
  • Cystic fibrosis: caused by recessive alleles of the CFTR gene.
  • Carrier × carrier: each child has a ¼ chance of being affected.
  • Each fertilisation is independent. Earlier children do not change the odds.
Quick check
?Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene. Two parents who are both heterozygous for this gene have three children, all of whom have cystic fibrosis. Which statement correctly describes the probability that their fourth child will have cystic fibrosis?
Mission 3

The test cross

  • A tall plant could be TT or Tt. You cannot tell by looking.
  • Test cross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt) plant.
  • tt passes on only t, which masks nothing. So each offspring shows the allele it got from the tall plant.
  • All tall offspring: the plant is probably TT.
  • Any dwarf offspring: the plant must be Tt. Expect about 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
Quick check
?To find out whether a purple-flowered pea plant is homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp), a breeder crosses it with a white-flowered plant (pp). Why is a white-flowered plant used for this test cross?
Mission check

?In mice, the allele B for black fur is dominant to the allele b for brown fur. A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse. Of the 9 offspring, 5 are black and 4 are brown. Which conclusion is correct?

Like the short pea plant in the hook, the brown pups reveal a recessive allele that was there all along, hidden in a heterozygote.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Make a guess: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Mission 4

When neither allele is dominant

  • Sometimes the heterozygote differs from both homozygotes.
  • Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.
  • Example: four o'clock plant, Mirabilis jalapa. Red × white gives pink flowers.
  • Notation: one letter for the gene, superscripts for alleles: Cᴿ and Cᵂ. No lower case, as neither is recessive.
  • The alleles stay separate in the heterozygote. They do not blend.
  • Say: "both alleles are expressed" — not: "the alleles mix".
Mission 4

Worked cross: two heterozygotes

  • Each heterozygote (CᴿCᵂ) makes Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes in equal numbers.
  • Offspring: 1 CᴿCᴿ : 2 CᴿCᵂ : 1 CᵂCᵂ.
  • Each genotype has its own phenotype. So the phenotype ratio is also 1 : 2 : 1, not 3 : 1.
  • Pink × pink: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • So the card's pink plants are CᴿCᵂ. Red and white return in the next generation.
Quick check
?In the four o'clock plant Mirabilis jalapa, a cross between a red-flowered plant and a white-flowered plant gives only pink-flowered offspring. Two of these pink plants are crossed. What phenotypes are expected in their offspring?
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of plant, flower colour is controlled by one gene with two alleles that show incomplete dominance. Plants homozygous for one allele have red flowers, and plants homozygous for the other allele have white flowers. Which description of the heterozygous plants' flowers is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, a cross of red-flowered plants with white-flowered plants produced only pink-flowered plants. A student claims that flower colour is controlled by one gene with two incompletely dominant alleles, so that pink-flowered plants are heterozygous. Which result of crossing pink-flowered plants with white-flowered plants would best support the student's claim?

Pink plants pass on red or white unchanged, exactly as the hook's pink plants did.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Make a guess: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Mission 5

Sex chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 22 pairs of autosomes + one pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Female: XX. Male: XY.
  • Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries X or Y. So the sperm decides the sex of the zygote.
  • The X carries far more genes than the much smaller Y.
  • A gene on a sex chromosome is sex-linked. Most are on the X: X-linked.
  • A male has one copy of each X-linked gene. A female has two.
Mission 5

Writing an X-linked cross

  • Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting), Xʰ (haemophilia). The Y carries no allele.
  • Haemophilia: a recessive X-linked allele; a clotting factor is missing, so blood clots slowly.
  • Carrier mother × unaffected father: XᴴXʰ × XᴴY.
  • Gametes: Xᴴ or Xʰ · Xᴴ or Y.
  • Offspring: XᴴXᴴ, XᴴXʰ (carrier), XᴴY, XʰY (affected).
  • Daughters: none affected, ½ carriers. Sons: ½ affected.
  • Label the diagram fully: phenotypes, genotypes, gametes, offspring, sexes.
Quick check
?A woman who is a carrier of haemophilia (XᴴXʰ) has children with a man who does not have haemophilia (XᴴY). Their first child is a boy. What is the probability that he has haemophilia?
Mission 5

Why sons are affected more often

  • A male's one X comes from his mother. One recessive allele is enough: XʰY is affected.
  • A female needs two: XʰXʰ. That needs an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
  • An affected father passes his X to every daughter (all carriers) and to no son.
  • So X-linked recessive traits never pass father → son.
  • Say: "a son inherits his X chromosome from his mother" — not: "haemophilia passes from father to son".
Mission 5

Reading a pedigree

  • Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected. Generations I, II, III; individuals numbered left to right.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is recessive; both parents carry it.
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → the allele is dominant.
  • Recessive allele + affected daughter of an unaffected father → not X-linked, so autosomal.
  • A father passes his X to no son. If a son can only have got the allele from his father, the gene is not X-linked.
Quick check
?A man and a woman who are both unaffected by a genetic disorder have three children: an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected daughter later has four children with an unaffected man, and all four are unaffected. What can be deduced about the disorder?

?From the earlier screen: Prince Leopold had haemophilia; his wife did not carry the allele. Which statement about their two children, Charles Edward and Alice, is correct?
Mission 5

Predicting from a pedigree

  • Step 1: dominant or recessive? Look for unaffected parents with an affected child.
  • Step 2: autosomal or X-linked? Check affected daughters' fathers and affected fathers' sons.
  • Step 3: write the genotypes you know (affected recessives; carriers revealed by children).
  • Step 4: use a cross to give a probability for the next child.
  • Keep "sex-linked" for genes on sex chromosomes. Genes linked on the same autosome are a different idea.
Mission check

?In birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W: males are ZZ and females are ZW. In a hypothetical species of bird, a feather trait is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on the Z chromosome; the W chromosome carries no allele of this gene. In a population in which this allele is present, which sex is expected to show the trait more often, and why?

It is not about being male. It is about having only one copy of the chromosome that carries the gene.

Mission 6

Mission 6: If a gene is faulty, can we find it before birth, or even fix it?

Make a guess: If a gene is faulty, can we find it before birth, or even fix it?

Mission 6

Why screen?

  • Genetic screening tests people for particular alleles.
  • Heritable conditions: e.g. alleles for cystic fibrosis.
  • Carriers: people with one recessive allele and no symptoms, who could pass it on.
  • Health risks: e.g. BRCA1 and BRCA2 alleles raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.
  • Genetic counselling: a trained counsellor explains results, risks for future children, and the options. The decisions stay with the person.
Mission 6

Screening embryos and foetuses

  • Carrier screening: test adults. If both partners carry a recessive allele, each child has a 1 in 4 chance of the condition.
  • PGD (pre-implantation genetic diagnosis): embryos made by IVF; one or a few cells removed and tested; an unaffected embryo is implanted.
  • CVS (chorionic villus sampling): about 11–14 weeks; a sample of placenta cells.
  • Amniocentesis: from about 15 weeks; a needle takes amniotic fluid containing foetal cells.
  • CVS and amniocentesis each carry a small risk of miscarriage (about 0.5%).
Quick check
?Two carriers of a cystic fibrosis allele already have one child with cystic fibrosis. At genetic counselling they ask about a second pregnancy. What is the chance that the next child has cystic fibrosis?
Mission 6

Weighing screening

  • Possible benefits:
    • Early diagnosis and treatment; time to prepare.
    • Informed choices about having children.
  • Concerns:
    • Miscarriage risk from CVS and amniocentesis; false positive and false negative results.
    • Decisions about ending a pregnancy, or discarding embryos after PGD.
    • Knowing about an untreatable, late-onset condition such as Huntington's disease may cause distress.
    • Privacy: could insurers or employers use the results?
    • Fear that selection could extend to non-medical traits.
  • Ethical viewpoints to apply:
    • Rights and duties: e.g. a right to know, or not to know.
    • Maximising good outcomes: weigh total benefits against total harms.
    • Autonomy: people make informed decisions for themselves.
    • Virtue: what would a good, caring person do?
  • Present several viewpoints; weigh them with the evidence given.
  • Screening finds a faulty allele; it does not fix it. Adding a working copy to body cells (gene therapy) is a separate treatment, approved so far for only a few conditions.
Mission check

?Carrier screening shows that a woman carries one cystic fibrosis allele. Her partner is not a carrier. Which statement is correct?

Screening turns a vague worry into a precise risk. What to do with that risk is a choice, weighed from several viewpoints.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Mutations arise spontaneously from copying and repair errors, at random: the environment selects among them but does not direct them.
  • Mutation is the only source of new alleles; selection then changes their frequency.

Mission 2

  • CF is caused by any of many mutations in the CFTR gene; too little Cl⁻ moves, water leaves the mucus by osmosis, and sticky mucus blocks airways, the pancreatic duct and the reproductive tract.
  • Effects follow from Fick's law in the lungs, blocked enzymes in digestion, and blocked or absent sperm ducts and thick cervical mucus in reproduction.

Mission 3

  • Alleles are forms of one gene; genotype + environment → phenotype; one dominant allele is enough for the dominant phenotype.
  • Heterozygote × heterozygote → 3 : 1; a test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals a hidden recessive allele.

Mission 4

  • With incomplete dominance the heterozygote is intermediate; heterozygote × heterozygote gives 1 : 2 : 1.

Mission 5

  • X-linked recessive alleles show in any male who carries one (one X), but in females only when homozygous; sons get their X from their mother.
  • Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; then an affected daughter with an unaffected father → autosomal.

Mission 6

  • Screening identifies carriers, affected embryos or foetuses and drug responses; it informs choices and raises ethical questions.
