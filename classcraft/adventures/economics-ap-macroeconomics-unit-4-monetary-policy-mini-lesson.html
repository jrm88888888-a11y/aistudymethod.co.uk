Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can't you open an account at a central bank, and what does it do instead?

Make a guess: Why can't you open an account at a central bank, and what does it do instead?

Mission 1

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the center of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 1

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • The Fed also oversees and regulates banks, and conducts monetary policy (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • The Fed has a dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. It aims for 2% inflation over the longer run (PCE price index).
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 1

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • Reserve requirements: a minimum share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • The Fed's policy rate is its target range for the federal funds rate, the rate banks charge each other for overnight loans of reserves. Rates on car loans, credit cards and mortgages tend to move with it.
  • The Fed's tools: the discount rate, interest on reserve balances, open market operations and the required reserve ratio (set to 0% in the US since March 2020).

Mission 2

Mission 2: If the Fed cannot order banks to charge more, how does it push interest rates up across the country?

Make a guess: If the Fed cannot order banks to charge more, how does it push interest rates up across the country?

Mission 2

The Fed's goals and its target

  • The Federal Reserve (the Fed) is the US central bank.
  • Dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices (2% inflation over the longer run).
  • The FOMC (Federal Open Market Committee) meets 8 times a year to set policy.
  • Its policy rate is the federal funds rate (FFR): the overnight rate banks charge each other to borrow reserves.
  • The FOMC sets a target range. Example: from 19 December 2024 the range was 4¼% to 4½%.
  • The Fed does not set the rate on each loan. It steers the FFR, and other rates follow.
Mission 2

The Fed's four tools

  • Open market operations (OMOs): buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds reserves and raises the monetary base; selling drains them.
  • Discount rate: the rate the Fed charges banks that borrow from it directly.
  • Interest on reserve balances (IORB): an administered rate the Fed pays banks on reserves held at the Fed.
  • Required reserve ratio: the minimum share of deposits banks must hold as reserves. Set to 0% since 26 March 2020.
  • Expansionary: buy bonds, lower the discount rate and IORB, lower the reserve ratio.
  • Contractionary: the opposite.
Mission 2

Limited reserves: the old system

  • Before 2008, banks held only small amounts of reserves: under 50 billion dollars in 2007.
  • Reserve market graph: vertical axis "Federal funds rate", horizontal "Quantity of reserves".
  • Supply of reserves (S) is vertical: the Fed sets it with OMOs.
  • Demand for reserves (D) slopes down: when reserves are scarce, banks pay more to borrow them.
  • In this limited-reserves system, S cuts D on its sloping part.
  • So small OMOs move the FFR: a bond purchase shifts S right and the FFR falls.
Mission 2Worked example

An open market purchase

Illustrative data, limited-reserves system:

  • The Fed buys 5 billion dollars of bonds from banks. Bank reserves rise by 5 billion dollars.
  • Required reserve ratio = 10%. Money multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10.
  • Maximum change in the money supply = 5 × 10 = 50 billion dollars.
  • Reserve market: S shifts right; the FFR falls.
  • Money market: money supply shifts right; the nominal interest rate falls.
  • It is a maximum: banks may keep extra reserves, and people may hold cash.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: in a limited-reserves system with a required reserve ratio of 20%, the Fed sells 2 billion dollars of bonds to banks. What is the maximum change in the money supply?
Mission 2

Ample reserves: the system today

  • After 2008 the Fed bought bonds on a huge scale. Reserves rose to over 3 trillion dollars (2024).
  • In 2019 the Fed said it would keep operating with an ample supply of reserves.
  • With so many reserves, banks will not lend them out for much less than IORB, so D goes flat at IORB.
  • S cuts D on its flat part. Small OMOs no longer move the FFR.
  • To change the FFR, the Fed changes its administered rates: mainly IORB. Raise IORB → the flat part rises → the FFR rises.
  • OMOs are still used, but mainly to keep reserves ample.
  • Say: "in an ample-reserves system, the Fed moves the FFR by changing IORB" — not: "by changing the quantity of reserves".

Mission 2

From the policy rate to AD

  • A lower FFR pulls down other short-term rates: car loans, credit cards, business loans.
  • Borrowing is cheaper, so investment (I) and interest-sensitive consumption (C) rise.
  • AD shifts right: real GDP and the price level rise.
  • Use for a recessionary gap: lower the FFR target (expansionary).
  • For an inflationary gap: raise the FFR target (contractionary); AD shifts left.
  • Draw three linked graphs: reserve market (FFR) → money market (nominal interest rate; MS shifts right in the AP model) → AD-AS.
Mission 2Worked example

Closing a recessionary gap

Illustrative data:

  • Recessionary gap: real GDP is 200 billion dollars below full employment (Yf).
  • The Fed lowers IORB, and the FFR falls. Investment rises by 40 billion dollars.
  • MPC = 0.8, so the spending multiplier = 1 ÷ (1 − 0.8) = 5.
  • AD shifts right by up to 40 × 5 = 200 billion dollars: enough to close the gap.
  • Result: real GDP rises toward Yf; the price level rises; unemployment falls.
Quick check
?The US economy has an inflationary gap and the Fed operates with ample reserves. Which sequence shows the Fed's response correctly?
Mission 2

Explain: lags and limits

  • Time lags: it takes time to spot a problem, decide, and for spending to respond.
  • Zero lower bound: the Fed held the target range at 0% to ¼% from December 2008 to December 2015, and from March 2020 to March 2022. It cannot cut far below zero.
  • At the lower bound it bought long-term bonds on a large scale (quantitative easing) to push down long-term rates.
  • Confidence: if firms expect weak sales, cheaper loans may not raise investment much.
  • On a graph: if investment is not sensitive to the interest rate, AD shifts only a little.
Mission check

?A central bank in another country runs an ample-reserves system: its banks hold far more reserves than they need. It sells bonds worth a small share of those reserves and leaves the rate it pays on reserves unchanged. What happens to its overnight interbank rate?

When reserves are ample, the quantity of reserves no longer sets the policy rate; the rate paid on reserves does.

Mission 3

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • US: the Federal Reserve (the Fed). Its FOMC sets a target for the federal funds rate.
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 3

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • The Fed's dual mandate: maximum employment and stable prices. It aims for 2% inflation (PCE price index, since 2012).
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 3

The central bank's tools

  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds money to the banking system; selling drains it.
  • Minimum reserve requirements: the share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • Fed tools: open market operations, the discount rate, interest on reserve balances (an administered rate) and the reserve requirement.
  • With ample reserves, the Fed moves the federal funds rate mainly by changing its administered rates (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
Mission 3

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a recessionary gap.
  • Expansionary fiscal and monetary policy together shift AD right by more.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).
Mission 3

From the money market to AD-AS

  • Expansionary policy in the limited-reserves model: the Fed buys bonds. With ample reserves it lowers its administered rates (IORB) instead.
  • Money market: MS shifts right, MS1 → MS2. The nominal interest rate falls, i1 → i2.
  • Lower rates: investment and interest-sensitive consumption rise.
  • AD-AS: AD shifts right. Real GDP and PL rise. A recessionary gap closes.
  • Contractionary policy: in the limited-reserves model the Fed sells bonds and MS shifts left; with ample reserves it raises IORB. Either way rates rise and AD shifts left.
  • Calculation (limited-reserves model, illustrative data): the Fed buys 10 billion dollars of bonds from banks; required reserve ratio 10%.
    • New excess reserves = 10 billion dollars.
    • Money multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.10 = 10.
    • Maximum increase in the money supply = 10 × 10 = 100 billion dollars.

Mission 3

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • US: the FOMC raised the federal funds rate target from 0–0.25% (March 2020 to March 2022) to 5.25–5.50% (July 2023). CPI inflation peaked at 9.1% (June 2022, BLS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 3

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
  • Limits:
    • Lags: time to recognize the problem, then time for the economy to adjust as borrowing and spending respond.
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 4

Mission 4

A market for saving and borrowing

  • Savers lend their unspent income (through banks and bond markets). Borrowers use it, mostly for investment in new capital.
  • The market for loanable funds brings them together.
  • Its price is the real interest rate: what lenders earn and borrowers pay in purchasing power.
  • Supply slopes up: a higher real rate rewards saving.
  • Demand slopes down: at a lower real rate, more investment projects are worth borrowing for.
  • Equilibrium: real interest rate r1, quantity of loanable funds Q1.
  • If the rate is above r1, there is a surplus of funds: lenders compete and the rate falls. Below r1, a shortage pushes it up.
Mission 4

National saving

  • The supply of loanable funds comes from national saving.
  • National saving = private saving + public saving.
  • Public saving = tax revenue − government spending. A budget deficit is negative public saving.
  • Illustrative data (billion): private saving 900, taxes 600, government spending 700.
  • Public saving = 600 − 700 = −100.
  • National saving = 900 + (−100) = 800.
  • Say: "a budget deficit reduces public saving" — not: "a deficit is the same as the national debt".
Quick check
?Private saving is 500 billion. The government collects 300 billion in taxes and spends 350 billion. What is national saving?
Mission 4

The open economy

  • Savers in other countries can lend here too, and domestic savers can lend abroad.
  • Net capital inflow = foreign saving flowing in − domestic saving flowing out.
  • Investment = national saving + net capital inflow.
  • Illustrative data: national saving $800 billion, net capital inflow $150 billion.
  • Investment = 800 + 150 = $950 billion.
  • A country with a net capital outflow invests less than it saves.

Mission 4

Crowding out

  • In the lab, a deficit of 100 raises the real rate from 6% to 7%.
  • Total borrowing rises to 550, but the government takes 100 of it.
  • Private investment falls from 500 to 450: it is crowded out.
  • Less private investment today → a smaller capital stock → slower long-run growth.
  • How much crowding out? It depends on how far the rate rises, and how sensitive investment is to it.
Quick check
?The government gives firms a tax credit for each new machine they buy (an investment tax credit). Other things equal, what happens in the loanable funds market?
Mission 4

Capital follows the real rate

  • Financial capital flows toward countries with higher real interest rates.
  • Step 1: the US real rate rises above rates abroad.
  • Step 2: foreign savers buy US assets: net capital inflow rises, so the supply of loanable funds shifts right. This limits the rise in the real rate.
  • Step 3: to buy US assets they need dollars: demand for the dollar rises, so the dollar appreciates (see Mission 5).
  • A central bank that raises its policy rate can set off the same chain.
Quick check
?Real interest rates in the United States rise relative to those in other countries. Other things equal, what happens?
Mission check

?Worried about their jobs, households decide to save a larger share of their income. Other things equal, what happens in the loanable funds market?

More saving lowers the real interest rate and raises the quantity of funds borrowed for investment.

Mission 5

Who wants a currency, and who sells it?

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand for the home currency comes from foreigners who need it to:
    • buy the country's exports of goods and services
    • buy its financial assets: bonds, shares, bank deposits (portfolio investment)
    • set up or buy firms there (inward FDI)
  • Supply of the home currency comes from residents who sell it to buy imports and foreign assets.
  • Demand for dollars rises when foreigners want more US goods, services or assets.
  • A US tariff or quota on European goods cuts US imports, so Americans supply fewer dollars: the dollar appreciates.
  • Financial flows are huge and move in seconds. News about interest rates hits the currency within minutes.
Mission 5

Money flows toward the higher real interest rate

  • Investors compare the return on assets in different countries.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Illustrative data:
Nominal rateInflationReal rate
Country A5%4%1%
Country B3%1%2%
  • A has the higher nominal rate, but B has the higher real rate.
  • Other things equal, financial capital flows out of A and into B.
  • Demand for B's currency rises: B's currency appreciates.
  • Financial capital flows toward the country with the relatively higher real interest rate: a net capital inflow.
  • Say: "capital flows toward the higher real interest rate" — not: toward the higher nominal rate.
Quick check

Mission 5

From a rate decision to the whole economy

  • The Fed lowers its administered rates, such as interest on reserve balances (IORB); the federal funds rate falls:
  • Real interest rates fall in the short run → financial capital flows out → the currency depreciates.
  • Exports become cheaper abroad; imports more expensive at home → net exports rise → AD rises.
  • Effects:
    • output rises and unemployment falls (if there is spare capacity)
    • inflation rises: more expensive imports, and AD closer to capacity
  • A rate rise works the other way: appreciation, lower net exports, lower AD.
  • Say: "the dollar depreciates, so net exports increase" — not: "net exports increase, so the dollar depreciates".
Quick check
?The Fed raises its administered rates, such as interest on reserve balances (IORB), and the federal funds rate rises. Inflation expectations do not change. Which chain of effects is most likely?
Mission 5

Case: the yen and the dollar in 2022

  • In 2022 the US central bank (the Federal Reserve) raised its target for the federal funds rate from 0–0.25% to 4.25–4.50%.
  • The Bank of Japan kept its short-term policy rate at −0.1%.
  • The gap in interest rates widened, and funds flowed toward US assets.
  • Real returns rose too: the yield on 10-year inflation-protected US government bonds went from −0.97% (3 January) to 1.73% (20 October).
  • Yen per US dollar: 115.27 on 3 January 2022; 149.82 on 20 October 2022.
  • Worked numbers:
    • Value of the dollar in yen: (149.82 − 115.27) ÷ 115.27 × 100 = +30.0%
    • Value of the yen in dollars: 1 ÷ 115.27 = 0.008675; 1 ÷ 149.82 = 0.006675. Change = −23.1%
  • A 30% rise in one currency is not a 30% fall in the other.
  • The dollar rose against most currencies: the Fed's broad dollar index rose 11.3% from 3 January to 26 September 2022.
Mission 5

Fiscal policy, interest rates and the dollar

  • Expansionary fiscal policy: the government spends more or taxes less, so its budget deficit rises.
  • It borrows more: demand for loanable funds rises → the real interest rate rises.
  • US assets pay more → financial capital flows in → demand for dollars rises → the dollar appreciates.
  • Exports fall, imports rise → net exports fall, offsetting part of the rise in AD.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy works the other way.
  • Fiscal policy also raises AD, real output and the price level directly.
  • Say: "the deficit raises the real interest rate, which attracts capital inflow" — not: "the deficit makes the dollar weaker".
Mission 5

Loanable funds in an open economy

  • National saving = private saving + public saving.
  • In an open economy: investment = national saving + net capital inflow.
  • Illustrative data: national saving 3.0 trillion dollars; net capital inflow 0.5 trillion → investment = 3.5 trillion dollars.
  • A net capital inflow adds to the supply of loanable funds at home.
  • When US real interest rates rise relative to Europe's:
    • US: capital inflow → supply of loanable funds shifts right; the dollar appreciates.
    • Europe: capital outflow → its supply of loanable funds shifts left; its real rate rises; the euro depreciates.
  • In the short run the Fed can change the real interest rate, and so net capital inflows.

  • Takeaway: deficit → demand for loanable funds up → real interest rate up → capital inflow → demand for dollars up → appreciation → net exports down.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.

Mission 2

  • The Fed targets a range for the federal funds rate, to pursue maximum employment and stable prices.
  • Tools: open market operations, the discount rate, interest on reserve balances and the required reserve ratio (0% since 2020).
  • Limited reserves: OMOs shift supply on the sloping part of reserve demand. Ample reserves (today): the FFR follows IORB.
  • A lower FFR raises I and C, shifting AD right to close a recessionary gap; a higher FFR does the reverse.

Mission 3

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.

Mission 4

  • Loanable funds: supply = national saving (private + public), demand = borrowing for investment; price = real interest rate.
  • A budget deficit raises the real rate and can crowd out private investment.
  • Investment = national saving + net capital inflow; capital flows toward higher real interest rates.

Mission 5

  • Capital flows toward the higher real interest rate, so a rise in a country's real rate tends to make its currency appreciate.
  • Monetary policy works through the exchange rate: a rate cut → depreciation → higher net exports and AD.
  • Expansionary fiscal policy raises the real interest rate, attracts capital inflow, appreciates the dollar and lowers net exports.
