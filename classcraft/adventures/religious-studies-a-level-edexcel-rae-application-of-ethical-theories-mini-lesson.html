Mission 1

From pacifism to just war

  • Many early Christian writers (e.g. Tertullian, Origen) opposed Christians fighting, though some Christians did serve as soldiers. Jesus taught "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44) and told Peter, "Put your sword back into its place" (Matthew 26:52).
  • After Constantine (Edict of Milan, 313) Christians shared responsibility for the Roman Empire and its defence.
  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), City of God (413–426): war is always a tragedy, caused by sin, but can be a duty to restore peace and punish wrong.
  • Peace is "the tranquillity of order" (City of God XIX.13, Dods translation): war is justified only as a way back to it.
  • Also cited: Romans 13:4 (the ruler "doesn't bear the sword in vain") and John the Baptist's advice to soldiers, which does not tell them to leave the army (Luke 3:14).

Mission 1Read the source

Aquinas' three conditions

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40, a. 1 (English Dominican translation):

> "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary."

  • 1. Authority of the sovereign: only a public authority, responsible for the common good, may declare war; not a private person.
  • 2. Just cause: those attacked deserve it "on account of some fault": e.g. a wrong that must be righted.
  • 3. Right intention: to advance good and avoid evil, aiming at peace. Aquinas, quoting Augustine, condemns "the cruel thirst for vengeance" and "the lust of power".
  • The idea of double effect is traced to Aquinas (q. 64, a. 7, on killing in self-defence): an act may have two effects, only one intended.

Mission 1

Jus ad bellum: going to war

  • Jus ad bellum (Latin: right to war): when is it just to start a war? Later thinkers added to Aquinas' three:
  • Just cause (e.g. self-defence against aggression); legitimate authority; right intention.
  • Last resort: peaceful means (diplomacy, sanctions) tried or clearly hopeless.
  • Reasonable chance of success: no hopeless sacrifice of lives.
  • Proportionality: the good expected must outweigh the harm war will cause.
  • Often also: public declaration, and comparative justice (one side's grievance clearly outweighs the other's).
  • The Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) §2309 lists modern conditions in this tradition.
Mission 1

Jus in bello: fighting justly

  • Jus in bello (right conduct in war): how must a war be fought? Applies to both sides, whether or not their cause is just.
  • Discrimination: distinguish combatants from non-combatants; never target civilians.
  • Proportionality of means: no more force than the military aim needs.
  • No means evil in themselves (mala in se): e.g. torture, mass rape, poison gas.
  • Double effect (Aquinas): a strike on a military target that foreseeably kills some civilians may be allowed, if the deaths are not intended, not the means to the goal, and proportionate.
Mission 1

Non-combatant immunity

  • Non-combatant immunity: civilians, wounded soldiers and prisoners may not be deliberately attacked.
  • In law: Geneva Conventions (1949) and Additional Protocol I (1977): the "principle of distinction".
  • G. E. M. Anscombe (1919–2001), "Mr Truman's Degree" (1956): she opposed Oxford honouring President Truman, because choosing to kill the innocent as a means to an end (Hiroshima, Nagasaki) is murder.
  • Problems: area bombing in the Second World War (e.g. Dresden, 1945); fighters hiding among civilians; dual-use targets (power stations, bridges); drones far from any battlefield.
  • Michael Walzer (b. 1935), Just and Unjust Wars (1977): only a "supreme emergency" might override the rule; critics say no exception should exist.

Mission 1

Jus post bellum: justice after war

  • Jus post bellum: how should a war end, and what does the winner owe?
  • Brian Orend, The Morality of War (2006), and Walzer: a just peace should vindicate the rights that were violated, not seek revenge.
  • Principles: proportion (no humiliating, punitive peace); discrimination (punish leaders responsible, not a whole people); compensation; rehabilitation (rebuilding institutions); war crimes trials with fair procedure.
  • Examples often discussed: the Treaty of Versailles (1919), criticised as punitive; the Nuremberg trials (1945–46) and the Marshall Plan (from 1948) as models of justice and rebuilding.
  • Augustine's aim, peace as order restored, is the root of the idea.
Mission 1

How the theory evolved

  • Cicero (106–43 BC): war only to live in peace unharmed.
  • Augustine (5th c.) and Gratian's Decretum (c. 1140) brought the idea into Church law; Aquinas (13th c.) set the three conditions.
  • Francisco de Vitoria (c. 1483–1546): on Spain's conquest of the Americas, both sides may sincerely believe they are just; differences of religion are no just cause.
  • Hugo Grotius (1583–1645), On the Law of War and Peace (1625): just war as international law based on natural law, binding whatever one's religion.
  • 20th century: Hague (1899, 1907) and Geneva Conventions; UN Charter (1945): force only in self-defence (Article 51) or with Security Council authority.
  • Today: Walzer (1977); "Responsibility to Protect" (UN, 2005).
  • Spec: "reasons for and influences on the development of the theory".
Mission 1

Applying the theory to real wars

  • Applying the theory means testing each criterion with evidence, and admitting where people disagree.
  • Gulf War (1991): UN Security Council authority; aim to reverse the invasion of Kuwait; widely judged to meet jus ad bellum; debate over in bello (e.g. attacks on infrastructure).
  • Iraq War (2003): no explicit new UN authorisation; the main stated cause (weapons of mass destruction) was not borne out; Pope John Paul II and many church leaders opposed it; supporters cited earlier UN resolutions and the removal of a brutal regime.
  • Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine (2022): Ukraine's defence is claimed under self-defence (UN Charter Art. 51); questions of in bello and post bellum remain live.
  • Spec: "contemporary conflicts that may be evaluated against the theory".
Mission 1Evaluate

Is just war theory successful?

  • Claim: Just war theory is successful, as a theory and in practice.
  • For: It sets limits states accept in law (UN Charter, Geneva Conventions); it lets citizens and churches criticise their own governments.
  • For: It holds together Christian love of neighbour (protecting the innocent) and realism about evil.
  • Against: Every side claims a just cause; criteria such as "proportionality" and "last resort" are vague.
  • Against: Modern war (bombing, drones, WMD) makes discrimination hard; pacifists say it gives war a moral cover (see Mission 2).
  • Judgement criteria: Success as a theory (clear, consistent) or in practice (does it actually restrain)? Can it fit modern weapons? Who judges whether the criteria are met?
  • Spec: "the success of the Just War Theory as a theory and in practice".

Mission 2

Why weapons of mass destruction are different

  • WMD: nuclear, chemical, biological and radiological weapons, able to kill vast numbers at once.
  • Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945): the only nuclear weapons used in war.
  • Effects: blast and fire over whole cities; radiation sickness and cancers for decades; possible "nuclear winter" after a large exchange.
  • So they struggle to meet jus in bello: they cannot discriminate between soldiers and civilians, and their harm is hard to call proportionate.
  • Law: Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1993); Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968); Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017; the UK and other nuclear states have not joined).
  • Second Vatican Council (Gaudium et Spes, 1965, §80): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities is a crime against God and humanity (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Deterrence: the peace of fear

  • Deterrence: possessing nuclear weapons, ready to use them, so that no enemy dares attack. MAD (mutually assured destruction): each side could destroy the other.
  • Bentham (act utilitarian): weigh this policy's likely pleasures and pains: peace kept for decades (extent, duration) against a small chance of catastrophic suffering (intensity).
  • Mill (rule utilitarian): which general rule maximises happiness: "Possess, never use first" or "abolish"?
  • The paradox of deterrence: if using the weapons would be wrong, can it be right to intend or threaten to use them?
  • Catholic teaching moved: John Paul II (1982) judged deterrence acceptable only as a step towards disarmament; Pope Francis (Hiroshima, 2019) called even the possession of nuclear weapons immoral.

Mission 2

Absolute pacifism

  • Absolute pacifism: all war and lethal violence is wrong, whatever the cause.
  • Quakers (Religious Society of Friends): the Peace Testimony, from their declaration to Charles II (1660), renouncing outward wars and fighting for any end.
  • Anabaptist churches (Mennonites, Amish): non-resistance, from the Sermon on the Mount.
  • Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910), The Kingdom of God Is Within You (1894): Jesus' command not to resist evil by force applies to states too.
  • Others root nonviolence in other traditions: Gandhi's ahimsa (non-harm) and satyagraha (holding to truth).
  • Criticisms: it may leave the innocent unprotected; Reinhold Niebuhr (1892–1971), "Why the Christian Church Is Not Pacifist" (1940): love sometimes requires force against tyranny.
Mission 2

Relative, selective and nuclear pacifism

  • Relative (contingent) pacifism: war is wrong in almost every case; force may be used only rarely, e.g. as a police action or to stop genocide.
  • Selective pacifism: refusing to fight in particular wars judged unjust (e.g. some objectors to the Vietnam War).
  • Nuclear pacifism: any war using nuclear (or other mass destruction) weapons is wrong, because such weapons can never meet discrimination and proportionality.
  • Nuclear pacifism can be held by a just war thinker: it applies the theory, rather than rejecting it.
  • Some textbooks use "selective pacifism" for opposing only wars that use weapons of mass destruction; check your class notes.
  • Spec wording: "absolute, relative/selective and nuclear pacifism".
Mission 2

Pacifist movements and pressure groups

  • Fellowship of Reconciliation (1914): Christian pacifists across denominations.
  • Conscientious objectors in the First World War: the Military Service Act (1916) allowed exemptions on grounds of conscience; many objectors were imprisoned.
  • Peace Pledge Union: grew from Dick Sheppard's 1934 pledge to renounce war (named 1936).
  • Pax Christi (1945): Catholic peace movement; CND (Campaign for Nuclear Disarmament, 1958), with Bertrand Russell as first president.
  • ICAN (International Campaign to Abolish Nuclear Weapons): Nobel Peace Prize 2017, after the nuclear ban treaty.
  • Role: shaping law (conscience clauses, treaties), public opinion and support for objectors.

Mission 2

Did named wars succeed? Do wars bring benefits?

  • Second World War (1939–45): achieved its goal of defeating Nazi Germany, at a cost of tens of millions of lives.
  • Falklands (1982): British control restored in ten weeks; around 900 killed.
  • Iraq (2003): Saddam Hussein removed; years of insurgency followed. Afghanistan (2001–2021): ended with the Taliban back in power.
  • Perceived advantages of war: technology (radar, jet engines, mass-produced penicillin, early computers), the post-1945 welfare state, new roles for women at work.
  • Critics: these benefits could come without war; they cannot justify starting one (just cause comes first).
  • Spec: "perceived advantages of war such as technological development"; "success of named wars".
Mission 2

Can pacifism work?

  • Successes of nonviolence: Indian independence (1947); the US civil rights movement; Poland's Solidarity and the 1989 revolutions in Central Europe.
  • Research by Erica Chenoweth and Maria Stephan, Why Civil Resistance Works (2011), argues that nonviolent campaigns have often succeeded more than violent ones.
  • Hard cases: Nazi Germany; the Rwandan genocide (1994): could nonviolence have stopped them?
  • Pacifists reply: war's costs are always underestimated; nonviolent methods are rarely given the resources that war receives.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can nuclear deterrence be justified?

  • Claim: Possessing nuclear weapons as a deterrent is morally justified.
  • For: No war between the major nuclear powers since 1945 (supporters credit deterrence); the act utilitarian calculation may favour it.
  • For: One-sided disarmament could invite blackmail; just war thinkers accept defence of the innocent.
  • Against: It depends on a readiness to kill millions of civilians: the paradox of deterrence; nuclear pacifists and Pope Francis judge possession itself wrong.
  • Against: Accidents, false alarms and proliferation: a small risk each year adds up over decades.
  • Judgement criteria: Is it wrong to intend what it would be wrong to do? How should tiny risks of catastrophe be weighed? Are there realistic paths to disarmament?

Mission 3

The traditional Christian framework

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex belongs within marriage, between one man and one woman, for life.
  • Two purposes of sex in marriage: unitive (binding the couple in love) and procreative (open to children).
  • So the tradition counts as wrong: sex before marriage (fornication), sex with someone other than your spouse (adultery), and same-sex sexual acts.
  • Catholic teaching sets this out in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992): e.g. §2353 (fornication), §2380–2381 (adultery), §2357–2359 (homosexuality).
  • Many Protestant and Orthodox Christians have held the same framework, from different sources (scripture first, or scripture with tradition).
  • The question for this mission: what happens when Christians re-read that framework?

Mission 3

Premarital sex, adultery and promiscuity

  • Premarital sex: traditional teaching forbids it; Paul: "because of sexual immoralities, let each man have his own wife" (1 Corinthians 7:2, WEBBE).
  • Many liberal Christians now distinguish casual sex from sex in a committed relationship heading towards marriage, and judge the second less harshly or not wrong.
  • Adultery (extramarital sex) is condemned almost universally: religious and secular ethics both see a broken promise, deception and harm to a spouse and children.
  • Promiscuity (many casual partners): criticised by religious ethics as treating sex as without meaning, and by many secular thinkers for risk of harm and objectifying others.
  • Mercy matters too: Jesus refuses to condemn a woman caught in adultery, yet tells her "sin no more" (John 8:11).

Mission 3Diversity

Same-sex relationships: a spectrum

  • Catholic: same-sex attraction is not itself sinful, though the Catechism calls the inclination "objectively disordered" (§2358); same-sex acts cannot be approved; people must be treated with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (Catechism §2358). Fiducia Supplicans (2023) allows short, non-liturgical blessings of couples, including same-sex couples, without approving the union.
  • Many evangelicals and the Orthodox: same-sex acts are against scripture (Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27); some stress pastoral care and celibacy.
  • Church of England: does not marry same-sex couples; Prayers of Love and Faith (commended 2023) can be used to pray for same-sex couples.
  • Churches that marry same-sex couples: Quakers in Britain (decision 2009), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church in Britain (2021), Church of Scotland (2022, for ministers who choose to).
  • Secular perspectives: humanists and most liberal secular ethics: consenting adult relationships are a private good; equal treatment is a matter of justice and rights.
Mission 3

Contraception and childlessness

  • Lambeth Conference 1930 (Resolution 15): Anglican bishops accept contraception where there is a morally sound reason. Most Protestant churches now leave it to a couple's conscience.
  • Pope Paul VI, Humanae Vitae (1968): every marital act must stay open to life (§11); artificial contraception is excluded (§14); natural family planning, using infertile periods, is allowed (§16).
  • Many Catholics in Britain and elsewhere do not follow §14: a gap between official teaching and practice.
  • Secular view: contraception gives control over fertility, protects health, and was central to women's equality.
  • Childlessness: Catholic teaching sees children as a gift of marriage; an infertile couple's marriage is fully valid. Deliberately excluding children from the outset is treated as a serious defect of consent in Catholic canon law (Canon 1101 §2). Many Protestants and secularists treat being child-free as a legitimate choice.
Mission 3Diversity

Beyond Christianity

  • Islam: sex only within marriage (nikah): "And come not near unto adultery. Lo! it is an abomination and an evil way." (Qur'an 17:32, Pickthall). Mainstream Sunni and Shi'a scholars forbid same-sex acts, citing the story of Lut (e.g. 7:80–81); some progressive Muslim groups dissent. Many scholars permit contraception, citing hadith on withdrawal ('azl).
  • Judaism: sex is good and a duty within marriage; the first command is "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917). Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 binding; contraception often allowed for women with rabbinic advice. Reform and Liberal: many rabbis officiate at same-sex weddings; Liberal Judaism in Britain was an early supporter.
  • Pattern: in each tradition the split falls between those who treat the law as fixed and those who re-read it through ethical principle.
Mission 3

How teachings changed

  • Re-reading scripture: e.g. Leviticus as part of a holiness code for ancient Israel; Romans 1 about idolatry and excess, not loving partnerships (contested by traditionalists).
  • New knowledge: psychology treats sexual orientation as a stable part of identity, not a chosen vice.
  • Experience: Christians knowing gay and lesbian Christians in faithful partnerships.
  • Institutions decide: Church of England House of Bishops, Issues in Human Sexuality (1991): same-sex partnerships fall short of the ideal, but lay people who enter them in conscience may be welcomed; clergy may not.
  • Then: Lambeth 1998 Resolution I.10 (homosexual practice "incompatible with Scripture"); Living in Love and Faith (2020); Prayers of Love and Faith (2023).

Mission 3

Secularism and the law

  • Secularism: religion's influence on law and public life declines; law rests on consent, harm and equality.
  • Sexual Offences Act 1967: male homosexual acts partly decriminalised in England and Wales (Scotland 1980; Northern Ireland 1982).
  • Civil Partnership Act 2004; Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 (England and Wales; first weddings 2014); Scotland 2014; Northern Ireland 2020.
  • The 2013 Act protects religious freedom: no religious body must conduct same-sex weddings.
  • The contraceptive pill on the NHS from 1961 (at first for married women); no-fault divorce in England and Wales from 2022, so adultery need no longer be alleged.
  • Debate: did secular change drive church change, or did Christians help drive the law (e.g. some church leaders backed the 1957 Wolfenden Report)?
Mission 3

Vardy and Dominian

  • Peter Vardy (b. 1945), The Puzzle of Sex (1997; revised 2009).
  • Traces how Christian sexual teaching developed: shaped by its culture, and by Augustine's suspicion of desire after the Fall.
  • Argues that contraception has broken the old link between sex and reproduction, and worries that sex is now treated as a commodity (paraphrase).
  • Warns that sex without love or commitment risks treating people as objects (paraphrase).
  • Jack Dominian (1929–2014), Catholic psychiatrist; Let's Make Love: The Meaning of Sexual Intercourse (2001).
  • Sex in a committed relationship is deeply personal: it affirms each partner's worth and sexual identity, heals and reconciles, expresses hope for the future, and gives thanks (paraphrase).
  • So sex needs a lasting, reliable relationship; casual sex cannot carry these meanings.
  • He criticised Humanae Vitae: contraception can protect the couple's loving union.

Do religious teachings still have a role?

  • Claim: Religious teachings on sex have a continuing role in sexual ethics.

Mission 4

Natural law applied

  • Aquinas: the natural purpose (telos) of sex is reproduction and the education of children (primary precepts), within a stable society.
  • So sex belongs in marriage, which gives children a secure home. Premarital sex and adultery fail that purpose; adultery also harms ordered living in society.
  • Same-sex acts and contraception separate sex from reproduction, so traditional natural law judges them wrong (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 154).
  • Catholic teaching today adds the unitive purpose (Humanae Vitae, 1968).
  • Issues raised: infertile couples marry and have sex: so is reproduction really necessary? Same-sex behaviour occurs in nature: what counts as "natural"? Proportionalists may allow exceptions for a proportionate reason.
Mission 4

Situation ethics applied

  • Fletcher: only agape is always good; rules about sex are guides, not absolutes.
  • Personalism: put people first. Ask: is anyone used, deceived or harmed? Is this the most loving act?
  • Premarital sex: may be right in a loving, responsible relationship; wrong if it exploits.
  • Adultery: almost always betrays someone and harms them, so usually wrong, but not by rule.
  • Same-sex relationships: a faithful, loving relationship can be right.
  • Polyamorous relationships: right only if everyone knows and consents, and no partner or child is neglected; love and justice must be shared fairly.

Mission 4

Kant applied

  • Humanity formula: never treat a person merely as a means. Kant thought sexual desire on its own does exactly that: it wants the other's body, not the person (Lectures on Ethics, paraphrase).
  • His answer: marriage, a lifelong, equal and reciprocal union, in which each gives themselves and receives the other (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797).
  • Adultery breaks a promise: a maxim of breaking promises cannot be universalised.
  • Kant himself condemned same-sex acts as against nature.
  • Issues raised: many modern Kantians argue that honest, consenting, committed relationships, same-sex included, can treat each partner as an end. Is Kant's conclusion his principle, or his culture?
Mission 4

Utilitarianism applied

  • Bentham (act): weigh pleasure and pain. Consensual sex that harms no one adds to happiness.
  • Bentham wrote an essay (c. 1785, published only in 1978) arguing that same-sex acts should not be punished, because they cause no harm.
  • Adultery: the pain of betrayal to a spouse and children usually outweighs the pleasure.
  • Mill (often read as a rule utilitarian): rules of fidelity and honesty, generally followed, maximise happiness; higher pleasures of mind and feeling outrank merely bodily ones.
  • Contraception: reduces unwanted pregnancies and the spread of infection, so utilitarians usually support it.
  • Issues raised: can a calculus measure love or betrayal? Could secret adultery that is never found out be "right" for an act utilitarian?

Mission 4Read the source

Mill's harm principle

John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), On Liberty (1859), Chapter 1:

> "…the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

> "Over himself, over his own body and mind, the individual is sovereign."

Q1 On this principle, may the law forbid sex between consenting adults in private?

Show answer

No: no harm to others, so the law has no right to interfere.

Q2 Which laws on sex would still pass Mill's test?

Show answer

Laws protecting others from harm: consent, protection of children, laws against coercion and exploitation.

Mission 4

Private choice or society's business?

  • Wolfenden Report (1957): the law should not punish private, consensual homosexual acts between adults; there is a realm of private morality that is not the law's business (paraphrase). Led to the Sexual Offences Act 1967.
  • Patrick Devlin (1905–1992), The Enforcement of Morals (1965): a society is held together by shared morality; the law may defend it against conduct that ordinary people find deeply offensive.
  • H. L. A. Hart (1907–1992), Law, Liberty and Morality (1963): following Mill, only harm, not offence or disapproval, justifies criminal law.
  • Today's law still regulates sex: consent (Sexual Offences Act 2003), age of consent, bigamy, image-based abuse.
  • Religious voices disagree: some agree with Devlin that law teaches values; many accept a secular law while keeping stricter standards for believers.

Mission 4Evaluate

Are normative theories useful in sexual ethics?

  • Claim: Normative theories are useful for deciding sexual ethics.
  • For: They give reasons, not just reactions: a utilitarian or Kantian argument can be tested.
  • For: Situation ethics and utilitarianism attend to real people and harms; Kant's "never merely as a means" names the wrong in exploitation.
  • Against: Natural law and Kant reached conclusions on homosexuality that many now see as products of their age; theories may just rationalise prior views.
  • Against: Sex involves love, trust and vulnerability: hard to calculate (utilitarianism) or universalise (Kant).
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory give clear guidance? Does it respect persons? Can it handle new kinds of relationship? Does it depend on religious belief?
  • Spec: "the success or otherwise of contributions from ethical theory".
Mission 4

Changing social attitudes: strengths and weaknesses

  • Strengths: less shame and secrecy; protection from discrimination; marriage open to same-sex couples; contraception has given people, especially women, more control over their lives.
  • Weaknesses (argued by critics, religious and secular): sex treated as a consumer product; pornography and casual sex linked to objectification; fewer stable relationships for children.
  • Supporters answer: stability is better served by consent, honesty and equal rights than by stigma.
  • Ethical theories cut both ways: Kant's ban on objectification is used by conservatives (against casual sex) and liberals (against coercion).
  • Spec: "strengths and weaknesses of changing social attitudes".
Mission 4Worked answer

Applying and evaluating one theory

Task: "Kantian ethics is the most useful approach to premarital sex." Discuss. One paragraph:

  • Point: Kant's humanity formula is useful because it names what is wrong in exploitative sex.
  • Develop: sex that treats a partner as a means (e.g. deception, pressure) fails respect for persons, whatever pleasure it produces.
  • Counter: but Kant's own conclusion (sex only in marriage) seems to go beyond the formula; consenting, honest partners can respect each other outside marriage.
  • Judge (for this paragraph): useful as a test of how people treat each other; less useful as a rule about where sex belongs.
  • Use the theory and a scholar together (e.g. Vardy on objectification).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Augustine: war is a tragic duty to restore peace (the tranquillity of order); Aquinas: authority, just cause, right intention.
  • Jus ad bellum (going to war), jus in bello (discrimination, proportion, double effect, non-combatant immunity) and jus post bellum (a just peace).
  • The theory grew from church teaching into international law (Vitoria, Grotius, UN Charter, Geneva); its success depends on clear criteria and honest judges.

Mission 2

  • WMD struggle to meet discrimination and proportionality; deterrence rests on a threat that raises the paradox of intending a wrong act.
  • Pacifism: absolute (Quakers, Tolstoy), relative/selective, nuclear; movements (FoR, PPU, Pax Christi, CND, ICAN) and its practicality are debated.

Mission 3

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex within lifelong marriage of a man and a woman, unitive and procreative; churches now range from that view to celebrating same-sex marriage.
  • Change came through re-reading scripture, new knowledge, experience, institutions (Humanae Vitae 1968; Issues in Human Sexuality 1991) and a more secular law.
  • Vardy traces how teaching developed; Dominian grounds sex in a lasting, personal relationship.

Mission 4

  • Natural law asks about purpose; Kant about treating people as ends; situation ethics about agape in the situation; utilitarianism about happiness and harm.
  • Mill and Hart: law only to prevent harm; Devlin: law may defend a shared morality.
  • Evaluate theories by clarity, respect for persons, ability to handle new cases and dependence on belief.
