Mission 1

Mission 1: Why will a shop hand over real goods for a piece of paper that is worth almost nothing on its own?

Make a guess: Why will a shop hand over real goods for a piece of paper that is worth almost nothing on its own?

Mission 1

Life without money

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • You have eggs and want shoes. You must find someone who has shoes and wants eggs.
  • This is the double coincidence of wants. It is rare, so barter wastes time.
  • More problems:
    • how many eggs is a pair of shoes worth? Every item needs a price in every other item.
    • you can't split a cow to buy a loaf.
  • Money solves these problems.
Mission 1

What money does

  • Medium of exchange: money is accepted in payment for goods and services. Sell eggs for money, then buy shoes with it. No double coincidence needed.
  • Unit of account: prices are measured in money, so values are easy to compare.
  • Store of value: money can be saved and used later.
  • Standard for deferred payment: debts can be paid later in money, e.g. a loan repaid over five years.
  • A banknote is paper worth almost nothing in itself. It works as money because people accept it, trusting that others will accept it too.
  • The central bank works to keep the value of money stable, so the trust holds.
Mission 1

What makes good money

Good money is:

  • acceptable: people will take it in payment
  • durable: it does not wear out quickly
  • portable: easy to carry
  • divisible: can be split into small amounts
  • homogeneous: every unit of the same value is the same
  • limited in supply (scarce): if anyone could make more, it would lose its value
  • hard to forge
  • Say: "money is acceptable" — not: "money is valuable because of what it is made of".
Quick check
?A shop prices a jacket at 40 and a shirt at 20, so a customer can see at once that the jacket is worth two shirts. Which function of money does this show?
Mission 1

Money is more than notes and coins

  • Forms of money:
    • coins and banknotes (cash)
    • bank deposits: money in current and savings accounts
  • Most money is bank deposits. Bank of England (2014): deposits were about 97% of the UK's broad money; notes and coins about 3%.
  • Paying by debit card, phone or bank transfer moves bank deposits from one account to another.
  • The money is the deposit, not the card.
  • A credit card is not money either: it is a way of borrowing. The bill is paid later, with money.
  • In the past, many things served as money: shells, salt, gold and silver coins.
Quick check
?Sam has 50 in coins, 400 in a bank current account, a debit card for that account, and a credit card with a borrowing limit of 1,000. How much money does Sam have?
Mission 1

The central bank

  • Each country (or currency area) has a central bank: the bank for the government and for the banks.
  • Main roles:
    • sets the main interest rate to keep inflation low and stable
    • keeps the financial system stable: supervises banks, and lends to banks in trouble as the lender of last resort
    • issues banknotes
    • banker to the government: holds its accounts and helps it borrow
    • banker to commercial banks: they keep accounts there and settle payments with each other
    • manages the country's reserves of foreign currency and gold, and may act on the exchange rate
  • Say: "the central bank sets the main interest rate" — not: "the central bank sets every interest rate". Banks set their own rates (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Commercial banks

  • Commercial banks (high street banks) are businesses owned by shareholders. They aim to make a profit.
  • For savers: a safe place for money, interest on savings, and a way to make payments.
  • For borrowers: loans, overdrafts and mortgages (loans to buy a home).
  • For firms: loans that fund investment in new machines and buildings.
  • Also: changing foreign currency, financial advice.
  • Banks bring savers and borrowers together. They pay savers a lower interest rate than they charge borrowers.
  • Illustrative data: a bank pays 3% on 100,000 of deposits (3,000) and lends the 100,000 at 7% (7,000). The difference, 4,000, pays its costs and gives its profit.
Quick check
?How does a commercial bank mainly make a profit from savers and borrowers?
Mission check

?In some prisoner-of-war camps in the Second World War, prisoners got food parcels and swapped items. Cigarettes came to be accepted in almost every swap, even by non-smokers. Why did this make trading easier?

Anything that nearly everyone will accept in payment can work as money, even a cigarette.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Make a guess: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Mission 2

What is an interest rate?

  • Interest: the price of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it.
  • Interest rate: interest as a percentage of the amount, usually per year.
  • Savers lend their money to a bank, so the bank pays them interest.
  • Borrowers pay interest to the bank on loans, overdrafts, credit cards and mortgages.
  • One rate change pulls two ways: a higher rate is good news for savers and bad news for borrowers.
Mission 2

Interest rates and households

When interest rates rise:

  • Saving is more rewarding, so people save more.
  • Borrowing costs more, so people borrow less, especially for big items bought on credit: cars, furniture, holidays.
  • Households with variable-rate mortgages pay more each month, leaving less to spend.
  • On a fixed-rate loan, the payments change only when the fixed deal ends.
  • Result: consumer spending usually falls.
  • When interest rates fall, the opposite: less saving, more borrowing, more spending.
  • Winners and losers: savers living on interest (often retired people) gain from a rise; borrowers lose.
Quick check
?A central bank raises interest rates. Which outcome is most likely?
Mission 2

What else shapes household choices?

  • Income: richer households spend more in total and save more. Poorer households spend almost all their income.
  • Interest rates: higher rates encourage saving and discourage borrowing.
  • Confidence: when people expect good times, they spend and borrow more; when they fear losing their job, they save.
  • Age:
    • young adults often borrow (study, a first home)
    • middle-aged workers often save for retirement
    • retired people often spend their savings
  • Culture and attitudes: saving habits differ between societies. Some religions forbid paying or receiving interest, so banks offer other products (e.g. Islamic banking shares profits instead).
  • Also: how easy it is to get credit, and how much wealth people already have.
Quick check
?Which household is most likely to be borrowing rather than saving?
Mission check

?Interest rates fall from 5% to 2%. Which household is most likely to increase its spending in the next few months?

The same rate change helps some households and hurts others; who borrows and who saves decides which way spending moves.

Mission 3

Mission 3: What decides how much a job pays, and why can two jobs pay so differently?

Make a guess: Why are some jobs paid much more than others?

Mission 3

A market for workers

  • In the labour market, workers supply labour and employers demand it.
  • The wage is the price of labour.
  • Derived demand: firms want workers because people want the goods and services they make. More online shopping → more delivery drivers wanted.
  • The same is true for every factor of production: land, labour, capital and enterprise are demanded for what they help to produce.
  • Diagram: wage on the vertical axis, number of workers on the horizontal.
    • Demand for labour slopes down: at a lower wage, firms hire more.
    • Supply of labour slopes up: at a higher wage, more people want the job.
Mission 3

What changes the demand for labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the demand curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • demand for the product changes (a boom in home deliveries → more drivers wanted)
    • productivity changes (each worker produces more, so each is worth more to the firm)
    • the cost of machines that can replace workers changes (cheaper self-checkouts → fewer cashiers wanted)
    • the cost of other inputs changes
  • Say: "a higher wage causes a contraction in demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls". The curve has not moved.
Mission 3

What changes the supply of labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the supply curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • population grows or shrinks
    • migration: workers arrive from, or leave for, other areas or countries
    • age structure: more people of working age
    • retirement age or school-leaving age changes
    • female participation: more women in paid work (childcare, attitudes)
    • skills and qualifications: more people trained for a job
    • mobility: how easily people can change job or move
    • non-wage factors of a job: conditions, hours, holidays, training, status
Quick check
?Which of these would shift the supply curve of nurses to the right?
Mission 3

How the wage is set

  • Step 1: demand for labour D and supply of labour S.
  • Step 2: they cross at the equilibrium wage (We) and employment (Le).
  • Above We: more people want the job than firms want to hire (excess supply). The wage is pushed down.
  • Below We: firms cannot fill jobs (excess demand, a shortage). The wage is pushed up.
  • Step 3: a shift in D or S gives a new We and Le.
Mission 3

Worked example: shifts

  • Illustrative data. Warehouse workers in a town: We = £12 an hour, Le = 520 workers.
  • Demand rises (online orders boom): D shifts right.
    • New wage £14, new employment 640.
    • Wage ↑ and employment ↑.
  • Supply rises instead (100 more people want the work at each wage): S shifts right.
    • New wage £11, new employment 560.
    • Wage ↓ but employment ↑.

  • Two jobs pay differently when their demand and supply differ: strong demand for the workers, or few people able and willing to do the job, means a higher wage.

?Many people move into a town looking for warehouse work. Other things equal, what happens in the town's market for warehouse workers?

  • Occupational choice depends on wage and non-wage factors.

  • A job with poor non-wage factors (dangerous, night shifts) must usually pay more to attract workers.
Mission 3

Why pay differs

  • UK, April 2025: the median full-time employee earned £766.60 a week. A full-time worker on the National Living Wage (£12.21 an hour, 37.5 hours) earned about £458 (£457.88).
  • Skills and training: few people can do highly skilled jobs, so supply is low and pay high.
  • Productivity and demand: workers whose output is worth more to employers are paid more.
  • Non-wage factors: unpleasant or risky jobs pay more; attractive ones can pay less.
  • Sector: public sector pay is often set nationally, with more job security and pensions; private sector pay varies more.
  • Discrimination: some groups are paid less for the same work. UK gender pay gap, April 2024: 7.0% for full-time employees; 13.1% for all employees. Part of the gap reflects hours and occupation, not only discrimination.
  • Say: "pay depends on the supply of and demand for workers" — not: "pay depends on how useful the job is".
Quick check
?Brain surgeons are paid far more than care workers, although both jobs matter to society. Which is the best explanation?
Mission 3

Moving to where the jobs are

  • Occupational mobility: ease of moving between jobs. Blocked by lack of skills or qualifications.
  • Geographical mobility: ease of moving between places. Blocked by house prices and rents, family ties, moving costs, not knowing about jobs.
  • Immobility means vacancies in one job or area while people elsewhere are unemployed.
  • Policies: training and retraining, help with moving costs, more housing, better job information.
Quick check
?A miner loses his job when the mine closes. In the same town there are vacancies for software developers, but he does not have the skills. What does this show?
Mission check

?A country raises the age at which young people may leave education from 16 to 18. In the market for workers aged 16 to 17, what is the most likely effect?

Anything that changes how many people can or will do a job shifts supply, and moves pay and jobs in opposite ways.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can workers win higher pay without anyone losing their job?

Make a guess: A group of workers wins pay above the market wage. What happens to the number of jobs?

Mission 4

What trade unions do

  • A trade union: an organisation of workers that acts for its members.
  • Collective bargaining: the union negotiates with employers for all its members at once, not one by one.
  • Aims:
    • higher pay
    • better working conditions and health and safety
    • shorter hours, more holidays
    • job security and training
    • legal advice and support for members
  • If talks fail, unions may take industrial action: a strike (stop work), work to rule (do only the minimum the contract requires), an overtime ban.
Mission 4

Unions in numbers

  • UK, 2025: 6.6 million employees were union members.
  • Union density (members as a % of employees): 22.4%, about 1 employee in 5.
    • Public sector: 48.5%. Private sector: 12.1%.
  • Membership peaked at 13.2 million in 1979 (a different measure, from union records).
  • Members' average pay was 5.3% higher than non-members', before allowing for other differences (job, skills, sector).
  • Why fewer members? Fewer jobs in mining and factories, more small firms, part-time and self-employed work, and changes to union law.
Mission 4

What makes a union strong?

  • A union has more bargaining power when:
    • most workers in the job are members
    • strikers are hard to replace, by other workers or by machines
    • the work is essential and a strike quickly stops production
    • the employer is profitable and can afford a rise
    • demand for the product is strong, so higher costs can be passed on in prices
    • unemployment is low, so employers struggle to find other workers
    • the law and the public support it
Mission 4

A union and the wage

  • Illustrative data. A competitive labour market: We = £12 an hour, Le = 500 workers.
  • Step 2: the union wins a wage of £14, above We.
    • Firms hire fewer: a contraction of demand for labour to 400.
    • More people want the job: 600.
    • Excess supply of 200: some workers are left without these jobs.
    • Members in work are paid more; others lose out.
  • Step 3: instead, the union agrees changes that raise productivity. Each worker is worth more, so demand for labour shifts right.
    • New equilibrium: £14 and 600 workers. Pay and jobs rise.
  • The job loss in step 2 is smaller where demand for labour is inelastic: workers hard to replace, wages a small share of costs.
Quick check
?Use the market in the previous screen (We = £12, Le = 500; firms hire 450 workers at £13, and 550 people want the job). The union wins a wage of £13 instead of £14. How many fewer workers are employed than before?
Mission 4

The minimum wage

  • A minimum wage: the lowest hourly wage employers may legally pay.
  • It is a price floor in the labour market. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium wage.
  • UK: a National Minimum Wage since 1999. From 1 April 2026: £12.71 an hour (age 21 and over; called the National Living Wage), £10.85 for ages 18–20, £8.00 for ages 16–17.
  • Germany: a national minimum wage since 1 January 2015, starting at €8.50 an hour.
  • Reasons:
    • raise the pay of the lowest-paid and reduce poverty
    • stop employers exploiting workers with little bargaining power
    • make work pay more than not working
    • reduce inequality
Mission 4

The minimum wage diagram

  • Step 1: the market wage We = £12, employment Le = 500 (illustrative data, as an earlier screen).
  • Step 2: a minimum wage Wmin = £13, drawn as a horizontal line above We.
  • Step 3: at £13:
    • firms hire 450 (a contraction along the demand curve)
    • 550 people want work (an extension along the supply curve)
    • excess supply = 550 − 450 = 100: unemployment caused by the minimum wage
  • Those who keep their jobs gain; those who lose jobs or cannot find one lose.
  • Say: "a minimum wage below the equilibrium wage has no effect" — not: "it pulls the wage down to the minimum".
Mission 4Graph lab

Set the minimum wage

  • Takeaway: a floor below We does nothing; above We, fewer jobs, and more so when demand for labour is elastic.
Quick check
?Use the market in an earlier screen. At £14, firms want 400 workers and 600 people want work. The minimum wage is set at £14. How much unemployment does it create in this market?
Mission 4

Minimum wage: for, against, evidence

ForAgainst
Higher pay for the lowest-paidMay cut jobs or hours, especially where machines can replace workers
Less poverty among people in workHigher costs can mean higher prices
Workers may be more motivated; staff stay longerSmall firms may struggle to pay
Low-paid workers spend moreDoes not help households with no one in work
  • Evidence: when New Jersey (USA) raised its minimum wage in 1992, a study by Card and Krueger (1994) found no fall in fast-food jobs compared with nearby Pennsylvania. Other economists, using different data, found a small fall.
  • UK: the Low Pay Commission (2024) said it had not found strong evidence that the National Living Wage cut jobs or hours.
  • Effects depend on how high the minimum wage is set: evidence from moderate rises may not apply to big ones.
  • As a poverty policy, the minimum wage helps the working poor. It does not reach the unemployed, so governments also use benefits, progressive taxes, education and healthcare.
Mission check

?A country sets a minimum wage above the market wage for hotel workers. In the same year, tourism booms and hotels are full. A year later, hotel workers' pay is higher and no fewer of them are employed. Which is the best explanation?

A higher wage on its own costs jobs; a rise in the demand for labour can lift pay and jobs together.

Mission 5

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Workers: a dentist, an electrician, a web designer.
    • Firms: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Regions: a city known for finance, or for films.
    • Countries: one exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 5

Adam Smith's pin workshop

  • In 1776, Adam Smith described a small pin workshop in The Wealth of Nations.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 separate operations: one worker draws out the wire, another straightens it, a third cuts it, a fourth sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons:
    • Skill: repeating one task makes a worker faster and better at it.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machines: a small, simple task is easier to do with a machine, so machines get invented.
Mission 5

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity (output per worker) rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same pay, same hours: more output and a lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 5 workers, each paid 80 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 100 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 5 workers make 160 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 5

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower cost per unit, more output from the same workers. Lower costs can mean more profit.
  • Workers: quicker to train for one task; can become expert; may earn more as their productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices and more products, often of better quality.
  • The economy: more output from the same scarce resources.
Mission 5

The downsides

  • Boredom: doing the same task all day can lower motivation and quality, and raise absence and staff turnover.
  • Narrow skills: if a machine takes over the task, or demand changes, a worker may struggle to find another job.
  • Interdependence: each stage depends on the others. If one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Less variety: products made in large, standard batches can all look the same.
  • Smith himself warned that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can dull a worker's mind.
Quick check
?A car factory buys one key electronic part from a single supplier abroad. A fire stops that supplier's production, and within a week the car factory has to stop too. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show?
Mission 5

Regions and countries specialise too

  • Regions specialise where they have the right resources or skills.
  • Example: Stoke-on-Trent in England grew as "the Potteries", making pottery from local clay and coal.
  • Countries specialise in what they can produce well, then trade.
  • Example: copper is Chile's biggest export.
  • Benefits: more output in total, lower prices, a wider choice of imports.
  • Risks: if world demand for the product falls, the whole region or country suffers. Relying on one product is risky.
  • Free trade (no taxes or limits on imports and exports) lets countries specialise and swap. A later lesson looks at trade in depth.
Mission 5

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of most other things.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Barter: swapping goods directly, with no money.
  • Barter needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other has, at the same time.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange quicker and easier. Mission 1 looks at money in depth.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show?
Mission check

?A hospital laundry keeps the same 6 workers, the same hours and the same machines. Instead of each worker washing, drying and folding whole batches, each now does one stage only. Sheets cleaned per worker rise by a quarter. Which explanation fits?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Money is a medium of exchange: it ends the need for a double coincidence of wants.
  • It is also a unit of account, a store of value and a means (standard) of deferred payment; most money is bank deposits.
  • The central bank sets the main interest rate and keeps the financial system stable.
  • Commercial banks link savers and borrowers, and profit from the gap between interest rates.

Mission 2

  • An interest rate is the price of borrowing and the reward for saving, as a percentage per year.
  • Higher rates encourage saving and discourage borrowing, spending and investment; lower rates do the opposite.
  • Income, confidence, age and culture also shape spending, saving and borrowing.

Mission 3

  • The wage is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour.
  • Shifts in demand move wage and employment the same way; shifts in supply move them opposite ways.
  • Pay differs with skills, demand, non-wage factors, sector and discrimination.

Mission 4

  • Unions bargain collectively; a wage above equilibrium raises members' pay but can cut jobs.
  • Pay and jobs rise together when the demand for labour shifts right, for example through higher productivity.
  • A minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess supply of labour; the evidence on job losses is mixed.

Mission 5

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (skill, time saved, machines) and cut cost per unit.
  • Downsides: boredom, narrow skills, interdependence, less variety.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.
