Mission 1

What revelation means

  • Revelation: God (or ultimate reality) making known something humans could not find out on their own.
  • Believers say God is transcendent (beyond the world), so humans need God to take the first step.
  • Two kinds:
  • Special revelation: God revealed to particular people at particular times.
  • General revelation: God revealed to everyone, everywhere, through ordinary things.
Mission 1

Special revelation

  • Direct, to particular people or groups, often life-changing.
  • Prophets receiving God's word (Moses at Sinai; the Prophet Muhammad in the cave of Hira).
  • Visions and dreams: seeing or hearing something sacred (see Mission 4).
  • Miracles: events showing God acting in the world (see Mission 3).
  • In a person: Christians believe God is revealed most fully in Jesus.
Mission 1

General revelation

  • Open to anyone, at any time: no special event is needed.
  • Nature: the beauty, order and size of the universe point to its Creator.
  • Conscience and reason: an inner sense of right and wrong; the ability to think about God.
  • History and inspirational people: seeing God at work in events and in holy lives.
  • Most Christians say nature shows that God exists and is powerful, but God's love and purposes are known through special revelation.

Mission 1

Revelation in the Bible

  • Covenant: a binding agreement in which God reveals himself and makes promises.
  • Noah: never again a flood; the rainbow is "a sign of a covenant between me and the earth" (Genesis 9:13, WEBBE).
  • Abraham: God calls him to a new land and promises descendants (Genesis 12:1–3; 15).
  • Jesus: the "new covenant" (Luke 22:20).
  • What it shows: God is personal, faithful, keeps promises, acts in history.
Mission 1

The Qur'an as revelation

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is Allah's own word, revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibril over about 23 years, from 610 CE.
  • The first words: "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth" (Surah 96:1, Pickthall).
  • Allah speaks to humans only "by revelation or from behind a veil, or (that) He sendeth a messenger" (Surah 42:51, Pickthall).
Mission 1Diversity

Nature as general revelation: contrasting beliefs

  • Many Christians: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE). Nature shows God's power (Romans 1:20). Catholics teach God can be known through creation by reason (CCC 36).
  • Some Protestants: Karl Barth (1886–1968) taught that nature on its own gives no true knowledge of God; only God's revelation in Christ does.
  • Atheists and Humanists: nature shows natural processes, explained by science (evolution, physics).
  • Nature also contains cruelty and disaster; to many non-religious people it does not look like the work of a loving God.
  • Humanists still feel awe at nature, without seeing it as a message from God.
Mission 1

Value and problems

  • Value: revelation can give knowledge of God that reason cannot; it guides life; it can lead people to faith and change their lives.
  • Conflicting revelations: religions report different ideas of the divine (personal or impersonal; one God or many forms). They cannot all be literally true.
  • Alternative explanations: illness, drugs, tiredness, wishful thinking or upbringing.
  • Lying or mistaken: a person might invent a revelation, or honestly misunderstand an experience.
  • Responses: believers test claims against scripture, tradition and the person's life ("by their fruits"). Some, like John Hick (1922–2012), suggest different revelations show different sides of one ultimate reality.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Revelation is a reliable way of knowing about God."

  • Reasons for: if God exists and is personal, God could make himself known; many lives have been transformed; scripture and tradition test claims; general revelation is open to everyone.
  • Reasons against: revelations conflict; they cannot be tested by others; natural explanations are possible; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: revelation may be reliable for the person who receives it, but not proof for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Who can check a revelation? Do differences between religions undermine all revelation, or only some? Do changed lives count as evidence?

Mission 1Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs in contemporary British society about nature as general revelation.

  • Belief 1 (Christian): nature reveals God. Source: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). Development: the order and beauty of the universe show God's power and wisdom, so many Christians feel led to worship.
  • Belief 2 (Humanist): nature reveals nothing about God. Development: science explains the universe through natural processes, and suffering in nature does not fit a loving designer.

Mission 2

What is prayer?

  • Prayer: communicating with God. An old Christian definition calls it "the raising of the mind and heart to God" (St John Damascene, 8th century).
  • Christians pray in four main ways (ACTS): Adoration (praise), Confession (saying sorry), Thanksgiving, Supplication (asking, for yourself or others).
  • Set prayers, such as the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13), are shared words. Informal (extempore) prayer is in a person's own words.
  • Prayer can be alone or together, spoken, sung or silent.
  • For believers it is an experience of God: a relationship, not only a request list.
  • Your spec treats answered and unanswered prayer as possible examples of revelation: God making himself known.
Mission 2Read the source

Asking according to God's will

"This is the boldness which we have towards him, that if we ask anything according to his will, he listens to us. And if we know that he listens to us, whatever we ask, we know that we have the petitions which we have asked of him." (1 John 5:14–15, WEBBE)

  • The letter is written so that believers "may know that you have eternal life" (1 John 5:13, WEBBE).
  • Verses 16–17 (paraphrase): if you see a fellow believer committing a sin "not leading to death", pray for them, and God will give them life.

Show answer

Asking "according to his will".

Q2 Why might a Christian say verse 16 shows prayer is for others, not only for yourself?

Show answer

Mission 2

When prayer is answered

  • Jesus: "Ask, and it will be given you. Seek, and you will find." (Matthew 7:7, WEBBE)
  • Some prayers seem answered in the way the person expects: a recovery, a safe journey, a decision made clear.
  • To the believer this can feel like God acting personally: it strengthens faith, or begins it.
  • Others see God's answer in peace or strength to cope, even when events do not change.
  • Answered prayer counts as special revelation for the believer: God shown in a particular event.

Mission 2

When prayer seems unanswered

  • Many Christians say God answers every prayer: yes, no or not yet.
  • Jesus in Gethsemane: "Please remove this cup from me. However, not what I desire, but what you desire." (Mark 14:36, WEBBE) The cup was not removed.
  • Paul asked three times to be freed from a "thorn in the flesh"; the answer: "My grace is sufficient for you" (2 Corinthians 12:9, WEBBE).
  • Jesus teaches that God, like a good parent, gives "good things to those who ask him" (Matthew 7:11, WEBBE): not always the thing asked for.
  • When people do not know how to pray, "the Spirit himself makes intercession for us" (Romans 8:26, WEBBE).
Mission 2Diversity

Christians differ on what prayer does

  • Catholic and Orthodox: prayer is above all communion with God; saints are asked to pray for us; some healings are investigated and recognised (e.g. Lourdes).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic: expect God to heal and act today in answer to prayer (James 5:16).
  • Reformed (Calvinist): prayer does not change God's plan; God uses prayer as one of the ways his plan is carried out.
  • Liberal Protestant: prayer mainly changes the one who prays: their attitude, courage and love.
  • All agree prayer is not a slot machine: its heart is the relationship with God.
Mission 2

The atheist and Humanist challenge

  • Coincidence: with millions of prayers, some will match what happens by chance.
  • Confirmation bias: people remember the "hits" and forget the "misses".
  • Natural causes: many illnesses improve anyway; hope and calm can help recovery.
  • Tests: a large 2006 US study of prayer for heart-surgery patients found no benefit from being prayed for.
  • Unfairness: why would a loving God answer one person's prayer for a child's recovery and not another's? For some, unanswered prayer leads to loss of belief.
  • Humanists: quiet reflection and support from others can help, for natural reasons.

Mission 2

Christian responses to the challenge

  • Not a test of God: Jesus refuses to put God to the test (Matthew 4:7); many Christians say an experiment cannot measure a relationship.
  • "No" is an answer: Gethsemane and Paul's thorn show faithful prayer that was not granted.
  • The answer may be strength: comfort, patience and courage are real changes.
  • Testimony: many believers report answers too specific to be chance.
  • Faith comes first: most Christians do not base belief on results; prayer is part of trusting God.
Mission 2

Hindu prayer

  • Hindus pray through puja (worship of the deity in the murti), japa (repeating a divine name) and mantras.
  • The Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10), recited at dawn and dusk (paraphrase): "We meditate on the glorious light of the divine Savitr; may it inspire our minds."
  • Many Hindus see it as the greatest mantra: it asks not for things but for a mind awakened to the divine.
  • Answered prayer can deepen bhakti (loving devotion) to a chosen deity (ishta-devata) and lead to belief.
  • Unanswered prayer: many Hindus say outcomes follow karma; God gives what is truly good; the Gita teaches acting without clinging to results (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, paraphrase).
Mission 2

Buddhist "prayers"

  • Most Buddhists do not pray to a creator God. "Prayer" means chanting, recitation and aspiration.
  • Om mani padme hum: the mantra of Avalokitesvara, the bodhisattva of compassion; recited and spun on prayer wheels, especially in Tibet.
  • Reasons Buddhists give: to develop metta (loving-kindness), to share merit with others (including the dead), to open the mind to awareness and calm.
  • Theravada: chanting recalls the Buddha's qualities; no one outside you can do the work (Dhammapada 165, paraphrase).
  • Mahayana: devotion to bodhisattvas; Pure Land Buddhists call on Amida Buddha ("Namu Amida Butsu") trusting his help.
  • Divergent: some Buddhists see such prayer as vital; others as a help for beginners, less important than meditation.
Quick check
?A Theravada Buddhist chants every morning: "May all beings be happy and free from suffering." What is the main purpose of this chant?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Answered prayer proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: many detailed reports of answers; changed lives; prayer is experienced as a real relationship; 1 John 5:14–15 promises God listens.
  • Reasons against: coincidence and confirmation bias; natural recovery; tests show no effect; many prayers seem to go unanswered.
  • Another view: answered prayer may give the believer good reason to trust God, without proving anything to others.
  • Other traditions: for many Hindus and Buddhists the value of prayer lies in the change in the mind, not in proof.
  • Judge it by: Can a "no" ever count against God? Is personal experience enough? What would count as proof?
Mission check

The same event can be read as God's answer or as chance. For believers, prayer is a relationship with God, so a "no" is not the end of it.

Mission 3

What is a miracle?

  • A miracle: an extraordinary event that seems to break the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God.
  • David Hume (1711–1776): "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature" (An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, 1748, Section X).
  • Believers add that a miracle has meaning: it shows something about God or answers prayer.
  • Some believers also call a striking, well-timed coincidence a miracle, if they see God at work in it.
  • Miracles are a form of special revelation (see Mission 1): God acting in the world.
Mission 3Read the source

The official's son

John 4:43–54: at Cana, a royal official begs Jesus to come and heal his dying son in Capernaum.

  • "Jesus said to him, 'Go your way. Your son lives.' The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him, and he went his way." (John 4:50, WEBBE)
  • On the way his servants meet him: the fever left the boy at the very hour Jesus spoke.
  • "He believed, as did his whole house." (John 4:53, WEBBE)
  • John calls this "the second sign" (John 4:54): miracles are signs pointing to who Jesus is.

Show answer

Mission 3

Lourdes

  • In 1858, Bernadette Soubirous (1844–1879), aged 14, reported 18 appearances of the Virgin Mary at a grotto in Lourdes, France. A spring appeared there.
  • Millions of pilgrims now visit each year; some report healings.
  • The Lourdes Medical Bureau (from 1883) examines claimed cures. A cure must be of a serious illness, sudden, complete and lasting, with no medical explanation.
  • Only then can the local bishop declare it a miracle. Very few cures pass.
  • One example: Sister Bernadette Moriau, a French nun disabled for decades, recovered suddenly in 2008 after a pilgrimage; her bishop declared it a miracle in 2018.
Mission 3

The Qur'an: the greatest miracle

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an itself is the greatest miracle: the Prophet could not read or write, yet brought words no one could match.
  • "Say: Verily, though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like thereof" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall).
  • Other miracles: the Night Journey (Surah 17:1); the splitting of the moon (Surah 54:1); miracles of earlier prophets, such as Isa healing the sick by Allah's leave.

Mission 3

The argument from miracles

  • 1. Miracles happen. 2. Only God could cause them. 3. So God exists.
  • Strengths: eyewitness accounts across centuries; cures examined by doctors (Lourdes); lives changed; miracles fit a God who cares and acts.
  • Christians also see miracles as signs of who Jesus is (John 4:54) and of God's love.
  • Weaknesses: every step can be challenged.
Mission 3

Scientific and Humanist objections

  • Hume (Enquiry, 1748, Section X): our experience that the laws of nature hold is very strong; a report of a miracle is weaker. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."
  • Hume adds: few reliable witnesses; people love wonder stories; and miracles from rival religions cancel each other out.
  • Science: cures can come from spontaneous remission, the placebo effect, wrong diagnosis or coincidence.
  • Unexplained is not the same as explained by God: science may explain it later.
  • Unfairness: why would God heal one person and not millions of others? (Maurice Wiles, 1923–2005, God's Action in the World, 1986.)
  • Humanists: wonder at nature, but trust evidence; no miracle has passed a controlled test.
Mission 3

Religious responses

  • Careful testing: Lourdes doctors (believers and non-believers) reject most claims. The Church does not call every recovery a miracle.
  • Laws describe, God acts: laws of nature describe what normally happens; the God who made them can act in new ways.
  • Signs, not shows: miracles are rare by purpose, pointing to God, not solving every problem.
Quick check
?Which of these is one of David Hume's reasons for not believing reports of miracles?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Miracles prove that God exists."

  • Reasons for: examined cures with no medical explanation (Lourdes); eyewitness accounts (John 4); changed lives.
  • Reasons against: Hume: testimony is weaker than experience of nature's laws; spontaneous remission and placebo; rival miracle claims; why heal some and not others?
  • Another view: a miracle may strengthen faith for those who already believe, without proving God to sceptics.
  • Judge it by: Is "no explanation yet" good evidence? Can testimony ever outweigh experience? Why are miracles rare?
Mission 3Worked answer

Different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs in contemporary British society about miracles.

  • Belief 1 (Christian): God can and does perform miracles. Source: Jesus healed the official's son by his word (John 4:50). Development: so many Christians pray for healing and accept investigated cures, as at Lourdes.
  • Belief 2 (Humanist): miracles do not happen. Development: recoveries have natural causes such as spontaneous remission or placebo, and Hume argued testimony is weaker than our experience of nature's laws.

Mission 4

What is a vision?

  • A vision: seeing or hearing something sacred that others normally cannot: a holy figure, a light, a voice, a scene.
  • Some are seen with the eyes, some in the mind, some in dreams.
  • Visions are a form of special revelation (see Mission 1): given to particular people, often with a message.
  • Why might they lead to belief in God? They feel real and personal, can carry knowledge the person did not have, and often change lives.
Mission 4Read the source

Abram's vision

"After these things the LORD's word came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'" (Genesis 15:1, WEBBE)

  • God takes Abram outside: "Look now towards the sky, and count the stars" (Genesis 15:5, WEBBE). His descendants will be as many.
  • "He believed in the LORD, who credited it to him for righteousness." (Genesis 15:6, WEBBE)
  • God then makes a covenant with Abram, promising the land (Genesis 15:18).

Show answer

Mission 4

The Transfiguration

  • Matthew 17:1–13: Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain.
  • "His face shone like the sun, and his garments became as white as the light." (Matthew 17:2, WEBBE)
  • Moses and Elijah appear, talking with Jesus.
  • A voice from a bright cloud: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him." (Matthew 17:5, WEBBE)
  • The disciples fall down in fear; Jesus tells them not to tell anyone until he is raised from the dead.
  • Significance for Christians: it shows Jesus as the Son of God; Moses (the Law) and Elijah (the Prophets) are seen as pointing to him. Three people saw it together.
Mission 4

Joan of Arc

  • Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431), a French peasant girl.
  • From about age 13 she heard voices and saw visions of St Michael, St Catherine and St Margaret.
  • They told her to help Charles VII drive the English from France. In 1429 she led French forces to lift the siege of Orléans.
  • Captured, she was tried by a pro-English Church court and burned in Rouen in 1431.
  • A retrial declared her innocent (1456); the Catholic Church canonised her (made her a saint) in 1920.
  • For Catholics, her courage and the results of her mission are signs that the visions were genuine.

Mission 4

"Just hallucinations"?

  • Atheists and Humanists argue visions give no proof of God.
  • Hallucinations: the brain can produce sights and voices through illness, fever, drugs, lack of sleep or fasting. Some writers suggest Joan of Arc had a neurological illness (a guess; no medical records exist).
  • Wish-fulfilment: people may see what they long for (Sigmund Freud, The Future of an Illusion, 1927).
  • Expectation: visions usually fit the person's own religion (Catholics see Mary; Hindus see Krishna).
  • Lack of evidence: no one else can check a private vision. The person might also be lying or mistaken.
Mission 4

Religious responses

  • Shared visions: at the Transfiguration three disciples saw the same thing; hallucinations are usually private.
  • Fruits: a genuine vision leads to good, lasting change (Joan's courage; Abram's faith).
  • Tested by tradition: the Catholic Church investigates claims carefully and approves few. A vision must agree with scripture and Church teaching.
  • God can use natural means: even if the brain is involved, God may be at work through it.
Quick check
?Which point would a Christian most likely use to argue that the Transfiguration was not a hallucination?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Visions prove that God exists."

  • Reasons for: visions feel real and personal; some are shared (Matthew 17); they change lives (Joan of Arc); some bring messages the person could not have known.
  • Reasons against: hallucination, wish-fulfilment and expectation explain them; they cannot be checked; visions conflict between religions; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: a vision may be strong evidence for the person who has it, but weak evidence for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Does a natural cause rule out God? Do shared visions matter? Should the fruits of a vision count as evidence?
Mission check

A vision is hard to test from outside. Believers look at its message, the person and its fruits; critics look for natural causes.

Mission 5

Two shapes, and the question of the soul

  • Afterlife: existence of some kind after death.
  • Linear view (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life → death → judgement → eternal destiny.
  • Cyclical view (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): birth → death → rebirth, again and again (samsara), shaped by karma (actions and their results), until liberation.
  • Soul: the non-physical, spiritual part of a person; for many believers, the real self that survives death.
Mission 5

Christian beliefs: judgement, heaven and hell

  • Death is not the end: Christians believe the risen Jesus has defeated death.
  • Judgement: God judges each person, through Christ; faith and how people lived both matter (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Heaven: eternal life with God. Many Christians, including Catholic teaching, see it as a state of being with God more than a place in the sky.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God. Catholic teaching describes it as a person's own final rejection of God.
  • Purgatory (Catholic): a state of cleansing after death for those who die in God's friendship but are not yet fully pure; the living pray for them. Most Protestants reject it: they find no clear basis for it in the Bible.
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead too, but do not use the Catholic doctrine of purgatory.
  • At the end of time Christ will return, the dead will be raised, and there will be a Last Judgement.

Mission 5

Resurrection of the body or survival of the soul?

  • Survival of the soul (popular belief, and the Greek idea of immortality): the soul floats free of the body for ever; the body was only a shell.
  • Resurrection of the body (Catholic and mainstream Christian teaching): the whole person is raised, body and soul, by God.
  • Catholic teaching: at death the soul is separated from the body and meets God; at the end of time it is reunited with a transformed, glorified body.
  • Not resuscitation: Lazarus (John 11) came back to ordinary life and later died again; resurrection means a new, deathless life.
  • Some liberal Christians see resurrection as spiritual only.

Mission 5

Islam: akhirah

  • Akhirah: life after death, one of the key Muslim beliefs (Sunni and Shi'a alike). This life is a test.
  • At death the soul is taken by the angel of death; it waits in barzakh (a barrier) until the Day of Resurrection (Qur'an 23:100).
  • Day of Judgement: all are raised; each person's deeds are weighed (Qur'an 101:6–9).
  • Jannah (paradise) for the faithful who did good; Jahannam (hell) for those who rejected God and did evil. God is merciful and just.
  • Muslims differ on whether descriptions of paradise and hell are literal or point to realities beyond words.
Mission 5

Judaism: this world and the world to come

  • The Tenakh says little about the afterlife; Judaism focuses on living this life by the mitzvot.
  • Later books and the rabbis teach resurrection: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917)
  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come": the age after the Messiah, or the life of the soul after death.
  • Orthodox Jews affirm bodily resurrection. Many Reform Jews speak instead of the soul's survival, or of living on through deeds and memory.
Mission 5

Hinduism: atman, samsara, moksha

  • Atman: the eternal self or soul in every living being. It is never born and never dies.
  • At death the atman passes into a new body: reincarnation (transmigration).
  • "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones … / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Bhagavad Gita 2:22, Arnold 1885).
  • Karma decides the next birth. Samsara is the cycle; moksha is release from it.
  • Advaita Hindus: in moksha the atman realises it is one with Brahman. Many Vaishnava Hindus: the soul lives for ever in loving devotion to God (Vishnu or Krishna).
Mission 5

Sikhism: the soul's journey to mukti

  • The soul (atma) is a spark of God. It passes through many life forms (transmigration, samsara), shaped by karma.
  • A human birth is rare and precious: the chance to become close to God.
  • Mukti: liberation from rebirth; the soul merges with Waheguru. It comes through God's grace (nadar), remembering God's name (naam simran), service (sewa) and living as a gurmukh (God-centred), not manmukh (self-centred).
  • Many Sikhs see heaven and hell as states of being close to or far from God, more than places.
Mission 5

Buddhism: rebirth without a fixed soul

  • Anatta: there is no permanent, unchanging self. A person is five ever-changing skandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness).
  • Everything is impermanent (anicca), including "me".
  • So rebirth, not reincarnation: no soul moves on, but a stream of kamma-energy continues, like a flame lit from another flame.
  • Rebirth may be in one of six realms: gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. None is permanent.
  • Nibbana (Sanskrit nirvana) ends rebirth: the fires of greed, hatred and ignorance go out.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Belief in life after death makes this life more valuable."

  • Reasons for: life is a test or preparation; how we live is judged (Matthew 25; Qur'an 99:7–8: "whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then"). Karma means every act counts. Human life is rare and precious (Sikh, Buddhist, Hindu). Hope gives comfort and courage to care for the dying.
  • Reasons against: focus on the next life may lessen effort in this one. Humanists: because this is our only life, it is all the more precious; people live on through memory and good done.
  • How belief shapes living: prayer, charity, avoiding sin, cremation or burial customs, care for the dying.
  • Judge it by: Does hope of reward make good acts less generous? Which view better motivates care for others? Can value come without an afterlife?

Mission 6

What could count as evidence?

  • Nobody can return from a full death and be tested, so people argue from other kinds of evidence:
  • Experience: near-death experiences; remembered lives; the paranormal (contact with the dead through mediums, ghosts).
  • Logic: reasoning that a soul or a just God makes an afterlife likely.
  • Reward: justice seems to need a life where good and evil are finally put right.
  • Comfort and meeting loved ones: hope that death is not the end of love.
  • Revelation: what scripture and religious teachers say.
  • Each one can be questioned. That is this mission.
Mission 6

Near-death experiences

  • Near-death experience (NDE): what some people report after coming close to death, e.g. in cardiac arrest.
  • Common features: leaving the body and seeing it from above; a tunnel; a bright, loving light; meeting dead relatives; a review of one's life; a sense of being sent back.
  • Made widely known by Raymond Moody, Life After Life (1975).
  • Real? Reports are similar across cultures; some people describe events in the room they could not have seen.
  • Illusory? The dying brain short of oxygen, drugs, or dream-like states (REM intrusion) can produce similar effects; the person did not actually die; reports are made afterwards and memory changes.
  • Believers usually treat NDEs as support for faith, not the basis of it.
Mission 6

Remembered lives and the paranormal

  • Remembered lives: some young children describe a previous life: names, places, the way they died.
  • Ian Stevenson (1918–2007), psychiatrist, Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966), collected hundreds of such cases, many in India, Sri Lanka, Lebanon and Turkey.
  • Critics: cases come mainly where rebirth is already believed; families may shape the child's story; checks were made after families had met.
  • Paranormal: mediums claim to pass messages from the dead; some people report ghosts.
  • Critics: many mediums have been exposed as frauds; "cold reading" (clever guessing from clues) explains many messages.
  • Christians, Muslims and Jews mostly reject contacting the dead: the Torah forbids anyone "who consults with a familiar spirit, or a wizard, or a necromancer" (Deuteronomy 18:11).
  • Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs often accept remembered lives as fitting rebirth; Christians and Muslims usually explain them another way.

Mission 6

Logic, reward, comfort: how believers use them

  • Logic: Christians and Muslims: God who made life the first time can raise the dead (Qur'an 36:79). Hindus and Sikhs: differences at birth (talent, suffering) make sense if karma carries over from past lives.
  • Reward: a good and just God will reward goodness and right injustice, which often goes unpunished here.
  • Comfort / loved ones: belief helps mourners; Christians "don't grieve like the rest, who have no hope" (1 Thessalonians 4:13).
Mission 6

The case against

  • Lack of evidence: no claim has been confirmed under controlled tests.
  • Mind depends on the brain: brain damage changes memory and personality; with no working brain, there is nothing left to experience (physicalism).
  • Fraud: fake mediums and invented stories damage trust in all such reports.
  • Comfort: belief may be wishful thinking: people believe because they fear death or loss.
  • Social control: promises of heaven and threats of hell can keep people obedient. Karl Marx (1818–1883) called religion "the opium of the people" (1844): it soothes the poor instead of changing their situation.
  • Non-religious views of the soul: Humanists and most atheists see the "self" as the activity of the brain; it ends at death. People live on only in memories, children and the good they did.
Mission 6

Is death the end of the person?

  • Yes: Epicurus (341–270 BCE): death is nothing to us, because when death is, we are not (paraphrase). So there is nothing to fear.
  • Humanists: this is our one life; make it good for ourselves and others.
  • No (Catholic): death ends earthly life, but not the person. The soul lives on and meets God; the whole person will be raised (1 Corinthians 15).
  • Most religions agree: death ends this body; they differ on what, if anything, continues.

Mission 6Diversity

Replying to the objections

  • Lack of evidence: religious believers say the afterlife is known by revelation and faith, not lab tests; absence of proof is not proof of absence.
  • Christians point to the resurrection of Jesus and its witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:6).
  • Mind and brain: dualists say the brain may be how the soul acts in the body, as a radio receives a signal; damage the radio and the signal seems to change.
  • Comfort: something can be comforting and true. Many teachings (judgement, hell, karma) are not comforting at all.
  • Fraud: fake mediums do not show that every claim is false; most religions reject mediums anyway.
  • Social control: early Christians, the first Muslims in Makkah and early Sikhs were persecuted minorities, not rulers; believers point to prophets and Gurus who challenged the powerful.

Mission 6Evaluate

"There is no good evidence for life after death."

  • Reasons for: no claim survives controlled testing; NDEs have brain-based explanations; remembered-life cases may be shaped by culture; fraud is common; mind depends on brain.
  • Reasons against: NDE reports are consistent and some are hard to explain; Stevenson's best cases remain puzzling; billions find revelation and religious experience convincing; Christians point to eyewitnesses of the risen Jesus.
  • Another view: evidence may not be the right test: belief in an afterlife is a matter of faith or trust, like trusting a person.
  • Judge it by: What should count as "good" evidence for something beyond death? Is personal testimony enough? Does lack of proof show there is nothing?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Revelation is God making himself known: special (prophets, visions, miracles, Jesus, scripture received) and general (nature, conscience, reason, scripture read by all).
  • Christians see Jesus as the culmination of revelation (Hebrews 1:1–4); Muslims see the Qur'an as Allah's word revealed through Jibril.
  • Problems: conflicting revelations, alternative explanations, lying or mistake; believers reply with tests of scripture, tradition and fruits.

Mission 2

  • Prayer is communication with God (adoration, confession, thanksgiving, supplication); set and informal; for believers an experience of God.
  • Answered prayer can lead to belief; 1 John 5:14–15 ties it to God's will. Unanswered prayer is met with Gethsemane, Paul's thorn and "yes, no, not yet"; critics point to chance, bias and failed tests.
  • Hindus pray through puja, japa and the Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10); Buddhists chant (Om mani padme hum) to grow metta, share merit and open the mind.

Mission 3

  • A miracle is an extraordinary event, beyond the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God and to carry meaning: John 4:43–54; Lourdes (Bernadette Moriau).
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as the greatest miracle (Surah 17:88).
  • The argument from miracles faces Hume's challenge, scientific explanations and the "why some?" problem; believers reply with careful testing and the meaning of signs.

Mission 4

  • A vision is a form of special revelation: seeing or hearing something sacred. Examples: Abram (Genesis 15), the Transfiguration (Matthew 17), Joan of Arc.
  • Atheists and Humanists explain visions as hallucinations, wish-fulfilment or expectation; believers point to shared visions, fruits and testing by tradition.

Mission 5

  • Linear views (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life, judgement, heaven or hell; resurrection. Cyclical views (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): karma, rebirth, liberation (moksha, nibbana, mukti).
  • Catholics believe in the resurrection of the body, not just survival of the soul (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

Mission 6

  • Arguments for: NDEs, remembered lives, the paranormal, logic, reward, comfort, meeting loved ones, revelation.
  • Objections: lack of evidence, mind depends on brain, fraud, wishful thinking, social control (Marx).
  • Religious replies: revelation and faith; Jesus' resurrection; comfort can still be true; the Gita: the unenlightened do not see the soul.
