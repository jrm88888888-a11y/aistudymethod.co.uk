Mission 1: If you drained the oceans, what landscape would you see on the sea floor?

Make a guess: If you drained the oceans, what landscape would you see on the sea floor?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.3-1.a global distribution, area and volume of the oceans

Mission 1

One ocean, five names

  • There is "only one global ocean"; it covers 71% of Earth's surface.
  • Its named parts: Pacific, Atlantic, Indian, Arctic and, now widely recognised, the Southern Ocean.
  • The Southern Ocean runs from the coast of Antarctica to 60° S.
  • Boundaries between named oceans are lines drawn by people; the water is continuous.
Mission 1

How big is it?

  • Area: about 361.8 million km².
  • Volume: about 1.33 billion km³.
  • Mean depth: about 3,682 m.
  • It holds about 97% of Earth's water.
Quick check
?Ocean volume is 1.332 billion km³ and ocean area is 361.8 million km². What is the mean depth?
Mission 1

Uneven distribution

  • Ocean is not spread evenly between hemispheres: the Southern Hemisphere "may be called the water hemisphere", the Northern "the land hemisphere".
  • Depth is uneven too: shallow seas fringe the continents; most water lies in deep basins.
  • The deep floor (abyssal plains) covers about 70% of the ocean floor.
  • So "distribution" means where the ocean is and how deep it is.
Mission 1

The continental margin

  • Continental shelf: the gently sloping, submerged edge of a continent, "usually less than a few hundred feet deep".
  • Its width varies: "narrow or nearly nonexistent in some places"; elsewhere it extends "for hundreds of miles".
  • Continental slope: the steep drop beyond the shelf edge, from about 90 m to 3,000 m (300–10,000 ft).
  • Shelf and slope are still continental crust; the deep floor beyond is oceanic crust.
Mission 1

The abyssal plain

  • Below about 3,000 m (10,000 ft) lie the abyssal plains.
  • They cover about 70% of the ocean floor, the largest habitat on Earth.
  • They are very flat: fine sediment settles slowly over the uneven volcanic crust and buries it.
  • Rising from them: seamounts, underwater mountains with at least 1,000 m of relief.
Quick check
?Which sequence would a submarine cross, moving from the coast out to the deep ocean floor?
Mission 1

Ridges and rifts

  • The mid-ocean ridge is "the longest mountain range on Earth", about 65,000 km long.
  • It forms where plates move apart and magma rises to fill the gap.
  • Its crest lies at an average depth of about 2,500 m (8,200 ft).
  • On slow-spreading ridges (for example the Mid-Atlantic Ridge, about 2.5 cm a year) a rift valley runs along the crest: typically 1.4 km deep and 20–40 km wide.
  • Fast-spreading ridges "lack rift valleys".
Mission 1

Trenches

  • Trenches are "the deepest parts of the ocean floor and are created by subduction".
  • Where two oceanic plates meet, one sinks under the other and a trench forms.
  • Example: the Mariana Trench, western Pacific; its Challenger Deep is about 10,935 m deep.
  • Ocean–continent example: the Peru–Chile Trench, where the Nazca Plate sinks under South America.
Mission 1

Guyots: flat-topped seamounts

  • A guyot is an isolated volcanic mountain with a flat summit more than 200 m below sea level.
  • 1: It forms as a volcanic island near a ridge crest. 2: Waves erode its top flat at sea level.
  • 3: As the plate moves away from the ridge, the sea floor subsides, carrying the flat top deep under water.
  • In the Pacific, where guyots are most common, most summits lie about 1,000–2,000 m down.
Quick check
?Why is a guyot's summit flat but far below the reach of waves today?
Mission check

?A survey line runs from a coast across 300 km of water less than 150 m deep, down a steep drop to a wide flat floor at 4,500 m, then up to a range of peaks with a deep notch along its crest. Which features has it crossed, in order?

Ocean basins share one pattern: shelf → slope → abyssal plain, broken by ridges (with rifts where spreading is slow), trenches at subduction zones, and seamounts and guyots rising from the plain.

Mission 2

Surface currents and gyres

  • Wind, tides and differences in temperature and salinity drive ocean currents.
  • The Coriolis effect deflects moving water to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern.
  • Surface currents form gyres: five major ones, the North and South Pacific, North and South Atlantic, and Indian Ocean subtropical gyres.
Mission 2

Warm and cold currents

  • Western sides of ocean basins: warm currents flow poleward (Gulf Stream, Kuroshio, Brazil, East Australian).
  • Eastern sides: cold currents flow towards the equator (California, Canary, Humboldt, Benguela).
  • Warm currents warm and moisten the air above them; cold currents cool it, often bringing fog and dry coasts.
  • Polar water flows south as cold currents too, e.g. the Labrador Current.
Quick check
?A current flows towards the equator along the west coast of southern Africa. What is it most likely to be?
Mission 2

Salinity across the oceans

  • Average salinity is about 35 parts per thousand.
  • Salinity varies with temperature, evaporation and precipitation.
  • "Generally low at the equator and at the poles, and high at mid-latitudes": subtropical high pressure brings dry air and strong evaporation; the equator gets heavy rain; polar seas get meltwater.
  • River mouths and enclosed seas differ from the open ocean.
Mission 2

Temperature across the oceans and with depth

  • Horizontally, sea-surface temperature is highest in the tropics and falls towards the poles, following incoming sunlight; warm currents push warm water poleward along the western sides of basins.
  • A warm, wind-mixed surface layer sits above the thermocline, "the transition layer between warmer mixed water at the ocean's surface and cooler deep water below".
  • The thermocline is "semi-permanent in the tropics, variable in temperate regions … and shallow to nonexistent in the polar regions".
  • Deep water is cold and nearly uniform in temperature.
  • A matching sharp change in salinity with depth is a halocline.
Quick check
?Why is surface salinity usually higher near 25°N than at the equator?
Mission 2

The North Atlantic: surface currents

  • The Gulf Stream flows north-east from the Gulf of Mexico. NOAA: "the main conveyor of heat from south to north in the Atlantic".
  • It carries more water than all the world's rivers combined.
  • Its extension, the North Atlantic Drift, carries warm water towards north-west Europe.
  • The Canary Current returns water south along north-west Africa; the cold Labrador Current flows south past Canada.
Mission 2

The North Atlantic: deep currents

  • Below the surface, thermohaline circulation is driven by density, set by temperature and salinity.
  • When sea ice forms, the remaining seawater gets saltier and denser, and sinks. NOAA says the global ocean conveyor belt gets its "start" in the Norwegian Sea.
  • The Atlantic part is the AMOC (Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation): warm water north near the surface, cold, dense water south at depth.
  • At 26.5°N, over its first year of measurement, it averaged 18.7 Sv and ranged from 4.0 to 34.9 Sv (1 Sv = 1 million m³ per second).
Mission 2

Does the Gulf Stream warm Europe?

  • Seager et al. (2002) modelled why north-west Europe's winters are milder than eastern North America's at the same latitude.
  • Ocean heat transport warms winters on both sides of the North Atlantic "by a few degC", in an essentially uniform way.
  • The contrast between the two sides is mainly due to "advection by the mean winds": westerly winds bring mild ocean air to Europe and cold continental air to eastern Canada.
  • Exam move: one model study is evidence, not proof. Say what it found, then weigh it.
Mission 2

A changing conveyor

  • IPCC (2021): the AMOC "is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios".
  • It has "medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100".
Mission check

?A newspaper claims: "Without the Gulf Stream, Britain's winters would be as harsh as Labrador's." What does the evidence on these screens suggest?

Partly. The Gulf Stream and the rest of the ocean's heat transport warm winters on both sides of the North Atlantic by a few °C. Seager et al. (2002) found that the prevailing westerlies, bringing mild maritime air to Europe, explain most of its advantage.

Mission 3

Light fades fast

  • Euphotic (sunlight) zone: the upper 200 m.
  • Below 200 m so little light reaches that "photosynthesis is no longer possible".
  • Twilight (dysphotic) zone: 200–1,000 m; aphotic zone below 1,000 m: no sunlight.
  • So primary producers (phytoplankton, seaweed) are confined to a thin top layer.
Mission 3

Temperature with depth

  • Wind and waves mix the surface layer and spread its warmth.
  • Below it the thermocline: temperature falls quickly with depth.
  • Deep water is cold and steady: below about 1,000 m to about 4,000 m temperature "remains constant"; deeper still it is near freezing.
  • A strong thermocline acts as a lid: warm, light surface water does not mix easily with the cold, dense water below.
Mission 3

Nutrients: the hidden limit

  • Producers need nutrients (such as nitrate and phosphate) as well as light.
  • Dead organisms sink, so nutrients build up in deep water, below the light.
  • Upwelling brings "cold, nutrient-rich water" to the surface, acting like fertiliser.
  • "Good fishing grounds typically are found where upwelling is common".
Quick check
?Why is surface water above a strong thermocline often low in nutrients?
Mission 3

Latitude: three ocean zones

  • Tropics: the thermocline is "semi-permanent", so mixing is weak; much open tropical water has low chlorophyll.
  • Equator and west coasts: upwelling makes a "band of cool, plant-rich waters" at the Equator, and high productivity off western North and South America and southern Africa.
  • Polar seas: the thermocline is "shallow to nonexistent"; nutrients build up in the dark winter, then phytoplankton "flourish" when light returns in spring and summer.
  • Temperate seas: the thermocline is "variable", often deepest in summer.
Quick check
?NASA satellite maps show very high chlorophyll in polar seas in summer but not in winter. Which factor limits polar productivity in winter?
Mission 3

The inter-tidal zone

  • The inter-tidal zone lies between high and low tide.
  • Life faces drying out, changing temperature and wave energy with every tide.
  • Organisms sort into bands by height: high (barnacles, limpets, mussels), middle (sea stars, anemones), low, where life is "more abundant" because water protects it.
  • Light, nutrients and oxygen are plentiful, so producers (seaweeds, microscopic algae) grow on site.
Mission 3

An inter-tidal food web

  • Producers: seaweed and algae.
  • Grazers and filter feeders: limpets and snails graze; barnacles and mussels filter plankton.
  • Predators: sea stars, crabs; at high tide fish and seals; at low tide shorebirds.
  • Decomposers: seaweed washed ashore "decomposes and recycles nutrients".
  • Many links and many species in a small area: a complex web with high biodiversity.
Mission 3

The deep water: food from above

  • The deep floor is mostly abyssal plain, about 70% of the ocean floor.
  • No light, so no photosynthesis; much of the deep floor is "a frigid environment with few life forms".
  • Food arrives as marine snow: "a shower of organic material falling from upper waters".
  • Deep animals "filter marine snow from the water or scavenge it from the seabed": a food chain that depends on the surface.
Mission 3

Vents: food from chemicals

  • In 1977, scientists on a spreading ridge near the Galápagos found rich life around hydrothermal vents.
  • Microbes "feed on the chemicals spewing from the vent, converting the chemicals into energy" (chemosynthesis).
  • "Microbes form the base of complex deep-ocean food webs".
  • Tubeworms have no mouth or gut; microbes inside them share the energy.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares the two ecosystems?
Mission 3

Comparing biodiversity

  • Inter-tidal: plentiful light, nutrients and oxygen; many species packed into zones; stresses are drying, heat and waves.
  • Abyssal plain: dark, cold, food-poor; few life forms per area, but spread over the largest habitat on Earth.
  • Vents: dense life in small patches, built on microbes and a few specialised animals.
  • Exam move: compare the energy source, the length of the food chain and the limiting factor, not just "more" or "fewer" species.
Mission check

?Two cameras sit on flat abyssal plain at 4,000 m, far from any ridge. Site X lies beneath the equatorial upwelling band; Site Y beneath clear, low-chlorophyll open tropical ocean. Which site should have more animals feeding on the floor?

The deep floor depends on the surface: where light and nutrients make surface water productive, more marine snow feeds life far below. Only at vents do microbes make food from chemicals instead.

Mission 4

Mission 4: The world now farms more fish and shellfish than it catches. Does that solve overfishing?

Make a guess: The world now farms more fish and shellfish than it catches. Does that solve overfishing?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.3-2.a case study of one renewable biological ocean resource (krill): use and management, stakeholder values and context, resilience and key thresholds
Mission 4

Antarctic krill

  • Antarctic krill (Euphausia superba): "a small shrimp-like crustacean" of the Southern Ocean.
  • "Krill live in dense swarms and are key to the ecosystem".
  • They are "a food source for most of the larger animals in the region", including baleen whales such as blue and humpback whales.
  • A renewable resource: stocks regrow, if harvest stays within their capacity to recover.
Mission 4

Use: who fishes, and for what?

  • Commercial krill fishing began in 1961/62, when two Soviet research vessels took 47 tonnes.
  • The rise in catches since 2010 "is largely driven by Norway". Over 2015–2025: Norway 62.6% of the catch, China 20.8%, Republic of Korea 8.4%.
  • Krill is caught for its omega-3-rich oil, for fishmeal, pet food and dietary supplements.
  • Fishing now focuses on the Antarctic Peninsula (Bransfield Strait), the South Orkneys and South Georgia.
Mission 4

Fish farms need feed

  • In 2022, aquaculture overtook capture fisheries as the main producer of aquatic animals.
  • In 2024 only 27.9% of farmed aquatic animals were non-fed species, such as mussels and filter-feeding carp. So about 72% came from species that are fed.
  • In 2024, about 21 million tonnes of aquatic animal production went to non-food uses, "mainly fishmeal and fish oil".
  • Feed links fish farms to wild catches, including krill meal.
Quick check
?Why can growth in fish farming increase pressure on a wild resource such as krill?
Mission 4

Management: CCAMLR

  • The krill fishery is managed by the international Commission for the Conservation of Antarctic Marine Living Resources (CCAMLR).
  • It follows an "ecosystem approach": its Ecosystem Monitoring Program, set up in 1985, tracks krill predators to separate the effects of fishing from natural change.
  • A large survey in 2000 estimated krill biomass in the main fishing area (Area 48) at 56 million tonnes.
  • The precautionary catch limit for Area 48 is 5.61 million tonnes a season.
Mission 4

Key threshold: the trigger level

  • A lower trigger level caps the catch at 620,000 tonnes a season until catches can be spread across smaller areas.
  • It is the sum of the largest catch ever taken in each subarea; it exists to stop the whole limit being taken from "a small part of the overall area".
  • From 2009, a measure split the trigger level between subareas. Members did not reach consensus to renew it, so it expired at the end of the 2024 season.
  • Why it matters: krill predators such as penguins and seals forage near their colonies, so local overfishing can harm them even when the total is small.
Mission 4

2025: the threshold is reached

  • Catch in Area 48 (tonnes): 2015 225,632; 2020 450,781; 2024 498,350; 2025 624,918.
  • 2025 was the historical maximum: it reached the trigger level, and the fishery was closed early for the first time.
  • August 2025 catches were just 3,036 tonnes, against more than 100,000 in July; the season normally runs to 30 November.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, by what percentage did the krill catch rise from 2024 to 2025?
Mission 4

Stakeholders and their values

  • Fishing nations and companies value krill economically. In 2025, Norway took 326,307 tonnes (52%) and China 231,083 tonnes (37%).
  • Scientists and conservation groups value krill as the base of the food web. In 2021, three humpback whales were caught in krill trawls, the first whale deaths recorded in the fishery.
  • Political context: CCAMLR members must agree measures together. After the 2025 closure, the Commission "was unable to reach consensus on a revised conservation measure".
Mission 4

Resilience under pressure

  • Resilience: a stock's ability to recover from disturbance. Krill's vast numbers make the stock resilient to small catches.
  • But the climate is changing: over 90 years, krill distribution in the south-west Atlantic "has contracted southward", with reduced recruitment of juveniles.
  • Fishing is concentrated in a few places, close to where predators feed.
Mission 4

Evaluating the management

  • Strength: FAO rates all 15 assessed Antarctic stocks as biologically sustainable in 2023, calling the region "a global benchmark for precautionary, ecosystem-based fisheries management".
  • Strength: the trigger level worked as designed: the fishery closed in 2025 once catches reached the 620,000-tonne trigger, about 11% of the 5.61 million tonne limit.
  • Weakness: the rule spreading the catch has lapsed, members cannot agree a replacement, and climate change is shifting the stock.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the trigger level's role?
Mission check

?Fish farms now produce more aquatic animals than wild fisheries catch. Has that taken the pressure off Antarctic krill?

No. Farming more fish moves the pressure: about 72% of farmed aquatic animals are fed, and wild fish and krill supply meal and oil. Antarctic krill catches rose to a record 624,918 tonnes in 2025, reaching CCAMLR's trigger level and closing the fishery early for the first time.

Mission 5

Stock or flow?

  • Stock (non-renewable) resources form far more slowly than we use them: oil, gas, sea-bed minerals.
  • Flow (renewable) resources are renewed continuously by natural processes: tides (Moon and Sun), waves (wind).
  • Using a flow does not shrink it; using a stock does.
  • So the management questions differ: stocks raise depletion and damage; flows raise cost, site and ecological impacts.
Mission 5

Offshore oil and gas

  • In 2015 offshore fields gave "nearly 30% of total global oil production".
  • Five countries gave 43% of offshore output: Saudi Arabia (13%, the largest), Brazil, Mexico, Norway, USA.
  • "Most offshore production is in shallow waters", but projects are moving into deep water.
  • Deeper wells mean higher cost and higher risk if control is lost.
Quick check
?Offshore fields gave nearly 30% of world oil in 2015, and Saudi Arabia gave 13% of offshore output. About what share of world oil came from Saudi offshore fields?
Mission 5

Who decides: managing oil and gas

  • A coastal state's exclusive economic zone (EEZ) may extend up to 200 nautical miles; it licenses oil and gas there.
  • Risk is managed by national regulators. On 20 April 2010 the Deepwater Horizon blowout killed 11 workers; about 134 million gallons spilled.
  • The US then split its regulator: from 1 October 2011, BSEE enforces safety and environmental rules, separate from BOEM, which manages resource development.
  • Separating "who sells the licence" from "who checks safety" aims to reduce conflicts of interest.
Mission 5

Tidal power

  • Tidal range (barrage): captures the height difference between high and low tide.
  • Tidal stream: turbines turned by tidal currents.
  • Tides are "relatively predictable" on daily, two-weekly and yearly cycles.
  • La Rance, France: the first barrage, 1966, 240 MW. Sihwa, South Korea: 254 MW, operating from 2012.
  • By 2012, about 514 MW of tidal range and about 6 MW of tidal stream had been deployed worldwide.
Mission 5

Wave power

  • Wave energy is pre-commercial: more than 100 pilot and demonstration projects, few near commercial use (2014).
  • Best resources: deep water at medium-high latitudes, where waves are strongest.
  • Barriers: the harsh ocean environment and cost: estimates for early projects ran at €330–630 per MWh.
  • So the flow is huge in theory, but little is yet captured.
Quick check
?Why can grid operators plan around tidal power more easily than around wave power?
Mission 5

Managing flow resources

  • Barrages close off estuaries; IRENA notes their potential "is limited due to ecological constraints", including effects on fish and marine life.
  • Sites are few: tidal range needs a large tidal range and a suitable estuary; tidal stream needs fast currents.
  • Wave devices must survive storms for decades, which drives up cost.
  • The trade-off: renewable and low-carbon, but costly and site-limited, with local ecological impacts.
Mission 5

Sea-bed minerals: three deposits

  • Polymetallic nodules: lumps on the abyssal plain, mostly at 4,000–6,000 m; mainly manganese and iron, with nickel, copper, cobalt and rare earth elements.
  • Cobalt-rich ferromanganese crusts: coat seamounts and ridges at <400 to >5,000 m; cobalt up to 2%.
  • Polymetallic sulfides: form at hydrothermal vents on mid-ocean ridges; copper, lead, zinc, silver and gold.
  • Ferrous minerals: iron and manganese (used mainly in steel). Non-ferrous: copper, nickel, cobalt, zinc, lead, silver, gold.
Quick check
?Which sea-bed deposit would supply mainly non-ferrous metals?
Mission 5

Who manages the deep sea bed?

  • Beyond national jurisdiction lies "the Area": "The Area and its resources are the common heritage of mankind".
  • The International Seabed Authority (ISA) issues exploration contracts: 19 are for nodules, 17 of them in the Clarion-Clipperton Zone of the Pacific.
  • A 1979 test-mining track, resurveyed in 2023, still showed altered communities: impacts "will be persistent over at least decadal timeframes".
  • So minerals are non-renewable twice over: the metal is a stock, and the habitat recovers over decades or more.
Mission 5

Comparing management

  • Oil and gas: inside EEZs; national licensing and safety regulation (e.g. BSEE after 2010).
  • Wave and tidal: inside national waters; planning consent weighs ecology against low-carbon power.
  • Deep-sea minerals: mostly beyond EEZs; one global body (ISA) under UNCLOS.
  • Exam move: link each resource to its type (stock or flow), its scale of governance, and its main impact.
Mission check

?A coastal state has an offshore gas field 80 nautical miles out, a fast tidal current in a strait, and nodule fields 400 nautical miles offshore. Which statement is correct?

Oil, gas and sea-bed metals are stocks: they can run out, and extraction damages slow-to-recover habitats. Tides and waves are flows that will not run out; their limits are cost, site and ecology.

Mission 6

The ocean as a global commons

  • A global commons is a resource open to all and owned by no one, such as the high seas and their fish.
  • Hardin's tragedy of the commons (1968): each user gains from taking more, while the cost of depletion is shared by all, so the resource is overused.
  • Escaping the tragedy needs rules: property rights, quotas, protected areas or treaties.
Mission 6

UNCLOS: the zones

  • The UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS, 1982) came into force in 1994; it has 172 parties.
  • Territorial waters: up to 12 nautical miles (nm) from the coast; full sovereignty, but foreign ships have a right of innocent passage.
  • Contiguous zone: up to 24 nm, for customs, immigration and sanitary control.
  • Exclusive economic zone (EEZ): up to 200 nm, with sovereign rights over living and non-living resources. Beyond lie the high seas, open to all states.
Mission 6

The seabed: shelf and the Area

  • A coastal state's continental shelf can extend beyond 200 nm where its continental margin does. Its outer limit may not lie beyond 350 nm from the coast or 100 nm beyond the 2,500 m depth line (whichever is further); on submarine ridges, 350 nm is the limit.
  • Claims beyond 200 nm go to the UN Commission on the Limits of the Continental Shelf (CLCS) for scientific review.
  • The seabed beyond national limits is the Area, "the common heritage of mankind", managed by the International Seabed Authority (ISA).
Quick check
?A foreign trawler fishes 150 nm off a coastal state, beyond its territorial waters. Who has the right to manage that fishing?
Mission 6

Marine reserves

  • National waters are 39% of the ocean, and about 23% of them are protected or conserved (Protected Planet, live figure, read Nov 2026; it changes as sites are added). The high seas (areas beyond national jurisdiction) are 61% of the ocean, and only 1.45% of them is protected.
  • The High Seas (BBNJ) Agreement was adopted on 19 June 2023 and entered into force on 17 January 2026, with 104 parties by October 2026.
  • A central aim: a system of area-based management tools, including marine protected areas, on the high seas.

Mission 6

Does UNCLOS manage resources well?

  • Strengths: clear zones turn much of the coastal ocean into managed national space; the ISA regulates seabed minerals in the Area, through exploration contracts, 17 of them for nodules in the Pacific's Clarion-Clipperton Zone.
  • Weaknesses: the high seas remain a commons, and only 1.45% is protected; enforcement far offshore is hard.
  • New treaties (BBNJ) try to close the gap: a response to the tragedy of the commons.
Mission 6

Arctic oil and gas

  • USGS (2008): 90 billion barrels of undiscovered oil and 1,669 trillion cubic feet of gas may lie north of the Arctic Circle, about 84% of it offshore.
  • September Arctic sea-ice area fell by about 40% between 1979–88 and 2010–19; the Arctic is likely to be practically ice-free in September at least once before 2050.
  • Less ice means easier access, which raises the stakes of who owns the seabed.
Mission 6

Overlapping claims

  • CLCS submissions: Russia (2001; revised 2015), Denmark/Greenland (December 2014), Canada (May 2019).
  • Denmark states that Canada's outer limits "would overlap" with Greenland's northern shelf, and that "there are potential overlaps of entitlement".
  • Central to the dispute: the Lomonosov Ridge, "a sliver of continental crust that divides the Arctic Ocean into its two main basins".
Mission 6

Attitudes and actions

  • Each state backs its claim with seabed science. Denmark argues that the ridges are "morphologically continuous with the land mass of Greenland".
  • In the 2008 Ilulissat Declaration, the five Arctic Ocean coastal states committed to the law of the sea and to "orderly settlement of any possible overlapping claims".
  • In 2014, Denmark/Greenland and Russia agreed not to object to the CLCS considering each other's submissions. The CLCS adopted recommendations on Russia's revised submission in February 2023.

Mission check

?A company finds metal-rich nodules on the deep seabed of the central Pacific, 1,500 nm from any coast. Who controls whether it may mine there?

Under UNCLOS, coastal states hold rights over their continental shelf to 200 nm, and further where the shelf extends. Beyond lies the Area, managed by the ISA as common heritage. In the Arctic, Russia, Denmark and Canada's claims overlap; the CLCS reviews the science and the states negotiate the boundaries.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • One global ocean: 71% of Earth's surface, about 361.8 million km², 1.33 billion km³, mean depth 3,682 m.
  • Relief: shelf and slope at the continental margin; abyssal plains (70% of the floor); mid-ocean ridges with rift valleys on slow-spreading ridges; trenches at subduction zones; guyots (wave-planed seamounts that subsided).

Mission 2

  • Salinity peaks in the subtropics; temperature falls through the thermocline; the North Atlantic has warm surface flow north and cold deep flow south.

Mission 3

  • Light: euphotic zone to 200 m. Temperature: mixed layer, thermocline, cold deep water. Nutrients: build up at depth; upwelling and polar mixing bring them up. Tropics: strong thermocline, low nutrients; poles: light-limited in winter.
  • Inter-tidal: producers on site, complex web, high biodiversity under drying and wave stress. Deep water: marine snow from above, or chemosynthesis at vents.

Mission 4

  • Krill is a renewable resource at the base of the Antarctic food web, fished mainly by Norway and China for oil, meal, pet food and supplements.
  • CCAMLR's precautionary limit (5.61 Mt) and trigger level (620,000 t) are thresholds; the trigger was reached in 2025, while climate change and disagreement between members test the stock's resilience.

Mission 5

  • Stock: offshore oil and gas (nearly 30% of world oil, 2015); nodules, crusts, sulfides (ferrous Mn and Fe; non-ferrous Cu, Ni, Co, Zn, Pb, Ag, Au). Flow: tidal (La Rance 240 MW; Sihwa 254 MW) and wave (pre-commercial).
  • Management: national licensing and regulation in EEZs (BSEE after Deepwater Horizon); ISA for "the Area", where test-mining impacts persist for decades.

Mission 6

  • Zones: territorial waters (12 nm), contiguous zone (24 nm), EEZ (200 nm), high seas beyond; seabed: continental shelf, then the Area (ISA).
  • The high seas are a global commons prone to overuse; marine reserves and the BBNJ Agreement aim to protect them.
