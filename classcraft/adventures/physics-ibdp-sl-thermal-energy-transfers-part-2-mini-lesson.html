Mission 1

Mission 1: A metal bench and a wooden bench sit in the same cold park. Why does the metal one feel colder?

Make a guess: A metal bench and a wooden bench sit in the same cold park. Why does the metal one feel colder?

Mission 1

Three ways energy moves

  • Conduction: energy passed through a material, particle to particle. The material stays put.
  • Convection: a warm fluid (liquid or gas) moves and carries its energy with it.
  • Radiation: electromagnetic waves, mostly infrared at everyday temperatures. Needs no medium.
  • Net energy transfer is always from hotter to colder, by itself. It stops when the temperatures are equal: thermal equilibrium.
  • "Cold" is not a thing that flows. Something cools because it loses energy.
Quick check
?A vacuum flask has double glass walls with a vacuum between them. Which statement explains how the vacuum layer helps keep hot coffee in the flask hot?
Quick check
?One end of a glass rod is held in a flame. How does the molecular model explain conduction of energy along the rod?
Mission 1

Conduction: the particle picture

  • Hot end: particles vibrate with more kinetic energy on average.
  • They collide with slower neighbours and pass kinetic energy on, and so on along the material.
  • The net transfer comes from the difference in kinetic energy of neighbouring particles. Each particle stays in place; only energy moves along.
  • Metals conduct far better: they also have free electrons that carry energy quickly through the lattice.
Mission 1

The rate of conduction

  • ΔQ/Δt = −kA(ΔT/Δx)
  • A: cross-sectional area. ΔT/Δx: temperature gradient along the direction of flow.
  • The minus sign: energy flows down the gradient. Where T falls along +x, ΔT/Δx < 0, so ΔQ/Δt > 0 along +x.
  • k: thermal conductivity, W m⁻¹ K⁻¹. A property of the material alone, set by how its atoms are arranged and interact.
  • Typical k: copper ~400 · steel ~50 · glass ~1 · wood ~0.1 · still air ~0.025.
  • More area, bigger temperature difference, thinner layer → faster flow.
Mission 1

Worked example: a window

  • Single-glazed window: area 1.5 m², glass 4.0 mm thick, k = 0.80 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹.
  • Inner face of the glass 6.3 °C, outer face 5.7 °C.
  • Take x from inside to outside: ΔT/Δx = (5.7 − 6.3)/(4.0 × 10⁻³) = −150 K m⁻¹.
  • ΔQ/Δt = −0.80 × 1.5 × (−150) = +180 W: outwards.
  • Only 0.6 K across the glass, yet 180 W flows: glass is thin. Most of the insulation comes from the thin layers of still air on each side.
Quick check
?The rate of thermal energy transfer by conduction is given by ΔQ/Δt = −kA(ΔT/Δx). What does the minus sign show?
Mission 1

Convection

  • Fluid near a heater warms and expands: same mass, more volume, so lower density.
  • Cooler, denser fluid around it sinks and pushes the warm fluid up (buoyancy).
  • The warm fluid cools, becomes denser, sinks again: a convection current.
  • Only in fluids: solid particles cannot flow.
  • Not "heat rises". Warm fluid rises, because it is less dense.
Mission 1

Radiation

  • Every object above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation.
  • Hotter objects emit far more, and at shorter wavelengths.
  • The only mechanism that crosses a vacuum: how the Sun's energy reaches Earth.
  • Dull, dark surfaces are good emitters and good absorbers; shiny surfaces are poor at both.
Quick check
?In some refrigerators the cooling unit is at the top of the cabinet, not the bottom. Why does this cool the whole cabinet well?
Mission check

?Two slabs, P and Q, have the same area and the same thickness, and the same temperature difference is held across each. P is copper (k = 400 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹) and Q is glass (k = 0.80 W m⁻¹ K⁻¹). Which statement about the rates of energy transfer by conduction is correct?

The benches are at the same temperature. Steel's much larger k means it conducts energy away from your warm hand far faster, so your skin cools quickly and the metal feels colder.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Make a guess: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Mission 2

A perfect absorber and emitter

  • All matter above absolute zero turns some of its internal energy into electromagnetic radiation.
  • A black body absorbs all the radiation that falls on it. None is reflected or transmitted.
  • It is also the best possible emitter at its temperature.
  • Its spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of.
  • Stars are very good approximations to black bodies.
Quick check
?Predict first. A black body is heated to a higher temperature. How does its emission spectrum change?
Mission 2

Spectrum curves and temperature

  • Plot intensity per unit wavelength against wavelength: a smooth, continuous curve.
  • Hotter body: more at every wavelength. The peak is higher.
  • Hotter body: the peak moves to shorter wavelength.
  • The total power (area under the curve) rises steeply with temperature.
  • Colour shows this: red-hot (cooler) → yellow → white → bluish (hotter).
Mission 2

Wien's displacement law

  • Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • λmax T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K
  • T must be in kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • The Sun: λmax ≈ 500 nm, so T ≈ 5800 K.
Mission 2

Worked example: a red star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 850 nm (infrared, just beyond red light).
  • Use metres: 850 nm = 850 × 10⁻⁹ m.
  • T = 2.9 × 10⁻³ ÷ 850 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.4 × 10³ K
  • Longer peak wavelength → cooler surface. This star looks red.
Mission 2

Stefan–Boltzmann law

  • Total power radiated ∝ surface area × T⁴.
  • L = σAT⁴ (L: luminosity, the total power, in W)
  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (Stefan–Boltzmann constant).
  • T⁴ matters: double T → 16 × the power.
Quick check
?A small metal sphere of radius 5.0 cm is heated to 527 °C. Treat it as a black body and take σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. What power does the sphere emit as thermal radiation?
Mission check

?The graph shows the intensity per unit wavelength emitted by a black body as a function of wavelength λ. The absolute temperature of the black body is then doubled. At what wavelength does the spectrum of the heated black body peak?

Peak wavelength ∝ 1/T. So the colour of a star (where its spectrum peaks) gives its surface temperature: bluish stars are hotter than reddish ones.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Make a guess: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Mission 3

Luminosity

  • Luminosity, L: the total power a star emits, in watts.
  • Often given in multiples of the Sun's luminosity, L⊙ (3.83 × 10²⁶ W).
  • Stars are close to black bodies.
  • L = σAT⁴, with A = 4πR² the star's surface area.
  • Same temperature, twice the radius → 4 × the luminosity.
Mission 3

What we receive

  • The star's power spreads over a sphere of area 4πd² at distance d.
  • Apparent brightness, b: the power received per unit area, in W m⁻².
  • b = L/(4πd²)
  • Inverse-square law: twice as far → ¼ as much per square metre.
  • A faint star may be weak, or just far away.
Quick check
?A star has a luminosity of 40 L⊙ and is 25 ly from the Earth. Take L⊙ = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W and 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. What is the apparent brightness of the star at the Earth?
Mission check

?From the Earth, star A appears 4 times as bright as star B. Star B is 3 times as far from the Earth as star A. What is the ratio (luminosity of A) ÷ (luminosity of B)?

Brightness alone cannot tell you a star's luminosity. Measure its distance too; then L = 4πd² × (power received per square metre).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Conduction, convection, radiation; net transfer is always hotter → colder. Only radiation crosses a vacuum.
  • ΔQ/Δt = −kA(ΔT/Δx): energy flows down the temperature gradient.
  • Convection: warm fluid expands, becomes less dense and is pushed up by denser fluid.

Mission 2

  • A black body absorbs all incident radiation; its spectrum depends only on T. Hotter: more at every λ, peak at shorter λ.
  • Wien: λmax T = constant (≈ 2.9 × 10⁻³ m K).
  • L = σAT⁴.

Mission 3

  • Luminosity is a star's total power output: L = 4πR²σT⁴.
  • Apparent brightness b = L/(4πd²): a faint star may be weak or distant.
