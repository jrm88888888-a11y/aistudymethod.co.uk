Mission 1

What makes a gurdwara

  • Gurdwara means "door (or gateway) of the Guru".
  • What makes a building a gurdwara is the presence of the Guru Granth Sahib, the scripture Sikhs treat as their living Guru since 1708.
  • A converted church, a warehouse, a house: each becomes a gurdwara when the Guru Granth Sahib is installed with respect.
  • Outside: the Nishan Sahib, a saffron or blue flag with the Khanda emblem, shows that a gurdwara is here.
  • No ordained priesthood: a granthi reads and cares for the scripture, but any competent Sikh may do so.
Mission 1

Inside the prayer hall (darbar)

  • The Guru Granth Sahib rests on a raised platform (takht or palki) under a canopy (chanani), wrapped in cloths (rumalas).
  • A reader waves a chaur (whisk) over it: an old sign of royal honour, here given to the Guru.
  • Everyone covers their head, removes shoes and sits on the floor, lower than the scripture: no special seats for the rich or powerful.
  • Men and women often sit on different sides by custom; the Rahit Maryada does not require it.
  • No tobacco or alcohol may be brought in.
  • At night the scripture is carried to a resting room (sachkhand or sukhasan room) and opened again at dawn.
Mission 1

The diwan: what happens in worship

  • Kirtan: singing hymns (shabads) from the Guru Granth Sahib, usually by ragis with harmonium and tabla.
  • Katha: explanation of the hymns.
  • Ardas: the standing prayer that remembers the Gurus, the martyrs and the Khalsa, and asks for the good of all (Sarbat da bhala).
  • Hukamnama (vak lao): the scripture is opened at random and the first hymn on the left-hand page is read as the Guru's guidance for the day.
  • Karah parshad: a sweet offering of flour, sugar and ghee, shared equally with everyone present.
  • Each element directs attention to the Guru's word, not to the person leading: one way Sikhs work against haumai (ego, self-centredness).
Mission 1

Freedom of worship

  • Who may lead? Any Sikh, man or woman, of any caste, who is competent: able to read Gurmukhi correctly and sing or explain the hymns.
  • Who may attend? Anyone, Sikh or not, of any religion, caste or sex, if they follow the gurdwara's customs.
  • This expresses Guru Nanak's teaching that all people share the one divine light, and his rejection of caste and priestly privilege.
  • Freedom of worship also reflects Guru Tegh Bahadur's martyrdom (1675), remembered as defending other people's freedom to worship.

Mission 1

Where langar comes from

  • Langar: the free community kitchen, and the meal served in it. Every gurdwara has one.
  • Guru Nanak: at Kartarpur (c. 1522) his community farmed, worshipped and ate together.
  • Mata Khivi, wife of Guru Angad, ran the langar; the Guru Granth Sahib praises her care (Guru Granth Sahib 967).
  • Guru Amar Das: "pehle pangat, pichhe sangat": first sit in line (pangat) for the meal, then join the congregation. Sikh tradition says even the Mughal emperor Akbar ate on the floor before meeting him.
Mission 1

Langar as sewa and equality

  • Sewa: selfless service. Volunteers cook, serve and wash up: physical sewa. Donating food or money: material sewa.
  • Everyone sits together in a row (pangat), usually on the floor, and is served the same simple vegetarian food.
  • Vegetarian, so that no guest is excluded by what they may or may not eat.
  • In Guru Nanak's Punjab, caste rules forbade eating with or from lower castes: eating together was a public rejection of caste.
  • Spiritual and material needs: the hungry are fed; those who serve learn humility.
  • Serving others without reward is a remedy for haumai: the self stops being the centre.

Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, practice, significance

Question: Explain how the langar expresses Sikh beliefs.

  • Belief: all people share the one divine light, so caste and status are meaningless before God.
  • Practice: everyone sits in the pangat and receives the same food, served by volunteers.
  • Source: Guru Amar Das's "pehle pangat, pichhe sangat"; the Rahit Maryada's rule against distinctions of high and low.
  • Development: serving without reward is sewa, which works against ego (haumai) and is part of a Sikh's spiritual path.

Mission 2

Vaisakhi 1699: the Khalsa

  • Vaisakhi 1699, Anandpur: Guru Gobind Singh asks the gathered Sikhs who will give their head for the faith.
  • Five men, from different castes and regions, step forward one by one: the Panj Piare ("Five Beloved Ones").
  • The Guru initiates them with amrit ("nectar") and founds the Khalsa ("the pure"): a community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Then the Guru asks the Five to initiate him: Guru and disciple become equals.
  • (Story and its sources in another lesson.)
Mission 2

The amrit ceremony

  • Also called amrit sanskar or khande di pahul.
  • Held in private before the Guru Granth Sahib; five amritdhari Sikhs take the place of the Panj Piare.
  • Water and sugar (patashe) are stirred in an iron bowl with a khanda (double-edged sword) while five banis (prayers) are recited.
  • Each initiate drinks amrit five times; it is sprinkled five times on the eyes and five times on the hair; all drink from the same bowl, a sign that caste is abolished.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited; initiates, who come wearing the 5Ks, are taught the Rehat (code of conduct).
  • Open to men and women of any caste or background who are ready to keep the discipline.
Mission 2

Singh, Kaur and the discipline

  • Men take the name Singh ("lion"); women take Kaur ("princess").
  • These replace caste surnames: all Khalsa Sikhs are one family.
  • Initiates vow to avoid the four kurahits (cardinal prohibitions): cutting hair; eating ritually slaughtered (kutha) meat; sexual relations outside marriage; tobacco.
  • Daily discipline: the set banis morning and evening, nam simran, honest work, sharing, sewa.
Mission 2

The 5Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, kept covered (men wear a turban, many women too): accepting God's will and the natural form God gave.
  • Kangha: a small wooden comb: cleanliness and order, in body and mind.
  • Kara: a steel bangle on the wrist: one God without beginning or end; a reminder to do only good with one's hands.
  • Kachera: cotton undershorts: modesty, self-control, readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: a sword: defending justice and the weak.
  • Together: an outward uniform of the Khalsa, and reminders of an inner discipline.

Mission 2

Four kinds of Sikh

  • Amritdhari: has taken amrit; keeps the 5Ks and the Rehat.
  • Keshdhari: keeps uncut hair (and usually a turban) but has not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: follows the Gurus' teaching but cuts their hair and has not taken amrit. Often explained as "slow adopter" (on the way, gradually); some read sahaj as "inner peace".
  • Patit: an apostate: an amritdhari who breaks a kurahit, especially cutting hair, without seeking forgiveness. Some use the word more widely for any Sikh who cuts their hair.
  • Many Sikhs are keshdhari or sahajdhari all their lives; most Sikhs worldwide have not taken amrit.

Mission 3

Two kinds of Sikh festival

  • Gurpurbs: anniversaries of the Gurus' births and deaths (e.g. Guru Nanak's birth; Guru Arjan's martyrdom).
  • Melas ("fairs"): gatherings on dates shared with the wider Punjabi calendar, re-shaped by Sikh history: Vaisakhi, Bandi Chhor Divas (Diwali), Hola Mohalla.
  • Sikh tradition says Guru Amar Das first asked Sikhs to gather before the Guru at Vaisakhi and Diwali, giving old festivals a Sikh focus.
  • A festival in the gurdwara usually includes an akhand path (unbroken 48-hour reading of the Guru Granth Sahib), kirtan, Ardas and langar.
Mission 3

Vaisakhi 1699

  • Vaisakhi: Punjabi New Year in the solar calendar and the start of the wheat harvest, around 13–14 April.
  • At Anandpur in 1699, Guru Gobind Singh asked the gathered Sikhs: who will give his head for the Guru?
  • Five men came forward one by one and went into a tent. They returned dressed like the Guru: the Panj Piare (five beloved ones).
  • He prepared amrit (sugar-water stirred with a double-edged sword, the khanda) and initiated them into the Khalsa; then asked them to initiate him.
  • Men took the name Singh, women later Kaur; all were to keep the 5Ks (see Mission 2).
  • The five came from different castes and regions: the Khalsa was one family, without caste.
Mission 3

Vaisakhi today

  • Akhand path ending on Vaisakhi morning; special kirtan and Ardas.
  • The Nishan Sahib is taken down, its pole washed, and a new cover (chola) and flag raised.
  • Amrit sanchar: many gurdwaras hold Khalsa initiation on or near Vaisakhi.
  • Nagar kirtan: a procession through the streets, led by five Sikhs representing the Panj Piare, with the Guru Granth Sahib carried on a float; free food for passers-by.
  • In Punjab, also harvest fairs and bhangra: cultural celebration alongside religious meaning.

Mission 3

The release of Guru Hargobind

  • Guru Hargobind (Guru 1606–1644), sixth Guru, was held in Gwalior Fort on the orders of the Mughal emperor Jahangir.
  • Sikh tradition tells that when he was to be freed, he refused to leave without the 52 Hindu rulers (rajas) imprisoned with him.
  • Jahangir said only those who could hold on to the Guru's cloak could go. The Guru had a cloak made with 52 tassels, and all 52 walked out.
  • He reached Amritsar at Diwali; Sikhs lit lamps round the Harmandir Sahib to welcome him.
  • He became known as Bandi Chhor: "the one who frees prisoners".
Mission 3

Diwali / Bandi Chhor Divas today

  • Harmandir Sahib and gurdwaras worldwide are lit with lamps, candles and lights; fireworks in Amritsar.
  • Akhand path, kirtan, Ardas and langar.
  • Many Sikhs call the day Bandi Chhor Divas (Day of Liberation) to mark its Sikh meaning; others still say Diwali, and many families celebrate alongside Hindu neighbours.
  • The date follows the lunar month, so it moves each year with Diwali (October or November).
Mission 3Read the source

Diwali in the Guru Granth Sahib?

  • The Bandi Chhor story is not in the Guru Granth Sahib: the scripture is hymns, not a history of the Gurus, and its first form, the Adi Granth (1604), predates the release. The story is kept by Sikh tradition and historians.
  • Bhai Gurdas (c. 1551–1636) uses Diwali as an image (Var 19:6, paraphrase): on the night of Diwali lamps are lit, stars appear in the sky, flowers bloom in gardens; yet all of these fade, while the light of the Guru's word endures.

Show answer

Mission 4

What the Guru Granth Sahib is

  • The Sikh scripture: 1,430 pages (angs) in the standard printing; poetry meant to be sung.
  • Arranged mainly by 31 raags (musical modes). Within each raag: the Gurus in order, then other saints.
  • It opens with the Japji of Guru Nanak, beginning with the Mool Mantar (see Mission 6).
  • Authors: six Gurus (the first five and the ninth), bhagats (Hindu and Muslim saints), court poets (bhatts) and a few Sikhs of the Gurus.
  • Each hymn is headed by its Guru's number: "Mahala 1" is Guru Nanak, "Mahala 5" Guru Arjan.
Mission 4

Gurmukhi

  • Gurmukhi: "from the mouth of the Guru". Tradition credits Guru Angad with shaping the alphabet from scripts already used in Punjab.
  • It wrote the spoken language of the people, not Sanskrit (Brahmins' scripture) or Persian (the court).
  • Effect: ordinary Sikhs, of any caste, could read the hymns for themselves.
  • It also gave the Panth its own visible written identity.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib is installed only in Gurmukhi; translations are for study.
Mission 4

From pothis to the Guru

  • Guru Nanak kept his hymns in a book (pothi), passed to his successors (tradition).
  • Goindval Pothis (1570s, Guru Amar Das): the earliest surviving collection.
  • 1604, Adi Granth: Guru Arjan, with Bhai Gurdas as scribe; often identified with the Kartarpur Pothi.
  • 1706, Damdama Sahib: Guru Gobind Singh, with Bhai Mani Singh as scribe, adds Guru Tegh Bahadur's hymns.
  • 1708, Nanded: this text is declared the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Adi Granth ("first book") is the 1604 compilation; Guru Granth Sahib is the final text, as Guru. Many Sikhs use both names for the scripture.
  • Scholars argue over this history. The historian Gurinder Singh Mann studies early manuscripts and sees the text growing in stages; many Sikh scholars, such as the Institute of Sikh Studies, hold that this approach undermines a scripture they regard as revealed.

Mission 4

The bhagat bani

  • Bhagat bani: hymns of 15 bhagats (saints), Hindu and Muslim, from the 12th to the 16th centuries.
  • Many of low caste: Kabir (weaver), Ravidas (leatherworker), Namdev (cloth-printer), Sain (barber), Dhanna (farmer).
  • A Muslim Sufi: Sheikh Farid. A Brahmin teacher: Ramanand.
  • Included because their teaching agreed with the Gurus': one formless God, the Name, against caste and empty ritual.
  • Placed after the Gurus in each raag, but given the same reverence. The Gurus sometimes add verses responding to them.
Mission 4Read the source

Farid and Ramanand

  • Ramanand (GGS 1195, paraphrase): Where should I go? My home is full of joy. I set out with sandalwood and incense to worship God in a temple, but the Guru showed me God within my own heart.
  • Farid (Saloks of Farid, GGS 1377–1384, paraphrase): do not strike back at someone who strikes you; go home with humility. Elsewhere: answer evil with good, and do not let anger fill your mind.

Show answer

Mission 5

The Word is the Guru

  • Guru: often explained as the teacher who leads from darkness to light. Sikhs believe God is the true Guru (Satguru), who speaks through the Word (shabad, bani).
  • The Word is the Guru, and the Guru is the Word; in the Word is all nectar (GGS 982, paraphrase).
  • The ten Gurus carried one light; since 1708 the Word in the scripture is the Guru.
  • The Guru's Word turns the self from haumai (self-centred pride, ego) towards God.
  • So Sikhs speak of the Guru Granth Sahib, not "the Sikh Bible".
Mission 5

Treated as a living Guru

  • Placed on a raised throne (takht or manji sahib), under a canopy, wrapped in cloths (rumalas).
  • A chaur (fan) is waved over it, as over a king.
  • Prakash: opened at dawn, often carried in on the head. Sukhasan: closed at night and carried to its resting room.
  • Everyone covers the head, removes shoes, bows and sits lower than the Guru.
  • No image of God or the Gurus is worshipped: the Word is at the centre.
Mission 5

In worship and ceremony

  • All worship happens in its presence: kirtan (singing its hymns), katha (sermon on a hymn), Ardas (prayer).
  • Akhand path: unbroken reading of all 1,430 pages, about 48 hours, by a relay of readers.
  • Naming: a name begins with the first letter of a hymn opened at random.
  • Anand Karaj (marriage): the couple walks four times around it while the Lavan (GGS 773–774) are sung.
  • Amrit initiation and funerals (Kirtan Sohila; a reading completed afterwards) take place in its presence.
Mission 5Read the source

Vak lao: taking the Guru's word

  • Vak lao ("take a saying"): open the Guru Granth Sahib at random; read the hymn at the top of the left-hand page (if it began on the page before, go back to its start).
  • This is the hukamnama: the Guru's command for the day, often written up for the sangat.
  • The daily hukamnama from the Harmandir, Amritsar, is shared worldwide.
  • Sikhs take a vak before decisions and at ceremonies, as advice from the living Guru.

Q1 Why open at random?

Show answer

To receive the Guru's guidance rather than choose a passage to suit oneself.

Mission 5Diversity

Showing respect today

  • Some families keep a Guru Granth Sahib at home in its own room, with daily prakash and sukhasan; many keep only a small prayer book (gutka) and go to the gurdwara.
  • Worn or damaged copies are respectfully cremated, not thrown away.
  • The Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee (SGPC, Amritsar) controls printing of the full text.
  • Debates: the use of translations and screens in worship, especially among young Sikhs abroad.
  • Practice varies, but all aim at the same respect: treating the Word as the Guru.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Sikhs worship the Guru Granth Sahib."

  • It can look like it: royal treatment, bowing, offerings, the chaur, rest at night.
  • It can look like it: some Sikh reformers warn against ritualism, e.g. paying for akhand paths without listening.
  • Against: Sikhs say they bow to the Guru's Word, through which God is known; worship is of God alone (Waheguru, "Wonderful Lord", the name Sikhs most use for God).
  • Against: the scripture itself rejects idol worship; it is consulted and obeyed, like a living teacher, not prayed to as a god.
  • Judge it by: is "worship" about the act or the intention? Does it function like an idol? How do Sikhs describe their own practice?

Mission 6

Waheguru: one, wonderful

  • Waheguru: the name for God most used by Sikhs; roughly "Wonderful Lord" or "Wondrous Enlightener".
  • Sikhs repeat it in meditation; it is the Gurmantar given at Khalsa initiation.
  • Oneness: God is one, the only one, with no second and no rival.
  • The scripture begins with the numeral 1: Ik Onkar, "One God" (Guru Granth Sahib 1).
  • One God for all people, not a tribal God of Sikhs.
Mission 6Read the source

The Mool Mantar

Guru Granth Sahib 1, word by word (paraphrase):

  • Ik Onkar: one God, the one reality.
  • Satnam: Truth is God's name.
  • Karta Purakh: the Creator, present in creation.
  • Nirbhau, Nirvair: without fear, without hate.
  • Akal Murat: beyond time, undying.
  • Ajuni, Saibhang: never born; self-existent.
  • Gurprasad: known by the Guru's grace.
  • Sikhs believe Guru Nanak (1469–1539) first spoke it. Mool = root: the root of Sikh belief.

Mission 6

The Japji: shape and use

  • Japji Sahib, by Guru Nanak, opens the Guru Granth Sahib (pages 1–8).
  • Shape: Mool Mantar → opening verse → 38 pauris (stanzas) → closing verse (salok).
  • Personal meditation: the first of the daily prayers (Nitnem), recited in the early morning (amrit vela).
  • Initiation: Japji is the first of the five prayers recited as amrit is prepared; the Panj Piare recite the Mool Mantar and the initiates repeat it.
  • Many Sikhs know it by heart.
Mission 6Read the source

Japji, pauris 1, 2 and 20

Guru Granth Sahib 1–4 (paraphrase):

  • Pauri 1: God is not reached by thinking, even a hundred thousand times; nor by silence; nor by piling up the world's goods. The way is to walk in God's hukam (will).
  • Many translations say "by thinking"; others read the word as ritual washing. Either way: no human technique reaches God.
  • Pauri 2: all things come to be by God's command; no one is outside it. Whoever understands it does not speak in haumai (ego).
  • Pauri 20: water washes dirty hands; but a mind stained by wrongdoing is cleansed only by love of the Name (nam).

Q1 According to pauri 20, what cleanses a mind stained by wrongdoing?

Show answer

Love of God's Name.

Q2 On the "by thinking" reading, why does pauri 1 reject thinking alone?

Show answer

God is beyond human reasoning; God is reached by living in God's will. On either reading, no human technique reaches God.

Mission 6

An awesome, liberating presence

  • Awe: Japji piles up the countless (asankh): countless worlds, devotees, scholars; even the time of creation is known only to the Creator (pauris 17–22, paraphrase).
  • Wonder at God's greatness, not argument, is the fitting response.
  • Closing verse: air is the Guru, water the father, earth the great mother; deeds are read out before Dharam, the righteous judge; those who meditated on the Name are freed, and many are freed with them (Guru Granth Sahib 8, paraphrase).
  • Liberating: release comes by grace and the Name, not by ritual (pilgrimage and austerity earn only "a sesame seed" of merit: pauri 21, paraphrase).
  • (Nam, hukam, grace and the five realms of pauris 34–37: next missions.)

Mission 7

Ik Onkar: one without a second

  • Ik Onkar: God is one; nothing exists beside God or outside God's power.
  • Before creation, Guru Nanak says, there was only darkness for countless ages: no earth, no sky, only God's hukam (will) (Guru Granth Sahib 1035, paraphrase).
  • Creation came from God's single word, and the whole expanse appeared (Japji pauri 16, paraphrase).
  • Symbol ੴ: the numeral 1 joined to Onkar, with an arc reaching outwards: one God, without end.
Mission 7

Nirgun and sargun

  • Nirgun: God without qualities: beyond form, beyond anything the mind can grasp.
  • Sargun: God with qualities: present and knowable in creation, the Word and the Guru.
  • One God, two ways of being known: God is nirgun and is also sargun (Sukhmani, Guru Granth Sahib 287, paraphrase).
  • Sargun does not mean God is born as an avatar (Ajuni): God is present in creation, never incarnate in one body.
Mission 7

Beyond the world, within it

  • Transcendent: beyond creation, time and space (Akal, Nirankar: timeless, formless).
  • Immanent: present in everything: the light within all is God's light (Guru Granth Sahib 13, paraphrase).
  • Creator and sustainer: God makes, supports and watches over all life.
  • Omnipotent and omniscient: all-powerful (everything happens within hukam) and all-knowing.
Mission 7

A personal God

  • Sikh scripture speaks to God as you: you are my father, my mother, my kin and my brother (Guru Granth Sahib 103, paraphrase).
  • God is also friend, beloved husband (the soul as bride), True King (Sacha Patshah).
  • So the formless God can be loved and spoken to.
  • These images are ways of relating, not claims that God has a body or gender.

?A student writes: "Because Sikhs believe God is sargun, they believe God was born on earth as a human being." What is the best correction?
Mission 7Evaluate

"The most important Sikh teaching about God is that God is personal."

  • For: scripture addresses God as father, mother, friend and beloved; devotion and nam simran assume a God who can be loved.
  • For: a personal God gives meaning to grace (nadar) and to prayer.
  • Against: Ik Onkar comes first in the Mool Mantar: oneness is the root of every other teaching, including equality.
  • Against: God is also nirgun; stressing the personal could shrink God to human terms.
  • Judge it by: order and emphasis in the Mool Mantar; what shapes Sikh practice most; whether personal and nirgun teachings can be ranked at all.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A gurdwara is wherever the Guru Granth Sahib is installed; worship includes kirtan, Ardas, hukamnama and karah parshad.
  • Langar, from Guru Nanak, Mata Khivi and Guru Amar Das, is free, vegetarian and shared in a line: sewa and equality in action.

Mission 2

  • 1699: Guru Gobind Singh founded the Khalsa; amrit (khande di pahul) initiates men and women of any caste; names Singh and Kaur; four kurahits.
  • The 5Ks: kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan: an outward uniform and inner discipline.
  • Amritdhari, keshdhari, sahajdhari (sehajdhari) and patit Sikhs hold different views on how essential initiation and the 5Ks are.

Mission 3

  • Vaisakhi: Punjabi New Year and the founding of the Khalsa (Anandpur, 1699): identity, sacrifice, belonging, protecting the oppressed.
  • Bandi Chhor Divas: Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior with 52 rulers and return to Amritsar at Diwali: freedom, light over darkness.

Mission 4

  • The Guru Granth Sahib: 1,430 pages, in Gurmukhi, arranged by raag; hymns of six Gurus, the bhagats and others.
  • Stages: Goindval Pothis → Adi Granth (1604) → Damdama (1706) → declared Guru (1708).

Mission 5

  • The Word is the Guru; since 1708 the scripture is the living Guru, treated with royal respect (takht, chaur, prakash, sukhasan).
  • All worship and rites of passage take place in its presence; vak lao takes its guidance.

Mission 6

  • Waheguru is one, true (Sat) and revealed through the Word (shabad); the Mool Mantar is the root statement of these beliefs.
  • The Japji (Guru Nanak) moves from who God is to how to reach God: hukam, the Name, grace and awe.

Mission 7

  • Ik Onkar: one God without a second, both nirgun (beyond qualities) and sargun (present in creation), transcendent and immanent, personal, creator and sustainer.
  • Sikhs debate which teaching about God matters most: oneness or a personal God.
