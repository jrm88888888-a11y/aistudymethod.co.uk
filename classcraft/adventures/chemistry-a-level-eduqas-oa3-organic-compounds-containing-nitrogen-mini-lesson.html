Mission 1

What is an amine?

  • Take ammonia, NH3. Replace H atoms with alkyl or aryl groups: an amine.
  • Primary: one C on the N, e.g. butylamine, CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2, and phenylamine, C6H5NH2.
  • Secondary: two C on the N, e.g. (CH3)2NH. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3N.
  • Every amine N has a lone pair. That drives all its chemistry.
  • Amide, not amine: N bonded to a C=O carbon (–CONH2, –CONHR).
Quick check
?Which statement about the compound (CH3)3CNH2 is correct?
Mission 1

Amines are bases

  • The N lone pair accepts H⁺: amines are bases, like ammonia.
  • In water, a weak base: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺ + OH⁻. The solution is alkaline.
  • With acids, salts: CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻ (butylammonium chloride). No water forms.
  • Add NaOH to the salt and the free amine comes back: RNH3⁺ + OH⁻ → RNH2 + H2O.
  • Back to the opening question: fishy-smelling amines are volatile. Lemon's acid turns them into ionic salts, which don't evaporate.
Mission 1

Which is the strongest base?

  • Base strength depends on how available the N lone pair is to accept H⁺.
  • Alkyl groups push electrons towards N (positive inductive effect). Lone pair more available: stronger base than NH3.
  • Phenyl: the lone pair is partly delocalised into the ring. Less available: much weaker base than NH3.
  • Order: primary aliphatic amine > ammonia > phenylamine.
Quick check
?Which lists ethylamine, ammonia and phenylamine in order of base strength, strongest first?
Mission 1

Making aliphatic amines

  • Route 1: halogenoalkane + excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube.
  • CH3CH2CH2CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 + NH4Br
  • Excess NH3 makes the primary amine the main product, but some 2° and 3° amine still forms.
  • Route 2: reduce a nitrile: CH3CH2CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2. Only the primary amine forms.
  • Reducing agent: LiAlH4 (lithal) in dry ether.
Mission 1

Making aromatic amines

  • Reduce nitrobenzene with tin and concentrated HCl, heated under reflux.
  • C6H5NO2 + 6[H] → C6H5NH2 + 2H2O
  • In all that acid, the product is the phenylammonium ion, C6H5NH3⁺: a salt.
  • Add NaOH(aq) to free phenylamine: C6H5NH3⁺ + OH⁻ → C6H5NH2 + H2O.
  • Nitrobenzene comes from nitrating benzene.
Quick check
?Nitrobenzene is heated under reflux with tin and concentrated hydrochloric acid. Excess sodium hydroxide solution is then added before the phenylamine is separated. Why is the NaOH needed?
Mission 1

Amines + acyl chlorides: amides

  • The N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbonyl carbon of an acyl chloride.
  • Ammonia gives a primary amide, RCONH2. A primary amine gives a secondary (N-substituted) amide, RCONHR′.
  • CH3COCl + 2CH3CH2CH2CH2NH2 → CH3CONHCH2CH2CH2CH3 + CH3CH2CH2CH2NH3⁺Cl⁻
  • Product: N-butylethanamide. Adding CH3CO– to N is ethanoylation.
Mission 1

Amides: make, split, dehydrate

  • From acids: RCOOH + NH3 → RCOO⁻NH4⁺; heat strongly → RCONH2 + H2O.
  • Acid hydrolysis, heat: CH3CONH2 + H2O + HCl → CH3COOH + NH4Cl
  • Alkaline hydrolysis, heat: CH3CONH2 + NaOH → CH3COONa + NH3 (turns damp red litmus blue).
  • Dehydration: heat with P4O10 (phosphorus(V) oxide): CH3CONH2 → CH3CN + H2O.
  • Nitriles hydrolyse too, heated with dilute acid: CH3CN + 2H2O + HCl → CH3COOH + NH4Cl.
Mission check

?A reaction mixture contains phenylamine and some unreacted nitrobenzene, dissolved in an organic solvent. It is shaken with dilute hydrochloric acid in a separating funnel. Which compound moves into the water layer, and why?

Same chemistry as lemon on fish. An acid turns an amine into a salt, which stays in water instead of escaping.

Mission 2

Making benzenediazonium chloride

  • Nitrous acid, HNO2, is unstable: make it in the flask from sodium nitrite and hydrochloric acid.
  • Nitrous acid is also called nitric(III) acid.
  • NaNO2 + HCl → HNO2 + NaCl
  • Phenylamine in HCl, kept below 10 °C (ice bath; in practice about 0–5 °C), reacts with it:
  • C6H5NH2 + HNO2 + HCl → C6H5N2⁺Cl⁻ + 2H2O
  • The product is benzenediazonium chloride. Its ion has the group –N⁺≡N on the ring.
Mission 2

Keep it cold

  • Above about 10 °C, the diazonium ion decomposes: nitrogen gas bubbles off and phenol forms.
  • C6H5N2⁺ + H2O → C6H5OH + N2 + H⁺
  • It survives when cold because the –N⁺≡N group is delocalised with the ring.
  • Aliphatic primary amines have no ring to stabilise the ion: it decomposes even when cold.
  • e.g. CH3CH2NH2 + HNO2 → CH3CH2OH + N2 + H2O (fizzing with N2).
Quick check
?Benzenediazonium chloride is made and used below 10 °C. What happens if the solution warms up?
Mission 2

Coupling with phenol

  • Dissolve phenol in NaOH(aq) and keep it cold.
  • Add cold benzenediazonium chloride: a yellow-orange solid forms at once.
  • The product is 4-hydroxyazobenzene: two rings joined by –N=N–, the azo group. (The parent compound, azobenzene, is an orange solid.)
  • C6H5N2⁺Cl⁻ + C6H5OH + NaOH → C6H5N=NC6H4OH + NaCl + H2O
  • The nitrogen is kept. The new bond is at the 4-position of the phenol ring, opposite the OH.
Mission 2

Coupling with phenylamine

  • Phenylamine couples the same way, in weakly acidic solution.
  • Product: 4-aminoazobenzene, a yellow dye.
  • C6H5N2⁺ + C6H5NH2 → C6H5N=NC6H4NH2 + H⁺
  • Both couplings are electrophilic substitutions: the diazonium ion is a weak electrophile.
  • It reacts only with rings activated by –OH (or –O⁻) or –NH2.
Quick check
?Cold benzenediazonium chloride is added to a cold solution of phenol in NaOH(aq). Which is the organic product?
Mission 2

Why azo compounds are coloured

  • A substance is coloured if it absorbs some visible light: electrons move to a higher electronic level.
  • Benzene's gap between levels is large: it absorbs only ultraviolet, so it looks colourless.
  • Joining two rings through –N=N– gives one extended delocalised system.
  • More delocalisation → electronic levels closer together → smaller ΔE.
  • Smaller ΔE → longer wavelength absorbed (E = hc/λ), now in the visible.
  • The –N=N– group that brings this about is the chromophore.
Mission 2

The colour you see

  • You see the wavelengths that are not absorbed: the complementary colour.
  • Absorbs violet → looks yellow. Absorbs blue → looks orange. Absorbs green → looks red.
  • More extended delocalisation shifts absorption to longer wavelength: yellow → orange → red.
  • Groups like –OH and –NH2 on the rings extend the delocalisation further.
Quick check
?An azo dye absorbs most strongly at about 480 nm, in the blue part of the visible spectrum. What colour does its solution appear in white light?
Quick check
?Which reagents and conditions, in two steps, convert phenylamine into the yellow-orange azo dye 4-hydroxyazobenzene?
Mission check

?Benzenediazonium chloride couples with phenol to give a yellow-orange dye. With naphthalen-2-ol (two fused rings) it gives an orange-red dye. Why is the second dye redder?

Benzene alone absorbs only ultraviolet. Linking rings through –N=N– extends delocalisation, shrinks ΔE and moves absorption into the visible.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Amines: the N lone pair makes them bases (salts with acids, freed by alkali) and nucleophiles.
  • Make them: halogenoalkane + excess NH3; nitrile reduction; nitroarene + Sn / conc. HCl, then NaOH.
  • Base strength: aliphatic amine > NH3 > phenylamine (lone-pair availability).
  • Amines + acyl chlorides → N-substituted amides.

Mission 2

  • Phenylamine + HNO2 (NaNO2 + HCl) below 10 °C → benzenediazonium chloride. Warm: phenol + N2.
  • Couple with phenol (alkaline) or phenylamine → azo dye, –N=N– between two rings.
  • Extended delocalisation → smaller ΔE → visible light absorbed → complementary colour seen.
