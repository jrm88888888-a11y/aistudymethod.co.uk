Mission 1

Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Is anything not scarce?

  • Economic good: uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Almost everything.
  • Free good: needs no scarce resources; more is available than people want, even at a zero price. Zero opportunity cost.
  • Examples: air on a remote mountain, seawater, sunlight.
  • Free of charge is not the same as a free good. A state school uses teachers, buildings and tax revenue: an economic good given at a zero price.
  • Free goods can become scarce: clean air in a polluted city.
  • Say: "a free good has zero opportunity cost" — not: "a free good is anything with a zero price".
Quick check
?Which of the following is most likely to be a free good?
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Firm: money spent on a new machine cannot also pay for an advert campaign.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. another lesson compares market, planned and mixed economies.
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is opportunity cost in practice?

It depends on:

  • Information: the next best alternative may be unknown or uncertain when the choice is made.
  • Valuation: many alternatives have no price (time, health, a clean environment), so they are hard to compare.
  • Time: some costs fall on future generations, as when a resource is over-used.
  • Whose cost: a firm's choice may impose costs on others (pollution) that it does not count.
  • Judgement: the idea always applies, but measuring it can be hard.
Mission check

?A city makes its buses free to ride. The buses are paid for from the city's tax revenue. Which statement about this choice is correct?

Scarce resources have other uses, so even a "free" service costs society the next best thing those resources could have made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does each extra unit of one thing cost a country more and more of another?

Make a guess: A country moves workers from farms to factories. Each extra batch of machines costs it more food than the last. What is the most likely reason?

Mission 2

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources and technology.
  • Your course calls it the production possibility frontier (PPF). Use "PPF" in answers.
  • Assumptions: only two goods; a fixed quantity and quality of resources; fixed technology; on the curve, all resources are fully and efficiently used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice and trade-offs.

Mission 2

Shape: why the curve bows outward

  • Increasing opportunity cost → the curve is concave to the origin (bowed outward).
  • Why: resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first workers moved to machines are those best at machines and worst at farming. Later ones are expert farmers.
  • Constant opportunity cost → a straight line. Resources are equally good at both goods.
  • The slope of the curve at any point shows the opportunity cost there.
  • Be ready to draw both: concave (increasing) and straight-line (constant).
Mission 2Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. The more food is made, the more machines each extra tonne costs. On a straight line the cost is constant.
Mission 2

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources used → productively efficient.
  • Inside (point U): some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
  • Why an economy can be inside: a recession, unemployed workers, idle factories, strikes, resources stuck in the wrong uses.
  • Outside (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Allocative efficiency: the one point on the curve that best matches what society wants. The curve alone can't show which point that is.
  • From U, more of both goods is possible with no opportunity cost: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?On a PPC for food and machines, an economy is at a point inside the curve. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are reallocated between the goods. The curve stays put.
  • Shift outward: more or better resources, or better technology or productivity (education, training, investment, new discoveries).
  • Shift inward: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, depleted resources.
  • Skewed (non-parallel) shift: the change affects one good only. A better machine-making method moves the machines end only; the food end stays put.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government estimated the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" (reallocation) — not: "a shift" for a change of mix.
Mission 2Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: a skewed outward shift. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 2

More output: two routes

  • Actual growth: output rises, e.g. from U (inside) to a point on the curve. Uses spare resources. Short run.
  • Potential growth: the curve shifts outward. The economy's capacity rises. Long run.
  • Short-run growth = moving towards the curve; long-run growth = shifting it.
  • Both together: the economy reaches a point beyond today's curve.
  • Say: "actual growth" (more output) vs "potential growth" (more capacity) — not: "growth" for both.
Mission 2

The PPC and AD/AS

  • The LRAS curve is vertical at full-employment output (Yf): the most the economy can produce sustainably, just as on the PPC.
  • Outward PPC shift ↔ LRAS shifts right.
  • Point inside the PPC ↔ real GDP below Yf.
Quick check
?A country's farms and factories are unchanged, but thousands of skilled workers emigrate. At the same time, the government moves some workers from farming into building machines. How should this be shown on a PPC for food and machines?
Mission 2Evaluate

What the PPC can and can't tell you

  • It shows what is possible, not what is best. Choosing a point needs a value judgement about what society wants.
  • It depends on assumptions: two goods, fixed resources and technology. Real economies make millions of goods and change constantly.
  • Growth from an outward shift takes time. Investment, training and research pay off years later.
  • The curve shows output, not who gets it or the environmental cost of producing it.
  • It depends on why an economy is inside the curve: a short recession and a long-run problem (e.g. skills in the wrong places) need different fixes.

Mission 3: What sets the most a country can produce, and can extra spending push past it?

Make a guess: What sets the most a country can produce, and can extra spending push past it?

Mission 3

LRAS: the economy's capacity

  • Long run: long enough for money (nominal) wages and all other input prices to adjust fully to the price level.
  • Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): the real output the economy can produce when all its resources are fully employed.
  • This is its productive capacity (potential output), sometimes called the full-employment level of output, Yfe.
  • In the classical (neo-classical, monetarist) view, LRAS is vertical at that output.
  • Capacity depends on the quantity and quality of land, labour, capital and enterprise, and on technology, not on the price level.
  • "Full employment" still leaves some unemployment (people between jobs, for example). It is not zero.
Mission 3

Worked example: when all costs catch up

  • Illustrative data. The same firm as before: price 20, wages 12, materials 4, profit per unit 4.
  • Short run: the price level rises 10%, wages are stuck at 12, so profit per unit rises to 5.6. Output rises.
  • Long run: the wage contract is renegotiated. Wages catch up: 12 × 1.1 = 13.2.
  • Now every price and cost is 10% higher: 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4.
  • 4.4 is exactly 4 × 1.1: real profit per unit is unchanged.
  • So firms have no lasting reason to produce more. Output returns to capacity.
  • As wages rise, SRAS shifts left until the economy is back on LRAS: this is the link between SRAS and LRAS.
  • A higher price level, the same real output: the long-run curve is vertical.
Quick check
?Why is the long-run aggregate supply curve drawn vertical?
Mission 3

The Keynesian AS curve

  • Keynes argued an economy can stay below full employment for a long time: money wages rarely fall, even with mass unemployment.
  • The Keynesian AS curve has three sections (often drawn as a reverse L):
    • Horizontal: lots of spare capacity. Firms can hire idle workers and machines without raising costs. Output rises; the price level does not.
    • Upward-sloping: bottlenecks. Some industries and skills run short, so costs and prices start to rise.
    • Vertical at Yfe: full capacity. More output is impossible; only the price level rises.
  • Keynesian economists draw this as their long-run AS. The classical view draws LRAS vertical only.

Illustrative data. Keynesian AS: flat at a price level of 100 up to output 800; vertical at Yfe = 1000.

  • Takeaway: with spare capacity, the Keynesian curve says more AD raises output (600 → 800) at the same price level; with a vertical LRAS, in the long run more AD only raises the price level.
Mission 3

Two views, two policy messages

  • Classical/monetarist view:
    • the economy returns to Yfe by itself, as wages and prices adjust
    • in the long run, more AD only raises the price level
    • to raise output for good, raise capacity: supply-side policies
  • Keynesian view:
    • an economy can stay below Yfe; adjustment is slow or does not happen
    • with spare capacity, more AD (e.g. government spending) raises output and employment without much inflation
    • near Yfe, more AD mostly raises prices
  • Output below capacity: a negative output gap. Above it: a positive output gap.
  • Both views agree that capacity limits real output. They disagree on whether, and how fast, an economy returns to Yfe by itself.
Quick check
?An economy is getting close to full capacity. Some industries cannot find enough skilled workers; others still have idle machines. The government raises its spending, shifting AD to the right. According to the Keynesian AS curve, what is the most likely result?
Mission 3

What shifts LRAS

  • LRAS shifts right when capacity grows:
    • Quantity of factors: a larger labour force (population growth, net migration, more people choosing to work), more capital from investment, new natural resources.
    • Quality of factors: education, training and health raise human capital; better machines.
    • Technology: new methods let the same inputs make more.
    • Productivity and efficiency: more output per worker or per unit of capital.
    • Institutions: secure property rights, rule of law, a sound financial system, low corruption.
  • LRAS shifts left if capacity shrinks: war or natural disaster destroys capital, emigration, an ageing population.
  • Some changes shift both SRAS and LRAS: higher productivity cuts costs now and raises capacity. A temporary rise in oil prices shifts SRAS only.
  • Supply-side policies aim to shift LRAS right: education and training, infrastructure, tax incentives, deregulation, competition policy.
Mission 3

LRAS and the PPC tell the same story

  • The PPC shows the most an economy can make of two goods with all resources fully used.
  • LRAS shows the same capacity as a single total of real output.
  • On the PPC → output at LRAS. Inside the PPC → output below capacity (unused resources).
  • PPC shifts outwards ↔ LRAS shifts right: long-run economic growth.

Mission 3

Real world: Japan's shrinking workforce

  • Japan's working-age population (aged 15–64) peaked in 1995 at about 86.9 million.
  • By 2024 it was about 73.7 million: around 15% lower.
  • Fewer people of working age, other things equal, means less capacity: a force pushing LRAS left.
  • But LRAS also depends on how many of those people work, on capital, skills and technology.
  • Question to weigh: which of these could offset a shrinking workforce, and how fast?
Mission 3Evaluate

Can policy shift LRAS?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education and infrastructure take years, sometimes decades, to raise capacity.
  • Cost. Supply-side spending has an opportunity cost and adds to government borrowing until it pays off.
  • Whether it works. Training may not match the skills firms need; tax cuts may not change effort much.
  • Where the economy is. With a large negative output gap, Keynesians argue that weak AD, not capacity, is the main problem.
  • Side effects. Deregulation or lower benefits may raise inequality; there are trade-offs to weigh.
Mission check

?An economy is producing at its full-employment level of output. A boom in export orders shifts AD to the right. Its resources and technology do not change. According to the vertical LRAS model, what happens in the long run?

Capacity sets real output in the long run. More spending alone cannot push past it.

Mission 4

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 4

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 4

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 4

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 4

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 4

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 4

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission 4

Barter and its problem

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • Problem 1: it needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other offers, at the same time.
  • Problem 2: no common measure: with 100 goods, every good needs a price in terms of the other 99 (4,950 exchange rates).
  • Problem 3: indivisibility: a cow cannot be split to buy one pair of shoes.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: anything widely accepted in payment. Sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange far cheaper and quicker.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show, and how does money solve it?
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods; on it = productively efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable.
  • Bowed out = increasing opportunity cost (resources not equally suited); straight line = constant.
  • Reallocation = movement along; more or better resources or technology = outward shift (skewed if one good only).

Mission 3

  • LRAS shows the economy's capacity: the output possible with all resources fully employed.
  • Classical/monetarist: LRAS vertical. Keynesian: AS horizontal with spare capacity, then rising, then vertical at Yfe.
  • LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of factors, technology and institutions.

Mission 4

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.
