Mission 1: If love is the only rule, does a Christian still need any other moral rules?

Make a guess: If love is the only rule, does a Christian still need any other moral rules?

Mission 1

Three ways to make a moral decision

  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), American Episcopal priest and ethicist; Situation Ethics: The New Morality (1966).
  • Legalism: fixed rules decide in advance, for every case. Fletcher: it puts the law before people (his examples: some Pharisees; rigid Catholic and Protestant moral codes).
  • Antinomianism ("against law"): no rules or principles at all; each decision is spontaneous. Fletcher: it is unprincipled and chaotic.
  • Situationism: the middle way. Enter each situation with the moral rules of your community as guides, but set any of them aside if love is better served.
  • Eduqas: agape as the "middle way" between the extremes of legalism and antinomianism.
Mission 1

The "new morality" of the 1960s

  • After 1945, many in Britain and America questioned inherited authority: in church, family, sexual morality and law.
  • John A. T. Robinson (1919–1983), Bishop of Woolwich, Honest to God (1963), chapter 6, "The New Morality": Christians should be guided by love alone, not by a code handed down from above.
  • Robinson drew on Bonhoeffer, Tillich and Bultmann; he gave evidence for the defence in the 1960 Lady Chatterley's Lover trial.
  • Fletcher's book three years later turned the idea into a method.
Mission 1

Agape: a love that is chosen

  • Greek has several words for love (the New Testament mainly uses agape and philia). C. S. Lewis, The Four Loves (1960), describes four: storge (affection), philia (friendship), eros (romantic love), agape (self-giving love; Lewis calls it "charity").
  • Agape is the love of God shown in Jesus: unconditional, self-giving, for every neighbour, including enemies.
  • Fletcher: agape is an act of the will, not a feeling. It is not the same as liking someone.
  • It always seeks the neighbour's good, and it can be "calculating": it thinks hard about consequences.
Mission 1Read the source

Love in Luke and Paul

Asked what the law requires, a lawyer gives the two great commandments, ending "…and your neighbour as yourself." He asks, "Who is my neighbour?" Jesus tells of a Samaritan who cares for a wounded Jewish traveller after a priest and a Levite pass by (Luke 10:25–36). Then:

> He said, "He who showed mercy on him." Then Jesus said to him, "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37, World English Bible, British Edition)

> Love is patient and is kind. (1 Corinthians 13:4)

> But now faith, hope, and love remain—these three. The greatest of these is love. (1 Corinthians 13:13)

On one common reading, the priest and Levite let religious rules or caution come first; the Samaritan put the wounded man's need first.

Mission 1

Jesus and Paul: situationists?

  • Sabbath: Jesus defends his hungry disciples for picking grain, citing David eating the holy bread (Mark 2:23–26; 1 Samuel 21:1–6).

> "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27)

  • He heals on the Sabbath, asking whether it is lawful to do good or harm (Mark 3:1–6).
  • The whole law hangs on love of God and neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40).
  • Paul: "Love therefore is the fulfilment of the law." (Romans 13:10)
  • But: Jesus also says he came not to abolish the law but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17), and makes some rules stricter (divorce, Mark 10:2–12). Whether Jesus was a situationist is disputed.

Mission 1

The boss principle and four working principles

  • Boss principle: agape alone is in charge; every other rule serves it.
  • Pragmatism: a moral choice must work in practice, towards love's goal.
  • Relativism: rules are relative to the situation; but always relative to love, which is not relative. ("Relative", not "anything goes".)
  • Positivism: love is accepted by faith, as a free commitment, not proved by reason ("theological positivism"). Christians start from "God is love" (1 John 4:8).
  • Personalism: people, not laws or things, are at the centre. Ask "who is helped?", not "what is the rule?"
Mission 1

The six propositions

Fletcher's six propositions (paraphrase):

1. Only love is intrinsically good; nothing else is good in itself.

2. Love is the ruling norm of Christian decision; it replaces all laws.

3. Love and justice are the same: justice is love distributed to everyone affected.

4. Love wills the neighbour's good, whether we like them or not.

5. Only the end (love) justifies the means.

6. Love decides situationally, not by rules prescribed in advance.

  • Eduqas calls these the six fundamental principles: love is the only good; love is the ruling norm of Christianity; love equals justice; love for all; loving ends justify the means; love decides situationally.
Quick check
?A situationist says: "Agape cannot be shared only with those close to me; it must be weighed out fairly among everyone affected." Which of Fletcher's propositions is this?
Mission 1

Conscience: a verb, not a noun

  • Fletcher rejects conscience as a thing (noun): an inner voice, a faculty, or a store of rules.
  • Conscience is what we do (verb): the work of making a decision, creatively, in a real situation.
  • It looks forward (deciding what to do), not mainly backward (guilt over what was done).
  • So there is no ready-made answer inside us; each person must work out what love requires.
  • Part of Fletcher's rejection of other approaches: conscience is not a fixed guide.
Mission 1

What kind of theory?

  • Teleological: judges an act by its end (telos): love.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right is its loving outcome for the people involved.
  • Relativist: the right act varies with the situation, though love itself is absolute.
  • Christian: Fletcher presents it as Christian ethics; love is the one absolute, accepted by faith.
  • Unlike utilitarianism, the good to be maximised is agape, not pleasure (comparison in Mission 2).
Mission 1Worked answer

Explaining the theory

Task: explain why Fletcher saw situation ethics as a middle way.

  • Point: Fletcher rejected legalism (rules before people) and antinomianism (no principles at all).
  • Scholar and source: He made agape the only absolute (Situation Ethics, 1966), drawing on Jesus: "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27).
  • Develop: Rules remain useful guides from the community's experience, but love may set them aside, as the Samaritan put the wounded man's need first (Luke 10:37).
  • Link: So the theory is relativist in method but has one fixed norm, which is why Fletcher calls it a middle way.
Mission check

?A school forbids phones in exams. A student whose mother is in hospital asks to keep hers switched on with the invigilator. Which comment is most clearly situationist reasoning?

For Fletcher, rules are like a map drawn from past journeys. Use it, but if love sees a better path in this place, take it.

Mission 2

How to apply situation ethics

  • 1. The situation: what exactly is happening, and what is at stake?
  • 2. The people: who is affected? (personalism: persons, not rules, come first.)
  • 3. The rules: what guidance does the community's experience give? (useful, not binding.)
  • 4. Love's verdict: which choice brings the most agape to everyone affected? (pragmatism, relativism, with love as the one absolute.)
  • Fletcher's answers can differ from case to case, by design.
Mission 2

Fletcher's own case: Mrs Bergmeier

  • From Fletcher's book (paraphrase): at the end of the Second World War, a German mother is held in a Soviet camp in Ukraine. Her husband and children, back in Berlin, are searching for her.
  • Only the sick and the pregnant are being released. She asks a guard to make her pregnant; she is released and reunited with her family.
  • The family welcome the baby, and love him for what he did for them.
  • Fletcher asks: did she do right? The rule against adultery is set beside the good of her husband, her children and the new child.
  • Fletcher's point is the question, not a fixed answer: legalism cannot even see the dilemma.
Mission 2

Applied to same-sex relationships

  • Personalism and love wills the neighbour's good: the question is whether the relationship serves the good of both partners and those around them.
  • So many situationists judge a faithful, loving same-sex relationship right: no rule outranks agape.
  • Biblical rules often cited (Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27) would be guides, set aside if love is better served.
  • A situationist judges any relationship, same-sex or not, by agape: an exploitative one would be wrong.
  • Different Christian views: Catholic teaching: same-sex acts cannot be approved, while people must be treated with respect, compassion and sensitivity (Catechism 2357–2358, paraphrase); many evangelicals agree on the acts. The Methodist Church in Britain (2021) and the Scottish Episcopal Church (2017) allow same-sex marriage.
  • Evaluation issue: "whether agape should replace religious rules".
Mission 2

Applied to polyamorous relationships

  • Polyamory: more than one loving, intimate relationship at once, with the knowledge and consent of everyone involved.
  • Relativism: monogamy is a strong rule of the community, but no rule except love is absolute.
  • A situationist would ask: is everyone fully informed and consenting? Are any children cared for? Is anyone exploited or harmed by jealousy or neglect?
  • Love and justice are the same: can love be distributed fairly among several partners and their families?
  • Critics: Christian marriage is between two people who "will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; see Genesis 2:24). Some argue that exclusive commitment is itself part of what agape asks.
  • Situation ethics does not give one answer: it gives a method. UK law recognises marriage only between two people.

Mission 2

Situation ethics and utilitarianism

  • Similar: both are teleological and consequentialist; both judge acts case by case; both reject fixed rules as final.
  • Fletcher borrows the utilitarian procedure: an "agapeic calculus", seeking the greatest amount of neighbour welfare for the largest number of neighbours (paraphrase).
  • Different: the good maximised is agape, not pleasure or happiness.
  • Different: Fletcher's starting point is a faith commitment (positivism); utilitarianism claims to rest on observable facts about pleasure and pain.
Mission 2Read the source

Barclay's critique

William Barclay (1907–1978), Scottish New Testament scholar, Ethics in a Permissive Society (1971), chapter 4 (paraphrase):

  • Agrees: love is central; rigid legalism is wrong; morality needs freedom.
  • Extreme cases: most of Fletcher's examples are extraordinary. Ordinary life needs ordinary rules; hard cases do not show we can do without law.
  • Too much freedom: deciding everything alone, with no ready answer, is a burden most people are not ready to carry.
  • People need law: as a child is trained by habit, people need rules; without love, freedom easily becomes licence.
  • Grace is missing: Fletcher says little about God's power to change people.

Show answer

Q2 Why does he think law is still needed?

Show answer

Most people do not want total freedom; rules train us, and freedom without love becomes licence.

Mission 2

Is Fletcher's agape really religious?

  • Religious, yes: agape comes from the New Testament (1 Corinthians 13; Luke 10); positivism means accepting "God is love" by faith.
  • Not really: the method never needs God; love could mean "wanting the best for the person involved". A humanist could use it unchanged.
  • Fletcher himself later left Christian belief and was named Humanist of the Year by the American Humanist Association (1974).
  • Individualistic and subjective? Without absolute rules, each person decides alone; two people may reach opposite "loving" answers.
  • Reply: love is not whatever I feel; it is judged by its effects on others, and community rules still guide.
Mission 2Diversity

How churches responded

  • Catholic: Pope Pius XII criticised "situation ethics" in 1952, and the Holy Office (1956) forbade it in Catholic teaching; Veritatis Splendor (1993) teaches that some acts are always wrong.
  • Protestant critics: Paul Ramsey, Deeds and Rules in Christian Ethics (1967): love itself needs rules ("rule agapism").
  • Liberal Protestants: Robinson and others welcomed the stress on persons over codes.
  • Evangelicals: generally reject it: scripture's commands are God's revealed will, not optional guides.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is situation ethics a helpful method?

  • Claim: Situation ethics is a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Person-centred; flexible in hard cases; avoids legalism's cruelty; one clear principle; rooted in Jesus' ethic of love.
  • For: It aims at justice ("love distributed") for everyone affected.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; love's verdict is subjective; it can seem to justify adultery or killing (Barclay; Pius XII).
  • Against: It may promote immoral behaviour by those who rationalise their wishes as "love"; it may be humanism with Christian words.
  • Judgement criteria: Can ordinary people use it day to day? Is agape the only intrinsic good? Does it keep what religious rules protect? Does it need God?
  • Spec issues: whether it promotes immoral behaviour; the extent to which it promotes justice.
Quick check
?Which strength of situation ethics does even Barclay, a critic, accept?

Mission 3

Situation ethics applied

  • Fletcher: only agape is always good; rules about sex are guides, not absolutes.
  • Personalism: put people first. Ask: is anyone used, deceived or harmed? Is this the most loving act?
  • Premarital sex: may be right in a loving, responsible relationship; wrong if it exploits.
  • Adultery: almost always betrays someone and harms them, so usually wrong, but not by rule.
  • Same-sex relationships: a faithful, loving relationship can be right.
  • Polyamorous relationships: right only if everyone knows and consents, and no partner or child is neglected; love and justice must be shared fairly.
  • Full treatment of these two applications.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

Are normative theories useful in sexual ethics?

  • Claim: Normative theories are useful for deciding sexual ethics.
  • For: They give reasons, not just reactions: a utilitarian or Kantian argument can be tested.
  • For: Situation ethics and utilitarianism attend to real people and harms; Kant's "never merely as a means" names the wrong in exploitation.
  • Against: Natural law and Kant reached conclusions on homosexuality that many now see as products of their age; theories may just rationalise prior views.
  • Against: Sex involves love, trust and vulnerability: hard to calculate (utilitarianism) or universalise (Kant).
  • Judgement criteria: Does the theory give clear guidance? Does it respect persons? Can it handle new kinds of relationship? Does it depend on religious belief?
  • Spec issues: "whether situation ethics promotes immoral behaviour"; "the extent to which situation ethics promotes justice".
Mission check

?A married couple, with young children, are considering an "open marriage" in which each may have other partners, with full knowledge and consent. Which reading of situation ethics is accurate?

Situation ethics gives no rule-book answer; it asks what agape requires for every person the choice affects.

Mission 4

Judging by results

  • Teleological (from telos, end or goal): an act is judged by the goal it achieves.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right or wrong is its consequences, not the act in itself or the motive.
  • Utilitarianism: the right act is the one with the best consequences for happiness: the most pleasure and least pain overall.
  • Founded in its classic form by Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (printed 1780, published 1789).
  • Contrast: deontological ethics (Kant, natural law) judge the act itself, by duty or rule.
Mission 4

Bentham's world

  • Enlightenment: morality and law should rest on reason and evidence, not tradition or religious authority.
  • Empiricism: Hume had grounded morals in human feelings of pleasure and approval.
  • The phrase "greatest happiness for the greatest numbers" had already appeared in Francis Hutcheson (1725); Bentham also credited Joseph Priestley and the Italian penal reformer Cesare Beccaria (1764).
  • Industrial Revolution Britain: growing cities, harsh punishments, a chaotic legal system that Bentham attacked in A Fragment on Government (1776).
  • Bentham wanted a science of morals and law that anyone could check.
Mission 4Read the source

Two sovereign masters

Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), chapter I, paragraph 1:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."

  • Two claims: pleasure and pain determine what we do (a claim about human psychology), and point out what we ought to do (a moral claim).

Q1 Which words make the moral claim?

Show answer

"point out what we ought to do"

Q2 Which words make the psychological claim?

Show answer

"determine what we shall do"

Mission 4

The principle of utility

  • Utility: the tendency of an act to produce pleasure, benefit or happiness, or to prevent pain.
  • Principle of utility: approve an act if it tends to increase the happiness of those affected; disapprove if it tends to decrease it.
  • "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" (A Fragment on Government, 1776): the measure of right and wrong.
  • Hedonism: pleasure is the only thing good in itself; pain the only thing bad in itself. Happiness = pleasure and the absence of pain.
  • Equality: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham's rule, as Mill reports it).
  • Quantity only: kinds of pleasure do not differ in value. Bentham: push-pin (a children's game) is as valuable as poetry, if it gives as much pleasure.

Mission 4

The hedonic calculus

Bentham's way of measuring the pleasure or pain an act will cause (IPML, chapter IV), seven factors:

  • Intensity: how strong? · Duration: how long?
  • Certainty: how likely? · Propinquity (remoteness): how soon?
  • Fecundity: will it lead to more pleasure? · Purity: is it free from pain afterwards?
  • Extent: how many people are affected?
  • Bentham's own memory verse: "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— / Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."
  • Eduqas wording: intensity, duration, certainty, remoteness, fecundity, purity and extent.
Quick check
?Reading for pleasure tends to make further pleasures possible: new interests, friendships and skills. Which factor of the hedonic calculus does this describe?
Mission 4

Act utilitarianism

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act, in its own situation, by its consequences. No act is right or wrong in itself.
  • So lying, or breaking a promise, can be right if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain here.
  • Relativist: the right act is relative to the situation and its consequences; only the principle of utility is fixed.
  • Bentham applied it above all to law and government: a law is good if it increases the community's happiness.
  • Eduqas: act utilitarianism as a form of moral relativism, a consequentialist and teleological theory.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is Bentham's act utilitarianism a good guide?

  • Claim: Bentham's act utilitarianism is a good way to decide what is right.
  • For: Simple and practical; based on something everyone recognises (pleasure and pain); every person counts equally.
  • For: Looks at real consequences; secular, so usable by anyone; inspired real reforms (and includes animals).
  • Against: Can justify injustice: harming an innocent minority if the majority gain enough pleasure.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; pleasures are hard to measure and compare; motives and rights seem to count for nothing.
  • Judgement criteria: Can pleasure be measured? Does justice for individuals matter more than totals? Is pleasure really the only good?
Mission 4Worked answer

Using the calculus

Task: explain how Bentham's hedonic calculus would decide whether a town should replace a park with a car park.

  • Point: Bentham would compare the pleasure and pain each option produces for everyone affected.
  • Apply the factors: extent (many shoppers vs local families), duration (permanent loss of green space), fecundity (exercise and community events lead to further pleasures), purity (pollution afterwards).
  • Develop: The option with the greater balance of pleasure over pain is right. No act is right in itself: this is act utilitarianism.
  • Link: The answer could change with the facts (no act is right or wrong in itself), which is both a strength (flexibility) and a weakness (uncertainty).
Mission check

?A city council can afford either a big fireworks festival or a free vaccination clinic. Which reasoning is Bentham's act utilitarianism?

Bentham's answer might well be the clinic. But he would reach it by adding up pleasure and pain, not by ranking kinds of pleasure.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fletcher's situation ethics (1966) is a middle way between legalism and antinomianism; Robinson's "new morality" (1963) is its setting.
  • Agape (self-giving love, willed not felt) is the only absolute: the boss principle; the four working principles are pragmatism, relativism, positivism and personalism; the six propositions explain love's role.
  • Conscience is a verb: the act of deciding. Fletcher drew on Jesus (Luke 10; Mark 2:27) and Paul (1 Corinthians 13; Romans 13:10).

Mission 2

  • Applying situation ethics: the situation, the people, the rules as guides, then love's verdict.
  • Same-sex and polyamorous relationships are judged by agape, not by fixed rules; churches differ sharply.
  • Close to utilitarianism in method (an "agapeic calculus"), but the good is agape. Critics: Barclay (extreme cases; the need for law; too much freedom), Catholic teaching (Pius XII, Veritatis Splendor), Ramsey; is agape really religious, and is the method subjective?

Mission 3

  • Situation ethics asks what agape requires for every person affected; rules are guides, not absolutes.

Mission 4

  • Utilitarianism is teleological and consequentialist: the right act produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. For Bentham, pleasure is the only good, measured by quantity.
  • The hedonic calculus: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent. Act utilitarianism judges each act afresh (relativist).
