Mission 1

Mission 1: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Make a guess: Each parent passes on chromosomes, yet the number never doubles. How is it halved so exactly?

Mission 1

Why halve?

  • Body cells are diploid (2n): two sets of chromosomes, one set from each parent. Human 2n = 46.
  • Gametes are haploid (n): one set. Human n = 23.
  • Fertilization fuses two gametes: n + n = 2n.
  • Without halving, the number would double every generation.
  • Meiosis is the reduction division: one diploid cell → four haploid cells.
  • Say: "a haploid cell has one of every chromosome" — not: "half of every chromosome".
Mission 1

Homologous pairs

  • Homologous chromosomes: a matching pair, one maternal, one paternal.
  • Same size and shape; the same genes at the same loci.
  • They may carry different alleles of those genes.
  • After replication, each homologue has two identical sister chromatids.
  • Sister chromatids are copies. Homologues are not.
  • Say: "the two chromatids of one chromosome are identical copies" — not: "one chromatid came from each parent".
Quick check
?A diploid nucleus of an organism with 2n = 14 undergoes meiosis. What is produced?
Mission 1

Meiosis I: the pairs separate

  • DNA is replicated once, before meiosis I. Then come two divisions.
  • Prophase I: chromosomes condense. Homologues pair up, forming a bivalent.
  • Pairing is called synapsis.
  • Non-sister chromatids cross over at points called chiasmata. The nuclear envelope breaks down; the spindle forms.
  • Metaphase I: pairs line up on the equator. Each pair faces the poles independently of the others.
  • Anaphase I: homologues are pulled to opposite poles. Sister chromatids stay joined.
  • Telophase I: two cells, each haploid. Each chromosome still has two chromatids.
Mission 1

Meiosis II: the chromatids separate

  • No DNA replication between the two divisions.
  • Prophase II: a new spindle forms in each cell.
  • Metaphase II: chromosomes line up singly on the equator.
  • The kinetochore of each chromatid is attached to a microtubule from one pole.
  • Anaphase II: centromeres divide; sister chromatids move to opposite poles.
  • Proteins at the centromeres break down, freeing the chromatids.
  • Telophase II: nuclear envelopes form. Four haploid cells, each chromosome a single chromatid.
  • Cytokinesis follows each division: cleavage furrow (animals) or cell plate (plants).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Make a guess: Brothers and sisters share both parents, yet each gets a different genetic mix. Where does it come from?

Mission 2

Three shuffles

  • Every gamete a parent makes carries a new combination of alleles.
  • Shuffle 1: how each homologous pair lines up at metaphase I.
  • Shuffle 2: crossing over between homologues in prophase I.
  • Shuffle 3: random fertilization: which gamete meets which.
  • Say: "meiosis makes new combinations of alleles" — not: "meiosis makes new alleles".
Mission 2

Shuffle 1: independent assortment

  • At metaphase I, each homologous pair faces the poles at random.
  • Each pair's orientation is independent of every other pair's.
  • So each gamete gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes.
  • The term is random assortment of chromosomes.
  • Say: "each pair lines up independently" — not: "all the maternal chromosomes go to one pole".
Mission 2

Shuffle 2: crossing over

  • In prophase I, homologues pair up (a bivalent).
  • Non-sister chromatids (one from each homologue) break and rejoin at chiasmata.
  • They exchange equivalent sections, so alleles swap between homologues.
  • The result: recombinant chromatids, carrying new combinations of alleles.
  • This is also called recombination.
  • So even the four cells from one meiosis can all differ.
  • Say: "non-sister chromatids exchange sections" — not: "sister chromatids swap sections".
Quick check
?Which statement describes crossing over during meiosis?
Mission 2

Shuffle 3, and why sex pays

  • Any sperm can fertilize any egg: random fertilization. Each zygote is a new combination from two parents.
  • Say: "variation arises at random; selection acts on it afterward" — not: "offspring get the variation they need".
Mission 2

When separation fails: non-disjunction

  • Non-disjunction: a homologous pair (anaphase I) or two sister chromatids (anaphase II) fail to separate.
  • One gamete gets an extra chromosome (n + 1); another lacks it (n − 1).
  • Fertilized by a normal gamete → a zygote with 2n + 1 (three copies: trisomy) or 2n − 1.
  • Mitosis copies this into every body cell.
  • Down syndrome: usually three copies of chromosome 21, so 47 chromosomes.
  • The chance of non-disjunction in eggs rises with the mother's age.
  • Say: "the extra chromosome comes from non-disjunction in meiosis, before fertilization" — not: "it is made at fertilization".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical animal with 2n = 10, nondisjunction produces an egg with 6 chromosomes. The egg is fertilized by a normal sperm, and the zygote develops into an offspring. Which prediction about the offspring's body cells is correct?
Mission check

?A mutation in a hypothetical species of animal prevents crossing over during meiosis, but meiosis is otherwise normal. Compared with the gametes of a normal individual, which prediction about the gametes of an individual with this mutation is correct?

Take one shuffle away and the others still work. Siblings differ because meiosis shuffles twice and fertilization deals once more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Make a guess: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Mission 3

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA). Its position on a chromosome is its locus.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene, at the same locus.
  • Body cells are diploid: two copies of each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has, e.g. Tt.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable traits. It results from the genotype and the environment.
  • Gametes are haploid: one allele of each gene. Fertilization makes a diploid zygote with one allele from each parent.
  • Say: "two alleles of the gene for height" — not: "a gene for tall and a gene for short".
Mission 3

Dominant and recessive

  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present. Capital letter: T.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when homozygous (tt). Lower-case letter: t.
  • So TT and Tt plants are equally tall.
  • Why? T codes for a functional protein; one copy makes enough of it.
  • t usually codes for a non-functional or absent product. It is still there, and still passed on.
  • Say: "the recessive allele has no effect in the heterozygote" — not: "the dominant allele switches it off".
Quick check
?In peas the allele T for tall stems is dominant to the allele t for dwarf stems, and TT and Tt plants are equally tall. What is the reason that the heterozygote is not shorter than the homozygous dominant plant?
Mission 3

The monohybrid cross

  • P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf). Gametes: all T; all t. F1: all Tt, all tall.
  • F2: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes T and t gametes in equal numbers.
  • A Punnett square gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
  • The two alleles separate into different gametes. That is the law of segregation.
  • As probabilities: P(tt) = ½ × ½ = ¼. P(Tt) = (½ × ½) + (½ × ½) = ½.
  • Product rule: P(A and B) = P(A) × P(B). Sum rule: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B).
  • The hook: both tall parents were Tt. The short plant is the ¼ that got t from each.

Mission 3

A recessive disorder in a family

  • Some human disorders are caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene.
  • Heterozygotes are unaffected carriers. Two carriers can have an affected child.
  • Cystic fibrosis: caused by recessive alleles of the CFTR gene.
  • Carrier × carrier: each child has a ¼ chance of being affected.
  • Each fertilization is independent. Earlier children do not change the odds.
Quick check
?Cystic fibrosis is caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene. Two parents who are both heterozygous for this gene have three children, all of whom have cystic fibrosis. Which statement correctly describes the probability that their fourth child will have cystic fibrosis?
Mission 3

The test cross

  • A tall plant could be TT or Tt. You cannot tell by looking.
  • Testcross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt) plant.
  • tt passes on only t, which masks nothing. So each offspring shows the allele it got from the tall plant.
  • All tall offspring: the plant is probably TT.
  • Any dwarf offspring: the plant must be Tt. Expect about 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
Quick check
?To find out whether a purple-flowered pea plant is homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp), a breeder crosses it with a white-flowered plant (pp). Why is a white-flowered plant used for this test cross?
Mission check

?In mice, the allele B for black fur is dominant to the allele b for brown fur. A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse. Of the 9 offspring, 5 are black and 4 are brown. Which conclusion is correct?

Like the short pea plant in the hook, the brown pups reveal a recessive allele that was there all along, hidden in a heterozygote.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Make a guess: If two genes sit on the same chromosome, can they ever be separated?

Mission 4

Two genes on different chromosomes

  • A plant is RrYy. R and Y are on different chromosomes.
  • In meiosis I, each homologous pair lines up independently of the other pair.
  • So R is just as likely to go with Y as with y.
  • Gametes: RY, Ry, rY, ry in equal numbers.
  • This is independent assortment.
  • Each gamete still gets one allele of each gene (segregation).
Mission 4

Worked example: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1

Peas: R round > r wrinkled; Y yellow > y green. F1 RrYy × RrYy.

  • Each parent makes 4 gamete types: a 4 × 4 Punnett square, 16 cells.
  • Group the cells by phenotype: 9 round yellow : 3 round green : 3 wrinkled yellow : 1 wrinkled green.
  • Short cut: each gene gives 3 : 1. P(round and yellow) = ¾ × ¾ = 9/16.
  • 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 is a phenotype ratio. The 16 cells hold 9 different genotypes.
  • Mendel's real F2: 315 : 108 : 101 : 32. Close to 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 (tested in a later lesson).
Mission 4

The dihybrid test cross: 1 : 1 : 1 : 1

  • Cross the double heterozygote with a double homozygous recessive: RrYy × rryy.
  • rryy gives only ry gametes. So each offspring shows the gamete from the heterozygote.
  • Four gamete types, equally likely → 1 : 1 : 1 : 1.
  • Round yellow : round green : wrinkled yellow : wrinkled green.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of plant, genes A, B and D are on different chromosomes, and each gene has one dominant and one recessive allele. Two plants with genotype AaBbDd are crossed. What is the probability that an offspring has the genotype AaBbDd?
Mission 4

Linked genes

  • Genes whose loci are on the same chromosome are linked.
  • The AP term is genetically linked.
  • Their alleles travel together into gametes. They do not assort independently.
  • But in prophase I, crossing over between non-sister chromatids can swap them.
  • A crossover between the two loci gives recombinant chromatids: new combinations of alleles.
  • The closer the loci, the less often a crossover falls between them.
  • Say: "autosomal linkage" — not "sex linkage". Linkage means the same chromosome, any chromosome.
  • So the answer to the mission's question: yes. Crossing over separates linked alleles, but only in some meioses.
Quick check
?In a testcross involving two genes that are close together on the same chromosome, most offspring show the parental combinations of traits and a small percentage show new combinations. Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 4

AaBb × aabb. Illustrative data, 100 offspring:

  • Unlinked genes would give about 1 : 1 : 1 : 1. This is far from it.
  • Two large classes: parental combinations (AB and ab were on the same chromosomes in the heterozygote).
  • Two small classes: recombinants (Ab, aB), made by crossing over.
  • Parental classes are the large ones. They need not be "both dominant" and "both recessive".
Mission 4

Recombination frequency and map units

  • Recombination frequency = recombinant offspring ÷ total offspring × 100%.
  • From the earlier screen: (8 + 7) ÷ 100 × 100% = 15%.
  • 1% recombination = 1 map unit. So the genes are 15 map units apart.
  • Farther apart → more crossovers between them → higher frequency.
  • The maximum is about 50%: then the genes behave as if unlinked.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Make a guess: Why do some inherited conditions turn up far more often in sons than in daughters?

Mission 5

Sex chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 22 pairs of autosomes + one pair of sex chromosomes.
  • Female: XX. Male: XY.
  • Every egg carries an X. A sperm carries X or Y. So the sperm decides the sex of the zygote.
  • The X carries far more genes than the much smaller Y.
  • A gene on a sex chromosome is sex-linked. Most are on the X: X-linked.
  • A male has one copy of each X-linked gene. A female has two.
  • Y-linked genes pass only from father to son.
  • Not every species uses XY: in birds, females are ZW and males ZZ; in bees, males develop from unfertilized eggs (haploid).
Mission 5

Writing an X-linked cross

  • Write the allele as a superscript on the X: Xᴴ (normal clotting), Xʰ (hemophilia). The Y carries no allele.
  • Hemophilia: a recessive X-linked allele; a clotting factor is missing, so blood clots slowly.
  • Carrier mother × unaffected father: XᴴXʰ × XᴴY.
  • Gametes: Xᴴ or Xʰ · Xᴴ or Y.
  • Offspring: XᴴXᴴ, XᴴXʰ (carrier), XᴴY, XʰY (affected).
  • Daughters: none affected, ½ carriers. Sons: ½ affected.
Quick check
?A woman who is a carrier of hemophilia (XᴴXʰ) has children with a man who does not have hemophilia (XᴴY). Their first child is a boy. What is the probability that he has hemophilia?
Mission 5

Why sons are affected more often

  • A male's one X comes from his mother. One recessive allele is enough: XʰY is affected.
  • A female needs two: XʰXʰ. That needs an affected father and a carrier or affected mother.
  • An affected father passes his X to every daughter (all carriers) and to no son.
  • So X-linked recessive traits never pass father → son.
  • Clue in cross data: sons and daughters show different ratios, and reciprocal crosses give different results.
  • Say: "a son inherits his X chromosome from his mother" — not: "hemophilia passes from father to son".
Mission 5

Reading a pedigree

  • Square = male; circle = female; shaded = affected. Generations I, II, III; individuals numbered left to right.
  • Two unaffected parents with an affected child → the allele is recessive; both parents carry it.
  • Two affected parents with an unaffected child → the allele is dominant.
  • Recessive allele + affected daughter of an unaffected father → not X-linked, so autosomal.
  • A father passes his X to no son. If a son can only have got the allele from his father, the gene is not X-linked.
Quick check
?A man and a woman who are both unaffected by a genetic disorder have three children: an affected daughter, an unaffected son and an unaffected daughter. The affected daughter later has four children with an unaffected man, and all four are unaffected. What can be deduced about the disorder?

Mission 5

Predicting from a pedigree

  • Step 1: dominant or recessive? Look for unaffected parents with an affected child.
  • Step 2: autosomal or X-linked? Check affected daughters' fathers and affected fathers' sons.
  • Step 3: write the genotypes you know (affected recessives; carriers revealed by children).
  • Step 4: use a cross to give a probability for the next child.
  • Keep "sex-linked" for genes on sex chromosomes. Genes linked on the same autosome are a different idea (see Mission 4).
Mission check

?In birds, the sex chromosomes are Z and W: males are ZZ and females are ZW. In a hypothetical species of bird, a feather trait is caused by a recessive allele of a gene on the Z chromosome; the W chromosome carries no allele of this gene. In a population in which this allele is present, which sex is expected to show the trait more often, and why?

It is not about being male. It is about having only one copy of the chromosome that carries the gene.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Meiosis is a reduction division: one DNA replication, two divisions, four haploid cells, so fertilization restores the diploid number.
  • Meiosis I separates homologous pairs (the halving); meiosis II separates sister chromatids.

Mission 2

  • Independent assortment (orientation of pairs at metaphase I) and crossing over (prophase I) give every gamete a new combination of alleles.
  • Random fertilization multiplies the combinations again.
  • Non-disjunction gives gametes n + 1 or n − 1; Down syndrome is trisomy 21.

Mission 3

  • Alleles are forms of one gene; genotype + environment → phenotype; one dominant allele is enough for the dominant phenotype.
  • Heterozygote × heterozygote → 3 : 1; a test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals a hidden recessive allele.

Mission 4

  • Unlinked genes assort independently: 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 from two double heterozygotes, 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 from a test cross.
  • Linked genes travel together; crossing over between the loci makes a minority of recombinants.
  • Recombination frequency (%) = map units.

Mission 5

  • X-linked recessive alleles show in any male who carries one (one X), but in females only when homozygous; sons get their X from their mother.
  • Pedigrees: unaffected parents with an affected child → recessive; then an affected daughter with an unaffected father → autosomal.
