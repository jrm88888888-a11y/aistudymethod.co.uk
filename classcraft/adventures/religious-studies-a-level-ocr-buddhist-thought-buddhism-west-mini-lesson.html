Mission 1

How Buddhism reached Britain

  • Scholars first: the Pali Text Society (1881) translated the Pali Canon; Edwin Arnold's poem The Light of Asia (1879) made the Buddha's life widely known.
  • Early British monk: Allan Bennett, ordained in Burma as Ananda Metteyya, led a Buddhist mission to London in 1908.
  • Societies and viharas: Christmas Humphreys' Buddhist Lodge (1924, later the Buddhist Society); the London Buddhist Vihara (1926), founded by the Sri Lankan reformer Anagarika Dharmapala.
  • From the 1960s: Samye Ling, the first Tibetan centre in the West (Scotland, 1967); the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order (1967, now Triratna); Throssel Hole Soto Zen monastery (1972); Chithurst (1979) and Amaravati (1984).
  • Every major school is now present in Britain: Theravada, Zen, Tibetan, Pure Land, Nichiren and new Western forms.
Mission 1

Migration and diversity

  • Migration brought whole communities: Sri Lankans, Thais, Burmese, Chinese, Japanese; Vietnamese refugees from the late 1970s; Tibetans after 1959.
  • They founded temples as religious and community centres: language, festivals such as Wesak, food, care for elders.
  • At the 2021 Census, about 0.5% of people in England and Wales described themselves as Buddhist.
  • Diversity: many traditions, languages and ethnic groups; temples of one tradition may serve several nationalities.
  • Spec: the two main reasons for the spread are migration and the popularity of Buddhist ideas.
Mission 1

Why Buddhism became popular

  • No creator God and no demand for "blind faith": it fits a scientific, questioning age (the Kalama Sutta's call to test teachings).
  • Meditation and mindfulness: practical help with stress and the mind; mindfulness-based therapy is now used in the NHS.
  • Ethics without commandments: compassion, non-harm, personal responsibility.
  • Counter-culture: from the 1950s–60s, writers and seekers looked East (D. T. Suzuki's books on Zen; the Beat writers).
  • Role models: the Dalai Lama and Thich Nhat Hanh (see Mission 2).
  • Openness: people can attend a class or retreat without converting.
Mission 1

Heritage and convert Buddhism

  • Heritage Buddhism: the Buddhism of people whose families came from Buddhist countries and settled in Britain, including their British-born descendants. Convert Buddhism: that of people who have chosen Buddhism.
  • Typical heritage emphasis: devotion, offerings, merit (punya) through giving to monks, festivals, chanting in Pali or other Asian languages, family and community.
  • Typical convert emphasis: meditation, study, retreats, lay-led groups, less ritual, often in English.
  • These are tendencies: many heritage Buddhists meditate; many converts give dana to monks; second-generation British Buddhists blur the line.
  • The binary comes from scholarship: Charles Prebish wrote of "two Buddhisms" in America (1979). Others have questioned it: Jan Nattier proposed three routes in (imported by seekers, exported by missions, carried by migrants) (paraphrase).
  • Some find the labels uncomfortable when they imply that one group is more "authentic" or more "modern".

Mission 1

Buddhism in popular culture

  • Films: Little Buddha (1993), Seven Years in Tibet (1997), Kundun (1997) brought Tibetan Buddhism to mass audiences.
  • "Zen" became a marketing word for calm and minimal design; Buddha heads appear as garden and home decor (some Buddhists find this disrespectful).
  • "Karma" in everyday speech often means cosmic payback, not intentional action and its fruit.
  • Stereotypes: the always-serene monk; Buddhism as purely peaceful and apolitical; Tibet as a magical Shangri-La (from James Hilton's novel Lost Horizon, 1933).
  • Scholars such as Donald Lopez (Prisoners of Shangri-La, 1998) argue that Western fantasies of Tibet obscured real Tibetans' history and religion.
  • Media stereotypes can attract people to Buddhism and mislead them about it at the same time.
Mission 1Diversity

The Dalai Lama seen from inside and outside

  • Many Tibetan Buddhists: an emanation of Avalokitesvara; a lama giving teachings and empowerments; for decades the political leader of Tibetans in exile (until 2011); a symbol of national identity.
  • Many Western non-Buddhists: a smiling Nobel Peace laureate (1989), moral teacher of kindness, author of best-sellers on happiness; widely quoted online, often with sayings he never said.
  • Critics: the Chinese government calls him a separatist; followers of the protector Dorje Shugden protested his advice against that practice.
  • Georges Dreyfus (spec reading) contrasts his traditional role, bound up with ritual and protective deities, with his international image as a modern, rational teacher (paraphrase).
  • Spec issue: compare how figures such as the Dalai Lama are viewed by Buddhists and non-Buddhists, and their significance to each.

Does Western media distort Buddhism?

  • Claim: Western popular culture distorts Buddhism more than it spreads understanding.
  • For: stereotypes (Shangri-La, the serene monk, "zen" as lifestyle) hide history, conflict and diversity.
  • For: a celebrity Dalai Lama can stand in for all Buddhism, sidelining Theravada and heritage communities.
  • Against: films and famous teachers lead many people to real practice and study.

Mission 2

Two lives, two exiles

  • The 14th Dalai Lama, Tenzin Gyatso (b. 1935): recognised as a child in Tibet; fled to India in 1959 after the failed uprising against Chinese rule; based in Dharamsala; Nobel Peace Prize 1989; gave up his political role in 2011.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022): Vietnamese Zen (Thiền) monk; worked for peace during the Vietnam War; exiled from 1966; founded Plum Village in France (1982); returned to live in Vietnam in 2018 and died there (2022).
  • Both: monks, refugees, prolific authors, teaching Western audiences in plain language.
  • Dalai Lama's key note: compassion and kindness as the basis of happiness, for believers and non-believers alike.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh's key note: mindfulness in ordinary actions, and interbeing: nothing exists by itself alone.
Mission 2

Portrayal: the teacher and the image

  • Media image of the Dalai Lama: the laughing monk, Nobel laureate, author of happiness books, quoted (and misquoted) online; his religious role for Tibetans, as a lama giving teachings and empowerments (initiations into tantric practice), is less often shown, while his political campaign is widely reported (see Mission 1).
  • Media image of Thich Nhat Hanh: the gentle "father of mindfulness", invited to speak to tech companies and politicians; his roots in Vietnamese Zen and his war-time activism are often left out.
  • Effect: both images attract many people to Buddhism, but can present it as a calm lifestyle without rebirth, ritual, monastic life or Asian communities.
  • Their own part: both chose to present Buddhism in universal, non-sectarian terms for Western audiences: adaptation as well as media distortion.
  • Spec issue: how the fame of such figures may shape, "and possibly distort", Western understandings of Buddhism.

Mission 3

Buddhism meets a secular West

  • Secularisation: religion loses its place as the source of truth and morality, becomes a private matter, and is attacked as irrational (see Mission 1).
  • Some Buddhists respond by preserving tradition (Amaravati) or adapting it (Triratna).
  • Secular Buddhism goes further: it presents the dharma as a way of life that needs no belief in rebirth, karma as a cosmic law, or other unseen realms.
  • Spec term: inculturation: a religion changing and adapting as it meets a new culture, here Western science, ideas and culture.
  • Your spec pairs Batchelor with Paul Knitter.
Mission 3

Stephen Batchelor

  • Stephen Batchelor (b. 1953, Scotland): ordained as a monk in the Tibetan Gelug tradition (1974), then trained in Korean Seon (Zen); left monastic life in 1985 and became a lay teacher.
  • Key works: Buddhism Without Beliefs (1997); Confession of a Buddhist Atheist (2010); After Buddhism (2015); Secular Buddhism (2017).
  • He argues that the Buddha taught a practical, this-worldly path, later overlaid by Indian metaphysics and religious institutions.
  • Dharma practice is something you do, not something you believe.
Mission 3Read the source

Four truths as four tasks

In the Buddha's first sermon (the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta), each of the four noble truths comes with something to do (paraphrase):

  • Dukkha is to be fully understood.
  • Its arising (craving) is to be abandoned.
  • Its ceasing is to be realised.
  • The path is to be developed.

Batchelor reads this as the heart of the matter: not four truths to believe, but four tasks to carry out: embrace suffering, let go of reactive craving, see its stopping, cultivate the path (paraphrase of After Buddhism).

Q1 What changes if the truths are tasks?

Show answer

The test of a Buddhist becomes what they do and how they live, not what they assent to.

Q2 Which word in the sermon supports his reading?

Show answer

Each truth has a verb: understood, abandoned, realised, developed.

Mission 3

Agnostic about rebirth and karma

  • In Buddhism Without Beliefs, Batchelor calls for an agnostic stance: neither affirming nor denying rebirth, since it cannot be known.
  • Later works lean further: he treats rebirth and karma as a moral cosmos the Buddha inherited from his culture, not his distinctive teaching.
  • What remains central: dependent arising, mindfulness, the ethical path, and awakening understood as a way of living freely now.
  • He values the Kalama Sutta's call to test teachings in experience.
  • Critics, including traditional teachers such as the Theravada scholar-monk Bhikkhu Bodhi, reply that rebirth and a transcendent nibbana run through the whole Pali Canon; removing them may reduce Buddhism to therapy.

Mission 3

Paul Knitter: dual belonging

  • Paul Knitter (b. 1939): American Catholic theologian, formerly a missionary priest; long known for a pluralist theology of religions.
  • Without Buddha I Could Not Be a Christian (2009): Buddhist practice and ideas helped him remain a Christian.
  • In 2008 he formally took refuge in the Three Jewels while remaining a Catholic: dual belonging.
  • He uses Buddhist emptiness and interbeing to rethink God: not a person "out there" but the connecting, inter-being Spirit in which all exists (paraphrase).
  • Jesus and the Buddha both as awakened teachers; prayer as silent, meditative presence; Buddhist wisdom joined to Christian commitment to justice.
  • Critics: Catholic theologians say he weakens the uniqueness of Christ; some Buddhists say a Buddhism that fits a God is not Buddhism.
Mission 3Diversity

Batchelor and Knitter compared

  • Starting point: Batchelor: former monk, now a lay teacher who sets religious belief aside. Knitter: Christian theologian who found Buddhism.
  • God: Batchelor: a Buddhist atheist, no God and no transcendent realm. Knitter: keeps God, reinterpreted non-dually.
  • What they take from Buddhism: both stress practice, meditation and experience over doctrine.
  • How they Westernise: both put the individual's experience and choice at the centre; both reinterpret rebirth and the afterlife with agnostic humility; both bring Buddhism into dialogue with modern Western thought (science; Christian theology).
  • Spec issue: "the ways in which they have adopted and 'Westernised' Buddhist ideas".

Have Batchelor and Knitter Westernised Buddhism too far?

  • Claim: Batchelor and Knitter have changed Buddhism so much that it is no longer Buddhism.
  • For: Batchelor sets aside rebirth and karma, found throughout the Pali Canon.
  • For: Knitter fits Buddhist ideas around a God, which most Buddhist traditions do not teach.
  • Against: Buddhism has always adapted (India → China → Japan); inculturation is normal.

Mission 4

What "Engaged Buddhism" means

  • Engaged Buddhism: applying Buddhist teaching and practice to the social, political and environmental causes of suffering, not only to the individual mind.
  • It works on two levels at once: inner change (mindfulness, compassion) and outer change (service, protest, campaigning).
  • Liberationist: aims to free people from suffering caused by structures: war, poverty, caste, oppression, environmental damage.
  • It is a movement across many traditions (Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana, Western convert groups), not a separate school.
Mission 4

Origins: Vietnam

  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022), Vietnamese Zen monk (Lam Te/Linji lineage).
  • 1950s–60s: war in Vietnam. Monks and nuns faced a choice: stay in the monastery, or go out to the bombed villages.
  • His answer: do both. Mindfulness that ignores suffering around you is not real mindfulness (paraphrase).
  • 1964–65: founds the School of Youth for Social Service: young lay people and monastics rebuild villages, run clinics and schools.
  • 1966: founds the Order of Interbeing (Tiep Hien), with fourteen precepts; leaves on a peace tour and is exiled from Vietnam.
  • 1967: his book Vietnam: Lotus in a Sea of Fire spreads the phrase engaged Buddhism in English.
  • 1982: founds Plum Village in France, which spreads engaged practice to the West.
Mission 4

Origins: many roots, not one founder

  • China, 1920s–30s: the monk Taixu (1890–1947) calls for a "Buddhism for human life" that serves society.
  • India, 1956: B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) leads a mass conversion of Dalits (formerly called "untouchables") at Nagpur: Buddhism as escape from caste oppression.
  • Sri Lanka, 1958: A. T. Ariyaratne (1931–2024) founds Sarvodaya Shramadana: village self-help based on generosity and shared labour.
  • Thailand: Sulak Sivaraksa (b. 1933) critiques consumerism; co-founds the International Network of Engaged Buddhists (1989).
  • Aims shared across these: reduce suffering here and now; transform the causes (greed, hatred, delusion) in people and in institutions; act non-violently.
Mission 4

Why a Buddhist might engage

  • Dukkha is the starting point of the Four Noble Truths: the Buddha taught to end suffering, and much suffering has social causes.
  • Karuna (compassion) and metta (loving-kindness): wishing beings free from suffering leads to action.
  • Dependent origination: nothing exists alone; my peace depends on others' peace.
  • The precepts: the first precept (not harming) and right livelihood concern how society treats life.
  • Bodhisattva ideal (Mahayana): vow to work for the awakening of all beings (often described as putting off one's own final nirvana).
Mission 4Read the source

> "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129)

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5)

Mission 4

Interbeing

  • Thich Nhat Hanh's key word: interbeing. It restates dependent origination and emptiness in everyday terms.
  • His best-known image (paraphrase): look deeply at a sheet of paper and you see the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger in it. The paper "inter-is" with everything.
  • So the suffering of others is not outside me: their hunger, fear or anger is part of my world, and my actions feed into theirs.
  • Seeing leads to acting: for Thich Nhat Hanh, real mindfulness is engaged mindfulness (paraphrase of Peace Is Every Step, 1991).
  • Name of his order: Order of Interbeing.
Mission 4

The Fourteen Precepts (1): free minds, open eyes

  • Source: Thich Nhat Hanh, Interbeing: Fourteen Guidelines for Engaged Buddhism (1987; revised 1993). Plum Village now calls them the Fourteen Mindfulness Trainings.
  • Your spec: "the Fourteen Precepts".
  • 1: Do not worship any doctrine or ideology, even Buddhist ones: teachings are guides, not absolute truth.
  • 2: Do not think your present knowledge is final; stay open to others' views.
  • 3: Do not force views on anyone, children included, by authority, threat, money, propaganda or indoctrination.
  • 4: Do not avoid suffering or close your eyes to it; find ways to be with those who suffer.
  • 5: Do not pile up wealth while millions are hungry; live simply.
  • 6: Do not keep anger or hatred; look deeply to understand and transform it.
  • 7: Do not lose yourself in distraction; return to the present moment through mindful breathing.
  • (All paraphrased.)
Mission 4

The Fourteen Precepts (2): speech, community, life

  • 8: Do not speak words that cause division; work to reconcile conflicts.
  • 9: Speak truthfully; have the courage to speak out about injustice, even when it is dangerous.
  • 10: Do not use the Buddhist community for personal gain or turn it into a political party; but it should take a clear stand against oppression and injustice.
  • 11: Do not work in a job that harms people or nature (right livelihood).
  • 12: Do not kill or let others kill; find every means to protect life and prevent war.
  • 13: Do not take what belongs to others; stop others profiting from human suffering or the suffering of other beings.
  • 14: Do not mistreat your body; for lay members, sexual relations only with love and long-term commitment; monastics keep celibacy.
  • Notice the pattern: the first three guard the mind against dogma, the rest turn mindfulness outwards into society.

Mission 4Diversity

New movement or old Buddhism?

  • "It is new": Christopher Queen (with Sallie King, Engaged Buddhism, 1996) asks whether it is almost a new "vehicle", shaped by modern ideas such as human rights, democracy and social justice.
  • "It is old": Thich Nhat Hanh, Sulak Sivaraksa and others: Buddhism was always engaged. The Buddha advised kings, admitted people of all castes to the Sangha and taught lay duties (Sigalovada Sutta, Digha Nikaya 31); the emperor Ashoka provided medical care for people and animals, and dug wells (Rock Edict 2).
  • Traditional caution (some Theravada monastics): the monastic path is renunciation; the Vinaya limits monks' political involvement; social work is mainly the laity's role.
  • Mahayana emphasis: the bodhisattva who returns to the marketplace makes engagement feel native, not borrowed.
  • Damien Keown's assessment.

Mission 5

From mindfulness to activism

  • Social activism: organised action to change society: campaigns, protest, service, building alternatives.
  • Why a Buddhist may see it as practice, not a distraction:
  • Compassion (karuna): wishing beings free from suffering is incomplete without action.
  • Interdependence: harm to forests, the poor or the enemy is not separate from me.
  • Right action and right livelihood: the path includes how I act and earn in the world.
  • Non-violence (ahimsa): it shapes the method as much as the goal.
Mission 5

Why the movement grew

  • Suffering on a new scale: war in Vietnam and Cambodia, caste in India, poverty, nuclear weapons, climate change.
  • The primary drive: to combat suffering here and now, not only to gain a positive rebirth or one's own awakening.
  • High-profile leaders: the 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935) leads non-violent resistance over Tibet (Nobel Peace Prize, 1989); Thich Nhat Hanh.
  • Media coverage: images of monks marching or of peace walks reach a global audience.
  • Organisations: the Buddhist Peace Fellowship (founded 1978, Hawaii) and Sakyadhita (founded 1987, Bodh Gaya: an international association of Buddhist women; see Mission 7).
Mission 5

Environmental awareness and action

  • Thai "ecology monks": from the late 1980s, monks wrap trees in orange robes and ordain them, so cutting them would feel like harming a monk.
  • A Buddhist Declaration on Climate Change ("The Time to Act Is Now", 2009): drafted by David Loy and John Stanley with Bhikkhu Bodhi; calls for urgent cuts in emissions, grounded in interdependence and non-harm.
  • Plum Village: plant-based food, low-carbon living, and teaching that we must "fall in love with the Earth" (paraphrase of Thich Nhat Hanh).
  • Teachings used: dependent origination; the first precept extended to all living beings; craving (tanha) as the root of overconsumption.
  • Your spec's area: environmental awareness and action.
Mission 5

Joanna Macy: the greening of the self

  • Joanna Macy (1929–2025), Buddhist scholar and activist; Mutual Causality in Buddhism and General Systems Theory (1991); World as Lover, World as Self (1991).
  • Dependent co-arising as mutual causality: everything conditions everything else, like a living system.
  • The greening of the self: the self is not a separate thing; widened, it becomes an ecological self that includes the forest and future generations.
  • So protecting the Earth is not self-sacrifice: it is the self acting for its wider self (paraphrase).
  • Despair work / The Work That Reconnects: pain for the world is a sign of interconnection and compassion, not a weakness; facing it frees energy for action.
  • Her view of the movement: engagement flows naturally from core Buddhist insight; it is not a Western add-on.
Mission 5

Opposition to oppression, injustice and poverty

  • Caste: Ambedkar's Navayana movement (from 1956): Dalits embrace Buddhism to reject caste; schools, hostels, legal rights.
  • Poverty: Sarvodaya Shramadana (Sri Lanka): villages build roads, wells and pre-schools together; awakening of the person and the village together.
  • Tibet: the Dalai Lama's Middle Way Approach: genuine autonomy sought only through non-violence and dialogue.
  • Myanmar, 2007 ("Saffron Revolution"): monks march and overturn their alms bowls, refusing alms from the military: a traditional Vinaya sanction used as protest.
  • Women: Sakyadhita campaigns for education and ordination for Buddhist women.
Mission 5

War and peace

  • Vietnam: Thich Nhat Hanh's School of Youth for Social Service helped victims on all sides; he called for a ceasefire and was exiled for it.
  • Cambodia: Maha Ghosananda (d. 2007) leads the Dhammayietra peace walks from 1992, through land once mined, after the Khmer Rouge genocide.
  • Buddhist Peace Fellowship: founded by Robert and Anne Aitken and others; campaigns against nuclear weapons and war, works with prisoners.

> "Victory breeds hatred, for the conquered is unhappy. He who has given up both victory and defeat, he, the contented, is happy." (Dhammapada 201, Müller 1881)

Mission 5Diversity

Could a Buddhist ever accept war?

  • Never: the first precept; Dhammapada 5 and 201; the emperor Ashoka renounced conquest after the Kalinga war (Rock Edict 13). Most engaged Buddhists hold this view.
  • Defence of the Dharma: the Sri Lankan chronicle Mahavamsa (c. 5th–6th century CE) tells how King Dutthagamani was reassured after a war against a non-Buddhist king; some Sri Lankan monks have used it to back war (studied by Tessa Bartholomeusz, In Defense of Dharma, 2002).
  • Compassionate killing? A Mahayana text, the Upayakausalya Sutra, tells of a ship's captain (the Buddha in a past life) who kills a robber to save 500 merchants and to spare the robber the karma of mass murder; he accepts the bad karma himself.
  • History: Brian Victoria, Zen at War (1997), documents Japanese Zen support for militarism before 1945; many Zen bodies have since apologised.
  • Most Buddhists distinguish intention (cetana) and self-defence from aggression, and hold that killing always carries karmic weight.
Mission 5

Damien Keown: a scholar's assessment

  • Damien Keown (b. 1951), The Nature of Buddhist Ethics (1992); Buddhist Ethics: A Very Short Introduction (2005).
  • New: Engaged Buddhism is largely a modern development: early texts say much about personal ethics but little about political or social structures.
  • Western influence: its language of rights and justice shows contact with Western thought; critics ask whether its values are Buddhist or imported.
  • But grounded: Keown argues such values can be rooted in Buddhist teaching: e.g. human rights are implicit in the precepts ("Are There Human Rights in Buddhism?", 1995).
  • Task ahead: for the movement to last, it needs a clearer doctrinal basis linking action to the goal of nirvana (paraphrase).
  • Compare Macy: she sees engagement as flowing directly from dependent co-arising; Keown sees it as a modern development that must be justified from the sources.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should engagement be central to the Buddhist path?

  • Claim: Social engagement is central to the Buddhist path, not a distraction from it.
  • For: the path aims to end dukkha, and much dukkha is social; karuna without action is empty.
  • For: interbeing / dependent origination: there is no private awakening apart from others; the bodhisattva ideal (a vow to work for the awakening of all beings).
  • Against: the Buddha left home; nirvana is reached by insight, not by changing society; time in politics is time not spent on practice.
  • Against: activism can bring anger and attachment to results; monks in politics have also backed nationalism and violence (e.g. Japanese Zen support for militarism before 1945).
  • Criteria: Which goal comes first: awakening or relief of suffering? Can the two be separated? Does it matter who acts (lay or monastic)? Does engagement undo the stereotype of Buddhism as inward-looking, or misread the tradition?
  • Your spec's discussion points: centrality, the "inward-looking" stereotype, whether war could ever be accepted.

Mission 6

Two different questions

  • Q1: Can a woman reach awakening (Nirvana; become an arhat, Pali arahant)?
  • Q2: Can a woman become a fully enlightened Buddha (samma-sambuddha), who rediscovers and teaches the Dharma for a whole age?
  • Many texts answer yes to Q1 and no, not in a female body to Q2.
  • Keep two more levels apart: theoretically (what the teaching allows) and realistically (whether women get ordination, teachers, education and respect).
Mission 6

Early texts: women can awaken

  • When Ananda asks whether women who go forth can reach the fruits of the path up to arahantship, the Buddha says yes (Cullavagga X.1; see Mission 7).
  • Therigatha ("verses of the elder nuns"): about 73 poems by early nuns describing their awakening, e.g. Patacara, Kisa Gotami.
  • Soma and Mara (Samyutta Nikaya 5.2, paraphrase): Mara taunts that a woman's "two-finger wisdom" cannot reach the goal. Soma replies: what does being a woman matter, when the mind is concentrated and insight arises? Only someone who thinks "I am a woman" or "I am a man" is Mara's target.
  • So the earliest teaching treats insight, not sex, as what liberates.
Mission 6

Early and Theravada limits

  • Bahudhatuka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 115, paraphrase): it is impossible for a woman to be a fully enlightened Buddha, a wheel-turning king, Sakka, Mara or Brahma; possible for a man.
  • Later Theravada commentary lists eight conditions for a future Buddha to receive the prediction of Buddhahood; one is being male.
  • Traditional Buddha-images describe the 32 marks of a great man, including male physical marks.
  • A karma explanation sometimes given: female birth reflects less merit; women make merit (e.g. by supporting monks) hoping for a male rebirth.
  • Many Theravada teachers today stress the first answer: arahantship is fully open to women, and that is the goal of the path for all.

Mission 6Read the source

The Dragon King's daughter

Lotus Sutra, "Devadatta" chapter (paraphrase):

  • Manjushri says the eight-year-old daughter of the dragon king Sagara is wise and close to awakening. A bodhisattva doubts it: the Buddha himself practised for countless ages.
  • Shariputra objects: a woman's body cannot be a vessel for the Dharma; women face five obstacles (they cannot be Brahma, Indra, Mara, a wheel-turning king, or a Buddha).
  • She offers the Buddha a precious jewel, which he accepts at once. She says she will become a Buddha even faster than that.
  • Instantly her body becomes male; he completes the bodhisattva practices and becomes a Buddha in a southern world, teaching all beings.

Q1 Which of Shariputra's assumptions does the story overturn?

Show answer

That a young, female, non-human being cannot reach Buddhahood, or only after ages.

Q2 Why do some readers call the story limiting?

Show answer

She still becomes male before Buddhahood.

Mission 6

Other Mahayana voices

  • Lotus Sutra overall: all beings possess the potential for Buddhahood (One Vehicle); the Buddha also predicts Buddhahood for his aunt Mahapajapati and his former wife Yasodhara (ch. 13 in Kumarajiva).
  • Vimalakirti Sutra, ch. 7: a goddess changes Shariputra into a woman's form to show that "female" and "male" have no fixed essence: all things are neither male nor female (emptiness).
  • Srimaladevi Sutra: Queen Srimala teaches the Dharma with the Buddha's approval.
  • Tara (Tibetan Buddhism): vows to reach and to teach awakening in a female body.
  • Pure Land: Amida's vows include one that women who wish it will no longer be reborn as women (often read as freeing them from social disadvantage).
  • So Mahayana is not one view: some texts affirm women fully; some still need a male body at the end.

Mission 6

Rita Gross: Buddhism after patriarchy

  • Rita M. Gross (1943–2015), American scholar and practitioner; Buddhism After Patriarchy (1993).
  • History: Buddhist institutions have been androcentric (male-centred); women's stories were often forgotten.
  • Doctrine: core teachings (anatta, emptiness, buddha-nature) give no basis for fixed gender roles; the Dharma is "neither male nor female".
  • So: Buddhism's commitment to ending suffering for all, and its teaching that gender is empty, make it inherently feminist at its core.
  • Reconstruction: recover women's history; train and recognise women teachers; change institutions to fit the teaching.
Mission 6

Salgado and Bartholomeusz: whose feminism?

  • Tessa Bartholomeusz (d. 2001), Women under the Bo Tree (1994): history of Sri Lankan lay nuns (dasa sil mata, "ten-precept mothers") from the late 19th century; women created a respected religious life without full ordination, amid colonial and nationalist change.
  • Nirmala S. Salgado, Buddhist Nuns and Gendered Practice (2013): warns against judging nuns by Western liberal feminist ideas of rights and equality; many dasa sil matas value renunciation and do not seek higher ordination; their own understanding should come first.
  • Contrast with Gross: Gross reconstructs Buddhism using feminist values; Salgado asks whether those values fit Asian nuns' own aims.
Mission 6Evaluate

Does Buddhism align with feminism?

  • Claim: Buddhism is inherently feminist.
  • For: anatta and emptiness: no fixed male or female self; women's awakening affirmed (Therigatha, Soma); Gross.
  • For: modern movements (Sakyadhita, bhikkhuni revival) draw on the Buddha's own founding of the nuns' order.
  • Against: texts barring a female Buddha (e.g. MN 115) and the eight garudhammas (see Mission 7) place women below men.
  • Also against: the Dragon King's daughter becomes male; and "feminism" is itself a Western framework that may not fit Buddhist renunciants (Salgado).
  • Criteria: Teaching or institutions? Early texts or later ones? Whose feminism, and whose voices count: scholars, monks, nuns, laywomen?
  • Discussion: the significance of contrasting traditional and modern views of women's capacity for enlightenment.

Mission 7

The fourfold assembly

  • The Buddha's community has four parts: bhikkhus (monks), bhikkhunis (nuns), upasakas (laymen), upasikas (laywomen).
  • Bhikkhuni (Pali) / bhikshuni (Sanskrit): a fully ordained nun, who keeps the nuns' Vinaya (311 rules in the Theravada Vinaya; 348 in the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya used in East Asia).
  • Full ordination (upasampada) is given by a quorum of the Sangha; for nuns the Vinaya requires a dual ordination: by bhikkhunis, then by bhikkhus.
  • Below full ordination: novices (samaneri) and probationers (sikkhamana).
Mission 7Read the source

Mahapajapati Gotami asks to go forth

Cullavagga X.1 (also Anguttara Nikaya 8.51), paraphrase:

  • Mahapajapati Gotami, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother, asks him three times to let women go forth. Three times he refuses.
  • She and many Sakyan women shave their heads, put on robes and walk to Vesali. She stands outside, feet swollen, in tears.
  • Ananda asks: are women able to reach stream-entry and the further fruits, up to arahantship? The Buddha: they are.
  • Ananda reminds him she nursed him as a baby. The Buddha agrees, if she accepts eight weighty rules. She accepts them "like a garland".
  • The text adds a prediction: because women have gone forth, the true Dhamma will last 500 years instead of 1,000.

Q1 What reason does Ananda's question give for admitting women?

Show answer

They can reach the same goal as men.

Q2 Which parts of the story do critics see as later additions?

Show answer

The eight rules and the 500-year prediction.

Mission 7

The eight garudhammas

Pali garudhamma ("weighty rules"), paraphrased:

  • Your spec uses the Sanskrit form gurudharma.
  • 1. A nun, even of 100 years' standing, bows to a monk ordained that day.
  • 2. Nuns do not spend the rains retreat where there are no monks.
  • 3. Every fortnight nuns ask the monks for the observance day and for instruction.
  • 4. At the end of the rains, nuns invite criticism from both Sanghas.
  • 5. A nun guilty of a serious offence undergoes discipline before both Sanghas.
  • 6. A probationer trained for two years seeks full ordination from both Sanghas.
  • 7. A nun must never abuse or revile a monk.
  • 8. Nuns may not admonish monks; monks may admonish nuns.
  • Effect: the nuns' order depends on and ranks below the monks' order.
Mission 7

Origins, controversies, and the role of men

  • Traditional readings: the rules protected women in ancient Indian society and kept the Sangha respected; Mahapajapati accepted them freely.
  • Critical readings (e.g. Bhikkhu Analayo; Bhikkhu Sujato; Ute Hüsken): rule 6 assumes a probationer stage that did not yet exist; some rules duplicate lesser offences found elsewhere in the Vinaya; the 500-year prediction did not come true. So the rules and the prediction may be later additions by a male monastic community.
  • Today: some nuns accept them as a training in humility; others reject or reinterpret rule 1.

Mission 7

How the line was broken, and where it survives

  • Ordination needs living bhikkhunis (dual ordination). Once the last nuns die, who can ordain new ones?
  • Sri Lanka: the Theravada bhikkhuni order dies out by about the 11th century, after war and invasion; Myanmar by about the 13th century. Thailand has no record of a bhikkhuni order.
  • 433 CE: Sri Lankan nuns travel to China and help give the dual ordination at Nanjing: the Chinese lineage descends partly from Sri Lanka.
  • Mahayana lineages continue unbroken today in China, Taiwan, Korea and Vietnam, under the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
  • Tibet: the full bhikshuni ordination of its own Vinaya (Mulasarvastivada) was never established; women ordain as novices. Some have taken full ordination from East Asian nuns. Separately, in Bhutan, 2022, over 140 women received full ordination.
  • Thailand today: a 1928 order of the Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women; the Thai Sangha still applies it.
  • Your spec: "Mahayana female monastic traditions and lineage".

Mission 7

Thailand: a changing society

  • Tourism after the 1980s: mass tourism brings Western lifestyles and values; it also brings a visible sex industry, linked to rural poverty and lack of education for girls.
  • Changing roles outside religion: women in higher education, business and politics (e.g. a woman Prime Minister, 2011).
  • Rights from secular governments: women vote from 1932; equality of men and women in the 1997 Constitution; the Gender Equality Act 2015.
  • But in religion: a 1928 order of the Thai Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women as nuns or novices. The Thai Sangha still applies it.
  • So Thai women have more secular rights than religious ones: a gap campaigners stress.
Mission 7

The late 1990s revival

  • 1987: Sakyadhita ("Daughters of the Buddha"), the International Association of Buddhist Women, founded at Bodh Gaya (Karma Lekshe Tsomo, Ayya Khema, Chatsumarn Kabilsingh and others). Aims: unite Buddhist women, education, research, and full ordination where women want it.
  • 1996, Sarnath: ten Sri Lankan women receive full ordination with Korean bhikkhunis and Sri Lankan monks.
  • 1998, Bodh Gaya: an international ordination led by the Taiwanese order Fo Guang Shan includes Theravada women.
  • From 1998, Sri Lanka: bhikkhuni ordinations held in Sri Lanka itself (Dambulla), now continuing in Sri Lanka's own Sangha.
  • 2009, Australia: Ajahn Brahm's monastery ordains four bhikkhunis; his monastery is removed from the Thai forest lineage that trained him.
  • Discussion point: the role and aims of Sakyadhita.
Mission 7

Dhammananda

  • Bhikkhuni Dhammananda (Chatsumarn Kabilsingh, b. 1944): Thai scholar of Buddhist philosophy; co-founder of Sakyadhita.
  • Her mother, Voramai Kabilsingh, took full ordination in Taiwan (1971).
  • She becomes a novice in Sri Lanka (2001) and a bhikkhuni (2003): widely described as the first Thai woman fully ordained as a Theravada bhikkhuni; abbess of Songdhammakalyani monastery near Bangkok.
  • Her arguments (paraphrase):
  • The Buddha himself founded the bhikkhuni Sangha; Buddhism needs all four assemblies.
  • The 1928 order binds Thai monks, not Sri Lankan ones; it is not a rule of the Vinaya.
  • Ordination gives women education, dignity and teaching roles, and protects poor rural women from exploitation.
  • The Thai Sangha does not recognise her ordination.
Mission 7Diversity

The ordination debate

  • For revival:
  • The Buddha ordained women and affirmed their awakening.
  • Dharmaguptaka and Theravada Vinayas descend from close early schools; and Sri Lankan nuns founded the Chinese line.
  • Some (e.g. Bhikkhu Bodhi; Bhikkhu Analayo) argue monks alone may ordain nuns when no nuns exist, as at the very start.
  • Against revival:
  • The Vinaya requires dual Theravada ordination; once broken, only a future Buddha could restart it.
  • East Asian nuns follow a different Vinaya school; mixing lineages is invalid.
  • Harmony of the Sangha and respect for senior monks' rulings.
  • Another voice: some mae chi and dasa sil matas prefer their freedom from monks' authority and do not want full ordination (see Salgado).

Mission 7Evaluate

Why does the treatment of women differ so widely?

  • Claim: Differences in how Buddhist women are treated come more from culture than from Buddhist teaching.
  • For: the same tradition varies by country: thilashin respected in Myanmar, mae chi often marginal in Thailand; secular rights in Thailand moved faster than religious ones.
  • For: where lineages survived (Taiwan, Korea), nuns lead large, educated communities.
  • Against: the Vinaya itself (dual ordination, garudhammas) creates the barrier, whatever the culture.
  • Against: some women's choices (dasa sil mata, mae chi) reflect their own religious aims, not only culture.
  • Criteria: Text, law, or practice? Whose authority: senior monks, scholars, or women themselves? Does restoring ordination serve all women, or only some?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Buddhism reached Britain through scholars, early converts and, above all, migration; its popularity rests on meditation, a non-theistic outlook and famous teachers.
  • Heritage and convert Buddhism differ in emphasis (merit and devotion; meditation), but the line is blurred.
  • Popular culture spreads and distorts Buddhism; the Dalai Lama is seen differently by Tibetan Buddhists and by Western non-Buddhists.

Mission 2

  • The Dalai Lama (Tibetan, exile from 1959) teaches compassion and kindness as a secular ethic; Thich Nhat Hanh (Vietnamese Zen, exile from 1966) taught mindfulness in daily acts and interbeing.
  • Their fame spreads Buddhism and can also simplify it.

Mission 3

  • Secular Buddhism (Stephen Batchelor) presents the dharma as a practical way of life: the four truths as tasks; agnostic about rebirth.
  • Paul Knitter's dual belonging uses Buddhist ideas to rethink Christian faith; with Batchelor, he shows Buddhism being Westernised.

Mission 4

  • Engaged Buddhism applies Buddhist teaching to the social and structural causes of suffering, alongside inner practice.
  • It grew from many roots (Taixu, Ambedkar, Sarvodaya, Sulak) and above all from Thich Nhat Hanh in wartime Vietnam: interbeing, the Order of Interbeing and its fourteen precepts.

Mission 5

  • Buddhist activism spans the environment (tree ordination, climate declaration), oppression and poverty (Ambedkar, Sarvodaya, Tibet, Myanmar) and peace (Thich Nhat Hanh, Ghosananda, BPF).
  • Macy grounds activism in dependent co-arising and the ecological self; Keown sees a modern development that must be rooted in the sources.
  • Buddhists disagree over whether engagement is central or a distraction, and over whether war could ever be accepted.

Mission 6

  • Early texts affirm women's awakening (arahantship); texts such as MN 115, and later Theravada, say a Buddha cannot be female.
  • Mahayana is mixed: the Dragon King's daughter attains Buddhahood instantly but becomes male first; Vimalakirti and Tara deny or transcend fixed gender.

Mission 7

  • Mahapajapati Gotami became the first bhikkhuni after Ananda's plea; the eight garudhammas placed nuns under monks, and critics question whether they are original.
  • The Theravada line died out (Sri Lanka by the 11th century); Mahayana lines survive.
  • Eight- and ten-precept women (mae chi, thilashin, dasa sil mata) filled the gap with very different status.
  • Revival from 1996–98 (Sarnath, Bodh Gaya, Sri Lanka); Sakyadhita (1987) and Dhammananda (bhikkhuni 2003) lead the Thai debate against the 1928 ban.
