Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Mission 1

Revelation: God makes himself known

  • Revelation: Christians believe God shows people what they could not find out alone: who God is and what God wants.
  • General revelation: through the world, nature and conscience: open to everyone.
  • Special revelation: through particular events and people: the prophets, the scriptures, and above all Jesus, the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
  • Christians believe the Bible records and passes on God's revelation: it is the Word of God.
Mission 1

Inspiration

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God guided the human writers.
  • Most Christians believe the writers used their own language, style and culture, while God worked through them.
  • So, for most Christians, the Bible is both fully human words and the Word of God.
  • Christians differ on what follows: is every detail without error, or the message God intended?
Mission 1Read the source

Catholic teaching

  • Dei Verbum (Second Vatican Council, 1965), chapter 3 (paraphrase):
  • God is the author of scripture; the human writers are true authors too, using their own powers (DV 11).
  • Scripture teaches without error the truth God wanted written for the sake of our salvation (DV 11).
  • To understand it, readers must look at what the human author meant, including the literary form (DV 12).
  • Catholics read scripture with Tradition, guided by the Magisterium (the Church's teaching authority).
  • Q: According to Dei Verbum, what kind of truth is scripture guaranteed to teach? (The truth God wanted written for our salvation.)

Mission 1Diversity

Three views of the Bible's authority

  • The literal Word of God: every word is from God and true in every detail, including history and science (e.g. creation in six 24-hour days). Held by fundamentalist Christians and some evangelicals.
  • The inspired (revealed) Word of God: God's message comes through human writers; some parts are symbolic or reflect their time. Held by most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants.
  • A source of guidance and teaching: written by people inspired by their faith in God; a wise and treasured guide, open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Implications: views on creation and science, on women's roles in church, on ethical questions.
Mission 1

What kind of writing is Genesis 1–3?

  • The literary type (form) of the creation accounts affects what they teach.
  • Literal history: a scientific and historical account of how the world began (creationists).
  • Symbolic / theological story: truth told in figurative language: God created all, creation is good, humans are made in God's image, sin damages everything.
  • Catholic teaching: Genesis uses figurative language but tells real truths, e.g. about the Fall (CCC 390, paraphrase); it is compatible with science.
  • Many Christians see the two accounts (Genesis 1 and 2), with different orders of creation, as a sign that the writers were not giving a scientific record; literalists read them as fitting together.

Mission 1Evaluate

"The Bible is the only authority Christians need."

  • Reasons for: the Bible is "God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16). Protestant sola scriptura: Church teaching and tradition can err, but God's Word does not. Every Christian can read it for themselves.
  • Reasons against: the Bible needs interpreting, and Christians disagree on what it means. The Church chose which books belong in it. Catholics: Scripture, Tradition and Magisterium belong together. Modern questions (e.g. IVF, AI) are not addressed directly; reason and conscience are needed.
  • Another view: many Protestants hold scripture as the highest authority, but still value creeds, reason and experience.
  • Judge it by: Can a text interpret itself? Who decides between interpretations? How do Christians handle questions the Bible does not mention?

Mission 2

Mission 2: If the Bible says nothing about a choice, how do Christians decide what is right?

Make a guess: If the Bible says nothing about a choice, how do Christians decide what is right?

Mission 2

Many sources, one decision

  • Christians make moral decisions using several sources together:
  • The Bible, especially Jesus' teaching and example.
  • Church teaching: for Catholics, Tradition and the Magisterium (the Church's teaching office).
  • Reason: thinking carefully about what is good for people.
  • Conscience: the inner sense of right and wrong.
  • Christians differ on which source carries most weight when they seem to clash.
Mission 2

Natural law

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae (1265–1274): God made the world with a purpose and order.
  • Humans can discover that order by reason: this is natural law.
  • The first principle: "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided" (ST I-II, q. 94, a. 2).
  • Primary precepts (basic goods): preserve life; reproduce; educate the young; live in an ordered society; know and worship God.
  • From the goodness of creation: God made everything "very good" (Genesis 1:31), so human nature itself points towards what is truly good.
  • Paul: people who never heard God's law still "show the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15).
Mission 2

Natural law and the sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: human life is holy, because it comes from God and is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Preserving life is a primary precept, so natural law reasons that it is always wrong to directly take innocent human life.
  • Catholic teaching applies this from conception to natural death: abortion and euthanasia are wrong; care for the dying (palliative care) is encouraged (CCC 2258, 2270, 2277, 2279, paraphrase).
  • Catholics hold that natural law can be known by everyone, religious or not, so the Church argues from reason in public debate.

Mission 2

Conscience

  • Conscience: the inner judgement that tells a person what is right or wrong in a particular situation.
  • Catholics call it the voice of God within: a law people did not make for themselves, which calls them to love good and avoid evil (Gaudium et Spes 16).
  • Catholic teaching also calls conscience a judgement of reason (CCC 1778).
  • Primacy of conscience: a person must follow a certain conscience, even when it is mistaken.
  • Catholic teaching adds that conscience must be formed well: by scripture, Church teaching, prayer and advice.
  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890): conscience is "the aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk, 1875): Christ's first representative within each person.
  • For the redeemed: Christians believe grace and the Spirit help conscience see more clearly.
Mission 2Read the source

Gaudium et Spes 16 and CCC 1777–1781

  • Gaudium et Spes 16 (Second Vatican Council, 1965, paraphrase): deep within, people find a law they did not make, which they must obey. Conscience is a person's "most secret core and sanctuary", where they are alone with God.
  • CCC 1777 (paraphrase): conscience urges a person, at the right moment, to do good and avoid evil; it judges particular choices.
  • CCC 1778: conscience is a judgement of reason, by which a person recognises the moral quality of an act.
  • CCC 1779–1780: people must listen to their conscience; it needs reflection and self-examination; it is part of human dignity.
  • CCC 1781: conscience lets a person take responsibility and admit wrong done; it can lead to repentance and hope in God's mercy.

Mission 2Diversity

When Bible, Church and conscience seem to clash

  • Catholic: conscience must be followed, but formed by scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium; a conscience that ignores them may be in error.
  • Many evangelical Protestants: the Bible is the final authority; conscience must be "captive to the Word of God" (Luther at Worms, 1521, paraphrase). Some warn that conscience is affected by sin (Jeremiah 17:9).
  • Liberal Protestants: reason and conscience may lead a Christian to read some Bible passages as limited by their time.
  • Quakers: the Inner Light (God's Spirit in each person) guides decisions, tested together in the meeting.
Quick check
?Martin Luther said his conscience was "captive to the Word of God". What does this suggest about how many Protestants see conscience?
Mission 2Evaluate

"A Christian should always follow their conscience."

  • Reasons for: Gaudium et Spes 16: conscience is where God's voice is heard. Catholic teaching: a certain conscience must be obeyed. Newman would toast conscience first and the pope afterwards. Acting against conscience damages a person's integrity.
  • Reasons against: conscience can be wrong or badly formed (CCC 1790–1791); people have done terrible things "in conscience". Many Protestants say the Bible must judge conscience. Church teaching and community advice protect against self-deception.
  • Another view: follow conscience, but only after forming it carefully: the two belong together.
  • Judge it by: What happens when conscience is mistaken? Who checks a person's conscience? Is "following conscience" the same as doing what one wants?
Mission check

Christians weigh the Bible, Church teaching, reason and conscience together; Catholics hold that a well-formed conscience must be followed.

Mission 3

What is an ecumenical council?

  • An ecumenical council is a gathering of the bishops of the whole world, with the Pope, to teach and decide for the whole Church.
  • Its teaching is part of the conciliar Magisterium: the Church's teaching authority exercised together.
  • Catholics count 21 such councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), called "Vatican II" because it was the second council held at the Vatican (in St Peter's Basilica), after Vatican I (1869–1870).
  • Earlier councils mostly met to settle a dispute or answer a challenge (e.g. Trent, 1545–1563, after the Reformation).
  • Vatican II was different: no single crisis. Its aim was renewal: John XXIII's word was aggiornamento, Italian for "bringing up to date".
Mission 3

The story, 1959–1965

  • 25 January 1959: Pope John XXIII surprises the Church by announcing a council.
  • 11 October 1962: the Council opens. Over 2,000 bishops attend, from every continent.
  • 3 June 1963: John XXIII dies. The new Pope, Paul VI, continues the Council.
  • Four autumn sessions (1962, 1963, 1964, 1965), with drafts revised between them.
  • Every document was debated and voted on by the bishops, then approved by the Pope.
  • 8 December 1965: the Council closes, having approved 16 documents: 4 constitutions, 9 decrees, 3 declarations.
  • Christians from other Churches (Orthodox, Anglican, Protestant) were invited as observers.

Mission 3

A Council that listened

  • Aggiornamento meant renewing how the Church lives and speaks, without changing its faith.
  • Consultative: before the Council, bishops worldwide were asked what it should discuss.
  • Drafts were debated in open sessions; many were rejected and rewritten; every document was voted on by the bishops.
  • Experts (theologians) advised; from 1963 some lay people attended as auditors, and from 1964 women too.
  • The hierarchy stayed: Pope, bishops, priests, deacons. But Lumen Gentium taught collegiality: bishops together, with the Pope, share responsibility for the whole Church.
  • After the Council, Paul VI set up the Synod of Bishops (1965) to keep consulting.
Mission 3

The four key documents

The four constitutions are the Council's most important teaching.

  • Dei Verbum ("The Word of God", 1965): how God reveals himself; Scripture and Tradition.
  • Lumen Gentium ("Light of the nations", 1964): what the Church is.
  • Sacrosanctum Concilium ("This sacred Council", 1963): the liturgy (the Church's worship).
  • Gaudium et Spes ("Joy and hope", 1965): the Church in the modern world.
  • Each takes its name from its first words in Latin.
Mission 3

Dei Verbum: God speaks

  • God reveals himself above all in Jesus Christ.
  • Revelation is passed on through Scripture and Tradition, which flow from the same divine source and form one deposit of faith (DV 9–10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God but serves it: it teaches only what has been handed on (DV 10, paraphrase).
  • Easy access to the Bible should be open to all; Catholics are urged to read it (DV 22, 25).
  • For Catholic living: Bible study groups, Scripture in the vernacular, a longer selection of readings at Mass.
Mission 3

Lumen Gentium: what the Church is

  • The Church is a mystery: a visible community and a spiritual reality, a sign of union with God (LG 1).
  • It is the People of God (ch. 2) before it is a hierarchy: all the baptised share in Christ's work.
  • The hierarchy (ch. 3): bishops, as successors of the apostles, with the Pope as head; collegiality.
  • The laity (ch. 4) and a universal call to holiness (ch. 5): holiness is for everyone, not just priests and religious.
  • For Catholic living: lay readers, catechists and parish councils; every Catholic called to be a saint.
Mission 3

Sacrosanctum Concilium: worship renewed

  • The liturgy is the summit towards which the Church's activity is directed and the source of its strength (SC 10, paraphrase).
  • Aim: the full, active participation of the whole people (SC 14), not just the priest.
  • Permitted the vernacular (local language) alongside Latin (SC 36); this soon became normal practice.
  • More Scripture in worship; simpler, clearer rites.
  • For Catholic living: Mass in English, the people saying the responses, and later the priest usually facing the people across the altar (introduced by guidelines in 1964, after the Council's first document).

Mission 3

Gaudium et Spes: the Church in the world

  • Opens: the joys and hopes, griefs and anxieties of people today, especially the poor, are those of Christ's followers too (GS 1, paraphrase; more in Mission 4).
  • Human dignity, the common good, family, culture, economics, peace (GS 78).
  • Science (GS 36): research done in a truly scientific way, and respecting moral law, never really conflicts with faith, because the things of the world and the things of faith come from the same God (paraphrase).
  • The Council regretted attitudes, sometimes among Christians, that failed to respect the proper autonomy of science; a footnote points to a study of Galileo.
  • For Catholic living: Catholics can welcome science (e.g. the Big Bang, evolution) as studying God's creation; social action is part of faith.

Mission 3

What changed?

  • Worship: Mass in the vernacular; altar facing the people; lay readers and ministers of Holy Communion.
  • Laity: parish councils; lay people as catechists, chaplains and teachers of the faith.
  • Other Christians: ecumenism (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964): dialogue and prayer for unity.
  • Other religions: Nostra Aetate (1965): the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions (NA 2, paraphrase); a new relationship with Jews.
  • Science: GS 36 shaped later teaching, e.g. John Paul II's statements on Galileo (1992) and on evolution (1996).
Mission 3Diversity

One Council, different readings

  • The Church's official teaching receives the Council as the work of the Holy Spirit, and it shapes parish life everywhere.
  • Renewal in continuity: Benedict XVI (2005) argued that the Council must be read in continuity with earlier teaching, not as a break.
  • "Spirit of the Council": some Catholics want reforms to go further (e.g. more lay and women's leadership) and appeal to the Council's spirit of openness.
  • Traditionalists: some prefer the older Latin Mass; a small group (the Society of St Pius X, founded by Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, 1970) rejects parts of the Council's teaching, e.g. on religious liberty and ecumenism.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Vatican II was the most important event in the Catholic Church in the last 100 years."

  • Reasons for: it changed the experience of every Catholic every Sunday (SC); it gave lay people a new role (LG); it opened dialogue with other Christians, other religions and science (GS 36, Nostra Aetate).
  • Reasons against: its teaching is not new faith but "aggiornamento", so the deeper continuity matters more; some say later developments (e.g. the global growth of the Church in Africa and Asia, or the papacy of John Paul II) shaped today's Church as much; traditionalist critics see some changes as loss.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "important": doctrine, daily practice, or numbers affected? Did the Council change the faith or how it is lived? Whose experience of the Church do you measure?

Mission 4

Love your neighbour, in concrete ways

  • Jesus joins two commands: love God, and "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31, quoting Leviticus 19:18).
  • The Good Samaritan: the neighbour is "he who showed mercy", and "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:37).
  • Love must be concrete: "if a brother or sister is naked and in lack of daily food" and you only wish them well, "what good is it?" (James 2:15–16).
  • Catholic Social Teaching (CST): the Church's teaching on how society should be organised so that every person can flourish.
  • Its modern form begins with Pope Leo XIII's letter on workers, Rerum Novarum (1891).
Mission 4

Every person in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image … male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Imago Dei (Latin, "image of God"): every human being has dignity: worth that does not depend on wealth, ability, age or usefulness.
  • This is the root of all CST: if every person bears God's image, harming or ignoring them wrongs God.
Quick check
?Why does belief in imago Dei lead Catholics to work for social justice?
Mission 4

Key principles of CST

  • Dignity of the human person: every life matters, from conception to natural death.
  • Common good: society should give everyone what they need to flourish, not just the majority.
  • Solidarity: we are one human family; others' suffering is our concern.
  • Preferential option for the poor: the needs of the poorest come first.
  • Subsidiarity: decisions taken as close as possible to the people affected.
  • Care for creation: the earth is a gift for all, including future generations.
  • Peace: built on justice, not just the absence of war.
  • CST asks for charity (meeting today's need) and justice (changing what causes the need).
Mission 4Read the source

The Magisterium speaks: GS 1 and EG 53–54

  • Gaudium et Spes 1 (Vatican II, 1965): the joys and hopes, the griefs and anxieties of people today, especially the poor and afflicted, are the joys and hopes, griefs and anxieties of Christ's followers too (paraphrase).
  • Evangelii Gaudium 53 (Pope Francis, 2013): as the Church says "thou shalt not kill", it must say "thou shalt not" to an economy of exclusion and inequality; such an economy kills. People are treated as disposable: a "throw away" culture (paraphrase).
  • EG 54: Francis criticises the belief that economic growth will by itself bring justice to the poor ("trickle-down"), and warns of a "globalisation of indifference" (paraphrase).
  • This is the Magisterium (conciliar in GS, pontifical in EG) applying the Gospel to society.
  • Q: What does GS 1 say about the Church's relationship with people's suffering? (It shares their griefs and anxieties as its own.)
Mission 4

Justice, peace and reconciliation

  • Justice: giving each person what is due to them as a child of God: "let justice roll on like rivers" (Amos 5:24).
  • Peace: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9).
  • Reconciliation: God "reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18): healing broken relationships between people and with God.
  • Signs of the Kingdom: where justice, peace and reconciliation grow, God's reign is becoming visible (the Lord's Prayer).
Mission 4

Caring for creation is loving your neighbour

  • The earth belongs to God: "The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness" (Psalm 24:1). Humans are put in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15).
  • Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): the cry of the earth and the cry of the poor are linked (paraphrase).
  • Pollution, climate change and lost farmland hit the poorest first and hardest.
  • So caring for the environment is a concrete way of loving neighbours, near and far, and future generations.
  • Examples: parish energy saving, buying less, campaigning on climate justice.
Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

"Giving to charity is enough: changing society is not the Church's job."

  • Reasons for: Jesus fed and healed individuals; he did not run a political campaign. Charity is direct, personal and non-divisive. Some Christians argue the Church should stay out of politics and focus on salvation.
  • Reasons against: CST says faith cannot be kept private (EG 183) and that structural causes of poverty must be tackled (EG 202). The prophets denounced unjust rulers (Amos 5:24). If laws keep people poor, charity alone can never be enough.
  • Another view: both together: charity meets today's need; justice changes tomorrow. Catholics may disagree about which policies best serve the common good.
  • Judge it by: Does helping individuals change the causes of poverty? Where is the line between Gospel justice and party politics? What did Jesus' example ask of his followers?

Mission 5

Faith in action

  • "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Catholic Social Teaching (see Mission 4) is lived out by individuals, parishes and agencies.
  • Agencies work at different levels: local (next door), national, global.
  • Each one puts a CST principle to work: dignity, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • This mission starts with CAFOD, the agency every course studies.
  • Then a local agency (SVP) beside a global one, and one Catholic figure.
Mission 5

CAFOD: what it does

  • CAFOD: the Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, the official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962.
  • Part of Caritas Internationalis, the Church's worldwide network of charities.
  • Works through local partners (often local churches) rather than sending its own staff to run projects.
  • Emergency relief: food, water and shelter after disasters and wars.
  • Long-term development: farming, clean water, health, education, livelihoods.
  • Campaigning: asking UK governments and businesses to change unjust policies on debt, trade and climate.
  • Parishes and schools raise money on Family Fast Days (Lent and harvest).
Mission 5

CAFOD: why it does it

  • Imago Dei: every person has dignity, so no one should be left in poverty.
  • Matthew 25:40: "you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me."
  • Solidarity: one human family; distance does not make someone less a neighbour (Luke 10:37).
  • Option for the poor and justice: charity for today, campaigns to change causes.
  • Partnership: local people decide and lead; this respects their dignity and subsidiarity.
  • Help is given to anyone in need, regardless of religion.
Mission 5

CAFOD and caring for creation

  • Catholics believe creation is good (Genesis 1:31) and a gift for all.
  • CAFOD sees environmental damage as an injustice to the poorest, who suffer first from drought, floods and failed harvests.
  • Sustainability work: helping farmers adapt to a changing climate, e.g. drought-resistant crops, water storage, clean energy.
  • Campaigns in the UK on climate and fair use of resources, inspired by Pope Francis' Laudato Si' (2015).
  • LiveSimply award: parishes and schools commit to live simply, sustainably and in solidarity with people in poverty.

Mission 5

SVP: love next door

  • Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP), founded in Paris in 1833 by a student, Blessed Frédéric Ozanam (1813–1853), and friends.
  • Named after St Vincent de Paul (1581–1660), a French priest who served the poor.
  • Mostly lay volunteers in local groups called conferences, often in a parish.
  • Aims: person-to-person help: visiting the lonely, sick, prisoners and families in need at home; food, furniture and friendship; seeking to tackle the causes of poverty.
  • Help is given to anyone, regardless of faith; the volunteer sees Christ in the person (Matthew 25:36).
  • Two agencies: SVP (local: face-to-face in your town) + CAFOD (national and global: partners abroad, campaigns in the UK) show love of neighbour at every level.

Mission 5

One Catholic figure: St Óscar Romero

  • Óscar Romero (1917–1980), Archbishop of San Salvador, El Salvador, from 1977.
  • A quiet, cautious bishop, changed by the murder of his Jesuit friend Rutilio Grande (1977), who worked with poor farmers.
  • Justice: in weekly radio sermons he named killings, torture and the poverty of landless farmers.
  • Peace: he rejected violence on all sides, army, death squads and armed rebels alike.
  • Reconciliation: he called the powerful to conversion. On 23 March 1980 he appealed directly to soldiers to stop the killing.
  • Shot dead the next day, 24 March 1980, while celebrating Mass. Canonised by Pope Francis in 2018.
  • Influence: a symbol of the option for the poor; his statue is among the twentieth-century martyrs above Westminster Abbey's west door.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Supporting a charity is the best way for a Catholic to love their neighbour."

  • Reasons for: agencies such as CAFOD have expertise and reach that one person lacks; they tackle causes as well as symptoms; giving through a parish unites a whole community in solidarity.
  • Reasons against: Matthew 25 asks for personal acts (visiting the sick and prisoners), which money cannot replace; SVP's founders insisted on person-to-person contact; love of neighbour starts at home, with family and the people next door; prayer and peacemaking matter too.
  • Romero loved his neighbours by speaking for justice and peace, not by fundraising.
  • Another view: different ways suit different people and stages of life; CST asks for both charity and justice.
  • Judge it by: Does the way of loving change the causes of need? Does it involve real relationship? Is it open to everyone, whatever their circumstances?

Mission 6

Agape: love that gives

  • Agape (Greek): selfless, unconditional love that seeks the good of others, expecting nothing back.
  • Christians see it in Jesus: healing, feeding, forgiving, and giving his life.
  • The Two Greatest Commandments: love God "with all your heart… soul… mind, and… strength", and "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:30–31)
  • The Good Samaritan shows who my neighbour is: anyone in need, even an enemy. "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37)
  • Catholics add: creation is God's gift and "very good" (Genesis 1:31), so caring for the earth is loving the neighbour who depends on it.
  • Jesus taught that whatever is done for "one of the least of these my brothers" is done for him (Matthew 25:40).
  • Christian charity work begins here: love of God shown as love of neighbour.
Mission 6

CAFOD and Caritas

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development): official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962; grew out of Family Fast Days started by Catholic women in 1960.
  • Family Fast Day: eat a simple meal and give what you save (Lent and autumn).
  • Works with local Catholic and other partners: emergencies, health, water, farming, education; campaigns for justice.
  • Helps people regardless of religion.
  • Caritas Internationalis: the worldwide confederation of national Catholic charities (CAFOD is the member for England and Wales; SCIAF for Scotland, Trócaire for Ireland).
  • Why: Catholic social teaching: human dignity, the common good, solidarity, and a preferential option for the poor.
Mission 6

CAFOD and sustainability

  • Sustainability: meeting today's needs without harming the earth for future generations.
  • Pope Francis' encyclical Laudato Si' (2015) calls the earth "our common home" and links care for creation with care for the poor (paraphrase).
  • CAFOD's work: helping farmers adapt to drought and floods (resilient crops, water harvesting, tree planting); clean energy such as solar power; campaigning in the UK on climate change.
  • Parishes and schools are encouraged to live more simply and sustainably.
  • Belief behind it: creation is good (Genesis 1:31) and belongs to God: "The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness" (Psalm 24:1). Humans are stewards: put in the garden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15).
  • Significance: harming creation harms the poorest first, so sustainability is love of neighbour in concrete terms.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe God reveals himself, and the Bible is the inspired ("God-breathed") record of that revelation (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Views of authority: literal Word, inspired Word (most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants), or a human guide inspired by faith.
  • The Bible is used in worship, at home, through reading aids and groups, for comfort and moral guidance.

Mission 2

  • Natural law (Aquinas): reason discovers the purposes God built into a good creation; preserving life is basic, so Catholics uphold the sanctity of life.
  • Conscience is a judgement of reason where Catholics believe God's voice is heard (GS 16; CCC 1777–1781); it must be formed and followed.
  • Christians differ on the weight of Bible, Church, reason and conscience.

Mission 3

  • Vatican II (1962–1965), called by John XXIII and closed by Paul VI, sought renewal (aggiornamento) of the Church's life.
  • Its four key documents: Dei Verbum (revelation), Lumen Gentium (the Church), Sacrosanctum Concilium (liturgy), Gaudium et Spes (the Church in the modern world).
  • Its consultative process and GS 36 (science and faith from one God) shape Catholic views today.

Mission 4

  • CST applies love of neighbour to society: dignity (imago Dei), common good, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • GS 1 and EG 53–54: the Magisterium makes the poor's griefs the Church's own and rejects an "economy of exclusion"; justice, peace and reconciliation are signs of the Kingdom.

Mission 5

  • CAFOD (1962) puts CST into action globally through partners: emergencies, development, campaigning; help regardless of religion.
  • SVP (local) and CAFOD (global), CAFOD's sustainability work, and St Óscar Romero's justice, peace and reconciliation.

Mission 6

  • Agape and the Two Greatest Commandments (Mark 12:30–31) motivate Christian charity.
  • CAFOD's sustainability work expresses belief in the goodness of creation.
