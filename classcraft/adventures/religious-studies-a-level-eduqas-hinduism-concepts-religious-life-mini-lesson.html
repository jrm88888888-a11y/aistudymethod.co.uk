Mission 1

Vedanta: the end of the Veda

  • Vedanta means "end of the Veda": the Upanishads, the last and most philosophical part of the Vedas.
  • It became a darshana (school of philosophy), also called Uttara Mimamsa: inquiry into the later Veda.
  • Every Vedanta school comments on three texts, the prasthana-traya (triple foundation): the Upanishads, the Brahma Sutras (Badarayana; date uncertain) and the Bhagavad Gita.
  • The central question: how are Brahman, the atman (self) and the world related?
  • Three great answers: Advaita (non-dual; Shankara), Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual; Ramanuja), Dvaita (dual; Madhva). Ramanuja and Madhva.
Mission 1

One without a second

  • For Shankara's Advaita, Brahman alone is ultimately real: "one only, without a second" (Chandogya 6:2:1, paraphrase).
  • Nirguna Brahman: without qualities; beyond thought and words. It can only be described by negation: neti, neti ("not this, not this", Brihadaranyaka 2:3:6).
  • Positively, it is described as sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss.
  • Saguna Brahman (with qualities) or Ishvara (the Lord): Brahman as seen from within the world: creator, worshipped as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess. Real at the everyday level, not the final truth.
  • Shankara teaches that atman is Brahman: the true self is not a part of Brahman but Brahman, fully.
  • Your spec calls this monist: God and the soul are one.
Mission 1Read the source

"You are that"

  • Chandogya Upanishad 6 (paraphrase): Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu. Salt dissolved in water cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes of it (6:13). The subtle essence pervades everything; "that is the Self, and you are that" (tat tvam asi).
  • Shankara reads "you are that" as identity: the self and Brahman are one, not two.
  • The Gita on the self (Arnold):

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never; / Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:20, Arnold)

Mission 1

Adhyasa: superimposition

  • Adhyasa: seeing one thing as another. Shankara opens his Brahma Sutra commentary with it.
  • Rope and snake: at dusk a rope looks like a snake. The snake is superimposed on the rope. You feel real fear; but there was only ever a rope.
  • Shell and silver: mother-of-pearl glints like silver.
  • In the same way we superimpose body, mind and individuality onto the self ("I am tall", "I am sad"), and the qualities of the self onto body and mind.
  • Advaitins call the power by which the one Brahman appears as many maya.
  • Ignorance (avidya) does not make the rope vanish; knowledge removes the snake. So, for Shankara, liberation is by jnana (knowledge).

Mission 1

Maya and avidya

  • Maya (in Advaita): the power by which the one Brahman appears as many. Often translated "illusion", but the world is not "nothing".
  • Advaitins call maya indescribable: not real (it is not there in the final truth) and not unreal (it is experienced; it is not like a "son of a barren woman").
  • Avidya: ignorance in the individual; it hides Brahman and projects the world.
  • Samsara is the result: the self takes itself to be a body-mind with karma, and is reborn.
Mission 1

Three levels of reality

  • Advaitins distinguish three levels:
  • Illusory (pratibhasika): dreams, the snake in the rope. Real only while the mistake lasts.
  • Mundane, empirical (vyavaharika): the everyday world of people, gods, karma, dharma, science and worship. Fully real for us: you must still keep your promises and avoid the bus.
  • Ultimate (paramarthika): Brahman alone.
  • Each level is cancelled only by the one above: waking cancels the dream; knowing Brahman cancels the separateness of the world.
  • Shankara himself mostly contrasts two standpoints (everyday and ultimate); later Advaitins set out three.

Mission 2

The world as God's body

  • Vishishtadvaita ("non-duality of the qualified") is the school of Ramanuja (11th–12th century), a devotee of Vishnu. For Ramanuja there is one reality, Brahman, but it is qualified by souls and matter.
  • Brahman is Vishnu: personal, with infinite good qualities (saguna). Not beyond all qualities.
  • Souls (chit) and matter (achit) are real and eternal. They are the body of God; God is their inner soul and controller.
  • As a body belongs to a person and cannot exist apart, the world belongs to God: inseparable, yet distinct.
  • "You are that" means: you, whose inner self is Brahman, belong to that Brahman. Not strict identity.
  • The world is not illusion. Maya is God's wonderful creative power.

Mission 2

Madhva: five eternal differences

  • Madhva (traditionally 1238–1317 CE), from Udupi, Karnataka, where he set up the Krishna temple.
  • Dvaita: dualism. For Madhva, Vishnu alone is independent; everything else is dependent on him.
  • Five eternal differences: God and souls; God and matter; soul and soul; soul and matter; matter and matter.
  • He reads Chandogya 6:8:7 as "atat tvam asi": "you are not that". A soul is a reflection of God, never God.
  • Souls are graded: some are fit for liberation; some remain in samsara; some go to darkness. A unique and much-debated teaching.
  • Liberation: by bhakti grounded in right knowledge of God, through Vishnu's grace. Liberated souls enjoy bliss, each to its own capacity.
  • Your spec calls this monotheist: God (paramatman) and the soul (jiva-atman) are different.

Mission 3

One reality, three functions

  • Trimurti: "three forms". Three functions of saguna Brahman (God with qualities):
  • Brahma: creator. Vishnu: preserver (sustainer). Shiva: destroyer (and transformer).
  • The three work as a cycle: what is created is sustained, then dissolved, then created again.
  • For many Hindus they are not three separate gods competing but three faces of the one divine power behind the universe.
  • In Hindu belief time is a wheel, not a straight line: the cycle repeats over vast ages. One "day of Brahma" is traditionally 4.32 billion years, and an equal night follows.
  • The idea appears in later texts such as the Maitri Upanishad and the Puranas, more than in the Vedas.
Mission 3

Brahma, the creator

  • Hindus believe Brahma creates the universe at the start of each cycle and is the source of the Vedas.
  • Often shown with four heads (facing the four directions, linked with the four Vedas), sitting on a lotus.
  • Holds a water pot, prayer beads and a book; his partner is Saraswati, goddess of learning; his vehicle a swan (hamsa).
  • In Vaishnava stories he is born from a lotus growing from Vishnu's navel.
  • Today he is rarely worshipped: very few temples (the best known at Pushkar, Rajasthan).
  • Puranic stories explain this; another reason often given: creation is complete for this cycle, so devotion turns to the God who sustains and saves.
Mission 3

Vishnu, the preserver

  • In Hindu belief Vishnu sustains the universe and protects dharma (right order).
  • Shown with four arms holding a conch (shankha), a discus (chakra), a mace (gada) and a lotus (padma); often blue-skinned.
  • Rests on the serpent Ananta (Shesha) on the cosmic ocean between cycles.
  • Partner: Lakshmi, goddess of good fortune; vehicle: the eagle Garuda.
  • Comes down as an avatar when dharma declines: e.g. Rama and Krishna.
  • For Vaishnavas, Vishnu is the Supreme God, not only one of three (see Mission 4).
Mission 3

Shiva, the destroyer

  • In Hindu belief Shiva dissolves the universe at the end of each cycle, so that it can be renewed: destruction as transformation.
  • Also destroys ignorance and ego: a great yogi, meditating on Mount Kailash.
  • Nataraja, "lord of the dance": dancing in a ring of fire; drum (creation), flame (destruction), raised hand (protection), foot on a dwarf (ignorance).
  • Other symbols: trident, third eye, crescent moon, the River Ganga in his hair; worshipped often as the lingam.
  • Partner: Parvati; vehicle: the bull Nandi.
  • For Shaivas, Shiva is the Supreme God who performs all cosmic acts (see Mission 4).

Mission 3

Time as a wheel

  • Hindu time is cyclic, not a straight line with one beginning and end.
  • Four yugas (ages), each shorter and less righteous: Satya (Krita), Treta, Dvapara, Kali (our age).
  • One cycle of four yugas = 4,320,000 years (a mahayuga).
  • 1,000 mahayugas = one kalpa = one day of Brahma (4.32 billion years). An equal night follows: pralaya, dissolution.
  • Brahma's lifetime is 100 of his years; then all is dissolved, and a new Brahma begins again.
  • The Trimurti maps onto this wheel: creation (Brahma) → preservation (Vishnu) → destruction (Shiva) → creation again.
Mission 3

Breaking the wheel

  • The cosmic cycle mirrors the personal cycle of samsara: birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma.
  • Within the universe, nothing lasts: even gods and heavens pass away (Gita 8:16).
  • The aim is moksha: liberation from the wheel of samsara.
  • Those who reach God are not reborn: "they ... that reach to Me, / Taste birth no more" (Gita 8:16, Arnold).
  • So the Trimurti points beyond itself: creation, preservation and destruction are the wheel; liberation is release from it.
  • Many Hindus see Shiva's destruction of ignorance and Vishnu's grace as what makes release possible.
Mission 3

Many deities, one Brahman

  • Hindus worship many deities: Ganesha, Hanuman, Lakshmi, Saraswati, Murugan, Durga and more.
  • For most Hindus these are forms or aspects of the one Brahman, not rival gods.
  • In the Gita (11:15), Arjuna sees within Krishna's universal form "Brahma, sitting lone / Upon His lotus-throne" (Arnold): the gods are all contained in the one.
  • Ishta-devata: a person's chosen deity, worshipped as supreme for them.
  • Smarta Hindus worship five deities (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess, Surya, Ganesha) as equal forms of Brahman.

Mission 3Diversity

The Trimurti seen from each tradition

  • Vaishnavas: Vishnu is the Supreme; Brahma is born from his navel; Shiva acts under his authority, or is his devotee.
  • Shaivas: Shiva is the Supreme, who performs all cosmic acts (in Shaiva Siddhanta, five: creation, preservation, destruction, concealment and grace); Brahma and Vishnu are his agents.
  • Shaktas: the Goddess (Devi) is the Supreme power who brings forth Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
  • So for many Hindus the Trimurti is one way of picturing the divine, not the centre of their faith.
  • Art reflects this: the famous three-headed figure in the Elephanta Caves (6th century) shows Shiva in three aspects, not Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva.
Mission 3Evaluate

How important is the Trimurti?

  • Claim: The Trimurti is central to Hindu belief and practice.
  • For: It explains the cycle of creation, preservation and destruction in one picture of saguna Brahman.
  • For: It links cosmic time with samsara and the hope of liberation; it teaches that all deities are one.
  • Against: Hardly anyone worships "the Trimurti"; Brahma has very few temples.
  • Against: Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each place one deity above the three; the concept is late and appears little in the Vedas.
  • Against: Other concepts, such as Brahman, atman, karma and dharma, shape daily life more.
  • Judgement criteria: Central to belief, or to worship? For which tradition? Compared with which concepts?
  • Also weigh: how important the Trimurti is compared with the other concepts you have studied.

Mission 4

Three theistic traditions

  • Theistic: centred on a personal Supreme God (saguna Brahman).
  • Vaishnavism: Vishnu (and his avatars) supreme. Shaivism: Shiva supreme. Shaktism: the Goddess, Devi or Shakti, supreme.
  • Each has its own scriptures (beside the Vedas), temples, festivals, marks, saints and schools.
  • They grew over many centuries, especially from the time of the epics and Puranas (c. 300 BCE–1000 CE).
  • Smartas worship several deities as equal forms of one Brahman.
  • Many Hindus respect all the deities while devoting themselves to one.
Mission 4

Vaishnavism

  • Supreme deity: Vishnu, with Lakshmi; worshipped especially as his avatars Krishna and Rama.
  • Bhakti: loving devotion and surrender (prapatti) to God, who saves by grace; chanting the divine names.
  • Ritual worship: temple and home puja to the murti; offering food that becomes prasad; the tulsi plant; great temples such as Tirupati, Srirangam and Puri.
  • Moral and ethical values: dharma as Vishnu's order; many Vaishnavas are strict vegetarians; humility and service of other devotees.
  • Schools: Ramanuja's Sri Vaishnavism; Madhva; Gaudiya (Chaitanya, ISKCON); Pushti Marg; Swaminarayan.
  • Mark: a vertical U- or V-shaped tilak on the forehead.
Mission 4

Shaivism

  • Supreme deity: Shiva, with Parvati; worshipped in the lingam, a smooth, rounded stone pillar, symbol of formless divine presence.
  • Bhakti: Tamil poet-saints, the 63 Nayanars; the five-syllable mantra Om Namah Shivaya; festival Maha Shivaratri.
  • Ritual worship: pouring water and milk over the lingam and offering bilva leaves; temples such as Varanasi's Kashi Vishwanath and the twelve Jyotirlingas.
  • Asceticism: Shiva is the great yogi; many sadhus are Shaiva, with ash (vibhuti), matted hair and rudraksha beads.
  • Schools: Shaiva Siddhanta (Tamil, based on the Agamas); Kashmir Shaivism (non-dual; Abhinavagupta, c. 950–1016); Lingayats or Virashaivas (Basava, 12th century), who wear a small lingam on the body.
  • Mark: three horizontal lines of ash (tripundra).

Mission 4Evaluate

Separate religions?

  • Claim: Vaishnavism, Shaivism and Shaktism are best seen as separate religions.
  • For: Each has its own Supreme God, scriptures, temples, saints, theology and initiation.
  • For: In history they sometimes competed; some devotees worship only their own deity.
  • Against: They share the Vedas, dharma, karma, samsara and moksha, and the idea of one Brahman.
  • Against: Many Hindus move between them: a Vaishnava family may visit a Shiva temple at Shivaratri.
  • Against: "Religion" is a Western category that may not fit.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a religion: beliefs, practices, institutions, self-understanding? Do devotees see themselves as Hindus first?
  • Also weigh: whether Vaishnavism and Shaivism can be seen as religions in their own right.

Mission 5

Karma: action and its results

  • Karma means action, and the results that action brings.
  • Every intentional act (in deed, word or thought) leaves an impression that will bear fruit, now or in a later life.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV.4.5 (paraphrase): as a person acts, so they become; doing good, one becomes good; doing evil, one becomes evil. What a person desires shapes what they do.
  • Karma is not fate: today's choices make tomorrow's karma.
  • Karma is not a judge's sentence: most Hindus see it as a moral law of cause and effect, like a seed growing into a plant.
Mission 5

Karma and universal order

  • In the Vedas, rta is the order of the cosmos: seasons, sacrifice and truthful conduct all fit one pattern.
  • Karma carries that order into moral life: every act has a fitting consequence, so the universe is just in the long run.
  • It applies to all beings, of every people and every birth; it is not a rule for Hindus only.
  • Who runs the law? Some schools see it as impersonal; theistic Vedanta (e.g. Ramanuja, Madhva) teaches that God dispenses the fruits of action.
  • Righteous living (dharma) produces good karma; adharma produces suffering.
Mission 5Read the source

Samsara: the cycle of lives

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and by desire and ignorance.

> "To man's frame / As there come infancy and youth and age, / So come there raisings-up and layings-down / Of other and of other life-abodes, / Which the wise know, and fear not." (Gita 2:13, Arnold)

> "Some Jivas (individual Souls) enter wombs to be embodied; others go into immovable forms, according to their deeds and knowledge." (Katha 5:7, Paramananda)

  • What travels? The atman, with a subtle body (sukshma sharira) that carries the karmic impressions.

Mission 5

Three kinds of karma

  • Sanchita (accumulated): the whole store of karma from past lives, not yet bearing fruit. Arrows in the quiver.
  • Prarabdha (fruit-bearing): the part of that store that has begun to work out in this life: body, family, lifespan. An arrow already shot: it cannot be called back.
  • Kriyamana or agami (karma in the making): karma made now by present acts. The arrow being fitted to the bow: still in your hands.
  • Liberation, on the common Vedanta account: knowledge burns sanchita, stops new kriyamana sticking; prarabdha runs its course until this body dies.
Quick check

Mission 5

Realms and types of rebirth

  • Rebirth can be as a god (deva), a human, an animal, or an immovable form such as a plant (Katha 5:7). Tradition speaks of 8,400,000 forms of life.
  • Realms (lokas): heavenly worlds (svarga), the earth, and hell-worlds (naraka). Time in heaven or hell is temporary: when the karma is used up, the soul is born again.
  • Chandogya Upanishad V.10.7 (paraphrase): those of good conduct soon enter a good birth; those of bad conduct a low birth, even as an animal.
  • So neither heaven nor hell is the final goal. The goal is moksha: release from the whole cycle.
Mission 5

Why a human birth matters

  • Animals and other beings mostly live out their karma; they do not choose with full moral awareness.
  • Humans can reflect, choose and act deliberately: they make new karma and can seek moksha.
  • So a human birth is precious and rare: an opportunity, not a reward to waste.
  • Gods also live out good karma in heaven, and must be reborn when it is spent.
  • Spec issue: the comparative importance of the present life and the next life.
Mission 5

Good karma still binds

  • Good deeds bring good results, but a good result is still a result: it ties the soul to more births, even in heaven. A golden chain still binds.
  • Sakam karma: action done with desire for its fruit (reward, praise, heaven). It binds.
  • Nishkam karma: action done without desire for its fruit, as duty or as offering to God. It does not bind.
  • Karma yoga: the path of selfless action; purifies the mind and leads towards liberation.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is karma fair?

  • Claim: Karma is a just way of explaining why lives turn out differently.
  • For: Everyone receives what their own acts produce: no favourites, no randomness.
  • For: It gives a reason to act well, and hope: the future is not fixed.
  • Against: No one remembers past lives; can it be just to suffer for what you cannot recall?
  • Against: It can be misused to blame the poor, the sick or the disabled for their condition; many Hindus reject that use.
  • Against: If my present is shaped by the past, how free am I? Response: prarabdha sets the situation, not the choice.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit experience? Does it leave room for free will? Does it lead to compassion or to blame?
  • Spec issue: the impact of teachings about karma and reincarnation on the lifestyle of Hindus.

Mission 6

Varna + ashrama + dharma

  • Varnashramadharma: the dharma that fits your varna (class) and your ashrama (stage of life).
  • Its defenders present it as how universal order becomes social order: each person has a place and a duty, and society is held together as the cosmos is.
  • It is presented in the classical texts as an ideal social order: everyone's needs met by everyone's work. Critics see it as a justification of inequality.
  • Also called Caturvarnashramadharma: catur, "four" (four varnas, four ashramas).
Mission 6Read the source

The Purusha Sukta

Rig Veda 10.90 (paraphrase): the gods offer the cosmic Purusha (Person) in a great sacrifice. From his body the world is made. Verses 11–12:

  • From his mouth: the brahmana (priest).
  • From his arms: the rajanya (ruler, later called kshatriya).
  • From his thighs: the vaishya (merchant, farmer).
  • From his feet: the shudra (servant, labourer).
  • In the next verses the moon comes from his mind, the sun from his eye, the sky from his head.

Q1 What does the hymn suggest about society?

Show answer

Society as one body, each part with its own function; readers disagree whether head-to-foot order also implies a ranking.

Q2 What does it not mention?

Show answer

Jatis, untouchability, or rules about marriage and food.

Mission 6

Four varnas, four kinds of duty

  • As the classical texts describe them:
  • Brahmanas (brahmins): study and teach the Veda, perform ritual.
  • Kshatriyas: protect and rule; fight just wars.
  • Vaishyas: trade, farming, cattle.
  • Shudras: service of the other three.
  • The first three are dvija, "twice-born": the sacred-thread ceremony (upanayana) marks a second, spiritual birth.
  • The Gita lists each varna's qualities (Gita 18:41–44): serenity and learning for brahmins; courage for kshatriyas; trade and farming for vaishyas; service for shudras (paraphrase).
  • Your spec spells the four: brahmanas, kshatriyas, vaishyas, sudras.

Mission 6Diversity

By birth or by nature?

Krishna in the Gita:

> "I am He / Made the Four Castes, and portioned them a place / After their qualities and gifts." (Gita 4:13, Arnold)

  • The Sanskrit says by guna (qualities) and karma (actions).
  • By birth: the Dharma Shastras (e.g. Manusmriti) assign varna by birth; duties are inherited.
  • By nature: many reformers read varna as a matter of qualities: Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj); Vivekananda; today ISKCON and others.
Mission 6

Varna and jati

  • Varna: the four-fold scheme of the texts; ideal, all-India.
  • Jati: "birth group": thousands of local communities, often linked to an occupation, usually marrying within the group (endogamy), ranked by ideas of ritual purity.
  • Overlap: jatis claim or are given a place within a varna; that place can be disputed and has shifted over time.
  • Difference: the texts know four varnas; daily social life in India has been organised far more by jati.
  • English "caste" (from Portuguese casta) is used for both, which causes confusion.
  • Communities placed outside the four varnas were treated as "untouchable"; many now call themselves Dalits.

Mission 6

Jati in modern India: the debate

  • The Constitution of India (1950) abolished "untouchability" (Article 17) and banned discrimination by caste (Article 15); reserved places in education and jobs help disadvantaged groups.
  • Jati still shapes marriage, social networks and politics for many Indians; caste discrimination continues and is contested.
  • Views differ:
  • Reform: varna as an ideal of interdependent duties; jati ranking and untouchability as corruption (Gandhi's position for much of his life).
  • Abolition: the whole system is unjust and must go (B. R. Ambedkar, 1891–1956, who left Hinduism for Buddhism in 1956).
  • Identity: some value jati as community and heritage, without hierarchy.
Mission 6Evaluate

Does the Purusha Sukta make caste sacred?

  • Claim: The Purusha Sukta gives religious authority to the caste system.
  • For: It is shruti (revealed scripture), the highest authority, and it places the classes in a body from head to foot.
  • For: Later law books (Manusmriti) build birth-based rules on it.
  • Against: It is a late hymn (Book 10) and one image among many; it may describe the unity of society, not a ranking.
  • Against: It names four functions, not thousands of jatis or untouchability.
  • Against: Other teachings point another way: the wise look equally on a brahmin, a cow and an outcaste (Gita 5:18); varna by qualities (4:13).
  • Judgement criteria: What did the hymn mean then? How has it been used? What weight do other texts carry?

Mission 6

Varnashramadharma and righteous living

  • Doing one's svadharma well is righteous living: it earns punya (merit) and good karma.
  • Good karma leads to a better rebirth; on the traditional view, a better birth may be into another varna (Chandogya V.10.7).
  • The purusharthas are pursued within varnashramadharma: what counts as right artha for a kshatriya is not the same as for a brahmin.
  • The Gita adds: duty done without desire for its fruit leads beyond karma towards moksha.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vedanta interprets the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; Shankara's Advaita holds that Brahman alone is real and atman is Brahman.
  • The world appears through maya and avidya, by adhyasa (superimposition, like a rope seen as a snake); there are illusory, mundane and ultimate levels of reality.

Mission 2

  • Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita): souls and matter are real and form God's body; liberation is loving communion with Vishnu, like him but not him, reached by bhakti, surrender and grace.
  • Madhva (Dvaita): five eternal differences; God alone independent; souls graded; "you are not that".

Mission 3

  • In Hindu belief the Trimurti is three functions of saguna Brahman: Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva destroys (and renews).
  • Time is cyclic: yugas, kalpas, Brahma's day and night (Gita 8:17–19); liberation is release from the wheel of samsara.
  • Many deities, one Brahman; Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each see their deity as supreme over the three.

Mission 4

  • Vaishnavism (Vishnu supreme; bhakti, puja, ethical life), Shaivism (Shiva supreme; lingam, bhakti, asceticism) and Shaktism (Devi supreme) are the main theistic traditions.
  • Whether they are separate religions or one family is debated.

Mission 5

  • Karma: action and its fitting results, a law within the universal order; samsara: rebirth driven by karma, desire and ignorance (Gita 2:13; Katha 5:7).
  • Three kinds: accumulated (sanchita), fruit-bearing (prarabdha), karma in the making (kriyamana/agami).
  • Human birth is the chance to act freely and seek moksha; selfless action (nishkam karma, karma yoga) does not bind (Gita 2:47).

Mission 6

  • Varnashramadharma: duty by class (varna) and stage of life (ashrama), cosmic order as social order.
  • The Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90.11–12): brahmana from the mouth, rajanya from the arms, vaishya from the thighs, shudra from the feet.
  • Varna is a four-fold textual scheme; jatis are thousands of local birth groups; Hindus debate birth versus qualities (Gita 4:13) and the place of jati today.
