Mission 1

Mission 1: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Make a guess: About half of the 800-mile Trans-Alaska Pipeline runs above the ground. Why not bury all of it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.4 environmental impacts of a major energy resource development (oil, coal or gas field) and its distribution networks
Mission 1

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay, on Alaska's Arctic coast, in 1968.
  • The Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS) runs about 1,290 km (800 miles) from Deadhorse to the port of Valdez. It was completed in 1977 and has carried more than 18 billion barrels.
  • It is raised on supports for 420 miles and buried for 380.
Mission 1

Warm oil, frozen ground

  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost; in places it is up to 80% ice.
  • Oil was expected to flow at about 70–80 °C (158–176 °F); buried, it "would thaw a cylindrical area 20 to 30 feet in diameter within a decade".
  • Thawed ice-rich ground loses volume, so the pipe could sink and break.
  • So the pipe is raised where permafrost is unstable, and buried in thawed soils, bedrock or thaw-stable ground.
Quick check
?Why is TAPS buried along some stretches but raised along others?
Mission 1

The oil field's footprint

  • Oilfields need roads, gravel pads, pipelines and gravel mines across the tundra.
  • Raynolds and colleagues mapped 62 years of change (1949–2011) at Prudhoe Bay.
  • By 2010, over 34% of the intensively mapped area was affected by oil development.
  • Impacts on the landscape: dust, flooding beside roads, and changed vegetation and drainage.
  • Where ground ice melts, the ground caves in, forming thermokarst: "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls".
Mission 1

The distribution network

  • Spills of more than 55 gallons on TAPS averaged about 7.6 a year in 1977–1994 and 1.5 a year in 1995–2025.
  • From Valdez, tankers carry the oil south. On 24 March 1989 the Exxon Valdez grounded on Bligh Reef in Prince William Sound, spilling nearly 11 million gallons of Prudhoe Bay crude.
  • So the impacts of an energy development reach far beyond the field: along the pipeline and the shipping route.
Mission 1

A warming climate adds risk

  • The Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe in 1979–2021.
  • Hjort et al. project that 550 km of TAPS lies where near-surface permafrost may thaw by 2050 (a projection).
  • Across the Russian Arctic, 45% of hydrocarbon extraction fields are in regions where thaw-related ground instability could cause severe damage (projection).
Quick check
?Which of these is an environmental impact of the distribution network, not the oil field itself?
Mission check

?Engineers plan a pipeline to carry warm oil across ice-rich permafrost in Siberia. They propose raising long stretches on supports. Why?

Buried warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost; the ground would collapse into thermokarst ("irregular surfaces of marshy bowls") and take the pipe with it. So pipelines are raised over unstable permafrost and buried only where thaw does no harm.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Britain once made almost three-quarters of its electricity from coal. What replaced it, and at what cost?

Make a guess: Britain once made almost three-quarters of its electricity from coal. What replaced it, and at what cost?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.4 strategies to increase energy supply: oil and gas exploration, nuclear power, development of renewable sources
Mission 2

Why countries try to increase supply

  • Energy security: reliable, affordable supply that a country can control.
  • UK North Sea production peaked in 1999; UK total energy production is now 68% below that peak.
  • In 2025, 43.3% of the energy used in the UK was imported.
  • Strategies to increase supply: explore for more oil and gas · build nuclear · develop renewables (wind, solar, bioenergy).
Mission 2

The UK's changing electricity mix

  • Share of UK electricity generated (our calculation from DESNZ figures, TWh):
    • 1990: coal 72% · nuclear 20% · oil 6% · gas 0.1%
    • 2025: coal 0% · wind and solar 36% · gas 32% · other renewables 14% · nuclear 12%
  • The last coal-fired power station closed in September 2024: Ratcliffe-on-Soar.
  • Renewables' share of generation in 2025: 52.1%.
Quick check
?Which statement about UK electricity generation is supported by the DESNZ data?
Mission 2

Oil and gas exploration

  • Exploring for new reserves extends domestic supply and cuts import dependence.
  • 2023: the government backed future North Sea licensing rounds; the 33rd round was expected to award over 100 licences.
  • The government argued domestic gas has about one quarter the carbon footprint of imported liquefied natural gas (LNG).
  • Shale gas: in 2019 England imposed a moratorium on fracking after the regulator found earthquakes could not be accurately predicted (Preston New Road, Lancashire).
  • In 2024 the government said it would ban new coal mining licences. Exploration policy is contested and changes with governments.
Mission 2

Nuclear power: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: low-carbon, steady output, small land area. UK nuclear generation fell from 85.1 TWh (2000) and 71.7 TWh (2016) to 35.9 TWh in 2025, as plants closed and outages rose.
  • Costs: Hinkley Point C. The National Audit Office (2017) said the deal "locked consumers into a risky and expensive project"; expected top-up payments rose from £6 billion to £30 billion.
  • Sizewell C: final investment decision July 2025; target construction cost about £38 billion (2024 prices); government is the largest shareholder.
  • Long-lived radioactive waste and long build times are further costs.
Quick check
?Why did the National Audit Office criticise the Hinkley Point C deal?
Mission 2

Wind, solar and other renewables

  • Benefits: home-produced (security), very low emissions in use, fast to build in small units.
  • Wind and solar's share of UK electricity reached a record 36.3% in 2025.
  • Costs: output varies with weather. DESNZ notes "the intermittent nature of solar and wind" meant new capacity did not cover the closure of big fossil-fuelled stations. Backup, storage and grid links are needed.
  • Bioenergy supplied 38% of the UK's low-carbon primary energy in 2025; nuclear 22%; wind 21%.
Mission check

?A country imports most of its gas and wants supply that is both more secure and lower-carbon. Which judgement is best supported by the UK evidence?

Coal was replaced mainly by gas and then by wind and solar, with nuclear shrinking. Each strategy trades off security, cost and environment: exploration keeps fossil fuels flowing; nuclear is low-carbon but costly and slow; renewables are home-grown but variable.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Acid rain was the big energy worry of the 1980s. Why do we hear so little about it today?

Make a guess: Acid rain was the big energy worry of the 1980s. Why do we hear so little about it today?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.4 strategies to manage energy consumption, including reducing demand
  • 3.2.5.4 sustainability issues of energy production, trade and consumption: acid rain, enhanced greenhouse effect, nuclear waste, energy conservation
Mission 3

Managing consumption: two routes

  • Energy efficiency: the same service with less energy (insulation, efficient boilers, LED lighting).
  • Reducing demand: using less of the service (lower thermostat, fewer car journeys, walking or cycling).
  • Energy conservation combines both: saved energy is never produced, traded or burned.
  • UK domestic energy use per household: 1.9 toe (2000) to 1.2 toe (2025), tonnes of oil equivalent.
Mission 3

UK strategies

  • Insulation schemes: the Energy Company Obligation (from 2013) requires larger energy firms to install insulation and heating measures.
  • About 4.9 million measures were installed in 2.9 million properties under government schemes, 2013–2025.
  • GB homes with cavity wall insulation: 15.41 million; loft insulation (≥125 mm): 17.70 million (end of 2025).
  • Building Regulations set thermal standards for new homes. Prices matter too: DESNZ links falling domestic use to efficiency and, recently, to higher energy prices.
  • Not every sector is falling: transport used 55.0 mtoe in 2025, more than in 1990 (48.6).
Quick check
?Which action reduces demand, rather than improving efficiency?
Mission 3

Acid rain

  • Burning coal and oil releases sulfur dioxide (SO₂); it combines with water vapour to form acid rain, which can travel long distances.
  • Acid rain damages forests and freshwater habitats; UK emissions harmed Scandinavia in the 1970s and 1980s.
  • UK SO₂ emissions fell by 98% since 1990, to 84 thousand tonnes in 2024.
  • Why: closure or biomass conversion of coal power stations, low-sulfur fuels, and flue gas desulfurisation at remaining coal plants.
  • International agreement matters, because acid rain crosses borders: the UK's limits are set under the Convention on Long-Range Transboundary Air Pollution (CLRTAP) and UK regulations.
Quick check
?What mainly explains the fall in UK sulfur dioxide emissions since 1990?
Mission 3

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Natural greenhouse effect: gases such as CO₂ and water vapour absorb outgoing heat and keep Earth habitable.
  • Enhanced effect: burning fossil fuels adds CO₂, so more heat is retained.
  • Atmospheric CO₂ reached 423.9 ppm in 2024; the 3.5 ppm rise was the largest since modern measurements began in 1957.
  • Human-caused warming, 2010–2019 compared with 1850–1900: best estimate 1.07 °C.
  • UK CO₂ emissions: 284.8 Mt in 2025; greenhouse gas emissions 53.6% below 1990.
Mission 3

Nuclear waste

  • UK inventory (stock and future arisings, 2022): about 4.45 million m³ of packaged waste.
    • High level waste (HLW): about 1,470 m³, but about 14,000,000 TBq of radioactivity at 2100.
    • Intermediate level: about 496,000 m³, about 1,000,000 TBq.
    • Low level: about 1,340,000 m³, about 130 TBq.
  • HLW comes from reprocessing spent fuel at Sellafield; vitrified HLW is to be stored for at least 50 years before disposal.
  • Higher-activity wastes are being kept in stores; the UK plans a geological disposal facility (GDF) for them. Finland's Posiva says it is the first in the world to begin disposing of spent fuel deep in bedrock, at ONKALO.
Quick check
?High level waste is about 0.03% of the UK's packaged nuclear waste by volume. Why is it still the hardest to manage?
Mission 3

Energy conservation as a sustainability strategy

  • Every unit of energy not used avoids SO₂, CO₂ and, for nuclear electricity, waste.
  • UK energy use per unit of GDP (the energy ratio) has fallen by about 2½% a year since 1990.
  • But savings can be offset: the transport sector's use is still above its 1990 level.
Mission 3

Production, trade and consumption

  • Production: acid rain and CO₂ from burning; long-lived waste from nuclear power.
  • Trade: moving fuels uses energy and carries risks (pipelines, tankers); importing fuel moves the emissions of extraction to other countries.
  • Consumption: CO₂ from homes, transport and industry; conservation cuts all three stages.
  • Exam move (AQA "Evaluate"): weigh which issue a strategy solves against which new issue it creates, then judge.
Mission check

?A country closes its coal power stations and replaces them with nuclear power. Which judgement about sustainability is best?

Acid rain faded from the news because the UK and others cut SO₂ at source (less coal, cleaner fuels, desulfurisation). Every energy choice has sustainability costs somewhere: CO₂, waste or land. Conservation is the only option that reduces all of them.

Mission 4: Over half the world's iron ore comes from just two countries. Why there, and who buys it?

Make a guess: Over half the world's iron ore comes from just two countries. Why there, and who buys it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.5 mineral security studied through iron ore or one specified non-ferrous ore (this page: iron ore)

Mission 4

Why iron ore matters

  • This page studies one ore in depth: iron ore.
  • "The only source of primary iron is iron ore".
  • Iron becomes steel: world steel use was 1,718.2 Mt in 2025.
  • Mineral security: reliable, affordable access to the ores an economy needs.
Mission 4

End uses

  • World steel use by sector, 2025:
    • Buildings and infrastructure 52%
    • Mechanical equipment 16% · Automotive 12% · Metal products 10%
    • Other transport 5% · Electrical equipment 3% · Domestic appliances 2%
  • In the USA, 98% of iron ore products went to the steel industry.
  • So iron ore demand tracks construction above all.
Mission 4

Components of demand

  • China made 960.8 Mt of the world's 1,848.9 Mt crude steel in 2025: 52% (our calculation).
  • China's apparent iron ore consumption in 2024: 1,513.7 Mt of 2,553.6 Mt worldwide: 59% (our calculation).
  • In 2025 steel demand declined in China, offset by growth in Egypt, India, Saudi Arabia and Vietnam.
  • Demand components: construction, manufacturing, replacement of old stock; growth in emerging economies.
Quick check
?Which change would most reduce world demand for iron ore?
Mission 4

Sources: who mines it

  • Mine production 2025, usable ore:
    • Australia 980 Mt (37.7%) · Brazil 420 Mt (16.2%)
    • India 310 Mt · China 290 Mt
    • World 2,600 Mt
  • Australia and Brazil together: 53.8% (our calculation).
  • China mines a lot but uses far more: it is the world's largest importer.
Mission 4

Reserve distribution and grade

  • Reserves: ore that can be mined at a profit now. World: 200,000 Mt of crude ore holding 87,000 Mt of iron.
  • Crude ore reserves: Australia 59,000 Mt, Russia 35,000, Brazil 34,000, China 17,000.
  • Grade (iron ÷ crude ore, our calculation): Australia 46%, Brazil 44%, China 18%.
  • World resources exceed 900 billion tons of ore: far more than reserves.
Quick check
?China's reserves are 17,000 Mt of crude ore containing 3,000 Mt of iron. What is their average grade?
Mission 4

Role in global commerce

  • 2024: 1,740.5 Mt exported of 2,604.5 Mt produced: two-thirds of iron ore is traded across borders (our calculation).
  • Australia exported 901.6 Mt (52% of world exports); China imported 1,238.2 Mt (73% of world imports) (our calculation).
  • China's iron ore imports were worth US$132.4 billion; 60.1% came from Australia (our calculation).
  • Iron ore was 24% of the value of all Australia's goods exports (our calculation).
Mission 4

Geology: banded iron formations

  • Most ore in the Hamersley Province, Pilbara, sits in banded iron formation (BIF): thin layers of iron oxide and silica.
  • These rocks are ancient: one Hamersley BIF unit is about 2.46 billion years old.
  • Enrichment: hot fluids rose along faults; later, supergene weathering near the surface (Mesozoic to Tertiary) removed other minerals.
  • Result: high-grade ore of 60–68% iron: the "transformation of BIF to high-grade ore".
Mission 4

Location and working

  • The Hamersley Range holds about 80% of Australia's identified iron ore reserves.
  • Enriched ore formed "close to the present topographic surface": shallow, so it is dug by open-pit mining.
  • High grade means it can be shipped with little processing.
  • Working needs infrastructure: Rio Tinto runs 18 mines, nearly 2,000 km of railway and 4 port terminals.
  • Exam move (AQA "Explain"): link geology (BIF, enrichment) → grade and depth → cost of working → export role.
Quick check
?Why can much Pilbara iron ore be exported with little processing?
Mission check

?Two undeveloped deposits: X holds 3,000 Mt of 30% iron rock deep below ground, 600 km from the coast; Y holds 800 Mt of 62% iron ore at the surface, beside an existing railway. Which is more likely to be developed first, and why?

Iron ore matters because steel underpins construction and industry. Production is concentrated where geology made high-grade, shallow ore, chiefly Australia and Brazil, and trade links them to the dominant buyer, China.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Iron ore crosses an ocean before it becomes steel. Where along that journey does the damage happen?

Make a guess: Iron ore crosses an ocean before it becomes steel. Where along that journey does the damage happen?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.5 environmental impacts of a major mineral extraction scheme and its distribution networks
  • 3.2.5.5 sustainability issues in ore extraction, trade and processing
Mission 5

The scheme: Rio Tinto's Pilbara system

  • Pilbara, Western Australia: 18 mines, a rail network of nearly 2,000 km, 4 port terminals at Dampier and Cape Lambert.
  • Four power stations supply the operations and nearby towns.
  • AutoHaul: driverless heavy-haul trains moving over 1 million tonnes of ore a day.
  • A "major mineral extraction scheme" here = mines + railway + ports: impacts arise along the whole network.
Mission 5

At the mine: habitat loss

  • Yandicoogina Junction South West and Oxbow project: about 2,200 ha of potential habitat of the northern quoll and Pilbara olive python "may be impacted".
  • Both are nationally protected species.
  • Approval required an offset: at least $3 million over five years.
  • The offset area covers 163,214 ha, with about 66,000 ha of their habitat; the main action is baiting feral cats.
Mission 5

At the mine: groundwater

  • Many pits go below the water table, so water is pumped out: dewatering.
  • Rio Tinto's own test: "Does the removal of water from the operational areas … impact regional aquifers?".
  • Abstraction from the Bungaroo and Millstream aquifers "is of significant concern to Traditional Owner groups".
  • Responses: a desalination plant (8 GL a year) to ease pressure on them; managed aquifer recharge to return water underground.
Quick check
?A mine will clear 2,200 ha of habitat. The company funds feral-cat control over 163,214 ha elsewhere. What is this an example of?
Mission 5

At the mine: cultural heritage

  • May 2020: Rio Tinto "allowed the tragic destruction of the Juukan Gorge rock shelters", an "ancient, sacred site" of the Puutu Kunti Kurrama and Pinikura (PKKP) People.
  • The site was on Country where the Brockman 4 iron ore mine operates.
  • May 2025: a Co-Management Agreement with the PKKP Aboriginal Corporation sets how heritage proposals are handled "throughout the mine lifecycle".
  • Heritage loss cannot be offset: once destroyed, it is gone.
Mission 5

Along the network: railway and ports

  • Port dust: stockpiles, conveyors and ship-loading raise dust; fine PM10 is "associated with potential adverse health effects".
  • Cape Lambert terminal lies 3.5 km from the town of Point Samson; dust plans cover nearby towns.
  • Turtles: flatback, hawksbill and green turtles nest beside Cape Lambert, November to March.
  • Port threats: light spill, noise, vibration, dredging. Lights can misorient hatchlings away from the sea.
  • Rail automation saves "almost 1.5 million kilometres of road travel every year" moving drivers.
Quick check
?Which impact comes from the scheme's distribution network rather than from the mines?
Mission 5

Sustainability of extraction and trade

  • Finite stock: iron ore is a non-renewable resource; each tonne mined is gone.
  • Economic risk: iron ore was 24% of the value of Australia's goods exports in 2024 (our calculation). Prices fell: US$112.07 a tonne (first 9 months of 2024) to US$99.07 (same period, 2025).
  • Environment: habitat, water and dust impacts last as long as the mines do.
  • Social: heritage and Traditional Owner rights; company towns depend on one industry.
Mission 5

Sustainability of processing: steel

  • Steelmaking emitted about 1.92 tonnes of CO2 per tonne of crude steel (reporting companies, 2025).
  • For Rio Tinto, about two-thirds of its Scope 3 emissions (those from customers using its products) come from steelmaking.
  • Most processing is overseas: China took 73% of world iron ore imports in 2024 (our calculation).
  • Rio Tinto targets: 50% cut in its own emissions by 2030, net zero by 2050; it plans a green iron demonstration plant at Kwinana.
Quick check
?Rio Tinto's Scope 3 emissions come from others using or moving its products. Which activity causes about two-thirds of them?
Mission 5

Evaluating the whole chain

  • Extraction: habitat, groundwater, heritage. Partly managed (offsets, recharge, co-management); heritage loss is irreversible.
  • Trade: rail and ports bring dust, light and dredging; one export dominates the economy and prices swing.
  • Processing: the main source of the company's Scope 3 emissions, mostly in another country, so beyond Australian regulation.
  • Exam move (AQA "Evaluate"): judge each stage on scale, reversibility and who bears the cost, then reach an overall judgement.
Mission check

?A new iron ore mine is planned in a dry region. A railway will carry ore to a port beside a turtle nesting beach, for export to steelworks abroad. Which statement best evaluates its sustainability?

A mining scheme's impacts run along its whole network: habitat, water and heritage at the mine; dust and light at the ports; and heavy CO2 emissions where the ore is processed into steel, mostly overseas.

Mission 6

Futures, not forecasts

  • A resource future is one possible path for supply and demand, not a prediction.
  • Each path depends on four kinds of change:
    • technological (new ways to extract, save or replace);
    • economic (prices, costs, investment);
    • environmental (climate change, pollution limits);
    • political (policies, trade, conflict).
  • Agencies model scenarios: same starting point, different assumptions.
Mission 6

Energy futures: three IEA scenarios

  • Current Policies (CPS): only policies already in force; oil demand grows to 113 mb/d in 2050.
  • Stated Policies (STEPS): also policies announced; oil demand "levels off around 2030 at 102 mb/d" then slowly declines.
  • Net Zero Emissions by 2050 (NZE): a path to net zero energy CO₂ by 2050.
  • In all three, coal falls; electricity demand rises about 40% to 2035 (CPS, STEPS), over 50% in NZE.
Mission 6

What moves energy futures

  • Technology: solar additions; in CPS they "stall at around today's levels of 540 GW" a year to 2035.
  • Political: energy is now "a core issue of economic and national security".
  • Environmental: climate targets separate NZE from CPS.
  • Economic: the cost of solar, batteries and fuels decides what gets built.
  • So "running out" of oil is not the main question; which future we choose is.
Quick check
?What separates the IEA's Current Policies and Stated Policies scenarios?
Mission 6

Water futures: a changing cycle

  • Nearly 3 billion people and over half of world food production are in areas where total water storage is projected to decline.
  • Green water (soil moisture and vegetation) "generates around half of all rainfall over land".
  • Environmental change (warming, land clearing) is destabilising rainfall patterns.
  • Technology and management: about 8% of freshwater withdrawals could be reclaimed from wastewater each year; roughly 40% of urban water is lost through leaks.
Mission 6

Water futures in practice

  • Singapore: imports raw water from Malaysia's Johor River under an agreement that "expires in 2061"; demand "expected to almost double by 2065".
  • Response: NEWater (recycled water, since 2002) and desalination; reverse osmosis uses about 3.5 kWh per m³.
  • Jordan: the Aqaba–Amman project will desalinate Red Sea water and pipe it 450 km north, 300 million m³ a year, expected by the end of 2028.
  • Its emissions are capped at 3.2 kg CO₂ per m³: water security bought with energy.
Quick check
?Which change most directly pushes Singapore towards NEWater and desalination?
Mission 6

Mineral ore futures

  • Demand for battery and grid minerals is rising: lithium demand rose nearly 30% in 2024.
  • Copper: the mine pipeline "points to a potential 30% supply shortfall by 2035".
  • Lithium: demand "expected to push the market into deficit by the 2030s".
  • Yet lithium prices "fell by over 80% since 2023": a glut today can mean a shortage later, as low prices delay new mines.
  • Frontier supply: seabed nodules, with exploitation rules in development since 2014.
Mission 6

Who controls ore futures?

  • Political: one country is "the dominant refiner for 19 out of 20" energy-related strategic minerals.
  • More than half of a broader group of energy-related minerals face some form of export controls.
  • Technological: recycling grows, but "two-thirds of global battery recycling capacity growth since 2020 has been in China".
  • Environmental: deep-sea mining and new mines face assessment and opposition.
  • Concentration, not geology, is the main near-term risk: "The key risk for critical minerals is high levels of market concentration".
Quick check
?Lithium prices "fell by over 80% since 2023". If the 2023 price is set at 100, what is the most the price could be now?
Mission 6

Futures are linked: the resource nexus

  • Water needs energy: desalination at about 3.5 kWh per m³.
  • Energy needs minerals: grids, batteries and turbines need copper and lithium.
  • Minerals need water: the Water Commission calls for closed-loop water systems in metal mining.
  • A future that solves one shortage can deepen another.
  • Exam move ("Assess …"): compare two futures for one resource, name the driver of each, and judge which is more likely on the evidence.
Mission check

?A coastal country plans that by 2040 all new cars will be electric and half its water will come from desalination. Which risk to this future is best supported?

No resource simply "runs out" on a set date. Its future depends on technology, prices, environmental limits and politics, and the futures of energy, water and minerals are tied together.

Mission 7

Planning a whole basin

  • Integrated drainage basin management (drainage basin planning) plans land and water use across a whole basin, not river by river or sector by sector.
  • It balances water for the environment, farms, towns, industry and culture, and involves all the stakeholders.
  • It is part of SDG target 6.5: integrated water resources management "at all levels, including through transboundary cooperation".
Mission 7

Case study: the Murray–Darling Basin, Australia

  • The basin covers about 1 million km² of south-eastern Australia and is home to 2.4 million people.
  • "Over two-thirds of Australia's irrigation occurs in the Murray–Darling Basin".
  • It spans parts of New South Wales, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia and the Australian Capital Territory, each with its own water plans.
Mission 7

Resource security and human welfare

  • Irrigated farming supports jobs and towns across the basin; rivers, wetlands and floodplain forests depend on the same water.
  • Taking too much water threatens the environment; taking too little threatens farm incomes and towns. That is the resource-security problem.
  • First Nations people have cultural and economic interests in the rivers, and seek a share in decisions.
  • So the plan must decide how much water each use gets, and who carries the cost of change.
Mission 7

The Basin Plan: allocating water

  • The Basin Plan 2012, made under the Water Act 2007, "sets the amount of water that can be taken from the Basin each year": a sustainable diversion limit.
  • Governments agreed to recover 2,750 GL a year for the environment (about a 20% cut in water for consumptive use) and an additional 450 GL a year.
Mission 7

Outcomes so far

  • Recovered water has "improved river flows, connectivity, and ecosystem and biodiversity outcomes".
  • But the Plan "will not be fully implemented within the original timeframe or budget" (Productivity Commission, 2024).
  • Only 26 GL a year of the extra 450 GL had been recovered, and less than half of a 605 GL supply-measure offset.
  • 10 of 20 New South Wales water resource plans, due in 2019, were still not in place.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, what percentage of the extra 450 GL a year had been recovered?
Mission 7

Who bears the costs?

  • Water purchases from irrigators should be gradual "to avoid driving water market disruption and community adjustment pressures": farming towns bear costs.
  • Governments need to improve how they "partner and share decision making with First Nations people".
  • So environmental gains depend on who gives up water, and how they are helped.
Quick check
?Which statement best weighs the Murray–Darling Basin Plan?
Mission 7

Wetlands: surface stores under pressure

  • Since 1970, about 411 million ha of wetlands, roughly 22% of the global total, have been lost; losses continue at 0.52% a year.
  • About 25% of the remaining wetlands are in poor condition. Pressures include farming, pollution, infrastructure, disrupted water flows and climate change.
  • The Ramsar Convention (adopted at Ramsar, Iran, on 2 February 1971) has 172 parties and over 2,530 listed sites, covering about 14–17% of the world's wetlands.
  • Listing a site does not control the water that reaches it from upstream.
Mission check

?In Florida, water once "flowed slowly from the Kissimmee River to Florida Bay", forming the Everglades. Canals and levees drained much of it for farms and towns. The restoration plan (CERP), authorised in 2000, has 68 components and aims to restore the "quantity, quality, timing, and distribution" of water. Can it bring the wetland back?

Wetlands depend on water from the whole basin, so basin-wide planning is the tool. The Murray–Darling Plan has improved river health but is behind schedule and costly for farm towns. Restoration is possible, but slow, costly and contested.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.5.7 case study of a specified place where the physical environment affects the availability, cost and use of water, energy or ore
Mission 8

Two water-scarce countries

  • Singapore: an island city-state at the southern tip of the Malay Peninsula. GNI per person $81,760 (2025).
  • Jordan: a country in Western Asia, mostly dry. GNI per person $5,260 (2025).
  • Renewable internal freshwater per person, 2022: Singapore 106 m³, Jordan 61 m³.
  • Resource security means reliable access to water, food and energy, not self-sufficiency.
Mission 8

Singapore's physical environment

  • The government says Singapore is "amongst the most water-stressed countries in the world".
  • Reasons it gives: "the lack of natural water resources such as large rivers, natural springs or glaciers" and "limited land to collect and store rainwater".
  • So two-thirds of the land surface is used as water catchment, draining through more than 8,000 km of drains and canals into 17 reservoirs.
  • Physical limits on availability push Singapore towards costly, energy-hungry sources.
Mission 8

The Four National Taps

  • Local catchment: rain collected in reservoirs.
  • Imported water: up to 250 million gallons a day from Malaysia's Johor River, under an agreement that expires in 2061.
  • NEWater: used water treated by microfiltration, reverse osmosis and UV disinfection into "ultra-clean, high-grade reclaimed water".
  • Desalinated seawater.
  • NEWater and desalination are "weather-resilient".
Mission 8

Singapore: the cost of water is energy

  • Desalination by reverse osmosis "uses about 3.5kWh/m³ of energy".
  • About 95% of Singapore's electricity comes from imported natural gas.
  • Water is "priced to recover the full cost of its supply and production".
  • Water demand "is expected to almost double by 2065": more desalination and recycling means more energy.
Quick check
?A desalination plant produces 100,000 m³ of drinking water a day using 3.5 kWh per m³. How much energy does it use each day?
Mission 8

Recycling and conservation

  • Recycling: NEWater has been produced since 2002. It is used "mainly for industrial and air-con cooling purposes", and wafer fabrication plants are its biggest users.
  • Conservation: household use fell from 165 litres per person per day (2003) to 141 (2019).
  • Tools: full-cost pricing, water-efficient fittings and a Mandatory Water Efficiency Labelling Scheme.
  • Both cut the need for new supply, so they make the system more sustainable.
Mission 8

Jordan: severe scarcity

  • Jordan has about 61 m³ of renewable water per person a year, "much less than the minimum 500 cubic metres a person needs as per global standards".
  • Water stress was an estimated 105.2% in 2023: Jordan withdrew more freshwater than its renewable supply.
  • 10 of its 12 main aquifers are "being used faster than they can naturally recharge"; some fall by more than 10 m a year.
  • "Close to half of Jordan's drinking water is lost before it reaches people's homes"; around half the population receives water only once a week.
Mission 8

Jordan: the cost of new supply

  • The Aqaba–Amman Water Desalination and Conveyance Project will take Red Sea water at Aqaba, desalinate it and pipe it 450 km north to Amman: 300 million m³ a year, expected by the end of 2028.
  • The lender capped emissions at 3.2 kg of CO₂ per m³ and describes plans for solar power.
  • Energy is the catch: Jordan produced only 12% of the energy it used in 2022.
  • Jordan grew 80 thousand tonnes of cereals in 2023 against a supply of 3,539 thousand tonnes; 86% of its water footprint lay outside the country (1996–2005).
Quick check
?What physical constraint does Singapore's government give for its water stress?
Mission 8

Security is not self-sufficiency

  • Both countries import most of their food and energy. The difference is reliability of access.
  • Singapore: money, diverse sources (four taps), stockpiles and technology; its GNI per person is about 15.5 times Jordan's (our calculation).
  • For food, Singapore's government names four pillars: "Diversify Imports, Global Partnerships, Stockpile and Grow Local".
  • Jordan: overdrawn aquifers, high losses and weekly supply; new desalination will depend on imported energy or new solar.
  • Risks remain for both: dependence on partners, energy imports and shocks to shipping routes.
Mission check

?Country X is dry, wealthy and gets most of its drinking water from several desalination plants, with a year's stored reserve. Country Y is poorer and relies on one shared aquifer whose level falls each year. Which is more water-secure, and why?

Singapore is more secure. Its GNI per person is about 15.5 times Jordan's; it has four water "taps", a diversified food strategy and stockpiles. Jordan has 61 m³ of renewable water per person, aquifers in decline, and about half its people get water once a week. Security means reliable access, not self-sufficiency.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • TAPS is raised for 420 of its 800 miles because warm oil would thaw ice-rich permafrost.
  • Impacts spread from field (pads, roads, dust, thermokarst) to network (pipeline spills, the Exxon Valdez).

Mission 2

  • Supply strategies: oil and gas exploration, nuclear, renewables. UK electricity moved from 72% coal (1990) to 0% coal and 36% wind and solar (2025).

Mission 3

  • Manage consumption by efficiency and reducing demand: UK home energy per household fell from 1.9 to 1.2 toe (2000–2025), helped by insulation schemes, regulations and prices.
  • Sustainability issues: acid rain (UK SO₂ −98% since 1990), enhanced greenhouse effect (CO₂ 423.9 ppm in 2024), nuclear waste (HLW tiny in volume, huge in radioactivity), conservation reduces them all.

Mission 4

  • Iron ore → steel: 52% of steel goes into buildings and infrastructure; China makes about half the world's steel and uses about three-fifths of its iron ore.
  • Australia and Brazil mine over half the world's ore and hold the largest high-grade reserves, because enriched banded iron formations lie near the surface; two-thirds of ore is traded, mostly by sea to China.

Mission 5

  • Rio Tinto's Pilbara scheme (18 mines, nearly 2,000 km of railway, 4 port terminals) affects habitat, groundwater and Aboriginal heritage at the mines, and air quality and turtle nesting at the ports.
  • Sustainability depends on the whole chain: finite ore and price swings in extraction and trade, and high CO2 emissions in steelmaking, which happens mainly in China.

Mission 6

  • Futures are scenarios: the IEA's CPS, STEPS and NZE differ mainly in policy; water futures depend on climate and land change plus reuse and desalination; ore futures depend on demand, prices and concentrated supply.
  • Energy, water and minerals are linked: solving one can strain another.

Mission 7

  • The Murray–Darling Basin Plan allocates water between irrigation and the environment: rivers are healthier, but recovery is late and its costs fall on farming communities.

Mission 8

  • Singapore's physical limits (no large rivers, little land to store rain) make water costly: desalination and recycling run on imported energy.
  • Jordan's dry climate and overdrawn aquifers make supply unreliable; its new desalination project needs energy it mostly imports.
