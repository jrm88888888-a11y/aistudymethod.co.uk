Mission 1

Mission 1: DNA uses just four kinds of base. How can that be enough to hold the instructions for a whole organism?

Make a guess: DNA uses just four kinds of base. How can that be enough to hold the instructions for a whole organism?

Mission 1

The message is in the sequence

  • The sugar–phosphate backbone is the same all along. Only the bases vary.
  • The order of bases, the base sequence, is the information.
  • Any base can follow any other: nothing fixes the order.
  • Same four bases, different order, different message. Like letters spelling different words.
  • The four bases are the basis of a code.
  • DNA is the genetic material of all living organisms. Some viruses use RNA, but viruses are not considered living.
  • Say: "the information is in the base sequence" — not: "in the shape of the helix".
Mission 1

Worked example: how many sequences?

  • 1 position: 4 possible bases.
  • 2 positions: 4 × 4 = 16 sequences.
  • 3 positions: 4 × 4 × 4 = 4³ = 64.
  • n positions: 4ⁿ. Possibilities multiply; they do not add.
  • 10 bases: 4¹⁰ = 1 048 576 sequences.
  • Any length, any sequence: no upper limit. Capacity is effectively limitless.
  • Economy: a human body-cell nucleus, a few micrometres across, holds about 6 × 10⁹ base pairs (two sets of chromosomes).
  • Say: "4ⁿ" — not: "4 × n" or "n⁴".
Quick check
?How many different base sequences are possible for a single DNA strand that is 10 nucleotides long?
Mission 1

Pairing copies the message and sends it out

  • Copy: the strands separate; each is a template. Free nucleotides pair with complementary bases. Two identical molecules result.
  • Express: a gene's sequence is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), again by complementary pairing (A with U).
  • Complementarity rests on hydrogen bonding: each base hydrogen-bonds stably only with its partner.
  • One rule, two jobs. Details: DNA replication, transcription.
Quick check
?The template strand of a gene has the base sequence TACGGA at one position. What base sequence will the mRNA transcribed from it have at that position?
Mission 1

Too simple to be the genes?

  • By the 1940s, chromosomes were known to contain DNA and protein.
  • Protein: 20 kinds of amino acid. Endless variety.
  • DNA: just 4 bases on a repetitive backbone.
  • The tetranucleotide hypothesis: DNA is the four bases repeated, in equal amounts.
  • If so: 25% of each base in every organism, and no room for a message.
  • Two lines of evidence: Chargaff's base data and the Hershey–Chase experiment.

Mission 1

Hershey and Chase: what gets inside?

  • A bacteriophage is a virus that infects bacteria: a protein coat around DNA.
  • Phosphorus is in DNA, not protein. Sulfur is in protein, not DNA.
  • Batch 1: grown with ³⁵S → protein coats labelled. Batch 2: grown with ³²P → DNA labelled.
  • Each batch infects bacteria. A blender shakes the coats off the cell surfaces.
  • A centrifuge spins the bacteria into a pellet. Free coats stay in the liquid.
  • Result: ³⁵S mostly in the liquid; ³²P mostly in the pellet. New phages carry ³²P.
  • So DNA enters the cell and directs new phages. DNA is the genetic material.
  • Only possible once radioisotopes became available as research tools.
Quick check

Most of the ³²P was in the pellet and most of the ³⁵S was in the liquid above it, and the new phages produced by the bacteria contained ³²P. What do these results show?
Mission check

?Two DNA molecules from two hypothetical species of bacteria each contain the same number of nucleotides, and in both, 30% of the bases are adenine, 30% thymine, 20% guanine and 20% cytosine. The two molecules carry different genetic information. Which statement best explains how this is possible?

Four bases are plenty: the message is in their order, and the order can be anything, at any length.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Make a guess: A bacterium has no nucleus. So where does it keep its genes, and what else does it manage without?

Mission 2

What every cell has

  • Every cell has: a plasma membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and DNA.
  • Cytoplasm is mainly water: the solvent for metabolism.
  • The membrane is lipid: its hydrophobic core is a barrier to water-soluble substances.
  • DNA is the genetic material of every living organism. Some viruses use RNA, but viruses are not considered living.
  • Two kinds of cell:
    • Prokaryotic (bacteria, archaea): no nucleus.
Mission 2

Inside a bacterium

  • No nucleus. One circular DNA molecule lies free in the cytoplasm, in the nucleoid region.
  • The DNA is naked (no histones) and in a loop.
  • Ribosomes are smaller: 70S (eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S).
  • No membrane-bound organelles: no mitochondria, no ER, no Golgi.
  • Cell wall of peptidoglycan, outside the plasma membrane.
  • IB model cell: a Gram-positive eubacterium, such as Bacillus or Staphylococcus.
  • Say: "the DNA is in the nucleoid" — not: "the DNA is in a small nucleus".
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a Bacillus cell is correct?

  • A cell wall does not tell you the cell type. Look for a nucleus.
Quick check
?A unicellular organism is found to have a cell wall, a plasma membrane and ribosomes, but no nucleus. Its DNA is a single circular molecule, not associated with histones, lying in the cytoplasm. A student concludes that the organism is a prokaryote. Which evaluation of this conclusion is correct?
Mission 2

Why a bacterium needs its wall

  • Bacterial cytoplasm is rich in solutes, so in dilute surroundings water enters by osmosis.
  • The wall is strong: it resists swelling and stops the cell bursting.
  • Water and small solutes pass through the wall; the plasma membrane, inside it, controls what enters the cell.
  • Strip the wall away (e.g. with the enzyme lysozyme) and a bacterium in water bursts.
Mission check

?An electron micrograph shows a cell about 3 µm long. It has a cell wall, a paler fibrous region of DNA not enclosed by any membrane, many small dense granules, and no membrane-bound organelles. How should the cell be classified?

No nucleus, but not disorganised: the genes sit in the nucleoid, and the cell manages without membrane-bound organelles.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Make a guess: A virus cannot feed, grow or divide. So how does one virus become millions?

Mission 3

A virus is not a cell

  • A virus particle is acellular: no cytoplasm, no ribosomes, few or no enzymes.
  • It has genetic material, DNA or RNA, never both.
  • The genetic material sits inside a capsid: a coat of protein.
  • Shared by all viruses: small, fixed size; DNA or RNA; protein capsid; no cytoplasm; few or no enzymes.
  • Viruses have no metabolism and are replicated only inside a host cell, so they are not self-sustaining. The cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life.
  • Viruses do not divide. The host cell's ribosomes, enzymes and ATP make new virus particles.
  • Say: "virus particle" — not: "virus cell".
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all viruses?
Mission 3

Two very different viruses

  • Bacteriophage (phage): infects bacteria.
    • DNA inside a protein head; a protein tail injects the DNA.
    • Example: phage lambda: double-stranded DNA; no envelope.
  • HIV: infects human helper T cells.
    • RNA (two single strands) inside a protein capsid.
    • An envelope: a lipid bilayer taken from the host cell, studded with glycoproteins.
    • Carries the enzyme reverse transcriptase: HIV is a retrovirus.
  • Coronaviruses: single-stranded RNA; enveloped, with spike proteins.
  • So viruses are diverse: DNA or RNA, single- or double-stranded, enveloped or not.

  • Most viruses are 10–100× smaller than a bacterium; many need an electron microscope to be seen.
Mission 3

How a host cell makes viruses

  • (1) Attachment: viral proteins bind to complementary receptors on the host cell.
  • (2) Entry: the nucleic acid enters. A phage injects its DNA; the capsid stays outside.
  • (3) Synthesis: the host's ribosomes, enzymes, nucleotides, amino acids and ATP make viral nucleic acid and proteins.
  • (4) Assembly: new particles self-assemble.
  • (5) Release: phage enzymes break down the wall; the cell bursts (lysis), releasing new phages.
  • This is the lytic cycle.
  • Example: phage lambda in E. coli.
  • One cell can release hundreds of new particles: that is how numbers explode.
Quick check
?Bacteriophage lambda does not multiply in a sterile nutrient broth containing amino acids, nucleotides and ATP, but does multiply when living E. coli cells are added to the broth. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

The lysogenic cycle

  • Lambda can take a second route in E. coli.
  • (1) The phage DNA integrates into the host chromosome: it is now a prophage.
  • (2) No new phages are made. Each time the host divides, the prophage is copied with the host DNA.
  • (3) A trigger (e.g. UV light damaging DNA) makes the prophage leave the chromosome and enter the lytic cycle.
  • Say: "the prophage is dormant: it makes no phages" — not: "lysogeny is a slow lytic cycle".
Quick check
?A culture of E. coli cells carrying the lambda prophage is growing normally. It is exposed to ultraviolet light, and about an hour later most of the cells lyse and release phages. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

Where did viruses come from?

  • Viruses are too diverse for one origin: probably several origins, from different organisms.
  • Ideas include: escaped genes of cells that gained a capsid; parasitic cells that lost most of their parts.
  • Viruses use the same genetic code as cells: they arose from cellular life.
  • Shared features (tiny, capsid, total dependence on a host) suit extreme obligate parasitism.
  • So these features may be convergent evolution, not evidence of one common ancestor.
  • Say: "similar features evolved independently" — not: "all viruses share one ancestor because they look alike".
Mission 3

Viruses evolve fast

  • Influenza and HIV copy their genomes with enzymes that do not proofread: high mutation rate.
  • Huge numbers of particles, short generation times: many variants, fast selection.
  • Influenza: point mutations alter surface antigens (antigenic drift) → vaccine updated yearly.
  • HIV: drug-resistant variants arise within one patient → treated with a combination of drugs at once.
  • Two related viruses in the same host cell can recombine: new mixes of genes.
  • Influenza A's genome is in 8 segments: two strains in one cell can reassort (antigenic shift), as in the 2009 pandemic strain.
  • Mutations are random; drugs and antibodies select variants that already exist.
  • Say: "resistant variants are selected" — not: "the virus mutates to escape the drug".
Quick check
?In 2009 a new strain of influenza A appeared with genome segments derived from swine, avian and human influenza viruses, and it caused a pandemic. Which process produced this strain?
Mission check

?The antibiotic tetracycline blocks bacterial ribosomes but does not block the ribosomes in the cytoplasm of human cells. A doctor is asked whether tetracycline would stop influenza viruses multiplying in the cells lining a patient's airways. Which answer is correct?

One virus becomes millions because each infected cell becomes a factory, using its own ribosomes, enzymes and ATP to build new particles.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Make a guess: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Mission 4

Carbon: the backbone

  • A covalent bond: two atoms share a pair of electrons.
  • Carbon has four outer electrons, so it forms up to four covalent bonds.
  • Single bonds, or a mix of single and double bonds.
  • Partners: other carbon atoms, or other non-metals (H, O, N, P, S).
  • Carbon links to carbon again and again: chains (branched or unbranched) and rings (single or multiple).
  • Result: a huge diversity of compounds.
  • Scientific conventions are agreed internationally, e.g. SI prefixes: kilo 10³, centi 10⁻², milli 10⁻³, micro 10⁻⁶, nano 10⁻⁹.
Quick check
?Carbon forms an enormous diversity of compounds, on which life is based. Which statement about the bonding of a single carbon atom is correct?
Mission 4

Monomers and polymers

  • Monomer: a small unit from which larger molecules are made.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
MonomerPolymer
Monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)Polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
Amino acidPolypeptide (protein)
NucleotidePolynucleotide (DNA, RNA)
  • Triglycerides are built the same way, but are not polymers: one glycerol + three fatty acids, not a repeating chain.
Mission 4

Condensation: building

  • Condensation: joins two molecules with a new covalent bond and eliminates a molecule of water.
  • One monomer loses an –OH, the other an –H. Together: H2O.
  • One water per bond formed.
  • Repeat: monomer → dimer → polymer.
  • Cells use enzymes for each joining reaction.
Mission 4

Hydrolysis: breaking

  • Hydrolysis: breaks a covalent bond between two molecules and uses a water molecule.
  • The water is split: –H goes to one product, –OH to the other.
  • One water per bond broken.
  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes make it fast; polymers in plain water break down extremely slowly.
  • Say: "condensation releases water; hydrolysis uses water" — not: the other way round.
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides are joined by a condensation reaction to form a disaccharide. What happens in this reaction?
Mission 4

Worked example: counting the water

  • Bonds sit between monomers. An unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds.
  • So building it releases n − 1 water molecules; hydrolysing it uses n − 1.
  • Example: 5 glucose molecules joined into one unbranched chain.
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.
  • Bonds: 5 − 1 = 4. Water released: 4.
  • Mr of chain = 5 × 180 − 4 × 18 = 900 − 72 = 828.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it exactly: 828 + 4 × 18 = 900.
Quick check
?A polypeptide made of 100 amino acids is completely hydrolysed into free amino acids. How many water molecules are used in the process?

  • RNA: condensation links the phosphate of one nucleotide to the sugar of the next.
Mission 4

Anabolism and catabolism

  • Metabolism: all the enzyme-catalysed reactions in an organism.
  • Anabolism: building larger molecules from smaller ones. Needs energy.
  • Examples: condensation to make macromolecules: protein synthesis, glycogen formation; photosynthesis.
  • Catabolism: breaking larger molecules into smaller ones. Releases energy.
  • Examples: hydrolysis of macromolecules into monomers in digestion; oxidation of substrates in respiration.
Quick check
?In the small intestine, maltase converts each maltose molecule into two glucose molecules. How is this reaction classified?
Mission check

?Seeds store starch: unbranched and branched chains of thousands of glucose units. During germination, enzymes hydrolyse the starch completely to glucose. Starting from 1.00 g of starch, about what mass of glucose forms?
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.

Building releases one water per link, so breaking down has to put each one back.

Mission 5: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 5

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
  • After replication, each chromosome is two DNA molecules (chromatids), held together until anaphase.
Quick check
?A cut in the skin heals as new skin cells are produced by cell division. Why must DNA replication take place before each of these divisions?
Mission 5

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand in condensation reactions, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug inhibits helicase but has no effect on DNA polymerase. What will happen to DNA replication in a cell treated with the drug?
Mission 5

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?During replication, a section of a template strand with the base sequence TACGGA is copied. Which statement describes the new strand built on this section?
Mission 5

Accurate, but not perfect

  • Every new strand is built against an original template, so the sequence is conserved.
  • Only complementary bases pair stably, so nearly every nucleotide added is correct.
  • Very rarely, a wrong base is added. If not corrected, it is copied in every later replication.
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Make a guess: How did one experiment with heavy nitrogen show that every new DNA molecule keeps half of the old one?

Mission 6

Three possible models

  • Conservative: the parent molecule stays whole; the copy is all new.
  • Semi-conservative: each new molecule has one parent strand + one new strand.
  • Dispersive: old and new DNA are mixed in pieces along every strand.
  • In 1958, Meselson and Stahl designed an experiment that gave each model a different prediction.
Mission 6

The experiment

  • Bases contain nitrogen. ¹⁵N is a heavy isotope; ¹⁴N is the normal, light one.
  • E. coli were grown for many generations on ¹⁵N: all their DNA became heavy.
  • They were moved to a ¹⁴N medium. Every new strand made after that is light.
  • Samples were taken after each generation (one round of replication).
  • DNA was extracted and spun in a caesium chloride density gradient.
  • DNA settles where its density matches the solution: heavier DNA settles lower.
Mission 6

Read the data

Generations in ¹⁴NBands seen
0one band: heavy
1one band: intermediate
2two bands, equal amounts: intermediate and light
  • Intermediate (hybrid) DNA = one ¹⁵N strand + one ¹⁴N strand.
  • Light DNA = two ¹⁴N strands.
  • Read each row as a test. Which models survive it?
Quick check
?Bacteria were grown for many generations with only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N), so all their DNA was heavy. They were then moved to a medium with only light nitrogen (¹⁴N). After one round of replication, all the DNA had a density midway between heavy and light. After a second round, half the DNA was of this intermediate density and half was light. What do these results show about replication?
Mission 6

Which model fails, and when

  • Conservative predicts generation 1: two bands, heavy and light. Seen: one intermediate band. Refuted at generation 1.
  • Dispersive predicts generation 1: one intermediate band. That fits.
  • But dispersive predicts generation 2: still one band, lighter than intermediate. Seen: two bands. Refuted at generation 2.
  • Semi-conservative predicts both results. It is supported.
  • Say: "the results support the semi-conservative model" — not: "the results prove it".

  • The two original ¹⁵N strands never break up. Each ends up in one hybrid molecule.
  • So there are always exactly 2 hybrid molecules; all the rest are light.

Mission check

?The experiment is run in reverse. Bacteria are grown for many generations on light nitrogen (¹⁴N), then moved to a medium containing only heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N). Replication is semi-conservative. What will the DNA show after two generations on ¹⁵N?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The base sequence is the information; the backbone is the same throughout. Complementary pairing lets the sequence be copied and passed to RNA.
  • 4ⁿ possible sequences for n bases: capacity is effectively limitless.
  • Chargaff's data falsified the tetranucleotide hypothesis; Hershey and Chase showed that DNA, not protein, enters the cell.

Mission 2

  • A prokaryote keeps one circular DNA molecule in the nucleoid, with no nucleus and no membrane-bound organelles; it still has a membrane, cytoplasm, ribosomes and (usually) a wall.

Mission 3

  • A virus is an acellular particle: DNA or RNA in a protein capsid, with no metabolism; host cells make and assemble new particles, often hundreds per cell.
  • Lytic cycle: attach, inject, synthesise, assemble, lyse. Lysogenic: phage DNA integrates as a dormant prophage until induced.

Mission 4

  • Monomers join by condensation (one water released per bond) and polymers are split by hydrolysis (one water used per bond).

Mission 5

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.

Mission 6

  • Meselson and Stahl (1958): heavy (¹⁵N) DNA, then growth on ¹⁴N; one intermediate band after 1 generation, intermediate + light after 2.
  • Generation 1 refutes conservative; generation 2 refutes dispersive; semi-conservative predicts both.
