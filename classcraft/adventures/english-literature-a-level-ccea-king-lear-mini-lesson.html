Mission 1

Why does King Lear tell the same story twice?

Make a guess: Why does King Lear tell the same story twice?

King Lear · structure and the double plotScreen 1

The play in five acts

King Lear was written c.1605–06 and acted before James I at court on 26 December 1606.

ActWhat happens
1Lear divides Britain between Goneril and Regan after a love test. He banishes Cordelia, whom France marries without a dowry, and Kent. Edmund turns Gloucester against Edgar with a forged letter. Lear quarrels with Goneril over his hundred knights.
2Edgar flees and disguises himself as Poor Tom, a mad beggar. Kent, now disguised as a servant, is put in the stocks. Regan and Goneril cut Lear's train to nothing; he goes out into the storm.
3On the heath Lear goes mad, with the Fool, Kent and Poor Tom. Gloucester helps Lear; Edmund betrays him; Cornwall blinds him and is fatally wounded by a servant.
4Edgar leads his blind father to Dover, where Gloucester tries to leap from a cliff. Cordelia lands with a French army and is reunited with Lear. Goneril and Regan both desire Edmund.
5Edmund and Albany win the battle; Lear and Cordelia are captured. Edgar defeats Edmund in single combat. Goneril poisons Regan and kills herself. Edmund's order to kill Cordelia is cancelled too late; Lear enters with her body and dies.

King Lear · structure and the double plotScreen 3

Two plots, one pattern

  • The main plot comes from the old story of King Leir; the subplot, Gloucester and his sons, Shakespeare took from Sidney's Arcadia.
  • The plots cross: Edmund betrays his father for helping Lear, and both sisters want Edmund.
  • A. C. Bradley argued that "the sub-plot simply repeats the theme of the main story", so we feel we are watching "something universal".

King Lear · structure and the double plotScreen 6

From order to catastrophe

  • Order breaks in Act 1 (the map divided); complication and disorder grow through Acts 2–3 (the storm); Act 4 brings recognitions; Act 5 the catastrophe.
  • In Shakespeare's main sources Leir wins back his throne; Shakespeare's Cordelia dies.
  • Two early texts: the 1608 Quarto has the mock trial (3.6); the 1623 Folio cuts it and gives the last speech to Edgar, not Albany. Most editions, like ours, merge them.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does King Lear tell the same story twice?

Shakespeare joins two stories so that each mirrors the other. Gloucester's blinding makes Lear's blindness visible, and the repetition suggests that the breaking of the "bond cracked ’twixt son and father" is not one family's accident but a condition of the play's world.

Mission 2

Does Lear learn anything, or does he just suffer?

Make a guess: Does Lear learn anything, or does he just suffer?

King Lear · Lear's power, folly and sufferingScreen 1

Power as performance

  • Lear gives away "the sway, / Revenue, execution of the rest" but keeps "The name, and all the addition to a king;". He wants the title without the work.
  • His love test makes love a public speech with land as the prize.
  • Kent names the danger: "When majesty falls to folly."
  • Regan says he "hath ever but slenderly known himself".
  • AQA's aspects of tragedy fit him closely: pride, folly, blindness and insight.

King Lear · Lear's power, folly and sufferingScreen 5

Redemption or ruin?

  • A. C. Bradley (1904) suggested calling the play "The Redemption of King Lear": suffering leads him to humility and love.
  • Bradley believed Lear dies in "an unbearable joy", thinking Cordelia breathes: "Look there, look there!" (a Folio line).
  • Against this: Lear's last lines also say "Thou’lt come no more, / Never, never, never, never, never!". Many later critics see no redemption (mission 7).

Mission 3

Is Edmund a born villain, or made one by his world?

Make a guess: Is Edmund a born villain, or made one by his world?

King Lear · Gloucester, Edgar and EdmundScreen 1

The Gloucester family

  • Gloucester has two sons: Edgar, legitimate and older, and Edmund, illegitimate and younger.
  • In 1.1 Gloucester jokes about Edmund's conception in front of him: "there was good sport at his making". Edmund "hath been out nine years".
  • Under primogeniture the eldest legitimate son inherited the land; an illegitimate son was shut out.
  • Shakespeare took the story of a deceived father and two sons from Sidney's Arcadia.

King Lear · Gloucester, Edgar and EdmundScreen 4

Edmund: rationalist and performer

  • He mocks astrology as "the excellent foppery of the world": his choices are his own.
  • He plays a part for every victim, and confides in us in soliloquy: "Let me, if not by birth, have lands by wit;".
  • Dying, he tries to save Lear and Cordelia: "Some good I mean to do, / Despite of mine own nature." It is too late.
  • Hazlitt (1817) said his lack of hypocrisy "is the only relief in the character of Edmund".
King Lear · Gloucester, Edgar and EdmundScreen 5

Gloucester and Edgar

  • Gloucester believes the letter at once and disowns Edgar: "I never got him."
  • Yet he defies Regan and Cornwall to help Lear; Edmund betrays him; he is blinded and cries "O my follies! Then Edgar was abus’d."
  • Edgar is "a brother noble" whom Edmund thinks easy to fool. He becomes Poor Tom, his father's guide, then Edmund's armed challenger.
  • To dying Edmund he says "The gods are just": Gloucester's adultery "Cost him his eyes." Many readers find this harsh.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Is Edmund a born villain, or made one by his world?

Both. Shakespeare shows a world that names Edmund "base" and gives everything to Edgar, then lets Edmund choose each crime with open eyes. His soliloquies make us understand him; his actions stop us excusing him.

Mission 4

Are Goneril and Regan monsters, or daughters with a point?

Make a guess: Are Goneril and Regan monsters, or daughters with a point?

King Lear · Goneril, Regan, Cordelia and the FoolScreen 1

Three daughters, one test

  • Goneril: "Sir, I love you more than word can wield the matter;". Regan claims to love him even more.
  • Cordelia: "Nothing, my lord." She loves him "According to my bond; no more nor less." A husband, she says, will rightly take "Half my love".
  • In 1603 Sir Brian Annesley's eldest daughter tried to have the old man declared unfit to manage his estate; his youngest, Cordell, defended him. Some scholars link this to the play.

King Lear · Goneril, Regan, Cordelia and the FoolScreen 4

How the sisters change

  • Their early case: Lear "always loved our sister most", struck Goneril's gentleman (1.3), and keeps a hundred knights.
  • Then they cut his train to nothing: "What need one?" (2.4). Regan: "Shut up your doors." as the storm begins.
  • Goneril cries "Pluck out his eyes." (3.7); Regan stabs the servant who defends Gloucester.
  • They become rivals for Edmund. Goneril poisons Regan, then kills herself.
  • Albany calls them "Tigers, not daughters" (a Quarto-only passage).
King Lear · Goneril, Regan, Cordelia and the FoolScreen 5

Reading the sisters

  • Hazlitt (1817) found them "so thoroughly hateful that we do not even like to repeat their names".
  • Kathleen McLuskie (1985) argued that the play's emotional power depends on accepting a father's authority, so female independence looks monstrous; a feminist reader can resist that pull.
  • Ask: does the play give their case a fair hearing before it condemns them?
King Lear · Goneril, Regan, Cordelia and the FoolScreen 6

Cordelia and the Fool

  • Cordelia refuses "that glib and oily art". She returns with "love, dear love, and our ag’d father’s right:".
  • Bradley: "Of all Shakespeare's heroines she knew least of joy". Hazlitt saw "a little of her father's obstinacy" in her.
  • The Fool is a licensed truth-teller: "Now thou art an O without a figure:". He vanishes after 3.6.
  • Lear's "And my poor fool is hang’d!" most likely means Cordelia, but recalls the Fool.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Are Goneril and Regan monsters, or daughters with a point?

Both. Act 1 gives Goneril and Regan a real case against a rash father and his "riotous" knights, but from Act 2 their actions grow from firmness into cruelty. Critics such as McLuskie ask whether the play stacks the deck by making female power look monstrous.

Mission 5

Lear and Edmund both pray to Nature. Do they mean the same thing?

Make a guess: Lear and Edmund both pray to Nature. Do they mean the same thing?

King Lear · nature, order and kingshipScreen 1

Two Natures

  • For Lear, Gloucester and Albany, Nature is a God-given order of bonds: king and subject, father and child. To break it is unnatural.
  • Lear appeals to "The offices of nature, bond of childhood," (2.4).
  • For Edmund, Nature is energy and appetite, against "the plague of custom" (1.2).
  • The play keeps testing which is true. Lear asks: "Is there any cause in nature that makes these hard hearts?" (3.6).

King Lear · nature, order and kingshipScreen 4

A king divides Britain

  • James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603, uniting the crowns; a common British identity was a major aim of his reign.
  • James's sons held the titles Duke of Cornwall and Duke of Albany, like Regan's and Goneril's husbands.
  • The play was acted before James on 26 December 1606. Its first scene shows a king splitting Britain with a map.
  • Andrew Hadfield reads this as an inversion of James's union.
King Lear · nature, order and kingshipScreen 5

Eclipses, treason and the Gunpowder Plot

  • On 5 November 1605 Catholic plotters were caught trying to blow up Parliament as James arrived for its opening.
  • A lunar and a solar eclipse followed over London that autumn, two weeks apart.
  • So Gloucester's "These late eclipses" and "in palaces, treason" (1.2) sounded topical to the first audiences.
  • Edmund mocks such fears as "the excellent foppery of the world": a sceptical voice inside the play.
King Lear · nature, order and kingshipScreen 6

Power stripped bare

  • Mad, Lear sees authority as a cover: "Robes and furr’d gowns hide all." Justice serves the rich: "Plate sin with gold, / And the strong lance of justice hurtless breaks;".
  • Blind Gloucester prays that "distribution should undo excess, / And each man have enough."
  • Jonathan Dollimore (Radical Tragedy, 1984) read the play as being about power, property and inheritance, not the human spirit ennobled by suffering.

?Lear and Edmund both pray to Nature. Do they mean the same thing?

No. Lear's Nature is an order of bonds that children and subjects must honour; Edmund's Nature is energy and appetite that custom tries to chain. The play, written as James I tried to unite Britain, shows what happens when both the bonds and the kingdom are torn apart.

Mission 6

King Lear · sight, blindness and madnessScreen 1

A play full of eyes

  • Goneril loves Lear "Dearer than eyesight". Lear tells Kent "Out of my sight!", and Kent replies "See better, Lear;".
  • Gloucester, about to read the forged letter: "if it be nothing, I shall not need spectacles."
  • Rejected by Goneril, Lear asks: "Where are his eyes?"
  • Sight stands for judgement. Both fathers look hard at their children and still misjudge them.

King Lear · sight, blindness and madnessScreen 4

The blinding

  • In 3.7 Regan and Cornwall bind Gloucester, their host, to a chair. Cornwall puts out one eye.
  • A servant draws his sword to stop him and wounds Cornwall; Regan stabs the servant dead. Cornwall puts out the other eye: "Out, vile jelly!"
  • Only then does Regan tell him that Edmund betrayed him. He understands at once: "O my follies! Then Edgar was abus’d."
  • The Quarto ends the scene with servants caring for him; the Folio cuts these lines.

?Gloucester only sees the truth once he's blind. How can that be?

Gloucester's eyes let him be fooled by appearances, a letter and a son's performance. Blinded, he learns the truth and judges by feeling: "I see it feelingly." Lear's madness works the same way, so the play keeps placing insight where we least expect it.

Mission 7

After all that suffering, is there any comfort in King Lear?

Make a guess: After all that suffering, is there any comfort in King Lear?

King Lear · critical readingsScreen 1

Four centuries of reactions

DateReaction
1681Nahum Tate's version: Lear restored, Cordelia married to Edgar. Bradley mocked its moral "that Truth and Virtue shall at last succeed".
1765Samuel Johnson sided with the public's preference for Tate; Cordelia's death had shocked him so much he could hardly reread the last scenes.
c.1811Charles Lamb: "The Lear of Shakespeare cannot be acted." A happy ending, he wrote, ignores "the living martyrdom that Lear had gone through".

King Lear · critical readingsScreen 2

Bradley: redemption (1904)

  • A. C. Bradley suggested calling the play "The Redemption of King Lear": suffering purifies Lear into love.
  • He saw two groups in conflict, as if Shakespeare were "regarding Love and Hate as the two ultimate forces of the universe".
  • Yet he admitted that Cordelia's death "does not seem at all inevitable".
  • Later critics attacked his habit of treating characters as real people.
King Lear · critical readingsScreen 3

Kott: the absurd (1964)

  • In King Lear, or Endgame (in Shakespeare Our Contemporary, English translation 1964), Jan Kott compared the play to Beckett's absurdist theatre.
  • For Kott there is no just order. Gloucester's "fall" at Dover is a grotesque clown show, a suicide without meaning.
  • He saw the Fool as the one who knows that the world is not rational.
  • Peter Brook's 1971 film of the play drew on Kott's ideas.
King Lear · critical readingsScreen 4

Feminist and psychoanalytic readings

  • Kathleen McLuskie (1985) argued that the play's emotional power depends on accepting a father's authority, so women who resist it look monstrous.
  • Coppélia Kahn (The Absent Mother in King Lear, 1986) noted that Lear's queen is missing: he demands mothering from his daughters.
  • Kahn links this to "O, how this mother swells up toward my heart!": Lear's breakdown is a "female" hysteria.
King Lear · critical readingsScreen 5

Materialist and historicist readings

  • Jonathan Dollimore (Radical Tragedy, 1984) read the play as being about power, property and inheritance; the idea that suffering ennobles hides its politics.
  • Stephen Greenblatt (Shakespeare and the Exorcists, 1988) traced Poor Tom's devils to Harsnett's 1603 attack on fake exorcism: the play stages ritual as theatre, and no miracle saves Cordelia.

Mission checkScreen 9

?After all that suffering, is there any comfort in King Lear?

Critics have never agreed. Bradley found redemption and "an unbearable joy"; Kott found an absurd universe; feminist and materialist critics ask whose comfort the play offers. Audiences once found the ending so unbearable that Tate's happy version ruled the stage until 1838. The strongest answers weigh the reconciliation of 4.7 and 5.3 against "Never, never, never, never, never!".

Recap

Big ideas

  • King Lear (c.1605–06) moves from order broken in Act 1, through the storm's disorder, to recognitions in Act 4 and a catastrophe that Shakespeare's sources did not have.
  • The Gloucester subplot (from Sidney's Arcadia) mirrors the Lear plot: misjudged children, suffering, care from the wronged child, death between grief and joy. Bradley saw this doubling as making the suffering universal.
  • The 1608 Quarto and 1623 Folio differ (the mock trial; who speaks last); most editions merge them.
  • Lear wants "The name, and all the addition to a king" without the power: his pride and rashness (AQA's "folly") start the tragedy.
  • His suffering comes in stages, each stripping away part of his kingship, from "Who is it that can tell me who I am?" to "I am a very foolish fond old man,".
  • Bradley read this as redemption ending in "an unbearable joy"; the ending's despair lets you argue against him (AO5).
  • The Gloucester family mirrors Lear's: a father misjudges his sons and suffers for it.
  • Edmund attacks "the plague of custom" (primogeniture and the label "base"), but chooses each betrayal; Shakespeare makes him a charming, rational performer.
  • Gloucester learns the truth only when blinded; Edgar moves from victim to disguised guide to avenger.
  • Cordelia's plain "Nothing, my lord." and "According to my bond" contrast with her sisters' flattery.
  • Goneril and Regan have grievances in Act 1, but escalate to cruelty; Hazlitt saw them as hateful, McLuskie reads the play as making female independence monstrous.
  • The Fool tells truths no courtier dares ("an O without a figure") and vanishes after 3.6.
  • "Nature" means a sacred order of bonds to Lear, Gloucester, Albany and Cordelia; to Edmund it means appetite free of custom.
  • Contexts that sharpen the play: James I's union of the crowns (1603), his sons' titles of Cornwall and Albany, the court performance of December 1606, the Gunpowder Plot and the eclipses of 1605.
  • Mad Lear and blind Gloucester see power as a cover for injustice; Dollimore reads the play as about power, property and inheritance.
  • For AO3, use information from outside the play to explain a line, not as a history lesson.
  • Sight in King Lear stands for judgement: the fathers look but misjudge ("See better, Lear;").
  • Gloucester's paradox "I stumbled when I saw." links the blinding (3.7) to insight; Lear's madness brings "Reason in madness!".
  • Three madnesses: Lear's real breakdown, Edgar's performance as Poor Tom (with devils from Harsnett, 1603), the Fool's licensed folly.
  • For AO5, the Dover scene supports both Edgar's "cure" and Kott's grotesque reading.
  • Reception: Tate's happy ending (1681–1838), Johnson's shock, Lamb's "cannot be acted"; the tragic ending returned in 1823 and 1838.
  • Bradley (1904) read redemption and joy; Kott (1964) the absurd; McLuskie and Kahn the place of women and the absent mother; Dollimore power and property; Greenblatt ritual emptied into theatre.
  • Use critics to test evidence, not as decoration: each view fits some lines and not others.

  • Robes and furr’d gowns hide all. (4.6, Lear) Power hides injustice.
  • So distribution should undo excess, / And each man have enough. (4.1, Gloucester) Suffering leads to a vision of fairness.
  • Is there any cause in nature that makes these hard hearts? (3.6, Lear) The play asks where cruelty comes from.
  • See better, Lear; (1.1, Kent) The first call to true sight.
  • I stumbled when I saw. (4.1, Gloucester) Paradox: sight misled, blindness teaches.
  • ’Tis the time’s plague when madmen lead the blind. (4.1, Gloucester) Edgar as Poor Tom leads his father: an image of the whole play.
  • A man may see how the world goes with no eyes. (4.6, Lear) Madness and blindness see truly.
  • I see it feelingly. (4.6, Gloucester) Insight through suffering.
  • Reason in madness! (4.6, Edgar) Lear's madness speaks truth.
  • As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods, / They kill us for their sport. (4.1, Gloucester) The bleakest view of the gods.
  • Pray you now, forget and forgive: (4.7, Lear) Reconciliation with Cordelia.
  • Is this the promis’d end? (5.3, Kent) The ending as apocalypse.
  • Never, never, never, never, never! (5.3, Lear) Total loss; the line most against redemption.
  • Look there, look there! (5.3, Lear (Folio)) Joy or delusion: Bradley's key evidence.
  • The oldest hath borne most; we that are young / Shall never see so much, nor live so long. (5.3, Edgar (Folio; Albany in the Quarto)) The survivors' sober verdict.
