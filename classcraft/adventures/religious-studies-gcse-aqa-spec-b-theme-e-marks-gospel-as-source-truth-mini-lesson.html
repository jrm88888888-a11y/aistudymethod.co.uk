Mission 1

Mission 1: Why did Jesus describe God's Kingdom as a seed, not a palace?

Make a guess: Why did Jesus describe God's Kingdom as a seed, not a palace?

Mission 1

The Kingdom and the parables

  • Jesus' first words in Mark: "The time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News." (Mark 1:15)
  • Kingdom of God: God's reign or rule, not a place on a map; wherever God's will is done.
  • Jesus teaches about it in parables: short stories from everyday life with a deeper meaning.
  • Farming images: his hearers knew sowing, soil, weeds and harvest.
  • Parables invite hearers to think and decide: "Whoever has ears to hear, let him hear." (Mark 4:9)
Mission 1

The parable of the sower

  • A farmer scatters seed widely by hand (4:3).
  • Seed on the path: birds eat it (4:4).
  • On rocky ground: springs up fast, but has no root; the sun scorches it (4:5–6).
  • Among thorns: choked, no fruit (4:7).
  • On good soil: a huge harvest, "thirty times, … sixty times, … one hundred times" (4:8).
  • Much seed seems wasted; yet the good soil gives a rich harvest.
Mission 1

Four kinds of hearers

  • "The farmer sows the word." (Mark 4:14) The seed is God's message.
  • Path: they hear, but Satan takes the word away at once (4:15).
  • Rocky ground: receive it with joy, but fall away under "oppression or persecution" (4:16–17).
  • Thorns: "the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches" choke it (4:19).
  • Good soil: hear, accept and bear fruit (4:20).

Mission 1

The growing seed

  • Found only in Mark (4:26–29).
  • A man scatters seed, then sleeps and rises; the seed grows "though he doesn't know how" (4:27).
  • "The earth bears fruit by itself: first the blade, then the ear, then the full grain in the ear." (Mark 4:28)
  • When the grain is ripe, the harvest comes (4:29): a common biblical image of God's final judgement.
  • Meaning: the Kingdom grows by God's power, quietly and in stages; humans sow but cannot force it.
  • Significance: patience and trust for Christians who see little result.
Mission 1Read the source

The mustard seed

"It's like a grain of mustard seed, which, when it is sown in the earth, though it is less than all the seeds that are on the earth, yet when it is sown, grows up and becomes greater than all the herbs, and puts out great branches, so that the birds of the sky can lodge under its shadow." (Mark 4:31–32)

  • Birds nesting in a great tree was a prophets' image of nations gathering under God's rule (Ezekiel 17:23).

Q1 What contrast does the parable draw?

Show answer

A tiny beginning, a huge result.

Q2 What might the birds stand for?

Show answer

Mission 1

Four ways to understand the Kingdom

  • Present reality: the Kingdom is "at hand" (1:15); seen now in healings, forgiveness and changed lives.
  • Future hope: the full Kingdom comes at the end, at the harvest (4:29), when the Son of Man comes (13:26).
  • Inner state: God reigns in a person's heart: the good soil that hears and accepts (4:20).
  • Community: the Kingdom as a people gathered under God, like birds in the branches (4:32); many see this in the Church.
  • Most Christians hold several together: "already, but not yet".
Mission 1Diversity

Christians and the Kingdom today

  • Catholic teaching: the Church is the seed and beginning of the Kingdom on earth; it grows towards completion at the end of time.
  • Many evangelicals: the Kingdom begins in personal conversion, the inner response to the word; it shows in a changed life.
  • Social action and liberation theology: the Kingdom is built where the hungry are fed and the oppressed set free.
  • Future-focused Christians (e.g. some Adventists and Pentecostals) stress Christ's Second Coming to establish the Kingdom fully.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists may value the call to build a better society, without belief in God's reign.
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

"The Kingdom of God is more about the future than the present."

  • Reasons for: the harvest is still to come (4:29); the mustard seed has not yet grown; Jesus speaks of the Son of Man "coming in clouds" (13:26); suffering and injustice show God's reign is not yet complete.
  • Reasons against: "the time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand" (1:15); healings and exorcisms show it already here (3:27); the good soil bears fruit now (4:20); the scribe is "not far from God's Kingdom" (12:34).
  • Another view: "already, but not yet": present in seed, future in harvest.
  • Judge it by: What do the seed parables suggest about time? What does "at hand" mean? Does stressing the future make Christians less active now, or more hopeful?

Mission 2

Mission 2: A good man asked Jesus how to reach eternal life, then walked away sad. What was the price?

Make a guess: A good man asked Jesus how to reach eternal life, then walked away sad. What was the price?

Mission 2

Who gets in?

  • On the road to Jerusalem (Mark 10) and in the Temple (Mark 12), Jesus teaches what it takes to enter God's Kingdom.
  • Many assumed the Kingdom belonged to the important: the respected, the rich, the religious experts.
  • Jesus turns this upside down: "many who are first will be last, and the last first" (10:31).
  • Entry is God's gift, received with trust; it also asks a response: following Jesus and loving God and neighbour.
  • Three scenes: children, a rich man, and a scribe who asks about the greatest commandment.
Mission 2

Jesus and the children

  • People bring little children for Jesus to touch; the disciples rebuke them (10:13).
  • Children then had little status: not yet able to keep the Law or contribute.
  • Jesus is indignant: "Allow the little children to come to me! Don't forbid them, for God's Kingdom belongs to such as these." (Mark 10:14)
  • "Whoever will not receive God's Kingdom like a little child, he will in no way enter into it." (Mark 10:15)
  • He takes them in his arms and blesses them (10:16).
  • Like a child: trusting, dependent, receiving a gift without claiming to have earned it.

Mission 2

The rich man

  • A man runs, kneels: "Good Teacher, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?" (Mark 10:17)
  • Jesus lists commandments about others: do not murder, commit adultery, steal, lie, defraud; honour your parents (10:19).
  • "Teacher, I have observed all these things from my youth." (Mark 10:20)
  • "Jesus looking at him loved him" (10:21).
  • "One thing you lack. Go, sell whatever you have and give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven; and come, follow me" (10:21).
  • "His face fell at that saying, and he went away sorrowful, for he was one who had great possessions." (Mark 10:22)
Mission 2Read the source

The camel and the needle

"It is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye than for a rich man to enter into God's Kingdom." (Mark 10:25)

"Jesus, looking at them, said, 'With men it is impossible, but not with God, for all things are possible with God.'" (Mark 10:27)

  • The largest animal they knew, the smallest opening: a deliberate exaggeration.

Show answer

Q2 Is anyone saved by their own effort, according to 10:27?

Show answer

No: salvation is possible only by God's power.

Mission 2Diversity

Must Christians sell everything?

  • Literal, for some: monks, nuns and friars take a vow of poverty. St Antony of Egypt and St Francis of Assisi gave away everything after hearing Jesus' words to the rich man (Matthew 19:21).
  • Catholic teaching: Jesus' call to the rich man is a call to all to put God first; for some it becomes a vocation to religious poverty.
  • Personal command: many Christians say this was what this man needed: his wealth held him back; each person must let go of whatever comes first.
  • Stewardship: wealth is held on trust from God, to be used generously for others (tithing, charity, fair business).
  • Prosperity teaching: some preachers say God rewards faith with wealth; most churches reject this as contrary to this passage.
  • The "needle gate" in Jerusalem, sometimes used to soften the saying, is a later legend.
Mission 2

The greatest commandment

  • A scribe asks: "Which commandment is the greatest of all?" (12:28)
  • Jesus quotes the Shema, said daily by devout Jews (Deuteronomy 6:4–5):
  • "Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one. You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength." (Mark 12:29–30)
  • And adds Leviticus 19:18: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:31)
  • The scribe agrees: love matters "more … than all whole burnt offerings and sacrifices" (12:33).
  • Jesus: "You are not far from God's Kingdom." (Mark 12:34) A friendly scribe: not every religious leader opposed Jesus.
Quick check
?Where do the two commandments Jesus names as the greatest come from?
Mission 2

Love in practice

  • Agape: self-giving love that seeks the good of others, not a feeling only.
  • Who is my neighbour? In Luke's Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37), anyone in need, even an enemy.
  • Love of God and love of neighbour cannot be separated (1 John 4:20).
  • Catholic: love of neighbour is the basis of Catholic Social Teaching: dignity, the common good, care for the poor.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists hold the Golden Rule (treat others as you would want to be treated) without belief in God.
Mission 2Evaluate

"It is impossible to be rich and a true follower of Jesus."

  • Reasons for: "Go, sell whatever you have" (Mark 10:21); the camel and the needle (10:25); "You can't serve both God and Mammon" (Matthew 6:24); wealth breeds trust in riches (10:24).
  • Reasons against: "all things are possible with God" (10:27); Jesus' command was to one man whose wealth held him back; Joseph of Arimathea was "a rich man" and "also Jesus' disciple" (Matthew 27:57); wealth can do great good if shared.
  • Another view: the issue is not owning wealth but trusting in it (10:24).
  • Judge it by: Is the command for everyone or for this man? How rich is "rich"? What do Christians do with wealth, not just how much they have?
Mission check

?A successful businesswoman keeps every rule she knows, gives a little to charity, and asks a Christian mentor what more she needs to do. Which reply is closest to Jesus' words to the rich man?

To enter the Kingdom, Mark says, receive it like a child, let nothing come before following Jesus, and love God and neighbour.

Mission 3: Why did Jesus keep choosing the company that respectable people avoided?

Make a guess: Why did Jesus keep choosing the company that respectable people avoided?

Mission 3

Who was pushed aside, and why?

  • People with skin disease ("leprosy"): under the Law they were unclean and lived apart: "He shall dwell alone. His dwelling shall be outside of the camp." (Leviticus 13:46)
  • Tax collectors: collected money for Herod or Rome, often took extra; seen as collaborators and dishonest.
  • "Sinners": people seen as not keeping God's Law.
  • Gentiles (non-Jews): outside God's covenant; many Jews avoided eating with them to keep purity laws.
  • Reasons were not only prejudice: purity laws protected holiness, worship and the community's identity under foreign rule.
  • Illness was often linked with sin, so the sick could be blamed as well as excluded.
Mission 3

The man with leprosy

  • He kneels and begs: "If you want to, you can make me clean." (Mark 1:40)
  • Jesus, "moved with compassion", touches him: "I want to. Be made clean." (Mark 1:41)
  • Touching made a person unclean; here the touch makes the leper clean.
  • Jesus sends him to the priest with the offering "which Moses commanded" (1:44): he respects the Law and restores the man to the community.
  • He is told to say nothing, but spreads the news; now Jesus must stay outside the towns (1:45). He takes the outsider's place.

Mission 3

The call of Levi

  • Levi, son of Alphaeus, sits at his tax office by the lake (2:14).
  • Jesus says, "Follow me." "And he arose and followed him." (Mark 2:14)
  • Then Jesus eats in Levi's house with "many tax collectors and sinners" (2:15).
  • Eating together meant acceptance and friendship.
  • Scribes and Pharisees object: "Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?" (Mark 2:16)

Mission 3

The Syro-Phoenician woman

  • Near Tyre, in Gentile territory. A woman, "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race", begs Jesus to free her daughter (7:24–26).
  • Jesus: "Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs." (Mark 7:27)
  • Children = Israel; dogs = Gentiles, a common insult.
  • She answers: "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs." (Mark 7:28)
  • "For this saying, go your way." Her daughter is freed, healed at a distance (7:29–30).
  • Readings: Jesus tests her faith · his mission was to Israel first, then to all · some scholars think the woman changes Jesus' mind.
  • An outsider three times over: a Gentile, a woman, the mother of a possessed child.
Quick check
?Jesus first speaks of "the children's bread" and "the dogs". Which reading of the Syro-Phoenician woman's story do many Christians give?
Mission 3

Why Jesus acted differently

  • Compassion: he is "moved with compassion" (1:41) before he heals.
  • The Kingdom: God's reign is open to all who respond, Jew and Gentile, clean and unclean (1:15).
  • Mission: like a doctor, he goes to those who need him (2:17).
  • Holiness that spreads: instead of being made unclean by contact, his touch makes others clean.
  • Faith over status: the people praised are often outsiders (the woman, 7:29).
  • Jesus does not abolish the Law: he sends the leper to the priest. He challenges how it is used to exclude.
Mission 3Diversity

Significance for Christians today

  • Inclusion: churches welcome people others reject: refugees, prisoners, people with HIV, the homeless.
  • Care for the sick: The Leprosy Mission (founded 1874) still treats leprosy; St Damien of Molokai lived and died among leprosy patients in Hawaii.
  • Catholic teaching: every person has God-given dignity; the Church's preferential option for the poor puts the most excluded first.
  • Different emphases: some Christians stress welcome first; others stress the call to repentance that comes with it (1:15; 2:17).
  • Non-religious view: Humanists share the value of equal dignity for all, based on shared humanity, not on God.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Christians should welcome everyone without conditions, as Jesus did."

  • Reasons for: Jesus touched the leper (1:41), ate with sinners (2:15–16), healed a Gentile's daughter (7:29); he praised a Roman's faith (Matt 8:10); "you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40).
  • Reasons against: Jesus calls people to "repent" (1:15; 2:17): welcome leads to change; he sends the leper to the priest, keeping the Law; welcoming a person is not approving everything they do.
  • Another view: "without conditions" may mean no one is excluded from the invitation, while following still asks a response.
  • Judge it by: What is the difference between welcome and approval? Does Jesus' practice set limits? How do churches balance welcome with their teaching?

Jesus crossed the lines of purity, politics and race to include people; Christians see his welcome as a model, with the invitation to change that comes with it.

Mission 4

Women around Jesus

  • In the first century most women had little public role; men led worship and public life, and a woman's testimony counted for less in court.
  • Mark's Gospel gives women unusual importance: many act with faith or insight where men fail.
  • Simon's mother-in-law, healed, "served them" (1:31): the word Jesus uses of himself, "to serve" (10:45).
  • The woman with a haemorrhage, called "Daughter" (5:34); the Greek woman (7:26).
  • At the cross: women "who, when he was in Galilee, followed him and served him" (Mark 15:41). Followed and served: the marks of a disciple.
Mission 4

The Greek woman

  • A Gentile woman begs Jesus to free her daughter (7:25–26).
  • Jesus' saying about the children's bread and the dogs (7:27) seems to refuse her.
  • She answers back: "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs." (Mark 7:28)
  • Many commentators note she is the only person in Mark who wins an argument with Jesus.
  • "For this saying, go your way." (7:29) Her daughter is freed.
  • Discipleship: humble yet bold trust; she calls Jesus "Lord" and does not give up.
Mission 4

The widow at the treasury

  • In the Temple courts, Jesus warns against scribes who "devour widows' houses" yet make long prayers (12:38–40).
  • He sits opposite the treasury. Many rich people give large sums (12:41).
  • A poor widow puts in two small coins, together a quadrans, a tiny amount (12:42).
  • Widows often had no income and no male protector: among the poorest people.
  • Jesus calls his disciples to notice her (12:43).
Mission 4Read the source

"All that she had"

"Most certainly I tell you, this poor widow gave more than all those who are giving into the treasury, for they all gave out of their abundance, but she, out of her poverty, gave all that she had to live on." (Mark 12:43–44)

Q1 How can two tiny coins be "more"?

Show answer

They were all she had; the rich gave what they could spare.

Q2 What does Jesus measure a gift by?

Show answer

Its cost to the giver, not its size.

Mission 4

The anointing at Bethany

  • Two days before Passover, the chief priests and scribes look for a way to kill Jesus (14:1–2).
  • At Bethany, in the house of Simon the leper, a woman breaks a jar of costly nard and pours it on Jesus' head (14:3).
  • Some complain: it could have been sold "for more than three hundred denarii" (about a year's wages) and given to the poor (14:4–5).
  • Jesus: "She has done a good work for me." (Mark 14:6) "She has anointed my body beforehand for the burying." (Mark 14:8)
  • Anointing the head was how kings were made: Messiah means "anointed one". She anoints the Messiah who will die.
  • "Wherever this Good News may be preached … that which this woman has done will also be spoken of for a memorial of her." (14:9)
  • Then Judas goes to the chief priests for money (14:10–11): her costly gift, his betrayal for money.

Mission 4Diversity

Women in the Church today

  • All Christians honour the women of the Gospels as models of faith and service.
  • Catholic: priests are men only, following Jesus' choice of twelve men; women serve as religious sisters, teachers, lay ministers and leaders of organisations. Mary Magdalene's day was raised to a feast in 2016, honouring her as "apostle to the apostles".
  • Orthodox: priesthood also male only; women honoured as saints and "myrrh-bearers".
  • Church of England, Methodist and many other Protestants ordain women as ministers, priests and bishops, citing the women at the tomb and Galatians 3:28.
  • Some evangelical churches teach different but equal roles: men lead, women serve in other ways.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists support full equality for women and see restrictions as unjust.
Mission 4Diversity

Giving like the widow today

  • Sacrificial giving: many Christians give a fixed share of income; some give a tithe (one tenth).
  • Cost, not size: the poorest gifts count fully with God; churches value time and care as well as money.
  • Simple living: some, like religious orders, give up possessions to serve.
  • Justice: the widow's story also warns religious leaders not to exploit the poor (12:40).
  • Non-religious view: Humanists also value generosity, but would not urge the poor to give all they have.

Mission 4Evaluate

"In Mark's Gospel, the women are better disciples than the Twelve."

  • Reasons for: the Greek woman understands where the disciples are slow; the woman at Bethany accepts Jesus' death, which Peter rejected (8:32); women stay at the cross when the men flee (14:50; 15:40–41); women are first at the empty tomb.
  • Reasons against: the women at the tomb also flee and say nothing (16:8); the Twelve are chosen, sent out and later lead the Church; Mark shows weakness in all disciples, to encourage readers who fail.
  • Another view: Mark contrasts insiders and outsiders, not men and women: anyone can be a true disciple.
  • Judge it by: What makes a "good" disciple in Mark? Does 16:8 change the picture? Is it fair to compare a few named stories with the Twelve's whole journey?
Mission check

?Two pupils donate to a hospice appeal. One, from a wealthy family, gives £50 from a large allowance. The other gives £5, all she had saved for a school trip. Applying Mark 12:41–44, how would many Christians compare the gifts?

Jesus measures a gift by its cost to the giver; many Christians see in the widow, and in other women in Mark, the self-giving that disciples are meant to have.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Jesus' own disciples failed to free a suffering boy. What went wrong?

Make a guess: Jesus' own disciples failed to free a suffering boy. What went wrong?

Mission 5

Exorcism in Mark

  • Exorcism: driving out an evil (Mark says "unclean") spirit or demon from a person.
  • In the first century, many Jews and Gentiles believed spirits could seize people and cause illness, fits, silence or violence.
  • Mark presents Jesus' exorcisms as a battle: God's Kingdom against Satan.
  • Jesus: no one can plunder a strong man's house "unless he first binds the strong man" (Mark 3:27).
  • Jesus commands with a word. Other exorcists of the time used spells, names or objects.
  • The spirits recognise who Jesus is, even when people do not.
Mission 5

The boy: a scene of failure

  • Jesus comes down from the Transfiguration with Peter, James and John (9:2–13).
  • Below, a crowd, arguing scribes, and the other disciples, who have failed.
  • The father: his son has a spirit that makes him mute, throws him down, makes him foam at the mouth (9:17–18).
  • "I asked your disciples to cast it out, and they weren't able." (Mark 9:18)
  • Jesus: "Unbelieving generation, how long shall I be with you?" (Mark 9:19)
  • Ill "from childhood"; thrown into fire and water. The father: "If you can do anything, have compassion on us and help us." (9:21–22)
Mission 5Read the source

"Help my unbelief!"

"Jesus said to him, 'If you can believe, all things are possible to him who believes.' Immediately the father of the child cried out with tears, 'I believe. Help my unbelief!'" (Mark 9:23–24)

  • Jesus turns the father's "if you can" back on him: the question is not Jesus' power but the father's trust.

Q1 What two things does the father say at once?

Show answer

He believes, and he admits his belief is weak.

Q2 Does Jesus refuse to help because the faith is weak?

Show answer

No: he heals the boy, and takes him by the hand, 9:25–27.

Mission 5

"Why couldn't we?"

  • The boy seems dead; "Jesus took him by the hand and raised him up" (9:26–27).
  • Privately the disciples ask: "Why couldn't we cast it out?" (Mark 9:28)
  • Jesus: "This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer and fasting." (Mark 9:29)
  • Problems of discipleship shown here:
  • Weak faith: the "unbelieving generation" includes the disciples (9:19).
  • Relying on themselves: earlier they had been given authority and had cast out demons (6:7, 13); perhaps they treated it as their own power, not God's.
  • Lack of prayer: Jesus has just been on the mountain, where God's voice spoke (9:7); below, they have been arguing (9:14).
  • Pride: straight after, they argue about who is the greatest (9:33–34).

Mission 5

A boy on the edge

  • AQA lists this story under those disregarded by society.
  • Many today think the boy's symptoms (falling, foaming, rigid body) resemble epilepsy.
  • In the first century such fits were often blamed on spirits or sin; sufferers could be feared and kept apart.
  • He and his father are dependent and desperate: the crowd gathers to watch (9:25).
  • Jesus asks about the boy's history, shows compassion, and touches him: "took him by the hand" (9:27).
  • Significance: Christians are called to treat people with illness, including mental illness, as people to be cared for, not feared.
Mission 5Diversity

Exorcism today

  • Spiritual evil is real: Catholic, Orthodox and many evangelical Christians believe evil spirits exist.
  • Catholic teaching: a priest may perform a formal exorcism only with the bishop's permission, and only after checking that the person is not suffering from an illness, which is a matter for medicine (Catechism of the Catholic Church 1673, paraphrase).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic churches practise deliverance ministry: prayer to free people from evil.
  • Medical reading: many Christians think Mark describes conditions we now treat as epilepsy or mental illness; Jesus' care still matters.
  • Symbolic reading: the stories show Jesus freeing people from whatever enslaves them; some read "Legion" as a picture of Roman occupation.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists see possession as a pre-scientific explanation; they warn that exorcism can harm vulnerable people who need medical help.
Quick check
?Which statement best matches the Catholic Church's approach to exorcism today?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Mark's exorcism stories have little meaning for Christians today."

  • Reasons for: modern medicine explains fits and mental illness; belief in possession can lead to harm; many Christians now read these stories as about illness, not demons.
  • Reasons against: many Christians still believe in spiritual evil and pray for deliverance; the stories show Jesus' authority over all evil; the boy's story teaches prayer and honest faith ("Help my unbelief!", 9:24); they model compassion for people others fear.
  • Humanist view: the stories have historical interest and show compassion, but no supernatural truth.
  • Judge it by: Does the meaning depend on believing in demons? Who decides what a story means today? Does a medical explanation remove a spiritual one?
Mission 5Worked answer

Problems of discipleship

Question: Explain two problems of discipleship shown in the healing of the boy (Mark 9:14–29).

  • Problem 1: weak faith. Source: Jesus calls them an "unbelieving generation" (9:19). Development: Christians today may also doubt, and need to say, like the father, "Help my unbelief!"
  • Problem 2: relying on their own strength. Source: "This kind can come out by nothing but by prayer" (9:29). Development: Christians believe service depends on God, so prayer comes before action.

In Mark, even trusted disciples fail when they rely on themselves; Christians take the boy's story as a call to honest faith and prayer.

Mission 6

Mark's two endings

  • The oldest complete manuscripts of Mark end at 16:8: the women flee the empty tomb "for they were afraid".
  • Most Bibles also print a longer ending (16:9–20), probably added in the 2nd century, with appearances, a commission and the ascension.
  • Catholic teaching (Council of Trent, 1546) accepts 16:9–20 as part of inspired Scripture.
  • Many Protestant Bibles print it with a note; Christians still read and preach from it.
  • Either way, both endings point forward: the story continues with the disciples.
Mission 6Read the source

Commission and ascension

Jesus appears to the Eleven at table and rebukes their "unbelief and hardness of heart" (16:14).

"Go into all the world and preach the Good News to the whole creation. He who believes and is baptised will be saved; but he who disbelieves will be condemned." (Mark 16:15–16)

  • Signs will follow believers: driving out demons, new languages, healing the sick (16:17–18).
  • He "was received up into heaven and sat down at the right hand of God." (Mark 16:19)
  • "They went out and preached everywhere, the Lord working with them" (16:20).

Mission 6

Faith in Mark

  • Jesus' first message: "Repent, and believe in the Good News." (Mark 1:15)
  • Faith brings healing: "your faith has made you well" (5:34; 10:52); the friends' faith for the paralysed man (2:5).
  • Unbelief limits it: at Nazareth "he could do no mighty work there" (6:5–6).
  • Faith and fear: "Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?" (4:40); "Don't be afraid, only believe." (5:36)
  • Faith with doubt: "I believe. Help my unbelief!" (Mark 9:24)
  • Faith in prayer: "Have faith in God" (11:22).
  • Faith in Mark = trust in Jesus and God's power, shown in action.
Mission 6Diversity

Views on faith

  • Catholic: faith is God's gift and a free human response; it is lived out in the sacraments and in good works (faith "working through love").
  • Many Protestants: saved by grace through faith alone (Luther, 1483–1546): faith as personal trust in Christ.
  • Pentecostal Christians stress faith for healing today, as in Mark's miracles.
  • Some liberal Christians: faith is trust and commitment more than accepting miracle stories literally.
  • Mark's realism: even the disciples lack faith (4:40); the father's "help my unbelief" gives hope to doubters.
  • Humanists and many atheists: beliefs should follow evidence; they question faith as a reason for belief.

Mission 6

Mark's demands, then and now

  • Deny yourself, take up your cross (8:34): readiness to suffer for Jesus.
  • Leave everything (1:18; 10:28) and go light (6:8).
  • Serve, not be served (10:43–45).
  • Have faith in God (11:22).
  • For the first disciples: leaving work and family, travelling, persecution, often martyrdom.
  • For 21st-century Christians: priorities and money, speaking up for justice, serving others, standing firm under pressure; for some, prison or death.
Mission 6

Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945)

  • German Lutheran pastor and theologian.
  • Opposed the Nazi takeover of the churches; a leader in the Confessing Church; ran an illegal seminary for its pastors.
  • The Cost of Discipleship (1937): attacks "cheap grace" (forgiveness without changed life) and calls for "costly grace" (following Christ whatever the price).
  • Returned to Germany from the USA in 1939 to share his people's suffering.
  • Joined the resistance against Hitler; arrested 1943; executed at Flossenbürg, 9 April 1945.
  • Mark 8:34: he took up his cross, losing his life for following his faith.
Mission 6

Oscar Romero (1917–1980)

  • Catholic Archbishop of San Salvador, El Salvador, from 1977.
  • At first cautious; changed after his friend Fr Rutilio Grande, a Jesuit priest, was murdered (1977).
  • Spoke on the radio against killings and repression of the poor by the army and death squads.
  • In his Sunday homily the day before he died, he called on soldiers to stop the killing.
  • Shot dead while celebrating Mass, 24 March 1980.
  • Canonised (declared a saint) by Pope Francis, 2018.
  • Mark's demands: a leader who served the poor (10:43–45) and took up his cross (8:34).
Mission 6

Mother Teresa (1910–1997)

  • Albanian Catholic nun who worked in Calcutta (Kolkata), India.
  • Founded the Missionaries of Charity (1950): care for the dying, the sick, orphans and people with leprosy.
  • Sisters take vows of poverty, chastity, obedience, and wholehearted free service to the poorest of the poor.
  • Nobel Peace Prize 1979; canonised 2016 as St Teresa of Calcutta.
  • Mark's demands: gave up comfort and possessions (10:21, 28); served the least (10:45).
  • Criticisms: some critics questioned standards of medical care in her homes and her stance on some issues; supporters reply that her aim was loving presence to the dying.
Mission 6Diversity

Many ways to be a disciple

  • Heroic witness: a few Christians are called to risk their lives, like Bonhoeffer (killed by the Nazis, 1945) and Romero (shot in El Salvador, 1980).
  • Everyday discipleship: most follow Jesus through family, work, honesty, prayer and service in their own community.
  • Vocations: priests, ministers, monks and nuns, missionaries.
  • Evangelical Christians stress telling others about Jesus; Catholic social teaching and many other Christians stress action for justice; most hold both.
  • Persecuted Christians today: in some countries, being baptised can cost family, work or freedom.
  • Non-religious view: Humanists can admire these lives of courage and service, but say they do not need religious faith.

Mission 6Evaluate

"To be a true disciple today, a Christian must be ready to die for their faith."

  • Reasons for: Jesus says "take up his cross" (Mark 8:34; Matthew 16:24); Bonhoeffer and Romero did die; some Christians face that choice today.
  • Reasons against: most disciples serve in ordinary ways; Jesus calls people to serve (Mark 10:45) and to make disciples (Matthew 28:19), not to seek death; Mother Teresa's witness was a long life of service.
  • Another view: "ready" is the key word: every disciple must put Christ first, even if few are ever asked to die.
  • Judge it by: Is readiness the same as action? Where in the world, and in what kind of society, is the Christian living?

Discipleship today takes many forms, but the core demand is the same: put Christ first, whatever it costs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Kingdom of God is God's reign, "at hand" in Jesus; parables reveal it to those with ears to hear.
  • The sower shows four responses to the word.
  • The growing seed shows growth by God's power, quietly and in stages; the mustard seed, growth from tiny to great.
  • Christians understand the Kingdom as present and future, inner and communal: "already, but not yet".

Mission 2

  • The Kingdom belongs to those who receive it like children: with trust, as a gift.
  • The rich man kept the commandments but could not let go of his wealth; "all things are possible with God".
  • The greatest commandments are to love God with everything and your neighbour as yourself.

Mission 3

  • First-century purity laws, politics and fear of sin pushed aside lepers, tax collectors, sinners and Gentiles.
  • Jesus touched, ate with and healed them, out of compassion and because the Kingdom is for all who respond.

Mission 4

  • Women in Mark show faith (the Greek woman), insight (the anointing) and faithfulness (the cross and tomb) where the Twelve often fail.
  • The widow gave "all that she had to live on": Jesus values a gift by its cost; some also read a warning against exploiting the poor.
  • Christians differ on women's roles in the Church today; all honour these women as models of discipleship.

Mission 5

  • Mark's exorcisms show Jesus' authority over evil and the coming of God's Kingdom; the spirits know who he is.
  • The disciples' failure with the boy shows problems of discipleship: weak faith, self-reliance, lack of prayer, pride.
  • Christians differ on possession today (spiritual, medical, symbolic); Humanists see a pre-scientific explanation.

Mission 6

  • Mark 16:14–20: the risen Jesus sends the Eleven to preach to all creation and is taken up to God's right hand.
  • Faith in Mark is trust, often found in outsiders and mixed with doubt.
  • Bonhoeffer, Romero and Mother Teresa show Mark's demands lived in the 20th century; most Christians live them in everyday ways.
