Mission 1

Mission 1: Hold a heavy bag still for ten minutes and your arms ache. How much work have you done on the bag?

Make a guess: Hold a heavy bag still for ten minutes and your arms ache. How much work have you done on the bag?

Mission 1

Work is energy transferred

  • A force does work when its point of application moves in the direction of the force.
  • Work done = force × distance moved in the direction of the force.
  • Work done = energy transferred. Both in joules.
  • 1 J = the work done when a force of 1 N moves its point of application 1 m in its own direction.
Mission 1

A force at an angle

  • Only the component along the displacement does work.
  • W = Fs cos θ, θ = angle between the force and the displacement.
  • θ = 0: W = Fs. θ = 90°: W = 0. The force changes only the direction of motion.
Quick check
?A child pulls a sledge 25 m along level snow using a rope held at 30° above the horizontal. The tension in the rope is a constant 80 N. How much work does the tension do on the sledge?
Mission 1

Positive, negative or zero

  • Work is a scalar. Its sign shows which way energy goes.
  • θ < 90°: positive work. The force transfers energy to the object.
  • θ > 90°: negative work. The force takes energy away: friction, braking.
  • θ = 90°: zero. A crate slides along a level floor: its weight and the normal force do no work.
  • No displacement: zero. Holding a load still does no work on it.
Quick check
?A worker uses a rope to lower a box straight down at constant speed. Which statement about the work done on the box during the descent is correct?
Mission 1

Worked example: pushing a crate

  • A 150 N push at 40° below the horizontal moves a crate 6.0 m along a level floor. Friction is 90 N.
  • Push: W = 150 × 6.0 × cos 40° = +690 J.
  • Friction (θ = 180°): W = 90 × 6.0 × cos 180° = −540 J.
  • Weight and normal force (θ = 90°): 0 J each.
  • Each force's work is found separately. Use the angle between that force and the displacement.
Mission check

?A car drives at constant speed around a flat circular bend of radius 50 m. The friction force from the road on the car is 4000 N and acts towards the centre of the bend. How much work does this force do on the car while it goes a quarter of the way around?

Work needs a displacement along the force. The held bag does not move, so you do no work on it. Your arms ache because your muscles use energy internally to keep exerting the force.

Make a guess: Two smooth slides drop the same height: one steep, one gentle. Which gives you more speed at the bottom?

Mission 2

Kinetic energy

  • Kinetic energy: the energy an object has because it is moving.
  • Eₖ = ½mv²
  • A scalar: never negative; no direction.
  • Double the mass → double the kinetic energy. Double the speed → four times the kinetic energy.
Mission 2

Gravitational potential energy

  • Gravitational potential energy: energy due to position in a gravitational field.
  • ΔEₚ = mgΔh
  • Valid near the surface, where g is roughly constant.
Mission 2

Where the equations come from

  • Momentum p = mv, so v = p/m. Then Eₖ = ½m(p/m)² = p²/2m.
Quick check
?Trolley P has a mass of 2.0 kg and trolley Q has a mass of 8.0 kg. The two trolleys have momenta of the same magnitude. How do their kinetic energies compare?
Mission 2

Elastic potential energy

  • A stretched or compressed spring stores elastic potential energy.
  • EH = ½k(Δx)², Δx = extension or compression.
  • It is the area under the force–extension line (a triangle), within the limit of proportionality.
Mission 2

Conservation of energy

  • Energy cannot be created or destroyed.
  • It changes form, or is transferred into or out of a system.
  • Mechanical energy = kinetic + gravitational potential + elastic potential.
  • No friction or drag: mechanical energy stays constant. Loss of potential energy = gain of kinetic energy.
  • With friction or drag: some becomes internal energy and sound. The total is still conserved.
Mission 2

Worked example: a falling ball

  • A ball is dropped from 1.8 m. Air resistance is negligible.
  • Loss of GPE = gain of KE: mgh = ½mv². m cancels: v = √(2gh).
  • v = √(2 × 9.8 × 1.8) = 5.9 m s⁻¹.
  • Only the vertical drop matters. A smooth slide of any shape, same drop: the same speed.
Quick check
?A child slides from rest down a straight slide with negligible friction and reaches a speed v at the bottom. A second straight slide has the same steepness, but its top is four times as high above its bottom. What speed does the child reach at the bottom of the second slide?
Mission 2

Worked example: a spring launcher

  • A spring (k = 400 N m⁻¹) is compressed 0.050 m, then fires a 0.020 kg ball along a smooth, level track.
  • Energy stored = ½ × 400 × 0.050² = 0.50 J.
  • All of it becomes kinetic energy: ½ × 0.020 × v² = 0.50 → v = 7.1 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up instead: elastic PE → KE → GPE. At the top, ½kΔx² = mgΔh.
Quick check
?A spring of spring constant 250 N m⁻¹ is compressed by 0.080 m and then released. It launches a block of mass 0.40 kg along a smooth horizontal track. All the elastic potential energy becomes the block's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the block?
Mission check

?Three identical stones are thrown from the top of a sea cliff with the same speed: one straight up, one horizontally and one straight down. Air resistance is negligible. Which stone hits the sea with the greatest speed?

Only the drop in height matters, not the path. Two smooth slides of the same height give the same speed at the bottom; the gentle one just takes longer.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Identical twins climb the same stairs, one running, one walking. Who does more work? Who is more powerful?

Make a guess: Identical twins climb the same stairs, one running, one walking. Who does more work? Who is more powerful?

Mission 3

Power

  • Power = rate of doing work = rate of energy transfer.
  • P = ΔW/Δt
  • Unit: the watt. 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹.
  • Same work in half the time: twice the power.
Mission 3

Worked example: climbing stairs

  • A 60 kg student climbs stairs 3.0 m high in 4.0 s.
  • Work done = mgΔh = 60 × 9.8 × 3.0 = 1.8 kJ (1764 J).
  • Power = 1764 ÷ 4.0 = 440 W.
  • Walk up in 8.0 s: the same work, half the power.
Mission 3

Power and speed: P = Fv

  • A force F moves its point of application Δs in time Δt: W = FΔs.
  • P = W/Δt = F × Δs/Δt = Fv.
  • Car at a steady 30 m s⁻¹: driving force = resistive forces = 600 N.
  • Power needed = 600 × 30 = 18 kW.
Quick check
?A cyclist rides along a level road at a constant speed of 8.0 m s⁻¹. The total resistive force on the cyclist and bicycle is 25 N. What power does the cyclist's driving force develop?
Mission 3

Efficiency

  • Efficiency = useful energy output ÷ total energy input.
  • Or: useful power output ÷ total power input.
  • Symbol η, as a fraction or a percentage.
  • Always less than 100%. The rest is wasted, mostly as thermal energy in the surroundings.
Mission 3

Worked example: a motor

  • An electric motor takes 200 W. It lifts a 20 kg load 3.0 m in 5.0 s.
  • Useful power = mgΔh/t = 20 × 9.8 × 3.0 ÷ 5.0 = 118 W.
  • Efficiency = 118 ÷ 200 = 0.59, or 59%.
  • Wasted power = 200 − 118 = 82 W, mostly heating the motor and its surroundings.
Quick check
?A Sankey diagram for a car engine has an input arrow representing 200 kJ of chemical energy from the fuel. It divides into four output arrows: 60 kJ of useful mechanical work, 100 kJ of thermal energy carried away by the exhaust gases, 30 kJ of thermal energy removed by the cooling system, and 10 kJ of sound. What is the efficiency of the engine?
Mission 3

Sankey diagrams

  • One arrow in: the total energy (or power) input.
  • Arrows out: useful output, and each wasted output.
  • Arrow width ∝ energy. Output widths add up to the input width.
  • Useful arrow goes straight on; wasted arrows bend away.
  • Efficiency = width of useful arrow ÷ width of input arrow.
Quick check
?Which statement is true of every correctly drawn Sankey diagram?
Mission check

?Motor A raises a 200 N load through 3.0 m in 6.0 s. Motor B raises a 100 N load through 6.0 m in 4.0 s. Both lift at constant speed. Which statement is correct?

Work depends on force and distance, not time. The runner and the walker do the same work against gravity; the runner does it faster, so develops more power.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Double a car's speed. How much longer is its braking distance?

Make a guess: Double a car's speed. How much longer is its braking distance?

Mission 4

Net work changes kinetic energy

  • The work done by the resultant force = the change in kinetic energy.
  • From F = ma and v² = u² + 2as: Fs = ½mv² − ½mu².
  • Work done by the resultant force on a system = change in its energy: here, its kinetic energy.
  • Net work positive: speeds up. Negative: slows down. Zero: constant speed.
Mission 4

Worked example: braking

  • A 1200 kg car at 20 m s⁻¹ brakes with a constant 8000 N resultant force.
  • Final Eₖ = 0, so F × s = ½mu².
  • s = ½ × 1200 × 20² ÷ 8000 = 30 m.
  • At 40 m s⁻¹: s = ½ × 1200 × 40² ÷ 8000 = 120 m.
  • Braking distance ∝ u²: double the speed, four times the distance.
Quick check
?A child on a toboggan slides across level snow. The child and toboggan have a total mass of 50 kg and slow down from 8.0 m s⁻¹ to 6.0 m s⁻¹. What is the magnitude of the net work done on the child–toboggan system during this time?
Mission 4

Friction and drag dissipate energy

  • Friction and drag do negative work on a moving object.
  • That mechanical energy becomes internal (thermal) energy and sound. The total is still conserved.
  • Change in mechanical energy = work done by non-conservative forces (friction, drag).
Mission 4

Worked example: a toboggan run

  • A 50 kg toboggan and rider start from rest. They descend 12 m vertically along a 40 m slope and reach 12 m s⁻¹.
  • Eₚ lost = 50 × 9.8 × 12 = 5880 J. Eₖ gained = ½ × 50 × 12² = 3600 J.
  • Work done by non-conservative forces ≈ −2.3 kJ. Average resistive force = 2280 ÷ 40 ≈ 57 N.
Quick check
?A skier of mass 60 kg starts from rest at the top of a slope and descends through a vertical height of 20 m. At the bottom her speed is 16 m s⁻¹. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the total work done on the skier by friction and air resistance during the descent?
Mission 4

Conservative and nonconservative forces

  • Conservative force (gravity, ideal spring): its work depends only on the start and end points, not the path.
  • Round trip back to the start: zero work. A potential energy can be defined.
  • Nonconservative force (friction, air resistance): its work depends on the path taken.
  • No potential energy exists for a nonconservative force.
Mission check

?A block on a level, frictionless surface is sped up by a constant horizontal force. The work needed to speed the block up from rest to speed v is W. How many times W is needed to speed the block up from v to 3v?

Work changes kinetic energy, and kinetic energy ∝ v². Braking from twice the speed removes four times the KE, so the same braking force needs four times the distance.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why must an electric car carry far more mass of battery than a fuel-burning car carries in fuel?

Make a guess: Why must an electric car carry far more mass of battery than a fuel-burning car carries in fuel?

Mission 5

Energy density

  • Energy density of a fuel: energy released per kilogram burned (or reacted). Unit J kg⁻¹, usually MJ kg⁻¹.
  • Energy from a mass m of fuel: E = m × energy density.
  • Sometimes quoted per unit volume (J m⁻³, or MJ per litre). Always check the unit given.
  • High energy density: less fuel to carry, store and transport for the same energy.
Mission 5

Fuels compared

  • Typical values, MJ kg⁻¹:
  • Wood ≈ 16 · coal ≈ 30 · petrol ≈ 46 · natural gas ≈ 55 · hydrogen ≈ 120.
  • Lithium-ion battery ≈ 0.7: about 1/60 of petrol's.
  • Uranium-235, fully fissioned: ≈ 8 × 10⁷: about 2 million times petrol.
  • Chemical fuels release energy from chemical bonds; fission from the nucleus, with tens of millions of times more energy per reaction.
Quick check
?Fully fissioned, uranium-235 has an energy density of 8.0 × 10⁷ MJ kg⁻¹. Coal has an energy density of 30 MJ kg⁻¹. What mass of coal releases the same energy as 1.0 kg of uranium-235?
Mission 5

Worked example: how much fuel?

  • A car engine gives 20 kW of useful power for 1.0 hour. Efficiency 0.25. Petrol: 46 MJ kg⁻¹.
  • Input power = useful ÷ efficiency = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 kW.
  • Energy in 1.0 h = 80 × 10³ × 3600 = 2.88 × 10⁸ J.
  • Mass = 2.88 × 10⁸ ÷ 46 × 10⁶ = 6.3 kg of petrol.
  • Battery and motor, efficiency ≈ 0.9: 20 kW × 1.0 h = 72 MJ useful needs 80 MJ stored → 80 ÷ 0.7 ≈ 110 kg of battery.
  • Divide by the efficiency: the input is always bigger than the useful output.
Quick check
?A coal-fired power station has an electrical power output of 600 MW and an overall efficiency of 0.40. The energy density of the coal it burns is 30 MJ kg⁻¹, that is, each kilogram burned releases 30 MJ. What mass of coal must the station burn each second?
Mission 5

Cells as energy sources

  • Chemical cell: a store of chemical energy, released as electrical energy while current flows.
  • Low energy density, but high efficiency (≈ 90%), no exhaust where it is used, quiet.
  • Rechargeable cells: refilled from the mains, so the energy's real source is whatever generated it.
  • Solar cell: no store. Electrical power out = light intensity (power per m²) × area × efficiency.
  • Example: 800 W m⁻² on a 1.6 m² panel, efficiency 0.20 → 800 × 1.6 × 0.20 ≈ 260 W.
Mission 5

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Renewable source: replaced naturally as fast as it is used (sunlight, wind). Non-renewable: a finite stock (coal, oil, gas, uranium).
  • Fossil fuels: high energy density, output on demand; finite; CO2 and other pollutants when burned.
  • Nuclear fuel: enormous energy density, no CO2 in operation; radioactive waste; high build cost.
  • Chemical cells: portable, steady output, work in the dark; low energy density; finite store; disposal and recycling.
  • Solar cells: no fuel, no emissions in use; output only in light, varies with weather and time of day; need storage for night.
Quick check
?A weather station on a remote mountain must run a 5 W sensor day and night, all year round. Nobody will visit it for several years. Which energy source fits best?
Mission check

?A delivery drone needs 1.8 MJ of useful energy per flight. Option 1: a battery pack storing 0.72 MJ kg⁻¹, delivered to the propellers with efficiency 0.90. Option 2: a petrol engine and generator (petrol 46 MJ kg⁻¹) with an overall efficiency of 0.20. About how many times greater is the mass of battery needed than the mass of petrol?

Mass of fuel = useful energy ÷ efficiency ÷ energy density. A battery's energy density is about 60 times lower than petrol's, so even at 0.9 efficiency a car needs well over ten times the mass.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • W = Fs cos θ: only the component along the displacement does work; work done = energy transferred.

Mission 2

  • Eₖ = ½mv²; ΔEₚ = mgΔh. Without resistive forces, PE lost = KE gained: only the height dropped matters.
  • Elastic PE EH = ½kΔx² joins the energy total.

Mission 3

  • P = W/t = Fv; efficiency = useful output ÷ total input (energy or power).
  • Sankey diagrams: output widths add up to the input width.

Mission 4

  • Work done by the resultant force = change in kinetic energy: Fs = ½mv² − ½mu².
  • Friction and drag turn mechanical energy into internal energy; total energy is conserved.

Mission 5

  • Energy density: energy per kilogram of fuel (check for per-volume values). Mass needed = useful energy ÷ efficiency ÷ energy density.
  • Chemical cells: portable, efficient, low energy density. Solar cells: renewable, no fuel, no output in the dark.
