Jane Eyre · structure and the bildungsromanScreen 3

A life told by the woman who lived it

  • A bildungsroman (German: "novel of formation") follows a young person growing up, through hardship, to maturity and a settled place in the world.
  • The subtitle is An Autobiography, and the 1847 title page named "Currer Bell" only as its editor, as if Jane were real.
  • The narrator is the adult Jane looking back: a retrospective first-person narrator. She is married, writing years later (chapter 38).
  • She controls the pace. In chapter 10 she skips eight years, because "this is not to be a regular autobiography".

Each home is a stage in Jane's growing up: injustice at Gateshead, discipline at Lowood, love and temptation at Thornfield, family and duty at Moor House, and a marriage she chooses at Ferndean. The adult Jane tells the story, lingering on the moments that changed her and skipping the years that didn't.

Mission 2

Poor, plain, little: why is Jane Eyre so hard to silence?

Make a guess: Poor, plain, little: why is Jane Eyre so hard to silence?

Jane Eyre · JaneScreen 1

Jane at ten

  • Brontë presents young Jane as a passionate child in a house that tells her "you are less than a servant" (ch 2).
  • John Reed calls her "rat"; the servants call her "a mad cat" when she fights back.
  • She tells John he is "like a slave-driver" and sees herself as a "rebel slave": she reads her treatment as injustice.
  • Locked in the red-room, her reason cries "Unjust!—unjust!"

Jane Eyre · JaneScreen 4

Passion and principle

  • At Lowood, Helen Burns and Miss Temple teach Jane control: by eighteen she seems "a disciplined and subdued character" (ch 10).
  • In chapter 16 she orders herself to draw "Portrait of a Governess, disconnected, poor, and plain."
  • Brontë makes her heroine plain: Gaskell's Life (1857) reports Brontë wanted a heroine as plain and small as herself.
  • Jane's passion stays, joined to principle.

?Poor, plain, little: why is Jane Eyre so hard to silence?

Brontë presents Jane as passionate from childhood, but her outbursts are about justice and self-worth, not bad temper. She learns control at Lowood, and by chapter 23 her passion is joined to principle: poor and plain, she still claims to be Rochester's equal in soul.

Mission 3

Rochester deceives Jane. Why does the novel reunite them?

Make a guess: Rochester deceives Jane. Why does the novel reunite them?

Jane Eyre · RochesterScreen 1

The master of Thornfield

  • First meeting (ch 12): Rochester's horse slips on ice and he has to lean on Jane: "necessity compels me to make you useful."
  • Brontë presents him as a Byronic hero, a type named after the brooding heroes of Lord Byron's poems: dark, proud, moody, with a guilty past.
  • He is about twice Jane's age, rich, and her employer: he pays her £30 a year.
  • He admits his faults: "I am a trite commonplace sinner" (ch 14).

Jane Eyre · RochesterScreen 4

Power and deception

  • Disguised as a gypsy fortune-teller (ch 18–19), he tries to draw out Jane's feelings.
  • He later admits, "I feigned courtship of Miss Ingram", to make Jane jealous (ch 24).
  • He proposes in chapter 23 while secretly married to Bertha Mason.
  • After the engagement he wants to dress her in silks and jewels; Jane insists on earning her keep: "I will not be your English Céline Varens."
Quick checkScreen 5

Jane Eyre · RochesterScreen 6

Fall and return

  • The wedding is stopped (ch 26). Rochester asks Jane to live with him abroad; she leaves (ch 27).
  • In ch 36 Jane learns that Bertha set Thornfield on fire. Rochester stayed until everyone else was out; when the house fell he lost a hand and his sight.
  • At Ferndean he admits "I did wrong" and says "I began to experience remorse, repentance" (ch 37).
  • The first meeting is reversed: now Jane leads him, and he can lean on her by choice.

?Rochester deceives Jane. Why does the novel reunite them?

Brontë does not excuse Rochester: his deceit is exposed, Jane leaves, and he suffers. The reunion comes only after he repents and Jane returns independent, so they meet as equals and the first scene on the icy lane is reversed.

Mission 4

A girl locked in the red-room, a wife locked upstairs. Why both?

Make a guess: A girl locked in the red-room, a wife locked upstairs. Why both?

Jane Eyre · the red-room, Bertha and the GothicScreen 1

Gothic in an English house

  • Gothic fiction (for example Ann Radcliffe's The Mysteries of Udolpho, 1794) uses old mansions, locked rooms, secrets, the supernatural and a heroine in danger.
  • Brontë uses these: the red-room (ch 2); a mirthless laugh in a passage "like a corridor in some Bluebeard’s castle" (ch 11); a fire (ch 15); a stabbing (ch 20); a torn veil (ch 25).
  • She often explains the supernatural: the red-room light was "in all likelihood, a gleam from a lantern".

Jane Eyre · the red-room, Bertha and the GothicScreen 4

Bertha: how the novel presents her

  • Bertha Antoinetta Mason is from Spanish Town, Jamaica. Rochester married her fifteen years before (ch 26), for a fortune of £30,000 arranged by his father (ch 27).
  • She is confined on Thornfield's third storey, guarded by Grace Poole.
  • We see her only through others, mainly Rochester, who calls her mad and blames her family and her "excesses" (ch 27). She never speaks in her own words.
  • Her mother is called "the Creole"; the novel leaves her background unclear, and critics debate it.

Jane Eyre · the red-room, Bertha and the GothicScreen 7

Two women, later readers

  • Jane and Bertha are linked: both locked up, both likened to wild animals ("mad cat", "hyena"), a mirror in each scene, one threatened with being tied (ch 2), one tied (ch 26).
  • Gilbert and Gubar (1979) read Bertha as Jane's dark double, acting out the anger Jane holds in.
  • Jean Rhys's Wide Sargasso Sea (1966) gives her a voice as Antoinette, renamed "Bertha" by her husband.
  • Post-colonial critics such as Gayatri Spivak (1985) argue the novel sacrifices the colonial woman for the English heroine.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A girl locked in the red-room, a wife locked upstairs. Why both?

Brontë makes the red-room and Bertha's room echo each other: locked doors, mirrors, a child threatened with being tied and a wife bound to a chair. Many readers see Bertha as Jane's dark double; post-colonial readers and Jean Rhys see a woman whose own story the novel silences. The strongest answers weigh both.

Mission 5

Does Jane end the novel free, or just married?

Make a guess: Does Jane end the novel free, or just married?

Jane Eyre · independence and genderScreen 1

A woman's position in the 1840s

  • An educated woman without money had few respectable jobs: governess, teacher or companion.
  • Under coverture, a wife's legal identity merged with her husband's: her property and earnings were his until the Married Women's Property Acts of 1870 and 1882.
  • Before 1857 divorce needed a private Act of Parliament; insanity became a ground only in 1937. Rochester says "I could not rid myself of it by any legal proceedings" (ch 27).

Jane Eyre · independence and genderScreen 4

Money, work and choice

  • Ch 10: Jane advertises for a post herself and leaves Lowood to earn £30 a year.
  • Ch 24: engaged, she hates being "dressed like a doll" and hopes for "ever so small an independency".
  • Ch 27: she will not stay as Rochester's mistress: "I care for myself."
  • Ch 31: at Morton she is "a village-schoolmistress, free and honest".
  • Ch 33: she inherits £20,000 from her uncle and shares it equally with her three cousins.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Does Jane end the novel free, or just married?

Brontë makes Jane earn her freedom first: she refuses to be kept, leaves when her principles demand it, works, and inherits money she shares. She returns to Rochester as "an independent woman". Some readers see the marriage as the reward of that freedom; others argue it still ends her story as a wife.

Mission 6

Brocklehurst, Helen, St John: whose faith does Jane choose?

Make a guess: Brocklehurst, Helen, St John: whose faith does Jane choose?

Jane Eyre · religionScreen 1

Faith and false religion

  • Brontë was the daughter of an Anglican clergyman, Patrick Brontë, perpetual curate of Haworth.
  • In the preface to the second edition (1847) she wrote: "Conventionality is not morality. Self-righteousness is not religion."
  • In 1848 Elizabeth Rigby, reviewing anonymously, called the novel "anti-Christian". The novel attacks hypocrisy, not faith.
Jane Eyre · religionScreen 2

Brocklehurst: religion as power

  • An Evangelical clergyman and Lowood's treasurer, he threatens ten-year-old Jane with hell (ch 4).
  • He orders girls' hair cut off, even hair that curls naturally, "to mortify in these girls the lusts of the flesh" (ch 7).
  • Then his wife and daughters arrive in "velvet, silk, and furs", hair "elaborately curled".
  • He is based on William Carus Wilson, who ran the Clergy Daughters' School at Cowan Bridge. Brontë's sisters Maria and Elizabeth fell ill there and died in 1825.

Jane Eyre · religionScreen 5

Helen and St John

  • Helen dies of consumption trusting God: "God is my father; God is my friend" (ch 9). Her gravestone says Resurgam, Latin for I shall rise again.
  • St John Rivers, a clergyman bound for India as a missionary, asks Jane to marry him for the work, not for love (ch 34).
  • Jane refuses but keeps her faith, praying "in my way" (ch 35).
  • The novel ends with St John's words (ch 38).

?Brocklehurst, Helen, St John: whose faith does Jane choose?

Brontë contrasts three kinds of religion: Brocklehurst's hypocrisy, Helen's patient endurance and St John's cold, ambitious duty. Jane learns from Helen and respects St John, but her faith keeps room for feeling and love: she prays "in my way".

Mission 7

"Reader, I married him." Why does Jane keep talking to you?

Make a guess: "Reader, I married him." Why does Jane keep talking to you?

Jane Eyre · context and Jane's voiceScreen 1

Who is telling the story

  • Jane narrates in the first person, looking back: "I have now been married ten years" (ch 38).
  • We hear two Janes: the child who feels, and the adult who judges. In the red-room chapter she says, "now, at the distance of—I will not say how many years, I see it clearly" (ch 2).
  • In shock she sees herself from outside, in the third person: "Jane Eyre, who had been an ardent, expectant woman" (ch 26).
Jane Eyre · context and Jane's voiceScreen 2

1847 and "Currer Bell"

  • Jane Eyre was published in October 1847 by Smith, Elder as an autobiography "edited by" Currer Bell: Charlotte Brontë.
  • The sisters chose names that could be men's because they feared critics were prejudiced against women writers.
  • In 1848 Elizabeth Rigby attacked the novel and said that if a woman wrote it, she had cut herself off from her own sex.
Jane Eyre · context and Jane's voiceScreen 3

The governess's place

  • A governess was a gentlewoman paid to teach a family's children: neither family nor servant. The 1851 census counted about 21,000 governesses in Britain.
  • Pay was low, with no security; many were looked down on. Brontë herself was a governess in 1839 and 1841.
  • At Thornfield Jane earns £30 a year. In chapter 17 she sits in the drawing-room while Rochester's guests discuss governesses.

Jane Eyre · context and Jane's voiceScreen 6

"Reader"

  • Jane speaks to the reader directly over thirty times: direct address.
  • It makes us her confidant, trusted with private feelings, and her judge: "Who blames me? Many, no doubt" (ch 12); "the reader shall judge" (ch 35).
  • It fits the novel's form as an autobiography, a life told to someone.
  • Her most famous sentence makes Jane the one who acts: "Reader, I married him." (ch 38).

Mission checkScreen 9

?"Reader, I married him." Why does Jane keep talking to you?

Jane tells her own story, years later, and keeps turning to "Reader" to confide in us and to ask us to judge her choices. A poor governess, dismissed by people like the Ingrams, takes charge of how her life is told, right up to "Reader, I married him."

Recap

Big ideas

  • The novel moves through five settings: Gateshead, Lowood, Thornfield, Moor House and Morton, Ferndean. Each tests Jane in a new way.
  • Jane Eyre is a bildungsroman told by a retrospective first-person narrator, the adult Jane, who controls the pace of her story.
  • You choose an extract-based or a discursive question; either way, know the plot place by place.
  • Young Jane is passionate and fights injustice: "I am not deceitful", she tells Mrs. Reed, and turns the charge back on her.
  • Lowood teaches her control; at Thornfield she disciplines her own feelings, but her passion returns as principled speech.
  • In chapter 23 the poor, plain governess claims equality of soul with her master.
  • Rochester is a Byronic hero: proud, moody, older and richer than Jane, with a guilty secret.
  • He manipulates and deceives Jane (the gypsy, Blanche Ingram, the bigamous proposal); the split chestnut tree foreshadows the broken wedding.
  • After the fire he is humbled and repentant; Jane chooses to return, and they meet as equals.
  • Brontë uses Gothic features (locked rooms, a mysterious laugh, fire, a torn veil) but often explains the supernatural away.
  • Bertha is presented through others' eyes, with animal imagery, and never speaks for herself.
  • The red-room and Bertha's room mirror each other; critics read Bertha as Jane's double, or as a colonial woman the novel silences.
  • In the 1840s a wife's property and earnings belonged to her husband, and divorce was almost impossible; middle-class women had few jobs.
  • Jane claims that "women feel just as men feel" and repeatedly chooses work and self-respect over dependence.
  • She marries only when she is independent, though readers debate how far marriage limits her freedom.
  • Brontë attacks false religion, not faith: "Self-righteousness is not religion."
  • Brocklehurst preaches self-denial while his family wears furs; Helen forgives and endures; St John offers duty without love.
  • Jane finds her own faith, guided by conscience and feeling.
  • Jane is a retrospective first-person narrator: the adult Jane, married ten years, judges the child and young woman she was.
  • Jane Eyre (1847) was published as an autobiography edited by "Currer Bell"; governesses were neither family nor servants, and often scorned.
  • Direct address makes the reader Jane's confidant and judge.
