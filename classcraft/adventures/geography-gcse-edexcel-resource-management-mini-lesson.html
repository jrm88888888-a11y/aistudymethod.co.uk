Mission 1

Classifying natural resources

  • Biotic: from living things, e.g. fish, timber, crops.
  • Abiotic: from non-living things, e.g. water, oil, minerals, soil.
  • Renewable: replaced naturally, e.g. sunlight, wind, water; fish and forests if not overused.
  • Non-renewable: finite on human timescales, e.g. coal, oil, gas, uranium.
Quick check
?How is a stock of cod in the North Sea best classified?
Mission 1

Exploiting environments

  • Fossil fuel extraction: mines, wells and pipelines for energy.
  • Fishing: only 62.3% of marine fish stocks were fished within biologically sustainable levels in 2021.
  • Farming: agricultural land covers 36.9% of the world's land area (2023); farming takes 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
  • Deforestation: clearing forest for farmland and timber, e.g. 9% of South America's forest loss became soy fields by 2016.
Mission 1

How environments change

  • Reduced biodiversity: habitats cleared; fish stocks overfished.
  • Soil erosion: without trees and roots, rain and wind wash and blow soil away.
  • Reduced water quality: fertiliser runoff, mine waste and oil spills pollute rivers and seas.
  • Reduced air quality: burning coal and forest fires release smoke and gases.
Quick check
?A forest on a steep slope is cleared for crops. Which environmental change is most likely first?
Mission 1

One system: water, food and energy

  • Farming uses water: 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
  • Food uses energy: the food chain uses about 30% of the world's energy.
  • Water uses energy: to "extract, pump, lift, collect, transport and treat water".
  • So a shock to one resource can spread to the others.
Mission 1

The 2026 Hormuz shock

  • In 2025, about 20 million barrels of oil a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • In March–May 2026, flows averaged about 2.7 million a day.
  • The UN: this hit "key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels", raising costs across food systems.
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
Mission check

?A long drought lowers rivers, so hydroelectric stations make less electricity and its price rises. Many farmers there use electric pumps to irrigate. What is the most likely effect on food?

Food, water and energy are linked. In 2026, an energy shock at Hormuz made fertiliser, gas and fuel dearer, so farmers and import-dependent countries far away felt it.

Mission 2

Fossil fuels: where they are produced

  • Natural resources are unevenly spread, because geology and climate differ.
  • Oil, 2024: United States 18.9% of world production, Russia 11.6%, Saudi Arabia 11.2%.
  • Gas, 2024: United States 25.0%, Russia 15.3%.
  • Coal, 2024: China 51.8%.
Mission 2

Forests, farmland, minerals and water

  • Forestry: five countries (Russia, Brazil, Canada, the United States and China) hold about 54% of the world's forest (2025).
  • Soil and agriculture: about 37% of the world's land is farmland (2023). It is concentrated where soils are fertile and there is enough warmth and water, such as temperate grasslands and large river plains; deserts, ice caps and high mountains have little.
  • Minerals: the DR Congo mined about 76% of the world's cobalt in 2024, a key metal in lithium-ion batteries.
  • Water: Northern Africa withdraws about 101% of its available fresh water each year; sub-Saharan Africa about 10% (2023).
Mission 2

The UK's resources

  • Farmland: 16.8 million hectares, 69% of the UK's area (2025).
  • Forestry: woodland covers 14% of UK land: 20% in Scotland, 10% in England (2026).
  • Fossil fuels: UK oil production in 2024 was about 78% below its 1999 peak; coal output is now close to zero.
  • Water: the Environment Agency classes an area as in serious water stress where "the current household demand for water is a high proportion of the current effective rainfall which is available to meet that demand", or is likely to be in future. In short: homes need a large share of the rain that can be supplied.
  • Much of south-east and eastern England (for example the Thames Water, Southern and Essex and Suffolk areas) is classed this way; the north-west and north-east are not.
Quick check
?Region P gets less rain than Region Q. Yet only Region Q is classed as in serious water stress. Which explanation fits the Environment Agency's test?
Mission 2

Who consumes most food and energy?

  • Food supply per person per day, 2023: United States 3,947 kcal; United Kingdom 3,435; world 3,006; India 2,668; Haiti 1,959.
  • About 645 million people were chronically undernourished in 2025.
  • Energy per person, 2024: Qatar 214,520 kWh; United States 76,800; United Kingdom 28,016; world 21,654; India 7,813.
  • Pattern: richer countries consume far more energy and food per person.
Quick check
?In 2024, energy use per person was 76,800 kWh in the United States, 21,654 kWh for the world and 7,813 kWh in India. How many times as much energy did the average person in the United States use as the average person in India? Give 1 decimal place.
Mission 2

The water footprint

  • The water footprint measures the fresh water used, or polluted, to make what people consume:
    • blue: water taken from rivers, lakes and groundwater
    • green: rainwater used by crops and pasture
    • grey: water needed to dilute pollution.
  • Per person, 1996–2005: world 1,385 m³ a year; United States 2,842; India 1,089; China 1,071.
Mission 2

Water hidden in food

  • Embedded (virtual) water: the water used to make a product.
  • World averages, litres per kg: beef 15,400; sheep meat 10,400; pig meat 6,000; chicken 4,300; pulses 4,000.
  • Agriculture makes up 92% of the world's water footprint.
Quick check
?World averages: beef 15,400, sheep meat 10,400 and chicken 4,300 litres of water per kg. How many times as much water does 1 kg of beef take as 1 kg of chicken? Give 1 decimal place.
Mission 2

Where beef's water comes from

  • Beef's footprint: 93.5% green, 3.6% blue, 2.9% grey.
  • For farm animals, 98% of the water footprint is in their feed.
  • So most of it is rain on pasture and feed crops, not water pumped from rivers.
  • But place matters: blue water from a stressed aquifer does more harm than rain on wet pasture.
Mission 2

Water use over 100 years

  • Global freshwater use has risen about six-fold since 1900.
  • It rose sharply from the 1950s; since 2000 it appears to be levelling off, or at least slowing.
  • Farming takes 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
Mission check

?A 250 g cotton T-shirt has a water footprint of: blue 1,230 litres, green 1,110 litres, grey 380 litres (global average). Compared with beef, how much of the T-shirt's water comes from rivers and aquifers?

Beef is not mainly draining rivers: 93.5% of its 15,400 litres per kg is green water (rain on pasture and feed), and only 3.6% is blue. Irrigated crops such as cotton draw far more on rivers and aquifers.

Mission 3

Two kinds of energy

  • Renewable: will not run out on a human timescale, because nature keeps renewing it.
  • Non-renewable: a fixed stock in the ground that is not replaced on a human timescale, so it runs out as we use it.
  • Renewable: solar, wind, hydro (HEP), tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass.
  • Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium).
Mission 3

What powers the world?

  • In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied 86% of the world's total energy.
  • Solar grew by 30% that year, and renewables were the largest source of growth.
  • So renewables are growing fast, but from a small base.
Quick check
?Which energy source is non-renewable?
Mission 3

Non-renewables: for and against

  • Coal, oil, gas: reliable on demand; supply chains exist. But burning them releases CO₂, and the stock runs out.
  • Gas is flexible: power stations can ramp up quickly to meet peaks, and it emits the least carbon of the fossil fuels.
  • Nuclear (uranium): very low carbon, steady output. But it makes radioactive waste and stations are expensive and slow to build.
Mission 3

Renewables: for and against

  • No fuel to buy and very low emissions once built.
  • Wind and solar are variable: no output on calm days or at night.
  • HEP: steady and controllable, but dams flood valleys and block fish.
  • Geothermal: steady, but only where hot rocks are near the surface (e.g. Iceland).
  • Biomass: can be stored and burnt on demand, but needs land and must be regrown to be renewable.
  • Tidal: predictable, but costly and can change estuary habitats. Wave: varies with the weather and is costly.
Quick check
?Which is the main disadvantage of solar power?
Mission 3

One non-renewable: natural gas

  • Production: drilled from rocks under the North Sea, or imported by pipeline and ship.
  • For: in 2025 gas made 31% of UK electricity; it can be turned up fast when demand peaks.
  • For: the lowest-carbon fossil fuel.
  • Against: still a fossil fuel; methane that leaks is a strong greenhouse gas.
  • Against: UK output has fallen. In 2000 the UK produced 112% of the gas it used; in 2024 only about 50%. The rest is imported, which affects energy security.
Mission 3

One renewable: wind

  • Growth: wind made 0.2% of UK electricity in 2000 and 29% in 2025.
  • It helped cut coal from about 40% of UK electricity in 2012 to 0% in 2024.
  • Benefits: no fuel cost, no CO₂ while generating, jobs in building and maintaining turbines.
  • Disadvantages: output varies with the wind; turbines change views and can kill birds.
  • Attitudes: in 2026, 79% of UK adults supported renewable energy, but only 36% would be happy with an onshore wind farm locally (14% unhappy).
Mission 3

Wind farms: a sustainable solution?

  • Environmental: cuts carbon; but turbines affect birds and landscapes.
  • Economic: no fuel to buy; but backup is needed on calm days, and that costs money.
  • Social: local jobs; but some residents object to noise and views.
  • Judgement: wind is sustainable as part of a mix, with backup or storage for calm spells.
Quick check
?Most UK adults support renewables, yet only 36% would be happy with a local onshore wind farm. What does this show?
Mission 3

Ways to increase supply

  • Find and drill new reserves: new oil and gas fields, deeper or offshore.
  • New technology: fracking (hydraulic fracturing) pumps water, sand and chemicals into shale to crack it and release gas or oil.
  • Build new power stations: nuclear, HEP dams, wind and solar farms.
  • Import energy by pipeline, ship or electricity cable.
  • Fracking in the USA: in 2023 the USA produced more crude oil than any country ever, as companies used fracking and horizontal drilling. US gas output rose about 2.1 times from 2005 to 2024.
Quick check
?How did fracking help the USA reduce an energy shortage?
Mission 3

Why not all wind?

  • Wind is free, but it varies: on calm days turbines make little.
  • Demand does not stop, so the grid needs sources it can turn up on demand (gas, HEP, biomass), storage, or imports.
  • The UK government calls gas "flexible and reliable", easy to increase for short peaks.
  • So countries build an energy mix: wind is a big part, not the whole.
Mission check

?A small island has plenty of sunshine and plans to get all its electricity from solar panels. What is the biggest problem it must solve?

Free energy is not the same as reliable energy. Wind and solar vary, so a grid also needs storage, imports or sources it can turn up on demand: that is why countries use a mix.

Mission 4

What is an energy mix?

  • Energy mix: the share of a country's energy that comes from each source.
  • Primary energy: all the energy used, for transport, heating, industry and electricity.
  • Electricity is only part of it. Cars and gas boilers use fuel directly.
  • Fossil fuels: coal, oil, gas. Renewables: wind, solar, hydro, biofuels. Nuclear is low-carbon but not renewable.
Mission 4

The UK's mix today

UK primary energy, 2024:

  • Oil 38.8% · gas 32.3% · wind 10.7% · nuclear 5.2% · coal 2.5% · solar 1.9% · biofuels 1.4% · hydro 0.7%
  • Fossil fuels together: 73.5%. All renewables: 21.3%.
  • So the UK still relies on fossil fuels, mainly oil for transport and gas for heating and power.
Mission 4

  • Gas replaced most of coal's share by 2000. Renewables grew mostly after 2010 (3.0% in 2010).
Mission 4

Electricity: the fastest change

UK electricity generation:

  • Coal: 64.6% in 1990 → 0.7% in 2024. (Government figures give a higher 1990 share, about 72%: datasets count generation differently.)
  • Renewables: 1.8% in 1990 → 50.6% in 2024. Wind alone: 29.3%.
  • Gas: 30.4% in 2024; nuclear 14.3%.
  • Britain's last coal power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in autumn 2024 (generation ended in September; the government reported coal power ending "in October").
Quick check

Mission 4

Why energy mixes differ around the world

  • Resources: Norway gets 64% of its energy from hydro (mountains, heavy rain); Qatar gets 71% from gas it extracts; India gets 57% from coal.
  • Wealth: energy used per person, 2024: Qatar 214,520 kWh · UK 28,016 · India 7,813 · Rwanda (2023) 530.
  • In Rwanda only 9% of people mainly cook with clean fuels (2024), against 100% in the UK. The rest rely mainly on polluting fuels and stoves.
  • Population: India uses about 5.9 times the UK's total energy, though each person uses about a quarter as much (our calculation).
Quick check
?A country is very wealthy, has few people, and has steep mountains with heavy rainfall. Which energy mix is it most likely to have?
Mission check

?A newspaper says: "Britain now runs mainly on renewable energy." In 2024, renewables made 50.6% of UK electricity, but only 21.3% of all primary energy; oil was 38.8% and gas 32.3%. Which judgement is best?

Coal's place was taken first by North Sea gas, then by wind and other renewables in electricity. But oil and gas still supply about 71% of all UK energy, and more of it is now imported.

Mission 5

A century of energy

  • World energy use: about 18,000 TWh (1920) → about 172,000 TWh (2023): about 9.6 times as much.
  • World population: 1.90 billion → 8.09 billion: about 4.3 times.
  • In 1920, coal gave about 55% and traditional biomass (wood, dung) about 39% of energy.
  • In 2023, oil, gas and coal together gave about 82%.
Mission 5

Why use keeps rising

  • Rising population: more people need heat, light, food and transport.
  • Economic development (wealth): industry, cars, flights and appliances. Energy use per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Technology: new machines and devices use energy; new technology also unlocks new supplies, such as US oil from fracking.
  • Over 100 years, all three have pushed demand up, and supply has grown to meet it.
Quick check
?From 1920 to 2023, world energy use grew about 9.6 times and population about 4.3 times. How did energy use per person change?
Mission 5

Developing coal, oil, gas and uranium

  • Coal: still the largest source of electricity, but its share fell from 38.0% (2000) to 33.1% (2025).
  • Oil: fracking helped make the USA the top oil producer, with 22% of world output in 2023.
  • Gas: its share of world electricity rose from 18.0% (2000) to 21.7% (2025).
  • Uranium (nuclear): output rose from about 2,540 to 2,812 TWh (2000–2025), but its share fell from 16.6% to 8.9%.
  • + Reliable supply, jobs, export income. − Carbon dioxide and air pollution, oil spills, mining scars, radioactive waste.
Mission 5

Developing HEP, wind and solar

  • Shares of world electricity, 2000 → 2025: hydro 17.2% → 14.0%; wind 0.2% → 8.6%; solar 0.0% → 8.8%.
  • Solar output: 2,143 TWh (2024) → 2,779 TWh (2025).
  • + Low-carbon, no fuel to import, falling costs.
  • − HEP dams flood valleys and can force people to move; wind and solar vary with the weather and use land; wind farms change views.
Quick check
?Which is a negative impact of developing a large HEP dam?
Mission 5

Adding, not replacing

  • 2025: total energy supply passed 600 exajoules, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".
  • But fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and still supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • So new solar adds to fossil fuels; it does not yet replace them.
Mission check

?A country's solar output doubles in five years. Over the same years its oil imports rise. Which explanation fits best?

World energy use keeps rising with population, wealth and technology. In 2025 solar grew about 30%, but total supply grew too, and fossil fuels still gave 86%: renewables are adding to, not yet replacing, fossil fuels.

Mission 6

Who has a say?

  • A stakeholder is anyone affected by, or able to affect, a decision.
  • Individuals: residents, energy users, workers.
  • Organisations: energy companies, industry bodies, campaign groups, advisory bodies.
  • Governments: national and local.
  • Views differ on exploitation (getting energy out) and consumption (using it).
Mission 6

Why manage energy sustainably?

  • Non-renewables run out, and in 2025 fossil fuels still gave 86% of the world's energy.
  • Burning them releases CO₂, which drives climate change.
  • Renewables need managing too: wind and solar farms use land and sea and can harm wildlife or landscapes.
  • Sustainable management meets today's needs without leaving future generations worse off.
Quick check
?Why do renewable resources still need sustainable management?
Mission 6

Individuals: support in general

  • UK, summer 2026: 79% supported renewable energy; 4% opposed.
  • But only 36% would be happy with an onshore wind farm built locally; 14% would be unhappy.
  • For a local solar farm: 42% happy, 14% unhappy.
  • People in rural areas were twice as likely to be unhappy about a local solar farm: 25% vs 12% in urban areas.
Mission 6

Individuals as energy users

  • In 2026, 79% of UK adults were concerned about climate change.
  • 80% said they give at least a fair amount of thought to saving energy at home.
  • A person's carbon footprint is the greenhouse gas their choices cause: heating, travel, food, goods.
  • Attitudes vary: support for renewables was 97% among those very concerned about climate change, but 44% among those not concerned.
Quick check
?In 2026, 79% of UK adults supported renewables, but only 36% would be happy with a local wind farm. What does the gap show?
Mission 6

Organisations

  • Offshore Energies UK (oil and gas industry body): "We will need oil and gas for decades to come"; it argues the UK should produce it rather than import it.
  • Greenpeace UK (campaign group) calls for "a just transition away from fossil fuels".
  • RWE (energy company) is building the Awel y Môr offshore wind farm off North Wales.
  • Climate Change Committee (advisory body): natural gas will still have "a key role to play" on the way to 2050.
Mission 6

Governments

  • UK law: net emissions in 2050 must be at least 100% lower than in 1990 (net zero).
  • North Sea: a bill introduced in November 2026 would end new licences to explore new oil and gas fields, but says oil and gas "will remain part of Britain's energy mix for decades to come".
  • Fracking: halted in England in 2019 after earthquakes near Preston New Road.
  • India: aims for about 50% of power capacity from non-fossil sources by 2030, while coal still gave 71% of its electricity in 2025.
Quick check
?Which view matches the oil and gas industry body Offshore Energies UK?
Mission 6

Why do views differ?

  • Interests: profits and jobs (companies, workers), bills (consumers), landscape (residents), climate (campaigners).
  • Scale: benefits like lower national emissions are shared widely; costs like a changed view fall on one place.
  • Time: some focus on secure supply now; others on climate in 2050.
  • Responsibility: governments must balance security, cost and emissions together.
Mission 6

Winning local support

  • Asked what would make them more likely to back local wind or solar farms, 84% chose at least one benefit.
  • Energy bill discounts for people living nearby: 62%; local jobs: 58%; community funding: 57%.
  • So managing energy sustainably means sharing benefits with the people who host the costs.
Mission check

?A national survey shows strong support for solar power, yet a village objects to a large solar farm on nearby fields. Which explanation fits best?

Support in principle (79% for renewables) is much higher than support next door (36% for a local wind farm, 42% for a solar farm). Benefits are shared nationally while the costs land locally; bill discounts and local jobs are the benefits most likely to change minds.

Mission 7

Two countries

  • UK: Human Development Index (HDI) 0.946, in the UN's "very high" group.
  • India: HDI 0.685, in the "medium" group.
  • Edexcel calls a very high-HDI country developed, and a high- or medium-HDI country emerging: so the UK is developed and India emerging.
Mission 7

India's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: coal 57%, oil 27%, gas 6%, hydro 3%, solar 3%, wind 2%, nuclear 1%.
  • So about 90% came from fossil fuels.
  • These figures leave out traditional fuels such as fuelwood and dung, which many rural homes still burn.
  • Electricity, 2025: coal 71%, solar 9%, hydro 9%, wind 5%, nuclear 3%, gas 2%.
Mission 7

The UK's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: oil 39%, gas 32%, wind 11%, nuclear 5%, coal 2%; other renewables make up the rest.
  • About 74% came from fossil fuels: oil mainly for transport, gas for heating and power.
  • Electricity, 2025: gas 31%, wind 29%, bioenergy 14%, nuclear 12%, solar 7%, coal 0.1%.
Quick check
?What is the biggest difference between the two countries' electricity?
Mission 7

Impacts in India

  • Emissions: India's CO₂ rose from 584 million tonnes (1990) to 3,176 million tonnes (2024), about 5.4 times.
  • But per person it is 2.2 tonnes, under half the UK's 4.6.
  • Imports: India produces only about 13% as much oil as it uses, so most must be imported.
  • Health: smoke from cooking on open fires and inefficient stoves causes household air pollution. The share of Indians cooking with clean fuels rose from 49% (2015) to 81% (2024).
Mission 7

Impacts in the UK

  • Emissions: UK CO₂ fell from 602 million tonnes (1990) to 318 million tonnes (2024), down 47%.
  • Coal ended: from about 40% of electricity in 2012 to 0% after Ratcliffe-on-Soar closed in 2024.
  • Imports: the UK produced about 50% of the gas it used in 2024, down from 112% in 2000.
Quick check
?India's total CO₂ is about ten times the UK's, but its CO₂ per person is lower. Why?
Mission 7

India: managing energy

  • Targets: by 2030, about 50% of power capacity from non-fossil sources, and a 45% cut in emissions per unit of GDP from 2005 levels.
  • Solar: from 0.5% of electricity (2015) to 9.4% (2025).
  • Clean cooking: the Ujjwala scheme has given poor households over 105 million LPG gas connections, with a free first refill and stove.
  • Industry: the PAT scheme sets energy-saving targets for big factories.
Mission 7

UK: managing energy

  • Law: the Climate Change Act requires UK emissions in 2050 to be at least 100% lower than in 1990: net zero.
  • Coal: coal power ended in 2024, and new coal mines are to be banned.
  • Offshore wind: a January 2026 auction secured a record 8.4 GW.
  • Homes: the £1 billion Great British Insulation Scheme upgraded 85,900 homes by October 2025.
Quick check
?Which strategy cuts demand for energy rather than adding new supply?
Mission 7

Judging sustainability

  • UK: emissions down 47%, coal gone, wind rising, net zero in law. But fossil fuels still give about 74% of energy, and half of gas is imported.
  • India: renewables and clean cooking rising fast, from a low base. But coal still gives 71% of electricity and emissions keep growing.
  • Different starting points: India's energy use per person is still rising; the UK's is falling.
  • Exam move: judge each country on what it has done, not only on its targets.
Mission check

?Country Z gets most of its electricity from coal, its demand is rising fast, and it is adding solar faster than any other source. Which judgement fits best?

Like India, Country Z is moving towards sustainability while fossil fuels still dominate. The UK has gone further (coal gone, emissions down 47%) but still relies on oil and gas. Neither is finished: sustainability is judged by direction and by the mix today.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Resources are biotic or abiotic, renewable or non-renewable; exploiting them can cut biodiversity and harm soil, water and air.
  • A shock to one resource spreads through the others.

Mission 2

  • Resources are unevenly spread: oil and gas (USA, Russia, Saudi Arabia), coal (China), cobalt (DR Congo); UK: farmland 69%, woodland 14%, North Sea oil falling, water stress in the south-east.
  • Richer countries consume far more food and energy per person.
  • Water footprint: blue (rivers, aquifers), green (rain), grey (dilution). Beef is mostly green water.

Mission 3

  • Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are renewed by nature; non-renewable fossil fuels and uranium are fixed stocks.
  • Every source has pros and cons; wind and solar are low-carbon but variable, so they work best in a mix. Supply can be raised by new reserves, new technology such as fracking, new power stations or imports.

Mission 4

  • UK primary energy 2024: oil 38.8%, gas 32.3%, renewables 21.3%, coal 2.5%. Coal fell from 43.8% (1970); gas replaced it, then renewables grew. Half of electricity is now renewable.
  • Mixes vary with resources (Norway hydro, Qatar gas), wealth (energy per person) and population (India's total use).

Mission 5

  • Energy use rose about 9.6 times since 1920, driven by population, development and technology; it is very unequal per person.
  • Non-renewables still dominate; HEP, wind and solar are growing, each with gains and costs.

Mission 6

  • Stakeholders (individuals, organisations, governments) see energy differently because of their interests, the scale at which they feel costs and benefits, and their time horizon.
  • Both renewable and non-renewable resources need sustainable management: fossil fuels run out and emit CO₂; renewables still affect land, wildlife and people.

Mission 7

  • India (medium HDI): coal 57% of energy, 71% of electricity; emissions rising; managing through solar, clean cooking (Ujjwala) and industrial efficiency (PAT). UK (very high HDI): fossil fuels about 74% of energy, coal power ended 2024; emissions down 47% since 1990; net zero law, offshore wind, insulation.
