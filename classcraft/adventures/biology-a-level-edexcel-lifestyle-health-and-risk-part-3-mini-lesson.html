Mission 1

Three kinds of variable

  • Independent variable: the one you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure. It may depend on the independent variable.
  • Controlled variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them constant.
  • Example: lamp distance (independent) → bubbles of gas per minute from pondweed (dependent). Temperature, CO2 supply and the piece of pondweed stay the same.
  • Change one variable at a time. If two change together, you cannot tell which caused the effect.
  • The hook: fertiliser and light changed together. The result is confounded.
Quick check
?A student places a lamp at different distances from a beaker of pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution kept at 25 °C, and counts the bubbles of gas released per minute at each distance. Which correctly identifies the variables?
Mission 1

Controls

  • A control is a set-up identical to the others except that the factor being tested is absent (or at a standard level).
  • It shows what happens without the factor, so any difference can be put down to that factor.
  • Negative control: no effect expected (e.g. boiled enzyme, or water in place of an extract).
  • Positive control: an effect is known to occur. It shows the method can detect one.
  • A control need not show "nothing". It gives the baseline for comparison.
  • Say: "temperature was a controlled variable" — not: "temperature was the control".
Quick check
?A student tests the claim that an extract from a hypothetical plant contains an enzyme that speeds up the breakdown of compound P. When P breaks down, the solution turns yellow. Tube 1 contains P, buffer and the extract. Tube 2 contains P and buffer, with water in place of the extract. Both tubes are kept at 25 °C, and the time each takes to turn yellow is recorded. Which statement best justifies including tube 2?
Mission 1

Questions and hypotheses

  • A testable question names what you will change and what you will measure: "Does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
  • A hypothesis is a provisional explanation that can be tested: "Rate rises with light intensity, because light drives the light-dependent reactions."
  • A prediction says what you expect to measure if the hypothesis is right.
  • The null hypothesis says the independent variable has no effect (or there is no difference). Statistical tests check whether the data let you reject it.
  • Data can support a hypothesis. They never prove it.
Quick check
?A student plans to test whether the number of double bonds in a fatty acid affects its melting point, using fatty acids with 0, 1, 2 and 3 double bonds that all have the same chain length. Which statement is an appropriate null hypothesis for this investigation?
Mission 1

Measuring the dependent variable

  • Biologists often measure a rate: change ÷ time.
  • Product formed over time: change in amount ÷ time taken.
  • e.g. 15 cm³ of oxygen in 30 s → 15 ÷ 30 = 0.50 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Time for a fixed change (a disc rises, a colour appears): rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. A disc that rises in 25 s → 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
  • A shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Measure the initial rate where you can: the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
  • Choose apparatus that suits the size of the change: a gas syringe or a colorimeter beats judging by eye.
Quick check
?A filter-paper disc soaked in catalase solution sinks in hydrogen peroxide and rises when enough oxygen has formed on it. In 1% hydrogen peroxide the disc took 50 s to rise; in 2% it took 20 s. Which statement about the reaction rate is correct?
Mission 1

Experiment, observation or model?

  • Experiment: you manipulate the independent variable and control the rest. Best evidence for cause and effect.
  • Observation (survey, field sampling): you record what is already there. Shows patterns and correlations, not cause.
  • Example: barnacle A grows only where barnacle B is absent (observation). Scrape B off marked plots and A moves in (field experiment).
  • Even a good experiment only indicates a cause; other differences may remain.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to test the hypothesis that two limpet species compete for algae on a shore. Which approach tests the hypothesis by experiment rather than by observation?
Mission 1

Repeats, samples and validity

  • Living things vary. One measurement may be unusual, so repeat and use the mean.
  • Repeats also expose anomalies: results far from the others.
  • Sample size: more individuals make the mean more likely to be near the true population mean.
  • Sample selection: choose at random, so the sample represents the population and no one picks "good" specimens.
  • In health-risk studies: large samples; groups matched for factors that also affect risk (e.g. age, smoking); people chosen to represent the population the conclusion is about.
  • Valid: the method answers the question asked. It needs controlled variables and a suitable dependent variable.
  • Results that come out the same when repeated are repeatable; when someone else repeats them with other equipment, reproducible.
  • "Reliable" is used for results that stay consistent when repeated.
  • Repeats cannot rescue an invalid design: the hook's plants could be measured 100 times and still be confounded.
Mission 1

Tables and graphs

  • Table: independent variable in the first column; repeats, then mean. Units in the headings, never in the cells. Same number of decimal places down each column.
  • Graph: independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.
  • e.g. "Rate / cm³ s⁻¹".
  • Choose a scale that uses over half the grid, in even steps.
  • Line graph: continuous independent variable. Bar chart: categories.
  • Scatter graph: looking for a correlation between two measured variables.
  • Do not extend a line beyond your data.
Mission check

Like the fertiliser and the window, two variables changed together. Control the second one, and the test becomes valid.

Mission 2

Accurate or precise?

  • Accurate: close to the true value.
  • Precise: repeats agree closely, with little spread about the mean.
  • Random error: unpredictable scatter (judging an end point, natural variation). It lowers precision. Repeats and a mean reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error: shifts every reading the same way (a balance not zeroed, a thermometer reading 2 °C high). It lowers accuracy. Repeats do not remove it; calibrating does.
  • Resolution: the smallest change an instrument can show.
  • Say: "precise but not accurate" — not: "precise, so accurate".
Quick check
?Two students each measured the angle of curvature of the same seedling shoot five times with a protractor. Student A recorded 28°, 29°, 28°, 29°, 28°. Student B recorded 22°, 35°, 30°, 26°, 37°. A later check showed that the true angle was 35° and that Student A had aligned the protractor with the edge of the pot instead of the vertical part of the stem. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission 2

Uncertainty and percentage error

  • Every reading has an uncertainty. On a scale: ± half the smallest division (thermometer marked every 1 °C: ±0.5 °C). Use the value in the question if one is given.
  • Percentage error = uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100.
  • Combined data: a change or difference needs two readings. Add their uncertainties.
  • Worked example: water warms from 20.0 °C to 35.0 °C; each reading ±0.5 °C.
  • Change = 15.0 °C, uncertainty = 0.5 + 0.5 = ±1.0 °C.
  • Percentage error = 1.0 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = 6.7%.
  • A larger measured quantity gives a smaller percentage error: measure bigger changes, or over longer times.
Quick check
?A potato cylinder's mass falls from 3.62 g to 3.40 g in a sucrose solution. The balance reads to ±0.01 g. What is the percentage error in the change in mass?
Mission 2

Anomalies, limitations, improvements

  • Anomaly: a result that does not fit the pattern. Look for a cause; repeat it; leave it out of the mean only with a stated reason.
  • Limitation: a feature of the method that reduces validity, accuracy or precision.
  • Match the improvement to the problem:
    • random scatter → more repeats, then a mean;
    • systematic error → zero or calibrate the instrument;
    • low resolution → an instrument with finer divisions;
    • an uncontrolled variable → control it (water bath, buffer).

Mission 2Practical

Working with living organisms

  • Core practical 1: caffeine and Daphnia heart rate.
  • Independent: caffeine concentration. Dependent: heart rate (beats per minute, counted under the microscope).
  • Control: same temperature (the microscope lamp warms the water, so limit time under it); similar-sized Daphnia; same time to settle before counting.
  • Core practical 13: temperature and development. E.g. % of brine shrimp eggs hatched over time at different temperatures; same mass of eggs, volume and salinity.
  • Use the fewest organisms that will answer the question; avoid harm; return them to culture.
  • Ethics of using invertebrates. Arguments for: simpler nervous systems and uncertain pain perception; large numbers and short life cycles. Against: they are living organisms; some invertebrates show pain-like responses.
  • UK law (Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022) names decapod crustaceans and cephalopods as sentient. Daphnia are not decapods.
Mission check

?A student uses a water bath to keep enzyme tubes at 30 °C. Her thermometer gives readings that agree closely: 30.0, 30.0, 30.5, 30.0 °C. A calibrated electronic probe in the same water bath reads 28.0 °C. Which improvement would make her temperature measurements more accurate?

Agreement shows precision, not truth. A systematic error moves every reading together, and only calibration finds it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Make a guess: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Mission 3

Single sugars

  • A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit: the monomer of carbohydrates.
  • Named by number of carbon atoms: triose (3 C), pentose (5 C), hexose (6 C).
  • Hexoses: glucose, fructose, galactose. Pentoses: ribose (in RNA and ATP), deoxyribose (in DNA).
  • Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6: same atoms, arranged differently. They are isomers.
Mission 3

Rings: count every carbon

  • In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mostly as rings.
  • Glucose: ring of 5 C + 1 O. Carbon 6 sits outside the ring, in a CH2OH group.
  • Ribose: ring of 4 C + 1 O. Carbon 5 sits outside.
  • Fructose (as in sucrose): ring of 4 C + 1 O, with two carbons outside.
  • So ring size does not tell you the sugar type. Count all the carbons.
  • In glucose, number the carbons from the one to the right of the ring O: C1, C2 … (In fructose, C1 is outside the ring.)
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides, P and Q, are drawn in their ring forms. In P the ring is made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, with one further carbon atom (in a CH2OH group) attached outside the ring. In Q the ring is also made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, but two further carbon atoms (each in a CH2OH group) are attached outside the ring. How should P and Q be classified?
Mission 3

α-glucose and β-glucose

  • Two forms of glucose: same formula, same ring.
  • They differ only at carbon 1.
  • α-glucose: –OH on C1 points below the ring.
  • β-glucose: –OH on C1 points above the ring.
  • A tiny difference with large effects: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen; β-glucose builds cellulose (see Mission 4).
  • Topic 1 uses α-glucose only; β-glucose returns in Topic 4 (cellulose).
  • Say: "the –OH on carbon 1 is inverted" — not: "β-glucose has different atoms".
Quick check
?Which statement about α-glucose and β-glucose is correct?
Mission 3

Two sugars make a disaccharide

  • Two monosaccharides join by condensation: a glycosidic bond forms and water is released.
  • Maltose = glucose + glucose. Two α-glucose, joined C1 to C4: a 1,4-glycosidic bond.
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose.
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose.
  • Hydrolysis splits a disaccharide back into its monosaccharides, using water.
Quick check
?The enzyme maltase, in the small intestine, acts on maltose. Which statement describes the reaction?
Mission 3

Glucose: built for transport and fuel

  • Soluble: its many –OH groups hydrogen-bond with water.
  • Transportable: dissolved in blood plasma, it reaches every cell.
  • Chemically stable: it does not break down on its own; enzymes control when it is oxidised.
  • Energy yield: oxidation in respiration releases about 16 kJ per gram.
  • But soluble means osmotically active. So cells store glucose as large polysaccharides instead (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "glucose dissolves because it is polar" — not: "because it is small".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly links a property of glucose to the way it is used in organisms?
Mission check

?Galactose and glucose both have the formula C6H12O6 and both form a ring of five carbons and one oxygen. They differ only in which way the –OH on carbon 4 points. Which statement is correct?

Same atoms, different arrangement: that is all it takes to make a new sugar, from glucose and fructose to α and β.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Make a guess: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Mission 4

One monomer, three polymers

  • Starch: energy store in plants.
  • Glycogen: energy store in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose: strong walls of plant cells.
  • All three are polymers of glucose, built by condensation.
  • Units are joined by glycosidic bonds.
  • What differs: which form of glucose, and how the units are connected.
Mission 4

Starch: amylose and amylopectin

  • Made of α-glucose.
  • Amylose: 1,4-glycosidic bonds only. Unbranched. Coils into a helix: compact.
  • Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds plus 1,6-glycosidic bonds at branch points, about every 25 units.
  • Starch grains in plant cells, e.g. potato tubers and seeds, contain both.
Mission 4

Glycogen

  • Made of α-glucose, like amylopectin.
  • 1,4 bonds, with 1,6 branches about every 10 units: more branched than amylopectin.
  • Very compact granules in liver and muscle cells.
  • Enzymes remove glucose only from chain ends. More branches = more ends = faster release.
  • Fits animals: they can need glucose for respiration very quickly.
Mission 4

What makes a good store

  • Compact: coiling and branching pack many glucose units into little space.
  • Relatively insoluble because the molecules are so large. So they do not draw water into the cell by osmosis.
  • Too large to leave the cell.
  • Easily hydrolysed to α-glucose when energy is needed; easily built up again by condensation.
  • Say: "relatively insoluble because they are so large" — not: "insoluble because they are non-polar".
Quick checkRead the data

The graph shows the mass of monosaccharide released over 30 minutes (illustrative data). Which statement correctly describes the data?
Quick check
?Liver cells store glucose in the form of glycogen. Why is the glucose not simply stored as free glucose?
Mission 4

Cellulose

  • (1) Made of β-glucose. To form each 1,4-glycosidic bond, every other unit is inverted (rotated 180°).
  • (2) The alternating units give straight, unbranched chains.
  • (3) Straight chains lie side by side, linked by many hydrogen bonds between their –OH groups: microfibrils.
  • Microfibrils group into fibres with high tensile strength.
  • Cell walls resist stretching, so plant cells can become turgid without bursting.
  • Say: "hydrogen bonds between chains" — not: "covalent cross-links" or "coiled like starch".
Quick check
?Cellulose gives plant cell walls high tensile strength. Which feature of cellulose is responsible for this?

Mission 5: If you eat a little more than you use every day, where does the extra energy end up?

Make a guess: If you eat a little more than you use every day, where does the extra energy end up?

Mission 5

Energy in, energy out

  • Energy intake: energy in the food and drink you take in.
  • Energy expenditure (output): energy your body uses.
  • Energy budget = intake − expenditure, usually per day, in kJ or MJ.
  • Intake = expenditure: energy balance. Body mass stays steady.
  • Intake > expenditure: positive balance (surplus).
  • Intake < expenditure: negative balance (deficit).
Mission 5

Where the energy goes

  • Basal metabolic rate (BMR): energy used at rest to stay alive: heartbeat, breathing, active transport, keeping body temperature.
  • BMR is the largest part of expenditure for most people.
  • Physical activity: the part you can change most.
  • Digesting and processing food: about 10% of expenditure.
  • BMR is higher with more body mass, and with more muscle.
  • Growth, pregnancy and breastfeeding raise energy needs.
Mission 5

Energy in food

  • Energy per gram, as used on UK food labels:
  • Fat: 37 kJ g⁻¹
  • Carbohydrate: 17 kJ g⁻¹ · Protein: 17 kJ g⁻¹
  • Alcohol: 29 kJ g⁻¹ · Fibre: 8 kJ g⁻¹
  • Fat gives more than twice the energy per gram of carbohydrate.
  • Labels give kJ and kcal: 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ.
  • Example (per 100 g bar, illustrative): 12 g fat, 64 g carbohydrate, 8 g protein, 5 g fibre → 444 + 1088 + 136 + 40 = 1708 kJ.
Quick check
?A 40 g handful of mixed nuts contains 20 g fat, 6 g carbohydrate and 8 g protein. Using 37 kJ g⁻¹ for fat and 17 kJ g⁻¹ for carbohydrate and protein, what is its energy content?
Mission 5

Worked example: a day's energy budget

  • Illustrative data for one person, one day.
  • Intake: 2100 + 3200 + 3600 + 1500 (snacks) = 10 400 kJ.
  • Expenditure: BMR 6800 + activity 1500 + digesting food 900 = 9200 kJ.
  • Budget: 10 400 − 9200 = +1200 kJ a day (surplus).
  • Over 30 days: 1200 × 30 = 36 000 kJ.
  • If all stored as fat: 36 000 ÷ 37 ≈ 970 g. An upper estimate: stored fat tissue also holds water and protein, and expenditure changes as mass changes.
Mission 5

When the budget is out of balance

  • Surplus: extra glucose is stored as glycogen in liver and muscle, but these stores are small.
  • The rest of the surplus is stored as triglycerides in adipose tissue → weight gain.
  • A long-term surplus can lead to obesity (see Mission 6).
  • Deficit: the body uses glycogen first, then mainly fat.
  • In a long or severe deficit, protein (e.g. muscle) is also broken down → weight loss.
  • Obesity raises the risk of CHD, high blood pressure and type 2 diabetes.
Quick check
?A student's energy intake is below their energy expenditure every day for six weeks. Which describes the energy stores the body uses?
Mission 5

Read the data: weight loss slows down

  • Illustrative data. A person cuts intake to a fixed amount below their starting expenditure, for 24 weeks.
  • Body mass: 90.0 kg (week 0) → 88.3 (4) → 86.8 (8) → 85.5 (12) → 84.3 (16) → 83.2 (20) → 82.2 (24).
  • Weeks 0–4: −1.7 kg. Weeks 20–24: −1.0 kg.
  • Intake did not change. So why does loss slow?
  • Lower body mass → lower BMR and less energy to move → expenditure falls.
  • So the deficit shrinks, and mass changes more slowly.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Two people have the same height and weight. Why might one have a higher risk of heart disease?

Make a guess: Two people have the same height and weight. Why might one have a higher risk of heart disease?

Mission 6

Body mass index

  • BMI = body mass (kg) ÷ height² (m²). Units: kg m⁻².
  • Example: 82 kg, 1.75 m → 82 ÷ 1.75² = 26.8 kg m⁻².
  • Adult categories:
  • below 18.5: underweight · 18.5–24.9: healthy weight
  • 25.0–29.9: overweight · 30.0 or more: obese
  • Obesity: so much body fat that health risk rises. BMI ≥ 30 is the usual indicator.
Quick check
?An adult has a mass of 68 kg and a height of 1.60 m. What is their BMI, and which category is it in?
Mission 6

Waist-to-hip ratio

  • Measure the waist (narrowest point, or midway between lowest rib and hip bone) and the hips (widest point).
  • WHR = waist ÷ hip. Same units cancel: no units.
  • Example: waist 94 cm, hips 102 cm → 94 ÷ 102 = 0.92.
  • High WHR = more fat stored around the abdomen (central fat).
  • WHO: substantially raised risk of metabolic complications (e.g. type 2 diabetes) at WHR ≥ 0.90 (men), ≥ 0.85 (women).
  • Central fat is more strongly linked with CHD than fat on the hips and thighs.
Mission 6

What each indicator misses

  • BMI uses mass, not fat. Muscle adds mass too, so a very muscular person can score "obese".
  • BMI says nothing about where fat is stored.
  • WHR shows fat distribution, but not total fat.
  • Both are quick, cheap and need no lab: good for screening large groups.
  • So use both, and treat each as an indicator, not a diagnosis.
  • Say: "BMI is an indicator of body fat" — not: "BMI measures body fat".
Mission 6

Read the data: INTERHEART

  • Real study: 12 461 people after a first heart attack (cases), 14 637 without (controls), 52 countries.
  • People split into five equal groups (quintiles) by BMI, and by WHR.
  • Odds ratio (OR): odds of heart attack in a group ÷ odds in the lowest group. OR 1 = no difference.
  • Top vs bottom quintile, BMI: OR 1.44 (before adjustment).
  • Top vs bottom quintile, WHR: OR 2.52 (adjusted for age, sex, region, smoking).
  • WHR quintiles 2–5: 1.15 → 1.39 → 1.90 → 2.52: a graded rise.
  • After allowing for WHR, the BMI OR fell to 1.12.

Mission 6

Using the knowledge to reduce CHD risk

  • Diet: keep energy intake in balance with expenditure; less saturated fat (lowers LDL cholesterol); less salt (lowers blood pressure).
  • Exercise: raises expenditure; lowers blood pressure; raises HDL cholesterol.
  • Smoking: stopping removes carbon monoxide (less haemoglobin carrying CO) and nicotine (raises heart rate and blood pressure; stickier platelets), and stops damage to the artery endothelium.
  • Indicators: BMI and WHR track change over time.
  • People weigh this evidence against cost, effort and other choices.
  • Say: "a risk factor increases the probability of CHD" — not: "a risk factor causes CHD".
Quick check
?A survey finds that people who eat more fruit have lower rates of CHD. A headline says: "Fruit prevents heart disease." Which is the best response?

  • Question: does storing juice open in a fridge lower its vitamin C?
  • IV: storage time. DV: volume of juice to decolourise the DCPIP.

  • Vitamin C is oxidised over time and by heat, so the stored juice has less.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Change one variable, measure one, keep the rest constant; a control gives the baseline for comparison.
  • A null hypothesis says "no effect"; data can support a hypothesis but never prove it.
  • Experiments manipulate a variable; observations record patterns. Random, repeated samples make a mean trustworthy.

Mission 2

  • Precise = repeats agree; accurate = close to the true value. Repeats reduce random error; calibration removes systematic error.
  • Percentage error = uncertainty ÷ measured value × 100; add uncertainties when a value comes from two readings.

Mission 3

  • Monosaccharides are named by their carbons (triose, pentose, hexose); isomers share a formula but not an arrangement; α- and β-glucose differ only at carbon 1.
  • Condensation of two monosaccharides forms a glycosidic bond: maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose).

Mission 4

  • α-glucose → starch (amylose helix; branched amylopectin) and glycogen (highly branched): compact, insoluble stores. β-glucose → straight cellulose chains hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils: strength.

Mission 5

  • Energy budget = intake − expenditure. A surplus is stored, mostly as fat in adipose tissue (weight gain, and over time obesity); a deficit draws on glycogen, then fat, then protein (weight loss).
  • Food energy: fat 37, carbohydrate and protein 17 kJ g⁻¹; expenditure is BMR + activity + digesting food.

Mission 6

  • BMI = mass ÷ height² (obese at 30 or more); WHR = waist ÷ hip (raised risk at 0.90 for men, 0.85 for women). WHR, which reflects central fat, is more strongly associated with heart attack than BMI.
  • Diet, exercise and not smoking lower CHD risk factors. CP2: more juice to decolourise DCPIP means less vitamin C.
