Mission 1

One question, five parts

  • Every religion and every theme is tested by five-part questions worth 24 marks each.
  • Your parts: 1, 2, 4, 5, 12 marks.
  • The first four parts test AO1: knowledge and understanding.
  • The 12-mark part tests AO2: analysis and evaluation.
  • So half of every question is about knowing; half is about arguing.
  • SPaG (spelling, punctuation, grammar and specialist terms) is marked on 12-mark answers.
  • SPaG: up to 3 extra marks on Paper 1, and up to 6 on Paper 2.
Mission 1

The short questions

  • 1 mark, multiple choice: "Which one of the following…?" Four options; choose one.
  • Read every option first. Distractors are often real terms used in the wrong place (e.g. omniscient for omnipotent).
  • 2 marks: "Give two…" or "Name two…". Two short, correct points. No explanation needed.
  • Spend little time here: these parts reward exact knowledge of key terms.

Mission 1

The 4-mark question

  • Typical wording: "Explain two ways…", or:
  • "Explain two contrasting beliefs…".
  • Each of the two points earns up to 2 marks: 1 for the point, 1 for developing it (why, how, what follows).
  • Contrasting means the beliefs must differ, not just be two different reasons.
  • Contrasts may be within Christianity (e.g. Catholic and another Christian view), with another religion, or with a non-religious view: follow the question.
  • Two developed points beat four undeveloped ones.
Mission 1Worked answer

4 marks: two beliefs that differ

Question: Explain two contrasting religious beliefs about pacifism.

  • Belief 1: Quakers (the Religious Society of Friends) are pacifists. Develop: they believe there is "that of God" in everyone, so killing another person can never be right.
  • Belief 2: The Catholic Church teaches that a just war may be fought as a last resort. Develop: a nation has a duty to defend its people, under strict conditions, so most Catholics are not strict pacifists.
  • Each belief: point (1) + development (1) = 2 marks.
Mission 1

The question with a source

  • 5 marks.
  • Typical wording: "Explain two religious beliefs about… Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching in your answer."
  • Two beliefs, each developed, as in the 4-mark question.
  • Plus a relevant source of wisdom and authority: a scripture passage (quoted or paraphrased), Church teaching, a creed, a hadith, a respected teacher.
  • The source must fit the belief it supports. Book and verse help but are not required; accuracy is.
  • Weave the source into a belief rather than tacking it on at the end.
Mission 1Worked answer

Two beliefs and a source

Question: Explain two Christian beliefs about forgiveness. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Christian belief and teaching in your answer.

  • Belief 1: Christians should forgive without limit. Source: when Peter asks if seven times is enough, Jesus says "until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22, WEBBE). Develop: so Christians try to forgive even when someone hurts them again and again.
  • Belief 2: God's forgiveness is linked to forgiving others. Source: "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors." (Matthew 6:12, WEBBE) Develop: Christians who pray the Lord's Prayer ask God to forgive them in the same way they forgive.
  • Two developed beliefs + an accurate, relevant source = the top of the mark range.

Mission 1

The 12-mark evaluation

  • Wording: a statement in quotation marks, then "Evaluate this statement."
  • Bullets tell you what to include. Typically: reasoned arguments for; reasoned arguments for a different point of view; religious arguments or teaching; non-religious arguments where allowed; a justified conclusion.
  • On Component 1, the bullets ask you to refer to Catholic teaching.
  • Top answers build chains of reasoning: claim → reason → teaching → so what?
  • Then weigh: say which arguments are stronger, and why.
Mission 1Worked answer

A 12-mark paragraph and conclusion

Statement: "Religious believers should always be pacifists."

  • For: Jesus taught "love your enemies" (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE). So: many Christians, like Quakers, argue that killing an enemy can never be loving.
  • Against: many Christians accept a just war as a last resort, to protect the innocent. So: refusing to fight could leave others to suffer.
  • Evaluate: the just war argument is strong because it also comes from love (for victims), but it depends on wars meeting strict conditions, which critics say almost never happens.
  • Justified conclusion: state your view, give the deciding reason, and say why the other side is weaker.
  • On SPaG: use terms accurately (pacifism, just war, agape).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Five parts: 1, 2, 4, 5, 12.
  • Explain questions: two points, each developed; add an accurate, relevant source where asked.
  • 12-mark: arguments for and against, religious teaching, a justified conclusion; SPaG counts.
