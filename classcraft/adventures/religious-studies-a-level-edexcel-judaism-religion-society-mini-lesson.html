Mission 1

Mashiach: the anointed one

  • Messiah comes from Hebrew mashiach, "anointed": a person marked with oil for a task God gives.
  • In the Tanakh, kings and priests were anointed: "Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him" (1 Samuel 16:13, JPS 1917), of David.
  • Even the Persian king Cyrus is called "His anointed" (Isaiah 45:1, JPS 1917) for freeing the exiles.
  • Later hope focused on one future king from David's line: the King Messiah (Melekh ha-Mashiach).
  • He is a human being, not divine; Jews pray for him, they do not pray to him.

  • "For out of Zion shall go forth the law" (Isaiah 2:3, JPS 1917).

Mission 1

The Messianic Age in the prophets

  • A king of David's line: "a shoot out of the stock of Jesse" (Isaiah 11:1, JPS 1917), filled with God's spirit.
  • Judge: God "shall judge between the nations" (Isaiah 2:4); the king judges the poor "with righteousness" (Isaiah 11:4).
  • Ingathering: God "will assemble the dispersed of Israel" (Isaiah 11:12, JPS 1917).
  • Temple rebuilt; Jerusalem a centre of worship for all nations.
  • Knowledge of God: "the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD, as the waters cover the sea" (Isaiah 11:9, JPS 1917).
  • Herald: Elijah, before "the great and terrible day of the LORD" (Malachi 4:5, JPS 1917; 3:23 in Hebrew).

Mission 1

Maimonides on the Messiah

  • Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings, chapters 11–12 (paraphrase):
  • Who: a king from the house of David, who studies Torah, keeps the mitzvot and leads Israel to keep them.
  • Test: if he rebuilds the Temple and gathers the exiles, he is certainly the Messiah. If he fails or is killed, he was not.
  • He need not work miracles.
  • Nature unchanged: "the world follows its normal course"; the wolf lying with the lamb (Isaiah 11:6) is a metaphor for peace among nations.
  • Purpose: not power or pleasure, but freedom to study Torah and know God.
  • Do not calculate the date. (Principle 12: wait for him.)
Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive today

  • Orthodox: a personal Messiah of David's line; the Amidah prays daily for "the sprout of David"; principle 12 in Ani Ma'amin.
  • Chabad Hasidim: campaign to hasten the Messiah through mitzvot.
  • Religious Zionists (Rav Kook, 1865–1935): the return to the Land is "the beginning of redemption".
  • Some Haredi groups (e.g. Satmar): a human-made state forces the end; only the Messiah should restore Israel.
  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) spoke of "the establishment of the kingdom of truth, justice, and peace among all men": a Messianic Age, not a person. Reform prayer books replaced "redeemer" with "redemption".
  • Masorti / Conservative: the Messianic hope affirmed; whether a person or a symbol is left open.
Mission 1

The Messiah in interfaith dialogue

  • In multicultural Britain, Jews live alongside Christians, who believe Jesus is the Messiah, and Muslims, who honour Jesus as Messiah; many Muslims also await the Mahdi.
  • Most Jews' answer: the Messiah's tasks (peace, Temple, ingathering) are not yet done; so the Messiah has not yet come.
  • Maimonides: Christianity and Islam spread knowledge of scripture and the commandments in the world, preparing it for the true Messiah (Laws of Kings 11, uncensored text, paraphrase).
  • Dabru Emet (2000), a statement by Jewish scholars: Jews and Christians differ over the Messiah, and the difference will last until God redeems the world (paraphrase).
  • Dialogue does not need agreement: it can respect a difference and share hope for peace.

Mission 2

Four possible answers

  • "Who is a Jew?" has more than one Jewish answer. Four factors keep coming up:
  • Birth: being born into the Jewish people.
  • Faith: believing in God and the teachings of Judaism.
  • Works: keeping the mitzvot (commandments) and living a Jewish life.
  • Ritual: ceremonies that mark belonging, such as brit milah and bar/bat mitzvah.
  • Jews are a people as well as a faith: someone can be Jewish by birth and not religious at all.
  • Groups weigh these factors differently; that is where the disagreements lie.
Mission 2

Born of a Jewish mother

  • Halakhah (Jewish law) defines a Jew as someone born of a Jewish mother, or someone who has converted according to halakhah (see Mission 3).
  • The father's status does not decide it: the Mishnah rules that the child of a non-Jewish mother takes the mother's status (Kiddushin 3:12, paraphrase); the Talmud derives the converse, that the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish (next screen).
  • This matrilineal rule is held by Orthodox and Masorti (Conservative) Judaism, and is the basis of Jewish status in the Chief Rabbinate of Israel.
  • Reasons given: the Talmud derives it from the Torah (next screen); some modern writers add that a child's mother is always known, or that mothers were the main teachers of faith at home. Those are later explanations, not the legal source.
Mission 2Read the source

Deuteronomy 7:3–4

> "neither shalt thou make marriages with them: thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son." (Deuteronomy 7:3, JPS 1917)

> "For he will turn away thy son from following Me, that they may serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:4, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Kiddushin 68b, paraphrase) notes "he" in verse 4 is the non-Jewish father, who would turn away "thy son": that is, the grandson born to your daughter is still called "thy son". So the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish.
  • The child of a Jewish father and a non-Jewish mother is not called "thy son"; halakhically, that child is not Jewish without conversion.

Q2 Is this rule written plainly in the verse, or derived by interpretation?

Mission 2

Once a Jew, always a Jew?

  • In halakhah, Jewish status by birth cannot be lost. "An Israelite, even though he has sinned, remains an Israelite" (Talmud Sanhedrin 44a, paraphrase).
  • So a Jew who never practises, or who joins another religion, is still halakhically Jewish (though regarded as a sinner).
  • Israeli law is different. The Law of Return (1950; amended 1970) gives the right to settle to Jews, their children and grandchildren. In 1962 Israel's Supreme Court ruled that Brother Daniel (Oswald Rufeisen), born Jewish but a Catholic monk, did not count as a Jew under that law.
  • The UK JFS case (Supreme Court, 2009): a Jewish school's admission test based on matrilineal descent was ruled ethnic discrimination. Jewish schools now use tests of practice instead.

Mission 2

Brit milah: the covenant of circumcision

  • "every male among you shall be circumcised" (Genesis 17:10, JPS 1917): the sign of the covenant with Abraham.
  • On the eighth day (Genesis 17:12), even on Shabbat or Yom Kippur; delayed if the baby is unwell (pikuach nephesh: saving life comes first).
  • Performed by a mohel (trained circumciser); the sandek holds the baby; an empty chair for Elijah; blessings and the baby's Hebrew name.
  • It does not make the boy Jewish: he is Jewish from birth. It brings him into the covenant and fulfils a mitzvah.
  • For girls: naming ceremonies (e.g. simchat bat, "joy of a daughter") in synagogue or at home.
Mission 2Diversity

Attitudes to circumcision

  • Orthodox and Masorti: a binding mitzvah from the Torah; a father's duty; neglecting it is serious.
  • Reform and Liberal: almost all practise and encourage it as the sign of the covenant; some trained Reform doctors act as mohalim.
  • Many secular Jews also circumcise their sons as a mark of belonging to the people.
  • A small minority question it on grounds of bodily autonomy or pain; some hold a non-surgical welcoming ceremony instead (sometimes called brit shalom). Most Jewish bodies reject this as replacing the covenant.
  • Outside pressure: a German court ruled against infant circumcision in 2012; Germany then passed a law protecting it. Jewish and Muslim groups defended it together.
Mission 2

Bar and bat mitzvah

  • Bar mitzvah (son of the commandment): at 13, a boy becomes responsible for keeping the mitzvot. It happens automatically by age.
  • The celebration: he is called up to read from the Torah (an aliyah), may lead prayers, and now counts in a minyan; he starts wearing tefillin.
  • Bat mitzvah (daughter of the commandment): halakhically at 12 for girls.
  • Orthodox: girls' celebrations often include a talk on Torah, study or a group ceremony (e.g. bat chayil in some UK communities); usually no Torah reading in a mixed service.
  • Reform and Liberal: girls at 13 do exactly what boys do, reading from the Torah. The first public American bat mitzvah was Judith Kaplan's in 1922.
  • Some Progressive communities also hold Kabbalat Torah or confirmation at 15–16.

Mission 2

Faith and works: a people as well as a religion

  • Faith: Maimonides' Thirteen Principles set out beliefs; some Jews see belief as central to being a "good" Jew, but not to being a Jew.
  • Works: for many Orthodox Jews, Jewishness is lived through the mitzvot: Shabbat, kashrut, prayer, study.
  • Secular and cultural Jews: identity through family, history, language, food, Israel, memory of the Holocaust. Humanistic Judaism (founded 1963) celebrates Jewish culture without belief in God.
  • M Persoff (historian of British Jewry; Faith Against Reason, 2008) shows how questions of status and conversion repeatedly divided the Chief Rabbinate from Progressive communities (paraphrase).
Mission 2Evaluate

Is birth the most important factor in Jewish identity?

  • Claim: Birth matters more than faith, works or ritual in making someone a Jew.
  • For: Halakhah defines Jewish status by birth (or conversion) alone; status cannot be lost (Sanhedrin 44a).
  • For: God's covenant is with a people and its descendants (Genesis 17:7); identity is inherited.
  • Against: Without practice and belief, identity may fade; Jewish life is lived through mitzvot and rituals.
  • Against: Many Progressive Jews accept children of a Jewish father raised as Jews (see Mission 3), and the JFS ruling challenged birth-based tests.
  • Judgement criteria: Status ("is a Jew") or quality ("lives as a Jew")? Whose authority decides: halakhah, a movement, the State of Israel?

Mission 3

A non-missionising religion

  • Judaism does not send missionaries or seek converts.
  • Reason: non-Jews do not need to become Jewish to be righteous. The rabbis taught that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase), if they keep the seven Noahide laws.
  • So a convert is not "saved" by converting; they take on the covenant and its 613 mitzvot.
  • Yet sincere converts are welcomed: a convert (ger, or ger tzedek, "righteous convert") becomes fully Jewish.
Mission 3Read the source

Ruth, the model convert

> "for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge; thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (Ruth 1:16, JPS 1917)

  • Ruth, a Moabite widow, refuses to leave her Israelite mother-in-law Naomi.
  • Naomi urges her to go back several times (Ruth 1:8–15). The rabbis drew from this the custom of discouraging a candidate at first.
  • Ruth becomes the great-grandmother of King David (Ruth 4:17).

Q1 Which two things does Ruth join?

Show answer

A people, "thy people", and a faith, "thy God": both together.

Q2 Why might rabbis turn a candidate away at first?

Show answer

To test sincerity, as Naomi tested Ruth.

Mission 3

The Shulchan Arukh: questions to the convert

  • Shulchan Arukh ("set table"), the law code of Joseph Karo (1488–1575), printed 1565; conversion in Yoreh De'ah 268, drawing on the Talmud (Yevamot 47a–b).
  • The candidate is asked (paraphrase): Why do you want to convert? Do you not know that Jews in this time are oppressed and suffer?
  • If they answer, "I know, and I am not worthy", they are accepted.
  • They are taught the basics of faith (God's unity; the ban on idolatry) and some lighter and stricter mitzvot, with their rewards and punishments, but not burdened with too much at once.
  • The aim: an informed, free and sincere commitment.
Mission 3

Three requirements, before a beth din

  • Acceptance of the mitzvot (kabbalat ol mitzvot): commitment to live by halakhah. In Orthodox law, this is essential.
  • Circumcision (milah) for men. If already circumcised, a drop of blood is drawn as a sign of the covenant (hatafat dam brit).
  • Immersion in a mikveh (ritual pool of natural water) for men and women: the person emerges as a Jew.
  • All before a beth din (rabbinical court) of three judges.
  • In Temple times an offering was also brought; since the Temple's destruction this is not possible.
  • The convert takes a Hebrew name as a child of Abraham and Sarah.

Mission 3

After conversion: a full member

  • A convert is fully Jewish, as if newly born into the people (Talmud Yevamot 22a, paraphrase).
  • It is forbidden to remind a convert of their past to shame them (Talmud Bava Metzia 58b).
  • "thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, JPS 1917).
  • The community's role: the beth din decides; a rabbi and synagogue teach and support the candidate for months or years; afterwards the community must accept the convert in marriage, worship and daily life.
  • Identity: conversion is joining a people as well as a faith (Ruth 1:16); converts often describe learning Jewish history and customs as becoming part of a family story.
Mission 3Diversity

Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive conversion

  • Orthodox: full commitment to halakhah (Shabbat, kashrut, family purity), usually after 1–3 years of study and living near an Orthodox community; milah and mikveh required. Orthodox batei din accept only Orthodox conversions.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic process with milah, mikveh and a beth din; commitment to Jewish practice, interpreted less strictly. Not accepted by Orthodox authorities.
  • Reform and Liberal: study (often about a year), commitment to Jewish life, and a beth din. Rules on milah and mikveh vary: British Reform generally asks for both; American Reform has not required them since the 1890s, though many rabbis encourage them.
  • Consequence: a convert may be Jewish in one movement and not in another. In Israel, Progressive conversions count for citizenship under the Law of Return (court rulings, latest 2021), but the Chief Rabbinate does not accept them for marriage.
  • M Persoff traces how Britain's Orthodox Chief Rabbinate has refused to recognise Progressive conversions, and how this hardened divisions between communities (paraphrase).
Mission 3

Patrilineal descent

  • Patrilineal descent: the child of a Jewish father (and a non-Jewish mother) can be Jewish without conversion.
  • US Reform (Central Conference of American Rabbis, 1983): a child of either Jewish parent is presumed Jewish if raised as a Jew, shown by "timely" public acts of Jewish identity (e.g. naming, Jewish education, bar/bat mitzvah). Reconstructionist Judaism adopted a similar policy in 1968.
  • UK Liberal Judaism also accepts a child of either Jewish parent raised as a Jew.
  • Orthodox and Masorti reject it: status passes through the mother (Kiddushin 68b). Such a person must convert to be Jewish in their eyes.
  • Note: the Reform rule asks more of some children (a Jewish upbringing), not only less: a child of a Jewish mother raised with no Jewish identity may not be presumed Jewish.
  • "Jewishness": the debate is over whether identity is a legal status (halakhah) or a lived, chosen belonging.

Mission 3Evaluate

Should halakhah be the only test of who is a Jew?

  • Claim: Halakhah (the mother's line or halakhic conversion) should be the only test of who is a Jew.
  • For: One clear standard keeps the Jewish people united; every movement accepts Orthodox conversions, but Orthodox authorities accept no others.
  • For: It rests on the Torah as interpreted by the oral law (Deuteronomy 7:4 in Kiddushin 68b; Yevamot 47a–b).
  • Against: It can exclude people who live fully Jewish lives (a child of a Jewish father raised in a synagogue).
  • Against: Progressive Jews see halakhah as evolving; equal treatment of fathers and mothers reflects modern values and many mixed families.
  • Judgement criteria: Unity or inclusion? Status or lived identity? Who holds authority: the beth din, a movement, the State of Israel?

Mission 4

The home: a small sanctuary

  • Judaism is lived as much at home as in the synagogue: Shabbat meals, festivals, kashrut, prayer, study.
  • The home is often called a mikdash me'at ("small sanctuary"): the table like the Temple altar.

> "and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thy house" (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917)

  • Parents are the first teachers of Torah; the Pesach seder is built around children's questions.
  • Shalom bayit (peace in the home) is a high value: family harmony is a religious goal.
Mission 4

Traditional roles

  • Orthodox Judaism teaches distinct but complementary roles for men and women.
  • Women are exempt from most time-bound positive mitzvot (e.g. tefillin, fixed public prayer) (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:7); a common explanation is their role in the home and with children.
  • Three mitzvot traditionally linked with women (Mishnah Shabbat 2:6): niddah (family purity), separating challah from dough, and lighting Shabbat candles.
  • Jewish identity passes through the mother in Orthodox and Masorti Judaism (see Mission 2).
  • Men: public prayer in a minyan, Torah study as a lifelong duty, leading services.
Mission 4Read the source

A woman of valour

> "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917)

> "She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and the law of kindness is on her tongue." (Proverbs 31:26, JPS 1917)

  • Eshet chayil ("woman of valour", Proverbs 31:10–31) is sung by many husbands and families at the Friday night table.
  • The woman in the poem runs a household, trades, buys a field and teaches with wisdom.

Q1 Which qualities does verse 26 praise?

Show answer

Q2 How could a feminist and a traditionalist read the poem differently?

Show answer

Mission 4

Why roles have changed

  • Society outside religion: women's education, paid work and leadership became normal in the 20th century.
  • Secular rights: UK votes for women (1918, equal in 1928); Sex Discrimination Act 1975; Equality Act 2010. Religious bodies have some exemptions, but expectations changed.
  • Different understandings of Torah and Halakhah:
  • Orthodox: halakhah is divine and binding; change happens only within it.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): Torah reflects its time; equality is a Jewish value that can override old rules.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhah binding but developing; many egalitarian rulings since the 1970s.
  • Interpretations of equality: equal dignity with different roles (Orthodox), or equal roles (Progressive)?

Mission 4

Women and Torah education

  • The Mishnah (Sotah 3:4) records a debate: Ben Azzai says a man should teach his daughter Torah; Rabbi Eliezer opposes teaching daughters Torah (paraphrase).
  • For centuries most women learned practical halakhah and prayer at home, not Talmud in yeshivah.
  • Sarah Schenirer (1883–1935) founded the Bais Yaakov schools for Orthodox girls in Kraków (1917), with rabbinic approval.
  • Today: advanced women's Torah study centres (e.g. Drisha in New York, Nishmat and Matan in Jerusalem); Haredi girls' schools teach Bible and law but usually not Talmud.
  • Progressive Judaism: identical Jewish education for girls and boys, including bat mitzvah with Torah reading.
Mission 4

Agunah: the chained woman

> "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand, and sendeth her out of his house" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish divorce needs a get: a document the husband gives freely to his wife, before a beth din (rabbinic court).
  • A civil divorce alone does not end a Jewish marriage in Orthodox law.
  • Agunah (plural agunot): a "chained" woman whose husband refuses to give a get, or has disappeared.
  • She cannot remarry in Orthodox law; children of a new relationship would be mamzerim, restricted in whom they may marry.
  • A man without a get faces fewer consequences: the system is unequal.
Mission 4

Breaking the chains

  • Prenuptial agreements: the husband agrees in advance to pay daily support until he gives a get (e.g. used by the United Synagogue and in the USA).
  • UK law: the Divorce (Religious Marriages) Act 2002 lets a civil court delay the final divorce until a religious divorce is given.
  • Pressure: some batei din name refusers publicly; Israel's rabbinical courts can impose sanctions, even prison.
  • Masorti: a clause in the ketubah (marriage contract) committing both to the beth din (the "Lieberman clause", 1954); in some cases annulment.
  • Reform and Liberal: civil divorce, or their own egalitarian get, so the problem largely does not arise.

Mission 4

Minyan: who counts?

  • Minyan: a quorum of ten adults needed for parts of public prayer (e.g. Kaddish, Kedushah, Torah reading).
  • Orthodox: ten men over bar mitzvah; women may attend but do not count. A mechitzah (partition) separates seating.
  • Masorti / Conservative: a 1973 ruling allowed women to count; most UK Masorti communities now count women, some do not.
  • Reform and Liberal: women and men count equally; mixed seating.
  • Women's prayer groups and partnership minyanim (from 2002) let Orthodox women read Torah and lead some parts of the service, while ten men still form the minyan.
  • Orthodox feminists, such as the Jewish Orthodox Feminist Alliance (JOFA, founded 1997), seek more roles for women within halakhah: women's prayer groups, partnership minyanim, and prenuptial agreements against get refusal.
Mission 4Diversity

Sexual and Jewish identity

  • Orthodox: gender roles and sexual norms are given by halakhah; same-sex sexual acts are forbidden, though many communities now welcome LGBT Jews as people.
  • Masorti / Conservative: practice varies; the US Conservative movement has approved same-sex wedding ceremonies since 2012.
  • Reform and Liberal: egalitarian roles; same-sex marriage celebrated; transgender Jews welcomed (more in Mission 5).
  • Spec debate: does Jewish identity (halakhah) or personal sexual identity come first?
Mission 4Evaluate

Can women be equal to men in Judaism?

  • Claim: Women can be fully equal to men in Judaism.
  • For: Progressive Judaism already gives equal roles; women in the home are central to Jewish life.
  • For: JOFA and Masorti show change within halakhah is possible (prenups, partnership minyanim).
  • Against: in Orthodox law only men give a get or form a minyan: structural inequality remains.
  • Against: if equality means identical roles, much of traditional Judaism must change; many women choose distinct roles.
  • Judgement criteria: equal dignity or equal roles? Who decides: halakhah, the community, women themselves?

Mission 5

Sex as a mitzvah

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is holy, not shameful; marriage is called kiddushin, "holiness".
  • Procreation: "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917) is counted as the first mitzvah. The school of Hillel (Mishnah Yevamot 6:6): at least a son and a daughter.
  • Companionship and pleasure: a husband owes his wife onah, her "conjugal rights" (Exodus 21:10, JPS 1917): a duty to her, not to himself.
  • So sex has value even when children are not possible (e.g. after menopause).
  • Holiness also brings boundaries: when, with whom and how.

Mission 5

Celibacy and marriage

  • "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18, JPS 1917): marriage is the norm.
  • Judaism has no celibate clergy: rabbis are expected to marry.
  • Talmud (Yevamot 62b–63b, paraphrase): a man without a wife lives without joy, blessing or goodness.
  • One sage, Ben Azzai, stayed unmarried to study Torah; the Talmud records him as an exception, and criticised.
  • Single people are full members of the community; marriage is valued, not forced.
  • Marriage is a covenant; divorce (with a get) is allowed when it fails.

Mission 5

Prohibited relations

  • Leviticus 18 and 20 list the arayot ("nakedness"): forbidden relations.
  • They include incest, adultery, sex during niddah (a woman's monthly period and the days after), male–male intercourse ("Thou shalt not lie with mankind, as with womankind", Leviticus 18:22, JPS 1917) and bestiality.
  • Gilui arayot (forbidden relations) is one of three sins a Jew must die rather than commit (Sanhedrin 74a).
  • Sex before marriage: forbidden in Orthodox halakhah; many progressive Jews accept committed relationships before marriage.
Mission 5Diversity

Same-sex relationships

  • Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 forbids male–male intercourse; rabbis also forbid sex between women (a lesser, rabbinic ban). Orientation itself is not a sin; acts are what halakhah addresses. Same-sex marriage is not recognised.
  • Orthodox responses vary: a 2010 US "Statement of Principles" (many Modern Orthodox rabbis) urged welcome and dignity for gay Jews; the UK Chief Rabbi's guide for Orthodox schools (2018) addresses LGBT+ pupils' wellbeing.
  • Masorti / Conservative (US): since 2006 allows gay and lesbian rabbis; since 2012 same-sex ceremonies.
  • Reform and Liberal: full equality: same-sex marriages, LGBT+ rabbis; Leviticus 18:22 read as tied to its ancient context, or outweighed by the dignity of every person made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
Mission 5

Gender roles

  • Orthodox: distinct roles: women exempt from many time-bound positive mitzvot (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:7), with central roles in home, kashrut and Shabbat; men lead public prayer.
  • Proverbs 31 ("A woman of valour") is sung on Friday night in praise of the wife.
  • Progressive: egalitarian: women and men share mitzvot, prayer leadership and the rabbinate.
  • Jewish identity and sexual identity: a debate over whether halakhah or personal identity should come first.

Mission 6

What is a rabbi?

  • Rabbi: "my teacher". A scholar of Torah and halakhah, not a priest: no sacrifices, no special holiness.
  • Roles today: teaching, deciding questions of halakhah, preaching, pastoral care, leading a community.
  • Semikhah: ordination, a certificate that the holder may teach and decide halakhah.
  • A knowledgeable adult Jew can lead prayers without being a rabbi (in Orthodox practice, a man leads); the question is who may hold authority.
  • So the debate is about authority, tradition and equality, not about holiness.
Mission 6

Regina Jonas

  • Regina Jonas (1902–1944), Berlin. Studied at the Liberal rabbinical college in Berlin.
  • Her thesis (1930) asked: can a woman hold rabbinic office according to halakhic sources? She argued yes.
  • Ordained privately in December 1935 by Rabbi Max Dienemann (Liberal): the first woman rabbi.
  • Taught, preached and gave pastoral care as Nazi persecution grew.
  • Deported to Theresienstadt in 1942, where she kept up rabbinic work; murdered at Auschwitz in 1944.
  • Largely forgotten until her papers were found in an archive in 1991.
Mission 6

From 1972: women rabbis today

  • 1972: Sally Priesand, ordained by the American Reform seminary (Hebrew Union College).
  • 1975: Jackie Tabick, the first British woman rabbi (Leo Baeck College, Reform).
  • 1977: Julia Neuberger (b. 1950), ordained at Leo Baeck College; rabbi of the South London Liberal Synagogue, the first British woman to lead her own congregation; later a member of the House of Lords (2004) and Senior Rabbi of West London Synagogue (2011–2020).
  • 1985: Amy Eilberg, first Conservative (Masorti) woman rabbi.
  • 2009: Sara Hurwitz, first graduate of the Orthodox women's programme later called Yeshivat Maharat (New York).
  • Today women serve as rabbis across Reform, Liberal, Reconstructionist and Masorti Judaism.

Mission 6

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • Second-wave feminism (1960s–70s) asked why women were shut out of public life; many leading secular feminists were Jewish (e.g. Betty Friedan).
  • Jewish women asked the same of Judaism: no minyan, no aliyah, no ordination, no get without the husband.
  • 1971–72: Ezrat Nashim, a group of Conservative Jewish women, issued a "Call for Change": count women in the minyan, let them be witnesses, rabbis and cantors.
  • Rachel Adler, "The Jew Who Wasn't There" (1971): halakhah treats women as outside the full community (paraphrase;).
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: rethinking God-language, Torah and history from women's experience (Plaskow).
  • Impact: new rituals (naming ceremonies for girls, bat mitzvah, women's Rosh Chodesh groups), women scholars, and equal-rights campaigns beyond the synagogue.
Mission 6

Koltun; Moore and Kaplan

  • Elizabeth Koltun (ed.), The Jewish Woman: New Perspectives (1976): an early collection of Jewish feminist writing (paraphrase): women seek full participation in Jewish life and want to create new rituals, renewing tradition from within, not leaving it.
  • Marion A. Kaplan and Deborah Dash Moore (eds), Gender and Jewish History (2011) (paraphrase): gender is a key lens on Jewish history; women's roles in home, work, charity and community organisations shaped Jewish life but were long left out of the record.
  • Kaplan's research (The Jewish Feminist Movement in Germany, 1979) on the Jüdischer Frauenbund (League of Jewish Women, Germany, 1904) shows earlier Jewish feminists working within communal life.
  • Use them for development: Jewish feminism did not begin in 1972, and it seeks recognition of what women already did.

  • Maimonides, Laws of Kings 1:5 (paraphrase): from "king" (not "queen"), the rabbis learned that only men are appointed to positions of authority (serarah) in Israel.

Mission 6

Orthodox objections

  • Authority: Maimonides' rule against women in serarah.
  • Obligation: women are exempt from time-bound mitzvot like fixed prayer, so they cannot fulfil men's obligation as prayer leaders.
  • Community rules: a woman does not count in a minyan, and traditionally does not serve as a witness.
  • Modesty: concerns about kol isha (a woman's singing voice) and tzniut.
  • Tradition: no precedent in rabbinic history; fear of copying Reform.
  • Many Orthodox women answer: the roles are different but of equal dignity; the home and teaching are already leadership.
Mission 6

Orthodox women leaders today

  • Yoatzot halakhah (halakhic advisers), trained from 1997 at Nishmat in Jerusalem: answer women's questions on family purity laws.
  • Maharat title (from 2009); some graduates use Rabba or Rabbi. Rabbi Avi Weiss led this.
  • Opposition: the Rabbinical Council of America (2010, 2015) and the Orthodox Union (2017) ruled against ordaining or appointing women as clergy.
  • UK: Dina Brawer became the first British Orthodox woman ordained (2018, through Yeshivat Maharat); the United Synagogue does not appoint women rabbis.
  • Open Orthodox supporters: the modern rabbi is mainly a teacher and pastor, not the ancient serarah.

Mission 6Diversity

Responses across movements

  • Haredi: reject women rabbis and feminist change as against Torah; strong honour for women's home role.
  • Modern Orthodox: many welcome women scholars and advisers but stop at ordination; a minority (Open Orthodox) ordain women.
  • Masorti / Conservative: ordain women since 1985 (JTS decision 1983), arguing from halakhic development.
  • Reform and Liberal: full equality; already in 1922 the American Reform rabbis (CCAR) said women could not justly be denied ordination.
  • Feminist critique of all: equal access to roles is only the start; the tradition itself must change.

Mission 6Evaluate

Development or break?

  • Claim: The ordination of women is a genuine development of Judaism, not a break with it.
  • For: precedents (Deborah; learned women like Beruriah); Jonas argued from halakhic sources.
  • For: the modern rabbinate is teaching and care; women already teach Torah and give rulings (yoatzot).
  • Against: Maimonides' rule and the minyan and witness laws are long-standing halakhah.
  • Against: many Orthodox see it as importing secular feminism; the RCA and OU reject it.
  • Judgement criteria: what is the source of authority (halakhah, ethics, community)? What counts as continuity? Whose voice decides?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Messiah (mashiach, "anointed") is a human king of David's line; the prophets picture an age of peace, judgement, ingathering and knowledge of God (Isaiah 2, Micah 4, Isaiah 11).
  • Maimonides: the Messiah is known by rebuilding the Temple and gathering the exiles; nature is unchanged.
  • Orthodox Jews await a personal Messiah; Reform Jews look to a Messianic Age that people help to build.

Mission 2

  • Halakhah: a Jew is born of a Jewish mother or converted; birth status cannot be lost.
  • Faith, works and ritual shape how Jews live their identity; secular Jews show identity can be cultural as well as religious.
  • Brit milah on the eighth day and bar/bat mitzvah at 13 (12 for Orthodox girls) mark covenant and adult duty, not membership.

Mission 3

  • Judaism welcomes sincere converts but does not seek them; Ruth is the model.
  • Shulchan Arukh (Yoreh De'ah 268): questions, teaching, acceptance of the mitzvot, circumcision (men) and mikveh (all), before a beth din of three.
  • Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive movements differ on conversion and on patrilineal descent, so "Jewishness" depends on who is asked.

Mission 4

  • The home is a "small sanctuary"; Orthodox Judaism teaches complementary roles, Progressive Judaism equal roles; society, secular rights and readings of Torah drive the debate.
  • An agunah is "chained" when a husband refuses a get; remedies include prenups, the UK 2002 Act and court pressure; Reform and Liberal Jews avoid the problem.
  • Minyan: Orthodox counts ten men; most Masorti and all Reform and Liberal communities count women; Orthodox feminists (JOFA) seek change within halakhah.

Mission 5

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is a mitzvah: for procreation, and for closeness and pleasure (onah, the wife's right).
  • Celibacy is not an ideal: marriage is the norm, and rabbis marry.
  • Orthodox Jews forbid same-sex acts while many stress welcome for gay Jews; Reform and Liberal Jews affirm same-sex marriage.

Mission 6

  • Regina Jonas (ordained 1935, murdered 1944) was the first woman rabbi; Reform ordained Sally Priesand in 1972, Britain's Jackie Tabick and Julia Neuberger followed (1975, 1977), Masorti in 1985, a small Orthodox stream from 2009.
  • Jewish feminism grew from second-wave feminism and Jewish women's exclusion from minyan, ordination and divorce rights, and has changed ritual, scholarship and leadership.
  • Orthodox objections rest on Maimonides' rule on authority, minyan and witness laws; Reform rests on equality as a Jewish value.
