Mission 1

Doing before understanding

  • Judaism's core statement is a call, not a proof: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
  • At Sinai the people said: "All that the LORD hath spoken will we do, and obey." (Exodus 24:7, JPS 1917). The rabbis noted that doing comes before understanding (na'aseh ve-nishma).
  • So Jewish life centres on covenant and mitzvot (commandments), more than on a creed.
  • Yet Jewish thinkers have argued with philosophy for over a thousand years: Saadia Gaon (882–942), Maimonides (1138–1204), Spinoza (1632–1677), Mendelssohn (1729–1786), Buber (1878–1965).
  • This dialogue asks: how has philosophy shaped Jewish belief, and how has Jewish belief answered philosophy?
Mission 1

Maimonides: a creed for Judaism

  • Moses Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204), Commentary on the Mishnah (Sanhedrin, chapter 10): the Thirteen Principles of the Faith.
  • They include (paraphrase): God exists and is the cause of all; God is one; God is incorporeal (has no body); God is eternal; only God is to be worshipped; prophecy is true; Moses is the greatest prophet; the Torah is from heaven and will not be changed; God knows human deeds; God rewards and punishes; the Messiah will come; the dead will be resurrected.
  • For Maimonides, holding these is what makes a person part of Israel and gives a share in the world to come.
  • Many Jews recite them today in the hymn Yigdal and in the "Ani Ma'amin" ("I believe") form.
  • Impact: a philosopher turned Judaism's beliefs into propositions, which is belief that.
Mission 1

Proofs, or the God of Abraham?

  • Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190, II.1): proofs from Aristotle's physics (motion needs a first, unmoved, incorporeal mover) and from contingency (beings that can cease to exist need a necessary being). The second is close to Aquinas' Way 3 (Component 1); Aquinas read Maimonides.
  • Judah Halevi (c. 1075–1141), The Kuzari (c. 1140): the philosophers' God is a remote first cause; the God of Abraham is known through history: the Exodus and the public revelation at Sinai, witnessed by a whole people (compare Deuteronomy 4:33).
  • Component 1 links: Hume's and Russell's criticisms challenge Maimonides-style proofs; Halevi's approach is untouched by them, but faces the question of whether the historical testimony is reliable.
  • Many Jews see proofs as supporting a faith that rests on covenant, not as its foundation.

Mission 1

Can we speak of God at all?

  • The Tenakh uses human images: God walking in the garden (Genesis 3:8), as King and Father. Yet "man shall not see Me and live" (Exodus 33:20, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides' via negativa (Guide I.50–60): we can say only what God is not; positive attributes describe God's actions, not God's essence. Anthropomorphic verses are figurative (Thirteen Principles: God has no body).
  • Buber, I and Thou (1923): God is the Eternal Thou: one who can be addressed, not described.
  • Component 1 challenge: verification (Ayer) and falsification (Flew) say God-talk is meaningless. Responses: the via negativa makes no empirical claim; Wittgenstein's language games: meaning lies in use within a form of life, such as prayer and observance.
  • Meaningful for whom? "The LORD is one" means a way of life to a practising Jew; to an outsider it may look like an unverifiable claim.
Mission 1

Evil and the justice of God

  • Jewish texts argue with God: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917). Job is answered not with an explanation but with God's greatness (Job 38–41).
  • After the Holocaust:
  • Ignaz Maybaum (1897–1976), The Face of God after Auschwitz (1965): the Holocaust as churban (destruction), Israel as a remnant and suffering servant through whom the world moves forward (paraphrase). God's justice and covenant affirmed, at great cost.
  • Richard Rubenstein (1924–2021), After Auschwitz (1966): after Auschwitz the God who acts in history is "dead" for us and the covenant of election cannot be held (paraphrase). Judaism continues as community and ritual.
  • Component 1 links: Hick's soul-making theodicy and Griffin's process theodicy (God not omnipotent; compare Hans Jonas, The Concept of God after Auschwitz, 1984).
  • Coherence: can an omnipotent, just God be squared with the Holocaust? Jewish thinkers range from affirmation to protest to rejection.
Mission 1

Self, death and afterlife: changing beliefs

  • Early Tenakh: the dead go down to She'ol, a shadowy place: "The dead praise not the LORD" (Psalm 115:17, JPS 1917). 1 Samuel 28: Samuel is brought up from the dead.
  • The person as a unity: "the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul" (Genesis 2:7, JPS 1917).
  • Later Tenakh: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake" (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917): resurrection.
  • Rabbinic and Maimonides: resurrection is a Principle; but Maimonides' highest afterlife (olam ha-ba) is the soul's intellectual knowledge of God, without body.
  • Pittsburgh Platform (Reform, 1885): affirmed the soul's immortality but rejected bodily resurrection and literal heaven and hell (paraphrase).
  • Kabbalah: some teach gilgul (reincarnation of souls).
  • Component 1 links: Descartes' dualism fits immortality of the soul; the unity view of Genesis 2:7 fits resurrection and raises the personal-identity problem (is the raised person the same person?).

Mission 1

Sources of authority and reason

  • Tenakh (Written Torah) and Talmud (Oral Torah), interpreted by rabbis.
  • Orthodox: the Torah is from heaven (Torah min ha-shamayim), complete and unchangeable. Reform/Liberal: inspired, but written by human beings; open to reinterpretation.
  • Reason inside the tradition: "It is not in heaven" (Deuteronomy 30:12, JPS 1917): in the Talmud (Bava Metzia 59b) the rabbis overrule a heavenly voice and decide by majority reasoning.
  • Saadia Gaon, Beliefs and Opinions (933): reason and revelation reach the same truths. Maimonides, Guide II.25: had Aristotle proved the world eternal, he would read Genesis figuratively. Reason can shape interpretation.
  • Challenge: Spinoza, Theological-Political Treatise (1670): the Bible is a human, historical book; reason, not scripture, gives truth. Excommunicated by Amsterdam's Jewish community (1656); later a forerunner of biblical criticism.
Mission 1

Belief in, or belief that?

  • Belief that: accepting propositions as true ("that God exists", "that the dead will rise"). Maimonides' Principles.
  • Belief in: trust and commitment to someone. Hebrew emunah means faithfulness and trust: "he believed in the LORD" (Genesis 15:6, JPS 1917).
  • Buber, Two Types of Faith (1951): Jewish emunah is trust in God within a relationship; he contrasts it with pistis, faith as acknowledging that something is true, which he linked with Paul's Christianity (paraphrase).
  • Mendelssohn, Jerusalem (1783): Judaism has no compulsory dogmas; it is revealed law, while its truths about God are open to reason (paraphrase).
  • Debate today: must a Jew believe anything? Orthodox thinkers stress the Principles; many others stress practice and trust.
  • Philosophy's relevance: it tests "belief that" with arguments; "belief in" may survive even when proofs fail.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Philosophy has challenged Jewish belief more than it has supported it."

  • For: Spinoza's critique of scripture; Hume's and Russell's criticisms of proofs; verification and falsification; Rubenstein's "death of God" after Auschwitz.
  • Against: Saadia and Maimonides used philosophy to defend and purify belief (one, incorporeal God); the via negativa answers anthropomorphism; Buber's dialogue philosophy renewed faith for many; Mendelssohn defended Judaism's place in a rational age.
  • Another view: because Judaism centres on practice and emunah, attacks on "belief that" reach less of it than they would reach a creed-centred faith.
  • Judge it by: Is Judaism mainly beliefs or mainly a covenant life? Did philosophy change belief, or only its expression? For whom (Orthodox, Reform, secular Jews) is the claim true?

Mission 2

Sinai and prophecy: experience in Judaism

  • The founding experience is public: "Did ever a people hear the voice of God speaking out of the midst of the fire, as thou hast heard, and live?" (Deuteronomy 4:33, JPS 1917)
  • Corporate, not private: the whole people at Sinai (Exodus 19). Compare corporate experience in Component 1.
  • Prophecy: Moses spoke with God "mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:8, JPS 1917); Elijah heard "a still small voice" (1 Kings 19:12, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides (Guide II.32–48): prophecy is an overflow from God to the intellect and then the imagination of a person prepared by learning and moral perfection. A philosophical account of a religious experience. Compare imaginative and intellectual visions (Component 1).
  • Rabbinic teaching: prophecy ended with Haggai, Zechariah and Malachi; later experience is less authoritative than the Torah.
Mission 2

Encounter, awe and union

  • Martin Buber, I and Thou (1923): God is met in the I–Thou relationship, in and through relationships with people and the world: the Eternal Thou. Not a special vision but a way of meeting.
  • Abraham Joshua Heschel (1907–1972), God in Search of Man (1955): "radical amazement" at the world opens a person to God (short phrase); God seeks humans first.
  • Otto's numinous (Component 1): Isaiah's vision: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3, JPS 1917): awe and fear before the wholly other.
  • Kabbalah and Hasidism: devekut (cleaving to God) in prayer, close to the mystical experience described by James and Stace, though most Jewish mystics keep God and the self distinct.
Mission 2

Testing experience

  • Philosophical challenges (Component 1): experiences cannot be checked independently; science explains them (brain states, psychology); Freud: wish-fulfilment.
  • Swinburne's principles of credulity and testimony: accept how things seem and what others report, unless there is reason to doubt. Many Jews would apply this to the testimony of Sinai handed down.
  • Judaism's own test is the Torah: a prophet whose sign comes true but who says "Let us go after other gods" must not be followed, for "the LORD your God putteth you to proof" (Deuteronomy 13:3, JPS 1917; 13:4 in Hebrew numbering).
  • So experience is judged by covenant and community, not the other way round. Halakhah is not decided by visions (compare Bava Metzia 59b).
  • Impact: this caution made Jewish thought less dependent on private experience than some Christian mysticism.

Mission 2

Miracles in Jewish thought

  • At the Red Sea "the LORD caused the sea to go back by a strong east wind all the night" (Exodus 14:21, JPS 1917): a natural means, at God's moment.
  • Mishnah Avot 5:6 (paraphrase): ten things were created at twilight on the eve of the first Sabbath, among them the mouth of the earth that swallowed Korah and the mouth of Balaam's donkey. Miracles built into creation from the start.
  • Maimonides follows this: God does not change nature's order case by case; miracles were planned within it.
  • Nachmanides (Ramban, 1194–1270): besides open miracles, there are hidden miracles in every event of daily life.
  • In the daily Amidah, Jews thank God for the miracles that are with them every day. Hanukkah: the oil that lasted eight days (Talmud, Shabbat 21b), alongside the military victory.
Mission 2

Dialogue with Hume and Wiles

  • David Hume (1711–1776), Enquiry (1748), Section X: "no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (Hume, Enquiry X).
  • Jewish replies: miracles are not the foundation of faith. Maimonides (Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 8:1, paraphrase): Israel believed Moses because of what they themselves experienced at Sinai, not because of his signs. Natural-means accounts (14:21; Avot 5:6) avoid the "violation" definition Hume attacks.
  • Maurice Wiles (1923–2005), God's Action in the World (1986): an interventionist God who worked some miracles but did not prevent Auschwitz or Hiroshima would be arbitrary; God's one act is the whole of creation (paraphrase).
  • Jewish replies: the Auschwitz question is sharpest for Jews. Avot 5:6 and Nachmanides' hidden miracles are close to Wiles' single creative act; but most Jews still affirm that God acts in history (Exodus, Purim, the return to Zion).

Mission 2

Science and Judaism

  • Maimonides (Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 2:2): contemplating God's works leads to love and awe of God. Study of nature is a religious act.
  • Evolution (Darwin, 1859): different Jewish responses:
  • Literal (some Haredi teachers): six days and a young earth.
  • Harmonising: Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935) welcomed evolution as a picture of creation rising towards perfection; Genesis teaches meaning, not biology.
  • Concordist: Gerald Schroeder (b. 1938), Genesis and the Big Bang (1990): by relativity, six days from creation's own frame equal about 15 billion years from ours (paraphrase).
  • Reform and most Modern Orthodox: no conflict: science asks how; Torah asks why and how to live.
  • The Big Bang: many welcome a universe with a beginning as fitting creation (Genesis 1:1); Nachmanides spoke of creation from a single tiny point.

Mission 2

Truth claims of other religions

  • Chosen people: chosen for responsibility, not superiority: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will visit upon you all your iniquities" (Amos 3:2, JPS 1917). A "light of the nations" (Isaiah 42:6, JPS 1917).
  • The righteous of the nations: non-Jews who keep the seven Noahide laws (Talmud, Sanhedrin 56a) have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2; Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Kings 8:11). So Judaism does not seek converts.
  • Maimonides on Christianity and Islam (Kings 11:4, uncensored text, paraphrase): they prepare the world for the Messiah by spreading knowledge of Torah and commandments, though he held Torah's truth to be complete.
  • "Let all the peoples walk each one in the name of its god, but we will walk in the name of the LORD our God" (Micah 4:5, JPS 1917).
  • Dialogue with Hick's pluralism: many Jews already hold an inclusive hope for the righteous; but Orthodox teaching keeps the unique truth of Torah, while some Reform thinkers come close to pluralism. Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Dignity of Difference (2002), argued that God is found in difference; he revised passages after Orthodox criticism.

Mission 2

How coherent are the beliefs?

  • Foreknowledge and freedom: "All is foreseen, yet freedom of choice is given" (Rabbi Akiva, Mishnah Avot 3:15, paraphrase). Torah commands choice: "therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917).
  • Maimonides: God's knowledge is not like ours, so it does not compel; critics (Gersonides, 1288–1344) said God knows the possibilities, not each free choice. Compare Boethius in Component 1.
  • Miracle and natural law: Avot 5:6 makes miracles part of the natural order, so they do not break it; but then are they still acts of God in history?
  • Science and creation: literal and concordist readings must fit Genesis to science; non-literal readings fit more easily but may seem to give up the plain meaning.
  • Chosenness and universal justice: can one people be chosen by a God who is "good to all" (Psalm 145:9)? Jewish answer: chosen to serve.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Jewish beliefs about miracles and experience are reasonable."

  • For: God acts through nature (14:21; Avot 5:6), so no conflict with science; Sinai is presented as a public, corporate event; the Torah tests experience (Deuteronomy 13), guarding against self-deception; Swinburne's credulity and testimony support trusting tradition.
  • Against: Hume: testimony to the Exodus is ancient and from interested witnesses; historians find little outside evidence for the Exodus; Wiles: miracles at the Red Sea but not at Auschwitz make God arbitrary; experience can be explained psychologically.
  • Another view: for many Jews these beliefs are "belief in": trust lived in memory and ritual (Pesach, Hanukkah), not hypotheses to prove.
  • Judge it by: "Reasonable" as based on reason, or as consistent with reason? Which Jewish account (literal, hidden, natural)? For whom is the belief meaningful?

Mission 3

The Jewish moral toolkit

  • Mitzvot: 613 commandments of the written Torah, interpreted in the oral Torah. Halakhah (AQA: Halakah) = Jewish law, "the way to walk".
  • Imitating God: "walk in His ways" (Deuteronomy 28:9). The Talmud: as God clothes the naked, visits the sick and comforts mourners, so should you (Sotah 14a, paraphrase).
  • Pikuach nephesh: saving a life overrides almost every mitzvah, because Jews are to "live by" the laws (Leviticus 18:5; Yoma 85b).
  • Human dignity: every person is made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27); the rabbis call this kavod ha-beriyot (respect for people).
  • Justice and love: "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20, JPS 1917); "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself" (Leviticus 19:18, JPS 1917).
  • Keep this toolkit in view: each theory in this mission picks out a different part of it.
Mission 3

Kant: where Judaism seems to agree

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals (1785): morality is duty, known by reason; consequences do not make an act right.
  • Agreement 1: a mitzvah binds whether or not it pays off. Many are kept with no visible benefit.
  • Agreement 2: Kant's humanity formula (never treat a person merely as a means) echoes the image of God and kavod ha-beriyot.
  • Agreement 3: Hillel (1st century BCE) summed up the Torah for a would-be convert: what is hateful to you, do not do to your neighbour; the rest is commentary (Shabbat 31a, paraphrase). It looks like universalising.
  • But Kant himself said this "golden rule" is too weak: it leaves out duties to oneself and duties of kindness (Groundwork, footnote to Section II).
Mission 3Read the source

Autonomy or heteronomy?

Kant, Groundwork (1785), Section II, tr. T. K. Abbott (1873):

> "If the will seeks the law which is to determine it anywhere else than in the fitness of its maxims to be universal laws of its own dictation … there always results heteronomy."

  • Autonomy: the rational will gives the moral law to itself.
  • Heteronomy: the law comes from outside the will: from desire, reward, or another's command.

Q1 Why might Kant call obeying a command because God gave it heteronomous?

Show answer

The will does not give itself the law; it takes it from an outside authority.

Q2 Could a Jew who keeps the mitzvot also see them as rational?

Show answer

Yes: e.g. Hermann Cohen, next screen, and the rabbis' search for reasons for the commandments, ta'amei ha-mitzvot.

Mission 3

Kant's challenge and three Jewish replies

  • Kant, Religion within the Bounds of Mere Reason (1793): Judaism is a set of statutory, political laws, not a moral religion (paraphrase). A deep challenge.
  • Reply 1: law is training in obedience to God. The Talmud: greater is one who is commanded and does than one who acts uncommanded (Kiddushin 31a, paraphrase). The opposite of Kant's ranking.
  • Reply 2: Judaism is the religion of reason. Hermann Cohen (1842–1918), Religion of Reason out of the Sources of Judaism (1919): Jewish ethical monotheism is the source of the universal ethics Kant described.
  • Reply 3: mitzvot are not ethics at all. Yeshayahu Leibowitz (1903–1994): the commandments exist to serve God; judging them by human ethics makes humans, not God, the measure.
  • Impact on practice: Reform's Pittsburgh Platform (1885) said "we accept as binding only its moral laws", keeping only ceremonies that elevate life.

Mission 3

Bentham: consequences inside the law

  • Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789): right = what produces the greatest happiness; measure pleasure and pain (the hedonic calculus).
  • Judaism does weigh consequences:
  • Pikuach nephesh: break Shabbat to save a life; the law exists for life (Yoma 85b).
  • For the good of society (mipnei tikkun ha-olam): the rabbis changed rules whose results harmed people (Mishnah Gittin 4).
  • Hillel's prozbul: debts were cancelled in the seventh year, so lenders stopped lending. Hillel let loans pass to a court so the poor could borrow again (Mishnah Shevi'it 10:3).
  • But the reasoning is inside the law: consequences guide how it is applied; they do not replace it.
Mission 3

Where the calculation stops

  • Yehareg ve'al ya'avor: "be killed rather than transgress". Three sins may not be done even to save your life: idolatry, murder, forbidden sexual relations (Sanhedrin 74a).
  • On murder the Talmud asks: who says your blood is redder than his? (Sanhedrin 74a, paraphrase). One life may not be weighed against another.
  • If attackers say "hand over one of you or we kill you all", the group may not hand over a person (Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 5:5, from Jerusalem Talmud Terumot 8:4).
  • Bentham's calculus could favour handing one over. Halakhah forbids it: a deontological limit.
  • Criticism from a utilitarian: the rule causes more deaths. Jewish reply: every life is a whole world; it is not a number to trade.
Mission 3

Virtue: character through commandments

  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics: virtue is a settled character, and "by doing just actions we come to be just" (Book II, tr. Chase).
  • Jewish ideal of middot (character traits): Pirkei Avot asks, who is mighty? One who masters his inclination (Avot 4:1, paraphrase).
  • Maimonides (1138–1204), Mishneh Torah, Laws of Character (Hilkhot De'ot) 1:4, and Eight Chapters (c. 1168), ch. 4: follow the middle path between extremes, as Aristotle did.
  • But Maimonides departs from Aristotle: go to the extreme in humility and keep far from anger (Hilkhot De'ot 2:3).
  • The Mussar movement of Israel Salanter (1810–1883): daily study and self-examination to refine character.
  • So mitzvot and character are not rivals: practice of the law is meant to form the person.

Mission 3Diversity

Is there an ethic beyond halakhah?

  • Aharon Lichtenstein (1933–2015), "Does Jewish Tradition Recognize an Ethic Independent of Halakha?" (1975): yes, in part. The Torah asks for lifnim mi-shurat ha-din (going beyond the letter of the law) and to "do that which is right and good" (Deuteronomy 6:18, JPS 1917).
  • Leibowitz (Modern Orthodox, Israel): no ethic outside the commandments is religious; the mitzvot alone are the service of God.
  • Most Orthodox thinkers: ethics is part of halakhah, and halakhah has the final word.
  • Reform and Liberal Judaism: ethics comes first; ritual law is a matter of informed choice.
  • Masorti / Conservative: halakhah binds but develops, and ethical concerns such as human dignity shape rulings.
  • So "Jewish ethics" names more than one structure: a key point for any essay on this topic.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Jewish ethics is best described as deontological."

  • For: mitzvot bind as commands of God whatever the outcome; the three cardinal sins are absolute; the Talmud praises the commanded person.
  • Against (consequential): pikuach nephesh and tikkun ha-olam show rules applied for their effects on life and society.
  • Against (character): the aim is a holy person (Leviticus 19:2): middot, Mussar, imitating God.
  • Against (not Kantian): the law's authority is God, not autonomous reason, so even "deontological" fits only loosely.
  • Judge it by: Which features are central and which exceptions? Does the answer differ between Orthodox and Reform? Can a covenant ethic fit modern categories at all?
Mission 3Worked answer

A synoptic paragraph

Question: "Jewish ethics cannot be reconciled with utilitarianism." Evaluate this view.

  • Point: Jewish law contains consequential reasoning (AO1): pikuach nephesh lets Jews break Shabbat to save a life (Yoma 85b).
  • Dialogue: this resembles Bentham, who judged acts by their effects on happiness.
  • Critical step (AO2): the resemblance is limited: murder may not be done even to save many (Sanhedrin 74a), and the measure is life under God's law, not pleasure.
  • Link back: so consequences work within a deontological frame, not as its foundation.
  • AQA 25-mark tip: one sustained line of argument; use Component 1 theories accurately; weigh views, don't list them.

Mission 4

From ancient text to new case

  • New questions go to a posek (decider of Jewish law), who writes a responsum (teshuvah): a written answer reasoning from Torah, Talmud, codes and earlier responsa.
  • Method: find the nearest principle or precedent, then argue by analogy. Saving life (pikuach nephesh) and human dignity weigh heavily.
  • Orthodox: halakhah binds; responsa decide.
  • Masorti / Conservative: halakhah binds but develops; the Committee on Jewish Law and Standards (CJLS) issues rulings.
  • Reform / Liberal: tradition guides, but ethics and individual conscience decide (informed choice).
  • On each issue below, notice which theory from Mission 3 the reasoning most resembles.
Mission 4Read the source

A life not yet born

Mishnah Oholot 7:6 (paraphrase):

  • If a woman is in mortal danger in childbirth, the foetus is removed, even in pieces, because her life comes first.
  • Once its head (or greater part) is born, it may not be touched: one life is not set aside for another.
  • The Talmud adds: in the first 40 days an embryo is "mere water" (Yevamot 69b, paraphrase).

Q1 When does the foetus gain the full protection of a person?

Show answer

At birth: once the head or greater part has emerged.

Q2 Why is this not a utilitarian calculation?

Show answer

After birth, two lives are equal and may not be traded, whatever the outcome.

Mission 4

Abortion, embryos and designer babies

  • Abortion: not murder in halakhah, but serious. Orthodox: permitted to save the mother's life or health; some authorities allow it for severe foetal illness (e.g. Rabbi Eliezer Waldenberg), others are stricter (Rabbi Moshe Feinstein). Reform: the woman's decision, made with guidance.
  • Embryo research: spare IVF embryos outside the womb, before 40 days, have low status; most authorities permit research that may save life.
  • Cloning: debated. Some (e.g. J. David Bleich, b. 1936) urge caution about identity and family; others see therapeutic cloning as healing.
  • Designer babies: embryo screening to avoid serious disease (e.g. Tay-Sachs) is widely accepted; choosing traits for preference is widely opposed.
  • Genetic engineering: humans may heal and "subdue" the earth (Genesis 1:28). Most rule that gene transfer is not the forbidden mixing of kinds (Leviticus 19:19). Warning against hubris: a virtue concern.
Mission 4

End of life and capital punishment

  • A goses (dying person) is a living person in every respect; hastening death is forbidden (tractate Semachot 1).
  • But one may remove an impediment to dying (the Rema's example: a woodchopper's noise keeping a soul from leaving), Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 339:1, gloss.
  • So: active euthanasia and assisted suicide forbidden in Orthodox and most Masorti and Reform rulings; withholding burdensome treatment often allowed. Israel's Dying Patient Act (2005) follows this line.
  • Contrast: Bentham would ask only whether ending life reduces suffering; Kant thought suicide treats one's own person merely as a means.
  • Capital punishment: the Torah prescribes it, but the rabbis made it almost impossible. A court that executes once in seven years is called destructive; some say once in seventy (Mishnah Makkot 1:10). Israel's civilian courts have carried out only one execution (Eichmann, 1962).

Mission 4

Animals: life, death and suffering

  • Tza'ar ba'alei chayim: causing animals needless suffering is forbidden (Bava Metzia 32b). Help a struggling donkey, even your enemy's (Exodus 23:5); "Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn" (Deuteronomy 25:4, JPS 1917).
  • Food: meat permitted after the Flood (Genesis 9:3), by shechitah (a swift cut by a trained shochet). Rav Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935) saw vegetarianism as the ideal of the messianic future. Many Jews question intensive farming on tza'ar ba'alei chayim grounds.
  • Experiments: whatever is needed for medicine is permitted (Rema, Even Ha-Ezer 5:14), but not cruelty for no need.
  • Blood sports: Ezekiel Landau (1713–1793), Noda BiYehudah, forbade hunting for sport: killing for pleasure is cruel and not the Jewish way.
  • Organs: pig heart valves and organs are permitted; only eating pork is forbidden, and pikuach nephesh applies.
Mission 4

Animals in dialogue

  • Bentham: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (Introduction, ch. 17, note).
  • Judaism agrees that animal suffering counts in itself, but not that animal and human lives weigh equally: humans may use animals for real needs.
  • Kant (Lectures on Ethics): only indirect duties to animals; cruelty matters because it hardens people towards humans (paraphrase). Halakhah's duties are direct, though the rabbis also say cruelty damages character.
  • Virtue: the bird's-nest law (Deuteronomy 22:6–7) is read by Nachmanides (1194–1270) as training in compassion.

Mission 4

Theft and lying

  • "Thou shalt not steal" (Exodus 20:15, JPS 1917); "Ye shall not steal; neither shall ye deal falsely, nor lie one to another" (Leviticus 19:11, JPS 1917).
  • Geneivat da'at ("stealing the mind"): creating a false impression is a form of theft (Chullin 94a).
  • To save a life you may take another's property, but you must repay (Bava Kamma 60b): pikuach nephesh overrides, but the owner's right remains.
  • Truth and peace: one may alter the truth for the sake of peace (Yevamot 65b), as God did when reporting Sarah's words to Abraham (Genesis 18:12–13).
  • Hillel and Shammai on praising a plain bride: Shammai says describe her as she is; Hillel says call every bride lovely and gracious (Ketubot 16b–17a). The law follows Hillel.
  • Kant ("On a Supposed Right to Lie", 1797): never lie, even to a murderer at the door. Halakhah: lying to save life is required.

Mission 4

Marriage

  • "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18, JPS 1917). Marriage is a mitzvah and holy: kiddushin means "sanctification".
  • The ketubah (marriage contract) protects the wife's rights; divorce by a get is permitted (Deuteronomy 24:1). The problem of the agunah (a "chained" wife) is taught in an earlier lesson.
  • Goals: companionship, shalom bayit (peace in the home), children ("be fruitful, and multiply", Genesis 1:28).
  • Kant (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797) defined marriage as a lifelong contract for mutual use of each other's sexual capacities, to keep each as an end (paraphrase). Judaism also uses contract, but within covenant and holiness.
  • Virtue: marriage as the place where love, patience and generosity are trained.
Mission 4Diversity

Same-sex relationships and transgender matters

  • Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 forbids sex between men; the prohibition is on acts, not orientation. A 2010 Statement of Principles by Modern Orthodox rabbis called for welcome and dignity for gay Jews, without changing halakhah.
  • Masorti / Conservative: CJLS ruling (2006) by Dorff, Nevins and Reisner, citing kavod ha-beriyot (human dignity), permitted gay and lesbian rabbis and same-sex unions, while keeping the narrowest reading of Leviticus 18:22. Other rulings were also accepted.
  • Reform / Liberal: full equality; same-sex weddings in synagogues.
  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Engendering Judaism (1998): a covenant of partners (brit ahuvim) in place of acquisition-based kiddushin, open to same-sex couples (paraphrase).
  • Transgender: Orthodox authorities debate (Deuteronomy 22:5 on clothing; surgery; status after transition). Reform (URJ resolution, 2015) and Conservative (2016 resolution) affirm transgender people's full inclusion.
  • Dialogue: Kantian dignity and autonomy and Bentham's attention to harm have shaped progressive rulings; Orthodox thinkers reply that dignity is defined by God's law, not by autonomy.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Halakhah gives better guidance on these issues than utilitarianism."

  • For: fixed limits protect the vulnerable (the dying, the unborn after birth, the one person a crowd might sacrifice); centuries of case law; clear answers.
  • For: Bentham's calculus can justify killing or lying whenever the sums favour it.
  • Against: utilitarianism counts animal suffering and harm to people directly, and adapts quickly to new technology.
  • Against: halakhah gives different answers in different movements, and Orthodox rulings on sexuality are criticised as causing harm.
  • Judge it by: Clarity? Protection of every person? Flexibility for new cases? Whose authority decides?

Mission 5

Wealth: a blessing with duties

  • Wealth is not sinful; it is a gift held in trust from God. Who is rich? One who rejoices in his portion (Avot 4:1, paraphrase).
  • Tzedakah means "justice", not "charity": giving to the poor is owed. "Thou shalt surely open thy hand unto thy poor and needy brother" (Deuteronomy 15:11, JPS 1917).
  • A Jewish court could even compel giving (Bava Batra 8b).
  • But do not give away more than a fifth, or you may become poor yourself (Ketubot 50a).
  • Maimonides' eight levels of giving (Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14): the highest is helping someone become self-supporting (a loan, a partnership, a job).
Mission 5

Wealth in dialogue

  • Bentham: give where it adds most happiness; a pound means more to the poor than to the rich. Tzedakah also aims at relieving need, but is owed as justice, not calculated.
  • Kant: kindness to others is a wide (imperfect) duty: required, but you choose when and how. Halakhah is more exact: fixed obligations (a tenth is the custom; a fifth is the upper limit).
  • Virtue: Maimonides says giving one coin a thousand times trains generosity better than giving a thousand coins once (Commentary on the Mishnah, Avot 3:15, paraphrase). Compare Aristotle: we become generous by acting generously.
  • So on wealth, Judaism mixes duty, outcome (self-sufficiency) and character.

Mission 5

Tolerance

  • Judaism traditionally does not seek converts. Non-Jews need only keep the seven Noahide laws (Sanhedrin 56a) to be righteous.
  • The pious of the nations of the world have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase). Maimonides adds: if they keep the laws as commanded by God (Laws of Kings 8:11).
  • Darkhei shalom ("ways of peace"): support the non-Jewish poor with the Jewish poor (Gittin 61a).
  • "The stranger … thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, JPS 1917).
  • Tolerance within Judaism is harder: Orthodox bodies do not recognise most non-Orthodox conversions or rabbis. Pluralists ask for respect without full agreement.
  • Dialogue: J. S. Mill's liberal tolerance rests on autonomy; Jewish tolerance rests on the image of God and covenant, so it can tolerate others without saying all views are equally true.
Mission 5

Freedom of religious expression

  • Dina de-malkhuta dina: "the law of the land is the law" (Shmuel, Nedarim 28a). Jews obey just civil law, but not a law that forces them to break the Torah.
  • UK: Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights (Human Rights Act 1998) protects religious practice, within limits for others' rights.
  • Shechitah: UK law allows slaughter without stunning for religious communities; Denmark banned it (2014), and the EU's top court allowed a Flemish ban (2020). Animal-welfare arguments are often utilitarian; Jewish bodies argue shechitah is humane and a religious right.
  • Brit milah (circumcision): a German court (Cologne, 2012) called it bodily harm; Germany then passed a law protecting it (2012).
  • JFS case (UK Supreme Court, 2009): admission by matrilineal descent was ruled ethnic discrimination. A clash between equality law and halakhic definitions of who is a Jew.

Mission 5Read the source

Free to choose

> "I have set before thee life and death, the blessing and the curse; therefore choose life, that thou mayest live, thou and thy seed" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917)

  • Rabbi Akiva: everything is foreseen, yet freedom of choice is given (Avot 3:15, paraphrase).
  • The Talmud: everything is in the hands of Heaven except the fear of Heaven (Berakhot 33b, paraphrase).
  • Maimonides (Laws of Repentance 5:1–4): free will is a pillar of the Torah. If God decreed our acts, commandments, reward and punishment would make no sense.
  • Teshuvah (repentance, "return") presupposes freedom: you can change.

Q1 Why would "choose life" make no sense without free will?

Show answer

A command to choose assumes the choice is really ours.

Q2 What tension does Akiva's saying hold together?

Show answer

God's foreknowledge and human freedom.

Mission 5Diversity

Free will and responsibility in dialogue

  • Libertarian: Maimonides: humans are truly free; God's knowledge does not cause our acts (and is beyond our understanding).
  • Near-compatibilist: Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410/11), Light of the Lord (1410): our choices have causes, yet we are responsible because we will them; reward and punishment follow as consequences.
  • Hard determinist challenge: Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677), expelled by the Amsterdam Jewish community (1656): in Ethics (1677) free will is an illusion of people unaware of the causes of their actions.
  • Conditions of responsibility in halakhah: adulthood (13 for boys, 12 for girls); sound mind (a shoteh, mentally incapable person, is exempt); no duress (the Merciful One exempts one who is forced: Avodah Zarah 54a, paraphrase).
  • Compare Component 1: halakhah links responsibility to understanding right and wrong and to freedom, like libertarians and compatibilists.
  • Hard problem: Pharaoh's heart, which God "hardened" (Exodus 9:12). Maimonides: refusing repentance was Pharaoh's punishment for earlier free sins.

Mission 5

Conscience in Judaism

  • Biblical Hebrew has no word for "conscience"; the lev (heart) knows and chooses. "The spirit of man is the lamp of the LORD, searching all the inward parts" (Proverbs 20:27, JPS 1917).
  • Conscience is formed by Torah: study and mitzvot train the heart (the yetzer hatov, good inclination).
  • Yet moral sense can question: Abraham to God over Sodom, "shall not the Judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • The Hebrew midwives "feared God, and did not as the king of Egypt commanded them" (Exodus 1:17, JPS 1917): conscience against a ruler.
  • "Thou shalt do that which is right and good" (Deuteronomy 6:18, JPS 1917): Nachmanides reads it as a call to go beyond the letter of the law.
Mission 5Diversity

How far can conscience overrule?

  • Orthodox: conscience must be trained by and submit to halakhah; where they clash, the law is the better guide. In Haredi communities, da'at Torah gives weight to great rabbis' guidance.
  • Leibowitz: the Akedah (binding of Isaac, Genesis 22) shows faith placing God's command above human moral feeling.
  • Kant (The Conflict of the Faculties, 1798): Abraham should have answered that killing his son is certainly wrong, but that the voice is God's is not certain (paraphrase). Conscience and reason test any command.
  • Reform: Eugene Borowitz (1924–2016), Renewing the Covenant (1991): covenantal autonomy: the self decides, but as a Jew in relationship with God and the Jewish people, not as Kant's isolated reason.
  • Component 1 links: Aquinas (conscience as reason applying principles), Butler (conscience as supreme authority), Freud (conscience as superego, learned guilt). Orthodox views are closest to Aquinas; Freud challenges any religious authority for conscience.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Other ethical perspectives have strengthened Jewish ethics more than they have threatened it."

  • For: Aristotle shaped Maimonides' ethics of character; Kant shaped Cohen and Reform's ethical monotheism; dignity and harm arguments informed new rulings on sexuality and gender.
  • For: dialogue forced Jews to state the reasons for commandments clearly.
  • Against: Kant's autonomy challenges the authority of revelation itself; Reform's "binding only its moral laws" set aside much of halakhah.
  • Against: secular rights and utilitarian welfare arguments now challenge shechitah, milah and Jewish school admissions, and in some countries restrict them.
  • Judge it by: Strengthened for whom (Orthodox or Reform)? Does adapting to outside ethics weaken the sources of authority (Torah, Talmud)? Can Judaism learn from others without giving up its covenant?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Maimonides made a creed (Thirteen Principles) and used Aristotelian proofs and the via negativa; Halevi grounded faith in Israel's history; Buber spoke of the Eternal Thou.
  • Afterlife beliefs moved from She'ol to resurrection (Daniel 12:2) and immortality of the soul (Maimonides, Pittsburgh 1885), linked to God's justice; the Holocaust sharpened the problem of evil (Maybaum, Rubenstein).
  • Authority: Tenakh and Talmud interpreted by reason (Saadia, Maimonides), challenged by Spinoza; faith as emunah ("belief in") vs creed ("belief that").

Mission 2

  • Jewish religious experience centres on public revelation (Sinai) and prophecy, explained philosophically by Maimonides and as encounter by Buber and Heschel; the Torah tests experience (Deuteronomy 13).
  • Miracles are often God acting through nature (Exodus 14:21; Avot 5:6; Nachmanides' hidden miracles), which shapes responses to Hume and Wiles; science is read as study of God's works, with literal, harmonising and concordist (Schroeder) views of Genesis.
  • Judaism holds the unique truth of Torah with hope for the righteous of all nations (Noahide laws); coherence questions include foreknowledge and freedom (Avot 3:15).

Mission 3

  • Key principles: mitzvot and halakhah, imitating God, pikuach nephesh, human dignity, justice and love of neighbour.
  • Kant: agreement on duty and dignity; conflict over autonomy (Kant on Judaism; Cohen, Leibowitz, Reform replies). Bentham: consequences guide halakhah (pikuach nephesh, prozbul) but not beyond the three cardinal sins. Virtue: middot, Maimonides' middle path (with exceptions), Mussar.
  • Jewish ethics is mainly deontological in structure, with consequential and character elements; Orthodox and Reform weigh these differently.

Mission 4

  • Rabbis apply principles and precedent to new cases (responsa); Orthodox, Masorti and Reform weigh law and ethics differently.
  • Life and death: abortion to save the mother; early embryos have low status; no active euthanasia but impediments may be removed; capital punishment nearly abolished. Animals: no needless suffering, but real human needs allowed.
  • Theft and lying: forbidden, but truth may bend for peace and must give way to save life (contrast Kant). Marriage: holy covenant. Sexuality and gender: the sharpest differences between Orthodox and progressive Judaism.

Mission 5

  • Wealth is a trust: tzedakah is owed as justice, limited to a fifth, aimed at self-sufficiency. Tolerance rests on the Noahide laws and darkhei shalom; freedom of religious expression meets dina de-malkhuta dina and modern rights law (shechitah, milah, JFS).
  • Free will: "choose life"; Akiva's paradox; Maimonides (libertarian), Crescas (near-compatibilist), Spinoza (determinist). Responsibility needs adulthood, sound mind, no duress.
  • Conscience is formed by Torah; Orthodox subordinate it to halakhah, Reform speaks of covenantal autonomy; Kant, Aquinas, Butler and Freud sharpen the debate.
