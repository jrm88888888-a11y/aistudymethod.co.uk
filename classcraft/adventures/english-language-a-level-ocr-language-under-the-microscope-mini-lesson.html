Language in context · mode, field, tenorScreen 2

Mode is a continuum

  • Typical speech: spontaneous, interactive, ephemeral, shared context (this, over there), non-fluency.
  • Typical writing: planned, edited, permanent, self-contained, denser noun phrases.
  • Many texts sit between. A group chat is written but synchronous (near real time), interactive and informal, with speech-like ellipsis and emoji for tone.
  • A court transcript records speech, but in a formal, rule-bound setting, kept as a permanent record.

Function, and Halliday's three kinds of meaning

  • Function: what a text is for: to inform, persuade, instruct, entertain, or keep a relationship going (phatic talk: hiya, how's things?).
  • Most texts have several: Text B questions a witness, tests evidence and creates a record.
  • Halliday linked context to three metafunctions: field shapes ideational meaning (content), tenor shapes interpersonal meaning (relationships), and mode shapes textual meaning (organisation).

?A group chat and a court transcript: why do they look nothing alike?

Their whole context differs. The chat is fast, synchronous typing between friends about a meet-up; the transcript records formal, rule-bound talk between unequal participants about evidence, kept as a permanent record. Mode, field, tenor and function each leave traces, so strong answers link features to the factor that best explains them.

Mission 2

‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

Make a guess: ‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 1

Saying and doing

  • Pragmatics studies meaning in context: what a speaker means, not only what the words say.
  • Austin (1962) showed that utterances are speech acts: the locution (the words said), the illocution (what the speaker is doing: requesting, warning, promising) and the perlocution (the effect on the listener).
  • A direct speech act matches form and function: Shut the window is an imperative used as a command.
  • An indirect speech act doesn't: It's cold in here is a declarative that can work as a request (Searle 1975).
Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 2

Grice: reading between the lines

  • Grice (1975): conversation works because we assume speakers are cooperating: the Cooperative Principle.
  • Four maxims: Quantity (as much information as needed), Quality (say what's true), Relation (be relevant), Manner (be clear).
  • We assume It's cold in here is relevant, so we ask why it's said now, and infer a request: an implicature.
  • A speaker who openly flouts a maxim creates an implicature; one who covertly violates it misleads (a lie violates Quality).
Quick checkScreen 3
?Invented example. A parent looks at an untidy bedroom and says: I've told you a million times to tidy up. Which maxim is flouted, and what is implied?
Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 4

Politeness and face

  • Face is the public self-image people want to keep (from Goffman). Brown and Levinson (1987): positive face is the wish to be liked; negative face, the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Requests, orders and criticism are face-threatening acts. Speakers choose a strategy:
  • Positive politeness: friendliness, compliments, in-group terms (mate), inclusive we.
  • Negative politeness: hedges, apologies, modal verbs, indirectness (Could I possibly…?).
  • Off record: a deniable hint (It's cold in here). Bald on record: no softening (Shut the window).

Discourse and cohesion

  • Discourse is how a whole text or conversation is organised: openings, sequences, closings. Discourse markers (so, anyway, right) signal shifts.
  • Cohesion is the grammatical and lexical ties that hold a text together (Halliday and Hasan 1976):

Mission checkScreen 9

?‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

The listener infers it. The words are a statement, but in context (an open window, a cold speaker) the listener assumes the remark is relevant and works out the implicature. As an off-record request, it lets the speaker ask without imposing, protecting both people's face.

Mission 3

Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

Make a guess: Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

Spoken language · conversationScreen 1

Normal non-fluency

FeatureInvented example
Filler (voiced pause)erm, um
Unvoiced pauseI think (.) yes; (.) is a micropause, (2) two seconds
False startI was- we went on Tuesday
Self-repairon Monday, no, Tuesday
Repetitionthe the bus
  • Spontaneous speech is planned while it's spoken, so every fluent speaker shows normal non-fluency.
  • These features can buy planning time, hold the floor or soften what's said.
  • Other spoken features: ellipsis, discourse markers (so, anyway), tag questions (isn't it?), deixis (this, over there).
Spoken language · conversationScreen 2

Taking turns

  • Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson (1974) showed that conversation is organised turn by turn: mostly one speaker at a time, with smooth changes.
  • Turns change at a transition relevance place (TRP): where a turn could be complete (a finished clause, falling intonation, a pause).
  • The speaker can select the next speaker (What do you think, Ali?), or someone self-selects.
  • Overlap: brief simultaneous talk near a TRP, often supportive. Interruption: breaking in mid-turn to take the floor.
  • Back-channelling (mm, yeah) shows listening without taking the turn.
Spoken language · conversationScreen 3

Adjacency pairs

  • An adjacency pair is two linked turns by different speakers: a first pair part makes a particular second pair part expected (Schegloff and Sacks 1973).
  • Question and answer; greeting and greeting; offer and acceptance or refusal; request and grant or refusal.
  • Preferred responses (accepting an invitation) usually come quickly and simply.
  • Dispreferred responses (refusing) tend to be delayed, hedged and explained: oh (.) erm I'd love to but I'm working.
  • So non-fluency can be a signal, not a slip.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

No. Fillers, pauses, false starts and overlaps are normal non-fluency in spontaneous speech. They often do a job: buying planning time, managing turns, signalling a dispreferred response or softening a face threat. Strong analysis says what each feature does in context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Mode, field, tenor (or audience) and function (or purpose), plus genre, describe a text's context.
  • Mode is a continuum: electronic texts such as group chats blend features of speech and writing.
  • Halliday: field shapes ideational meaning, tenor interpersonal meaning, mode textual meaning.
  • OCR: genre, mode and context.
  • Speech acts (Austin 1962; Searle 1975): locution, illocution, perlocution; direct and indirect speech acts.
  • Grice (1975): the Cooperative Principle and four maxims; flouting creates implicature, violating misleads.
  • Brown and Levinson (1987): positive and negative face; positive, negative, off-record and bald-on-record strategies.
  • Discourse structure and cohesion (Halliday and Hasan 1976): reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion.
  • Normal non-fluency: fillers, pauses, false starts, self-repairs, repetition; plus ellipsis, discourse markers, tag questions and deixis.
  • Turn-taking (Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson 1974): TRPs, selecting and self-selecting, overlap vs interruption, back-channelling.
  • Adjacency pairs (Schegloff and Sacks 1973): first and second pair parts; dispreferred responses are delayed, hedged and explained.
