Mission 1: If the universe began with a Big Bang, is there still room for a Creator?

Make a guess: If the universe began with a Big Bang, is there still room for a Creator?

Mission 1

Two kinds of question

  • Science asks how: what happened, in what order, by what processes. It answers from evidence that can be tested.
  • Religion often asks why: is there a purpose? Who, if anyone, is behind it?
  • A creation account (creation story) is a sacred text about the beginning of the world and its meaning.
  • Many religious people say the two kinds of question fit together. Others think one side has to give way.
  • The key word is interpretation: how a sacred text is read decides whether it clashes with science.
Mission 1Read the source

Genesis 1

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)

"God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)

  • Genesis 1: God creates by speaking, over six days, then rests on the seventh (Genesis 2:1–3). Humans come last.
  • Genesis 2 (from 2:4): a second account. God forms the man from dust before plants and animals, then the woman.

Mission 1

The Big Bang

  • Scientists' best account: about 13.8 billion years ago the universe was extremely hot and dense, and it has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  • Evidence: distant galaxies are moving away from us (red shift); the cosmic microwave background, faint radiation left over from the early universe.
  • Stars, then galaxies and planets, formed over billions of years. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago.
  • The theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, in 1927 and 1931.
  • Lemaître kept science and faith distinct: the theory describes the physical beginning; it neither proves nor disproves God.
  • Brian Cox (born 1968), physicist and broadcaster, explains the universe's history from the Big Bang using evidence alone; he does not appeal to God to explain it (paraphrase of his public view).

Mission 1Diversity

Christians read Genesis differently

  • Literal reading (creationists, e.g. some evangelical Christians): Genesis is accurate history; God made everything in six days; many say the earth is only thousands of years old (young earth). The Big Bang is rejected or reinterpreted.
  • Non-literal reading (most Catholics, Anglicans, Orthodox, many Protestants): Genesis is true about who and why, not a science report. The "days" can be stages or a poetic framework.
  • These Christians see the Big Bang as how God created: science and faith are compatible.
  • Shared belief: God is Creator; the universe depends on God and is good; humans have a special place in it.
  • Catholic and most other Christian teaching: God creates ex nihilo (out of nothing). Science studies how the universe develops; it cannot explain why there is something rather than nothing.
  • Your spec asks for the diversity of Christian views: give both readings.

Mission 1Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Allah is the one Creator of everything. The Qur'an speaks of creation in six days (Qur'an 7:54), read by many Muslims as six periods.
  • "Have not those who disbelieve known that the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall). Many Muslims see this as fitting the Big Bang.
  • Qur'an 36:81: the One who created the heavens and the earth is able to create again: "He is the All-Wise Creator" (Pickthall).
  • Judaism: God created the universe (Genesis 1–2; the Torah is the first part of the Tenakh).
  • Orthodox Jews: Genesis is God's word; some read it literally, many read the days as stages; the Jewish calendar counts years from creation.
  • Reform Jews: Genesis teaches meaning and values; the Big Bang and an ancient universe are accepted.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 1Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: the universe goes through endless cycles of creation, preservation and dissolution (for many Hindus, Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva are three aspects of Brahman; Vaishnavas and Shaivas see Vishnu or Shiva as supreme). Many Hindus say a cyclical, vast universe fits modern cosmology.
  • The Hymn of Creation (Rig Veda 10.129): before creation there was neither being nor non-being; the hymn ends by asking who really knows how creation arose, and wonders if even the highest knows (paraphrase).
  • Bhagavata Purana 12.4.4: at each dissolution, while Brahma sleeps, Narayana (Vishnu) draws the whole universe back into himself. Bhagavad Gita 11.7: Krishna shows Arjuna the whole universe within his own body (paraphrases).
  • Sikhism: no step-by-step creation story like Genesis; Waheguru (God) created everything by the divine will (hukam). The Mool Mantar (Guru Granth Sahib 1) calls God Karta Purakh, the Creator.
  • Buddhism: no creator God and no single creation story. World-systems arise, decay and are reborn in cycles, without a known first beginning.
  • The Buddha refused to answer whether the universe is eternal: such questions do not lead to the end of suffering (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63: the parable of the poisoned arrow).
Mission 1

Does science count against God?

  • Atheists and Humanists (non-religious people who live by reason and human values) point out that the universe can be explained by science without God.
  • Argument: if physics can describe the universe from its earliest moments, a Creator is an unnecessary extra. Creation stories were early attempts to explain what science now explains.
  • Many add that a literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence.
  • Religious replies:
    • The Big Bang needs a cause: why is there a universe at all? (the First Cause argument)
    • The laws of physics are finely balanced for life; some see design in this.
    • Science explains how, not why: it cannot rule out a Creator.
Quick check
?Many Buddhists say that the question "How did the universe begin?" is not important to answer. Why?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Science has shown that God did not create the universe."

  • Reasons for: the Big Bang explains the universe's development by physical laws. A literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence. Explanations that need fewer causes are often preferred (no need for God).
  • Reasons against: science explains how the universe developed, not why there is anything at all. The Big Bang suggests a beginning, which fits belief in creation (Lemaître; creation ex nihilo). Many scientists are believers.
  • Other views: Buddhists see the question as unimportant; many Hindus and Sikhs see the universe's vastness as fitting their scriptures.
  • Judge it by: Do science and religion answer the same question? Does a beginning need a cause? Does it depend on how scripture is read?

Mission 2

Why the world has value

  • For many religious people the world has value because God made it and called it "very good" (Genesis 1:31).
  • Awe and wonder: the deep amazement people feel at nature's size, beauty and order. Many believers see God in it.
  • "The heavens declare the glory of God." (Psalm 19:1)
  • The opposite idea: the world as a commodity, something to be bought, sold and used up for profit.
  • Non-religious people may also feel awe and value the world for its own sake and for future generations.
Mission 2Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

"God said to them, 'Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth.'" (Genesis 1:28)

"The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15)

"The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness; the world, and those who dwell in it." (Psalm 24:1)

  • Psalm 8:6: God made humans "ruler over the works of your hands".

Q1 Which verse gives humans the job of looking after the earth?

Show answer

Genesis 2:15: "cultivate and keep it".

Q2 If "the earth is the LORD's", who owns the world?

Show answer

God; humans only look after it.

Mission 2

Dominion or stewardship?

  • Dominion: rule or authority over creation (Genesis 1:28).
  • Some have read dominion as permission to use nature without limit. The historian Lynn White (1967) blamed this reading for part of the ecological crisis.
  • Stewardship: humans look after the world on God's behalf and must answer to God for it.
  • Most Christians and Jews today read dominion as stewardship: responsible rule, like a good king who cares for his people.
  • Responsibility: a duty to care for the world for others now and for future generations.
Quick check
?A farmer says: "This land belongs to God. I farm it, but one day I will have to answer for how I treated it." Which idea is she expressing?
Mission 2

Use and abuse of the environment

  • Pollution: harmful substances in air, water or land (exhaust fumes, plastic waste, chemical run-off, oil spills).
  • Global warming: burning fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases, which trap heat and change the climate.
  • Natural resources: materials from the earth. Non-renewable (coal, oil, gas, metals) can run out; renewable (sun, wind, water) are replaced naturally.
  • Sustainability (sustainable development): meeting today's needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs.
  • Responses: recycling, renewable energy, conservation, campaigning, changing what people buy and eat.
  • Global citizenship: seeing yourself as responsible for the whole world, not just your own country.
Mission 2

Christian responses

  • Stewardship means acting: Christians support recycling, renewable energy, conservation and campaigns for climate justice.
  • Catholic teaching: humans' dominion over creation is not absolute; it must respect the good of others, including future generations (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2415, paraphrase).
  • Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): care for "our common home"; the poor suffer most from environmental damage (paraphrase and short phrase).
Mission 2Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: humans are khalifah (vicegerents, trustees) on earth: "I will create a vicegerent on earth" (Qur'an 2:30, Yusuf Ali).
  • Allah set the mizan (balance) in creation: "That ye exceed not the measure" (Qur'an 55:8, Pickthall).
  • Fitra: the natural state of creation and of human nature, in harmony with Allah's will (Qur'an 30:30).
  • Qur'an 7:54: sun, moon and stars are "subservient by His command" (Pickthall).
  • On the Day of Judgement (akhirah) Muslims believe they will answer for how they used the earth.
  • Judaism: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • Bal tashchit (do not destroy): even in war, do not cut down fruit trees (Deuteronomy 20:19); extended by the rabbis to all needless waste.
  • Tikkun olam (repairing the world) and gemilut hasadim (acts of loving kindness) include care for the environment.
  • Honi (Talmud): Honi meets a man planting a carob tree that will fruit only after 70 years; the man says he plants for his descendants, as his ancestors planted for him (Taanit 23a); and the teaching that if you are planting a sapling when told the Messiah has come, finish planting first (Avot de-Rabbi Natan) (paraphrase).
  • Maimonides' 10th Principle: God knows all human deeds, so humans are accountable for how they treat the world.
Mission 2Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: Brahman is present in all things; harming nature harms the sacred. Ahimsa (non-violence) extends to the environment.
  • Bhagavad Gita 7.10: Krishna is the original seed of all beings; Bhagavata Purana 7.14.9: treat animals as you would your own children (paraphrase).
  • Examples: the Bishnoi community's protection of trees; the Chipko movement (1970s), villagers hugging trees to stop logging.
  • Sikhism: God is present in creation; air is the Guru, water the father, and earth the great mother (Japji, closing verse, Guru Granth Sahib 8, paraphrase).
  • Guru Granth Sahib 1037 (Guru Nanak): before creation there was only the primal void; from it God formed air, water, earth and sky (paraphrase).
  • Guru Granth Sahib 21: wherever one looks, God is seen pervading creation (paraphrase).
  • Many gurdwaras and Sikh groups (e.g. EcoSikh) run environmental projects; sewa (selfless service) includes caring for the earth.
  • Buddhism: everything is interdependent (pratitya samutpada, dependent arising): harming nature harms all beings.
  • "Because he has pity on all living creatures, therefore is a man called Ariya." (Dhammapada 270, Müller)
  • In Buddhist teaching, the First Precept (do not harm living beings) and the Second Precept (do not take what is not given) apply to nature and its resources.
  • Right Action (Eightfold Path) applies to the earth's resources too; Buddhist Action Month each June encourages practical action.

Mission 2

Non-religious views

  • Humanists and many atheists see no Creator, but argue humans have strong reasons to protect the planet.
  • This is the only world we have, and there is no afterlife; the well-being of people and animals now and in future depends on it.
  • They rely on scientific evidence about climate change and pollution.
  • Humanist Climate Action: a network of Humanists UK that campaigns and shares practical action on climate change.
  • Some non-religious thinkers give nature value in itself; others value it for its use to humans and other sentient beings.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Humans have the right to use the earth's resources as they wish."

  • Reasons for: Genesis 1:28 gives dominion; human needs (food, homes, jobs, escaping poverty) matter. Some argue technology will solve shortages.
  • Reasons against: "The earth is the LORD's" (Psalm 24:1): humans are stewards, not owners. Humanists: future generations have interests too. Pollution and climate change harm the poorest most (Laudato Si').
  • Other traditions agree: Muslims are khalifah, not owners; bal tashchit, ahimsa and the Precepts forbid needless harm.
  • Judge it by: What does "dominion" mean? Do future generations have rights? Do animals and nature have value in themselves?
Mission 2Worked answer

Different beliefs

Question: Explain two different religious beliefs about humans' relationship with the natural world.

  • Belief 1: Most Christians believe humans are stewards. Development: God put the man in Eden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15), so they recycle, save energy and support climate campaigns.
  • Belief 2: Buddhists believe all things are interdependent. Development: harming nature harms all beings, so they keep the First and Second Precepts by avoiding harm and waste.

Mission 3

Evolution in brief

  • Evolution: living things change over many generations; new species develop from earlier ones.
  • Natural selection: individuals with features that suit their environment survive and reproduce more, so those features spread.
  • "Survival of the fittest" (Herbert Spencer's phrase, later used by Darwin): "fittest" means best suited, not strongest.
  • Evidence: fossils, shared anatomy, and DNA showing all life is related.
  • Life on Earth began over 3.5 billion years ago. Modern humans (Homo sapiens) appeared about 300,000 years ago, sharing ancestors with other apes.
Mission 3Read the source

Darwin

Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859), closing sentence:

> "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one; … from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being, evolved."

  • Darwin also wrote The Descent of Man (1871), applying evolution to humans.
  • Later editions read "breathed by the Creator into a few forms". Darwin moved towards agnosticism in later life.

Show answer

Mission 3

Dawkins

  • Richard Dawkins (born 1941), biologist and atheist: The Selfish Gene (1976), The Blind Watchmaker (1986), The God Delusion (2006).
  • Natural selection explains living things that look designed without any designer: a "blind watchmaker".
  • So, he argues, there is no need for God to explain human origins, and belief in God is very probably mistaken.
  • Humans are not specially created; they are one product of the same process as all life.
  • Darwin and Dawkins are your spec's named non-religious views on evolution.

Made in God's image

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

  • Imago Dei (image of God): humans can reason, love, choose and relate to God.
  • Humans are made last in Genesis 1 and given responsibility for creation (Genesis 1:26–31).

Mission 3Diversity

Christian responses

  • Creationists (some evangelical and other Christians): God created each kind directly and Adam and Eve as the first humans. Human evolution from earlier species is rejected.
  • Theistic evolution (most Catholics, Anglicans, Orthodox and many Protestants): evolution is how God created life; God gives each human a soul.
  • Catholic teaching (Pope Pius XII, Humani Generis, 1950): the body may have evolved; the spiritual soul is created directly by God.

Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Allah created the first human, Adam, from clay and breathed His Spirit into him (Qur'an 32:7–9).
  • "Who made all things good which He created, and He began the creation of man from clay" (Qur'an 32:7, Pickthall).
  • Most Muslims accept change within species; many reject the idea that humans descended from other species, because Adam was created specially. Some Muslim scientists accept full evolution as Allah's method.
  • Judaism: God made humans in the divine image (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Some Orthodox Jews read Genesis literally and reject evolution; many Orthodox and most Reform Jews accept it as the way God created.
  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science takes things apart to see how they work; religion puts them together to see what they mean (paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: few Hindus see a conflict. The universe is ancient and cyclical; the atman (eternal self) is not a product of evolution.
  • Sikhism: God is the Creator of all life by hukam (divine will); many Sikhs accept evolution as part of how hukam works.
  • Buddhism: no creator; most Buddhists accept evolution. All beings are interconnected and change constantly (anicca).
  • The poisoned arrow (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63): speculating about origins does not lead to the end of suffering.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Evolution and belief that God created humans cannot both be true."

  • Reasons for: a literal Genesis (six days, Adam first) conflicts with evolution. Natural selection explains apparent design without God (Dawkins). Evolution involves suffering and extinction, which some find hard to fit with a loving Creator.
  • Reasons against: many believers accept theistic evolution: God creates through natural processes. Catholic teaching: the body may evolve; the soul is God's direct creation (Pius XII, John Paul II). Genesis teaches meaning, not biology (many Christians, Reform Jews).
  • Other views: Buddhists see no conflict because they hold no creator; many Hindus and Sikhs see evolution as part of the divine order.
  • Judge it by: Must sacred texts be read literally? Can a process be both natural and God-given? Does evolution explain the soul and moral value?

Mission 4

Sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: the belief that human life is holy (sacred) and has special value, because it is given by God.
  • So life must be protected and respected from its beginning to its natural end.
  • Many believers add: only God may decide when a life begins and ends. "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away." (Job 1:21)
  • The value is intrinsic: it does not depend on health, ability, age or usefulness.
  • Non-religious people may also say every human life has great value, but not because it is God-given.
Mission 4Read the source

Made by God

"God created man in his own image." (Genesis 1:27)

"Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Before you were born, I sanctified you." (Jeremiah 1:5)

"Don't you know that your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit who is in you, whom you have from God? You are not your own" (1 Corinthians 6:19)

  • Genesis 1:31: God saw everything he had made, "and, behold, it was very good".

Q1 Which verse says God knows a person before birth?

Show answer

Jeremiah 1:5.

Q2 Why would "you are not your own" matter for decisions about life and death?

Show answer

Life belongs to God, so it is not simply ours to end.

Mission 4

Quality of life

  • Quality of life: how good a person's life is: their health, freedom from pain, independence, relationships, ability to enjoy life.
  • Doctors use it when choosing treatments; patients use it when deciding what they want.
  • Arguments based on quality of life say that a life's value can depend on what it is like for the person living it.
  • Questions: who decides? Can a life with great suffering still be worth living? Can quality of life be measured?
  • Many believers use both ideas: life is sacred, and suffering should be relieved (e.g. by good medical care).

Mission 4Diversity

Christian views

  • Strong sanctity of life (Catholic teaching; many evangelical and fundamentalist Christians, who read the Bible as literally true in all it teaches): innocent human life must never be deliberately ended, whatever its quality.
  • Weaker sanctity of life (many liberal Protestants, some Anglicans): life is God's gift, but love (agape) and compassion can mean that quality of life matters in hard cases.
  • All agree that the sick, disabled and dying are equally valuable, and that suffering should be relieved (hospices, medical care).
Mission 4Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: life is a gift and a trust from Allah, who alone decides when each life ends (Qur'an 3:145).
  • "whosoever killeth a human being for other than manslaughter or corruption in the earth, it shall be as if he had killed all mankind, and whoso saveth the life of one, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall)
  • Qur'an 6:151: "slay not the life which Allah hath made sacred, save in the course of justice" (Pickthall).
  • Judaism: life is created by God in the divine image (Genesis 1:27) and is of supreme value. "Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917).
  • Pikuach nefesh: saving a life overrides almost every other commandment, including Shabbat.
  • B. Yoma 84b: the Talmud teaches that to save life one must break Shabbat at once, without delay or asking permission (paraphrase).
  • Orthodox Jews tend to apply sanctity of life strictly; Reform Jews give more weight to quality of life and personal conscience.
Mission 4Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: every being has an atman (eternal self), part of Brahman. "Indestructible, Learn thou! the Life is, spreading life through all" (Bhagavad Gita 2:17, Arnold).
  • Bhagavad Gita 18:61: the Lord dwells in the hearts of all beings (paraphrase).
  • Ahimsa (non-violence) protects all life. A human birth is a rare chance to reach moksha.
  • Sikhism: God's light (jot) is in every being; life is a gift of God's grace and a chance to become gurmukh (God-centred).
  • Guru Granth Sahib 441: "O my mind, you are the embodiment of the divine light; recognise your own origin" (Guru Amar Das, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: there is no creator, but life is precious: a human rebirth is a rare chance to reach enlightenment.
  • The First Precept (Khuddakapatha 2): I undertake the training to refrain from taking life (paraphrase).
  • "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129, Müller)
  • The Eightfold Path (Right Action) and the Middle Way guide how life is lived and protected.

Mission 4

Non-religious views and Singer

  • Humanists: human life is valuable because it is the only life we have; there is no God and no afterlife. Value comes from human experience, relationships and choices.
  • So quality of life and personal autonomy (the right to make decisions about your own life) matter greatly.
  • Peter Singer (born 1946), Practical Ethics (1979), Rethinking Life and Death (1994): the traditional sanctity of life gives value to human life just because it is human. He calls this speciesism.
  • Singer argues that the capacity to suffer, and to be self-aware and plan a future, are what matter morally; so some animals have more of these than some humans (paraphrase).
Quick check
?What is Peter Singer's main criticism of the traditional sanctity of life?
Mission 4Evaluate

"The sanctity of life matters more than the quality of life."

  • Reasons for: life is God's gift (Job 1:21; 1 Corinthians 6:19). Judging lives by quality risks treating disabled, sick or old people as less valuable. Quality is hard to measure and can change.
  • Reasons against: compassion (agape; karuna, Buddhist compassion) means suffering matters.
  • Humanists stress autonomy: people should judge their own lives. Singer: the sanctity doctrine is speciesist and ignores suffering.
  • Middle positions: many believers hold both: life is sacred, so it is never ended deliberately, but quality of life guides care (e.g. refusing burdensome treatment).
  • Judge it by: Is value given by God or by experience? Who decides? Can the two ideas be held together?

  • Teaching 1: Life is created by God. Source: "God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27). Development: so Christians believe every human has equal worth, whatever their health.
  • Teaching 2: Saving one life is like saving the whole of humanity. Source: Qur'an 5:32. Development: so Muslims believe they must protect life, and ending it wrongly is a grave sin.
Mission check

?A hospital chaplain visits a young man badly injured in a rugby accident. He says: "My life is worth nothing now." Which reply best reflects a sanctity-of-life view?

Sanctity of life says a life's worth is given, not earned; quality of life asks what the life is like for the person living it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Creation accounts (Genesis 1–2, the Qur'an, Hindu scriptures) say the universe has a source and meaning; Buddhists have no creation story, and Sikh scripture describes creation without a step-by-step account.
  • The Big Bang (first proposed by Lemaître, a Catholic priest) is accepted by many believers as how creation happened; literalists reject it where it conflicts with scripture.
  • Atheists and Humanists say science explains the universe without God; believers reply that science answers how, not why.

Mission 2

  • Many believers value the world because God made it; awe and wonder point to its Creator. The commodity view treats it as a resource to use up.
  • Most Christians and Jews read dominion as stewardship; Muslims speak of khalifah; Hindus, Sikhs and Buddhists stress ahimsa, God in creation and interdependence.
  • Humanists protect the environment for present and future well-being, using scientific evidence (e.g. Humanist Climate Action).

Mission 3

  • Evolution by natural selection (Darwin, 1859) explains how species, including humans, developed; Dawkins argues it removes the need for God.
  • Creationists reject human evolution; most Christians accept theistic evolution, and Catholic teaching says the soul is created directly by God (Pius XII, 1950; John Paul II, 1996).
  • Muslims, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs and Buddhists respond in different ways; for many there is no conflict.

Mission 4

  • Sanctity of life: life is holy and God-given (imago Dei; Job 1:21); quality of life: how good a life is for the person.
  • Every religion values life: Qur'an 5:32 and pikuach nefesh; atman and ahimsa; God's light in all (Sikhism); the First Precept and precious human rebirth (Buddhism).
  • Christians differ: strong sanctity (Catholic and many fundamentalist Christians) or weaker sanctity, where agape lets quality of life count in hard cases.
  • Humanists and Singer stress quality of life, autonomy and suffering; Catholics reply that value never depends on abilities.
