Mission 1

Seven people give the same wrong answer. Do you go along with them?

Make a guess: Seven people give the same wrong answer. Do you go along with them?

Social influence · conformity

Going along with the majority

  • Conformity is changing your behaviour or opinions to match a group, because of real or imagined group pressure.
  • Majority influence: a larger group pressures one person, or a few, to adopt its view.
  • You may go along to fit in and avoid looking odd, or because you think the group must know better.
  • Conformity is not obedience: obedience means following a direct order from an authority figure.
Key study Social influence · conformity

Asch (1951)

Aim
To see whether people would conform to a majority giving an obviously wrong answer.
Method
Lab experiment; 50 male American students, each with 7 confederates. Each person said aloud which of three lines matched a standard line; 18 trials, 12 critical (all confederates wrong).
Results
Participants conformed on 32% of critical trials; 75% conformed at least once; 25% never did. Alone, under 1% made errors.
Conclusion
Majority influence is powerful, even when the right answer is obvious.
Strength
Controlled and standardised: every participant saw the same lines and answers, so it can be replicated.
Weakness
Artificial task with little at stake; only American men in the 1950s; participants were deceived.
Social influence · conformity

The group and the task

  • These are situational factors: features of the situation, not the person.
  • Group size: with one other person giving a wrong answer, almost nobody conformed; with two, about 13% of answers; with three, about 32%. More than three made little difference.
  • Anonymity: answering privately, e.g. in writing, reduces conformity.
  • Task difficulty: when the lines are closer in length, conformity rises. Unsure people look to others.
Social influence · conformity

What the person brings

  • Dispositional factors are features of the person, such as personality.
  • Self-esteem: people with low self-esteem conform more; confident people trust their own judgement.
  • Locus of control: people who feel outside forces control their lives (external locus) conform more.

Mission check

?Seven people give the same wrong answer. Do you go along with them?

Quite possibly, at least once. In the classic line test about three in four people went along with a wrong majority at least once, although most answers stayed correct. How likely you are to conform depends on the situation (group size, anonymity, task difficulty) and on you, for example your self-esteem.

Mission 2

A stranger in uniform gives you an order. Why is it so hard to say no?

Make a guess: A stranger in uniform gives you an order. Why is it so hard to say no?

Social influence · obedience

Following orders

  • Obedience is following a direct order from an authority figure, someone with the power to give orders.
  • Conformity is pressure from a group of equals; obedience comes from someone of higher status.
  • People obey when they see the authority as legitimate: it has the right to give that order, like a police officer or a teacher.
  • Symbols such as a uniform or an official building make authority look legitimate.
Key study Social influence · obedience

Milgram (1963)

Aim
To see whether ordinary people would obey an order to harm an innocent person.
Method
Controlled lab study at Yale; 40 male volunteers from a newspaper advert, paid $4.50. As "teacher", each gave what he believed were electric shocks (15 to 450 volts) to a "learner" (an actor) for wrong answers; an experimenter in a lab coat used set prods.
Results
All 40 went to at least 300 volts; 65% went to the full 450 volts. Many showed stress, such as sweating and trembling.
Conclusion
Ordinary people will obey a legitimate authority, even to harm someone.
Strength
Standardised prods and scripted protests, so it can be replicated; variations tested each factor.
Weakness
Deception, distress and pressure to continue raise ethical concerns, though participants were debriefed.
Social influence · obedience

Agents of authority

  • Milgram's Agency theory: in the autonomous state you act on your own conscience and feel responsible for what you do.
  • Faced with a legitimate authority, you shift into the agentic state: you act as the authority's agent and feel they are responsible.
  • This agentic shift explains why people obey orders they believe are wrong: "I was only following orders."
Social influence · obedience

Authority, distance and culture

  • Authority: obedience is higher when the authority looks legitimate (uniform, lab coat, official building).
  • Proximity: obedience falls when the victim is closer or the authority is further away.
  • In Milgram's variations, 65% obeyed fully; 40% with the learner in the same room; 20.5% with orders by phone.
Social influence · obedience

The authoritarian personality

  • Adorno's theory: some people have an Authoritarian Personality, which makes them highly obedient.
  • Traits: strong respect for authority, rigid "black-and-white" thinking, and hostility towards people seen as lower status.
  • Cause: a strict upbringing with harsh discipline and love that depended on good behaviour.
  • It is measured with a questionnaire, the F-scale. People who scored high tended to be more obedient.

Mission check

?A stranger in uniform gives you an order. Why is it so hard to say no?

The uniform makes the person look like a legitimate authority. Once you accept that, you tend to shift into the agentic state and feel that they, not you, are responsible. How close the authority is, and personality (such as the authoritarian personality), also affect whether you obey.

Mission 3

A man collapses on a train. Does a cane or a bottle change who helps?

Make a guess: A man collapses on a train. Does a cane or a bottle change who helps?

Social influence · bystander behaviour

When others are watching

  • A bystander sees an emergency but is not directly involved. Bystander intervention is when a bystander helps.
  • The bystander effect: people are less likely to help when others are present.
  • One reason is diffusion of responsibility: with others around, each person feels less responsible for helping.
  • The presence of others is a situational factor.
Social influence · bystander behaviour

Weighing up the cost

  • Bystanders weigh the costs of helping (danger, effort, time, embarrassment, disgust) against the costs of not helping (guilt, blame).
  • The higher the cost of helping, the less likely people are to help.
  • The cost of helping is a situational factor.
  • Piliavin et al.'s arousal: cost–reward model: seeing an emergency is upsetting, and people choose whatever reduces that feeling at the lowest cost.
Social influence · bystander behaviour

Who is more likely to help

  • Personal factors are features of the bystander.
  • Similarity to victim: we are more likely to help people we see as like us, e.g. the same age, gender or group.
  • Expertise: people with relevant skills, such as first aid or medical training, are more likely to help, and to help well.
  • Other personal factors include the bystander's mood and gender.
Key study Social influence · bystander behaviour

Piliavin et al. (1969)

Aim
To see how victim type (ill or drunk), race and number of bystanders affect helping.
Method
Field experiment on New York subway trains with about 4,450 passengers. A male actor collapsed, carrying a cane (ill) or smelling of alcohol with a bottle (drunk). Observers recorded who helped and how fast.
Results
The cane victim was helped on 95% of trials (62 of 65); the drunk victim on 50% (19 of 38). 90% of first helpers were men. Larger groups did not help less.
Conclusion
Helping depends on its cost; diffusion of responsibility is weaker when the victim is in plain view.
Strength
Natural setting, passengers unaware, so behaviour was realistic.
Weakness
No consent, debrief or right to withdraw; some passengers may have been upset.

Mission check

?A man collapses on a train. Does a cane or a bottle change who helps?

Yes. In Piliavin's subway study the man with a cane was helped on 95% of trials and the man who seemed drunk on 50%. Bystanders weigh up the cost of helping, and helping someone drunk seems riskier and more unpleasant. Who helps also depends on the bystander, for example expertise and similarity to the victim.

Mission 4

In a crowd, polite people do things they'd never do alone. Why?

Make a guess: In a crowd, polite people do things they'd never do alone. Why?

Social influence · crowds and collective behaviour

Crowds can harm or help

  • Collective behaviour is how people act together as part of a group or crowd.
  • Anti-social crowd behaviour harms people or property, e.g. rioting, looting or fighting at a football match.
  • Pro-social crowd behaviour helps others, e.g. a crowd cheering on charity runners, or strangers working together to help in an emergency.
  • The same crowd processes can push behaviour either way.
Social influence · crowds and collective behaviour

Lost in the crowd

  • Deindividuation: losing your sense of personal identity in a crowd.
  • Anonymity (masks, uniforms, darkness, a big crowd) means you are unlikely to be identified, and responsibility feels shared.
  • With lower self-awareness, you follow the crowd's norms instead of your own standards.
  • The norms matter: in one study, anonymous people dressed in robes like the Ku Klux Klan gave stronger shocks than people dressed as nurses.
Social influence · crowds and collective behaviour

Personality and morality

  • Locus of control: people with an internal locus (who feel in control of their actions) are less easily swept along by a crowd than those with an external locus.
  • Morality: people with a strong sense of right and wrong are less likely to join anti-social crowd behaviour and more likely to help.
Key study Social influence · crowds and collective behaviour

Haney, Banks and Zimbardo (1973)

Aim
To see whether prison brutality comes from the personalities of guards and prisoners or from the situation.
Method
Mock prison in a Stanford University basement. 21 healthy male students (from 75 who answered an advert; paid $15 a day) were randomly assigned as 10 prisoners or 11 guards. Guards wore uniforms and dark glasses; prisoners wore smocks and numbers.
Results
Guards became hostile and abusive; prisoners became passive and distressed. Five prisoners were released early. Planned for two weeks, it was stopped after six days.
Conclusion
The situation, not personality, caused the behaviour; uniforms and anonymity deindividuated both groups.
Strength
Random allocation and screening make personality an unlikely cause.
Weakness
Serious distress to participants; small all-male student sample; guards may have been acting.

Mission check

?In a crowd, polite people do things they'd never do alone. Why?

Being in a crowd can cause deindividuation. Feeling anonymous and sharing responsibility lowers self-awareness, so people follow the crowd's norms instead of their own. If those norms are anti-social, behaviour gets worse; if they are pro-social, it can get better. Personal factors, such as morality and locus of control, help some people resist.

Mission 5

Most people go along. A few refuse. What helps someone resist?

Make a guess: Most people go along. A few refuse. What helps someone resist?

Social influence · resisting and changing

Not alone

  • A unanimous majority or authority is hard to resist. One other person who refuses breaks the pressure: this is social support.
  • In Asch's line test, one ally giving the right answer cut conformity to about 5%.
  • In a Milgram variation, when two fellow "teachers" refused, only 10% went to 450 volts, compared with 65% normally.
  • A resister shows refusing is possible, so you don't stand out alone.
Social influence · resisting and changing

Preventing blind obedience

  • Blind obedience: obeying without asking whether the order is right.
  • Education: people who know about obedience research are more likely to question orders.
  • Disobedient role models: seeing others refuse makes refusing easier.
  • Personal responsibility: reminding people they are responsible for their actions keeps them in the autonomous state.
  • Question legitimacy: organisations can encourage staff to ask whether an order is right, and to speak up safely.
Social influence · resisting and changing

Social and cultural issues

  • Society: a community of people who share laws, institutions and customs.
  • A social issue is a problem that affects many people in a society, e.g. crime, riots or prejudice.
  • Culture: the shared beliefs, values, norms and customs of a group, passed between generations.
  • Social influence research helps explain social issues, e.g. deindividuation in riots, or the bystander effect when nobody helps.
  • Findings from one culture may not apply in another.

Mission check

?Most people go along. A few refuse. What helps someone resist?

Support. Seeing even one other person refuse makes resisting much easier, because the group or authority is no longer unanimous. Education, disobedient role models and a sense of personal responsibility also help prevent blind obedience.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Conformity is changing your behaviour or opinions to match a majority, even when the majority is clearly wrong.
  • Situational factors (group size, anonymity, task difficulty) and dispositional factors (personality, such as self-esteem and locus of control) affect how much people conform.
  • Key question, how do others affect you? A majority can change what you say, even about something obvious.
  • Obedience is following a direct order from an authority figure; symbols such as uniforms make an authority look legitimate.
  • Key question, how do others affect you? The situation (a legitimate authority, how close the authority and victim are) and personality (the authoritarian personality) both affect obedience.
  • The bystander effect: people help less when others are present, because of diffusion of responsibility; the cost of helping also matters.
  • Personal factors such as similarity to the victim and expertise make some people more likely to help.
  • Key question, how do others affect you? Piliavin et al. (1969) found more help for an ill victim than a drunk one, and no diffusion of responsibility, supporting the arousal: cost–reward model.
  • Crowds can produce pro-social or anti-social collective behaviour.
  • Deindividuation: anonymity and shared responsibility lower self-awareness, so people follow the crowd's norms.
  • Key question, how do others affect you? Haney, Banks and Zimbardo (1973) showed uniforms and roles changed behaviour within days.
  • Social support: one other person who resists makes it much easier to resist a majority or an authority.
  • Ways to prevent blind obedience: education, disobedient role models, personal responsibility and questioning legitimacy.
  • Debate, social and cultural issues: social influence research explains social issues such as riots and bystander apathy, but findings from one culture may not apply in another.
