Mission 1

Mission 1: What makes a whole country suddenly stop spending, even when pay has not changed?

Make a guess: What makes a whole country suddenly stop spending, even when pay has not changed?

Mission 1

Spend it or save it

  • Disposable income (Yd): income after direct taxes, plus state benefits (transfers).
  • Households do two things with it: consume or save.
  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Saving (S): disposable income not spent. Yd = C + S.
  • C is the largest part of AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Saving is a withdrawal (leakage) from the circular flow.
Mission 1

Average and marginal propensities

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC) = ΔC ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income spent.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS) = ΔS ÷ ΔYd. The share of extra income saved.
  • Every extra unit of disposable income is spent or saved, so MPC + MPS = 1.
  • Average propensity to consume (APC) = C ÷ Yd. The share of all income spent.
  • Average propensity to save (APS) = S ÷ Yd. APC + APS = 1.
  • Say: "MPC is the change in consumption over the change in income" — not: "consumption over income". That is an average, not a marginal, share.

Mission 1

The consumption function

  • Consumption function: C = a + bYd. Illustrative: C = 40 + 0.75Yd.
  • Autonomous consumption (a = 40): spent even at zero income, from past saving or borrowing.
  • Induced consumption (bYd): rises with income. The slope b is the MPC (0.75).
  • Step 2: the 45° line, where C = Yd. The function crosses it at Yd = 160: the break-even income, where S = 0.
  • Below 160, C > Yd: dissaving. Above 160, the gap below the 45° line is saving.
  • Savings function: S = −a + (1 − b)Yd = −40 + 0.25Yd. Its slope is the MPS.
  • A change in Yd is a movement along the function. Any other influence shifts it.

Mission 1

What else moves spending

  • Interest rates: a rise makes borrowing dearer (cars, furniture, mortgages) and saving more rewarding. C usually falls.
  • Confidence and expectations: fear of job loss or lower future income → more precautionary saving.
  • Wealth effects: rising house or share prices make owners feel richer and able to borrow → C rises.
  • Taxes and benefits: change disposable income.
  • Household debt and credit: heavily indebted households, or banks that stop lending, cut C.
  • Step 2: C rises with the price level unchanged → AD shifts right, AD1 → AD2.
  • Say: "a rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".
Mission 1

Real world: the 2020 saving surge

  • In spring 2020, lockdowns closed shops and jobs felt at risk.
  • UK: the household saving ratio rose from 9.6% (January to March) to a record 29.1% (April to June 2020).
  • US: the personal saving rate hit 33% in April 2020, up from 12.7% in March.
  • These are the first official estimates (ONS, BEA). Later revisions moved them a little; the surge stayed.
  • Two reasons:
  • Forced saving: little to spend on.
  • Precautionary saving: fear about jobs and health.
  • Most incomes were protected by furlough and support schemes, yet C fell sharply.
Quick check
?House prices fall sharply across a country. Incomes and interest rates do not change. What is the most likely effect on consumption?
Mission 1

Saving is not investment

  • Saving: income not spent by households. A withdrawal.
  • Investment: spending by firms on new capital goods (machines, buildings). An injection.
  • Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • Saving and investment are done by different people, for different reasons.
  • Banks may lend savings to firms, but planned saving need not equal planned investment.
  • Say: "investment is spending on capital goods" — not: "investing in shares".
Mission 1Evaluate

Will a cut in interest rates boost spending?

It depends on:

  • Confidence: worried households may still save, whatever the rate.
  • Borrowers or savers? Borrowers gain; savers lose interest income and may spend less.
  • Debt and credit: if banks will not lend, cheaper rates help little.
  • Time lags: fixed-rate loans and contracts delay the effect.
  • Size of the cut: a small cut may change little.
Quick check
?In economics, which of these counts as investment?
Mission check

?Share prices rise strongly over a year (illustrative). Households' disposable incomes and interest rates do not change, and consumer confidence improves. What happens?

Spending depends on income, but also on wealth, confidence, interest rates and credit. Any of them can move C even when pay is unchanged.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Make a guess: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Mission 2

One injection, many rounds

  • The government spends 100 (million) on a new bridge. An injection.
  • Builders earn 100. They spend some of it and withdraw the rest (saving, tax, imports).
  • Illustrative data: 80% of each extra unit of income is spent on domestic output.
  • Round 1: 100 → round 2: 80 → round 3: 64 → round 4: 51.2 → …
  • Each person's spending is someone else's income.
  • The rounds add up to 500: national income rises by 5 times the injection.
  • Multiplier effect: a change in an injection causes a larger final change in national income.
  • It works in reverse too: a cut in an injection reduces income by more than the cut.
Mission 2Graph lab

Re-spend more

  • Takeaway: the more of each round is re-spent, the bigger the multiplier.
Mission 2

The multiplier formula

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of each extra unit of income that is spent. MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. On the first screen of this mission it was 0.8.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS): the share of extra income saved.
  • Multiplier (k) = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Closed economy, no government: 1 − MPC = MPS, so k = 1 ÷ MPS.
  • Final change in national income = k × change in injection.
  • Example: MPC = 0.8. k = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. A rise of 100 in investment → national income rises by 500.
  • Say: "real national income rises by k times the injection" — not: "the injection is multiplied at once". It takes many rounds.
Quick check
?In a closed economy with no government, the MPC is 0.75. Investment rises by 40 billion. If there is spare capacity, by how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 2

Saving, tax and imports: MPW

  • Out of each extra unit of national income, three withdrawals: saving, tax, imports.
  • MPS, MPT, MPM: the shares of extra income saved, paid in tax and spent on imports (marginal propensities to save, tax and import).
  • MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM. Here MPC = the share spent on domestic output, so MPC + MPW = 1.
  • k = 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM).
  • Cambridge: MPT is the MRT, so k = 1 ÷ (MPS + MRT + MPM).
  • Illustrative data: MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.2.
EconomyWithdrawalsk
Closed, no governmentMPS = 0.11 ÷ 0.1 = 10
Closed, with government0.1 + 0.2 = 0.31 ÷ 0.3 = 3.33
Open, with government0.1 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.51 ÷ 0.5 = 2
  • Exports rise by 30 billion in the open economy: national income rises by 2 × 30 = 60 billion.
  • More withdrawals → a smaller multiplier.
Quick check
?An open economy has MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.25 and MPM = 0.1 (illustrative data). Government spending rises by 20 billion. By how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 2

The multiplier on the AD/AS diagram

  • Step 1: an injection rises (say G). AD shifts right by the first-round amount: AD1 → AD2.
  • Step 2: the multiplier adds further rounds: AD2 → AD3.
  • Step 3: with upward-sloping AS, part of the extra demand raises the price level. Real output rises by less than the full shift.
  • Near full capacity, most of the effect is on prices, not real output.
Mission 2

How big in reality?

  • Real-world multipliers are estimated, not observed. Estimates vary widely.
  • IMF (2012): forecasts for 2010–11 had assumed fiscal multipliers of about 0.5. Its own estimate for the years after the 2008 crisis was higher: about 0.9 to 1.7.
  • Multipliers tend to be larger in deep recessions with spare capacity.
  • They tend to be smaller near full capacity, in very open economies, or if interest rates rise in response.
  • Economists still disagree on the size: it matters for debates on stimulus and austerity.
Mission 2Evaluate

How strong is the multiplier?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: near full capacity, extra demand mostly raises prices.
  • Withdrawals: the more of each round that is saved, taxed or spent on imports, the smaller the multiplier.
  • Confidence: worried households may save extra income.
  • Time lags: the rounds take months to work through.
  • Size: a small injection in a large economy barely shows.
Quick check
?A large car factory closes, and investment in the region falls sharply. What happens to national income, other things equal?
Mission check

?Two economies get the same rise in government spending. In Economy X, households spend much of any extra income on imports. In Economy Y, they buy mostly home-produced goods. Saving and tax rates are the same. Which statement is correct?

The more of each round that is withdrawn (saving, tax, imports), the smaller the multiplier.

Mission 3

The consumption function

  • C = a + bY
  • a = autonomous consumption: spending even when income is zero (from savings or borrowing).
  • bY = induced consumption: spending that rises with income.
  • b = the MPC: the slope of the consumption function.

  • At low income, saving is negative (dissaving). At Y = 160, C = Y: the break-even level of income.
Mission 3

The savings function

  • S = −a + (1 − b)Y
  • Here: S = −40 + 0.25Y.
  • −40 = autonomous dissaving; 0.25 = the MPS: the slope of the savings function.
  • MPC + MPS = 1 (closed economy, no government): 0.75 + 0.25 = 1.
  • APC = C ÷ Y. At Y = 400: 340 ÷ 400 = 0.85. The APC falls as income rises, because autonomous consumption is spread over more income.
  • Say: "MPC is the slope; APC is total C ÷ total Y" — not: "MPC = C ÷ Y".
Mission 3

The 45-degree line

  • Plot aggregate expenditure (AE) up the side and national income (Y) along the bottom.
  • The 45° line joins every point where AE = Y.
  • It is a reference line: it shows where planned spending exactly matches output.
  • Above the line: spending exceeds output. Below it: output exceeds spending.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: C = 50 + 0.8Y. What is saving when national income is 500?
Mission 3

Equilibrium income

  • Closed economy, no government: AE = C + I.
  • Illustrative data: C = 40 + 0.75Y; investment I = 60 (autonomous).
  • AE = 100 + 0.75Y. Equilibrium where AE crosses the 45° line: AE = Y.
  • Y = 100 + 0.75Y → 0.25Y = 100 → Y = 400.
  • Check: at Y = 400, S = −40 + 0.25 × 400 = 60 = I. Withdrawals = injections.
Mission 3

How the economy gets there

  • At Y = 500: AE = 40 + 375 + 60 = 475. Output exceeds spending by 25.
  • Unsold goods pile up: an unplanned rise in stocks (inventories). Firms cut output: Y falls.
  • At Y = 300: AE = 40 + 225 + 60 = 325. Spending exceeds output by 25.
  • Stocks run down unexpectedly. Firms raise output: Y rises.
  • Only at Y = 400 do firms sell exactly what they produce: no reason to change output.
  • Say: "equilibrium income: planned spending equals output" — not: "the best level of income".

Illustrative data, same model: C = 40 + 0.75Y, I = 60, equilibrium 400.

  • Takeaway: a change in autonomous spending changes equilibrium income by the multiplier, 1 ÷ (1 − MPC), times the change.
Mission 3

Adding government and trade

  • Open economy with government: AE = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Tax and imports also leak out of each round. The slope of AE is the share of extra income spent on domestic output: 1 − MPW.
  • MPW = MPS + MRT + MPM.
  • Illustrative data: MPS = 0.1, MRT = 0.2, MPM = 0.1. MPW = 0.4. Slope of AE = 0.6. Multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.4 = 2.5.
  • Exports fall by 10 → AE shifts down by 10 → equilibrium income falls by 2.5 × 10 = 25.
  • More leakages → a flatter AE line → a smaller multiplier.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for a closed economy with no government: C = 30 + 0.6Y, and investment is 50. What is equilibrium national income?
Mission 3

Full-employment income vs equilibrium income

  • Full-employment income (Yfe): the output produced when all who want to work at current wages have jobs.
  • Equilibrium income (Ye): where AE = Y.
  • Keynes's key point: nothing guarantees Ye = Yfe. The economy can settle below full employment and stay there.
  • Illustrative data: Yfe = 480, but Ye = 400.
  • Output is 80 short of full employment: workers stay unemployed while firms have no reason to raise output.
Mission 3

Deflationary and inflationary gaps

  • Deflationary gap: the amount by which AE falls short of output at full-employment income.
  • Measured vertically, at Yfe, between the 45° line and AE.
  • Here: at Y = 480, AE = 40 + 360 + 60 = 460. Gap = 480 − 460 = 20.
  • Raise AE by 20 (e.g. investment up by 20, or government spending of 20 added) → new Ye = 400 + 4 × 20 = 480. Full employment.
  • Inflationary gap: the amount by which AE exceeds output at Yfe. Real output cannot rise further, so the excess demand pushes up prices.
  • Link: shortfall in income (80) = multiplier (4) × deflationary gap (20).
  • Say: "the deflationary gap is the shortfall in spending at full employment (20)" — not: "the gap between Ye and Yfe (80)".
Mission 3

Worked example: an inflationary gap

  • Illustrative data: Yfe = 1,000. AE at Y = 1,000 is 1,020.
  • Spending exceeds what the economy can produce at full employment by 20: an inflationary gap of 20.
  • To remove it, AE must fall by 20: cut G, raise taxes, or let higher interest rates reduce C and I.
  • If the MPC were 0.8 (closed economy, no government), the multiplier would be 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. In this model, Ye would sit 5 × 20 = 100 above Yfe, but real output cannot rise beyond Yfe: the excess shows up as inflation.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a closed economy with no government has full-employment income of 600. Equilibrium income is 520, and the MPC is 0.75. What is the size of the deflationary gap?
Mission 3Evaluate

How useful is the Keynesian cross?

It depends on:

  • Fixed prices: the model holds the price level constant until full employment. In reality prices start rising before Yfe (AD/AS is needed for that).
  • Knowing Yfe: full-employment income is estimated, not observed, so the gap's size is uncertain.
  • Stable propensities: the MPC and MPM can change with confidence and wealth, so the multiplier is not fixed.
  • Time: equilibrium is reached through many rounds; policy may arrive late.
  • Supply side: the model focuses on demand. If unemployment is structural, more AE will not close it.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: an economy's full-employment income is 800. At that income, planned aggregate expenditure would be 830. Which statement is correct?

Equilibrium income is set by spending, and it can fall short of, or press beyond, full-employment income.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does business spending on new machines and buildings swing so much more than household spending?

Make a guess: Why does business spending on new machines and buildings swing so much more than household spending?

Mission 4

What counts as investment

  • Investment (I): spending by firms on capital goods used to produce future output.
  • Examples: factories, offices, machines, vehicles, computers, software.
  • Fixed capital: long-lasting assets such as buildings and machinery. In national accounts: gross fixed capital formation.
  • Inventories (stocks) of raw materials and unsold goods: also counted in investment.
  • Buying shares or second-hand assets is not investment: no new capital is made.
  • I is an injection into the circular flow and a component of AD.
Mission 4

Gross and net investment

  • Depreciation (capital consumption): capital worn out or out of date in a year.
  • Gross investment: all spending on new capital goods, including replacements.
  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation. The change in the capital stock.
  • Illustrative data: capital stock 1,000 at the start of the year.
  • Gross investment 150, depreciation 100 → net investment +50. Capital stock ends at 1,050.
  • Gross investment 80, depreciation 100 → net investment −20. Capital stock shrinks to 980.
  • Say: "net investment is negative" when replacement falls short — not: "firms bought no capital".
Quick check
?In a year, an economy's gross investment is 120 billion and depreciation is 135 billion (illustrative data). Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

What drives investment

Firms invest when the expected return beats the cost.

  • Interest rates: the cost of borrowing, or the return given up on retained profits. Higher rates → less I.
  • Expectations and business confidence: expected future sales and profits.
  • Growth of demand: firms need more capacity when sales rise.
  • Technology: new machines can cut costs or make new products.
  • Business taxes: profit taxes and tax allowances on investment change the return.
  • Credit availability: firms need banks willing to lend.
Mission 4

Worked example: is the project worth it?

Illustrative data. A firm can buy a machine for 1,000,000. It expects a return of 6% a year (60,000).

  • Borrow at 4%: interest cost 40,000 a year < 60,000 return. Worth it.
  • Borrow at 7%: interest cost 70,000 a year > 60,000 return. Not worth it.
  • Using its own savings? It still gives up 4% or 7% it could earn elsewhere: the opportunity cost.
  • If expected sales fall, the expected return falls too, at any interest rate.

Rates, confidence and AD

Mission 4

Animal spirits

  • The return on a factory comes over 10, 20 or 30 years. Nobody knows that future well.
  • Keynes (1936) called the urge to invest anyway "animal spirits": "a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction".
  • Moods spread: optimism → many firms invest; pessimism → many wait.
  • Investment can be postponed: an old machine can run another year. Bread and rent cannot wait.
  • So investment rises and falls far more than consumption over the cycle.
Mission 4

The accelerator

  • Accelerator: net investment depends on the change in output (demand), not its level.
  • Net investment = capital–output ratio × change in output.
  • Illustrative data: 1 machine per 100 units of output. No machines wear out.

  • Year 5: demand steady → net investment zero.
  • Small changes in the growth of demand cause big swings in investment.
Quick check
?A firm needs 1 machine for every 50 units of output. Machines do not wear out. Demand over four years is 500, 600, 700 and 750 units (illustrative data). What happens to net investment between year 3 and year 4?
Mission 4

Multiplier-accelerator and the cycle

  • Multiplier: a rise in I raises income by more than the rise in I (see Mission 2).
  • Accelerator: faster income growth raises I further.
  • Together: I ↑ → income ↑↑ → I ↑ → … a boom.
  • When growth slows, the accelerator cuts I → the multiplier cuts income → a downturn.
  • Ceiling: full capacity limits output. Floor: gross investment cannot fall below zero.
  • So the two can turn the economy, giving the economic cycle.
Mission 4Evaluate

Will cheaper borrowing raise investment?

It depends on:

  • Expectations: in a slump, low rates may not outweigh gloomy sales forecasts.
  • Credit: after a financial crisis, banks may refuse to lend at any rate.
  • Spare capacity: firms with idle machines need no new ones.
  • Time lags: projects take months or years to plan.
  • Accelerator limits: firms may not expect a rise in demand to last; the capital–output ratio is not fixed; idle capacity breaks the link.
Mission check

?After a financial crisis, the central bank cuts interest rates to a record low. Business confidence collapses, and banks sharply cut lending to firms. What is most likely to happen to investment?

Investment depends on expected returns and finance, not just the interest rate. That is why it swings so much.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Yd = C + S. Consumption is the largest part of AD.
  • Interest rates, confidence, wealth, taxes and credit move C; a change in C shifts AD.
  • MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔYd; MPC + MPS = 1. In C = a + bYd, b is the MPC.

Mission 2

  • An injection raises national income by more than itself, through rounds of spending.
  • k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • With saving, tax and imports: k = 1 ÷ MPW.

Mission 3

  • C = a + bY; S = −a + (1 − b)Y. Autonomous parts do not depend on income; induced parts do.
  • Equilibrium income: AE = Y (where AE crosses the 45° line); withdrawals = injections.
  • Equilibrium need not be at full employment. Deflationary gap: AE short of Yfe at Yfe. Inflationary gap: AE above Yfe at Yfe.

Mission 4

  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation.
  • I depends on interest rates, expectations, demand growth, technology, taxes and credit.
  • Accelerator: net investment depends on the change in output.
