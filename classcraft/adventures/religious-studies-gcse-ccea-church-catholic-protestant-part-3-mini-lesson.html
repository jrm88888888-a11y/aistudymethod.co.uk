Mission 1

The church year

  • Christians mark the life of Jesus in a yearly cycle: the church year (liturgical year).
  • In Western churches it begins with Advent, four Sundays before Christmas.
  • Order: Advent → Christmas → Epiphany → Lent → Holy Week → Easter → Ascension → Pentecost → Ordinary Time.
  • Fixed feasts keep their date (Christmas, 25 December); movable feasts depend on Easter.
  • Colours in church mark the season: purple for preparation, white or gold for celebration, red for the Spirit and martyrs, green for Ordinary Time.
  • Significance: the whole story of salvation is retold every year, so believers live it, not just learn it.
Mission 1

Advent: getting ready twice

  • Advent (Latin adventus, "coming"): the four weeks before Christmas.
  • Christians prepare for two comings of Jesus:
    • his birth in Bethlehem, celebrated at Christmas;
    • his Second Coming at the end of time, as judge and king.
  • Advent wreath: a candle lit each Sunday; in many churches a fifth, white candle for Christ on Christmas Day.
  • Advent services: carol services and an Advent procession moving from darkness to light.
  • Prayer, fasting and charity: Catholics are encouraged to go to confession; many churches collect food and gifts for those in need.
  • Advent calendars began as a way of counting the days in prayer.
Mission 1Read the source

Two birth accounts

  • Luke 2:1–20 (paraphrase): a census sends Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem; Jesus is born and laid in a manger; angels announce to shepherds a Saviour, "Christ the Lord", and sing "Glory to God in the highest".
  • Matthew 2:1–12 (paraphrase): wise men (Magi) from the east follow a star and ask in Jerusalem where the king of the Jews is born; King Herod is troubled; they find the child and bring gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Luke stresses the poor and lowly: shepherds are first to hear.
  • Matthew stresses Jesus as king, recognised by non-Jews from far away.

Show answer

Luke: shepherds. Matthew: Magi.

Q2 Does Matthew say how many Magi came?

Show answer

No. "Three" is a tradition based on the three gifts.

Mission 1

Christmas celebrates the Incarnation

  • Incarnation: Christians believe God the Son became a human being in Jesus. "The Word became flesh" (John 1:14).
  • Christmas celebrates that God came close to humanity, as a vulnerable baby.
  • The Bible gives no date for Jesus' birth. 25 December was chosen centuries later.
  • Significance:
    • God shares human life, so every human life has dignity;
    • joy and thanksgiving for God's gift;
    • giving to others, especially the poor, as Jesus was born poor.
  • Say: "Christmas celebrates the Incarnation" — not: "Christmas celebrates the resurrection".
Mission 1

How Christians celebrate Christmas

  • Midnight Mass (Catholic) or Midnight Communion (Anglican): the first celebration of Christmas, late on Christmas Eve.
  • Christmas Day services; Orthodox churches that follow the old (Julian) calendar celebrate on 7 January.
  • Carol services, often Nine Lessons and Carols: Bible readings from the Fall to the birth of Jesus.
  • Crib (nativity scene): begun by St Francis of Assisi in 1223.
  • Christingle: an orange (the world), a candle (Jesus, the Light of the World), a red ribbon (his blood), fruits (God's gifts).
  • Nativity plays, giving presents, family meals, and helping the lonely and homeless.

Mission 1

Where 25 December came from

  • The first record of Christians celebrating Jesus' birth on 25 December: Rome, AD 336.
  • Why that date? Historians give two main theories:
    • it replaced a Roman winter festival of the "Unconquered Sun";
    • it was calculated: Jesus was believed to have been conceived on 25 March, so born nine months later.
  • Many Eastern Christians first celebrated the birth on 6 January.
  • 1647: the Puritan Parliament banned Christmas in England as unbiblical; it returned in 1660.
  • 1840s: Christmas trees, cards and Dickens' A Christmas Carol (1843) shape the modern British Christmas.
Mission 1

Epiphany: Christ shown to the world

  • Epiphany (Greek, "appearing" or "revealing"): 6 January, the twelfth day after Christmas.
  • Western churches: the visit of the Magi. Jesus is revealed as Saviour of all nations, not only the Jews.
  • The gifts are traditionally read as signs: gold for a king, frankincense for God, myrrh for his death.
  • Eastern churches (Theophany): Jesus' baptism; priests bless water, sometimes throwing a cross into the sea or a river.
  • Customs:
    • Chalking the doors: "20 + C + M + B + 26" in blessed chalk: the year and the Magi's traditional names, or Christus mansionem benedicat, "May Christ bless this house";
    • King cake: a cake with a small figure or bean hidden inside; the finder is "king" for the day;
    • in Spain and Latin America, children receive gifts on 6 January (Three Kings' Day).

Mission 1

Christmas in Britain today

  • A public holiday: 25 and 26 December. Many people who rarely attend church go at Christmas.
  • Schools hold nativity plays and carol services; the monarch's Christmas broadcast often speaks of faith.
  • Social significance: families gather, people give to charity, churches open their doors for lunches and night shelters.
  • Commercialisation: shopping, advertising and Father Christmas can take centre stage; many people celebrate without the religious meaning.
  • For Christians it remains a holy day: worship, the Incarnation, and generosity to others.
Mission 1Diversity

Different ways and different attitudes

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican: the liturgical year in full; a night service (Midnight Mass for Catholics); a season of feasting from Christmas to Epiphany.
  • Many Protestant churches: carol services, nativity plays, preaching on the birth; less emphasis on the liturgical calendar.
  • Traditional Quakers: every day is equally holy, so no special festivals (many Quakers today mark Christmas informally).
  • Some Reformed churches keep only what the Bible commands, so they do not keep Christmas as a church feast.
  • Jehovah's Witnesses (who reject the Trinity) do not celebrate Christmas.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Christmas has lost its religious meaning in Britain today."

  • For: spending, adverts and Father Christmas dominate; most people do not go to church at Christmas.
  • For: many families celebrate with no mention of Jesus; for some, it is mainly a holiday.
  • Against: carol services and Midnight Masses are among the fullest services of the year.
  • Against: nativity plays, charity appeals and church lunches keep the story and its values public.
  • Against: for Christians the meaning is unchanged: the Incarnation is celebrated whatever the shops do.
  • Judge it by: Meaning for whom: Christians, or society as a whole? Can commercial and religious Christmas exist side by side? Are generosity and family gatherings themselves religious values?

Mission 2

Lent: forty days

  • Lent: the 40 days of preparation before Easter (Sundays are not counted in the West).
  • It recalls Jesus' 40 days in the wilderness, fasting and tempted, before his ministry (Matthew 4:1–11, paraphrase).
  • In the early Church, Lent was when new believers prepared for baptism at Easter.
  • A season of repentance (turning back to God) and self-discipline. Purple in church.
  • Significance: Christians share Jesus' struggle so as to share his victory at Easter.
Mission 2

Shrove Tuesday and Ash Wednesday

  • Shrove Tuesday: to be "shriven" was to confess sins and be forgiven. Rich foods were used up: pancakes.
  • Ash Wednesday: the first day of Lent.
  • Ashes (from burning last year's palm crosses) are marked in a cross on the forehead, with words such as:

> "For you are dust, and you shall return to dust." (Genesis 3:19)

  • Or: "Repent, and believe the Good News" (based on Mark 1:15).
  • Meaning: human life is short; turn back to God.
  • Catholics fast on Ash Wednesday (and Good Friday). Anglicans, Lutherans and Methodists also often hold Ash Wednesday services.
Mission 2

Keeping Lent today

  • Jesus names three practices: giving to the poor, prayer, fasting (Matthew 6:1–18, paraphrase).
  • Giving up a luxury (chocolate, social media), or taking up something good: extra prayer, volunteering.
  • Charity: Lent appeals, e.g. CAFOD's Family Fast Day: eat simply and give the money saved.
  • Catholics often pray the Stations of the Cross on Fridays; many churches run Lent study groups.
  • Orthodox Great Lent: a stricter fast (usually no meat, dairy or wine) from Clean Monday.
  • Some evangelical and free churches do not keep Lent formally; Christians are free to fast at any time.

Mission 2

Palm Sunday and Maundy Thursday

  • Palm Sunday: Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey; crowds wave branches and shout "Hosanna!" (Matthew 21:1–11, paraphrase).
    • Customs: processions with palms; palm crosses given out; the Passion is read, looking ahead to the cross.
  • Maundy Thursday: the Last Supper (Matthew 26:17–29). Jesus gives bread and wine as his body and blood; Christians see this as the first Eucharist.
    • "Maundy" from Latin mandatum, "commandment": love one another (John 13:34).
    • Foot-washing: priests or ministers wash people's feet, following Jesus:

> "If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet." (John 13:14)

  • That night: the altar is stripped bare; people keep watch, remembering Jesus' prayer and arrest in Gethsemane.
Mission 2

Good Friday and Holy Saturday

  • Good Friday: Jesus' crucifixion (Matthew 27:45–55: darkness at noon; Jesus' cry; the Temple curtain torn; a centurion says he was the Son of God, paraphrase).
  • Why "Good"? The word: "good" here is an old English sense meaning "holy" (some say it came from "God's Friday", but dictionaries doubt this).
  • Christians also explain the name by what they believe his death achieved: salvation, forgiveness and reconciliation with God.
  • Customs: a bare church; veneration of the cross; the Stations of the Cross (14 scenes of the way to Calvary); a three-hour service (noon to 3 p.m.); walks of witness by churches together; fasting; hot cross buns.
  • Catholic churches celebrate no Mass on Good Friday.
  • Holy Saturday: quiet waiting at the tomb. After dark, the Easter Vigil begins:
    • a new fire; the Paschal candle is lit and carried into the dark church: "The light of Christ";
    • readings from creation to the resurrection; baptisms; the first Eucharist of Easter.
Mission 2

Easter Sunday

  • Women find the tomb empty. The angel says:

> "He is not here, for he has risen, just like he said." (Matthew 28:6)

  • Easter celebrates Jesus' resurrection: the most important festival of the Christian year.
  • Customs: sunrise services outdoors (the women came at dawn); flowers; white and gold; "Alleluia" sung again after Lent; feasting.
  • Eggs: new life breaking out, like the empty tomb. Orthodox Christians dye them red.
  • Orthodox Pascha: a midnight service; candles lit from one flame; "Christ is risen!" "He is risen indeed!"
  • Origins: linked to the Jewish Passover (Pascha); its date is the first Sunday after the first full moon after the spring equinox. Orthodox churches calculate it by the older (Julian) calendar, so their Easter is often later.

"If Christ has not been raised"

Some Christians in Corinth said there is no resurrection of the dead (1 Corinthians 15:12). Paul answers:

> "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14)

> "But now Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruit of those who are asleep." (1 Corinthians 15:20)

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22)

  • "The last enemy that will be abolished is death." (1 Corinthians 15:26)
  • First fruit: the first part of the harvest, a promise that the rest will follow.

Mission 2

Easter in Britain today

  • Public holidays: Good Friday, and Easter Monday (except in Scotland); school holidays around them.
  • Churches are fuller at Easter; many hold walks of witness and Passion plays in public places.
  • Commercial Easter: chocolate eggs, bunnies, cards and adverts; for many people a spring holiday.
  • For Christians: the most important festival; every Sunday is a "little Easter", recalling the resurrection.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Easter is more important than Christmas for Christians."

  • For: Paul makes the resurrection the foundation of faith: without it, "your faith also is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14); Christians believe Jesus' death and resurrection bring salvation.
  • For: Easter has the longest preparation (Lent) and season (fifty days); Sunday worship recalls it every week; the Orthodox call it the "Feast of Feasts".
  • Against: without the Incarnation there would be no death and resurrection; Christmas shows God's love in becoming human.
  • Against: Christmas reaches more people in Britain today, so it may matter more for sharing the faith.
  • Another view: they are one story; ranking them splits what belongs together.
  • Judge it by: Which belief is foundational? Importance in belief, or in practice? Importance for Christians, or for society?

Mission 3

After Easter: the fifty days

  • Easter is not one day but a season of fifty days (Eastertide).
  • Day 40: Ascension. Jesus appeared to his disciples for forty days, then was taken up to heaven (Acts 1:3, 9, paraphrase).
  • Day 50: Pentecost (Greek, "fiftieth"). The Holy Spirit comes on the apostles (Acts 2).
  • Pentecost was already a Jewish festival, Shavuot: a harvest feast that also remembers the giving of the Law at Sinai.
  • The Paschal candle, lit at the Easter Vigil, burns at services throughout the season.
Mission 3

Ascension Day

  • A Thursday, forty days after Easter. Jesus is taken up and a cloud hides him; angels say he will return in the same way (Acts 1:9–11, paraphrase).
  • Meaning for Christians:
    • Jesus' earthly work is complete; he reigns with the Father ("seated at the right hand", in the Creeds);
    • he takes human nature into heaven;
    • he will come again;
    • he promises the Holy Spirit and sends the disciples out.
  • Customs:
    • extinguishing the Paschal candle after the Gospel: Jesus' bodily presence on earth has ended (an older custom; many Catholic churches now keep it lit until Pentecost);
    • processions, such as English parishes "beating the bounds" at Ascensiontide;
    • an all-night vigil, and nine days of prayer to Pentecost, waiting for the Spirit as the apostles did.
Mission 3Read the source

Wind and fire

Before ascending, Jesus promises:

> "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, …" (Acts 1:8)

  • Acts 2:1–4 (paraphrase): on the day of Pentecost the disciples are together in one place. A sound like a rushing wind fills the house. Something like tongues of fire rests on each of them. They are filled with the Holy Spirit and speak in other languages.
  • Peter preaches to the crowd; about three thousand are baptised that day (Acts 2:41, paraphrase).

Q2 Why is Pentecost called the "birthday of the Church"?

Mission 3

Celebrating Pentecost

  • Red vestments and decorations: the fire of the Spirit. Some worshippers wear red.
  • Whitsun ("White Sunday"): the old English name, from the white robes of those baptised that day.
  • Baptisms and confirmations: receiving the Spirit.
  • Whit walks: processions of witness by churches, especially in north-west England.
  • Kneeling Prayers: on Pentecost evening, Orthodox Christians kneel for the first time since Easter, while the priest says long prayers for the Spirit. Churches are often decorated with green branches.
  • Pentecostal and charismatic churches stress the Spirit's gifts today, such as speaking in tongues and healing.

Mission 3

Saints' Days

  • In the New Testament, "saints" means all believers (e.g. Romans 1:7).
  • The Church also honours saints: people of outstanding holiness, many of them martyrs.
  • Each has a feast day, often the day of their death: their "birthday" into heaven.
  • All Saints' Day (1 November) honours all saints, known and unknown.
  • St Patrick's Day (17 March): the patron saint of Ireland; services across denominations and a public holiday in Northern Ireland.
  • Catholic and Orthodox: saints are examples, and they also pray for believers (intercession). The Catholic Church formally canonises saints.
  • Protestant: saints are examples of faith, a "cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1, paraphrase); prayer is to God alone. Many Presbyterian churches do not keep saints' days.
Mission 3

Harvest

> "While the earth remains, seed time and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night will not cease." (Genesis 8:22)

  • Harvest Thanksgiving: usually September or October.
  • Modern form begun by Robert Hawker at Morwenstow, Cornwall, in 1843.
  • Churches are decorated with produce; hymns such as "We plough the fields and scatter".
  • Gifts are shared: today often tins and packets for food banks, or donations to aid agencies.
  • Meaning: thanks to God as Creator and provider; care for creation; sharing with the hungry.
  • Very popular in Protestant churches in Northern Ireland, often with a harvest supper.

Mission 3

How festivals have changed

  • Holy days into holidays: in the UK, the Whit Monday holiday became a fixed late-May bank holiday (1971).
  • Some feasts are moved to Sunday, so that more people can attend.
  • Shops open on Sundays and feast days; fewer people keep fasts such as Lent strictly.
  • Online services: many churches have streamed festival worship since 2020.
  • Harvest: from fresh produce to tins for food banks.
  • Commercial pressure: Christmas and Easter are heavily marketed in many countries; Halloween (All Hallows' Eve, the night before All Saints) is now mainly a secular event.
  • Societies with many faiths and none: Christian festivals sit alongside others.
Mission 3Diversity

How Christians feel about the changes

  • Welcome them: adapting is how the Church has always reached people; online worship lets the housebound join in.
  • Concerned: holy days become days off; the meaning gets lost; preparation (fasting) disappears.
  • Keep the tradition: Orthodox and traditional Catholic communities often keep the old dates, fasts and services.
  • Use them as opportunities: e.g. some churches hold "light parties" at Halloween, or carol services in shopping centres.
  • Most Christians agree the meaning matters more than the form; they disagree on how much the form can change without losing it.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Ascension and Pentecost matter as much as Christmas and Easter."

  • For: the story of salvation is incomplete without them: Jesus reigns, and the Spirit empowers the Church.
  • For: Pentecost is the Church's "birthday"; the Ascension is in both Creeds; the Orthodox count both among their Great Feasts.
  • Against: Easter is the foundation: without the resurrection there is nothing to ascend from.
  • Against: in practice, far fewer people attend; Ascension is often moved to a Sunday; society barely notices them.
  • Another view: they are one season: Ascension and Pentecost complete Easter rather than compete with it.
  • Judge it by: Importance in belief or in practice? For Christians or for society? Does the Spirit's work today make Pentecost most relevant?

Mission 4

What baptism is

  • Baptism is the ceremony of initiation: Christians believe it makes a person a member of the Church, the Body of Christ.
  • It uses water, with the words "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13–17), and he sent his followers out "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Most churches baptise babies (infant baptism). Some baptise only people old enough to say they believe (believers' baptism).
  • Most Christians call it a sacrament; Baptists and many Pentecostals call it an ordinance.
Mission 4Read the source

Born of water and Spirit

Jesus to Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher who came to him at night:

> "Most certainly I tell you, unless one is born of water and Spirit, he can't enter into God's Kingdom." (John 3:5)

> "We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)

Q1 According to John 3:5, what two things are needed to enter God's Kingdom?

Show answer

Being born of water and of the Spirit.

Q2 In Romans 6:4, what does going into and coming out of the water stand for?

Show answer

Dying and rising with Christ to a new life.

Mission 4

Into the life of the Trinity

  • Baptism is "in the name of" Father, Son and Holy Spirit: Christians believe the person is joined to God's own life.
  • Father: the baptised become God's adopted children.
  • Son: they share in Jesus' death and resurrection (Romans 6:4).
  • Holy Spirit: they receive the Spirit, who makes them holy.
  • Catholic teaching: baptism washes away original sin and all sin, gives grace, and marks the person for ever, so it is given only once.
  • It is the "door" to the other sacraments: no one receives Confirmation or the Eucharist without being baptised.
Mission 4

Infant baptism: the rite

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian churches baptise babies; usually at a parish service.
  • Welcome: the priest or minister traces the sign of the cross on the baby's forehead.
  • Promises: parents and godparents reject evil and profess the faith for the child; the congregation promises support.
  • Water: poured three times over the head (or the baby is dipped), with the Trinity words: a sign of washing from sin and new birth.
  • Catholic rite: anointing with chrism (perfumed oil: sharing in Christ's role as priest, prophet and king), a white garment (clothed with Christ, cleansed from sin), and a candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle (the light of the risen Christ).
  • In the Presbyterian Church in Ireland, the minister sprinkles or pours water; parents promise to bring the child up in the faith; there is no anointing or candle.
  • Godparents (sponsors) promise to help the child grow in faith; Catholic godparents must be confirmed Catholics aged at least 16.

Mission 4

Why baptise babies?

  • Grace is a gift: God's love comes first; a baby cannot earn it and does not need to.
  • Original sin: Catholics believe baptism frees from original sin, so it should not be delayed (Catholic teaching also entrusts unbaptised children to God's mercy).
  • Whole households were baptised in the early Church (Acts 16:15, 16:33).
  • A covenant sign: Presbyterian and other Reformed Christians compare it to circumcision, given to Jewish baby boys (Colossians 2:11–12).
  • Jesus welcomed children and said the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14).
  • The child later makes the promises personally, at confirmation.
Mission 4

Believers' baptism

  • Practised by Baptists, most Pentecostals and many independent churches, for people old enough to profess faith (often teenagers or adults).
  • Testimony: the candidate tells the church how they came to faith in Jesus.
  • Immersion: the minister and candidate stand in a baptistery (pool in the church floor), river or sea; the candidate is lowered backwards under the water and raised up.
  • Symbolism: dying to the old life, buried with Christ, rising to new life (Romans 6:4).
  • Reasons: Peter said, "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38): faith comes first; Jesus was baptised as an adult; the New Testament never clearly describes a baby being baptised.
  • For Baptists, baptism is an act of obedience and public witness; it does not itself save.
Mission 4

Dedication

  • Churches that practise believers' baptism often hold a dedication or thanksgiving service for a new baby.
  • No water. The minister prays for the child; parents promise to bring the child up to know Jesus; the church promises support.
  • Model: Hannah dedicates Samuel to God (1 Samuel 1:27–28); Jesus blesses children (Mark 10:16).
  • The child can later choose believers' baptism.
Mission 4Diversity

The debate over infant baptism

  • Catholic: baptism is necessary for salvation for those who know the Gospel, and frees from original sin; babies should be baptised soon after birth; unbaptised adults are baptised after preparation, usually at the Easter Vigil.
  • Orthodox: infants receive baptism, chrismation and Communion together: full members from the start.
  • Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian: infant baptism is a sign of God's grace and covenant; adults who were not baptised as babies are baptised on profession of faith.
  • Baptist, most Pentecostal: only believers should be baptised; many Baptist churches baptise people who were baptised as babies, seeing infant baptism as not yet a baptism of faith.
  • Common ground: almost all churches accept each other's baptism if water and the Trinity words were used (Baptists are the main exception for infant baptism; some Orthodox churches also baptise converts afresh).

Mission 4Evaluate

"Babies should not be baptised."

  • Reasons for: faith should be a free personal choice. The New Testament pattern is "repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38). Baptism by immersion pictures death and resurrection (Romans 6:4), which a baby cannot understand.
  • Reasons against: grace is God's gift, not a reward for understanding. Households were baptised (Acts 16:33). Original sin (Catholic view) means baptism should not wait. The child can confirm the promises later.
  • Judge it by: Is baptism mainly God's action or the believer's response? What does the New Testament show? Does the community's promise make up for the child's?
Mission 4Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs about infant baptism.

  • Belief 1: Catholics believe babies should be baptised. Develop: baptism frees a person from original sin and gives God's grace, which a baby can receive without earning it.
  • Belief 2: Baptists believe only believers should be baptised. Develop: baptism is a public sign of a person's own faith, so it must follow repentance, as in Acts 2:38.

Mission 5

One meal, many names

  • Christians share bread and wine, as Jesus did at his Last Supper. Most churches call it a sacrament (Baptists: an ordinance).
  • Eucharist: Greek for "thanksgiving".
  • Mass: Catholic name (from the Latin dismissal, "Go, it is the sending").
  • Holy Communion: sharing with Christ and each other (Anglican, Methodist and many others).
  • Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread: many Protestant churches.
  • Divine Liturgy: the Orthodox service in which the Eucharist is celebrated.
Mission 5Read the source

The Last Supper

At the Passover meal, the night before he died:

> "As they were eating, Jesus took bread, gave thanks for it, and broke it. He gave to the disciples and said, 'Take, eat; this is my body.'" (Matthew 26:26)

> "for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins." (Matthew 26:28)

  • Paul's earlier account adds Jesus' command: "Do this in memory of me." (1 Corinthians 11:24)

Mission 5

The Catholic Mass

  • Liturgy of the Word: readings from the Bible and a homily (sermon).
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist: the priest says Jesus' words over unleavened bread (hosts) and wine (the consecration).
  • Catholics believe the bread and wine become the body and blood of Christ: the Real Presence.
  • Transubstantiation: the substance (what it really is) changes; the appearances (taste, look) of bread and wine remain (Council of Trent, 1551).
  • The Mass makes present Christ's one sacrifice on the cross; it does not repeat it.
  • Catholics must attend Mass on Sundays; those prepared and free from serious sin receive Communion.

Mission 5

Anglican and Lutheran Holy Communion

  • Church of England: Holy Communion is the main Sunday service in many parishes; people kneel or stand at the altar rail and receive both bread and wine.
  • Anglican beliefs vary: some (Anglo-Catholics) believe in a real, objective presence; many evangelical Anglicans stress a spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Lutherans: Christ's body and blood are truly present "in, with and under" the bread and wine; the bread is not changed in substance.
Mission 5

Memorial and spiritual presence

  • Memorial view (Huldrych Zwingli, 1484–1531): "this is my body" means "this represents my body". The bread and wine are symbols that help believers remember Christ's death.
  • Baptist practice: the Lord's Supper, often monthly; bread and small individual cups of (often non-alcoholic) wine are passed along the rows; usually all believers are welcome.
  • Spiritual presence (John Calvin, 1509–1564; Presbyterian and Reformed): the bread stays bread, but by the Holy Spirit believers truly feed on Christ through faith.
  • Presbyterian Church in Ireland: Communion several times a year; elders bring bread and wine to people in their seats; the minister reads Jesus' words from 1 Corinthians 11.
  • All these churches agree: Jesus commanded it, it proclaims his death "until he comes" (1 Corinthians 11:26), and it unites believers.

Mission 5Diversity

A spectrum of belief

  • Catholic: transubstantiation; Real Presence remains, so consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Orthodox: a real change, called a mystery, not defined in philosophical terms; leavened bread and wine given together on a spoon; babies receive too.
  • Lutheran: real presence in, with and under the bread and wine.
  • Anglican: a range, from real presence to spiritual presence.
  • Presbyterian (Reformed): spiritual presence received by faith.
  • Baptist and many evangelicals: memorial.
  • Quakers and The Salvation Army: no outward Communion; inward communion with Christ.
Mission 5

Why it matters

  • Unity with Christ: Catholics and Orthodox believe they receive Christ himself; others meet him spiritually or remember him.
  • Unity with each other: "we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Forgiveness and strength: grace to live as Christians in the week ahead.
  • Remembering and hoping: looking back to the cross and forward to Christ's return.
  • Sent out: the Mass ends by sending people to serve others.
  • A sadness: Christians disagree about who may receive, so many cannot share Communion together.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The Eucharist is the most important act of Christian worship."

  • Reasons for: Jesus commanded it (1 Corinthians 11:24). Catholics call it "source and summit"; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present. It unites the Church across the world.
  • Reasons against: many Protestants put preaching the Bible at the centre, with Communion monthly. Quakers and The Salvation Army worship without it. Jesus also stressed love in action (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus command? Is Christ present in it in a special way? Which practice most changes how Christians live?
Mission 5Worked answer

Catholic and Protestant interpretations

Question: Explain Catholic and Protestant interpretations of the Eucharist.

  • Catholic: at the consecration, bread and wine become Christ's body and blood (transubstantiation). Source: "this is my body" (Matthew 26:26), taken literally. Develop: so hosts are reserved in the tabernacle and adored.
  • Protestant (Baptist): the Lord's Supper is a memorial. Source: "Do this in memory of me" (1 Corinthians 11:24). Develop: so the bread is shared as a symbol, and the focus is remembering and obeying.
  • Common ground: both obey Jesus' command and proclaim his death.

Mission 6

The church is its people

  • church (small c): a building. Church (capital C): the community of believers.
  • The New Testament word ekklesia means an assembly: people called together.
  • A local church (in Catholic and Anglican life, a parish) is a community in one place.
  • It is the centre of Christian identity: where believers worship, are baptised, married and buried, and belong.
Mission 6

What a local church does

  • The first Christians met for teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayer, and shared with anyone in need (Acts 2:42–47, paraphrase).
  • Today a local church offers:
    • Worship: Sunday services, the sacraments, festivals;
    • Rites of passage: baptism, confirmation, marriage, funerals;
    • Learning: Bible study, preparation for the sacraments, groups for new Christians;
    • Fellowship: belonging, friendship, support in hard times;
    • Service: help for anyone in need, believer or not.
Mission 6

Nurturing families

  • Sunday schools and children's groups: Bible stories, prayer, friendship. (Begun in 1780 by Robert Raikes in Gloucester, to teach poor children to read.)
  • Youth clubs: safe places to meet and talk about faith and life.
  • Parent and toddler groups: support for parents, especially new or isolated ones.
  • Visiting the elderly and sick: at home, in hospital or care homes; Catholics bring Holy Communion to the housebound.
  • Preparation for baptism and marriage; support for the bereaved.
  • Why: Christians see the family as the first place faith is learned; the church acts as a wider family.

?A Catholic parish trains volunteers to take Holy Communion each Sunday to parishioners who are housebound. Which aim of the local church is this mainly fulfilling?
Mission 6

Food banks

  • A food bank gives emergency food to people in crisis.
  • Many are run by churches, often in the network of the charity Trussell (founded by Christians; its first food bank opened in Salisbury in 2000).
  • How it works: a professional (such as a doctor or social worker) refers someone; they receive several days' food, a welcome, and help to find longer-term support.
  • Volunteers come from many churches together, and from people of other faiths and none.
  • Why: "I was hungry and you gave me food… you did it to me" (Matthew 25:35–40, paraphrase); love of neighbour.
  • Some Christians also campaign on the causes of poverty, not only its effects.
Mission 6

Street pastors

  • Street pastors: trained volunteers from local churches who patrol town centres late on Friday and Saturday nights.
  • Begun in London in 2003 by the Rev Les Isaac (Ascension Trust).
  • They care, listen and help: water, flip-flops for people who can't walk in their shoes, foil blankets, sweeping up broken glass, staying with vulnerable people until help arrives.
  • They work alongside police and councils; they do not preach unless someone asks about their faith.
  • Why: to be Christ's presence on the streets; love of neighbour in practice (Luke 10:25–37, paraphrase).

Mission 6

Ecumenism and outreach

  • Ecumenism locally: churches of different denominations work and pray together.
    • Joint services, Good Friday walks of witness, the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity;
    • shared buildings in some towns;
    • joint projects: food banks, night shelters, street pastors.
  • Outreach: reaching beyond the congregation.
    • Courses exploring faith (e.g. Alpha); holiday clubs; carol services in public places;
    • debt advice (e.g. Christians Against Poverty), community cafés, warm spaces.
  • How this helps the believer: belonging, support, a way to live out faith. How it helps the area: services, friendship, people who notice those in need.
Mission 6

Community cohesion in Northern Ireland

  • Community cohesion: people from different backgrounds getting on, with a shared sense of belonging.
  • During the Troubles (late 1960s–1998) the two main communities were largely identified as Catholic and Protestant, so churches found themselves on either side of a divided society.
  • Churches have also worked to heal it:
    • the Clonard–Fitzroy Fellowship (from 1981): Catholic and Presbyterian congregations meeting across the divide;
    • Fr Alec Reid of Clonard Monastery helped open the way to the peace process;
    • the leaders of the four main churches (Catholic, Presbyterian, Church of Ireland, Methodist) speak and act together;
    • cross-community youth projects and Corrymeela.
  • Today churches also welcome newcomers and refugees.
  • Critics note that separate schools and church-linked identities can still keep communities apart.
Mission 6

Changing moral and social trends

  • Fewer people attend church, and more say they have no religion.
  • Family life has changed: more couples live together without marrying; divorce is more common.
  • Law has changed in Northern Ireland: abortion law was liberalised (2019–20) and same-sex marriage became legal (2020).
  • Individualism and consumerism: Sunday as a day for shopping and sport.
  • Churches respond differently:
    • some uphold traditional teaching and speak publicly for it (e.g. the Catholic Church, the Presbyterian Church in Ireland);
    • some members and smaller churches adapt their views;
    • many focus on pastoral care for people whatever their situation, and on new forms of worship.

Mission 6Evaluate

"The local church is still the heart of its community today."

  • For: food banks, street pastors, warm spaces and toddler groups serve everyone, not only members.
  • For: churches mark the big moments of life (baptisms, weddings, funerals) and gather the community at Christmas and in times of tragedy.
  • For: churches have helped build peace and cohesion in Northern Ireland.
  • Against: most people rarely attend; many towns have councils, charities and community centres that do the same work.
  • Against: in some places churches have divided communities rather than united them.
  • Judge it by: Heart for whom: believers or everyone? Measured by attendance, or by service? Does it differ between villages, cities and Northern Ireland?
Mission check

?A village church has only twelve regular members. It runs a weekly lunch and warm space for anyone, visits housebound neighbours, and holds a joint Sunday service once a month with the Methodists down the road. Which statement best describes what this church is doing?

A church is its people: it worships, cares for its members, and serves the whole community, believers or not.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christmas celebrates the Incarnation: God the Son born as Jesus (Luke 2: shepherds; Matthew 2: Magi); the date 25 December is a 4th-century tradition.
  • Advent prepares for Jesus' birth and his Second Coming, with prayer, wreaths, services and charity.
  • Epiphany (6 January) celebrates Christ revealed to all nations: the Magi in the West, Jesus' baptism in the East.

Mission 2

  • Holy Week moves from Palm Sunday (entry) and Maundy Thursday (Last Supper) to Good Friday (the cross); Easter celebrates the resurrection, the most important Christian festival.
  • Lent: forty days of prayer, fasting and giving, from Ash Wednesday, recalling Jesus in the wilderness.

Mission 3

  • Ascension (day 40) celebrates Jesus returning to the Father; Pentecost (day 50) celebrates the Spirit's coming with wind and fire, the Church's birthday.
  • Saints' Days honour holy people (with different Catholic and Protestant views of their role); Harvest thanks God as provider and shares with the hungry.
  • Festivals have changed (holidays, Sundays, streaming, commerce); Christians differ on whether this is loss or opportunity.

Mission 4

  • Baptism is the sacrament (or ordinance) of initiation, with water and the name of the Trinity.
  • Infant baptism stresses God's grace, original sin (Catholic) and the covenant; believers' baptism stresses personal faith and the dying-and-rising picture of immersion.
  • Godparents promise to support a baptised child.
  • Dedication blesses a baby without water.

Mission 5

  • The Eucharist (Mass, Holy Communion, Lord's Supper) goes back to Jesus' Last Supper: "this is my body… my blood of the new covenant".
  • Catholics: transubstantiation and the Real Presence, the "source and summit"; Orthodox: a real change, a mystery; Lutherans: real presence; Anglicans: a range; Reformed: spiritual presence; Baptists: memorial.

Mission 6

  • The local church is a community of believers, the centre of worship and belonging, serving its whole area: food banks, street pastors, family support, visiting.
  • Churches can build community cohesion in Northern Ireland, and face challenges from changing moral and social trends.
