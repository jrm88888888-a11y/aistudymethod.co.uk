Mission 1: Why did a Guru ask a huge crowd which of them would give him their head?

Make a guess: Why did a Guru ask a huge crowd which of them would give him their head?

Mission 1

Before 1699: a harvest festival

  • Vaisakhi falls in April (usually 13 or 14 April). In Punjab it marks the spring harvest and the solar new year.
  • Also spelt Baisakhi; your spec uses this spelling too.
  • Guru Amar Das (3rd Guru) asked Sikhs to gather at Goindwal at Vaisakhi, to meet the Guru and each other.
  • So it was already a Sikh gathering day before 1699.
  • For Sikhs today, its main meaning comes from what happened at Anandpur in 1699.
Mission 1

Anandpur, 1699: the call

  • Guru Gobind Singh called Sikhs to Anandpur for Vaisakhi. Sikhs had suffered persecution; his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur, had been executed (1675).
  • Sikh accounts tell the story like this. Before the huge gathering he drew his sword and asked: who will give his head for the Guru?
  • After a silence, one man stood. The Guru took him into a tent and came out with a bloodied sword.
  • He asked again, and again: five men in all came forward.
  • Then the Guru brought all five out, alive, dressed alike in new clothes.
  • Sikhs call them the panj pyare (the five beloved ones).
Mission 1

The panj pyare

  • Bhai Daya Singh, Bhai Dharam Singh, Bhai Himmat Singh, Bhai Mohkam Singh, Bhai Sahib Singh.
  • In traditional accounts they came from different regions of India (from Lahore in the north to Bidar in the south).
  • They came from different castes: a trader, a farmer, a water-carrier, a tailor and a barber.
  • Their courage and devotion are remembered in every Ardas.
  • Today five respected amritdhari Sikhs act as panj pyare: they lead Amrit ceremonies and processions.
Mission 1

Amrit and the birth of the Khalsa

  • The Guru stirred water in an iron bowl with a khanda (double-edged sword) while reciting prayers.
  • Sugar sweets were added: Khalsa courage should come with sweetness and kindness.
  • Sikh tradition says the Guru's wife added the sweets. Your spec names Mata Sundari; some accounts name Mata Jito or Mata Sahib Kaur. Mata Sundari later guided the Khalsa for many years after the Guru's death (1708).
  • The five drank amrit from the one bowl: a break with caste rules.
  • Sikh accounts say the Guru then asked the five to give amrit to him: Guru and Khalsa stand as equals.
  • Men took the name Singh (lion), women Kaur (princess).
  • They became the Khalsa ("pure", belonging to God), keeping the Five Ks and a strict code of life.
  • Thousands are said to have taken amrit that day.

?Why is it important to Sikhs that Guru Gobind Singh asked the panj pyare to give amrit to him after he had given it to them?
Mission 1

Why Vaisakhi matters

  • It celebrates the birth of the Khalsa and a Sikh identity that can be seen (the Five Ks, the turban).
  • Commitment: the panj pyare show complete dedication to God and the Guru.
  • Equality: different castes drank from one bowl and shared one name.
  • Courage: standing up for faith and for others' freedom, as Guru Tegh Bahadur had done.
  • Renewal: many Sikhs choose Vaisakhi to take amrit themselves, or to renew their commitment.
Mission 1

How Sikhs celebrate

  • Akhand Path ending on Vaisakhi morning; kirtan and talks on the events of 1699; Ardas; karah prashad; langar.
  • Nishan Sahib: the flagpole is taken down and washed, and dressed with a new cloth and flag.
  • Amrit ceremonies: led by five Khalsa Sikhs acting as panj pyare.
  • Nagar kirtan ("town singing"): a street procession led by five Sikhs as panj pyare, with the Guru Granth Sahib on a decorated float, kirtan, and displays of gatka (Sikh martial art).
  • Along the route, Sikhs serve free food and drink to everyone, including passers-by.
  • Gurdwaras are decorated; families wear new clothes and visit relatives.

Mission 1Diversity

India and Britain

  • In Punjab, India: a public holiday; harvest fairs, music and bhangra; very large gatherings at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, at Anandpur Sahib, and at Damdama Sahib (Talwandi Sabo).
  • In Britain: usually celebrated at the nearest weekend, since 13/14 April is often a working or school day.
  • British cities with large Sikh communities hold big nagar kirtans; some city councils host public Vaisakhi events.
  • In Britain the religious meaning (the Khalsa) is often more to the fore; the harvest side matters less away from Punjab's farms.
  • In Britain, Vaisakhi is also a chance to explain Sikhism to neighbours of other faiths.
Mission 1Diversity

Different understandings

  • Amritdhari Sikhs (initiated) often see Vaisakhi as a day of commitment: renewing their vows, or helping others take amrit.
  • Sahajdhari Sikhs (not initiated) celebrate fully too, while some feel not yet ready to take amrit.
  • Festival or remembrance? Some see Vaisakhi as a gurpurb-like celebration of the Khalsa's birth; others stress the harvest and new-year festival shared with Punjabi neighbours of all faiths.
  • Some Sikhs worry that music, fairs and parties overshadow the religious meaning; others say joy and community are part of the festival.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Vaisakhi is the most important Sikh festival."

  • Reasons for: it marks the birth of the Khalsa, which shapes Sikh identity today; many take amrit then; it is the largest public Sikh celebration in Britain.
  • Reasons against: Guru Nanak's birthday celebrates the founder of the faith; martyrdom days (Guru Arjan, Guru Tegh Bahadur) and Bandi Chhor Divas carry deep meaning; Sikhs believe no single day matters more than daily nam japna and sewa.
  • Another view: Sikhs weigh this differently: some give Guru Nanak's gurpurb the greater weight; many amritdhari Sikhs feel Vaisakhi most strongly.
  • Judge it by: What is being remembered? Which festival most shapes Sikh life afterwards? Are festivals more important than daily practice?

Mission 2

Gurpurbs and melas

  • Gurpurb: "the Guru's day". Mostly the birth or death anniversary of one of the Gurus.
  • The death of a Guru killed for the faith is a martyrdom (shaheedi) day.
  • Mela: a "fair" or gathering. Examples: Vaisakhi, Divali, Hola Mohalla (a festival of martial skills begun by Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur).
  • Many melas fall on dates also kept by Hindus. Guru Amar Das asked Sikhs to gather around the Guru at Vaisakhi and Divali, giving those days a Sikh meaning.
  • Common practices: an akhand path (a continuous 48-hour reading of the Guru Granth Sahib) ending on the day, kirtan (hymn-singing), decorations, karah prashad (blessed sweet food) and langar (free shared meal).
  • Your spec says Baisakhi and Bandi Chhor Divas.
Mission 2

Bandi Chhor: the Guru who freed prisoners

  • Guru Hargobind, the sixth Guru, was held in Gwalior Fort by the Mughal emperor Jahangir (early 1600s).
  • Jahangir agreed to free him. The Guru refused to go unless 52 Hindu princes (rajas) held there were freed too.
  • Jahangir said: only those who can hold on to your cloak may leave.
  • The Guru had a cloak made with 52 tassels. Each prince held one and walked out with him.
  • He reached Amritsar at Divali time; the Harmandir Sahib was lit with lamps to welcome him.
  • He became known as Bandi Chhor: "the one who frees prisoners". Bandi Chhor Divas: "day of release".

?Why is Guru Hargobind called "Bandi Chhor"?
Mission 2

Divali for Sikhs

  • Same time of year as the Hindu Divali (October or November), but a different story.
  • Hindus remember, for example, Rama's return to Ayodhya; Sikhs remember Guru Hargobind's release.
  • At the Harmandir Sahib: the buildings and pool are lit with lamps; fireworks over Amritsar.
  • In gurdwaras: kirtan, Ardas (the community prayer), karah prashad, langar.
  • At home: lamps or candles, sweets and gifts with family and friends.
  • Many Sikhs also remember Bhai Mani Singh, a Sikh scholar executed in Lahore in the 1730s after a dispute over a Divali gathering at Amritsar.
  • Many Sikhs use the day to speak up for human rights: freedom for people unjustly imprisoned.
  • Some Sikhs prefer the name Bandi Chhor Divas, to show the Sikh meaning; others say Divali and share the season with Hindu neighbours.
Mission 2

Guru Nanak's birthday

  • Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan.
  • Most Sikhs celebrate at the full moon of the month of Kartik (usually November).
  • An akhand path ends on the morning of the gurpurb.
  • Nagar kirtan: a street procession singing hymns. The Guru Granth Sahib is carried on a decorated float, led by five Sikhs (panj pyare) carrying the Nishan Sahib (Sikh flag).
  • Gurdwaras are lit up; there are talks on the Guru's life, kirtan through the night and langar for everyone.
  • Many pilgrims travel to Nankana Sahib for the day.
  • Meaning: thanks for the Guru who first taught the Sikh path: one God, equality, honest work, sharing.
Mission 2

Martyrdoms: Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur

  • Guru Arjan (fifth Guru) compiled the Adi Granth and built the Harmandir Sahib.
  • In 1606 he was tortured and killed in Lahore on the orders of Jahangir: Sikhs remember him seated on a burning-hot iron plate.
  • His martyrdom day falls in early summer. Sikhs set up chhabeel stalls giving cold sweet drinks to anyone passing: kindness answers cruelty.
  • Guru Tegh Bahadur (ninth Guru) was beheaded in Delhi in 1675 on the orders of the emperor Aurangzeb.
  • He had stood up for Hindu Pandits from Kashmir who were being forced to convert: he died defending other people's freedom of religion.
  • Gurdwara Sis Ganj Sahib in Delhi marks the place. His day falls in late November or December.
  • Martyrdom days are kept more quietly: kirtan, an akhand path, talks on the Guru's sacrifice, langar.

Mission 2Diversity

Same festival, different places and views

  • In India (Punjab): Guru Nanak's birthday is a public holiday; huge crowds at historic gurdwaras; pilgrimages to Nankana Sahib and Amritsar.
  • In Britain: celebrations often move to the nearest weekend, because of school and work.
  • British nagar kirtans go through town streets (e.g. Southall, Birmingham, Leicester), with police and council planning.
  • British gurdwaras often open langar to neighbours and invite schools: a chance to share Sikh values and teach children their heritage.
  • Dates differ too: some Sikhs follow the Nanakshahi calendar (fixed dates), others the traditional lunar dates.
  • Different understandings: for some, gurpurbs mainly remember history; for others they are chances for spiritual renewal through the Guru's word. Some criticise fireworks and spending as distractions from the Gurus' message.
Quick check
?A gurdwara in Britain holds an akhand path and a nagar kirtan, led by five Sikhs carrying the Nishan Sahib, on the Sunday nearest the full moon of Kartik. Which gurpurb is it most likely to be?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Festivals are the most important part of Sikh life."

  • Reasons for: festivals keep the Gurus' stories alive for the next generation; they gather the sangat (community); nagar kirtans and langar show Sikh values in public; martyrdoms inspire courage and justice.
  • Reasons against: the Gurus stressed daily devotion: Nam Japna (meditating on God's name), honest work and sharing; a festival once a year cannot replace this. Some see too much spending and fireworks. Guru Nanak criticised empty ritual.
  • Another view: festivals and daily life support each other: the festival renews the commitment that daily life keeps.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus themselves teach about ritual? What keeps faith strong in a minority community such as Britain? Is "important" about frequency or about impact?
Mission check

?At Bandi Chhor Divas, a gurdwara in Leeds collects letters of support for people around the world who are imprisoned for their beliefs. Which part of the Sikh story does this most directly reflect?

Sikh festivals turn history into action: remembering the Gurus means standing for freedom, justice and the sangat today.

Mission 3

An unbroken reading

  • Akhand Path: a continuous, unbroken (akhand) reading (path) of the whole Guru Granth Sahib, from the first page to the last.
  • All 1,430 angs, read aloud, without stopping: it takes about 48 hours.
  • A team of trained readers (pathis) takes turns, often about two hours each.
  • At each change, the new reader joins mid-line so that not a word is missed.
Mission 3

Beginning and end

  • Beginning: karah prashad is prepared; six verses of Anand Sahib are sung; Ardas is said; a hukamnama is taken; the reading starts.
  • During: the sangat comes and goes to listen at all hours; langar is served throughout.
  • End (bhog): the final pages are read; Anand Sahib is sung; Ardas is said; a hukamnama is taken; karah prashad and langar are shared.
  • Often kirtan is sung at the bhog, and the family who arranged the path thank the sangat.
Quick check
?During an Akhand Path, a reader's two-hour turn ends in the middle of a hymn. What happens?
Mission 3

Where did it come from?

  • Its exact origin is not recorded.
  • Many Sikhs link it to the 18th century, when Sikhs were persecuted and often scattered. Groups would read the whole scripture in one continuous sitting, so that the complete Guru's word could be heard when they could gather.
  • Some traditions trace continuous readings back to the time of Guru Gobind Singh.
  • Since then it has become part of Sikh life worldwide: in gurdwaras every week and in homes.
Mission 3

When is an Akhand Path held?

  • Joy: a birth, a wedding, moving into a new home, opening a business, success in exams.
  • Sorrow or hardship: after a death, during illness, at a time of trouble.
  • Festivals and gurpurbs: timed so that the bhog falls on the morning of the festival day.
  • Anniversaries: e.g. of a family member's death.
  • It may be held in a gurdwara or, if a clean, separate room is ready, at home.
Mission 3

Gurpurbs and melas

  • Gurpurbs: anniversaries linked to the Gurus, such as their births and martyrdoms.
  • Celebrated with an Akhand Path ending on the day, kirtan, talks on the Guru's life, karah prashad, and langar.
  • Gurdwaras are decorated with lights; some hold street processions (nagar kirtan).
  • Melas are fairs held at festival times, e.g. Hola Mohalla at Anandpur, begun by Guru Gobind Singh, with martial-arts displays.
  • Different understandings: some Sikhs stress the devotion (the reading, the hymns); others value the fairs and processions for uniting the community. Some warn that fairs can drift into entertainment. In Britain, many celebrate on the nearest weekend.
Mission 3

Why it matters

  • For the community: the sangat hears the whole of the Guru's word together; many share the sewa (reading, listening, cooking langar, giving money); it unites families and gurdwaras at key moments.
  • For the individual: a way to thank God in joy and find comfort in sorrow; hearing Gurbani steadies the mind (nam japna); some Sikhs train to read, a deep act of devotion.
  • How Sikhs take part: reading a turn, listening at any hour (even through the night), cooking or serving langar, donating, attending the bhog.
Mission 3Diversity

Akhand Path and other complete readings

  • Sahaj Path (easy reading): a complete reading at a slower pace, over days, weeks or months, at home or in the gurdwara, with time to understand.
  • Saptahik Path: a complete reading spread over seven days.
  • Some Sikhs see the Akhand Path as a powerful offering of the whole community.
  • Others worry that paying readers who read with no one listening, or holding one only for good luck, turns it into ritual: the Rahit Maryada warns against both.
  • Many say what matters is listening and living by the words, whichever kind of reading is chosen.
Quick check
?A family plans an Akhand Path to thank God for a new baby. Which practice would the Rahit Maryada say is wrong?
Mission 3Evaluate

"An Akhand Path is the best way for Sikhs to mark an important event."

  • Reasons for: the whole Guru's word is heard; the sangat shares the sewa; it fits joy and sorrow alike; it ends with Ardas, hukamnama and langar, bringing together worship, guidance and equality.
  • Reasons against: a Sahaj Path gives more time to understand the words; nam japna and sewa can mark events every day; if no one listens, it becomes ritual; it costs time and money not every family has.
  • Another view: the occasion matters less than the attitude: any act that turns the family to the Guru marks the event well.
  • Judge it by: Is hearing every word or understanding the words more important? Who takes part? Does it change how people live afterwards?
Mission check

?A British Sikh family has lost their grandfather. They arrange for a Sahaj Path at home over three weeks instead of an Akhand Path, reading a little each evening together. How would most Sikhs view this?

An Akhand Path is the whole Guru speaking, without a break, at the moments that matter: and someone must be listening.

Mission 4

Amritsar: the pool of nectar

  • Amritsar (Punjab, India) means "pool of nectar".
  • Guru Ram Das, the fourth Guru, began the city in the 1570s and had a large pool (sarovar) dug.
  • Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, completed the pool and built a gurdwara in the middle of it.
  • Traders and craftworkers settled; the city grew into a centre of both faith and business.
Mission 4

Building the Harmandir Sahib

  • Harmandir Sahib: "temple of God". Often called the Golden Temple; also Darbar Sahib.
  • Building began in 1588. Sikh tradition says the Muslim Sufi saint Mian Mir was invited to lay the foundation stone: a sign of openness.
  • In 1604 Guru Arjan installed the newly compiled Adi Granth (the scripture, later the Guru Granth Sahib) inside; Baba Buddha became its first reader (granthi).
  • Destroyed in the 1700s by Afghan invaders and rebuilt by the Sikhs.
  • Maharaja Ranjit Singh covered the upper storeys in gold in the early 1800s: hence "Golden Temple".
Mission 4

Features and their meaning

  • Four doors, one on each side: open to people of every direction, caste and religion.
  • Built lower than the land around it: visitors step down to enter, a lesson in humility.
  • Stands in the sarovar, reached by a causeway from a gateway (Darshani Deori).
  • A marble walkway (parikrama) runs round the pool.
  • Inside: the Guru Granth Sahib, and kirtan (hymns from it) sung from early morning to late at night.
  • Facing the causeway: the Akal Takht. Nearby: the huge langar hall.
Quick check
?Visitors step down into the Harmandir Sahib, and it has doors on all four sides. What do most Sikhs say these two features teach?
Mission 4

A pilgrim's day

  • Before dawn: the Guru Granth Sahib is carried in a palanquin (palki) from the Akal Takht and opened in the Harmandir Sahib (prakash).
  • Pilgrims cover their heads, remove shoes and wash their feet.
  • Many bathe (ishnan) in the sarovar, then walk the parikrama clockwise.
  • They queue to bow (matha tekna) before the Guru Granth Sahib and listen to kirtan.
  • They receive karah prashad.
  • They do sewa (selfless service): cleaning the walkway, minding shoes, washing dishes.
  • Langar: volunteers serve a free vegetarian meal to a huge number of people every day, all sitting on the floor in rows (pangat), whatever their wealth, caste or religion.
  • At night: the Guru Granth Sahib is carried back to the Akal Takht to rest (sukhasan).
Mission 4

The Akal Takht

  • Your spec spells it Akhal Takht: "throne of the Timeless One" (God).
  • Built by Guru Hargobind in 1606, after his father Guru Arjan's martyrdom.
  • He wore two swords: miri (worldly power and justice) and piri (spiritual authority).
  • The Harmandir Sahib stands for piri; the Akal Takht for miri. They face each other.
  • It is the highest of the five takhts (seats of Sikh authority). Its leader (jathedar) can issue hukamnamas (edicts) to Sikhs worldwide.
  • It was badly damaged in 1984 when the Indian army entered the complex (Operation Blue Star), and later rebuilt. Many Sikhs still remember this with deep pain.
Mission 4

Jallianwala Bagh

  • A walled garden a short walk from the Harmandir Sahib.
  • On 13 April 1919, Vaisakhi day, a large unarmed crowd gathered there.
  • British-led troops under Brigadier-General Reginald Dyer blocked the exit and fired on them.
  • Hundreds were killed and many more wounded: Sikhs, Hindus and Muslims.
  • Today it is a memorial: bullet marks in the walls, the Martyrs' Well.
  • Visitors remember those who died for freedom; it is a key event in India's independence struggle.
  • Many Sikh pilgrims include it in a visit to Amritsar: faith, history and justice side by side.
Quick check
?Why do the Harmandir Sahib and the Akal Takht stand facing each other?
Mission 4Diversity

Should Sikhs go on pilgrimage?

  • Pilgrimage is not compulsory in Sikhism. Guru Nanak criticised the belief that bathing at holy places washes away sin.
  • Reasons many Sikhs visit: to see where the Gurus lived and taught; to feel the history; to join a huge sangat; to do sewa; to renew faith.
  • Other historical gurdwaras: Nankana Sahib (Guru Nanak's birthplace, Pakistan); Kartarpur (where he died); Anandpur Sahib (where the Khalsa began); the five takhts.
  • Some Sikhs stress the inner journey: God is everywhere; the local gurdwara and daily Nam Japna matter more than travel.
  • Some believe bathing in the sarovar brings healing; others call this superstition the Gurus rejected.
  • For Sikhs in Britain, a visit can be a link with family roots in Punjab.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Every Sikh should visit the Harmandir Sahib."

  • Reasons for: it is the place the Gurus built and the scripture was first installed; the sangat, kirtan and langar there show Sikh values on a vast scale; it strengthens identity, especially for Sikhs far from Punjab.
  • Reasons against: the Gurus taught that God is everywhere and that outward pilgrimage brings "a little merit"; cost and travel are not possible for all; daily Nam Japna and sewa at the local gurdwara matter more.
  • Another view: a visit is a gift, not a duty: worthwhile if it deepens inner devotion.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus teach about outward ritual? Does the experience change how a person lives? Is "should" a duty or an encouragement?
Mission check

?A Sikh grandmother in Glasgow is too ill to travel to Amritsar and is sad she will never see the Harmandir Sahib. What response would most Sikhs give her, based on the Gurus' teaching?

Amritsar is the heart of Sikh history and community, but the Gurus taught that the deepest pilgrimage is inward.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vaisakhi: a Punjabi harvest festival, a Sikh gathering since Guru Amar Das, and the birth of the Khalsa at Anandpur in 1699.
  • Panj pyare, amrit from one iron bowl, Singh and Kaur: commitment, courage and equality.
  • Celebrated with Akhand Path, a new Nishan Sahib, amrit ceremonies and nagar kirtan; in Britain at weekends, with a stronger religious focus.

Mission 2

  • Gurpurbs mark the Gurus' births and martyrdoms; melas such as Divali are gatherings given a Sikh meaning.
  • Divali / Bandi Chhor Divas remembers Guru Hargobind freeing himself and 52 princes; Guru Nanak's birthday is marked with an akhand path and nagar kirtan.
  • Guru Arjan (1606) and Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) are remembered as martyrs for truth and freedom of religion.

Mission 3

  • Akhand Path: unbroken reading of all 1,430 angs, about 48 hours, by relays of readers; starts and ends (bhog) with Anand Sahib, Ardas, hukamnama and karah prashad.
  • Held for births, weddings, deaths and gurpurbs; Sahaj Path is a slower complete reading.

Mission 4

  • Guru Ram Das began Amritsar; Guru Arjan built the Harmandir Sahib (1588–1604) and installed the scripture there.
  • Four doors (open to all) and a lower level (humility); pilgrims bathe, walk the parikrama, hear kirtan, do sewa and eat langar.
  • The Akal Takht (miri) faces the Harmandir Sahib (piri); Jallianwala Bagh (1919) is a nearby memorial.
