Mission 1

Mission 1: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 1

Three shapes of selection

  • Directional: one extreme is favoured. The mean shifts.
  • Stabilising: the intermediate (modal) phenotype is favoured; both extremes are selected against. The mean stays; the range narrows.
  • Disruptive: both extremes are favoured over the intermediate. The distribution can split into two peaks.
  • All of them change allele frequencies. Stabilising selection removes alleles for extreme phenotypes.
  • Say: "stabilising selection still changes allele frequencies" — not: "no change in the mean, so no evolution".
Mission 1

Named examples

  • Directional: antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  • Stabilising: human birth mass. In a large study of births at a London hospital, mortality was lowest at intermediate birth masses and higher for very light and very heavy babies (Karn and Penrose, 1951).
  • Disruptive: black-bellied seedcracker finches in Cameroon. Large-billed birds crack hard seeds; small-billed birds handle soft seeds; intermediates do worse at both (Smith, 1993).
  • Disruptive is not "selection after a disruption". It is defined by which phenotypes are favoured.
Quick check
?In a study of human births in the mid-twentieth century, babies of intermediate birth mass had the highest survival, while both very light and very heavy babies had higher mortality. Which type of selection does this show, and why?
Quick check
?Which statement about directional, disruptive and stabilising selection is correct?
Mission 1

Sexual selection

  • Sexual selection: selection by success in getting mates.
  • Mate choice: usually females choose males whose traits signal overall fitness.
  • Competition: males fight or hold territories for access to females.
  • Example: male birds of paradise: elaborate plumage and complex courtship displays; females are dull.
  • The plumage costs energy and attracts predators. It spreads because the gain in mating success outweighs the survival cost.
  • Say: "females prefer the trait, so its alleles rise in frequency" — not: "it is artificial selection because the female chooses".
Quick check
?Male birds of paradise have elaborate, brightly coloured plumage and perform complex courtship displays, while females are dull. The plumage is costly to grow and makes males easier for predators to see. Which explains how this plumage evolved?
Mission 1

Read the data: Endler's guppies

John Endler (1980) studied guppies from Trinidad streams. Males have coloured spots; spot pattern is heritable.

  • Two opposing pressures: females prefer more colourful males; predators catch conspicuous males.
  • Greenhouse ponds: same starting fish, three treatments: dangerous predator (Crenicichla), weak predator (Rivulus), no predator.
  • Result: spot number fell with Crenicichla and rose with Rivulus or no predator. With Crenicichla, spot size came to match the gravel.
  • Field transfer: guppies moved from a Crenicichla stream to a stream with only Rivulus. Two years later the males had more and larger spots.
  • Reading: control the selection pressure, and you control the direction of evolution.
Quick check
?Endler transferred guppies from a stream containing the dangerous predator Crenicichla to a stream that had no guppies and only the weak predator Rivulus, which rarely eats adult guppies. Two years later, the males in the new stream had on average more coloured spots, and larger spots, than the population they came from. Which is the best interpretation?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical songbird, clutch size (number of eggs) is heritable. Parents with very large clutches cannot feed all their chicks, and many starve; parents with very small clutches raise few young. Over many generations, the mean clutch size stays at 4 eggs, but nests with 2 or 6 eggs become rarer. Which statement is correct?

The mean stayed put, yet the population still evolved. Selection can hold the middle as well as push an extreme.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 2

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 2Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.
Quick check

Mission 2

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
Mission 2Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 2

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • Genotype frequencies that do not fit p², 2pq, q² → at least one condition is broken, e.g. non-random mating, or unequal survival of genotypes.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 2

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

MMMNNN
Observed178730391303
Expected (p², 2pq, q² × 6129)178430451300
  • p(M) = (2 × 1787 + 3039) ÷ (2 × 6129) = 6613 ÷ 12 258 = 0.5395. q(N) = 0.4605.
  • Expected MM = 0.5395² × 6129 ≈ 1784. Expected MN = 2 × 0.5395 × 0.4605 × 6129 ≈ 3045.
  • Keep four decimal places in p and q, or expected counts drift.
  • Observed and expected are very close.
Quick check
?Use the MN data on the previous screen. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 2

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Neo-Darwinism: Darwin's natural selection combined with genetics. Selection changes allele frequencies in the gene pool.
  • Geographically isolated populations have separate gene pools, and their allele frequencies can differ.
  • Database example: allele Iᴮ (ABO blood group) is about 0.2–0.3 in parts of Central Asia, but close to 0 in many Indigenous South American populations.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 2

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check
?A student searches an allele frequency database for the ABO blood group allele Iᴮ. The database gives a frequency of about 0.25 for a population in Central Asia and about 0.01 for an Indigenous population in South America. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 3

Gene flow

  • Gene flow: alleles moving between populations when individuals (or their pollen, seeds or gametes) arrive and breed.
  • Gene flow keeps populations' allele frequencies similar. It prevents divergence.
  • Isolation (a river, mountains, distance, or differences in breeding) reduces gene flow.
  • Then mutation, selection and chance change each gene pool separately: the populations diverge.
Quick check
?Which event would result in gene flow into a population of a hypothetical wildflower species that grows in a meadow?
Quick check
?Two populations of a hypothetical frog species live in neighbouring ponds connected by a stream, and frogs regularly move between the ponds and breed in both. The ponds have similar conditions. A dam is then built that stops all movement of frogs between the ponds. Which prediction about the two populations over many generations is best supported?
Mission 3

Read the data: allele frequency databases

  • Online allele frequency databases list how common each allele is in sampled human populations.
  • Example (illustrative data, rounded from the real pattern): the ABO blood group allele Iᴮ:
    • about 0.25 in some Central Asian populations;
    • about 0.01 in some Indigenous South American populations.
  • Geographically isolated populations have had little gene flow, so their gene pools changed separately, by chance and by selection.
  • They are still one species: only the frequencies differ.
  • Trap: an allele frequency of 0.25 is not "25% of people have blood group B".
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) For one allele of a bird species, a database gives a frequency of 0.40 on an island and 0.05 on the mainland 300 km away. Birds very rarely cross between the two. Which statement is best supported?
Mission check

?Two lakes were once joined by a stream, and fish of one species moved and bred freely between them. The stream dried up 2,000 years ago. Today the two fish populations differ in the frequencies of several alleles, but fish from the two lakes still produce fertile offspring when bred together. Which explanation is best supported?

Cut the flow of alleles, and two gene pools start to drift and diverge.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If people clear part of a rainforest, could the rest of it dry out and die?

Make a guess: If people clear part of a rainforest, could the rest of it dry out and die?

Mission 4

Stable means lasting, not unchanging

  • Stability: an ecosystem keeps the same type of community over long periods.
  • Populations still fluctuate; individuals die and are replaced.
  • Evidence: dated sediment cores with fossil pollen, leaves and animals show the same community, layer after layer.
  • Parts of the Amazon rainforest have persisted for tens of millions of years; the Namib Desert has been arid for about 55 million years.
  • Say: "the community persists while populations fluctuate" — not: "nothing changes".
Mission 4

What keeps an ecosystem stable

  • A continuous supply of energy: usually sunlight. Energy is lost as heat, so it cannot be recycled.
  • Recycling of nutrients: their supply is finite.
  • Genetic diversity: some individuals survive a new disease or a change in conditions.
  • Climate (temperature, rainfall) staying within the tolerance of the species present. It may vary, but not beyond those limits.
Quick check
?Which set of conditions is required for an ecosystem to remain stable?
Mission 4

Tipping points

  • An ecosystem can absorb change, up to a point.
  • Tipping point: a threshold. Past it, a small extra change shifts the ecosystem into a different state.
  • Change speeds itself up: positive feedback.
  • The new state can persist even if the original cause is reduced: it may not reverse.
  • So change is often not proportional to the cause: little happens, then a lot happens quickly.
Mission 4

The Amazon makes much of its own rain

  • Trees draw up soil water and transpire it: water vapour enters the air.
  • The vapour cools the forest, drives air flows and falls again as rain further inland.
  • Roughly half of the Amazon's rain comes from water recycled by the forest itself.
  • Clear enough forest: less vapour, less rain, longer dry seasons, more fire. The rest of the forest may die back to drier, open vegetation.
  • How much clearing is too much? Uncertain. Early models: about 40%. Later estimates including climate change and fire: about 20–25% (Lovejoy and Nobre, 2018).
  • About 17% of the original forest had been cleared by 2021, and at least as much again degraded.
Mission 4

Worked example: percentage change

Illustrative data: a region had 520 000 km² of forest in 1985 and 437 000 km² in 2020.

  • Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.
  • Change = 437 000 − 520 000 = −83 000 km².
  • −83 000 ÷ 520 000 × 100 = −16.0%.
  • The minus sign shows a decrease.
  • Divide by the original value, not the new one.
Quick check
?In 1970 the area of a rainforest was 4 000 000 km². By 2020 the area was 3 320 000 km². What is the percentage change in the area of forest?
Mission 4

Two more ecosystems near the edge

  • Taiga (boreal forest): warmer temperatures and less winter snow → drought → lower primary production, browning of trees → more frequent, more intense fires → old legacy carbon in the soil burns. The forest switches from storing carbon to releasing it.
  • Coral reefs: more CO2 → ocean acidification → less carbonate, so corals build skeletons more slowly. Warmer water → corals expel their zooxanthellae: bleaching; repeated or long bleaching kills corals.
  • Lose the corals and the whole reef ecosystem collapses.
Mission 4

Testing stability with a mesocosm

  • Mesocosm: a small model ecosystem used to test what affects stability.
  • Best: a sealed glass vessel. Matter cannot enter or leave, so nutrients must recycle; energy still flows: light in, heat out.
  • Aquatic or microbial mesocosms work better than terrestrial ones: e.g. pond water, sediment, water plants, snails, microorganisms.
  • Change one variable (e.g. light intensity); keep others constant; set up repeats.
  • Measure: dissolved oxygen, water clarity, survival of organisms over weeks.
  • Follow IB experimental guidelines: no harm to vertebrates; release or care for organisms properly afterwards.
Quick check
?Why is a sealed glass vessel preferable to an open tank for a mesocosm used to investigate ecosystem stability?
Mission 4

Keystone species

  • Keystone species: has an effect on community structure far larger than its numbers or biomass suggest.
  • Remove it and the community can collapse.
  • Classic evidence (Paine, 1966): on a rocky shore, the starfish Pisaster was removed from test plots. Mussels it used to eat took over the rock, and the number of species fell from 15 to 8.
  • Say: "a keystone species has a disproportionate effect" — not: "the most common species".
Quick check
?Sea otters make up a tiny fraction of the biomass of a kelp forest. Where otters were hunted to extinction, sea urchins multiplied and grazed the kelp down to bare rock, and the fish, seals and seabirds that depended on the kelp disappeared. What does this show about sea otters?
Mission check

?For decades, the phosphate entering a shallow lake rose steadily. The water stayed clear, with dense water plants, until one summer it turned green with algae and the plants died. Later, phosphate was cut back to the level of years before, but the water stayed green. What does this show?

Like the Amazon, the lake absorbed change until it crossed a threshold, then shifted to a new state that did not easily reverse.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Make a guess: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Mission 5

What succession is

  • Succession: change in the species making up a community, over time.
  • Primary succession: starts on bare ground with no soil: rock, cooled lava, sand, land left by a melting glacier.
  • Secondary succession: starts where soil remains after the community is destroyed: fire, flood, wind damage, felling, abandoned farmland.
  • Causes: a change in the abiotic environment (e.g. a glacier retreats) or a biotic change (organisms alter conditions).
Mission 5

Pioneers on bare rock

  • Pioneer species colonise first: e.g. lichens, then mosses.
  • Their features: spores spread by wind; survive drought, heat and cold; need almost no soil.
  • Lichens help weather the rock surface.
  • When pioneers die, decomposers turn them into humus.
  • Rock particles + humus = the first thin soil.
Mission 5

Each stage paves the way, then gives way

  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients: the environment is less hostile.
  • New species can now establish: grasses and ferns, then shrubs, then trees.
  • Taller plants shade out the earlier ones.
  • Each stage changes the environment so it suits other species better, and often suits itself less.
  • Say: "the pioneers changed conditions, which allowed other species to establish" — not: "pioneers prepare the ground for the next species".
Quick check
?Alder trees colonising ground exposed by a retreating glacier have nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules. Within decades the soil nitrogen content rises and spruce trees, which cannot grow on the bare ground, replace the alders. Which statement about the cause of this change is correct?
Mission 5

What rises as succession proceeds

  • Soil depth, humus, water and mineral content.
  • Plant size: mosses → shrubs → trees.
  • Primary production (biomass made per m² per year).
  • Species diversity and food-web complexity: more niches.
  • Nutrient cycling: more dead matter, more decomposers, faster recycling.
Quick checkRead the data
?Illustrative data.
Surveys were made on land exposed by a retreating glacier 10, 50, 100 and 200 years earlier. Mean plant height was 0.1 m, 3 m, 15 m and 30 m; the number of plant species was 6, 18, 25 and 31; and net primary production (kg m⁻² yr⁻¹) was 0.05, 0.4, 0.9 and 1.2. Soil depth and organic content increased throughout. Which conclusion do the data support?
Mission 5

The climax community

  • Climax community: the final, stable community, in equilibrium with its environment.
  • It persists until a disturbance; its populations still fluctuate.
  • Usually dominated by a few large species. In most of lowland Britain and Ireland: deciduous woodland (e.g. oak).
  • For any set of environmental conditions, succession tends to lead to one particular type of climax.
Mission 5

Succession held back

  • Human activity can stop succession before the climax.
  • Grazing livestock eat tree and shrub seedlings: grassland or moorland persists.
  • Also: mowing, controlled burning, draining wetlands for farming.
  • This is arrested succession. Stop the grazing or the drainage, and succession resumes.
Quick check
?Sheep have grazed a hillside for centuries and it is covered by grassland. When grazing was stopped on a fenced plot, shrubs appeared within ten years and young trees within twenty. What does this show?
Mission 5

Cyclical succession

  • Some ecosystems have no single, unchanging climax. Instead, communities cycle.
  • Example: beaver ponds. Beavers dam a stream: pond → beavers leave, dam fails → wet meadow → shrubs and trees regrow → beavers return.
  • Example: heather moorland. Old heather plants die back in the centre; mosses and lichens fill the gap; heather seedlings return.
  • The ecosystem persists; it is the cycle that is stable.
Quick check
?On a heather moor, heather plants pass through pioneer, building, mature and degenerate phases. In the degenerate phase the central branches die, opening a gap that is colonised by lichens and mosses before heather seedlings re-establish there. What does this show?
Mission check

?On a coastal sand-dune system, marram grass covers the young dunes nearest the sea. On older dunes further inland, marram is scarce and the dunes are covered by mosses, low shrubs and other grasses, growing in a thin layer of soil. Which explanation is correct?

On rock or on sand, pioneers change the ground they live on, and their changes let others take over. That is how bare ground becomes forest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilising selection favours the intermediate and narrows the range; both change allele frequencies.
  • Disruptive selection favours both extremes.
  • Sexual selection favours traits that win mates, even at a cost to survival.

Mission 2

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.

Mission 3

  • Gene flow keeps populations alike; isolation stops it, so allele frequencies diverge.

Mission 4

  • Stable ecosystems persist for long periods; they need energy input, nutrient recycling, genetic diversity and climate within tolerance.
  • Past a tipping point, an ecosystem shifts to a new state that may not reverse; the Amazon threshold is uncertain, perhaps 20–40% deforestation. Percentage change = (new − original) ÷ original × 100.

Mission 5

  • Pioneers colonise bare ground and change it; each stage makes the environment suit other species, until a stable climax community forms.
  • Grazing, mowing, burning or drainage can hold succession back; conservation often manages this deliberately.
  • Some ecosystems cycle between communities instead of reaching one fixed climax.
