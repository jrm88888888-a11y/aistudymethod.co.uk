Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 1

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • Economies of scale: as a firm grows, its average cost (cost per unit) falls.
  • Diseconomies of scale: as a firm grows too big, its average cost rises.
  • These happen in the long run, when the firm can grow all its inputs: bigger factories, more machines, more staff.
  • On the long-run average cost (LRAC) curve: falling part = economies of scale; lowest point = lowest cost per unit; rising part = diseconomies of scale.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises as output grows.
Mission 1

Internal economies of scale

  • Internal economies come from the firm's own growth.
  • Technical: bigger, specialised machines and mass production. Bigger containers cost less per unit of space.
  • Purchasing: buying in bulk earns discounts on materials.
  • Financial: banks see big firms as less risky, so they lend more cheaply.
  • Managerial: big firms can hire specialist managers (finance, marketing, production).
  • Risk-bearing: selling many products in many markets, so a fall in one is offset by others.
  • Marketing: one advert or brand is spread over far more sales.
Mission 1

Worked example: why bigger ships carry more cheaply

  • Think of a ship's cargo hold as a cube (a simplified model).
  • Make each side twice as long, 1 → 2.
  • Surface (steel needed to build it): 6 → 24 squares. ×4.
  • Volume (cargo it can carry): 1 → 8 cubes. ×8.
  • Steel per unit of cargo: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 → 24 ÷ 8 = 3. It halves. This is a technical economy of scale.
  • A bigger ship also needs only a slightly bigger crew.
  • Real data: the container ship MSC Irina, delivered in 2023, carries 24,346 twenty-foot containers.
Quick check
?An airline orders 100 new planes at once and gets a much lower price per plane than a small airline buying 2. Which economy of scale is this?
Mission 1

External economies of scale

  • External economies come from the growth of the whole industry, often in one area, not from the firm's own growth.
  • Skilled labour: a local pool of trained workers, and colleges that train them.
  • Infrastructure: better roads, ports, rail and internet links.
  • Suppliers: specialist suppliers set up nearby.
  • Clustering: firms in the same industry gather in one place and share ideas, workers and suppliers. E.g. technology firms in Silicon Valley.
  • Effect: every firm's average cost falls: the whole LRAC curve shifts down.
Mission 1Graph lab

Move along or shift?

  • Takeaway: the firm's own growth moves it along the curve (internal); the industry's growth shifts the curve (external).
Quick check
?Which of these is an external economy of scale for a car maker?
Mission 1

Diseconomies of scale

  • When a firm grows very large, average cost can rise. Why?
  • Communication: more layers of managers; messages are slow or get distorted.
  • Control and coordination: hard to keep many sites and departments working well together.
  • Motivation: workers feel like a small part of a huge machine; absence and staff turnover rise.
  • Bureaucracy: more rules, forms and checks slow decisions.
  • Distance: sites far apart, or in many countries, are harder to run and supply.
  • On the LRAC curve: the rising part, after the lowest point.
Quick check
?A restaurant chain grows fast and opens in 15 countries. Instructions from head office take weeks to reach managers, and staff say nobody listens to them. Its average cost per meal starts to rise. What is the best explanation?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A delivery firm grows over several years. At 1,000 parcels a day its total cost is £6,000 a day. At 5,000 parcels it is £20,000. At 20,000 parcels it is £100,000. What do these figures show?

Growth can cut cost per unit, but only up to a point. Judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do a few firms grow huge while most firms stay small all their lives?

Make a guess: Why do most firms stay small?

Mission 2

How big is a firm?

  • Ways to measure firm size:
    • number of employees (workers)
    • sales revenue (value of sales)
    • market share (its % of all sales in the market)
    • capital employed (money invested in the business)
  • A common size rule: small = fewer than 50 employees; medium = 50–249; large = 250 or more.
  • UK, start of 2024: 5.5 million private sector businesses.
    • 99.2% were small (0 to 49 employees).
    • 74% employed no one except the owner(s).
  • One firm can look big on one measure and small on another (a few staff, but huge sales).
Mission 2

Small firms: strengths and weaknesses

Strengths of small firmsWeaknesses of small firms
Personal service: they know their customersHigher average costs: few economies of scale
Flexible: quick decisions, quick to changeHarder to borrow, often at higher interest rates
Can serve a niche (a small, specialist market)Owner may lack skills in every area
Owner keeps controlOne lost customer or bad year can close the firm
  • Large firms are the mirror image: lower average costs, easier finance, wider range, money for research. But slower decisions and harder to manage.
Mission 2

Why most firms stay small

  • Small market: a village bakery or a local plumber only has so many customers.
  • Niche or personal service: customers want a specialist or a personal touch (a tailor, a hairdresser).
  • Owner's choice: owners want control, less risk or less stress, not maximum size.
  • Finance: banks see small firms as risky, so borrowing to grow is hard.
  • Few economies of scale in some industries: a bigger salon is not much cheaper per haircut.
  • Say: "the firm chose to stay small" — not: "the firm failed to grow". Staying small can be a sensible choice.
Quick check
?A family runs one bakery in a small village. It has made a profit for 20 years and has never grown. Which is the most likely reason it has stayed small?
Mission 2

Two ways to grow

  • Internal (organic) growth: the firm expands itself. More branches, more machines, new products, new markets. Slower, but lower risk and the owners keep control.
  • External growth: the firm joins with another firm.
    • Merger: two firms agree to combine into one.
    • Takeover: one firm buys another.
  • External growth is faster, but costly, and joining two businesses can go wrong (different ways of working).
  • Factors affecting growth: size of the market · access to finance · the owners' aims · managers' skills · competition · government rules.
Mission 2

Three kinds of merger

  • Think of a chain of production: raw materials → making → selling.
  • Horizontal: two firms at the same stage, in the same industry. Example: Sainsbury's and Asda (two supermarket chains) planned to merge; the UK competition authority (CMA) blocked it in 2019.
  • Vertical backward: a firm buys its supplier. Example: Tesco (a supermarket) took over Booker (a food wholesaler) in 2018.
  • Vertical forward: a firm buys a firm nearer the customer (a brewer buys pubs).
  • Conglomerate: firms in unrelated industries (a food company buys an insurance firm).
TypeMain aim
HorizontalEconomies of scale; bigger market share; fewer rivals
Vertical backwardSecure supplies; control quality and costs
Vertical forwardSecure outlets to sell through
ConglomerateSpread risk across different markets
Quick check
?A chocolate maker buys a company that grows cocoa beans. Which type of merger is this?
Mission 2

Bigger and cheaper? Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A bakery grows:
Loaves a dayTotal costAverage cost (TC ÷ output)
Before1,000£800£0.80
After5,000£2,500£0.50
  • Economies of scale (see Mission 1) cut the average cost per loaf by £0.30.
  • At a price of £1.00, profit per loaf rises from £0.20 to £0.50.
  • How growth can change what a firm does:
    • Keep the price and earn more profit per item.
    • Cut the price (say to £0.80, still £0.30 profit per loaf) and win customers from rivals.
    • Spend profits on new products, research or more growth.
  • Too big, and diseconomies of scale can push average cost back up.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A furniture firm makes 2,000 chairs a year at a total cost of £80,000. After growing, it makes 5,000 chairs at a total cost of £150,000. What is its new average cost per chair?
Mission 2

Growth: who gains, who loses?

Possible benefitsPossible costs
The firmLower average costs; more profit; less riskDiseconomies of scale; harder to manage
ConsumersLower prices; wider range; better productsFewer rivals, so higher prices or less choice
WorkersTraining; promotion; job securityJob cuts after a merger; feeling less valued
  • Which side wins depends on whether lower costs are passed on in lower prices, and on how much competition is left.
  • Governments can block mergers that would cut competition too far (Sainsbury's and Asda, 2019).
Quick check
?The two biggest food-delivery apps in a country agree to merge. Customers have few other apps to choose from. What is the main risk for customers?
Mission check

?A hairdresser has run one profitable salon for 15 years and has turned down chances to open more. In the same country, a car maker has merged with a rival and now sells all over the world. Which best explains the difference?

Firms grow huge where size cuts average cost and the market is big; where it does not, staying small makes sense.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do prices fall when rival sellers arrive, and what happens when only one firm is left?

Make a guess: Several new sellers open up in a busy market, and the price falls. Why?

Mission 3

From many sellers to one

  • A market structure describes how competitive a market is. Look at three things:
    • Number of producers: many, a few, or one.
    • Product differentiation: are the products the same, or made to look different (brands)?
    • Ease of entry: can new firms join easily?
  • Competitive market: many sellers, similar products, easy to join.
  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, often branded products, hard to join.
  • Monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, very hard to join.
Mission 3

Competitive markets

  • Features:
    • many buyers and sellers, none big enough to control the price
    • similar products, so buyers can switch easily
    • easy entry and exit: new firms can join or leave
    • buyers can compare prices (often online)
  • Why firms compete: to win customers, sales and profit.
  • Why competition happens: low start-up costs, many buyers, no legal limits on new firms.
  • Examples: market stalls, takeaways, hairdressers, phone cases sold online.
Mission 3

What competition does

WhoEffects of competition
ConsumersLower prices · more choice · better quality and service
ProducersPressure to cut costs and innovate · lower profits · weak firms may close
WorkersMore employers to choose from · but pressure on pay and job security as firms cut costs
  • Disadvantages: some firms close, so jobs go · costs wasted copying rivals and advertising · small firms may not afford research.
  • Say: "more sellers increase supply, so the price falls" — not: "more sellers lower demand".

Quick check

Mission 3

Monopoly

  • Pure monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market, with no close substitutes.
  • The firm is a price maker: it can choose its price (but if it charges more, it sells less).
  • Barriers to entry keep rivals out.
  • In practice, a firm with a large share has monopoly power. UK merger rules let the competition authority look closely at a merger that gives a firm 25% or more of supply.
  • Examples: the only water company in an area; a drug firm with a patent on a new medicine.
Mission 3

Barriers to entry

BarrierExample
LegalGovernment gives one firm a licence to run a service
PatentsA new invention protected, normally for up to 20 years
MarketingHuge advertising and strong brand loyalty
TechnologyRivals cannot copy secret know-how
Start-up costsBuilding a railway or an aircraft factory costs billions
Economies of scaleThe big firm's low average cost undercuts any newcomer
  • High barriers let a firm keep high profits for a long time: no new rivals arrive to compete them away.
Mission 3

Monopoly: good or bad?

  • Possible disadvantages:
    • higher prices and less output than a competitive market
    • less choice for consumers
    • little pressure to cut costs or improve quality
  • Possible advantages:
    • economies of scale can mean lower costs
    • high profits can pay for research (new medicines)
    • one network avoids wasteful copies (a natural monopoly: two sets of water pipes to every house would be a waste)
  • Whether consumers gain depends on whether lower costs are passed on and on regulation.
Quick check
?Which of these is NOT a barrier to entry into a market?
Mission 3

Oligopoly: a few big firms

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • Features: high barriers to entry · often branded products · firms are interdependent: each watches what its rivals do.
  • Concentration ratio: the combined market share of the largest firms.
  • Example: UK grocery shares, 12 weeks to 2 November 2025:
SupermarketShare
Tesco28.2%
Sainsbury's15.7%
Asda11.6%
Aldi10.6%
  • Four-firm concentration ratio = 28.2 + 15.7 + 11.6 + 10.6 = 66.1%.
Mission 3

Compete or collude?

  • Price war: firms cut prices again and again to win share. Great for consumers for a while; weak firms may leave.
  • Non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty cards, quality, after-sales service. Firms avoid a price war.
  • Collusion: firms secretly agree prices or share out the market instead of competing.
  • Cartel: a group of firms that collude. Illegal in the UK, the EU and many other countries.
  • Example: from 1997 to 2011, five major European truck makers colluded on prices. In 2016 the European Commission imposed fines totalling €2.93 billion.
Oligopoly: possible advantagesOligopoly: possible disadvantages
Economies of scaleCollusion keeps prices high
Profits fund research and new productsHigh barriers keep new firms out
Non-price competition can raise qualitySpending on advertising adds to costs
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a market, Firm W has a 30% share, Firm X 25%, Firm Y 15% and Firm Z 10%. Fifty small firms share the rest. What is the three-firm concentration ratio?
Mission 3

Keeping markets competitive

  • Governments use competition authorities (UK: the Competition and Markets Authority, CMA).
  • Mergers: the CMA can block a merger that would cut competition. In 2019 it blocked Sainsbury's and Asda.
  • Monopolies: regulators can cap prices and set quality targets (for example, for water companies).
  • Cartels: firms that collude can be fined.
  • Consumer protection: laws require goods to be safe, as described and of satisfactory quality.
  • Regulation costs money, and regulators may lack information about firms' real costs.
Mission check

?A government gives one company the only licence to run trains between two cities. There is no other practical way to travel between them. Compared with several companies competing on the route, what is most likely?

Remove competition and a firm can charge more; that is why governments regulate monopolies.

Mission 4

Mission 4: What decides how much a job pays, and why can two jobs pay so differently?

Make a guess: Why are some jobs paid much more than others?

Mission 4

A market for workers

  • In the labour market, workers supply labour and employers demand it.
  • The wage is the price of labour.
  • Derived demand: firms want workers because people want the goods and services they make. More online shopping → more delivery drivers wanted.
  • Diagram: wage on the vertical axis, number of workers on the horizontal.
    • Demand for labour slopes down: at a lower wage, firms hire more.
    • Supply of labour slopes up: at a higher wage, more people want the job.
Mission 4

What changes the demand for labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the demand curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • demand for the product changes (a boom in home deliveries → more drivers wanted)
    • productivity changes (each worker produces more, so each is worth more to the firm)
    • the cost of machines that can replace workers changes (cheaper self-checkouts → fewer cashiers wanted)
    • the cost of other inputs changes
  • Say: "a higher wage causes a contraction in demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls". The curve has not moved.
Mission 4

What changes the supply of labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the supply curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • population grows or shrinks
    • migration: workers arrive from, or leave for, other areas or countries
    • age structure: more people of working age
    • retirement age or school-leaving age changes
    • female participation: more women in paid work (childcare, attitudes)
    • skills and qualifications: more people trained for a job
    • mobility: how easily people can change job or move
    • non-wage factors of a job: conditions, hours, holidays, training, status
Quick check
?Which of these would shift the supply curve of nurses to the right?
Mission 4

How the wage is set

  • Step 1: demand for labour D and supply of labour S.
  • Step 2: they cross at the equilibrium wage (We) and employment (Le).
  • Above We: more people want the job than firms want to hire (excess supply). The wage is pushed down.
  • Below We: firms cannot fill jobs (excess demand, a shortage). The wage is pushed up.
  • Step 3: a shift in D or S gives a new We and Le.
Mission 4

Worked example: shifts

  • Illustrative data. Warehouse workers in a town: We = £12 an hour, Le = 520 workers.
  • Demand rises (online orders boom): D shifts right.
    • New wage £14, new employment 640.
    • Wage ↑ and employment ↑.
  • Supply rises instead (100 more people want the work at each wage): S shifts right.
    • New wage £11, new employment 560.
    • Wage ↓ but employment ↑.
ChangeCurveWageEmployment
Demand risesD rightUpUp
Demand fallsD leftDownDown
Supply risesS rightDownUp
Supply fallsS leftUpDown
  • Two jobs pay differently when their demand and supply differ: strong demand for the workers, or few people able and willing to do the job, means a higher wage.

Quick check
?Many people move into a town looking for warehouse work. Other things equal, what happens in the town's market for warehouse workers?
Mission 4

Human capital

  • Human capital: the skills, knowledge and experience of workers.
  • It grows through education, training and experience on the job.
  • More human capital → higher productivity → greater demand for those workers → higher pay.
  • It also raises the supply of skilled workers, so firms can fill skilled jobs.
  • Who pays? Individuals (time, fees), firms (training) and governments (schools, colleges).
Mission 4

Moving to where the jobs are

  • Occupational mobility: ease of moving between jobs. Blocked by lack of skills or qualifications.
  • Geographical mobility: ease of moving between places. Blocked by house prices and rents, family ties, moving costs, not knowing about jobs.
  • Immobility means vacancies in one job or area while people elsewhere are unemployed.
  • Policies: training and retraining, help with moving costs, more housing, better job information.
Quick check
?A miner loses his job when the mine closes. In the same town there are vacancies for software developers, but he does not have the skills. What does this show?
Mission check

?A country raises the age at which young people may leave education from 16 to 18. In the market for workers aged 16 to 17, what is the most likely effect?

Anything that changes how many people can or will do a job shifts supply, and moves pay and jobs in opposite ways.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can workers win higher pay without anyone losing their job?

Make a guess: A group of workers wins pay above the market wage. What happens to the number of jobs?

Mission 5

What trade unions do

  • A trade union: an organisation of workers that acts for its members.
  • Collective bargaining: the union negotiates with employers for all its members at once, not one by one.
  • Aims:
    • higher pay
    • better working conditions and health and safety
    • shorter hours, more holidays
    • job security and training
    • legal advice and support for members
  • If talks fail, unions may take industrial action: a strike (stop work), work to rule (do only the minimum the contract requires), an overtime ban.
Mission 5

Unions in numbers

  • UK, 2025: 6.6 million employees were union members.
  • Union density (members as a % of employees): 22.4%, about 1 employee in 5.
    • Public sector: 48.5%. Private sector: 12.1%.
  • Membership peaked at 13.2 million in 1979 (a different measure, from union records).
  • Members' average pay was 5.3% higher than non-members', before allowing for other differences (job, skills, sector).
  • Why fewer members? Fewer jobs in mining and factories, more small firms, part-time and self-employed work, and changes to union law.
Mission 5

What makes a union strong?

  • A union has more bargaining power when:
    • most workers in the job are members
    • strikers are hard to replace, by other workers or by machines
    • the work is essential and a strike quickly stops production
    • the employer is profitable and can afford a rise
    • demand for the product is strong, so higher costs can be passed on in prices
    • unemployment is low, so employers struggle to find other workers
    • the law and the public support it
Mission 5

A union and the wage

  • Illustrative data. A competitive labour market: We = £12 an hour, Le = 500 workers.
  • Step 2: the union wins a wage of £14, above We.
    • Firms hire fewer: a contraction of demand for labour to 400.
    • More people want the job: 600.
    • Excess supply of 200: some workers are left without these jobs.
    • Members in work are paid more; others lose out.
  • Step 3: instead, the union agrees changes that raise productivity. Each worker is worth more, so demand for labour shifts right.
    • New equilibrium: £14 and 600 workers. Pay and jobs rise.
  • The job loss in step 2 is smaller where demand for labour is inelastic: workers hard to replace, wages a small share of costs.
Quick check
?Use the market in the previous screen (We = £12, Le = 500; firms hire 450 workers at £13, and 550 people want the job). The union wins a wage of £13 instead of £14. How many fewer workers are employed than before?
Mission 5

The minimum wage

  • A minimum wage: the lowest hourly wage employers may legally pay.
  • It is a price floor in the labour market. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium wage.
  • UK: a National Minimum Wage since 1999. From 1 April 2026: £12.71 an hour (age 21 and over; called the National Living Wage), £10.85 for ages 18–20, £8.00 for ages 16–17.
  • Germany: a national minimum wage since 1 January 2015, starting at €8.50 an hour.
  • Reasons:
    • raise the pay of the lowest-paid and reduce poverty
    • stop employers exploiting workers with little bargaining power
    • make work pay more than not working
    • reduce inequality
Mission 5

The minimum wage diagram

  • Step 1: the market wage We = £12, employment Le = 500 (illustrative data, as an earlier screen).
  • Step 2: a minimum wage Wmin = £13, drawn as a horizontal line above We.
  • Step 3: at £13:
    • firms hire 450 (a contraction along the demand curve)
    • 550 people want work (an extension along the supply curve)
    • excess supply = 550 − 450 = 100: unemployment caused by the minimum wage
  • Those who keep their jobs gain; those who lose jobs or cannot find one lose.
  • Say: "a minimum wage below the equilibrium wage has no effect" — not: "it pulls the wage down to the minimum".
Mission 5Graph lab

Set the minimum wage

  • Takeaway: a floor below We does nothing; above We, fewer jobs, and more so when demand for labour is elastic.
Quick check
?Use the market in an earlier screen. At £14, firms want 400 workers and 600 people want work. The minimum wage is set at £14. How much unemployment does it create in this market?
Mission 5

Minimum wage: for, against, evidence

ForAgainst
Higher pay for the lowest-paidMay cut jobs or hours, especially where machines can replace workers
Less poverty among people in workHigher costs can mean higher prices
Workers may be more motivated; staff stay longerSmall firms may struggle to pay
Low-paid workers spend moreDoes not help households with no one in work
  • Evidence: when New Jersey (USA) raised its minimum wage in 1992, a study by Card and Krueger (1994) found no fall in fast-food jobs compared with nearby Pennsylvania. Other economists, using different data, found a small fall.
  • UK: the Low Pay Commission (2024) said it had not found strong evidence that the National Living Wage cut jobs or hours.
  • Effects depend on how high the minimum wage is set: evidence from moderate rises may not apply to big ones.
Mission check

?A country sets a minimum wage above the market wage for hotel workers. In the same year, tourism booms and hotels are full. A year later, hotel workers' pay is higher and no fewer of them are employed. Which is the best explanation?

A higher wage on its own costs jobs; a rise in the demand for labour can lift pay and jobs together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Economies of scale: average cost falls as a firm grows. Diseconomies of scale: it rises.
  • Internal economies (technical, purchasing, financial, managerial, risk-bearing, marketing) come from the firm's own growth.
  • External economies come from the industry's growth and shift every firm's LRAC curve down.

Mission 2

  • Most firms are small: small markets, owners' choices and finance hold them back.
  • Firms grow internally or by mergers and takeovers; growth can lower average costs.
  • Mergers are horizontal, vertical (backward or forward) or conglomerate.

Mission 3

  • Market structures run from many sellers to one, depending on numbers, differentiation and ease of entry.
  • Competition lowers prices and profits and widens choice; barriers to entry protect monopoly power.
  • In oligopoly a few firms may compete hard, use non-price competition, or collude.

Mission 4

  • The wage is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour.
  • Shifts in demand move wage and employment the same way; shifts in supply move them opposite ways.

Mission 5

  • Unions bargain collectively; a wage above equilibrium raises members' pay but can cut jobs.
  • Pay and jobs rise together when the demand for labour shifts right, for example through higher productivity.
  • A minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess supply of labour; the evidence on job losses is mixed.
