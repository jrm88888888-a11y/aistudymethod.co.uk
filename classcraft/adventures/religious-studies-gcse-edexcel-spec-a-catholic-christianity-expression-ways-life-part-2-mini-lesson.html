Mission 1

Why symbols?

  • A symbol is a sign that stands for something more than itself.
  • Christian symbols pack a belief into a simple image: who Jesus is, what he did, what Christians hope for.
  • They teach (even those who cannot read), identify Christians and their buildings, and focus prayer.
  • They appear on church walls and windows, vestments, altar cloths, jewellery, gravestones and car stickers.
  • The most important Christian symbol is the cross.
  • The grid also shows the fish, the Greek letters Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega, and the dove.
Mission 1

Cross and crucifix

  • Cross: reminds Christians that Jesus died on a cross to save people from sin. "The word of the cross is… to us who are being saved… the power of God." (1 Corinthians 1:18)
  • Plain (empty) cross: Jesus is no longer on it: he is risen. Common in Protestant churches; also used by Catholics.
  • Crucifix: a cross with the figure of Christ: his suffering and sacrifice out of love. Usual in Catholic churches, and in some Anglican and Lutheran ones; Orthodox use painted crosses.
  • Uses: on or above the altar; carried in processions; worn on a chain; held by the dying; the sign of the cross made over the body in prayer.
  • "If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me." (Matthew 16:24)

The cross that makes peace

"For he is our peace, who made both one, and broke down the middle wall of separation" (Ephesians 2:14)

"…and might reconcile them both in one body to God through the cross, having killed the hostility through it." (Ephesians 2:16)

  • Context (2:10–19, paraphrase): believers are God's work, made for good works (2:10); Jews and Gentiles, once divided, are brought near and become "fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God" (2:19).

Q1 According to 2:16, what did the cross do?

Show answer

Reconciled divided people to God and to each other; it killed the hostility.

Q2 How might this passage shape the use of the cross in a church?

Show answer

As a sign of peace and unity: all people one family before God.

Mission 1

The fish (Ichthus)

  • Ichthus (ΙΧΘΥΣ) is Greek for "fish".
  • Its letters begin the words Iesous Christos Theou Uios Soter: "Jesus Christ, God's Son, Saviour": a short creed.
  • Fish recall the Gospels: Jesus calls fishermen to be "fishers for men" (Mark 1:17) and feeds thousands with loaves and fish.
  • Used by early Christians in catacombs and on tombs; tradition says it was a secret sign in times of persecution.
  • Today: car badges, jewellery, logos: a public sign of faith.
  • Relevance: a whole statement of belief in one simple outline.
Mission 1

Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega

  • Chi-Rho (☧): the Greek letters Χ (ch) and Ρ (r), the first two letters of Christos (Christ, "anointed one").
  • Tradition links it with the emperor Constantine, who put Christ's sign on his soldiers' shields before winning the battle of the Milvian Bridge (AD 312).
  • Seen on vestments, altar cloths, tombs and church doors: Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah.
  • Alpha (Α) and Omega (Ω): the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End." (Revelation 22:13)
  • Meaning: Christ is eternal, there before creation and at the end of time.
  • At the Easter Vigil the priest marks Α, Ω and the year on the Paschal candle: all time belongs to the risen Christ.
Mission 1

Dove, eagle and lamb

  • Dove: the Holy Spirit. At Jesus' baptism "He saw the Spirit of God descending as a dove" (Matthew 3:16). Also peace: Noah's dove returned with an olive leaf (Genesis 8:11).
  • CCC 701 (paraphrase): the dove of the Flood and of Jesus' baptism; Christian art traditionally uses the dove for the Spirit.
  • Eagle: many church lecterns are brass eagles with outstretched wings: carrying God's Word to the world. The eagle is also St John's symbol, and a sign of rising to God: "They will mount up with wings like eagles." (Isaiah 40:31)
  • Lamb: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Christ the sacrifice, gentle and innocent; in Revelation the Lamb stands "as though it had been slain" (5:6): sacrificed yet alive.
Mission 1

The four evangelists

  • The four Gospel writers have symbols taken from the four living creatures (Ezekiel 1:10; Revelation 4:7).
  • Matthew: a man (or angel): his Gospel opens with Jesus' human family tree.
  • Mark: a lion: opens with John the Baptist, a voice "crying in the wilderness", like a lion's roar.
  • Luke: an ox (or calf), an animal of sacrifice: opens with the priest Zechariah in the Temple.
  • John: an eagle: his Gospel soars from the start: "In the beginning was the Word".
  • Found on pulpits, Bible covers, windows and around images of Christ: four Gospels, one Christ.

Mission 1Diversity

Symbols used in different ways

  • Catholic and Orthodox: rich use: crucifixes, painted crosses, Chi-Rho and Α–Ω on vestments, the Paschal candle, mosaics and icons.
  • Anglican and Lutheran: wide range: from crucifixes and vestments to plain crosses.
  • Many Protestant and evangelical churches: plain cross, simple communion table; symbols kept few so that the Word is central.
  • Quakers: no symbols in meeting houses.
  • Other settings: jewellery, car badges, school logos, gravestones, flags; some Christians worry a cross worn as fashion loses its meaning.
Mission check

?A hospital prayer room is shared by Christians of every denomination. The chaplains choose one plain wooden cross with no figure on it. Which is the most likely reason?

Every Christian symbol is a compressed belief: the cross, plain or with Christ's figure, says the same thing with a different emphasis.

Mission 2

Faith as drama

  • Christianity is built on a story: creation, fall, the life, death and resurrection of Jesus, the end of time.
  • Drama lets people see and enter the story, not just hear it.
  • Christian worship is already dramatic: on Palm Sunday people process with palms; the Passion is read in parts (the congregation as the crowd); on Maundy Thursday the priest washes feet; on Good Friday people come forward to the cross; at the Easter Vigil a new fire is lit in the dark.
  • CCC 2567 (paraphrase): God always calls first; prayer is our answer, a two-way call that the Catechism describes as a "covenant drama" running through the whole history of salvation and engaging the heart.
  • So drama can be prayer, teaching and mission at once.
Mission 2

Mystery plays

  • Medieval English cycles of short plays telling the Bible story from Creation to the Last Judgement.
  • Performed in towns such as York, Chester and Wakefield, often around the feast of Corpus Christi (summer).
  • Each play was staged by a craft guild (a "mystery", from a word for a trade), often on a pageant wagon moved through the streets: e.g. shipwrights staged Noah's Ark.
  • In English, not Latin, with humour and everyday characters, so ordinary people could understand and remember.
  • Stopped at the Reformation (York's last medieval performance: 1569); revived in the 20th century (York and Chester, 1951) and still performed, often by local amateurs.
Mission 2

Passion plays

  • A passion play dramatises Jesus' suffering, death and resurrection (his "Passion"), as told in Matthew 26–28 and the other Gospels.
  • Oberammergau, Bavaria: during plague in 1633 the villagers vowed to perform the Passion if spared; first performed 1634. Now staged every ten years by the villagers: about 1,800 took part in 2022 (delayed from 2020 by Covid-19).
  • Modern public passion plays: Good Friday in Trafalgar Square, London; The Passion staged across Port Talbot, Wales, over Easter 2011.
  • Warning from history: some passion plays blamed "the Jews" for Jesus' death and fuelled hatred. Since the Second Vatican Council (Nostra Aetate, 1965) the Catholic Church teaches that Jews as a people must not be blamed; Oberammergau has rewritten its script accordingly.
Mission 2Read the source

Matthew 26–28

Matthew 26–28 tells the story every passion play retells: the Last Supper, Gethsemane, arrest, trials, Peter's denial, crucifixion, burial and resurrection.

  • In Gethsemane: "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire." (Matthew 26:39)
  • On the cross: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46)
  • At the tomb: "He is not here, for he has risen, just like he said." (Matthew 28:6)

Q1 What does Matthew 26:39 show about Jesus?

Show answer

He is afraid, yet obeys his Father's will: fully human and fully obedient.

Q2 Why must a passion play include Matthew 28:6?

Show answer

Without the resurrection the story ends in defeat; Christians believe the cross leads to new life.

Mission 2

Dramatised prayer: the Stations of the Cross

  • Fourteen stations: pictures or carvings around the walls of a Catholic church, each showing a moment on Jesus' way to the cross.
  • From condemned to death (1), takes up his cross (2) … Simon of Cyrene helps (5) … Veronica wipes his face (6, from tradition) … dies (12) … laid in the tomb (14). Many add a 15th: the resurrection.
  • Believers walk from station to station, kneel or bow, read, pray and reflect: "We adore you, O Christ, and we praise you, because by your holy cross you have redeemed the world."
  • Especially on Fridays in Lent and Good Friday; the Pope leads them at the Colosseum in Rome.
  • It began with pilgrims in Jerusalem walking the Via Dolorosa; the Stations bring that pilgrimage into every church.
Mission 2

Drama for mission and evangelism

  • Street and public drama: passion plays in city squares; Stations of the Cross staged through city streets at Catholic World Youth Days.
  • Nativity plays in schools and churches: for many children their first meeting with the Christmas story.
  • Theatre and musicals: from medieval Bible plays to Jesus Christ Superstar (1970 album; stage 1971).
  • Film and television: Mel Gibson's The Passion of the Christ (2004, Matthew 26–28 and the other Gospels); the series The Chosen (from 2019), watched by Christians of many denominations.
  • Influence: some viewers say drama brought them to faith or deepened it; churches use these works for discussion groups.
  • Criticism: drama may add to, or change, the Bible; violence or commercial aims can distract from the message.
Mission 2Diversity

Different views on drama

  • Catholic and Anglican: welcome drama in liturgy (Holy Week) and outside it (passion plays, nativities).
  • Orthodox: the Divine Liturgy itself is richly dramatic, but plays are not part of worship.
  • Evangelical and Pentecostal: often use short drama sketches in services and youth work to make a point vividly.
  • Some Protestants (especially Puritans in the 17th century) opposed theatre as frivolous or deceitful; the theatres were closed in 1642. Some still feel actors should not play Jesus.
  • In church or elsewhere? Some say drama belongs in church, where it is prayer; others take it to the streets, where it is mission.
Quick check

Mission 2Evaluate

"Drama is the best way to share the Christian message today."

  • Reasons for: drama reaches people who never read the Bible or enter a church; it engages emotions as well as minds; the Passion is itself a dramatic story; passion plays and films have influenced many; Jesus taught through vivid stories (parables).
  • Reasons against: drama may change or add to scripture; it can become entertainment, not faith; the Word preached and the sacraments are central (many Protestants and Catholics would say); Christian lives of service may speak louder than any performance; some find actors playing Jesus disrespectful.
  • Judge it by: Is "best" about reaching most people, or about changing lives? Can drama be prayer as well as performance? Does it matter where it happens: church, street or screen?
Mission check

?A Catholic school replaces an ordinary assembly with a silent walk around the playground, stopping at fourteen posters of Jesus' journey to the cross, with a short prayer at each. Which statement best describes what they are doing?

Christian drama can be prayer (the Stations), teaching (nativity plays) and mission (passion plays): all three let people step into the story they believe.

Mission 3

Why sing at all?

  • Christians have sung in worship from the start: "speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs; singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19).
  • Music does three jobs:
  • Praise: it gives words to joy and thanks to God.
  • Unity: many voices become one voice, one community.
  • Memory and teaching: sung words stick, and carry belief.
  • The Catechism (CCC 2641) says the first Christians sang the psalms in a new way, as songs about Christ, and wrote new hymns about his life, death and rising (paraphrase).
Mission 3

Psalms: the Bible's songbook

  • The Book of Psalms: 150 songs and prayers of ancient Israel, sung by Jesus and the first Christians.
  • They cover every mood: praise, thanks, sorrow, anger, trust.
  • "Oh come, let's sing to the LORD. Let's shout aloud to the rock of our salvation!" (Psalm 95:1)
  • At Mass: the Responsorial Psalm follows the first reading; a cantor sings verses, everyone sings the response.
  • Daily prayer: monks, nuns, priests and many lay Catholics pray the psalms in the Liturgy of the Hours.
Mission 3

Plainchant

  • Plainchant (plainsong): one unaccompanied melody line, sung in unison, with a free rhythm that follows the words.
  • Gregorian chant is the Latin plainchant of the Western Church, named after Pope Gregory I (pope 590–604). Most of it took shape later, in the 8th–9th centuries.
  • Written in neumes: square notes on a four-line stave.
  • Words come first: no harmony, no beat, nothing to distract from the prayer.
  • Significance: calm and prayerful; the same chants are sung across the world and down the centuries.
  • Used today in monasteries, cathedrals, Latin Masses and seasons such as Lent and Holy Week.
Mission 3Read the source

Musicam Sacram (1967)

After the Second Vatican Council, the Church issued Musicam Sacram ("Sacred Music"), an Instruction of the Sacred Congregation of Rites, 5 March 1967. In paraphrase:

  • Worship takes a nobler form when it is sung: prayer is expressed more beautifully, hearts are united by the union of voices, minds are raised to heavenly things, and the singing looks forward to the worship of heaven (§5).
  • The people should take part by singing, especially the acclamations and responses (§16).
  • In sung Latin services, Gregorian chant has "pride of place" (§50).

Q1 Give one reason the document gives for singing worship rather than saying it.

Show answer

It unites hearts; raises minds to heaven; foreshadows heaven's worship.

Q2 Who is meant to sing: the choir alone, or everyone?

Show answer

Everyone: the people's singing comes first, especially acclamations.

  • The specification spells it "Musicum Sacrum"; the document's title is Musicam Sacram.

Mission 3

Traditional hymns

  • A hymn is a song of praise in verses (stanzas), sung by the whole congregation to a repeated tune, often with organ.
  • Hymns spread fast at the Reformation: Martin Luther's "A Mighty Fortress" (c. 1529).
  • English hymn writers: Isaac Watts, "When I survey the wondrous cross" (1707); Charles Wesley, "And can it be" (1738).
  • Catholic hymns: Thomas Aquinas's Pange lingua (1264); John Henry Newman's "Praise to the Holiest in the height" (1865).
  • At Mass, hymns are often sung at the entrance, the offertory, Communion and the end.
  • Significance: hymns teach doctrine in memorable words ("sung theology") and let everyone join in.
Mission 3

Mass settings

  • The Ordinary of the Mass: the sung texts that stay the same each week: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus) and Agnus Dei.
  • A Mass setting is one composer's music for these texts.
  • Chant: e.g. Missa de Angelis, still sung by congregations.
  • Polyphony (several independent voice parts): Palestrina, Missa Papae Marcelli (published 1567); William Byrd, Mass for Four Voices (c. 1592–93), written for English Catholics who had to worship in secret.
  • Classical: Mozart, Coronation Mass (1779).
  • Modern congregational: James MacMillan, Mass of Blessed John Henry Newman (2010), written for Pope Benedict XVI's visit to Britain.
Mission 3Diversity

Who sings what, and where?

  • Catholic: chant, hymns, Mass settings, cantor and organ; Responsorial Psalm every Sunday.
  • Orthodox: the whole Liturgy is sung, usually by an unaccompanied choir; traditionally no instruments.
  • Anglican: cathedral choirs sing Choral Evensong; psalms in Anglican chant.
  • Presbyterian: metrical psalms and hymns; some congregations sing psalms only.
  • Quakers: usually no set music; silence is the setting for worship.
  • When and why: chant in Lent and Holy Week (simplicity, penance); psalms at every Mass (the Church's prayer book); hymns at weddings, funerals and school Masses (everyone knows them).
  • Other settings: carol services, concerts of Mass settings, Taizé and monastery retreats, school assemblies.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Traditional music is the best music for worship."

  • Reasons for: tested by centuries; deep, biblical words; links worshippers to the whole Church, past and present; calm, not about performers.
  • Also for: chant has "pride of place" in Latin liturgy (Musicam Sacram §50).
  • Reasons against: Latin chant and old hymn language can be hard to understand; young people may find it remote; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1).
  • Also against: Musicam Sacram also values music that lets the people take part.
  • Another view: "best" depends on the occasion: chant at a requiem, a lively hymn at a school Mass.
  • Judge it by: Does it help everyone pray and take part? Do the words express the Church's beliefs? Does it suit the occasion?
Mission check

?A Catholic parish has families from Poland, Nigeria, the Philippines and England. For the Easter Vigil, the priest has everyone sing the "Holy, holy" (Sanctus) in a simple Latin chant that all can learn. Which reason best fits Catholic teaching on music?

Sung prayer unites voices and hearts; traditional music adds a link to the whole Church, past and present.

Mission 4

What counts as "contemporary"?

  • Contemporary worship music: songs written from the 1960s onwards in current musical styles.
  • Typical features: simple, repeated choruses; everyday language; guitars, keyboard, drums; words on a screen; a worship band leading.
  • Worship songs often speak directly to God ("you", "I", "we").
  • Modern hymns keep verses and doctrine but use modern tunes, e.g. Keith Getty and Stuart Townend, "In Christ Alone" (2001).
  • Also contemporary, but quiet: Taizé chants from the ecumenical Taizé community in France: short lines repeated many times as a prayer.
Mission 4

Graham Kendrick

  • Graham Kendrick (born 1950): English songwriter, from a Baptist background; one of the pioneers of British worship songs.
  • "The Servant King" (1983), also known as "From heaven you came": Jesus leaves heaven to serve, and suffers in Gethsemane; it echoes Philippians 2:6–8.
  • "Meekness and Majesty" (1986): Jesus fully God and fully human.
  • "Shine, Jesus, Shine" (1987): Christ as light of the world, asked to fill the nation.
  • Co-founded March for Jesus (1987): Christians singing in city streets as public witness.
  • His songs are in Catholic and Protestant hymn books and sung in schools.
Mission 4

Casting Crowns

  • Casting Crowns: American Christian rock band formed in 1999 by Mark Hall, a youth pastor, with students from his church.
  • Hall kept working as a youth pastor while the band toured: the songs come from church life.
  • "Who Am I": wonder that the God of the universe knows and loves each person (compare Psalm 8:4).
  • "Praise You in This Storm": trusting and praising God in suffering, even when prayers seem unanswered.
  • "If We Are the Body": challenges churches that ignore outsiders; Christians as the Body of Christ must welcome them.
  • Heard more on Christian radio, at concerts and at youth events than sung at Mass.

Mission 4

Charismatic worship and tongues

  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship stresses the gifts (charismata) of the Holy Spirit, as at Pentecost (Acts 2:4).
  • Long "times of worship": singing flows from song to song; people raise hands, clap, pray aloud.
  • Catholics too: the Catholic Charismatic Renewal began in 1967 and has prayer groups in many parishes.
Mission 4Read the source

Catechism 2641

The Catechism (CCC 2641) begins by quoting Paul: "speaking to one another in psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs; singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord" (Ephesians 5:19). Then, in paraphrase:

  • The first Christians read the psalms in a new way, singing in them the mystery of Christ.
  • Filled with the Spirit, they also wrote new hymns about what God had done in Jesus: his Incarnation, death, resurrection and ascension.
  • Praise of God (doxology) springs from wonder at all God has done to save.

Q1 According to CCC 2641, were the first Christians content with old songs only?

Show answer

No: they sang the psalms and also composed new hymns.

Q2 What were the new songs about?

Show answer

Christ: his Incarnation, death, resurrection and ascension.

  • Link: "Sing to the LORD a new song!" (Psalm 96:1).

Mission 4

Where and when it is used

  • Protestant churches: in many evangelical, charismatic and Pentecostal churches, the band-led time of worship is the heart of the service.
  • Catholic parishes: often at youth and school Masses, Confirmation, prayer groups; worship songs sit alongside hymns and the Responsorial Psalm.
  • At Mass, music must fit the liturgy: the people's parts (Gloria, Sanctus) keep their set words, even in a modern musical setting.
  • Other settings: youth festivals and large Catholic youth events, Christian concerts and radio, school assemblies, street marches, streaming at home.
  • Why: to reach young people, to let people pray in their own musical language, to share faith with outsiders.
Mission 4Diversity

The debates

  • For contemporary music: everyday language people understand; emotional and personal; draws young people and newcomers; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1); early Christians wrote new hymns.
  • The Catechism makes that last point: the first Christians composed new hymns about Christ (CCC 2641).
  • Against (or cautious): some lyrics are thin on doctrine, or focus on "I" more than "we"; repetition can feel shallow; can feel like a concert, with the band performing; can push out the Church's older treasury of music.
  • Some Catholic writers, including Joseph Ratzinger (later Pope Benedict XVI) in The Spirit of the Liturgy (2000), argued that some pop and rock styles do not suit the liturgy.
  • Orthodox churches keep unaccompanied singing; Quakers often use no music at all.
  • Most churches mix old and new.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Contemporary worship songs are better than traditional hymns for worship today."

  • Reasons for: understood by everyone; personal and heartfelt; attract young people and newcomers; a "new song" (Psalm 96:1); continues what the first Christians did, who wrote new hymns.
  • Also for: CCC 2641 says the first Christians composed new hymns about Christ.
  • Reasons against: traditional hymns carry richer doctrine in many verses; tested over centuries; shared by all generations; less dependent on a band's skill; less risk of performance.
  • Another view: both are needed; the question is which helps this congregation pray on this occasion.
  • Judge it by: Do the words express Christian belief accurately? Can the whole congregation join in? Does it suit the occasion?
Mission 4Worked answer

Two ways music expresses belief

Question: Explain two ways contemporary music is used to express Christian belief. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Way 1: songs put beliefs about Jesus into words people remember. Development: Kendrick's "The Servant King" retells how Jesus gave up heaven to serve, so worshippers learn the Incarnation as they sing.
  • Way 2: songs express praise for what God has done. Source: CCC 2641 says the first Christians composed new hymns about Christ's Incarnation, death, resurrection and ascension. Development: so Catholics see new songs as continuing that tradition.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two ways, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.
Mission check

?A hospital chapel holds a Sunday service. One week a nurse leads a guitar worship song; the next week the chaplain uses an old chant. Most Christians who use both would say music is fit for worship when…

Christians judge worship music by what it says and how it helps people pray; old and new can both serve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The cross is the central Christian symbol: plain (risen Christ) or crucifix (sacrifice); altar, table and different crosses emphasise different aspects of belief.
  • Chi-Rho = Christ; Alpha–Omega = Christ eternal.
  • Dove = Spirit; man, lion, ox and eagle = the four evangelists.

Mission 2

  • Christian drama retells the story of faith: passion plays (Matthew 26–28), e.g. Oberammergau since 1634.
  • Mystery plays: medieval guild cycles from creation to judgement.
  • The Stations of the Cross are dramatised prayer: the Church as a people of God journeying with Christ and serving others.
  • Drama is used for mission and evangelism; Christians differ on its place in worship and its risks.

Mission 3

  • Christians sing to praise God, unite as one body, and remember what they believe: psalms, plainchant and hymns are the traditional forms.

Mission 4

  • Contemporary worship music uses current styles, simple repeated choruses and bands; modern hymns keep doctrine with new tunes.
  • Graham Kendrick (English, "The Servant King", "Shine, Jesus, Shine") and Casting Crowns (American band, Mark Hall) are key examples; CCC 2641 shows the Church has always written new songs.
