Mission 1

Mission 1: A plant stem can grow longer, and even bend towards a window, without making new cells. How?

Make a guess: A plant stem can grow longer, and even bend towards a window, without making new cells. How?

Mission 1

Chemical messengers from the tips

  • Plants coordinate growth with chemical signals: plant hormones.
  • CCEA calls them plant growth substances.
  • Made in growing regions (shoot and root tips); they move to other tissues and act there.
  • No glands, no blood: they move from cell to cell or in the vascular tissue.
  • The best known: auxin. The main natural auxin is IAA (indoleacetic acid).
Mission 1

Auxin makes cells longer

  • IAA acts in the region just behind the tip.
  • It promotes cell elongation, not cell division.
  • More elongation on one side than the other → the organ bends.

Mission check

?Stem segments were floated in an auxin solution or in water for two days. Segments in auxin grew 30% longer (illustrative data). Both groups had the same number of cells per segment. What explains the extra length?

A stem can lengthen without a single new cell: auxin makes the cells it already has longer.

Mission 2

Mission 2: With no brain or nerves, how does a plant know when to wake a seed, open a flower or ripen its fruit?

Make a guess: With no brain or nerves, how does a plant know when to wake a seed, open a flower or ripen its fruit?

Mission 2

A team of plant hormones

  • Plants use several hormones, not just auxin. They often act together.
  • Auxins: cell elongation (see Mission 1).
  • Cytokinins: cell division.
  • Gibberellins (GA): stem elongation; seed germination.
Mission 2

Gibberellins make longer stems

  • Internode: the stretch of stem between two nodes (where leaves attach).
  • Gibberellins promote elongation of internodes: cells elongate (and divide).
Quick check
?Dwarf pea plants sprayed with gibberellin grew as tall as untreated tall plants. Tall plants sprayed with gibberellin grew only a little taller than untreated tall plants (illustrative data). Which conclusion do these results support?
Mission 2

Cytokinins: more cells, balanced growth

  • Cytokinins promote cell division.
  • Stem elongation needs all three:
  • Cytokinins → more cells (division).
  • Auxins → longer cells (elongation).
  • Gibberellins → longer internodes.
Quick check
?A plant growth substance is applied to a young stem. Its cells divide more often, but each new cell grows no longer than usual. Which substance was most likely applied?
Mission 2

Phytochrome and flowering

  • Phytochrome: a pigment in leaves, in two interchangeable forms.
  • P660 absorbs red light → rapidly converts to P730.
  • P730 absorbs far-red light → rapidly converts to P660.
  • In the dark, P730 converts slowly to P660.
  • Daylight has more red than far-red: P730 builds up by day.
  • Critical night length: the dark period needed to remove P730.
  • Short-day plants flower only if P730 is removed (long nights).
  • Long-day plants flower only if P730 is not removed (short nights).
  • Growers flower plants out of season: blackout covers (longer nights) or night-time lighting (P730 kept high).

Mission check

?A short-day plant is given 14 hours of darkness each night and flowers. A second group gets the same nights, but with a 5-minute flash of red light in the middle of each night: it does not flower. A third group gets red then far-red in the middle of each night: it flowers. What explains these results?

A five-minute flash can decide whether a plant flowers: the plant reads the colour of light, not how much it gets.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Make a guess: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Mission 3

A motor neurone

  • Cell body: nucleus and most of the cytoplasm.
  • Dendrites: many short fibres; carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: one long fibre; carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Axon terminals end at an effector (a muscle or gland).
  • Dendrons link the dendrites to the cell body.
Mission 3

Myelin

  • Many axons are wrapped in a myelin sheath.
  • Made by Schwann cells, which wrap their membrane round and round the axon.
  • Myelin is lipid-rich: an electrical insulator.
  • Gaps between sheath sections: nodes of Ranvier.
  • Non-myelinated axons have no sheath and no nodes.
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a neurone is correct?
Mission 3

The resting potential

  • At rest, the inside of the axon is negative relative to the outside.
  • Typical value: about −70 mV. This is the resting potential.
  • A membrane with a charge difference across it is polarised.
  • Membrane potential = voltage inside relative to outside.
  • The neurone is not "off": it is held ready to fire.
  • Holding this charge difference costs energy: a membrane pump uses ATP all the time, even when no impulse is sent.
Mission check

?An electron micrograph of a transverse section through a peripheral axon shows the axon surrounded by many tightly packed concentric layers of membrane. Outside the layers is a thin rim of cytoplasm containing a nucleus. What are the layers, and what is their effect?

A neurone is never working alone: its Schwann cells insulate it, and the resting membrane is held polarised, ready to fire.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Make a guess: Why do some nerve signals race faster than a car while others creep along at walking pace?

Mission 4

An action potential

  • A nerve impulse is an action potential: a brief reversal of the membrane potential, lasting about 1–2 ms.
  • Resting: −70 mV.
  • Depolarisation: a stimulus raises the potential to threshold (about −55 mV), then it shoots up to about +30 to +40 mV.
  • Repolarisation: it falls back towards −70 mV.
  • Hyperpolarisation: a brief dip below −70 mV, then back to rest.
Mission 4

All or nothing

  • Below threshold: no action potential.
  • At or above threshold: a full-size action potential, the same size every time.
  • This is the all-or-nothing principle.
  • The smallest stimulus that reaches threshold is the threshold stimulus.
  • So a stronger stimulus cannot make a bigger action potential.
  • It makes more action potentials per second: a higher frequency.
Quick check
?Two oscilloscope traces (illustrative data) were recorded from the same sensory neurone. With a weak stimulus there were 5 action potentials in 100 ms; with a strong stimulus there were 20 action potentials in 100 ms. In both traces every action potential peaked at +30 mV. What do the traces show?
Mission 4

The refractory period

  • Straight after an action potential, that region cannot fire again: the refractory period.
  • The membrane must repolarise before another action potential can develop.
  • Effects: impulses are discrete (separate); they travel one way; their frequency is limited.
  • Worked example: refractory period = 2 ms.
  • Step 1: 2 ms = 0.002 s.
  • Step 2: maximum frequency = 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 impulses per second.
Mission 4

Local currents carry it along

  • Current flows in local circuits between the active region and the next, depolarising it to threshold.
  • The region behind is refractory (it cannot fire again yet), so the impulse moves forwards only.
  • The ions only move a short way: the impulse travels, not the ions.
Mission 4

Myelin speeds things up

  • Myelinated fibres conduct much faster than non-myelinated fibres of similar diameter.
  • Myelin insulates: no ions cross the membrane under it.
  • So action potentials occur only at the nodes. Local currents spread to the next node.
  • The impulse jumps node to node: saltatory conduction. Fewer regions to depolarise → faster.
  • Say: "myelin insulates" — not: "myelin conducts the impulse".
Mission 4

What sets the speed?

  • Myelination: the biggest effect.
  • Axon diameter: wider axon → less resistance to current flow inside it → faster.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Make a guess: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Mission 5

Inside a synapse

  • A synapse is a junction between a neurone and another cell.
  • Presynaptic side: the axon terminal, the synaptic knob.
  • It is also called the synaptic bulb.
  • It holds vesicles of neurotransmitter and many mitochondria.
  • The synaptic cleft: a gap of about 20 nm.
  • Postsynaptic membrane: carries specific receptor proteins.
  • At a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
  • Other neurotransmitters: noradrenaline, GABA.
Mission 5

Transmission, step by step

  • (1) An action potential depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
  • (2) Calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • (3) Ca²⁺ is a signal inside the neurone: vesicles move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. ACh is released by exocytosis.
  • (4) ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to its complementary receptors.
  • (5) The receptors open Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation: an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  • If the depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential starts in the postsynaptic neurone.
  • Say: "ACh binds to receptors with a complementary shape" — not: "ACh enters the next neurone".
Quick check
?What is the role of calcium ions in the release of neurotransmitter from a presynaptic neurone?
Mission 5

Clearing the cleft

  • The signal must stop, or the postsynaptic cell would keep firing.
  • Neurotransmitter is removed: broken down, or taken back into the presynaptic neurone (reuptake).
  • Acetylcholinesterase (in the cleft) hydrolyses ACh into choline and ethanoic acid.
  • These diffuse back into the synaptic knob and are recombined into ACh, using ATP from the mitochondria.
  • ACh is then repackaged into vesicles.
Mission 5

One-way traffic

  • Signals cross a synapse in one direction only: presynaptic → postsynaptic.
  • Why: vesicles of neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side.
  • And receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
Quick check
?Why can a signal pass in only one direction across a synapse?
Mission 5

Neurone to muscle

  • A synapse can link a neurone to an effector cell, such as a muscle fibre.
  • Motor neurone → skeletal muscle fibre: the neuromuscular junction.
  • Its neurotransmitter is acetylcholine: the same one used at many synapses between neurones.
  • ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fibre membrane → depolarisation → the fibre contracts.
Quick check
?A motor neurone forms a neuromuscular junction with a skeletal muscle fibre. Which statement about acetylcholine at this junction is correct?
Mission check

?Calcium ions were removed from the fluid around a synapse. When the presynaptic neurone was stimulated, action potentials still reached the presynaptic terminal, but no response was recorded in the postsynaptic neurone. What does this result show?

The gap is crossed by a chemical, and the chemical is released only when calcium tells the vesicles to go.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plant hormones are made in growing regions and move to other tissues; auxin (IAA) promotes cell elongation, and unequal elongation bends an organ.

Mission 2

  • Gibberellins lengthen internodes and trigger germination by switching on amylase.
  • Red light converts phytochrome to its far-red-absorbing form; far-red or long darkness reverses it.

Mission 3

  • A neurone: cell body, many short dendrites, one long axon; myelin insulates the axon except at nodes of Ranvier.
  • Myelin is made by Schwann cells.
  • At rest the inside is about −70 mV: the membrane is polarised.

Mission 4

  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (depolarisation); then K⁺ channels open (repolarisation). Action potentials are all-or-nothing; strength is coded by frequency.
  • Myelinated and wider fibres conduct faster.
  • Myelin restricts action potentials to the nodes (saltatory conduction).

Mission 5

  • Action potential → Ca²⁺ enters the presynaptic terminal → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → diffusion across the cleft → binding to complementary receptors → Na⁺ in → EPSP → action potential if threshold is reached.
  • One-way, because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic.
  • Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh down so the signal stops.
