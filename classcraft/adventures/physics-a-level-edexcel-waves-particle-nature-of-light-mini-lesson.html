Mission 1: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Make a guess: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Quick check
?A loudspeaker plays a steady note to a listener 5 m away in a room with still air. Which of the following travels from the loudspeaker to the listener?
Mission 1

What a wave carries

  • A progressive (travelling) wave transfers energy from place to place.
  • It does not transfer matter. Each particle oscillates about a fixed position.
Mission 1

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Waves on a string; all EM waves.
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound; a slinky pushed along its length.
  • Longitudinal waves have compressions (particles close, pressure higher) and rarefactions (spread out, pressure lower).
  • Describe a longitudinal wave by its molecular displacement and its pressure variation.
Mission 1

Describing a wave

  • Displacement: distance and direction of a particle from its rest position.
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: distance between neighbouring points that move in step, e.g. crest to crest.
  • Period T: time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f: oscillations per second, in Hz.
  • f = 1/T.
Mission 1

The wave equation

  • In one period the wave moves on one wavelength: v = λ/T, so v = fλ.
  • For sound in air and waves on a string, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Change f in the same medium: λ changes, v stays the same.
  • Example: sound at 340 m s⁻¹, f = 680 Hz → λ = 340/680 = 0.50 m.
Mission 1

Two graphs, two meanings

  • Displacement–position graph: a snapshot of the whole wave. Read λ and A.
  • Displacement–time graph: one particle over time. Read T and A.
  • Same shape, different axis: always check the x-axis label.
  • Particle direction: for a wave moving right, a point copies what the point just to its left did a moment ago.
Quick check
?A transverse wave travels along a stretched string. Adjacent crests are 1.2 m apart. A graph of displacement y against time t for one point on the string is a sine curve: y = 0 at t = 0, y is a maximum at t = 5 ms, y = 0 at t = 10 ms, y is a minimum at t = 15 ms and y = 0 at t = 20 ms, after which the pattern repeats. What is the wave speed?
Mission 1

Graphs of a longitudinal wave

  • Plot displacement along the direction of travel (+ = forwards) against rest position.
  • The graph looks like a transverse wave, but the particles move along the axis.
  • Compression centre: displacement zero, with particles on both sides displaced towards it.
  • Rarefaction centre: displacement zero, with neighbours displaced away from it.
Quick check
?A sound wave travels in the +x direction through air. Displacements of air layers in the +x direction are positive. At one instant the graph of displacement s against rest position x is a sine curve: s = 0 at x = 0, s = −2.0 μm at x = 15 cm, s = 0 at x = 30 cm, s = +2.0 μm at x = 45 cm and s = 0 at x = 60 cm. Where, between x = 0 and x = 60 cm, is the centre of a rarefaction?
Mission check

?A vibrator sends waves along a stretched string at constant tension. At 25 Hz the wavelength is 0.80 m. The frequency is raised to 100 Hz. What is the new wavelength, and how long does a wave now take to travel 4.0 m along the string?

The medium sets the speed. A higher note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength, but the same speed: it arrives no sooner.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Make a guess: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Mission 2

Phase

  • Phase: how far through its cycle a particle is.
  • One cycle = 360° = 2π rad.
  • Points one wavelength apart move exactly together: in phase.
  • Points half a wavelength apart always move opposite ways: antiphase (180°, π rad).
Mission 2

Phase difference from distance

  • Fraction of a cycle = Δx/λ.
  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ (rad) = 360° × Δx/λ.
  • Example: λ = 0.80 m, points 0.20 m apart. Δx/λ = ¼ cycle = 90° = π/2 rad.
Quick check
?Two points on a progressive wave of wavelength 1.2 m are 0.40 m apart along the direction of travel. What is the phase difference between their oscillations?
Mission 2

Phase difference from graphs

  • Two displacement–time traces, same period T. Find the time shift Δt between matching points (crest to crest).
  • φ = 2πΔt/T = 360° × Δt/T.
  • Example: T = 8.0 ms; crests 2.0 ms apart → ¼ cycle = 90°.
  • On a displacement–position graph, use the separation Δx and λ instead.
  • The trace that reaches its crest first leads.
Quick check
?Two displacement–time graphs, P and Q, show the oscillations of two points on a wave. Both have a period of 6.0 ms. Each crest of Q comes 1.0 ms after the matching crest of P. What is the phase difference between P and Q?
Mission 2

Pressure and displacement in sound

  • Compression centre: neighbours displaced towards it. Particle displacement there is zero; pressure is highest.
  • Rarefaction centre: displacement zero; pressure lowest.
  • Where displacement is greatest, the pressure is normal.
  • So the pressure graph is shifted by a quarter of a wavelength from the displacement graph.
Mission check

?A loudspeaker emits a steady 500 Hz note. Two microphones are placed on a straight line from the loudspeaker, 0.17 m apart. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. How do the oscillations detected by the two microphones compare?

Phase difference = 2πΔx/λ. Half a wavelength apart means antiphase: the two corks always move in opposite directions.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Sound crosses a room in a split second. Does any air actually travel from the speaker to your ear?

Make a guess: Sound crosses a room in a split second. Does any air actually travel from the speaker to your ear?

Quick check
?A student at one end of a long steel railway track puts an ear to the rail. At the other end, 1.0 km away, the rail is struck once with a hammer. The student hears two separate sounds, 0.20 s and 2.9 s after the blow. Which conclusion do these observations support?
Mission 3

Speed of sound depends on the medium

  • Rough speeds: air ≈ 340 m s⁻¹, water ≈ 1500 m s⁻¹, steel ≈ 5000–6000 m s⁻¹.
  • The source sets f; the medium sets v. Into a new medium, f stays the same and v changes, so λ = v/f changes.
Mission 3

Speed of sound: two-beam oscilloscope (CP6)

  • Signal generator drives a loudspeaker at a known f; set the timebase to show a few cycles.
  • Channel 1: the signal-generator output (the same signal as the speaker). Channel 2: a microphone in front of the speaker.
  • Start with the two traces in phase. Move the microphone away along a ruler.
  • The traces drift apart and come back in phase each time the microphone moves one wavelength.
  • Move through several wavelengths and divide: smaller percentage uncertainty. Then v = fλ.
Quick check
?In core practical 6, the signal generator is set to 2.00 kHz. Starting with the two traces in phase, a student moves the microphone 0.850 m directly away from the loudspeaker. The traces come back into phase 5 times during this movement, the last exactly at the final position. What is the speed of sound?
Mission check

?A singer on a stage is 17 m from a listener. Her sound reaches the listener 0.050 s later. Which observation is the best evidence that the air itself does not make the trip from the singer to the listener?

Same for air: each layer only oscillates about its own position. Energy reaches your ear; the air that was at the speaker stays there.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Make a guess: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Mission 4

Superposition

  • When two waves or pulses overlap, they interfere.
  • Principle of superposition: the resultant displacement = the sum of the individual displacements.
  • Displacements have signs. Up (+) and down (−) can cancel.
  • Pulses pass through each other. Afterwards each carries on unchanged, as if the other was never there.
Quick check
?Two pulses of identical shape and size but opposite displacement travel towards each other along a stretched string. At one instant they overlap exactly and the whole string is momentarily straight. Which statement about this instant, and what follows it, is correct?
Mission 4

Adding waves on a graph

  • To add two waves, add their displacements point by point.
  • Same frequency, in phase (crest meets crest): amplitude A1 + A2. Constructive interference.
  • Same frequency, in antiphase (crest meets trough): amplitude |A1 − A2|. Destructive interference. Equal amplitudes cancel completely.
  • Anything in between gives an amplitude in between.
Quick check
?The graph shows the displacement y of a stretched string against position x at time t = 0. Pulse A is travelling to the right and pulse B to the left, each at a speed of 1.0 cm s⁻¹. What is the displacement of the string at x = 6.0 cm when t = 3.0 s?
Mission 4

Two sources: path difference

  • Two sources, S1 and S2, emit waves in phase, with the same wavelength λ.
  • At a point P: path difference = |S2P − S1P|.
  • Path difference = nλ (0, λ, 2λ, …): crest meets crest. Constructive: loud, bright, large amplitude.
  • Path difference = (n + ½)λ (½λ, 1½λ, …): crest meets trough. Destructive: quiet, dark, small amplitude.
  • Result: a fixed pattern of maxima and minima. Energy is not destroyed at the minima; it is redistributed to the maxima.
Mission 4

Coherence

  • A steady pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • Two separate lamps are not coherent: their phase difference changes randomly, far too often to follow (light is emitted in short, unrelated bursts). The pattern shifts too fast to see.
  • Coherent sources come from one source: two speakers on one signal generator; two slits lit by the same light.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Two speakers, in phase, play 850 Hz. Speed of sound 340 m s⁻¹. λ = v/f = 340 ÷ 850 = 0.400 m.
  • Point P: 3.00 m from one speaker, 3.60 m from the other. Path difference = 0.60 m = 1.5λ → destructive: quiet.
  • Point Q: 3.00 m and 3.80 m. Path difference = 0.80 m = 2λ → constructive: loud.
  • Walk from Q to P: the sound fades from loud to quiet in a few tens of centimetres.
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound in phase at a frequency of 500 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. At which of these path differences is the sound quiet because of destructive interference?
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers side by side are driven by two separate signal generators, each set to 500 Hz. A listener walking across the room hears no steady pattern of loud and quiet places. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two dippers vibrating in phase act as coherent sources S1 and S2 on a ripple tank, producing waves of wavelength 2.0 cm. Point P is 31.0 cm from S1 and 37.0 cm from S2. What kind of interference occurs at P?

Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: loud. Step to where it is a half-wavelength more, and crest meets trough: almost silent.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Rewire one of two speakers backwards. Why do the loud and quiet spots swap places?

Make a guess: Rewire one of two speakers backwards. Why do the loud and quiet spots swap places?

Mission 5

Interference in terms of phase

  • Recall: phase difference is how far one oscillation is ahead of another, as a fraction of a cycle: 2π × path difference/λ (in radians).
  • Sources in phase: path difference nλ → phase difference 2nπ → constructive.
  • Path difference (n + ½)λ → phase difference (2n + 1)π → destructive.
  • Total phase difference at a point = phase difference at the sources + phase difference from the paths.
  • Sources in antiphase add π everywhere: every maximum becomes a minimum, and every minimum a maximum.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Coherent sources in phase, λ = 0.40 m.
  • Path difference 0.10 m: phase difference = 2π × 0.10/0.40 = π/2 rad (90°). Neither maximum nor minimum: an in-between amplitude.
  • Path difference 0.20 m: 2π × 0.20/0.40 = π rad → destructive.
  • Path difference 0.40 m: 2π rad → constructive.
Quick check
?Two coherent sources emit waves in phase. The wavelength is 0.60 m. At a point P, the waves arrive with a phase difference of 3π rad. What is the path difference at P, and what happens there?
Mission 5

Lasers

  • Laser light is monochromatic (one wavelength) and coherent, in a narrow, intense beam. Ideal for interference.
Mission check

?Two dippers on a ripple tank vibrate with the same frequency and amplitude, but in antiphase. The wavelength is 2.0 cm. Point P is 5.0 cm further from one dipper than from the other. What happens at P?

Wiring a speaker backwards adds a phase difference of π everywhere. Every loud spot (path difference nλ) becomes quiet, and every quiet spot becomes loud.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Make a guess: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Mission 6

Two waves, opposite directions

  • Two waves with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose.
  • Usually a wave and its own reflection from a fixed end or the end of a pipe.
  • Add the displacements at each point at t = 0, T/4, T/2: some points never move; others swing the most.
  • The pattern does not travel. No energy is carried along it.
  • This is a stationary (standing) wave.
Mission 6

Nodes and antinodes

  • Node: zero amplitude at all times. Antinode: maximum amplitude.
  • Node to next node: λ/2. Node to next antinode: λ/4.
  • Amplitude varies with position: zero at nodes, largest at antinodes.
  • Between two neighbouring nodes, all points move in phase.
  • Points on opposite sides of a node are in antiphase (phase difference π rad).
Mission 6

Two kinds of wave compared

  • Energy: progressive carries it along; stationary does not (stored, swapping between kinetic and potential).
  • Amplitude: progressive, the same everywhere (no losses); stationary, varies from zero (node) to maximum (antinode).
  • Phase: progressive, changes steadily along the wave; stationary, in phase within a loop, antiphase across a node.
  • Wavelength: progressive, crest to crest; stationary, twice the node-to-node distance.
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in a standing wave. Its nodes are at x = 0, 0.40 m, 0.80 m and 1.20 m, measured from one end. Points P, Q and R on the string are at x = 0.10 m, 0.20 m and 0.70 m. Which statement about the oscillations of P, Q and R is correct?
Mission 6

Strings

  • A fixed end must be a node.
  • Fixed at both ends: a whole number of loops fits, L = nλ/2.
  • n = 1: the first harmonic (one loop, λ = 2L). Then n = 2, 3, …: second, third harmonics.
  • Wave speed v fixed by the string, so f = v/λ = nv/2L: only these frequencies "fit".
Mission 6

Wave speed on a string

  • v = √(T/μ): T = tension (N), μ = mass per unit length (kg m⁻¹).
  • Tighter string → faster waves → higher notes. Heavier string → lower notes.
  • Example: tension 90 N, mass per unit length 1.0 × 10⁻³ kg m⁻¹ → v = √(90 000) = 300 m s⁻¹.
  • Length 0.60 m: first harmonic f = 300 ÷ 1.20 = 250 Hz.
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in its first harmonic. The tension in the string is then increased to four times its original value, with the length and the string unchanged. By what factor does the frequency of the first harmonic change?
Mission check

?The diagram shows the envelope of a standing wave on a string of length L = 1.20 m that is fixed at both ends. What is the wavelength of the waves on the string?

The ends must be nodes, so only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit. With v fixed, only the matching frequencies can build up: the instrument's notes.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can stationary waves measure a wave's speed without timing anything?

Make a guess: How can stationary waves measure a wave's speed without timing anything?

Mission 7

Investigating a vibrating string

  • String fixed at a vibration generator, passing over a pulley to a mass hanger. Tension T = weight of the hanging masses.
  • Signal generator: raise f slowly until the string vibrates in one loop: first harmonic. Read f.
  • Length l: move the bridge (or generator). μ: weigh a measured length of each string, or use the maker's value.
  • Change one variable at a time:
  • f ∝ 1/l (T, μ fixed) · f ∝ √T (l, μ fixed) · f ∝ 1/√μ (l, T fixed).
  • Graphs for straight lines through the origin: f against 1/l; f² against T.
Mission 7

Using the graph

  • v = √(T/μ) and, for the first harmonic, λ = 2l. So f = v/2l: f against 1/l has gradient v/2.
  • Example: gradient 40 m s⁻¹ → wave speed v = 80 m s⁻¹.
  • With T = 3.2 N: μ = T/v² = 3.2 ÷ 6400 = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ kg m⁻¹.
  • Tips: judge the loop's largest amplitude with a strobe or by eye from several frequencies either side; measure l between the nodes, not to the pulley's edge.
Quick check
?Three strings are each fixed at both ends and under the same tension. String 1 has length l and mass per unit length μ. String 2 has length l and mass per unit length 4μ. String 3 has length l/2 and mass per unit length 4μ. Which ranking of the frequencies f1, f2 and f3 of their first harmonics is correct?
Mission check

?A loudspeaker emitting a 1000 Hz note faces a hard wall. A microphone moved along the line between them detects positions of minimum loudness 0.17 m apart. What is the speed of sound?

Nodes are λ/2 apart. Measure their spacing, double it for λ, and v = fλ: a ruler and a known frequency replace the stopwatch.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A progressive wave transfers energy, not matter. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ travel; longitudinal: oscillation ∥ travel.
  • f = 1/T and v = fλ. For sound and waves on strings, the medium sets v and the source sets f.

Mission 2

  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ = 2πΔt/T, in radians; × 360°/2π for degrees. λ apart: in phase. λ/2 apart: antiphase.
  • Wavefronts join points in phase and are λ apart; rays are perpendicular to them.
  • In sound, pressure is greatest at a compression, where particle displacement is zero.

Mission 3

  • Sound: a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions; the medium oscillates about fixed positions and carries energy, not itself. It needs a medium; v = fλ, set by the medium.
  • CP6: traces back in phase every wavelength moved; v = fλ.

Mission 4

  • Superposition: add displacements. Waves and pulses pass through each other unchanged.
  • Sources in phase: path difference nλ → constructive; (n + ½)λ → destructive.
  • A steady interference pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency, constant phase difference.

Mission 5

  • Phase difference = 2π × path difference/λ. In phase (2nπ) → constructive; antiphase ((2n + 1)π) → destructive.
  • Coherent sources: same frequency, constant phase difference. Make them from one source (one laser, one signal generator).

Mission 6

  • Stationary wave: two identical waves travelling opposite ways; nodes λ/2 apart; no energy carried along.
  • Fixed–fixed string or open–open pipe: L = nλ/2, all harmonics. Node at one end, antinode at the other: odd harmonics only.
  • Speed on a string rises with tension and falls with mass per unit length.

Mission 7

  • String: v = √(T/μ) and f = v/2l; f ∝ 1/l, f ∝ √T, f ∝ 1/√μ.
