Why do linguists take one sentence apart, level by level?

  • AQA calls these the methods of language analysis.
Language levels · lexis and semanticsScreen 2

Lexis: the words chosen

  • Lexis is the vocabulary of a text; semantics is the study of meaning.
  • Semantic field: a group of words from one area of meaning (cosy, characterful, charming: comfort and charm).
  • Formality: formal, informal, colloquial, slang; jargon is the specialist lexis of a field.
  • Where words come from: loanwords (bijou, from French), compounds (low-maintenance), neologisms (new words).
  • Lexis varies with the user and the time: AQA's list mentions social and historical variation.
Language levels · lexis and semanticsScreen 3

Semantics: how words mean

  • Denotation is a word's literal meaning; connotation is what it suggests.
  • Synonyms rarely mean quite the same: small, compact and cramped share a denotation but not a connotation.
  • A euphemism is a mild word for something unwelcome (compact for small).
  • Hyponymy: a general hypernym (home) includes specific hyponyms (cottage, flat, bungalow).
  • Collocation: words that habitually go together (sought-after location).
  • Polysemy: one word with several related meanings (character).

?Why do linguists take one sentence apart, level by level?

Because each level asks a different question (how it sounds, which words, how it's built, how it looks, what it does in context), analysing level by level makes you systematic and stops you missing choices. The aim is never the list: it's how those choices work together to make meaning in context.

‘Fruit flies like a banana.’ How is grammar the punchline?

Language levels · grammarScreen 3

Phrases and clauses

  • A phrase is built round a head word: noun phrase (a ripe banana), verb phrase (must have flown), prepositional phrase (like an arrow).
  • A clause centres on a verb phrase. Its elements: subject, verb, object, complement, adverbial.
  • A main clause can stand alone; a subordinate clause can't: adverbial (when the bell rings), relative (modifies a noun) or nominal (that she'd left, as an object).
  • Finite verbs show tense (rings); non-finite clauses use an -ing or -ed participle or a to-infinitive (to win).

Language levels · grammarScreen 6

Morphology: inside words

  • A morpheme is the smallest unit of meaning. Free morphemes stand alone (help); bound morphemes can't (un-, -ful, -ly).
  • Inflectional morphemes mark grammar without making a new word. English has eight: plural -s, possessive 's, third-person -s, past -ed, participles -ing and -en, comparative -er, superlative -est.
  • Derivational morphemes make new words, often changing class: help (verb), helpful (adjective), unhelpfully (adverb).
Language levels · grammarScreen 7

How your exam asks this

  • Grammar, including morphology, is one of the methods of language analysis you apply to every text: Paper 1 Section A (25 + 25, then a 20-mark comparison) and Paper 2 Section B (40).
Your answerScreen 8
?In flies, the bound morpheme -s can mark a plural noun or a third-person verb. It adds grammar but doesn't make a new word. What type of morpheme is it?
Mission checkScreen 9

?‘Fruit flies like a banana.’ How is grammar the punchline?

Grammar is the punchline. In the first half flies is a verb and like an arrow a prepositional phrase; in the second fruit flies is a noun phrase (the insects), like is a verb and a banana its object. The words look parallel, but the structure isn't, so the reader is tricked into the wrong reading.

Mission 3

‘Bath’ with a short or long A: how do you write down a sound?

Make a guess: ‘Bath’ with a short or long A: how do you write down a sound?

Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 1

Three ways into sound

  • Phonetics: how speech sounds are physically made and heard.
  • Phonology: how a language organises sounds into a system. A phoneme is the smallest sound unit that changes meaning: bat and pat differ only in /b/ and /p/, a minimal pair.
  • Prosody (your spec says prosodics): intonation, stress, pitch, tempo and volume across an utterance.
  • Phonemes go in slashes, /b/; detailed sounds in square brackets, [ʔ].
  • Spelling is a poor guide: bath, laugh and aunt spell one RP vowel three ways.
Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 2

Writing a vowel: the BATH question

WordRP (southern)Northern English
bath/bɑːθ//bæθ/
grass/ɡrɑːs//ɡræs/
trap/træp//træp/
  • The IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) gives one symbol per sound. AQA's spec prints no symbol sheet, so learn the common RP (Received Pronunciation) symbols.
  • RP bath has the long vowel of palm: ː marks length. Northern bath has the short vowel of trap (many northern speakers say a more open [a]).
  • The two accents use different phonemes in this set of words: the TRAP–BATH split.
Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 3

Consonants: voice, place, manner

  • Voicing: do the vocal folds vibrate? /p/ is voiceless, /b/ voiced; /θ/ (thin) voiceless, /ð/ (this) voiced.
  • Place: bilabial /p b m/, dental /θ ð/, alveolar /t d s z n/, velar /k ɡ ŋ/, glottal /h/.
  • Manner: plosive /p b t d k ɡ/, fricative /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/, nasal /m n ŋ/, affricate /tʃ dʒ/, approximant /l r j w/.
  • /ŋ/ is one sound: the end of sing.

Mission 4

‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

Make a guess: ‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 1

Saying and doing

  • Pragmatics studies meaning in context: what a speaker means, not only what the words say.
  • Austin (1962) showed that utterances are speech acts: the locution (the words said), the illocution (what the speaker is doing: requesting, warning, promising) and the perlocution (the effect on the listener).
  • A direct speech act matches form and function: Shut the window is an imperative used as a command.
  • An indirect speech act doesn't: It's cold in here is a declarative that can work as a request (Searle 1975).
Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 2

Grice: reading between the lines

  • Grice (1975): conversation works because we assume speakers are cooperating: the Cooperative Principle.
  • Four maxims: Quantity (as much information as needed), Quality (say what's true), Relation (be relevant), Manner (be clear).
  • We assume It's cold in here is relevant, so we ask why it's said now, and infer a request: an implicature.
  • A speaker who openly flouts a maxim creates an implicature; one who covertly violates it misleads (a lie violates Quality).
Quick checkScreen 3
?Invented example. A parent looks at an untidy bedroom and says: I've told you a million times to tidy up. Which maxim is flouted, and what is implied?
Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 4

Politeness and face

  • Face is the public self-image people want to keep (from Goffman). Brown and Levinson (1987): positive face is the wish to be liked; negative face, the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Requests, orders and criticism are face-threatening acts. Speakers choose a strategy:
  • Positive politeness: friendliness, compliments, in-group terms (mate), inclusive we.
  • Negative politeness: hedges, apologies, modal verbs, indirectness (Could I possibly…?).
  • Off record: a deniable hint (It's cold in here). Bald on record: no softening (Shut the window).

Discourse and cohesion

  • Discourse is how a whole text or conversation is organised: openings, sequences, closings. Discourse markers (so, anyway, right) signal shifts.
  • Cohesion is the grammatical and lexical ties that hold a text together (Halliday and Hasan 1976):
TypeInvented example
Reference: anaphoric (back) or cataphoric (forward)The window's open. Can you shut it?
SubstitutionI need a jumper. Have you got one?
EllipsisWho opened the window? Sam. (opened the window is left out)
Conjunctionso, however, because, then
Lexical cohesionrepetition, synonyms, a field: cold, freezing, chilly

Mission checkScreen 9

?‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

The listener infers it. The words are a statement, but in context (an open window, a cold speaker) the listener assumes the remark is relevant and works out the implicature. As an off-record request, it lets the speaker ask without imposing, protecting both people's face.

Language levels · graphology and multimodalityScreen 1

What graphology covers

AspectLook for
Typographytypeface (serif, sans serif, script), size, weight, case, italics
Layoutposition, alignment, columns, white space, headings, what stands out (salience)
Colourcontrast, associations, a consistent colour scheme
Imagephotographs, drawings, logos, icons, emoji
Writing systemspelling, punctuation, symbols such as & and :)
  • Graphology is how a text looks, and how that look makes meaning.
  • AQA: ‘the visual aspects of textual design and appearance’.
Language levels · graphology and multimodalityScreen 2

Multimodal texts

  • A multimodal text makes meaning through more than one mode: words, images, layout, colour and, online, sound and moving image.
  • Ask how the modes work together: does the image reinforce the words, add to them, or contrast with them?
  • Typefaces carry conventional associations: a serif face often suggests tradition or formality; a script or handwritten face, personal warmth; a sans serif face, modern clarity. Context decides.
  • Graphology works with the other levels: a handwritten look often goes with informal lexis.

Language levels · graphology and multimodalityScreen 6

From feature to effect

  • Name the feature precisely (a thin gold serif typeface), not a nice font.
  • Say what it suggests in this context: for whom, for what purpose, in what genre.
  • Link levels: Menu B's serif type, the loanword velouté and a single price for seven courses all build exclusivity.
  • Avoid stock claims such as colour makes it eye-catching: say why this colour, here.
  • Associations are conventions, not rules: an expensive restaurant may choose a chalkboard to look rustic.

?Chalkboard menu or glossy print: what is the typeface telling you?

The typeface signals identity and relationship. Hand-drawn chalk suggests a personal, informal welcome; thin serif print suggests formality and exclusivity. These are conventions, not rules, so the strongest analysis links the look to the lexis, the purpose and the audience in this context.

Mission 6

Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

Make a guess: Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

Spoken language · conversationScreen 1

Normal non-fluency

FeatureInvented example
Filler (voiced pause)erm, um
Unvoiced pauseI think (.) yes; (.) is a micropause, (2) two seconds
False startI was- we went on Tuesday
Self-repairon Monday, no, Tuesday
Repetitionthe the bus
  • Spontaneous speech is planned while it's spoken, so every fluent speaker shows normal non-fluency.
  • These features can buy planning time, hold the floor or soften what's said.
  • Other spoken features: ellipsis, discourse markers (so, anyway), tag questions (isn't it?), deixis (this, over there).
Spoken language · conversationScreen 2

Taking turns

  • Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson (1974) showed that conversation is organised turn by turn: mostly one speaker at a time, with smooth changes.
  • Turns change at a transition relevance place (TRP): where a turn could be complete (a finished clause, falling intonation, a pause).
  • The speaker can select the next speaker (What do you think, Ali?), or someone self-selects.
  • Overlap: brief simultaneous talk near a TRP, often supportive. Interruption: breaking in mid-turn to take the floor.
  • Back-channelling (mm, yeah) shows listening without taking the turn.
Spoken language · conversationScreen 3

Adjacency pairs

  • An adjacency pair is two linked turns by different speakers: a first pair part makes a particular second pair part expected (Schegloff and Sacks 1973).
  • Question and answer; greeting and greeting; offer and acceptance or refusal; request and grant or refusal.
  • Preferred responses (accepting an invitation) usually come quickly and simply.
  • Dispreferred responses (refusing) tend to be delayed, hedged and explained: oh (.) erm I'd love to but I'm working.
  • So non-fluency can be a signal, not a slip.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Real talk is full of ‘erm’, overlaps and half-sentences. A problem?

No. Fillers, pauses, false starts and overlaps are normal non-fluency in spontaneous speech. They often do a job: buying planning time, managing turns, signalling a dispreferred response or softening a face threat. Strong analysis says what each feature does in context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The levels: phonetics, phonology and prosody; lexis and semantics; grammar (syntax and morphology); graphology; pragmatics; discourse.
  • AQA calls them the methods of language analysis; they underpin every paper.
  • Lexis and semantics: semantic field, formality, loanword, compound, denotation and connotation, euphemism, hyponymy, collocation, polysemy.
  • Open classes (noun, verb, adjective, adverb) take new words; closed classes (pronoun, determiner, preposition, conjunction, auxiliary) rarely do. Class depends on function: this book (determiner), I like this (pronoun).
  • Phrases have heads; clauses have elements (subject, verb, object, complement, adverbial); subordinate clauses can be adverbial, relative or nominal, finite or non-finite.
  • Morphology: free and bound morphemes; eight inflections; derivation changes words and often their class.
  • AQA and OCR say grammar, including morphology.
  • Phonetics (how sounds are made), phonology (the sound system and its phonemes), prosody (intonation, stress, pitch, tempo, volume).
  • IPA symbols in slashes record phonemes: RP /bɑːθ/, northern /bæθ/. Consonants are described by voicing, place and manner.
  • Connected speech: elision, assimilation, glottal stop.
  • Speech acts (Austin 1962; Searle 1975): locution, illocution, perlocution; direct and indirect speech acts.
  • Grice (1975): the Cooperative Principle and four maxims; flouting creates implicature, violating misleads.
  • Brown and Levinson (1987): positive and negative face; positive, negative, off-record and bald-on-record strategies.
  • Discourse structure and cohesion (Halliday and Hasan 1976): reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion.
  • Graphology covers typography, layout, colour, image and the writing system itself.
  • Multimodal texts combine modes; ask whether image and words reinforce, extend or contrast.
  • Feature → effect in context → link to other levels; associations are conventions, not rules.
  • Normal non-fluency: fillers, pauses, false starts, self-repairs, repetition; plus ellipsis, discourse markers, tag questions and deixis.
  • Turn-taking (Sacks, Schegloff and Jefferson 1974): TRPs, selecting and self-selecting, overlap vs interruption, back-channelling.
  • Adjacency pairs (Schegloff and Sacks 1973): first and second pair parts; dispreferred responses are delayed, hedged and explained.
  • Use the transcription key printed with the data.
