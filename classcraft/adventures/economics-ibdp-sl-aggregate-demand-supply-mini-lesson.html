Mission 1

Aggregate demand

  • Aggregate demand (AD): total planned spending on a country's goods and services at each price level, in a given time period.
  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Who spends: households (C), firms (I), government (G), the rest of the world (X), minus spending that goes abroad (M).
  • Axes: "Price level" (vertical) and "Real national output (Y)" or "Real GDP" (horizontal).
  • The AD curve slopes down: at a lower price level, more real output is demanded.
  • Say: "price level" — not: "price", on a macro diagram.
Mission 1

The four components

  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Investment (I): firms' spending on capital goods: machines, buildings, equipment. (Plus new housing.)
  • Government spending (G): on goods and services, e.g. teachers' pay, roads. Transfer payments (pensions, benefits) are not in G: they are spent later as C.
  • Net exports (X − M): foreigners' spending on our exports minus our spending on imports.
  • Saving is income not spent. Investment is spending on new capital. Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • C is by far the largest component. Real data: consumer spending was 67.5% of US GDP in 2023. It is also well over half of GDP in the UK.
  • Investment is smaller but swings far more over the business cycle.
Quick check

Mission 1

Why the AD curve slopes down

  • AD is not a market demand curve. When the price level rises, there are no cheaper home-produced goods to switch to.
  • Instead, a higher price level cuts real output demanded through three effects:
  • Wealth (real balance) effect: money and savings buy less, so households feel poorer → C falls.
  • Interest rate effect: buying the same goods needs more money → demand for money rises → interest rates rise → borrowing costs more → C and I fall.
  • International trade effect: home goods become dearer relative to foreign goods → X falls, M rises.
  • A lower price level works the other way: real output demanded rises.
Mission 1

Movement along AD or shift of AD?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD.
  • Price level up: a contraction of real output demanded. Price level down: an extension.
  • A change in anything else that changes C, I, G or X − M → a shift of AD.
  • More spending at every price level: AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2). Less: AD shifts left.
  • The three effects of the previous screen explain the slope. They are never reasons for a shift.
  • Say: "a rise in the price level causes a contraction along AD" — not: "a rise in the price level shifts AD left".
Quick check
?A rise in world oil prices pushes up the general price level in a country. Nothing else that affects spending changes. How should this be shown on the country's AD diagram?
Mission 1

What shifts C and I

Consumption (C) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Disposable income rises (e.g. income taxes are cut).
  • Interest rates fall: borrowing is cheaper, saving earns less.
  • Wealth rises (house or share prices go up).
  • Consumer confidence improves.
  • Household debt is lower, or credit is easier to get.
  • People expect prices to rise, so they buy now.

Investment (I) rises, shifting AD right, when:

  • Interest rates fall: projects cost less to finance.
  • Business confidence improves: firms expect more sales and profit.
  • New technology makes new equipment worth buying.
  • Business taxes (e.g. on profits) are cut.
  • Corporate debt is lower, so firms can borrow more easily.

The reverse of each shifts AD left.

Mission 1

What shifts G and net exports

  • G: set by government priorities and fiscal policy, e.g. more spending on health or roads → AD right.
  • Exports (X) rise when:
    • Incomes abroad rise (trading partners grow; the world economy booms).
    • The currency depreciates: exports become cheaper abroad.
    • Other countries cut trade barriers (e.g. remove tariffs).
    • Non-price factors improve: quality, design, reliability, reputation.
  • Imports (M) rise, cutting X − M, when:
    • Real income at home rises: people buy more of everything, including imports.
    • The currency appreciates: imports become cheaper at home.
    • The country removes its own tariffs or quotas.
  • Say: "a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home" — not the other way round.

Mission 1Evaluate

How far will AD shift?

It depends on …

  • Which component changes: C is the largest part of spending in most economies (often well over half), so a small % change in C moves AD a lot. In very open economies, X − M matters more.
  • Confidence and debt: a cut in interest rates may barely lift spending if households and firms are pessimistic or heavily in debt.
  • Time lags: changes in interest rates or taxes take months to affect spending.
  • Offsetting changes: higher G paid for by higher taxes cuts C; extra spending may leak into imports.
  • Judge: the direction of a shift is usually clear; its size is uncertain. What it does to output and prices also depends on aggregate supply (see Mission 6).

AD slopes down because of what the price level does to real wealth, interest rates and trade, not because buyers swap one home good for another.

Mission 2

Spend it or save it

  • Disposable income (Yd): income after direct taxes, plus state benefits (transfers).
  • Households do two things with it: consume or save.
  • Consumption (C): household spending on goods and services.
  • Saving (S): disposable income not spent. Yd = C + S.
  • C is the largest part of AD = C + I + G + (X − M).
  • Saving is a withdrawal (leakage) from the circular flow.
Mission 2

What else moves spending

  • Interest rates: a rise makes borrowing dearer (cars, furniture, mortgages) and saving more rewarding. C usually falls.
  • Confidence and expectations: fear of job loss or lower future income → more precautionary saving.
  • Wealth effects: rising house or share prices make owners feel richer and able to borrow → C rises.
  • Taxes and benefits: change disposable income.
  • Household debt and credit: heavily indebted households, or banks that stop lending, cut C.
  • Expected price level: if prices are expected to rise, buying now brings spending forward.
  • Step 2: C rises with the price level unchanged → AD shifts right, AD1 → AD2.
  • Say: "a rise in consumer confidence shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".
Mission 2

Real world: the 2020 saving surge

  • In spring 2020, lockdowns closed shops and jobs felt at risk.
  • UK: the household saving ratio rose from 9.6% (January to March) to a record 29.1% (April to June 2020).
  • US: the personal saving rate hit 33% in April 2020, up from 12.7% in March.
  • These are the first official estimates (ONS, BEA). Later revisions moved them a little; the surge stayed.
  • Two reasons:
  • Forced saving: little to spend on.
  • Precautionary saving: fear about jobs and health.
  • Most incomes were protected by furlough and support schemes, yet C fell sharply.
Quick check
?House prices fall sharply across a country. Incomes and interest rates do not change. What is the most likely effect on consumption?
Mission 2

Saving is not investment

  • Saving: income not spent by households. A withdrawal.
  • Investment: spending by firms on new capital goods (machines, buildings). An injection.
  • Buying shares or putting money in a bank is saving, not investment.
  • Saving and investment are done by different people, for different reasons.
  • Banks may lend savings to firms, but planned saving need not equal planned investment.
  • Say: "investment is spending on capital goods" — not: "investing in shares".
Mission 2Evaluate

Will a cut in interest rates boost spending?

It depends on:

  • Confidence: worried households may still save, whatever the rate.
  • Borrowers or savers? Borrowers gain; savers lose interest income and may spend less.
  • Debt and credit: if banks will not lend, cheaper rates help little.
  • Time lags: fixed-rate loans and contracts delay the effect.
  • Size of the cut: a small cut may change little.

Mission 3

What counts as investment

  • Investment (I): spending by firms on capital goods used to produce future output.
  • Examples: factories, offices, machines, vehicles, computers, software.
  • Fixed capital: long-lasting assets such as buildings and machinery. In national accounts: gross fixed capital formation.
  • Inventories (stocks) of raw materials and unsold goods: also counted in investment.
  • Buying shares or second-hand assets is not investment: no new capital is made.
  • I is an injection into the circular flow and a component of AD.
Mission 3

Gross and net investment

  • Depreciation (capital consumption): capital worn out or out of date in a year.
  • Gross investment: all spending on new capital goods, including replacements.
  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation. The change in the capital stock.
  • Illustrative data: capital stock 1,000 at the start of the year.
  • Gross investment 150, depreciation 100 → net investment +50. Capital stock ends at 1,050.
  • Gross investment 80, depreciation 100 → net investment −20. Capital stock shrinks to 980.
  • Say: "net investment is negative" when replacement falls short — not: "firms bought no capital".
Quick check
?In a year, an economy's gross investment is 120 billion and depreciation is 135 billion (illustrative data). Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

What drives investment

Firms invest when the expected return beats the cost.

  • Interest rates: the cost of borrowing, or the return given up on retained profits. Higher rates → less I.
  • Expectations and business confidence: expected future sales and profits.
  • Growth of demand: firms need more capacity when sales rise.
  • Technology: new machines can cut costs or make new products.
  • Business taxes: profit taxes and tax allowances on investment change the return.
  • Credit availability: firms need banks willing to lend.
  • Corporate indebtedness: highly indebted firms invest less.
Mission 3

Worked example: is the project worth it?

Illustrative data. A firm can buy a machine for 1,000,000. It expects a return of 6% a year (60,000).

  • Borrow at 4%: interest cost 40,000 a year < 60,000 return. Worth it.
  • Borrow at 7%: interest cost 70,000 a year > 60,000 return. Not worth it.
  • Using its own savings? It still gives up 4% or 7% it could earn elsewhere: the opportunity cost.
  • If expected sales fall, the expected return falls too, at any interest rate.

Mission 3

Animal spirits

  • The return on a factory comes over 10, 20 or 30 years. Nobody knows that future well.
  • Keynes (1936) called the urge to invest anyway "animal spirits": "a spontaneous urge to action rather than inaction".
  • Moods spread: optimism → many firms invest; pessimism → many wait.
  • Investment can be postponed: an old machine can run another year. Bread and rent cannot wait.
  • So investment rises and falls far more than consumption over the cycle.
Mission 3Evaluate

Will cheaper borrowing raise investment?

It depends on:

  • Expectations: in a slump, low rates may not outweigh gloomy sales forecasts.
  • Credit: after a financial crisis, banks may refuse to lend at any rate.
  • Spare capacity: firms with idle machines need no new ones.
  • Time lags: projects take months or years to plan.
Mission check

?After a financial crisis, the central bank cuts interest rates to a record low. Business confidence collapses, and banks sharply cut lending to firms. What is most likely to happen to investment?

Investment depends on expected returns and finance, not just the interest rate. That is why it swings so much.

Mission 4

Aggregate supply in the short run

  • Aggregate supply (AS): the total real output that all firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level, in a given period.
  • Axes: Average price level (vertical), Real output (real GDP) (horizontal).
  • Short run: some costs, especially money wages (wages in cash terms, also called nominal wages), are fixed. Firms cannot change every cost straight away.
  • The SRAS curve slopes upwards: a higher price level goes with more real output.
  • Long run: all costs can adjust, so the long-run curve (LRAS) is drawn differently.
Mission 4

Why SRAS slopes upwards

  • Sticky wages: money wages are set in contracts and pay deals, often for a year. They do not rise straight away when prices rise.
  • So when the price level rises:
    • firms get more for each unit they sell
    • many costs per unit stay the same
    • profit per unit rises, so firms supply more
  • Rising costs near capacity: to produce more, firms pay overtime and use older, less efficient machines. Extra output costs more, so it needs a higher price level.
  • The reverse holds too: a lower price level squeezes profit per unit, so firms cut output.
  • Say: "a higher price level", "real output" — not: "a higher price", "quantity". This is the whole economy, not one market.
Mission 4

Worked example: a firm with a fixed wage

  • Illustrative data. A firm sells each unit for 20.
  • Costs per unit: wages 12 (fixed by contract for a year), materials 4.
  • Profit per unit: 20 − 12 − 4 = 4.
  • The price level rises by 10%. The firm's price and its materials both rise by 10%:
    • price: 20 × 1.1 = 22
    • materials: 4 × 1.1 = 4.4
    • wages: still 12
  • New profit per unit: 22 − 12 − 4.4 = 5.6, up 40%.
  • Producing more now pays, so output rises: a movement up along SRAS.
  • When wages are renegotiated up 10% (to 13.2), profit per unit is 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4: the same as 4 once prices are 10% higher. The extra reason to produce disappears in the long run.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells its product for 50. Per unit, it pays 30 in wages, fixed by contract for the year, and 10 for materials. The price level then rises by 10%, and the firm's price and materials cost rise by 10% too. What is the firm's new profit per unit?
Mission 4

Movement along SRAS or shift?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS.
    • Price level rises → output rises: an extension of SRAS.
    • Price level falls → output falls: a contraction of SRAS.
  • A change in firms' costs (anything other than the price level) → a shift of SRAS.
    • Costs rise → SRAS shifts left (up): less output at each price level.
    • Costs fall → SRAS shifts right (down).
  • Say: "SRAS shifts left" — not: "SRAS falls" or "SRAS moves up".

Mission 4

Real world: the 1973–74 oil shock

  • In October 1973, Arab oil producers began an embargo on oil exports to the United States and other countries.
  • The price of oil rose from 2.90 dollars a barrel before the embargo to 11.65 dollars a barrel in January 1974: about four times as much.
  • Oil is an input to transport, power, plastics and fertiliser, so costs rose across the economy: SRAS shifted left.
  • The US was in recession from November 1973 to March 1975, while prices rose fast.
  • Rising prices and falling output together: stagflation.
  • Caution: US inflation was already rising before the embargo. The oil shock added to it; it was not the only cause.
Mission 4Evaluate

How much damage does a cost shock do?

It depends on:

  • Size and duration. A brief price spike may barely move SRAS; a lasting rise shifts it for years.
  • How much firms use the input. Energy-heavy industries (steel, chemicals, transport) are hit far harder than services.
  • Second-round effects. If workers win higher money wages to keep up with prices, SRAS shifts left again.
  • Firms' margins. Firms with high profits may absorb some of the cost rather than raise prices.
  • Policy. Subsidies or tax cuts can offset some of the rise in costs, at a cost to public finances.
Mission check

?New software lets workers in most firms produce 10% more each hour. Money wages and AD do not change. What is the most likely short-run effect?

Anything that changes firms' costs per unit shifts SRAS; only a change in the price level moves the economy along it. Higher productivity also raises capacity in the long run: a separate, long-run effect.

Mission 5

LRAS: the economy's capacity

  • Long run: long enough for money (nominal) wages and all other input prices to adjust fully to the price level.
  • Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): the real output the economy can produce when all its resources are fully employed.
  • This is its productive capacity (potential output), sometimes called the full-employment level of output, Yfe.
  • In the monetarist/new classical view, LRAS is vertical at the full-employment level of output.
  • Capacity depends on the quantity and quality of land, labour, capital and enterprise, and on technology, not on the price level.
  • "Full employment" still leaves some unemployment (people between jobs, for example). It is not zero.
Mission 5

Worked example: when all costs catch up

  • Illustrative data. The same firm as before: price 20, wages 12, materials 4, profit per unit 4.
  • Short run: the price level rises 10%, wages are stuck at 12, so profit per unit rises to 5.6. Output rises.
  • Long run: the wage contract is renegotiated. Wages catch up: 12 × 1.1 = 13.2.
  • Now every price and cost is 10% higher: 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4.
  • 4.4 is exactly 4 × 1.1: real profit per unit is unchanged.
  • So firms have no lasting reason to produce more. Output returns to capacity.
  • As wages rise, SRAS shifts left until the economy is back on LRAS: this is the link between SRAS and LRAS.
  • A higher price level, the same real output: the long-run curve is vertical.
Quick check
?Why is the long-run aggregate supply curve drawn vertical?
Mission 5

The Keynesian AS curve

  • Keynes argued an economy can stay below full employment for a long time: money wages rarely fall, even with mass unemployment.
  • The Keynesian AS curve has three sections (often drawn as a reverse L):
    • Horizontal: lots of spare capacity. Firms can hire idle workers and machines without raising costs. Output rises; the price level does not.
    • Upward-sloping: bottlenecks. Some industries and skills run short, so costs and prices start to rise.
    • Vertical at Yfe: full capacity. More output is impossible; only the price level rises.
  • IB calls it the Keynesian AS curve, set against the monetarist/new classical LRAS.
Mission 5Graph lab

Illustrative data. Keynesian AS: flat at a price level of 100 up to output 800; vertical at Yfe = 1000.

  • Takeaway: with spare capacity, the Keynesian curve says more AD raises output (600 → 800) at the same price level; with a vertical LRAS, in the long run more AD only raises the price level.
Mission 5

Two views, two policy messages

  • Classical/monetarist view:
    • the economy returns to Yfe by itself, as wages and prices adjust
    • in the long run, more AD only raises the price level
    • to raise output for good, raise capacity: supply-side policies
  • Keynesian view:
    • an economy can stay below Yfe; adjustment is slow or does not happen
    • with spare capacity, more AD (e.g. government spending) raises output and employment without much inflation
    • near Yfe, more AD mostly raises prices
  • Equilibrium below the full-employment level: a deflationary (recessionary) gap. Above it: an inflationary gap.
  • Both views agree that capacity limits real output. They disagree on whether, and how fast, an economy returns to Yfe by itself.
Quick check

Mission 5

What shifts LRAS

  • LRAS shifts right when capacity grows:
    • Quantity of factors: a larger labour force (population growth, net migration, more people choosing to work), more capital from investment, new natural resources.
    • Quality of factors: education, training and health raise human capital; better machines.
    • Technology: new methods let the same inputs make more.
    • Productivity and efficiency: more output per worker or per unit of capital.
    • Institutions: secure property rights, rule of law, a sound financial system, low corruption.
  • LRAS shifts left if capacity shrinks: war or natural disaster destroys capital, emigration, an ageing population.
  • Some changes shift both SRAS and LRAS: higher productivity cuts costs now and raises capacity. A temporary rise in oil prices shifts SRAS only.
  • The vertical section of the Keynesian AS shifts right in the same way.
Mission 5

LRAS and the PPC tell the same story

  • The PPC shows the most an economy can make of two goods with all resources fully used.
  • LRAS shows the same capacity as a single total of real output.
  • On the PPC → output at LRAS. Inside the PPC → output below capacity (unused resources).
  • PPC shifts outwards ↔ LRAS shifts right: long-run economic growth.
  • Long-term growth: an outward PPC shift and an increase in LRAS.

Mission 5

Real world: Japan's shrinking workforce

  • Japan's working-age population (aged 15–64) peaked in 1995 at about 86.9 million.
  • By 2024 it was about 73.7 million: around 15% lower.
  • Fewer people of working age, other things equal, means less capacity: a force pushing LRAS left.
  • But LRAS also depends on how many of those people work, on capital, skills and technology.
  • Question to weigh: which of these could offset a shrinking workforce, and how fast?
Mission 5Evaluate

Can policy shift LRAS?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education and infrastructure take years, sometimes decades, to raise capacity.
  • Cost. Supply-side spending has an opportunity cost and adds to government borrowing until it pays off.
  • Whether it works. Training may not match the skills firms need; tax cuts may not change effort much.
  • Where the economy is. With a large negative output gap, Keynesians argue that weak AD, not capacity, is the main problem.
  • Side effects. Deregulation or lower benefits may raise inequality; there are trade-offs to weigh.
Mission check

?An economy is producing at its full-employment level of output. A boom in export orders shifts AD to the right. Its resources and technology do not change. According to the vertical LRAS model, what happens in the long run?

Capacity sets real output in the long run. More spending alone cannot push past it.

Mission 6

Where AD meets AS

  • Macroeconomic equilibrium (short run): where AD = SRAS.
  • It sets two things at once: the price level and real output (real GDP).
  • Above it: planned output > planned spending. Unsold stock piles up, so firms cut output and prices.
  • Below it: spending > output. Stocks run down, so firms raise output and prices.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD or SRAS.
  • A change in anything else (confidence, interest rates, costs) → a shift of the curve.
  • Say: "an extension of SRAS" for a movement up the curve — not: "SRAS increases".
Mission 6

Long-run equilibrium

  • LRAS is vertical at potential (full-employment) output, Yp.
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD and SRAS cross on LRAS.
  • There, unemployment is at its natural rate: no cyclical unemployment, but some frictional and structural unemployment.
  • Inflation is stable: no pressure from too much or too little spending.
  • In the short run, equilibrium can sit left or right of LRAS. Then the economy has an output gap.
Mission 6

The Keynesian picture

  • Keynesian AS is reverse-L shaped. Three sections:
    • flat: lots of spare capacity; more output with no rise in the price level
    • rising: bottlenecks appear; output and prices both rise
    • vertical at full-employment output: full capacity; only prices can rise
  • Label it Yp, as on the LRAS diagram.
  • Equilibrium is where AD crosses AS, and it can sit on the flat section, far below full-employment output.
  • In the Keynesian model, that equilibrium can last: a deflationary (recessionary) gap may persist (see Mission 7).

Mission 6

Demand shocks

  • Demand shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of AD.
  • Negative: AD shifts left. Examples: a collapse in confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession cutting exports.
    • Price level falls (or rises more slowly), real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Positive: AD shifts right. Examples: an export boom, a burst of confidence, a sudden rise in government spending.
    • Price level rises, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a demand shock, prices and output move in the same direction.
  • Real world: in 2008–09 banks cut lending and confidence collapsed. Real GDP fell in 2009 in the US, the UK, the euro area and Japan.

Mission 6

Supply shocks

  • Supply shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of SRAS, usually from a change in costs.
  • Negative: SRAS shifts left. Examples: a jump in oil or gas prices, a poor harvest, a jump in import prices.
    • Price level rises, real output falls, unemployment rises.
    • Rising prices and rising unemployment: stagflation.
  • Positive: SRAS shifts right (e.g. a fall in world commodity prices).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a supply shock, prices and output move in opposite directions.
  • Some shocks hit capacity itself: war or a natural disaster destroys capital, so LRAS shifts left too.
  • Real world: in 1973–74 oil exporters cut supply and the oil price roughly quadrupled. Many rich countries had rising inflation and rising unemployment.
  • In 2022 energy prices jumped after Russia's invasion of Ukraine. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022 (ONS).

Mission 6

Output gaps

  • Output gap: the difference between actual real output and potential (full-employment) output.
  • Deflationary (recessionary) gap: equilibrium output below Yp. Inflationary gap: equilibrium output above Yp.
  • The unemployment caused by a shortfall in AD is cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
  • Show cyclical unemployment as a deflationary gap: AD left of the full-employment level.
  • Output gap (%) = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.
  • Say: "output below potential is a negative gap", even if output is still growing.

Mission 6

Back to the long run: automatic adjustment

  • Monetarist/new classical view: wages and prices are flexible in the long run.
  • Recessionary gap: unemployment is above the natural rate → nominal wages fall → costs fall → SRAS shifts right → back to LRAS at a lower price level.
  • Inflationary gap: unemployment is below the natural rate → nominal wages rise → SRAS shifts left → back to LRAS at a higher price level.
  • In the long run, output returns to full employment and unemployment to its natural rate.
  • Keynesians doubt that wages fall: a deflationary gap can persist (see Mission 7).
  • How fast and how far this works.
Mission 6

Closing a gap with policy

  • Expansionary monetary policy: the central bank cuts interest rates → borrowing is cheaper → consumption and investment rise → AD shifts right → the deflationary gap closes.
  • Contractionary monetary policy: higher interest rates → AD shifts left → the inflationary gap closes.
Mission 6Evaluate

How big is the effect of a shock?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy starts. With spare capacity, a rise in AD mostly raises output. Near full capacity, it mostly raises prices.
  • Size and length of the shock. A short, one-off oil price spike differs from a lasting rise.
  • Time. Short-run effects can fade as wages and prices adjust (see Mission 7).
  • Policy response. Central banks and governments may offset a demand shock; a supply shock is harder.

Mission 7

Two views of aggregate supply

  • Monetarist/new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output, Yp.
    • In the long run, output is set by capacity alone: the quantity and quality of resources and technology.
  • Keynesian view: AS is reverse-L shaped.
    • flat with large spare capacity, rising as bottlenecks appear, vertical only at full capacity
  • The two views agree at full capacity: more AD there only raises prices.
  • They disagree about below full capacity: does the economy get back to full-employment output on its own, and how fast?
Mission 7

A recessionary gap closes itself (in theory)

  • Start: AD has fallen. Output is below full employment; unemployment is above its natural rate.
  • With many people looking for work, nominal wages fall (and other input prices fall).
  • Lower costs → SRAS shifts right.
  • The price level falls, so more real output is bought (a movement down along AD).
  • This continues until SRAS meets AD on LRAS.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment back at its natural rate, price level lower.
  • Adjustment can be slow. Until it happens, output is lost and unemployment stays high, so governments and central banks may act instead.
Mission 7

An inflationary gap closes itself

  • Start: AD has risen. Output is above full employment; unemployment is below its natural rate.
  • Labour is scarce, and workers expect prices to keep rising: they win higher nominal wages.
  • Higher costs → SRAS shifts left.
  • The price level rises again; real output falls back.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate, price level higher than before the boom.
  • So a lasting rise in AD has no lasting effect on real output: only the price level is higher.
  • Say: "SRAS shifts", not "LRAS shifts": capacity has not changed.

Mission 7

Why a slump can last

  • Why nominal wages may not fall:
    • wage contracts last a year or more
    • trade unions resist pay cuts
    • minimum wage laws set a floor
    • firms prefer to lay off some workers than cut everyone's pay and damage morale
  • Prices can be slow to fall too: changing them has costs.
  • Keynes's second point: even if wages fall, workers' incomes fall. Spending falls, so AD may shift left again.
  • Falling prices raise the real burden of debts: households and firms may cut spending further.
  • Result: the economy can sit on the flat part of Keynesian AS, below full-employment output, for years.
  • A deflationary (recessionary) gap that persists.
  • Keynes (1923): "In the long run we are all dead."

Mission 7

Real world: the Great Depression

  • US, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; consumer prices fell by about a quarter.
  • Unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Wages and prices did fall, but output stayed far below capacity for years.
  • Keynes (The General Theory, 1936): an economy can be stuck with too little demand; government spending can lift it.
  • Friedman and Schwartz (1963): the US money supply fell by about a third from 1929 to 1933. The central bank let it happen: a policy mistake, not proof that markets cannot adjust.
  • Both views agree the slump was deep and long. They disagree on why, and so on the cure.
Mission 7

What each view implies for policy

  • Keynesian: in a deep slump, use expansionary fiscal policy (more government spending, lower taxes).
    • AD shifts right on the flat part of AS: real output and jobs rise, with little rise in the price level.
  • Monetarist/new classical: expansionary policy at full employment raises output only for a while.
    • Wages then rise, SRAS shifts left, and output returns to its full-employment level at a higher price level.
    • So focus on supply-side policies (shift LRAS right) and a stable, low inflation rate.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy (less spending, higher taxes) closes an inflationary gap in both models: AD shifts left.
  • Show both: expansionary fiscal policy closing a deflationary gap on a Keynesian AS diagram, and on a monetarist/new classical diagram.
  • Today most economists accept parts of both. The real argument: how slowly does the economy adjust, and how well can policy be timed?

Mission 7Evaluate

How long is the long run?

It depends on:

  • Wage and price flexibility. Contracts, unions, minimum wages and labour-market rules decide how fast SRAS shifts back towards LRAS.
  • Expectations. If people expect low, stable inflation, wages may adjust faster; if confidence collapses, spending may keep falling.
  • Size of the gap. A small gap may close in a few quarters; a deep slump with falling prices and heavy debts can take years.
  • Policy timing. Policy acts with lags; badly timed stimulus can add to inflation once recovery has begun.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • AD = C + I + G + (X − M): planned spending on real output at each price level. Consumption is the largest component.
  • A change in the price level moves the economy along AD (wealth, interest rate and trade or exchange rate effects); a change in anything else that alters C, I, G or X − M shifts it.
  • The size of a shift depends on the component, confidence, time lags and offsetting changes.

Mission 2

  • Yd = C + S. Consumption is the largest part of AD.
  • Interest rates, confidence, wealth, taxes and credit move C; a change in C shifts AD.

Mission 3

  • Net investment = gross investment − depreciation.
  • I depends on interest rates, expectations, demand growth, technology, taxes and credit.

Mission 4

  • SRAS slopes upwards because money wages and some other costs are sticky in the short run.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS. A change in costs (wages, raw materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, productivity) → a shift.

Mission 5

  • LRAS shows the economy's capacity: the output possible with all resources fully employed.
  • Classical/monetarist: LRAS vertical. Keynesian: AS horizontal with spare capacity, then rising, then vertical at Yfe.
  • LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of factors, technology and institutions.

Mission 6

  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS. Long-run equilibrium: on LRAS, at full employment and the natural rate of unemployment.
  • Demand shocks move the price level and real output the same way; supply shocks move them opposite ways (stagflation).
  • Output below Yp is a deflationary (recessionary) gap; above it, an inflationary gap.

Mission 7

  • Without policy, flexible wages shift SRAS until the economy is back on LRAS: output at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate.
  • Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards and falling incomes cut spending, so a negative gap can persist; the vertical-LRAS view expects fairly quick self-correction.
  • Policy follows: Keynesians favour fiscal stimulus in a slump; the vertical-LRAS view favours supply-side policy and stable inflation.
