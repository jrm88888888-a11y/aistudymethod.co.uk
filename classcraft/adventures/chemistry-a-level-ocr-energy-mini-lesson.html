Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Make a guess: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Mission 1

Only differences can be measured

  • A voltmeter needs two electrodes, so it measures a potential difference between two half-cells.
  • No single half-cell's potential can be measured on its own.
  • So one half-cell is chosen as the reference and given the value 0 V.
  • Every other half-cell is measured against it.
Mission 1

The standard hydrogen electrode

  • Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): H2 gas bubbling over platinum in a solution of H⁺(aq).
  • Conditions: H2 at 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺(aq), 298 K.
  • Half-equation: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g). E⦵ = 0.00 V by definition.
  • Platinum is inert: it carries electrons and gives a surface for the reaction. It is often coated with fine platinum black.
  • Zero is an agreed value, not "no reaction".
Mission 1

Building a cell to measure E⦵

  • Electrodes: carry electrons between the half-cell and the wire.
  • Solutions: hold the ions that react in each half-cell.
  • Salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in KNO3(aq)): ions move through it to keep each solution neutral. No electrons pass through it.
  • High-resistance voltmeter: draws almost no current, so it reads the full potential difference.
  • In the lab: clean the metal with emery paper; use KNO3, not KCl, if Ag⁺ is present (AgCl would precipitate).
Quick check
?Why is the standard electrode potential of the hydrogen half-cell, 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g), given as 0 V?
Mission 1

Measuring E⦵ for nickel

  • Connect a standard Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell to the SHE.
  • The voltmeter reads 0.25 V, and nickel is the negative electrode: electrons leave it.
  • So E⦵(Ni²⁺/Ni) = −0.25 V. Negative electrode against the SHE → negative E⦵.
  • The whole difference is assigned to nickel, because the SHE is 0 V.
Mission 1

Why conditions matter

  • E changes with concentration, temperature and gas pressure.
  • Standard: 298 K, 100 kPa for gases, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ for ions. Only then is it E⦵.
  • More Ni²⁺(aq): the equilibrium Ni²⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Ni shifts right, so E becomes less negative.
  • For two ions of one element, the two concentrations may simply be equal (equimolar).
Mission 1

Half-cells with no metal: platinum electrodes

  • Some couples have no solid metal: Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺, Cl2/Cl⁻.
  • Dip platinum into a solution of both forms: Fe³⁺(aq) and Fe²⁺(aq), 1.00 mol dm⁻³ each.
  • For a gas (Cl2), bubble it over platinum in Cl⁻(aq), like the SHE.
  • Measured against the SHE: E⦵(Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺) = +0.77 V.
Mission 1

Reading a table of E⦵ values

  • Listed in order, the values form the electrochemical series.
  • More positive E⦵: the species on the left is more easily reduced.
  • More negative E⦵: the species on the right is more easily oxidised.
  • So the strongest oxidising agent is on the left at the most positive end; the strongest reducing agent is on the right at the most negative end.
  • Compare signed values: −2.37 V is the lowest value, not the "largest".

Mission 2

Mission 2: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Make a guess: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Mission 2

Making gaseous atoms

  • Every ionic solid can be traced back to its elements in their standard states.
  • Enthalpy change of atomisation, ΔatH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state.
  • Metal: Na(s) → Na(g), +107 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Chlorine: ½Cl2(g) → Cl(g), +121 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Atomisation is always endothermic.
Mission 2

Making gaseous ions

  • First ionisation energy: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms each lose one electron to form 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions. Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻, +496. Endothermic.
  • First electron affinity: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms each gain one electron to form 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions. Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g), −349. Exothermic.
  • Second electron affinity: O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g). Endothermic: the negative ion repels the incoming electron.
  • Units: kJ mol⁻¹. All values here are standard enthalpy changes.
Mission 2

Lattice enthalpy: forming the lattice

  • Lattice enthalpy, ΔLEH: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic lattice is formed from its gaseous ions, under standard conditions.
  • Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) → NaCl(s). Always exothermic: negative.
  • The more negative the value, the stronger the ionic bonding.
  • The video uses the IB convention: separating the solid, +790. Your board defines the reverse, so for NaCl the value is about −790 kJ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?Which equation, with the sign of its enthalpy change, represents the enthalpy change of lattice formation of sodium chloride?
Mission 2

Reading a Born–Haber cycle

  • A Born–Haber cycle is Hess's law for an ionic solid.
  • Enthalpy change of formation, ΔfH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states, under standard conditions.
  • Two routes from the elements to the solid: directly (ΔfH⦵), or through gaseous atoms and ions.
  • Draw it as an energy-level diagram: up = endothermic, down = exothermic.
  • Elements in standard states sit at zero.
  • Both routes give the same total, so one unknown step can be found.
  • You must be able to construct the cycle: steps in order, with correct species and state symbols at each level.
Mission 2

Worked example: lattice enthalpy of NaCl

  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH⦵(NaCl) −411 · ΔatH⦵ Na +107, Cl +121 · IE1(Na) +496 · EA1(Cl) −349.
  • Route through ions: 107 + 121 + 496 − 349 = +375 (Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g) level).
  • Hess's law: +375 + (lattice formation) = −411.
  • Lattice formation = −411 − 375 = −786 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Data books give about 787–790: step values come from different data sets.
  • Only the lattice step releases enough energy to make ΔfH⦵ negative.

Mission 2

Two charges: CaCl2 and oxides

  • Ca²⁺ needs IE1 + IE2. CaCl2 has two Cl⁻, so double the chlorine steps.
  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔfH⦵(CaCl2) −796 · ΔatH⦵ Ca +178, Cl +121 · IE1 +590, IE2 +1145 · EA1(Cl) −349.
  • Route to ions: 178 + 590 + 1145 + 2(121) + 2(−349) = +1457.
  • Lattice formation = −796 − 1457 = −2253 kJ mol⁻¹: almost three times NaCl.
  • Oxides (e.g. MgO, CaO, Na2O): atomise oxygen (½O2 → O), then add EA1 (exothermic) and EA2 (endothermic).

Mission 2

Charge, size and lattice strength

  • Smaller ions sit closer; higher charges attract more strongly.
  • Both make the lattice enthalpy more negative (more exothermic): stronger ionic bonding.
  • Size: NaF −923, NaCl −786, NaI −704 kJ mol⁻¹. Bigger anion, less exothermic.
  • Charge: MgO −3791 kJ mol⁻¹, about four times NaF, mainly because both charges are doubled (2+ and 2−).
Mission check

?Calcium oxide, CaO, is quicklime. Which expression gives the enthalpy change for Ca²⁺(g) + O²⁻(g) → CaO(s)? (ΔatH⦵ = enthalpy change of atomisation; IE = ionisation energy of Ca; EA = electron affinity of O)

No one measures the lattice step. Every other step is measured (or found from another cycle), so Hess's law round the Born–Haber cycle gives the one unknown. That is how the NaCl value is known.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Make a guess: Why do some salts make water colder as they dissolve, while others warm it up?

Mission 3

Dissolving in two steps

  • Step 1: break the lattice. Separate the ions into gaseous ions: energy absorbed.
  • Step 2: hydrate the ions. Water molecules surround each ion: energy released.
  • Water's δ− O faces cations; its δ+ H faces anions.
  • Absorbed > released: endothermic. Released > absorbed: exothermic.
Mission 3

What the thermometer tells you

  • In an insulated cup, the water is the surroundings; the dissolving salt is the system.
  • Temperature falls: energy flowed from the water into the dissolving salt. Endothermic.
  • Temperature rises: energy flowed from the dissolving salt into the water. Exothermic.
  • Energy transferred: q = mcΔT, then ΔsolH = −q ÷ n (n = moles dissolved), as in an earlier lesson. A temperature fall gives a positive ΔsolH.
Mission 3

Two definitions

  • Enthalpy change of solution, ΔsolH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a solute dissolves in water.
  • e.g. NaCl(s) + aq → Na⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).
  • Enthalpy change of hydration, ΔhydH⦵: the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous ions dissolves in water.
  • e.g. Na⁺(g) + aq → Na⁺(aq). Always exothermic.
  • One value per ion, not per compound.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the enthalpy change of hydration of magnesium ions?
Mission 3

The solution cycle

  • Gaseous ions can reach the solution two ways: form the lattice then dissolve it, or hydrate directly.
  • Hess's law: ΔLEH + ΔsolH = ΣΔhydH.
  • So ΔsolH = −ΔLEH + ΣΔhydH.
  • The lattice value is negative (formation), so −ΔLEH is positive: energy to break the lattice.
  • Multiply each ΔhydH by the number of those ions in the formula.
Mission 3

Worked example: sodium chloride

  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: lattice enthalpy (formation) of NaCl −787.
  • ΔhydH⦵: Na⁺ −406, Cl⁻ −378.
  • ΔsolH⦵ = +787 + (−406) + (−378) = +3 kJ mol⁻¹. Slightly endothermic.
  • Measured: about +4 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • A small difference between two big numbers: a 1 % change in either value can flip the sign.
Quick check
?Calculate ΔsolH⦵ of magnesium chloride, MgCl2. Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔhydH⦵ Mg²⁺ −1921; ΔhydH⦵ Cl⁻ −378.
  • Lattice enthalpy (formation) of MgCl2: −2526.
Mission 3

Charge, size and hydration

  • Hydration is more exothermic for ions with higher charge and smaller radius.
  • They attract the polar water molecules more strongly.
  • ΔhydH⦵ / kJ mol⁻¹: K⁺ −322, Na⁺ −406 (smaller); Mg²⁺ −1921 (2+ and smaller still).
  • The same factors make lattice enthalpies more exothermic (see Mission 2).
  • Dissolving depends on which effect wins, so the sign of ΔsolH is hard to predict.
Quick check

Mission check

Every salt pays to pull its ions apart and is repaid as water surrounds them. Which is bigger decides cold (KNO3, NH4NO3) or warm (MgCl2, CaCl2).

Mission 4

What entropy measures

  • Entropy, S: a measure of the dispersal of energy in a system. It is greater the more disordered the system.
  • More ways to arrange the particles and share out their energy: higher entropy.
  • Same conditions: gas > liquid > solid.
Mission 4

Predicting the sign of ΔS

  • Look first at moles of gas: more gas formed → ΔS positive; less gas → negative.
  • Melting, boiling, subliming: ΔS positive. Freezing, condensing: negative.
  • Dissolving an ionic solid: usually positive, as the ions spread through the water. Dissolving a gas: negative.
Quick check
?Which change has a negative entropy change, ΔS, for the system?
Quick check
?Consider the four changes W, X, Y and Z below at 298 K. Which change results in an increase in the entropy of the system? W: 2H2S(g) + O2(g) → 2S(s) + 2H2O(l) (exothermic) · X: NH3(g) → NH3(aq) (exothermic) · Y: 2C(s) + O2(g) → 2CO(g) (exothermic) · Z: N2(g) + 2O2(g) → 2NO2(g) (endothermic)
Mission 4

Calculating ΔS⦵

  • Standard entropies, S⦵, are absolute values: positive for every substance, elements included.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants), each times its coefficient.
  • Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (joules, not kJ).
  • Worked: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). S / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: CH4 186.3, O2 205.2, CO2 213.8, H2O(l) 70.0.
  • ΔS = [213.8 + 2(70.0)] − [186.3 + 2(205.2)] = 353.8 − 596.7 = −242.9 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. As predicted: 3 mol of gas → 1 mol.

Mission check

?A hot metal block is pressed against an identical cold one. Energy flows from hot to cold until both are at the same temperature. It never flows back to make one block hot again. Why?

Perfume molecules or energy: spread out, they have vastly more possible arrangements. So entropy increases, and the reverse, gathering back, is overwhelmingly unlikely.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Make a guess: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Mission 5

Two drives: enthalpy and entropy

  • Feasibility depends on ΔH and on TΔS: the entropy change times the temperature.
  • Water freezing: exothermic, but entropy falls. It happens only below 0 °C.
  • Ammonium chloride dissolving: endothermic, but entropy rises. It happens at room temperature.
Mission 5

The surroundings count too (background)

  • A reaction also changes the entropy of its surroundings.
  • Heat released spreads into the surroundings: their entropy rises. Heat absorbed: it falls.
  • The same heat matters more at low temperature.
  • You won't calculate this. Gibbs energy (next) includes it for you, using only the system's ΔH and ΔS.
Mission 5

Gibbs energy

  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS combines both drives in one value.
  • OCR calls this the Gibbs' equation.
  • Feasible when ΔG is negative.
  • ΔG says nothing about rate: a feasible reaction can be immeasurably slow.
Mission 5

Worked example: limestone at two temperatures

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). ΔH = +179.2 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS = +160.2 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (see Mission 4).
  • Units first: ΔS = +0.1602 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. T in kelvin.
  • 298 K: ΔG = 179.2 − 298(0.1602) = 179.2 − 47.7 = +131.5 kJ mol⁻¹. Not feasible.
  • 1200 K: ΔG = 179.2 − 1200(0.1602) = 179.2 − 192.2 = −13.0 kJ mol⁻¹. Feasible.
  • Assumes ΔH and ΔS stay the same at every temperature.
Quick check
?For C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g), calculate ΔG at 298 K.
  • ΔH = −136 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS = −120.7 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Answers in kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission 5

When ΔG changes sign

ΔHΔSΔG negative
−+at all temperatures
+−at no temperature
++above T = ΔH/ΔS
−−below T = ΔH/ΔS
  • At the switch-over ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH/ΔS (ΔS in kJ).
  • Limestone: T = 179.2 ÷ 0.1602 = 1118.6 K, about 1119 K (845 °C).
Quick check

Mission check

?Magnesium carbonate decomposes when heated: MgCO3(s) → MgO(s) + CO2(g). Why must it be heated, and above roughly what temperature does the decomposition become feasible?
  • ΔH = +100.7 kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS = +175.1 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.

Like limestone, the decomposition is entropy-driven: a gas forms from a solid. Heat until TΔS beats ΔH: about 575 K for MgCO3, about 1119 K for CaCO3. These are estimates (constant ΔH and ΔS assumed); kilns run hotter, which also makes the reaction fast.

Mission 6: If ΔG⦵ is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Make a guess: If ΔG⦵ is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Mission 6

Feasible, but slow

  • ΔG predicts whether a reaction is feasible. It says nothing about rate.
  • Usual cause: a high activation energy. At room temperature very few collisions have enough energy.
  • Diamond → graphite: ΔG⦵ = −2.9 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K, yet diamonds last for ever.
  • A mixture that isn't changing is not necessarily at equilibrium.
  • ΔG⦵ also assumes standard conditions; real concentrations, pressures and temperatures differ.
Quick check

Mission check

ΔG tells you a reaction can happen, not how fast. A feasible reaction can stop far short of products simply because it is too slow: kinetics holds it back.

Mission 7: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Make a guess: Why does a lithium coin cell give 3 V when an alkaline AA cell gives 1.5 V?

Mission 7

Cell potential from two E⦵ values

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive electrode) − E⦵(negative electrode). In a cell diagram: E⦵(right) − E⦵(left).
  • Reduction happens at the positive electrode (right-hand side of the diagram): the couple with the more positive E⦵.
  • Boards call E⦵cell the EMF, the standard cell potential or the standard emf.
  • Worked example: zinc and silver half-cells.
  • Ag⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Ag(s), E⦵ = +0.80 V. Zn²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ Zn(s), E⦵ = −0.76 V.
  • Ag⁺ is reduced; Zn is oxidised. E⦵cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.
  • Cell diagram: Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) ‖ Ag⁺(aq) | Ag(s).
  • Never multiply E⦵ by a coefficient: 2Ag⁺ or Ag⁺, the value is +0.80 V.

Mission 7

Will it react?

  • For an equation, find the species reduced: its E⦵ goes first.
  • E⦵cell positive: the reaction is feasible as written. Negative: the reverse is feasible.
  • Cl2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2Cl⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +1.36 − (+0.77) = +0.59 V. Chlorine oxidises Fe²⁺.
  • I2 + 2Fe²⁺ → 2I⁻ + 2Fe³⁺: +0.54 − (+0.77) = −0.23 V. No reaction; instead Fe³⁺ oxidises I⁻.
  • Shortcut, using the electrochemical series: the oxidised form of the more positive couple reacts with the reduced form of the less positive couple.

Mission 7

Why lithium gives a big voltage

  • Li⁺(aq) + e⁻ ⇌ Li(s): E⦵ = −3.04 V, the most negative common value.
  • So Li is the strongest reducing agent: it gives up electrons most readily.
  • E⦵cell is the gap between two E⦵ values. A very negative E⦵ makes a big gap with almost any partner.
  • Zinc (−0.76 V) is far less negative. An alkaline cell, with a zinc negative electrode, gives about 1.5 V.
  • A lithium coin cell reaches about 3 V with one cell, not two stacked.
  • Lithium reacts with water, so lithium cells use a non-aqueous electrolyte.
Mission 7

Background: E⦵cell and Gibbs energy

  • Beyond your course, but it explains the sign rule: ΔG⦵ = −nFE⦵cell (F = charge on 1 mol of electrons).
  • n = electrons transferred in the balanced equation: Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag, n = 2.
  • Positive E⦵cell → negative ΔG → feasible. Your exams use E⦵cell only.
Mission 7

Limits of E⦵ predictions

  • E⦵ values apply only at standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³. Change any of these and E changes.
  • Concentration: MnO2 + 4H⁺ + 2e⁻ ⇌ Mn²⁺ + 2H2O (+1.23 V) vs ½Cl2 + e⁻ ⇌ Cl⁻ (+1.36 V). E⦵cell = −0.13 V, yet concentrated HCl is oxidised to Cl2 by MnO2.
  • High [H⁺] and [Cl⁻] shift the potentials enough to make it feasible.
  • Kinetics: E⦵ says nothing about rate. Mg + cold water: E⦵cell = +1.54 V, but the reaction is very slow (high activation energy).
  • So "feasible" means "can happen", not "will be seen to happen".
Mission 7

Measuring a cell's voltage

  • Clean each metal strip with emery paper to remove the oxide layer.
  • Dip each into a solution of its ions, 1.00 mol dm⁻³, at 298 K.
  • Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in saturated KNO3(aq). (KCl would precipitate AgCl in a silver half-cell.)
  • Use a high-resistance voltmeter: almost no current flows, so it reads the maximum voltage.
  • Ions of one element in two oxidation states (e.g. Fe³⁺/Fe²⁺): platinum electrode, equimolar solution.
  • Compare with the prediction. Small differences come from non-standard concentrations or temperature and oxide films.
Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Only potential differences can be measured; the SHE is the 0 V reference.
  • E⦵ of a half-cell = the cell reading against the SHE under standard conditions, signed by direction of electron flow.
  • More positive E⦵: stronger oxidising agent on the left; more negative: stronger reducing agent on the right.

Mission 2

  • Born–Haber cycle: elements → gaseous atoms → gaseous ions → solid, equals ΔfH⦵. One unknown, found by Hess's law.
  • Lattice enthalpy (OCR) or lattice energy (Edexcel): formation from gaseous ions, always negative.

Mission 3

  • Dissolving = break the lattice (energy in) + hydrate the ions (energy out).
  • ΔsolH = −ΔLEH + ΣΔhydH (lattice value negative, formation).

Mission 4

  • Entropy: gas > liquid > solid. More moles of gas → ΔS positive.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants); S of an element is not zero.
  • Units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: convert to kJ before combining with ΔH.

Mission 5

  • ΔG = ΔH − TΔS, ΔS in kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T in K. ΔG negative: feasible.
  • Same signs of ΔH and ΔS: temperature decides, switching at T = ΔH/ΔS.

Mission 6

  • Feasible (favoured) does not mean fast: a high activation energy can stop a reaction in practice.

Mission 7

  • E⦵cell = E⦵(positive) − E⦵(negative) = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left). Never multiply E⦵ by coefficients.
  • Positive E⦵cell: feasible. But kinetics and non-standard conditions can change what you see.
  • Lithium's very negative E⦵ makes a large gap, so lithium cells give a large voltage.
