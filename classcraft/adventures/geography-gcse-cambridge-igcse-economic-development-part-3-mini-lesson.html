Mission 1

A farm is a system

  • Inputs go in: physical (sunlight, rain, soil, temperature) and human (labour, seeds, animals, fertiliser, machines, money).
  • Processes happen on the farm: ploughing, sowing, feeding, milking, harvesting.
  • Outputs come out: crops, milk, meat, wool, income, and also waste and pollution.
  • Some outputs are fed back in, such as manure or money for next year's seed.
  • Not every farm uses soil. In hydroponics, roots grow in a water-based nutrient solution instead of soil.
Quick check
?On a dairy farm, which is a process?
Mission 1

Arable, pastoral, mixed

  • Arable: growing crops (wheat, rice, vegetables).
  • Pastoral: rearing animals (cattle, sheep, goats) for meat, milk or wool.
  • Mixed: both crops and animals on the same farm.
  • Arable needs fertile, flatter land; pastoral often uses steeper, wetter or drier land unsuited to crops.
Mission 1

Subsistence and commercial

  • Subsistence: food grown mainly to feed the farmer's family, with little left to sell.
  • Commercial: food grown to sell for profit, often on a larger scale.
  • The world has more than 570 million farms, most of them small and family-run.
  • Small farms (under 2 hectares) work only about 12% of the world's farmland.
Quick check
?A family grows maize and beans on a small plot and eats almost all of it. What type of farming is this?
Mission 1

Farming without soil

  • Hydroponics: roots grow in a water-based nutrient solution instead of soil, sometimes held in a material such as coconut fibre.
  • Aeroponics: roots hang in the air and are sprayed with a nutrient mist.
  • Aquaponics: fish and plants together; fish waste feeds the plants, and the plants clean the water for the fish.
  • Useful where land is short: Singapore imports over 90% of its food and has only 1% of its land for food production.
  • But costly: an indoor "plant factory" needs about 247 kWh of electricity per kg of lettuce (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse. Singapore's farms have faced "inflationary pressures on energy and manpower costs", causing delays and "some exits".
Quick check
?Which farming type uses fish waste to feed plants?
Mission 1

Describe any farm

  • Hill sheep farm: pastoral, commercial.
  • Family rice paddy feeding a household: arable, subsistence.
  • Wheat and cattle farm: mixed, commercial.
  • Indoor lettuce in a city warehouse: commercial; hydroponic or aeroponic.
  • Fish tanks feeding vegetable beds: aquaponics.
Mission check

?A rooftop greenhouse grows tomatoes in nutrient-rich water. It uses electricity, workers and seed, and sells tomatoes to restaurants. Why does it count as a farming system?

Yes, it is farming. Any farm, with soil or without, is a system: inputs (light, water, nutrients, labour, money) go through processes (planting, feeding, harvesting) to make outputs (food, income, waste).

Make a guess: In 2023 the world's food supply came to about 3,000 calories per person per day. So why did around 645 million people go hungry in 2025?

Mission 2

Food security

  • FAO: "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life".
  • Food insecurity: people cannot get enough safe, nutritious food, all the time.
  • Note "all people" and "access": a country can have plenty of food while some of its people go hungry.
Mission 2

Calories per person: the world pattern

  • FAO measures the food available per person per day in kilocalories (kcal), its dietary energy supply.
  • World: 2,825 kcal in 2010; 3,016 kcal in 2023.
  • 2023: Europe 3,490 · Americas 3,438 · Asia 2,991 · Africa 2,556 kcal per person per day.
  • This is food supply, not what people actually eat (calorie intake): food later wasted in shops and homes still counts.
Mission 2

Surplus and deficit

  • Some countries have far more food per person than others. 2023, kcal per person per day: USA 3,947 · Brazil 3,423 · India 2,669 · Ethiopia 2,558.
  • A food surplus: a country produces more than it needs and can export the rest.
  • A food deficit: a country produces less than it needs, so it must import food or its people go short.
  • Richer countries can afford to import food, so a deficit in farming need not mean hunger.
Quick check
?In 2023, Europe's food supply was 3,490 kcal per person per day and Africa's was 2,556. By what percentage was Europe's supply larger than Africa's?
Mission 2

Why the world eats more

  • Rising population: 7.02 billion people in 2010; 8.09 billion in 2023.
  • Economic development: in Asia, supply per person rose from 2,694 to 2,991 kcal (2010–2023), "reflecting rising incomes and changing consumption patterns". People with more money eat more, and more meat and processed food.
  • Together: about 15% more people, each supplied with about 7% more food: about 23% more food in total.
Mission 2

Who goes hungry?

  • 2025: 7.8% of the world's population, around 645 million people, were undernourished: their calorie intake was too low.
  • Africa: about 309 million hungry people, 20.0% of its population; Asia: 292 million.
  • A wider measure, moderate or severe food insecurity (2025): Africa 56.6% · Latin America and the Caribbean 22.9% · Asia 20.3% · Northern America and Europe 8.7%.
Mission 2

Physical factors affecting food supply

  • Climate: drought and floods ruin harvests. Drought in 2010–11, then spikes in staple food prices, were causes of Somalia's famine.
  • Pests and disease: from early 2020, a massive desert locust upsurge broke out across eastern Africa, south-west Asia and around the Red Sea. One square kilometre of swarm can eat as much food in a day as 35,000 people.
  • Water stress: north-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water every year (2002–2008). Rainfall was close to normal; pumping for irrigation and other uses was the likely cause.
Mission 2

Human factors affecting food supply

  • Poverty: people cannot buy food or farm inputs. A healthy diet is unaffordable for one in three people worldwide (SOFI 2026).
  • Conflict: fighting stops farming and blocks aid. Somalia's 2011 famine "predominantly affected Al-Shabaab areas".
  • Technology: seeds, fertiliser and irrigation raise yields. Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Farmers without them get far less from their land.
  • Prices: food prices rise when energy and fertiliser cost more.
Quick check
?Somalia's 2011 famine killed about 258,000 people. Which set of causes best explains it?
Mission 2

Impacts of food insecurity

  • Undernutrition: 150 million children under five are stunted, short for their age after years of too little food.
  • Famine: a widespread lack of food causing deaths. The IPC declares famine only past strict thresholds, including more than two deaths per 10,000 people per day.
  • Rising prices: a study of 1990–2011 found that rises in food prices led to more social unrest (protests and riots).
  • Soil erosion: hungry farmers overuse land (overgrazing, no fallow years), leaving soil bare to wind and rain, so future harvests fall.
Mission 2

Problems at different levels of development

  • Low-income countries (LICs): undernourishment, stunting and, in crises, famine. Africa has the highest share of hungry people.
  • Middle-income countries (MICs): hunger falls as incomes rise, but poorer rural people can still go short; food insecurity "continues to be more prevalent in rural areas".
  • High-income countries (HICs): 8.7% of people in Northern America and Europe faced moderate or severe food insecurity in 2025.
Quick check
?Why can food insecurity lead to soil erosion?
Mission check

?Country X has an average food supply of 3,200 kcal per person per day, well above the world average. Yet a survey finds many children in its poorest rural district are stunted. What best explains this?

The 2023 supply of 3,016 kcal per person is an average of food available. Hunger depends on access: whether people can afford or reach food. Africa's supply is 2,556 kcal per person, and 20.0% of Africans are undernourished.

Mission 3

Feeding more people

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 → 4,229 kg per hectare, about 3 times as much.
  • Why yields rose: irrigation, fertiliser, high-yielding seeds and machinery (the Green Revolution).
  • Arable land per person halved: 0.359 ha (1961) → 0.173 ha (2023).
Mission 3

Changing diets

  • As incomes rise and people move to cities, diets change: more meat, dairy and processed food.
  • Meat supply per person in mainland China: 3.3 kg (1961) → 73.5 kg (2023).
  • Viet Nam: 41.0 kg (2010) → 60.5 kg (2023).
Quick check
?Which change is most likely to raise a country's demand for animal feed?
Mission 3

From diet to soybeans

  • The US Department of Agriculture calls China "the world's largest importer of soybeans".
  • It links this to "dietary shifts toward more animal proteins", creating "a strong demand for soybean meal used for livestock feed".
  • China's soybean imports: about 10.4 million tonnes (2000) → 103.4 million tonnes (2023).
  • In 2025 Brazil supplied 73.6%, the USA 15.0% and Argentina 7.1%.
Mission 3

Why food supplies went global

  • Demand: richer consumers want variety and out-of-season food all year.
  • Transport: refrigerated containers and air freight move food cheaply and quickly.
  • TNCs and supermarkets buy from wherever is cheapest or in season.
  • Trade policy: lower tariffs. China kept tariffs lower on soybeans than on soybean meal or oil.
  • Climate and land: some places, such as Brazil, can grow a crop on a huge scale.
Quick check
?World food exports grew from about $0.46 trillion (2000) to about $2.24 trillion (2024). About how many times larger were they in 2024?
Mission 3

Impacts: who gains, who loses?

  • Gains: more choice, food all year, export income and jobs in producing regions such as Brazil's soy belt.
  • Costs: food miles and transport emissions; small farmers may be undercut by cheap imports.
  • Dependence: countries that import much of their food are exposed to shocks. On the 2026 Middle East crisis, the UN said "import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
  • Land: new farmland can replace natural ecosystems (next screens).
Mission 3

Soy and the savanna

  • South America's soybean area more than doubled: 26.4 → 55.1 million ha (2000–2019).
  • "Most soybean expansion occurred on pastures originally converted from natural vegetation for cattle production".
  • But "9% of forest loss was converted to soybean by 2016", and nearly half of soy-driven deforestation was in Brazil's Cerrado.
  • The Cerrado is a tropical savanna biome.
Mission 3

Palm oil and the rainforest

  • Indonesia and Malaysia produce more than 80% of the world's palm oil.
  • About 68% of palm oil goes into food, 27% into industrial and consumer products, and 5% into biofuels.
  • In Southeast Asia, 45% of sampled oil palm plantations were on land that was forest in 1989.
  • The forests still suitable for new plantations hold many mammal and bird species "at risk of extinction".
Quick check
?Which statement best links consumers to ecosystem loss?
Mission check

?Between 2010 and 2023, meat supply per person in Viet Nam rose by almost half. Why might this matter to farmland on another continent?

Rising incomes mean more meat; livestock eat soybean meal. China's soybean imports rose about tenfold, and most came from Brazil, where soy spread onto old pasture and into the Cerrado: global interdependence.

Mission 4

Two ways to grow more food

  • Farm more land: clear forest or grassland, which costs habitats and carbon.
  • Get more from each field (intensification): water, better seeds, fertiliser, new technology.
  • In Asia's Green Revolution, the area planted with cereals grew by just 0.42% a year (1967–82); the extra food came from higher yields.
Mission 4

Irrigation

  • Irrigation: supplying water to crops by canals, wells, pumps, sprinklers or drip pipes.
  • It lets farmers grow crops in dry seasons and dry places, and grow more than one crop a year.
  • Risks: irrigation can cause salt build-up and falling groundwater where more is pumped than rain replaces.
  • North-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water each year (2002–2008), likely from pumping for irrigation and other uses.
Mission 4

The Green Revolution

  • From the 1960s, high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice and wheat were grown with fertiliser, pesticides and irrigation.
  • About 40% of Asia's cereal area grew these varieties by 1980, and about 80% by 2000.
  • Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Population grew 60%, but calories available per person rose by nearly 30%.
Quick check
?A farmer is given high-yielding rice seeds but has no irrigation and cannot afford fertiliser. What is the most likely result?
Mission 4

Green Revolution: who gained, and at what cost?

  • In Punjab, India, where land was shared fairly equally, the gains cut poverty sharply. In neighbouring Bihar, "where the land system was more feudal, poverty reduction was more limited".
  • Overuse of fertilisers and pesticides polluted waterways and killed beneficial insects and wildlife.
  • The lesson: technology raises supply, but who owns land and who can buy inputs decides who benefits.
Mission 4

Biotechnology: GM crops

  • Genetically modified (GM) crops have genes changed in a laboratory, for example to resist insects or add vitamins.
  • A 2016 US National Academies review found "no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health" from GM crops then on sale, and, in US data for soybean, cotton and maize, "no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields".
  • Golden Rice adds beta-carotene to fight vitamin A deficiency, which affects more than 15% of Filipino children under six. The Philippines approved it in July 2021; a court revoked the permit in April 2024.
Mission 4

Hydroponics, aeroponics and vertical farms

  • Hydroponics: plants grow with roots in water containing nutrients, without soil.
  • Aeroponics: roots hang in air and are sprayed with a nutrient mist.
  • Vertical farms stack these indoors under lights. Singapore imports over 90% of its food and has only about 1% of its land for growing food.
  • But lettuce from an indoor plant factory needs 247 kWh of purchased energy per kg (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse.
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, how many times as much purchased energy does the plant factory use per kg of lettuce as the greenhouse?
Mission 4

Appropriate technology

  • Appropriate technology suits local people's money, skills and materials, and can be repaired locally.
  • Examples: foot-powered treadle pumps, low-cost drip irrigation kits, rainwater-harvesting tanks, and simple storage to stop crops rotting.
  • It is cheap and helps small farmers directly, but each project is small.
Mission 4

More food is not the whole answer

  • Brazil's food supply was already above 3,300 kcal per person in 2010, and stayed there.
  • Yet undernourishment rose from below 2.5% to 3.4% in 2021, during an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • Strategies that raise supply (irrigation, HYVs, GM, hydroponics) need strategies that give people access: incomes, cash transfers, school meals.
Mission check

?A country doubles its wheat harvest with new seeds and irrigation. Then a recession puts millions out of work and hunger rises. What would most directly cut hunger now, and keep it down?

Brazil had plenty of food; hunger tracked access. Cash for poor families and school meals bought from family farms cut undernourishment below 2.5%; recession and COVID-19 pushed it back up; sustained policy brought it down again.

Mission 5

Food security

  • Food security: everyone, at all times, can get enough safe, nutritious food.
  • Food insecurity: people cannot get enough food, because there is too little food or because they cannot afford it or reach it.
  • Food supply: how much food is grown, raised, caught or imported.
  • Famine: the most extreme level of food insecurity, with widespread hunger and deaths.
Mission 5

Somalia: where its food comes from

  • Somalia is in the Horn of Africa. It is a low-income country.
  • Crops: the main Gu season harvest normally gives about 60% of Somalia's yearly cereal output.
  • Farmers along the Juba and Shabelle rivers also grow crops with pump irrigation.
  • Herders keep camels and other livestock; their herds give milk for the family.
Mission 5

What affects food supply?

  • Rainfall: in 2026 the Gu rains were below average and erratic. Severe drought in mid-June hit 25–70% of cropland in the main maize and sorghum areas.
  • Pests: flocks of Quelea birds attacked crops.
  • Rivers and fuel: low river levels and high fuel prices made pump irrigation costly.
  • Conflict: fighting disrupted trade, pushing cereal prices up.
  • Floods: heavy rains can also flood riverside farms.
Quick check
?Which factor earlier in this mission is a human factor, not a physical one?
Mission 5

2011: what caused the famine?

  • A joint FAO and FEWS NET study looked at the areas worst hit by three things:
    • the 2010–11 drought (physical);
    • spikes in staple food prices (economic);
    • limits on humanitarian access: aid could not reach people (political).
  • The UN declared famine on 20 July 2011.
Mission 5

Why aid could not reach people

  • Much of the famine area was controlled by al-Shabaab, an armed group. It banned the World Food Programme (WFP) in January 2010.
  • Donor governments' counter-terrorism rules also restricted aid to those areas.
  • A study for the Overseas Development Institute found that restrictions by both al-Shabaab and donors "hindered attempts to address the famine".
Mission 5

Problems caused by food insecurity

  • An estimated 258,000 people died between October 2010 and April 2012.
  • An estimated 133,000 of them were children under five.
  • Hunger weakens the body, so many famine deaths come from disease.
  • Other problems: malnutrition and stunted growth, people forced to leave home, lost herds and lost income.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. About what percentage of the people who died were children under five?
Mission 5

Food aid: what it is

  • Food aid: food given to people who cannot get enough, usually by UN agencies, charities or other governments.
  • Emergency food aid: food handed out in a crisis, to save lives now.
  • Aid can also be cash or vouchers, so people buy food in local markets.
  • Before the 2011 famine, the first warning came in August 2010, but the full-scale response "was not launched until July 2011".
Mission 5

Evaluating food aid

  • ✔ Saves lives: in the 2022 drought, famine was "averted for now". UNICEF judged that "there could have been many more deaths had humanitarian assistance not been scaled up".
  • ✘ Can come too late: in 2011, Oxfam and Save the Children said that "a culture of risk aversion caused a six month delay": donors waited for proof of disaster.
  • ✘ Can be stolen or blocked: in 2010 a UN monitoring group reported that up to 50% of WFP's food aid in Somalia was being diverted; WFP disputed this.
  • ✘ Can hurt local farmers: large amounts of free food can push down local market prices, so farmers earn less; long-term aid can also create dependency.
  • ✘ Does not grow more food: aid feeds people now but does not raise future harvests.
  • Exam move: "Evaluate" means weigh benefits against problems, then judge. Short-term: essential. Long-term: not enough on its own.
Quick check
?Which statement is the best evaluation of food aid in Somalia?
Mission 5

Increasing food supply

  • Water control: FAO's RESTORE project on the Shabelle river aims to divert and store "excess river water for use during dry periods", against both floods and drought. It targets 1.5 million people in Middle and Lower Shabelle.
  • Climate-adaptive farming: RESTORE also supports farming suited to drought and floods.
  • Healthy livestock: an FAO veterinary campaign in Jowhar and Qansax Dheere is expected to reach about 150,000 animals; healthy herds keep producing milk.
  • Other techniques: drought-tolerant seeds, better crop storage, and repairing irrigation canals.
Mission check

?A made-up country, Dryland, gets a drought warning eight months before famine is declared. Its harvest fails, food prices double, and an armed group blocks aid convoys. Which explanation, based on Somalia in 2011, best explains why the warning did not prevent famine?

The warning came in August 2010; the full response came in July 2011. Drought, price spikes and blocked access to aid let food insecurity become famine. Food aid saves lives only if it arrives in time; raising supply needs longer-term work such as water control.

Mission 6

What desertification is

  • "Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas", caused by many factors, "including human activities and climatic variations" (IPCC).
  • It means dry land losing its productivity: soil, plants and water. It is not simply sand dunes advancing.
  • Its range and intensity have increased in some dryland areas over recent decades (high confidence).
Mission 6

The Green Sahara: a natural change

  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had high rainfall, plants, permanent lakes and people.
  • Slow changes in Earth's orbit made northern summers sunnier, which strengthened the African monsoon and brought rain north.
  • As that orbital effect faded, the monsoon weakened. Losing plants and gaining dust made it drier still (feedbacks).
  • So the Sahara became desert mainly through natural climate change, not people.
Mission 6

The Sahel: drought

  • In the Sahel, summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (driest year 1984), and has partly recovered since.
  • A cooler north Atlantic is linked to a drier Sahel.
Quick check
?What best explains why the Green Sahara became desert about 5,000 years ago?
Mission 6

How people cause desertification

  • Population growth raises demand for food, fuel and water. In the West African Sahel, cropland doubled after 1975 and settlement area grew by about 150%.
  • Over-cultivation: farming the same land without rest exhausts the soil.
  • Overgrazing: too many animals strip the plants. In the Sahel it reduced the store of seeds in the soil, so plants struggle to return.
  • Fuel wood removal: most people in developing countries still rely on biomass, mostly fuelwood, for a large share of their energy, with the highest dependence in Sub-Saharan Africa. It is linked to tree loss in some drylands, but not in others.
Mission 6

Soil erosion: the downward spiral

  • Bare soil has no roots to hold it and no plants to shade it.
  • Wind blows the fine, fertile topsoil away; intense rain washes it off.
  • With thinner soil, less water soaks in, so fewer plants grow and more soil is lost: a positive feedback.
Quick check
?A village doubles its goats and cattle on the same grazing land. Which cause of desertification is this?
Mission 6

Managing water and soil

  • Rainwater harvesting: zaï pits (small planting basins) and stone bunds (lines of stones along the contour) catch runoff. There is robust evidence that rainwater harvesting raises crop production in drylands.
  • Drip irrigation delivers water to the roots and uses less than flood or furrow irrigation.
  • Terracing and contour farming (ploughing and planting along the contours of a slope) slow runoff and hold soil on hillsides.
  • Leaving crop residues on fields protects the soil: even 100 kg a hectare can substantially cut wind erosion.
Mission 6

Trees and appropriate technology

  • Shelter belts (rows of trees) reduce wind erosion. In northern China, mixed tree belts cut surface wind and raised soil moisture by up to 30%.
  • Great Green Wall: launched by the African Union in 2007 to restore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030. It aims to grow "a mosaic" of trees, grasslands and other vegetation, not a single line of trees.
  • Appropriate technology: cheap and simple, so local people can build and repair it. Zaï pits and stone bunds are examples; solar-powered drip irrigation raised farm incomes in Benin.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of appropriate technology to reduce desertification?
Mission 6

Do the strategies work?

  • Great Green Wall: by 2017, about 4 million hectares had been restored in its intervention areas, about 4% of the target; other sources give almost 17.8 million hectares across the wider region.
  • IPCC: earlier tree-planting in the Sahel and North Africa "cost a lot of money without significant success", and reaching the targets is "questionable" without more funding.
  • Each US$1 spent restoring degraded land can bring about US$3–6 of social returns over 30 years, yet farmers often do not adopt the methods, because they pay the costs up front.
  • Sustainable methods are cheap, local and keep working: water harvesting, bunds and mixed planting.
Mission check

?In a semi-arid district, rainfall has not changed for 30 years. But herds have doubled, and trees are cut for cooking fuel. The land is degrading. What is the main cause?

The Green Sahara ended about 5,000 years ago mainly through natural change: a weaker monsoon, made worse by feedbacks. Today, in the Sahel, climate and people both drive desertification, in proportions that vary from place to place (IPCC, high confidence).

Mission 7

Two kinds of energy

  • Renewable: will not run out on a human timescale, because nature keeps renewing it.
  • Non-renewable: a fixed stock in the ground that is not replaced on a human timescale, so it runs out as we use it.
  • Renewable: solar, wind, hydro (HEP), tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass.
  • Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium).
Mission 7

What powers the world?

  • In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied 86% of the world's total energy.
  • Solar grew by 30% that year, and renewables were the largest source of growth.
  • So renewables are growing fast, but from a small base.
Quick check
?Which energy source is non-renewable?
Mission 7

Fuelwood: renewable or not?

  • Many people in low-income countries still rely on fuelwood for much of their energy, most of all in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Around 2.1 billion people cook on open fires or inefficient stoves burning wood, dung, crop waste, kerosene or coal.
  • Fuelwood is renewable if trees are cut no faster than they regrow.
  • It becomes non-renewable if cutting outpaces regrowth and woodland is lost.
  • The IPCC found fuelwood use linked to deforestation in some dryland areas but not in others: it depends on the rate of use.
Quick check
?A village has always cut fuelwood exactly as fast as its woodland regrows. Its population doubles and it cuts twice as much. What happens to fuelwood?
Mission 7

Non-renewables: for and against

  • Coal, oil, gas: reliable on demand; supply chains exist. But burning them releases CO₂, and the stock runs out.
  • Gas is flexible: power stations can ramp up quickly to meet peaks, and it emits the least carbon of the fossil fuels.
  • Nuclear (uranium): very low carbon, steady output. But it makes radioactive waste and stations are expensive and slow to build.
Mission 7

Renewables: for and against

  • No fuel to buy and very low emissions once built.
  • Wind and solar are variable: no output on calm days or at night.
  • HEP: steady and controllable, but dams flood valleys and block fish.
  • Geothermal: steady, but only where hot rocks are near the surface (e.g. Iceland).
  • Biomass: can be stored and burnt on demand, but needs land and must be regrown to be renewable.
  • Tidal: predictable, but costly and can change estuary habitats. Wave: varies with the weather and is costly.
Quick check
?Which is the main disadvantage of solar power?
Mission 7

Ways to increase supply

  • Find and drill new reserves: new oil and gas fields, deeper or offshore.
  • New technology: fracking (hydraulic fracturing) pumps water, sand and chemicals into shale to crack it and release gas or oil.
  • Build new power stations: nuclear, HEP dams, wind and solar farms.
  • Import energy by pipeline, ship or electricity cable.
  • Fracking in the USA: in 2023 the USA produced more crude oil than any country ever, as companies used fracking and horizontal drilling. US gas output rose about 2.1 times from 2005 to 2024.
Quick check
?How did fracking help the USA reduce an energy shortage?
Mission 7

Judging strategies, including sustainable ones

  • Judge each strategy on cost, reliability, emissions and impact on people and places.
  • Example: the planned Swansea Bay tidal lagoon would cost £1.3 billion to build. The UK government said the same electricity over 60 years would cost about £400 million from offshore wind, and rejected the lagoon on cost.
  • Sustainable strategies also cut demand: insulation, efficient machines, using less.
  • Energy that is never used needs no new power station and emits no CO₂.
Mission 7

Why not all wind?

  • Wind is free, but it varies: on calm days turbines make little.
  • Demand does not stop, so the grid needs sources it can turn up on demand (gas, HEP, biomass), storage, or imports.
  • The UK government calls gas "flexible and reliable", easy to increase for short peaks.
  • So countries build an energy mix: wind is a big part, not the whole.
Mission check

?A small island has plenty of sunshine and plans to get all its electricity from solar panels. What is the biggest problem it must solve?

Free energy is not the same as reliable energy. Wind and solar vary, so a grid also needs storage, imports or sources it can turn up on demand: that is why countries use a mix.

Make a guess: In 2025, solar power generation grew by about 30%. In the same year, the world used more fossil fuel than ever. How can both be true?

Mission 8

A century of energy

  • World energy use: about 18,000 TWh (1920) → about 172,000 TWh (2023): about 9.6 times as much.
  • World population: 1.90 billion → 8.09 billion: about 4.3 times.
  • In 1920, coal gave about 55% and traditional biomass (wood, dung) about 39% of energy.
  • In 2023, oil, gas and coal together gave about 82%.
Mission 8

Why use keeps rising

  • Rising population: more people need heat, light, food and transport.
  • Economic development (wealth): industry, cars, flights and appliances. Energy use per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Technology: new machines and devices use energy; new technology also unlocks new supplies, such as US oil from fracking.
Quick check
?From 1920 to 2023, world energy use grew about 9.6 times and population about 4.3 times. How did energy use per person change?
Mission 8

Who uses the energy?

  • Energy use per person, 2024: Qatar 214,520 kWh; USA 76,800; China 34,514; UK 28,016; world average 21,654; India 7,813.
  • Rwanda, 2023: about 530 kWh per person.
  • Pattern: highest in oil-rich Gulf states and high-income countries; lowest in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 8

Who produces it? Surplus and deficit

  • Top oil producers, 2023: USA 22%, Saudi Arabia 11%, Russia 11% of world output.
  • Fossil fuel produced compared with all energy used, 2024 (our calculation):
    • Qatar about 4.1 times; Norway 3.8; Australia 3.0; Saudi Arabia 2.2; Russia 1.7: energy surplus.
    • UK about 0.34; Germany 0.08; Japan almost 0: energy deficit, so they must import.
Mission 8

Energy security

  • Energy security: "reliable, affordable access to all fuels and energy sources" (IEA).
  • A country that imports most of its energy is less secure.
  • Singapore makes about 95% of its electricity from imported natural gas.
  • Jordan imported energy equal to 95% of its supply in 2022.
Quick check
?Which country is most likely to face energy insecurity?
Mission 8

What affects supply?

  • Physical: where oil, gas, coal and uranium lie; sun, wind and steep rivers for renewables.
  • Cost of exploitation: deep-sea, Arctic or remote reserves cost more to extract and transport.
  • Technology: from 2009, "hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling" lifted US output to a record 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023.
  • Political: conflict or sanctions. In March–May 2026, oil through the Strait of Hormuz fell to about 2.7 million barrels a day, from about 20 million.
Mission 8

Different energy at different levels of development

  • Low-income countries: many rely on fuelwood, charcoal and dung for cooking. In 2024 only 22% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had clean cooking, against 75% worldwide.
  • 655 million people had no electricity in 2024; 86% of them lived in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • High-income countries: grid electricity, gas for heating, oil for transport, and a growing share of renewables and nuclear.
  • Within a country: cities are more often on the grid; remote rural areas rely more on fuelwood or off-grid solar, which the UN calls "especially suited for low-income and remote areas".
Quick check
?Why does a rural village in a low-income country often use fuelwood rather than grid electricity?
Mission 8

Adding, not replacing

  • 2025: total energy supply passed 600 exajoules, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".
  • But fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and still supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • So new solar adds to fossil fuels; it does not yet replace them.
Mission check

?A country's solar output doubles in five years. Over the same years its oil imports rise. Which explanation fits best?

World energy use keeps rising with population, wealth and technology. In 2025 solar grew about 30%, but total supply grew too, and fossil fuels still gave 86%: renewables are adding to, not yet replacing, fossil fuels.

Mission 9

Two countries

  • UK: Human Development Index (HDI) 0.946, in the UN's "very high" group.
  • India: HDI 0.685, in the "medium" group.
  • India is your named country; the UK is used for contrast.
Mission 9

India's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: coal 57%, oil 27%, gas 6%, hydro 3%, solar 3%, wind 2%, nuclear 1%.
  • So about 90% came from fossil fuels.
  • These figures leave out traditional fuels such as fuelwood and dung, which many rural homes still burn.
  • Electricity, 2025: coal 71%, solar 9%, hydro 9%, wind 5%, nuclear 3%, gas 2%.
Mission 9

The UK's energy mix

  • 2024 energy use: oil 39%, gas 32%, wind 11%, nuclear 5%, coal 2%; other renewables make up the rest.
  • About 74% came from fossil fuels: oil mainly for transport, gas for heating and power.
  • Electricity, 2025: gas 31%, wind 29%, bioenergy 14%, nuclear 12%, solar 7%, coal 0.1%.
Quick check
?What is the biggest difference between the two countries' electricity?
Mission 9

Impacts in India

  • Emissions: India's CO₂ rose from 584 million tonnes (1990) to 3,176 million tonnes (2024), about 5.4 times.
  • But per person it is 2.2 tonnes, under half the UK's 4.6.
  • Imports: India produces only about 13% as much oil as it uses, so most must be imported.
  • Health: smoke from cooking on open fires and inefficient stoves causes household air pollution. The share of Indians cooking with clean fuels rose from 49% (2015) to 81% (2024).
Mission 9

Impacts in the UK

  • Emissions: UK CO₂ fell from 602 million tonnes (1990) to 318 million tonnes (2024), down 47%.
  • Coal ended: from about 40% of electricity in 2012 to 0% after Ratcliffe-on-Soar closed in 2024.
  • Imports: the UK produced about 50% of the gas it used in 2024, down from 112% in 2000.
Quick check
?India's total CO₂ is about ten times the UK's, but its CO₂ per person is lower. Why?
Mission 9

India: managing energy

  • Targets: by 2030, about 50% of power capacity from non-fossil sources, and a 45% cut in emissions per unit of GDP from 2005 levels.
  • Solar: from 0.5% of electricity (2015) to 9.4% (2025).
  • Clean cooking: the Ujjwala scheme has given poor households over 105 million LPG gas connections, with a free first refill and stove.
  • Industry: the PAT scheme sets energy-saving targets for big factories.
Mission 9

UK: managing energy

  • Law: the Climate Change Act requires UK emissions in 2050 to be at least 100% lower than in 1990: net zero.
  • Coal: coal power ended in 2024, and new coal mines are to be banned.
  • Offshore wind: a January 2026 auction secured a record 8.4 GW.
  • Homes: the £1 billion Great British Insulation Scheme upgraded 85,900 homes by October 2025.
Quick check
?Which strategy cuts demand for energy rather than adding new supply?
Mission 9

Judging sustainability

  • UK: emissions down 47%, coal gone, wind rising, net zero in law. But fossil fuels still give about 74% of energy, and half of gas is imported.
  • India: renewables and clean cooking rising fast, from a low base. But coal still gives 71% of electricity and emissions keep growing.
  • Different starting points: India's energy use per person is still rising; the UK's is falling.
Mission check

?Country Z gets most of its electricity from coal, its demand is rising fast, and it is adding solar faster than any other source. Which judgement fits best?

Like India, Country Z is moving towards sustainability while fossil fuels still dominate. The UK has gone further (coal gone, emissions down 47%) but still relies on oil and gas. Neither is finished: sustainability is judged by direction and by the mix today.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A farm is a system of inputs, processes and outputs, described as arable/pastoral/mixed and subsistence/commercial.
  • Hydroponics (nutrient solution), aeroponics (nutrient mist) and aquaponics (fish plus plants) grow food without soil, using little land but often much energy.

Mission 2

  • Food security: all people, at all times, can get enough safe, nutritious food. Supply per person is highest in Europe and the Americas and lowest in Africa.
  • Physical factors (climate, pests, water stress) and human factors (poverty, conflict, technology, prices) affect supply and access.
  • Food consumption rises with population and incomes. Food insecurity brings undernutrition, famine, rising prices, social unrest and soil erosion.

Mission 3

  • Food production rose with higher yields.
  • Consumption changed as incomes and cities grew.
  • Food supplies globalised through demand, cheap transport, TNCs and trade policy; impacts include choice and export income, but also food miles, dependence and land clearing.

Mission 4

  • More food: irrigation, the Green Revolution's HYVs, GM crops, hydroponics and aeroponics. Each raises supply, with costs (groundwater, pollution, energy, inequality). Appropriate technology suits local people's money, skills and materials.
  • Ending hunger needs access as well as supply, kept up through crises.

Mission 5

  • Somalia's food supply depends on rains, rivers, pests, prices and conflict; in 2011 drought, price spikes and blocked aid caused famine.
  • Food aid saves lives in a crisis but can be late, blocked or diverted; projects such as RESTORE aim to increase supply in the long term.

Mission 6

  • Desertification is drylands losing productivity. Causes: drought and climate change, plus population pressure, over-cultivation, overgrazing and fuel wood removal; soil erosion speeds it up.
  • Solutions that last are cheap and local: water harvesting, bunds, shelter belts and mixed planting.

Mission 7

  • Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are renewed by nature; non-renewable fossil fuels and uranium are fixed stocks. Fuelwood is renewable only if cut no faster than it regrows.
  • Every source has pros and cons; wind and solar are low-carbon but variable, so they work best in a mix. Supply can be raised by new reserves, new technology such as fracking, new power stations or imports; sustainable strategies also cut demand.

Mission 8

  • Energy use rose about 9.6 times since 1920, driven by population, development and technology; it is very unequal per person.
  • Surplus countries produce more than they use; deficit countries import and are less energy secure.

Mission 9

  • India (medium HDI): coal 57% of energy, 71% of electricity; emissions rising; managing through solar, clean cooking (Ujjwala) and industrial efficiency (PAT). UK (very high HDI): fossil fuels about 74% of energy, coal power ended 2024; emissions down 47% since 1990; net zero law, offshore wind, insulation.
