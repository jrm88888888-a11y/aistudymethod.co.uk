Mission 1

One God, no other

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one God, with no partners, rivals or parts.
  • Jews say it twice a day in the Shema: God is one (Hebrew echad).
  • God alone is worthy of worship: no intermediaries, no idols (Exodus 20:3–5).
  • Ethical monotheism: the one God is also the source of justice and goodness, and demands them from people. Belief and conduct cannot be separated.
  • The phrase became central in 19th-century Jewish thought (e.g. Hermann Cohen, 1842–1918), but the idea runs through the prophets.

Mission 1

Creator, sustainer, judge

  • Jewish teaching describes God in these roles:
  • Creator: "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917) Most Jewish thinkers: creation from nothing.
  • Sustainer / controller: creation is not a one-off. The morning prayer praises God who "in his goodness renews every day, continually, the work of creation" (paraphrase).
  • Eternal: "from everlasting to everlasting, Thou art God" (Psalm 90:2). God does not change (Malachi 3:6).
  • Judge: God rewards and punishes: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • Judgement is just and merciful; Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are the "days of judgement".
Mission 1

No body, no likeness

  • Jews believe God is incorporeal: no body, no shape, no physical comparison.
  • At Sinai (Horeb) "ye saw no manner of form" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917): so no image of God may be made.
  • Bible phrases like God's "hand" or "voice" are metaphors: "the Torah speaks in human language" (Talmud, Berakhot 31b, paraphrase), a principle Maimonides uses.
  • Your spec: "no physical comparison whatsoever".

Mission 1

What God is like: attributes

  • Jewish teaching describes God as:
  • Omnipotent: all-powerful; nothing is beyond God.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing, including human thoughts and the future.
  • Omnibenevolent / merciful: "merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6, JPS 1917): the start of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on fast days and festivals.
  • Holy: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3), set apart and pure.
  • Just and perfect: God's acts are right and complete.
  • Many Jewish thinkers (e.g. Maimonides) say such words describe what God does, not what God is in essence.
Mission 1

Maimonides' Thirteen Principles

  • Moses Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204), Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin chapter 10 (c. 1168).
  • About God (1–5): (1) God exists and is the cause of all; (2) God is one; (3) God has no body; (4) God is eternal (first and last); (5) only God may be worshipped.
  • Revelation (6–9): prophecy; Moses is the greatest prophet; the Torah is from God; it will not be changed.
  • Reward and the end (10–13): God knows human deeds; rewards and punishes; the Messiah will come; the dead will be raised.
  • Sung in Yigdal and recited as Ani Ma'amin ("I believe") in many prayer books.
  • Critics: Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410/11) and Joseph Albo (Sefer ha-Ikkarim, c. 1425) counted fewer principles (Albo: three). Some argue Judaism stresses practice more than creed.
Mission 1

Obligations to God and to people

  • Jewish law distinguishes two kinds of mitzvot:
  • Bein adam la-Makom ("between a person and God"): prayer, Shabbat, kashrut.
  • Bein adam la-chavero ("between a person and their fellow"): honesty, charity, kindness.
  • Yom Kippur atones for sins against God, but sins against people need the other person's forgiveness first (Mishnah Yoma 8:9, paraphrase).
  • "Only to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God" (Micah 6:8, JPS 1917).
  • D Cohn-Sherbok (Dan Cohn-Sherbok, b. 1945), paraphrase: belief in one God is the foundation of Jewish life, but modern Jews interpret God in very different ways, from traditional to naturalist.
  • G Robinson (George Robinson), Essential Judaism (2000), paraphrase: Judaism is lived more through deeds than creeds; love of God is shown in how people are treated.
Mission 1Diversity

One God, many understandings

  • Orthodox: God as in Maimonides' principles: personal, revealing the Torah, rewarding and punishing.
  • Kabbalah and Hasidism: God is Ein Sof ("without end"), beyond all thought, revealed through ten sefirot; some Hasidic teachers say nothing exists apart from God.
  • Reform: ethical monotheism is the essence of Judaism; God known through conscience and history more than through law.
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) described God as "the power that makes for salvation": a force in nature and people, not a supernatural person.
  • Humanistic Judaism: Jewish identity without belief in God.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is belief in one God the foundation of Judaism?

  • Claim: Monotheism is the essence and foundation of Judaism.
  • For: The Shema is the first prayer learned and the last said; Maimonides' first principles are about God.
  • For: Ethics depends on the one God: one God, one moral law for all people.
  • Against: Judaism stresses practice (halakhah) over creed; some argue there is no single required belief.
  • Against: Humanistic and many secular Jews keep Jewish identity without belief in God.
  • Against: Covenant, Torah and peoplehood may be as central as monotheism.
  • Judgement criteria: Essence for whom: the Torah, the rabbis, or Jews today? Is a foundation the same as the most important thing?

Mission 2

Three parts: TaNaKh

  • Tenakh is an acronym: Torah + Nevi'im + Ketuvim. 24 books in the Jewish count, mostly in Hebrew.
  • Torah (teaching, instruction): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The heart of the Tenakh.
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): Joshua to Kings (history); Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
  • Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot (scrolls, e.g. Esther, Ruth), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
  • "Torah" can also mean all Jewish teaching, written and oral.
Mission 2

Given at Sinai

  • The Torah's account: after the Exodus, Israel camps at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19). God makes a covenant with the people.
  • God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); Moses receives the rest of the teaching.
  • The people promise to do all God has said (Exodus 24:7); the rabbis read the Hebrew order, "we will do" before "we will hear", as accepting before fully understanding.
  • Moses writes the Torah and gives it to the priests and elders (Deuteronomy 31:9).
  • Revelation is to a whole people, not one individual: the root of Jewish identity.
  • 613 mitzvot (commandments) are counted in the Torah.

Mission 2

Chosen for a task

  • "The LORD thy God hath chosen thee to be His own treasure, out of all peoples" (Deuteronomy 7:6, JPS 1917). Not for size: "ye were the fewest of all peoples" (Deuteronomy 7:7, JPS 1917).
  • Chosenness means obligation: "You only have I known of all the families of the earth; therefore I will visit upon you all your iniquities." (Amos 3:2, JPS 1917)
  • A Cohen (Abraham Cohen, 1887–1957), Everyman's Talmud (1932), paraphrase: the rabbis link Israel's election to its acceptance of the Torah; a midrash says the Torah was offered to other nations, and Israel alone accepted.
  • G Robinson (George Robinson), Essential Judaism (2000), paraphrase: chosenness is a responsibility to live by the mitzvot as an example, not a claim to be superior.
  • Revelation and identity: Torah study, Shabbat and festivals keep Jews a distinct people in every land.
  • Reconstructionists (following Mordecai Kaplan) reject the idea of chosenness altogether.
  • Your spec calls God "the Almighty".
Mission 2Diversity

Word of God or inspired word?

  • Orthodox: Torah min ha-shamayim ("Torah from heaven"). God gave the whole Torah to Moses; every letter is divine, complete, unchangeable. Maimonides' 8th Principle.
  • "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans over centuries; it records Israel's encounter with God. Ethical teaching binds; ritual laws may change.
  • Modern scholarship (e.g. the theory of several sources behind the Torah) shaped this view.
  • Masorti (Conservative): divine in origin, passed on and shaped by humans; law still binding but developing.
  • D Cohn-Sherbok (b. 1945), Judaism: History, Belief and Practice (2003), paraphrase: modern scholarship on Mosaic authorship divides Orthodox from non-Orthodox Jews.
  • All treat the Torah as holy and central.

Mission 2

The Tenakh in worship and life

  • Synagogue: the Torah is read on Shabbat, Mondays, Thursdays and festivals; the yearly cycle ends and restarts at Simchat Torah. Public reading needs a minyan.
  • A haftarah from the Nevi'im follows the Shabbat reading; the Megillot are read at festivals (Esther at Purim, Ruth at Shavuot).
  • Psalms fill daily prayer; the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9) is in the mezuzah and tefillin.
  • Home and life: study, Shabbat table discussion of the week's portion; guidance for ethics and law.
  • Beliefs shape use: Orthodox Jews follow the laws as divine command; some Reform and Masorti communities read a third of each portion a week (a three-year cycle) and choose which practices to keep.
Mission 2

The Sefer Torah: care and study

  • Sefer Torah: handwritten in Hebrew by a trained scribe (sofer) on parchment, with a quill; one error makes it unfit until corrected.
  • Kept in the Aron Hakodesh (ark), facing Jerusalem; dressed in a mantle; read with a yad (pointer) so hands do not touch the text.
  • People stand when the ark is opened; it is lifted and shown to the congregation. On return: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her" (Proverbs 3:18, JPS 1917).
  • A worn-out scroll is not thrown away but buried in a Jewish cemetery.
  • Yeshiva: a place of study of Tenakh and Talmud, often in pairs (chavruta), where the oral Torah opens the written one.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the Torah still the main authority?

  • Claim: The Torah remains the main authority in Judaism today.
  • For: it is read in every synagogue, holds the 613 mitzvot and is treated with unique honour.
  • For: both Orthodox and Reform Jews call it holy and base their identity on Sinai.
  • Against: in practice the Talmud and later codes decide how laws apply; rabbis interpret.
  • Against: many secular and Progressive Jews give individual conscience and modern ethics the final say.
  • Judge it by: Does "authority" mean honour or decision-making? Can the written Torah be separated from its interpretation? Which Jews?

Mission 3

From slavery to Sinai

  • The book of Exodus tells that, after the Exodus from Egypt, Israel camps at Mount Sinai "in the third month" (Exodus 19:1).
  • Moses goes up the mountain; God sends a message to the whole people.
  • God first recalls what he has done: "how I bore you on eagles' wings" (19:4). Rescue comes before law.
  • The people prepare for three days: washing clothes, keeping a boundary round the mountain (19:10–15).
  • Thunder, fire, smoke and a shofar: God speaks the Ten Commandments to the whole people (Exodus 20).

Mission 3

The Ten Commandments

  • Hebrew: Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the ten words" or "ten statements" (compare 34:28, "the ten words").
  • Two stone tablets, traditionally five statements on each: the first five (God, no idols, God's name, Shabbat, honouring parents) are linked with duties to God, the second five (murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting) with duties to people.
  • Jewish count: the first statement is "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917). Belief in God comes first.
  • Most Christians count differently: none counts "I am the LORD thy God" as a separate statement. Catholics and Lutherans take it with "no other gods" and "no idols" as the first, and split coveting in two; most Protestants and the Orthodox make "no other gods" the first and "no idols" the second.
  • They are 10 of the 613 mitzvot. Medieval rabbis (e.g. Saadia Gaon) saw all 613 as grouped under the ten.
  • The rabbis removed the daily reading of the Ten Commandments in the synagogue so that no one would claim only these ten came from Sinai (Talmud, Berakhot 12a).

Mission 3

The status of Moses

  • Moshe Rabbenu, "Moses our teacher": the greatest prophet, but never worshipped.
  • "There hath not arisen a prophet since in Israel like unto Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face" (Deuteronomy 34:10, JPS 1917).
  • God speaks with him "mouth to mouth" (Numbers 12:8); he is "very meek" (Numbers 12:3).
  • Maimonides' 7th Principle: Moses' prophecy is above that of every other prophet. 8th: the whole Torah now in our hands was given to Moses.
  • A Cohen (Abraham Cohen, 1887–1957), Everyman's Talmud (1932), paraphrase: the rabbis treat Moses as the channel of both the written and the oral Torah, yet stress his humility; the Torah, not Moses, is the authority.
  • G Robinson (George Robinson), Essential Judaism (2000), paraphrase: Moses matters as lawgiver and teacher; Jewish practice deliberately keeps attention on the Torah he brought, not on Moses himself.
  • Moses has no tomb: "no man knoweth of his sepulchre" (Deuteronomy 34:6), often read as preventing a cult around him.

Mission 3Diversity

Sinai today

  • Orthodox: the covenant is binding law (halakhah): all applicable mitzvot, as interpreted by the oral Torah. Revelation at Sinai is historical.
  • Conservative (Masorti): halakhah binding, but it develops; Sinai as an ongoing revelation through interpretation.
  • Reform / Liberal: the moral law (e.g. the Ten Commandments) binds; ritual laws are chosen where they bring meaning. Classical Reform's Pittsburgh Platform (1885) accepted "only the moral laws" as binding; later Reform statements (1999) value more ritual practice.
  • Shavuot celebrates the giving of the Torah; the Ten Commandments are read in the synagogue, and the congregation stands.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the Sinai covenant mainly a legal contract?

  • Claim: The Sinai covenant is above all a legal agreement.
  • For: It has stated requirements, a written record and sanctions, like a legal agreement.
  • For: It is the source of halakhah: Jewish law still applies it in courts (beit din) and daily life.
  • Against: It begins with rescue and love ("eagles' wings"), not a contract; obedience answers grace.
  • Against: Prophets later describe the covenant as a marriage (Hosea 2:21–22 in Jewish numbering).
  • Against: Some say covenant could become a tool of religious control (law enforced by priests); others answer that the people freely consent (24:7).
  • Judgement criteria: What does "legal" mean: binding, or impersonal? Do Orthodox and Reform Jews experience it the same way?

Mission 4

A mitzvah is a commandment

  • Mitzvah (plural mitzvot) means commandment: something God commands, not just a kind act.
  • The Talmud counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah: 248 positive ("do") and 365 negative ("do not") (Makkot 23b).
  • Many apply only in the Land of Israel, in Temple times or to priests; fewer than 300 can be kept by most Jews today.
  • A Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah, "son" or "daughter of the commandment": now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is how Jews live out the covenant made at Sinai (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

Towards God, towards people

  • The rabbis sort mitzvot by direction:
  • Bein adam la-Makom: between a person and God (e.g. Shabbat, prayer, kashrut).
  • Bein adam la-chavero: between a person and another person (e.g. honesty, charity, not gossiping).
  • Both kinds are commands of God: wronging a person is also a sin against God.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person, you must first put things right with that person.
  • Your spec: "obligations between people and obligations between humans and God".

Mission 4

Two tablets

  • The usual Jewish count:
  • Tablet 1: (1) I am the LORD your God · (2) no other gods, no idols · (3) do not take God's name in vain · (4) keep Shabbat · (5) honour father and mother.
  • Tablet 2: (6) do not murder · (7) do not commit adultery · (8) do not steal (the Talmud reads this as kidnapping) · (9) no false witness · (10) do not covet.
  • Common reading: tablet 1 is between people and God, tablet 2 between people.
  • Why are parents on tablet 1? The Talmud calls God, father and mother partners in making a person (Kiddushin 30b).
  • Christians number them differently (most start with "no other gods"): do not mix the two systems.

Mission 4

Ten headings for 613

  • Rabbinic tradition reads the Ten Sayings as ten categories that hold all 613 mitzvot.
  • Saadia Gaon (882–942) wrote liturgical poems (Azharot) arranging all 613 under the ten.
  • Rashi (1040–1105), on Exodus 24:12, repeats it (paraphrase): all 613 are contained in the Ten; Saadia showed which belong to each.
  • Examples: "keep Shabbat" heads all the festival laws; "do not steal" heads honest weights and fair trade.
  • Earlier, Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE–c. 50 CE) also called the ten "headings" of the special laws.
  • So the Ten are a summary and a doorway, not a replacement for the 613.
  • Status: Jews believe the Ten were given by God at Sinai through Moses, whom Maimonides calls the greatest prophet, and written on the tablets (see Mission 3).
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox, Reform, Hasidic

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God (Torah min ha-shamayim, "Torah from heaven"); every mitzvah that can be kept today is binding; the Oral Torah fixes how.
  • Reform: the Torah was written by inspired humans; the ethical mitzvot remain central; ritual mitzvot are chosen by informed choice. Pittsburgh Platform (1885): "we accept as binding only its moral laws". Later Reform statements (Pittsburgh 1999) encourage more ritual practice.
  • Hasidic: fully Orthodox in practice, often stricter (hiddur mitzvah, beautifying the mitzvah); stress joy, kavanah (intention) and devekut (clinging to God) while doing each mitzvah.
  • Masorti (Conservative) Jews hold halakhah binding but developing.

Mission 5

What "chosen" means

  • The Torah says God chose (Hebrew bachar) Israel: the descendants of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob.
  • Israel is God's am segulah, God's treasured people (see Exodus 19:5; Deuteronomy 7:6).
  • The choice is rooted in the covenants with Abraham and at Sinai.
  • Before reading the Torah in synagogue, Jews bless God "who has chosen us from all peoples and given us his Torah": chosen for the Torah.
  • Anyone can join: a sincere convert (ger) becomes fully part of the chosen people. Chosenness is not a matter of race.

Mission 5

Chosen for a task

  • Obligation more than privilege (as many Jews put it): Jews accept 613 mitzvot; other peoples are not asked to.
  • A kingdom of priests (Exodus 19:6): to serve God and bring others closer to him.
  • "A light of the nations" (Isaiah 42:6; 49:6): to show by example what living by God's will looks like.
  • Kiddush ha-Shem ("sanctifying God's name"): a Jew's good conduct honours God; bad conduct (chillul ha-Shem) shames God's name.
  • Chosenness has brought suffering as well as honour: in the musical Fiddler on the Roof (1964), Tevye asks God to choose someone else once in a while.
  • Your spec calls God "the Almighty".

Mission 5

Who chose whom? A Cohen and G Robinson

  • A Cohen (Abraham Cohen, 1887–1957), Everyman's Talmud (1932), paraphrase: the rabbis linked Israel's election to its acceptance of the Torah. A midrash says God offered the Torah to other nations first, and each refused; Israel accepted (Sifre Deuteronomy 343).
  • Another midrash: God held the mountain over the people "like a barrel" (Talmud, Shabbat 88a): the choice was God's, and the duty unavoidable.
  • So in rabbinic thought Israel chose as well as was chosen.
  • G Robinson (George Robinson), Essential Judaism (2000), paraphrase: chosenness is a responsibility to live by the mitzvot and to be an example; it is not a claim to be superior, and converts share it fully.
Mission 5

Revelation and identity

  • At Sinai, God was revealed to a whole people, not to one prophet alone (see Mission 3). Jewish identity is therefore shared and passed on.
  • Revelation shapes the whole of life: food (kashrut), time (Shabbat, festivals), home (mezuzah), study, charity, family.
  • These practices kept Jews a distinct people across centuries of exile with no land of their own.
  • Often quoted (Ahad Ha'am, 1856–1927, paraphrase): more than the Jews have kept Shabbat, Shabbat has kept the Jews.
  • Community life follows: synagogue, Torah study, the minyan (a quorum of ten for communal prayer: ten men in Orthodox practice, ten adults in most non-Orthodox communities), mutual care.
Mission 5

Everyone else in God's plan

  • In the Torah God made a covenant with Noah and all humanity first (Genesis 9).
  • The rabbis drew from it seven Noahide laws for all people: no idolatry, blasphemy, murder, theft, sexual immorality or eating flesh from a live animal; and set up courts of justice.
  • "The righteous of the nations have a share in the world to come" (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase). Non-Jews do not need to become Jews to have a share in the world to come.
  • So Judaism does not seek converts, though it accepts sincere ones.
  • The prophets imagine all peoples worshipping God: "My house shall be called a house of prayer for all peoples" (Isaiah 56:7, JPS 1917).
  • God cares for other nations too: "Are ye not as the children of the Ethiopians unto Me, O children of Israel?" (Amos 9:7, JPS 1917).

Mission 5Diversity

Chosenness across Jewish movements

  • Orthodox: chosenness is real and lasting; Jews have extra duties. Some thinkers (Judah Halevi, Kuzari, 12th century) spoke of a special spiritual capacity in Israel; others (Maimonides) based it on the Torah alone, open to any convert.
  • Reform: the "mission of Israel": to spread ethical monotheism by example among the nations (classical Reform: Pittsburgh Platform, 1885; Columbus Platform, 1937).
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) rejected chosenness as implying superiority; Reconstructionist prayer books changed the Torah blessing to "who has drawn us to his service".
  • Humanistic and many secular Jews reject divine choice, but keep a strong sense of shared history and peoplehood.
Mission 5

Chosenness and other faiths

  • Dialogue: Jews take part in the Council of Christians and Jews (founded in Britain, 1942) and Jewish–Muslim forums.
  • Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Dignity of Difference (2002), paraphrase: one God made a diverse world; the covenant with Noah is for all; difference between faiths can be a blessing, not a threat.
  • Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964), paraphrase: Jews should cooperate with other faiths on shared moral and social concerns, but not debate the inner truths of each faith, which cannot be translated.
  • Critics of chosenness: Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677), Theological-Political Treatise (1670), argued that Israel's election concerned only its state and prosperity at that time. Others say any idea of chosenness risks seeming exclusive.
  • Antisemites have twisted "chosen" into claims of Jewish superiority or conspiracy; Jewish teachers reply that the idea is about duty, not domination.

Mission 5Evaluate

Does chosenness hinder relations with other faiths?

  • Claim: Belief in being chosen makes good relations with other faiths harder.
  • For: "Chosen" can sound exclusive; outsiders may hear superiority, and it has been misused by enemies of Jews.
  • For: A distinct people with its own laws may limit social mixing (e.g. kashrut, marriage within the faith).
  • Against: Chosenness is for service: "a light of the nations". Noahide teaching gives other peoples a real place with God.
  • Against: Judaism does not seek converts, so it has no reason to compete with other faiths for followers.
  • Against: Sacks and many others argue that a confident identity makes respectful dialogue easier.
  • Judgement criteria: What do Jews themselves mean by "chosen"? Which movement's view is weighed? Is the obstacle the belief or its misuse by others?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one, eternal, incorporeal God, creator, sustainer and judge, who alone is worshipped and who demands justice (ethical monotheism).
  • Maimonides' Thirteen Principles summarise belief; Jewish traditions interpret the one God differently.
  • Duties to God and duties to people both matter.

Mission 2

  • The Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah tells of its own giving at Sinai through Moses, within a covenant.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the Torah is the complete, unchangeable Word of God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Reform and Liberal Jews see it as inspired but humanly written.
  • The Sefer Torah is read publicly, studied, and treated with great care: ark, mantle, yad, burial.

Mission 3

  • In the Exodus account, God, who had rescued Israel, offers a covenant at Sinai: "if" Israel keeps it, they will be a treasured, holy nation; the people agree (Exodus 19:8).
  • The Ten Commandments begin, in Jewish counting, with "I am the LORD thy God".
  • Sinai is the defining covenant of law, unlike the near-unconditional promise to Abraham; scholars compare its form with suzerainty treaties.

Mission 4

  • A mitzvah is a commandment from God; the Talmud counts 613, between people and God and between people.
  • The Ten Sayings (Aseret ha-Dibrot) begin with God's self-introduction; rabbinic tradition (Saadia, Rashi) reads them as ten categories of the 613.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all 613 binding; Reform Jews treat ethical mitzvot as binding and ritual as informed choice; Hasidic Jews keep Orthodox practice with stress on joy and intention.

Mission 5

  • Israel was chosen out of God's love and covenant, not for size or merit (Deuteronomy 7:6–8); chosenness brings obligations (Amos 3:2) and the task of being "a light of the nations".
  • Non-Jews have their own covenant through Noah: the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come, so Judaism does not seek converts.
  • Views differ across Orthodox, Reform, Reconstructionist and secular Jews; they shape interfaith relations (Sacks, Soloveitchik).
  • A Cohen: election linked to accepting the Torah; G Robinson: chosenness as responsibility; revelation shapes identity and the whole of community life.
