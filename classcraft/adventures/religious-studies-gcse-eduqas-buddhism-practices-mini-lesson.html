Mission 1

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community". The third of the Three Jewels: Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha.
  • Narrow meaning: the monastic community of monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis).
  • Wider meaning: the "fourfold assembly": monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen.
  • Deepest meaning: the noble Sangha: all who have reached at least the first stage of awakening.
  • Many Mahayana and Western Buddhists use "sangha" for any group practising together.
Mission 1

How the Sangha began

  • After his Enlightenment, the Buddha taught his first sermon to five ascetics at the Deer Park, Sarnath.
  • One, Kondanna, understood, and asked to join him: the first monk. The others soon followed.
  • Within months there were sixty arhats. The Buddha sent them out to teach "for the welfare of the many".
  • Later, his stepmother and aunt Mahapajapati led women who asked to be ordained: the first nuns.
Mission 1

Going for refuge

  • Buddhists recite the Three Refuges (Tisarana), often three times:
  • Buddham saranam gacchami: I go for refuge to the Buddha (the teacher).
  • Dhammam saranam gacchami: … to the Dhamma (the teaching).
  • Sangham saranam gacchami: … to the Sangha (the community).
  • A refuge is a safe place: a shelter from dukkha that does not change.
  • Saying the refuges marks someone as a Buddhist; they are chanted at the start of most ceremonies.

Mission 1

Giving and receiving

  • Monks and nuns depend on lay people; lay people depend on them.
  • Lay people give (dana): food, robes, medicine, shelter.
  • Dana to monks is one of the central Buddhist offerings.
  • The monastic Sangha gives: teaching, chanting, blessings, advice, and an example of the path.
  • The alms round: in Theravada countries, monks walk silently with bowls each morning; people place food in them.
  • Giving makes merit; the monk's silence lets the giver give freely.
  • Kathina festival, after the rains retreat: lay people offer new robe cloth.

Mission 2

Kinds of Buddhist places

  • Temple: a building for devotion, teaching and meditation.
  • Vihara: "dwelling place": a monastery where monks or nuns live; lay people visit. Mainly Theravada.
  • Gompa: Tibetan, "solitary place": a hall for meditation and learning, often within a monastery.
  • Shrine: a focus for devotion, in a temple, a home or outdoors.
  • Stupa: a dome-shaped monument holding relics (pagoda in East Asia, chorten in Tibet). Buddhists walk around it clockwise.
Mission 2

The shrine room

  • The heart of most Buddhist places: a room with a shrine.
  • A buddharupa ("form of the Buddha") sits raised at the front: nothing higher than the Buddha's teaching.
  • Visitors remove shoes, sit lower than the rupa, and avoid pointing their feet at it.
  • Used for: chanting, puja (devotion), offerings, meditation, talks.
Mission 2

Reading a buddharupa

  • Each feature recalls a quality, not a portrait:
  • Ushnisha (bump on the head): wisdom, awakening.
  • Long earlobes: once weighed down by princely jewels, now given up.
  • Urna (a curl or dot between the eyebrows): the Buddha's insight, often shown sending out light.
  • Half-closed eyes: meditation; looking inward.
  • Postures: seated (meditation, Enlightenment), standing (teaching), lying (parinibbana).
  • Styles differ: Thai rupas often gold and slender; Tibetan shrines hold many Buddhas and bodhisattvas; Japanese Pure Land temples centre on Amida.
Mission 2

Mudras: hands that speak

  • Mudras: hand positions on rupas, each recalling an event or quality.
  • Earth-touching (right hand to the ground): the Enlightenment at Bodh Gaya, calling the earth to witness against Mara.
  • Teaching wheel (hands at the chest forming circles): the first sermon at the Deer Park, Sarnath.
  • Meditation (hands resting in the lap): calm concentration.
  • Fearlessness (right palm raised): protection, peace.
  • Giving (right hand down, palm out): generosity.
  • Buddhists may make mudras themselves in puja and meditation, to identify with the qualities of buddhas and bodhisattvas.
Mission 2Read the source

The Vakkali Sutta

Vakkali Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87), in summary (paraphrase):

  • Vakkali, a sick monk, longs to see the Buddha in person.
  • The Buddha comes and says: enough! What is there to see in this decaying body? Whoever sees the Dhamma sees me; whoever sees me sees the Dhamma.

Q1 What does the Buddha say matters more than seeing his body?

Show answer

Seeing, understanding, the Dhamma.

Q2 What does this suggest a buddharupa is for?

Show answer

Pointing to the Dhamma, not being worshipped as the Buddha himself.

Mission 2

Offerings and veneration

  • Flowers: they wilt, a reminder of impermanence (anicca).
  • Light (candles, lamps): the wisdom that drives out the darkness of ignorance.
  • Incense: its scent spreads, like the good influence of the Dhamma and of a good life.
  • Food and water: generosity. Tibetans often offer seven water bowls in a row.
  • Buddhists often bow three times: to the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.
  • This is veneration (deep respect and gratitude), not worship of a god.
  • Your spec: veneration rather than worship.

Mission 2

Bodhi tree and library

  • Library: monasteries keep and study scriptures; many lay people borrow books and attend classes.
  • Meditation hall: quiet space for group practice.
  • Stupa in the grounds of many temples and viharas.
Mission 2

Artefacts in a gompa

  • Wheel of Life painting, often at the entrance: samsara, the six realms and the three poisons at the centre.
  • Mandala: a circular design of an enlightened world, used as a focus in meditation. Sand mandalas are swept away when finished: impermanence.
  • Prayer wheels: cylinders holding written mantras such as Om mani padme hum; turning them clockwise is like reciting the mantra, spreading compassion.
  • Thangkas: painted scrolls of Buddhas and bodhisattvas.
  • Chanting fills the hall at puja, with bells, drums and horns.
Mission 2Diversity

In Britain and elsewhere

  • Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar: a temple (wat) is a large complex: ordination hall, stupas, monks' quarters; the centre of village life.
  • Tibet, Nepal, Bhutan: large monasteries with gompas, prayer flags and prayer wheels.
  • Japan: temple halls, gardens and family graves.
  • Britain: often a converted house or building, used by people of many backgrounds. Examples:
  • Buddhapadipa Temple, Wimbledon (Thai, Theravada) · Samye Ling, Scotland (Tibetan; founded 1967) · London Buddhist Centre (Triratna; a former fire station) · Amaravati monastery, Hertfordshire (Theravada).
  • In Britain, many centres run meditation classes for non-Buddhists, and use English in teaching and chanting.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Buddhists do not need special places to practise."

  • Reasons for: the Buddha was enlightened under a tree. Meditation and the precepts can be practised anywhere. Many Buddhists have a home shrine.
  • The Vakkali Sutta: seeing the Dhamma matters more than seeing the Buddha's form.
  • Reasons against: temples and viharas house the monastic Sangha, which lay people support. Group chanting, teaching and festivals need shared space. A shrine room helps the mind settle.
  • Judge it by: Theravada or Mahayana? Monastic or lay? Britain or a Buddhist country? What is the purpose of practice?

Mission 3

What puja is

  • Puja means honouring or devotion: bowing, making offerings and chanting before an image of the Buddha.
  • Most Buddhists do not see the Buddha as a creator God. Buddhists teach that he was a human being who became fully enlightened and taught the way.
  • So puja is usually described as veneration (deep respect and gratitude), not worship of a god.
  • Your spec's phrase: puja is "veneration rather than worship".
  • Puja expresses gratitude to the Three Jewels: the Buddha, the Dhamma (his teaching) and the Sangha (the community).
  • It also trains the mind: calm, focus, generosity and humility.
Mission 3

Offerings and what they mean

  • Before the buddharupa (image of the Buddha) many Buddhists bow three times: once for each of the Three Jewels.
  • Flowers: fresh, then they wilt. A reminder of anicca (impermanence).
  • Light (candles, lamps): the Buddha's wisdom driving out the darkness of ignorance.
  • Incense: its scent spreads, as the Dhamma and good conduct spread.
  • Food and water: generosity. Tibetan shrines often have a row of seven water bowls.
  • Dana (giving): lay people offer food to monks and nuns, who live on gifts. Giving builds merit and loosens greed.
  • Mudras: hand gestures. In some Mahayana and Tibetan puja, people form a Buddha's or bodhisattva's mudra to identify with that figure's qualities.
Mission 3

Puja at home and in the vihara

  • At home: a small shrine with a buddharupa, candle, flowers and incense. Short daily puja: bowing, offerings, chanting, perhaps meditation.
  • In the vihara or temple: often led by monks or nuns. Communal chanting, offerings, a Dhamma talk, meditation, dana.
  • Temple puja is used on festival and uposatha days (moon days) and for blessings; home puja fits daily life.
  • Different types (daily or festival, alone or together, formal or simple) suit different moments: the variety lets every Buddhist practise, whatever their life.
Mission 3

Chanting

  • Chanting is two things at once: devotion (honouring the Buddha) and concentration (the rhythm steadies the mind).
  • Theravada Buddhists chant in Pali. Most puja opens: Namo tassa bhagavato arahato samma-sambuddhassa ("Homage to the Blessed One, the Worthy One, the Fully Enlightened One"), three times.
  • Tisarana (Three Refuges), going for refuge to the Tiratana (Three Jewels): Buddham saranam gacchami "I go to the Buddha for refuge", then the Dhamma, then the Sangha.
  • Your spec calls this chanting the Triratna: the Three Jewels.
  • Mahayana: Nichiren Buddhists (e.g. Soka Gakkai) chant Nam Myoho Renge Kyo ("devotion to the Mystic Law of the Lotus Sutra"). They believe it awakens the Buddha-nature within.
  • Pure Land Buddhists chant Namu Amida Butsu ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha").

  • Müller's old words: "the Law" = the Dhamma; "the Church" = the Sangha.

Mission 3

Mantras and malas

  • A mantra is a sacred sound or phrase repeated many times.
  • Tibetan: Om mani padme hum, the mantra of Avalokiteshvara, the bodhisattva of compassion.
  • Mahayana and Tibetan Buddhists recite mantras to evoke enlightenment: repeating a Buddha's or bodhisattva's mantra calls up that being's qualities in the reciter's own mind.
  • Mala: a string of beads, usually 108, to count recitations. Moving a bead with each mantra keeps the mind on task.
  • Japanese Buddhists use a juzu in chanting; Tibetans use malas for mantras; some meditators count breaths on a mala.
Mission 3

Objects of devotion

  • Buddharupa: an image of the Buddha. Its calm face, posture and mudra remind Buddhists of his qualities: peace, wisdom, compassion.
  • Shrine: the place where the image, offerings and often texts are set out; the focus for puja.
  • Bodhi tree: the tree under which the Buddha was enlightened. Many viharas grow one, from cuttings descended from the original, and honour it with offerings.
  • Vakkali Sutta: the monk Vakkali longed to keep looking at the Buddha's body. The Buddha asked why he wanted to look at a body that would decay, and taught: whoever sees the Dhamma sees me; whoever sees me sees the Dhamma (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase).
  • So an image points beyond itself: the Buddha is met in his teaching, not in a statue.
  • Mudras on images: e.g. the earth-touching gesture (enlightenment), the teaching wheel (first sermon). (More in Mission 2.)

Mission 3Diversity

Different Buddhists, different puja

  • Theravada (e.g. Sri Lanka, Thailand, UK viharas): Pali chanting, offerings, dana to monks, Mangala and other protective chants.
  • Tibetan: mantras, prostrations, butter lamps, water bowls, prayer wheels and flags, visualisation.
  • Pure Land: reciting Namu Amida Butsu in gratitude and trust in Amida Buddha's vow.
  • Nichiren: chanting Nam Myoho Renge Kyo before the Gohonzon, a scroll inscribed with the mantra.
  • Zen: simple altars and short chanting (e.g. the Heart Sutra); seated meditation is central.
  • Triratna (UK-founded): puja in English, e.g. the Sevenfold Puja.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Buddhists do not need puja."

  • Reasons for: "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers" (Dhammapada 276, Müller). Zen and many Western Buddhists put meditation and ethics first.
  • Reasons against: puja builds faith, gratitude and calm, which the path needs. Chanting steadies the mind for meditation. For Pure Land and Nichiren Buddhists, chanting is the heart of practice. Communal puja holds the Sangha together.
  • Also for: the Vakkali Sutta points away from images to the Dhamma.
  • Judge it by: Is puja a means or an end? Does it depend on the tradition? Can a mind be trained without devotion?

Mission 4

Training the mind

  • The Pali word for meditation, bhavana, means "cultivating" or "developing": growing good states of mind.
  • Buddhists believe the Buddha reached enlightenment through meditation, seated under the Bodhi tree.
  • Meditation is part of the Eightfold Path: right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration (the samadhi section).
  • Two main aims: calm (a steady, focused mind) and insight (seeing reality as it is).
  • Insight brings wisdom, which ends the craving that causes dukkha.
  • "Through zeal knowledge is gotten, through lack of zeal knowledge is lost" (Dhammapada 282, Müller). The Pali word Müller renders "zeal" is yoga, which most modern translators render "meditation".
Mission 4

Samatha: calm

  • Samatha: concentration and tranquillity.
  • Main method: mindfulness of breathing (anapanasati). Sit upright; rest attention on the breath, at the nostrils or belly.
  • Beginners often count breaths, one to ten, then start again.
  • The mind wanders; you notice and gently return. That return is the training.
  • Aim: a calm, one-pointed mind, free of restlessness, desire and ill will for a while.
  • Deep calm (jhana, meditative absorption) is possible for experienced meditators.
  • Calm is the base for insight: a still pond shows what lies at the bottom.
Mission 4

Metta bhavana: loving kindness

  • Metta bhavana: developing loving kindness, warm goodwill to all beings.
  • Your spec spells it mettabhavana.
  • Five stages, often taught as: yourself → a good friend → a neutral person → a difficult person → all beings everywhere.
  • Wishes such as: "May you be well. May you be happy. May you be free from suffering."
  • Aim: to replace ill will and fear with kindness that has no favourites.
  • It is a form of samatha (calm), and it shapes action: Buddhists link it to the ethical teaching of metta.
Mission 4

Vipassana: insight

  • Vipassana: insight, seeing clearly.
  • The meditator watches body, feelings, mind and mental objects (the four foundations of mindfulness), noting each as it arises and passes.
  • The aim is direct insight into the three marks of existence: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • Seeing this for oneself, not just believing it, loosens craving and leads towards nibbana.
  • Samatha calms the mind; vipassana uses the calm mind to see. Many Theravada teachers combine them.

Mission 4

Visualisation

  • Mainly Mahayana and Tibetan (Vajrayana) practice.
  • The meditator builds a detailed mental image of a Buddha or bodhisattva: colour, posture, mudra, surroundings.
  • Example: picturing Avalokiteshvara (compassion) or Manjushri (wisdom), reciting their mantra.
  • Aim: to develop that being's qualities in oneself. In Tibetan practice the meditator may identify with the figure, then let the image dissolve into emptiness.
  • Thangkas (painted scrolls) and mandalas help build the image.
  • Buddhas and bodhisattvas are a focus of devotion and of meditation at once: the Buddha's own enlightenment through meditation is the model.
Mission 4Read the source

Two Mahayana sutras

  • Surangama Sutra (paraphrase): many enlightened beings describe how they awoke. The bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara turned his hearing inward, listening not to sounds but to the nature of hearing itself, until the gap between hearer and heard fell away. Manjushri praises this method as the best for people of this world.

Q1 What does the Surangama Sutra suggest meditation can do?

Show answer

Turn attention back on the mind itself, leading to awakening.

Mission 4Diversity

Different schools, different emphases

  • Theravada: samatha (breathing, metta) and vipassana; visualising Buddhas is rare, though recollecting the Buddha's qualities is practised.
  • Zen: zazen; wary of images in meditation, which can become something to cling to.
  • Pure Land: visualisation of Amitayus/Amida and his land; many Pure Land Buddhists, especially Jodo Shinshu in Japan, rely instead on reciting Namu Amida Butsu in faith.
  • Tibetan: visualisation and mantra central, alongside calm and insight.
  • Lay Buddhists: traditionally, in many Asian Buddhist countries, monks and nuns meditated most and many lay people focused on giving, precepts and puja (lay meditation has grown since the 20th century). In the West, many lay Buddhists start with meditation.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Meditation is the most important Buddhist practice."

  • Reasons for: the Buddha was enlightened through meditation. Three steps of the Eightfold Path concern the mind. Wisdom, which ends dukkha, comes through insight.
  • Reasons against: ethics (sila) comes first: without the precepts the mind cannot settle. Many lay Buddhists practise mainly through giving, keeping precepts and puja. For Pure Land Buddhists, faith in Amida and the nembutsu matter most.
  • Another view: ethics, meditation and wisdom are one path (the Threefold Way); none works alone.
  • Judge it by: Which practice did the Buddha stress? Does the answer differ for monks and lay people? For Theravada and Mahayana?
Mission check

For Buddhists, calm steadies the mind and insight sees through it; together they make meditation a path to wisdom, not just a way to relax.

Mission 5

Death in Buddhist teaching

  • Anicca: everything that arises passes away. Death is certain; its time is not.
  • Buddhists believe that at death a person is reborn according to their kamma (intentional actions). Nothing fixed passes on (anatta): the monk Nagasena (in the Milindapanha) compared rebirth to a flame lit from another flame.
  • The state of mind at death matters: Buddhists believe a calm, wholesome mind leads towards a good rebirth.
  • So rites aim to help the dying person's mind, to help their rebirth, and to teach the living about impermanence.
Mission 5

Theravada rites

  • Before death: monks may chant; family remind the dying person of their good deeds, so the last thoughts are wholesome.
  • Funeral: monks chant a verse on impermanence (all conditioned things arise and pass away; their stilling is bliss: paraphrase).
  • A white cloth is offered to monks on behalf of the dead (pamsukula).
  • Water-pouring: relatives pour water from a jug into a cup until it overflows, while monks chant. As water flows down to fill the low ground, so merit flows to the dead.
  • Your spec: transferring merit to help the dead person's rebirth.
  • Cremation is usual. Mourners in Sri Lanka often wear white.
  • Almsgiving (dana) to monks on the dead person's behalf on the 7th day, after three months and each year.

Mission 5

Japan

  • Most Japanese funerals are Buddhist, whatever the family's other practice.
  • A wake (tsuya) and a funeral with chanting led by a priest.
  • The priest gives the dead person a posthumous Buddhist name (kaimyo), written on a wooden memorial tablet (ihai).
  • The importance of the name: it marks the dead person as a disciple of the Buddha; the tablet is kept in the family's home shrine (butsudan), where the family make offerings.
  • Cremation is almost universal. Relatives lift bone fragments into the urn with chopsticks; the ashes are buried in the family grave.
  • Memorial services on the 7th and 49th days and on anniversaries.
  • Obon (mid-summer): families welcome back the spirits of their ancestors with lanterns and offerings (festival in Mission 6).
Mission 5

Tibet: the bardo

  • Tibetan Buddhists believe in the bardo: the "in-between" state between death and rebirth, lasting up to 49 days.
  • At death, a lama may guide the dying person's consciousness out of the body (phowa).
  • For the next weeks, a lama reads from the Tibetan Book of the Dead (Bardo Thodol, "Liberation through Hearing in the Bardo") to the dead person.
  • Monks chant; the family make offerings to monks, light butter lamps and give to charity.
  • Aim: help the dead person recognise what they meet and so reach liberation, or at least a good rebirth.
Mission 5Read the source

The Tibetan Book of the Dead

A lama speaks to the dead person, by name (paraphrase of the Bardo Thodol):

  • At the moment of death, a clear light dawns. It is the true nature of your own mind. Recognise it and you are free.
  • If you do not, peaceful and then wrathful figures will appear. Do not fear them: they are projections of your own mind.
  • If you still do not recognise this, you will be drawn towards a womb. Choose a good rebirth calmly.

Q1 Why read the text aloud to someone who has died?

Show answer

Tibetan Buddhists believe consciousness can still hear in the bardo and can be guided.

Q2 What does the text say the frightening figures are?

Show answer

Not beings outside: projections of one's own mind, so there is nothing to fear or cling to.

Mission 5

Sky burial

  • In much of Tibet, the body is taken to a high open place and offered to vultures: sky burial (jhator, "giving alms to the birds").
  • It is done with chanting by monks and great care by specialists.
  • Generosity: the body feeds other living beings, a last act of giving.
  • Impermanence: for Tibetan Buddhists the body is now an empty vessel; they believe the consciousness has already left.
  • Practical: hard, frozen ground for burial; little wood for cremation.
  • High lamas may be cremated or preserved in stupas.

Mission 5Diversity

Same beliefs, different customs

  • Shared: impermanence; kamma and rebirth; a calm mind at death; merit made for the dead; monks or priests chant.
  • Theravada: merit-transfer (water-pouring), cremation, almsgiving on set days.
  • Japan: posthumous name, cremation, ashes buried in the family grave, ancestor memorials.
  • Tibet: guidance through the bardo, 49 days of rites, sky burial.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Buddhist death rites are more for the living than for the dead."

  • Reasons for: each person's kamma is their own. The chanting on impermanence teaches the mourners. Giving and gathering help the family grieve and make merit themselves.
  • Reasons against: Theravada Buddhists dedicate merit to help the dead person's rebirth.
  • Also against: Tibetan Buddhists believe the bardo guidance can bring liberation. Japanese memorial rites keep caring for the dead for years.
  • Another view: the two cannot be separated: love for the dead is expressed by the living.
  • Judge it by: Can merit be shared? How do the traditions differ? What does anatta imply for "the dead person"?

Mission 6

Why festivals?

  • Festivals remember key events in the Buddha's life and teaching.
  • They renew commitment: to the Three Jewels and to the precepts.
  • They gather the Sangha: monks, nuns and lay people together.
  • They are a time for merit: giving (dana), keeping precepts, meditating.
  • Most follow the lunar calendar, so dates move. Traditions date them differently.
Mission 6

Wesak

  • The most important festival for many Buddhists, at the full moon of the month of Vesakha (usually May).
  • Your spec: Wesak celebrates the Buddha's birth; in the Theravada tradition also his enlightenment and death.
  • Theravada Buddhists believe all three happened on the same full-moon day in different years.
  • Customs: cleaning and decorating homes and viharas; lanterns and lights (light = enlightenment); bathing the buddharupa; offerings; listening to the Dhamma.
  • Many lay Buddhists keep eight precepts for the day and give dana to monks.
Mission 6

Parinirvana Day

  • A Mahayana festival, usually on 15 February.
  • It marks the Buddha's death at Kushinagar, aged 80, and his passing into final nirvana (parinirvana): no more rebirth.
  • Customs: readings about the Buddha's last days; meditation; reflection on impermanence; remembering those who have recently died.
  • Why celebrate a death? Buddhists believe the Buddha reached what all beings seek: freedom from dukkha.

?On 15 February, a Mahayana group reads about the Buddha's last days, remembers friends who died this year and meditates. Which festival are they keeping?
Mission 6

Vassa and Kathina

  • Vassa (Rain Retreat): about three months in the monsoon season (roughly July to October).
  • Origin: the Buddha asked monks to stay in one place during the rains instead of travelling, so as not to damage crops and small creatures.
  • Monks and nuns study, meditate and teach more intensively.
  • Kathina: within a month after Vassa ends, lay people offer cloth for robes and other gifts to the monastery. The cloth may be sewn into a robe on the same day.
  • It thanks the Sangha, and giving at Kathina is believed to bring great merit.

Mission 6Diversity

Who keeps what?

  • Theravada (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, UK viharas): Wesak (birth, enlightenment, death), Vassa (the Rain Retreat), Kathina (robe-cloth giving after it), Uposatha (moon observance days).
  • Mahayana: Parinirvana Day; in Japan, Hanamatsuri (the Buddha's birth) and Obon (welcoming ancestors' spirits).
  • Tibetan: the Buddha's enlightenment and parinirvana in the fourth Tibetan month (Saga Dawa); Lama Tsong Khapa Day (remembering a great Tibetan teacher; especially the Gelug school).
Mission 6Evaluate

"Festivals are not important for Buddhists in Britain today."

  • Reasons for: the Buddha did not command festivals; the path is about training the mind. Many British Buddhists are converts who focus on meditation. Dates and customs vary, so there is no shared calendar.
  • Reasons against: in a minority setting, festivals gather the Sangha and pass the tradition to children. Wesak recalls enlightenment, the goal itself. Parinirvana Day teaches impermanence. Dana and precepts at festivals make merit.
  • Judge it by: Does importance mean "required" or "valuable"? Does it differ between heritage and convert communities? Can retreats do the same job?
Mission check

?A visitor reads a UK vihara's calendar: in May, lanterns, bathing the buddharupa and eight precepts; in February, readings about the Buddha's last days. She says: "So Buddhists celebrate his birthday and mourn his death, like any famous person." Which reply fits Buddhist teaching best?

Buddhist festivals remember the Buddha's life, but they point beyond it: to enlightenment, impermanence and the practice that leads there.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Sangha (community) is the third Jewel; Buddhists go for refuge to Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.
  • The Buddha founded the Sangha after his first sermon; monks and nuns live simply and depend on lay people's dana, giving teaching in return.

Mission 2

  • Buddhists practise in temples, viharas (monasteries), gompas (halls for meditation and learning), shrines and at stupas.
  • A shrine room centres on a raised buddharupa; offerings of flowers, light and incense express respect and teach impermanence and wisdom: veneration, not worship.
  • Places differ between Theravada, Mahayana and Triratna, and between Britain and Buddhist countries.

Mission 3

  • Puja (bowing, offerings, chanting) is veneration of the Buddha and the Three Jewels, done at home and in the vihara.
  • Chanting and mantras are both devotion and concentration; malas count recitations.

Mission 4

  • Meditation (bhavana) develops calm (samatha, e.g. mindfulness of breathing) and insight (vipassana into anicca, dukkha, anatta).
  • Metta bhavana grows loving kindness in five stages.
  • Zazen (Zen) and visualisation of Buddhas and bodhisattvas (Mahayana, Tibetan) show how schools differ.

Mission 5

  • Death rites rest on anicca, kamma, rebirth and the importance of a calm mind at death.
  • Theravada: merit-transfer, cremation, almsgiving. Japan: posthumous name, ashes buried, ancestor memorials. Tibet: bardo guidance (Tibetan Book of the Dead), sky burial.

Mission 6

  • Wesak (May full moon) recalls the Buddha's birth and, for Theravada Buddhists, his enlightenment and death; Parinirvana Day (Mahayana, February) marks his final nirvana.
  • Vassa is the three-month Rain Retreat; Kathina ends it with gifts of robe cloth.
