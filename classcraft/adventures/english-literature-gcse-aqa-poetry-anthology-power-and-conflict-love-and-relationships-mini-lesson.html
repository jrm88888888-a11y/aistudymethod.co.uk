Mission 1

A break-up poem full of silence. What is the speaker hiding?

Make a guess: A break-up poem full of silence. What is the speaker hiding?

Love and Relationships · When We Two PartedScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Lord Byron (1788–1824) was a Romantic poet, famous and often scandalous.
  • The poem was first published in his Poems in 1816.
  • Byron later dated it 1808. Many scholars think the date was false, meant to hide who the poem is about.
  • It is usually linked to Lady Frances Wedderburn Webster, whom Byron courted in 1813; in 1816 she was publicly linked to a scandal with the Duke of Wellington.
  • Treat this as background: the poem itself never names her.

Stanzas 1 and 2

Love and Relationships · When We Two PartedScreen 3

Then and now

  • Stanza 1 looks back to the parting: pale cheek, cold kiss, "Sorrow to this".
  • Stanza 2 shifts to now: "what I feel now". She has broken her vows and her name is spoken with "shame".
  • Four eight-line stanzas, short lines of two or three beats, rhymed ABABCDCD: tight and controlled, like a man holding feelings in.
  • Cyclical structure: the last line returns to "silence and tears". Nothing has moved on.

Secrecy and shame

  • "They" talk about her in front of him, but "They know not I knew thee": he can't show his pain.

?A break-up poem full of silence. What is the speaker hiding?

The speaker hides a secret relationship ("In secret we met"). Now her name is gossiped about with "shame", he can't show his grief, so the poem begins and ends in "silence and tears". Many readers also hear wounded pride beneath the sorrow.

Mission 2

Everything in nature kisses. Love poem or sales pitch?

Make a guess: Everything in nature kisses. Love poem or sales pitch?

Love and Relationships · Love's PhilosophyScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822) was a radical Romantic poet.
  • The poem was first published by Leigh Hunt in The Indicator on 22 December 1819. In one manuscript Shelley headed it "An Anacreontic": a light poem of love and pleasure, after the Greek poet Anacreon.
  • Shelley wrote against marriage laws that bound lovers: "Love withers under constraint: its very essence is liberty" (note to Queen Mab, 1813).
Extract Love and Relationships · Love's PhilosophyScreen 2

"Love's Philosophy" (whole poem)

Love and Relationships · Love's PhilosophyScreen 3

An argument in two stanzas

  • The title promises philosophy: a reasoned case.
  • Each stanza gives evidence from nature (fountains, rivers, winds; mountains, waves, sunlight, moonbeams), then draws a conclusion as a question.
  • Stanza 1 claims that joining is God's rule for everything.
  • Stanza 2 adds a warning: a flower that rejects another "would be forgiven" by no one.
  • Many readers see a persuasive speaker; some see playful wit rather than real pressure.
Love and Relationships · Love's PhilosophyScreen 4

Form: building to a question

  • Two eight-line stanzas, rhymed ABABCDCD, with a light, song-like rhythm.
  • Stanza 1 uses feminine rhymes (two-syllable rhymes): river / for ever, Ocean / emotion, single / mingle.
  • The last line of each stanza is the shortest, just five syllables: "Why not I with thine?" and "If thou kiss not me?"
  • Both questions are left unanswered: we never hear the beloved's reply.

Love and Relationships · Love's PhilosophyScreen 6

Language: from mingling to kissing

  • Stanza 1's verbs are gentle and abstract: "mingle", "mix", "meet".
  • Stanza 2's verbs are physical: "kiss", "clasp". The personification of nature makes physical love seem natural.
  • "This sweet work" sums up nature's evidence as a kind of labour, and asks what it is "worth" without his kiss.
  • Archaic "thou" and "thine" make the address intimate and direct.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Everything in nature kisses. Love poem or sales pitch?

It's both. The speaker builds a two-stanza argument from nature ("a law divine") to persuade the beloved, ending each stanza with a short question that is never answered. Readers differ on whether that's charming wit or pressure: the strongest answers argue one way from the evidence.

Mission 3

He says she felt no pain. Why shouldn't we believe him?

Make a guess: He says she felt no pain. Why shouldn't we believe him?

Love and Relationships · Porphyria's LoverScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Robert Browning (1812–1889) first published the poem in the Monthly Repository in January 1836, as "Porphyria".
  • In 1842 he paired it with another poem under the title "Madhouse Cells"; it became "Porphyria's Lover" in 1863.
  • It is a dramatic monologue: one character speaks, revealing more than he means to. The speaker is not Browning.
  • The speaker kills Porphyria. This lesson deals with that factually.

Love and Relationships · Porphyria's LoverScreen 5

An unreliable speaker

  • He claims to read her mind: "at last I knew / Porphyria worshipp'd me".
  • He is calm and precise about the killing, then treats it as what she wanted: her "darling one wish".
  • "Mine, mine": he wants to possess her, and to freeze her as "Perfectly pure and good".
  • Readers debate why: madness (the 1842 title), obsession, or a lover who can't accept her ties to a social world ("pride, and vainer ties").
Love and Relationships · Porphyria's LoverScreen 6

Form: calm control

  • One unbroken block of 60 lines in iambic tetrameter (eight syllables, four beats): "The rain set early in to-night,".
  • The rhyme pattern ABABB repeats in every five lines: night / awake / spite / lake / break.
  • The regular form matches his controlled, reasonable voice, which makes what he describes more disturbing.
  • Pathetic fallacy opens it: "The sullen wind was soon awake,".
  • The ending, "And yet God has not said a word!", leaves judgement open: no sign from God, either way.

Mission checkScreen 9

?He says she felt no pain. Why shouldn't we believe him?

In a dramatic monologue we hear only the speaker. He repeats that she "felt no pain", claims she "worshipp'd" him and calls the killing her "darling one wish": all self-justification. Readers argue about why he acts (madness, possession, class), but the strongest answers show how Browning makes us distrust him.

Mission 4

It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

Make a guess: It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 1

Context in brief

  • Elizabeth Barrett (1806–1861) was a famous poet when Robert Browning wrote to her in January 1845; he first visited her that May.
  • They married secretly on 12 September 1846 and left for Italy. Her father never forgave her and cut her off.
  • Sonnets from the Portuguese (44 sonnets) were written during the courtship and published in her Poems, 1850, framed as if translations because they were so personal.
Extract Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 2

Sonnet 29 (whole poem)

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 3

What she says

  • Lines 1–4: her thoughts of him grow like wild vines over a tree, until only "straggling green" is seen and the tree is hidden.
  • Lines 5–8: "Yet": she refuses to have thoughts "instead of thee". She wants the real man.
  • Lines 8–11: she commands the tree to shake off the vines: "Renew thy presence".
  • Lines 12–14: when he is with her, she needn't think of him: she is "too near thee".
Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 4

Form: a Petrarchan sonnet

  • 14 lines of iambic pentameter: five beats a line (ten syllables, plus an unstressed extra one in the "thee" lines).
  • Octave rhymed ABBAABBA (bud / tree / see / wood…), then a sestet rhymed CDCDCD (bare / insphere thee / everywhere…).
  • The sestet's D rhymes are all feminine and all end on "thee": insphere thee, hear thee, near thee. He is at the end of every one.
  • The turn comes early, at "Yet" in line 5, and the sestet then turns thought into action.

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 6

Language: from thoughts to presence

  • The extended metaphor runs through the poem: vines, leaves, tree, boughs, trunk, shadow.
  • "O my palm-tree": a direct, adoring apostrophe; a palm is tall, strong and upright.
  • Exclamations and dashes ("I think of thee!—", "dearer, better!") show urgent feeling.
  • "A new air": being near him feels like breathing again.
  • "Thee" and "thy": intimate, and the language of the older sonnet tradition.

?It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

The opening and ending don't contradict each other. Her thoughts of him grow like wild vines that hide the real man; she wants them swept away so he is present. When he is near, she doesn't need to think of him at all: "I am too near thee."

Mission 5

Grey leaves, a white sun. Why is this love poem so drained?

Make a guess: Grey leaves, a white sun. Why is this love poem so drained?

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) is best known for novels such as Tess of the d'Urbervilles, but wrote poetry all his life.
  • The poem is dated 1867, when Hardy was in his late twenties, but he published it only in his first collection, Wessex Poems, in 1898.
  • Neutral tones are colourless shades: grey, white, dull.
  • Many readers see the poem's bleakness as typical of Hardy's pessimism about love and fate.
Extract Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 2

"Neutral Tones" (whole poem)

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 3

Structure: back to the pond

  • Four quatrains, each rhymed ABBA (day / God / sod / gray). The enclosing rhyme closes each stanza like a frame.
  • Stanzas 1–3: the memory of one winter day with a lover.
  • Stanza 4: "Since then", the present. He has learned "that love deceives".
  • Cyclical structure: stanza 4 lists the same face, sun, tree and pond. The memory returns whenever love hurts him.

Relationships poetry · Neutral TonesScreen 5

Language: a dead love

  • Pathetic fallacy: a pale sun, a "starving sod", gray leaves. The landscape is as lifeless as the love.
  • Stanza 3's paradox: her smile is "the deadest thing / Alive enough to have strength to die".
  • Simile: her bitter grin passes "Like an ominous bird a-wing", a bad omen.
  • Alliteration: love "wrings with wrong", a harsh, twisting sound.
  • "Your" and "us" address the lover directly, but she never speaks.

?Grey leaves, a white sun. Why is this love poem so drained?

The "neutral tones" are a mirror. The pale sun, starving ground and gray leaves match a love that is dying in front of them, and the last stanza shows the same scene returning to him "Since then" whenever love deceives.

Mission 6

She fears him; he longs for her. Who should we pity?

Make a guess: She fears him; he longs for her. Who should we pity?

Love and Relationships · The Farmer's BrideScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Charlotte Mew (1869–1928) published The Farmer's Bride with Harold Monro's Poetry Bookshop in 1916; the poem had first appeared in The Nation in 1912.
  • It is a dramatic monologue: a farmer tells the story of his marriage in a rural dialect ("When us was wed", "she runned away").
  • We hear only his voice. His young wife barely speaks.
  • He rushed the marriage, with no time to court her: "more's to do / At harvest-time than bide and woo."

Love and Relationships · The Farmer's BrideScreen 5

Language: her, through his eyes

  • Animal and nature imagery: "like a mouse", "Shy as a leveret", "Straight and slight as a young larch tree". Tender, but it makes her wild, not human: something to catch.
  • Her one "speech" is silent: "'Not near, not near!' her eyes beseech". He voices it for her.
  • "I've hardly heard her speak at all": the poem's silence is hers.
  • Dialect makes him sound simple and sincere, which draws some sympathy.
Love and Relationships · The Farmer's BrideScreen 6

Form: control slips

  • Five stanzas of 9, 10, 10, 12 and 5 lines, with an irregular rhyme scheme (couplets, triplets and alternate rhymes) and uneven line lengths.
  • Stanza 4 turns to winter and an empty house: he wants "Some other in the house".
  • The short last stanza reaches the attic: "'Tis but a stair / Betwixt us."
  • Then his calm breaks: "Oh! my God! the down, / The soft young down of her, the brown, / The brown of her—her eyes, her hair, her hair!"

Mission checkScreen 9

?She fears him; he longs for her. Who should we pity?

Most readers pity both. The farmer is lonely and sincere, but his own story (the chase, the locked door, the stair) shows a young wife who is terrified and voiceless. Mew lets us hear him and judge him at the same time.

Mission 7

Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Make a guess: Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A tightly controlled poem (ABABCDCD, short lines) that moves from the parting to the speaker's present pain and ends where it began.
  • The love was secret, so the grief is silent; her name is now spoken with "shame".
  • Pair it with poems about endings, silence or memory, such as "Neutral Tones".
  • Two stanzas, each building evidence from nature to a short, unanswered question.
  • Personification ("kiss", "clasp") and the "law divine" make physical love seem natural and holy.
  • Pair it with poems of desire or nature imagery, such as "Sonnet 29".
  • A dramatic monologue: the speaker kills Porphyria and calmly justifies it, so we read against him.
  • Regular iambic tetrameter and an ABABB pattern give a controlled voice to a disturbing story.
  • Pair it with "The Farmer's Bride" (male speakers, silent women) or "Love's Philosophy" (desire).
  • A Petrarchan sonnet: an extended metaphor of vines and tree, an early turn ("Yet") and a sestet of commands.
  • Every sestet D rhyme ends on "thee": the beloved's real presence is the goal.
  • Pair it with "Love's Philosophy" or "Letters from Yorkshire".
  • Pathetic fallacy and drained colour turn one winter scene into an image of dead love.
  • Four ABBA quatrains; the last stanza returns to the pond: a memory that shapes how he sees love.
  • Pair it with "When We Two Parted".
  • A dramatic monologue in dialect: only the farmer speaks; his bride's fear shows through his words.
  • Animal imagery (hare, mouse, leveret) is tender but makes her prey; the ending's repetition shows his desire breaking through.
  • Pair it with "Porphyria's Lover" (male speakers, silent women).
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • In silence and tears, (Stanza 1, line 2) The opening image, repeated at the end: cyclical structure.
  • Colder thy kiss; (Stanza 1, line 6) Coldness foretells the end of love.
  • I hear thy name spoken, (Stanza 2, line 7) Her name is now public gossip.
  • A knell to mine ear; (Stanza 3, line 2) Metaphor: the love has died; he mourns.
  • In secret we met— (Stanza 4, line 1) Explains why his grief must be silent.
  • Nothing in the world is single; (Stanza 1, line 5) The argument's premise: everything pairs.
  • All things by a law divine (Stanza 1, line 6) Claims God's authority for desire.
  • Why not I with thine?— (Stanza 1, line 8) The first short, unanswered question.
  • See the mountains kiss high Heaven (Stanza 2, line 1) Personification: physical love as natural.
  • If thou kiss not me? (Stanza 2, line 8) The final demand: the beloved stays silent.
  • The sullen wind was soon awake, (Line 2) Pathetic fallacy: a menacing opening.
  • Porphyria worshipp'd me (Line 33) He claims to know her feelings.
  • That moment she was mine, mine, fair, (Line 36) Repetition: possession.
  • I am quite sure she felt no pain. (Line 42) The insistent claim that makes us doubt him.
  • And yet God has not said a word! (Line 60) The open ending: no judgement given.
  • my thoughts do twine and bud (Line 1) Starts the extended metaphor: thoughts as growing vines.
  • the straggling green which hides the wood (Line 4) Her thoughts hide the real man.
  • I will not have my thoughts instead of thee (Line 6) The turn: she wants him, not imagination.
  • Renew thy presence (Line 8) Her commands begin, leading into the sestet.
  • I do not think of thee—I am too near thee. (Line 14) The resolution: presence beats thought.
  • And the sun was white, as though chidden of God, (Stanza 1, line 2) Pathetic fallacy: even the sun is drained and scolded.
  • The smile on your mouth was the deadest thing (Stanza 3, line 1) Paradox: a smile that is dead.
  • Like an ominous bird a-wing (Stanza 3, line 4) Simile: a bad omen for the love.
  • keen lessons that love deceives (Stanza 4, line 1) The bitter lesson he has learned since.
  • And a pond edged with grayish leaves. (Stanza 4, line 4) The cyclical ending: back to the pond.
  • When us was wed she turned afraid (Stanza 1, line 4) Dialect; her fear begins with the marriage.
  • We chased her, flying like a hare (Stanza 2, line 6) Simile: she is hunted like an animal.
  • And turned the key upon her, fast. (Stanza 2, line 10) She is trapped in the house.
  • I've hardly heard her speak at all. (Stanza 3, line 10) She has no voice in the poem.
  • 'Tis but a stair / Betwixt us (Stanza 5, lines 2–3) The small distance he longs to cross.
