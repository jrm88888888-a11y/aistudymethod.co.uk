Poetry Anthology: Power & Conflict / Love & Relationships

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of the AQA GCSE English Literature Poetry Anthology (Paper 2, Section B). Your school studies ONE of two 15-poem clusters — Power and Conflict or Love and Relationships — and in the exam you compare TWO poems from your cluster. It drills the poems, terminology, comparison and method → effect — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest comparison, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The two clusters

Fifteen poems — one cluster

The anthology comes in two clusters of 15 poems each. Your school teaches one, and you compare poems within it — you never compare across clusters.

  • Power and Conflict: Ozymandias (Shelley), London (Blake), The Prelude (Wordsworth), My Last Duchess (R. Browning), The Charge of the Light Brigade (Tennyson), Exposure (Owen), Storm on the Island (Heaney), Bayonet Charge (Hughes), Remains (Armitage), Poppies (Weir), War Photographer (Duffy), Tissue (Dharker), The Emigrée (Rumens), Checking Out Me History (Agard), Kamikaze (Garland).
  • Love and Relationships: When We Two Parted (Byron), Love's Philosophy (Shelley), Porphyria's Lover (R. Browning), Sonnet 29 'I think of thee' (E. B. Browning), Neutral Tones (Hardy), Letters from Yorkshire (Dooley), The Farmer's Bride (Mew), Walking Away (Day-Lewis), Eden Rock (Causley), Follower (Heaney), Mother, any distance (Armitage), Before You Were Mine (Duffy), Winter Swans (Sheers), Singh Song! (Nagra), Climbing My Grandfather (Waterhouse).

Key idea: the poems cluster around shared themes — Power & Conflict explores human power, the power of nature, and the reality of war/loss; Love & Relationships explores romantic love, family love, distance and change. You compare on theme, language, form/structure and tone.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

The anthology comparison is worth 30 marks and rewards AO1, AO2 and AO3 (there are no SpaG marks in this question):

What matters most: a sustained comparison that keeps both poems side by side, driven by method and effect. Context (AO3) earns marks but must illuminate a poet's choice, not sit as a history block.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader:

method + evidence → effect on readere.g. "the caesura in the line (method) jolts the reader, mirroring the speaker's broken thought (effect)"

Useful methods to spot in poetry: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, symbolism, pathetic fallacy, semantic field, sibilance, enjambment, caesura, volta, first-person speaker, dramatic monologue, iambic pentameter, rhyme, structure and form.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a stanza

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not a set poem) annotated the way you'd mark up a real anthology poem in the exam:

The iron hills stood silent, cold and grey,
while wind clawed at the ruined, crumbling wall;
no crown, no name — the years had washed away
the proud stone that once had promised all.
  • "iron" — metaphor: power made hard, unfeeling and permanent — or so it seems.
  • "clawed" — personification: nature is hostile and active, wearing power down.
  • "crown" — symbol of authority, now absent: human power does not last.
  • "washed away" — imagery of erosion: time outlasts even proud monuments (compare Shelley's "colossal wreck" in Ozymandias).

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your comparison paragraphs need.

The core skill · comparison

Compare — don't write two essays

The examiner rewards a sustained comparison: keep both poems side by side and use comparative connectives to link them on the same idea:

  • Similarity: similarly, likewise, both, in the same way, equally.
  • Difference: whereas, however, by contrast, unlike, on the other hand, while.
Integrated vs block comparison

✔ Integrated: discuss one idea (e.g. power of nature) in BOTH poems in the same paragraph, linked by a connective — this is the stronger structure.

△ Block: all of poem A, then all of poem B — weaker, because the comparison is thin.

Habit: after a point on poem A, reach straight for a connective — "whereas…", "similarly…" — and make the matching point on poem B.

Form & voice

Form and the dramatic monologue

Form and structure carry meaning too, so comment on them for AO2. A powerful example is the dramatic monologue — a single speaker unknowingly revealing their own character:

  • My Last Duchess (Power & Conflict) — the Duke's controlling, jealous nature leaks out as he shows off a portrait.
  • Porphyria's Lover (Love & Relationships) — a disturbed speaker calmly narrates a murder, revealing his obsession with control.

Also watch: a volta (a turn in argument or mood — often in sonnets), enjambment (lines running on to build momentum), caesura (a mid-line pause that jolts), and regular vs broken rhyme mirroring order or disorder.

Argument skill

Open with a comparative thesis

Begin with a thesis that answers the exact comparison and names both poems' angle. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "Compare how poets present the power of nature in two poems."

Thesis: "Both poets present nature as an overwhelming force that dwarfs humanity, yet where the first uses violent personification to make it feel hostile, the second, through steady imagery, suggests an indifferent power that simply outlasts us."

Why it works: it takes a comparative position, names a method for each poem, and points to an effect — so every paragraph can prove one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — from BOTH poems

The printed poem is in front of you, but the second poem is not — so learn a bank of short, flexible quotations from your likely comparison poems. Weave them into your own sentence:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The poem is about a broken statue. The quote is: 'the lone and level sands stretch far away.'"

✔ "Shelley's image of a 'colossal wreck' reduces a once-mighty king to rubble, making his boast bitterly ironic."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — quote the few words you'll actually analyse, and do it for both poems so the comparison stays balanced.

Context & AO3

Context serves the analysis

Context can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the poem. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture. Useful anthology contexts include:

  • Romanticism (Shelley, Wordsworth, Blake) — nature's sublime power; distrust of tyranny (e.g. Ozymandias).
  • War — WW1 for Owen's Exposure; the Crimean War for Tennyson's Charge of the Light Brigade; modern conflict in Armitage's Remains.
  • Love & family — Victorian courtship (E. B. Browning), the pain of a child leaving home (Armitage's Mother, any distance).

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

The anthology comparison (Section B) is worth 30 marks — budget roughly 45 minutes. Spend the first 5 minutes planning:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the theme + the command "compare how").
  • Choose your second poem — the one from your cluster that best matches the theme and gives you contrasts.
  • Jot 3 comparison points — each covering both poems (theme / language / form & structure).
  • Write a one-line comparative thesis, then write, using comparative connectives.

Half-open book: the named poem is printed, but the second is not. Learn short quotations and a couple of context points for your best comparison poems in advance.

Worked model · Section B

A comparison paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two poems (here, generic versions of Ozymandias and Storm on the Island), with method, effect, connectives and a touch of context:

Model paragraph (generic)

"In the first poem, the image of the 'colossal wreck' reduces a boastful ruler to rubble, its harsh consonants echoing his fall and mocking human power as fleeting. By contrast, the second poem's nature is a present, active threat: the personified sea that 'spits' makes the villagers powerless now, not centuries later. Both poets, however, share a Romantic sense that nature and time dwarf humanity — a fear sharpened by a post-war awareness that human control is an illusion."

Spot the moves: method + effect for each poem, comparative connectives ("by contrast", "both"), and context woven in to explain the effect — AO1, AO2 and AO3 together.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Two essays, not one: writing all of poem A, then all of poem B, with no real comparison.
  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Unbalanced: lots on the printed poem, barely anything on the second.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't serve a point.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.

Fix them all with one habit: every paragraph makes one comparative point, proves it with a short quote from each poem, and links method to effect — with a connective between them.

Going deeper

Compare tone and pattern, not just words

The strongest answers compare on four levels, so you always have something to say:

  • Theme: what idea each poem explores (power of nature, loss, longing…).
  • Language: imagery, semantic fields, connotations of key words.
  • Form & structure: sonnet vs free verse, enjambment, volta, stanza shape.
  • Tone: bitter, tender, ironic, nostalgic — and how it shifts.

Tip: a difference in tone or form is often your sharpest contrast — e.g. a tightly controlled sonnet versus a fragmented free-verse poem can mirror order versus chaos.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

The task: Section B, 30 marks — compare TWO poems from your ONE cluster (Power & Conflict OR Love & Relationships)

AO1: a comparative argument, supported by short quotes from both poems

AO2: method + terminology + evidence → effect on the reader

AO3: weave in context (Romanticism, war, Victorian love) that explains a choice

Comparison: integrate both poems with connectives — whereas, however, both, similarly

Timing: ~45 min; plan 5 min, choose your second poem well, keep it balanced
