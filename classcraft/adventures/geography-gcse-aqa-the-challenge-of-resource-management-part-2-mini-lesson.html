Mission 1

Mission 1: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Make a guess: In January 2018 Cape Town, a city of just over 4 million people, warned that its taps could be turned off in April: "Day Zero". What pushed it to the edge, and how did it pull back?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.3 global patterns of water surplus and deficit
  • 3.2.3.3 reasons for increasing water consumption (economic development, rising population)
  • 3.2.3.3 factors affecting availability (climate, geology, pollution, over-abstraction, limited infrastructure, poverty)
  • 3.2.3.3 impacts of water insecurity (disease, pollution, food production, industrial output, potential conflict)
Mission 1

Where the fresh water is

  • Only about 2.5% of Earth's water is fresh.
  • Of that fresh water, 68.7% is locked in ice and snow and 30.1% is underground.
  • Rivers hold about 0.006% of it.
  • So the water people can easily reach is a tiny, unevenly spread share.
Mission 1

Surplus and deficit

  • Water surplus: supply is greater than demand. Water deficit: demand is greater than supply.
  • Water stress: demand uses up a high share of the supply. WRI counts "extremely high" stress as using at least 80% of the available supply.
  • In 2023, 25 countries, home to a quarter of the world's people, faced extremely high water stress each year.
  • The most water-stressed region is the Middle East and North Africa.
Mission 1

Two kinds of scarcity

  • Physical scarcity: more than 75% of river flows are already withdrawn for farms, industry and homes. Common in dry regions.
  • Economic scarcity: water exists, but money, skills and institutions limit access; less than 25% is withdrawn.
  • Much of sub-Saharan Africa has economic scarcity.
Quick check
?A country has large rivers, and it withdraws less than a quarter of their flow. Many villages have no pipes, wells or treatment. What is this?
Mission 1

What water is used for

  • Worldwide, agriculture takes about 71% of fresh water withdrawals; industry and domestic use (homes and services) share the rest.
  • Richer countries usually use a bigger share for industry and homes.
Mission 1

Why use keeps rising

  • Global water demand "has more than doubled since 1960".
  • Drivers: "growing populations and industries like irrigated agriculture, livestock, energy production and manufacturing".
  • Economic development: richer people eat more meat, buy more goods and use more water at home.
  • Demand is growing fastest in sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 1

What affects availability

  • Climate: low rainfall and high evaporation mean less water.
  • Geology: permeable rocks (chalk, sandstone) store groundwater; impermeable rocks make water run off quickly.
  • Pollution: dirty water cannot be used without treatment.
  • Over-abstraction: taking water faster than it is replaced.
  • Limited infrastructure and poverty: without pipes, pumps and treatment works, people cannot reach water that exists.
Quick check
?In which place is water shortage caused mainly by people, not the climate?
Mission 1

Cape Town: what pushed it to the edge

  • 2015–2017: "three consecutive years of below average rainfall", a drought with a return time of more than 100 years.
  • Climate change made such a drought about three times as likely.
  • Storage in the six largest dams fell from 100% (2014) to 71%, 60% and then 38%, measured each November.
  • Demand had risen as the city grew.
Mission 1

Cape Town: how it pulled back

  • From 1 February 2018, each person was limited to 50 litres a day.
  • Farm water was cut by up to 60%, and water prices for businesses more than doubled.
  • Daily use fell from over 1,000 million litres in early 2016 to just below 500 million litres, about half.
  • Day Zero was put off on 7 March 2018; winter rain then filled the dams to above 75%.
Mission 1

Impacts of water insecurity

  • Disease: unsafe drinking water causes about 505,000 diarrhoeal deaths a year; it can spread cholera, dysentery and typhoid.
  • Pollution: when water is short, people use dirtier sources and waste is less diluted.
  • Food: Cape Town's farm cuts cost jobs, especially for seasonal workers.
  • Industrial output: factories need water for cooling and processing, so shortages can cut output. In Cape Town, commercial and industrial users cut water use by about 20%.
  • Potential conflict: Egypt told the UN Security Council in 2025 that it is "entirely dependent on the Nile", after Ethiopia opened a large dam upstream.
Quick check
?Which is an impact of water insecurity, not a cause of it?
Mission check

?A city's reservoirs fall for three dry years in a row while its population grows. Which plan best matches how Cape Town avoided Day Zero?

A rare three-year drought met rising demand. Cape Town halved its daily use, from over 1,000 to just below 500 million litres, and winter rain in 2018 refilled the dams.

Mission 2

Mission 2: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Make a guess: The Lower Mekong's fisheries land an estimated 2.3 million tonnes of fish a year, worth about US$11 billion. Its hydropower capacity could more than double. Can one river be both a great fishery and a giant power station?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

Mission 2

Ways to increase water supply

  • Dams and reservoirs: store water from wet seasons for use in dry ones.
  • Water transfers: move water from an area of surplus to an area of shortage.
  • Diversion: send a river's water along a new course, e.g. into canals.
  • Desalination: remove salt from sea water.
  • Groundwater abstraction: pump water from aquifers.
Mission 2

Dams and reservoirs: the Mekong

  • The Mekong flows nearly 5,000 km through six countries.
  • Lower Mekong: 88 hydropower projects already operate, with over 13,257 MW of capacity.
  • 20 more are being built and 14 planned. If all are built, capacity could reach 27,302 MW.
  • Benefits: electricity, income from selling power to neighbours, and flood and drought mitigation.
Quick check
?Use the capacity figures on the last screen. By what factor could the Lower Mekong's hydropower capacity grow?
Mission 2

Who gains, who loses?

  • "Dams trap sediment" that keeps the Mekong Delta fertile.
  • Dams block fish migration, which "threatens the food security and income of millions of people".
  • About 80% of the nearly 65 million people in the lower basin depend on the river.
  • Gains go mostly to power users and governments; losses fall mostly on fishers and delta farmers downstream.
Mission 2

Water transfer: Kielder, north-east England

  • Kielder Water, Northumberland, holds 200 billion litres; the scheme was finished in 1982.
  • It was planned for "a predicted rise in demand for water" from industry and homes.
  • Water is "released into the Rivers Tyne, Derwent, Wear and Tees" to be taken out downstream.
  • It also keeps up "minimum flow levels at times of low natural rainfall".
  • Costs: reservoirs flood land, and transfer schemes are costly to build and run.
Mission 2

Diversion: the Aral Sea

  • In the 1960s the Soviet Union diverted the rivers feeding the Aral Sea to turn desert into cotton farms.
  • The Aral Sea "was once the fourth largest" lake in the world.
  • In 2014 the eastern part of its southern basin "completely disappeared".
  • Diversion can grow crops, but at a huge cost downstream.
Mission 2

Desalination: Beckton, London

  • Thames Water's plant at Beckton, east London, opened in 2010. It turns salty water from the Thames estuary into drinking water.
  • It can supply enough for about 1 million people.
  • It cost about £200 million and uses about twice as much energy as a normal treatment works, so it is meant for dry periods.
Quick check
?Water stored in a reservoir in one river basin is released into rivers in other basins so that it can be used there. Which strategy is this?
Mission check

?A country plans a large dam on a river that flows on into a neighbouring country with big river fisheries and a farmed delta. What is the most likely outcome?

Lower Mekong capacity could grow about 2.06 times, but dams trap sediment and block fish migration in a fishery worth about US$11 billion a year. One river can do both only with trade-offs.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Beijing has about a twentieth of China's average water per person. Is moving a river the answer?

Make a guess: Beijing has about a twentieth of China's average water per person. Is moving a river the answer?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.3 an example of a large-scale water transfer scheme to show its advantages and disadvantages
Mission 3

A dry north, a wet south

  • Satellites show water storage falling in North China and rising in South China.
  • Beijing has less than 100 m³ of water per person a year from its own area: 1/20 of China's average, 1/80 of the world's.
  • That is far below the 500 m³ level of "absolute scarcity".
  • To fill the gap, Beijing over-pumped groundwater: the water table fell from 15 m below ground in 2000 to 26 m in 2014.
Mission 3

What is a water transfer?

  • A water transfer moves water from an area of surplus to an area of deficit, through canals, tunnels and pipelines.
  • China's South–North Water Transfer was launched in 2002.
  • Plan: about 45 km³ a year from the Yangtze basin, through three canal and pipeline routes.
  • Two routes, central and eastern, are mapped; the western route is "still being designed".
Mission 3

The central route

  • Starts at the Danjiangkou Reservoir on the Han River, the Yangtze's largest tributary. Capacity 29 km³.
  • Planned transfer: 9.5 km³ a year, rising to 13 km³ by 2030.
  • Supplies more than 20 major cities, including Beijing and Tianjin.
  • Water reached Beijing from December 2014.
Quick check
?Why is the South–North Water Transfer a transfer scheme?
Mission 3

Advantages: water for Beijing

  • 5.2 km³ of water reached Beijing from the central route in 2015–2019.
  • How Beijing used it:

  • A more reliable supply in droughts and climate extremes.
Mission 3

Advantages: groundwater recovers

  • Beijing pumped less groundwater as piped water replaced it.
  • The water table rose by 3 m: from 25.7 m below ground (Dec 2014) to 22.7 m (Dec 2019).
  • The study found diverted water explained about 40% of the groundwater recovery in 2006–2018; less irrigation explained about 30%, wetter years about 30%.
Quick check
?Beijing's water table rose by 3 m after the central route opened. What does the research conclude?
Mission 3

Disadvantages: the source region

  • More than 300,000 people were resettled from the Danjiangkou Reservoir area.
  • To keep the water clean, farm pesticides and fertilisers are limited and polluting industries are banned, "restricting socioeconomic development" there.
  • The planned 9.5 km³ a year is about 30% of the river flow into Danjiangkou.
Mission 3

Disadvantages: downstream and cost

  • Below the dam, navigation and environmental flows on the Han River "were negatively impacted".
  • In low-flow periods, taking water out risks seawater intrusion into the Yangtze estuary.
  • Users in the north pay higher prices to cover building and running costs.
  • The diverted water has not been used for farming, because it is too expensive.

Mission check

?A dry country plans a pipeline to carry water from a dam in a wet mountain valley to its fast-growing capital. Which pair shows one likely advantage and one likely disadvantage?

Moving the Han River's water north eased Beijing's shortage and helped its groundwater recover, but the costs fell on people and rivers in the south.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

Mission 4

Why sustainable water?

  • Sustainable water supply: meets people's needs now without running down supplies or harming the environment for the future.
  • England: households use 144 litres per person per day.
  • England may need about 4 billion extra litres a day by 2050.
Mission 4

Water conservation

  • Water conservation: using less water and wasting less.
  • England's legal target: 122 litres per person per day by 2038, on the way to 110 by 2050; plus less leakage from pipes.
  • How: fixing leaks, water meters, efficient taps, showers and toilets, and a tighter standard of 110 litres for new homes.
Quick check
?Household use falls from 144 to 110 litres per person per day. What is the percentage cut?
Mission 4

Recycling and grey water

  • Grey water: lightly used water from basins, showers, baths and washing machines, reused to flush toilets or water gardens. Not for drinking.
  • Recycling: treating used water so it can be used again.
  • Rainwater harvesting: at Eddington, Cambridge, homes have two supplies: collected rainwater flushes toilets and waters gardens.
  • This cuts drinking-water use from 144 to 80 litres per person per day.
Mission 4

Groundwater management

  • Groundwater: water stored in rocks underground (aquifers).
  • England: 27% of groundwater bodies are over-abstracted.
  • North-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water over the whole region each year (2002–2008), most likely from pumping for irrigation.
  • Management: licences and meters to limit pumping, monitoring water levels, and helping rain soak back in (recharge). Cutting abstraction has returned 101 million litres a day to England's chalk streams.
Quick check
?Which action is an example of groundwater management?
Mission 4

A local scheme in Kenya

  • Makueni and Machakos counties, south-east Kenya: dryland farming areas.
  • Rain falls in short seasons, and much of it runs off.
  • The average person in Makueni uses about 20 litres of water a day (from a 2014 study, reported in a 2023 paper); compare 144 in England.
  • Kenya is a lower-middle-income country: use it as your LIC/NEE example.
Mission 4

How a sand dam works

  • A small concrete wall built across a seasonal river.
  • Floods drop sand behind the wall. In the rainy season, water fills the gaps between sand grains.
  • In the dry season, people draw water from the sand with handpumps or by digging.
  • Water held in sand evaporates less than water in an open reservoir.
Mission 4

The scheme

  • Built by local communities with the Africa Sand Dam Foundation and Sand Dams Worldwide.
  • Water is used for drinking, farming, livestock and brickmaking.
  • In 2019, sensors were fitted to 30 handpumps to record how much water people took every hour.
Quick check
?Why do communities store dry-season water in the sand rather than in an open pond?
Mission 4

Does it work?

  • People took over 20 times more water from the handpumps in the long dry season than in any other season.
  • At 21 wells, water was still being drawn at the end of at least one long dry season.
  • But not all sand dams supply water all year.
  • In a survey of 97 Kenyan sand dams, at 57% water was found in most sand samples; only 43% had active water collection; silt cut capacity by a median 25%.
Mission 4

Is the water safe?

  • Most water from sand-dam sources had faecal bacteria.
  • Pump wells were less contaminated than open scoop holes.
  • Yet 74% of communities saw the water as clean, and 72% said few or no people treat it.
  • In the dry season, 33% of samples were too salty.
Quick check
?Which conclusion best fits the evidence on Kenya's sand dams?
Mission check

?A dry farming region in another country has rivers that flow only after rain. Villages need more water in the dry season. Which plan is the most sustainable?

Sand stores rain-season water out of the sun, so less evaporates. In south-east Kenya, people took over 20 times more water from sand-dam handpumps in the long dry season; protected pumps and treatment keep it safer.

Mission 5: In 2025, solar power generation grew by about 30%. In the same year, the world used more fossil fuel than ever. How can both be true?

Make a guess: In 2025, solar power generation grew by about 30%. In the same year, the world used more fossil fuel than ever. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.4 factors affecting supply: physical, cost, technology, political

Mission 5

A century of energy

  • World energy use: about 18,000 TWh (1920) → about 172,000 TWh (2023): about 9.6 times as much.
  • World population: 1.90 billion → 8.09 billion: about 4.3 times.
  • In 1920, coal gave about 55% and traditional biomass (wood, dung) about 39% of energy.
  • In 2023, oil, gas and coal together gave about 82%.
Mission 5

Why use keeps rising

  • Rising population: more people need heat, light, food and transport.
  • Economic development (wealth): industry, cars, flights and appliances. Energy use per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Technology: new machines and devices use energy; new technology also unlocks new supplies, such as US oil from fracking.
Quick check
?From 1920 to 2023, world energy use grew about 9.6 times and population about 4.3 times. How did energy use per person change?
Mission 5

Who uses the energy?

  • Energy use per person, 2024: Qatar 214,520 kWh; USA 76,800; China 34,514; UK 28,016; world average 21,654; India 7,813.
  • Rwanda, 2023: about 530 kWh per person.
  • Pattern: highest in oil-rich Gulf states and high-income countries; lowest in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Mission 5

Who produces it? Surplus and deficit

  • Top oil producers, 2023: USA 22%, Saudi Arabia 11%, Russia 11% of world output.
  • Fossil fuel produced compared with all energy used, 2024 (our calculation):
    • Qatar about 4.1 times; Norway 3.8; Australia 3.0; Saudi Arabia 2.2; Russia 1.7: energy surplus.
    • UK about 0.34; Germany 0.08; Japan almost 0: energy deficit, so they must import.
Mission 5

Energy security

  • Energy security: "reliable, affordable access to all fuels and energy sources" (IEA).
  • A country that imports most of its energy is less secure.
  • Singapore makes about 95% of its electricity from imported natural gas.
  • Jordan imported energy equal to 95% of its supply in 2022.
  • Surplus areas are secure; deficit areas risk energy insecurity.
Quick check
?Which country is most likely to face energy insecurity?
Mission 5

What affects supply?

  • Physical: where oil, gas, coal and uranium lie; sun, wind and steep rivers for renewables.
  • Cost of exploitation: deep-sea, Arctic or remote reserves cost more to extract and transport.
  • Technology: from 2009, "hydraulic fracturing and horizontal drilling" lifted US output to a record 12.9 million barrels a day in 2023.
  • Political: conflict or sanctions. In March–May 2026, oil through the Strait of Hormuz fell to about 2.7 million barrels a day, from about 20 million.
Mission 5

Impacts of energy insecurity

  • Exploring difficult, sensitive areas: deep oceans, the Arctic, tar sands; risk of spills and habitat damage.
  • Economic costs: in early April 2026, the North Sea Dated oil price "reached an all-time high of $144 per barrel".
  • Food production: the 2026 Hormuz disruption hit "fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels", raising farm costs.
  • Industrial output: firms cut production when energy is scarce or costly.
  • Conflict: where demand exceeds supply, countries may compete for resources and routes.
Quick check
?Which is an environmental cost of energy insecurity?
Mission 5

Adding, not replacing

  • 2025: total energy supply passed 600 exajoules, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".
  • But fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and still supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • So new solar adds to fossil fuels; it does not yet replace them.
Mission check

?A country's solar output doubles in five years. Over the same years its oil imports rise. Which explanation fits best?

World energy use keeps rising with population, wealth and technology. In 2025 solar grew about 30%, but total supply grew too, and fossil fuels still gave 86%: renewables are adding to, not yet replacing, fossil fuels.

Mission 6

Mission 6: If wind is free and never runs out, why don't we get all our electricity from it?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 6

Two kinds of energy

  • Renewable: will not run out on a human timescale, because nature keeps renewing it.
  • Non-renewable: a fixed stock in the ground that is not replaced on a human timescale, so it runs out as we use it.
  • Renewable: solar, wind, hydro (HEP), tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass.
  • Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium).
Mission 6

What powers the world?

  • In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied 86% of the world's total energy.
  • Solar grew by 30% that year, and renewables were the largest source of growth.
  • So renewables are growing fast, but from a small base.
Quick check
?Which energy source is non-renewable?
Mission 6

Non-renewables: for and against

  • Coal, oil, gas: reliable on demand; supply chains exist. But burning them releases CO₂, and the stock runs out.
  • Gas is flexible: power stations can ramp up quickly to meet peaks, and it emits the least carbon of the fossil fuels.
  • Nuclear (uranium): very low carbon, steady output. But it makes radioactive waste and stations are expensive and slow to build.
Mission 6

Renewables: for and against

  • No fuel to buy and very low emissions once built.
  • Wind and solar are variable: no output on calm days or at night.
  • HEP: steady and controllable, but dams flood valleys and block fish.
  • Geothermal: steady, but only where hot rocks are near the surface (e.g. Iceland).
  • Biomass: can be stored and burnt on demand, but needs land and must be regrown to be renewable.
  • Tidal: predictable, but costly and can change estuary habitats. Wave: varies with the weather and is costly.
Quick check
?Which is the main disadvantage of solar power?
Mission 6

Why not all wind?

  • Wind is free, but it varies: on calm days turbines make little.
  • Demand does not stop, so the grid needs sources it can turn up on demand (gas, HEP, biomass), storage, or imports.
  • The UK government calls gas "flexible and reliable", easy to increase for short peaks.
  • So countries build an energy mix: wind is a big part, not the whole.
Mission check

?A small island has plenty of sunshine and plans to get all its electricity from solar panels. What is the biggest problem it must solve?

Free energy is not the same as reliable energy. Wind and solar vary, so a grid also needs storage, imports or sources it can turn up on demand: that is why countries use a mix.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Northern England sits on a vast store of shale gas. Why did England stop fracking for it?

Make a guess: Northern England sits on a vast store of shale gas. Why did England stop fracking for it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.4 an example of fossil fuel extraction: advantages and disadvantages
Mission 7

What is fracking?

  • Shale gas is natural gas (mainly methane) trapped in a tight rock called shale.
  • Gas cannot flow out of shale on its own.
  • Fracking (hydraulic fracturing) pumps water, sand and chemicals down a well to make hairline cracks; sand props them open so gas flows out.
  • It is a fossil fuel extraction method: the product is still natural gas.
Mission 7

How much gas is there?

  • The British Geological Survey estimated the gas in place in the Bowland Shale of northern England.
  • Central estimate: 1,329 trillion cubic feet, with a 50% chance the true value is higher.
  • But gas in place is not gas you can extract. The amount that can be recovered is unknown and "likely to be smaller".
  • Only test drilling can show how much is commercially recoverable.
Quick check
?Why might the 1,329 trillion cubic feet figure overstate how useful the Bowland Shale is?
Mission 7

Advantage: energy security

  • In 2000, the UK produced 112% of the gas it used; by 2024 only about 50%.
  • As North Sea output falls, the UK imports more gas.
  • In 2018, 85% of UK households used gas cookers and heaters, and imports were forecast to supply about 73% of UK gas by 2035.
  • Shale gas "could add to the diversity of our gas supplies and help to support our energy security".
Mission 7

More possible advantages

  • Lower carbon than coal: gas has "the lowest carbon emissions of all the fossil fuel".
  • Flexible: gas power can be increased quickly for peaks in demand.
  • Investment and jobs near well sites, and tax income.
  • Proven elsewhere: fracking and horizontal drilling helped the USA set a world record for crude oil output in 2023.
Quick check
?Which argument for UK fracking is about energy security?
Mission 7

Preston New Road, Lancashire

  • The company Cuadrilla fracked two wells here: Oct–Dec 2018 and 15–26 August 2019.
  • Fracking caused felt earthquakes: magnitude 1.6 in 2018 and 2.9 in August 2019.
  • Rules used a traffic-light system: at magnitude 0.5 or above, fracking had to stop and be checked.
  • Quakes below magnitude 2.0 are usually not felt at the surface. The 2019 quake was well above that.
Mission 7

Other disadvantages

  • Still a fossil fuel: burning gas releases CO₂.
  • Methane leaks: methane is a stronger greenhouse gas than CO₂, so leaks must be tightly controlled.
  • Disturbance to residents living near the site.
  • Air quality: Environment Agency monitoring 400 m away found air met legal standards, with no substantially higher pollution from the well during fracking. Not every feared impact happened.
Quick check
?Under the traffic-light rules, what had to happen after a magnitude 0.8 tremor?
Mission 7

The decision

  • On 2 November 2019 the government announced a moratorium (a halt) on fracking in England.
  • Reason: a report by the Oil and Gas Authority found it was not possible to predict the probability or size of quakes linked to fracking.
  • The government noted "the disturbance caused to residents" near Preston New Road.
  • In November 2026, a new Energy Independence Bill proposed ending new onshore oil and gas licences in England, "including licences that could be used for shale-gas fracking".
Mission 7

Weighing it up

  • For: home-grown gas; less reliance on imports; less carbon than coal; investment and jobs.
  • Against: induced earthquakes that could not be predicted; methane and CO₂; local disturbance; recoverable gas unknown.
  • Judgement: the government decided the risks to communities outweighed uncertain benefits.
  • Exam move: name the place (Preston New Road, Lancashire), give a figure for each side, then judge.
Mission check

?A country imports most of its gas. A company starts fracking near a town; tremors keep passing the safety limit, and scientists cannot predict how big the next one might be. Using the UK case, what is the most likely government response?

England halted fracking in November 2019 after a magnitude 2.9 tremor at Preston New Road, because the size and likelihood of quakes could not be predicted. The benefits (home-grown gas, fewer imports) were real but uncertain; the risks to local people were felt.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.3.4 sustainable energy: individual use and carbon footprints; conservation (designing homes, workplaces and transport for sustainability); demand reduction; technology to increase efficiency in the use of fossil fuels
  • 3.2.3.4 example of a local renewable energy scheme in an LIC or NEE
Mission 8

Your carbon footprint

  • Carbon footprint: the greenhouse gases from the goods and services a person or country consumes, wherever in the world they are emitted.
  • It includes fuel burned at home for heating and in private vehicles.
  • UK, 2023: 10 tonnes CO₂e per person, down from a peak of 16 tonnes in 2004.
Mission 8

Where the UK footprint comes from

  • Imports: more than half of the UK footprint is emitted abroad, making things the UK buys.
  • Direct household emissions (heating homes and driving cars): about 16–20% of the total.
  • 2023: direct household emissions were 122 million tonnes CO₂e; heating alone 61 million tonnes.
Quick check
?Which of these counts in the UK's carbon footprint but is not emitted in the UK?
Mission 8

Energy conservation by design

  • Homes: about a third of heat lost from an uninsulated home escapes through the walls. Cavity wall insulation should pay for itself in five years or less.
  • Sustainable design: insulation, draught-proofing, efficient glazing, heat pumps and solar panels.
  • Workplaces: efficient lighting, heating controls, working from home.
  • Transport: public transport, cycling and walking routes, electric vehicles.
Mission 8

Demand reduction and efficiency

  • Demand reduction: using less energy, e.g. lower thermostats, smart meters that show use, car-sharing, fewer flights.
  • Efficiency in using fossil fuels: getting more useful energy from each unit burned:
    • combined heat and power (CHP): a power station's waste heat warms nearby buildings;
    • combined-cycle gas turbines: hot exhaust gas makes extra electricity;
    • efficient boilers, hybrid and more fuel-efficient engines.
Quick check
?A power station pipes its waste heat to warm a nearby hospital. Which strategy is this?
Mission 8

A local scheme in northern Peru

  • The eastern slopes of the Andes in northern Peru are among the least developed parts of the country.
  • Steep land and scattered villages make the national grid hard to extend: few people had grid electricity.
  • But high rainfall feeds many streams: a large hydro resource.
  • Peru is an upper-middle-income country; use it as an NEE.
Mission 8

How micro-hydro works

  • A canal takes part of a river's flow to a small storage pond (forebay).
  • A penstock (pipe) carries the water down a steep slope to a turbine that turns a generator.
  • Power is sent to the village and stepped down to 220 V for homes.
  • Micro-hydro: turbines of about 1 to 200 kW. No large dam or reservoir.
Mission 8

The scheme

  • Practical Action (an NGO) installed 47 micro-hydro schemes, total 1,568 kW, in this region.
  • About 5,044 families (around 30,000 people) get metered electricity.
  • Most turbines are made by small Peruvian companies to Practical Action designs.
  • Tamborapa: connections doubled from 200 to 400 homes; 90% of the increase came from people returning with their businesses.
Quick check
?47 schemes have a combined capacity of 1,568 kW. What is the average capacity of one scheme?
Mission 8

What changed for people

  • Jobs and income: in a 2005 survey, 26% of families had started or expanded a business; 60% said their income had risen.
  • New businesses: bakeries, welders, furniture makers, internet cafés, a milk cooler.
  • Health: electric light replaced kerosene lamps, so less smoke indoors; health centres run vaccine fridges.
  • Money: families save £20–£60 a year on kerosene and batteries.
Mission 8

Is it sustainable?

  • Renewable: rain-fed streams, no fuel to buy.
  • Local: turbines built by small local firms, three or four a year each; metered bills help pay for upkeep.
  • Small-scale: diverts part of a stream; no reservoir floods the valley.
  • Limits: many load controllers are imported from the UK or Canada; schemes run about 80% of the time after maintenance and repairs.
Quick check
?Which feature most helps the Peru schemes keep working for decades?
Mission check

?A remote valley in another country gets reliable, metered electricity from a small local hydro scheme for the first time. Which outcome would most likely bring former residents back?

In Tamborapa, connections doubled from 200 to 400 homes, and 90% of the increase was people returning with their businesses. A small, local, renewable scheme made it possible to earn a living in the village.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fresh water is scarce and uneven: deficits come from climate (physical scarcity) or from lack of money and pipes (economic scarcity).
  • Water insecurity brings disease, lost food and industrial output, and the risk of conflict over shared rivers.

Mission 2

  • Dams and reservoirs store water and make power, but trap sediment and block fish.
  • Other ways to increase supply: transfers (Kielder), diversion (Aral Sea), desalination (Beckton).

Mission 3

  • Water transfer: surplus area → deficit area. China's central route takes Danjiangkou Reservoir water from the Han River to Beijing, Tianjin and over 20 cities.
  • Advantages: reliable supply; Beijing's water table rose 3 m (2014–19). Disadvantages: 300,000+ resettled, limits on the source region, weaker downstream flows, higher prices.

Mission 4

  • Sustainable water: conserve (England: 144 → 122 litres by 2038), reuse grey water and harvest rainwater (Eddington: 144 → 80), and manage groundwater so pumping stays below recharge.
  • Local scheme (LIC/NEE): sand dams in Makueni and Machakos, Kenya, store water in sand; handpump use rose over 20 times in the long dry season, but reliability, siltation and water quality vary.

Mission 5

  • Energy use rose about 9.6 times since 1920, driven by population, development and technology; it is very unequal per person.
  • Surplus countries produce more than they use; deficit countries import and are less energy secure.

Mission 6

  • Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are renewed by nature; non-renewable fossil fuels and uranium are fixed stocks.
  • Every source has pros and cons; wind and solar are low-carbon but variable, so they work best in a mix.

Mission 7

  • Fracking cracks shale to release natural gas. Advantages: energy security as North Sea gas declines; lower carbon than coal; jobs and investment.
  • Disadvantages: felt earthquakes (ML 2.9, Preston New Road, 2019), methane and CO₂, local disturbance. England imposed a moratorium in November 2019.

Mission 8

  • Carbon footprints count what we consume, wherever it is emitted (UK: 10 t per person in 2023). Cut them by conservation (insulation, design, transport), demand reduction, and more efficient use of fossil fuels (CHP, combined cycle).
  • Local renewable scheme (NEE): Practical Action's 47 micro-hydro schemes in northern Peru supply about 5,044 families; local manufacture and metered bills make them sustainable, though some parts are imported.
