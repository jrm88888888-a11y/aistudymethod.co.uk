The perfect tense (le passé composé)

The passé composé is French's main past tense for completed events — "I ate", "she went", "we saw". It is built from two pieces: a present-tense helper (avoir or être) plus a past participle. This lesson builds it step by step: regular and irregular participles, which verbs take être, and how the participle agrees.

Edexcel iGCSE topics. Every example is drawn from the five topic areas — je suis parti en vacances (home & abroad), j'ai passé un examen (education & employment), j'ai vu ma famille (personal life & relationships), on a recyclé le plastique (the world around us) and j'ai mangé au restaurant (social activities, fitness & health).

What the perfect tense does

One tense for completed past events

The passé composé reports an action that happened and finished. In English it usually matches "I did" or "I have done":

  • J'ai mangé une pizza. → I ate a pizza.
  • J'ai mangé une pizza. → I have eaten a pizza.
  • Elle est allée à Paris. → She went to Paris.

Why "composé"? Because it is composed of two words: a helper verb in the present, then the past participle. You never use just one word on its own — je mangé is wrong. It must be j'ai mangé.

Building block · Step 1

Regular past participles

For regular verbs, the past participle is easy — it depends only on the infinitive ending:

familyrule & example
-er verbs-er → -é · parler → parlé
-ir verbs-ir → -i · finir → fini
-re verbs-re → -u · vendre → vendu

Say it out loud: -er becomes -é (chop the r, add an accent), -ir becomes -i (just drop the r), and -re becomes -u. So manger → mangé, choisir → choisi, attendre → attendu.

The helper · Step 2

Most verbs take avoir

The great majority of verbs use avoir in the present as their helper. Put avoir first, then the participle:

parler — to speak (with avoir)
personform
j'ai parlé
tuas parlé
il / elle / ona parlé
nousavons parlé
vousavez parlé
ils / ellesont parlé

The pattern: only the helper changes for each person — the participle stays the same with avoir. So j'ai mangé, il a fait, nous avons vu. Learn the present of avoir cold (from cluster 2) and this tense is half done.

The other helper · être verbs

A special set takes être

Most verbs use avoir, but a group of movement / change-of-state verbs use être as the helper instead. A classic memory trick is DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP:

  • Devenir (became), Revenir (came back)
  • Monter (went up), Rester (stayed), Sortir (went out)
  • Venir (came), Arriver (arrived), Naître (was born), Descendre (went down), Entrer (entered), Retourner (returned), Tomber (fell), Rentrer (came home), Aller (went), Mourir (died), Partir (left)

Also all reflexives. Every reflexive verb (with me, te, se…) takes être too: je me suis levé = "I got up". So aller → je suis allé, partir → il est parti.

The être rule · agreement

With être, the participle agrees

When the helper is être, the past participle agrees with the subject — just like an adjective. Add -e for feminine, -s for plural:

aller — to go (with être)
subjectform
il (m)est allé
elle (f)est allée
ils (m pl)sont allés
elles (f pl)sont allées

Reflexives too: je me suis levé (a boy) but je me suis levée (a girl). With avoir verbs there is no subject agreement — elle a mangé stays plain. Agreement is an être-only rule.

Reflexives in the past

Getting up: je me suis levé(e)

Reflexive verbs (with me, te, se, nous, vous) always take être in the passé composé, so the participle agrees with the subject:

je + me + suis + levé(e)reflexive pronoun · être helper · agreeing participle
  • je me suis levé — I got up (a boy speaking)
  • je me suis levée — I got up (a girl speaking, extra -e)
  • elle s'est levée — she got up · ils se sont levés — they got up

Order matters: the reflexive pronoun comes before the helper (me suis, not suis me). Every "washing / getting up / going to bed" verb from cluster 3 works this way in the past.

Negatives in the past

Wrapping ne … pas around the helper

To make the passé composé negative, the ne … pas wraps around the helper, not the participle:

  • Je n'ai pas mangé. — I did not eat / have not eaten.
  • Elle n'est pas allée. — She did not go.
  • Nous n'avons pas vu le film. — We did not see the film.

Key point: the pas sits after the helper and before the participle — n'ai pas mangé, not n'ai mangé pas. The two negative words hug the little helper verb.

Putting it together

Full sentences in the past

Now combine everything — pick the right helper, form the participle, and (with être) make it agree:

  • avoir verb: Hier, j'ai regardé un film. — Yesterday I watched a film.
  • irregular participle: Nous avons pris le train. — We took the train.
  • être verb + agreement: Elles sont arrivées à huit heures. — They (f) arrived at eight.
  • reflexive: Je me suis couché tard. — I went to bed late.

Your checklist: (1) which helper — avoir or être? (2) what is the participle — regular or irregular? (3) with être, does it need -e / -s / -es? Run through these three and you will not go wrong.

Watch out

The four classic slips

  • Missing helper: je mangé → j'ai mangé. Always two words.
  • Infinitive not participle: j'ai manger → j'ai mangé. Listen: -er (say "ay") vs -é — spell the participle with the accent.
  • Wrong helper: elle a allée → elle est allée. Movement verbs take être.
  • No agreement with être: elles sont arrivé → elles sont arrivées.
Recap

Your perfect-tense toolkit

Formula: present of avoir or être + past participle = a completed past action ("I ate / have eaten").

Regular participles: -er → -é (parlé), -ir → -i (fini), -re → -u (vendu).

Key irregulars: avoir→eu, être→été, faire→fait, prendre→pris, mettre→mis, voir→vu, boire→bu, lire→lu, écrire→écrit, dire→dit, venir→venu, vouloir→voulu, pouvoir→pu, devoir→dû.

Most verbs take avoir — participle stays plain (j'ai mangé, il a fait).

DR & MRS VANDERTRAMP verbs + all reflexives take être, and the participle agrees: il est allé, elle est allée, ils sont allés, elles sont allées; je me suis levé(e).
