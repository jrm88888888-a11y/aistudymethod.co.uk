Mission 1: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Make a guess: How can you titrate a tablet that won't even dissolve in water?

Mission 1

When you can't titrate directly

  • Some samples can't go straight into a titration: an insoluble solid (CaCO3, MgO), a slow reaction, or no sharp end point.
  • Back titration: add a known excess of a standard acid. The solid reacts completely.
  • Then titrate the leftover acid with a standard alkali.
  • Acid that reacted = acid added − acid left over.
  • Indigestion remedies (hydroxides and carbonates) neutralise excess stomach acid. A back titration finds how much active ingredient a tablet contains.
Mission 1

Worked example: an indigestion tablet

  • A 1.250 g tablet (active ingredient CaCO3) is added to 50.0 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The leftover acid needs 22.40 cm³ of 0.400 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • CaCO3(s) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g). Assume nothing else in the tablet reacts with acid.
  • Acid added: 0.500 × 0.0500 = 0.02500 mol.
  • Acid left: n(NaOH) = 0.400 × 0.02240 = 0.00896 mol (HCl : NaOH = 1 : 1).
  • Acid reacted: 0.02500 − 0.00896 = 0.01604 mol.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.01604 ÷ 2 = 0.00802 mol; mass = 0.00802 × 100.1 = 0.803 g = 64.2% of the tablet.

Mission 1

Dilutions and portions

  • A solution is made up to 250.0 cm³ in a volumetric flask. A 25.0 cm³ portion is pipetted out and titrated.
  • The portion holds one tenth of the total moles. Scale back up: × 10.
  • Diluting changes concentration, not moles: c1V1 = c2V2.
  • Track moles through every step. Scale at the right moment, and say what each number is the moles of.

Mission 1

Water of crystallisation by titration

  • Washing soda is Na2CO3·xH2O. Titration finds x.
  • 2.86 g is dissolved and made up to 250.0 cm³. 25.0 cm³ portions need 20.00 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl (methyl orange).
  • Na2CO3(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → 2NaCl(aq) + H2O(l) + CO2(g).
  • n(HCl) = 2.000 × 10⁻³ mol, so n(Na2CO3) = 1.000 × 10⁻³ mol in 25.0 cm³; 0.01000 mol in 250.0 cm³.
  • Mr = 2.86 ÷ 0.01000 = 286. Water: 286 − 106.0 = 180 = 10 × 18.0, so x = 10.
Mission 1

Aspirin by titration

  • Aspirin, C9H8O4 (Mr 180.0), has one –COOH group: a weak monoprotic acid.
  • Dissolve it in ethanol. Titrate with NaOH(aq), using phenolphthalein (weak acid + strong base). Ratio 1 : 1.
  • Example: 25.0 cm³ of aspirin solution needs 15.30 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • n = 0.100 × 0.01530 = 1.53 × 10⁻³ mol; c = 1.53 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.0612 mol dm⁻³ (0.275 g of aspirin in the sample).
  • Titrate cold and quickly: warm NaOH also hydrolyses the ester group, using extra NaOH.
  • Or use that hydrolysis: warm with a measured excess of NaOH (2 mol NaOH per mol aspirin), then back titrate the leftover NaOH with standard acid.
Mission check

?An impure sample of magnesium oxide, an insoluble base, is analysed by back titration. MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l). Assume the impurities do not react with acid.
  • 0.400 g of the sample is added to 25.0 cm³ of 1.00 mol dm⁻³ HCl (excess). The leftover acid needs 14.30 cm³ of 0.500 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. What is the percentage by mass of MgO in the sample? (Aᵣ: O 16.0, Mg 24.3)

You never have to dissolve the solid in water. Dissolve it in a measured excess of acid, titrate what is left, and the difference is what the solid used.

Mission 2: How can the same chemical burn away a wart and protect a newborn baby's eyes?

Make a guess: How can the same chemical burn away a wart and protect a newborn baby's eyes?

Mission 2

Skin and pH

  • Healthy skin is slightly acidic: its surface is about pH 5.
  • Strong acids and alkalis are corrosive: they denature the proteins in skin cells.
  • Alkalis also hydrolyse fats in cell membranes (saponification), so an alkali burn keeps spreading deeper.
  • Acids coagulate surface proteins, which slows their spread. Both still need treating at once.
Mission 2

Acid or alkali on the skin

  • Dry powder (e.g. quicklime, CaO): brush it off first. With water it forms Ca(OH)2 and releases heat (see Group II).
  • Then flood with plenty of cool running water for at least 20 minutes. Remove contaminated clothing.
  • Water dilutes and washes away the acid or alkali, carries away heat, and brings the skin back towards its normal pH.
  • Do not try to neutralise it with a strong acid or alkali: neutralisation is exothermic, and the neutraliser is itself corrosive.
  • Then get medical help.
Quick check
?A builder gets a splash of dry quicklime powder, CaO, on his forearm. What should be done first?
Mission 2

Corrosive chemicals and warts

  • A wart is a small growth of thickened skin, caused by a virus (human papillomavirus).
  • Treatment: a corrosive chemical destroys the wart's skin cells, layer by layer.
  • Salicylic acid (2-hydroxybenzoic acid, the starting material for aspirin) paints are used daily for weeks. It breaks down the thick keratin layer.
  • Soak and gently file away the dead skin between applications.
  • Protect the healthy skin around the wart (e.g. with petroleum jelly or a plaster): the acid attacks healthy skin just as well.
Mission 2

A silver nitrate stick

  • A caustic pencil has a tip of solid silver nitrate, AgNO3 (often mixed with KNO3).
  • Moisten the tip and press it on the wart: concentrated Ag⁺ ions combine with the proteins in the cells and kill them.
  • The treated skin turns grey-black: Ag⁺ is reduced to silver metal, helped by light. It wears off as the skin renews.
Quick check
?Why must the skin around a wart be protected before a salicylic acid or silver nitrate treatment is applied?
Mission 2

Silver nitrate in the eye

  • Newborn babies can catch a serious eye infection from bacteria in the birth canal (ophthalmia neonatorum).
  • From 1881 (Credé), a drop of dilute silver nitrate was put in each eye at birth (later standardised at 1%). It greatly reduced blindness from this infection.
  • Dilute silver nitrate was also used to treat eye infections, for the same reason.
  • Ag⁺ ions kill bacteria: they bind to sulfur-containing (–SH) groups in bacterial proteins and enzymes.
  • Dilute, so it kills bacteria without destroying the eye. Even so, it often irritated the eye.
  • It has largely been replaced by antibiotic ointments.
Quick check
?The eye drops were a 1% (w/v) solution: 1.00 g of AgNO3 in every 100 cm³. What is the concentration of AgNO3 in mol dm⁻³? (Ar: Ag 107.9, N 14.0, O 16.0)
Mission 2

Strength decides the job

  • Concentrated Ag⁺ (solid stick): destroys tissue, so it removes warts.
  • Dilute Ag⁺ (about 1% drops): kills bacteria but does little lasting harm to the eye.
  • Same ion, same action on proteins: the concentration decides whether it kills only microbes or the cells too.
Mission check

?In the 1960s, dressings soaked in 0.5% silver nitrate solution were used on large burns, while sticks of almost pure silver nitrate are used to remove warts. Which explanation fits both uses?

One ion, two jobs. A strong dose destroys a wart; a weak one protects an eye from infection. (Light only turns used silver nitrate black: the black is silver metal. The work is done by Ag⁺.)

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Back titration: acid reacted = acid added − acid left over; then use the equation's ratio.
  • With dilutions and portions, track moles step by step and scale (× 10 for a 25 of 250 portion).

Mission 2

  • Acid or alkali on skin: brush off any solid, then flood with plenty of water. Don't neutralise with a strong acid or alkali.
  • Warts are removed by corrosive chemicals (salicylic acid, silver nitrate); protect the healthy skin around them.
  • Silver nitrate: concentrated destroys tissue (warts); dilute (about 1%) kills bacteria (newborn eye drops, now mostly replaced by antibiotics).
