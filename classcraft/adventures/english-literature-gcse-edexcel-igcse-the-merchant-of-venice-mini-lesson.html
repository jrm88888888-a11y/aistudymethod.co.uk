Mission 1

Three weddings and a happy ending. So why do many audiences not laugh?

Make a guess: Three weddings and a happy ending. So why do many audiences not laugh?

The Merchant of Venice · plot and genreScreen 1

The play in five acts

The Merchant of Venice was probably written in 1596–7 and first printed in 1600. It moves between Venice (trade and law) and Belmont, Portia's estate.

ActWhat happens
1Bassanio needs money to woo Portia. Antonio, his ships at sea, borrows 3,000 ducats for three months from Shylock. The bond's penalty is a pound of Antonio's flesh. In Belmont, Portia must marry whoever chooses the right one of three caskets.
2Morocco chooses gold and Arragon silver: both fail. Shylock's daughter Jessica elopes with Lorenzo, taking money and jewels. Bassanio sails for Belmont.
3Shylock vows revenge. Bassanio chooses lead and wins Portia. Antonio's ships are reported lost; the bond is forfeit and he is arrested.
4At the trial Portia, disguised as a young lawyer, defeats Shylock. He loses his wealth and is forced to become a Christian. Portia asks for Bassanio's ring as her fee.
5In Belmont, the wives tease their husbands about the rings, then reveal the truth. Antonio's ships are safe.
The Merchant of Venice · plot and genreScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • International GCSE Component 2 Section B (open book, unmarked prescribed edition): one 30-mark essay from a choice of two; context is AO4.
  • Essay practice is for class. These missions build what every answer needs: knowing the play, its methods and its key lines.
The Merchant of Venice · plot and genreScreen 3

Two places, four plots

  • Venice: a trading city of merchants, ships, money and law. The bond plot (Antonio and Shylock) happens here.
  • Belmont: Portia's country house of love, music and riddles. The casket plot (who wins Portia) happens here.
  • The Jessica and Lorenzo subplot runs from Venice to Belmont.
  • The ring plot begins in Venice (Act 4) and ends in Belmont (Act 5).
  • Bassanio links the worlds: he borrows in Venice to woo in Belmont.

The Merchant of Venice · plot and genreScreen 5

A comedy with a problem

  • Comedy: disguise and wit that end in marriages and order restored. The First Folio (1623) printed the play as a comedy.
  • Here three couples marry and Antonio's ships come home.
  • But the happy ending needs Shylock's defeat: in Act 4 he loses his wealth and religion. He is absent from Act 5.
  • Problem play: F. S. Boas's 1896 label for darker comedies such as Measure for Measure. Some critics apply it here.

?Three weddings and a happy ending. So why do many audiences not laugh?

The play has a comedy's shape: disguise, wit, three marriages, ships safe. But that happy ending depends on Shylock losing his wealth and being forced to convert, and he is absent from Act 5. That is why many call it a problem comedy, and why the next missions look closely at Shylock and the trial.

Mission checkScreen 8

?He demands a pound of flesh. Villain, victim, or both?

The play supports both readings. Shylock is abused, mocked and robbed, and he argues powerfully that a Jew is as human as a Christian; but he also plots a man's death and refuses mercy. The best answers weigh both, and ask how Shakespeare makes the audience feel at each moment.

Mission 3

Gold, silver or lead: why does the dullest casket hold the prize?

Make a guess: Gold, silver or lead: why does the dullest casket hold the prize?

The Merchant of Venice · Portia and the casketsScreen 1

Portia's position

  • Portia is a rich heiress, but her dead father's will decides her husband: she must marry whoever chooses the right casket.
  • She complains that "the will of a living daughter curb’d by the will of a dead father" (1.2).
  • Each suitor swears that if he fails he will never marry anyone.
  • Context: daughters were expected to obey fathers over marriage, and under English coverture a wife's property passed to her husband's control.

The Merchant of Venice · Portia and the casketsScreen 5

Does Portia help him?

  • Before he chooses, Portia says "I could teach you / How to choose right, but then I am forsworn." She claims she won't break her oath.
  • Then a song is sung. Its rhymes, "bred", "head", "nourished", all chime with "lead", and it warns that "fancy" (infatuation) "engend’red in the eyes" soon dies.
  • Some readers think Portia is dropping a hint; others say the song only matches Bassanio's own thinking. Treat it as an open question.
The Merchant of Venice · Portia and the casketsScreen 6

Portia: power and limits

  • Wit: in 1.2 she mocks every suitor in sharp prose.
  • Prejudice: when Morocco fails she says "Let all of his complexion choose me so." (2.7): relief tied to his skin colour. This is racism; many productions now make audiences notice it.
  • Submission? After Bassanio wins, she calls herself unschooled and gives him her house, servants and herself (3.2).
  • Control: disguised as a lawyer she wins the trial (4.1), then uses the ring to test her husband (Act 5).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Gold, silver or lead: why does the dullest casket hold the prize?

The caskets test what a suitor values. Gold and silver reward people who trust appearances or their own worth; lead rewards one who sees past show and will "give and hazard all". The same idea, that appearances deceive, runs through the play, from Antonio's "goodly apple rotten at the heart" to the trial.

Mission 4

Is the play antisemitic, or is it about antisemitism?

Make a guess: Is the play antisemitic, or is it about antisemitism?

The Merchant of Venice · prejudice and contextScreen 1

Jewish people and England

DateEvent
1290Edward I expels all Jews from England.
1590sOnly a small group of people of Jewish descent live in London, outwardly Christian.
1594Roderigo Lopez, the Queen's physician, of Jewish descent, is executed for allegedly plotting to poison her. Marlowe's The Jew of Malta is revived.
1596–7Shakespeare writes the play.
1656Under Cromwell, Jewish people are allowed to form communities in England again.
  • So most of the first audience had probably never knowingly met a Jewish person: they knew stage and sermon stereotypes.
The Merchant of Venice · prejudice and contextScreen 2

Prejudice in the play's world

  • Antisemitism: hostility to Jewish people because they are Jewish.
  • The stage already had Jewish villains, such as Marlowe's Barabas (c.1589).
  • The Church called lending at interest (usury) sinful. Yet London's moneylenders were Christians; none of its hidden Jewish community is known to have lent money. The Jewish usurer was a stereotype.
  • The Christians call Shylock "the Jew" far more than his name: "Which is the merchant here? And which the Jew?" (4.1).

The Merchant of Venice · prejudice and contextScreen 5

One play, many audiences

  • 1600: the first printed title page advertises Shylock's cruelty.
  • 1741: Charles Macklin plays him as a terrifying villain.
  • 1814: Edmund Kean gives him dignity and passion; in 1879 Henry Irving plays him as a dignified, wronged man.
  • 1933–39: Nazi Germany staged the play in more than 50 productions, using it to spread antisemitism.
  • Since 1945, productions usually play Shylock sympathetically and stage the Christians' abuse openly.

Readers and productions disagree. Mission 5

Shylock wins in law, then loses everything. Is that justice or mercy?

Make a guess: Shylock wins in law, then loses everything. Is that justice or mercy?

The Merchant of Venice · mercy and justiceScreen 1

The trial, step by step

StepWhat happens in Act 4 Scene 1
1The Duke asks for mercy. Shylock refuses, giving no reason but his "lodg’d hate" of Antonio. He refuses Bassanio's 6,000 ducats and sharpens his knife.
2Portia arrives, disguised as a young lawyer. She tells Shylock he must be merciful; he answers that he craves the law.
3She confirms the bond. Shylock readies the scales to weigh the flesh.
4The twist: the bond gives flesh but no blood. Shylock asks for his money instead; it is refused.
5A law against an alien who seeks a citizen's life puts his life at the Duke's mercy.
6The Duke spares his life. Antonio asks that Shylock become a Christian and leave his wealth to Jessica and Lorenzo.

The Merchant of Venice · mercy and justiceScreen 4

Justice against mercy

  • Shylock stands on justice, the letter of the law: "I stand here for law."
  • Portia argues for mercy, a Christian virtue; "We do pray for mercy" may echo the Lord's Prayer.
  • The irony: she wins not by mercy but by reading the law more strictly than Shylock: "Thou shalt have justice more than thou desir’st."
  • Forced conversion: some Elizabethan Christians might have seen it as offering salvation; most modern audiences see cruelty.

The strongest answers weigh the spared life against what is taken, and consider how 1590s and modern audiences might differ.

Mission 6

In this play, is a bond about money or about love?

Make a guess: In this play, is a bond about money or about love?

The Merchant of Venice · money, bonds and friendshipScreen 1

Money in Venice

  • Venice was a great trading city. Antonio is a merchant: his wealth is in ships (argosies) at sea.
  • Antonio lends money gratis (free); Shylock lends at interest. Christian teaching called interest, usury, a sin.
  • Yet an English Act of 1571 again let lenders charge up to 10%, so the first audience lived with lending at interest too.
  • Antonio sneers that friendship never takes "A breed for barren metal" (1.3): money should not "breed" more money.

Mission 7

Every big speech in this play tries to persuade. Which ones work?

Make a guess: Every big speech in this play tries to persuade. Which ones work?

The Merchant of Venice · rhetoric and key linesScreen 1

Verse and prose

  • Blank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter, ten syllables with five beats, mostly weak-strong: the QUAL-i-TY of MER-cy IS not STRAIN’D. It suits serious, public speech.
  • Prose has no regular rhythm: Portia and Nerissa's jokes (1.2), Launcelet's clowning.
  • Shylock moves between them: verse in most of 1.3 and in 4.1, a torrent of prose in 3.1.
  • Rhyming couplets fill the casket scrolls and close scenes.
The Merchant of Venice · rhetoric and key linesScreen 2

The Merchant of Venice · rhetoric and key linesScreen 6

Do the big speeches persuade?

  • The Duke expects "a gentle answer"; Shylock replies only that it is his "humour".
  • "Hath not a Jew eyes?" changes no Christian's mind in 3.1.
  • After the mercy speech, Shylock answers "My deeds upon my head!"
  • Bassanio begs Portia to bend the law; she refuses.
  • What wins the trial is the bond's exact wording. The speeches move the audience more than the listeners on stage.

?Explain how Shakespeare uses language to persuade in one key line.

Choose one line from this mission (for example Antonio's "The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose." or Shylock's "You take my house" lines). In 2–4 sentences, name a method, explain its effect, and say whether it persuades the listener on stage, the audience, or both.

Model answer

Antonio's line "The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose." casts Shylock as the devil, so his Bible story about Jacob seems dangerous rather than clever. The image "A goodly apple rotten at the heart" repeats the play's warning that fair outsides hide corruption. It persuades Bassanio, but the audience may notice the irony: Antonio, who has spat on Shylock, accuses him of hiding falsehood behind a good outside.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Every big speech in this play tries to persuade. Which ones work?

The play's great speeches rarely change the minds of the people they're aimed at. Shylock won't be moved by mercy, the Christians won't be moved by "Hath not a Jew eyes?", and the trial is won by the bond's exact words. The speeches work on the audience instead, which is why they are the lines worth knowing.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Two worlds: Venice (bond plot: money and law) and Belmont (casket plot: love and choice), linked by Bassanio, Jessica's elopement and the ring plot.
  • Comic shape: disguise, wit, marriages, ships restored. The "problem": the ending is built on Shylock's ruin and forced conversion, and he vanishes before Act 5.
  • The exam is open book: knowing where things happen lets you find lines quickly in your clean copy.
  • Shylock appears in five scenes (1.3, 2.5, 3.1, 3.3, 4.1) and never in Act 5.
  • "Hath not a Jew eyes?" uses rhetorical questions to argue for shared humanity, then turns that argument into a justification for revenge.
  • The play gives evidence for villain and victim: the strongest answers weigh both and track the audience's shifting response.
  • Link him to context: Christian hostility to Jews and to moneylending (see MOV4).
  • Portia is rich but bound by her father's will; the caskets test what each suitor values.
  • Gold (Morocco) and silver (Arragon) trust show and self-worth; lead (Bassanio) rewards looking past "ornament".
  • Portia is witty, prejudiced towards Morocco, conventionally modest in 3.2, and in control in Acts 4 and 5.
  • Context: fathers' authority over marriage, and coverture, help explain Portia's limits.
  • Context: Jews expelled from England in 1290; the Lopez case and The Jew of Malta revival in 1594; Christian hostility to usury. Most of the first audience knew Jewish people only through stereotypes.
  • The play uses antisemitic stereotypes and also lets Shylock expose the Christians' cruelty and hypocrisy.
  • Productions have changed: villain (Macklin, 1741), dignified victim (Kean, Irving), Nazi propaganda (1930s), mostly sympathetic since 1945.
  • Context is AO4: tie each fact to a moment in the play.
  • Shylock demands justice by the letter of the bond; Portia's mercy speech fails to move him; she then wins by an even stricter reading of the law.
  • The "mercy" Shylock receives is harsh: he loses his wealth and is forced to become a Christian.
  • "The quality of mercy" uses a simile ("as the gentle rain") and links mercy to God and kingship.
  • Venice runs on trade and credit; Christians condemned usury, though England allowed interest up to 10% from 1571.
  • Three bonds mix money and love: the loan, Antonio and Bassanio's friendship, and the marriage ring.
  • Dramatic irony in 4.1 ("Your wife would give you little thanks") sets up the ring test of Bassanio's loyalty.
  • Blank verse (iambic pentameter) for serious public speech; prose for jokes, servants and Shylock's raging 3.1; couplets for scrolls and scene endings.
  • Key methods: rhetorical questions, repetition, antithesis, lists of three, similes and metaphors. Always say what the method does.
  • The big speeches persuade the audience more than their listeners; the trial is won by exact words, not eloquence.
  • In the open-book exam, use your clean copy to find short, exact quotations quickly.

Key quotations to learn

  • In sooth I know not why I am so sad, (Act 1 Scene 1 (Antonio)) The comedy opens with an unexplained sadness.
  • Let the forfeit / Be nominated for an equal pound / Of your fair flesh (Act 1 Scene 3 (Shylock)) The "merry" bond that drives the Venice plot.
  • All that glisters is not gold, (Act 2 Scene 7 (Morocco's scroll)) The casket plot's lesson: appearances deceive.
  • Then must the Jew be merciful. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) The trial turns on mercy and justice.
  • I am not well; send the deed after me (Act 4 Scene 1 (Shylock)) Shylock's last words: the comedy's shadow.
  • I hate him for he is a Christian, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Shylock, aside)) His hatred stated to the audience from the start.
  • Hath not a Jew eyes? (Act 3 Scene 1 (Shylock)) The claim to shared humanity.
  • If you prick us, do we not bleed? (Act 3 Scene 1 (Shylock)) Rhetorical questions that can't be denied.
  • The villainy you teach me I will execute (Act 3 Scene 1 (Shylock)) He says Christians taught him revenge.
  • I would not have given it for a wilderness of monkeys. (Act 3 Scene 1 (Shylock)) Grief for Leah's ring: love, not just money.
  • so is the will of a living daughter curb’d by the will of a dead father (Act 1 Scene 2 (Portia)) Her limits: a father's will decides her marriage.
  • All that glisters is not gold, (Act 2 Scene 7 (Morocco's scroll)) Appearances deceive.
  • The world is still deceiv’d with ornament. (Act 3 Scene 2 (Bassanio)) Why he rejects gold and silver.
  • Is an unlesson’d girl, unschool’d, unpractis’d; (Act 3 Scene 2 (Portia)) Conventional modesty, soon proved ironic.
  • Let all of his complexion choose me so. (Act 2 Scene 7 (Portia)) Her racism towards Morocco.
  • For suff’rance is the badge of all our tribe. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Shylock)) Patient endurance of abuse as a shared Jewish experience.
  • And spet upon my Jewish gaberdine, (Act 1 Scene 3 (Shylock)) The Christian hero's abuse, described.
  • To spet on thee again, to spurn thee too. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Antonio)) The abuse confirmed, without shame.
  • Which is the merchant here? And which the Jew? (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) Shylock identified by religion, Antonio by trade.
  • You have among you many a purchas’d slave, (Act 4 Scene 1 (Shylock)) He exposes Christian hypocrisy.
  • The quality of mercy is not strain’d, (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) Mercy can't be forced.
  • It is an attribute to God himself; (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) Mercy as a holy, Christian virtue.
  • I stand here for law. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Shylock)) Shylock's trust in justice.
  • This bond doth give thee here no jot of blood. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) The twist that defeats him.
  • Thou shalt have justice more than thou desir’st. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) Justice turned against Shylock.
  • Gaoler, look to him. Tell not me of mercy. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Shylock)) Mercy refused before the trial.
  • I owe the most in money and in love, (Act 1 Scene 1 (Bassanio)) Money and love in one debt.
  • My purse, my person, my extremest means (Act 1 Scene 1 (Antonio)) He offers money and body.
  • Since you are dear bought, I will love you dear. (Act 3 Scene 2 (Portia)) A pun joining cost and love.
  • Your wife would give you little thanks for that (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia, disguised)) Dramatic irony that sets up the ring test.
  • I once did lend my body for his wealth, (Act 5 Scene 1 (Antonio)) The bond remembered, and offered again.
  • The devil can cite Scripture for his purpose. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Antonio)) Shylock cast as the devil; appearance and reality.
  • A goodly apple rotten at the heart. (Act 1 Scene 3 (Antonio)) Fair outside, corrupt inside.
  • I’ll have my bond, speak not against my bond. (Act 3 Scene 3 (Shylock)) Repetition: he won't be moved.
  • It blesseth him that gives and him that takes. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Portia)) Antithesis in the mercy speech.
  • you take my life / When you do take the means whereby I live. (Act 4 Scene 1 (Shylock)) Metaphor: wealth as life.
  • So shines a good deed in a naughty world. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Portia)) Light against dark at the play's end.
