Sport & Society

Sport is a mirror of the society that produces it. This mini-lesson follows that mirror from pre-industrial popular recreation, through the Industrial Revolution and rational recreation, to today's commercialised, media-driven sport — taking in the Olympics, ethics and deviance, technology and equal opportunities.

Framework · society and sport

Society shapes sport

To explain any feature of a sport, Edexcel wants you to link it back to the society that produced it. Ask what the social factors allowed or prevented:

  • Time — did people have free time to play and watch?
  • Transport — could players and crowds travel to fixtures?
  • Education and literacy — could rules be written down and shared?
  • Class, gender and money — who was allowed to take part, and who was excluded?

Use it everywhere: the same checklist explains why popular recreation was local and rough, why rational recreation could be codified, and why modern sport is global and commercial.

History · popular recreation

Pre-industrial popular recreation

The everyday sport of the pre-industrial poor was popular recreation — games such as mob football that erupted on festival and holy days.

  • Unwritten and variable — with low literacy, rules were passed on by word of mouth and differed from place to place.
  • Occasional and local — poor transport kept play within a village; there was no regular fixture list.
  • Rough, simple and rural, often with wagering, and reflecting a sharply divided class system.

Contrast: the gentry enjoyed refined, exclusive pursuits, while the masses played the boisterous mob games — sport mirrored the class structure.

History · the Industrial Revolution

The Industrial Revolution & urban sport

Migration to the factory towns first disrupted old recreations, then created the conditions for organised sport:

  • The Saturday half-day and later reforms gave workers regular free time.
  • The railways made travel to away fixtures possible, so leagues and cup competitions could form.
  • Urban populations supplied both players and paying spectators, and rising wages gave people money to spend on leisure.

Turning point: once time, transport and money aligned, sport could become regular, organised and watched by crowds — the seedbed of rational recreation.

History · rational recreation

Rational recreation & the public schools

Rational recreation was orderly, rule-bound and respectable. Two engines drove it:

  • Codification and NGBs — public schools and universities standardised rules, and new National Governing Bodies administered the games nationally.
  • Muscular Christianity — the belief that vigorous team games built moral character, health, teamwork and courage, spread through the schools and the church.

Legacy: factory and church teams gave ordinary people organised sides to play for, carrying rational recreation out of the schools and into wider society.

20th century · amateur vs professional

Amateurism, professionalism & the ethics

The nineteenth-century gentleman amateur played for the love of the game without payment — something only the wealthy could afford. This Corinthian ethic prized fair play and taking part over victory.

  • Professionalism — being paid to play — opened sport to the working class but raised concerns about winning becoming everything.
  • The Lombardian ethic is the win-at-all-costs mindset that sits opposite the amateur ideal.

Today: elite sport is openly professional, yet governing bodies still promote fair play, respect and sportsmanship as core values.

Global events · the Olympics

The Olympics & the political shop window

The modern Olympic Games were revived by Pierre de Coubertin, with the first modern Games held in Athens in 1896. His aim was that international sport could build friendship and understanding between nations.

Yet governments quickly saw the political value of mega-events:

  • Shop window — hosting or winning lets a nation display its status, wealth and organisation to a global audience.
  • Protest and boycott — the huge platform has been used for boycotts, demonstrations and statements of ideology.

Weigh it up: hosting can inspire participation and regenerate a city, but the cost is enormous and the original ideals can be overshadowed by politics and commerce.

The media & sport

The impact of the media

Each new medium has changed how sport looks and how it earns:

  • Newspapers created early fame and rivalries.
  • Television reached mass audiences; free-to-air coverage raised sport's profile enormously.
  • Pay-per-view and subscription brought major income, but put some coverage behind a paywall.
  • Social media lets athletes reach fans directly and build a personal brand.

Positive and negative: media money raises standards, wages and exposure, but it can dictate kick-off times, prompt rule changes for broadcast, and concentrate coverage on the most marketable sports and stars.

Ethics & deviance

Deviance: doping & violence

Alongside fair play sits deviance — behaviour outside sport's accepted norms:

  • Doping — using banned substances or methods to gain an unfair edge. It is dangerous to health and undermines fair competition.
  • Violence — by players (foul play, on-field aggression) and by spectators (crowd disorder and hooliganism).

Policing doping: the World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA) sets the banned list and coordinates testing worldwide, working to keep sport clean and protect athletes. Remember the difference between gamesmanship (bending the spirit of the game within the rules) and true deviance (breaking them).

Technology in sport

The role of technology

Technology shapes both the contest and the preparation for it:

  • Officiating — ball-tracking such as Hawk-Eye and the Video Assistant Referee (VAR) support accurate decisions.
  • Analysis — video analysis and GPS tracking give coaches objective data on workload, movement and tactics.

For and against: technology improves accuracy, fairness and safety, but critics say it can interrupt the flow of play, remove human drama, and advantage the best-funded performers.

Equal opportunities · barriers

Barriers to participation & how to widen it

Access to sport is uneven. Barriers cluster around four groups:

  • Gender — stereotyping, fewer role models, less media coverage of women's sport.
  • Race / ethnicity — discrimination and stacking (channelling groups into certain positions).
  • Disability — access, adapted equipment and suitable opportunities.
  • Socio-economic status — the cost of kit, membership and travel.

Widening participation: targeted funding and campaigns, visible role models, more balanced media coverage, affordable and accessible facilities, and inclusive schemes that break down stereotypes.
