Mission 1

Energy profiles

  • Energy (y) against progress of reaction (x).
  • Activation energy, Ea: from the reactants up to the top of the barrier.
  • ΔH: from reactants to products. Products lower: exothermic. Products higher: endothermic.
  • Ea is always positive, even for an exothermic reaction.
  • This is an enthalpy profile diagram.
Quick check

Mission 1

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

  • This is the Boltzmann distribution.
  • The molecules in a gas have a range of energies at any temperature.
  • y: number of molecules with a given energy. x: energy.
  • Starts at the origin: no molecule has zero energy. Rises to a peak, the most likely energy.
  • Long tail that never reaches the energy axis.
  • Area under the curve = total number of molecules. Area beyond Ea = molecules that can react.
Mission 1

Two temperatures

  • Hotter sample: peak lower and further right; the curve is broader.
  • Total area unchanged: same number of molecules.
  • Area beyond Ea is much larger: a much bigger fraction of collisions can succeed.
  • Ea does not change with temperature.
  • So a small temperature rise gives a large rate rise: near room temperature, +10 °C often roughly doubles the rate.
  • So the rate increases as temperature rises (in Module 5 you will see this as a larger rate constant, k).
Quick check
?A Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution is plotted for a sample of gas at 300 K, with number of particles on the y-axis and kinetic energy on the x-axis. The curve starts at the origin, rises to a peak and falls to a long tail that approaches the energy axis. Which describes the curve for the same sample at 400 K?
Mission 1

Catalysts on an energy profile

  • A catalyst provides an alternative route with a lower Ea. It is not used up.
  • Reactant and product energies are unchanged, so ΔH is unchanged.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts. Raw potato contains catalase, which catalyses 2H2O2 → 2H2O + O2.
Mission 1

The reverse reaction's barrier

  • The reverse reaction starts from the products: its Ea runs from the products up to the peak.
  • Read it off the profile. As a check: Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH.
  • Example: Ea(forward) = 60 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔH = −90 kJ mol⁻¹, so Ea(reverse) = 60 + 90 = 150 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea(forward) and Ea(reverse) by the same amount.
Mission 1

A catalyst on the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve

  • Same temperature, so the same curve.
  • Mark a second, lower Ea(catalysed), to the left of Ea(uncatalysed).
  • The area beyond Ea(catalysed) is larger: more molecules have enough energy.
  • The molecules have no extra energy; the barrier is lower.

Mission check

?A mixture of hydrogen and oxygen can stay unchanged at room temperature for years. In Döbereiner's lamp (1823), a jet of hydrogen in air strikes a piece of platinum and reacts at once, with no flame or spark to start it. Which explanation is correct?

The potato works the same way. Its enzyme, catalase, gives the decomposition a much lower Ea, so far more collisions succeed. In the bottle there is no catalyst.

Mission 2

What a catalyst does

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up overall.
  • It gives a different route with a lower activation energy.
  • It does react: it is used in one step and re-formed in a later one.
  • So its overall amount stays the same. One catalyst particle works again and again.
  • It does not change ΔH or the equilibrium yield.
Mission 2

Two kinds of catalyst

  • Homogeneous: in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. all in solution).
  • It reacts via an intermediate species, then is re-formed.
  • Heterogeneous: in a different phase (usually a solid with gases or liquids).
  • The reaction happens on its surface.
  • Transition metals and their compounds are good at both.
Mission 2

Catalysts at work

  • Catalysts let industry run at lower temperatures: less energy, less fossil fuel burned, less CO2.
  • That cuts costs and improves sustainability.
  • Some catalysts are toxic, though: a risk to weigh up.
  • MnO2 speeds up the decomposition of H2O2.
  • Cu²⁺ ions speed up the reaction of zinc with acids.

Mission check

?High in the atmosphere, chlorine atoms destroy ozone in two steps. Overall: O3 + O → 2O2. Which statement is correct?
  • Step 1: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2. Step 2: ClO• + O → Cl• + O2.

That's how a catalyst joins in, changes, and comes out unchanged. One chlorine atom can go around this cycle many thousands of times.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do ammonia plants squeeze their gases to about 200 times atmospheric pressure?

Make a guess: Why do ammonia plants squeeze their gases to about 200 times atmospheric pressure?

Mission 3

Le Châtelier's principle

  • Disturb a system at equilibrium and the position shifts to oppose the change (partly).
  • Position of equilibrium: the composition of the equilibrium mixture.
  • Concentration and pressure change the position, not K.
  • Temperature changes the position and K.
  • A catalyst changes neither.
Mission 3

Changing a concentration

  • N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g).
  • Add N2: the position shifts right: more NH3.
  • Remove NH3: the position shifts right, replacing some of it.
  • The shift only partly uses up the added N2.
  • K is unchanged.
  • Why: adding N2 makes the concentration ratio smaller than Kc. Net forward reaction raises it until it equals Kc again.
Quick check
?The following system is at equilibrium in a sealed container: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). More hydrogen is injected, and the temperature and volume are kept constant. Which statement about the new equilibrium mixture, compared with the original one, is correct?
Mission 3

Changing the pressure

  • Raise the pressure by reducing the volume: the position shifts to the side with fewer moles of gas.
  • N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3: 4 mol of gas → 2 mol, so it shifts right.
  • Equal moles of gas on each side: no shift.
  • Only gases count. K is unchanged.
  • Why: squeezing changes the concentration ratio away from Kc; the mixture reacts until the ratio equals the same Kc.
Quick check
?CH4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ CO(g) + 3H2(g). The pressure is increased, by reducing the volume, at constant temperature. What happens?
Mission 3

Changing the temperature

  • Raise T: the position shifts in the endothermic direction.
  • Forward exothermic (ΔH < 0): heating shifts it left; K decreases.
  • Forward endothermic (ΔH > 0): heating shifts it right; K increases.
  • Temperature is the only change that alters K.
  • Order of events: the temperature change alters K first; the mixture then shifts until its ratio fits the new K.
Quick check
?The forward reaction in the following equilibrium is exothermic: 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g). The temperature of the equilibrium mixture is increased at constant pressure. How do the amount of SO3 and the value of K at the new equilibrium compare with those at the original equilibrium?
Mission 3

Catalysts, and the summary

  • A catalyst speeds up forward and backward reactions equally.
  • Equilibrium is reached sooner: same composition, same K.

Mission 3

Seeing a shift in the lab

  • Coloured equilibria show the shift directly.
  • 2CrO4²⁻(aq) (yellow) + 2H⁺(aq) ⇌ Cr2O7²⁻(aq) (orange) + H2O(l).
  • Add acid: more H⁺, shifts right: orange. Add alkali: OH⁻ removes H⁺, shifts left: yellow.
  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ (pink) + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl4]²⁻ (blue) + 6H2O. Forward endothermic.
  • Warm it: bluer. Cool it in ice: pinker. Add concentrated HCl: bluer.
  • Method: equal volumes in test tubes; change one factor; compare with an untouched control tube.
Quick check

Mission 3

The Haber process: a compromise

  • N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), ΔH = −92 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Pressure about 200 atm: shifts right (4 mol gas → 2) and speeds it up. Higher still costs more in energy, plant and safety.
  • Temperature about 450 °C: lower T gives a higher yield (exothermic) but is too slow. 450 °C: lower yield, much faster.
  • Catalyst: iron. Faster; no change to the yield.
  • Only about 15% is converted per pass. NH3 is removed by cooling (it liquefies); unreacted N2 and H2 are recycled.
Quick check
?Percentage of NH3 in the equilibrium mixture at 200 atm (illustrative values): 350 °C, 57% · 450 °C, 36% · 550 °C, 22%. Why do ammonia plants run at about 450 °C rather than 350 °C?
Mission check

?Methanol is made from CO(g) + 2H2(g) ⇌ CH3OH(g). The forward reaction is exothermic. Plants run it at high pressure. Why does raising the pressure, by reducing the volume, increase the equilibrium yield of methanol?

High pressure shifts the position towards fewer moles of gas. Ammonia is the same story: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 goes from 4 mol of gas to 2, so about 200 atm pushes the position towards ammonia. High pressure also speeds up the reaction, but that is a separate rate effect.

Even higher pressure would raise the yield further, but costs more than it earns: about 200 atm is a compromise.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Maxwell–Boltzmann: heating spreads the curve (same area, lower peak, further right), so far more molecules have E ≥ Ea.
  • A catalyst lowers Ea by an alternative route: same curve, Ea line moves left; ΔH unchanged.
  • Ea(reverse) = Ea(forward) − ΔH.

Mission 2

  • A catalyst reacts in one step and is re-formed in a later one, by a route with lower Ea.
  • Homogeneous: same phase, via an intermediate. Heterogeneous: different phase, on the surface.

Mission 3

  • Le Châtelier: the position shifts to partly oppose a change. Only temperature changes K; a catalyst changes neither position nor K.
  • Industry balances yield, rate and cost: about 200 atm and 450 °C for ammonia.
