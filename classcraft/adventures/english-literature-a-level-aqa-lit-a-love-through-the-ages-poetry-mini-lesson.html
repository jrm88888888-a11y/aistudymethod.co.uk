Love through the ages · pre-1900 love poetryScreen 5

What stays, what changes

  • What recurs: desire, fear of time, constancy, grief. Shakespeare insists "Love’s not Time’s fool"; Rossetti still asks to be remembered.
  • What changes: the conventions (Petrarchan worship, metaphysical argument, carpe diem, Romantic ballad) and the social codes: marriage, chastity, class, the Church.
  • C. S. Lewis (The Allegory of Love, 1936) argued that medieval courtly love was a new way of feeling, marked by humility, courtesy, adultery and a religion of love. Conventions, on this view, shape feeling.
  • Others see recurring feelings in period dress. Weigh both.

?From Wyatt to Hardy: has love changed, or only how poets write it?

Both, in a sense. The anthology's poems return to the same feelings (desire, the fear of time, constancy, grief), but each age shapes those feelings through its own conventions and social codes. The strongest comparisons say what recurs and how its framing changes; C. S. Lewis's view that conventions shape the feeling itself is one position you can test.

?Donne, Keats, Tennyson on death: why do they sound centuries apart?

Each poet writes inside his period's beliefs and poetic habits. Donne argues Death down with metaphysical wit and Christian certainty; Keats's lone Romantic self faces "nothingness"; Tennyson gives a dramatic speaker the Victorian doubt about where the dead are. The movement labels came later, so use them as lenses, not boxes.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The pre-1900 anthology runs from the Tudor court (Wyatt, 1530s) to 1901: read each poem in its period and as part of a conversation across time.
  • Conventions change (Petrarchan worship, metaphysical argument, carpe diem, Romantic protest, Victorian restraint and satire); feelings such as desire, time and loss recur.
  • Section C compares a poetry and a prose text (open book); Section B compares two unseen poems. Both reward connections (AO4) and context (AO3).
  • Metaphysical (Donne): argument, conceit, paradox and dramatic address; Romantic (Keats): the feeling, solitary "I", imagination and mortality; Victorian (Tennyson): dramatic voices, elegy and doubt.
  • Contexts that explain the shift: Reformation faith and new science; revolution and the individual; geology, evolution and unsettled faith.
  • Love poems change voice across the ages too: in Section B, ask which period's habits (wit and argument, the lone Romantic "I", Victorian restraint) each unseen poem uses.

Key quotations to learn

  • Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds, (Shakespeare, Sonnet 116) Constancy defined by what love is not.
  • Time’s winged chariot hurrying near; (Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress") Time as the enemy of love: the carpe diem argument.
  • The grave’s a fine and private place, (Marvell, "To His Coy Mistress") Ironic understatement: death used to persuade.
  • Had we never loved sae kindly, (Burns, "Ae Fond Kiss") Love and loss: parting as the price of love.
  • Better by far you should forget and smile (Rossetti, "Remember") Victorian restraint: love that releases the beloved.
  • “O didn’t you know I’d been ruined?” said she. (Hardy, "The Ruined Maid") Satire of the Victorian sexual code.
  • Death, be not proud, though some have callèd thee (Donne, Holy Sonnet X) Apostrophe: the argument begins.
  • One short sleep past, we wake eternally, (Donne, Holy Sonnet X) Christian certainty: death as sleep.
  • When I have fears that I may cease to be (Keats, sonnet (1818)) The Romantic self facing mortality.
  • Till Love and Fame to nothingness do sink. (Keats, sonnet (1818)) No consolation: thought dissolves desire.
  • Ah Christ, that it were possible (Tennyson, Maud II.iv) Longing for the dead as a cry.
  • What and where they be. (Tennyson, Maud II.iv) Victorian doubt about the afterlife.
