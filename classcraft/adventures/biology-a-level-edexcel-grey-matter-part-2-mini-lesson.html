Mission 1

Mission 1: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Make a guess: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Mission 1

Adding up: summation

  • One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
  • Spatial summation: several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter at the same time → their EPSPs add.
  • Temporal summation: one presynaptic neurone fires several times in quick succession → its EPSPs add before they fade.
  • Total reaches threshold → action potential. Below threshold → none (all-or-nothing).
Mission 1

Brakes: inhibitory synapses

  • An inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g. GABA) opens channels that let Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out.
  • The inside becomes more negative than at rest: hyperpolarised.
  • This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).
  • The membrane is now further from threshold: an action potential is much less likely.
  • EPSPs and IPSPs act on the same membrane, so they sum: excitation minus inhibition.
  • Say: "an IPSP hyperpolarises the membrane" — not: "an inhibitory synapse is just a weaker excitatory one".

?A postsynaptic neurone has a resting potential of −70 mV and a threshold of −55 mV. At the same moment it receives two excitatory inputs, each producing a +8 mV change, and one inhibitory input producing a −6 mV change. What is the result?
Quick check
?One presynaptic neurone synapses with a postsynaptic neurone. A single impulse gives an EPSP below threshold. When the presynaptic neurone fires several impulses in rapid succession, the postsynaptic neurone fires an action potential. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 1

Drugs at synapses: find the step

  • Ask: which step does the drug change? Release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
  • Same shape as the neurotransmitter → binds and activates receptors → more stimulation.
  • Binds and blocks receptors → less stimulation.
  • Inhibits the breakdown enzyme or blocks reuptake → neurotransmitter stays in the cleft → stimulation lasts longer.
Quick check
?Organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase. Predict the effect at the neuromuscular junctions of a poisoned animal.
Mission 1

Brain chemicals and health

  • Dopamine is a neurotransmitter in brain areas that control movement.
  • Parkinson's disease: dopamine-secreting neurones in the substantia nigra die → too little dopamine.
  • Result: slow movement, tremor, stiff muscles.
  • Serotonin: lower serotonin activity has been linked to depression. Whether it is a main cause is still debated.
  • Knowing which chemical is out of balance guides the design of new drugs, e.g. drugs that block serotonin reuptake (SSRIs).
Mission 1

L-Dopa and MDMA

  • Dopamine cannot cross the blood–brain barrier. L-Dopa, the molecule it is made from, can.
  • In the brain, an enzyme converts L-Dopa → dopamine. More dopamine is released; symptoms ease.
  • L-Dopa treats symptoms; it does not replace lost neurones.
  • MDMA (in ecstasy) binds to serotonin reuptake transporters.
  • It blocks reuptake and makes them release serotonin into the cleft → serotonin stays high → postsynaptic neurones stimulated more.
  • Afterwards, serotonin stores are depleted.
Quick check
?Why are people with Parkinson's disease given L-Dopa rather than dopamine itself?
Mission check

?A drug binds to GABA receptors on a postsynaptic neurone. When GABA is also bound, the drug makes the receptor's chloride ion channel open more often. What is the effect on the postsynaptic neurone?

A neurone fires only when its accelerators outweigh its brakes; a drug that strengthens the brakes quietens it.

Mission 2

Receptors are transducers

  • A receptor responds to one kind of stimulus only: it is specific.
  • It is a transducer: it converts stimulus energy (pressure, light, heat) into an electrical change in a membrane.
  • If the change reaches threshold, action potentials start in a sensory neurone.
  • In the eye, the action potentials start further along the pathway, in neurones of the optic nerve.
  • Below threshold: no impulses at all.
  • Say: "the receptor converts the stimulus into an electrical signal" — not: "the receptor sends the stimulus to the brain".
Mission 2

How a rod responds to light

  • Dark: Na⁺ flows in through open cation channels; Na⁺ is pumped out. Rod is depolarised (about −40 mV).
  • Depolarised rod releases glutamate, which inhibits the bipolar neurone.
  • Light: rhodopsin absorbs it and breaks into retinal and opsin (retinal changes shape: cis → trans).
  • Opsin activates enzymes that close the cation channels → Na⁺ still pumped out → rod hyperpolarises (about −70 mV).
  • Glutamate release stops → bipolar neurone depolarises → action potentials in the optic neurone.
  • Say: "light hyperpolarises the rod" — not: "light depolarises the rod".
Quick check
?A rod cell is moved from darkness into light. What happens to the rod and its bipolar neurone?
Mission check

?In one inherited condition, a mutation in a rod protein keeps the rod's cation channels open even in light. Which prediction is correct?

Light is signalled by a rod switching off: block the switch and the message is lost.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A plant stem can grow longer, and even bend towards a window, without making new cells. How?

Make a guess: A plant stem can grow longer, and even bend towards a window, without making new cells. How?

Mission 3

Chemical messengers from the tips

  • Plants coordinate growth with chemical signals: plant hormones.
  • Made in growing regions (shoot and root tips); they move to other tissues and act there.
  • No glands, no blood: they move from cell to cell or in the vascular tissue.
  • The best known: auxin. The main natural auxin is IAA (indoleacetic acid).
  • A tropism is a directional growth response to a directional stimulus: towards it = positive; away from it = negative.
Mission 3

Auxin makes cells longer

  • IAA acts in the region just behind the tip.
  • It promotes cell elongation, not cell division.
  • IAA stimulates proton pumps: H⁺ ions are secreted into the cell wall (the apoplast).
  • The wall's pH falls. Cross-links between cellulose molecules loosen.
  • The cell takes up water by osmosis; turgor pressure stretches the loosened wall.
  • Say: "the cross-links loosen and the wall stretches" — not: "the acid dissolves the cellulose".
  • More elongation on one side than the other → the organ bends.
Quick check
?Which statement describes how auxin promotes the elongation of a plant cell?
Mission 3

Bending towards the light

  • Light from one side (lateral light) is detected at the shoot tip.
  • IAA is redistributed to the shaded side. It is not destroyed by light.
  • Shaded-side cells elongate more than lit-side cells.
  • Unequal growth bends the shoot towards the light: positive phototropism.
  • Takes hours to days: it is growth, not movement.
  • Say: "the shaded side elongates more" — not: "the lit side grows towards the light".
Quick check
?A seedling is grown in a box with a window on its left side only. After two days the shoot has curved towards the window. Which statement explains the curvature?
Mission 3

IAA switches genes on

  • IAA enters the nucleus and binds a receptor protein.
  • This leads to the breakdown of repressor proteins.
  • The repressors were blocking transcription factors.
  • Freed transcription factors bind promoters of auxin-response genes → transcription starts.
  • New proteins (e.g. proton pumps, wall-loosening proteins) → cell elongation.
  • So IAA brings about a response both by fast changes at the cell wall and by changing gene expression.
Mission check

?A thin plastic sheet is pushed down through the tip of a shoot so that it separates the half facing a lamp from the shaded half. The plastic stops any chemical crossing from one half to the other. The shoot is lit from one side for a day. It grows upwards without bending. What is the best explanation?

Bending is just unequal elongation: block the hormone gradient and the bend disappears.

Mission 4

Mission 4: With no brain or nerves, how does a plant know when to wake a seed, open a flower or ripen its fruit?

Make a guess: With no brain or nerves, how does a plant know when to wake a seed, open a flower or ripen its fruit?

Mission 4

Phytochrome: a light switch for genes

  • Phytochrome: a light-absorbing protein, in two interchangeable forms.
  • Pr absorbs red light (660 nm) → converts to Pfr.
  • Pfr absorbs far-red light (730 nm) → converts to Pr. In the dark, Pfr slowly reverts to Pr.
  • Daylight has more red than far-red: Pfr builds up. Pfr is the active form.
  • Pfr moves into the nucleus and alters the activity of transcription factors → genes switched on or off.
  • Responses: germination of light-sensitive seeds, greening of seedlings, flowering at the right time of year.

Mission check

?A short-day plant is given 14 hours of darkness each night and flowers. A second group gets the same nights, but with a 5-minute flash of red light in the middle of each night: it does not flower. A third group gets red then far-red in the middle of each night: it flowers. What explains these results?

A five-minute flash can decide whether a plant flowers: the plant reads the colour of light, not how much it gets.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Make a guess: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Mission 5

Where the main parts are

  • Cerebrum: the largest part, on top. Two cerebral hemispheres, left and right.
  • Its outer layer, the cortex, is folded grey matter.
  • Cerebellum: at the back, below the cerebrum.
  • Medulla oblongata: in the brain stem, where the brain joins the spinal cord.
  • Hypothalamus: small, at the base of the brain in the centre, just above the pituitary gland.
Mission 5

What each part does

  • Cerebral hemispheres: conscious thought, voluntary movement, interpreting the senses, language, learning and memory.
  • Cerebellum: coordinates skeletal muscle contraction: smooth, accurate movement; balance and posture.
  • Medulla oblongata: involuntary control of heart rate and breathing.
  • Hypothalamus: homeostasis, e.g. body temperature and water balance; controls the pituitary gland.
  • No part works alone: neurones link the regions, and one action draws on several, e.g. the cerebrum starts a reach and the cerebellum makes it smooth.
  • Say: "the cerebrum starts a movement; the cerebellum coordinates it" — not: "the cerebellum decides to move".
Quick check
?Which statement describes the role of the cerebellum in the control of body movements?

Mission check

?After a stroke, a patient can plan to reach for a cup and knows exactly where it is. But her arm moves jerkily and overshoots, and she sways when she stands. Which part of the brain is most likely damaged?

One movement, two regions: the cerebrum decides, the cerebellum makes it smooth. Damage one, and you see which job was lost.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • EPSPs and IPSPs sum on one membrane; an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold. IPSPs hyperpolarise.
  • Predict a drug's effect from the step it changes: release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
  • Parkinson's disease: too little dopamine, treated with L-Dopa; MDMA raises serotonin in the cleft.

Mission 2

  • Receptors are specific transducers: a stimulus opens (or closes) ion channels, changing the membrane potential; impulses start only at threshold.
  • Light breaks rhodopsin into retinal and opsin, closes cation channels and hyperpolarises the rod; glutamate release stops and the optic neurone fires.

Mission 3

  • Plant hormones are made in growing regions and move to other tissues; auxin (IAA) promotes cell elongation, and unequal elongation bends an organ.
  • Phototropism: auxin moves to the shaded side; shaded cells elongate more; the shoot bends towards light.

Mission 4

  • Red light converts phytochrome to its far-red-absorbing form; far-red or long darkness reverses it.

Mission 5

  • Cerebral hemispheres (conscious thought, voluntary movement, memory), cerebellum (coordination, balance), medulla (heart rate, breathing), hypothalamus (homeostasis, pituitary).
  • CT and MRI show structure; fMRI and PET show activity.
