Mission 1

Ethics from duty

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (1785).
  • Deontological: from Greek deon, "duty". Right and wrong lie in the act and its motive, not in the consequences.
  • Absolutist: a moral duty holds in every case, for every rational being, whatever the outcome.
  • Morality comes from reason, not from feelings, custom or results. Any rational being can work out what is right.
  • Autonomy: the rational will gives itself the moral law. Obeying a rule only because someone (even God) commands it is heteronomy.
Mission 1

Kant's world

  • Pietist Lutheran upbringing in Königsberg, Prussia: religion of inner disposition and moral seriousness, not outward show.
  • The Enlightenment: "Have courage to use your own understanding" was its motto, he wrote in 1784 (What is Enlightenment?, paraphrase). Ethics must rest on reason, not on authority.
  • Newton's science: nature runs by universal laws. Kant looks for the moral law that binds all rational beings in the same way.
  • Rousseau: Kant credited him with teaching him to honour ordinary human beings: the root of human dignity in his ethics.
  • Frederick the Great's Prussia (to 1786): a state run on duty and law, with wide freedom of debate. Under his successor, Kant was censored for writing on religion (1794).
Mission 1

The good will and duty

  • "Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will." (Groundwork I, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Talents, courage and even happiness can be used badly. Only a will set on doing right is good in itself.
  • A good will acts from duty: out of respect for the moral law.
  • Kant's shopkeeper does not overcharge a child. He acts in accordance with duty, but if he does it only because honesty is good for business, it has no moral worth.
  • An act done from inclination (because you like doing it) may be "amiable", but its moral worth comes only from the motive of duty.

Mission 1

Two kinds of command

  • A maxim is the personal rule behind an action: "When I need money, I will borrow it and promise to repay."
  • A hypothetical imperative: "If you want X, do Y." "If you want to pass, revise." It binds only those who want X.
  • So it cannot be morality: drop the desire and the command loses its force.
  • A categorical imperative: "Do Y." It commands unconditionally, whatever you want, because the act is good in itself.
  • Morality must be categorical: it binds every rational being, whatever their desires.
Mission 1Read the source

The formula of universal law

  • Anthology extract 2 (Groundwork, second section; paraphrase): act only on a maxim you could at the same time will to be a universal law, as if it were a law of nature.
  • Contradiction in conception: Kant's lying promise. If everyone made false promises when in need, no one would believe a promise; the maxim destroys itself. Result: a perfect duty (never do this).
  • Contradiction in will: a world where no one helps others can exist, but no rational being could will it, since they will one day need help. Result: an imperfect duty (do this sometimes, as you judge).

Q1 Why can the lying promise not even be thought of as a universal law?

Show answer

Promising would cease to exist, so the lie could not work.

Q2 Is the test about whether universal lying would have bad results?

Show answer

No: it is about whether the maxim contradicts itself.

Mission 1

The formula of the end in itself

  • "So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only." (Groundwork II, Abbott trans. 1873)
  • Anthology extract 2 (paraphrase): rational beings have dignity, not a price; treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means.
  • Rational beings set their own goals, so they have intrinsic worth.
  • Using a bus driver to get to school is fine: he has agreed and is paid. Lying to someone to get their money is not: they cannot agree to what they do not know.
  • Applies to yourself too: Kant thought suicide and neglect of your own talents fail this test.
  • Say: "never merely as a means" — not: "never as a means". Everyday cooperation uses people as means, with their consent.
Mission 1

The kingdom of ends

  • Third formulation (paraphrase): act as if you were a law-making member of a kingdom of ends: an ideal community where every rational being is both author and subject of the moral law.
  • Combines the first two: laws everyone could will (universal law) for beings who are all ends (end in itself).
  • Every person is a legislator: morality is autonomous, not imposed.
  • Anthology extract 2 (paraphrase): Kant calls the kingdom of ends a systematic union of rational beings under common laws: an ideal, not a description of any actual society.

Testing a maxim

Task: show how Kant's categorical imperative applies to handing in a friend's essay as your own.

  • Maxim: "When I am short of time, I will submit others' work as mine."
  • Universal law: if everyone did this, no submitted work would be taken as the author's own; the practice of assessment collapses, so the maxim cannot be conceived as law: a perfect duty not to do it.
  • End in itself: the teacher is deceived and used merely as a means to a grade.
  • Link: the act is wrong in itself, whatever the grade, whatever the risk of being caught.

Mission 2

W D Ross: prima facie duties

  • W. D. Ross (1877–1971), The Right and the Good (1930). Shares Kant's view that duty, not outcome, makes acts right.
  • But duties are many and can clash. Each is a prima facie duty ("at first sight"): binding unless a stronger duty overrides it.
  • Ross's list: fidelity (keep promises, tell the truth) · reparation · gratitude · justice · beneficence · self-improvement · non-maleficence (do not harm).
  • In a clash, our actual duty is found by careful intuition in the situation, not by a formula.
  • Against Kant: no duty is absolute. Against utilitarianism: a promise binds even if breaking it would produce slightly more good.
Mission 2Read the source

Ross on conflicting duties

  • Ross (paraphrase, The Right and the Good, ch. 2): if I have promised to meet a friend about a trivial matter, and I come upon an accident where I can help the victims, I should break the promise.
  • The duty to keep promises does not vanish. It is outweighed, and leaves a residue: I owe my friend an explanation and apology.
  • Ross treats the considered convictions of thoughtful people as the data of ethics, as sense-perceptions are the data of science (paraphrase).

Q1 Why does Ross say I still owe my friend something?

Show answer

The prima facie duty of fidelity remains; it was only overridden.

Q2 What does Ross use, instead of Kant's universal-law test, to decide between duties?

Show answer

Intuitive judgement in the particular situation.

Mission 2

Thomas Nagel: constraints and luck

  • Thomas Nagel (b. 1937), The View from Nowhere (1986), ch. IX.
  • Agent-neutral reasons: anyone has reason to want less suffering in the world. Agent-relative reasons: restrictions on what I may do.
  • His example (paraphrase): after a crash on a lonely road you need a car to get the injured to hospital. You could get the keys only by twisting a child's arm. Most people feel you must not, though the outcome would be better.
  • Nagel's explanation: deliberately aiming at harm makes your will be guided by evil. That is why deontological constraints feel binding, even though he finds them hard to justify fully.
  • Moral luck (Mortal Questions, 1979): Kant said the good will's worth is untouched by luck; yet we judge two equally careless drivers differently if only one kills a child. Our judgements depend on luck Kant says should not matter.
Mission 2

Kant applied today

  • Human rights: every person has dignity, not a price; rights cannot be traded away for others' benefit.
  • Medical ethics: informed consent respects the patient as an end; no research on people without consent.
  • Business: deceiving customers or treating workers as mere tools fails the formula of humanity.
  • Rule-based ethics in law and professional codes: clear duties applied to all, whatever the result.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Kant's ethics a helpful method of moral decision-making?

  • Claim: Kantian ethics gives a helpful method for deciding what is right.
  • For: universal and impartial: the same rule for everyone; no special pleading.
  • For: dignity: forbids using people, even for good results; supports human rights.
  • For: motive matters: judges the will, which is within our control, not outcomes, which are not.
  • Against: conflicting duties: truth-telling vs protecting life. Kant ("On a Supposed Right to Lie", 1797) still forbade lying to the murderer at the door.
  • Against: cold reason: no moral worth for compassion or love; the friend who visits only from duty seems worse, not better.
  • Against: too abstract: the tests say what form a rule must take, but give little help in messy, fast-moving real cases.
  • Against: tailored maxims: a maxim can be described narrowly ("lie only to save a hiding friend") to pass the test.
  • Against: Ross: no duty is absolute; Nagel: moral luck shows outcomes affect judgement.
  • Replies: some Kantians (e.g. Christine Korsgaard, 1986) argue that the Groundwork tests themselves permit lying to the murderer; Kant held we have a duty to cultivate sympathetic feelings (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797).
  • Judgement criteria: Can it handle clashes? Does it fit moral experience? Does it protect the vulnerable? Is it usable in real time?
Mission 2Diversity

Kant and religious ethics

  • Close to Kant: absolute duties resemble the Decalogue; humanity as an end echoes humans made in the image of God (Genesis 1:27); Catholic natural law also holds lying always wrong (Augustine, Aquinas).
  • Close but different: the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12) resembles universalisability; Kant said it is only derived from his principle, with limitations, and cannot be a universal law (Groundwork II, footnote).
  • Far from Kant: obeying God's command because God commands it is heteronomy for Kant; many Christians root morality in God's will and grace.
  • Situation ethics (Fletcher): love, not fixed rules, decides; Fletcher would class Kant's absolutism as "legalism".
  • Kant's own religion: Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason (1793) reads religion through morality.

Mission 2Worked answer

Weighing a criticism

Issue: Is Kantian ethics too reliant on reason?

  • Point: Critics say Kant ignores sympathy, empathy and love: compassion gives no moral worth.
  • Evidence: the philanthropist whose sympathy is extinguished but who helps from duty is Kant's example of moral worth (Groundwork I).
  • Counter: Kant does not condemn feelings; he wants a motive that holds when feelings fail, and says we should cultivate sympathy.
  • Evaluate: whether this reply works depends on whether love can itself be a moral motive. Many Christians say yes (1 Corinthians 13 praises love above every other gift); Kant says feelings cannot be commanded.

Mission 3

Character, not rules

  • Most theories ask: "What should I do?" Virtue ethics asks: "What kind of person should I be?"
  • It is agent-centred: it judges character, then acts as expressions of character.
  • A virtue (Greek aretē, "excellence") is a settled good quality of character: courage, honesty, generosity.
  • A good person reliably does good acts, for the right reasons, and wants to.
  • Source: Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics.
Mission 3

Aristotle's world

  • Student at Plato's Academy in Athens for about 20 years; tutor to Alexander; founded the Lyceum (335 BCE).
  • Greek culture prized aretē: from Homer's heroes to the citizen of the polis (city-state).
  • For Aristotle, ethics is part of politics: we flourish only in a community, and good laws train good habits.
  • Teleology: everything has a telos (purpose). A good knife cuts well; a good human lives the life proper to a human.
  • He works from experience and the views of the wise, not from Plato's abstract Form of the Good.
Mission 3

Eudaimonia and the human function

  • Every action aims at some good. The highest good is chosen for its own sake: eudaimonia.
  • Eudaimonia: flourishing, living well and doing well. Not a passing feeling of happiness.
  • Function argument (Book I): the function (ergon) of a human is what humans alone do: activity guided by reason.
  • So eudaimonia is rational activity in accordance with virtue, over a complete life (one fine day does not make a happy life).
  • It also needs friends and some external goods: health, enough wealth, a community.
Mission 3

Two kinds of virtue

  • Intellectual virtues: excellences of thinking, learned by teaching and experience.
  • The key one for ethics is phronesis (practical wisdom): seeing what to do in this situation. Others include sophia (wisdom) and technē (skill).
  • Moral virtues: excellences of character (courage, temperance, generosity, truthfulness), formed by habit.
  • Greek ēthos (character) comes from ethos (habit): Aristotle's own wordplay in Book II.
Mission 3Read the source

We become what we do

  • Anthology extract 3 (Book II; paraphrase): moral virtue comes from habit; we become just by doing just acts and brave by doing brave acts, as builders become builders by building.
  • Virtues are not in us by nature: a stone that naturally falls cannot be trained to rise. But we are able to acquire them.
  • Builders learn by building, lyre-players by playing. Character is trained the same way.
  • So upbringing makes "a great difference, or rather all the difference." (II.1, Peters trans.)

Q1 According to Aristotle, which comes first: just acts or a just character?

Show answer

Just acts, repeated until they become a settled character.

Q2 Why does the stone example matter?

Show answer

It shows virtues are not natural instincts; they must be formed.

Mission 3

The golden mean

  • A moral virtue is a mean between two vices: one of deficiency, one of excess.
  • Courage: cowardice (deficiency of confidence) · courage · rashness (excess).
  • Temperance: insensibility · temperance · self-indulgence.
  • Generosity: meanness · generosity · prodigality (wasteful giving).
  • Truthfulness about oneself: mock-modesty · truthfulness · boastfulness.
  • The mean is relative to us: a meal too little for Milo the wrestler is too much for a beginner.
  • Feeling and acting "at the right times, about the right things, towards the right people, for the right end, and in the right way" (II.6, paraphrase).
Mission 3

Where there is no mean

  • Anthology extract 3 (paraphrase): not every action or feeling has a mean; spite, shamelessness and envy, and acts such as adultery, theft and murder, are bad in themselves.
  • These are wrong in themselves: there is no right amount of theft.
  • The mean is not an average: sometimes it lies nearer one extreme (courage is nearer rashness than cowardice).
  • So it is hard to be good: anyone can get angry; being angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, is rare (II.9, paraphrase).
  • Say: "the mean is the right response, judged by reason" — not: "the mean is always a moderate amount".
Quick check

Mission 3

Practical wisdom and role models

  • Anthology extract 3 (paraphrase): virtue is a settled state of choosing the mean relative to us, fixed by reason as the person of practical wisdom would fix it.
  • The phronimos (practically wise person) sees what each situation needs. Moral virtue and phronesis need each other.
  • Virtuous role models: we learn by watching and imitating people of good character, as apprentices learn a craft.
  • Rules of thumb help, but ethics is not an exact science (Book I): it fits the case.
Mission 3

Vices and four kinds of person

  • Vices come in pairs around each virtue; we must steer away from the one we lean towards.
  • Book VII: four states of character.
  • Virtuous: wants what is right and does it gladly.
  • Continent (enkratic): wants what is wrong, but does right by self-control.
  • Incontinent (akratic): knows what is right, but desire wins.
  • Vicious: wants what is wrong and does it without regret.
  • Sign of real virtue: pleasure in good acts (Book II.3).
Quick check

Mission 3Worked answer

Explaining the mean

Task: explain Aristotle's doctrine of the mean.

  • Point: moral virtue is a settled state, a mean between a vice of deficiency and a vice of excess.
  • Evidence: courage lies between cowardice and rashness (NE II.7).
  • Develop: the mean is relative to us and found by practical wisdom; some acts (theft, adultery, murder) have no mean.
  • Link: this shows virtue ethics is about trained judgement, not mechanical rules.
  • AO1 tip: cite the anthology extract by number and paraphrase it accurately.

Mission 4

Anscombe: the revival

  • G. E. M. Anscombe (1919–2001), "Modern Moral Philosophy" (Philosophy, 1958).
  • Modern ethics uses a "moral ought" that only makes sense in a law conception of ethics: a law needs a lawgiver (God).
  • Many modern thinkers have dropped belief in a divine lawgiver but kept the language of law: the "ought" has lost its meaning.
  • Her proposal: stop doing moral philosophy until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology, and return to Aristotle's concepts: virtue, flourishing, human nature.
  • She criticised consequentialism (her word) for allowing anything if the outcome is good enough.
  • (Anscombe was a Catholic; she thought the divine-law framework true, but argued the revival on Aristotelian grounds.)
Mission 4

Philippa Foot

  • Philippa Foot (1920–2010), Virtues and Vices (1978), Natural Goodness (2001).
  • Virtues are beneficial: to their possessor and to others.
  • Virtues are correctives: courage corrects a tendency to fear; temperance corrects excessive desire.
  • Virtue engages the will, not just skill: a skilled liar is not thereby good.
  • Natural Goodness: human virtues are like the good roots of a tree or a wolf hunting with its pack: natural goodness for our kind of life.
  • Contemporary use: her work underpins naturalistic virtue ethics and applied work (e.g. her essay "Euthanasia", 1977).
Mission 4

Alasdair MacIntyre

  • Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025), After Virtue (1981).
  • Modern moral debates (abortion, war) never end because we use fragments of an older moral tradition, torn from their context.
  • Virtues are defined through practices: chess, medicine, farming, music. Each has internal goods (the excellence of the game) and external goods (money, fame).
  • A virtue is a quality that lets us achieve internal goods; it needs the narrative unity of a whole life and a living tradition.
  • His example (paraphrase): a child paid in sweets to play chess has reason to cheat; one who comes to love the game does not.
  • Hope lies in communities that sustain the virtues through a dark age.

Mission 4

Virtue ethics applied

  • Ask: what would a virtuous person do, and what would doing this make me?
  • Abortion: Rosalind Hursthouse (b. 1943, "Virtue Theory and Abortion", 1991) sets aside rights and asks whether a decision is callous, light-minded, or wise and responsible.
  • Euthanasia: compassion and justice may pull different ways; Foot (1977) argued that charity could favour some cases while justice limits them.
  • Animals: is intensive farming cruel, and what does it do to our character?
  • Professions: the virtues of a good doctor (compassion, honesty) or business leader (integrity) in codes of practice.

Mission 4

Three challenges

  • Not a practical guide: "be virtuous" does not say what to do in a dilemma. Reply: the philosopher Rosalind Hursthouse: virtue terms give rules ("do what is honest, not what is cruel"), plus role models.
  • Cultural relativism: virtues differ by culture: Homeric warriors prized pride; Christians prize humility. Reply: Aristotle and the philosopher Philippa Foot ground virtues in shared human nature and needs.
  • Virtues used for immoral acts: a brave and loyal bank robber. Reply: Aristotle: virtue includes a right end chosen by practical wisdom; Philippa Foot: courage in a wrongful act does not operate as a virtue.
  • Also: circularity (a virtuous act is what a virtuous person does, and vice versa); moral luck in upbringing; whose role model?
  • Strengths: whole person; motives and emotions count; flexible; explains moral growth and education.
Quick check

Mission 4Evaluate

Is virtue ethics useful when faced with a moral dilemma?

  • Claim: Virtue ethics gives real help when facing a moral dilemma.
  • For: it attends to the particular case; practical wisdom sees what rules miss.
  • For: role models and virtue rules ("be honest, be kind") give guidance.
  • Against: in a true dilemma two virtues clash (honesty vs kindness); "do what the virtuous person would do" may not say which wins.
  • Against: in a crisis, the person without a formed character gets little immediate help.
  • Religious compatibility: Aquinas added the theological virtues (faith, hope, love) to the classical virtues; Jesus' Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–10) praise qualities of character, not rules; the theologian Stanley Hauerwas (b. 1940) builds Christian ethics on character formed by the Church. Against: divine command theorists put God's command first.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "useful": a decision procedure, or a better decider? Is any theory more useful in real dilemmas?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Kant: only a good will is good without qualification; moral worth comes from acting from duty, not from inclination or self-interest.
  • Morality is a categorical imperative ("Do Y"), not a hypothetical one ("If you want X, do Y").
  • Formulations: universal law (contradictions in conception and will, perfect and imperfect duties); humanity as an end, never merely a means.
  • The kingdom of ends: act as a law-making member of a community of ends.

Mission 2

  • Ross: duties are prima facie and can clash; intuition finds the actual duty. Nagel: deontological constraints are agent-relative; moral luck challenges Kant.
  • Strengths: universality, dignity, motive. Weaknesses: conflicting duties, the murderer at the door, emotion, abstraction.

Mission 3

  • Virtue ethics asks what kind of person to be.
  • For Aristotle, the goal is eudaimonia, flourishing through rational activity in accordance with virtue.
  • Moral virtues are formed by habit; each is a mean between deficiency and excess, relative to us; some acts have no mean.
  • Practical wisdom (phronesis) finds the mean.
  • Virtuous, continent, incontinent and vicious characters differ in desire as well as act.

Mission 4

  • Anscombe (1958) sparked the revival.
  • Foot: virtues as beneficial correctives; MacIntyre: practices, narrative, tradition.
  • Challenges: not a practical guide; cultural relativism; virtues used for immoral acts; each has replies.
