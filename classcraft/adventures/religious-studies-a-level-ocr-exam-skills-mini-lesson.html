Mission 1: Three essays, two hours, 120 marks: what turns knowledge into a 40-mark argument?

Make a guess: Three essays, two hours, 120 marks: what turns knowledge into a 40-mark argument?

Mission 1

The paper

  • Each of your three papers: 2 hours, 120 marks, four essay questions; answer three.
  • Each essay: 40 marks, AO1 and AO2 together, marked with a levels-of-response grid.
  • About 16 marks AO1 (knowledge and understanding) and 24 marks AO2 (analysis and evaluation): AO2 is the larger share.
  • About 40 minutes per essay, including planning.
  • Questions can come from any part of the specification; two may come from the same section.
Mission 1

Question types

  • "Critically assess …": weigh strengths and weaknesses; reach a judgement.
  • "To what extent …": a judgement of degree: fully, partly, hardly.
  • "'…' Discuss.": a statement to agree with, reject or qualify.
  • "Evaluate …": judge how good, successful or convincing something is.
  • "Critically compare …": weigh two views against each other, not just describe both.
  • All of them want an argument with a judgement.
Mission 1

Choosing your three

  • Read all four questions before you choose. Underline key terms.
  • Ask: Can I state my answer in one sentence? If not, think twice.
  • Ask: Do I know the best objection to my answer?
  • Choose for argument, not for the amount you remember.
  • A familiar topic with an awkward angle ("only", "rather than") is a different question from the one you revised.

Mission 1

The thesis

  • Your thesis is your answer to the question, in one sentence, stated in the introduction.
  • E.g. "Kantian ethics is rigid in theory, but its principles give real guidance in practice, so it is not too rigid to be useful."
  • Define the key terms ("rigid", "useful") and signal your route.
  • A thesis may be qualified; it must still answer.
  • Every paragraph should be readable as a step towards it.
Mission 1

AO1 inside AO2

  • OCR marks AO1 and AO2 together: knowledge must serve the argument.
  • Explain a theory only as far as your point needs it, then use it.
  • No "first describe, then evaluate" halves: each paragraph mixes both.
  • Accurate terms, scholars and sources show AO1; what you do with them shows AO2.
Mission 1Read the source

What counts as scholarship

OCR's specification says its levels-of-response grids credit learners for using "scholarly views, academic approaches and sources of wisdom and authority" to support their arguments, and that credit is given for any appropriate ones, not only those suggested in the specification (paraphrase of the second part).

Q1 Must you use the scholars named in the specification?

Show answer

No: any appropriate scholar, approach or source is credited, though the listed ones are a safe starting point.

Q2 What must the scholarship be used for?

Show answer

To support an argument, not as decoration.

Mission 1

Sustaining a critical argument

  • Each paragraph: point (linked to thesis) → explain with a scholar or source → challenge → respond → mini-judgement.
  • Use scholars in dialogue: "Ross agrees that duty matters, but…".
  • Build: start with your strongest supporting point, meet the strongest objection mid-essay, end with the decisive criterion.
  • Keep the question's key terms in your topic sentences.

Mission 1Evaluate

An essay plan as an argument map

  • Question: "Kantian ethics is too rigid to be useful in making moral decisions." Discuss.
  • For the statement: Kant's 1797 essay holds that one may not lie even to a murderer at the door; duties can clash with no rule to settle them.
  • Against: the categorical imperative gives clear tests (universal law; never treating people merely as means); Ross (The Right and the Good, 1930) keeps duty but makes it flexible with prima facie duties (binding unless outweighed by a stronger duty).
  • Against: Kantians such as Christine Korsgaard argue that the formula of universal law can permit lying to the murderer (paraphrase).
  • Judgement criteria: What does "useful" require: clear answers, or right answers? Is rigidity a fault, or what gives morality its authority?
  • No conclusion is given here: the plan supports more than one.
Mission 1Worked answer

One 40-mark paragraph

  • Point: The strongest case for rigidity is Kant's own answer to the murderer at the door.
  • Explain: In 1797 Kant argued that one may not lie even to save a friend, because truthfulness is an unconditional duty.
  • Challenge: Korsgaard replies that a lie to a deceiver can pass the universal law test, so Kant's system need not reach Kant's verdict.
  • Respond: But then the theory's usefulness depends on interpreters correcting its author.
  • Mini-judgement: So the charge of rigidity sticks to Kant's applications more than to the categorical imperative itself.
Mission 1

Timing and conclusions

  • 5 minutes: plan (thesis, 3–4 paragraph points, the main objection, your criterion).
  • About 30 minutes: write. Keep an eye on the clock after each paragraph.
  • About 5 minutes: conclusion and a quick read.
  • Conclusion: answer the question in its own words; show which criterion decided it; no new material.
  • A finished third essay is worth more than a perfect first one: don't overrun.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Three of four 40-mark essays per paper, in 2 hours; AO1 and AO2 assessed together, AO2 the larger share.
  • Choose for argument; state a thesis; use any appropriate scholarship as evidence; meet the strongest objection.
  • Plan briefly, keep to about 40 minutes per essay, and conclude by answering the question in its own words.
