Mission 1

Enrichment: what the mix brings

  • La mixité – people of different origins living, learning and working together. (Who came and when: see Mission 4.)
  • Language: everyday French borrows from Arabic (algèbre, magasin; slang toubib – doctor, kif-kif – the same), from English (le week-end) and more.
  • Food: couscous, nems and kebabs are part of ordinary French menus.
  • Music: raï from Algeria; zouk from the Antilles; French rap mixes many origins.
  • Sport: the 1998 World Cup winners were nicknamed « black-blanc-beur »; Zinedine Zidane is the son of Algerian immigrants.
  • Paris 2024: at the Olympic opening ceremony, Aya Nakamura (born in Mali) sang with the band of the Garde républicaine.

Mission 1

Tolerance and respect: what the data say

  • The CNCDH (France's national human rights commission) measures tolerance each year: l'indice longitudinal de tolérance.
  • 2025: 64 out of 100, one of the best scores since 1990. The long-term trend is upwards.
  • But in 2025 police and gendarmerie recorded 9,737 crimes and offences with a racist, xenophobic or anti-religious motive, 5% more than in 2024.
  • The law: since 1972 (loi Pleven), inciting racial hatred, discrimination or violence is an offence.
  • Watch out: la tolérance – tolerance; tolérer – to put up with. Le respect goes further: treating others as equals.
Mission 1

Diversity: a lesson for life

  • At school: civic and moral education (l'enseignement moral et civique); a yearly week of action against racism and antisemitism around 21 March.
  • Exchanges: Erasmus+; the Franco-German Youth Office (OFAJ, created 1963).
  • Le service civique (since 2010): paid voluntary missions for 16–25-year-olds, mixing young people of all backgrounds.
  • At work: la Charte de la diversité (2004): employers sign up to promote diversity in hiring.
  • In the neighbourhood: sports clubs, associations, la Fête des voisins.
  • Model: C'est en côtoyant des gens différents qu'on apprend à dépasser ses préjugés. – It is by mixing with different people that you learn to overcome prejudice.

Mission 1

Benefits and challenges

  • Benefits often cited: new ideas and creativity, languages, links with other countries, workers in sectors short of staff.
  • Challenges often cited:
  • segregation in housing and schools: some areas concentrate poverty and recent immigrants;
  • religion in public life: laws ban conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (2004) and face coverings in public spaces (2010);
  • identity: how to be both French and attached to another culture.
  • France's model: the Republic recognises citizens, not communities (see Mission 5). Critics of this model say it ignores real differences; its defenders say it guarantees equality.
  • Watch out: le communautarisme is a term used in French debates for groups living apart; it is contested, so attribute it: ce que certains appellent le communautarisme.

Mission 2

Who are the marginalised?

  • La marginalisation / l'exclusion sociale: being pushed to the edge of society – cut off from work, housing, services or social links.
  • Groups often named:
  • people living in poverty; homeless people (les sans-abri, les SDF – sans domicile fixe);
  • some young people with no job, studies or training;
  • elderly people living alone (l'isolement);
  • people with disabilities (les personnes handicapées);
  • some immigrants and undocumented migrants (les sans-papiers);
  • Travellers (les gens du voyage); people in remote rural areas far from services.
  • Watch out: un(e) marginal(e) – someone on the margins; plural des marginaux.
Mission 2

The numbers

  • Poverty line (le seuil de pauvreté): 60% of the median income. In 2023: €1,288 a month for a single person.
  • 2023: 15.4% of the population below it – about 9.8 million people, the highest rate since the series began in 1996 (INSEE).
  • Housing (Fondation pour le logement des défavorisés, 2026 report):
  • about 350,000 people are homeless (sans domicile);
  • in all, 4.2 million have no home of their own or are badly housed (mal-logées).
  • The foundation was called the Fondation Abbé Pierre until 2025.
  • Reason for the change: in 2024, reports commissioned by Emmaüs and the foundation made public allegations of sexual assault against Abbé Pierre (1912–2007).

Mission 2

Why do people end up on the margins?

  • Triggers: losing a job, separation, illness or disability, debt, leaving care or prison, addiction.
  • Structural causes: housing costs, low-paid or insecure work, lack of qualifications, discrimination (see Mission 5).
  • The vicious circle (le cercle vicieux): no address → hard to find a job → no income → no housing.
  • Isolation makes it worse: losing friends and family support.
  • Watch out: la précarité – insecurity (of work, housing, income); un emploi précaire – an insecure job.
  • Model: Il suffit parfois d'un accident de la vie pour basculer dans l'exclusion. – Sometimes one of life's blows is enough to tip someone into exclusion.
Mission 2

Who helps?

  • The state:
  • le RSA (revenu de solidarité active, 2009): a minimum income for people with little or no income;
  • le 115: the emergency number for homeless people (le Samu social);
  • la loi DALO (2007): an enforceable right to housing;
  • free or low-cost health cover for people on low incomes.
  • Associations:
  • Emmaüs (1949, founded by the Abbé Pierre);
  • les Restos du cœur (1985, founded by the comedian Coluche): free meals;
  • le Secours populaire, le Secours catholique, ATD Quart Monde.

Mission 2

Attitudes towards the marginalised

  • Solidarity: millions give time or money; the Restos du cœur rely on volunteers (les bénévoles).
  • Suspicion: some say benefits create dependency (l'assistanat); others reply that most people want to work and that many entitled people do not even claim aid (le non-recours).
  • Not in my street: plans for shelters sometimes meet local opposition.
  • Who is responsible? Some stress personal responsibility; others stress causes beyond the person's control (housing, jobs, health).
  • The 2023 reform (loi pour le plein emploi): since 2025, RSA recipients are registered with France Travail and are expected to do 15 hours of activity a week (training, work placements…), with exceptions.

  • Les droits vont avec des devoirs. – Rights come with duties.

  • Les personnes concernées ont surtout besoin d'un logement, de soins et d'un vrai suivi. – They mainly need housing, care and real support.

Mission 2

Marginalisation seen by immigrants

  • Les quartiers prioritaires: priority neighbourhoods where poverty and unemployment are much higher than average; many residents are immigrants or their children.
  • La discrimination à l'adresse: since 2014, discriminating against someone because of where they live is illegal. Residents say an address can still close doors.
  • Les sans-papiers: undocumented migrants cannot work legally; risk of exploitation and poor housing.
  • Les chibanis: elderly North African men who came to work in the 1960s–70s; many live alone in workers' hostels (les foyers), far from their families.
  • Feeling of not belonging in either country (see Mission 5): « Ici, on me voit comme un étranger ; là-bas aussi. » (example text)

Mission 3

Crime: what are we talking about?

  • French law sorts offences (les infractions) into three levels:
  • une contravention – a minor offence (e.g. a parking fine) → le tribunal de police
  • un délit – a mid-level offence (e.g. theft, assault) → le tribunal correctionnel
  • un crime – a very serious offence (e.g. murder, rape, armed robbery) → la cour d'assises (with a jury) or, since 2023 for some crimes, la cour criminelle départementale (judges only)
  • Watch out: un crime is not the everyday English "a crime". Shoplifting is un délit.
  • General word for crime as a social problem: la délinquance, la criminalité.
  • Your turn: is stealing a car un crime or un délit?
    Show answer

    un délit : le vol de voiture is judged by le tribunal correctionnel.

Mission 3

Prisons: the numbers

  • 1 October 2026: 90,937 people held in French prisons (Ministry of Justice figures), a record.
  • One year earlier: 84,862, so a rise of about 7%.
  • There are far fewer places than prisoners: many cells hold extra people; some sleep on mattresses on the floor.
  • The Council of Europe ranks French overcrowding among the worst in Europe.
  • In 2020 the European Court of Human Rights ruled in J.M.B. c. France that conditions in several French prisons were degrading.
  • Useful phrases:
  • Les prisons sont surpeuplées. – Prisons are overcrowded.
  • Le taux d'occupation dépasse 100 %. – The occupancy rate is over 100%.
  • Les conditions de détention sont indignes. – Prison conditions are unworthy (degrading).
Mission 3

La prison : échec ou succès ? Argue both sides

  • Pour la prison (arguments its supporters make):
  • La prison protège la société contre les criminels dangereux. – Prison protects society from dangerous criminals.
  • Elle punit et dissuade : la peur de la prison empêche certains de passer à l'acte. – It punishes and deters.
  • Les victimes ont besoin que justice soit faite. – Victims need justice to be done.
  • Contre la prison (arguments its critics make):
  • La prison est l'école du crime. – Prison is a school for crime.
  • Les détenus perdent leur emploi, leur logement et leurs liens familiaux. – Prisoners lose jobs, homes and family ties.
  • La surpopulation rend la réinsertion presque impossible. – Overcrowding makes rehabilitation almost impossible.
  • Linkers for balance: d'un côté … de l'autre · certes … mais · en revanche
  • Your turn: link the two ideas with certes … mais.
    Show answer

    Certes, la prison protège la société, mais elle prépare mal la réinsertion.

Mission 3

D'autres sanctions: alternatives to prison

  • le travail d'intérêt général (TIG) – unpaid community work (created 1983).
  • le bracelet électronique – an electronic tag: the offender stays at home at set hours.
  • le sursis probatoire – a suspended sentence with conditions (treatment, training, compensating the victim).
  • une amende – a fine; le stage de citoyenneté – a citizenship course.
  • la justice restaurative – restorative justice: offender and victim (or other victims) meet, with trained staff.
  • Law of 23 March 2019 (in force March 2020): prison sentences of one month or less are banned; sentences of up to six months should in principle be served outside prison (e.g. under electronic tag).
  • Supporters: cheaper, keeps jobs and families, reduces overcrowding.
  • Critics: seen as too soft; tags and community work need staff to supervise them.
Mission 3

Two landmark choices

  • The death penalty (la peine de mort) was abolished by the law of 9 October 1981, carried by Justice Minister Robert Badinter.
  • Since 2007 the ban has been written into the Constitution.
  • Young offenders: since 30 September 2021, le Code de la justice pénale des mineurs applies.
  • Under-13s are presumed unable to understand what they did (présomption de non-discernement); the judge can decide otherwise.
  • The priority for minors is education, not punishment: l'éducatif prime sur le répressif.
  • Debate: some politicians want tougher sentences for minors; youth workers and many judges argue that education prevents reoffending better.
  • la majorité pénale – age of criminal majority (18 in France).
Mission 3

Model opinions to reuse

  • À mon avis, la prison devrait être réservée aux crimes les plus graves. – In my view, prison should be kept for the most serious offences.
  • Il est essentiel que les détenus soient préparés à leur sortie. – It is essential that prisoners are prepared for release. (il est essentiel que + subjunctive)
  • Si on investissait davantage dans la réinsertion, il y aurait moins de récidive. – If we invested more in rehabilitation, there would be less reoffending. (si + imperfect → conditional)
  • Bien que les peines alternatives coûtent moins cher, elles ne rassurent pas toujours le public. – Although alternative sentences cost less, they do not always reassure the public. (bien que + subjunctive)
  • Watch out: éventuellement = possibly, not "eventually" (finalement).

Mission 4

Who counts as an immigrant?

  • INSEE (France's statistics office): un immigré = a person born abroad as a foreigner who lives in France.
  • Once an immigrant, always counted as one, even after becoming French.
  • un étranger = a person without French nationality (some were born in France).
  • un descendant d'immigrés = born in France with at least one immigrant parent. Not an immigrant.
  • 2025 (provisional): 8.0 million immigrants = 11.6% of the population.
  • One in three (33%) has become French.
  • Born in Africa: 49%; born in Europe: 30%.
  • Top countries of birth: Algeria (12.6%), Morocco (11.7%), Portugal (7.2%), Tunisia, Italy, Turkey, Spain.
  • Watch out: un émigré = someone who left a country. Les immigrés arrivent, les émigrés partent.

Mission 4

Waves of immigration

  • 19th century: France's birth rate is low; industry needs workers. Belgians (northern mills), Italians (south-east).
  • 1920s: after the First World War's losses, Poles for the coal mines of the North; Italians; Armenians after the 1915 genocide; Russians after the revolution.
  • 1939: about half a million Spanish Republicans cross the Pyrenees (la Retirada).
  • 1945–1974, les Trente Glorieuses: reconstruction and growth. Workers from Italy, Spain, then Portugal, Algeria, Morocco, Tunisia, West Africa.
  • Many live in shanty towns (les bidonvilles, e.g. Nanterre) until the 1970s.
  • 1962: about a million rapatriés (pieds-noirs) and harkis arrive from Algeria. They are French, not counted as immigrants.
  • 1974: economic crisis; the government suspends labour immigration.
  • 1976: family reunification (le regroupement familial) allows workers' families to join them.
  • Late 1970s: refugees from Vietnam, Cambodia and Laos.
  • Since the 1990s: more varied origins; students, skilled workers, asylum seekers; Ukrainians since 2022.
Mission 4

Why people came: push and pull

  • les facteurs d'attraction – pull factors: jobs, wages, family, studies, safety, the French language.
  • les facteurs de répulsion – push factors: poverty, war, persecution, dictatorship.
  • fuir la guerre / la misère – to flee war / poverty
  • chercher du travail – to look for work
  • rejoindre sa famille – to join one's family
  • demander l'asile – to apply for asylum; un réfugié – a refugee
  • s'installer en France – to settle in France

  • Watch out: fuir is irregular: ils fuient (present), ils ont fui (perfect), ils fuyaient (imperfect).

Mission 4

Contributions: the economy

  • Immigrants rebuilt France after 1945: mines, steel, building sites, car factories (e.g. Renault at Boulogne-Billancourt).
  • Today many work in sectors short of staff: building, care for the elderly, cleaning, hotels and restaurants, farming.
  • Many hospital doctors trained abroad, often outside the EU.
  • Immigrants also set up businesses: shops, restaurants, start-ups.
  • Economists debate the net effect on public finances: most studies find it small, positive or negative depending on the method.
  • Useful phrases:
  • Les immigrés occupent souvent des emplois pénibles et mal payés. – Immigrants often do hard, badly paid jobs.
  • Ils contribuent à l'économie en travaillant, en consommant et en payant des impôts. – They contribute by working, spending and paying taxes. (en + present participle)
Mission 4

Contributions: culture, science, sport

  • Science: Marie Curie, born in Warsaw, Nobel Prizes in 1903 (physics) and 1911 (chemistry).
  • Literature: Romain Gary (born in Vilnius); Leïla Slimani (born in Rabat, prix Goncourt 2016).
  • Art: Picasso and Chagall worked in France for decades; Chagall became French in 1937.
  • Song: Yves Montand (born in Italy); Aya Nakamura (born in Bamako, Mali).
  • Sport: the 1998 World Cup team was celebrated as black-blanc-beur; Zinedine Zidane, born in Marseille to Algerian parents, is a descendant of immigrants.
  • Food and language: couscous is an everyday French dish; words such as toubib (doctor) and kif come from Arabic.
  • Since 2007 the Musée national de l'histoire de l'immigration (Palais de la Porte Dorée, Paris) tells this story.

Mission 5

Three models of living together

  • Assimilation (l'assimilation): newcomers drop their culture and adopt the host culture completely.
  • Integration (l'intégration): newcomers join a shared public life (language, laws, values) and may keep their culture in private life.
  • Multiculturalism (le multiculturalisme): the state recognises communities and their differences (e.g. the UK, Canada).
  • France's official model: republican integration. The Republic sees citizens, not communities.
  • In French debate, le communautarisme is a critical word: people living apart, by community.
  • Watch out: s'intégrer (reflexive) = to integrate oneself, fit in. Il s'est bien intégré. / La société intègre les nouveaux arrivants.
  • Your turn: "She has integrated well." → Elle s'est bien intégrée. (être + agreement)
Mission 5

The republican integration path

  • Newcomers who want to stay long-term sign the contrat d'intégration républicaine (CIR) (since 2016).
  • It includes civic training (values of the Republic: liberty, equality, fraternity, secularism) and French classes if needed.
  • Law of 26 January 2024, applied from 1 January 2026:
  • a civic exam (40 multiple-choice questions, pass mark 32) for a multi-year residence card, a 10-year card and naturalisation;
  • higher French levels: B1 for a 10-year card (carte de résident), B2 for naturalisation.
  • Supporters: language and shared values are the keys to a job and to social life.
  • Critics: harder tests may keep out people who work and pay taxes but had little schooling.
  • maîtriser la langue – to master the language; un titre de séjour – a residence permit

Mission 5

What makes integration easier or harder?

  • Easier (les facteurs qui facilitent l'intégration):
  • maîtriser le français – speaking French
  • trouver un emploi stable – a stable job
  • l'école – school, which mixes children of all origins
  • les associations, le sport, le bénévolat – clubs, sport, volunteering
  • Harder (les obstacles):
  • la barrière de la langue – the language barrier
  • la non-reconnaissance des diplômes – foreign qualifications not recognised
  • la concentration dans certains quartiers – living in concentrated, poorer areas (la ghettoïsation in critics' terms)
  • les discriminations – discrimination at work, in housing, in police checks

Mission 5

Discrimination: the law

  • Discriminer: to treat someone less well because of a protected characteristic.
  • The Penal Code (art. 225-1) lists more than 25 criteria, including origin, religion, sex, disability, address, surname.
  • Penalty: up to 3 years in prison and a €45,000 fine.
  • The Défenseur des droits (since 2011) is an independent authority that receives complaints and investigates.
  • Forms: la discrimination à l'embauche (hiring) · au logement (housing) · le contrôle au faciès (identity checks based on appearance).
  • In 2016 France's highest court (la Cour de cassation) found the State liable for discriminatory identity checks.
  • Vocabulary: un préjugé – a prejudice · un stéréotype · le racisme · subir une discrimination – to suffer discrimination · porter plainte – to make a complaint
Mission 5

The evidence: testing

  • Testing (le testing): researchers send matched fake applications that differ in one detail only, here the name.
  • Study for the Ministry of Labour (DARES), 2019–2021: 9,600 applications to 2,400 real job offers.
  • Call-back rate: 33.3% for French-sounding names, 22.8% for North African-sounding names.
  • = 31.5% fewer call-backs, with identical CVs.
  • Survey Trajectoires et Origines 2 (Ined/INSEE, 2019–2020): 37% of men who immigrated from the Maghreb said they had been unfairly refused a job in the previous five years, against 15% of men with no migration background.
  • Phrase: À CV égal, un candidat au nom maghrébin a moins de chances d'être rappelé. – With the same CV, a candidate with a North African name is less likely to be called back.

Mission 5

Ethnic statistics: should France count? Argue both sides

  • France does not, in principle, record people's ethnic or racial origin in official data.
  • In 2007 the Constitutional Council ruled that studies may not be based on ethnic or racial origin; they may use country of birth, nationality and parents' origin.
  • For ethnic statistics (arguments supporters make):
  • On ne peut pas combattre ce qu'on ne mesure pas. – You cannot fight what you do not measure.
  • D'autres pays, comme le Royaume-Uni, les utilisent. – Other countries use them.
  • Against (arguments opponents make):
  • Classer les citoyens par origine contredit l'égalité républicaine. – Sorting citizens by origin contradicts republican equality.
  • Cela risque de figer les identités. – It risks fixing identities.

Mission 5

Community life and alienation

  • Community activities (la vie associative): cultural associations, sports clubs, homework help, places of worship, neighbourhood festivals. They build links and help newcomers.
  • Alienation (l'aliénation, le sentiment d'exclusion): some descendants of immigrants say they feel ni d'ici ni de là-bas – neither from here nor from there.
  • Urban unrest has brought these issues to the front:
  • 2005: after two teenagers died in an electricity substation in Clichy-sous-Bois while fleeing a police check, riots spread for three weeks; a state of emergency was declared.
  • 2023: after the death of Nahel, 17, shot by a police officer during a traffic stop in Nanterre, riots broke out in many towns.
  • Views differ: some stress discrimination and poverty in these areas; others stress the need to restore order and the authority of the State.
  • les quartiers prioritaires – priority neighbourhoods; la politique de la ville – urban policy

  • Loin d'être un obstacle, la diversité est une richesse, à condition que chacun respecte les valeurs communes. – Far from being an obstacle, diversity is an asset, provided that everyone respects shared values. (à condition que + subjunctive)

Mission 6

What is laïcité?

  • La laïcité: the State is separate from religions and neutral towards them; every citizen is free to believe or not to believe.
  • Law of 9 December 1905 (separation of the Churches and the State):
  • Art. 1: « La République assure la liberté de conscience. Elle garantit le libre exercice des cultes… »
  • Art. 2: « La République ne reconnaît, ne salarie ni ne subventionne aucun culte. »
  • Constitution of 1958, art. 1: « La France est une République indivisible, laïque, démocratique et sociale. »
  • Three principles: neutrality of the State · freedom of conscience and worship · equality of citizens whatever their beliefs.
  • Watch out: laïque works for both genders (une école laïque, un État laïque); the masculine spelling laïc also exists (un État laïc). Un culte = a religion, a form of worship; "a cult" is une secte.
Mission 6

How laïcité was built

  • 1789: Declaration of the Rights of Man, art. 10: no one may be troubled for their opinions, « même religieuses ».
  • 1881–1882: Jules Ferry laws: state primary school becomes free, compulsory and secular; one day off a week lets families arrange religious teaching outside school.
  • 1905: separation law.
  • 1946 / 1958: laïcité written into the Constitution.
  • 1989: three girls excluded from a collège in Creil for wearing headscarves. The Conseil d'État says religious signs are not in themselves incompatible with laïcité.
  • 2004: law banning conspicuous religious signs for pupils in state schools.
  • 2010: law banning covering the face in public space.
  • 2021: law "reinforcing respect for the principles of the Republic".
  • 2023: the abaya and qamis (long robes) banned in state schools.

Mission 6

Who must be neutral?

  • The State and its agents (civil servants, state-school teachers, hospital and town-hall staff): no religious sign at work, whatever its size.
  • Users of public services (patients, parents, university students, people at the town hall): free to wear religious signs.
  • Exception: pupils in state primary and secondary schools. The 2004 law bans signs by which pupils conspicuously show a religion (headscarf, kippah, large cross). Discreet signs are allowed.
  • Why the difference? Pupils are minors and school is compulsory; adult students at university are free users.
  • Everyone, everywhere in public space: no face covering (2010 law; fine up to €150).
  • Private companies: employees are free, unless the company's rules require neutrality for a justified reason (law of 2016).
  • Watch out: the 2004 law uses ostensiblement (visibly, conspicuously), not ostentatoire (showy).
Mission 6

Religion in France today

  • The census has not asked about religion since 1872. Figures come from surveys.
  • INSEE survey (2019–2020, people aged 18–59): 51% no religion · 29% Catholic · 10% Muslim · 9% other Christian.
  • Christian heritage: 6 of 11 public holidays have Christian origins (e.g. la Toussaint, Christmas).
  • Churches built before 1905 belong to the State or the communes, which maintain them (e.g. Notre-Dame de Paris belongs to the State).
  • Private schools, mostly Catholic, can sign a contract with the State: it pays their teachers; they follow the national curriculum.
  • Alsace-Moselle was German in 1905, so the law never applied there. It keeps the 1801 Concordat: the State pays clergy of four religions (Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Jewish); state schools offer religious education.

Mission 6

Current debates

  • 2016: some seaside towns banned the burkini. The Conseil d'État suspended one ban: no proven threat to public order.
  • 2016: Christmas cribs in town halls are allowed only as a cultural or festive display, not as a religious statement (Conseil d'État).
  • 2023: the Conseil d'État upheld the ban on the abaya in state schools.
  • Sport: in 2023 the Conseil d'État upheld the French Football Federation's ban on religious signs in its matches. At the 2024 Olympics, French athletes could not wear a headscarf.
  • February 2025: the Senate voted to ban religious signs in competitions run by sports federations. In early 2026 the National Assembly had still not debated the bill.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enrichment: language (Arabic, English borrowings), food, music (raï, zouk, rap), sport (1998, Zidane), Paris 2024.
  • Tolerance: CNCDH index 64/100 in 2025 (one of the best since 1990), but 9,737 recorded racist offences (+5%); loi Pleven 1972.
  • Learning diversity: school, OFAJ, service civique (2010), Charte de la diversité (2004); challenges: segregation, religion in public life, identity.

Mission 2

  • Who: people in poverty, homeless, isolated elderly, disabled people, some young people, some immigrants and sans-papiers.
  • Numbers: 15.4% poor in 2023 (9.8 million, INSEE); about 350,000 homeless, within 4.2 million homeless or badly housed (2026).
  • Help: RSA, 115, DALO; Emmaüs, Restos du cœur; debate on RSA conditions (15 hours a week since 2025).

Mission 3

  • Key terms: un délit (most offences) vs un crime (the most serious); une peine, avec sursis, la réinsertion, la récidive.
  • Record prison population (90,937 in October 2026) and the 2019 reform favouring alternatives: TIG, electronic tag, sursis probatoire.
  • Argue both sides with certes … mais, bien que + subjunctive, si + imperfect → conditional.

Mission 4

  • Immigré (born abroad as a foreigner) ≠ étranger (not French) ≠ descendant d'immigrés (born in France).
  • 8.0 million immigrants (11.6%) in 2025; top origins Algeria, Morocco, Portugal.
  • Waves: industry (19th c.), 1920s, Trente Glorieuses, 1974 stop, 1976 family reunification. Contributions in the economy, science, arts, sport and food.

Mission 5

  • Models: assimilation, republican integration (France), multiculturalism; s'intégrer is reflexive.
  • Integration path: CIR; from 2026 a civic exam and higher French levels (B1, B2).
  • Discrimination: illegal (Penal Code art. 225-1), measured by testing (33.3% vs 22.8% call-backs) and TeO2; debate on ethnic statistics.

Mission 6

  • 1905: the State recognises, pays and subsidises no religion and guarantees freedom of worship; laïcité is in the 1958 Constitution.
  • State agents must be neutral; users are free; pupils in state schools may not wear conspicuous signs (2004); face covering is banned in public space (2010).
