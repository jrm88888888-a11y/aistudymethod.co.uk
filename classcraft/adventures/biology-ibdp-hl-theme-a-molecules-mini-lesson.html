Mission 1: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Make a guess: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Mission 1

The medium for life

  • Water is the most abundant molecule in cells: about 70% of a typical cell's mass.
  • Most reactions of life happen in aqueous solution (dissolved in water).
  • The first cells originated in water. Water is still the medium for life, on land as well as in the sea.
  • Every one of those properties starts with the shape and charges of one small molecule.
Mission 1

A polar molecule

  • Each O–H bond is covalent: a pair of electrons is shared.
  • Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does.
  • Unequal sharing makes a polar covalent bond: oxygen is δ−, each hydrogen δ+.
  • The molecule is bent (about 104.5°), so the charges do not cancel. Water is a dipole: a δ− end and a δ+ end.
  • Overall, the molecule is neutral.
  • Say: "partial charges (δ+, δ−)" — not: "water is made of ions".
Mission 1

Hydrogen bonds

  • The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of another molecule: a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. The O–H bonds within a molecule are covalent.
  • Each hydrogen bond is weak, much weaker than a covalent bond. In liquid water they constantly break and re-form.
  • One molecule can hydrogen-bond to up to four neighbours. Millions of weak bonds add up to a strong effect.
  • Draw two or more molecules: δ− by each O, δ+ by each H, a dashed line from H of one molecule to O of the next.
  • Say: "hydrogen bond between water molecules" — not: "the hydrogen bond between H and O in the molecule".

Mission 1

Why some things dissolve and others don't

  • Ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻): water's δ− oxygens face cations; its δ+ hydrogens face anions. Each ion is surrounded by water and separates from the crystal.
  • Polar molecules (e.g. glucose, with many –OH groups): form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Charged and polar substances are hydrophilic: they dissolve.
  • Non-polar molecules (e.g. oils): no charges to attract water. Water molecules stay hydrogen-bonded to each other and exclude them. They are hydrophobic.
  • Solvation: hydrogen bonds between solute and water; attractions between ions and polar water molecules.
  • Dissolving is physical: molecules stay whole; no covalent bonds break.
  • Say: "ions are attracted to the partial charges of water" — not: "ions form hydrogen bonds with water".
Mission 1

The solvent at work

  • Metabolism: reactants dissolved in the cytoplasm can move and collide. Most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution.
  • Transport: dissolved substances are carried in a moving stream of water.
  • Animals: blood plasma carries glucose, amino acids, ions and urea.
  • Plants: xylem sap carries mineral ions; phloem sap carries sucrose.
  • Some molecules work because they are insoluble: the hydrophobic tails of membrane phospholipids form a barrier.
Quick check

Mission check

Mission 2

Cohesion and surface tension

  • Cohesion: water molecules attract each other by hydrogen bonds.
  • At the surface, molecules are pulled inwards and sideways, not outwards.
  • Result: surface tension. The surface resists breaking, like a stretched film.
  • A pond skater stands on the surface without breaking it, held up by surface tension: a habitat on the water surface.
  • Say: "cohesion" for water attracted to water — not: for water attracted to anything else.
Mission 2

Pulled up the xylem

  • (1) Water evaporates from the walls of leaf cells and diffuses out of the stomata: transpiration.
  • (2) Water is drawn out of the xylem to replace it, through the cell walls. This puts the xylem water under tension.
  • (3) Cohesion keeps the column continuous: hydrogen-bonded molecules are pulled up as one unbroken unit.
  • This is the cohesion–tension mechanism.
  • Xylem vessels are dead. No ATP is used: the energy comes from the Sun evaporating water.
  • Tension = negative pressure potential in the xylem.
  • Say: "water is pulled up under tension" — not: "water is pushed up from the roots".
Mission 2

Adhesion and capillary action

  • Adhesion: water is attracted to other polar or charged substances by hydrogen bonds or charge attractions.
  • Examples: cellulose, glass, soil particles. Not wax: non-polar.
  • Adhesion + cohesion pull water along narrow spaces: capillary action.
  • Soil: water moves through narrow pores between particles towards roots.
  • Leaves: water is drawn through cellulose cell walls to replace water that evaporates.
  • Capillary action works over short distances only; it does not lift water up a tree.

Mission 2

A temperature buffer

  • Specific heat capacity: energy needed to raise 1 kg by 1 °C. Water: about 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.
  • That is high: about 4 times that of air, per kilogram.
  • Why: much of the energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds, not into making molecules move faster.
  • So water's temperature changes slowly. Lakes and seas are stable habitats.
  • Bodies are mostly water, so body temperature is buffered too: enzymes stay near their optimum.
  • Say: "temperature changes slowly / stays stable" — not: "water keeps things warm".

Mission 2

The loon and the seal

  • Black-throated loon (Gavia arctica): flies in air and dives in water.
  • Air: little buoyancy, so wings must give lift. Water: high viscosity, so a streamlined body and feet set far back to push.
  • Water's buoyancy resists diving: loons have less air-filled bones than most flying birds.
  • Ringed seal (Pusa hispida): lives in Arctic seas near 0 °C.
  • Water's buoyancy supports most of its weight. Its streamlined body cuts drag.
  • Water conducts heat away fast, so a thick layer of blubber insulates its body.

Mission 2

Where did Earth's water come from?

  • Hypothesis: much of Earth's water was delivered by water-bearing asteroids that struck the early Earth: an extraplanetary origin.
  • Earth kept its water for two reasons.
  • Gravity: strong enough to stop water vapour escaping to space.
  • Temperature: low enough for vapour to condense into liquid oceans.
  • Liquid water for billions of years gave life time to evolve.
Mission 2

Water and the search for life

  • Life as we know it needs liquid water: its solvent, its transport medium, the place its reactions happen.
  • So the search for life elsewhere starts by looking for liquid water.
  • Goldilocks zone: the range of distances from a star where surface temperatures could allow liquid water.
  • It depends on the star: closer in for a dim star, further out for a bright one.
  • Being in the zone means liquid water is possible. Water, and life, must still be detected.
Quick check

Mission check

?In a transpiring tree, an air bubble forms in one xylem vessel and fills a short length of it. Water in that vessel stops rising, although neighbouring vessels keep working. Which explanation is correct?

The cohesion that holds up the hook's insect also holds a xylem column together: evaporation pulls, and cohesion passes the pull all the way down.

Mission 3

Mission 3

Life began in water

  • The first cells originated in water.
  • Their chemicals were dissolved in it, met in it and reacted in it.
  • Every cell since has kept a watery inside.
Mission 3

Water is still the medium for life

  • Most life processes happen in aqueous solution.
  • Cytoplasm is mostly water: most metabolic reactions happen there.
  • Substances are transported dissolved in water: blood plasma, xylem sap.
  • True on land too: a tree or a bird carries the water inside its cells.
  • Say: "water is the medium in which life's reactions happen" — not: "water just fills the space between organelles".
Mission 3

Where Earth's water came from

  • Much of Earth's water is thought to have been delivered by asteroids that collided with the young Earth.
  • Earth kept it because:
    • its gravity is strong enough to hold water vapour in the atmosphere;
    • it is cool enough for water vapour to condense into liquid oceans.
  • Water over billions of years gave life time to evolve.
  • Goldilocks zone: the range of distances from a star where a planet's surface could hold liquid water.
  • So the search for extraterrestrial life looks for liquid water first: necessary, but not proof of life.
Quick check
?Much of Earth's water is thought to have arrived with asteroids. Which pair of conditions then let Earth keep it as liquid oceans?
Mission 3

Early Earth

  • Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago.
  • No free oxygen in the atmosphere.
  • No oxygen → no ozone layer → intense ultraviolet light reached the surface.
  • More carbon dioxide and methane → strong greenhouse effect → higher temperatures.
  • Volcanoes and lightning supplied energy. Carbon compounds could form spontaneously, by reactions that do not happen today.
Mission 3

The Miller–Urey experiment

  • 1953: Stanley Miller, with Harold Urey, modelled the early atmosphere in a sealed glass apparatus.
  • Gases: methane, ammonia, hydrogen, water vapour. Energy: electric sparks (lightning). Water boiled and condensed in a cycle.
  • After about a week: amino acids and other carbon compounds had formed.
  • Strengths: carbon compounds can form from inorganic molecules, with no living thing involved. Repeatable.
  • Limitations: the early atmosphere is now thought to have had more CO2 and N2, less methane and ammonia. Products were monomers, not polymers, and certainly not cells.
  • Repeats with revised gas mixtures still give amino acids.
  • Say: "it made some building blocks" — not: "it created life".
Quick check
?Geologists now think that the early atmosphere contained more carbon dioxide and nitrogen, and less methane and ammonia, than the mixture Miller and Urey used. What is the best evaluation of the experiment in the light of this?
Mission 3

From chemicals to a cell: four requirements

  • A cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life. Today, cells arise only from pre-existing cells.
  • The first cells needed four things to emerge:
    • Catalysis: molecules that speed up reactions.
    • Self-replication of molecules: a molecule that can be copied.
    • Self-assembly: structures that form on their own.
    • Compartmentalisation: a boundary that keeps the contents together.
  • Hard to test: early-Earth conditions cannot be recreated exactly, and the first protocells did not fossilise.
  • Still science: each hypothesis makes testable predictions (e.g. simulation experiments).
Mission 3

Vesicles build themselves

  • Fatty acids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic head, a hydrophobic tail.
  • In water, the tails are excluded from the water and end up together; the heads face the water.
  • They form a bilayer, which closes into a hollow sphere: a vesicle.
  • No enzymes, no cell, no energy input: self-assembly.
  • Inside a vesicle, chemistry can become different from outside: the first compartment.
Quick check
?Fatty acids added to water at a suitable pH coalesce into spherical bilayers, forming vesicles. What causes this to happen spontaneously?
Mission 3

The RNA world

  • Puzzle: today, DNA needs protein enzymes to copy it, and proteins need DNA to code for them. Which came first?
  • Proposal: RNA came first, before DNA and before protein enzymes.
  • RNA can store information in its base sequence and be copied by base pairing.
  • Some RNA molecules are catalysts: ribozymes.
  • So RNA could have been both the genes and the enzymes of the earliest cells.
  • Still seen today: in the ribosome, rRNA catalyses peptide bond formation.
Quick check
?Why is RNA, rather than DNA, presumed to have been the first genetic material?
Mission check

?In a laboratory model of early life, fatty acids are mixed in water with short RNA molecules that can catalyse the copying of RNA strands. Vesicles form, and some of them trap RNA inside. Which requirement for the first cells does the vesicle add, that the RNA on its own does not provide?

Water, carbon compounds, a self-copying catalyst and a self-made boundary: none is a cell alone, but together they meet the requirements for the first one.

Mission 4

LUCA: the last universal common ancestor

  • LUCA: the most recent population from which all living organisms descend.
  • Evidence:
    • The genetic code is (almost) universal. Codon assignments are largely arbitrary, so sharing one code points to one ancestor.
    • Some genes are shared by all organisms, e.g. for ribosomal RNA and proteins.
  • LUCA was not the first cell. Other early forms of life probably existed.
  • They left no descendants: likely out-competed by LUCA and its descendants, and extinct.
Quick check
?Codon UGG specifies tryptophan in bacteria, fungi, plants and animals alike. Why is this kind of observation taken as evidence for a common ancestor of all life?
Mission 4

How do we date the first cells?

  • Radiometric dating of rocks: long-lived isotopes (e.g. uranium-238) decay at known rates.
  • Fossils in dated rock: microfossils and stromatolites (layered mats made by bacteria).
  • A fossil gives a minimum age: life is at least that old.
  • Molecular clock: count differences in conserved gene sequences; calibrate the rate of change with dated fossils; work back to LUCA. Wide uncertainty.
  • Not carbon-14: it is useless beyond about 50 000 years.

Mission 4

Did LUCA live at a hydrothermal vent?

  • Hydrothermal vents: hot, mineral-rich water gushing from the deep seafloor. No light, no oxygen; rich in hydrogen, CO2 and iron–sulfur minerals.
  • Evidence 1: fossils. Microscopic iron-rich tubes and filaments in ancient seafloor vent precipitates (at least 3.77 billion years old), resembling vent bacteria today.
  • Evidence 2: genomes. Genes conserved across bacteria and archaea suggest LUCA was anaerobic, heat-loving, and used H2 and CO2: a vent lifestyle.
  • No DNA survives from then: the genomic evidence comes from living organisms.
Quick check
?Which pair of findings provides evidence that LUCA evolved in the vicinity of hydrothermal vents?
Mission 4

Endosymbiosis

  • Start: a single-celled ancestor that already had a nucleus and reproduced sexually.
  • (1) It took in an aerobic prokaryote by endocytosis. Not digested: it lived on inside.
  • (2) Over generations it became the mitochondrion. All eukaryotes descend from this cell.
  • (3) Later, in some lineages only, a cell that already had mitochondria took in a photosynthetic cyanobacterium: the chloroplast.
  • So plants and algae have both; animals and fungi have mitochondria only.
  • Endosymbiosis: one organism living inside another, to mutual benefit.
Mission 4

The evidence inside the organelles

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are like bacteria, not like the rest of the cell:

  • Their own circular, naked DNA.
  • Their own 70S ribosomes (the cytoplasm has 80S).
  • They divide by splitting in two; a cell cannot make new ones from scratch.
  • A double membrane: the inner one was the prokaryote's own membrane.
  • A theory is strong when it explains many observations and makes predictions that are confirmed. Endosymbiosis does both.
  • Say: "shared features show shared ancestry" — not: "they do the same job as bacteria, so they came from bacteria".
Quick check
?If mitochondria originated as free-living prokaryotes taken into a larger cell, which features would the theory of endosymbiosis predict mitochondria to have?
Mission 4

One ancestor for all eukaryotes

  • All eukaryotes descend from one unicellular ancestor with a nucleus and sexual reproduction; mitochondria were acquired after that.
  • A feature shared by every eukaryote is best explained by inheritance from that ancestor.
Mission check

Which conclusion is consistent with this evidence and with the nature of scientific theories?

Mission 5

Transpiration: the price of carbon dioxide

  • Stomata open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis.
  • Water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
  • Water vapour diffuses out through the open stomata: transpiration.
  • Transpiration is an unavoidable consequence of gas exchange.
  • Say: "water vapour diffuses out while stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens stomata to lose water".
Quick check
?Why is transpiration described as a consequence of gas exchange rather than as a process the leaf carries out for its own sake?
Mission 5

Xylem vessels: built for flow

  • Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to leaves.
  • Dead cells, no contents: nothing obstructs the flow.
  • End walls lost: cells join into one continuous tube.
  • Lignified walls: rigid, so the vessel does not collapse under tension; also waterproof, and support the plant.
  • Pits: gaps in the lignin. Water moves sideways between vessels and out to leaf cells.
  • Say: "lignin stops the vessel collapsing under tension" — not: "lignin is only for support".
Quick check
?Why are the walls of xylem vessels thickened with lignin?
Mission 5

Where the xylem is

  • Stem: vascular bundles in a ring near the outside. Pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: xylem inside, phloem outside, cambium between.
  • Cortex between the ring and the epidermis.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 5

Cohesion–tension: pulled from the top

  • (1) Water evaporates from leaf cell walls. Water is drawn out of the xylem, through the cell walls, to replace it.
  • (2) This puts the xylem water under tension.
  • Tension is a negative pressure potential.
  • (3) Cohesion: hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, so the whole column is pulled up unbroken.
  • Adhesion: water is attracted to the hydrophilic vessel walls, which helps it creep up narrow spaces.
  • The flow from roots to leaves is the transpiration stream.
  • Passive: no ATP. The energy comes from the Sun evaporating water.
  • Say: "water is pulled up under tension" — not: "the roots pump water up".
Quick check
?What generates the force that draws water up the xylem from the roots to the leaves?
Mission check

?Florists cut the stems of fresh flowers under water. A stem cut in air and then put in a vase often wilts, even though its cut end is in water. Which explanation is correct?

The tree and the vase work the same way: evaporation pulls, cohesion passes the pull down, and the column must stay unbroken.

Mission 6Practical

The potometer

  • Cut the shoot under water, at a slant. Fit it to the potometer under water: no air gaps.
  • Seal joints with petroleum jelly. Dry the leaves. Let the shoot settle for 5 minutes.
  • Let in one air bubble. Time how far it moves along the scale. Reset it from the reservoir. Repeat; take a mean.
  • Change one factor (e.g. fan, lamp distance). Keep the others, and the same shoot, constant.
  • Rate of water uptake = distance the bubble moves ÷ time. Multiply the distance by the tube's cross-sectional area to turn it into a volume.
  • Say: "a potometer measures water uptake" — not: "water loss". A little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells.
Mission 6Practical

Looking inside a stem

  • Stain: toluidine blue turns lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) blue-green and other walls purple. Phloroglucinol with acid turns lignin red.
  • Thin sections let light through: cut with a sharp blade, keep sections wet.
Mission 6

Drawing a plan diagram

  • A plan diagram shows tissue outlines only: no individual cells.
  • Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading.
  • Keep the proportions of the tissues true.
  • Label with ruled lines that do not cross.
  • Annotate functions: xylem (water and ions), phloem (sugars), cortex (storage, packing), epidermis (protection).

Mission 7

The nucleotide

  • DNA and RNA are polymers. Their monomers are nucleotides.
  • Each nucleotide has three parts:
  • a pentose (5-carbon sugar) in the middle
  • a phosphate group, bonded to the sugar
  • a nitrogen-containing base, also bonded to the sugar
  • DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose + phosphate + one base: Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine or Thymine.
  • RNA nucleotide: ribose + phosphate + one base: A, C, G or Uracil.
  • Draw it as circle (phosphate), pentagon (sugar), rectangle (base).
  • Say: "a nucleotide contains a base" — not: "a nucleotide is a base".
Quick check
?Which is a correct description of a single nucleotide?
Mission 7

Nucleotides join into a strand

  • Condensation: the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next. Water is released.
  • The bond is a sugar–phosphate bond (also called a phosphodiester bond).
  • Repeated, this builds a sugar–phosphate backbone: one continuous chain of covalent bonds. Strong.
  • The bases stick out from the backbone. Each is bonded only to its own sugar.
  • n nucleotides in a chain: n − 1 bonds, n − 1 waters released.
  • Say: "the backbone is sugar–phosphate–sugar" — not: "the bases are joined into a chain".
Mission 7

A strand has two different ends

  • The pentose's carbons are numbered 1′ to 5′ ("five prime").
  • One end of a strand has a free phosphate on a 5′ carbon: the 5′ end.
  • The other end has a free –OH on a 3′ carbon: the 3′ end.
  • New nucleotides join only at the 3′ end. The incoming phosphate bonds to the 3′ –OH.
  • So a strand grows in the 5′ → 3′ direction.
  • Say: "5′ and 3′ name the two ends of every strand" — not: "the 5′ strand and the 3′ strand".
Quick check
?A modified nucleotide lacks the hydroxyl (–OH) group on the 3′ carbon of its sugar but is otherwise normal. During the synthesis of a DNA strand, one of these modified nucleotides is added to the growing strand. Which prediction about the strand is most likely correct?
Mission 7

Two strands: the double helix

  • DNA has two polynucleotide strands.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds.
  • Pairing is complementary: A with T, G with C. Never A–G or C–T.
  • The strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions.
  • The paired strands twist into a double helix.
  • One hydrogen bond is weak. Thousands along a molecule hold the strands firmly together.
  • Yet each can break: the strands can be separated without breaking the backbone.
  • Say: "complementary bases" — not: "matching" or "identical bases".
Mission 7

Purines and pyrimidines

  • Purines: A and G. Two rings (a double ring).
  • Pyrimidines: C, T and U. One ring.
  • Every base pair is one purine + one pyrimidine: A–T, G–C.
  • So every pair is the same width. Two purines would be too wide; two pyrimidines too narrow.
  • The helix keeps one regular shape whatever the base sequence: a stable structure.
  • Say: "a purine pairs with a pyrimidine" — not: "purines pair with purines".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Unequal sharing of electrons makes water polar (δ− O, δ+ H); hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
  • Charged and polar substances dissolve (hydrophilic); non-polar ones are excluded (hydrophobic). That makes water the medium for metabolism and transport.

Mission 2

  • Hydrogen bonds give cohesion (surface tension; unbroken xylem columns) and a high specific heat capacity (stable temperatures).
  • Adhesion gives capillary action; water's density, viscosity and conductivity shape life in water.

Mission 3

  • The first cells arose in water, and water remains the medium for most life processes.
  • Early Earth (no O2 or ozone, high CO2 and CH4, UV, lightning) allowed carbon compounds to form abiotically, as Miller–Urey showed; the first cells needed catalysis, self-replication, self-assembly and compartmentalisation.
  • RNA can carry information and catalyse, so it is the presumed first genetic material.

Mission 4

  • A universal genetic code and genes shared by all life point to LUCA; fossils (minimum ages), radiometric dating and molecular clocks date early life; vent microfossils and conserved genes place LUCA near hydrothermal vents.
  • Mitochondria, then (in some lineages) chloroplasts, arose by endosymbiosis: their circular DNA, bacteria-like ribosomes, division and double membranes are the evidence.

Mission 5

  • Xylem vessels are dead, hollow, lignified tubes with pits: a continuous path that does not collapse under tension.
  • Evaporation from leaves puts the xylem under tension; cohesion pulls the whole column up. No ATP is spent.

Mission 6

  • Light, temperature and air movement raise transpiration; humidity lowers it. All act through stomatal opening or the water-vapour gradient.
  • Lignin stains identify xylem (and sclerenchyma); plan diagrams show tissue outlines only.

Mission 7

  • Nucleotide = pentose + phosphate + base. Condensation joins nucleotides into a strand with a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • DNA: two strands held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, G–C), twisted into a double helix. RNA: ribose, uracil, one strand.
  • The two strands are antiparallel.
  • Strands have a 5′ end and a 3′ end and grow only at the 3′ end; every pair is purine + pyrimidine, so the helix has a constant width.
