Who decided which books are 'classics'?

Critical theory · literary value and the canonScreen 1

What is the canon?

  • A canon first meant the books of scripture a church accepted as authoritative.
  • The literary canon is the set of works treated as most valuable: taught, reprinted, examined and written about.
  • No single body decides it. Critics, universities, exam boards (through set texts), publishers, prizes and readers all shape it.
  • Literary value is the quality that is said to earn a work its place. Whether that value is in the text, or given to it by readers, is the debate.
Critical theory · literary value and the canonScreen 2

Arguments about value

  • Samuel Johnson (Preface to Shakespeare, 1765): the only test is "length of duration and continuance of esteem".
  • F. R. Leavis (The Great Tradition, 1948) prized moral intensity in Austen, George Eliot, James and Conrad.
  • Harold Bloom (The Western Canon, 1994): value is aesthetic; he dismissed political critics as a School of Resentment.
  • Terry Eagleton (Literary Theory, 1983): value judgements reflect the interests of powerful groups.
  • Feminist and post-colonial critics ask who was left out.
Critical theory · literary value and the canonScreen 3

How your exam asks this

  • Lit A NEA, Independent critical study: one 2,500-word comparative essay on two texts, one pre-1900, plus a bibliography; 50 marks, 20%. Your texts must allow access to a range of critical views, including over time: the history of a text's reputation is that range.
  • In both, AO5 must include how readings have changed over time.

Canons change

  • Wuthering Heights (1847, published as by "Ellis Bell") startled critics with its passion. Leavis (1948) praised it but left it outside his tradition as a one-off. By the late 1980s it was called the twentieth century's favourite Victorian novel.
  • The Awakening (1899) was mostly attacked by reviewers and nearly vanished; from the late 1960s, as the women's movement grew, it was recovered. It is now a Lit A set text.

Mission 2

Who is telling this story, and why should you trust them?

Make a guess: Who is telling this story, and why should you trust them?

Critical theory · narrative theoryScreen 1

Who speaks?

  • The narrator is not the author. Wayne Booth (The Rhetoric of Fiction, 1961) added the implied author: the author as the whole text suggests them.
  • Gérard Genette (Narrative Discourse, 1972): a homodiegetic narrator is a character in the story (Jane Eyre, Nick Carraway, Lockwood); a heterodiegetic narrator stands outside it (the narrator of Tess).
  • Frame narrative: Lockwood's diary holds Nelly Dean's story, which holds other voices.
Critical theory · narrative theoryScreen 2

Who sees, and when?

  • Focalisation (Genette) asks who sees: zero (an all-knowing view), internal (through one character's perception) or external (outside only).
  • In Jane Eyre, the older Jane speaks while the younger Jane often sees: "Reader, I married him." (Chapter 38) is the narrator looking back.
  • Order: analepsis (flashback), prolepsis (flash-forward). Duration: scene, summary, ellipsis. Frequency: how often an event is told.
Critical theory · narrative theoryScreen 3

How your exam asks this

  • Lit A NEA, Independent critical study: one 2,500-word comparative essay on two texts, one pre-1900, plus a bibliography; 50 marks, 20%. Narrative theory sharpens AO2, which AQA links especially to structure, and gives you critical terms to apply and evaluate (AO5).
  • NEA texts can't be exam set texts: these model the method.

Critical theory · narrative theoryScreen 6

Unreliable narrators

  • Booth: a narrator is unreliable when their account or values don't match the norms of the work as a whole.
  • Signs: contradictions, other characters' reactions, a gap between what is shown and what is claimed.
  • Lockwood keeps misreading: he takes "a heap of dead rabbits" for cats (Chapter 2).
  • Nick Carraway says he is "inclined to reserve all judgements" (Chapter 1), yet claims "I am one of the few honest people that I have ever known." (Chapter 3).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Who is telling this story, and why should you trust them?

Every story is told by a narrator, not the author, from a position of their own. Trust is tested, not assumed: compare what a narrator claims with what the text shows, as with Lockwood calling a suspicious, unwelcoming Heathcliff "A capital fellow!".

Recap

Big ideas

  • The canon is the set of works treated as most valuable; critics, schools, exam boards, publishers and readers shape it.
  • Value debates: Leavis (moral intensity), Bloom (aesthetic value), Eagleton (value is shaped by power); feminist and post-colonial critics ask who is left out.
  • Reputations change: Wuthering Heights and The Awakening rose long after publication.
  • Choose texts with a range of critical views over time; the history of a text's reputation helps meet AO5.
  • Genette's questions: who speaks (homodiegetic or heterodiegetic voice), who sees (focalisation) and when (order, duration, frequency).
  • Booth: the narrator isn't the author; an unreliable narrator's account or values clash with the norms of the work, as with Lockwood and, many readers argue, Nick Carraway.
  • Narrative theory gives you precise terms for structure (AO2) and critical ideas to apply and evaluate (AO5).
