Literary value and the canon

Literary value and the canon is one of the aspects of the AQA Critical Anthology, and it is the most philosophically demanding thing on the course. It asks a question that sounds naive and is not: what makes a book good, and who decided?

  • Where it is assessed: the NEA (Theory and independence) — where an essay may be linked to this aspect of the Anthology — and, in the exams, wherever you handle AO5.
  • The two words: value (is quality in the text, in the reader, or in the institution?) and canon (the body of works a culture agrees to keep).
  • The trap: concluding that value is entirely subjective and therefore nothing can be said. That is where the argument starts, not where it ends.

Value · the question

Where does literary value live?

Three broad answers, each defensible, each with a cost:

  • In the text (intrinsic value). Some works are simply better made: more complex, more coherent, more resistant to exhaustion. The New Critics of the mid-twentieth century held that the poem was a self-sufficient verbal object whose value lay in its internal organisation — irony, ambiguity, tension. Cost: the criteria of complexity turn out to be historically specific and to favour the kinds of writing the critics already admired.
  • In the reader (subjective / affective value). A book is good because of what it does to you. Cost: it makes disagreement unresolvable and cannot explain why some judgements are so widely shared over such long periods.
  • In the institution (constructed value). Value is conferred by publishers, reviewers, prize juries, universities, examination boards. What we call greatness is the residue of a long process of selection. Cost: pressed too far, it cannot explain why some institutional favourites die and some neglected books return.

The mature position: these are not three rival answers so much as three components. Value is real, and it is also made. A text must be capable of sustaining attention — but which texts get the attention is a historical, institutional and political matter.

Canon · what it is

The canon and how it is made

The canon is the set of works a culture treats as worth preserving, teaching and returning to. The word comes from the church's list of authorised scripture — which tells you a great deal about how it functions.

The mechanisms that make a canon:

  • Publication and reprinting — a book that goes out of print is on its way out of the canon regardless of its quality.
  • Reviewing and prizes — gatekeepers with cultural authority.
  • Anthologies and syllabuses — including this one. Your set-text list is a canon in miniature, and it is revised, which proves that canons are not permanent.
  • Universities and criticism — a text that generates good essays gets taught, and a text that gets taught generates essays.
  • Adaptation — film and television keep books alive far more efficiently than critics do.

The observable fact that settles the debate: canons change. Writers once central are now unread; writers once ignored are now examined. If value were purely intrinsic and self-evident, this could not happen. If value were purely arbitrary, the changes would be random — and they are not: they track shifts in who is allowed to read, write, teach and judge.

Critique

Who was left out, and how the argument was won

The great canon debates of the twentieth century were arguments about exclusion.

  • Feminist critique — the canon was overwhelmingly male, and the criteria of greatness (scope, ambition, public subject matter) quietly reflected the kinds of lives men were permitted to lead. Recovering women's writing meant not only adding names but questioning the criteria. Virginia Woolf's A Room of One's Own (1929) made the material version of the case: writing requires money and a door that shuts.
  • Post-colonial critique — a canon of the metropolitan centre, in which the empire appears as scenery. Chinua Achebe's 1975 lecture attacking the racial vision of Conrad's Heart of Darkness is the standard example of a canonical text being arraigned rather than admired.
  • Marxist critique — the canon reflects who had the leisure to write and the education to be read; the working-class voice arrives late and is often admitted only as testimony rather than as art.
  • The counter-argument — Harold Bloom's The Western Canon (1994) insisted that aesthetic value is real, that it is not reducible to politics, and that a criticism which selects texts by the identity of their authors abandons literature for sociology.

You are not required to pick a side — you are required to argue. The strongest position notices that both camps assume you can tell good writing from bad, and that the real disagreement is about whether the standards used to do so are innocent.

Sharpening

Criteria: what do we actually mean by good?

If you are going to argue about value, you need to name the criteria you are using. The usual candidates:

  • Complexity and density — the text rewards re-reading; it does not exhaust itself on first contact.
  • Formal control — the work's parts are doing something for each other. This is checkable, and it is why AO2 exists.
  • Originality — it does something the tradition had not done. But note: originality is only visible against a tradition, so this criterion is historical, not absolute.
  • Truthfulness / insight — it tells us something about being alive that we recognise. The most persuasive criterion and the least testable.
  • Moral or political worth — the most contested. Does a book's politics affect its value as literature? The honest answer is that most readers behave as though it does and most critics deny that it should.
  • Endurance — it lasts. But this is a result of value, not a definition of it, and circular arguments from endurance are the commonest error in this topic.

The circularity trap: it is great because it has lasted, and it has lasted because it is great. Spot this in your own writing and cut it out.

Reception

Taste, and how it is manufactured

A useful sociological angle: taste is not innocent.

  • Cultural capital — Pierre Bourdieu argued that taste functions as a marker of class. Knowing which books to admire is a form of wealth, transmitted at home and cashed in at interviews. Literary judgement, on this account, is partly a mechanism of social sorting.
  • The high/low divide — genre fiction (crime, romance, fantasy, horror) has been excluded from the canon for reasons that turn out, when examined, to be about readership as much as about writing. Note the irony that your own Paper 2 studies crime writing as a literary genre — the boundary is being renegotiated in the specification you are sitting.
  • The examination board as canon-maker — a set-text list confers value, and it is periodically revised. You are inside the mechanism you are studying.
  • Popularity — sales are not value, but nor are they irrelevant: many canonical writers were commercial entertainers, and the distinction between art and entertainment is younger and shakier than it looks.

The genuinely uncomfortable question, and a superb NEA line of enquiry: does calling a book great mean anything more than that people like us have agreed to keep teaching it? Argue that it does — but earn it.

Method

Writing well about value

This is the aspect of the Anthology where students most often produce essays that are all opinion and no evidence. Discipline:

  1. Anchor the abstract argument in a text, always. The question is never what is literary value? in the abstract; it is what does the value of this text consist in, and who has said otherwise?
  2. Use reception history as evidence. How was the text received on publication? Was it prosecuted, ignored, adored, forgotten, revived? That record is data, and it is available.
  3. Name your criteria and defend them. If you claim a text is valuable for its formal control, demonstrate the control on the page.
  4. Argue against yourself once. The best essays on value contain the sentence beginning Against this, it might be objected that... and then answer the objection.
  5. Avoid the two dead ends: the platitude that everything is subjective, and the piety that the great books are great.

Under exam conditions, value and canon questions rarely appear directly — but the thinking pays off in AO5 across every paper. A candidate who understands that a text's reputation is a historical event, not a fact of nature, writes better about everything.

Synthesis

Holding both truths at once

The position that scores highest is neither the pious defence of great books nor the flat announcement that value is a fiction. It is this:

Value is real, and value is made.a text must be capable of sustaining attention — but which texts receive attention is decided by history, institutions and power

What follows from holding both:

  • You can admire a text and still analyse the machinery that made it admirable.
  • You can criticise a canon without pretending that all writing is equally good.
  • You can take your own responses seriously as evidence, while remaining suspicious of where they came from.
  • You can treat criticism itself as part of literature's life — the canon is not a list but an argument that is still going on, and your essay is a contribution to it.

The sentence to leave the exam hall with: To ask why we value a text is not to attack it. It is the most serious form of attention we know how to pay.

Recap

The big ideas to take into the exam

Where it sits: an aspect of the AQA Critical Anthology, assessed in the NEA (Theory and independence) — and it sharpens AO5 in every paper

Three locations of value: in the text (New Criticism: complexity, formal control) · in the reader (affective) · in the institution (constructed by publishers, prizes, syllabuses)

The canon: a list a culture agrees to keep — made by reprinting, reviewing, prizes, anthologies, universities and adaptation. Your set-text list is a canon in miniature, and it gets revised

The decisive fact: canons CHANGE, and the changes track shifts in cultural authority — which defeats both pure intrinsicism and pure randomness

The critiques: feminist (Woolf) · post-colonial (Achebe on Conrad) · Marxist (who had the leisure to write) — and Bloom's defence of aesthetic value against them

Criteria: complexity · formal control · originality · insight · moral worth · endurance — and BEWARE the circular argument from endurance

Taste: Bourdieu's cultural capital: literary judgement is also a claim to belong; the high/low divide is being renegotiated (crime writing is now an A-level genre)

The mature position: value is real AND value is made — admire the text and interrogate its reputation in the same breath
