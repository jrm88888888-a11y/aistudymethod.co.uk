Mission 1

Mission 1: As countries grow richer, why do workers move from farms to factories and then to services?

Make a guess: As countries grow richer, why do workers move from farms to factories and then to services?

Mission 1

What less-developed economies tend to have in common

  • Low income per head and a large share of people in poverty.
  • A large share of workers in agriculture, often small-scale and low-productivity.
  • A large informal sector: unregistered, untaxed work.
  • High birth rates and a young population; higher infant mortality.
  • Low savings and investment; weak infrastructure.
  • Exports concentrated in a few primary commodities.
  • These are tendencies, not rules: developing economies differ hugely from each other.
Mission 1

Three sectors

  • Primary: extracting natural resources: farming, fishing, forestry, mining.
  • Secondary: manufacturing and construction.
  • Tertiary: services: retail, finance, health, education, tourism.
  • As countries develop, the main share of jobs and output moves primary → secondary → tertiary.
  • Real data: farming's share of the world's jobs fell from 42.8% in 1991 to 26.6% in 2023.
  • In high-income economies, most workers are in services. The share of jobs in manufacturing has fallen: deindustrialisation.
Mission 1

Why the structure changes

  • Productivity in farming rises: better seeds, fertiliser, machines. Fewer workers produce the food a country needs.
  • Demand shifts: as incomes rise, people spend only a little more on food. Spending on manufactured goods, then services, grows faster.
    • Food has a low income elasticity of demand; many services are income elastic.
  • So workers move to factories and cities, and later into services.
  • In rich economies, manufacturing becomes more capital-intensive, and some moves to lower-cost countries. Its share of jobs falls even if its output rises.
  • Say: "the share of workers in farming falls" — not: "farm output falls".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In Country H today, 3% of workers are in the primary sector, 20% in the secondary sector and 77% in the tertiary sector. Thirty years ago the secondary share was 35%. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 1

Population: births, deaths and migration

  • Birth rate: live births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Death rate: deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Infant mortality rate: deaths of babies under one year old per 1,000 live births.
  • Natural increase = birth rate − death rate. Net migration = immigrants − emigrants.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): birth rate 35, death rate 10 per 1,000.
    • Natural increase = 35 − 10 = 25 per 1,000 = 2.5% a year.
    • At 2.5% a year the population doubles in about 70 ÷ 2.5 = 28 years.
  • Demographic transition: death rates fall first (clean water, vaccines, medicine), so population grows fast. Birth rates fall later (lower infant mortality, education of women, urban living, contraception).
  • Rapid growth means many young dependants: more spending on schools, less saving per head. A young workforce can also be a demographic dividend if there are jobs for it.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country J has a birth rate of 30 per 1,000, a death rate of 8 per 1,000 and net migration of −4 per 1,000 (more people leave than arrive). By what percentage does J's population change in a year?
Mission 1

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of people living in towns and cities.
  • Real data: 55% of the world's people lived in urban areas in 2018. In 2018 the UN projected 68% by 2050.
  • Pull factors: jobs in industry and services, higher wages, schools, hospitals.
  • Push factors: low farm incomes, lack of land, drought.
  • Benefits: firms and workers gain from being close together; services are cheaper to provide.
  • Costs: informal settlements (slums), congestion, pollution, pressure on water and housing.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are these characteristics causes or results of low development?

It depends on:

  • Direction of cause: a large farm sector may hold incomes down, but low incomes also keep demand for other goods low.
  • Population: rapid growth strains resources, but a young workforce can be a dividend if there are jobs and schools.
  • Urbanisation: cities raise productivity if housing, water and transport keep up; otherwise slums grow.
  • Policy and institutions: two countries with the same features can develop very differently.
  • So: these features describe where a country is; they do not on their own decide where it goes.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In Country Z, new seeds and machines double the output of each farm worker. As incomes rise, people spend only a little more on food. What is most likely to happen to the share of Z's workers in farming?

Rising productivity on farms and changing patterns of spending together move workers out of farming.

Mission 2

The poverty cycle

  • A poverty cycle (poverty trap): low income causes the things that keep income low.
  • Low income → low saving → low investment → little capital per worker → low productivity → low income.
  • A second loop: low income → poor health and education → low human capital → low productivity → low income.
  • Each arrow is a link. Break one link, and the cycle can turn into a growth path.
Quick check
?In a poverty cycle, low incomes lead to low saving. Why does low saving keep incomes low in the future?
Mission 2

People and places

  • Human capital: the skills, knowledge and health of workers. Poor schooling and ill health cut productivity.
  • Infrastructure: roads, ports, power, water, telecoms. Without them, firms face high costs and cannot reach markets.
Mission 2

Institutions and corruption

  • Institutions: the rules of the game: courts, laws, tax systems, banks.
  • Property rights: secure, legal ownership. Without them, people invest less, and land cannot be used as collateral for a loan.
  • Weak governance: unstable or ineffective government deters investment.
  • Corruption: bribes raise costs, divert public money (including income from oil or mining) and reward connections over productivity.
Quick check
?In one region, families have farmed the same land for generations but have no legal title to it. Which effect on development is most likely?
Mission 2Evaluate

Which barrier matters most?

It depends on:

  • The binding constraint: a new port does little if the roads to it are unsafe. The barrier that binds first matters most.
  • Interactions: corruption worsens capital flight and debt; ill health weakens education.
  • The country: a landlocked mineral exporter faces different barriers from a coastal farming economy.
  • Time scale: education and health raise growth only over decades.
  • Evidence: some resource-rich countries grew fast, e.g. Botswana with diamonds, often linked to strong institutions. Resource wealth does not have to hold a country back.
Mission check

?A government gives farmers in a remote region free high-yield seed. Harvests rise by 40%. A year later, farm incomes have barely risen. Which is the most likely explanation?

Removing one barrier may not help if another, such as poor infrastructure, still binds.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can opening up to world markets and foreign firms make a poor country richer?

Make a guess: Can opening up to world markets and foreign firms make a poor country richer?

Mission 3

Letting markets lead

  • Market-oriented strategies: reduce the state's role; let prices, competition and private firms allocate resources.
  • The idea: free markets give incentives to produce what the country does best, and draw in foreign capital.
  • Main tools:
  • Trade liberalisation: cut tariffs and quotas.
  • Inward FDI: attract foreign firms to build plants.
  • Privatisation and deregulation.
  • The opposite approach, interventionist strategies, is in Mission 4.
Mission 3

Trade liberalisation

  • Trade liberalisation: removing barriers to trade such as tariffs and quotas.
  • Domestic price falls toward the world price (Pw).
  • Consumers gain: lower prices, more choice.
  • Resources move to sectors with a comparative advantage, so output can rise.
  • Cheaper imported machinery and inputs help firms.
  • But: protected domestic firms shrink or close; the government loses tariff revenue.
  • Workers who lose jobs may not quickly find new ones.
Mission 3Graph lab

Cutting a tariff

A country imports rice. World price 30; a tariff of 10 makes the domestic price 40 (illustrative data).

  • Takeaway: removing a tariff lowers price and raises imports; the consumer gain is bigger than the producer and revenue losses combined.
Mission 3

Worked example: who gains, who loses?

  • Tariff 10 → 0. Price 40 → 30.
  • Consumption 120 → 140; domestic production 60 → 40; imports 60 → 100.
  • Consumer surplus +1,300: (120 + 140) ÷ 2 × 10.
  • Producer surplus −500: (60 + 40) ÷ 2 × 10.
  • Tariff revenue −600: 10 × 60.
  • Net gain = 1,300 − 500 − 600 = +200: the two deadweight loss triangles (100 each) are recovered.
  • Say: "domestic production contracts along the supply curve" — not: "supply falls".
Mission 3

Inward FDI

  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a foreign firm builds or buys a business here and controls it.
  • Benefits:
  • Adds capital and foreign currency that the country is short of.
  • Jobs, tax revenue, exports.
  • Technology transfer and skills; local suppliers learn.
  • Costs:
  • Profit repatriation: profits go back to the parent company, so GNI rises by less than GDP.
  • Transfer pricing can cut taxes paid locally.
  • Few links if parts are imported; firms may leave when costs rise ("footloose").
  • Governments may give up tax revenue (tax breaks) or loosen rules to attract them.
Quick check
?A foreign-owned factory adds 500 million a year to a country's output. It sends 200 million of its profits a year back to its parent company abroad. By how much does the country's GNI rise?
Mission 3

Privatisation and deregulation

  • Privatisation: selling state-owned firms (power, phones, banks) to private owners.
  • Aim: profit incentive → efficiency, investment, innovation; sale revenue for the government.
  • Deregulation: removing rules that block competition or new entry.
  • Risks: a state monopoly becomes a private monopoly; prices may rise; the poor may lose access; sales can be corrupt.
  • Needs a strong regulator: weak institutions (see Mission 2) limit the gains.
Mission 3Evaluate

Markets or the state?

It depends on:

  • Institutions: markets need property rights, courts and regulators. Without them, privatisation and liberalisation may enrich a few.
  • Timing and sequencing: sudden opening can close local firms before new ones grow.
  • Market failures: markets underprovide education, health and infrastructure.
  • Who gains: growth can rise while inequality widens.
  • Evidence: fast-growing East Asian economies combined export markets with active governments. Economists still debate how much of each a country needs.
Mission check

?A country attracts a foreign car-assembly plant by giving it a 10-year tax holiday. The plant imports almost all its parts and sends its profits to its parent company. Which judgement is best supported?

FDI helps most when it creates links, skills and tax revenue in the host country.

Mission 4: Can a government make a poor country richer by stepping in where markets do too little?

Make a guess: Can a government make a poor country richer by stepping in where markets do too little?

Mission 4

Why governments step in

  • Interventionist strategies: the state actively directs resources to raise growth and development.
  • The case: in poor countries, markets often fail.
  • Merit goods (education, health) are underconsumed.
  • Infrastructure is underprovided by private firms.
  • Infant industries cannot yet compete with established foreign firms.
  • The opposite approach, market-oriented strategies, is in Mission 3.
Mission 4

Import substitution

  • Import substitution industrialisation (ISI): replace imported manufactures with goods made at home.
  • Tools: tariffs and quotas on imports (protectionism), subsidies and cheap loans to chosen industries.
  • Aim: grow infant industries until they can compete; save foreign currency; create factory jobs.
  • Examples: much of Latin America (1950s–1970s); India before its 1991 reforms.
  • Problems: protected firms have little reason to cut costs; small home markets limit economies of scale; consumers pay higher prices; firms lobby to keep protection.
Quick check
?A country puts high tariffs on imported cars to build up its own car industry. Twenty years later, its car makers still cannot compete without the tariffs. Which problem of import substitution does this show best?
Mission 4

Investing in people

  • Human capital raises productivity: education, training, health.
  • Education and health are merit goods: underconsumed in a free market.
  • Why? Information failure, positive externalities and families unable to borrow against future earnings.
  • Government role: free or subsidised schooling and health care; vaccination; clean water.
  • Limits: costly for a small tax base; quality matters as much as places; returns take decades.
Mission 4

Infrastructure

  • Infrastructure: energy, transport, telecoms, clean water, sanitation.
  • Private firms underprovide it: high upfront cost, long payback, and many benefits go to people who cannot be charged.
  • Benefits: lower costs for firms; access to markets; healthier workers.
  • Risks: projects chosen for political reasons; corruption; debt if funded by borrowing.
Quick check
?Many families in a low-income country send their children to work rather than to school, even though schooling raises lifetime earnings. Which is the best explanation for this underconsumption?
Mission 4Evaluate

How well does intervention work?

It depends on:

  • Government capacity: honest, skilled officials and good information. Otherwise government failure: wrong industries chosen, corruption, waste.
  • Discipline: support that is tied to results (e.g. export targets) works better than open-ended protection.
  • Funding: a small tax base limits what the state can do; borrowing adds debt.
  • Time scale: human capital and infrastructure pay off slowly.
  • Evidence is mixed: much protected industry in Latin America stayed high-cost, while East Asian governments steered industry and still grew fast. Most strategies combine state and market.
Mission check

?A low-income country plans new roads linking remote villages to towns. Which is the strongest economic argument for the government, rather than private firms, to fund them?

Where benefits cannot be charged for, the market underprovides, which is the core case for intervention.

Mission 5: When rich countries give or lend to poorer ones, when does it actually help them grow?

Make a guess: When rich countries give or lend to poorer ones, when does it actually help them grow?

Mission 5

What counts as aid

  • Aid: a voluntary transfer of resources to a developing country on better-than-market terms.
  • Humanitarian aid: short-term relief after a disaster or conflict: food, shelter, medicine.
  • Development aid: longer-term help to raise living standards: schools, clinics, roads, water.
  • Bilateral aid: from one government to another. Multilateral aid: through bodies such as the World Bank.
  • Forms: grants (never repaid), concessional (soft) loans (low interest, long repayment), technical assistance (experts, training).
  • Tied aid: must be spent on goods or services from the donor country.
Mission 5

Official development assistance (ODA)

  • ODA: aid from governments, given to promote the economic development and welfare of developing countries, as grants or concessional loans.
  • Measured by the OECD's Development Assistance Committee (DAC).
  • UN target: donors give 0.7% of their GNI as ODA.
  • 2024 (OECD, preliminary): DAC donors gave 212.1 billion US dollars, 0.33% of their combined GNI. Aid fell 7.1% in real terms on 2023.
  • Only 4 donors went above 0.7%: Denmark, Luxembourg, Norway and Sweden.
  • Illustrative data: a donor with GNI of 3,000 bn giving 0.3% gives 9 bn. At 0.7% it would give 21 bn: a gap of 12 bn.
Quick check
?After an earthquake, a donor government flies in tents and medicine. Separately, it lends a poor country money at 1% interest over 40 years to build a power line, on condition that the cables are bought from the donor's own firms. Which description is correct?
Mission 5

How aid could raise growth

  • Poor countries often face a savings gap: low incomes → low saving → low investment (see Mission 2).
  • They may also face a foreign currency gap: too little export revenue to import machinery and fuel.
  • Aid can fill both gaps.
  • Short run: aid spent in the economy raises AD: AD1 → AD2.
  • Long run: aid spent on infrastructure, health and education raises productive capacity: LRAS shifts right.
  • Aid spent on consumption, or lost to corruption, may give only the short-run boost.

Where the aid goes

Illustrative data. A country receives aid grants worth 5% of its GDP for one year.

  • Takeaway: aid raises long-run capacity only when it is spent on things that raise future output.
Mission 5

The case for and against aid

  • For:
    • fills the savings and foreign currency gaps
    • saves lives quickly in a disaster
    • funds merit goods (vaccination, schooling) that markets under-provide
  • Against:
    • dependency: governments may rely on aid rather than build a tax system
    • corruption and fungibility: aid for clinics can free other funds for less useful spending
    • tied aid can cost more than buying at world prices
    • food aid can lower prices for local farmers and cut their output
    • large inflows can push up the exchange rate, making exports less competitive
  • Economists disagree on aid's overall effect on growth. Evidence varies by country, period and type of aid.
Mission 5

Why external debt can become a trap

  • External debt: money owed to foreign lenders, often in a foreign currency such as US dollars.
  • Repayments need foreign currency, earned mainly from exports.
  • Worked example (illustrative data):

1. Debt: 2 bn US dollars. Exchange rate: 100 local units per dollar → debt = 200 bn local units.

2. The currency depreciates to 150 per dollar → debt = 2 × 150 = 300 bn local units: 50% more, with no new borrowing.

3. Yearly interest at 5% = 0.1 bn dollars: 10 bn local units before, 15 bn after.

  • Debt service: yearly interest plus repayments. Every dollar spent on it is a dollar not spent on health, education or imports of capital goods.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country owes 500 million US dollars. Its currency falls from 10 to 12.5 local units per dollar. What happens to the debt measured in local currency?
Mission 5

Debt relief

  • Debt relief: cancelling, reducing or rescheduling a country's debt.
  • HIPC Initiative (Heavily Indebted Poor Countries), launched by the IMF and World Bank in 1996.
  • The Multilateral Debt Relief Initiative (MDRI), from 2005, added 100% relief on eligible debts to the IMF, World Bank and African Development Fund for countries completing HIPC.
  • Countries had to meet conditions, including a poverty reduction strategy. By February 2023, 36 countries had completed the process (IMF), most of them in Africa.
  • For: frees money for health, education and infrastructure; can restore access to credit.
  • Against: moral hazard (governments may borrow carelessly, expecting future relief); savings may not reach the poor; lenders may lend less in future.
  • Say: "debt relief cuts debt service payments" — not: "debt relief gives the country new money to spend".
Mission 5

The IMF and the World Bank

  • Both set up at Bretton Woods in 1944. Member countries own them; votes depend largely on each country's financial contribution (its quota at the IMF, its shares at the World Bank), so richer countries have more say.
  • IMF (International Monetary Fund):
    • watches members' economies and global financial stability (surveillance)
    • lends to countries with balance of payments problems, usually short to medium term
    • loans come with conditionality: e.g. cut the budget deficit, reform taxes or the exchange rate
  • World Bank:
    • finances long-term development projects: roads, power, water, schools
    • lends to middle-income countries (IBRD) and gives grants and very low-interest loans to the poorest countries (IDA)
  • Critics: in the 1980s and 1990s, structural adjustment conditions (spending cuts, privatisation, liberalisation) were blamed for hurting the poor. Supporters say they restored stability and growth. Conditions now place more weight on protecting social spending.
Quick check
?A country cannot pay for its imports. Its currency is falling fast and it has run out of foreign currency reserves. Which body is it most likely to turn to, and on what terms?
Mission 5

NGOs and remittances

  • NGOs (non-governmental organisations): charities and other non-profit groups, independent of governments.
    • Strengths: local knowledge; work directly with communities; can reach people a weak government cannot.
    • Limits: small scale; patchy coordination; can depend on donations that change with the news.
  • Remittances: money sent home by people working abroad.
    • 2023 (World Bank): about 656 billion US dollars went to low- and middle-income countries: more than FDI, and more than ODA, that year.
    • They go straight to households: they pay for food, school fees and small businesses.
    • But they can fall in a downturn abroad, and they do not directly build public infrastructure.
Quick check
?A government receives large aid grants for its health service. It then cuts its own health budget by the same amount and spends the money on new military aircraft. What problem does this show?
Mission 5Evaluate

Does aid help growth and development?

It depends on:

  • What it is spent on: infrastructure, health and education can shift LRAS; consumption gives a short-run boost at most.
  • Governance: corruption and weak institutions cut the share that reaches its target.
  • Type and terms: humanitarian vs development; tied vs untied; grants vs loans that add to debt.
  • Size and timing: large inflows can push up the exchange rate; aid that stops suddenly can stall projects.
  • Alternatives: trade access, FDI and remittances may matter more for some countries.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two countries each receive aid worth 3% of GDP a year for ten years. Country P spends it on rural roads and child vaccination, and audits show little is lost. In country Q, much of it is spent on imported consumer goods and some is lost to corruption. Which outcome is most likely?

Aid can help growth when it builds productive capacity and good institutions make sure it is used well.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Less-developed economies tend to have more workers in agriculture, younger populations and more informal work.
  • As incomes rise, jobs move from primary to secondary to tertiary sectors.
  • Natural increase = birth rate − death rate; add net migration for total change.

Mission 2

  • A poverty cycle: low income → low saving and human capital → low investment and productivity → low income.
  • Weak institutions, insecure property rights, corruption and poor infrastructure deter investment; which barrier matters most depends on the country.

Mission 3

  • Market-oriented strategies use trade, prices and private firms to raise growth.
  • Removing a tariff lowers price and raises imports; consumers gain more than producers and the government lose.
  • FDI brings capital and technology, but profits may leave and links may be few.
  • How well markets work depends on institutions, sequencing and who gains.

Mission 4

  • Interventionist strategies respond to market failures: merit goods, infrastructure, infant industries.
  • Import substitution protects infant industries, but may leave them high-cost; export promotion faces world competition.
  • Results depend on government capacity, discipline and funding.

Mission 5

  • Aid: humanitarian or development; bilateral or multilateral; grants, soft loans, tied or untied. ODA target 0.7% of GNI.
  • Aid raises AD now; it raises LRAS only when spent on productive capacity. Debt in foreign currency gets heavier when the currency falls; debt relief cuts debt service.
  • IMF: balance of payments lending with conditions. World Bank: long-term development finance. NGOs and remittances also matter.
