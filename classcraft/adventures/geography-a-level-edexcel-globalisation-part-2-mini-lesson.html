Mission 1

Mission 1: In 2024 one inflow of money into Tajikistan equalled almost half of its GDP. Which flows matter most, and for which places?

Make a guess: In 2024 one inflow of money into Tajikistan equalled almost half of its GDP. Which flows matter most, and for which places?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.5b international migration to global hub cities and regions: high-wage (Singapore, London) and low-wage (UAE, Saudi Arabia)
  • 3.5c economic, social, political and environmental costs and benefits of migration for host and source locations
Mission 1

An interdependent world

  • Interdependence: places rely on each other, so a change in one affects others.
  • Economic: when oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz were disrupted in 2026, IEA members released stocks.
  • Political: NATO members treat an attack on one as an attack on all.
  • Social: migrants and their families link places through money, ideas and visits.
  • Environmental: 195 Parties adopted the Paris Agreement in 2015, because emissions anywhere warm everywhere.
Mission 1

Remittances: money sent home

  • Remittances: money that migrants send to their families.
  • To low- and middle-income countries: $608 billion (2023), $665 billion (2024), $704 billion (2025).
  • Largest totals, 2024: India $137.7 billion, Mexico $67.9 billion, Philippines $40.3 billion.
  • As a share of GDP, 2024: Tajikistan 47.2%, Tonga 39.4%, Nicaragua 26.6%, Nepal 26.0%.
Mission 1

Flows that promote stability and growth

  • Money: to low- and middle-income countries in 2023, remittances ($608 billion) exceeded FDI ($408 billion) and aid ($252 billion).
  • Remittances go straight to households: food, schooling, health care, housing.
  • People: migrants fill labour shortages in host economies.
  • Ideas and technology: returning migrants bring skills, contacts and new practices.
Mission 1

Flows that cause inequality and injustice

  • Costs: sending money cost an average of 6.36% of the amount sent in Q3 2025; the UN target is "less than 3 per cent" by 2030.
  • Uneven investment: the least developed countries got 2.7% of world FDI in 2025.
  • Dependence: 95 of 142 developing countries depended on commodities for over 60% of merchandise exports in 2019–21.
  • Loss of workers: source places lose working-age people, often the most skilled.
Quick check
?Which statement best shows that unequal flows can both help and harm the same place?
Mission 1

Global hubs: high-wage migration

  • Global hub cities concentrate finance, law and other advanced services. In 2024 London ranked Alpha++ and Singapore Alpha+ among world cities.
  • They attract high-wage migrants: professionals with scarce skills.
  • In 2021, 40.6% of London's usual residents were born outside the UK.
  • Migration links hubs to many source regions, deepening interdependence.
Mission 1

The Gulf: low-wage migration

  • The UAE and Saudi Arabia draw large numbers of low-wage migrant workers.
  • International migrants were 74% of the UAE's population in 2024.
  • Remittances paid out in 2024: UAE $58.5 billion; Saudi Arabia $46.6 billion. For comparison, the USA paid out $105.3 billion.
  • Those outflows are inflows for source countries in South Asia and beyond.
Mission 1

Costs and benefits: host and source

  • Host, benefits: labour for growth sectors; skills; tax revenue.
  • Host, costs: pressure on housing and services; political tension over immigration.
  • Source, benefits: remittances (Nepal 26.0% of GDP in 2024); returning skills.
  • Source, costs: loss of young and skilled workers; families split; dependence on jobs abroad.
  • Environmental: rapid growth of host cities raises demand for energy and water.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of an economic benefit for a source country?
Mission check

?A student says: "World trade is worth trillions, so trade is always the flow that matters most." Which response is best supported by this mission?

The hook's inflow was remittances: 47.2% of Tajikistan's GDP in 2024. Globally, trade is far larger, but for Tajikistan, Tonga or Nepal, money from migrants is huge relative to the size of the economy. These unequal flows build interdependence, and they bring both gains and costs.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Nearly half of the world's languages are endangered, yet 4 in 10 Londoners were born abroad. Is the world becoming more alike or more diverse?

Make a guess: Nearly half of the world's languages are endangered, yet 4 in 10 Londoners were born abroad. Is the world becoming more alike or more diverse?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.6a cultural diffusion and a westernised global culture via TNCs, media, tourism and migration (changing diets in Asia; Paralympic movement)
  • 3.6b cultural erosion of language, food, dress and social relations; sometimes deliberate (Papua New Guinea)
Mission 2

Culture and cultural groups

  • Culture: the shared, learned way of life of a group: language, religion, food, music, dress, customs.
  • A cultural group shares some of these traits and a sense of belonging.
  • Ethnicity: a group's shared sense of common origin and culture. Identity: how people see themselves; one person can hold several identities.
Mission 2

Cultural diffusion and a "westernised" global culture

  • Cultural diffusion: traits spread from place to place, carried by TNCs, global media, tourism and migration.
  • Media: US films took 63% of cinema admissions in Europe in 2024 (69% in 2023); European films took 32.8%.
  • Westernisation: the spread of Western, especially US, brands, media and consumer habits.
Mission 2

Changing diets in Asia: fast food in China

  • KFC opened its first restaurant in China in Beijing in 1987.
  • By 31 December 2025 there were 12,997 KFC restaurants in over 2,500 Chinese cities.
  • The menu mixes fried chicken with "rice dishes, congees, fresh vegetables, desserts, coffee, tea": a Western format adapted to local tastes.
  • Changing diets affect people (what and where they eat) and the environment (what is farmed, and how).
Mission 2

New awareness: the Paralympic movement

  • Dr Ludwig Guttmann organised the first Stoke Mandeville Games for wheelchair athletes.
  • The first Paralympic Games, in Rome in 1960, featured 400 athletes from 23 countries.
  • Paris 2024: "as many as 4,400 athletes", 22 sports and 549 medal events.
  • Global media and sport have spread awareness of opportunities for disabled people.
Quick check
?Paralympic athletes rose from 400 (Rome 1960) to as many as 4,400 (Paris 2024). How many times as many is that?
Mission 2

Cultural erosion: languages

  • Of about 7,000 documented languages, "nearly half are considered endangered".
  • In one database of 6,511 spoken languages, 37% were threatened or worse; at least 1,500 could stop being spoken by 2100.
  • Greater road density and more years of schooling were linked to endangerment; contact with other languages in itself was not.
  • Erosion can be deliberate (policies that suppress a group's language or way of life) or a side effect of roads, schools and markets. Papua New Guinea lies in the New Guinea hotspot of endangered languages.
Mission 2

More diverse places

  • In 2021, 40.6% of London's usual residents were born outside the UK (36.7% in 2011); in Brent, 56.1%.
  • Flows run both ways: Netflix's most-watched non-English series is South Korea's Squid Game (season 1), with 265.2 million views in its first 91 days.
Quick check
?Which evidence most challenges the idea that global culture is simply being westernised?
Mission 2

Homogenisation, heterogenisation, hybridisation

  • Homogenisation: places becoming more alike (language loss, global brands).
  • Heterogenisation: places becoming more diverse (migration to London).
  • Hybridisation: traits mixing into something new. Example: congee at KFC in China.
  • The answer depends on scale and on which indicator you choose.
Mission check

?In a fictional city: (1) a global coffee chain adds local pastries to its menu; (2) the share of residents born abroad rises; (3) fewer children speak the local language. Which processes are these, in order?

Both. Globally, between 37% and nearly half of languages are endangered, depending on the definition; locally, 40.6% of Londoners were born abroad. Western brands and films spread, but they adapt, and culture flows from South Korea to the world too.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Swiss voters backed limits on immigration in 2014. What did their government actually do next?

Make a guess: Swiss voters backed limits on immigration in 2014. What did their government actually do next?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.6c opposition to globalisation over cultural, economic and environmental exploitation
  • 3.8a open borders and FDI created mixed societies and diasporas; tensions (rise of extreme nationalism in Europe)
  • 3.8c groups retaining cultural identity and control of resources versus embracing change (First Nations, Canada)
Mission 3

Immigration as a political issue

  • Documented immigration: people who enter with permission (visas, free movement, refugee status).
  • Undocumented (irregular) immigration: people who enter or stay without permission.
  • Anti-immigration movements campaign to reduce or limit immigration; others campaign to keep borders open. Elections and referendums turn these positions into measurable results.
  • Government responses range from quotas and visa rules to border enforcement, regularisation and integration policy.
Mission 3

Case study: Switzerland

  • International migrants were 18.7% of Switzerland's population in 1990 and 31.1% in 2024 (UN estimates); the world figure in 2024 was 3.7%.
  • Citizens can force a national vote on a change to the constitution: a popular initiative. Support for limiting immigration can therefore be measured directly.
  • Switzerland is not in the EU, but has bilateral agreements with it, including the free movement of persons.
Quick check
?Switzerland's migrant share rose from 18.7% (1990) to 31.1% (2024). Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

The 2014 vote

  • 9 February 2014, popular initiative "Against mass immigration": 50.3% yes, accepted by 12 full and 5 half cantons; turnout 56.6%.
  • The Federal Council said the initiative "calls for quotas for all foreign nationals".
  • It had recommended rejection: firms need to recruit abroad, and quotas risked the bilateral agreements with the EU.
  • The initiative's title states its supporters' framing ("mass" immigration); the result shows a narrow, not overwhelming, majority.
Mission 3

The government's response

  • In December 2017 the Federal Council adopted rules implementing the new constitutional article on immigration (Article 121a).
  • The main measure, from 1 July 2018, was a job vacancy registration requirement, intended to help place job seekers registered at regional unemployment centres.
  • Article 121a was implemented without quotas on EU citizens; free movement with the EU continued (the 2020 initiative sought to end it).
  • Exam move: compare what a proposal asked for with what the government adopted. A vote and its implementation can differ.
Quick check
?Why did the Swiss response in 2017–18 differ from the 2014 initiative's demand for quotas?
Mission 3

2020 and 2026: two more votes

  • 27 September 2020, "For moderate immigration (Limitation Initiative)", which "aims to end the free movement of persons with the EU": 38.3% yes, rejected. The Federal Council said ending free movement would threaten jobs.
  • 14 June 2026, "No to Switzerland of 10 million! (Sustainability Initiative)": population "must remain below 10 million until 2050"; 45.2% yes, rejected. The Federal Council said it threatened prosperity, security and the bilateral path with the EU.
  • Support for limits has moved between about 38% and 50%: a persistent political issue, not a settled one.
Mission 3

Opposing globalisation: economic exploitation

  • Cochabamba, Bolivia: Aguas del Tunari, a consortium in which Bechtel held 27.5%, began running the city's water system on 1 November 1999.
  • Bechtel says rates rose by an average of 35% from January 2000; Earthjustice, an environmental law NGO, says "by more than 50 percent and in many cases much higher".
  • After protests peaking in April 2000, the government rescinded the contract. The company's claim at the World Bank's arbitration centre was settled; the tribunal formally noted the case's end on 28 March 2006.
Mission 3

Cultural and environmental opposition

  • Cultural: Slow Food began in Rome in March 1986 after "the opening of a fast food restaurant near the Spanish Steps" sparked protest; its 1989 manifesto was signed by delegates from Italy and 14 other countries.
  • Environmental: Greenpeace's 2006 report Eating up the Amazon said fast-food and supermarket chains were "serving meat likely fed soy grown on destroyed Amazon rainforest".
  • Opposition comes from citizens, NGOs and some governments (Bolivia rescinded a contract).
Quick check
?Which pairing of example and type of opposition is correct?
Mission 3

Open borders, mixed societies, tensions

  • Open borders (EU free movement), deregulation and FDI drew workers and created mixed societies and diasporas: in Switzerland, 31.1% of residents were international migrants by 2024.
  • Tensions show up in votes: three Swiss initiatives to limit immigration won 50.3%, 38.3% and 45.2%.
  • Edexcel's suggested context is the rise of extreme nationalism in Europe. Use measurable evidence (vote shares, election results) and attribute labels such as "extreme" to the source that uses them.
Mission 3

First Nations in Canada

  • Census 2021: 1.8 million Indigenous people, 5.0% of Canada's population; over 70 Indigenous languages and over 600 First Nations.
  • Statistics Canada: "The colonial history of Canada has profoundly impacted Indigenous peoples, their governance, languages and cultures".
  • The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act became law on 21 June 2021.
  • UNDRIP Article 32: states shall obtain Indigenous peoples' "free and informed consent prior to the approval of any project affecting their lands or territories and other resources".
Mission 3

Retain or embrace?

  • Retaining identity and control: language revival, self-government, and consent over mining, pipelines or forestry on traditional lands (UNDRIP Article 32).
  • Embracing change: some communities negotiate jobs, revenue or ownership stakes in the same projects.
  • The positions can sit inside one nation, and change over time.
  • Exam move (Edexcel 3.8c): name the group, the resource or cultural trait, and whether it is being kept, adapted or traded for benefits.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a group seeking to retain control of physical resources?
Mission check

?The UK ended EU free movement in 2021; its new points-based system aimed to "reduce overall levels of migration". Net migration then peaked at 944,000 (year ending March 2023) and fell to 171,000 (year ending December 2025) after further rule changes in 2024–25. What does this show about governments' responses to immigration as a political issue?

Immigration stays a political issue because votes, labour needs and international agreements pull in different directions. Switzerland voted for limits in 2014 but adopted a job registration requirement, not quotas; the UK's numbers rose, then fell, after its rules changed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Make a guess: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.7a economic (single and composite), social (HDI, GII) and environmental (pollution indices) measures of development
Mission 4

Single and composite measures

  • A single indicator measures one thing (life expectancy).
  • A composite index combines several (the HDI).
  • Measures can be economic, social or environmental. Each answers a different question.
Mission 4

Economic measures

  • GNI per capita (PPP): income per person, adjusted for what money buys locally. 2023: Guyana $46,959, Sri Lanka $12,616.
  • Economic growth: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 and 19.3% in 2025; oil and gas "have contributed to rapid GDP growth since 2019".
  • Economic sector balance: the share of output or jobs in primary, secondary and tertiary activity. A shift into oil changes Guyana's balance.
  • Limits: averages hide inequality; income can jump before lives change.
Mission 4

Social measures

  • Life expectancy at birth (2023): Guyana 70.2, Sri Lanka 77.5 years.
  • Mean years of schooling (2023): Guyana 8.7, Sri Lanka 10.8.
  • Infant mortality (deaths before age 1 per 1,000 live births, WHO estimates, 2024): Guyana 23.2, Sri Lanka 5.2.
  • Guyana's infant mortality estimate has a wide range: 12.8 to 42.8.
Quick check
?Guyana's infant mortality rate (23.2) is about how many times Sri Lanka's (5.2)?
Mission 4

A composite: the HDI

  • Three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (expected and mean years of schooling), a decent standard of living (GNI per capita, PPP).
  • Each becomes an index from 0 to 1 between fixed goalposts, e.g. life expectancy 20–85 years, income $100–$75,000 (logged).
  • HDI = geometric mean of the three: (I_health × I_education × I_income)^(1/3).
Quick check
?Guyana's 2023 dimension indices are health 0.772, education 0.652, income 0.929. Their ordinary average is 0.784, but UNDP publishes an HDI of 0.776. Why is the published value lower?
Mission 4

A second composite: the GII

  • The Gender Inequality Index measures disadvantage for women in three dimensions, from 0 (equal) to 1:
    • reproductive health: maternal mortality, adolescent births
    • empowerment: parliamentary seats, secondary education
    • labour market: labour force participation.
  • 2023: Denmark 0.003 (rank 1), Yemen 0.838 (rank 172), world 0.455.
  • Sri Lanka: 0.367 (rank 93); women held 5.3% of its parliamentary seats.
Mission 4

Environmental quality: a pollution measure

  • PM2.5: annual mean concentration of fine particles in the air (µg/m³), WHO estimates, 2023:
    • Norway 5.1 · Guyana 10.0 · Sri Lanka 21.2 · India 42.5 · Niger 50.7
  • In 2019, 99% of the world's people lived where WHO air quality guideline levels were not met.
  • Environmental measures can rank countries differently from income or HDI: Guyana's air is cleaner than Sri Lanka's.
Mission 4

Evaluating the measures

  • Validity: does it measure what it claims? GNI says little about health; seat shares say little about real power.
  • Reliability: are the data accurate, current and comparable? Estimates come with ranges; the GII's maternal mortality data refer to 2020, its other indicators to 2023.
  • Built-in judgements: income above $75,000 adds nothing to the HDI, a ceiling nine countries exceeded in 2023.
  • Coverage: the HDI ranks 193 countries, the GII only 172.
Quick check
?Which is a problem of validity rather than reliability?
Mission check

?Country P has three times Country Q's income per person, but lower life expectancy and fewer years of schooling. Their HDIs are equal. Why?

Sri Lanka scores higher on health (77.5 years against 70.2) and schooling (10.8 years against 8.7), and its infant mortality is far lower. The HDI logs income and takes a geometric mean, so Guyana's income of $46,959 against $12,616 cannot make up the gap: both score 0.776.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In 1990, two in three people in East Asia and the Pacific lived in extreme poverty. By 2025, fewer than 1 in 50 did. So why did the number of extremely poor people in sub-Saharan Africa rise?

Make a guess: In 1990, two in three people in East Asia and the Pacific lived in extreme poverty. By 2025, fewer than 1 in 50 did. So why did the number of extremely poor people in sub-Saharan Africa rise?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.7b widening income inequality globally and nationally (Gini coefficient); winners and losers
  • 3.7c contrasting regional trends in development and environmental management since 1970
Mission 5

Measuring extreme poverty

  • World Bank international poverty line: $3.00 a person a day (2021 PPP dollars); below it is extreme poverty.
  • PPP (purchasing power parity) adjusts for price differences between countries.
  • Set in June 2025, replacing the $2.15 line (2017 prices).
Mission 5

Contrasting regional progress

  • Extreme poverty rate, 1990 → 2025 (2025 is a nowcast):
    • East Asia and Pacific: 67.0% → 1.7%
    • South Asia: 49.7% → 2.1%
    • Sub-Saharan Africa: 61.5% → 44.1%
  • World: 43.4% → 9.95%.
Mission 5

Rate down, number up

  • Sub-Saharan Africa's rate fell (61.5% → 44.1%).
  • Its population grew from 521 million to 1,323 million.
  • So the number in extreme poverty rose from 320.6 million to 583.8 million.
  • Exam move: say whether a figure is a rate (share) or a number (count): they can move in opposite directions.
Quick check
?In 2025, 583.8 million of the world's 816.9 million extremely poor people lived in sub-Saharan Africa. What share is that?
Mission 5

Economic trends since 1970

  • GDP per person (constant 2015 US$), 1970 → 2025:
    • East Asia and Pacific: $1,925 → $13,812 (about 7.2 times)
    • South Asia: $389 → $2,473 (about 6.4 times)
    • Sub-Saharan Africa: $1,377 → $1,615 (about 1.2 times; it fell to $1,280 by 1990)
    • North America: $24,881 → $65,508
  • East Asia's usual explanation: export-led manufacturing that plugged into global supply chains.
Mission 5

Widening extremes

  • North America's GDP per person was 18 times sub-Saharan Africa's in 1970; 41 times in 2025.
  • The absolute gap grew from about $23,500 to about $63,900 per person.
  • Some regions converged on the richest: East Asia and Pacific rose from about 8% of North America's GDP per person (1970) to about 21% (2025), while pulling away from sub-Saharan Africa (1.4 → 8.6 times).
  • Globalisation's outcomes are differential: winners and losers between regions.
Quick check
?Which evidence best supports "a widening development gap at the extremes"?
Mission 5

Measuring inequality: Lorenz and Gini

  • A Lorenz curve plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of people, poorest first.
  • USA, 2024: poorest 20% received 5.1% of income; richest 20% 47.1%. Cumulative: 5.1, 15.1, 30.2, 52.8, ~100%.
  • The Gini coefficient is the area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve, as a share of the area under the line: 0 = all equal, 100 = one person has everything.
  • USA Gini 2024: 41.8.
Mission 5

National trends

  • Gini index (World Bank):
    • UK (developed): 28.0 (1970) → 35.7 (1990) → 32.4 (2021)
    • USA (developed): 36.6 (1970) → 41.8 (2024)
    • China (emerging): 28.2 (1981) → 43.7 (2010) → 36.0 (2022)
    • Brazil (emerging): 63.2 (1989) → 50.3 (2024)
  • World Bank (2016): within-country inequality was higher than 25 years earlier, but since 2008, for every country where it widened, two narrowed.
Quick check
?China's Gini rose from 28.2 (1981) to 43.7 (2010), then fell to 36.0 (2022). Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

Winners and losers: environments

  • Fossil CO₂ per person, 1970 → 2024: Asia 1.26 t → 4.87 t; Europe 9.21 t → 6.53 t (−29%); Africa 0.81 t → 1.00 t.
  • Forest area, 1990 → 2023: Latin America and Caribbean −14%; sub-Saharan Africa −10%; East Asia and Pacific +4%.
  • Growth has cost some environments, while richer regions are cutting emissions per person.
Mission 5

Contrasting environmental management

  • Europe: the EU Emissions Trading System (from 2005) has cut emissions from covered installations by about 47% since 2005.
  • China: a national ETS since 2021 covers about 8 billion tonnes CO₂, more than 60% of the country's CO₂; it limits emissions per unit of output, not total emissions.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: per-person emissions low, but forest loss continues.
  • Exam move ("Assess"): weigh economic and environmental trends together; say which regions gained, which lost, and on which measure.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes contrasting regional trends since 1970?
Mission check

?Region X: poverty rate fell from 50% to 40% while its population tripled. Region Y: rate fell from 50% to 10% while its population rose by half. Which region now has more extremely poor people than before?

Sub-Saharan Africa is like Region X: its rate fell only from 61.5% to 44.1% while its population rose from 521 million to 1,323 million, so the number rose from 320.6 to 583.8 million. East Asia's rate fell to 1.7% as its GDP per person rose about seven-fold since 1970.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Transporting the world's fruit and vegetables releases almost twice as much greenhouse gas as growing them. So is "buy local" the answer?

Make a guess: Transporting the world's fruit and vegetables releases almost twice as much greenhouse gas as growing them. So is "buy local" the answer?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.9a local groups and NGOs promote local sourcing (Transition Towns) as a response to globalisation; economic, social and environmental costs and benefits
Mission 6

Food miles

  • A carbon footprint is all the greenhouse gas emitted by a product or activity, in CO₂-equivalent (CO₂e).
  • Food miles: the distance food travels from where it is produced to where it is eaten.
  • Li et al. (2022) estimate food-miles emissions at about 3.0 Gt CO₂e, about 19% of total food-system emissions, because they include the whole upstream supply chain.
Quick check
?Food-miles emissions are about 3.0 Gt CO₂e, about 19% of food-system emissions. Roughly how large are total food-system emissions?
Mission 6

Where transport matters most

  • Freight for fruit and vegetables is 36% of food-miles emissions, "almost twice the amount of greenhouse gases released during their production".
  • Air freight is rare, about 0.16% of food miles, but emits about 50 times more CO₂e than a ship per tonne-kilometre.
  • So "local" matters most for fresh produce and anything flown.
Mission 6

But production often matters more

  • Impacts can vary 50-fold among producers of the same product; the lowest-impact animal products "typically exceed those of vegetable substitutes".
  • In one study of diets across the EU, food transport was only 6% of emissions, while dairy, meat and eggs were 83% (as reported by Our World in Data).
  • For most diets, what is eaten and how it is produced matter more than how far it travels.
Quick check
?One study puts transport at about 19% of food emissions; another, of EU diets, at 6%. Why can both be right?
Mission 6

Localism: the Transition movement

  • Localism: making places more self-reliant, with more food, energy and money produced and spent locally.
  • The Transition Towns movement "began in Britain and Ireland in 2006" and now spans 67 countries; Transition Network lists "around 1,000 groups" on its map.
  • Transition Town Totnes, Devon: "it all started right here in Totnes".
  • Groups share goals: "cutting fossil fuel use, boosting local economies, and strengthening community connections".
Mission 6

What Transition groups do

  • Transition groups "create renewable energy projects, support local food production, encourage new businesses, bring nature back into cities".
  • Typical projects: community gardens, repair cafés, local food initiatives.
  • The network's own count from 21 projects: 43 new social enterprises and 21 tonnes of food saved from landfill a year. These are self-reported totals.
Mission 6

Benefits of local sourcing

  • Economic: money spent with local producers circulates locally (a local multiplier); new small businesses and social enterprises.
  • Social: stronger community connections; volunteering; awareness of where food comes from.
  • Environmental: lower transport emissions for fresh produce, and no air freight.
Mission 6

Costs of local sourcing

  • Economic, elsewhere: exporters in developing countries depend on distant buyers. In 2024 Kenya exported US$281.3 million of vegetables, of which US$81.8 million (29%) went to the UK.
  • Environmental: "local" is not automatically low-carbon; production method and diet matter more (see above).
  • Social and economic, at home: projects rely on volunteers and grants, and local food can cost more or offer less choice out of season.
Quick check
?Which is an economic cost of a campaign to replace imported vegetables with local ones?
Mission 6

Evaluating localism

  • Scale: Transition groups act locally; global food-system emissions are about 15.8 Gt CO₂e a year (calculated: 3.0 ÷ 0.19).
  • Who gains, who loses: local producers gain; distant exporters may lose.
  • What matters most: shifting diets towards plant-based foods often cuts more than shortening supply chains.
  • Exam move (Edexcel 3.9a): sort each point into economic, social, environmental and cost or benefit, then judge at which scale it matters.
Mission check

?A Transition group in an English town campaigns for local shops to replace Kenyan vegetables with locally grown ones. In 2024, 29% of Kenya's vegetable exports by value went to the UK. Is "buy local" the answer here?

Sometimes. Transport is about 19% of food emissions, and for fruit and vegetables it outweighs production, so local sourcing helps most for fresh and air-freighted produce. But diet usually matters more, and one place's local gain can be an exporter's loss.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Shoppers pay extra for ethical labels. Do farmers, workers and the environment actually gain?

Make a guess: Shoppers pay extra for ethical labels. Do farmers, workers and the environment actually gain?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.9b fair trade and ethical consumption may reduce environmental degradation and trade inequality, and improve working conditions
Mission 7

Ethical consumption

  • Ethical consumption: choosing goods partly for how they affect people and the environment, not only for price.
  • Fair trade schemes aim to give producers in poorer countries a better, more stable deal.
  • Other schemes target working conditions (factory safety) or the environment (farming standards).
  • NGOs, trade unions and pressure groups run or push many of them.
Mission 7

How Fairtrade works

  • A minimum price: a floor paid when market prices fall below it. For washed Arabica coffee it rose from $1.40 to $1.80 per pound, for contracts from 1 August 2023.
  • A Fairtrade Premium on top, which producer organisations invest together.
  • The Fairtrade Standards "incorporate comprehensive social, economic, and environmental criteria" and aim to "protect workers' rights".
Mission 7

Fairtrade's scale, 2024

  • 1,824 producer organisations; 1.87 million farmers and workers; 90% of them farmers.
  • €201.6 million of Premium paid to producers.
  • 75% of the farmers and workers were in Africa and the Middle East.
Quick check
?In 2024 Fairtrade producers reported growing 525,547 tonnes of certified coffee; buyers reported buying 142,413 tonnes on Fairtrade terms. What percentage was sold as Fairtrade?
Mission 7

Does it reduce the inequalities of trade?

  • Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of global coffee exports in 2009; its benefits are "hotly debated".
  • A study for the UK government in Ethiopia and Uganda found that "in some cases" wage workers in areas with Fairtrade producer organisations were "significantly worse paid" than in comparable areas without them.
  • So the gains go mainly to certified farmers on the share they sell as Fairtrade; hired workers may not gain.
Mission 7

Working conditions: the Bangladesh Accord

  • After the Rana Plaza factory collapse in April 2013, over 40 companies and global and Bangladeshi trade unions signed the Accord on fire and building safety. More than 220 brands later joined.
  • It is a "legally-binding agreement between brands & trade unions", with independent inspections.
  • As of 17 March 2022: 1,392 factories covered; overall remediation progress on the problems found 92%; 401 factories had completed all their initial remediation.
Quick check
?The Accord reported 92% remediation progress, yet only 401 of 1,392 factories had completed all their initial remediation. How can both be true?
Mission 7

The environment

  • Fairtrade's standards include environmental criteria, and the Premium can fund community or farm projects.
  • But Fairtrade's own Premium data show where money is spent, not the "outcomes or impacts" of that spending.
  • So environmental gains are possible but not well measured: an evidence gap.
Mission 7

Local action, global links

  • The Fair Trade Towns campaign began in Garstang, UK, in 2000; by 2017 there were over 2,000 in 29 countries.
  • A town's purchasing choices connect to producer co-operatives far away: local pressure groups acting on global trade.
  • Exam move: say who gains (certified farmers, some workers), how much (share of sales), and what is not yet measured (environmental impact).
Mission check

?A supermarket switches all its bananas to a fair trade label. Based on the evidence, who is most likely to gain?

Some gain, some of the time. Fairtrade gives certified producers a price floor and a Premium (€201.6 million in 2024), but only about 27% of certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms, hired workers may not gain, and environmental impacts are not well measured. The binding Bangladesh Accord fixed most safety problems, but by 2022 only 401 of 1,392 factories had fixed every one.

Make a guess: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

Mission 8

England's balance

  • In 2024/25, English local authorities managed 25.2 million tonnes of waste:
    • incineration: 12.7 Mt, 50.3%
    • recycling: 10.4 Mt
    • landfill: 1.4 Mt, 5.5%.
  • England's waste-from-households recycling rate: 43.8% in 2024.
Quick check
?English local authorities managed 25.2 million tonnes of waste in 2024/25 and sent 10.4 million tonnes for recycling. What percentage was recycled?
Mission 8

Recycling varies by place

  • Among England's local authorities, household waste recycling rates ranged from 17.0% to 63.9% in 2024/25.
  • Lowest: Tower Hamlets (inner London) 17.0%. Highest: Stratford-on-Avon District (West Midlands) 63.9%.
  • Likely reasons to test: housing type (flats with shared bins vs houses with gardens), what each council collects, and how often.
Mission 8

Recycling varies by product

  • Plastics: only about 9% of all plastic waste had been recycled (estimate cited in 2018).
  • E-waste: 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled in 2022.
  • Food waste collected separately for recycling in England rose 6.7% to 535 thousand tonnes in 2024.
  • Recycling reduces resource consumption and ecological footprints only when recycled material replaces new material. The EU's circular material use rate was 12.2% in 2024.
Quick check
?Which is the most plausible explanation to test for Tower Hamlets' low recycling rate?
Mission 8

Trade: why waste crossed oceans

  • "Upward of half of the plastic waste intended for recycling has been exported".
  • High-income countries supplied 87% of plastic waste exports (1988–2016).
  • China had imported a cumulative 45% of the world's plastic waste since 1992.
Mission 8

China closes its door

  • In 2017 China announced a policy "permanently banning the import of nonindustrial plastic waste", in force from the end of 2017.
  • Imports, thousand tonnes, 2016 → 2018:
    • China 8,801.6 → 51.4 (UN Comtrade, all plastic scrap, weight estimated; Brooks et al., using the same UN database, give 7.35 million tonnes for 2016)
    • Malaysia 287.7 → 872.5; Thailand 69.5 → 552.7; Türkiye 152.9 → 424.6.
  • Exporters also had to handle more at home, where the alternatives were landfill or incineration.
Mission 8

Rules tighten

  • Basel Convention: plastic-waste amendments adopted in 2019, effective 1 January 2021.
  • EU: exports of plastic waste to non-OECD countries banned from 21 November 2026.
  • Limits: Türkiye's imports still rose to 677.7 thousand tonnes by 2024. Rules shifted flows rather than stopping them.
Quick check
?Malaysia imported 287.7 thousand tonnes of plastic waste in 2016 and 872.5 thousand tonnes in 2018. How many times as much did it import in 2018?
Mission check

?In 2022, 5.1 billion kg of e-waste crossed borders; an estimated 3.3 billion kg went from high-income to middle- and low-income countries through uncontrolled movements. If a major importer banned e-waste imports, what does the plastic case suggest?

China banned most plastic waste imports from the end of 2017. Its imports fell 99.4%, while Malaysia's tripled and Thailand's and Türkiye's rose. Exporters had to recycle, incinerate or landfill more at home, and the Basel Convention amendments (2021) now control these flows more tightly.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Places are interdependent economically, politically, socially and environmentally; unequal flows of money, people, ideas and technology bring both growth and inequality.
  • High-wage migrants go to hubs such as London and Singapore; low-wage migrants to the UAE and Saudi Arabia; both host and source gain and lose.

Mission 2

  • TNCs, media, tourism and migration diffuse a westernised culture (KFC in China) and new awareness (the Paralympics), while cultural erosion threatens languages.
  • The world is becoming more alike and more diverse at once, depending on scale and indicator.

Mission 3

  • Votes and elections measure support for limiting immigration; governments respond by balancing that support against labour needs and agreements (Switzerland 2014–2026).
  • Opposition to globalisation can be economic (Cochabamba), cultural (Slow Food) or environmental (Amazon soy); some groups retain control of culture and resources while others embrace change (First Nations).

Mission 4

  • Economic (GNI per capita, growth, sector balance), social (life expectancy, infant mortality, schooling) and composite (HDI, GII) measures can rank the same countries differently. So can environmental measures such as PM2.5.

Mission 5

  • Rate and number differ: slow falls in rate plus fast population growth raised sub-Saharan Africa's number of extremely poor people.
  • Since 1970 East and South Asia have grown fast while sub-Saharan Africa stalled; the gap between the richest and poorest regions widened (18 → 41 times).
  • Gini/Lorenz show national inequality: rising in the UK and USA since 1970; rising then falling in China; falling in Brazil. Environmental trends and management also differ by region.

Mission 6

  • Food miles are about 19% of food-system emissions on one global estimate (6% for EU diets in another); for fresh fruit and vegetables, transport can outweigh production.
  • Transition Towns (from 2006, Totnes) promote local food and energy: economic, social and environmental benefits locally, with costs for distant exporters and limits of scale.

Mission 7

  • Fair trade and ethical consumption can raise producer incomes, improve working conditions and set environmental standards.
  • The gains are real but limited and uneven: small market shares, gaps for hired workers, and environmental impacts that are rarely measured.

Mission 8

  • Recycling rates vary by place (17.0% to 63.9% across English councils) and by product (plastics about 9% globally).
