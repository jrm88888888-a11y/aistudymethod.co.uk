Mission 1

Mission 1: A body is found with no witnesses. What clues inside and on it reveal how long ago the person died?

Make a guess: A body is found with no witnesses. What clues inside and on it reveal how long ago the person died?

Mission 1

Five clues

  • Forensic scientists estimate the time since death from:
    • Body temperature: useful for about the first day.
    • Muscle contraction (rigor mortis): about the first two days.
    • Extent of decomposition: days to weeks.
    • Stage of succession of organisms on the body: days to months.
    • Forensic entomology (insect life stages): days to weeks.
  • Each gives a range, not an exact time.
  • Clues are combined: the true time must fit all of them.
Mission 1

Body temperature

  • In life, respiration releases heat; core temperature stays near 37 °C.
  • After death, respiration stops. No heat is produced.
  • The body loses heat to its surroundings until it reaches ambient temperature.
  • Faster cooling: cold surroundings, wind, wet clothing, water, a small or thin body.
  • Slower cooling: warm surroundings, clothing or bedding, more body fat.
  • Measured in the core (rectum or liver), not the skin.
  • Once the body reaches ambient temperature, this clue gives only a minimum time.

Mission 1

Rigor mortis: muscles lock

  • After death, no oxygen reaches the muscles. Aerobic respiration stops.
  • Anaerobic respiration runs briefly: lactate builds up, pH falls. Then ATP runs out.
  • Calcium ions leak from the stores in the muscle fibres. Myosin heads bind to actin: cross-bridges form.
  • ATP is needed to detach myosin from actin. With no ATP, the cross-bridges stay locked. Muscles become rigid.
  • Rigor passes off when enzymes break down the muscle proteins.
  • Typical pattern near room temperature (it varies widely):
    • Starts in small muscles (face, jaw) about 2–6 h after death.
    • Whole body rigid by about 12 h (sometimes up to 24 h).
    • Passes off over about 24–48 h.
  • Warmer surroundings, or hard exercise before death: faster onset.
  • Say: "no ATP, so myosin cannot detach from actin" — not: "no ATP, so the muscles contract".
Quick check
?Why do the muscles of a body become rigid a few hours after death?
Mission 1

Decomposition

  • Autolysis: the body's own enzymes, released from dead cells, start to digest tissues.
  • Putrefaction: bacteria, mostly from the gut, spread through the body and digest it.
  • Visible stages (Payne, 1965, pig carcasses): fresh → bloated → active decay → advanced decay → dry → remains.
    • Skin turns greenish, first over the abdomen.
    • Bacterial gases bloat the body.
    • Soft tissues liquefy; finally only bone, hair and dry skin remain.
  • Faster when warm, moist and open to oxygen and insects; slower when cold, dry or buried.
  • The stage reached gives a broad time range.
Mission 1

Decomposers recycle carbon

  • Decomposers: bacteria and fungi that feed on dead organic matter.
  • They secrete enzymes onto the body: digestion happens outside their cells (extracellular digestion).
  • Proteins, carbohydrates and lipids → small soluble molecules (amino acids, sugars, fatty acids).
  • They absorb these and use them in respiration.
  • Respiration releases the carbon as carbon dioxide to the air (in waterlogged, anaerobic places, some as methane).
  • Plants take up CO2 in photosynthesis: carbon is recycled.
  • Without decomposers, carbon would stay locked in dead bodies.
Quick check
?A dead mouse decomposes on the soil surface over several weeks. What happens to most of the carbon that was in its body?
Mission 1

Succession on a body

  • A body is a new habitat. Organisms colonise it in a predictable sequence: a succession.
  • Each group changes the body and makes it suitable for the next.
    • Bacteria first, from inside the body.
    • Blowflies arrive within minutes to hours and lay eggs in wounds and openings.
    • Flesh flies and beetles that prey on fly larvae follow.
    • Once the body is drying, beetles that eat dry skin, and mites, take over.
  • The groups present show the stage of succession, and so a time range.
  • Like succession on bare rock: colonisers change conditions; others replace them.
Mission 1Worked example

Forensic entomology

  • Blowfly life cycle: egg → larva (3 instars) → pupa → adult.
  • Development is controlled by enzymes, so its rate depends on temperature.
  • Find the oldest insect stage on the body. Its age = the minimum time since death.

Mission 2

PCR: copy one stretch of DNA

  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies one chosen stretch of DNA, in a tube.
  • It needs:
    • the template DNA sample
    • two primers: short single strands of DNA
    • free DNA nucleotides
    • a heat-stable DNA polymerase
  • The usual enzyme is Taq polymerase.
  • The cycle repeats, about 25–35 times. Each cycle doubles the target.
Mission 2

One cycle, three steps

  • 1 Denature, about 95 °C. Hydrogen bonds between bases break. The strands separate.
  • 2 Anneal, about 55 °C. Primers bind by complementary base pairing to each end of the target, one on each strand.
  • 3 Extend, about 72 °C. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer, building new complementary strands.
  • The new strands become templates in the next cycle.
  • Say: "heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands" — not: "heat breaks the DNA into nucleotides".
Mission 2

Why primers? Why a heat-stable enzyme?

  • DNA polymerase can only add to an existing strand. Primers give it a start.
  • The two primers sit either side of the target. Only the DNA between them is amplified.
  • Primers also stop the separated strands re-pairing at once.
  • Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs.
  • It is not denatured at 95 °C, so it works through every cycle. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first cycle.
  • 72 °C is close to Taq's optimum temperature.
  • Say: "primers anneal to complementary sequences" — not: "primers copy the DNA" or "primers separate the strands".
Quick check
?Why is Taq polymerase, rather than a DNA polymerase from human cells, used in the polymerase chain reaction?
Mission 2

Worked example: how many copies?

  • Every molecule is copied in every cycle, so the number doubles each cycle.
  • Copies after n cycles = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Example: 1 molecule, 30 cycles.
    • 1 × 2³⁰ = 1 073 741 824
    • About 1.1 × 10⁹ copies: over a billion.
  • Example: 5 molecules, 3 cycles: 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 5 × 2³ = 40.
  • This assumes every strand is copied each cycle. In a real tube, copying slows in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.
  • Say: "multiply by 2ⁿ" — not: "multiply by 2 × n".
Quick check
?A PCR reaction starts with 3 double-stranded DNA molecules of the target section. How many double-stranded molecules of the target are present after 4 complete cycles?
Mission 2

Gel electrophoresis: sorting by length

  • DNA samples go into wells at one end of a gel. A voltage is applied across the gel.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups). Every fragment moves towards the positive electrode.
  • The gel is a mesh. Shorter fragments slip through more easily and travel further.
  • Fragments of the same length travel the same distance: they form one band.
  • A ladder of fragments of known length runs in one lane, for comparison.
  • Say: "shorter fragments travel further" — not: "longer fragments travel further because they carry more charge".
Quick check
?Three DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1200 base pairs are loaded into the same well of a gel and a voltage is applied. Which statement describes the result?
Mission 2Practical

Running a DNA gel

  • Core practical 14: use gel electrophoresis to separate DNA fragments of different length.
  • Method:
    • Cast an agarose gel with a comb; remove the comb to leave wells.
    • Cover the gel with buffer. Wells at the negative end.
    • Load each sample with a dense loading dye. Load a ladder in one lane.
    • Run at a set voltage until the dye nears the far end. Stain to show the bands.
  • Keep the same for every lane: gel, voltage, run time. The ladder is the reference.
  • Measure each band's distance from its well. Compare with the ladder to estimate length.

Sizing a fragment

Mission 2

What PCR and gels are used for

  • Forensics: amplify DNA from a hair, a drop of blood or skin cells, then compare it (see Mission 3).
  • Paternity and relationships: compare amplified regions between people, animals or plants.
  • Diagnosing infections: detect a pathogen's DNA or RNA, even at low levels.
  • Genetic testing: amplify a gene, then test it for a particular allele.
  • Ancient and degraded DNA: amplify the little that survives.
  • Before sequencing: make enough copies of a gene to read its base sequence.
Mission check

?The human ACE gene has two common alleles. Allele I contains an extra stretch of about 300 base pairs that allele D lacks. One pair of primers on either side of this stretch gives a 490 base pair product from allele I and a 190 base pair product from allele D. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, one person's lane shows two bands. Which interpretation is correct?

Primers pick the stretch to copy; the gel sorts the copies by length. Together they turn a trace of DNA into a genotype.

Mission 3

Repeats that vary between people

  • Much of the genome does not code for proteins. Some of it is tandem repeats: a short sequence repeated end to end, e.g. GATA GATA GATA.
  • Forensic profiles use short tandem repeats (STRs): units of 2–6 bases.
  • The number of repeats at a locus varies between people. More repeats: a longer fragment.
  • You inherit one allele at each locus from each parent: one or two bands per locus.
  • Many loci, many alleles at each: the chance that two people share all of them is very low. Identical twins are the exception.
Mission 3

Making a profile

  • The method is called DNA profiling.
  • 1 Extract DNA from the sample.
  • 2 Amplify several repeat loci by PCR. Primers bind either side of each repeat.
  • 3 Separate the fragments by length: electrophoresis.
  • 4 Detect the bands: labelled primers or labelled probes make them visible. Compare samples.
Quick check
?Two people have different alleles at one short tandem repeat locus. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, their bands for this locus are in different places. What difference between the alleles does the gel detect?
Mission 3

Worked example: why test many loci?

  • Illustrative: suppose 1 in 10 unrelated people share a profile at one locus by chance.
  • Loci on different chromosomes are inherited independently. Multiply the chances.
  • 2 loci: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1 in 100.
  • 10 loci: (1/10)¹⁰ = 1 in 10¹⁰: about one person in 10 000 000 000.
  • Real casework: the UK system tests 16 repeat loci; the US core set has 20.
  • Relatives share more alleles, so their chance of matching is higher.
  • Say: "the chance of a coincidental match is very low" — not: "the profile proves the suspect did it".
Quick check
?A forensic laboratory compares a sample from a crime scene with a suspect's DNA using 20 different markers rather than one. Why does this make the result more reliable?
Mission 3

Reading a profile

  • Forensic match: the sample could come from a person only if every band matches.
  • One band different: that person is excluded.
  • Paternity: each band in a child came from the mother or the father.
    • Cross out the child's bands that match the mother.
    • Every band left must be in the father's profile.
    • The father has other bands the child did not inherit: that is expected.
  • Matching bands are consistent with a relationship. Different bands exclude one.
Quick check
?A DNA profile was prepared for a mother, her child and a man who may be the father. The fragment lengths (in base pairs) in each profile were: mother 100, 220, 260, 340; child 100, 150, 220, 300; man 150, 180, 300, 330. What can be concluded?
Mission 3

Uses: identity and relationships

  • Forensics: link a sample from a scene to a person, or exclude a suspect.
  • Paternity and family relationships; identifying human remains.
  • Animals and plants: confirm the parents of a pedigree animal; identify a crop variety; trace the source of illegally traded wildlife.
  • Profiles show how closely related two plants or two animals are: relatives share more bands.
Mission check

Mission 4

The HIV particle

  • HIV (human immunodeficiency virus) is a retrovirus.
  • Genome: two copies of single-stranded RNA.
  • Enzymes packed inside: reverse transcriptase (and others).
  • Capsid: a protein coat around the RNA.
  • Matrix: a protein layer under the envelope.
  • Envelope: a lipid bilayer, taken from the host cell membrane.
  • Attachment proteins (glycoproteins) stick out of the envelope.
Mission 4

Inside a helper T cell

  • (1) The attachment protein binds a CD4 receptor on a helper T cell.
  • (2) The envelope fuses with the cell membrane. Capsid enters, then releases RNA and enzymes.
  • (3) Reverse transcriptase copies the viral RNA into DNA.
  • (4) The viral DNA enters the nucleus and is inserted into the host cell's DNA.
  • (5) The host cell transcribes and translates it: viral RNA and proteins.
  • (6) New particles assemble and bud out, wrapped in host membrane.
  • The inserted DNA can stay inactive for years, then switch on.
Quick check
?HIV enters some white blood cells but not others. Which statement identifies the cells it mainly enters, and why?
Mission 4

Read the data: from HIV to AIDS

  • The graph: a typical untreated infection (schematic).
  • Weeks 2–6: virus multiplies fast. Helper T cells fall. Often flu-like illness.
  • Years: few or no symptoms. Helper T cells fall slowly.
  • AIDS: helper T cells below about 200 per mm³, or an AIDS-defining illness.
  • Few helper T cells: B cells are not activated (few antibodies); killer T cells are not stimulated.
  • So opportunistic infections and some cancers take hold: e.g. TB, pneumonia, Kaposi's sarcoma.
  • These, not HIV itself, cause most symptoms and deaths.
  • Later signs: weight loss, dementia. Death usually follows an opportunistic infection.
  • Say: "HIV destroys helper T cells" — not: "HIV destroys the immune system".
Quick check
?A person with untreated HIV infection eventually becomes unable to produce antibodies against new pathogens, even though tests show that B-lymphocytes are still present in their blood in substantial numbers. Which statement explains this?
Mission 4

TB: from a cough to a lung destroyed

  • Mycobacterium tuberculosis: spread in droplets from a cough.
  • (1) Inhaled into alveoli. Macrophages engulf it, but it survives inside them.
  • (2) Immune cells wall off infected macrophages: a tubercle (granuloma). Often no symptoms. Bacteria can stay dormant for years.
  • (3) If immunity weakens (e.g. HIV, malnutrition, old age), bacteria multiply. Lung tissue is destroyed.
  • Symptoms: persistent cough (sometimes with blood), fever, night sweats, weight loss.
  • Can spread to other organs. Untreated, often fatal.
  • HIV and TB together: each makes the other worse.
Mission 4

The evolutionary race

  • Hosts evolve defences. Pathogens evolve ways to evade them. Each selects the other.
  • The evasion mechanisms are the evidence: they only make sense as adaptations to host defences.
  • TB: waxy wall resists digestion; blocks lysosomes fusing with its vesicle; hides in tubercles; slows antigen presentation.
  • HIV: reverse transcriptase does not proofread. High mutation rate; billions of new virions a day.
  • HIV: surface antigens keep changing, so antibodies and memory cells fall behind.
  • HIV: kills the helper T cells that coordinate the response; hides as inactive DNA; reduces antigen display on infected cells.
  • Say: "random mutations; variants that escape are selected" — not: "the virus changes to escape".
Quick check
?Macrophages in the lungs engulf Mycobacterium tuberculosis, yet many of the bacteria stay alive for years. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two people infected with HIV were compared. Person X had 900 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ of blood and no other infections. Person Y had 150 helper T-lymphocytes per mm³ and had developed pneumonia caused by a fungus that rarely causes disease in healthy people. Which conclusion is best supported?

HIV turns helper T cells into virus factories and destroys them. Without them, other pathogens do the killing.

Mission 5

What makes a pathogen

  • A pathogen is an organism that causes disease.
  • Diseases caused by pathogens are infectious: they can pass from host to host.
  • Four groups include pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists (protists).
  • Most microbes on and in you cause no disease. Being a microbe does not make something a pathogen.
  • Two named pathogens: Mycobacterium tuberculosis, a bacterium (TB), and HIV, a virus (AIDS). How they invade cells: Mission 4.
Mission 5

Ways into the body

  • Gas exchange surface: inhaled droplets reach the lungs (TB, influenza).
  • Digestive system: contaminated food or water (cholera; M. bovis in milk).
  • Skin: cuts, bites, needles (malaria via mosquito bites; HIV via shared needles).
  • Reproductive system: sexual contact (HIV).
  • Eyes and the placenta (mother to foetus) are other routes.
  • Each route is a surface where the body's barriers can be breached (see Mission 6).
Quick check
?Which pathogen is matched to its usual route into the body?
Mission check

?One form of sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense, a single-celled eukaryote. It lives in wild antelope and cattle and reaches humans through the bite of tsetse flies. Which statement is correct?

A pathogen is defined by causing disease, not by being small or being a microbe. Then the question is how it gets in.

Mission 6

Skin and mucous membranes

  • Skin: an outer layer of tough, dead cells. Few pathogens can get through: a physical barrier.
  • Oily sebum keeps the skin surface acidic, inhibiting many pathogens: a chemical barrier.
  • Mucous membranes line the airways, gut, reproductive tract and eyes. No dead-cell layer here.
  • Their mucus traps pathogens. In the airways, cilia sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed.
  • These primary defences are always in place, before any infection.
Mission 6

More barriers

  • Tears contain lysozyme, an enzyme that hydrolyses bacterial cell walls.
  • Stomach acid kills most pathogens swallowed in food and water.
  • Skin and gut flora: harmless resident microbes outcompete pathogens for space and nutrients.
Quick check
?Chemicals in cigarette smoke stop the cilia in the airways from beating. Why do smokers get more chest infections?
Mission 6

Inflammation

  • Damaged tissue and mast cells release histamine and cytokines.
  • Arterioles dilate: more blood flows in. Heat and redness.
  • Capillary walls become more permeable: plasma, proteins and white blood cells leave. Swelling.
  • Cytokines attract phagocytes to the site.
Mission 6

Interferon

  • A cell infected by a virus releases interferon.
  • Interferon acts on neighbouring cells: they make proteins that block viral protein synthesis.
  • So the virus cannot reproduce in those cells, and its spread slows.
  • Interferon works against any virus: it is non-specific.

Mission 6

Phagocytosis

  • Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries (amoeboid movement) and move to the infection site.
  • A phagocyte recognises foreign molecules on the pathogen.
  • It engulfs the pathogen by endocytosis, enclosing it in a vesicle: a phagosome.
  • Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the pathogen.
  • Soluble products are absorbed.
  • Some phagocytes then display the pathogen's antigens.
  • Say: "digested inside a phagosome by lysosomal enzymes" — not: "enzymes are released onto the pathogen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium?
Mission check

?In a phagocyte, the lysosomes cannot fuse with phagosomes, but every other step works normally. What happens to a bacterium that meets this phagocyte?

Barriers keep most pathogens out. For those that get in, a phagocyte must both engulf and digest.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Body temperature, rigor mortis, decomposition, succession and insect life stages each give a time range; they are combined.
  • Rigor: no ATP, so myosin stays bound to actin. Decomposers digest dead matter with extracellular enzymes and respire the products, returning carbon to the air as CO2.

Mission 2

  • PCR repeats denature, anneal and extend; primers mark the target and each cycle doubles it (starting number × 2ⁿ).
  • Taq polymerase survives 95 °C; annealing about 55 °C; extension about 72 °C.
  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments travel further.

Mission 3

  • Repeat loci vary in length between individuals; PCR copies them and a gel sorts them by length.
  • Chances at independent loci multiply, so more loci make a chance match less likely.
  • One band that cannot be explained excludes; matching bands are consistent with, not proof of, identity or relationship.

Mission 4

  • HIV binds helper T cells, copies its RNA into DNA, inserts it into host DNA and is made by the cell; losing helper T cells leads to AIDS and opportunistic infections.

Mission 5

  • A pathogen is something that causes disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists include pathogens; most microbes do not.

Mission 6

  • Barriers stop entry: skin (physical and chemical), mucus and cilia; clotting seals cuts.
  • Innate defences respond the same way to any pathogen; phagocytes engulf by endocytosis and digest with lysosomal enzymes in a phagosome.
