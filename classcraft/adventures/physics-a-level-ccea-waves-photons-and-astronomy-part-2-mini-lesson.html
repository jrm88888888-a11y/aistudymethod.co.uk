Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do opticians describe a lens by its power rather than by its focal length?

Make a guess: Why do opticians describe a lens by its power rather than by its focal length?

Mission 1

Lens power

  • Power P = 1/f, with f in metres.
  • Unit: dioptre (D) = m⁻¹.
  • Converging lens: P positive. Diverging lens: P negative (f negative, real is positive).
  • f = 25 cm = 0.25 m → P = +4.0 D. f = −50 cm → P = −2.0 D.
  • More strongly curved lens → shorter f → larger power: it bends light more.
Mission 1

Lenses together

  • Thin lenses in contact: P = P1 + P2 + …
  • Example: +5.0 D with −2.0 D → +3.0 D, so f = 1/3.0 = 0.33 m.
  • Focal lengths do not add: you would need 1/f = 1/f1 + 1/f2. Powers just add.
Quick check
?A thin converging lens of focal length 40 cm is placed in contact with a thin diverging lens of focal length 20 cm. What is the power of the combination?
Mission 1

Finding f and testing the lens equation

  • Quick estimate: focus a distant window on a screen. Lens-to-screen distance ≈ f.
  • Accurate: illuminated object, converging lens, screen on an optical bench. Place the lens; move the screen until the image is sharp.
  • Record u and v for several positions (real images only, u > f).
  • Plot 1/v against 1/u: a straight line of gradient −1, with both intercepts equal to 1/f. This verifies 1/u + 1/v = 1/f.
Mission 1

Power in use

  • Lens equation in powers: 1/u + 1/v = P.
  • Example: object 0.50 m from a +3.0 D lens: 1/v = 3.0 − 1/0.50 = 1.0 → v = +1.0 m, a real image.
  • Camera lenses often contain a doublet: two thin lenses glued in contact. Their powers add, so a weak diverging lens can correct a strong converging one.
  • (Lenses spaced apart do not simply add.)
Quick check
?An object is 0.20 m from a thin converging lens of power +8.0 D. How far from the lens is the image?
Mission check

?A camera lens must have a focal length of 50 mm. It is made of two thin lenses in contact. One is converging, with focal length 40 mm. What must the focal length of the other be?

Power is 1/f in dioptres. Lenses in contact simply add their powers, which is why opticians, and lens designers, use it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do short-sighted people need lenses that spread light out rather than focus it?

Make a guess: Why do short-sighted people need lenses that spread light out rather than focus it?

Mission 2

The eye as a lens system

  • Light is refracted by the cornea (most of the bending) and then the eye lens.
  • A real, inverted, diminished image forms on the retina.
  • The lens-to-retina distance is fixed. So to focus at different distances, the eye changes its power.
  • Accommodation: ciliary muscles change the lens shape. Fatter lens → more powerful → focuses near objects.
  • Relaxed eye: thinnest lens, least power, focused at its far point.
Mission 2

Near point and far point

  • Near point: the closest point the eye can focus. Normal eye: about 25 cm.
  • Far point: the furthest point the eye can focus. Normal eye: infinity.
  • Range of vision: everything between the near point and the far point.
Quick check
?With age, the eye lens stiffens: the ciliary muscles can no longer make it as fat as before. How does this affect a person's vision?
Mission 2

Myopia (short sight)

  • Eye too powerful for its length (often the eyeball is too long).
  • Parallel rays from a distant object focus in front of the retina: blurred.
  • Far point is closer than infinity. Near point is often closer than 25 cm.
  • Correction: a diverging lens (negative power). It spreads the rays so they seem to come from the far point.
  • The eye can focus there, so the image lands on the retina.
Mission 2

Hypermetropia (long sight)

  • Eye too weak for its length (often the eyeball is too short).
  • Rays from a near object would focus behind the retina: blurred.
  • Near point is further than 25 cm. Distant objects can usually be seen.
  • Correction: a converging lens (positive power). Rays from an object at 25 cm seem to come from the eye's near point.
Quick check
?A student can read a page 20 cm away clearly, but road signs more than 3 m away look blurred. Which defect does she have, and which lens corrects it?
Mission 2

Where the lens puts the image

  • The lens forms a virtual image that the eye can focus. Treat the lens as at the eye.
  • Real is positive: virtual image distance v is negative.
  • Myopia: object at infinity → image at the far point. 1/f = 1/∞ + 1/v, so f = −(far point).
  • Far point 50 cm: f = −0.50 m, P = −2.0 D.
  • Hypermetropia: object at 0.25 m → image at the near point (v = −near point): 1/f = 1/0.25 + 1/v.
Quick check
?Asha's far point is 80 cm from her eye; Ben's is 2.0 m from his. Which statement about their correcting lenses is right?
Quick check
?A man sees distant hills clearly, but his near point is 1.2 m from his eye. He wants to read a page held 25 cm away. Which lens does he need, and what must it do?
Mission check

?A short-sighted woman's glasses have a power of −2.5 D. With them, she sees distant objects clearly. Where is her far point without glasses?

A short-sighted eye is too powerful. A diverging lens takes away power, so distant objects seem to sit at the eye's own far point.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can't you read up close through a short-sighted friend's glasses?

Make a guess: Why can't you read up close through a short-sighted friend's glasses?

Mission 3

Range of vision through a lens

  • The eye looks at the lens's virtual image. It sees clearly only if the image lies between its own near and far points.
  • So each end of the new range is an object distance u:
  • New near point: the u whose image is at the eye's near point.
  • New far point: the u whose image is at the eye's far point. Converging lens, eye's far point at infinity: u = f.
  • Diverging lens: distant objects stay in range if their image (at |f|) lies inside the eye's range.
  • Lens at the eye, real is positive, v negative: 1/u = P − 1/v.
Mission 3

Worked example: reading glasses

  • Long-sighted eye: near point 75 cm, far point infinity.
  • Lens for reading at 25 cm: P = 1/0.25 + 1/(−0.75) = 4.0 − 1.33 = +2.7 D (f = 0.375 m).
  • New near point: 25 cm, by design.
  • New far point: image at infinity → u = f = 38 cm.
  • New range: 25 cm to 38 cm. Further objects blur: take the glasses off to look across the room.
Quick check
?A long-sighted woman has a near point of 50 cm and a far point at infinity. She wears reading glasses of power +2.0 D. What is her range of vision while wearing them?
Mission 3

Worked example: short sight

  • Short-sighted eye: near point 10 cm, far point 25 cm.
  • Lens for distant objects: f = −0.25 m, P = −4.0 D.
  • New far point: infinity, by design.
  • New near point: v = −0.10 m → 1/u = −4.0 − 1/(−0.10) = −4.0 + 10 = 6.0 → u = 17 cm.
  • New range: 17 cm to infinity. The near point moves out a little: the price of seeing far.
  • A weaker lens under-corrects: the new far point is finite.
Quick check
?A short-sighted student's far point is 0.50 m from his eye. He wears old glasses of power −1.5 D. What is his far point while wearing them?
Mission 3

Someone else's glasses

  • Normal eye: near point 25 cm, far point infinity.
  • Through +2.0 D reading glasses: near point: 1/u = 2.0 + 4.0 → 17 cm. Far point: u = f = 50 cm.
  • Range 17 cm to 50 cm: close work is easier, the far side of the room blurs.
  • A lens shifts both ends of the range. It is right only for the eye it was chosen for.
Quick check
?A student with normal eyesight (near point 25 cm, far point at infinity) tries on a friend's glasses of power −2.5 D. What is her range of vision through them?
Mission check

?A short-sighted patient has a near point of 10 cm and a far point of 40 cm. She is given glasses of power −2.5 D. What is her range of vision while wearing them?

A diverging lens pushes the near point outwards. On a normal eye, −2.5 D glasses move it from 25 cm to 67 cm: close-up print blurs.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Make a guess: Two speakers play the same note. How can one step sideways make the sound almost vanish?

Mission 4

Superposition

  • When two waves or pulses overlap, they interfere.
  • Principle of superposition: the resultant displacement = the sum of the individual displacements.
  • Displacements have signs. Up (+) and down (−) can cancel.
  • Pulses pass through each other. Afterwards each carries on unchanged, as if the other was never there.
Quick check
?Two pulses of identical shape and size but opposite displacement travel towards each other along a stretched string. At one instant they overlap exactly and the whole string is momentarily straight. Which statement about this instant, and what follows it, is correct?
Mission 4

Adding waves on a graph

  • To add two waves, add their displacements point by point.
  • Same frequency, in phase (crest meets crest): amplitude A1 + A2. Constructive interference.
  • Same frequency, in antiphase (crest meets trough): amplitude |A1 − A2|. Destructive interference. Equal amplitudes cancel completely.
  • Anything in between gives an amplitude in between.
Quick check
?The graph shows the displacement y of a stretched string against position x at time t = 0. Pulse A is travelling to the right and pulse B to the left, each at a speed of 1.0 cm s⁻¹. What is the displacement of the string at x = 6.0 cm when t = 3.0 s?
Mission 4

Two sources: path difference

  • Two sources, S1 and S2, emit waves in phase, with the same wavelength λ.
  • At a point P: path difference = |S2P − S1P|.
  • Path difference = nλ (0, λ, 2λ, …): crest meets crest. Constructive: loud, bright, large amplitude.
  • Path difference = (n + ½)λ (½λ, 1½λ, …): crest meets trough. Destructive: quiet, dark, small amplitude.
  • Result: a fixed pattern of maxima and minima. Energy is not destroyed at the minima; it is redistributed to the maxima.
Mission 4

Coherence

  • A steady pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency and a constant phase difference.
  • Two separate lamps are not coherent: their phase difference changes randomly, far too often to follow (light is emitted in short, unrelated bursts). The pattern shifts too fast to see.
  • Coherent sources come from one source: two speakers on one signal generator; two slits lit by the same light.
  • To see clear interference, the waves should also have similar amplitudes and oscillate in the same direction.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Two speakers, in phase, play 850 Hz. Speed of sound 340 m s⁻¹. λ = v/f = 340 ÷ 850 = 0.400 m.
  • Point P: 3.00 m from one speaker, 3.60 m from the other. Path difference = 0.60 m = 1.5λ → destructive: quiet.
  • Point Q: 3.00 m and 3.80 m. Path difference = 0.80 m = 2λ → constructive: loud.
  • Walk from Q to P: the sound fades from loud to quiet in a few tens of centimetres.
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers connected to the same signal generator emit sound in phase at a frequency of 500 Hz. The speed of sound in air is 340 m s⁻¹. At which of these path differences is the sound quiet because of destructive interference?
Quick check
?Two loudspeakers side by side are driven by two separate signal generators, each set to 500 Hz. A listener walking across the room hears no steady pattern of loud and quiet places. Which statement explains this?
Mission check

?Two dippers vibrating in phase act as coherent sources S1 and S2 on a ripple tank, producing waves of wavelength 2.0 cm. Point P is 31.0 cm from S1 and 37.0 cm from S2. What kind of interference occurs at P?

Where the path difference is a whole number of wavelengths, crest meets crest: loud. Step to where it is a half-wavelength more, and crest meets trough: almost silent.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Rewire one of two speakers backwards. Why do the loud and quiet spots swap places?

Make a guess: Rewire one of two speakers backwards. Why do the loud and quiet spots swap places?

Mission 5

Interference in terms of phase

  • Recall: phase difference is how far one oscillation is ahead of another, as a fraction of a cycle: 2π × path difference/λ (in radians).
  • Sources in phase: path difference nλ → phase difference 2nπ → constructive.
  • Path difference (n + ½)λ → phase difference (2n + 1)π → destructive.
  • Total phase difference at a point = phase difference at the sources + phase difference from the paths.
  • Sources in antiphase add π everywhere: every maximum becomes a minimum, and every minimum a maximum.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Coherent sources in phase, λ = 0.40 m.
  • Path difference 0.10 m: phase difference = 2π × 0.10/0.40 = π/2 rad (90°). Neither maximum nor minimum: an in-between amplitude.
  • Path difference 0.20 m: 2π × 0.20/0.40 = π rad → destructive.
  • Path difference 0.40 m: 2π rad → constructive.
Quick check
?Two coherent sources emit waves in phase. The wavelength is 0.60 m. At a point P, the waves arrive with a phase difference of 3π rad. What is the path difference at P, and what happens there?
Mission 5

Lasers

  • Laser light is monochromatic (one wavelength) and coherent, in a narrow, intense beam. Ideal for interference.
Mission check

?Two dippers on a ripple tank vibrate with the same frequency and amplitude, but in antiphase. The wavelength is 2.0 cm. Point P is 5.0 cm further from one dipper than from the other. What happens at P?

Wiring a speaker backwards adds a phase difference of π everywhere. Every loud spot (path difference nλ) becomes quiet, and every quiet spot becomes loud.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Make a guess: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Mission 6

Two waves, opposite directions

  • Two waves with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose.
  • Usually a wave and its own reflection from a fixed end or the end of a pipe.
  • Add the displacements at each point at t = 0, T/4, T/2: some points never move; others swing the most.
  • The pattern does not travel. No energy is carried along it.
  • This is a stationary (standing) wave.
  • Conditions: two waves of the same type, frequency and speed (similar amplitude) moving in opposite directions; the length must suit the wavelength.
Mission 6

Nodes and antinodes

  • Node: zero amplitude at all times. Antinode: maximum amplitude.
  • Node to next node: λ/2. Node to next antinode: λ/4.
  • Amplitude varies with position: zero at nodes, largest at antinodes.
  • Between two neighbouring nodes, all points move in phase.
  • Points on opposite sides of a node are in antiphase (phase difference π rad).
Mission 6

Two kinds of wave compared

  • Energy: progressive carries it along; stationary does not (stored, swapping between kinetic and potential).
  • Amplitude: progressive, the same everywhere (no losses); stationary, varies from zero (node) to maximum (antinode).
  • Phase: progressive, changes steadily along the wave; stationary, in phase within a loop, antiphase across a node.
  • Wavelength: progressive, crest to crest; stationary, twice the node-to-node distance.
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in a standing wave. Its nodes are at x = 0, 0.40 m, 0.80 m and 1.20 m, measured from one end. Points P, Q and R on the string are at x = 0.10 m, 0.20 m and 0.70 m. Which statement about the oscillations of P, Q and R is correct?
Mission 6

Strings

  • A fixed end must be a node.
  • Fixed at both ends: a whole number of loops fits, L = nλ/2.
  • Lowest-frequency mode: one loop, λ = 2L. Next modes: two loops (λ = L), three loops (λ = 2L/3).
  • Wave speed v fixed by the string, so f = v/λ = nv/2L: only these frequencies "fit".
Mission 6

Air in pipes

  • Closed end: air cannot move → displacement node. Open end: displacement antinode.
  • Closed at one end: node–antinode, L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, …
  • Closed at one end: frequencies f1, 3f1, 5f1, … with f1 = v/4L.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A pipe 0.50 m long; speed of sound 340 m s⁻¹.
  • Closed at one end: λ = 4L = 2.0 m → f = 340 ÷ 2.0 = 170 Hz.
  • Next mode: L = 3λ/4 → f = 3 × 170 = 510 Hz. No 340 Hz.
Quick check
?A pipe of length 0.68 m is closed at one end and open at the other. The speed of sound in the air in the pipe is 340 m s⁻¹. Which gives, in increasing order, the lowest frequencies at which the air in the pipe resonates?
Mission check

?The diagram shows the envelope of a standing wave on a string of length L = 1.20 m that is fixed at both ends. What is the wavelength of the waves on the string?

The ends must be nodes, so only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit. With v fixed, only the matching frequencies can build up: the instrument's notes.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can stationary waves measure a wave's speed without timing anything?

Make a guess: How can stationary waves measure a wave's speed without timing anything?

Mission 7

Resonance tube

  • A tube stands in water; the water surface closes the lower end. Hold a vibrating tuning fork of known f over the open top.
  • Raise the tube slowly from low down. At the first loud resonance, a stationary wave fits: node at the water, antinode near the top.
  • First resonance length L1 ≈ λ/4.
  • End correction is not required: take L1 = λ/4, so v = fλ = 4fL1.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • Tuning fork 512 Hz.
  • L1 = 16.2 cm → λ = 4 × 0.162 = 0.648 m; v = 512 × 0.648 = 332 m s⁻¹.
  • Repeat with several forks; average, or plot L1 against 1/f (gradient v/4).
Quick check
?A tuning fork of frequency 384 Hz is held over a resonance tube. The first resonance occurs when the air column is 22.0 cm long. Ignoring any end correction, what is the speed of sound in the air?
Mission check

?A loudspeaker emitting a 1000 Hz note faces a hard wall. A microphone moved along the line between them detects positions of minimum loudness 0.17 m apart. What is the speed of sound?

Nodes are λ/2 apart. Measure their spacing, double it for λ, and v = fλ: a ruler and a known frequency replace the stopwatch.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • P = 1/f (f in m), unit dioptre; converging +, diverging −; thin lenses in contact: P = P1 + P2 + …

Mission 2

  • Myopia: eye too strong, far point too close, diverging lens. Hypermetropia: eye too weak, near point too far, converging lens.
  • The correcting lens puts a virtual image (v negative) at the eye's own far point or near point.

Mission 3

  • New range of vision: for each end, put the virtual image at the eye's own near or far point and solve 1/u = P − 1/v (v negative).
  • Reading glasses bring the far point in to u = f; myopia glasses push the near point out a little.

Mission 4

  • Superposition: add displacements. Waves and pulses pass through each other unchanged.
  • Sources in phase: path difference nλ → constructive; (n + ½)λ → destructive.
  • A steady interference pattern needs coherent sources: same frequency, constant phase difference.

Mission 5

  • Phase difference = 2π × path difference/λ. In phase (2nπ) → constructive; antiphase ((2n + 1)π) → destructive.
  • Coherent sources: same frequency, constant phase difference. Make them from one source (one laser, one signal generator).

Mission 6

  • Stationary wave: two identical waves travelling opposite ways; nodes λ/2 apart; no energy carried along.
  • String fixed at both ends: L = λ/2, λ, 3λ/2, …; pipe closed at one end: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, …

Mission 7

  • Resonance tube: first resonance at L ≈ λ/4; v = fλ.
