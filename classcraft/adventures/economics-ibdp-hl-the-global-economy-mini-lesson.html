Mission 1

Why countries trade

Benefits of international trade:

  • Increased competition: domestic firms face rivals, so they cut costs and innovate.
  • Lower prices for consumers and for firms buying inputs.
  • Greater choice: goods a country cannot make at all, and more varieties of those it can.
  • Acquisition of resources: oil, metals, foods a country lacks.
  • More foreign exchange earnings from exports.
  • Access to larger markets, so firms gain economies of scale.
  • More efficient resource allocation and production: resources shift into what each country does relatively well, so less is given up to get each good.

Mission 1

Comparative advantage

  • Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
  • Output table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = output of Y ÷ output of X.
Opportunity cost of…1 tonne of food1 roll of cloth
Country A300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 cloth600 ÷ 300 = 2 food
Country B240 ÷ 240 = 1 cloth240 ÷ 240 = 1 food
  • Food is cheaper (in cloth given up) in A: A has comparative advantage in food.
  • Cloth is cheaper (in food given up) in B: B has comparative advantage in cloth.
  • With different opportunity costs, each country has a comparative advantage in one good.
  • Input table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = input per unit of X ÷ input per unit of Y.
  • Same idea for people: a surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still leaves the typing to the receptionist. Each hour typing costs the surgeon an hour of surgery.
  • Say: "lower opportunity cost" — not: "can make more", which is absolute advantage.

Mission 1

Where does comparative advantage come from?

  • Factor endowments (Heckscher–Ohlin): a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively.
    • Plenty of labour → labour-intensive goods (garments, assembly).
    • Plenty of capital and skills → capital-intensive goods (aircraft, chemicals).
  • Climate and natural resources: land, minerals, sunshine, rainfall.
  • Technology and human capital: education, research, know-how.
  • History and scale: early starters gain experience and economies of scale.
  • Comparative advantage is dynamic: investment in skills and capital can change it over time.
Mission 1

Assumptions and limitations

The model assumes:

  • Constant opportunity costs (linear PPCs). In reality costs usually rise as a country specialises, so full specialisation is unlikely.
  • No transport costs and no trade barriers. Both can wipe out the gain.
  • Factors move freely between industries inside a country. In reality workers and machines are not easily switched.
  • Perfect knowledge, full employment, and only two countries and two goods.
  • Terms of trade fall between the two opportunity costs; where they settle decides who gains most.
  • Gains are totals: some groups inside each country lose.
Mission 1

Evaluate: how useful is comparative advantage?

It depends on…

  • Transport costs and barriers. Low costs make the gains real; high tariffs or freight cancel them.
  • Who gains. Total output rises, but workers in shrinking industries may lose unless they retrain or are compensated.
  • Risk of over-specialisation. Real case: ready-made garments earned over 80% of Bangladesh's export revenue in 2022–23. Strong comparative advantage, but heavy reliance on one industry.
  • Time. Today's comparative advantage may not be tomorrow's; some countries protect infant industries to build a new one.
  • What it misses. Much trade is between similar rich countries in similar goods (cars for cars), driven by economies of scale and variety.

Mission 2

What a country gains from trade

Benefits of international trade, in a more connected world:

  • Increased competition: domestic firms face foreign rivals, so they cut costs and innovate.
  • Lower prices: goods come from wherever they are made most cheaply.
  • Greater choice: more products and more varieties.
  • Acquisition of resources: oil, metals and foods a country lacks.
  • More foreign exchange earnings from exports, to pay for imports.
  • Access to larger markets: firms sell worldwide.
  • Economies of scale: bigger output lowers average costs.
  • More efficient resource allocation and production: resources move to what each country does relatively well.
  • These are gains for the country as a whole. Firms and workers that compete with imports can lose sales and jobs while resources move.

Mission 3

From no trade to free trade

  • Start with domestic demand and domestic supply only (no trade). Price settles at 7, quantity 50 (illustrative data).
  • Now open the border. A small country is a price taker: it can buy or sell any amount at the world price (Pw).
  • World price 4, below the domestic price:
    • consumers buy more: 80
    • domestic firms produce less: 20
    • the gap, 80 − 20 = 60, is imports.
  • World price above the domestic price: domestic firms sell more than home buyers want, and the surplus is exported.
  • Say: "domestic supply" — not just "supply": with trade, total supply at Pw is domestic output plus imports.
Mission 3

Worked example: imports, exports and surplus

Same model (illustrative data): domestic demand reaches zero at a price of 12; domestic supply starts at a price of 2.

  • Imports at Pw 4: 80 − 20 = 60. Import expenditure = 4 × 60 = 240.
  • If instead Pw were 9: domestic output 70, domestic consumption 30, exports 40. Export revenue = 9 × 40 = 360.
  • Opening to imports: consumer surplus rises by 195, producer surplus falls by 105, a net gain of 90.

Mission 3

The protectionist toolbox

Protectionism: government measures that shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

  • Tariff: a tax on imports (per unit, or a percentage of value).
  • Quota: a legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported.
  • Subsidy: a payment to domestic producers, so they can compete with imports or sell more abroad (export subsidy).
  • Embargo: a ban on trade in a good, or with a country.
  • Administrative barriers (non-tariff barriers, "red tape"): standards, regulations, licences and slow customs checks that make importing harder.
Mission 3

The tariff diagram

Follow the diagram step by step (illustrative data):

1. Free trade at Pw = 4: domestic output 20, consumption 80, imports 60.

2. A tariff of 2 per unit: the price in the home market rises to Pw + tariff = 6.

3. Domestic output extends to 40; consumption contracts to 60; imports fall to 20.

4. Government revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff: the rectangle.

5. Two triangles: the deadweight (welfare) loss.

  • Producer side: high-cost domestic firms replace cheaper imports (wasted resources).
  • Consumer side: buyers who valued the good above the world price no longer buy it.
  • Effects on stakeholders: consumers lose; domestic producers and the government gain; foreign producers sell less; society suffers a welfare loss.
  • Revenue and spending: domestic producers' revenue rises (higher price, more output); spending on imports falls, so foreign producers earn less export revenue.
  • Say: "domestic supply extends" — not: "supply increases": the curve does not shift.
Mission 3

Worked example: counting a tariff's effects

Tariff of 2 per unit, Pw 4 → 6 (illustrative data):

Tariff revenue2 × 2040
Producer surplus gain½ × (20 + 40) × 260
Consumer surplus loss½ × (80 + 60) × 2140

  • Import expenditure (paid to foreign firms) falls from 240 to 4 × 20 = 80.
  • Consumer spending: 4 × 80 = 320 → 6 × 60 = 360. Domestic producers' revenue: 4 × 20 = 80 → 6 × 40 = 240.

Mission 3

Quotas

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, free-trade imports 60.

  • A quota of 20 units: once 20 units have come in, no more imports.
  • Supply in the home market = domestic supply + 20: draw S + quota, 20 units right of domestic supply, starting at Pw.
  • Price rises until demand meets S + quota: price 6, consumption 60, domestic output 40.
  • Same price and quantities as the tariff of 2, with one key difference:
    • the gap (6 − 4) × 20 = 40 is not government revenue.
    • It goes to whoever holds the import licences (domestic importers or foreign exporters): the quota rent.
    • If foreign exporters hold the licences, the 40 leaves the country: an extra loss to the home economy.
  • Revenue and spending: domestic producers' revenue rises; spending on imports falls, because far fewer units are imported.
  • Stakeholders: consumers lose 140; domestic producers gain 60; licence holders gain 40; welfare loss 40 (20 + 20) if the licence holders are domestic, or 40 + 40 = 80 for the home economy if they are foreign. If the licences are auctioned, the government can collect the 40.
  • If demand grows, a quota lets the price rise further; a tariff lets imports grow at the same price.

Mission 3

Subsidies to domestic producers

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, imports 60. Subsidy of 2 per unit to domestic producers.

  • Domestic supply shifts right (down by 2): S → S + subsidy.
  • The price in the home market stays at Pw: consumers are unaffected (still buy 80).
  • Domestic output rises 20 → 40; imports fall 60 → 40.
  • Government pays 2 × 40 = 80: a cost to taxpayers (opportunity cost of other spending).
  • Revenue and spending: domestic producers' revenue rises (the same market price plus the subsidy, on more output); spending on imports falls; consumer spending is unchanged.
  • Producer surplus rises by ½ × (20 + 40) × 2 = 60. Welfare loss = 80 − 60 = 20: one triangle (inefficient domestic production), not two.
  • Harder to spot than a tariff, and it does not raise prices, but it must be paid for.
Mission 3

Export subsidies

  • Export subsidy: a payment per unit sold abroad.
  • Exporters now earn the world price plus the subsidy on each export, so they will not sell at home for less.
  • Illustrative data: Pw 9, export subsidy 1. Domestic price rises to 10; domestic consumption 30 → 20; output 70 → 80; exports 40 → 60.
  • Government cost 1 × 60 = 60. Consumer surplus falls by 25; producer surplus rises by 75. Net change in welfare: 75 − 25 − 60 = −10, a welfare loss of 10.
  • Foreign rivals face cheaper competition, and their governments may retaliate.
  • Real case: WTO members agreed in Nairobi (2015) to eliminate agricultural export subsidies.
Mission 3

Embargoes, red tape and other barriers

  • Embargo: a total ban. Real case: in August 2014 Russia banned many food imports from the EU, USA and others.
  • Administrative barriers: product standards (safety, labelling, health) and regulations that imports must meet.
    • Real case: the EU bans beef from cattle given growth hormones, which shuts out much US and Canadian beef. The WTO ruled against the ban in 1998; the EU kept it.
  • Standards can protect health, safety and the environment. They become protectionism when they are designed to block imports rather than reduce real risks.
Quick check
?Country X requires all imported toys to be tested in its own laboratories, which takes six months. Domestic toys are tested within a week. There is no limit on numbers and no tax. Which tool of protection is this?
Mission 3

Real case: washing machines, 2018

  • In 2018 the USA put safeguard tariffs on imported large washing machines.
  • The tariff rate was higher once imports passed a set number of units each year.
  • Researchers (Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, 2020) found:
    • washer prices rose by nearly 12%
    • dryers, which were not taxed, rose by about the same amount: dryers are complements, often bought as a pair, and sellers raised both prices
  • Domestic brands raised their prices too. Counting washers and dryers together, buyers in the USA paid more than the tariff itself.
  • Lesson: a tariff's cost can spread into related markets.
Mission 3

Evaluate: how much harm does a tariff do?

It depends on…

  • Elasticities. The more price-elastic domestic demand and supply, the more imports fall and the larger the welfare-loss triangles.
  • Country size. A large importer can push the world price down, so foreign producers bear part of the tariff; a small country cannot.
  • Retaliation. Partners may answer with tariffs on this country's exports.
  • Time. Protected firms may invest and catch up, or grow complacent.
  • The tool. Quotas fix quantity, so rising demand pushes prices up; tariffs raise revenue; subsidies avoid higher prices but cost taxpayers.
Mission check

?A small country puts a tariff on imported steel. It cannot affect the world price of steel. Which statement is correct?

In a small country, home buyers bear a tariff through higher prices; home firms and the government gain less than buyers lose.

Mission 4

Free trade: winners and losers

Free trade raises total output and consumption (comparative advantage). But:

  • Winners: consumers (lower prices, more choice), exporters, firms buying cheaper imported inputs.
  • Losers: firms and workers in import-competing industries.
  • Gains are spread thinly over millions of buyers; losses are concentrated in particular towns and industries.
  • Losers are easier to organise, so they lobby for protection.
Mission 4

For protection: new and strategic industries

  • Infant (sunrise) industry: a new domestic industry has high average costs, because it is small and inexperienced.
    • Established foreign firms produce at scale, at lower average cost.
    • Temporary protection lets the infant grow, gain economies of scale and learn, until it can compete at the world price.
    • Then the protection should be removed.
  • National security / strategic industries: food, energy, steel, defence equipment. Relying on imports is risky if trade is cut off by war or sanctions.
  • ELDC diversification: a low-income country that depends on a few primary exports may protect new manufacturing to spread its risks.
  • Say: "infant industry" — not "small firm": the argument is about costs falling as the industry grows.
Mission 4

For protection: unfair trade and standards

  • Dumping: selling exports below cost, or below the price charged at home, often supported by state subsidies.
    • Aim: win market share abroad and drive out rivals.
    • Anti-dumping duties are allowed under WTO rules, if dumping and injury are shown.
  • Unfair competition: rivals backed by state subsidies, or by very weak labour or environmental rules.
  • Health and safety: block unsafe food, medicines or toys.
  • Environmental standards: stop goods made in ways that cause serious pollution or harm wildlife.
  • These arguments are strongest when the risk is real; they are weakest when standards are a disguise for protecting home firms.
Mission 4

For protection: jobs, the balance of payments and revenue

  • Protecting jobs: slow the decline of sunset (declining) industries, giving workers time to retrain and limiting structural unemployment.
  • Balance of payments: tariffs switch spending from imports to home goods, reducing a current account deficit (at least for a time).
  • Government revenue: tariffs are easy to collect at the border. They matter most in low-income countries with weak income-tax systems.
Quick check

Mission 4

Against protection

  • Misallocation of resources: resources stay in industries where the country has no comparative advantage.
  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers. These hit poorer households hard, as they spend more of their income on goods.
  • Increased costs for firms that use imported inputs (steel for car makers, parts for electronics).
  • Inefficiency: protected firms lack the incentive to cut costs or innovate.
  • Retaliation: other countries answer with their own tariffs: a trade war that hurts exporters.
  • Reduced export competitiveness: dearer inputs and retaliation make exports harder to sell.
Mission 4

Real case: steel tariffs and retaliation, 2018

  • In March 2018 the USA put tariffs of 25% on steel and 10% on aluminium imports, using a national security argument.
  • The EU, Canada, Mexico, China and others retaliated.
    • The EU's duties (June 2018) hit US whiskey, motorcycles and jeans: goods from industries unrelated to steel.
  • US steelmakers gained protection; US firms that use steel faced higher input costs.
  • The case shows three arguments at once: security (for), retaliation (against) and higher costs for users (against).
Quick check
?Country Z puts a tariff on imported steel to protect its steelworks. Z's car makers, which export most of their cars, buy steel as a key input. What is the most likely effect on Z's car industry?
Mission 4

Evaluate: free trade or protection?

It depends on…

  • The real problem. For security, health or pollution, a targeted tool (regulation, a subsidy, a tax on pollution) usually does less harm than a tariff on all imports.
  • Whether protection is temporary. Infant industries need a clear end date; many never "grow up", and firms lobby to keep protection.
  • Retaliation and size. A small country gains little leverage; a trade war can cost exporters more than protection saves.
  • Time. Protection can save jobs in the short run; the long-run costs of inefficiency build up.
  • Stage of development. Arguments for diversification and revenue are stronger for low-income economies.
  • Free trade versus protection is a judgement: weigh efficiency gains against equity, security and sustainability goals.
Quick check

Mission 5

Three ways to agree on trade

  • Bilateral: two countries (e.g. the Australia–UK free trade agreement, 2023).
  • Regional: a group of neighbours (e.g. the EU, USMCA in North America, Mercosur in South America, the African Continental Free Trade Area).
  • Multilateral: almost every country together, through the World Trade Organization (WTO).
  • Bilateral and regional deals are preferential trade agreements: members get better terms than outsiders.
Mission 5

The ladder of integration

Each step up adds to the one below:

1. Free trade area (FTA): no tariffs or quotas between members; each member keeps its own tariffs on outsiders. Needs rules of origin. E.g. USMCA (which replaced NAFTA in 2020); ASEAN's free trade area.

2. Customs union: an FTA plus a common external tariff (CET) on outsiders. Members negotiate trade deals together. E.g. Mercosur.

3. Common market: a customs union plus free movement of labour and capital. E.g. the EU single market.

  • Say: "common external tariff" — not: "the members have no tariffs": they still tax imports from outsiders.
Quick check
?Four countries remove all tariffs on trade with each other and agree one tariff schedule for imports from the rest of the world. Workers still need visas to work in another member country. What kind of bloc is this?
Mission 5

Why join a bloc?

  • Bigger market: firms sell to all members tariff-free, gaining economies of scale.
  • More competition: lower prices and more efficient firms.
  • Foreign direct investment: outside firms build plants inside the bloc to avoid its external tariffs.
  • Freedom of labour (common market): more job opportunities; firms fill skill shortages.
  • Bargaining power: a bloc negotiates as one in world trade talks.
  • Political stability and cooperation between neighbours.
  • Trade creation: cheaper imports from partners replace dearer domestic output.
Mission 5

Trade creation and trade diversion

  • Trade creation: joining replaces higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a partner. Efficiency rises.
  • Trade diversion: joining switches imports from a lower-cost outside producer to a higher-cost partner, because only the outsider still pays the tariff. Efficiency falls.
  • Illustrative data, same domestic demand and supply as in Mission 3:
    • Before: the cheapest producer is outside, price 3; with a tariff of 2, the home price is 5. Imports 40, tariff revenue 80.
    • Join a customs union with a partner that sells at 4, tariff-free. The outsider still pays 3 + 2 = 5, so all imports now come from the partner. Home price 4; imports 60.
  • Trade creation: two triangles, each ½ × 1 × 10 = 5: a gain of 10.
  • Trade diversion: the 40 units once bought at 3 now cost 4: a loss of 1 × 40 = 40.
  • Net effect here: 10 − 40 = −30. Joining can reduce welfare when diversion outweighs creation.

Mission 5

The costs of blocs

  • Loss of sovereignty: in a customs union a member cannot set its own tariffs or sign its own trade deals; common rules limit national policy.
  • Challenge to multilateralism: energy goes into regional deals instead of global WTO talks; overlapping rules of origin add costs.
  • Outsiders lose: exporters outside the bloc face the external tariff while members compete tariff-free.
  • Adjustment costs: less efficient firms in some members shrink; some regions lose jobs.
  • Trade diversion can cut efficiency.
Mission 5

The WTO: aims and functions

  • Set up on 1 January 1995, replacing the GATT (1947). 166 members, covering about 98% of world trade.
  • Aim: use trade to raise living standards, create jobs and support sustainable development.
  • Functions:
    • a forum for negotiations to cut trade barriers ("rounds")
    • oversees its trade agreements
    • settles disputes between members
    • reviews members' trade policies
    • technical help for developing countries.
  • Core rule, non-discrimination:
    • most-favoured nation: a tariff cut for one member must go to all members
    • national treatment: imports, once in, are treated like home goods.
  • Allowed exceptions: free trade areas and customs unions, anti-dumping duties, special treatment for developing countries.
Mission 5

The limits of the WTO

  • Hard to agree: decisions need consensus among all members. The Doha Round, launched in 2001, has not been completed.
  • Agriculture and services: rich countries' farm support and barriers to services trade are hard to cut.
  • Unequal bargaining power: large economies have more negotiators, lawyers and leverage than small, poor members.
  • Weak enforcement: since December 2019 the Appellate Body, which hears appeals in disputes, has had too few members to work, because the USA blocked new appointments. A losing country can appeal into the void.
  • The rise of regional blocs is partly a response to slow global talks.
Quick check

Mission 5

Evaluate: building blocks or stumbling blocks?

It depends on…

  • Who the partners are. Low-cost partners mean more trade creation and less diversion.
  • How high the external tariff is. A high CET makes outsiders' losses larger.
  • A high CET also makes trade diversion more likely.
  • How deep the integration is. A single market removes non-tariff barriers too, but asks more sovereignty of its members.
  • Dynamic effects. Over time, bigger markets bring scale economies, competition and FDI; these can outweigh static diversion.
  • The global system. Blocs can be stepping stones to wider liberalisation, or can divide the world into rival trading groups.

Mission 6

Steps of economic integration

  • Countries can tie their economies together step by step.
  • Free trade area: no tariffs or quotas between members; each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders.
  • Customs union: free trade area + a common external tariff.
  • Common market: customs union + free movement of labour and capital.
  • Monetary union: members share a single currency, managed by one central bank that sets one monetary policy (one interest rate) for all.
  • A monetary union is usually built on a common market, then adds the common currency and common central bank.
  • No exchange rate between members, so no member can devalue against another.
  • Say: "a single currency and a single monetary policy" — not: "a single currency" alone.
Mission 6

The eurozone and the ECB

  • The euro began on 1 January 1999 (accounts and electronic payments); notes and coins followed in 2002.
  • In 2026, 21 of the 27 EU countries use the euro. Bulgaria joined most recently (2026).
  • The European Central Bank (ECB) sets monetary policy for the whole euro area. Its Governing Council includes the governors of every member's national central bank.
  • Primary objective: price stability, aiming for 2% inflation over the medium term.
  • One ECB interest rate applies in every member, whatever its own inflation or unemployment.
  • Fiscal policy (taxes, government spending) stays with each national government, within EU rules on deficits and debt.

?Three neighbouring countries already have free trade, a common external tariff and free movement of workers and capital between them. Which extra step would turn them into a monetary union?
Mission 6

What a single currency gains

  • No transaction costs: no fees for changing currency when trading within the union.
  • No exchange-rate uncertainty within the union: firms can price and plan without currency risk.
  • So more trade and more investment (FDI) between members are likely.
  • Price transparency: prices in one currency are easy to compare, which increases competition.
  • Credibility: a country with a history of high inflation can "borrow" the low-inflation record of a strong central bank, so inflation expectations and interest rates may fall.
  • Illustrative data: a firm sells 10 million worth of goods a year to other members, and currency changing costs 1%. Single currency saves 10 m × 0.01 = 0.1 m a year.
Mission 6

What a member gives up

  • No independent monetary policy: the central bank sets one interest rate for the whole union.
  • No exchange rate of its own to fall when its exports become uncompetitive.
  • So it loses two tools for dealing with an asymmetric shock: a shock that hits one member but not others.
  • A rate that suits the average member may be too high for one in recession and too low for one that is booming: "one size fits all".
  • Fiscal rules limit how far a government can borrow to support demand.
  • Real data: in 2013, unemployment averaged 27.8% in Greece and 4.9% in Germany. Both had the same ECB interest rate.
  • Adjustment then falls on wages and prices: an internal devaluation, which is usually slow and painful.
Mission 6

Worked example: one interest rate, different real rates

  • Illustrative data. Union central bank rate = 2% for all members.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate.
  • Country X is booming; inflation 5%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 5 = −3%.
  • Country Y is in recession; inflation 1%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 1 = +1%.
  • Borrowing is cheapest in real terms where the economy is already overheating.
  • So one rate can make the boom bigger in X and the slump deeper in Y.
Mission 6Graph lab

An asymmetric shock

Illustrative data. Country B starts at full-employment output (500). World demand for its main export collapses: AD falls by 40. B is in a monetary union; its partners are unaffected.

  • Takeaway: outside the union, a fall in B's currency and interest rate cushions the shock. Inside, B must wait for internal devaluation.

Mission 6

When does sharing a currency work? The optimal currency area

  • Optimal currency area (Mundell, 1961): a region where the gains of one currency outweigh the costs of losing separate exchange rates.
  • Conditions that make a monetary union more likely to succeed:
    • similar economic cycles: shocks hit members at the same time (symmetric), so one interest rate suits them all
    • labour mobility: workers move from slumping to booming regions
    • flexible wages and prices: costs can fall quickly where demand is weak
    • fiscal transfers: a shared budget sends money to regions in recession
    • high trade between members: the savings on transaction costs and currency risk are larger
  • Compare: US states share one currency, with a large federal budget that pays more benefits and collects less tax in states with weak economies.
  • Each condition is a matter of degree. Economists disagree on how far the eurozone meets them.
Quick check
?Two countries are thinking of adopting a single currency. Which feature would make them most suitable for one?
Mission 6Evaluate

Is a single currency worth it?

  • It depends on:
    • how similar members' economies are: symmetric shocks make one interest rate suit everyone
    • how well other shock absorbers work: labour mobility, flexible wages, fiscal transfers
    • how much members trade with each other: bigger trade means bigger gains from one currency
    • credibility: a country with a record of high inflation may gain more than one with a trusted central bank
  • Time matters: gains (lower costs, more trade) build slowly; the cost of a bad shock can arrive suddenly.
Mission check

?Northland and Southland share one currency and one central bank. World demand for timber, Southland's main export, collapses. Northland sells no timber and is unaffected. Why might Southland's recession last longer than it would with its own floating currency?

Sharing a currency removes two shock absorbers, so a shock that hits one member alone must be absorbed by its wages, prices and jobs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources. Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost.
  • Specialising by comparative advantage and trading at terms between the two opportunity costs lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.
  • The model rests on strong assumptions; the gains are real but unequally shared.

Mission 2

  • Trade brings competition, lower prices, choice, resources, foreign exchange, larger markets, economies of scale and more efficient allocation.

Mission 3

  • Free trade at a world price below the domestic price: consumption rises, domestic output falls, imports fill the gap.
  • A tariff or quota raises the domestic price: home producers gain, consumers lose more, and there is a deadweight loss. Tariff revenue goes to the government; quota rent goes to licence holders.
  • Subsidies, embargoes, administrative barriers and (for some) currency manipulation are other tools, each with its own winners and losers.

Mission 4

  • Free trade raises total income, but its losses are concentrated, so pressure for protection is strong.
  • Arguments for protection: infant industries, security, dumping and unfair competition, health, safety and environmental standards, jobs, the balance of payments, revenue and (for low-income economies) diversification.
  • Arguments against: misallocation, higher prices, less choice, higher costs, inefficiency, retaliation, weaker exports. The case for protection depends on the problem, the tool, time and retaliation.

Mission 5

  • Free trade area → customs union (common external tariff) → common market (free movement of labour and capital).
  • Blocs bring bigger markets, competition, FDI and bargaining power; they cost sovereignty and can harm outsiders and global talks.
  • Trade creation raises efficiency; trade diversion lowers it. The net effect depends on partners' costs and the external tariff.
  • The WTO negotiates, monitors and settles disputes under non-discrimination; consensus, unequal power and a stalled appeals system limit it.

Mission 6

  • A monetary union shares one currency and one central bank setting one interest rate.
  • Gains: no transaction costs or currency risk within the union, more trade and investment, price transparency. Costs: no own interest rate or exchange rate to deal with asymmetric shocks.
  • It works best in an optimal currency area: similar cycles, mobile labour, flexible wages, fiscal transfers, high trade.
