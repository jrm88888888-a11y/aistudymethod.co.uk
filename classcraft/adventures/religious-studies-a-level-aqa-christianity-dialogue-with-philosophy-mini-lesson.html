Mission 1

Four questions for every belief

  • The dialogue asks how Christian belief has influenced and been influenced by philosophy, past and present.
  • Test each belief with AQA's four issues:
  • Reasonable: is it based on reason (argued from evidence or logic), or at least consistent with reason (not contrary to it)?
  • Meaningful: do its statements say something, and for whom: believers only, or anyone?
  • Coherent: do its parts fit together, and with the rest of Christian belief?
  • Belief in / belief that: is faith assent to propositions, or trust in someone? Does philosophy matter for that kind of faith?
  • (These four issues are introduced in an earlier lesson; here they are applied to Christianity.)
Mission 1

God: faith seeking understanding

  • Anselm (1033–1109), Proslogion (1077–78): his original title was "Faith seeking understanding". He believes in order to understand (Proslogion 1, paraphrase), then gives the ontological argument.
  • Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica: God's existence can be demonstrated from the world (the Five Ways); Aristotle's philosophy shapes the doctrine of God as necessary being and unmoved mover.
  • Vatican I, Dei Filius (1870), paraphrase: God can be known with certainty from created things by the natural light of reason.
  • Influence both ways: Christian belief prompted these arguments; Greek philosophy reshaped how Christians describe God: immutable, impassible, timeless.
  • Challenge: Hume and Kant criticised the arguments. Kant said he had to limit knowledge to make room for faith (Critique of Pure Reason, 1787 preface, paraphrase).

Reason as a preamble

> "The existence of God and other like truths about God, which can be known by natural reason, are not articles of faith, but are preambles to the articles; for faith presupposes natural knowledge, even as grace presupposes nature" (Aquinas, Summa Theologica I, q. 2, a. 2, reply to obj. 1; English Dominican translation, 1920)

> "For the invisible things of him since the creation of the world are clearly seen, being perceived through the things that are made, even his everlasting power and divinity" (Romans 1:20, WEBBE)

Mission 1

Philosophy reshapes the doctrine of God

  • Rejecting natural theology: Pascal (1623–1662) contrasted the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob with the "God of the philosophers" (Memorial, 1654, paraphrase). Karl Barth (1886–1968), Nein! (1934): God is known only through revelation in Christ, not by human reason.
  • The problem of evil: Mackie's logical problem ("Evil and Omnipotence", 1955) presses the coherence of an omnipotent, wholly good God.
  • Responses that keep classical theism: the free will defence; Hick's soul-making theodicy (Evil and the God of Love, 1966).
  • A response that changes it: David Griffin (1939–2022), God, Power, and Evil (1976): God persuades but does not coerce; God is not omnipotent in the classical sense (process theology).
  • Impact: most Christians keep omnipotence; the debate shows philosophy can change doctrine, not only defend it.
Mission 1

Is talk of God meaningful?

  • Challenge: A. J. Ayer (1910–1989), Language, Truth and Logic (1936): statements not verifiable by experience are meaningless. Antony Flew (1923–2010), "Theology and Falsification" (1950): believers let nothing count against "God loves us", so the claim dies "the death by a thousand qualifications" (short phrase).
  • Cognitive responses: Hick's eschatological verification: God-talk will be verified (or not) after death. Analogy (Aquinas): "God is good" is meaningful by proportion. Via negativa: speak only of what God is not.
  • Non-cognitive or symbolic responses: R. M. Hare's bliks; Wittgenstein's language games (Philosophical Investigations, 1953): meaning is use within a form of life; Tillich (1886–1965): symbols participate in what they point to.
  • For whom? Language-game views make religious language meaningful to insiders; critics say most Christians intend factual claims meaningful to everyone.
  • Impact: some theologians (e.g. D. Z. Phillips, 1934–2006) developed anti-realist readings; most churches remain realist.

Mission 1

Self, death and afterlife

  • Biblical roots: God breathes life into dust and "man became a living soul" (Genesis 2:7, WEBBE): a unity of body and life. Paul: "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44, WEBBE)
  • Plato's influence: an immortal soul separable from the body shaped Augustine and much later piety.
  • Aristotle's influence: for Aquinas "the soul is the form of the body" (ST I, q. 76): a soul without a body is incomplete, so resurrection completes the person.
  • Descartes (1596–1650), Meditations (1641): mind and body are distinct substances; the mind is indivisible.
  • Challenge: materialism and neuroscience (Ryle's "ghost in the machine", The Concept of Mind, 1949) question a separable soul.
  • Christian responses: Hick's replica theory (Death and Eternal Life, 1976); Christian physicalism (e.g. Nancey Murphy, Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?, 2006): no separate soul; hope rests on bodily resurrection.
  • Coherence question: is a resurrected or replica person the same person?
Mission 1

Sources of wisdom and authority

  • Bible: "Every Scripture is God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16, WEBBE). Protestants: sola scriptura. Catholics: Scripture and Tradition, interpreted by the Magisterium (Dei Verbum, 1965).
  • Reason: Richard Hooker (1554–1600), Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (from 1594): Scripture first, then reason, then the Church's tradition.
  • Enlightenment challenge: Hume doubted that testimony to miracles could ever be reasonable; historical-critical study treated the Bible as human documents with sources and contexts.
  • Responses: liberal views of inspiration (human words through which God speaks); inerrancy defended by many evangelicals (Chicago Statement, 1978).
  • Impact: debates over authority shape every other topic: if reason judges Scripture, who has the final word?

Mission 1

Belief in and belief that

  • H. H. Price (1899–1984), "Belief 'In' and Belief 'That'" (Religious Studies, 1965): belief that is assent to a proposition ("God exists"); belief in is trust and commitment ("I believe in God").
  • Price asks whether belief-in can be reduced to belief-that plus an attitude of esteem. Many Christians say faith is more: a relationship.
  • "You believe that God is one. You do well. The demons also believe—and shudder." (James 2:19, WEBBE): belief-that alone is not saving faith.
  • Kierkegaard (1813–1855), Concluding Unscientific Postscript (1846): faith is passionate commitment in objective uncertainty; proofs miss the point.
  • Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932), "Reason and Belief in God" (1983): belief in God can be properly basic, rational without argument.
  • Relevance of philosophy: if faith is belief-in, failed proofs matter less; but belief-in seems to presuppose belief-that God exists.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Philosophical enquiry is irrelevant to Christian faith."

  • Reasons for: faith is belief-in, a trust that proofs neither create nor destroy (Kierkegaard). Barth: God is known by revelation only. Many believers come to faith through experience and community, not argument.
  • Reasons against: belief-in presupposes belief-that, which can be reasonable or not (Price). Philosophy has changed doctrine: Greek ideas of God, Aquinas on the soul, process theology. Christians are told to give "a reason concerning the hope" in them (1 Peter 3:15, WEBBE).
  • A middle view: philosophy is a servant of faith: it clarifies and defends beliefs (Aquinas' preambles) without being their foundation (Plantinga).
  • Judgement criteria: What kind of faith is meant? Does the answer differ for Catholics (Vatican I) and for Barthian Protestants? Has philosophy mainly supported or mainly challenged belief, past and present?
Mission 1Worked answer

A coherence paragraph

Question: "Christian beliefs about life after death are coherent." Evaluate this claim.

  • Point: belief in bodily resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:44) fits a Bible that sees the person as a unity (Genesis 2:7) and Aquinas' view that the soul is the form of the body.
  • Philosophical challenge: if the body decays, is the raised person the same person, or a copy? (Hick's replica theory accepts a copy is still "the same person" in a looser sense.)
  • Counter-point: belief in an immortal soul going to God at death (Luke 23:43) sits uneasily with resurrection unless one adds an intermediate state (the soul with God, awaiting the resurrection of the body), which many Christians do.
  • Judgement: decide for yourself whether the combination of soul and resurrection is coherent, and say which Christian tradition your judgement applies to.
  • Synoptic tip: in each paragraph, name a Component 1 thinker, a Christian source, and say which of AQA's four issues (reasonable, meaningful, coherent, belief in/that) is at stake.

Mission 2

Religious experience in Christianity

  • Scripture: Paul on the road to Damascus: "suddenly a light from the sky shone around him" (Acts 9:3, WEBBE). Isaiah's vision of the holy God (Isaiah 6).
  • Mystics: Teresa of Avila (1515–1582), The Interior Castle (1577): visions corporeal, imaginative and intellectual (AQA's three types of vision).
  • Corporate experience: Pentecost (Acts 2); charismatic renewal; the "Toronto Blessing" (1994).
  • Influence on philosophy: Schleiermacher (1768–1834) grounded religion in feeling (On Religion, 1799); William James (1842–1910) and Rudolf Otto (1869–1937) drew heavily on Christian cases.
  • Influence of philosophy on Christianity: Otto's numinous ("wholly other") and Stace's mystical union gave Christians new ways to describe worship and prayer.
Mission 2Read the source

Testing an experience

> "Beloved, don't believe every spirit, but test the spirits, whether they are of God, because many false prophets have gone out into the world." (1 John 4:1, WEBBE)

> "By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots." (William James, The Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902, Lecture I)

  • Teresa judged visions by their fruits: humility, peace, love, and agreement with Scripture and the Church.
  • James borrows Jesus' words (Matthew 7:16) to judge experiences by their effects, not their origin.

Q1 What does 1 John 4:1 assume about religious experiences?

Show answer

Some may be false; believers must test them.

Q2 Why might James's "fruits" test help answer a scientific challenge?

Show answer

Even if an experience has a brain cause (its "roots"), its value can be judged by its effects.

Mission 2

Is it reasonable to trust an experience?

  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): principle of credulity: if it seems to me that something is present, it probably is, unless there are special reasons to doubt. Principle of testimony: others' reports are probably true, unless there is reason to doubt.
  • Challenges from science: temporal-lobe stimulation and drugs can produce religious-seeming states; Freud saw religion as wish-fulfilment.
  • Challenge from diversity: Christians, Hindus and Buddhists report experiences that support different beliefs.
  • Christian responses: God may work through the brain; a natural cause does not rule out a divine one; experiences come within a tradition that tests them.
  • Belief in / belief that: for many Christians, experience strengthens trust (belief in) more than it proves a proposition.

Mission 2

Science and Christianity: four models

  • Ian Barbour (1923–2013), Religion in an Age of Science (1990): four ways science and religion relate.
  • Conflict: Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006): God is a failed scientific hypothesis. Young-earth creationists also see conflict, from the other side.
  • Independence: Stephen Jay Gould's NOMA (1997): science asks how, religion asks about meaning and value; non-overlapping.
  • Dialogue: John Polkinghorne (1930–2021), physicist and priest: science and theology both seek truth about one world; the universe's intelligibility points to a rational creator.
  • Integration: natural theology updated: fine-tuning and design arguments.
  • History both ways: Galileo's trial (1633); Darwin's Origin (1859); Georges Lemaître, a Catholic priest, proposed what became the Big Bang theory (1927); Pope John Paul II called evolution "more than a hypothesis" (1996).

Mission 2

Other religions' truth claims

  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12); Karl Barth judged all religion, Christianity included, by revelation.
  • Inclusivism: Karl Rahner (1904–1984): sincere followers of other faiths may be "anonymous Christians", saved by Christ without knowing it. Vatican II, Nostra Aetate (1965): the Church rejects nothing true and holy in other religions (paraphrase).
  • Pluralism: John Hick, God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): a "Copernican revolution": put God, not Christianity, at the centre. An Interpretation of Religion (1989): the Real in itself, experienced differently through different traditions, using Kant's distinction between things in themselves and things as they appear.
  • Philosophical problem: truth claims seem to conflict (e.g. Christians affirm the crucifixion of Jesus; many Muslims read Qur'an 4:157 as denying it). By the law of non-contradiction, both cannot be literally true.
  • Critiques of pluralism: Gavin D'Costa (1996): pluralism is itself an exclusive claim; Dominus Iesus (2000) reaffirms Christ as the unique saviour.

Exclusive verses, different readings

> "Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me.'" (John 14:6, WEBBE)

> "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12, WEBBE)

  • Exclusivist reading: explicit faith in Christ is required.
  • Inclusivist reading: salvation is through Christ, but people may receive it without knowing his name.
  • Pluralist reading (Hick): the verses are the language of love and devotion, not literal claims about other faiths.

Mission 2

Miracles: Hume, Wiles and Christian belief

  • David Hume (1711–1776), Enquiry (1748), Section X: "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence." Uniform experience of natural law outweighs any testimony to a miracle; and witnesses are often few, unschooled, eager to believe, and from "ignorant and barbarous nations".
  • Maurice Wiles (1923–2005), God's Action in the World (1986): a God who intervenes to cure some but not to stop Auschwitz or Hiroshima would be arbitrary and partial. So God acts in the single act of creating and sustaining the world, not in particular miracles.
  • Christian responses: Swinburne: testimony and religious significance can make some miracles reasonable to believe. Many Catholics and evangelicals accept physical miracles. Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976) demythologised the New Testament: miracle stories call for existential decision.
  • Test case: the resurrection. "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14, WEBBE). Is it a historical event, or a symbol of new life? Christians disagree; most churches teach a real bodily resurrection.
  • Coherence: Wiles' view avoids arbitrariness but critics say it removes answered prayer and the resurrection.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Christian belief in miracles is not reasonable."

  • Reasons for: Hume: belief should be proportioned to evidence, and laws of nature are supported by uniform experience. Wiles: an interventionist God would be morally arbitrary.
  • Reasons against: Swinburne's principles of credulity and testimony; Hume assumes uniform experience, which begs the question. A God who creates nature can act within it.
  • Reasons against: "reasonable" may mean consistent with reason, not proved by it (AQA's definition): a believer's trust in God (belief in) can make miracle-belief consistent.
  • Other views: anti-realist and Bultmannian readings keep the meaning of miracle stories without claims about breaking natural law.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as evidence? Does the resurrection differ from other miracles? Which sense of "reasonable" is in play?

Mission 3

The dialogue and its starting points

  • The dialogue asks how Christian ethics responds to, and has been shaped by, three secular approaches:
  • Deontological (duty): Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Groundwork of the Metaphysic of Morals (1785).
  • Teleological and consequential (outcomes): Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789).
  • Character-based: virtue ethics, from Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics.
  • Key Christian moral principles to test them against:
  • Love of God and neighbour: "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:31)
  • The Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12); the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); agape (self-giving love).
  • Imago Dei: humans made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), so sanctity of life and human dignity.
  • Justice, forgiveness, stewardship of creation.
Mission 3

Christian responses to Kant

  • Common ground:
  • Duty and absolute rules echo the commandments: some acts are wrong whatever the outcome.
  • The humanity formula (treat persons always as ends, never merely as means) fits imago Dei.
  • Universalisability resembles the Golden Rule, though Kant himself said the Golden Rule was not his principle.
  • Kant's moral argument: duty points to God, freedom and immortality as postulates of practical reason (Critique of Practical Reason, 1788).
  • Tensions:
  • Autonomy: Kant says the moral law comes from reason itself. Obeying God because God commands is heteronomy in his terms.
  • Many Christians hold theonomy: the moral law comes from God, though reason can know it (natural law).
  • Kant's Jesus is a moral example (Religion within the Bounds of Bare Reason, 1793), not a saviour by grace.

> "For love, as an affection, cannot be commanded, but beneficence for duty's sake may; even though we are not impelled to it by any inclination- nay, are even repelled by a natural and unconquerable aversion."

Q1 What kind of love does Kant think Jesus can command?

Show answer

Practical love: doing good from duty, seated in the will, not a feeling.

Q2 Where might a Christian disagree?

Show answer

Many Christians see agape as a gift of grace that changes the heart (Galatians 5:22), not only a duty done against inclination.

Mission 3

Christian responses to Bentham

  • Common ground:
  • Concern to reduce suffering: the hungry, the sick and the prisoner matter (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Impartiality: each to count for one, like neighbour-love that crosses boundaries (the Good Samaritan, Luke 10:25–37).
  • William Paley (1743–1805), The Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy (1785): a theological utilitarian. God wills human happiness, so we discover God's will by asking what tends to the general happiness.
  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), Situation Ethics (1966): a Christian consequentialism, but measured by agape, not pleasure.
  • Tensions:
  • Bentham's only good is pleasure; for Christians the highest good is God.
  • No act is wrong in itself, yet Paul rejects "Let's do evil, that good may come" (Romans 3:8).
  • Sanctity of life: an innocent person may not be sacrificed for the sum.
  • Bentham himself was hostile to organised religion, e.g. Not Paul, but Jesus (1823, as "Gamaliel Smith").

Mission 3

Christian responses to virtue ethics

  • Aristotle: eudaimonia (flourishing) is the goal; virtues are habits at the mean between excess and deficiency.
  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae, joined Aristotle to Christian faith:
  • Cardinal virtues: prudence, justice, fortitude (courage), temperance: acquired by practice.
  • Theological virtues: faith, hope and love (1 Corinthians 13:13): given by God's grace.
  • The goal becomes beatitude: union with God, not only flourishing in this life.
  • New Testament character lists: the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3–10); the fruit of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23); imitating Christ.
  • Modern revival: Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025), After Virtue (1981); Stanley Hauerwas (b. 1940), A Community of Character (1981): the Church as the community that forms Christian character.
  • Tensions: Aristotle's virtues are won by effort; Christians stress grace. Aristotle prizes the proud "great-souled" man; Christians prize humility. Virtue ethics gives little guidance in a hard case, where Christians also have commands.

Habit and fruit

> "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, and self-control." (Galatians 5:22–23)

Show answer

Aristotle: by repeated practice. Paul: as the Spirit's "fruit", grown in the believer.

Q2 How did Aquinas hold both together?

Show answer

Acquired virtues come by practice; the theological virtues are infused by grace.

Mission 3Diversity

How far is Christian ethics deontological, teleological or character-based?

  • Deontological strands: the Ten Commandments; divine command ethics (e.g. Karl Barth, 1886–1968: the good is what God commands); Catholic teaching on intrinsically evil acts; natural law, as AQA classes it (fixed primary precepts).
  • Teleological strands: natural law rests on a telos (God-given purposes); Paley's utilitarianism; Fletcher's situation ethics; proportionalism (e.g. Bernard Hoose, Proportionalism, 1987), which weighs proportionate reasons though AQA lists it with natural law under deontological; ethics shaped by the coming Kingdom of God.
  • Character-based strands: Aquinas' virtues; Hauerwas; Orthodox theosis (becoming like God); "What would Jesus do?"
  • Emphases: Catholic moral teaching joins natural law, virtue and the Magisterium; Reformed and evangelical Protestants stress scripture and grace; liberal Protestants are more open to situational and consequential reasoning.
  • Many conclude: Christian ethics is a hybrid; the question is which strand grounds the others.

Mission 3

How other ethical perspectives have shaped Christian ethics

  • Kant's dignity language entered Christian ethics: Karol Wojtyła (later Pope John Paul II), Love and Responsibility (1960): the "personalistic norm": a person must never be treated merely as a means.
  • Consequentialism pressed Christians to weigh outcomes: situation ethics in the 1960s; proportionalism among some Catholic moralists, rejected by John Paul II in Veritatis Splendor (1993).
  • Virtue revival began with G. E. M. Anscombe (1919–2001), a Catholic: "Modern Moral Philosophy" (1958) argued that "moral ought" makes sense only within a divine-law view, so secular ethics should return to virtue.
  • Challenges: if reason alone (Kant) or happiness (Bentham) can find what is right, what is the role of the Bible and the Church?
  • Support: both theories reach many Christian conclusions (respect for persons, care for the suffering), which some take as evidence of a moral law written in human hearts (Romans 2:14–15).

Mission 3Evaluate

"Christian ethics is best understood as character-based."

  • For: Jesus attacks inner attitudes, not only acts (anger and lust, Matthew 5:21–28). The Beatitudes and fruit of the Spirit describe character. Aquinas' synthesis and Hauerwas make virtue central. Imitating Christ is a pattern of life, not a rulebook.
  • Against: the commandments and Catholic teaching on intrinsically evil acts are deontological. Romans 3:8 rules out some acts whatever one's character. Natural law and situation ethics are teleological. Virtue alone may give little guidance in a hard case.
  • Another view: Christian ethics grounds everything in God: commands, purposes and character all flow from God's nature, so no secular label fits fully.
  • Judge it by: Which strand do the sources make basic? Does the answer differ between Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox traditions? Does the label capture grace, which none of the three secular theories includes?
Mission 3Worked answer

Building a synoptic paragraph

Question: "Christian ethics is incompatible with Kant's ethics." Evaluate this view.

  • Point: Kant and Christianity share absolute duties and respect for persons.
  • Evidence and link: the humanity formula parallels imago Dei (Genesis 1:27); Wojtyła's personalistic norm shows Christian ethics adopting it.
  • Counter: Kant's autonomy rejects obeying a command because God gives it; Christians hold theonomy.
  • Evaluation: so they are compatible in content but differ in foundation; how far that matters depends on whether foundations or conclusions define an ethic.
  • Skill tip: the 25-mark dialogue essay is synoptic: link Component 1 theory (Kant, Bentham, virtue) to Christian sources and reach a reasoned judgement.

Mission 4

Principles that do the work

  • Sanctity of life: life is God's gift; humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • Dominion and stewardship over animals and the earth (Genesis 1:28; 2:15).
  • Agape, justice, truthfulness, faithfulness.
  • Christians weigh these through scripture, tradition and Church teaching, reason and conscience, in different proportions:
  • Catholic: the Magisterium applies natural law (e.g. Evangelium Vitae, 1995).
  • Protestant: scripture first; wide range from conservative evangelical to liberal.
  • Orthodox: tradition, the Fathers and pastoral care (oikonomia: flexibility for the person's good).
  • Secular partners: Kant (persons as ends), Bentham (pleasure and pain of all who can suffer), virtue ethics (what a good person would do).
Mission 4

Beginning of life

  • Embryo research, cloning, "designer" babies: Catholic teaching treats the embryo as a human person from conception; it rejects embryo research that destroys embryos, cloning, and embryo selection (Dignitas Personae, 2008).
  • Many Protestants take a gradualist view: moral status grows with development; some accept regulated research on early embryos (UK law: up to 14 days).
  • Abortion: Catholic teaching: direct abortion is always wrong; double effect allows treatment that saves the mother's life where the death of the fetus is foreseen but not intended.
  • The Church of England opposes abortion in general but accepts it may be justified in some cases, e.g. where the pregnancy threatens the mother's life. Some liberal Protestants stress the woman's conscience.
  • Dialogue: Bentham weighs suffering and consequences (and sentience); Kant protects rational persons, so whether the embryo counts is the disputed point; virtue ethicists (e.g. Rosalind Hursthouse, "Virtue Theory and Abortion", 1991) ask what a wise, compassionate person would do.
Mission 4

End of life

  • Voluntary euthanasia and assisted suicide: the Catholic Church calls euthanasia a grave violation of God's law (Evangelium Vitae 65). It allows refusing extraordinary (burdensome) treatment and pain relief that may shorten life (double effect).
  • Leaders of the Church of England and the Catholic Church in England and Wales opposed the 2024–25 assisted-dying bill and stress palliative care.
  • Some Christians argue for it from compassion: e.g. Lord Carey, a former Archbishop of Canterbury (2014), and Archbishop Desmond Tutu (2016).
  • Capital punishment: the Catholic Catechism was revised in 2018 to call it "inadmissible" (CCC 2267). Some Christians support it from Genesis 9:6.
  • Dialogue: Kant forbade suicide as using oneself merely as a means (Groundwork II), yet defended the death penalty for murder (Metaphysics of Morals, 1797). Bentham judged punishment by deterrence and consequences. Peter Singer's preference utilitarianism supports voluntary euthanasia.

Mission 4

Animal life and death

  • Dominion (Genesis 1:26–28) has been read as permission to use animals; Genesis 9:3 allows meat-eating. Stewardship reads it as care on God's behalf.
  • Thomas Aquinas: animals lack rational souls; cruelty to them is wrong mainly because it trains cruelty to humans (Summa Contra Gentiles III.112).
  • Catholic Catechism (CCC 2415–2418): animals may be used for food and reasonable research that saves human lives, but needless suffering is contrary to human dignity. Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): creatures have value in themselves.
  • Andrew Linzey (b. 1952), Christianity and the Rights of Animals (1987): animals have "theos-rights": rights grounded in God's care for them.
  • The prescribed issues: intensive farming (welfare vs cheap food), scientific procedures and cloning (Dolly the sheep, 1996), blood sports (cruelty for pleasure), animal organs for human transplant (xenotransplantation).
  • Dialogue: Bentham counted animal suffering; Kant gave only indirect duties to animals, like Aquinas.
Mission 4Read the source

Two tests for how we treat animals

> "A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10)

Bentham, Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. XVII, footnote:

> "the question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

Q1 What does each make the key test?

Show answer

Proverbs: the character of the righteous person; Bentham: the animal's capacity to suffer.

Q2 Which secular approach is closest to Proverbs here?

Show answer

Virtue ethics: how we treat animals shows and shapes our character.

Mission 4

Theft and lying

  • "You shall not steal." "You shall not give false testimony against your neighbour." (Exodus 20:15–16)
  • Theft: Aquinas: in urgent and manifest need, taking what is needed to survive is "not properly speaking theft" (Summa Theologiae II-II q. 66 a. 7): surplus property is owed to the poor.
  • Lying: Augustine (On Lying, 395) and Aquinas (II-II q. 110 a. 3): every lie is a sin, even to save a life.
  • Bible cases debated: the Hebrew midwives (Exodus 1:19) and Rahab (Joshua 2:4) deceive to save lives. Aquinas: they were rewarded for their fear of God, not for the lie.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), "What Is Meant by Telling the Truth?" (1943, written in a Nazi prison): truthfulness means saying the right word within each relationship. His example: a child asked in front of the class whether his father drinks may deny it; the teacher who asked is the one at fault.
  • Dialogue: Kant agrees with Augustine (no lie, even to a murderer at the door, 1797). Bentham allows a lie that produces more happiness. Situation ethics and virtue ethics side with Bonhoeffer.
Mission 4

Marriage, same-sex relationships and transgender matters

  • Marriage: "they will be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24); "What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." (Mark 10:9) A sacrament for Catholics and Orthodox; a holy covenant for most Protestants.
  • Same-sex relationships, a range of views:
  • Catholic: same-sex sexual acts are not morally permissible; persons are to be accepted with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (CCC 2358). Fiducia Supplicans (2023) allows informal, non-liturgical blessings of couples.
  • Church of England: doctrine of marriage unchanged (one man and one woman); Prayers of Love and Faith for same-sex couples commended in 2023.
  • The Methodist Church in Britain (2021), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017) and Quakers permit same-sex marriage; many evangelical and Orthodox churches do not.
  • Transgender matters: Catholic documents (2019; Dignitas Infinita, 2024) reject "gender theory" and, as a rule, medical transition; Church of England guidance (2018) lets parishes adapt a baptismal-affirmation service to mark a person's transition.
  • Dialogue: Mill's harm principle and utilitarian wellbeing challenge traditional teaching; Kant himself held conservative views of sex; Christian supporters of change argue from agape and justice; traditionalists from scripture and natural law.
Mission 4

Genetic engineering

  • Somatic gene therapy (treating one patient's cells) is widely accepted, including by Catholic teaching (Dignitas Personae 26).
  • Germline editing (changes passed to descendants) is judged by Catholic teaching not permissible in its present state, and many Protestants oppose it too: unknown risks to future people who cannot consent; fear of eugenics. The 2018 birth of gene-edited twins in China was condemned worldwide.
  • "Playing God": humans overstep their place. Reply: Philip Hefner (1932–2024), The Human Factor (1993): humans are "created co-creators".
  • Dialogue: Bentham: weigh the suffering prevented against the risks. Kant: future people must be treated as ends, not designed products. Virtue: humility vs hubris (Michael Sandel, The Case against Perfection, 2007: a secular argument many Christians cite).

Mission 4Diversity

Has secular ethics changed Christian views?

  • Past: Enlightenment ideas of rights and dignity, alongside Christian campaigners such as William Wilberforce, fed into the abolition of the slave trade (1807).
  • Present: the Catholic Catechism's 2018 change on capital punishment; growing Christian concern for animal welfare (Laudato Si'); some churches' changes on same-sex marriage.
  • Support: secular theories often reach Christian conclusions (Kant on suicide and lying; utilitarian concern for animals and the poor).
  • Christian responses to change:
  • Development of doctrine (John Henry Newman, 1845): the Church's understanding grows without contradicting its core.
  • Conservatives: change driven by culture undermines scripture's authority.
  • Liberals: the Spirit leads the Church into truth over time (John 16:13).
  • Implication for authority: if teaching changes under outside pressure, is the source scripture, tradition, or reason?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Christian ethics has been weakened by its dialogue with secular ethics."

  • For: where teaching changes (capital punishment, sexuality in some churches), critics ask whether scripture or culture decides. Utilitarian and harm-principle reasoning has led many Western Christians to question traditional teaching.
  • Against: secular theories often support Christian conclusions. Dialogue has sharpened Christian reasoning (double effect, Bonhoeffer on truth). Aquinas already used Aristotle; drawing on outside reason is part of Christian tradition.
  • Another view: "weakened" depends on the source of authority: Catholics stress development within the Magisterium; many evangelicals see sola scriptura as the check; liberals welcome change.
  • Judge it by: Has the change been in conclusions or in foundations? Did Christian sources already contain the resources for change? Is a changing ethic weaker, or more responsive?
Mission check

?A Christian ethicist argues: "Germline editing to remove a fatal disease is permissible, because it reduces great suffering; but editing for height or intelligence is wrong, because it treats children as designed products." Which description is most accurate?

on applied issues, Christians draw on duty, outcomes and character together; the dialogue is often about reasons more than conclusions.

Mission 5

Wealth in Christian sources

  • Warnings: "It is easier for a camel to go through a needle's eye than for a rich man to enter into God's Kingdom." (Mark 10:25)
  • "For the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil." (1 Timothy 6:10): the love of money, not money itself.
  • Sharing: the first believers "had all things in common" (Acts 2:44); "whoever has the world's goods and sees his brother in need… how does God's love remain in him?" (1 John 3:17)
  • Wealth as stewardship: held on trust from God for others' good.
  • Three Christian responses:
  • Catholic Social Teaching (from Rerum Novarum, 1891): private property is legitimate, but goods have a universal destination: they exist for all.
  • Liberation theology: Gustavo Gutiérrez (1928–2024), A Theology of Liberation (1971): God's preferential option for the poor.
  • Prosperity gospel: faith brings material blessing; criticised by most churches as distorting scripture.
Mission 5

Wealth in dialogue

  • Bentham: extra money adds less happiness to the rich than to the poor, so redistribution can raise total happiness. Peter Singer, "Famine, Affluence, and Morality" (1972): if we can prevent great suffering without sacrificing anything of comparable moral importance, we ought to: close to Christian demands for giving.
  • Kant: beneficence is an imperfect duty: required, but with freedom over when and how.
  • Aristotle: liberality (generosity) is the mean between wastefulness and meanness. Aquinas lists avarice among the capital vices.
  • Challenge, past: Karl Marx (1818–1883): religion is "the opium of the people" (1844), consoling the poor instead of changing their condition.
  • Response: liberation theology uses Marxist social analysis while rejecting Marx's atheism; it was criticised by the Vatican (1984) and later partly embraced (Pope Francis).
  • Max Weber, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1905): Weber argued that Protestant thrift and disciplined work helped shape the spirit of modern capitalism.

Mission 5

Tolerance and freedom of religious expression

  • Past: Christian states persecuted heretics and other faiths (e.g. the Inquisition; wars of religion, 16th–17th centuries).
  • John Locke (1632–1704), A Letter Concerning Toleration (1689): faith cannot be forced, since God wants sincere belief; yet Locke excluded atheists and (in practice) Catholics.
  • J. S. Mill (1806–1873), On Liberty (1859): the harm principle: freedom of belief and expression unless it harms others.
  • Catholic turning point: Vatican II, Dignitatis Humanae (1965): religious freedom is a right grounded in human dignity.
  • Today: freedom of thought, conscience and religion (European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9). The blasphemy offence was abolished in England and Wales in 2008.
  • UK cases: Eweida v UK (2013): a BA worker's right to wear a cross upheld; Lee v Ashers Baking (2018): Christian bakers not compelled to write a message they disagreed with. Other Christian claimants lost (e.g. Ladele, 2013, a registrar who would not register civil partnerships).
Mission 5Diversity

Tolerance: what Christians disagree about

  • Tolerating other faiths: almost all churches now defend religious freedom for all, partly from Locke and Vatican II.
  • Mission and tolerance: evangelicals stress the right to share faith; exclusivist readings of John 14:6 raise the question of whether tolerance means accepting other views as true (it need not).
  • Christians as a minority: many Christians worldwide face persecution; freedom of religion protects them too.
  • Secular challenge: some argue religious exemptions privilege believers over others (e.g. equality law); some Christians argue that conscience exemptions protect everyone's freedom.
  • Key distinction: tolerance (allowing beliefs one thinks false) is not relativism (thinking no belief is false).
Mission 5

Free will and moral responsibility

  • "I have set before you life and death… Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19): humans are responsible for their choices.
  • Augustine (354–430): after the Fall, the will is bent towards sin; only grace frees it. Pelagius (c. 354–418): humans can choose good unaided; condemned as heresy (418).
  • John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes (1559): predestination: God elects who will be saved.
  • Jacobus Arminius (1560–1609): prevenient grace enables every person to accept or refuse God.
  • Council of Trent (1547): the free will is weakened but not destroyed; it cooperates with grace.
  • Dialogue: hard determinism says no one is ultimately responsible; Christians mostly reject it, as it seems to make judgement unjust. Libertarianism fits most Arminian and Catholic views. Many Calvinists are compatibilists (Jonathan Edwards, Freedom of the Will, 1754): we freely do what we want, though God determines our wants.
  • Challenge, present: neuroscience (e.g. Benjamin Libet's 1983 experiments) is used to question free will; interpretations are disputed.

Mission 5

Conscience in Christian thought

  • Paul: Gentiles "show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15).
  • Aquinas: synderesis (the inborn grasp of "do good, avoid evil") and conscientia (applying it to a case). Conscience can err: vincible ignorance is blameworthy; invincible ignorance is not.
  • Joseph Butler (1692–1752), Fifteen Sermons (1726): conscience is the supreme principle in human nature.
  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890), Letter to the Duke of Norfolk (1875): conscience is "the aboriginal Vicar of Christ"; he would toast "to Conscience first, and to the Pope afterwards".
  • Catholic teaching today: a person must follow a certain conscience, but has a duty to form it by scripture, Church teaching and prayer.
  • Protestant emphasis: the conscience bound by scripture (Luther at Worms, 1521).
  • Conscience in action: Bonhoeffer's resistance to Nazism; conscientious objectors in both world wars.
Mission 5Read the source

Conscience as authority

Joseph Butler, Fifteen Sermons, Sermon II (1726):

> "Had it strength, as it had right; had it power, as it had manifest authority, it would absolutely govern the world."

Q1 What does Butler say conscience has, and what does it lack?

Show answer

It has right and authority; it lacks the strength and power to make people obey it.

Q2 How does this fit Romans 2:15?

Show answer

Both see conscience as a God-given inner law with authority over a person's actions.

Mission 5

Challenges to the Christian view of conscience

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Ego and the Id (1923): conscience is the super-ego: parental and social rules internalised, felt as guilt. It need not be God's voice.
  • Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900), On the Genealogy of Morality (1887): "bad conscience" is instinct turned inward, which Christianity exploits.
  • Developmental psychology (e.g. Piaget, Kohlberg): conscience develops in stages, varying with upbringing.
  • Christian responses:
  • God can work through human development; the origin of conscience does not settle its authority.
  • Aquinas already allowed that conscience errs and must be formed.
  • Freud's account explains guilt, but not obviously the sense of being obliged to resist one's society, as Bonhoeffer did.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Conscience is a reliable guide for Christian moral decisions."

  • For: Romans 2:15 and Butler give conscience God-given authority. Newman puts it before the Pope. Bonhoeffer and conscientious objectors show conscience resisting unjust powers. Catholic teaching says a certain conscience must be obeyed.
  • Against: conscience errs (Aquinas), and can be shaped by culture and upbringing (Freud, Piaget). Consciences contradict one another. Many Christians have believed in conscience that persecution was right.
  • Another view: conscience is reliable only when formed: by scripture (many Protestants), by Church teaching (Catholic), by prayer and community (Orthodox). Then the real question is who forms it.
  • Judge it by: Does the origin of conscience (God or psychology) settle its authority? Can a guide that needs forming be called reliable? How do free will and responsibility depend on it?
Mission check

?A Catholic doctor is asked to take part in a procedure the Church teaches is wrong. She has studied the teaching and prayed, and is certain it is wrong. A colleague says: "Your 'conscience' is just Church rules you absorbed as a child." Which response draws most accurately on Christian thought?

Christians value conscience highly, but most hold that it must be formed. The debate is over who forms it, and how far secular accounts of its origin matter.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • AQA's four issues: reasonable (based on or consistent with reason), meaningful (and for whom), coherent, and belief in vs belief that.
  • God: Anselm and Aquinas join faith and reason; Pascal and Barth resist natural theology; Mackie, Hick and Griffin show philosophy reshaping doctrine; verification and falsification challenge meaning.
  • Afterlife and authority: Plato and Aristotle shaped Christian views of the soul; materialism and biblical criticism challenge them; Price, Kierkegaard and Plantinga reframe what faith is.

Mission 2

  • Religious experience: Christian sources and mystics; Swinburne's principles, James's fruits and 1 John 4:1 tests against challenges from science and diversity.
  • Science: Barbour's four models; Dawkins (conflict), Gould (independence), Polkinghorne (dialogue); history from Galileo to Lemaître.
  • Other religions: exclusivism, Rahner's inclusivism, Hick's Kantian pluralism and its critics; miracles: Hume's evidence and Wiles' arbitrariness against Swinburne and the centrality of the resurrection.

Mission 3

  • Christians find common ground with Kant (duty, persons as ends), Bentham (concern for suffering, impartiality) and virtue ethics (character, Aquinas' virtues), but differ over autonomy, pleasure as the good, and grace.
  • Christian ethics contains deontological, teleological and character-based strands; traditions weight them differently.
  • Secular ethics has shaped Christian thought (Wojtyła, situation ethics, proportionalism, Anscombe) and raised questions about the authority of Bible and Church.

Mission 4

  • Sanctity of life, stewardship, agape and truthfulness shape Christian responses, weighed differently by Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox Christians.
  • On euthanasia, animals, lying and genetics, secular theories sometimes support Christian views (Kant on suicide and lying) and sometimes challenge them (utilitarian arguments on euthanasia, harm-principle arguments on sexuality).
  • Changes in Christian teaching raise questions about authority: development, culture, or reason?

Mission 5

  • Wealth: stewardship and the universal destination of goods; liberation and prosperity theologies; dialogue with Bentham, Singer, Kant, Aristotle and Marx.
  • Tolerance: from persecution to Locke, Mill and Dignitatis Humanae; freedom of religious expression in UK law today.
  • Free will (Augustine, Pelagius, Calvin, Arminius, Trent; compatibilism) and conscience (Paul, Aquinas, Butler, Newman) face challenges from determinism, Freud and Nietzsche.
