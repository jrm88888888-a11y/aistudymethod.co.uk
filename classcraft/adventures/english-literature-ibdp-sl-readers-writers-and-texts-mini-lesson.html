Mission 1

Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

Make a guess: Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

IB areas of exploration · readers, writers and textsScreen 1

One text, three corners

  • Writer: makes choices of words, form and structure, in a context.
  • Text: the choices themselves, on the page. This is your evidence.
  • Reader: brings experience, values and context, and interprets.
  • This area asks how meaning is constructed by a writer and interpreted by readers, and how texts affect us.
IB areas of exploration · readers, writers and textsScreen 2

The writer's intention: evidence, not a verdict

  • You rarely know what a writer meant. Even a writer's own comment is another text to read.
  • Frankenstein's 1818 preface says the hero's views are "by no means to be conceived as existing always in my own conviction". In 1831 Mary Shelley wrote that, as far as she could recall, her husband had written that preface.
  • W. K. Wimsatt and Monroe Beardsley (1946) argued that intention outside the text shouldn't decide its meaning; Roland Barthes (1967) turned attention to the reader.

How your exam asks this

  • No exam question names an area of exploration: the areas organise your course. This one builds the close reading every component needs.
  • Paper 1: guided literary analysis of an unseen literary passage. SL: one of two passages, 20 marks.
  • A guiding question is offered for each passage.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Does a text mean what the writer meant, or what the reader finds?

Neither alone. A writer makes choices; readers interpret them, from their own context. The strongest IB answers argue from the text's choices (Chopin's narration lets us hear…) and show how they shape a reader's response, rather than guessing what the writer meant.

Mission 2

Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

Make a guess: Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 1

Seven concepts, seven questions

ConceptA question it helps you ask
IdentityHow does the text build or challenge who someone is?
CultureHow does it show or question shared values and customs?
CreativityHow does the writer make something new?
CommunicationHow does meaning pass between writer, text and reader?
PerspectiveWhose point of view shapes what we see?
TransformationWhat changes: a character, a reader, an older text?
RepresentationHow does the text depict people, groups or ideas?
IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 2

A lens, not a topic

  • A topic says what: Jane's independence in Jane Eyre.
  • A concept asks how and why: through identity, how does Brontë's first-person narration let Jane claim an identity others deny her?
  • Use one concept as the thread of an argument; don't tour all seven.

Where this counts

  • No criterion marks a concept: concepts help you build an argument.
  • Paper 2 (25 marks): its four general questions may not name a concept, but many turn on ideas like representation or perspective; one concept can link your two works.
  • Individual Oral: a concept can sharpen how both texts present your global issue.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

Use it as a lens. Pick the one concept that best opens your text, turn it into a how-question (how does Brontë's narration build Jane's identity?), and let it guide which methods and evidence you analyse. A concept named but not used earns nothing.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Writer, text and reader: you analyse the text's choices and how they shape a reader's response.
  • A writer's intention is rarely knowable; treat a writer's comments as another text, not a final answer.
  • Paper 1 tests this on an unseen literary passage.
  • The seven concepts: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation, representation.
  • Use one as a lens: it turns a topic into a how-question and an arguable thesis.
  • No criterion marks a concept; it helps Paper 2 and the IO.

Key quotations to learn

  • Free! Body and soul free! ("The Story of an Hour") Her private joy, which only the reader hears.
  • joy that kills ("The Story of an Hour", last line) The doctors' reading; the irony is completed by the reader.
  • I bid my hideous progeny go forth and prosper (Frankenstein, 1831 introduction) A writer sending her text out to readers.
  • by no means to be conceived as existing always in my own conviction (Frankenstein, 1818 preface) The author is not the hero: don't confuse a character's views with the writer's.
  • I am no bird; and no net ensnares me (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Identity: Jane rejects Rochester's image of her.
  • I am a free human being with an independent will (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) She names the identity she claims.
  • because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Representation: the labels others use for her.
  • I have as much soul as you (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Equality claimed across class.
