Mission 1

Mission 1: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 1

What counts as growth

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (real output) over time.
  • Rate of growth = % change in real GDP from one year to the next.
  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP removes price changes.
  • Total real GDP shows the size of the economy. Real GDP per capita (per person) is closer to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose by 2%" — not: "GDP rose by 2%", when you mean output.
Mission 1

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP500 bn540 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)500 bn515 bn
Population50 m51 m
  • Real growth = (515 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 3.0%.
  • Nominal growth = (540 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 8.0%. Most of that is higher prices.
  • Real GDP per capita: 500 bn / 50 m = 10,000 → 515 bn / 51 m = 10,098.
  • Growth per capita = (10,098 − 10,000) / 10,000 × 100 = 0.98%.
  • Population grew too, so output per person grew much less than total output.
  • Small rates compound. Real GDP per capita doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year, about 10 years at 7%, about 8 years at 9%.
  • After 35 years, 2% a year makes output per person about 2 times as big; 7% a year makes it about 10.7 times as big.

?Illustrative data: a country's real GDP rises from 800 bn to 820 bn in a year. Its population rises from 40 million to 41 million. What is the growth rate of real GDP per capita?
Mission 1

Actual and potential growth on a PPC

  • Actual growth: a rise in the output the economy actually produces (real GDP).
  • Potential growth: a rise in the economy's productive capacity: the most it could produce with all resources fully and efficiently used.
  • On a PPC:
    • point A inside the curve: unemployed resources (spare capacity)
    • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth, using spare capacity
    • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
  • Say: "potential growth: productive capacity increases" — not: "the economy produces more".
Mission 1

Short-run growth: AD rises

  • Start with spare capacity: real output Y1 is below full-employment output Yf.
  • AD rises (more consumption, investment, government spending or net exports): AD1 → AD2.
  • Real output rises Y1 → Y2; firms use idle workers and machines. The price level rises a little.
  • This is short-run (actual) growth: the economy moves towards its capacity. LRAS has not moved.
  • Close to Yf, more AD raises the price level more and real output less.
Mission 1

Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right

  • Long-run growth: productive capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right: LRAS1 → LRAS2.
  • Full-employment output rises: Yf1 → Yf2. This is the same event as the PPC shifting out.
  • With AD unchanged, real output rises and the price level falls.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve, the vertical section moves right: the economy's maximum output rises.
  • Long-run growth is what lets output keep rising year after year without rising inflation.

Mission 1

Causes of growth

  • Short run (actual growth): a rise in AD, when there is spare capacity: C, I, G or (X − M).
  • Long run (potential growth): more or better factors of production.
    • Quantity: a bigger labour force (migration, more people in work); more capital (investment).
    • Quality: education and training (human capital); new technology.
    • Institutions: property rights, rule of law, stable government; good infrastructure.
  • Investment does both: it raises AD now and capacity later.
Mission 1

Benefits and costs of growth

BenefitsCosts
Higher real incomes and living standardsPollution, emissions, depletion of resources
More jobs; less cyclical unemploymentGains can be unequal: income distribution may widen
More tax revenue for health, education, welfareInflation, if AD grows faster than capacity
Firms' profits fund more investmentMore imports: current account can worsen
Less absolute povertyInvestment now means less consumption now
  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year, lifting almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
Mission 1

Sustainable and inclusive growth

  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without reducing future generations' ability to meet theirs (idea from the Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • It needs growth that does not use up natural resources faster than they renew or degrade the environment for the future.
  • Possible conflicts:
    • growth vs low inflation: AD rising faster than LRAS raises the price level
    • growth vs environmental sustainability: more output, more resource use and emissions
    • growth vs equity: gains may go mainly to those with capital or skills
  • Say: "sustainable growth" — not: "slow growth". It is about whether growth can continue, not how fast it is.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: over ten years a country's real GDP grows by 6% a year. Over the same years the real incomes of its poorest 40% of households hardly change. Which description fits this best?
Mission 1Evaluate

Is economic growth always good?

It depends on:

  • The source: growth from productivity and investment can last; growth from AD alone with no spare capacity mainly raises prices.
  • Who gains: if most gains go to a few, average figures overstate how much most people's living standards rise.
  • The environment: growth with high emissions can lower future living standards; cleaner technology and carbon pricing can reduce the conflict.
  • The level of development: an extra 2% matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country than in a rich one.
Mission check

?An economy is at full capacity: unemployment is at its natural rate and firms are working at full capacity. The government wants output to keep rising over the next ten years. Which policy is most likely to achieve this?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Make a guess: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Mission 2

Three groups, not two

  • Start with the working-age population.
  • Usually everyone aged 15 or 16 and over, depending on the country.
  • Split it three ways:
    • Employed: did any paid work in the survey week (even 1 hour), or has a job but is temporarily away.
    • Unemployed: no job, available to start, and actively looking for work.
    • Economically inactive: everyone else.
  • ILO definition of unemployed: without a job, looked for work in the last 4 weeks, can start in the next 2 weeks (or has found a job and is waiting to start it).
  • Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • The labour force is also called the economically active population.
Mission 2

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 28.5 m, unemployed 1.5 m, inactive 20 m.
  • Labour force = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • = 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.
  • Not 1.5 ÷ 50 (= 3%): the inactive are not in the labour force.
  • Say: "5% of the labour force" — not: "5% of the population".
Mission 2

Inactive is not unemployed

  • Economically inactive: not working and not both looking and available.
  • Who? Full-time students, retired people, people looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled.
  • And discouraged workers: they want a job but have stopped looking, because they think none is available.
  • Discouraged workers are not counted as unemployed. Joblessness is understated.
  • Say: "economically inactive" — not: "unemployed", for someone who is not looking for work.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for a country: working-age population 8.0 m, employed 4.6 m, unemployed 0.4 m, inactive 3.0 m. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 2

Two ways to count

  • Labour force survey (ask people):
    • interviewers question a large sample of households about the week before
    • anyone who meets the ILO definition is counted as unemployed
  • Claimant count (count benefit claims):
    • counts people claiming benefits because they are unemployed
    • comes straight from benefit records, so no survey is needed
  • Many countries run a survey using the ILO definition. Some also publish a count of people registered as unemployed.
Mission 2

Which count is better?

Labour force survey (ILO)Claimant count
Who is countedEveryone meeting the ILO definitionOnly people claiming the benefits
MissesSampling error; people may answer wronglyJobless people not eligible or not claiming (e.g. partner earns, savings, aged under 18)
Changes in the rulesSame definition over time and across countriesBenefit rules change, so the count changes even if joblessness does not
Speed and costSlower and costlyQuick, cheap, available for small local areas
  • So the two figures usually differ, and can move apart.
Quick check
?Which person would be counted as unemployed in a labour force survey using the ILO definition, but not in the claimant count?
Mission 2

What the rate misses

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers count as inactive, not unemployed.
  • Underemployment:
    • part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed
    • people in jobs well below their skills count as employed too
  • Workers in the informal economy may be missed or misclassified.
  • One national rate hides big differences by region, age, gender and ethnic group.
  • Real case: in 2013 Spain's unemployment rate for under-25s was above 50% (Eurostat: 55.5%). Many under-25s are full-time students, so the youth labour force is small: this is a share of the young people in the labour force, not of all young people.
  • So the unemployment rate understates how many people want work, or more work.
Mission 2Evaluate

How well does the unemployment rate measure joblessness?

It depends on:

  • Hidden unemployment: in a long slump, more people give up looking. The rate can fall with no new jobs.
  • Underemployment: when firms cut hours instead of jobs, the rate rises less than lost work.
  • The method: survey response, benefit rules and the size of the informal economy all change what is counted.
  • So use it alongside other data: the employment rate, inactivity, hours worked and vacancies.

The unemployment rate can fall when people stop looking for work. Check employment and inactivity before calling a fall good news.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 3

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
  • Seasonal unemployment: jobs that disappear at certain times of year (farm harvests, beach resorts, ski resorts, winter construction).
  • Seasonal unemployment is predictable and recurs every year.
Mission 3

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
Mission 3

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • Also called demand-deficient unemployment.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand).
  • Real output falls below full-employment output: a deflationary (recessionary) gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: US unemployment rose from 5.0% in December 2007 to 10.0% in October 2009 (BLS).
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 3

Wages stuck above equilibrium

  • If wages are held above the market-clearing level, more people want to work than firms want to hire.
  • The excess supply of labour is unemployment.
  • Causes: a minimum wage set above equilibrium; trade union bargaining; wages that do not fall when demand for labour falls.
  • Two diagrams to know: a minimum wage above equilibrium; and a fall in the demand for labour in one industry or region with a wage that does not fall (structural).
  • IB textbooks usually class unemployment caused by labour market rigidities (minimum wages, union bargaining) as structural.
  • Debate: whether minimum wages cost jobs in practice depends on the level set and the market. Studies disagree; the evidence is in Mission 4.
  • Say: "an excess supply of labour at the minimum wage" — not: "a shortage of workers".

  • Takeaway: when wages cannot fall, a fall in labour demand shows up as unemployment, not as lower pay.
Mission 3

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • Natural rate = structural + seasonal + frictional unemployment.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
Mission 3

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Seasonal 0.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5 = 5.5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5.5 = 1.5 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "1.5 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "1.5% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

The costs of unemployment

  • Personal: lost income; skills fade; stress and worse mental and physical health.
  • Social: links with crime and family strain; whole communities decline when a big employer closes; inequality rises.
  • Economic:
    • lost output that can never be recovered: the economy is inside its PPC
    • lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so a larger budget deficit
    • long spells reduce skills, so potential output may fall too
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Mission 3

Unemployment or inflation: which costs more?

  • Unemployment: costs fall heavily on the jobless; lost output is gone for good; long spells cause lasting damage.
  • Inflation: costs are spread across everyone (uncertainty, lost purchasing power, redistribution).
  • Survey evidence: Di Tella, MacCulloch and Oswald (2001) found that people's reported well-being falls more with a 1-point rise in unemployment than with a 1-point rise in inflation.
  • But it depends on the rates: very high inflation (hyperinflation) can wreck an economy.
  • And on who you are: savers and people on fixed incomes fear inflation; workers in insecure jobs fear unemployment.
Quick check
?A deep recession keeps unemployment high for three years. Which effect could reduce the economy's potential output, not just its actual output?
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a law lifts the lowest pay, do some workers lose their jobs?

Make a guess: A government raises the minimum wage. What happens to the number of jobs?

Mission 4

A legal wage floor

  • A minimum wage: the lowest hourly wage an employer may legally pay.
  • It is a price floor in the labour market. It only has an effect if set above the equilibrium wage.
  • Aims: raise low pay · reduce in-work poverty and inequality · make work pay more than benefits.
  • UK example: a National Minimum Wage since 1999. The rate for workers aged 21 and over is called the National Living Wage.
  • Germany example: a national minimum wage since 1 January 2015, starting at €8.50 an hour.
Mission 4

The diagram: a competitive labour market

  • Step 1: labour market clears at We, Le.
  • Step 2: a minimum wage Wmin above We.
  • At Wmin, firms move up their demand curve: employment contracts to Ld.
  • More people want to work at Wmin: supply of labour extends to Ls.
  • Step 3: Ls − Ld is excess supply of labour: unemployment caused by the minimum wage.
  • Workers who keep their jobs earn more; those who lose jobs or cannot find one lose.
  • This is the minimum wage diagram for unemployment: a labour market rigidity, a cause of structural unemployment.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Ld = 900 − 50W; Ls = 100W − 300 (W in £ per hour, L = workers).
  • Equilibrium: 900 − 50W = 100W − 300 → We = £8, Le = 500.
  • Minimum wage £9: Ld = 900 − 450 = 450; Ls = 900 − 300 = 600.
  • Employment falls by 50. Unemployment (excess supply) = 600 − 450 = 150.
  • Wage +12.5%; employment −10%.
Quick check
?Use the market in the previous screen. The minimum wage is set at £10 instead. How much unemployment does it create?
Mission 4

How big is the effect?

  • The bite: the minimum wage as a % of the median wage. A higher bite affects more jobs.
  • UK bite: 45.6% in 1999, 52.5% in 2015; the government then set a target of two-thirds of median hourly pay, to be reached in 2024.
  • Elasticity of demand for labour: job losses are bigger where machines or other workers can replace low-paid staff, wages are a big share of costs, or customers can switch.
  • Elasticity of supply of labour: the more new job-seekers a higher wage draws in, the bigger the excess supply.
  • Firms can also respond in other ways: cut hours, training or perks · raise prices · accept lower profit · reorganise to raise productivity.
Mission 4Graph lab

Set the minimum wage

  • Takeaway: a floor below We does nothing; above We, the job loss grows with the bite and with the elasticity of demand for labour.
Mission 4

Evidence: what happened?

  • New Jersey, 1992: the state minimum wage rose from $4.25 to $5.05 an hour. Neighbouring Pennsylvania's did not change.
  • Card and Krueger (1994) surveyed fast-food restaurants in both states. They found no fall in New Jersey's fast-food employment relative to Pennsylvania's.
  • Critics (Neumark and Wascher, 2000), using payroll records, found a small fall. The debate on methods continues.
  • UK: the Low Pay Commission (2024) said it had "not found strong evidence" that the National Living Wage reduced employment or hours.
  • Effects depend on the bite: evidence from moderate rises may not apply to much larger ones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X has a minimum wage of 6 dollars an hour and a median wage of 10 dollars. Country Y has a minimum wage of 9 dollars and a median wage of 18 dollars. Other things equal, which minimum wage is more likely to reduce employment?
Mission 4

Abolishing minimum wages: a supply-side policy?

  • Abolishing minimum wages is a market-based labour market policy.
  • For: lower labour costs; more jobs for low-skilled and young workers; labour market more flexible; LRAS can shift right.
  • Against: lower pay for the poorest workers; more in-work poverty and inequality; employers with power over wages can pay less; lower spending by low-income households.
  • Debate: the size of the job gain depends on how far the minimum wage was above equilibrium.
Mission check

?A minimum wage is raised by the same amount above the market wage in two industries. In industry A, firms can easily replace staff with self-service machines. In industry B, there is no practical substitute for the workers, and wages are a small share of costs. Other things equal, where is the fall in employment likely to be larger?

The same rise in the minimum wage costs more jobs where workers are easy to replace.

Mission 5

Mission 5: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Make a guess: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Mission 5

Two routes to inflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive.
  • In the AD/AS model, the price level rises in two ways:
    • AD shifts right: spending pulls prices up → demand-pull inflation
    • SRAS shifts left: costs push prices up → cost-push inflation
  • One shift gives a one-off rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs the shift to keep happening.
  • Say: "inflation is a rising price level" — not: "inflation means prices are high".
Mission 5

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • What can push AD right: lower interest rates, easy credit, rising house prices (wealth), high confidence, an export boom, more government spending, tax cuts.
  • With spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises real output.
  • Near full capacity, firms cannot expand much, so extra AD mostly raises the price level.
  • On a Keynesian AS curve: the steeper the section AD moves along, the more of the rise is in prices.
Quick check
?Interest rates are cut and house prices climb, so households borrow and spend more. The economy is already close to full capacity. Which best describes what happens in the short run?
Mission 5

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push: firms' costs rise, so they supply less at each price level. SRAS shifts left.
  • Sources of higher costs:
    • wages rising faster than productivity
    • energy and raw materials (commodity prices)
    • imported inputs (a weaker currency or dearer world prices)
    • indirect taxes on inputs or goods
  • Result: the price level rises and real output falls. Unemployment rises: stagflation.
  • Real world: wholesale energy prices jumped in 2021–22. UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022; energy and food were the largest contributors.
  • Euro area inflation reached 10.6% in the same month.
  • Say: "costs shift SRAS left" — not: "costs shift AD".

Mission 5

Imported inflation and commodity prices

  • Imported inflation: higher prices of imports feed through to home prices.
  • Two triggers:
    • world prices rise (oil, gas, metals, food: commodity prices)
    • the home currency depreciates, so each import costs more in home currency
  • Imported inputs raise firms' costs: a cost-push shift of SRAS. Imported consumer goods raise the CPI directly.
  • Real world: sterling fell sharply after the UK's EU referendum in June 2016. UK CPI inflation rose from 0.5% (June 2016) to 3.1% (November 2017).
  • Say: "a depreciation makes imports dearer" — not: "a weaker currency makes imports cheaper".
Quick check
?Country X's currency is the crown. A firm there imports a part priced at 50 euros. The exchange rate falls from 1 crown = 1.25 euros to 1 crown = 1.00 euro. By how much does the part's price in crowns change?
Mission 5

Causes of deflation

  • Deflation also has a demand side and a supply side.
  • AD falls (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession): the price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • SRAS rises (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology): the price level falls, real output rises.
  • So falling prices are not always bad news. The cause matters.
  • A fall in AD can also leave the economy in a deflationary gap.
  • Say: "disinflation: inflation falls from 4% to 2%" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 5Evaluate

Which cause, and does it matter?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity. The closer to full capacity, the more a rise in AD shows up as inflation.
  • One-off or ongoing. A single jump in oil prices raises the price level once. It becomes lasting inflation only if wages, expectations or money growth follow.
  • The policy response. Higher interest rates curb demand-pull well. Against cost-push, they cut inflation only by deepening the fall in output.
  • Mixed causes. Real episodes often combine both. 2021–22 saw energy shocks and strong post-pandemic demand.
Mission check

Firms report full order books and staff shortages. World energy and raw material prices are unchanged. What is the most likely main cause of the inflation?

Check what happened to real output and jobs: they tell you whether prices were pulled or pushed.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Economic growth is an increase in real GDP.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity (AD); potential growth raises capacity (PPC shifts out, LRAS shifts right).
  • Long-run growth comes from more or better factors: labour, capital, human capital, technology, institutions.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment, widen inequality and add to inflation.

Mission 2

  • Working-age population = employed + unemployed + inactive. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Survey (ILO) and claimant count measure different things and often differ.
  • Discouraged and underemployed workers are not counted as unemployed, so the rate understates joblessness.

Mission 3

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Seasonal: jobs that vanish at certain times of year.
  • Natural rate = structural + seasonal + frictional.
  • Costs are personal, social and economic; lost output is never recovered.

Mission 4

  • A minimum wage above equilibrium creates excess supply of labour in a competitive market.
  • How many jobs it costs depends on the bite and on the elasticity of demand for labour.

Mission 5

  • Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity; prices and real output rise together.
  • Cost-push: higher costs shift SRAS left; prices rise while output falls.
