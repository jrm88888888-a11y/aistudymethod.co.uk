Mission checkScreen 8

No single narrator can be fully trusted. Walton is an admiring friend, Victor edits the record and tells it as a warning, and the Creature's eloquent defence reaches us through his enemy. Shelley's frames hand the judgement to the reader.

  • Shelley's subtitle asks what such a rebel costs.
Frankenstein · Victor and Promethean ambitionScreen 2

How Victor was made

  • At thirteen he reads the alchemists Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus, seeking "the philosopher's stone and the elixir of life".
  • His father calls Agrippa "sad trash" but explains nothing; Victor later blames him for not showing that "the principles of Agrippa had been entirely exploded".
  • At Ingolstadt, Krempe scorns him; Waldman inspires him.
  • Note the retrospective narrator: older Victor shares out the blame. His motives mix glory with healing: to "banish disease from the human frame".

Frankenstein · Victor and Promethean ambitionScreen 5

Creator, father, god

  • Victor dreams: "A new species would bless me as its creator and source"; "No father could claim the gratitude of his child so completely as I should deserve theirs."
  • He wants gratitude, not duties. Anne Mellor argues the novel shows a man trying to create a child without a woman.
  • At the creation "breathless horror and disgust filled my heart", and he flees.
  • His own rule: a study that weakens the affections is "certainly unlawful".

Mission checkScreen 8

?Prometheus stole fire for humanity. What did Victor steal?

Like Prometheus, Victor takes a power that was not his: the power to make life, which the novel treats as God's and, as Mellor argues, women's. Unlike Prometheus, he does not give his gift to anyone: he abandons it. Strong answers weigh his heroic aspiration against that failure of care.

Frankenstein · the CreatureScreen 2

Made, or made monstrous?

  • Locke (1690): the mind begins as a blank slate, filled by experience. Rousseau: people are naturally good and corrupted by society.
  • Godwin, Shelley's father, argued in Political Justice (1793) that circumstances form character; the 1818 edition is dedicated to him.
  • The Creature argues this case: "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend"; "My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I abhor".
  • Against it: he chooses to kill William and to frame Justine.

Silenced on stage and screen

  • Presumption; or, the Fate of Frankenstein (Richard Brinsley Peake, English Opera House, opened 28 July 1823): T. P. Cooke's creature does not speak. Shelley saw it on 29 August and was amused.
  • Marilyn Butler, reviewing Chris Baldick's study of the myth's afterlife (In Frankenstein's Shadow, 1987), notes that Peake's version first silenced the monster.
  • James Whale's 1931 film: Boris Karloff's creature grunts, and is given a criminal brain.
  • Reception turned the Creature into a mute brute made bad by nature. The novel argues the opposite.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The monster speaks better than his maker. Why give him the best lines?

Shelley gives the Creature the most persuasive voice so that we hear the case for the defence that Victor, Felix and the villagers refuse to hear. His eloquence makes "monster" a verdict passed on him; strong answers weigh it against his choices to kill.

Frankenstein · women in the novelScreen 2

Mothers and daughters

  • Mary Wollstonecraft's A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792) attacked an education that trained women for ornament and obedience. She died on 10 September 1797, days after giving birth to Mary.
  • In February 1815 Mary's premature daughter died; soon after, she recorded dreaming that the baby came back to life.
  • Ellen Moers (1974) called the novel a birth myth: its horror lies in revulsion from a newborn and the abandonment that follows.

Frankenstein · women in the novelScreen 6

Feminist readings

  • Gilbert and Gubar (1979) read the novel as a feminist rereading of Paradise Lost: the Creature plays Adam and Satan and, as their essay title says, a monstrous Eve.
  • Anne Mellor (1988): Victor's science usurps women's power to give birth; the 1831 Elizabeth becomes a passive, idealised 'angel in the house'.

Many critics read the women's marginal place as the novel's point: a man who makes life without a woman ends by destroying the women around him, including the female creature. Science kept in the dark

  • Victor withholds his method from Walton: "you expect to be informed of the secret with which I am acquainted; that cannot be".
  • We learn only fragments: "The dissecting room and the slaughter-house furnished many of my materials"; a "spark of being".
  • He works alone, "In a solitary chamber, or rather cell, at the top of the house", answerable to no one.
  • Shelley's interest is consequences and responsibility, not technique.

Frankenstein · science, society and the two textsScreen 6

Science and society

  • Victor's science is private and unchecked. Walton's crew acts as society: they demand a return, and he writes "I have consented to return".
  • Chris Baldick shows how the story became a modern myth, its monster used for revolution and the masses.
  • Wells's Martians have the greater science, yet are "slain, after all man's devices had failed, by the humblest things that God, in his wisdom, has put upon this earth" (Book Two, Ch. VIII).

Frankenstein · nature and the sublimeScreen 2

1816: the Alps in a cold summer

  • The summer of 1816 was cold and wet after the 1815 eruption of Mount Tambora. At the Villa Diodati, Byron proposed the ghost-story contest.
  • On 22–25 July 1816 the Shelleys were at Chamonix and visited the Mer de Glace. Percy Shelley's "Mont Blanc" was published in their History of a Six Weeks' Tour (1817).
  • Victor's climb to the glacier draws on that visit: landscape as lived experience, not decoration.

Nature in Frankenstein is a sublime presence that mirrors and tests the characters. It heals those open to it, such as Clerval, the young Creature and Victor in his better moments; it reveals and limits as Victor isolates himself, until the Arctic ice halts both pursuit and ambition.

Frankenstein · critical readingsScreen 3

Psychoanalytic readings

  • The dream fuses desire and death: a kiss turns the lover into the dead mother. Many readers see the creation as a wish to undo her death.
  • Freud's "The Uncanny" (1919): the familiar returning as strange, the double, the dead revived.
  • The Creature as Victor's double: "my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave".
  • Paul Sherwin (1981) sets out this Oedipal reading, then calls it reductive: the Creature has his own claims.

Frankenstein · critical readingsScreen 5

Testing a reading

  • Does it fit the words? Moretti calls Victor's fear of "a race of devils" one of the novel's most reactionary moments: a made being turned into a natural "race" to hate.
  • Does it meet the counter-evidence? Moretti's proletarian Creature also weeps for the De Laceys and mourns Victor: an individual, not only a class.
  • Which edition? Mellor's passive Elizabeth is truer of 1831 than 1818.
  • Does context support it? Baldick's Burke and the 1790s fit the novel's setting.

Write itScreen 7
?Short critical response, ungraded.

'The Creature is less a character than a symbol.' In 3–5 sentences, respond using one critical lens, one exact quotation, and one sentence that tests the reading.

Model answer

Moretti's Marxist reading makes the Creature a symbol of the working class: nameless, made from many bodies, and disowned by the master who made him. The text supports this when the Creature learns that a man without "descent" or "riches" is "a vagabond and a slave, doomed to waste his powers". Yet the reading flattens him: his grief for the De Laceys and his remorse over Victor's body are an individual's. He works as symbol and character at once.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Frankenstein is a frame narrative: Walton's letters hold Victor's tale, which holds the Creature's, which holds the De Laceys' story and Safie's letters.
  • Each narrator has a motive and a bias; Walton trusts the letters and his own sighting more than Victor's words, and Victor revised Walton's notes.
  • Shelley rebuilds the Gothic around science: the laboratory, the charnel house and the double replace the ghost.
  • Compare how narrators in your second text handle evidence and hearsay (AO4).
  • The subtitle joins two myths: Prometheus the fire-thief punished by the gods, and Prometheus the maker of humans from clay.
  • Victor's education (alchemists, a dismissive father, Waldman) is told by a retrospective narrator who spreads the blame.
  • Waldman's personified, female nature and Victor's dream of a grateful "new species" show ambition as mastery and self-glorification; Mellor reads it as male creation that excludes women.
  • Victor is both tragic overreacher and warning: weigh "Learn from me" against "yet another may succeed".
  • Wells's Martians turn the experimenters into the experimented-on: a ready AO4 link.
  • The Creature learns through the senses, the De Laceys, Volney, three books and Victor's journal; he calls himself "a monster" after learning society's values.
  • His narrative argues the Godwinian case that circumstances form character ("misery made me a fiend"), but he also chooses to kill.
  • His rhetoric (legal appeal, Miltonic antithesis, cause and effect) is the novel's most persuasive; Victor calls it dangerous.
  • The 1823 stage version and 1931 film silenced him and gave him a brute nature, reversing the novel's argument.
  • Compare who is given a voice in your second text and who is spoken about (AO4).
  • Almost every woman in the novel dies, vanishes or never replies: Caroline, Elizabeth, Justine, Safie, the female creature, Margaret.
  • Victor destroys the female creature because a "thinking and reasoning" female might refuse his compact: he fears female choice.
  • 1831 cuts Elizabeth's protest against Justine's execution and her wish for education, and makes her "a pretty present"; Mellor reads this as a more passive Elizabeth.
  • Moers (birth myth), Gilbert and Gubar (Paradise Lost and Eve) and Mellor (male creation without women) are the key feminist readings.
  • Compare how your second text presents women's voices and bodies under a society shaped by science or power (AO4).
  • Galvani (1791), Davy (1802) and Aldini (1803) made the reanimation of matter seem close to real; the 1818 Preface cites Erasmus Darwin.
  • Victor's method is withheld; Shelley's subject is the private, unchecked scientist and his responsibilities.
  • 1831 adds the Introduction (galvanism, "mock the stupendous mechanism of the Creator"), destiny, an adopted Elizabeth and Clerval's colonial plans, despite the claim "I have changed no portion of the story".
  • Mellor reads 1831 as more fatalistic, with nature as "immutable laws".
  • Compare how your second text tests society's faith in science (Wells's bacteria; AO4).
  • Burke's sublime (vast, powerful, terrifying from safety) shapes the Alpine scenes; Shelley distinguishes it from the beautiful.
  • The Shelleys' visit to Chamonix and the Mer de Glace (July 1816) stands behind Victor's climb.
  • Shelley weaves in Coleridge, Wordsworth and Percy Shelley; changing Wordsworth's "me" to "him" makes Clerval the Romantic ideal Victor has lost.
  • Nature heals, mirrors and limits: the blasted oak becomes Victor's image of himself.
  • Compare how nature defeats or resists human science in your second text (AO4).
  • A critical reading is a lens: use it, then test it against the words, the counter-evidence, the edition and the context.
  • Psychoanalytic: the dream fuses lover and dead mother; the Creature as Victor's double (Freud's uncanny); Sherwin warns this can be reductive.
  • Feminist: Moers's birth myth, Gilbert and Gubar's Paradise Lost and Eve, Mellor's male science usurping birth.
  • Marxist and political: Moretti's nameless, collective proletariat; Baldick's revolutionary monster after Burke.
  • Use reception history (AO3) and compare how critics read your second text's society (AO4).

Key quotations to learn

  • I bitterly feel the want of a friend (Letter II, Walton) The lonely listener who will idolise Victor.
  • a being which had the shape of a man, but apparently of gigantic stature (Letter IV, Walton) An independent witness sees the Creature before any tale is told.
  • corrected and augmented them in many places (Ch. XXIV (1818: Vol. III, Walton in continuation)) Victor edits the record of his own story.
  • He is eloquent and persuasive (Ch. XXIV (1818: Vol. III Ch. VII), Victor to Walton) Victor warns us how to read the Creature's chapters.
  • my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave (Ch. VII (1818: Vol. I Ch. VI), Victor) The Gothic double: the Creature as part of Victor.
  • exempt from the disadvantages of a mere tale of spectres or enchantment (1818 Preface (by Percy Shelley; reprinted 1831)) The story claims to be science, not a ghost tale.
  • They penetrate into the recesses of nature, and show how she works in her hiding places (Ch. III (1818: Vol. I Ch. II), Waldman) Science as entering a female nature.
  • A new species would bless me as its creator and source (Ch. IV (1818: Vol. I Ch. III), Victor) He casts himself as god.
  • No father could claim the gratitude of his child so completely as I should deserve theirs (Ch. IV (1818: Vol. I Ch. III; "their's")) Fatherhood imagined as gratitude owed to him.
  • Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge (Ch. IV (1818: Vol. I Ch. III), Victor to Walton) The tale framed as a warning.
  • like the archangel who aspired to omnipotence, I am chained in an eternal hell (Ch. XXIV (1818: Vol. III, Walton in continuation)) Victor's self-image as Satan: grandeur and damnation.
  • yet another may succeed (Ch. XXIV (1818: Vol. III), Victor's last words to Walton) His warning wavers at the very end.
  • This was indeed a godlike science (Ch. XII (1818: Vol. II Ch. IV), the Creature) Language as power, and his route to humanity.
  • Was I then a monster, a blot upon the earth (Ch. XIII (1818: Vol. II Ch. V)) The label learned from society.
  • I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel (Ch. X (1818: Vol. II Ch. II)) Milton turned into an accusation of Victor.
  • I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend (Ch. X (1818: Vol. II Ch. II)) The nurture argument in one line.
  • I am malicious because I am miserable (Ch. XVII (1818: Vol. II Ch. IX)) Cause and effect: suffering produces evil.
  • if I cannot inspire love, I will cause fear (Ch. XVII (1818: Vol. II Ch. IX)) His threat, balanced like his reasoning.
  • a thinking and reasoning animal (Ch. XX (1818: Vol. III Ch. III), Victor) The female creature imagined as a mind with a will.
  • might refuse to comply with a compact made before her creation (Ch. XX (1818: Vol. III Ch. III)) Victor's fear of female choice.
  • when I see a fellow-creature about to perish through the cowardice of her pretended friends (Ch. VIII (1818: Vol. I Ch. VII), Elizabeth in court) Elizabeth speaks out when others will not.
  • I almost began to think that I was the monster that he said I was (Ch. VIII (1818: Vol. I Ch. VII), Justine) Like the Creature, Justine is talked into being a "monster".
  • unless it were the dictate of your own free choice (Ch. XXII (1818: Vol. III Ch. V), Elizabeth's letter) She asks for a marriage freely chosen.
  • mine to protect, love, and cherish (Ch. I (1831 only), Victor) The 1831 Elizabeth as Victor's possession.
  • Perhaps a corpse would be re-animated; galvanism had given token of such things (1831 Introduction) Shelley links the novel to galvanism.
  • supremely frightful would be the effect of any human endeavour to mock the stupendous mechanism of the Creator of the world (1831 Introduction) 1831 frames creation as presumption against God.
  • Destiny was too potent, and her immutable laws had decreed my utter and terrible destruction (Ch. II (1831 only)) Victor blames fate.
  • infuse a spark of being into the lifeless thing that lay at my feet (Ch. V (1818: Vol. I Ch. IV)) The "spark": electricity as metaphor.
  • I have changed no portion of the story, nor introduced any new ideas or circumstances (1831 Introduction) Shelley's claim, which the revisions contradict.
  • Dr. Darwin, and some of the physiological writers of Germany (Preface (1818; reprinted 1831)) The science is presented as plausible.
  • They elevated me from all littleness of feeling (Ch. X (1818: Vol. II Ch. II), Victor) The sublime lifts the mind.
  • so beautiful yet terrific (Ch. VII (1818: Vol. I Ch. VI), the storm) The sublime in three words, just before the Creature appears.
  • my eyes were insensible to the charms of nature (Ch. IV (1818: Vol. I Ch. III)) Science cuts Victor off from nature.
  • Haunted him like a passion (Ch. XVIII (1818: Vol. III Ch. I), quoting Wordsworth) Clerval as the Romantic ideal.
  • Nought may endure but mutability (Ch. X (1818: Vol. II Ch. II), quoting Percy Shelley) The restless mind.
  • I am a blasted tree; the bolt has entered my soul (Ch. XIX (1818: Vol. III Ch. II)) Victor sees himself as nature destroyed.
  • I thought that I held the corpse of my dead mother in my arms (Ch. V (1818: Vol. I Ch. IV), Victor's dream) The core text for psychoanalytic readings.
  • my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave (Ch. VII (1818: Vol. I Ch. VI), Victor) The Creature as Victor's double.
  • a vagabond and a slave, doomed to waste his powers (Ch. XIII (1818: Vol. II Ch. V), the Creature) Class and property: the Marxist reading.
  • You are my creator, but I am your master (Ch. XX (1818: Vol. III Ch. III), the Creature) Master and servant reversed.
  • a race of devils would be propagated upon the earth (Ch. XX (1818: Vol. III Ch. III), Victor) Fear of the Creature's offspring; Moretti's "reactionary" moment.
  • Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition (Ch. XV (1818: Vol. II Ch. VII), the Creature) Milton reread: Gilbert and Gubar's starting point.
