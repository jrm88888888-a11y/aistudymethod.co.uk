Cases and word order

This is the beating heart of German. Three cases (nominative, accusative, dative) show the job of each noun, while a few iron rules fix the word order: the verb comes second, the subject inverts after a fronted phrase, and extra details line up as Time – Manner – Place.

What the cases do

Three jobs, three cases

The case shows what a noun is doing in the sentence:

  • Nominative = the subject (who does it). Der Mann kauft ein Buch.
  • Accusative = the direct object (what is acted on). Der Mann kauft ein Buch.
  • Dative = the indirect object (to/for whom). Er gibt dem Kind das Buch.

English hides this with word order ("the man gives the child the book"). German shows it with the article endings instead — so the endings really matter.

Prepositions · set 1

Prepositions that force the accusative

Five common prepositions always take the accusative — remember them together:

durch · für · gegen · ohne · umthrough · for · against · without · around
  • Ich gehe durch den Park. — through the park.
  • Das Geschenk ist für meinen Bruder. — for my brother.
  • Ich komme ohne meinen Freund. — without my friend.

Masculine gives it away: after these, der becomes den and ein/mein become einen/meinen.

Prepositions · set 2

Prepositions that force the dative

Another group always takes the dative. Learn the chant:

aus · bei · mit · nach · seit · von · zufrom · at · with · after/to · since · from/of · to
  • Ich fahre mit dem Bus. — by bus.
  • Ich komme aus der Stadt. — from the city.
  • Nach der Schule spiele ich Fußball. — after school.

Contractions: zu dem → zum, zu der → zur, bei dem → beim, von dem → vom. So "to school" is zur Schule.

Prepositions · set 3

The two-way (Wechsel) prepositions

Nine prepositions can take either case. The rule is about movement:

an · auf · hinter · in · neben · über · unter · vor · zwischenmotion (where to?) → accusative · position (where?) → dative
  • Ich gehe in die Stadt. — into town (movement → accusative).
  • Ich bin in der Stadt. — in town (position → dative).

Quick test: if you could ask wohin? ("where to?") use the accusative; if you ask wo? ("where?") use the dative.

The golden rule

The verb comes second

In a main clause the finite verb is always the second element. The first "slot" can be the subject, a time phrase, or a whole chunk — but the verb still follows it:

  • Ich fahre heute in die Stadt.
  • Am Wochenende spiele ich Fußball.

Count the slots, not the words: "Am Wochenende" is three words but one idea — so the verb comes right after it.

Inversion

Front something → flip the subject

If you start with anything other than the subject, the subject jumps to just after the verb. This is called inversion:

start with…sentence
subjectIch gehe morgen ins Kino.
time phraseMorgen gehe ich ins Kino.
place phraseIns Kino gehe ich morgen.

The verb never moves from slot 2 — it's the subject and the extra phrase that swap places around it.

Ordering the details

Time – Manner – Place

When a sentence has several adverbial details, German lines them up in a fixed order: Time → Manner → Place (the "TeKaMoLo" rule):

morgen · mit dem Bus · in die StadtWHEN → HOW → WHERE
  • Ich fahre morgen mit dem Bus in die Stadt. — Tomorrow I'm going into town by bus.

English is freer ("by bus into town tomorrow" all work), but German keeps Time first and Place last. Remember: When–How–Where.

Recap

Your cases & word-order toolkit

Cases: nominative = subject, accusative = direct object, dative = indirect object. Masculine changes most: der → den → dem.

Accusative prepositions: durch, für, gegen, ohne, um.

Dative prepositions: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu (→ zum, zur, beim, vom).

Two-way prepositions: movement (wohin?) → accusative; position (wo?) → dative.

Verb second, and inversion after a fronted phrase (Morgen spiele ich…).

TeKaMoLo: details go Time → Manner → Place.
