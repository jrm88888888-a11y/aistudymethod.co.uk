Historical Investigation (Coursework)

This is an optional route. AQA A-level History is option-based, but Component 3 is compulsory for every candidate. It is the non-exam assessment: 40 marks and 20% of the A-level, marked by your centre and moderated by AQA. This lesson teaches the skills and the rules — the content is yours to choose.

The Historical Investigation is an independently researched piece of extended writing of 3,500–4,500 words on a development or issue that has been subject to different historical interpretations. It is where you stop being a student of history and start doing it. It is also the component most often thrown away on avoidable rule breaches.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Look out for the Interpretations flags — A-level History is judged on how well you weigh competing arguments. Press Start when you're ready.

The rules · read this twice

Four rules that can invalidate your entry

AQA is unusually blunt about this. Get the rules wrong and no A-level result is issued. These are not style preferences — they are conditions of entry.

  • The 100-year rule. Your question must be set in the context of approximately 100 years. You may trace a development across a century, or focus on a narrower issue but place it in a century-long context.
  • The no-overlap rule. The investigation must not duplicate the content of Components 1 and 2. If you study 1C The Tudors and 2O Nazi Germany, you cannot write your NEA on either.
  • The 200-year rule. All three components together must cover a chronological range of at least 200 years.
  • The word limit. 3,500–4,500 words, excluding bibliography, footnotes and appendices — with a hard limit of 4,500. Exceeding it costs a five-mark penalty, applied by AQA, not by your teacher. Five marks out of forty is a grade.

The paperwork: your centre must submit an NEA title approval form to AQA by 20 October in the year before certification, giving your title, date range and your Component 1 and 2 options. AQA checks the rules before you write. If your title changes, a new form is required.

Choosing · the question

How to build a question that can actually be answered

AQA is explicit: the issue must be one that has promoted debate and differences of interpretation among historians, and there must be accessible primary sources. Test both before you commit.

  • Use exam-style formulations. AQA advises questions beginning 'To what extent…' or a quoted judgement followed by 'Assess the validity of this view.' These force an argument; 'Discuss the causes of…' invites a list.
  • Two legitimate shapes. Either a broad development traced across a century — for instance, how far the condition of a social group changed over a hundred years — or a narrower issue placed in a century-long context, so that change and continuity are still visible.
  • The historiography test: can you name two academic historians who disagree about it? If you cannot, change the question now. AO3 is 10 marks and it requires two academic interpretations — textbook and course-book authors do not count.
  • The source test: can you actually reach three primary sources of at least two different types? Archives, digitised collections, published document editions, Hansard, newspaper archives, memoirs, visual and material sources all qualify.

Warning about AO1: the mark scheme states that an investigation which fails to show an understanding of change and continuity across approximately 100 years cannot rise above Level 2 for AO1 — a maximum of 8 marks out of 20. The century is not decoration. It is the spine of the argument.

AO2 · primary sources

Three sources, two types, evaluated for value

AO2 is worth 10 marks and the requirement is precise: your investigation must contain an evaluation of three primary sources, and at least two different types must be used.

  • 'Types' means genres. Official publications, reports, diaries, speeches, letters, chronicles, eyewitness observation — plus artefacts, archaeological and visual sources. Three government reports is one type, and the mark scheme caps you accordingly.
  • Evaluate for value, not for 'bias'. 'This source is biased' is a Level 1 sentence. The mark scheme asks you to weigh: the perspective of the source; the social, political, intellectual, religious or economic context in which it was produced; its credibility, authority, authenticity, consistency and comprehensiveness; and any distortion or propaganda.
  • Integrate, don't quarantine. AQA is explicit that the three objectives must be woven through the body of the work. A separate chapter headed 'Source Evaluation' is the classic way to lose marks — the sources should be doing argumentative work where the argument needs them.

The move that earns Level 5: use contextual knowledge to test the source. Not 'Pepys was there, so this is reliable', but: 'Pepys wrote in cipher, for himself, with no expectation of publication — which makes his admissions of fear credible; but as a Navy Board official he saw the plague from a position of unusual security, and his diary tells us little about the parishes worst hit.'

AO3 · interpretations

Two academic historians — and why they disagree

AO3 is where NEA marks are most often lost, because it asks something the exam does not ask. AQA states that in the NEA you must go further and:

  • show an understanding of the limitations placed on historians — what sources were open to them, what languages they read, what archives were closed;
  • show an understanding of the significance of the time and/or context in which a historian writes;
  • compare and evaluate the two differing interpretations.

Only academic historians count. AQA is explicit that analysis based on textbook or course-book authors is not acceptable. You need monographs and journal articles.

What 'the time and context in which a historian writes' really means — a worked example. Historians of the Soviet Union writing before 1991 could not read Soviet archives; the 'archival revolution' after 1991 transformed what could be known. That is a limitation, not a failing — and noting it is exactly what Level 5 AO3 asks for. Equally: a historian of the Reformation writing in the 1960s worked in a discipline that prized top-down political narrative; the parish-level evidence that reshaped the field came later. Explain why two historians differ, not just that they do.

Not 'A says X, B says Y'But: 'A says X because of the sources and questions available to A; B says Y because…'

Research · how to actually do it

Finding the historiography and the sources

Independent research is a skill, and it is the one universities most want to see evidence of.

  • Start with a survey, then leave it behind. A good general history gives you the shape of the debate and — crucially — a bibliography. Mine the footnotes of serious books: that is how you find the two academic historians you need.
  • Journal articles are often better than books for the NEA: they are short, they state a thesis explicitly, and they argue against someone by name. That is AO3 handed to you.
  • Primary sources: published document collections; digitised newspaper archives; Hansard; the records of your local archive (local studies are often the strongest NEAs, because nobody has done them to death); memoirs; photographs and objects.
  • Keep a running bibliography from day one, with full details. Reconstructing references at midnight in April is how errors and accidental plagiarism happen.

Independence is a rule, not an aspiration. Several students in a centre may work on the same topic, but AQA states that centres may not direct students to the same sources, and each student must develop their own question and do their own evaluation. Your teacher may give general guidance and monitor progress — they may not write it with you.

Writing · structure and referencing

How to build the 4,000 words

The three objectives must be integrated, so structure by argument, never by objective.

  • Introduction (~400 words): the question, why it is contested, the two historians you will engage, and — in one sentence — your line. Do not save the argument for the end.
  • Body (~3,000 words): four to five analytical sections, each addressing a factor or a phase across the century. Inside each: your knowledge and judgement (AO1), primary evidence weighed for value (AO2), and historians engaged where they bear on the point (AO3).
  • Conclusion (~500 words): a substantiated judgement that answers the question you actually asked, acknowledging what would change your mind.
  • Footnotes are strongly advised by AQA: they demonstrate the range of your reading, validate the bibliography, and protect you against plagiarism. Use a consistent system and be scrupulous. AQA also notes that skill with footnotes is valued by universities.
  • Quotation: short quotations, paraphrase and footnotes — lengthy extracts are not required, and extensive verbatim copying can amount to malpractice.

Word-count discipline: footnotes, bibliography and appendices are excluded from the count. That is not a loophole for hiding argument in footnotes — but it does mean full referencing costs you nothing.

Marks · how it is judged

Reading the mark scheme like an examiner

Forty marks: AO1 20, AO2 10, AO3 10. Your centre marks it; AQA moderates.

  • AO1 Level 5 (17–20): a very good understanding of change and continuity across approximately 100 years, fully analytical, balanced, with a well-substantiated judgement and precise supporting detail. Note again the hard ceiling: no century, no more than Level 2.
  • AO2 Level 5 (9–10): a range of relevant, well-supported comments on the value of three sources of two or more types, producing a balanced and convincing judgement on their merits in relation to your topic.
  • AO3 Level 5 (9–10): a strong, well-substantiated evaluation of two interpretations, with reference to the time, context and/or limitations placed on the historians.

The honest summary: most NEAs that miss the top band do so for one of three reasons — the century is asserted rather than analysed; the sources are described rather than valued; or the historians are quoted rather than explained. Fix those three and you are in Level 5 territory.

Worked example · a question that works

Turning an interest into a title

Watch a weak idea become a strong title.

Attempt 1 — the enthusiasm

'The Suffragettes.' A topic, not a question. Nothing to argue, no century, no historians.

Attempt 2 — a question, but a bad one

'Why did women get the vote in 1918?' Better — but it is a narrow event, not a hundred-year context, and it invites narrative.

Attempt 3 — the title that passes

'To what extent was militancy responsible for the advance of women's political rights in Britain, c1850–1950?' Now: a judgement question; a context of approximately 100 years; a genuinely contested issue (historians disagree sharply about whether militancy helped or hindered); and abundant primary sources of several types — petitions, parliamentary debates, the suffrage press, police and Home Office records, memoirs, photographs.

Now check the fatal rules: does it overlap Component 1 or 2? If you are studying 1G Challenge and transformation: Britain, c1851–1964, it does — and it would invalidate your entry. That check must happen before you fall in love with the question, not after.

Recap

The rules and the skills, in one place

Length: 3,500–4,500 words, excluding footnotes and bibliography · hard limit 4,500 · 5-mark penalty above it

Context: Approximately 100 years — and AO1 is capped at Level 2 without it

Overlap: Must not duplicate Components 1 and 2 · all three together must span at least 200 years

Sources (AO2): Three primary sources, of at least two different types, evaluated for value, not 'bias'

Historians (AO3): Two academic historians — not textbook authors — evaluated for time, context and limitations
