Mission 1

Mission 1: If one company controls a whole market, why doesn't it just charge the highest price it can?

Make a guess: If one company controls a whole market, why doesn't it just charge the highest price it can?

Mission 1

A price maker

  • Pure monopoly: one firm supplies the whole market. No close substitutes. High barriers to entry.
  • Monopoly power: the power to set price above marginal cost. A firm with a large market share can have it without being the only seller.
  • The monopolist's demand curve is the market demand curve: it slopes down.
  • So it is a price maker: it chooses price, or output, but not both. Demand decides the other.
  • Demand = average revenue (AR): the price received per unit.
  • A monopolist must lower its price to sell more, unlike a firm in perfect competition (P = MR = AR = D).
Mission 1

Where monopoly power comes from

  • Legal barriers: patents, licenses, state franchises. A patent normally lasts up to 20 years.
  • Economies of scale: a large incumbent has lower average cost than any small entrant.
  • Control of a key resource or of the network buyers must use.
  • Sunk costs and brand loyalty: entry is costly and hard to recover.
  • Few close substitutes: the fewer the substitutes, the less price elastic the firm's demand.
  • More barriers and fewer substitutes mean more monopoly power, and power that lasts.

Mission 1

Profit is greatest where MR = MC

  • If MR > MC, one more unit adds more to revenue than to cost: raise output.
  • If MR < MC, the last unit adds more to cost than to revenue: cut output.
  • So profit is maximized at the output where MR = MC.
  • Price: go up from that output to the demand (AR) curve.
  • The monopolist charges P > MC at the profit-maximizing quantity.
  • A monopolist always produces where demand is price elastic: MR is positive there.
  • Say: "read price off the AR curve at the MR = MC output" — not: "price is set where MR = MC".

Mission 1

Worked example: price, profit and lost surplus

  • Illustrative data. AR = 50 − Q, so MR = 50 − 2Q. MC = ATC = 10.
  • MR = MC: 50 − 2Q = 10, so Q = 20.
  • Price from AR: 50 − 20 = 30.
  • Economic profit = (P − ATC) × Q = (30 − 10) × 20 = 400.
  • A competitive industry with the same costs: P = MC = 10, Q = 40.
  • Deadweight loss (a later screen explains it) = ½ × (40 − 20) × (30 − 10) = 200.
Mission 1

Extra profit, normal profit (zero economic profit) or a loss

  • Profit per unit = AR − ATC at the MR = MC output.
  • P > ATC: economic profit. P = ATC: zero economic profit (normal profit). P < ATC: a loss.
  • Short run: a loss-making monopolist keeps producing if price covers AVC. Below AVC it shuts down.
  • Long run: barriers stop entry, so profit above normal can last. A firm that cannot cover ATC in the long run leaves.
  • Say: "barriers to entry let economic profit persist in the long run" — not: "a monopoly always makes a profit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A monopolist maximizes profit at an output of 300 units. At that output, AR = 12, ATC = 9 and MC = MR = 6. What is its profit?
Mission 1

Monopoly versus perfect competition

  • Same costs, constant MC = ATC. Compare the two outcomes.
  • Perfect competition: P = MC, output Qc, price Pc.
  • Monopoly: MR = MC, so output is lower (Qm < Qc) and price is higher (Pm > Pc).
  • Rectangle (Pm − Pc) × Qm: consumer surplus transferred to the monopolist.
  • Triangle from Qm to Qc, below D and above MC: units worth more than they cost, never made. This is the deadweight loss.
  • Monopoly is allocatively inefficient: P > MC.
  • Say: "the rectangle is a transfer; the triangle is the deadweight loss" — not: "the whole fall in consumer surplus is lost".

Mission 1

Monopoly power as a market failure

  • Market failure: the market misallocates resources.
  • Monopoly power: price above MC, too little output, a deadweight loss.
  • The firm acts rationally for itself: it equates MR and MC, not marginal benefit to society and MC. The result is inefficient.
Mission 1

Taxing, subsidizing or capping a monopolist

  • Price ceiling at P = MC (illustrative: 10): MR becomes the ceiling up to the demand curve. The firm sells up to the demand curve: Q = 40, the allocatively efficient output. Here it earns only normal profit.
  • A ceiling between Pc and Pm also raises output; a ceiling above Pm has no effect; a ceiling below MC would cut output.
  • A binding ceiling set at or above MC raises quantity in a monopoly, unlike in a competitive market, where a binding ceiling cuts quantity. Price regulation targets the cause of the inefficiency.
Quick check
?A profit-maximizing monopolist pays a new lump-sum tax: a fixed amount, whatever it produces. What happens to its price and output?
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a town's only ferry operator sets one fare for all passengers. At its current fare, its marginal revenue is 4 and its marginal cost is 7 per passenger. What should it do to raise its profit?

A monopolist weighs each extra sale: the best price is where MR = MC, not the highest price it could charge.

Mission 2

What makes a monopoly natural

  • Some industries have huge fixed costs and low marginal costs: water pipes, rail track, power grids.
  • So average cost keeps falling as output rises, across the whole market.
  • Natural monopoly: economies of scale over the entire effective market demand.
  • A second network would duplicate the fixed cost: wasteful.
  • Say: "natural monopoly" for a cost reason — not: "a monopoly protected by law or a patent".
Mission 2

Worked example: one network or two?

  • Illustrative data. A town needs 300 connections. The network costs 1,200 (fixed). Each connection adds 2 (marginal cost).
  • One firm: ATC = 1,200 ÷ 300 + 2 = 4 + 2 = 6.
  • Two firms, 150 connections each: ATC = 1,200 ÷ 150 + 2 = 8 + 2 = 10.
  • Splitting the market raises average cost from 6 to 10.
  • Same marginal cost; the difference is the fixed cost spread over fewer users.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rail line has a fixed cost of 900 and a marginal cost of 3 per journey. The market is 150 journeys. If two firms each build their own line and each carries 75 journeys, what is each firm's average cost?
Mission 2

The natural monopoly diagram

  • ATC (LRAC) falls across the whole demand curve. MC lies below ATC.
  • Three possible prices:
    • Unregulated: MR = MC. High price, low output, profit above normal.
    • P = ATC: normal profit. Output higher, price lower.
    • P = MC: the allocatively efficient output. But P < ATC: a loss.
  • AP: P = MC is the socially optimal price; P = ATC is the fair-return price.
  • So a natural monopoly cannot be allocatively efficient and cover its costs without help.
  • Say: "MC is below ATC whenever ATC is falling" — not: "MC is above ATC".
Mission 2

Worked example: three prices

  • Illustrative data. AR = 40 − 0.1Q. MC = 4. Fixed cost = 1,800.
  • Unregulated (MR = MC): 40 − 0.2Q = 4 → Q = 180, P = 22. ATC = 1,800 ÷ 180 + 4 = 14.
    • Profit = (22 − 14) × 180 = 1,440.
  • P = ATC: Q = 300, P = 10. ATC = 1,800 ÷ 300 + 4 = 10. Normal profit.
  • P = MC: Q = 360, P = 4. ATC = 1,800 ÷ 360 + 4 = 9.
    • Loss = (9 − 4) × 360 = 1,800: the whole fixed cost.

Mission 2

Lump-sum or per-unit?

  • Lump-sum tax or subsidy: a fixed amount, whatever the output. It changes fixed cost only.
    • ATC shifts; MC does not. Price and quantity do not change. Only profit changes.
  • Per-unit tax or subsidy: changes MC. Price and quantity change.
  • At P = MC, the natural monopoly makes a loss. A lump-sum subsidy equal to the loss lets it stay open at the allocatively efficient output, without changing its MC.
  • Effective intervention targets the incentive that causes the failure: the monopolist's MR = MC choice. Price regulation changes that choice; a lump-sum subsidy covers the loss.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A regulator makes a natural monopoly charge P = MC = 6. It then sells 500 units, and its average total cost at that output is 8.5. What lump-sum subsidy lets it just cover its costs?
Mission check

Where average cost falls across the whole market, a second network duplicates the fixed cost. That is why such industries usually have one supplier, and why its prices are regulated.

Mission 3

Same product, different prices

  • Price discrimination: charging different buyers different prices for the same good or service, for reasons not linked to cost.
  • Examples: peak and off-peak train fares; child and adult cinema tickets; student discounts.
  • US examples: senior and student movie tickets; airline fares that vary by buyer.
  • Not price discrimination: a higher price because supplying costs more (e.g. delivery to a remote island).
  • Say: "price discrimination" only when cost is the same — not: for any price difference.
Mission 3

Three conditions

1. Market power: the firm must be a price maker. A price taker cannot set prices.

2. Separate groups with different price elasticities of demand, and a way to tell them apart (age, time, place, ID). The group with the more elastic demand gets the lower price.

3. No resale: buyers at the low price must not be able to sell on to those charged more.

  • If resale is easy, the low-price group buys for everyone (market seepage or arbitrage).
  • AP: the firm must have market power, identify buyers' willingness to pay, and prevent resale.
Quick check
?A firm with market power plans to charge students a lower price than other adults for an identical product. Its costs are the same for both groups. Which situation would stop this plan from raising its profit?
Mission 3

Three degrees

  • First degree (perfect): each buyer pays their own maximum willingness to pay.
  • Second degree: price depends on how much you buy: bulk discounts, lower price per unit after a set amount.
  • Third degree: different prices for different groups: age, time of day, location.
  • AP focuses on perfect price discrimination.
  • Real firms rarely know every buyer's maximum price. Personalized online pricing is an attempt to get closer.
Mission 3

Perfect price discrimination

  • Each unit sells at the buyer's maximum price, so the demand curve becomes the MR curve.
  • The firm produces where D = MC, the P = MC output: the same output as perfect competition.
  • All consumer surplus becomes producer surplus. Consumer surplus = 0.
  • No deadweight loss: allocatively efficient output.
  • Illustrative data (as): AR = 50 − Q, MC = 10.
    • Single price: Q = 20, profit 400, consumer surplus 200, deadweight loss 200.
    • Perfect price discrimination: Q = 40, producer surplus 800, consumer surplus 0, deadweight loss 0.
  • Say: "efficient but not equitable" — not: "consumers gain because output rises".
Quick check
?A single-price monopolist becomes able to practice perfect (first-degree) price discrimination. Its costs do not change. Compared with before, which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Who gains and who loses?

  • Producers: more profit. It may fund investment, or keep a service running (e.g. a rail route).
  • Consumers in the high-price group: pay more; lose consumer surplus.
  • Consumers in the low-price group: may be served for the first time.
  • Total output: may rise. Then some deadweight loss is removed.
  • Price discrimination raises profit by capturing consumer surplus.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a theme park charges local residents less than tourists for an identical day ticket. Its costs are the same for both groups. Which pair of conditions is needed for this to earn more profit than one price for all?

Different prices for the same thing need market power, groups with different elasticities, and no resale.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Make a guess: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Mission 4

Many firms, different products

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a differentiated product.
  • Low barriers to entry and exit.
  • Each firm has a little price-setting power: its product is not identical to rivals'.
  • Examples: cafés, hairdressers, restaurants, takeaways, a new style of café such as bubble tea.
  • US examples: restaurants, nail salons, food trucks.
  • Firms are small, so each one acts independently: it does not plan around one rival's reaction.
Mission 4

Standing out: non-price competition

  • Product differentiation: real or perceived differences (quality, design, location, service, brand).
  • Non-price competition: competing on anything but price: branding, advertising, loyalty cards, opening hours, new menu items.
  • Aim 1: shift the firm's demand curve right (more customers at each price).
  • Aim 2: make demand less price elastic (customers less willing to switch).
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve slopes downward because its product is differentiated" — not: "because it has few rivals".
Quick check
?A hairdresser in a busy town center raises its price slightly. It loses some customers but not all of them. Which best explains why?
Mission 4

The short run

  • Profit is maximized where MR = MC. Price is read up to the AR curve.
  • AP: economic profit where P > ATC, a loss where P < ATC, zero economic profit (normal profit) where P = ATC.
  • AP labels: D (the AR curve), MR, MC, ATC (the ATC curve).
  • In the short run, this looks just like the monopoly diagram (see Mission 1). The difference is what happens next.
Mission 4

Worked example: short-run profit

  • Illustrative data. A new bubble tea shop.
  • Demand: AR = 30 − 0.2Q, so MR = 30 − 0.4Q.
  • Costs: MC = 0.2Q. ATC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 30 − 0.4Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 50.
  • Price from AR: 30 − 0.2 × 50 = 20.
  • ATC at Q = 50: 360/50 + 0.1 × 50 = 7.2 + 5 = 12.2.
  • Economic profit = (P − ATC) × Q = (20 − 12.2) × 50 = 390.
Mission 4

From short run to long run

  • Profit above normal is a signal. Barriers are low, so new firms enter.
  • Each firm loses some customers to the newcomers: its demand curve shifts left.
  • More close substitutes: demand becomes more price elastic.
  • Entry stops when AR just touches (is tangent to) ATC: normal profit.
  • Losses work the other way: firms leave, and each remaining firm's demand shifts right until AR touches ATC again.
  • AP: in the long run, firms earn zero economic profit: D is tangent to ATC.
  • Say: "each firm's demand curve shifts left as firms enter" — not: "the market demand curve falls".

Mission 4

Worked example: the long run

  • Illustrative data. After entry, each shop's demand: AR = 18 − 0.125Q, so MR = 18 − 0.25Q.
  • Costs are unchanged: MC = 0.2Q; ATC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 18 − 0.25Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 40.
  • Price: 18 − 0.125 × 40 = 13.
  • ATC at Q = 40: 360/40 + 0.1 × 40 = 9 + 4 = 13.
  • P = ATC: profit per unit 0. Normal profit.
  • PED at the chosen price: SR −2; LR −2.6. Demand is more elastic after entry.
Mission 4

Excess capacity

  • The long-run tangency is on the falling part of ATC.
  • So output is below the output at the lowest ATC: excess capacity.
  • In the previous screen: output 40; lowest ATC (12) at 60. Excess capacity = 60 − 40 = 20 units.
  • Not productively efficient: P = ATC = 13 > lowest ATC = 12.
  • Not allocatively efficient: P = 13 > MC = 8. Buyers value one more unit more than it costs to make.
  • AP: long-run output is below the efficient scale, and P > MC.
  • Say: "excess capacity: output is below the level that minimizes ATC" — not: "the firm has too many workers".

Mission 4

Advertising

  • Advertising aims to shift the firm's demand right and make it less price elastic (brand loyalty).
  • It also raises costs: ATC shifts up.
  • Short run: if extra sales outweigh the extra cost, profit rises.
  • Long run: rivals copy the idea, and new firms enter. Profit returns to normal.
  • Informative advertising (prices, opening hours, what is new) helps buyers choose.
  • Persuasive advertising (image, brand) may add to costs and prices without making the product better.

Mission 5: When only a few big firms share a market, why do their prices so often move together?

Make a guess: When only a few big firms share a market, why do their prices so often move together?

Mission 5

A few firms dominate

  • Oligopoly: a market dominated by a few large firms.
  • High barriers to entry (economies of scale, brands, patents, high start-up costs).
  • Products may be identical (cement, crude oil) or differentiated (cars, smartphones, supermarkets).
  • Much non-price competition: advertising, branding, loyalty schemes.
Mission 5

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: each firm's best choice depends on what its rivals do.
  • So every decision (price, output, advertising) brings uncertainty: how will rivals react?
  • If one firm cuts its price, rivals may match the cut to protect their sales. All end up with lower prices and little extra market share.
  • If one firm raises its price, rivals may not follow, and it loses customers to them.
  • Matching cuts can spiral into a price war: each firm undercuts the others, and profits fall for all.
  • Say: "oligopolists are interdependent" — not: "oligopolists ignore their rivals" or "oligopolists always collude".
Mission 5

Compete or collude?

  • Non-collusive oligopoly: firms compete; each decides alone.
  • Collusive oligopoly: firms cooperate to limit competition, e.g. fixing prices or sharing out markets.
  • Overt (explicit) collusion: a formal agreement. A cartel is a group of firms that agree on prices or output.
  • Tacit collusion: no agreement, but firms behave as if there were one, e.g. following a price leader.
  • Why collude? Less uncertainty, higher joint profit, keeping new firms out.
  • Why not? Cartels are illegal in most countries, there may be fines, and members may cheat.
  • AP: oligopolists have an incentive to collude and form cartels.
Mission 5

When does a cartel work?

  • A cartel is more likely to hold when there are:
    • few firms (easier to agree and to watch each other)
    • similar costs and similar products (easier to agree one price)
    • high barriers to entry (no newcomers to undercut the cartel)
    • easy monitoring of rivals' prices and output (cheating gets spotted)
    • stable demand (less pressure to break ranks)
    • weak enforcement of competition law
  • Consequences: higher prices, lower output, high profit for members, less choice; buyers lose surplus.
Quick check
?Four firms in a market are thinking of forming a cartel. Which set of conditions makes the cartel most likely to last?
Mission 5

A cartel tries to act like a monopoly

  • Cartel members try to set the monopoly price and quantity, then share the market out.
  • Illustrative data: monopoly outcome P = 60, Q = 40. Perfect competition: P = 20, Q = 80.
  • Oligopolists often struggle to reach the monopoly outcome (cheating, price wars).
  • But price is still higher, and quantity lower, than in perfect competition: inefficient.
Mission 5

Worked example: the pull to cheat

  • Illustrative data: two firms, A and B. AR = 100 − Q. MC = ATC = 20.
  • Cartel quotas: 20 each. Q = 40, P = 60. Profit each: (60 − 20) × 20 = 800.
  • A secretly produces 25. Q = 45, so P = 100 − 45 = 55.
    • A: (55 − 20) × 25 = 875. A gains.
    • B: (55 − 20) × 20 = 700. B loses.
  • If both produce 25: Q = 50, P = 50. Each earns (50 − 20) × 25 = 750.
  • Each firm gains by cheating, whatever the other does. Together they lose. (Payoff matrix in Mission 6.)
  • AP: "AR = 100 − Q" is the market demand curve, P = 100 − Q. ATC is ATC.

Mission 5

Real cartels

  • OPEC: set up in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela. Members agree production targets to influence the world oil price.
  • In the US, price fixing is illegal under the Sherman Act (1890).
  • OPEC is made up of governments, not private firms. Competition laws on cartels do not apply to it in the same way.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: three airlines fly the same route at similar fares. One airline thinks about cutting its fare by 10%. Which is the strongest reason it may decide not to?

In oligopoly, every price decision depends on how rivals react. That is why prices often stay put or move together.

Mission 6

A game between firms

  • Game theory studies decisions where your result depends on others' choices too.
  • Every game has:
    • players (here, two firms)
    • strategies (the choices open to each player, e.g. high price or low price)
    • payoffs (what each player gets, e.g. profit, for each pair of choices)
  • Oligopoly fits: firms are interdependent (see Mission 5).
  • Say: "a strategy is a choice of action" — not: "a strategy is a payoff".

Mission 6

Dominant strategy

  • A dominant strategy is best for a player whatever the other player does.
  • Method: fix the rival's choice, then compare your payoffs.
  • A's view:
    • If B keeps to quota: cheat 875 > keep 800.
    • If B cheats: cheat 750 > keep 700.
  • So cheating is A's dominant strategy. B's payoffs mirror A's, so B's dominant strategy is also to cheat.
  • Say: "dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does" — not: "the strategy with the biggest number in the matrix".

Mission 6

Nash equilibrium

  • Nash equilibrium: an outcome where no player can do better by changing only its own strategy, given the other's choice.
  • Check each cell: would either player want to switch?
  • In the cartel game: (cheat, cheat) → (750, 750).
    • A switching to "keep" gets 700 < 750. B switching gets 700 < 750. No one gains: Nash equilibrium.
  • (keep, keep) → (800, 800) is not a Nash equilibrium: each firm could earn 875 by cheating.
  • When both players have a dominant strategy, the Nash equilibrium is where they both play it.
  • Say: "no player has an incentive to change strategy alone" — not: "both players get their best possible payoff".
Mission 6

The prisoner's dilemma

  • The story: two suspects are questioned in separate rooms. Each can confess or stay silent.
  • Confessing is each one's dominant strategy. Both confess, yet both would get shorter sentences if both stayed silent.
  • A prisoner's dilemma: each player follows its own dominant strategy, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated.
  • The cartel game is a prisoner's dilemma:
    • Nash equilibrium: (cheat, cheat): 750 each.
    • Cooperation: (keep, keep): 800 each.
  • Why cooperation fails: neither firm can trust the other, and a cartel agreement cannot be enforced in court.

No dominant strategy

  • Not every player has a dominant strategy. Find the Nash equilibrium by checking best responses.
  • Illustrative data. Each cell: (X's profit, Y's profit).

  • X: if Y is low, X picks low (30 > 20); if Y is high, X picks high (70 > 60). X has no dominant strategy.
  • X expects Y to play low, so X plays low. Nash equilibrium: (low, low) → (30, 40).
  • Some games have more than one Nash equilibrium. A game can have a Nash equilibrium even when neither player has a dominant strategy.
  • AP tip: mark each player's best response in every row and column. A cell where both are marked is a Nash equilibrium.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A monopolist faces the downward-sloping market demand, so MR < AR.
  • It produces where MR = MC and reads price off AR: higher price, lower output than perfect competition.
  • P > MC at the profit-maximizing quantity, so there is a deadweight loss; a price ceiling at P = MC removes it.

Mission 2

  • A natural monopoly has falling average cost across the whole market, so one firm supplies most cheaply.
  • Unregulated, it sets MR = MC; P = ATC gives normal profit; P = MC is efficient but loss-making.
  • A lump-sum subsidy covers the loss at P = MC without changing MC.

Mission 3

  • Price discrimination: different prices for the same product, not explained by cost.
  • Conditions: market power; groups with different PED that can be told apart; no resale.
  • Perfect price discrimination: output where P = MC, consumer surplus zero, no deadweight loss.

Mission 4

  • Many firms, differentiated products, low barriers: each firm has a downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand curve.
  • Short run: MR = MC; profit above normal or a loss is possible. Long run: entry or exit leaves normal profit (AR tangent to ATC).
  • Long run: excess capacity and P > MC, but buyers get variety.
  • AP: zero economic profit in the long run, output below efficient scale, P > MC.

Mission 5

  • Oligopoly: a few large firms, high barriers, interdependence and uncertainty.
  • Firms can compete or collude: overtly (cartels) or tacitly (price leadership). Cartels act like a monopoly but each member is tempted to cheat.
  • AP: oligopolists struggle to reach the monopoly outcome, but price is higher and quantity lower than in perfect competition.

Mission 6

  • Payoff matrix: players, strategies, payoffs; each cell is (row player, column player).
  • Dominant strategy: best whatever the rival does. Nash equilibrium: no player gains by changing only its own strategy.
  • Prisoner's dilemma: each plays its dominant strategy, and both end up worse off than if they had cooperated. That is why cartels are unstable.
  • Oligopolists struggle to reach the monopoly outcome, but price is higher and quantity lower than in perfect competition.
