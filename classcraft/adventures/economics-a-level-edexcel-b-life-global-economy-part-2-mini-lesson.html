Mission 1

Mission 1: Why would a country give up control over its own trade rules to join a club of its neighbours?

Make a guess: Why would a country give up control over its own trade rules to join a club of its neighbours?

Mission 1

Three ways to agree on trade

  • Bilateral: two countries (e.g. the Australia–UK free trade agreement, 2023).
  • Regional: a group of neighbours (e.g. the EU, USMCA in North America, Mercosur in South America, the African Continental Free Trade Area).
  • Multilateral: almost every country together, through the World Trade Organization (WTO).
  • Blocs shape the pattern of trade: members trade more with each other.
Mission 1

The ladder of integration

Each step up adds to the one below:

1. Free trade area (FTA): no tariffs or quotas between members; each member keeps its own tariffs on outsiders. Needs rules of origin. E.g. USMCA (which replaced NAFTA in 2020); ASEAN's free trade area.

2. Customs union: an FTA plus a common external tariff (CET) on outsiders. Members negotiate trade deals together. E.g. Mercosur.

3. Common market: a customs union plus free movement of labour and capital. E.g. the EU single market.

  • 4. Monetary union: a common market plus a single currency and one central bank. E.g. the euro area.
  • Say: "common external tariff" — not: "the members have no tariffs": they still tax imports from outsiders.
Quick check
?Four countries remove all tariffs on trade with each other and agree one tariff schedule for imports from the rest of the world. Workers still need visas to work in another member country. What kind of bloc is this?
Mission 1

Why join a bloc?

  • Bigger market: firms sell to all members tariff-free, gaining economies of scale.
  • More competition: lower prices and more efficient firms.
  • Foreign direct investment: outside firms build plants inside the bloc to avoid its external tariffs.
  • Freedom of labour (common market): more job opportunities; firms fill skill shortages.
  • Bargaining power: a bloc negotiates as one in world trade talks.
  • Political stability and cooperation between neighbours.
  • Trade creation: cheaper imports from partners replace dearer domestic output.
Mission 1

Trade creation and trade diversion

  • Trade creation: joining replaces higher-cost domestic production with lower-cost imports from a partner. Efficiency rises.
  • Trade diversion: joining switches imports from a lower-cost outside producer to a higher-cost partner, because only the outsider still pays the tariff. Efficiency falls.
  • Illustrative data, same domestic demand and supply as in a later lesson:
    • Before: the cheapest producer is outside, price 3; with a tariff of 2, the home price is 5. Imports 40, tariff revenue 80.
    • Join a customs union with a partner that sells at 4, tariff-free. The outsider still pays 3 + 2 = 5, so all imports now come from the partner. Home price 4; imports 60.
  • Trade creation: two triangles, each ½ × 1 × 10 = 5: a gain of 10.
  • Trade diversion: the 40 units once bought at 3 now cost 4: a loss of 1 × 40 = 40.
  • Net effect here: 10 − 40 = −30. Joining can reduce welfare when diversion outweighs creation.

Quick check
?Before joining a customs union, Country M imported all its cars from Japan, the lowest-cost producer, paying a 10% tariff. After joining, M buys its imported cars from a partner in the union, whose cars cost more to produce than Japan's but now enter tariff-free. What is this?
Mission 1

The costs of blocs

  • Loss of sovereignty: in a customs union a member cannot set its own tariffs or sign its own trade deals; common rules limit national policy.
  • Challenge to multilateralism: energy goes into regional deals instead of global WTO talks; overlapping rules of origin add costs.
  • Outsiders lose: exporters outside the bloc face the external tariff while members compete tariff-free.
  • Adjustment costs: less efficient firms in some members shrink; some regions lose jobs.
  • Trade diversion can cut efficiency.
  • Members may pay into a common budget and share policies they would not choose alone.
Mission 1

The EU single market

  • The Single European Market began on 1 January 1993.
  • Four freedoms: free movement of goods, services, capital and people.
  • The EU is also a customs union (common external tariff). The single market goes further, removing non-tariff barriers:
    • common product standards, or mutual recognition of each other's standards
    • no customs checks at internal borders
    • open public procurement and common competition rules.
  • 27 member countries since the UK left in 2020.
  • Other blocs to compare: ASEAN (South-East Asia, a free trade area) and NAFTA, replaced in 2020 by USMCA (an FTA, with no common external tariff).
Mission 1

The WTO: aims and functions

  • Set up on 1 January 1995, replacing the GATT (1947). 166 members, covering about 98% of world trade.
  • Aim: use trade to raise living standards, create jobs and support sustainable development.
  • Functions:
    • a forum for negotiations to cut trade barriers ("rounds")
    • oversees its trade agreements
    • settles disputes between members
    • reviews members' trade policies
    • technical help for developing countries.
  • Core rule, non-discrimination:
    • most-favoured nation: a tariff cut for one member must go to all members
    • national treatment: imports, once in, are treated like home goods.
  • Allowed exceptions: free trade areas and customs unions, anti-dumping duties, special treatment for developing countries.
  • Other bodies shape trade talks: the G20 (leaders of major economies), the IMF (balance of payments support) and the World Bank (development finance).
Mission 1

The limits of the WTO

  • Hard to agree: decisions need consensus among all members. The Doha Round, launched in 2001, has not been completed.
  • Agriculture and services: rich countries' farm support and barriers to services trade are hard to cut.
  • Unequal bargaining power: large economies have more negotiators, lawyers and leverage than small, poor members.
  • Weak enforcement: since December 2019 the Appellate Body, which hears appeals in disputes, has had too few members to work, because the USA blocked new appointments. A losing country can appeal into the void.
  • The rise of regional blocs is partly a response to slow global talks.
Quick check
?Countries P and Q are both WTO members and have no trade agreement with each other or anyone else. P cuts its tariff on bicycles from Q from 20% to 5%. Under WTO rules, what must P do?
Mission 1

Evaluate: building blocks or stumbling blocks?

It depends on…

  • Who the partners are. Low-cost partners mean more trade creation and less diversion.
  • How high the external tariff is. A high CET makes outsiders' losses larger.
  • A high CET also makes trade diversion more likely.
  • How deep the integration is. A single market removes non-tariff barriers too, but asks more sovereignty of its members.
  • Dynamic effects. Over time, bigger markets bring scale economies, competition and FDI; these can outweigh static diversion.
  • The global system. Blocs can be stepping stones to wider liberalisation, or can divide the world into rival trading groups.
Mission check

?Country K is in a free trade area with Country L. K's government proposes upgrading this to a customs union. Which power would K give up?

Each step up the ladder of integration trades away a national power for wider access: a customs union takes away a member's own tariffs on outsiders.

Mission 2

Why trade patterns change

  • Pattern of trade: what a country exports and imports, and with whom.
  • It changes because of:
    • Comparative advantage shifts: skills, technology and investment change relative costs.
    • Emerging economies grow: new low-cost producers and fast-growing markets (China, India, Vietnam).
    • Trading blocs and bilateral agreements: trade grows inside a bloc; leaving one raises barriers.
    • Exchange rates: a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.
    • Income growth abroad: richer buyers want more services and high-value goods.
    • Transport and communication costs: lower costs make distant partners worthwhile.
Mission 2

The UK's changing trade

  • Partners: the EU bought 47% of UK exports in 2010 and 2011, and 42% in 2023. It is still the UK's largest partner (52% of UK imports in 2023).
  • Products: services rose from 34% of UK exports in 2000 to 52% in 2023.
  • Reasons:
    • faster growth in markets outside Europe (US, Asia)
    • leaving the EU single market and customs union (2021) raised barriers to EU trade
    • comparative advantage in services: finance, business services, education, creative industries
    • decline of manufacturing under competition from emerging economies
  • Visible trade: trade in goods. Invisible trade: trade in services.
  • The UK usually runs a deficit on goods and a surplus on services.
  • Cheap imports raise living standards: the same income buys more. But they can cost jobs in import-competing industries.
Quick check
?The EU bought 47% of UK exports in 2010 and 42% in 2023. What can you conclude from these figures alone?
Quick check
?Over twenty years, Country V's exports shifted from rice and coffee to electronics and clothing. Its main export markets moved from neighbouring countries to North America and East Asia. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 2

Dearer exports: good news or bad?

Prices are one reason trade patterns change. When a country's export prices rise, it depends on…

  • Why they rose.
    • World demand for its exports rose: it sells at higher prices and keeps its buyers. Export earnings and incomes rise.
    • Costs at home rose, or its currency appreciated: its exports lose price competitiveness.
  • How buyers respond.
    • Rivals sell close substitutes (demand is price elastic): buyers switch, sales fall proportionally more than price rises, and export earnings fall.
    • Few substitutes (demand is price inelastic): sales fall a little, and export earnings rise.
  • Over time, buyers switching to rival suppliers changes who trades with whom.
  • Real case: in 2022 world energy and commodity prices surged. By 2022 each unit of Australia's exports (coal, gas, iron ore) bought 81% more imports than in 2015. Each unit of Japan's exports bought about 21% fewer: Japan imports most of its energy.
  • The other side: cheaper imports raise living standards; dearer imports lower them.
Mission check

?Country T sells clothing to Europe. Several rival countries sell very similar clothing. T's currency appreciates, so the prices of its clothing in euros rise by 10% (illustrative data). What is the most likely result?

Dearer exports are good news when buyers want more, and bad news when costs or the currency push prices up and buyers can go elsewhere.

Mission 3

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
  • The current account balance, often as a % of GDP, is a key macroeconomic indicator.
Mission 3

  • Balance of trade = exports − imports of goods and services.
  • Primary income: wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad.
  • Secondary income: transfers with nothing in return.
  • Credits > debits on an account: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Goods are visibles; services are invisibles.
  • Cheap imports raise living standards: incomes buy more and choice widens. But firms competing with imports may lose sales and jobs.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" when debits exceed credits — not: "the country made a loss".

Mission 3

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 3

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • Current + capital + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.
  • Errors and omissions: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.

Mission 4

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: £1 = $1.25. So $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system, the government or central bank sets the rate; a change is a devaluation or revaluation (see Mission 5).
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 4

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate £1 = $1.30.
  • A £500 phone sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans cost a UK buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = £50.
  • The pound appreciates to $1.50. Phone: 500 × 1.50 = $750. Jeans: 65 ÷ 1.50 = £43.33.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 4

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The exchange rate changes from 1 euro = 160 yen to 1 euro = 176 yen. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Much of the trading is for financial investment and speculation, not to pay for goods.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 4

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • US central bank (the Federal Reserve): top of its target range for interest rates 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission 4

Firms and exchange-rate risk

  • A stronger pound: UK exports cost foreigners more; imported materials cost UK firms less.
  • A weaker pound: the reverse. Read exchange rate data carefully: which currency is quoted per which?
  • Exchange-rate risk: a firm that will pay or be paid in foreign currency does not know the future rate.
  • Example (illustrative data): a UK importer must pay $130,000 in 3 months. Today £1 = $1.30: cost £100,000.
  • If the pound falls to $1.25: 130,000 ÷ 1.25 = £104,000, £4,000 more.
  • Forward contract: agree today to buy dollars at $1.29 in 3 months. Cost fixed at 130,000 ÷ 1.29 = £100,775.
  • Like insurance: certainty at a price. If the pound rises to $1.35 instead, the firm misses paying only £96,296.
  • A floating rate can also act as a policy instrument and shock absorber: a fall in the currency helps exporters without any policy decision.
Mission 4Evaluate

How far can you predict a currency?

  • It depends on:
    • several factors at once: interest rates may rise while growth slows; the effects can offset
    • expectations: speculators act on what they expect, so a rate can move before anything else changes
    • relative, not absolute: what matters is a country's interest rates, inflation and growth compared with other countries'
    • time horizon: day to day, financial flows dominate; over years, trade and competitiveness matter more
  • A central bank may also intervene (see Mission 5).

Mission 5

Three exchange rate systems

  • Floating: demand and supply in the currency market set the rate (see Mission 4). A rise is an appreciation, a fall a depreciation.
  • Fixed (a peg): the central bank commits to one rate against another currency or a basket. A deliberate cut in the official rate is a devaluation; a deliberate rise, a revaluation.
  • Managed float: the rate floats, but the central bank steps in to limit or steer its movements.
  • Real examples: the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at about 7.80 since 1983; the euro floats; Singapore manages its currency against a basket of its trading partners' currencies.
  • Say: "the pound depreciated" for a floating currency — not: "the pound was devalued". Devaluation is a policy decision under a fixed rate.
Quick check
?A country pegs its currency to the US dollar. Its government announces that the official rate will be cut from 2.0 to 1.6 US dollars per unit of its currency. Which term describes this?
Mission 5

Holding a fixed rate

  • Step 1: the market for the currency clears at the peg.
  • Step 2: demand for the currency falls (D1 → D2). At the peg, supply now exceeds demand: the market rate would fall below the peg.
  • Step 3: the central bank closes the gap. It buys its own currency, paying with foreign currency reserves. This adds to demand: D2 → D3, back to the peg.
  • Other tools: raise interest rates (attracts financial inflows, so more demand for the currency); exchange controls (limits on buying foreign currency); borrowing reserves from abroad.
  • Pressure the other way (demand rises): the central bank sells its own currency and builds up reserves, or cuts interest rates.
  • So under a fixed rate, the central bank, not the market, carries the adjustment.
Mission 5

The effective (trade-weighted) exchange rate

  • One currency has many exchange rates. They often move in different directions.
  • The effective exchange rate (trade-weighted index) is a weighted average of them, with weights from each partner's share of trade.
  • Base year = 100. Each partner's rate is an index.
  • Illustrative data:

Mission check

To hold a peg, the central bank must supply or absorb whatever gap opens at the fixed rate, in either direction.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Free trade area → customs union (common external tariff) → common market (free movement of labour and capital).
  • Then monetary union (single currency).
  • Blocs bring bigger markets, competition, FDI and bargaining power; they cost sovereignty and can harm outsiders and global talks.
  • Trade creation raises efficiency; trade diversion lowers it. The net effect depends on partners' costs and the external tariff.
  • The WTO negotiates, monitors and settles disputes under non-discrimination; consensus, unequal power and a stalled appeals system limit it.

Mission 2

  • Trade patterns change with comparative advantage, emerging economies, trading blocs, exchange rates, incomes and transport costs.
  • UK trade has shifted towards services and towards markets outside the EU.
  • Dearer exports raise export earnings when world demand is behind them; they cut earnings when costs or the currency push prices up and buyers can switch.

Mission 3

  • Current account: goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions = 0.

Mission 4

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 5

  • Floating: market sets the rate (appreciation/depreciation). Fixed: the central bank holds it (devaluation/revaluation). Managed: it floats, with intervention.
  • A peg is held by buying or selling the currency with reserves, and by interest rates.
  • Effective exchange rate = trade-weighted average of a currency's exchange rate indices.
