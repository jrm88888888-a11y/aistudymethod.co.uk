Mission 1

The atman: the real you

  • Atman = the eternal self, or soul, in every living being: humans, animals, plants.
  • It is not the body and not the mind: both are part of prakriti (matter) and change; the atman does not.
  • "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Bhagavad Gita 2.20, Arnold).
  • "Nor I, nor thou, nor any one of these, / Ever was not, nor ever will not be" (Bhagavad Gita 2.12, Arnold).
  • The atman is the part that is liberated from samsara.
Mission 1Read the source

Changing clothes

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / "These will I wear to-day!" / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh." (Bhagavad Gita 2.22, Arnold)

  • "To man's frame / As there come infancy and youth and age, / So come there raisings-up and layings-down / Of other and of other life-abodes" (Bhagavad Gita 2.13, Arnold).

Q1 In the image, what are the clothes, and who is the wearer?

Show answer

The clothes are bodies; the wearer is the atman.

Mission 1

Samsara: the wheel of rebirth

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth (reincarnation).
  • At death the atman leaves the body and takes a new one: human, animal or other, in this world or another loka.
  • What decides the next birth? Karma: the results of past actions (see Mission 2).
  • Samsara is seen as full of suffering and impermanence. The goal is to escape it.

Mission 1

Why we stay stuck: avidya, maya, attachment

  • Avidya = ignorance: not knowing who we really are; thinking "I am this body".
  • Maya = the power that makes the changing world seem to be all there is (see Mission 4).
  • From ignorance come selfishness, greed and anger: we grab for things that cannot satisfy the atman.
  • Detachment (vairagya): acting and enjoying life without clinging. "The soul which is not moved ... / Takes sorrow and takes joy indifferently, / Lives in the life undying!" (Bhagavad Gita 2.15, Arnold)
  • Gita 2.27: death is certain for the born, and birth for the dead, so the wise do not grieve (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Moksha: liberation

  • Moksha: release from samsara; the atman is free and is never reborn.
  • Reached through knowledge, devotion, selfless action or meditation.
  • Jivanmukti: being liberated while still alive. "When all desires dwelling in the heart cease, then the mortal becomes immortal and attains Brahman here." (Katha Upanishad 6.14, Paramananda)
Mission 1Diversity

Atman and Brahman: one or two?

  • Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual"; Shankara, c. 8th century CE): atman is Brahman. The sense of being separate is avidya. Moksha = realising this identity. A monist view.
  • Vishishtadvaita ("qualified non-dual"; Ramanuja, traditionally 1017–1137): atman is a real part of God, as a body belongs to a soul: one with God, yet distinct.
  • Dvaita ("dual"; Madhva, 1238–1317): atman and God (Vishnu) are eternally distinct. Moksha = eternal, blissful service to God. A monotheist view.
  • The sage Uddalaka teaches his son Svetaketu: as salt dissolved in water is everywhere in it though unseen, so is the Self; "tat tvam asi": "you are that" (Chandogya Upanishad 6.13, paraphrase). Advaitins read this as identity; Ramanuja and Madhva read it differently.
  • Brihadaranyaka 3.7.15–23: the "inner controller" dwells in the eye, ear and mind but is not known by them (paraphrase); Ramanuja used this to show God within, yet distinct from, the self.

Mission 1

Why it matters today

  • Respect for life: the same kind of atman lives in every being, so many Hindus are vegetarian and practise ahimsa.
  • Facing death: funerals (antyesti) include cremation, which many Hindus believe helps release the atman; ashes are often scattered in the Ganges or another river.
  • Living well: every action shapes the next life; the long goal is moksha.
  • Equality: many Hindus stress that the atman has no caste, gender or race.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The atman and Brahman are identical."

  • Reasons for: "tat tvam asi" (Chandogya 6.8.7 and following) read literally; the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (4.4.6) says one free of desire, being Brahman, goes to Brahman (paraphrase). Advaita explains why the self feels limited: avidya. Mystics describe experiences of unity.
  • Reasons against: if the atman were already Brahman, how could it be ignorant? (Ramanuja's objection). The Gita speaks of loving devotion to Krishna, which needs two. Dvaita: identity would leave no one to enjoy God's love.
  • Judge it by: Which reading fits the most scriptures? What happens to devotion (bhakti) on each view? Does the view explain suffering and ignorance?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain Hindu teachings about samsara and moksha.

  • Teaching 1: the atman is reborn in body after body (samsara). Source: "So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2.22). Development: so Hindus see death as a change, not an end, and try to live well because their deeds shape their next life.
  • Teaching 2: the goal is moksha, release from samsara. Development: Advaitins see it as realising that atman is Brahman; Dvaita Hindus as eternal loving service to God; either way, it is the final end of suffering.

Mission 2

Karma means action

  • Karma = "action", and the results that follow from it.
  • The law of karma: every intentional action (thought, word, deed) has a consequence for the one who does it.
  • Good actions bring good results; harmful actions bring suffering, in this life or a future one.
  • Karma as causality (cause and effect in the moral world) and as cosmic justice: no one escapes the results of their actions.
  • Karma is about intention: an accident is not the same as a cruel act.
Mission 2

Karma and samsara

  • Karma decides the next birth: what kind of body, family and circumstances the atman is born into.
  • Good karma → a better rebirth, perhaps in a higher loka. Bad karma → a harder rebirth.
  • But all karma, good or bad, keeps the atman in samsara: some Hindus say good karma is a golden chain, but still a chain.
  • So the goal is not just good karma: it is to be free of binding karma, which leads to moksha.
Mission 2

Three kinds of karma

  • Sanchita karma: the whole store of karma from all past lives.
  • Prarabdha karma: the part that has begun to ripen in this life: your body, family, many circumstances.
  • Your spec spells it "parabdha".
  • Kriyamana karma: karma being created now by today's choices; it will ripen later.
  • Classic picture: an archer. Arrows in the quiver = sanchita. An arrow already shot = prarabdha (it cannot be called back). The arrow now on the bow = kriyamana (still your choice).
  • Kriyamana karma links karma to ethics: right now, you choose.

Mission 2

Karma is not fate

  • Fate says: everything is fixed and you can do nothing. Karma says: your past shaped where you start, but you choose what you do now.
  • Prarabdha sets some limits: you cannot choose your birth, your body or some events.
  • Kriyamana is free: how you respond to those limits creates new karma.
  • So karma makes people responsible: blaming "fate" is not an excuse.
Mission 2

Sakam and nishkam karma

  • Sakam karma: action done with desire for its results (reward, praise, profit). It creates binding karma and keeps the atman in samsara.
  • Nishkam karma: action done without selfish desire, as duty or as an offering to God. It does not bind.
  • "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, Arnold)
  • This is the heart of karma yoga, the path of action.
  • Example: helping at a food bank to look good on social media (sakam) or simply because it is right and an offering to God (nishkam).
Mission 2Read the source

No escape from action

> "No man shall 'scape from act / By shunning action; nay, and none shall come / By mere renouncements unto perfectness." (Bhagavad Gita 3.4, Arnold)

> "But all thy dues discharging, for My sake, / With meditation centred inwardly, / Seeking no profit, satisfied, serene, / Heedless of issue—fight!" (Bhagavad Gita 3.30, Arnold)

  • Gita 3.5–9 (paraphrase): no one can stay without action even for a moment; action done as an offering (sacrifice) does not bind, but action for oneself does.
  • Gita 3.29 (paraphrase): the wise should not unsettle those who still act from desire.

Mission 2

Karma, suffering and freedom

  • But karma does not mean "they deserve it, so don't help": compassion (daya) and service (seva) are duties, and they create good karma.
  • Suffering can be a chance to use up old karma and grow spiritually.
  • Jivanmukti and karma: a person liberated while alive still lives out their prarabdha (the arrow already shot), but creates no new binding karma. "The flame of Knowledge wastes works' dross away!" (Bhagavad Gita 4.37, Arnold)
Mission 2Diversity

Who runs the law of karma?

  • An impersonal law: some Hindus see karma as a natural law, like gravity: no judge is needed.
  • God as overseer: many Vaishnava and Shaiva Hindus believe God gives the fruits of karma fairly, and can also forgive and show grace.
  • Grace beyond karma: in bhakti traditions, surrender to God can free a devotee from the bonds of karma (Ramanuja's prapatti, "surrender").
  • Emphases differ: Advaitins stress knowledge burning up karma; Vaishnavas stress grace; many ordinary Hindus stress simply doing good.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Karma is a fair explanation of suffering."

  • Reasons for: it makes the universe just: nothing happens without a cause. It makes people responsible for their actions. It explains why good people may suffer (karma from earlier lives).
  • Reasons against: no one remembers past lives, so it is hard to learn from them. It could be misused to blame victims of illness, poverty or caste. Many Hindus add God's grace or compassion, suggesting karma alone is not the whole answer.
  • Judge it by: Is it fair to be affected by deeds you cannot remember? Does belief in karma make people kinder or harsher in practice? Does adding grace weaken or strengthen the idea?
Mission 2Worked answer

Influence on Hindus today

Question: Explain two ways in which belief in karma influences Hindus today.

  • Way 1: Hindus try to act ethically, e.g. honestly and without violence. Development: every action creates kriyamana karma, which shapes the next birth.
  • Way 2: many Hindus try to act without selfish desire. Source: "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them" (Gita 2.47). Development: nishkam karma does not bind, so it leads towards moksha.

Karma explains where you start, not where you must end: what you do now is still your choice.

Mission 3

One and many

  • Most Hindus believe in one Supreme reality (Brahman).
  • That reality can show itself in many forms: the deities (devas, male; devis, female).
  • Your spec's term: deities are manifestations of saguna Brahman, Brahman "with qualities".
  • Each deity shows a different quality: power to create, to protect, to transform, wisdom, wealth, strength.
  • A Hindu often has an ishta-devata: a chosen deity to whom they feel closest.
  • Analogy many Hindus use: one actor, many roles; or one sun, many rays.
Mission 3

The Trimurti

  • Trimurti = "three forms". Three deities linked to the cycle of the universe:
  • Brahma, the creator: four heads facing every direction; holds the Vedas; rides a swan (hamsa). Very few temples are dedicated to him.
  • Vishnu, the preserver: holds conch, discus, mace and lotus; often blue; rides the eagle Garuda; comes to earth as avatars to restore dharma.
  • Shiva, the destroyer: ends what is old so new can begin; trident, third eye, the bull Nandi; worshipped as the linga; as Nataraja he dances the universe in and out of being.
  • Creation, preservation and destruction are one continuous cycle.

Mission 3

Shakti and the Tridevi

  • Shakti = divine power or energy, understood as female. Without Shakti, many Hindus say, the gods cannot act.
  • Each god has a consort who is his Shakti: the Tridevi ("three goddesses"):
  • Saraswati (with Brahma): knowledge, music, the arts; white sari, veena, swan; honoured by students.
  • Lakshmi (with Vishnu): wealth, good fortune, beauty; lotus, gold coins; welcomed at Diwali.
  • Parvati (with Shiva): devotion, the loving mother; mother of Ganesha. Her fierce forms are Durga (who defeats the buffalo-demon Mahishasura) and Kali.
  • Shaktas worship the Goddess (Devi) as the Supreme herself.
Mission 3

Ganesha and Hanuman

  • Ganesha: elephant-headed son of Shiva and Parvati. Remover of obstacles, lord of beginnings; worshipped first in many pujas and before exams, weddings or a new business. Rides a mouse.
  • Hanuman: the monkey-faced deity of strength and loyal devotion; servant of Rama in the Ramayana; leaps to Lanka to find Sita. Many Hindus recite the Hanuman Chalisa for courage.
  • Significance: deities model qualities Hindus want in their own lives: wisdom, devotion, courage.

Mission 3Diversity

Who is supreme?

  • Vaishnavas: Vishnu (or Krishna) is the Supreme. Brahma is created by him; Shiva is a great devotee or a lesser power. The Trimurti are not equal.
  • Shaivas: Shiva is Mahadeva, the great God. He alone does all the work of the Trimurti (creating, preserving, destroying), and more; Brahma and Vishnu act under him.
  • Shaktas: the Goddess (Devi) is the Supreme; the male gods depend on her power.
  • Smartas (often linked to Shankara): worship five deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi, Ganesha, Surya) as equal forms of one Brahman.
  • Many Hindus in Britain visit one mandir with shrines to several deities and see no contradiction.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Hindus who worship many deities are really monotheists."

  • Reasons for: most Hindus say the deities are forms of one Supreme (Brahman). Scripture teaches unity: the Kurma Purana (1.9) has Shiva say he and Vishnu are not separate; Rig Veda 1.164.46: "Truth is one; the wise call it by many names" (paraphrase). Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each worship one Supreme.
  • Reasons against: in practice people pray to different deities for different needs. Some Hindus in villages honour local gods and goddesses as real, distinct powers. Advaitins say the deepest truth is non-personal Brahman, so "monotheism" (one personal God) is not quite right either.
  • Other labels scholars use: henotheism (worshipping one god without denying others); monism (all is one reality).
  • Judge it by: What do Hindus themselves say? Does worship of a chosen deity exclude others? Does a Western word fit at all?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different Hindu beliefs about the Tri-murti.

  • Belief 1 (Vaishnava): Vishnu is the Supreme; Brahma is born from the lotus of Vishnu's navel, so the three are not equal. Development: Vaishnavas focus their worship on Vishnu and his avatars, such as Krishna.
  • Belief 2 (Shaiva): Shiva is Mahadeva, who alone creates, preserves and destroys. Development: Shaivas focus worship on Shiva, for example offering water and milk to the linga.

Mission 4

A universe that breathes

  • Most Hindus see time as cyclical: universes come into being, last, and dissolve, again and again, without beginning or end.
  • Each cycle: creation (Brahma) → preservation (Vishnu) → dissolution (Shiva). Then rest, then a new creation.
  • One "day of Brahma" (a kalpa) lasts 1,000 cycles of the four ages:
  • "If ye know Brahma's Day / Which is a thousand Yugas; if ye know / The thousand Yugas making Brahma's Night" (Bhagavad Gita 8.17, Arnold).
Mission 4Read the source

The Hymn of Creation

Rig Veda 10.129, the Nasadiya Sukta (paraphrase):

  • In the beginning there was neither existence nor non-existence; no air, no sky beyond (v. 1).
  • There was no death, no immortality, no day or night: only That One, breathing without breath (v. 2).
  • Darkness was hidden in darkness; from heat (tapas) That One was born; then came desire, the first seed of mind (vv. 3–4).
  • Who really knows? Where did creation come from? The gods came after the world was made, so who knows where it came from? (v. 6)
  • Whether it was made or not, the one who watches over it in the highest heaven surely knows, or perhaps he does not know (v. 7).

Mission 4

Creation stories and Aum

  • A widely told story (Puranas): between cycles, Vishnu lies on the serpent Shesha on the cosmic ocean. A lotus grows from his navel; in it Brahma is born and creates the worlds.
  • Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90): the universe comes from the sacrifice of a cosmic Person (Purusha): sun from his eye, sky from his head, earth from his feet (paraphrase).
  • Aum (Om): many Hindus say the universe begins as sacred sound; Aum is that first vibration and stands for Brahman. It is chanted at the start and end of prayers.
  • Different stories, one message: the universe comes from the divine and depends on it.
Mission 4

The four yugas

  • Each cycle has four ages (yugas), each shorter and darker than the one before:
  • Satya (Krita) Yuga: age of truth; dharma stands firm, like a bull on four legs.
  • Treta Yuga: dharma on three legs (the age of Rama).
  • Dvapara Yuga: dharma on two legs (the age of Krishna).
  • Kali Yuga: dharma on one leg; quarrels, greed and ignorance. Tradition dates its start to 3102 BCE; many Hindus believe we are living in it now.
  • Lengths in the ratio 4 : 3 : 2 : 1. Together they make a mahayuga of 4,320,000 years; then the cycle repeats.

Mission 4

Prakriti and purusha

  • Prakriti: matter, nature: everything physical, including the body, the senses and even the mind.
  • Purusha: spirit, pure consciousness, which is not made of matter.
  • "Know thou that Nature and the Spirit both / Have no beginning!" (Bhagavad Gita 13.19, Arnold)
  • Katha Upanishad (3.10–11) lists a ladder: senses → objects → mind → intellect → the great Atman → the Unmanifested → "beyond the Unmanifested is the Purusha (the Cosmic Soul); beyond the Purusha there is nothing" (Katha Upanishad 3.11, Paramananda).
  • Bondage = spirit identifying itself with matter.
Mission 4

The three gunas

  • All of prakriti is made of three gunas (qualities, or "strands"), mixed in different amounts:
  • Sattva: goodness, light, purity, calm, wisdom.
  • Rajas: passion, activity, desire, restlessness.
  • Tamas: darkness, laziness, ignorance, decay.
  • "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature" (Bhagavad Gita 14.5, Arnold).
  • Foods, actions and people show them: fresh fruit (sattva), spicy food (rajas), stale food or alcohol (tamas).

Mission 4

Maya

  • Maya: often translated "illusion": the power that makes the world look like the whole of reality.
  • Advaita reading: the world of many separate things is not ultimately real, like a rope mistaken in the dark for a snake. Only Brahman is fully real.
  • Theistic reading (Vaishnava, e.g. Ramanuja): the world is real; maya is God's power, and the illusion is forgetting God and treating the material world as all there is.
  • "Hard it is / To pierce that veil divine of various shows / Which hideth Me; yet they who worship Me / Pierce it and pass beyond." (Bhagavad Gita 7.14, Arnold)
  • Maya and the gunas: the three gunas are the threads of the "veil"; seeing through them is the way to liberation.
Mission 4

Many worlds, many beings

  • Many Hindu texts describe 14 lokas (worlds): 7 higher, with our earth (Bhu-loka) at the bottom of these, and 7 lower.
  • Devas: gods of light and nature (e.g. Indra, Agni, Surya). Powerful, but not the Supreme; they too live within the cycle.
  • Here "devas" means the gods of the heavens such as Indra and Agni, not the great deities (Vishnu, Shiva, Devi) whom many Hindus worship as the Supreme.
  • Asuras: powerful beings who oppose the devas and seek power for themselves.
  • Beings are born into higher or lower realms because of their karma.
  • "The worlds, Arjuna!—even Brahma's world— / Roll back again from Death to Life's unrest" (Bhagavad Gita 8.16, Arnold).
  • The heavenly realms of the Trimurti: Brahma-loka; Shiva-loka (Kailasa); Vishnu-loka, Vaikuntha.
  • "All this is Brahman" (Chandogya Upanishad 3.14.1, paraphrase): every world depends on Brahman.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Hindu cosmology fits well with modern science."

  • Reasons for: an immense, ancient universe (billions of years) rather than a young one; endless cycles echo some cyclic models in physics. Rig Veda 10.129 asks open questions, like a scientist. Many Hindus see no conflict, reading yugas (ages) and lokas (worlds) spiritually.
  • Reasons against: current mainstream cosmology describes one universe, about 13.8 billion years old, with no evidence that it repeats. Texts speak of lokas, devas and asuras (heavenly beings and their rivals), which science cannot test. Some Hindus read the yuga figures literally and some do not, so "fit" depends on the reading.
  • Judge it by: Are religion and science answering the same question (how or why)? Should ancient numbers be read literally? Does a "fit" make a belief more true, or only easier to hold?

Mission 5

Avatar: God comes down

  • Avatara (avatar) = "descent": the Supreme comes down into the world in a physical form.
  • Most avatars are of Vishnu, the preserver.
  • Why? Krishna explains: "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape" (Bhagavad Gita 4.7–8, Arnold).
  • Avatars come again and again, in every age, to protect the good and restore dharma.
  • An avatar is God's free choice; humans are reborn because of karma.
Mission 5

The ten avatars of Vishnu

  • The best-known list (Dashavatara): Matsya (fish), Kurma (tortoise), Varaha (boar), Narasimha (man-lion), Vamana (dwarf), Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the Buddha (in some lists Balarama), Kalki (still to come).
  • Each appears when dharma is under threat: e.g. Varaha lifts the earth from the ocean; Vamana's three steps win back the worlds.
  • Kalki is expected at the end of the present age, the Kali Yuga (see Mission 4).
  • The two most loved: Rama and Krishna.
Mission 5

Rama: the model of dharma

  • Hero of the Ramayana (traditionally by the sage Valmiki).
  • Prince of Ayodhya. To keep his father's promise, he accepts 14 years' exile with his wife Sita and brother Lakshmana.
  • The demon king Ravana kidnaps Sita; with Hanuman and an army of monkeys, Rama rescues her.
  • His return to Ayodhya is remembered at Diwali in many regions.
  • Called maryada purushottama: the ideal man who keeps every duty: as son, husband, brother and king.
  • Some Hindus question parts of the story, e.g. Sita's ordeal by fire and later banishment; many read these as tests of the hard choices dharma brings.
Mission 5

Krishna: God of love and teacher of the Gita

  • Born in Mathura; grows up among cowherds in Vrindavan.
  • Stories of the child Krishna stealing butter, and the young Krishna playing his flute for the gopis (cowherd girls), especially Radha: pictures of the soul's love for God (bhakti).
  • As an adult, Krishna is Arjuna's charioteer and teaches him the Bhagavad Gita before the great battle of the Mahabharata.
  • Gaudiya Vaishnavas (including ISKCON) go further: Krishna is not just an avatar but the Supreme Person himself, the source of all avatars (Bhagavata Purana 1.3.28, paraphrase).

Mission 5Diversity

Who counts as an avatar?

  • Vaishnavas: avatars are central; Rama and Krishna are fully God.
  • Shaivas: Shiva appears in many forms (e.g. Nataraja), but the avatar idea matters far less.
  • The Buddha: many Hindu lists make him Vishnu's ninth avatar. Buddhists do not accept this.
  • Jesus: some Hindus (e.g. many followers of Ramakrishna and Vivekananda) honour Jesus as an avatar. Most Christians reject this: they believe Jesus is God incarnate once, uniquely; avatars come many times.
  • Arya Samaj (founded by Dayananda Saraswati, 1875) rejects avatars and murti worship: God is formless.

Mission 5

Murti: the form of God in worship

  • Murti = "form" or "embodiment": a sacred image of a deity, of stone, metal, wood or clay.
  • In a mandir it is installed with a ceremony, prana pratishtha ("establishing the life-breath"): many Hindus believe the deity is then present in it.
  • It is treated as an honoured guest: woken, bathed, dressed, offered food, put to rest.
  • Darshan: seeing the deity and being seen by it, a blessing.
  • Food offered becomes prasad, blessed food shared with worshippers.
  • Your spec: murti as focus of worship and expression of an actual presence.

Mission 5Diversity

Presence or symbol?

  • Real presence: many Vaishnava and temple traditions hold that God truly dwells in the consecrated murti. Sri Vaishnavas call it archa avatara: God's descent into a worshipped image.
  • Aid to focus: many Hindus, including many Advaitins, see the murti as a symbol that steadies the mind on God; the goal is to see God everywhere.
  • Rejection: the Arya Samaj teaches that God is formless and should not be worshipped in images.
  • Many Hindus dislike the word "idol": they do not worship stone, but God through the form.

Mission 5Evaluate

"A murti is only a symbol of God."

  • Reasons for: God is beyond all form (nirguna Brahman); Gita 12.5 admits form is a help for those in a body. Many Hindus, including many Advaitins, say the murti points beyond itself. The Arya Samaj rejects image worship altogether.
  • Reasons against: prana pratishtha is believed to make the deity present; the murti is cared for as a living guest. Sri Vaishnavas call it an avatar (archa avatara). Gita 9.26: God accepts what is offered, as if really receiving it.
  • Judge it by: What do worshippers experience in darshan? What do the scriptures and temple rites assume? Can something be a symbol and a real presence at once?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hindus believe the atman is the eternal self, distinct from body and mind; it moves from body to body in samsara, shaped by karma.
  • In Hindu teaching, avidya (ignorance), maya and attachment keep the atman bound; moksha is release, possible even while alive (jivanmukti) for some schools.
  • Advaita: atman is Brahman. Vishishtadvaita: atman is part of God, yet distinct. Dvaita: atman is eternally distinct from God.

Mission 2

  • In Hindu teaching, karma is action and its results; it decides rebirth in samsara, and only action without binding desire leads towards moksha.
  • Sanchita (store), prarabdha (ripening now) and kriyamana (being made now): many Hindus say the past sets limits, but present choices are free, so karma is not fate.
  • The Gita teaches that sakam karma (with desire for results) binds; nishkam karma (without selfish desire, offered to God) does not (Gita 2.47).

Mission 3

  • Many Hindus see the deities as forms of one Supreme (saguna Brahman): Brahma creates, Vishnu preserves, Shiva destroys; Saraswati, Lakshmi and Parvati are their Shakti.
  • Hindus honour Ganesha as remover of obstacles and Hanuman for strength and devotion.
  • Vaishnavas, Shaivas and Shaktas each see a different form (Vishnu, Shiva, the Goddess) as Supreme; Smartas treat several forms as equal.

Mission 4

  • Most Hindus see the universe as cyclical: creation, preservation, dissolution, repeated; a day of Brahma is 1,000 cycles of four yugas, and many Hindus believe we live in the Kali Yuga.
  • In Hindu teaching, prakriti (matter) is woven from three gunas: sattva, rajas, tamas; maya is the power that hides the divine behind the world.
  • There are many worlds (lokas) with devas and asuras; Rig Veda 10.129 ends by asking who truly knows how creation began.

Mission 5

  • Hindus believe an avatar is a descent of the Supreme (mostly Vishnu) to restore dharma "from age to age" (Gita 4.7–8); Rama and Krishna are the best loved.
  • A murti is a sacred form; after prana pratishtha many Hindus believe the deity is present; others see it as an aid to focus; the Arya Samaj rejects image worship.
  • Who is an avatar is debated: Vaishnavas and Shaivas differ; Hindus may honour the Buddha or Jesus as avatars, which Buddhists and most Christians do not accept.
