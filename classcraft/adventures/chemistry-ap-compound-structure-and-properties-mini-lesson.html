Mission 1

Which ions atoms form

  • Metal atoms lose electrons: positive ions, cations.
  • Nonmetal atoms gain electrons: negative ions, anions.
  • Groups 1, 2 and 13 (3 in older numbering) lose all outer electrons: 1+, 2+, 3+.
  • Groups 15, 16 and 17 (5, 6, 7) gain enough to fill the outer shell: 3−, 2−, 1−.
  • Mg (2,8,2) loses two: Mg²⁺. N (2,5) gains three: N³⁻. Both now have neon's arrangement.
  • Charge = protons − electrons. The number of protons never changes.
  • Whether two elements bond, and how, depends on how their nuclei attract valence electrons.
Quick check

Mission 1

Ionic bonds and formulae

  • Ionic bonding: strong electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions.
  • It acts in all directions: each ion attracts every oppositely charged neighbor.
  • A metal with a nonmetal is generally ionic, but the properties are the best test of bonding type.
  • Formula: total charge zero. Mg²⁺ and N³⁻ → Mg3N2.
  • Name: cation first, then the anion ending -ide: magnesium nitride.
  • Roman numerals give a metal's charge: Fe³⁺ with O²⁻ is iron(III) oxide, Fe2O3.
  • The formula gives one formula unit: the simplest ratio of ions.

Mission 1

The giant ionic lattice

  • Ionic compounds form a giant lattice: a regular 3-D array of alternating cations and anions.
  • The array maximizes attractions between opposite charges and minimizes repulsions between like ones.
  • There are no molecules. Al2O3 means 2 Al³⁺ for every 3 O²⁻.
Mission 1

Properties from the lattice

  • High melting point, very low volatility: every ion is held by many strong attractions.
  • Very low vapor pressure for the same reason.
  • Brittle: a blow shifts a layer, like charges meet, they repel, and the crystal splits.
  • Conducts only when molten or in solution: then the ions are free to move and carry charge.
  • Solid: no conduction. The ions are fixed in the lattice, and there are no free electrons.
  • Often soluble in water: polar water molecules attract the ions out of the lattice.
  • Dissolving breaks ionic attractions and forms ion–dipole attractions, so it can be argued as physical or chemical.
Quick check
?A student uses a conductivity tester on three samples of potassium chloride: KCl(s) at room temperature, KCl(l) above its melting point, and KCl(aq) made with distilled water. Which result should the student predict?
Mission 1

How strongly a lattice holds together

  • The attraction is stronger when ions have larger charges and smaller radii (centers closer).
  • Coulomb's law: force ∝ q1q2 / r², with r the distance between ion centers.
  • Same charges, size decides: NaF melts at 993 °C, NaCl 801 °C, KCl 770 °C.
  • Double both charges, with ions about the size of NaF's: MgO (Mg²⁺, O²⁻) melts at about 2850 °C.

Mission check

?In a kettle's heating element, the hot metal wire is packed in powdered magnesium oxide, MgO, inside a metal tube. MgO is ionic, yet it is used here as an electrical insulator. Why doesn't it conduct?

Salt is the same. Its Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions can't move in the crystal, so no charge flows. Melt it or dissolve it, the ions move, and the bulb lights.

Mission 2

What a covalent bond is

  • Covalent bond: the strong electrostatic attraction between a shared pair of electrons and the nuclei of the two bonded atoms.
  • The pair sits between the nuclei; both pull on it, holding the atoms together.
  • Too close, and the two nuclei repel: the bond length is where attraction and repulsion balance.
  • Lone pair: a pair of outer electrons not used in bonding.
  • Octet rule: atoms tend to end up with eight outer electrons. A tendency, not a law.
Mission 2

Drawing the electrons

  • A Lewis diagram shows every valence electron: bonding pairs as lines, lone pairs as dots.
  • 1. Count the outer electrons. Add one per negative charge; remove one per positive charge.
  • 2. Join the atoms by single bonds. 3. Complete the outer atoms' octets with lone pairs.
  • 4. Spare electrons go on the central atom. Too few? Share more pairs: multiple bonds.
  • CO2: 16 outer electrons. O=C=O, two lone pairs on each O.
  • N2: 10 outer electrons. N≡N, one lone pair on each N.
  • An ion goes in square brackets, charge outside: [O–H]⁻, three lone pairs on O.
Mission 2

Fewer than eight, or more

  • The octet rule has limitations.
  • BF3: boron's three outer electrons make three bonds. B has only 6.
  • BeCl2: beryllium has only 4.
  • Odd-electron species: NO has 11 valence electrons, so one atom must have fewer than eight.
  • Atoms from period 3 on can hold more than eight: SF6 has 12 around S.

Single, double, triple

  • Single, double and triple bonds share one, two and three pairs.
  • More shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: the bond is shorter and stronger.

Mission 2

Energy and distance

  • Plot potential energy against the distance between two nuclei.
  • Far apart: no interaction, energy zero.
  • Closer: both nuclei attract both electrons. Energy falls.
  • Too close: nucleus–nucleus repulsion. Energy rises steeply.
  • The minimum sits at the bond length. Its depth below zero is the bond energy.
  • A triple bond: a deeper well at a shorter distance.
Quick check
?On a graph of potential energy versus internuclear distance for two atoms that form a diatomic molecule, which quantity is the bond energy of the molecule?
Mission 2

One atom supplies both electrons

  • Sometimes one atom supplies both electrons of a shared pair.
  • The donor has a lone pair. The acceptor has room for a pair.
  • H2O + H⁺ → H3O⁺: an O lone pair goes to H⁺. NH3 + H⁺ → NH4⁺ forms the same way.
  • Once formed, it is identical to the others: all three O–H bonds in H3O⁺ match.
Mission check

?Hydrogen cyanide, H–C≡N, has a carbon–nitrogen triple bond. Methylamine, CH3–NH2, has a carbon–nitrogen single bond. How does the C≡N bond compare with the C–N bond?

Nitrogen is N≡N, with three shared pairs: short and very strong.

Breaking it takes about 945 kJ/mol, against about 158 kJ/mol for N–N. So very few substances react with N2 under ordinary conditions.

Mission 3

The metallic bond

  • Metal atoms release their outer electrons into a shared pool of delocalized electrons.
  • Metallic bonding: cations in a sea of delocalized electrons, not tied to any one atom.
  • The ions form a regular giant metallic lattice.
  • Bonding is non-directional: each ion attracts the electrons all around it.
Mission 3

Why metals conduct and bend

  • Electrical conductivity: delocalized electrons drift when a voltage is applied. The ions stay put.
  • Thermal conductivity: delocalized electrons carry kinetic energy quickly through the metal.
  • Malleable (hammered into shape) and ductile (drawn into wires): layers of ions slide.
  • After sliding, every ion is still surrounded by delocalized electrons: the bonding is not broken.
  • An ionic lattice is different: a shift brings like charges together. They repel and the crystal shatters.
Quick check
?The diagram represents part of a metallic solid before and after a force shifts the upper layers. Which statement best explains why the metal stays in its new shape instead of breaking apart?
Mission 3

What makes metallic bonding stronger

  • Stronger with a higher ion charge and a smaller ion.
  • More delocalized electrons per ion, packed closer to each ion's center: stronger attraction.
  • Na⁺ → Mg²⁺ → Al³⁺: melting point 98 °C → 650 °C → 660 °C.
  • Down group 1 the ions get larger: Li 181 °C, Na 98 °C, K 63 °C.
  • Mass does not decide: heavier potassium melts lower than lithium.
Mission 3

Alloys

  • An alloy is a mixture of a metal with other elements: brass (copper, zinc), steel (iron, carbon).
  • Metallic bonding is non-directional, so other atoms fit into the lattice. The alloy still conducts.
  • Atoms of a different size disrupt the layers, so they slide less easily.
  • So alloys are usually harder and stronger than the pure metal.
Mission 3

Interstitial and substitutional alloys

  • Interstitial alloy: atoms of significantly different radii. The small atoms fill the spaces (interstices) between the large ones.
  • Example: steel. C atoms (77 pm) sit between Fe atoms (126 pm).
  • Interstitial atoms make the lattice more rigid: less malleable, less ductile.
  • Substitutional alloy: atoms of comparable radius. One atom takes the place of another in the lattice.
  • Example: brass. Zn atoms (134 pm) replace some Cu atoms (128 pm).
  • Both kinds keep a sea of mobile electrons, so they still conduct.
Quick check
?The table gives data for iron and carbon. Steel is an alloy of iron that contains a small amount of carbon. Based on the data, which statement correctly describes the arrangement of atoms in steel, with a valid reason?
Mission 3

Four types of structure

  • Four types of solid: ionic, metallic, covalent network and molecular.
  • Identify the particles and the forces between them. The properties follow.
Mission 3

Molecular solids

  • Molecular solids: separate molecules attracted by weak intermolecular forces.
  • Low melting points: only those weak forces are overcome. The covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Do not conduct: valence electrons are held in bonds and lone pairs within each molecule.
  • Some are made of very large molecules or polymers, e.g. polyethylene.

Mission 3

Bonding is a continuum

  • Ionic, covalent and metallic are models. Real bonds usually lie in between.
  • Bigger electronegativity difference: more ionic character. Smaller difference: more covalent.
  • Background (not examined on any course here): the bonding triangle plots average electronegativity (x) against difference (y). Corners: metallic, ionic, covalent.
  • NaCl: average 2.05, difference 2.3: near the ionic corner. High melting point, conducts when molten.
  • SnCl4: average 2.6, difference 1.2: polar covalent. Small molecules: liquid, no ions, no conduction.
Mission check

?Each liquid is tested with two electrodes and a bulb. Which liquid will not conduct electricity?

Tin metal has delocalized electrons. SnCl4, like TiCl4, is simple molecular: electrons are held in bonds inside molecules and there are no ions, so neither liquid conducts.

Mission 4

Electron pairs repel

  • Count the electron domains around the central atom: each bond (single, double or triple) or lone pair is one domain.
  • They repel, so they get as far apart as possible: VSEPR (valence shell electron pair repulsion).
  • 2 domains: linear, 180°. 3: trigonal planar, 120°. 4: tetrahedral, 109.5°.
  • BeCl2 is linear, BCl3 trigonal planar, CH4 tetrahedral.
Mission 4

  • Molecular geometry: where the atoms are.

Mission 4

What sets the angles

  • Lone pairs sit closer to the central atom, so they repel more than bonding pairs.
  • Repulsion: lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bonding pair > bonding pair–bonding pair.
  • Each lone pair squeezes the bond angles by about 2.5°.
  • CH4 109.5° → NH3 about 107° → H2O about 104.5°.
  • NH4⁺ has four bonding pairs, no lone pair: 109.5°, like CH4.
  • A double bond holds more electron density and repels more: ethene's H–C–H angle is about 117°.

Mission 4

Five and six pairs

  • From period 3 on, a central atom can hold more than eight outer electrons: an expanded octet.
  • Period 2 atoms (B, C, N, O, F) can't.
  • 5 pairs: trigonal bipyramidal. PCl5: three equatorial Cl at 120°, two axial Cl at 90° to them.
  • 6 pairs: octahedral. SF6: every F–S–F angle 90°.
Mission 4

  • Five pairs: lone pairs go equatorial, where they have fewer neighbors at 90°.
  • Six pairs: two lone pairs sit opposite each other, 180° apart.

Mission 5: A double bond shares twice as many electrons as a single bond. So why isn't it twice as strong?

Make a guess: A double bond shares twice as many electrons as a single bond. So why isn't it twice as strong?

Mission 5

Sigma bonds

  • σ bond: direct (head-on) overlap of orbitals between two nuclei.
  • Electron density lies on the axis between the nuclei.
  • Every single bond is a σ bond: all C–C and C–H bonds in alkanes.
  • Groups can rotate freely about a σ bond (free rotation).
Mission 5

Pi bonds

  • π bond: sideways overlap of two p orbitals, one on each atom.
  • Electron density lies above and below the axis, not on it.
  • Double bond = 1σ + 1π. Triple bond = 1σ + 2π.
  • The π bond stops rotation: groups on each C are fixed in place.
  • No rotation about the π bond: this allows geometric (cis-trans) isomers.
  • Each C of C=C: three bonding regions, trigonal planar, about 120°.
Quick check
?The Lewis diagram of acrylonitrile, C3H3N, is shown. How many sigma bonds and how many pi bonds are in one molecule of acrylonitrile?
Mission 5

Why C=C is shorter and stronger, but not twice as strong

  • C–C 154 pm, 347 kJ/mol. C=C 134 pm, 612 kJ/mol (average bond enthalpies).
  • Two shared pairs pull the nuclei closer: C=C is shorter and stronger than C–C.
  • Higher bond order: shorter and stronger bond.
  • But not twice as strong: the π bond is weaker than the σ bond (612 − 347 ≈ 265).
  • Sideways overlap of p orbitals is less effective than head-on overlap.

Mission 5

Resonance

  • If a double bond can be drawn in more than one equivalent place, each drawing is a resonance structure.
  • Atoms stay put; only electrons are redrawn. Structures are linked by ↔.
  • NO3⁻: three resonance structures. The real ion is one resonance hybrid: it never flips between drawings.
  • Its π electrons are delocalized: all three N–O bonds are identical, between single and double.
  • Evidence: in O3 both O–O bonds are 128 pm, between O–O (148 pm) and O=O (121 pm).
  • Resonance is needed to predict bond lengths and properties correctly.
Mission 5

Formal charge and the limits of Lewis diagrams

  • Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − ½ × bonding electrons.
  • Best Lewis diagram: octets obeyed, formal charges closest to zero, any negative charge on the more electronegative atom.
  • CO2: O=C=O gives 0, 0, 0. O≡C–O gives +1, 0, −1. So O=C=O is better.
  • Formal charges sum to the overall charge of the species.
  • Odd-electron species (NO, 11 e⁻; NO2, 17 e⁻) can't give every atom an octet. The Lewis model is only approximate here.
Quick check

Mission check

Mission 6

Stereoisomers and restricted rotation

  • Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space.
  • A C=C is a σ bond plus a π bond. The π bond is a sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the plane.
  • Twisting about the C=C would break the π bond: a large energy barrier. So there is no rotation.
  • Groups are locked on the same side or on opposite sides of the C=C.
  • This needs two different groups on each C=C carbon. Propene, CH2=CHCH3, has none: one carbon carries two H.
  • These are geometric isomers.
Mission 6

Naming cis and trans

  • cis: matching groups on the same side of the C=C.
  • trans: matching groups on opposite sides.
  • ClHC=CHCl: cis-1,2-dichloroethene and trans-1,2-dichloroethene.
  • Same atoms, same bonds, but two different compounds: they cannot turn into each other.

Mission 6

Background: mirror-image molecules

  • Background for the video: beyond the AP course.
  • A carbon bonded to four different groups is chiral. Its molecule has two mirror-image forms that cannot be superimposed.
  • Each form rotates plane-polarized light: by equal angles, in opposite directions.
  • A racemic mixture (50 : 50) shows no rotation: the effects cancel.
  • Mirror-image forms have the same mass and boiling point. They differ only in a chiral setting.
  • Your smell receptors are chiral, so the two carvones smell different: spearmint and caraway.
Mission check

?cis-1,2-Dichloroethene boils at about 60 °C; trans-1,2-dichloroethene boils at about 48 °C. Both are ClHC=CHCl. Which explains why they are two separate compounds with different boiling points?

Same atoms, same bonds, different arrangement in space: different compounds with different properties.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons; charges balance to zero in the formula.
  • A giant lattice of fixed ions: high melting point, brittle, conducts only when molten or dissolved.
  • Higher charges and smaller ions → stronger attraction.

Mission 2

  • A covalent bond is the attraction of two nuclei for a shared pair; in a co-ordinate bond one atom supplies both electrons.
  • More shared pairs: shorter, stronger bond. N≡N is why nitrogen is so unreactive.
  • The potential-energy minimum gives bond length (position) and bond energy (depth).

Mission 3

  • Metallic bonding: attraction between cations and delocalized electrons; non-directional, so metals conduct, are malleable and (usually) melt high.
  • To conduct, a substance needs mobile charged particles. Decide the structure, then explain the property.
  • Interstitial alloys (small atoms in gaps, e.g. steel) are more rigid; substitutional alloys (similar radii, e.g. brass) swap atoms. Both conduct.

Mission 4

  • Count every pair (or domain) around the central atom, lone pairs included; they spread as far apart as possible.
  • The shape names only the atoms. Lone pairs repel most and close up the bond angles.

Mission 5

  • σ: head-on overlap, on the axis. π: sideways p overlap, above and below; weaker; stops rotation. Double = σ + π; triple = σ + 2π.
  • 4, 3, 2 domains → sp³, sp², sp labels; resonance hybrids; best Lewis diagram has formal charges nearest zero.

Mission 6

  • A pi bond blocks rotation about C=C, giving cis and trans (geometric) isomers.
