Mission 1

Mission 1: Why did a Guru ask a huge crowd which of them would give him their head?

Make a guess: Why did a Guru ask a huge crowd which of them would give him their head?

Mission 1

Before 1699: a harvest festival

  • Vaisakhi falls in April (usually 13 or 14 April). In Punjab it marks the spring harvest and the solar new year.
  • Guru Amar Das (3rd Guru) asked Sikhs to gather at Goindwal at Vaisakhi, to meet the Guru and each other.
  • So it was already a Sikh gathering day before 1699.
  • For Sikhs today, its main meaning comes from what happened at Anandpur in 1699.
Mission 1

Anandpur, 1699: the call

  • Guru Gobind Singh called Sikhs to Anandpur for Vaisakhi. Sikhs had suffered persecution; his father, Guru Tegh Bahadur, had been executed (1675).
  • Sikh accounts tell the story like this. Before the huge gathering he drew his sword and asked: who will give his head for the Guru?
  • After a silence, one man stood. The Guru took him into a tent and came out with a bloodied sword.
  • He asked again, and again: five men in all came forward.
  • Then the Guru brought all five out, alive, dressed alike in new clothes.
  • Sikhs call them the panj pyare (the five beloved ones).
Mission 1Read the source

Gurbilas Patshahi 10

Gurbilas Patshahi 10 (an 18th-century account of the tenth Guru's life) (paraphrase):

  • The Guru stands before the assembly with a drawn sword and asks for a Sikh ready to offer his head.
  • One by one, five volunteers go with him into the tent; each time the Guru returns with his sword.
  • The five are brought out in fine clothes; the Guru prepares amrit and makes them his Khalsa.
  • The Guru then asks the five to give amrit to him, so that the Guru himself joins the Khalsa.

Q1 What did the test show about the five?

Show answer

They were ready to give everything, even their lives, for the Guru.

Q2 Why is it significant that the Guru received amrit from the five?

Show answer

It shows Guru and Khalsa as equals: the Guru is also a disciple.

Mission 1

Amrit and the birth of the Khalsa

  • The Guru stirred water in an iron bowl with a khanda (double-edged sword) while reciting prayers.
  • Sugar sweets were added: Khalsa courage should come with sweetness and kindness.
  • The five drank amrit from the one bowl: a break with caste rules.
  • Sikh accounts say the Guru then asked the five to give amrit to him: Guru and Khalsa stand as equals.
  • Men took the name Singh (lion), women Kaur (princess).
  • They became the Khalsa ("pure", belonging to God), keeping the Five Ks and a strict code of life (more in Mission 4).

Mission 1

Why Vaisakhi matters

  • It celebrates the birth of the Khalsa and a Sikh identity that can be seen (the Five Ks, the turban).
  • Commitment: the panj pyare show complete dedication to God and the Guru.
  • Equality: different castes drank from one bowl and shared one name.
  • Courage: standing up for faith and for others' freedom, as Guru Tegh Bahadur had done.
  • Renewal: many Sikhs choose Vaisakhi to take amrit themselves, or to renew their commitment.
Mission 1

How Sikhs celebrate

  • Akhand Path ending on Vaisakhi morning; kirtan and talks on the events of 1699; Ardas; karah prashad; langar.
  • Nishan Sahib: the flagpole is taken down and washed, and dressed with a new cloth and flag.
  • Amrit ceremonies: led by five Khalsa Sikhs acting as panj pyare.
  • Nagar kirtan ("town singing"): a street procession led by five Sikhs as panj pyare, with the Guru Granth Sahib on a decorated float, kirtan, and displays of gatka (Sikh martial art).
  • Along the route, Sikhs serve free food and drink to everyone, including passers-by.
  • Gurdwaras are decorated; families wear new clothes and visit relatives.

Mission 1Diversity

India and Britain

  • In Punjab, India: a public holiday; harvest fairs, music and bhangra; very large gatherings at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, at Anandpur Sahib, and at Damdama Sahib (Talwandi Sabo).
  • In Britain: usually celebrated at the nearest weekend, since 13/14 April is often a working or school day.
  • British cities with large Sikh communities hold big nagar kirtans; some city councils host public Vaisakhi events.
  • In Britain the religious meaning (the Khalsa) is often more to the fore; the harvest side matters less away from Punjab's farms.
  • In Britain, Vaisakhi is also a chance to explain Sikhism to neighbours of other faiths.
Mission 1Diversity

Different understandings

  • Amritdhari Sikhs (initiated) often see Vaisakhi as a day of commitment: renewing their vows, or helping others take amrit.
  • Sahajdhari Sikhs (not initiated) celebrate fully too, while some feel not yet ready to take amrit.
  • Festival or remembrance? Some see Vaisakhi as a gurpurb-like celebration of the Khalsa's birth; others stress the harvest and new-year festival shared with Punjabi neighbours of all faiths.
  • Some Sikhs worry that music, fairs and parties overshadow the religious meaning; others say joy and community are part of the festival.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Vaisakhi is the most important Sikh festival."

  • Reasons for: it marks the birth of the Khalsa, which shapes Sikh identity today; many take amrit then; it is the largest public Sikh celebration in Britain.
  • Reasons against: Guru Nanak's birthday celebrates the founder of the faith; martyrdom days (Guru Arjan, Guru Tegh Bahadur) and Bandi Chhor Divas carry deep meaning; Sikhs believe no single day matters more than daily nam japna and sewa.
  • Another view: Sikhs weigh this differently: some give Guru Nanak's gurpurb the greater weight; many amritdhari Sikhs feel Vaisakhi most strongly.
  • Judge it by: What is being remembered? Which festival most shapes Sikh life afterwards? Are festivals more important than daily practice?

Mission 2

Gurpurbs and melas

  • Gurpurb: "the Guru's day". Mostly the birth or death anniversary of one of the Gurus.
  • The death of a Guru killed for the faith is a martyrdom (shaheedi) day.
  • Mela: a "fair" or gathering. Examples: Vaisakhi, Divali, Hola Mohalla (a festival of martial skills begun by Guru Gobind Singh at Anandpur).
  • Many melas fall on dates also kept by Hindus. Guru Amar Das asked Sikhs to gather around the Guru at Vaisakhi and Divali, giving those days a Sikh meaning.
  • Common practices: an akhand path (a continuous 48-hour reading of the Guru Granth Sahib) ending on the day, kirtan (hymn-singing), decorations, karah prashad (blessed sweet food) and langar (free shared meal).
Mission 2

Bandi Chhor: the Guru who freed prisoners

  • Guru Hargobind, the sixth Guru, was held in Gwalior Fort by the Mughal emperor Jahangir (early 1600s).
  • Jahangir agreed to free him. The Guru refused to go unless 52 Hindu princes (rajas) held there were freed too.
  • Jahangir said: only those who can hold on to your cloak may leave.
  • The Guru had a cloak made with 52 tassels. Each prince held one and walked out with him.
  • He reached Amritsar at Divali time; the Harmandir Sahib was lit with lamps to welcome him.
  • He became known as Bandi Chhor: "the one who frees prisoners". Bandi Chhor Divas: "day of release".

Mission 2

Divali for Sikhs

  • Same time of year as the Hindu Divali (October or November), but a different story.
  • Hindus remember, for example, Rama's return to Ayodhya; Sikhs remember Guru Hargobind's release.
  • At the Harmandir Sahib: the buildings and pool are lit with lamps; fireworks over Amritsar.
  • In gurdwaras: kirtan, Ardas (the community prayer), karah prashad, langar.
  • At home: lamps or candles, sweets and gifts with family and friends.
  • Many Sikhs also remember Bhai Mani Singh, a Sikh scholar executed in Lahore in the 1730s after a dispute over a Divali gathering at Amritsar.
  • Some Sikhs prefer the name Bandi Chhor Divas, to show the Sikh meaning; others say Divali and share the season with Hindu neighbours.
Mission 2

Guru Nanak's birthday

  • Guru Nanak was born in 1469 at Talwandi, now Nankana Sahib in Pakistan.
  • Most Sikhs celebrate at the full moon of the month of Kartik (usually November).
  • An akhand path ends on the morning of the gurpurb.
  • Nagar kirtan: a street procession singing hymns. The Guru Granth Sahib is carried on a decorated float, led by five Sikhs (panj pyare) carrying the Nishan Sahib (Sikh flag).
  • Gurdwaras are lit up; there are talks on the Guru's life, kirtan through the night and langar for everyone.
  • Many pilgrims travel to Nankana Sahib for the day.
  • Meaning: thanks for the Guru who first taught the Sikh path: one God, equality, honest work, sharing.
Mission 2

Martyrdoms: Guru Arjan and Guru Tegh Bahadur

  • Guru Arjan (fifth Guru) compiled the Adi Granth and built the Harmandir Sahib.
  • In 1606 he was tortured and killed in Lahore on the orders of Jahangir: Sikhs remember him seated on a burning-hot iron plate.
  • His martyrdom day falls in early summer. Sikhs set up chhabeel stalls giving cold sweet drinks to anyone passing: kindness answers cruelty.
  • Guru Tegh Bahadur (ninth Guru) was beheaded in Delhi in 1675 on the orders of the emperor Aurangzeb.
  • He had stood up for Hindu Pandits from Kashmir who were being forced to convert: he died defending other people's freedom of religion.
  • Gurdwara Sis Ganj Sahib in Delhi marks the place. His day falls in late November or December.
  • Martyrdom days are kept more quietly: kirtan, an akhand path, talks on the Guru's sacrifice, langar.
Mission 2Read the source

"He gave his head"

Guru Gobind Singh (Guru Tegh Bahadur's son) wrote about his father in the Bachittar Natak (paraphrase):

  • He protected the forehead mark and sacred thread of the Hindus (their right to their own religion).
  • He gave his head, but did not give up his faith; he gave his life, but not his resolve.
  • Many Sikhs treat this poem as an authoritative account; its place in the Dasam Granth is debated by some scholars.

Mission 2Diversity

Same festival, different places and views

  • In India (Punjab): Guru Nanak's birthday is a public holiday; huge crowds at historic gurdwaras; pilgrimages to Nankana Sahib and Amritsar.
  • In Britain: celebrations often move to the nearest weekend, because of school and work.
  • British nagar kirtans go through town streets (e.g. Southall, Birmingham, Leicester), with police and council planning.
  • British gurdwaras often open langar to neighbours and invite schools: a chance to share Sikh values and teach children their heritage.
  • Dates differ too: some Sikhs follow the Nanakshahi calendar (fixed dates), others the traditional lunar dates.
  • Different understandings: for some, gurpurbs mainly remember history; for others they are chances for spiritual renewal through the Guru's word. Some criticise fireworks and spending as distractions from the Gurus' message.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Festivals are the most important part of Sikh life."

  • Reasons for: festivals keep the Gurus' stories alive for the next generation; they gather the sangat (community); nagar kirtans and langar show Sikh values in public; martyrdoms inspire courage and justice.
  • Reasons against: the Gurus stressed daily devotion: Nam Japna (meditating on God's name), honest work and sharing; a festival once a year cannot replace this. Some see too much spending and fireworks. Guru Nanak criticised empty ritual.
  • Another view: festivals and daily life support each other: the festival renews the commitment that daily life keeps.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus themselves teach about ritual? What keeps faith strong in a minority community such as Britain? Is "important" about frequency or about impact?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, story, meaning

Question: Explain two reasons why Divali is important to Sikhs.

  • Reason 1: It remembers Guru Hargobind's release from Gwalior Fort. Development: he would not leave without the 52 princes, so Sikhs see in it a duty to stand up for other people's freedom, not only their own.
  • Reason 2: It brings the sangat together at the gurdwara. Development: lamps, kirtan, karah prashad and langar renew faith together and pass the story on to children, which matters for Sikhs living as a minority in Britain.
  • (c) tip: two reasons, each developed, plus a source of wisdom and authority (e.g. the Bandi Chhor account of Guru Hargobind's life).

Mission 3: How can a Sikh baby's name come from a page of scripture opened at random?

Mission 3

Welcoming a baby

  • Sikhs see a child as a gift from God, born by God's will (hukam).
  • In many families, soon after birth, the Mool Mantar (the opening words of the Guru Granth Sahib) or "Waheguru" (Wonderful Lord) is whispered in the baby's ear: the first words the child hears are about God.
  • Some families give a drop of sweetened water (amrit), stirred with a kirpan, or of honey.
  • The family visits the gurdwara for the naming once the mother is well. There is no fixed day.
  • The Sikh code rejects the idea that birth makes a home "impure" for a set number of days.
Mission 3

Naam Karan, step by step

  • Naam Karan: "naming ceremony", in the gurdwara with the sangat (congregation).
  • The family brings karah prashad (sweet blessed food) and often a rumala (cloth) to cover the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Hymns of joy and thanks are sung.
  • The Guru Granth Sahib is opened at random and a hymn is read: the hukamnama, the Guru's "order" or guidance for that moment.
  • The first letter of the first word of the hymn is announced. A name is chosen beginning with that letter.
  • The name is announced and the sangat agrees, often with the shout "Jo bole so nihal, Sat Sri Akal".
  • Singh is added for a boy, Kaur for a girl.
  • Closing: six verses of Anand Sahib, Ardas (prayer) giving thanks, karah prashad shared; often langar.
Mission 3Read the source

The Sikh code of conduct

The Sikh Rahit Maryada (the Sikh code of conduct, approved by the Shiromani Gurdwara Parbandhak Committee in 1945), on birth and naming (paraphrase):

  • Chapter 11 (Article XVII) of the Rahit Maryada.
  • Go to the gurdwara when the mother is able to move about and bathe; the number of days does not matter.
  • Take the hukam by opening the Guru Granth Sahib. The granthi proposes a name beginning with the first letter of the hymn; it is announced once the sangat accepts it.
  • Add Singh to a boy's name and Kaur to a girl's.
  • Do not hold that birth causes ritual pollution: birth and death happen by God's will.

Mission 3

What the ceremony means

  • Guided by the Guru: the name comes from the Guru's word, so from the start the child's life is placed under the Guru's guidance.
  • Thanks to God: the child is God's gift, not something the family made alone.
  • Belonging: the sangat welcomes the child into the Sikh community.
  • Equality: the same ceremony for girls and boys; most Sikh names (e.g. Harpreet, Jaspreet) are used for both.
  • No superstition: no horoscopes, no lucky days, no "impure" period.
  • Many families celebrate with sweets and gifts for relatives and friends.
Mission 3

Singh and Kaur: where they come from

  • At Vaisakhi 1699, Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa (the community of initiated Sikhs).
  • He gave the men the name Singh ("lion") and the women Kaur (usually translated "princess").
  • In India, family surnames often showed caste. Using Singh or Kaur instead hides caste: all are equal members of one family, the Guru's.
  • Lion: courage to stand for truth and protect the weak.
  • Princess: dignity and royalty for every woman, whatever her birth.
  • A Kaur keeps her name for life; she does not have to take her husband's name.

Mission 3Diversity

Singh, Kaur and naming today

  • Most Sikhs receive Singh or Kaur at the naming ceremony.
  • Use varies in Britain: some use Singh or Kaur as their surname; many add a family name (e.g. Sandhu, Gill), with Singh or Kaur as a middle name.
  • Some Sikhs argue that family names bring caste back in, against the Guru's aim. Others say a family name is practical for official forms and shows family heritage, not caste pride.
  • Some Sikh women in Britain take a husband's surname; others keep Kaur, as the Rahit Maryada expects.
  • Khalsa (amritdhari) families tend to follow the Rahit Maryada closely: hukam letter, no superstition.
  • Some non-Khalsa families add Punjabi customs alongside, such as celebrating a first Lohri (winter festival) for a new baby; many now hold it for daughters as well as sons.
  • At Amrit Sanskar (a later mission), anyone without Singh or Kaur takes the name.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Singh and Kaur still matter for Sikh identity today."

  • Reasons for: they come from Guru Gobind Singh and mark belonging to the Khalsa family; they reject caste; Kaur gives women independent identity; a name is a daily reminder of courage and dignity.
  • Reasons against: many Sikhs now use family names as surnames, so Singh and Kaur often appear only as middle names; identity is shown more by belief and action (Nam Japna, sewa) than by a name; "Singh" is also used by some non-Sikhs in India, so it does not always identify a Sikh.
  • Judge it by: What did the Guru intend? Do names change how people treat each other? Is identity mainly inner or outward?
Mission 3Worked answer

Practice, meaning, source

Question: Explain two ways the naming ceremony is significant for Sikhs.

  • Point 2: Singh or Kaur is added. Development: this shows the child is an equal member of the Guru's family with no caste, as Guru Gobind Singh intended in 1699.

Mission 4

The Khalsa

  • Khalsa: "pure", or "belonging directly to the Guru". The community of initiated Sikhs.
  • Founded by Guru Gobind Singh, the tenth Guru, at Vaisakhi 1699 in Anandpur.
  • Five Sikhs offered their lives when he asked; they became the panj pyare ("five beloved ones") and the first to receive amrit (sweetened water).
  • Then the Guru asked them to give amrit to him: the Guru and the Khalsa are equal.
  • Ideal: the sant-sipahi (saint-soldier): devoted to God, ready to defend the weak.
Mission 4

The Five Ks

  • Kesh: uncut hair, usually covered by a turban (dastar) or scarf. Acceptance of God's creation as it is.
  • Kangha: small wooden comb. Cleanliness and order in body and life.
  • Kara: steel bracelet. One God without beginning or end; a reminder to use the hands for good.
  • Kachera: cotton shorts worn as underwear. Self-control and readiness for action.
  • Kirpan: small sword. Duty to defend the weak and stand for justice; never for attack.
  • Together: a uniform of identity and commitment, worn at all times by initiated Sikhs.
Quick check

Mission 4

Before the ceremony

  • Amrit Sanskar: the initiation ceremony (also Khande di Pahul). It is a choice, not a birth rite.
  • Open to anyone, male or female, of any background, old enough to understand the promises.
  • Held before the Guru Granth Sahib, often at Vaisakhi; in a gurdwara or another quiet place.
  • Five amritdhari Sikhs lead it, acting as the panj pyare; another reads from the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Candidates bathe, wash their hair, and come wearing the Five Ks, with head covered. They wear no symbols of other faiths.
Mission 4

The ceremony

  • Water and patashas (sugar crystals) are placed in an iron bowl.
  • The panj pyare stir it with a khanda (double-edged sword) while reciting five prayers (banis), including Japji Sahib and Anand Sahib.
  • Each person receives amrit five times to drink, five times sprinkled on the eyes, five times on the hair. Each time: "Waheguru ji ka Khalsa, Waheguru ji ki Fateh" ("The Khalsa belongs to God; victory belongs to God").
  • All then drink from the same bowl: no caste, no rank.
  • The Mool Mantar is recited together.
  • The rules (rahit) are explained: daily prayers, the Five Ks, honest living; never cut hair, eat meat killed by ritual slaughter, commit adultery or use tobacco.
  • Ardas, a hukamnama, karah prashad. Anyone without Singh or Kaur takes it now.
Mission 4Read the source

One with the Guru

  • Rahit Maryada, chapter 13 (Article XXIV) (paraphrase): any man or woman who believes in the Sikh faith and promises to live by it may be initiated; five Khalsa Sikhs prepare the amrit with the five banis; the initiated are told they are now children of the Guru, with Guru Gobind Singh as father and Mata Sahib Kaur as mother.

Mission 4

Why take amrit?

  • Commitment: a public promise to live fully by the Guru's teaching.
  • New birth: joining the Guru's family; Anandpur counted as the initiate's spiritual birthplace.
  • Equality and identity: one family, one uniform, no caste.
  • Discipline: daily prayers (Nitnem) and the rahit shape the whole of life.
  • Service: as a sant-sipahi, to serve and protect others.
  • For families: a joyful day; parents and children sometimes take amrit together; it can strengthen family faith, though some young people feel pressure, and the Rahit Maryada stresses it must be a free choice.
Mission 4Diversity

Amritdhari and sahajdhari

  • Amritdhari: initiated, members of the Khalsa; keep all Five Ks and the rahit.
  • Keshdhari: keep uncut hair (often a turban) but have not taken amrit.
  • Sahajdhari: "on the gradual path": believe in the Gurus and the Guru Granth Sahib but have not taken amrit and may cut their hair.
  • Amritdhari perspective: the Khalsa is the Guru's command; taking amrit completes a Sikh's commitment; the Five Ks are a uniform, not optional symbols.
  • Sahajdhari perspective: Guru Nanak taught inner devotion, Nam Japna and honest living; outward symbols without a pure heart are empty; many hope to take amrit when ready.
  • Many non-initiated Sikhs still wear a kara or keep kesh.
Mission 4Evaluate

"A Sikh must take amrit to be a true Sikh."

  • Reasons for: Guru Gobind Singh created the Khalsa as the complete form of the Sikh path; the Rahit Maryada expects Sikhs to take amrit, and its definition of a Sikh includes belief in the tenth Guru's amrit; the Five Ks show commitment openly.
  • Reasons against: many Sikhs read that definition as asking for belief in amrit, not for having already taken it; Guru Nanak's followers were Sikhs long before 1699; amrit taken without sincerity means little.
  • Another view: Sikhism is a journey: sahajdharis are on the path, amrit is its goal.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus command? Does outward commitment guarantee inner devotion? Who decides who counts as a "true" Sikh?
Mission 4Worked answer

Two different views

Question: Explain two different Sikh views about the importance of the Five Ks.

  • View 1 (amritdhari): the Five Ks are essential. Development: they are part of the promise made at Amrit Sanskar, a uniform given by Guru Gobind Singh, so removing them would break that commitment.
  • View 2 (sahajdhari): what matters most is inner devotion. Development: Guru Nanak taught Nam Japna and honest living, so a Sikh may follow the Gurus sincerely before taking amrit.
Mission check

?An amritdhari Sikh nurse is told to remove her kara for hand hygiene. She asks instead to push it up her arm and tape it during patient care. Which belief best explains her request?

Amrit Sanskar turns belief into a lifelong, visible promise; Sikhs differ on whether that promise is the start or the goal of the path.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Vaisakhi: a Punjabi harvest festival, a Sikh gathering since Guru Amar Das, and the birth of the Khalsa at Anandpur in 1699.
  • Panj pyare, amrit from one iron bowl, Singh and Kaur: commitment, courage and equality.
  • Gurbilas Patshahi 10 gives the account of the call for heads and the first amrit.
  • Celebrated with Akhand Path, a new Nishan Sahib, amrit ceremonies and nagar kirtan; in Britain at weekends, with a stronger religious focus.

Mission 2

  • Gurpurbs mark the Gurus' births and martyrdoms; melas such as Divali are gatherings given a Sikh meaning.
  • Divali / Bandi Chhor Divas remembers Guru Hargobind freeing himself and 52 princes; Guru Nanak's birthday is marked with an akhand path and nagar kirtan.
  • Guru Arjan (1606) and Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675) are remembered as martyrs for truth and freedom of religion.

Mission 3

  • Naam Karan: in the gurdwara, the Guru Granth Sahib is opened at random; the first letter of the hukamnama guides the name.
  • Singh ("lion") and Kaur ("princess"), given since 1699, show equality and reject caste.
  • How Sikhs use Singh, Kaur and family names today varies, and is debated.

Mission 4

  • The Khalsa began at Vaisakhi 1699; the Five Ks are kesh, kangha, kara, kachera, kirpan.
  • Amrit Sanskar: amrit stirred with a khanda by five Khalsa Sikhs, shared from one bowl; initiates promise to keep the rahit.
  • Amritdhari Sikhs see amrit as the Guru's command; sahajdhari Sikhs stress inner devotion and a gradual path.
