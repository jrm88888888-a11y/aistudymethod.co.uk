The Sign of Four · structure and the detective storyScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1 Section B: one question on the novel. You write about a printed extract, then about the novel as a whole. Usually 30 marks.
  • Marks reward your response and quotations (AO1), methods of language, form and structure (AO2) and context (AO3).
  • Closed book: learn your key quotations. Essay practice is for class.
  • AQA calls the novel The Sign of Four, the title of the 1890 book; the magazine and some editions call it The Sign of the Four.
The Sign of Four · structure and the detective storyScreen 3

The rules of the game

  • Edgar Allan Poe's story The Murders in the Rue Morgue (1841) set the pattern: a genius detective, a friend who narrates, a baffled police force, a locked room.
  • Doyle keeps every convention: Holmes, Watson as narrator, Athelney Jones of Scotland Yard, a room locked "on the inside" (ch 5).
  • Clues are laid out for the reader: a wooden-leg print, tiny bare footprints, a poisoned thorn.
  • The detective explains last: "You know my methods," Holmes tells Watson (ch 6).

The Sign of Four · structure and the detective storyScreen 7

The crime's own story

  • By chapter 10 we know who and how. Chapter 12 gives the why.
  • Small's embedded narrative: his own story told inside Watson's, from the 1857 Indian Rebellion to the night at Norwood.
  • Holmes says there is "nothing at all new to me in the latter part of your narrative, except that you brought your own rope" (ch 12).
  • Chapter 11 closes the third strand: the empty box and Watson's proposal.
Mission checkScreen 8

?The thieves are caught in chapter 10. Why are there two more?

The detective plot ends with the capture, but the treasure and romance plots end in chapter 11 and the crime's cause lies decades back. Chapter 12 lets Small tell how the treasure was taken at Agra in 1857, which explains every clue the London chapters raised.

Mission 2

Why does Holmes's story open and close with cocaine?

Make a guess: Why does Holmes's story open and close with cocaine?

The Sign of Four · HolmesScreen 1

The first page

  • Holmes is using cocaine, "a seven-per-cent. solution", three times a day for months (ch 1).
  • In 1890 cocaine was legal in Britain and sold by chemists in tonics and medicines; it was not tightly controlled until 1916.
  • Watson objects as "a medical man": it is "a pathological and morbid process".
  • Holmes's reply: "My mind," he said, "rebels at stagnation."

The Sign of Four · HolmesScreen 4

The reasoning machine

  • Observation then deduction: red earth on Watson's shoe means Wigmore Street; the stamps in his desk mean a telegram (ch 1).
  • From a watch, he reads Watson's brother's life: "I never guess."
  • Watson: "You really are an automaton,—a calculating-machine!" (ch 2).
  • Holmes: "A client is to me a mere unit,—a factor in a problem."
  • He also uses chemistry, disguise, a dog and the Baker Street Irregulars.
The Sign of Four · HolmesScreen 5

Brilliant, and flawed

  • Mood swings: "the blackest depression" between cases (ch 3).
  • Vanity: Watson notes his "egotism" over the brochure (ch 1).
  • Coldness about women: "Women are never to be entirely trusted,—not the best of them" (ch 9). Watson calls it "this atrocious sentiment".
  • Not infallible: Toby leads them to the wrong barrel (ch 7).
  • Last line: "there still remains the cocaine-bottle" (ch 12).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does Holmes's story open and close with cocaine?

The cocaine frames the case. Holmes says he can do without "artificial stimulants" when he has a problem; with none, life is "commonplace". Doyle presents a brilliant mind that needs puzzles, and a flaw that Watson, as a doctor, can see clearly.

Mission 3

Why is Watson glad when the treasure box is empty?

Make a guess: Why is Watson glad when the treasure box is empty?

The Sign of Four · Watson and Mary MorstanScreen 1

Watson, the narrator

  • An army doctor wounded by "a Jezail bullet" in the Afghan campaign (ch 1); now "a half-pay surgeon" (ch 7).
  • He tells the story retrospectively: "as she has often told me" (ch 5) hints at the future.
  • Holmes mocks his writing for adding "romanticism" to "an exact science" (ch 1).
  • So the romance plot is Watson's: he notices what Holmes ignores.
The Sign of Four · Watson and Mary MorstanScreen 2

Mary Morstan

  • A governess to Mrs. Cecil Forrester; her father, Captain Morstan, vanished in 1878 (ch 2).
  • Watson sees "a firm step and an outward composure"; Holmes calls her "a model client".
  • Watson also writes of "the angelic fashion of women" (ch 7): a Victorian ideal of the gentle, self-sacrificing woman.
  • Some readers see her as mainly an object of Watson's love; others point to her calm, her judgement, and Holmes's view that she "had a decided genius that way" (ch 12).

Honour, money and class

  • Watson calls himself "an army surgeon with a weak leg and a weaker banking-account" (ch 2) and "a half-pay surgeon" (ch 7).
  • His fear: Mary might think him "a mere vulgar fortune-seeker".
  • A gentleman's honour meant not using a woman's weakness or wealth to his advantage.

?Why is Watson glad when the treasure box is empty?

While Mary might become "the richest heiress in England", Watson, a half-pay surgeon, would not court her in case she thought him "a mere vulgar fortune-seeker". The empty box removes "the golden barrier", so he can say he loves her. Doyle's romance plot sets love above money.

Doyle gives no clean owner. The rajah hid it to back both sides, the four murdered for it, and Sholto broke his word for it. The jewels end scattered in the Thames, and Watson calls it "the ill-omened treasure of the Sholtos". Argue about the claims, but show how each one is stained.

Mission 5

Does the danger come from abroad, or from Britain's own men?

Make a guess: Does the danger come from abroad, or from Britain's own men?

The Sign of Four · empire and IndiaScreen 1

Empire comes home

  • In 1890 India was under British Crown rule; before 1858 the East India Company governed British India.
  • Major Sholto and Captain Morstan were officers "of the 34th Bombay Infantry" (ch 2).
  • Sholto came back rich, with "a staff of native servants" (ch 4), to a house called Pondicherry Lodge.
  • Thaddeus's room suggests "Eastern luxury" (ch 4): India as treasure and décor.
  • Watson's own wound is from the Afghan campaign (ch 1).
The Sign of Four · empire and IndiaScreen 2

1857 through Small's eyes

  • The Indian Rebellion began on 10 May 1857 at Meerut, as a mutiny of sepoys, Indian soldiers in the Company's army.
  • Both sides killed civilians; British reprisals were brutal, including against whole villages.
  • Small's account (ch 12) is one-sided: he uses racist, dehumanising names for the rebels.
  • Yet he admits they were "our own picked troops, whom we had taught and trained".
  • After 1857 the British built a convict settlement at Port Blair in the Andaman Islands: where Small, Sholto and Morstan meet.

The Sign of Four · empire and IndiaScreen 5

How the novel presents Tonga

  • Tonga, from the Andaman Islands, never speaks for himself. Others define him.
  • Holmes trusts a gazetteer, "the very latest authority" (ch 8). Its "cannibal" claims echo colonial shipwreck stories.
  • Watson uses animal imagery (ch 10). Small calls him a "faithful mate", yet exhibited him at fairs, billed as a cannibal, for money (ch 12).
  • He dies unmourned: "that strange visitor to our shores" (ch 10).
  • Arup K. Chatterjee (2020) argues Tonga shows the limits of British imperial anthropology, the "science" that ranked races.

?Does the danger come from abroad, or from Britain's own men?

The threat seems to arrive from abroad, in an Indian treasure and a man from the Andamans, but the novel traces it to greed and betrayal. Abdullah Khan proposes the Agra murder and all four men, Small among them, commit it to "be rich"; then Major Sholto, a British officer, cheats his partners, and the wealth of empire brings fear and death into a London suburb. The strongest answers weigh both and read the novel's imperial attitudes critically.

Mission 6

Small calls his crime justice. Does the novel agree?

Make a guess: Small calls his crime justice. Does the novel agree?

The Sign of Four · crime and justiceScreen 1

The official police

  • Athelney Jones of Scotland Yard: "Stern facts here,—no room for theories" (ch 6).
  • He arrests Thaddeus, the gatekeeper, the housekeeper and the Indian servant (ch 7). Only Lal Rao, the butler, turns out to be involved (ch 12).
  • The Standard praises his "trained and experienced faculties" (ch 8): Doyle's irony.
  • Holmes lets Jones have "all the official credit" (ch 9).
  • A convention since Poe: the private genius outdoes the official police.

The Sign of Four · London describedScreen 5

A city that spreads

  • The new suburbs are "the monster tentacles which the giant city was throwing out into the country" (ch 3): a metaphor of a creature growing.
  • Thaddeus calls his room "An oasis of art in the howling desert of South London" (ch 4).
  • Against this, Mrs. Forrester's house is "a tranquil English home in the midst of the wild, dark business" (ch 7).
  • So safety sits in small, lit, private spaces; danger in the open city.
The Sign of Four · London describedScreen 6

The river and the marshes

  • The chase: the launch's engines "like a great metallic heart" (ch 10).
  • It ends by "the melancholy Plumstead Marshes", with "pools of stagnant water".
  • Tonga's bones lie in "the dark ooze at the bottom of the Thames".
  • The jewels are scattered "over five miles or so" of the river (ch 12).
  • London's river swallows the empire's treasure and the stranger it brought.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does Doyle's London keep vanishing into fog?

Doyle's fog turns the real city into a setting for mystery. Lamps blur, faces "flitted from the gloom into the light", and the weather mirrors Watson's unease. Only Holmes, who names every street as the cab races on, can see through it.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Two stories: the investigation (forward, in London, over a few days) and the crime (back to India in 1857), told last by Small.
  • Doyle follows detective conventions: genius detective, admiring narrator, slow police, locked room, clues, final explanation.
  • Paper 1 Section B starts from an extract and moves out to the whole novel, so know where each chapter sits.
  • Holmes uses cocaine when he has no case; in 1890 it was legal and sold by chemists, but Watson warns of the harm.
  • His method: observation, then deduction; Watson calls him "a calculating-machine".
  • Doyle presents him as brilliant but flawed: moody, vain, cold about women, and not always right.
  • Watson narrates afterwards and adds the "romanticism" Holmes dislikes; the romance plot is his.
  • Mary is presented through Watson's admiring eyes as calm, kind and capable; readers debate how much the novel lets her act.
  • The treasure is a "barrier" (ch 7) and a "golden barrier" (ch 11): when it is lost, love is free to speak.
  • The box travels from a rajah, through a murder at Agra, to the Andamans, to Norwood, to the Thames.
  • Sholto confesses that "cursed greed" was his "besetting sin"; Small says the treasure brought "a curse" on each owner.
  • Every claim is stained: the strongest answers weigh them, not pick a winner without evidence.
  • Context: India under British rule; the 1857 Rebellion; the Andaman convict settlement founded after it.
  • Abdullah Khan's offer, "We ask you to be rich", turns a light on why the British characters are in India.
  • Tonga is presented only through others' words; read that presentation critically, as a product of imperial racial thinking.
  • Official justice is clumsy: Jones arrests the innocent and still gets the credit.
  • Small's "A pretty justice!" speech argues he earned the treasure by suffering; the novel lets him speak.
  • But Small is also a killer, and Watson's horror balances his case: argue both sides with evidence.
  • The fog (ch 1, ch 3) creates mood: pathetic fallacy and personification make the city gloomy and threatening.
  • Doyle's London spreads ("monster tentacles"), while safety is found in small private homes.
  • The Thames chase ends in marshes and "dark ooze", where the treasure and Tonga are lost.

Key quotations to learn

  • "Detection is, or ought to be, an exact science" (Ch I, "The Science of Deduction") Holmes's view of his work, against Watson's romance.
  • "when you have eliminated the impossible whatever remains, however improbable, must be the truth" (Ch VI, "Sherlock Holmes Gives a Demonstration") The locked-room method.
  • "You know my methods. Apply them" (Ch VI, "Sherlock Holmes Gives a Demonstration") The reader is invited to try too.
  • "This is all an insoluble mystery to me" (Ch V, "The Tragedy of Pondicherry Lodge") Watson's puzzlement keeps the suspense.
  • "nothing at all new to me in the latter part of your narrative" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Small's story confirms Holmes's deductions.
  • "My mind … rebels at stagnation." (Ch I, "The Science of Deduction") His need for problems.
  • "I cannot live without brain-work." (Ch I, "The Science of Deduction") Explains the cocaine.
  • "I never guess." (Ch I, "The Science of Deduction") Confidence in his method.
  • "You really are an automaton,—a calculating-machine!" (Ch II, "The Statement of the Case") Watson on his coldness.
  • "A client is to me a mere unit,—a factor in a problem." (Ch II, "The Statement of the Case") Reason over feeling.
  • "there still remains the cocaine-bottle" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") The ending circles back.
  • "a firm step and an outward composure" (Ch II, "The Statement of the Case") First impression of Mary.
  • "After the angelic fashion of women" (Ch VII, "The Episode of the Barrel") Watson's Victorian ideal of women.
  • "a mere vulgar fortune-seeker" (Ch VII, "The Episode of the Barrel") The charge Watson fears.
  • "This Agra treasure intervened like an impassable barrier between us." (Ch VII, "The Episode of the Barrel") Money as an obstacle (simile).
  • "the golden barrier was gone from between us" (Ch XI, "The Great Agra Treasure") The same image, now a metaphor.
  • "Whoever had lost a treasure, I knew that night that I had gained one." (Ch XI, "The Great Agra Treasure") Love outweighs riches.
  • "The cursed greed which has been my besetting sin" (Ch IV, "The Story of the Bald-Headed Man") Sholto's confession.
  • "so blind and foolish a thing is avarice" (Ch IV, "The Story of the Bald-Headed Man") Greed brings no pleasure.
  • "never brought anything but a curse yet upon the man who owned it" (Ch XI, "The Great Agra Treasure") The treasure as a curse.
  • "Look how I have earned it!" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Small's claim through suffering.
  • "It’s a dirty business" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Morstan's own judgement of the bargain.
  • "We only ask you to do that which your countrymen come to this land for. We ask you to be rich." (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Empire and greed linked.
  • "I am as ready to be rich as you can be" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Small agrees at once.
  • "our own picked troops, whom we had taught and trained" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") The rebels were the Company's own soldiers.
  • "the suggestion of Eastern luxury" (Ch IV, "The Story of the Bald-Headed Man") India brought home as décor.
  • "that strange visitor to our shores" (Ch X, "The End of the Islander") Tonga dies an outsider, unmourned.
  • "Stern facts here,—no room for theories" (Ch VI, "Sherlock Holmes Gives a Demonstration") Jones's confident blundering.
  • "You are welcome to all the official credit" (Ch IX, "A Break in the Chain") Holmes and the police.
  • "Justice!" snarled the ex-convict. "A pretty justice!" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Small's challenge to the law.
  • "Look how I have earned it!" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Suffering as a claim.
  • "we cannot tell how far justice may originally have been on your side" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Holmes listens before judging.
  • "the utmost horror of the man" (Ch XII, "The Strange Story of Jonathan Small") Watson's judgement balances Small's.
  • "See how the yellow fog swirls down the street" (Ch I, "The Science of Deduction") Holmes's dull, foggy world.
  • "a dense drizzly fog lay low upon the great city" (Ch III, "In Quest of a Solution") The fog sets the mood.
  • "Mud-coloured clouds drooped sadly over the muddy streets." (Ch III, "In Quest of a Solution") Personification, pathetic fallacy.
  • "they flitted from the gloom into the light, and so back into the gloom once more" (Ch III, "In Quest of a Solution") The crowd as human life.
  • "the monster tentacles which the giant city was throwing out into the country" (Ch III, "In Quest of a Solution") The city as a spreading creature.
  • "a tranquil English home in the midst of the wild, dark business" (Ch VII, "The Episode of the Barrel") Safety in the private home.
