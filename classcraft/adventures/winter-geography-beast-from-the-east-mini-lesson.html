The Beast from the East

3 missions and a finale. Where the cold came from, what it did, and what could be done about it. Guess, learn, then check.

Mission 1

Mission 1: How did Russia's winter air end up over Britain?

Make a guess: How did Russia's winter air end up over Britain?

Mission 1

Britain's usual winter

  • Most winter days, our air arrives from the Atlantic, from the west and south-west.
  • A ribbon of fast wind 5–7 miles up, the jet stream, steers Atlantic weather systems at us like a conveyor belt.
  • The result is mild, wet and windy: tropical maritime and polar maritime air masses dominate.
Mission 1

The wind turns round

  • Late February 2018: a large area of high pressure sat over Scandinavia.
  • Air circles a high clockwise (in our hemisphere), so over Britain the wind turned easterly.
  • It drew bitterly cold air from Finland, north-west Russia and the Barents Sea.
  • Geographers call this a polar continental air mass: very cold, very dry.
Quick check
?Why did the wind over Britain blow from the east in late February 2018?
Mission 1

The trigger, 30 km up

  • Above the weather layer is the stratosphere. In winter, strong westerly winds circle the Arctic there, 10–50 km up: the polar vortex.
  • On 12 February 2018 the Arctic stratosphere warmed suddenly and those winds weakened and reversed: a sudden stratospheric warming (SSW).
  • The disruption worked downwards over a week or more. On about 70% of occasions after an SSW, surface winds switch to easterly.
  • Not every SSW brings cold: January 2019 had one, and the UK stayed mild.
Quick check
?What is a sudden stratospheric warming?
Mission 1

Snow from the sea

  • Polar continental air is so dry that over land it gives frost and clear skies, little snow.
  • Crossing the North Sea, it picks up moisture and warmth from the water. The air becomes unstable.
  • Result: persistent snow showers, fed by North Sea moisture, streaming into eastern Britain (Met Office).
  • The showers lined up in convergence lines, so one town could be buried while the next stayed almost bare.
Mission check

?How did Russia's winter air end up over Britain?

A high over Scandinavia turned the wind easterly, drawing in continental air.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What did the cold actually do?

Make a guess: What did the cold actually do?

Mission 2

How cold, how much snow

  • Lowest temperature: −14.2 °C at Faversham, Kent, on 28 February.
  • 1 March was the coldest spring day in the Met Office's national series (from 1960), by daytime maximum. Tredegar (Blaenau Gwent) never rose above −4.7 °C, a UK record low maximum for March.
  • Snow depths at 09:00 on 2 March: 49 cm at Drumalbin (Lanarkshire), 49 cm at St Athan (south Wales), 46 cm at Glasgow Bishopton. Typical depths were 10–20 cm.
Mission 2

Storm Emma joins in

  • 1 March: a low from the Bay of Biscay, named Storm Emma by the Portuguese weather service, pushed north.
  • Its mild, moist air rode up over the cold air: heavy snow for southern and south-west England and south Wales, then freezing rain.
  • The Met Office issued two red warnings: Scotland's Central Belt on 28 February, and southern and south-west England and south Wales on 1 March.
Quick check
?Why did Storm Emma bring such heavy snow to south Wales and south-west England?
Mission 2

Social impacts

  • Thousands of schools closed across England, Wales and Scotland. Many areas lost power.
  • Cars were stranded overnight on the A31 in Hampshire and the M80 in Scotland. Glasgow Airport closed on 28 February; rail services were cancelled.
  • A man died after falling into a frozen lake in a London park. The ONS found a second, smaller peak in daily deaths in early March, which it says the cold spell could account for.
Mission 2

Economic impacts

  • 1 March: National Grid issued a gas deficit warning, calling for more gas as homes heated all day. Gas demand peaked at 214 GW at 6 pm, roughly four times the electricity grid's winter peak.
  • Insurers paid £194 million for burst pipes in January–March 2018, the most ever in a quarter (ABI).
  • Isolated North Pennines communities were supplied by helicopter.
Mission 2

Sorting the impacts

  • Social: people's lives and safety: schools, travel, power, health, deaths.
  • Economic: money: gas supply, insurance payouts, business and transport losses.
  • Environmental: the physical surroundings: lakes froze, freezing rain coated surfaces in ice, drifts of 50 cm or more blocked roads such as the A39 in Devon.
  • One event can sit in all three columns at once.
Quick check
?Insurers paid £194 million for burst pipes in the first three months of 2018. Which type of impact is this?
Mission check

?What did the cold actually do?

It stranded travellers, strained gas supplies and cost lives.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Could Britain have been readier?

Make a guess: Could Britain have been readier?

Mission 3

Reading a warning

  • The Met Office issues warnings up to seven days ahead for snow, ice, rain, wind, fog, heat and more.
  • The colour comes from a matrix of impact and likelihood. Yellow: check whether you are affected. Amber: think about changing plans. Red: a very high likelihood of a risk to life; avoid travelling.
  • Red is rare. It needs severe impacts and a very high chance of them. This event had two.
Quick check
?A red snow warning is issued for your area. What does red mean?
Mission 3

On the ground

  • Salt lowers the freezing point of the moisture on the road, so ice forms later or not at all. Highways England kept about 280,000 tonnes at 127 depots.
  • Cold Weather Payments: £25 for each 7 days averaging 0 °C or below, paid automatically to people on certain benefits in England and Wales (Scotland runs a different scheme).
  • The stratosphere gave an early clue: forecasters saw the SSW coming about 12 days before it happened.
Quick check
?How does salt keep a gritted road clear of ice?
Mission 3

Mild for our latitude

  • Western Europe's winters are far milder than eastern North America's at the same latitude.
  • The popular answer is "the Gulf Stream". Seager et al. (2002) tested it.
  • Their finding: the main cause is westerly winds carrying air warmed by an ocean that stores summer heat and releases it in winter; the Rockies help steer the winds. The current itself plays a smaller part.
  • Either way, the warmth reaches us only if the wind comes off the Atlantic. Turn the wind round, and Britain gets the winter of its latitude.
Mission 3

Is UK weather getting more extreme?

  • Heat: the Met Office's State of the UK Climate reports that days over 30 °C have more than quadrupled in Greater London, and the hottest day of the year has warmed by over 4.5 °C in a swathe from Kent to Lincolnshire.
  • Rain: the winter half-year of 2016–2025 was 13% wetter than 1961–1990.
  • Cold: the opposite trend. 25% fewer days of air frost than in 1961–1990; a December as cold as 2010 is about half as likely as in the 1960s.
  • Cold snaps still happen. The Met Office expects the risk of cold extremes to shrink, not to disappear.
Quick check
?What does Met Office evidence say about cold extremes in the UK?
Mission check

?Could Britain have been readier?

Partly. Warnings, salt and payments cut the harm; they cannot stop the cold.

Finale

How rare was it?

  • The Met Office rates it the UK's most significant spell of snow and low temperatures since December 2010.
  • By daytime maximum, 28 February 2018 was the coldest UK day since December 1995.
  • Cold returned on 17–19 March. Yet six years later, 2024 had the fewest ground-frost days on record.
Quick check
?"The Beast from the East proves the UK is not getting warmer." Why is this wrong?
Recap

The big ideas

  • Britain is usually mild because westerly winds carry Atlantic-warmed air. Turn the wind east and that warmth never arrives.
  • Feb 2018: a sudden stratospheric warming on 12 February, then a Scandinavian high, gave an easterly of polar continental air. The North Sea added the snow.
  • −14.2 °C at Faversham; 49 cm of snow at Drumalbin and St Athan; two red warnings; Storm Emma added heavy snow and freezing rain.
  • Impacts sort into social (schools, travel, deaths), economic (gas warning, £194 million in burst pipes) and environmental (ice, frozen lakes, drifts).
  • Management reduces harm, not weather: warnings up to seven days ahead, salt, Cold Weather Payments.
  • UK evidence: heat and heavy rain extremes rising; cold extremes falling but not gone. One cold spell is weather, not climate.
