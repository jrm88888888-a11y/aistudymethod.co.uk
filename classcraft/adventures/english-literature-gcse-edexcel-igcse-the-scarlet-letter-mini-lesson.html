The Scarlet Letter · structure and 'The Custom-House'Screen 5

Found, or made up?

  • The Surveyor says old Surveyor Pue's papers support "the authenticity of the outline".
  • Yet he admits taking "as much license as if the facts had been entirely of my own invention".
  • He calls himself a romance-writer. A romance happens on "a neutral territory, somewhere between the real world and fairy-land".
  • Expect real history (governors, Boston streets) mixed with symbol and the uncanny. This is the Romantic strand in American fiction.

?Why does a story of 1640s Boston begin in an 1840s office?

The Custom-House frames the novel as a record the narrator found in his own Salem office: a faded letter A and Surveyor Pue's papers. That makes the tale feel like history, but the narrator also admits he invented freely and calls himself a romance-writer, so we read the story as both history and symbol.

The Scarlet Letter · Hester PrynneScreen 6

A dangerous thinker

  • Alone, Hester thinks freely: "The world's law was no law for her mind." (ch XIII)
  • She might have stood "hand in hand with Ann Hutchinson" as a religious rebel (ch XIII).
  • "The scarlet letter was her passport into regions where other women dared not tread." (ch XVIII)
  • Yet her teachers "made her strong, but taught her much amiss".
  • Many 21st-century readers see a feminist heroine; others stress the narrator's doubts and her return to penitence (ch XXIV).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Sinner, rebel or saint: who is Hester Prynne?

Hawthorne gives Hester all three roles. She is publicly shamed as a sinner, becomes a near-saint to the sick and poor, and thinks thoughts more dangerous than her sin. The narrator admires her strength but doubts where her freedom leads, so the best answers follow her change and weigh his judgement.

Mission 3

Who does more harm: the hidden sinner or the man who hunts him?

Make a guess: Who does more harm: the hidden sinner or the man who hunts him?

The Scarlet Letter · Dimmesdale and ChillingworthScreen 1

Two men, one secret

  • Roger Chillingworth: Hester's husband, who arrives in ch III. He hides who he is and becomes the town's physician, or leech (an old word for a doctor, and a blood-sucking worm).
  • Arthur Dimmesdale: the young minister and, secretly, Pearl's father. His hand keeps going to his heart.
  • From ch IX they share a house. Some readers hear "chill" and "dim" in their names.

The Scarlet Letter · Dimmesdale and ChillingworthScreen 4

Dimmesdale's double life

  • His suffering makes his sermons more powerful, and the town adores him (ch XI).
  • He tells his flock he is "the worst of sinners". They revere him more.

The Scarlet Letter · Dimmesdale and ChillingworthScreen 7

Weighing the two

  • Dimmesdale (ch XVII): "That old man's revenge has been blacker than my sin. He has violated, in cold blood, the sanctity of a human heart."
  • The narrator calls Dimmesdale's act "a sin of passion, not of principle" (ch XVIII). Chillingworth's is cold and planned.
  • Yet Dimmesdale's silence leaves Hester to suffer alone for seven years.
  • After the minister dies, Chillingworth "positively withered up" and leaves Pearl his fortune (ch XXIV).

Hawthorne pairs two kinds of harm. Dimmesdale's sin was passion, but his silence lets Hester suffer alone; Chillingworth's revenge is cold and deliberate, and Dimmesdale calls it "blacker" than his own sin. The strongest answers weigh both, and notice that each man is destroyed by the secret.

Mission 4

Hester's sin is public; Dimmesdale's is secret. Which hurts more?

Make a guess: Hester's sin is public; Dimmesdale's is secret. Which hurts more?

The Scarlet Letter · sin and guiltScreen 1

Sin in Puritan Boston

  • To the Puritans, sin was breaking God's law, and in the colony God's law was also the town's law.
  • Hester's crime could carry death: "The penalty thereof is death." (ch III)
  • Instead she gets public shame. The stranger calls her "a living sermon against sin".
  • Wilson insists "the shame lay in the commission of the sin, and not in the showing of it forth".

The Scarlet Letter · sin and guiltScreen 4

A letter that sees other sins

  • In ch V the letter seems to give Hester "a sympathetic knowledge of the hidden sin in other hearts".
  • It throbs near a revered minister, a cold matron, a young maiden.
  • She wonders whether "a scarlet letter would blaze forth on many a bosom besides Hester Prynne's".
  • She fights the thought and tries to believe "no fellow-mortal was guilty like herself".
  • So Hester's is only the visible sin in a town of hidden ones.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Hester's sin is public; Dimmesdale's is secret. Which hurts more?

Hawthorne contrasts two kinds of guilt. Hester's sin is known, so she can suffer it openly and turn it into service; Dimmesdale's is hidden, so it "burns in secret" and eats him from within. He himself envies her. The strongest answers compare the two rather than choosing one.

The Scarlet Letter · Puritan BostonScreen 4

Hawthorne's own ancestors

  • In "The Custom-House" the Surveyor describes his first Salem ancestor as "a bitter persecutor" of Quakers.
  • That man's son was so involved in "the martyrdom of the witches" that their blood left "a stain upon him".
  • In history: William Hathorne, then John Hathorne, a magistrate who questioned the accused in 1692 and is not known to have repented.
  • The Surveyor says he will "take shame upon myself for their sakes".
  • Hawthorne probably added the "w" to his name to distance himself from them.

The Scarlet Letter · Puritan BostonScreen 6

Not only grey

  • The narrator warns: "we perhaps exaggerate the gray or sable tinge" of the age (ch XXI).
  • Bellingham's house glitters; Wilson loves "all good and comfortable things" (ch VIII).
  • Election Day brings music, wrestling and a procession.
  • It was the next generation that "wore the blackest shade of Puritanism".
  • Beyond the town, the forest is lawless: the home of the "Black Man" (the Devil), but also of freedom (chs XVI–XVIII).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Hawthorne's ancestor questioned 'witches'. How did that shape his Boston?

Hawthorne writes as the descendant of Puritan judges, one of them involved in the Salem witch trials, and his narrator takes shame for them. His 1640s Boston is full of real people and real punishments, and he judges its harshness, but he also shows its holidays, comforts and mercy. Use the context to explain the novel, not as a history lesson.

Mission checkScreen 8

?The letter A never changes. Why does its meaning keep changing?

The letter never changes, but its readers do. Wilson's sermon makes it hell-fire, the sick call it "Able", the guilty minister sees an A in the sky, and Pearl, "the scarlet letter endowed with life", makes it a living child. Hawthorne leaves the symbol open so we notice what each reading reveals about the reader.

The Scarlet Letter · Hawthorne's languageScreen 4

Never quite certain

  • Did the rose-bush spring up under Ann Hutchinson's feet? "we shall not take upon us to determine" (ch I).
  • Does the letter glow at night? "perhaps there was more truth in the rumor than our modern incredulity may be inclined to admit" (ch V).

?A short practice paragraph.

In 2–4 sentences, explain how Hawthorne uses light in the extract on screen 2. Use one quotation and name one method.

Model answer

When Hester throws off the letter, "forth burst the sunshine", turning fallen leaves "to gold". This pathetic fallacy makes the forest share the lovers' joy, as if their shame had been the gloom. Yet Hawthorne calls it "heathen Nature", "never subjugated by human law", so the light is beautiful but lawless, and the reader may suspect the joy cannot last.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does Hawthorne keep saying "perhaps" and "it may be"?

Hawthorne's narrator keeps offering more than one explanation (the rose-bush, the glowing letter, the A in the sky, the mark on Dimmesdale's breast) and then says "The reader may choose". That ambiguity suits a romance, set between "the real world and fairy-land", and makes us judge the evidence ourselves.

Recap

Big ideas

  • "The Custom-House" (1840s Salem, first person) frames chapters I–XXIV (1640s Boston, third person).
  • The frame claims a found manuscript but admits invention: a romance "between the real world and fairy-land".
  • Three scaffold scenes (chs II–III, XII, XXIII) give the novel its shape.
  • Your essay (exam or coursework) is marked equally for AO1, AO2 and AO4, so structure and context both count.
  • Hester moves from defiance (ch II) to service (ch XIII), rebellion (ch XVIII) and chosen penitence (ch XXIV).
  • The town's reading of the A changes ("Able"), though the narrator says it "had not done its office".
  • Present her as Hawthorne's construction: track his methods and his narrator's judgements, not just what she does.
  • Chillingworth hides his identity, becomes Dimmesdale's physician and slowly turns himself into a "fiend".
  • Dimmesdale's hidden guilt makes him a great preacher and a "remorseful hypocrite".
  • Hawthorne invites you to weigh cold revenge against passion and cowardice: argue it, don't just choose.
  • In Puritan Boston sin was a crime: shame was meant to bring repentance.
  • Hester's open sin becomes the visible one in a town of hidden ones; Dimmesdale's secret consumes him.
  • The narrator's moral is "Be true!", and both endings turn on truth told or chosen.
  • Real 1640s Boston: Bellingham, Wilson, Winthrop, Hibbins and Hutchinson, in a town where "religion and law were almost identical".
  • Hawthorne (writing in 1849–50) descends from Puritan judges, and his narrator judges them from two centuries later.
  • AO4 rewards context that explains the text: link every fact to a character, scene or method.
  • The same A is read as shame, "Angel", "Able" and finally a heraldic device on the tombstone.
  • Pearl is the letter come alive: Hester's treasure and her punishment, and the one who insists on the truth.
  • Track other symbols too: the rose-bush, sunshine, the forest, the black weeds.
  • Hawthorne's language: archaic speech, light and dark imagery, symbols, long sentences and a narrator who comments.
  • Nature often mirrors feeling (pathetic fallacy), as in the "flood of sunshine" (ch XVIII).
  • The narrator keeps explanations open ("The reader may choose among these theories"): use that ambiguity in your analysis.

Key quotations to learn

  • "It was the capital letter A." ("The Custom-House") The symbol, found before the story starts.
  • "as if the letter were not of red cloth, but red-hot iron" ("The Custom-House") Even the faded relic burns.
  • "the authenticity of the outline" ("The Custom-House") The narrator claims history, but only in outline.
  • "a neutral territory, somewhere between the real world and fairy-land" ("The Custom-House") Hawthorne's definition of romance.
  • "the black flower of civilized society, a prison" (Ch I, "The Prison-Door") The story opens at a prison door.
  • "Ye have both been here before, but I was not with you." (Ch XII, "The Minister's Vigil") The second scaffold scene echoes the first.
  • "with a burning blush, and yet a haughty smile" (Ch II, "The Market-Place") Shame felt but not performed.
  • "I will not speak!" (Ch III, "The Recognition") She protects Pearl's father.
  • "it was the taper of the sick-chamber" (Ch XIII, "Another View of Hester") The letter becomes a light.
  • "They said that it meant Able" (Ch XIII, "Another View of Hester") The town re-reads the symbol.
  • "The world's law was no law for her mind." (Ch XIII, "Another View of Hester") Hester as free thinker.
  • "The scarlet letter was her passport into regions where other women dared not tread." (Ch XVIII, "A Flood of Sunshine") Outcast status as freedom.
  • "he will be known!" (Ch III, "The Recognition") Chillingworth's quest begins.
  • "like a sexton delving into a grave" (Ch X, "The Leech and His Patient") His search as desecration.
  • "He had spoken the very truth, and transformed it into the veriest falsehood." (Ch XI, "The Interior of a Heart") Dimmesdale's false confessions.
  • "transforming himself into a devil" (Ch XIV, "Hester and the Physician") Chillingworth chose his change.
  • "That old man's revenge has been blacker than my sin." (Ch XVII, "The Pastor and His Parishioner") Dimmesdale's verdict.
  • "the sanctity of a human heart" (Ch XVII, "The Pastor and His Parishioner") What Chillingworth violates.
  • "a living sermon against sin" (Ch III, "The Recognition") The punishment's public purpose.
  • "a sympathetic knowledge of the hidden sin in other hearts" (Ch V, "Hester at Her Needle") Sin hidden everywhere.
  • "Mine burns in secret!" (Ch XVII, "The Pastor and His Parishioner") Secret guilt versus open shame.
  • "Of penance, I have had enough! Of penitence, there has been none!" (Ch XVII, "The Pastor and His Parishioner") Self-punishment is not repentance.
  • "the one sinner of the world" (Ch XXIII, "The Revelation of the Scarlet Letter") The confession at last.
  • "Be true! Be true! Be true!" (Ch XXIV, "Conclusion") The narrator's moral.
  • "religion and law were almost identical" (Ch II, "The Market-Place") Sin is also a crime.
  • "an erring woman's heart" (Ch III, "The Recognition") The narrator judges the judges.
  • "the martyrdom of the witches" ("The Custom-House") Hawthorne's ancestor and Salem.
  • "A writer of story-books!" ("The Custom-House") What his ancestors would think of him.
  • "we perhaps exaggerate the gray or sable tinge" (Ch XXI, "The New England Holiday") The Puritans were not only gloomy.
  • "the scarlet letter endowed with life!" (Ch VII, "The Governor's Hall") Pearl as the living symbol.
  • "she is the scarlet letter, only capable of being loved" (Ch VIII, "The Elf-Child and the Minister") Hester's view of Pearl.
  • "symbolize some sweet moral blossom" (Ch I, "The Prison-Door") The narrator offers a symbol at the start.
  • "the sunshine does not love you" (Ch XVI, "A Forest Walk") Light shuns the letter.
  • "pointing evidently towards her mother's breast" (Ch XIX, "The Child at the Brook-Side") Pearl demands the letter's return.
  • "ON A FIELD, SABLE, THE LETTER A, GULES." (Ch XXIV, "Conclusion") The last image: a red A on black.
  • "the black flower of civilized society, a prison" (Ch I, "The Prison-Door") Dark imagery from the first page.
  • "we shall not take upon us to determine" (Ch I, "The Prison-Door") The narrator refuses to decide.
  • "as with a sudden smile of heaven, forth burst the sunshine" (Ch XVIII, "A Flood of Sunshine") Pathetic fallacy.
  • "the sympathy of Nature" (Ch XVIII, "A Flood of Sunshine") The narrator names the effect.
  • "We impute it, therefore, solely to the disease in his own eye and heart" (Ch XII, "The Minister's Vigil") A natural explanation for a portent.
  • "The reader may choose among these theories." (Ch XXIV, "Conclusion") Ambiguity handed to the reader.
