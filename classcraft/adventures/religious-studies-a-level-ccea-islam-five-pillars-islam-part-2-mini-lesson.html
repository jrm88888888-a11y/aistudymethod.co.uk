Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do pilgrims at Makkah dress so simply and alike, and what changed when pilgrims began to fly?

Make a guess: Why do pilgrims at Makkah dress so simply and alike, and what changed when pilgrims began to fly?

Mission 1

The fifth pillar

  • Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah, 8–13 Dhu'l-Hijjah, the last month of the lunar year.
  • "Pilgrimage thereto is a duty men owe to Allah,- those who can afford the journey" (Qur'an 3:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • Once in a lifetime, for adult Muslims who are able: health, money (debts paid, family provided for) and a safe route.
  • Origins: Ibrahim and Isma'il raised the foundations of the Ka'bah (2:127); God told Ibrahim to "proclaim the Pilgrimage among men" (22:27).
  • The Prophet made one Hajj, the Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632). Its rites are the model.
  • Umrah: the "lesser pilgrimage", at any time, with fewer rites; it does not replace Hajj.
Mission 1

Ihram: the state of consecration

  • At the miqat (boundary points) pilgrims enter ihram with an intention.
  • Men wear two plain white unstitched cloths; women wear simple modest clothes, face and hands uncovered.
  • Forbidden in ihram: perfume, cutting hair or nails, hunting, sexual relations, quarrelling (2:197: "no obscenity, nor wickedness, nor wrangling in the Hajj").
  • The talbiyah: "Here I am, O God, here I am; You have no partner; praise, blessing and sovereignty are Yours."
  • Meaning: rich and poor look the same; ihram is often compared to the burial shroud: the pilgrim leaves the world behind.
Mission 1

The rites, day by day

  • Arrival: tawaf, seven circuits of the Ka'bah anticlockwise; sa'y, seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah.
  • 8th: to Mina, the tent city.
  • 9th: 'Arafat: wuquf (standing) in prayer from midday to sunset; the Prophet's Farewell Sermon was given here. Night at Muzdalifah, gathering pebbles.
  • The standing at 'Arafat is often seen as a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: all stand equal before God, asking forgiveness.
  • 10th (Eid-ul-Adha): stoning the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah); sacrifice; men shave or trim, women cut a lock; tawaf al-ifadah and sa'y.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three jamarat each day.
  • Farewell tawaf before leaving. Many also visit the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah (not part of Hajj).

> "And proclaim the Pilgrimage among men: they will come to thee on foot and (mounted) on every kind of camel, lean on account of journeys through deep and distant mountain highways" (Qur'an 22:27, Yusuf Ali)

  • Sa'y recalls Hajar, Ibrahim's wife, running between the hills to find water for the baby Isma'il; the spring of Zamzam appeared.

Mission 1

What Hajj means

  • Tawhid: the whole journey answers God's call ("Here I am").
  • Ibrahim's faith: his family's obedience is re-lived at the Ka'bah, Safa and Marwah, the jamarat and in the sacrifice.
  • Equality: "the most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is (he who is) the most righteous of you" (Qur'an 49:13, Yusuf Ali).
  • Unity of the ummah: Muslims of every nation, in one place, one dress, one prayer.
  • Judgement and forgiveness: a Hajj done without wrongdoing returns the pilgrim "as on the day his mother bore him" (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Malcolm X (1925–1965), on Hajj in 1964, wrote that seeing pilgrims of every colour as brothers changed his view of race (letter from Makkah; paraphrase).
Mission 1

Hajj in the 20th century

  • Rule of the Hijaz: Ibn Saud took Makkah and Madinah (1924–25); the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (1932) took charge of the Hajj.
  • Travel: the Hejaz Railway reached Madinah in 1908 but was wrecked in the First World War; from the mid-century, air travel replaced months-long journeys by land and sea.
  • Building: major expansions of the Grand Mosque from the 1950s; a Jamarat bridge (1963; rebuilt on several levels from 2006); fire-resistant tents at Mina after a fire in 1997.
  • Management: country quotas from 1988 (about one pilgrim per thousand Muslims); visas; health rules.
  • Tensions: the 1979 seizure of the Grand Mosque; a deadly clash with Iranian pilgrims in 1987; crushes in crowds.
  • Heritage: Saudi authorities demolished many historic sites (e.g. tombs at al-Baqi', 1925–26), seeing them as risks of shirk; many Shi'a and some Sunnis regard this as a grievous loss.

Mission 1Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims

  • Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims perform the same Hajj together, at the same places and times.
  • Twelver Shi'a add tawaf al-nisa', a further full tawaf (seven circuits, with its two-rak'ah prayer) needed before marital relations are lawful again.
  • Small differences in prayer continue during Hajj.
  • Shi'a also value ziyarah (visiting) to the shrines of the Prophet's family: Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad. These are highly honoured but never replace Hajj.
  • Politics have touched Hajj: e.g. Iran's boycott after 1987; disputes over the treatment of Shi'a pilgrims and holy sites.
Mission 1Evaluate

More than a personal journey?

  • Claim: Hajj is above all a personal spiritual journey.
  • Personal: intention, repentance and forgiveness are individual; each pilgrim stands alone at 'Arafat.
  • Personal: many describe it as the high point of their own faith.
  • More than personal: it unites the ummah across nations (49:13); millions act as one.
  • More than personal: it re-enacts a shared history (Ibrahim, Hajar, the Prophet) and shapes Muslim identity worldwide.
  • Criteria: Can the personal and communal be separated? What does the Qur'an stress (22:27–28)? Has modern mass travel made Hajj more communal or more of an individual experience?
Mission check

Mission 2

Mission 2: The Prophet's mosque was mud and palm trunks. What was it for, and do today's mosques still do the same?

Make a guess: The Prophet's mosque was mud and palm trunks. What was it for, and do today's mosques still do the same?

Mission 2

Masjid: a place of prostration

  • Masjid (Arabic): "place of sujud", of prostration. The English word mosque comes from it.
  • So the core of a masjid is simple: a clean place where Muslims bow to the ground before God.
  • A hadith says the whole earth was made a masjid for the Prophet's community: Muslims may pray anywhere clean (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • So a building is not required for salah. What, then, is a mosque for?
  • Jami' (gathering) mosque: a larger mosque where Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) is held.
  • CCEA spelling: Salat; Friday prayer Salat-ul-Jumu'ah; Medina.
Mission 2

The first masjid in Madinah

  • 622 CE, after the Hijrah: first the mosque at Quba, then the Prophet's own mosque in Madinah.
  • Built by the community together: mud-brick walls, palm-trunk pillars, palm-leaf roof, an open courtyard.
  • One building, many roles:
  • Worship: the five prayers, Friday prayer and khutbah; Bilal's adhan called people in.
  • Teaching: the Qur'an recited and explained; the suffa (shaded platform) sheltered poor Companions who studied there.
  • Government: disputes judged, decisions taken, delegations from other tribes received.
  • Welfare: charity given out; the needy fed and housed.
Mission 2Read the source

Mosques in the Qur'an

> "There is a mosque whose foundation was laid from the first day on piety; it is more worthy of the standing forth (for prayer) therein." (Qur'an 9:108, Yusuf Ali)

> "The mosques of Allah shall be visited and maintained by such as believe in Allah and the Last Day, establish regular prayers, and practise regular charity" (Qur'an 9:18, Yusuf Ali)

> "And the places of worship are only for Allah, so pray not unto anyone along with Allah." (Qur'an 72:18, Pickthall)

  • Many commentators link 9:108 to the mosque at Quba; others to the Prophet's mosque.

Q1 According to 9:18, who looks after mosques?

Show answer

Q2 What does 72:18 rule out inside a mosque?

Show answer

Mission 2

Design and features

  • Prayer hall: an open carpeted space with no seats, often with lines to keep rows straight. All stand shoulder to shoulder: equality before God.
  • Qiblah wall and mihrab: the niche showing the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah, so all face one centre.
  • Minbar: stepped pulpit for the khutbah (the Prophet's had three steps).
  • Washing facilities: for wudu before prayer; purity of body points to purity of heart.
  • Dome: often said to echo the heavens; helps carry sound. Not required: many mosques have none.
  • Minaret: the tower from which the adhan is called; a landmark that announces the mosque.
  • Decoration: no pictures of people or animals; calligraphy of Qur'an verses and geometric patterns point beyond the visible to the infinite God.
  • Also: a space for women (separate hall, gallery or area behind), a shoe rack, often classrooms and offices.

Mission 2Diversity

Roles of the masjid in the UK today

  • Religious: salah, Jumu'ah, Ramadan tarawih prayers, Eid, Qur'an recitation, nikah, funerals.
  • Educational: madrasah classes, adult study circles, talks.
  • Social: community meals (e.g. iftar), food banks, youth and sports clubs, care for the elderly.
  • Political (civic): voter registration, meetings with MPs and police, statements after local events.
  • Women: some mosques have full facilities, women trustees and classes; others little or no space. Muslim women's groups campaign for access, recalling that women prayed in the Prophet's mosque.
  • Shi'a centres (imambargah, husayniyyah) hold majlis gatherings, especially in Muharram.
  • Supported by the community: donations, sadaqah, volunteers, committees (9:18).

Mission 2Evaluate

Has the mosque kept its first purpose?

  • Claim: Today's mosques have kept the function of the first masjid in Madinah.
  • For: prayer and prostration remain central; many UK mosques again teach, feed and advise, as in Madinah.
  • Against: some mosques are prayer halls only, run by small committees; few have political or judicial roles.
  • Against: in Madinah women prayed and learned in the mosque; in some UK mosques women have little space.
  • Prostration first? Yes: the word masjid means place of sujud, and the earth itself is a masjid. Or no: the Prophet's mosque shows the community, not only the prayer, as its purpose.
  • Criteria: What counts as the mosque's "function": its acts, or its role as hub of the ummah? Should a minority community's mosque match a city-state's?

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Make a guess: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Mission 3

Coming to Britain

  • Small Muslim communities existed earlier: Yemeni and Somali seamen in Cardiff, South Shields and Liverpool; the Shah Jahan Mosque, Woking (1889).
  • 1950s–60s: men from Pakistan (many from Mirpur, Kashmir) and India (Gujarat) came to work in mills and foundries in Lancashire, Yorkshire and the Midlands.
  • Many planned to return. After the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 tightened entry, wives and children joined them: sojourners became settlers.
  • 1970s–80s: families from Bangladesh (Sylhet); East African Asians expelled from Uganda (1972).
  • 1990s onwards: refugees and migrants from Somalia, Bosnia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and elsewhere; also British converts.
  • 2021 Census, England and Wales: about 3.9 million Muslims (6.5% of the population).
Mission 3

Not one community but many

  • Ethnic diversity: South Asian heritage is the largest group; also Arab, Somali, Turkish, Iranian, Malaysian, Nigerian, white British and others.
  • Sunni traditions (most British Muslims): Deobandi (scholarly, reformist; many seminaries), Barelwi (devotional, Sufi-influenced), Salafi, the Tablighi Jamaat (preaching movement).
  • Shi'a: mainly Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashari); also Isma'ilis (the Nizari Isma'ilis are led by the Aga Khan).
  • Mosques often began along lines of village, language and school of thought.
  • Generations differ: many young British-born Muslims separate "culture" from "religion" and choose their own path.
Quick check
?Why did many Muslim migrant workers of the 1950s–60s change from planning to return home to settling permanently in Britain?
Mission 3

Freedom of religion and pluralism

  • European Convention on Human Rights (1950), Article 9: freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including to manifest belief "in worship, teaching, practice and observance". Limits allowed to protect public safety and the rights of others.
  • UK law: Human Rights Act 1998 brings Article 9 into British courts; Equality Act 2010 makes religion or belief a protected characteristic.
  • Religious pluralism: a modern secular state lets many faiths and none live side by side; the state favours none in law (in practice the Church of England remains established).
  • Test cases: R (Begum) v Denbigh High School (2006): the House of Lords upheld a school's refusal of the jilbab, since its uniform already included Muslim dress options.

Diversity as a sign

> "O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct." (Qur'an 49:13, Pickthall)

> "There is no compulsion in religion." (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall)

Show answer

Q2 Which verse could a British Muslim use to support freedom of religion for all?

Show answer

Mission 3

Segregation, assimilation, integration

  • Segregation: communities live separate lives: different streets, schools, workplaces.
  • Assimilation: a minority is absorbed into the majority culture and loses what makes it distinct.
  • Integration: full part in shared life (work, law, politics) while keeping a distinct faith.
  • After riots in Oldham, Burnley and Bradford (2001), the Cantle Report (2001) found communities living "parallel lives".
  • Causes debated: cheap housing near the mills, discrimination, choice to live near family and mosque, and others moving away.
  • Most British Muslim bodies reject both segregation and assimilation and argue for integration.
Mission 3

Food, dress, practice, education

  • Food: halal meat; school meals and workplace canteens; disputes about whether halal meat must be unstunned.
  • Dress: hijab worn widely; the face veil (niqab) debated in schools, courts and work. Some women choose no headscarf.
  • Practice: Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) in work or school time; prayer rooms; fasting in Ramadan during exams.
  • Education: evening Qur'an classes (madrasas); state-funded Muslim schools since 1998 (Islamia Primary, Brent); RE and sex education debates.
  • Spec spellings: Salat, Sharia.
Mission 3

Modesty and entertainment

  • Shared principle: entertainment is lawful (halal) unless it involves what is forbidden: alcohol, gambling (5:90), immodesty, or neglect of prayer.
  • Music: some scholars forbid instruments; many permit wholesome music. Yusuf Islam (Cat Stevens) gave up music after converting (1977), then returned to it.
  • Social life: pubs, clubs, mixed parties and dating raise questions for young British Muslims.
  • Responses: Muslim comedians, nasheed singers, modest fashion brands, halal social events.
  • Migration's impact: the questions are sharper where Islam is a minority and the surrounding culture is secular.
Mission 3Diversity

Taking part in democracy?

  • Participation (majority view): vote, stand for election, lobby. Basis: shura (consultation): believers are those "whose affairs are a matter of counsel" (Qur'an 42:38, Pickthall); also the common good (maslaha) and keeping agreements.
  • Examples: first Muslim MP (1997); first Muslim to serve in the Cabinet (2010); a Muslim Mayor of London (2016).
  • Rejection (a small minority): sovereignty belongs to God alone, so man-made law cannot be supported; voting is avoided. Hizb ut-Tahrir argued this; it was banned in the UK in 2024.
  • Quietism: some Salafis avoid politics but obey the law.
  • Links to AS 6: Sharia and democratic government.

Mission 3Evaluate

Can Muslims keep their identity in secular Britain?

  • Claim: Muslims in Britain can fully belong without losing their religious identity.
  • For: strong legal protection (Article 9, Equality Act); thriving mosques, schools, halal economy; many British-born Muslims are both confident Muslims and active citizens.
  • For: 49:13 gives religious grounds for taking part.
  • For: so does the shura tradition (consultation, 42:38).
  • Against: secular values (privacy of religion, alcohol-based social life, sexual ethics) pull young people away; some lose practice.
  • Against: Islamophobia and discrimination push some towards separate lives; dress codes and halal rules still cause conflict.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "identity": belief, practice, or community? Is assimilation ever possible without loss? Who decides?

Mission 4

Three challenges of secularisation

  • Secularisation: religion losing influence over society, institutions and minds.
  • Displacement: science and reason replace religion as the source of truth and moral values.
  • Privatisation: religion is pushed into the personal sphere: fine at home, out of place in public life.
  • Militant atheism: religion is not just false but irrational and harmful (Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion, 2006; Sam Harris, The End of Faith, 2004).
  • Your spec: "the challenge to preserve religious identity in a secular age".
Mission 4

Why Islam resists privatisation

  • For Muslims, Islam is a din: a whole way of life. Many hold that tawhid (God's oneness) leaves no area outside God's guidance.
  • So prayer five times a day, fasting, diet and dress are public by nature.
  • Reason: the Qur'an repeatedly calls people to reflect: "in the creation of the heavens and the earth … there are indeed Signs for men of understanding" (Qur'an 3:190, Yusuf Ali). Muslims answer "irrational" with this tradition of reason.
  • Responses vary: some Muslims become more visibly devout; some keep faith mainly private; some leave religion.

?Why do many Muslims find the privatisation of religion especially hard to accept?
Mission 4

Identity: dress and diet

  • Dress: modest clothing, hijab or niqab for some women, beards and caps for some men. A visible sign of belonging.
  • Diet: halal ("permitted") food; no pork, blood or alcohol (2:173, 5:3, 5:90). "Eat of what is on earth, Lawful and good" (Qur'an 2:168, Yusuf Ali).
  • Halal certification for shops and restaurants; halal options in many schools, hospitals and prisons.
  • Debates: slaughter without pre-stunning (campaigners want it banned; Muslim and Jewish groups defend it); whether stunned meat can be halal (certifiers differ).
  • Ramadan: fasting at school and work; employers and schools increasingly make room.
Mission 4

Prayer, mosques and self-reliance

  • Prayer: prayer rooms in workplaces, universities, airports; Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) in lunch breaks.
  • Places of worship: from rooms in houses (1960s) to purpose-built mosques. Britain's first purpose-built mosque: the Shah Jahan Mosque, Woking (1889). London Central Mosque opened in 1977.
  • Self-reliance: Muslim schools (the first state-funded, Islamia Primary, London, 1998); Islamic finance without interest; charities such as Islamic Relief (Birmingham, 1984).
  • Representation: the Muslim Council of Britain (1997) and other bodies speak for different groups.
Mission 4

Migration and a British Muslim identity

  • Migration from the 1950s: South Asia, then East Africa, the Middle East, Somalia and elsewhere.
  • First generation: kept village customs and languages; mosques run by community elders; imams from abroad.
  • Later generations: separate religion from culture; English sermons; British-trained imams; "British Muslim" identity.
  • Diversity: Deobandi, Barelwi, Salafi, Shi'a, Sufi, converts; mosques reflect it.
  • Two risks Muslims discuss: assimilation (losing faith) and segregation (cutting off from society).
Mission 4Evaluate

"Muslims in Britain can keep their identity without separating from society."

  • For: dress and diet are largely provided for and protected; many young Muslims hold a British Muslim identity.
  • For: prayer rooms, Muslim schools, charities and Islamic finance show confident participation.
  • Against: privatisation and militant atheism press faith inwards; some fear assimilation, others choose separation.
  • Another view: identity changes in every age: later generations separate religion from culture.
  • Judge it by: What counts as Muslim identity: belief, practice or culture? Who decides? What does society demand?

Secularisation pushes faith out of public life; many Muslims answer by keeping it visible and building their own institutions.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hajj: a once-in-a-lifetime duty for those able (3:97), retracing Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il and the Prophet's Farewell Hajj.
  • Ihram, tawaf, sa'y, 'Arafat, Muzdalifah, stoning, sacrifice: equality, obedience and a foretaste of Judgement.
  • 20th century: Saudi control, air travel, expansion, quotas and safety, and disputes over heritage.

Mission 2

  • Masjid means place of prostration; the first masjid in Madinah was also school, court, welfare centre and meeting place.
  • Features (prayer hall, qiblah wall and mihrab, minbar, wudu area, dome, minaret, calligraphy) serve salah and point to God's oneness.

Mission 3

  • Muslim migration to Britain grew from the 1950s; family settlement after 1962 built mosques, halal food and schools; British Muslims are diverse in ethnicity and school of thought.
  • Segregation, assimilation and integration are different responses; most Muslim bodies seek integration, protected by freedom of religion (Article 9).

Mission 4

  • Secularisation: displacement, privatisation and militant atheism; Islam as din resists privatisation.
  • Identity is kept through dress, diet, prayer, mosques and self-reliant institutions; Muslims differ on voting.
