Changing Spaces, Making Places

Place · concepts

Space, place and its three dimensions

Space is abstract and undifferentiated — a location on a map. Place is space made meaningful by human experience. Geographers describe place through three linked ideas:

  • Location — the absolute position of a place (its coordinates or address); where it is.
  • Locale — the material setting and physical character in which everyday social relations are played out.
  • Sense of place — the subjective meanings, memories and emotional attachments people invest in a place.

Placelessness: some argue that standardised, globalised landscapes (identical retail parks, chain coffee shops) erode local distinctiveness, producing "clone towns" that feel interchangeable — a loss of sense of place.

Place · perspectives & categories

Insiders, outsiders & categories of place

The same place is experienced differently depending on your relationship to it:

  • Insider perspective — a sense of belonging and familiarity; the place feels like home.
  • Outsider perspective — viewing a place as a visitor or stranger, often through stereotypes or surface impressions.

Places can also be categorised by how we encounter them:

  • Near vs far places — measured not only in distance but in emotional connection.
  • Experienced places — those we have physically visited or lived in.
  • Media / imagined places — known only through film, news, adverts or the internet (a virtual place).

Key idea: whether a place feels "near" or "far" is subjective — somewhere geographically distant can feel emotionally near, and vice versa.

Place · factors of change

Endogenous & exogenous factors

The character of a place is shaped by two sets of factors:

  • Endogenous factors — internal characteristics: location, topography and physical geography, land use, the built environment and infrastructure, and the demographic and economic characteristics of the population.
  • Exogenous factors — external relationships and flows into the place: movements of people, money, investment, resources and ideas that link it to other places.

Place · change over time

How places change & the shrinking world

Places are dynamic: their economy, demography and meaning shift as connections change. A key driver is time-space compression — as transport and communications speed up, the friction of distance shrinks and distant places feel "closer".

  • Faster travel and instant communication create a "shrinking world", deepening the flows that reshape places.
  • Economic change (deindustrialisation, the rise of services and the knowledge economy) rewrites the character and function of places.
  • Demographic change — migration, ageing, gentrification — alters who lives in a place and how it feels.

Analysing change: geographers often quantify it — for example, the percentage change in a place's population between two censuses.

Place · representation

Representing place

Places are known through representations — the ways they are portrayed. These fall into two types:

  • Formal representations — objective, quantitative sources such as census statistics, official data and maps. They aim to be measurable and neutral.
  • Informal representations — subjective portrayals through film, art, literature, music, photography and social media. They convey emotion, identity and viewpoint.

Informal representations produce imagined geographies — mental images of a place that may be selective, romanticised or stereotyped, and which can shape how outsiders perceive and treat it.

Evaluation: no representation is neutral. Even a map or census involves choices about what to include, so representations always carry the perspective of their creator.

Place · quantitative skills

Measuring place: the location quotient

To measure how specialised a place is in a particular industry, geographers use the location quotient (LQ). It compares the local share of employment in an industry with the national share:

LQ = local % in industry ÷ national % in industryLQ > 1 = concentration · LQ ≈ 1 = average · LQ < 1 = under-represented
Worked example

Locally, 10% work in manufacturing; nationally, 8% do.

LQ = 10 ÷ 8 = 1.25 — the place is above the national average for this industry.

Reading it: an LQ well above 1 shows a place is a cluster or specialist hub for that activity — useful evidence when characterising a place's economy.

Place · placemaking

Placemaking, rebranding & regeneration

Places are actively remade, especially where economic decline has damaged their image and function:

  • Regeneration — physical, economic and social renewal of a run-down area (new housing, transport, jobs).
  • Rebranding — changing the image or identity of a place to attract visitors, investors or residents (a new "brand" and marketing).
  • Reimaging — reworking the perceptions and associations attached to a place, often through culture, events or media.
  • Placemaking — the deliberate design and shaping of public spaces to strengthen identity and wellbeing.

Evaluation: regeneration can revive an economy but may spark gentrification — rising prices that displace existing communities — so "success" depends on whose perspective you take.

Place · players & measuring change

Players & measuring place quality

Reshaping a place involves competing players (stakeholders) with different power and priorities:

  • Government and local planners — set policy, grant permission and fund schemes.
  • TNCs and developers — bring investment but seek profit.
  • Local communities and residents — hold local knowledge and may support or resist change.

To compare places, analysts often build a composite index — averaging several standardised indicators (such as income, health and environment scores) into a single number so places can be ranked.

Worked example — composite index

A place scores 80, 90 and 70 on three indicators.

Index = (80 + 90 + 70) ÷ 3 = 80
