Mission 1

Space or place?

  • Space: a location or area, described by coordinates, size, distance.
  • Place: space that people have given meaning: memories, feelings, identity.
  • Yi-Fu Tuan (Space and Place, 1977) argued that space becomes place as we get to know it and attach value to it.
  • Places matter to people: belonging, identity, security, and where we choose to live, work and invest.
Mission 1

Why place is hard to define

  • A place has no single, fixed boundary: is "Blackpool" the promenade, the borough or the wider Fylde coast?
  • Different people give the same place different meanings.
  • Doreen Massey ("A global sense of place", 1991) argued that places are meeting points of flows and links to elsewhere, not sealed units with one identity.
  • So a place is better described by its connections and meanings as well as its location.
Mission 1

Two contrasting places

  • Blackpool (Lancashire coast) and Wokingham (Berkshire, west of London), Census 2021:
  • Population change 2011–21: Blackpool −0.7%; Wokingham +15.0%; England +6.6%.
  • Aged 65+: Blackpool 20.7%; Wokingham 17.2%; England 18.4%.
  • Born outside the UK: Blackpool 7.9%; Wokingham 18.9%; England 17.4%.
  • Jobs (residents in work): accommodation and food, Blackpool 10.0% vs Wokingham 2.9%; information and communication, 1.7% vs 15.0%.
  • Deprivation (IMD 2025, rank of average score, 296 districts): Blackpool 1st (most deprived); Wokingham 295th.
Mission 1

Endogenous and exogenous factors

  • Endogenous (inside the place): location, topography, land use, built environment and infrastructure, demographic and economic characteristics.
  • Blackpool: flat coastal site; promenade, piers and hotels of a seaside resort; older, less qualified workforce.
  • Wokingham: inland, commuter-belt location near Reading; newer housing estates; many residents in information and communication jobs.
  • Exogenous (relationships with other places): visitors and holiday choices for Blackpool; commuting, firms and investment linked to London and the Thames Valley for Wokingham.
  • When you build your own profiles, add cultural (traditions, events), political (who governs, how people vote) and natural characteristics.

Mission 1

Flows and time-space compression

  • Time-space compression (David Harvey, 1989): faster, cheaper transport and communication make distant places feel closer.
  • UK residents' visits abroad rose from 17.5 million in 1980 to 93.1 million in 2019.
  • For a seaside resort, beaches abroad came within easy reach of its visitors: an exogenous change (from outside the place) that resorts such as Blackpool must compete with.
  • For Wokingham, flows of people (more residents born abroad), investment and ideas (information and communication jobs) tied it more closely to global networks.
  • Sense of place shifts too: insiders may feel their town's identity changing as these flows change it.
Quick check

Mission check

?A new housing estate is built on an empty field. A year later, residents say it "has no sense of place yet". Following Tuan, what is most likely to turn this space into a place?

A place is a location plus meaning, shaped by features inside it and flows from outside, and seen differently by different people. Census and deprivation data measure how it changes; people's meanings explain why it matters.

Mission 2

What is a local place study?

  • A locality, neighbourhood or small community, urban or rural, where you live or study.
  • Goal: describe its character, explain how it developed, and show how people's lives changed.
  • OCR: two contrasting place profiles at a local scale; this is usually the first, your home place.
  • This mission models the method with one exemplar: Elephant and Castle, London.
Mission 2

The place profile

Six lenses (a checklist for any place study):

  • Demographic: age, ethnicity, migration.
  • Socio-economic: jobs, income, deprivation, housing tenure.
  • Cultural: languages, faiths, food, festivals, shops.
  • Political: who governs; campaigns; votes.
  • Built: housing types, street pattern, landmarks.
  • Natural: relief, rivers, green space.

Sources: quantitative (census, IMD, employment) and qualitative (photos, oral histories, planning documents, newspapers, social media).

Mission 2

Exemplar: Elephant and Castle

  • A neighbourhood in the London Borough of Southwark.
  • Built, then: the Heygate estate, completed 1974, about 3,000 people; 1,212 homes.
  • Built, now: Elephant Park, approved January 2013: 3,000 new homes; nearly half public space.
  • Cultural: a cluster of Latin American–owned businesses, surveyed by the community group Latin Elephant from July 2012.
  • Political: the shopping centre's demolition application was rejected in January 2018, then approved in January 2019.
Mission 2

The numbers: Southwark, Census 2011 → 2021

  • Population 288,300 → 307,600 (+6.7%; London +7.7%).
  • Households renting socially 43.7% → 39.7%: England's second-largest fall.
  • Ethnic group: White 54.2% → 51.4%; Black 26.9% → 25.1%; Asian 9.4% → 9.9%.
  • Born in Nigeria: 13,600 → 11,200 residents.
  • In work (16+, excluding full-time students): 59.6% → 62.7%; England fell 56.5% → 55.7%.

Mission 2

Lived experience: the qualitative layer

  • Oral and community records: Latin Elephant began in 2012 as a blog by a researcher studying Latin Americans in Elephant and Castle.
  • In 2021 it launched MyElephantStory, a digital archive of the area, on the first anniversary of the shopping centre's closure.
  • Residents' views: some Heygate residents said "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor" in demolition.
  • Official accounts: the council reports that 99% of its rehoused Heygate tenants chose to stay in Southwark.
  • Different voices, different experiences of the same change.
Mission 2

Connections and flows

  • People: Latin American traders and residents; Nigeria-born residents fewer than in 2011.
  • Money: the council partnered with Lend Lease (now Lendlease) in 2007 to redevelop the Heygate site; Lendlease lists Elephant Park beside projects in Sydney, Singapore, Malaysia and New York.
  • Ideas: national talk of "so-called sink estates" and a £140 million regeneration fund (2016); local ideas, such as a "Latin Quarter" vision (2014–16).
  • These flows tie one London neighbourhood to the region, the nation and the world.

Mission 2

Continuity and change in people's lives

  • Change: 595 council tenants rehoused; 99% stayed in Southwark; all offered the right to return.
  • Change: shopping centre closed 24 Sep 2020; traders' relocation began the next day, "although not everyone could be relocated".
  • Continuity: Latin American businesses and community groups still organise in the area.
Mission 2

Your own study, step by step

  • 1. Define the boundary (a ward, an LSOA cluster, a village).
  • 2. Profile it with the six lenses.
  • 3. Collect at least two quantitative and two qualitative sources, past and present.
  • 4. Trace flows and influences at each scale.
  • 5. Record whose lives changed, how, and how you experience it.
  • 6. State your sources' limits (date, scale, author's purpose).
Mission check

A strong place study mixes quantitative and qualitative sources, past and present, at the right scale, and traces the flows and influences that made the place.

When sources disagree

  • Slum share in M/East: 84.9% from the municipal census tables; 77.5% in an NGO newsletter.
  • Mumbai as a whole: 52.5% (municipal tables) vs 41.84% (NGO newsletter).

Mission 3

Lived experience: an insider NGO's account

Apnalaya, an NGO working in the ward, wrote in 2018:

  • "Since the 1960s, this ward has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city."
  • It reports an infant mortality rate of 66 per 1,000 live births (national average 41) and an average age at death of 39, from its own 2017 study.
  • It describes residents winning "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas and ration cards" through citizen campaigns.
  • Valuable voice; also an organisation with a mission. Check its figures against official data where possible.
Mission 3

Connections and flows

  • People: migrants from across India since the 1960s; residents resettled from inner-city slums.
  • Change over time: Mumbai's urban agglomeration grew from about 15.95 million (2000) to 20.2 million (2025): +27% (our calculation).
  • Resources: the ward has a large open landfill receiving "around 4500 metric tons of garbage every day" (Apnalaya's figure): a flow from the whole city into one ward.
  • Money: Mumbai is a global financial centre (Alpha city; central bank HQ), yet M/East "sits at the bottom of the 24 wards of Mumbai" on human development (Mumbai Human Development Report 2009, as quoted by Apnalaya).
  • Ideas: right-to-information requests and civic campaigns, such as "Mission 24".

Researching at a distance

  • Official data: census tables, municipal reports.

Mission 4

Two ways to show a place

  • Representation: how a place is portrayed: what is shown, by whom, for whom.
  • Formal (statistical) representation: census tables, maps, geospatial data, deprivation indices. Made by official bodies with published methods.
  • Informal representation: media made with a purpose or a point of view.
  • OCR's examples: TV, film, music, art, photography, literature, graffiti, blogs.
  • Neither is neutral: both select.
Mission 4

Slough, informally

  • A poem: John Betjeman's "Slough" asks for "friendly bombs" to fall on the town (as recalled in Parliament).
  • TV: the sitcom The Office and its manager David Brent are tied to Slough's image; in 2019 Slough's MP said the town has "a lot more than just David Brent and 'The Office' to offer".
  • 2002, an MP for another area: "I would not be as unkind as Mr. Betjeman in calling for friendly bombs to fall on Slough" — then used Slough as a unit of sprawl: "40 towns the size of Slough".
  • An outsider's image, still being repeated decades later.
Mission 4

Slough, formally

  • Population +13.0%, 2011–21 (England +6.6%).
  • Median age 34 (England 40).
  • 46.7% Asian, Asian British or Asian Welsh; 36.0% White (2021).
  • Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025: 105th of 296 districts (1 = most deprived); 0% of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived 10%.
  • The formal image: young, growing, diverse; more deprived than about two-thirds of districts, yet with no neighbourhood in the worst 10%. Not the poem's image.

Insiders answer back

Mission 4

Who feels they belong?

Community Life Survey, England, 2023/24: adults who feel they belong strongly to their immediate neighbourhood.

  • All adults: 61%.
  • Age: 16–24 54%; 25–34 51%; other age groups 59–73%.
  • Sex: female 63%; male 60%.
  • Sexual orientation: heterosexual 61%; gay or lesbian 49%; bisexual 45%.
  • Religion: Jewish 68%, Muslim 67%, Christian 66%; no religion 56%.
  • Role matters too: the same street means different things to a resident, a commuter and a visitor.
Mission 4

Same park, different place

  • ONS, Great Britain, Feb–Mar 2022: walking alone in a park or open space after dark.
  • Felt very or fairly unsafe: 82% of women, 42% of men.
  • Women aged 16–34 felt the most unsafe of any age and sex group using public transport alone after dark.
  • Perception depends on who you are, not only on the place.
Mission 4

Feelings change behaviour

  • Place attachment: an emotional bond between a person and a place.
  • Fear changes routines: adults who stopped walking in parks or open spaces after dark rose from 32% to 37% (women) and 18% to 24% (men), June 2021 → Feb–Mar 2022.
  • Attachment keeps people: 61% would still like to live in their area in five years; 84% of adults aged 75+.
  • Attachment mobilises people: at Elephant and Castle, London, the community group Latin Elephant objected at the January 2018 planning committee on the shopping centre.

Mission 4

How to judge any representation

  • Who made it? For whom? When? Why?
  • What is left out?
  • Check informal images against formal data. Then check the data: a census is only every 10 years; categories are chosen by officials; averages hide neighbourhoods.
  • Exam move ("Compare"): point-by-point similarities and differences, not two separate descriptions.
Mission check

?A seaside town's tourism video shows sunny beaches and busy cafés. Census data show an old population and high deprivation. A local blog posts photos of closed shops. Which evaluation is strongest?

Representations are made for purposes. Informal and formal images of the same place differ because they are built by different people for different audiences, and they can change how a place is treated.

Mission 5

Key terms

  • Social inequality: unequal access to income, housing, health, education, work and services.
  • Deprivation: lacking what most people in a society have. Multiple deprivation: several lacks at once.
  • Social segregation: groups living apart, by income or by background.
  • Informal settlement: homes without legal title or planning approval, often without services.
  • OCR uses AC, EDC and LIDC for levels of development.
Mission 5

Measuring inequality

  • England's Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025 ranks 33,755 small areas, of about 1,500 people each.
  • Seven weighted domains: income 22.5%; employment 22.5%; education 13.5%; health 13.5%; crime 9.3%; barriers to housing and services 9.3%; living environment 9.3%.
  • One index covers OCR's five measures: housing, healthcare, education, employment and access to services.
  • It is relative: it ranks areas, so some area is always "most deprived".
Mission 5

The digital divide

  • Digital divide: the gap between people who can and cannot use the internet and digital services.
  • UK, 2018: 5.3 million adults (10.0%) were internet non-users.
  • By region: 7.0% in London; 14.2% in Northern Ireland, the highest.
  • Households, 2018: 9% with a single adult aged 16–64 had no internet connection, against 1% with two adults aged 16–64.
  • Online services (jobs, benefits, health bookings) can widen other inequalities.

Mission 5

Between places: the global pattern

  • In 2022, 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements: 24.8% of the urban population.
  • Over 85% were in three regions: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia (362 million), Central and Southern Asia (334 million) and sub-Saharan Africa (265 million).
  • Informal jobs as a share of all jobs, 2024 (ILO modelled): low-income countries 88.3%; lower-middle 81.5%; upper-middle 50.8%; high-income 12.9%.
  • Inequality varies between places with development, and within every city.
Mission 5

Within one city: Mumbai

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011, in the city's own ward tables:
    • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%, calculated).
    • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%, calculated).
  • Praja Foundation, citing Census 2011, gives 77.5% for M/East.
  • The share depends on who defines and counts slums. Both sources put M/East above 3 in 4.
Mission 5

Daily life in M/East

Figures from Apnalaya, an NGO working in the ward, reported by Praja Foundation:

  • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 live births, against India's 41. Average age at death: 39.
  • Employment: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
  • Environment: a landfill receiving about 4,500 tonnes of waste a day; contaminated air and groundwater.
  • Services: the ward ranked last of Mumbai's 24 wards on human development (2009), and had the city's most complaints about water supply.
Mission 5

Why the gap?

  • Land values: formal housing takes the most valuable land; low-income households are pushed to cheaper, less serviced land.
  • Migration: since the 1960s M/East "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
  • Policy: it also took people "'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
  • Services: weaker water, health and schooling make it hard to escape low pay.
  • Exam move: name the factor, then the evidence, then the effect on daily life.

Mission 5

England: deprivation persists

  • The most deprived area is east of Jaywick, Clacton-on-Sea; seven of the ten most deprived are in Blackpool.
  • Deprivation clusters in large conurbations, former heavy-industry and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.
  • 82% of the most deprived tenth of areas in 2025 were also in the most deprived tenth in 2019.
Mission 5

The cycle of deprivation

  • Low income → poor housing and diet → poorer health and schooling → fewer qualifications → low-paid or no work → low income for the next generation.
  • Area effects reinforce it: weak services, crime, and a poor reputation that deters investment.
  • Breaking it needs action at several points at once.

  • 2.1-3.b government spending or cuts reduce, reinforce or create inequality
Mission 6

Moving out from the centre

  • Suburbanisation: people, then shops and offices, move from the inner city to the suburbs. Drivers: cars and commuter rail, cheaper land, more space.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the urban edge, to small towns and villages. Drivers: retirement, quality of life, remote working.
  • In parts of Europe, city populations have fallen with suburbanisation or counter-urbanisation, and with boundary reclassification.
Quick check
?A retired couple sell their flat in central Leeds and buy a cottage in a village 50 km away. Which movement is this?
Mission 6

London: in and out at once

  • In the year to mid-2025 London had positive natural change and net international inward migration.
  • But "this was more than offset by net outward migration to other areas of the UK".
  • London's population fell by 0.29%, "the first decrease since the year to mid-2021".
  • A city can gain from births and arrivals while losing more people outwards.
Mission 6

Deindustrialisation and the rise of services

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs and output in a place.
  • Causes: the global shift of factories to lower-cost countries, automation, firms relocating, and recessions.
  • Service economy: UK employment in services rose from 66.8% (1991) to 83.0% (2024); industry fell from 30.8% to 16.1% (ILO modelled).
  • This is structural economic change, driven largely by globalisation.
Mission 6

Detroit's population

  • US census counts for Detroit city:
    • 465,766 (1910); 1,849,568 (1950, the 5th largest US city); 1,027,974 (1990); 713,777 (2010); 639,111 (2020).
  • Most of the loss came early: 821,594 of the 1,210,457 people lost by 2020 had gone by 1990 (our calculation).
Mission 6

Detroit: the jobs left

  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963; "The postwar manufacturing boom was largely over by the mid-1950s" (reported by HNN).
  • Manufacturing jobs in the Detroit metro area: 380,900 (2000), 174,000 (2009).
  • People who could move for work, often younger and better paid, left first: selective out-migration.
Quick check
?By what percentage did Detroit city's population fall between 1950 (1,849,568) and 2020 (639,111)?
Mission 6

A deindustrialised city

  • Depopulation: down about 65% from 1950 to 2020.
  • Unemployment: the metro area's unemployment rate averaged 14.6% in 2009 (calculated from monthly data).
  • Low incomes: median household income (2020–24, in 2024 dollars) $39,938, about half the US $80,734.
  • Housing: 50.3% of homes owner-occupied, against 65.2% in the USA.
Mission 6

The spiral of decline

  • Factory closes → jobs lost → less local spending → shops and services close → those who can, leave → smaller tax base → fewer services, more dereliction → investors stay away → more closures.
  • England's index finds deprivation concentrated in "areas that have historically had large heavy industry manufacturing and/or mining sectors".
Quick check
?In the spiral of decline, why does a smaller tax base make things worse?
Mission 6

Structural change, opportunity and inequality

  • Losers: workers with factory skills, and places built around one industry. Detroit's median income is about half the US figure.
  • Winners: graduates in service jobs, concentrated in big service cities.
  • Lasting effect: deprivation persists in former industrial areas of England.
  • Opportunity now depends on qualifications and place more than before.
Mission 6

Cyclical change: booms and recessions

  • Cyclical change: short-term booms and recessions, on top of long-term structural change.
  • Detroit metro unemployment, annual average (calculated from monthly data): 3.4% (2000); 14.6% (2009, recession); 4.2% (2019); 11.7% (2020, pandemic; monthly peak 23.9% in May 2020).
  • Metro manufacturing jobs: 174,000 (2009); 258,800 (2019); 226,100 (2020).
  • Recessions hit factory workers and the low-paid first; recoveries do not reach everyone equally.
Mission 6

Government spending and inequality

  • England, decade to 2019–20: council spending per person was cut by 31% (£432) in the most deprived tenth of areas, against 16% (£134) in the least deprived.
  • Most deprived councils still spend more per person: 1.3 times the least deprived, down from 1.6 times in 2009–10.
  • Cuts can reduce, reinforce or create inequality, depending on who relies on the services cut.
Quick check
?In the decade to 2019–20, English councils' spending per person was cut by 31% in the most deprived tenth of areas and by 16% in the least deprived. What did this do to inequality between areas?
Mission 6

Signs of change?

  • Detroit's estimated population for July 2025 is 649,095, above the 2020 census count.
  • Metro manufacturing jobs were 237,900 in 2025: above 2009, far below 2000.
  • Caution: 2025 is an estimate; the jobs series covers the much larger metro area and starts only in 1990.
  • Exam move: separate structural decline (decades) from cyclical swings (years).
Mission check

?A northern English town loses its last large factory. Over 20 years its population falls, while commuter villages 20 km away grow. Its boundary and birth rate do not change. What best explains the town's decline?

Detroit lost about 65% of its people from 1950 to 2020, two-thirds of that loss before 1990, as factory jobs left from the 1950s and people moved out. Deindustrialisation and centrifugal movement (moving outwards from the centre) worked together.

Mission 7: Redcar's carbon emissions plunged in just two years. Why did nobody celebrate?

Make a guess: Redcar's carbon emissions plunged in just two years. Why did nobody celebrate?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.1-4.a the role of players in driving economic change, including at least one of local and national government, MNCs or international institutions
  • 2.1-4.a case study of one country or region impacted by structural economic change: characteristics before; the change and the players driving it; socio-economic, demographic, cultural and environmental impacts on people and place
Mission 7

Players in economic change

  • Structural economic change = a shift in the mix of a place's jobs, e.g. from manufacturing to services.
  • Players who drive it:
  • MNCs: invest in or leave a place.
  • National government: funds, regulates, rescues or refuses to.
  • Local government: plans land, bids for funds, attracts investors.
  • International institutions: set rules on trade and subsidies (e.g. the EU).
Mission 7

Before: Redcar and Cleveland to 2015

  • A Teesside borough of about 135,000 people (2014).
  • Steelmaking at Redcar: in 2015 an MP described "one hundred and seventy years of steelmaking" ending.
  • Environmental: industry produced 92% of the borough's 8,510 kt CO₂e in 2014 (our calculation).
  • Socio-economic: 2015 deprivation rank 49th of 326 English districts; 21.6% of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived 10%.
Mission 7

The change: SSI's closure, 2015

  • Owner: SSI UK, part of a Thai steel firm (an MNC).
  • SSI went into liquidation on 2 October 2015.
  • The minister said SSI "never made a profit" and its coke ovens were losing about £2 million a month.
  • Closure was a "hard closure … not mothballing"; about 2,100 people were affected by the announcement.
Mission 7

Global and international players

  • The Business Secretary blamed world overcapacity: "China is obviously one of the main countries … but there are others", naming Brazil, Russia and Turkey.
  • He said the problem "requires EU-wide action".
  • The industry minister cited EU state aid rules as "very onerous" limits on rescue.
  • So the change was driven by an MNC, global markets, an international institution (EU rules) and national government choices.
Quick check
?Which player's role in Redcar fits the label "international institution"?
Mission 7

Impacts on people: jobs and benefits

  • By 31 Aug 2016, 1,550 former SSI employees had claimed a benefit; 94% had ended that claim.
  • 610 supply-chain workers also claimed.
  • DWP warned: "It should not be assumed that those not claiming JSA were all in new jobs".
  • Government offered a support package of "up to £80 million".
Mission 7

Impacts on place: deprivation

  • 2025 deprivation rank: 37th of 296 districts; 25% of neighbourhoods in England's most deprived 10%.
  • In 2015 the share was 21.6%: deprivation did not improve after closure.
  • Demographic: population about 139,200 in 2024; it did not fall after closure.
Quick check
?Emissions fell from 8,510 kt CO₂e (2014) to 2,192 kt (2016). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 7

Impacts on environment

  • Borough emissions: 8,510 kt (2014) → 2,192 kt (2016) → 672 kt (2024).
  • About 99% of the 2014–16 fall was in the industry sector (our calculation): the steelworks closing, not a new green policy.
  • The site left was contaminated: Teesworks is "one of, if not the largest, brownfield remediation projects in Europe".
  • 450 acres remediated or in remediation (2023).
Mission 7

Players after the change: regeneration

  • National government: inward-investment initiative chaired by Lord Heseltine (2015); £71 million to prepare the site (2020).
  • Local/regional: the first Tees Valley Mayor, elected May 2017, proposed the South Tees Development Corporation, set up August 2017.
  • Freeport with tax sites on the site, announced March 2021.
  • Public resources planned to end-2024/25: £560 m, incl. £246 m government grants.
  • Jobs: 940 construction jobs; 2,295 direct jobs expected once sites operate.
Mission 7

Players in tension

  • Thai banks with a charge on SSI land refused to sell, so a compulsory purchase was needed.
  • A 50/50 joint venture with two local businessmen was agreed in 2020.
  • An independent review (2024) found "no evidence to support allegations of corruption or illegality" but issues "of governance and transparency".
  • Government issued a Best Value Notice (April 2025) and appointed an inspector (October 2026).
Mission check

?A foreign-owned car plant in region Q closes after years of losses, during a global glut of cars. Trade rules limit subsidies. A regional mayor wins national funds to clear the site for green industries. Which sentence best matches the OCR case-study structure?

At Redcar an MNC (SSI) failed in a world of overcapacity; EU rules and UK government choices shaped the response. Emissions fell by about 74%, but deprivation worsened, so the change was not a cause for celebration.

Mission 8: Why would a city spend millions on a year of art?

Make a guess: Why would a city spend millions on a year of art?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.1-5.a placemaking: how governments and organisations present places to attract investment and regeneration
  • 2.1-5.a architects and planners designing meaningful places, e.g. mixed community use, the 24-hour city
  • 2.1-5.a local community groups shaping places: residents' and heritage associations, social media
  • 2.1-5.b why places rebrand through reimaging and regeneration
  • 2.1-5.b rebranding strategies: sport, art, heritage, retail, architecture, food, singly or combined
Mission 8

Who makes a place's meaning?

  • Places are not only lived in; they are presented.
  • Government (national and local), corporations (developers, landowners) and community groups all try to create meanings.
  • Aim: to change behaviour: where people invest, visit, live, work, shop.
  • Key terms: placemaking (shaping a place and its image); place marketing; rebranding = reimaging (new image) + regeneration (physical, economic and social renewal).
Mission 8

Why rebrand?

  • Decline: lost industries, empty sites, falling population.
  • Image: a negative representation that deters investors and visitors.
  • Competition: cities compete for firms, students, tourists and events.
  • London 2012: a key legacy aim was "the regeneration and transformation of East London, one of the city's most deprived areas at the time".
Mission 8

Culture-led: Hull, UK City of Culture 2017

  • Purpose of the UK City of Culture title: "to drive culture-led regeneration, enhance social cohesion and foster economic growth".
  • Hull 2017 "leveraged over £676 million in investments for venue refurbishments and urban enhancements".
  • New venues created and existing ones redeveloped; Hull's Humber Street Gallery "supports visual artists in the city who were historically underserved".
  • Arts Council National Portfolio funding rose 35.7% in Hull.
  • Art + architecture + heritage combined.
Mission 8

Did the rebrand work?

DCMS's 2026 legacy study tested outcomes against comparison cities:

  • Tourism jobs: Hull shows "a positive increase but is not statistically significant".
  • Wellbeing: "Short-term impact but not sustained after event."
  • Arts funding: the title raises public funding, "however the funding is not sustained".
  • Jobs: "many jobs are temporary for the delivery of the year."
  • COVID-19 disrupted Hull's later economic data.
Quick check
?Hull's tourism jobs rose after 2017, but the increase was "not statistically significant". What does this mean?
Mission 8

Sport-led: London 2012

  • The Games acted as "a catalyst for the regeneration of a previously underdeveloped area in East London".
  • Today: Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, 560 acres, free to visit, "evolved into a thriving innovation district".
  • The park's own body claims £3.5 billion GVA and 38,300 jobs in the Legacy Area.
  • DCMS: tourism employment and turnover impacts "found a year after event"; no wellbeing impact found.
  • Caution: some jobs and visits would have happened anyway, or moved from elsewhere.
Mission 8

Planners and architects: designing meaning

  • Mixed community use: homes, work, leisure and public space in one district, used at different times of day.
  • The 24-hour city: planning for evening and night activity (transport, culture, food, safety), not just 9–5.
  • UK, 2022: 27% of the workforce, about 8.7 million people, usually worked in the evening or at night, 700,000 fewer than in 2016.
  • Example of mixed use: the Olympic Park's innovation district at Here East and Plexal beside parkland.
Mission 8

Community groups shape places too

  • Neighbourhood plans: communities write a plan; if approved at a referendum it has the same legal status as the local plan.
  • First in England: Upper Eden, Cumbria, approved by 90% of voters on 8 March 2013.
  • Residents' and traders' groups: Latin Elephant (Elephant and Castle, London) objected to the shopping-centre plan in 2018 and campaigned for traders' relocation.
  • Social media and digital archives: its MyElephantStory archive (2021) records the area's memory.
  • Heritage associations campaign to protect buildings and stories that give a place meaning.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Make a guess: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.1-5.b players in placemaking: government/EU funding, corporate bodies, not-for-profits, community groups
  • 2.1-5.b how and why some groups contest rebranding
Mission 9

Contested change

  • Regeneration (placemaking): improving a run-down area through investment in buildings, jobs and services.
  • Rebranding (place marketing): changing a place's image to attract investors, residents or visitors.
  • Players or stakeholders: residents, landowners, developers, councils, national government and NGOs.
  • Change is contested when these groups want different outcomes; rising land value often drives it.
Mission 9

Lagos: Otodo-Gbame

  • Lagos State's planning ministry estimated in 2013 that over 70% of Lagos's population lived in informal settlements (reported by Amnesty International).
  • On 9 October 2016 the State Governor announced that informal settlements along the waterways would be demolished within seven days.
  • Amnesty estimates that over 30,000 residents of the waterfront community of Otodo-Gbame were forcibly evicted, in three operations from November 2016 to April 2017.
  • The estimate deducts the people who remained (about 4,700) from an earlier population estimate.
Mission 9

Lagos: process and outcome

  • All 75 Otodo-Gbame evictees Amnesty interviewed said they had no prior written notice.
  • Amnesty reports that the evictions went ahead in violation of court orders of 7 November 2016 and 26 January 2017.
  • The government gave varying reasons, Amnesty reports, from denying responsibility to "claiming to save the environment from degradation … to preventing crime".
  • A luxury housing estate was being built on part of the land and water area (Amnesty, 2017).
Mission 9

London: the Heygate estate

  • A council estate in Southwark, completed in 1974, originally housing about 3,000 people; 1,212 homes, 1,020 council-owned.
  • A late-1990s survey found the buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
  • In a 1998 consultation, 96% of 500 responses favoured major regeneration.
  • The redevelopment "has been the subject of a two-decade long controversy".
Mission 9

Heygate: decisions and players

  • 2005: the 595 council tenants were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in Southwark, and all were offered the right to return.
  • 2007: Southwark Council partnered with the developer Lend Lease (now Lendlease).
  • 2010: renegotiated terms raised affordable housing from 11% to 25%, plus a council share of any extra profits.
  • January 2013: plans for 3,000 new homes approved; July 2014: demolition complete. "Nearly half of the new development is public space".
Mission 9

Players in placemaking

  • Government: Southwark Council led the scheme; the Mayor of London sets conditions on GLA funding. In some UK schemes, EU funds also paid for regeneration.
  • Corporate body: Lendlease, the development partner.
  • Not-for-profit organisations: in Lagos, Amnesty International and Justice and Empowerment Initiatives documented the evictions.
  • Community groups: residents' campaigns, such as the Heygate campaign quoted by the London Assembly.
Quick check
?Using the four types of player, which type is Lendlease?
Mission 9

Heygate: two views

  • The council: "the combination of housing management challenges and regeneration opportunities" justified demolition.
  • Some residents: "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • A local campaign called the years of uncertainty "a decade long act of attrition".
  • Across London, 50 regenerated estates almost doubled their homes, but lost some 8,000 social rented homes in all.
Quick check
?Which statement fits the London-wide evidence on 50 regenerated estates?
Mission 9

Why groups contest rebranding

  • A new name and image (the Heygate site is now marketed as Elephant Park) targets new buyers and investors.
  • Long-standing residents may lose homes, neighbours and shops, and their attachment to the place.
  • Benefits (new homes, a park) may go mainly to newcomers; costs fall on those displaced.
  • So contest comes from those who lose most: here, social tenants and leaseholders on the estate.
  • Resident ballots: from 18 July 2018, London landlords seeking GLA funding for estate regeneration that demolishes any social homes and builds 150 or more homes must win a residents' ballot.
Mission 9

Comparing the two neighbourhoods

  • Similar: lower-income homes cleared on valuable land; luxury development on part of the Lagos site; some Heygate residents say land value decided it.
  • Process: London, a council decision with rehousing priority and a right to return; Lagos, operations Amnesty documents as without notice or compensation, despite court orders.
  • Scale: the Heygate originally housed about 3,000 people; Amnesty estimates over 30,000 were evicted from Otodo-Gbame.
Quick check
?What is the strongest contrast between the two cases?
Mission check

?A London housing association wants GLA funding to demolish an estate of 400 social-rent homes and build 1,000 new homes. Who now has a formal say over whether demolition goes ahead?

Power over land decides: councils, developers and governments, in Lagos and London. Residents gain a real say only through protections such as secure tenure, enforced court orders, rehousing rights and, in London since 2018, resident ballots.

Mission 10

Mission 10: Hull was UK City of Culture in 2017. Years later, how could anyone prove whether it worked?

Make a guess: Hull was UK City of Culture in 2017. Years later, how could anyone prove whether it worked?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.1-5.c case study of a rebranded place: need, strategies, players, changed perceptions, relative success
Mission 10

Four kinds of evidence

  • Economic: income, poverty, employment.
  • Social: inequality, deprivation, health, life expectancy.
  • Demographic: population size, age structure, migration in and out.
  • Environmental: pollution, derelict and vacant land.
  • Judge change within the place over time, and against comparable places.
Mission 10

Relative or absolute?

  • Absolute change: the level itself moves (fewer people out of work).
  • Relative change: the place's position against others moves (its rank).
  • England's Index of Multiple Deprivation (IMD) is "the official measure of relative deprivation".
  • But income and employment domain scores are proportions: a score of 0.38 means "38% of the population is income deprived".
  • The indices are "not designed to provide 'backwards' comparability"; the 2025 index uses 55 indicators, against 39 in 2019.
Mission 10

Hull: why rebrand?

  • Hull, a major port, "suffered from steady structural socio-economic decline", especially as its fishing industry declined from the 1970s.
  • It carried a stigma: in 2003 it was named "the worst town in Britain".
  • IMD 2015: 3rd most deprived of 326 English districts (rank of average score); 45% of its neighbourhoods were in England's most deprived 10%.
  • Need: change both the reality (jobs, deprivation) and the image.
Mission 10

Hull: strategy and players

  • Strategy: a year as UK City of Culture 2017, "an opportunity to counter negative external perceptions".
  • Heritage was used to tell the city's story and "boost civic pride".
  • Players: City of Culture organisers, funders and city leaders shaped the message.
  • Investment around it: Siemens pledged £160m for a wind-turbine blade plant and 1,000 jobs.
  • Rebranding = changing a place's image to attract investors, visitors and residents.
Mission 10

Did perceptions change?

  • Participation: 90% of residents attended at least one event in the first three months.
  • Official evaluation: positive early results "were later scaled down".
  • Critics say organisers "tightly controlled a singular pride narrative" to "legitimise the entrepreneurial re-branding of the city".
  • So perception gains may be real but short-lived, and contested.
Mission 10

Hull: judging change with data

  • IMD rank (1 = most deprived): 3rd of 326 (2015) → 4th of 317 (2019) → 8th of 296 (2025).
  • Most deprived 10%: 45.2% of neighbourhoods (2015 and 2019) → 41.7% (2025).
  • Income score: 0.257 → 0.227; employment score: 0.202 → 0.166 (2015 → 2019).
  • Health domain rank: 32nd → 20th most deprived (2015 → 2019): relatively worse.
  • Mixed picture: modest absolute gains; still among England's most deprived districts.
Quick check
?Hull's employment deprivation score fell from 0.202 (2015) to 0.166 (2019). By how much did the share of employment-deprived people fall?
Quick check
?A council says: "Hull's rank moved from 3rd to 8th most deprived, 2015–2025, so deprivation fell." What is the strongest criticism?
Mission 10

Relative success

  • Compare outcomes with aims: image (perception) and reality (jobs, deprivation).
  • Compare with similar places that did not rebrand.
  • Compare short term (2017 visitors, pride) with long term (2019 and 2025 data).
  • Hull: high participation; modest absolute falls in income and employment deprivation; health relatively worse; still among the most deprived districts.
  • Exam move ("Assess the success …"): judge against aims, give data from two dates, and weigh who gained.
Mission check

?A seaside town says its regeneration scheme worked. Which single piece of evidence would best show that its residents are better off?

Prove success with absolute measures (income, employment, health, derelict land, pollution), on a consistent method, for the people who live there, compared with similar places. Ranks and visitor counts are only part of the picture.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Space vs place (Tuan); places as meeting points of flows (Massey); endogenous and exogenous factors.
  • Blackpool vs Wokingham: −0.7% vs +15.0% population (2011–21); IMD 2025 rank 1 vs 295; tourism vs information and communication jobs. Time-space compression (UK visits abroad 17.5 m in 1980 → 93.1 m in 2019) reshapes function and sense of place.

Mission 2

  • Profile a place with six lenses; use quantitative and qualitative sources; check scale and date.
  • Elephant and Castle: Heygate to Elephant Park, Latin American traders, global developer, national policy, contested change.

Mission 3

  • M/East, Mumbai: 807,720 people; 84.9% in slums and 83.4% literacy (aged 7+) vs 52.5% and 89.7% for Mumbai (Census 2011, municipal tables).
  • Flows: migrants and resettled families, the city's waste, global wealth beside a ward at the bottom of Mumbai's human development ranking, civic ideas.
  • Sources disagree (84.9% vs 77.5%): name the source and definition; triangulate.

Mission 4

  • Slough: a poem and a sitcom made an outsider image; census and IMD data paint a young, diverse, mid-ranked town; insiders contest the image.
  • Perception and attachment vary with age, sex, sexuality and religion, and they change behaviour (avoiding parks after dark, staying, campaigning).

Mission 5

  • Inequality is measured with several domains: income, employment, health, education, housing and access to services.
  • Land values, migration and resettlement concentrate deprivation in some wards; the cycle of deprivation keeps it there.
  • Two contrasting places: M/East and D ward; the digital divide is another measure.

Mission 6

  • Deindustrialisation (global shift, automation) can trigger a spiral of decline: depopulation, unemployment, low incomes.
  • Structural change, cycles and government spending all shape who gets opportunity.

Mission 7

  • Before: a steel borough with high industrial emissions and high deprivation. Change: SSI liquidation (Oct 2015), driven by an MNC's losses, global overcapacity and limits on state aid.
  • Impacts: 1,550 SSI benefit claims; deprivation share up (21.6% → 25%); emissions down 74% (2014–16); regeneration through STDC/Teesworks, a Freeport and contested governance.

Mission 8

  • Government, corporations and community groups create place meanings to shape investment, visits and residence.
  • Hull 2017 (culture-led): £676 million investment leveraged; tourism-jobs rise not significant; wellbeing gains short-lived.
  • London 2012 (sport-led): tourism effects a year on; park now an "innovation district".
  • Planners (mixed use, 24-hour city) and community groups (neighbourhood plans, Latin Elephant) also shape places.

Mission 9

  • Regeneration and rebranding are contested because gains and losses fall on different groups.
  • Players: government, corporate bodies, not-for-profits and community groups; each judges success by its own criteria.

Mission 10

  • Judge regeneration with economic, social, demographic and environmental variables; separate absolute change (levels) from relative change (ranks).
  • Hull's 2017 rebranding raised participation and pride; data show modest absolute gains, but it remains among England's most deprived districts.
