Mission 1

Mission 1: Eight young people in ten in France get the bac. So why do so many students feel under pressure?

Make a guess: Eight young people in ten in France get the bac. So why do so many students feel under pressure?

Mission 1

From 3 to 18

  • L'école maternelle (3–6): compulsory from age 3 since 2019.
  • L'école élémentaire (6–11): from CP to CM2.
  • Le collège (11–15): from la sixième to la troisième, ending with the diplôme national du brevet.
  • Le lycée (15–18): la seconde, la première, la terminale. Three tracks (voies): general, technological, vocational (professionnelle).
  • Since 2020, young people aged 16–18 must be in education, training or work.
  • Watch out: le collège is a secondary school for 11–15-year-olds, not a college. Year groups count down: la sixième is the first year of collège.
  • un(e) collégien(ne) · un(e) lycéen(ne) · un(e) étudiant(e) – a student in higher education only
Mission 1

The bac

  • Le baccalauréat ends lycée and opens the door to higher education.
  • Since the 2021 reform: 40% continuous assessment (le contrôle continu), 60% final exams.
  • Final exams: French (in première), philosophy, two specialist subjects (les spécialités), and le grand oral, a 20-minute oral.
  • New: since June 2026, pupils in the general and technological tracks also sit a two-hour math exam in première (it counts from the 2027 bac).
  • 2025 results: 91.6% passed (general track 96.2%, technological 90.9%, vocational 83.9%).
  • 80.1% of a generation now hold the bac.
  • Une mention – a distinction (assez bien, bien, très bien). Marks are out of 20; 10 is a pass.
  • Watch out: passer le bac = to sit the bac. To pass it: réussir le bac or avoir le bac.
Quick check
?Inès a passé le bac en juin, mais elle ne l'a pas eu. What happened?
Mission 1

After the bac

  • Students apply on Parcoursup (since 2018), a national platform: up to 10 wishes (vœux); each course ranks applicants.
  • L'université: licence (3 years), master (+2), doctorat (+3). Open to bac holders; selection for some courses.
  • 2026–27 fees for French and EU students: €178 a year for a licence, €255 for a master. Non-EU students can be charged €2,902 / €3,950; universities may exempt some of them (a May 2026 decree caps these exemptions).
  • Les grandes écoles (engineering, business, Sciences Po …): selective, often after two years of classes préparatoires and a competitive exam (un concours).
  • Shorter, job-focused courses: BTS (2 years), BUT (3 years).
  • L'alternance: study combined with paid work in a company (l'apprentissage).
Mission 1

Student issues

  • La précarité étudiante: many students struggle with rent, food and transport.
  • Housing: student halls run by the CROUS are cheap but too few; private rents in big cities are high.
  • Les bourses sur critères sociaux: means-tested grants.
  • Since 4 May 2026, a meal in a CROUS university restaurant costs €1 for every student (before, only grant holders and students in difficulty).
  • Many students work alongside their studies (un job étudiant), which can hurt results.
  • Other worries: stress over Parcoursup, choosing a path at 15–17, dropping out in the first year (le décrochage).
  • joindre les deux bouts – to make ends meet · avoir recours à l'aide alimentaire – to turn to food aid

Mission 1

Life at school: today's debates

  • Phones: banned in écoles and collèges (2018 law), and in lycées since September 2026 (law of 24 August 2026).
  • Bullying (le harcèlement scolaire) has been a criminal offense since 2022. Schools run the pHARe anti-bullying program.
  • Workload: long school days and heavy terminale timetables; debate about homework and stress.
  • Equality of opportunity: OECD PISA studies regularly find that in France pupils' results depend more on their social background than in the average OECD country.
  • Repeating a year (le redoublement) is now rare.
  • Religion at school and laïcité: see Mission 5.

Mission 1

Faut-il sélectionner à l'entrée de l'université ? Argue both sides

  • Arguments made by supporters of selection:
  • La sélection oriente les étudiants vers des filières où ils ont des chances de réussir. – It steers students to courses where they can succeed.
  • Elle réduit l'échec en première année. – It reduces first-year failure.
  • Arguments made by opponents:
  • Le bac doit rester la clé d'entrée à l'université. – The bac should remain the key to university.
  • La sélection renforce les inégalités sociales. – Selection reinforces social inequality.
  • Les algorithmes de Parcoursup manquent de transparence. – Parcoursup's algorithms lack transparency.
  • Balance: Tout dépend de la manière dont on accompagne les étudiants. – It all depends on how students are supported.

Mission 2: Why does France have a 35-hour week, and what happens when its workers go on strike?

Make a guess: Why does France have a 35-hour week, and what happens when its workers go on strike?

Mission 2

Working life in France

  • Les 35 heures: the legal working week since the Aubry laws (1998 and 2000). Hours beyond it are overtime (les heures supplémentaires), paid extra.
  • Les congés payés: five weeks of paid holiday (the first two weeks were won in 1936, the fifth in 1982).
  • Le SMIC: the national minimum wage, raised every year in line with prices.
  • Retirement: the 2023 reform began raising the minimum age from 62 to 64. In December 2025 Parliament suspended it: the legal age stays at 62 years and 9 months until January 2028.
  • Pôle emploi became France Travail in 2024: the public job center.
  • travailler à temps plein / à temps partiel – to work full-time / part-time
Mission 2

Contracts and the changing work scene

  • Un CDI (contrat à durée indéterminée) – a permanent contract. Un CDD (à durée déterminée) – a fixed-term contract. L'intérim – temping.
  • Young people often start with a CDD, an internship (un stage) or work-study (l'alternance).
  • Part-time jobs for students: un job d'été, du baby-sitting, la restauration rapide.
  • Remote work (le télétravail) spread after 2020, mainly in office jobs.
  • Platform work (l'ubérisation): delivery riders and drivers, often self-employed (auto-entrepreneurs), with fewer protections.
  • Attitudes: many workers say they look for meaning (le sens du travail) and a better work–life balance (l'équilibre vie professionnelle – vie privée).
  • Watch out: un stage = an internship or work placement (it can also be a short training course), never a theater stage.
Quick check
?Pendant ses études, Hugo a fait un stage de six mois chez Airbus. What did Hugo do?
Mission 2

The right to strike

  • A constitutional right: the 1946 Preamble, still in force, says « Le droit de grève s'exerce dans le cadre des lois qui le réglementent. »
  • Strikers are not paid for strike days. In public services, unions must give 5 days' notice (un préavis).
  • Minimum service: since 2007, transport staff must say 48 hours ahead whether they will strike, so users can be told which services will run; since 2008, primary schools must look after pupils during a teachers' strike.
  • Key movements:
  • May 1968: a general strike by millions; the accords de Grenelle raise the minimum wage by 35%.
  • 1995: weeks of transport strikes against a social-security and pensions plan.
  • 2019–2020: a long rail and metro strike against a pensions reform.
  • 2023: 14 national days of strikes and marches against raising the pension age to 64.
  • faire grève (no article) · être en grève · un(e) gréviste · un syndicat – a trade union · une manifestation – a protest march (not a "manifestation"). Protest in depth.
Quick check

Mission 2

Gender equality at work

  • INSEE, private sector, 2024:
  • On average, women earn 21.8% less than men over the year.
  • At the same working time, the gap is about 14%.
  • In the same job with the same employer, it is 3.6%.
  • Why the gap: more women work part-time; jobs and sectors differ (care, education); careers slow after children; few women at the top (le plafond de verre – the glass ceiling).
  • Laws: equal pay for equal work (1972); 40% women on the boards of large companies (Copé-Zimmermann law, 2011); a gender-equality index that companies with 50+ staff must publish (phased in 2019–2020); 30% women among senior executives of large firms by 2026, 40% by 2029 (Rixain law, 2021).

Mission 2

Argue both sides

  • La grève est-elle encore utile ?
  • Supporters: La grève a permis de grandes avancées sociales, comme les congés payés. – Strikes won major social progress, such as paid holidays.
  • Supporters: C'est souvent le seul moyen de se faire entendre. – It is often the only way to be heard.
  • Critics: Les usagers sont les premières victimes des grèves. – Users are the first victims of strikes.
  • Critics: Le dialogue social devrait passer avant le conflit. – Negotiation should come before conflict.
  • Les quotas de femmes sont-ils justes ?
  • Supporters: Sans quotas, la parité ne serait jamais atteinte. – Without quotas, parity would never be reached.
  • Critics: Il faut recruter selon les compétences, pas selon le sexe. – Recruitment should be by skills, not sex.
  • Balance: Certes …, mais … · D'un côté …, de l'autre …
Mission 2

Model sentences and exam tasks

Mission 3: Teenagers in France smoke far less than they did a few years ago. What changed, and what risks remain?

Make a guess: Teenagers in France smoke far less than they did a few years ago. What changed, and what risks remain?

Mission 3

How healthy are 17-year-olds?

  • The ESCAPAD survey (OFDT) questions 17-year-olds across France.
  • Daily smoking: 25.1% in 2017 → 15.6% in 2022.
  • Has tried cannabis: 39.1% → 29.9%. Regular cannabis use: 7.2% → 3.8%.
  • Alcohol: of those who drank in the last month, 36.6% had at least one heavy session (une alcoolisation ponctuelle importante, binge drinking).
  • Trend: most risky behaviors are falling, but binge drinking is still common.
  • le tabagisme – smoking (as a habit) · une enquête – a survey · la consommation – use, consumption · en baisse / en hausse – falling / rising
Mission 3

Why do young people take risks?

  • Reasons often given: curiosity, the group, stress, the search for thrills, feeling invincible.
  • Risk is not only drugs: dangerous driving, extreme sports, online challenges.
  • les sports extrêmes – extreme sports · le saut à l'élastique – bungee jumping · le parapente – paragliding
  • Some risks are chosen and prepared (training, equipment); others are suffered (addiction).
  • une conduite à risque – risky behavior · la dépendance – addiction, dependence · se sentir invincible – to feel invincible
  • Watch out: risquer de + infinitive = to be likely to (something bad): Tu risques de te blesser – You could hurt yourself.
Quick check
?En 2022, 15,6 % des jeunes de 17 ans fumaient tous les jours, contre 25,1 % en 2017. Which summary is accurate?
Mission 3

What the state does: prevention laws

  • 1991: loi Évin: strict limits on advertizing for tobacco and alcohol.
  • 2007–2008: smoking banned in enclosed public places, then in cafés and restaurants.
  • 2009: sale of tobacco and alcohol to under-18s banned.
  • 2017: plain packaging for cigarettes (le paquet neutre).
  • 2025: disposable e-cigarettes (les puffs) banned.
  • 29 June 2025: smoking banned on beaches, in parks, at bus shelters and near schools; fine €135.
  • la prévention – prevention · interdire – to ban · une amende – a fine · un fumeur / une fumeuse – a smoker
Mission 3

Diet and nutrition

  • Le repas gastronomique des Français (sitting down together, several courses) is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list (2010).
  • Nutri-Score (since 2017): a label from A (green) to E (red) on the front of food packs. It is voluntary for brands.
  • 2016: large supermarkets must give unsold food that is still edible to charities instead of destroying it.
  • Debates: la malbouffe (junk food), sugar in drinks, organic food, eating meat.
  • l'équilibre alimentaire – a balanced diet · grignoter – to snack · le gaspillage alimentaire – food waste · bio – organic (invariable: des produits bio)
Quick check
?Choose the best French for: "Young people are drinking less and less alcohol."
Mission 3

Stress and mental health

  • Pressure points for students: exams (le bac), choosing a course on Parcoursup, social media, the future.
  • Mental health was France's grande cause nationale for 2025, extended to 2026.
  • Mon soutien psy: up to 12 sessions a year with a psychologist, reimbursed by health insurance.
  • Help lines: 3114 (suicide prevention, 24/7) · Fil Santé Jeunes (for 12–25s).
  • la santé mentale – mental health · le mal-être – unhappiness, distress · être stressé(e) · l'anxiété (f) · demander de l'aide – to ask for help
  • Watch out: Je suis stressée (a girl speaking) agrees; le stress is a masculine noun.
Mission 3

Managing well-being

  • Physical activity: since 2022, primary schools must give 30 minutes of physical activity every day.
  • After Paris 2024, debate about turning the Olympic interest into regular sport for young people.
  • Sleep: screens late at night cut sleep; teenagers need more sleep than adults.
  • Balance: l'équilibre entre vie professionnelle et vie privée – work–life balance; leisure, relaxation, time with friends.
  • se détendre – to relax · faire du sport · bien dormir · déconnecter – to switch off (from screens)
  • Plus on dort mal, plus on a du mal à gérer le stress. – The worse you sleep, the harder stress is to manage.
Mission 3

Argue both sides

  • Should the state protect people from themselves?
  • Yes (arguments supporters make):
  • La prévention sauve des vies. – Prevention saves lives.
  • Les maladies liées au tabac coûtent cher à la collectivité. – Illnesses linked to tobacco cost society a lot.
  • No (arguments supporters make):
  • Chaque adulte doit rester libre de ses choix. – Every adult must stay free to make their own choices.
  • Trop d'interdictions rendent le risque plus attirant. – Too many bans make risk more attractive.
  • Linkers: Il est vrai que … ; toutefois … · Les partisans de … estiment que … · En revanche …
  • Your turn: "The more you ban, the more young people want to try."
    Show answer

    Plus on interdit, plus les jeunes ont envie d'essayer.

Mission 4

A freedom written into law

  • 1789, Declaration of the Rights of Man, article 11: « La libre communication des pensées et des opinions est un des droits les plus précieux de l'Homme. »
  • Law of 29 July 1881 on the freedom of the press: still the core text today.
  • Freedom has limits set by law:
  • la diffamation – defamation (a factual claim that damages a person's honor or reputation);
  • l'injure – insult;
  • la provocation à la haine – incitement to hatred or violence (e.g. on grounds of origin or religion);
  • l'apologie du terrorisme – glorifying terrorism.
  • Blasphemy is not an offense. You may mock or criticize a religion; you may not insult or incite hatred against people for their faith.
  • Watch out: la liberté d'expression – freedom of expression; la liberté de la presse – press freedom.
Quick check
?A French cartoonist publishes a drawing that mocks a religion's beliefs. No individual is targeted and there is no call to hatred. Under French law, what is the position?
Mission 4

When cartoons cost lives

  • Charlie Hebdo: a satirical weekly that had published caricatures of the Prophet Muhammad.
  • 7 January 2015: two gunmen attack its Paris office: 12 people killed, including cartoonists and two police officers.
  • 11 January 2015: about 4 million people march across France. Slogan: Je suis Charlie.
  • 16 October 2020: Samuel Paty, a history teacher, is murdered after showing caricatures in a lesson on freedom of expression.
  • The debate after these attacks (violence is condemned on all sides):
  • some defend an unconditional droit à la caricature – right to caricature;
  • others call for restraint out of respect for believers' feelings.
  • Watch out: un dessinateur – a cartoonist; un dessin de presse – a press cartoon.
Mission 4

The press: print, online, public

  • National dailies (les quotidiens nationaux): Le Monde, Le Figaro, Libération, Les Échos, L'Équipe (sport).
  • Regional press is strong: Ouest-France is the French daily with the biggest circulation.
  • Print sales fall; papers rely on digital subscriptions (les abonnements numériques) and paywalls.
  • Public broadcasting: France Télévisions, Radio France. The TV license fee (la redevance) was abolished in 2022; funding now comes from a share of VAT revenue.
  • Francophone media: TV5Monde, RFI (Radio France Internationale), Le Devoir (Québec), Le Soir (Belgium).
  • Your turn: "Fewer and fewer young people read a printed newspaper."
    Show answer

    De moins en moins de jeunes lisent un journal papier.

Quick check

Mission 4

Online: speed, falsehoods, rules

  • Most young people get news from social media (les réseaux sociaux), often through video.
  • Risks: les infox / les fake news – false news; la désinformation; le cyberharcèlement – online harassment; les bulles de filtre – filter bubbles.
  • 2018 law against the manipulation of information: in the three months before a national election, a judge, ruling within 48 hours, can order the spread of false news online to be stopped.
  • Arcom (since 2022): the regulator for broadcasting and online content.
  • EU Digital Services Act (fully in force 2024): platforms must remove illegal content and explain how their algorithms work.
  • Watch out: vérifier ses sources – to check your sources; un(e) internaute – an internet user.
Mission 4

Media, society and politics

  • Reporters sans frontières (RSF) 2026 index: France 25th of 180 countries.
  • RSF's concerns for France: ownership concentrated in a few large groups; protection of journalists' sources; unstable funding for public media.
  • Debate on ownership: business owners such as the Bolloré group (CNews, Europe 1, Le JDD) own several media.
  • Supporters say private investment keeps titles alive; critics fear owners influence editorial lines.
  • The C8 case: Arcom did not renew the TV channel's free-to-air license; C8 stopped broadcasting on the TNT on 28 February 2025.
  • Arcom cited repeated breaches of its obligations (it had been fined several times); the Conseil d'État upheld the decision.
  • Supporters: a regulator applying the rules. Critics, including the channel's presenters: a blow to pluralism.
Quick check
?L'Arcom n'a pas renouvelé l'autorisation de diffusion de C8 sur la TNT. What does this mean?
Mission 4

Faut-il limiter la liberté d'expression ? Argue both sides

  • For firmer limits (arguments supporters make):
  • Les mots peuvent blesser et pousser à la violence, surtout sur les réseaux sociaux. – Words can hurt and lead to violence, especially on social media.
  • Les plateformes devraient retirer plus vite les contenus haineux. – Platforms should remove hateful content faster.
  • Against new limits (arguments supporters make):
  • Ce qui choque les uns doit pouvoir être dit, sinon c'est la censure. – What shocks some people must be sayable, or it is censorship.
  • Qui décidera de ce qui est vrai ? Ni l'État ni les plateformes ne devraient jouer ce rôle. – Who will decide what is true? Neither the state nor platforms should play that role.
  • Linkers: D'un côté … de l'autre … · Certes …, mais … · Il n'en reste pas moins que …
  • Your turn: "It is true that satire shocks, but it is essential in a democracy."
    Show answer

    Certes, la satire choque, mais elle est essentielle en démocratie.

Mission 5

What is laïcité?

  • La laïcité: the State is separate from religions and neutral toward them; every citizen is free to believe or not to believe.
  • Law of 9 December 1905 (separation of the Churches and the State):
  • Art. 1: « La République assure la liberté de conscience. Elle garantit le libre exercice des cultes… »
  • Art. 2: « La République ne reconnaît, ne salarie ni ne subventionne aucun culte. »
  • Constitution of 1958, art. 1: « La France est une République indivisible, laïque, démocratique et sociale. »
  • Three principles: neutrality of the State · freedom of conscience and worship · equality of citizens whatever their beliefs.
  • Watch out: laïque works for both genders (une école laïque, un État laïque); the masculine spelling laïc also exists (un État laïc). Un culte = a religion, a form of worship; "a cult" is une secte.
Mission 5

How laïcité was built

  • 1789: Declaration of the Rights of Man, art. 10: no one may be troubled for their opinions, « même religieuses ».
  • 1881–1882: Jules Ferry laws: state primary school becomes free, compulsory and secular; one day off a week lets families arrange religious teaching outside school.
  • 1905: separation law.
  • 1946 / 1958: laïcité written into the Constitution.
  • 1989: three girls excluded from a collège in Creil for wearing headscarves. The Conseil d'État says religious signs are not in themselves incompatible with laïcité.
  • 2004: law banning conspicuous religious signs for pupils in state schools.
  • 2010: law banning covering the face in public space.
  • 2021: law "reinforcing respect for the principles of the Republic".
  • 2023: the abaya and qamis (long robes) banned in state schools.

Mission 5

Who must be neutral?

  • The State and its agents (civil servants, state-school teachers, hospital and town-hall staff): no religious sign at work, whatever its size.
  • Users of public services (patients, parents, university students, people at the town hall): free to wear religious signs.
  • Exception: pupils in state primary and secondary schools. The 2004 law bans signs by which pupils conspicuously show a religion (headscarf, kippah, large cross). Discreet signs are allowed.
  • Why the difference? Pupils are minors and school is compulsory; adult students at university are free users.
  • Everyone, everywhere in public space: no face covering (2010 law; fine up to €150).
  • Private companies: employees are free, unless the company's rules require neutrality for a justified reason (law of 2016).
  • Watch out: the 2004 law uses ostensiblement (visibly, conspicuously), not ostentatoire (showy).
Mission 5

Religion in France today

  • The census has not asked about religion since 1872. Figures come from surveys.
  • INSEE survey (2019–2020, people aged 18–59): 51% no religion · 29% Catholic · 10% Muslim · 9% other Christian.
  • Christian heritage: 6 of 11 public holidays have Christian origins (e.g. la Toussaint, Christmas).
  • Churches built before 1905 belong to the State or the communes, which maintain them (e.g. Notre-Dame de Paris belongs to the State).
  • Private schools, mostly Catholic, can sign a contract with the State: it pays their teachers; they follow the national curriculum.
  • Alsace-Moselle was German in 1905, so the law never applied there. It keeps the 1801 Concordat: the State pays clergy of four religions (Catholic, Lutheran, Reformed, Jewish); state schools offer religious education.

Mission 5

Current debates

  • 2016: some seaside towns banned the burkini. The Conseil d'État suspended one ban: no proven threat to public order.
  • 2016: Christmas cribs in town halls are allowed only as a cultural or festive display, not as a religious statement (Conseil d'État).
  • 2023: the Conseil d'État upheld the ban on the abaya in state schools.
  • Sport: in 2023 the Conseil d'État upheld the French Football Federation's ban on religious signs in its matches. At the 2024 Olympics, French athletes could not wear a headscarf.
  • February 2025: the Senate voted to ban religious signs in competitions run by sports federations. In early 2026 the National Assembly had still not debated the bill.
Mission 5

Two readings: argue both sides

  • A strict reading (arguments supporters make):
  • L'école doit protéger les élèves des pressions religieuses. – School must protect pupils from religious pressure.
  • Un espace public neutre permet à chacun de se sentir égal. – A neutral public space lets everyone feel equal.
  • An open reading (arguments supporters make):
  • La loi de 1905 impose la neutralité à l'État, pas aux citoyens. – The 1905 law requires neutrality of the State, not of citizens.
  • Ces interdictions visent surtout les musulmanes et risquent de les exclure. – These bans mainly affect Muslim women and may exclude them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Maternelle (compulsory from 3) → élémentaire → collège (brevet) → lycée (bac). Passer = sit; réussir / avoir = pass.
  • Bac 2025: 91.6% pass; 80.1% of a generation. Parcoursup since 2018; licence €178 a year.
  • Student issues: précarité, housing, grants, €1 CROUS meals for all since May 2026; phones banned up to lycée since September 2026.

Mission 2

  • 35-hour week (1998/2000), five weeks' paid holiday, SMIC; pension age held at 62 years 9 months until January 2028.
  • Right to strike in the 1946 Preamble; key movements 1968, 1995, 2019–20, 2023. Faire grève, être en grève depuis + present.
  • Pay gap (INSEE 2024): 21.8% overall, about 14% at equal hours, 3.6% in the same job; laws 1972, 2011, 2019 index, 2021.

Mission 3

  • 17-year-olds (ESCAPAD 2022): daily smoking 15.6% (25.1% in 2017); cannabis down; binge drinking still common.
  • Prevention: loi Évin 1991 → indoor bans 2007–08 → 18 minimum age 2009 → plain packs 2017 → outdoor bans 2025.
  • Mental health: national cause 2025–2026; Mon soutien psy (12 sessions); 3114.

Mission 4

  • Freedom of expression: 1789 (art. 11), law of 29 July 1881; limits: defamation, insult, incitement to hatred, glorifying terrorism; blasphemy is not an offense.
  • 2015 Charlie Hebdo, 2020 Samuel Paty; online rules: 2018 law, Arcom (2022), EU DSA.
  • RSF 2026: France 25th of 180; debates on ownership and the C8 decision (2025).

Mission 5

  • 1905: the State recognizes, pays and subsidizes no religion and guarantees freedom of worship; laïcité is in the 1958 Constitution.
  • State agents must be neutral; users are free; pupils in state schools may not wear conspicuous signs (2004); face covering is banned in public space (2010).
  • Compare: the US separates church and state through the First Amendment, but public-school students may usually wear religious clothing.
