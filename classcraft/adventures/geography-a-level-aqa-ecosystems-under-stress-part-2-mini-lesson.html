Mission 1: Leave bare rock and a pond untouched in lowland Britain for centuries. Why do both become similar woodland?

  • 3.1.6.4 succession and climatic climax in the British Isles illustrated by lithoseres and hydroseres

Mission 1

Succession

  • Succession: the change in a plant community over time at one site.
  • Sere: the whole sequence; each step is a seral stage.
  • Pioneer species colonise first; each stage alters the site so others can follow.
  • Pioneers are low plants that can live with little or no soil. A UK team studying bare chalk found that short bryophytes (mosses and their relatives) that often produce spores were more abundant early in succession, reflecting their "high colonising abilities".
  • Primary succession starts on a surface with no soil (bare rock, open water, new sand). Secondary succession starts where soil remains (abandoned field, felled wood).
  • Climatic climax: the final, stable community in balance with the regional climate.
Mission 1

Why stages replace each other

  • Pioneers trap dust, add dead matter and help weather the surface: soil begins and deepens.
  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients; taller plants then shade out the pioneers.
  • Over the sere: biomass, height, soil depth and usually species diversity increase.
  • Competition decides which species win at each stage; the climax species are the best competitors in that climate.
Mission 1

Lithosere: succession on bare rock

  • Lithosere: a sere that starts on bare rock.
  • Typical stages: lichens → mosses → grasses and herbs → shrubs (e.g. hawthorn, bramble) → pioneer trees (e.g. birch) → oak woodland.
  • A UK team followed primary succession on bare chalk across a 33-year chronosequence.
  • "Shorter bryophytes" (mosses and their relatives) that often produce spores were more abundant early in succession.
  • Later, vascular plants with a larger leaf area for their mass were more likely to appear.
Quick check
?Why are low plants such as mosses usually among the first colonisers of bare rock?
Mission 1

Hydrosere: succession in water

  • Hydrosere: a sere that starts in open fresh water.
  • Typical stages: submerged plants → floating-leaved plants (water lilies) → reed-swamp → fen (sedges) → carr (wet woodland of alder and willow) → drier woodland.
  • Dead plants and trapped silt build up, so the water gets shallower and the land edge advances.
  • Esthwaite North Fen (Lake District) was surveyed in 1914–16, 1929 and 1967–9.
  • The reed-swamp "advanced southward into open water"; since 1929 the main change was the spread of alder (Alnus glutinosa) woodland.
  • The edge moved 0.2–3.0 m a year, varying between periods; extra phosphate and nitrate may also be driving change.
Quick check
?At Esthwaite North Fen the reed-swamp edge moved 0.2–3.0 m a year. Over 50 years, what range of advance does that give?
Mission 1

Climatic climax: temperate deciduous woodland

  • In most of lowland Britain and Ireland, seres on bare rock and in open water both end in temperate deciduous woodland, dominated by oak (with ash, beech, hazel). The climate decides the end point, not the starting surface.
  • Climate (Oxford, 1991–2020; our calculation from Met Office station data):
  • January mean about 5 °C; July about 18 °C.
  • About 680 mm of rain a year, spread through the year (monthly means 43–73 mm).
  • Mild, moist conditions give a long growing season; cold winters make shedding leaves the best strategy.
Mission 1

Woodland structure, soil and life

  • Layers: canopy (oak, ash, beech); shrub layer (hazel, holly, hawthorn); field layer (bluebell, bramble, ferns); ground layer (mosses).
  • Soil: brown earth: leaf litter decays within a year or so; earthworms mix humus into a fertile, well-drained soil.
  • Fauna: many insects on oak; birds (woodpeckers, tits); mammals such as badgers and deer.
  • Biomass is mostly in the trees; nutrients cycle quickly through litter and soil.
Quick check
?Why do many woodland flowers, such as bluebells, flower in early spring?
Mission check

?Two identical bare-chalk quarries are left alone for 300 years: one in lowland England, one in Arctic tundra far beyond the tree line. They end with very different vegetation. Why?

The starting surface sets the sere, but the regional climate sets the end point. In lowland Britain and Ireland that end point is temperate deciduous woodland, unless people or local conditions hold it back.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.2 succession: seral stages, climatic climax, sub-climax, plagioclimax (sub-climax and plagioclimax here)

Mission 2

When succession stops early

  • A sere normally runs from pioneers to the climatic climax: in lowland Britain, temperate deciduous woodland.
  • But many British landscapes are not at climax. Something holds them back.
  • Two kinds of hold-up: natural factors (sub-climax) and human activity (plagioclimax).
Mission 2

Sub-climax

  • Sub-climax: a stage where succession is halted by a natural local factor, so the climatic climax is not reached.
  • Examples of arresting factors: permanently waterlogged ground; very thin or poor soil; exposure to wind or salt spray; frequent natural disturbance.
  • If the factor changes (e.g. the ground dries out), succession can continue.
  • Exam tip: name the factor and explain how it stops the next stage's species establishing.
Mission 2

Plagioclimax

  • Plagioclimax: a stable community held or deflected by human activity.
  • Typical actions: burning, grazing by livestock, mowing, drainage, tree felling.
  • It lasts only as long as the activity continues.
  • Examples in Britain: heather moorland (burning and grazing); sheep-grazed downland; hay meadows.
Quick check
?Which is the clearest example of a plagioclimax?
Mission 2

Heather moorland: how it began

  • Dominant plant: heather (Calluna vulgaris), a low evergreen shrub.
  • "Most heaths dominated by heather are a product of interference by humans and were historically preceded by forest".
  • Forest clearance opened the land; burning and grazing then kept trees out.
  • "Heaths have been maintained for sheep and red grouse, both of which are economically important".
  • Regular burning "has been the principle [sic] land management practice for heaths for over 200 years".
Mission 2

Why burn? Heather's life cycle

  • Heather plants pass through four phases on dry heaths:
  • Pioneer (0–6 years) → building (6–14) → mature (14–25) → degenerate (25+), when central branches "collapse and die".
  • After a light or moderate fire, heather resprouts from stem bases: young, nutritious shoots for sheep and grouse.
  • Grouse managers burn in patches to make "a mosaic of stands with diverse ages, heights, and densities".
  • A fire interval of about 15 years has been suggested for dry heaths at low to middle altitudes; about 20 years on wet bogs.
  • Why not wait longer? Beyond about 15 years, "the heather may be too old to sprout from stem bases".
Quick check
?A moor is burned in patches on a 15-year rotation, so every patch is burned once in 15 years. About what share of the moor is burned each year?
Mission 2

Grazing

  • Sheep eat young heather shoots and tree seedlings, so grazing helps stop trees establishing.
  • But heavy grazing eventually reduces heather cover, and summer grazing may harm it more than winter grazing.
  • So the plagioclimax depends on the right balance of burning and grazing: too little and trees invade; too much and grasses replace heather.
Mission 2

If management stops

  • "In the absence of fire, heather-dominated heaths or moors are replaced by birch and Scotch pine woodland"; in some areas bracken takes over.
  • Old heather reaches the degenerate phase and opens gaps where birch seedlings can grow.
  • Succession resumes towards woodland: the moor was a plagioclimax, not a climax.
  • Too-severe fire also deflects succession: if stem bases die, heather must regrow from seed, and severely burned areas "may remain bare for 70 years or more".
Mission 2

Managing moorland today

  • England's Heather and Grass etc. Burning (England) Regulations 2021 came into force on 1 May 2021.
  • A person "must not burn specified vegetation on a designated site on peat that is of a depth of more than 40 centimetres" without a licence.
  • A designated site here is both a European site and an SSSI.
  • Licences can be granted, e.g. "for the conservation, enhancement or management of the natural environment" or "to reduce the risk of wildfire", or where land is inaccessible to mechanical cutting: cutting is the main alternative to fire.
  • Exam move ("Assess"): weigh what burning keeps (heather, grouse, sheep grazing) against what it prevents (woodland succession) and the protection the law gives to deep peat.
Quick check
?Under the 2021 Regulations, which of these would need a licence?
Mission check

?Sheep have grazed a chalk hillside in southern England for centuries, keeping a short, flower-rich grassland. Grazing stops completely. What is most likely to happen, and why?

A plagioclimax lasts only while people keep up the burning, grazing or mowing that holds it. Heather moorland, like grazed downland, is a human-made stage; stop the management and succession resumes towards woodland.

Mission 3: Coral reefs cover a tiny part of the ocean floor. What decides where they can grow?

Make a guess: Coral reefs cover a tiny part of the ocean floor. What decides where they can grow?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.5 distribution and main characteristics of coral reef ecosystems; environmental conditions for reef development
  • 3.1.6.5 named, located reef: natural factors in health and survival (water temperature, acidity, salinity, algal blooms)
Mission 3

What a reef is

  • Reef-building corals are animals (polyps) that lay down limestone skeletons.
  • They live in partnership with algae called zooxanthellae: the coral gives shelter; the algae give it carbohydrates and oxygen.
  • The algae photosynthesise, so they "need light in order to produce food".
  • Reefs cover only about 1% of the world's oceans but are home to at least 25% of marine life.
Mission 3

Where reefs are

  • Most reef-building corals live in shallow tropical and subtropical waters, from about 35° N to 35° S.
  • They are "generally confined to tropical and semi-tropical waters".
  • Other corals exist in deep, dark, cold water, but they lack zooxanthellae and do not build shallow reefs.
  • So reef distribution follows warm, sunlit, salty shallow seas.
Mission 3

Conditions for reef growth

  • Temperature: they "cannot tolerate water temperatures below 64° Fahrenheit (18° Celsius)"; many grow best at 23–29 °C.
  • Salinity: they need very salty water, 32 to 42 parts per thousand.
  • Light: water must be clear; most reef-builders are limited to where light reaches about 30 m deep.
  • Some survive up to 40 °C for short periods, but sustained heat causes stress and bleaching.
Quick check
?Why are reefs often missing off the mouths of large tropical rivers?
Mission 3

Reef types and growth

  • Fringing reefs, the most common, grow seaward directly from the shore.
  • Barrier reefs lie farther out, separated from land by a lagoon.
  • Atolls form when a fringing reef keeps growing upward around a volcanic island that sinks below sea level.
  • Growth is slow: massive corals 0.3–2 cm a year; branching corals up to 10 cm a year.
Mission 3

A named reef: the Great Barrier Reef

  • Off Queensland, north-east Australia; it covers 344,400 km².
  • It is made up of about 3,000 individual reefs.
  • The Australian Institute of Marine Science (AIMS) surveys coral cover each year in three regions: Northern, Central, Southern.
  • Next: four natural factors in its health: temperature, acidity, salinity, algal blooms.
Mission 3

Water temperature: bleaching

  • Under heat stress, corals "expel the algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues" and turn white: bleaching.
  • A bleached coral "is not dead", but it can die if stress continues.
  • The 2024 event was the fifth mass bleaching on the reef since 2016.
  • Heat returned in summer 2025, with sea-surface temperatures 1–2.5 °C above average: a sixth event.
  • After the 2024 event, Northern hard coral cover fell from 39.8% (2024) to 30.0% (2025); bleaching was the main cause of death.
  • Recovery is possible: 35.1% in 2026, after monsoon cloud cooled the sea.
Quick check
?Northern Great Barrier Reef coral cover fell from 39.8% to 30.0% between 2024 and 2025. By about what percentage did coral cover fall?
Mission 3

Acidity

  • The ocean absorbs CO₂; surface pH has fallen by 0.1 units, about a 30% rise in acidity.
  • Fewer carbonate ions remain for organisms that build shells and skeletons.
  • So corals build their limestone more slowly and become more fragile.
  • Acidity acts everywhere on the reef at once; it adds to heat stress rather than replacing it.
Mission 3

Salinity: floods from the land

  • Heavy storm rain can dilute sea water near shore, and runoff can bleach corals.
  • On the reef, 2024 brought cyclones and floods; AIMS lists freshwater inundation among the causes of coral loss.
  • Corals need 32–42 parts per thousand, so a flood plume of fresh water can push salinity below their range.
  • Inshore reefs near river mouths are most exposed.
Mission 3

Algal blooms and starfish

  • Extra nutrients in the water boost phytoplankton (an algal bloom).
  • More plankton feeds more larvae of the crown-of-thorns starfish (COTS), so more survive.
  • Since 1962, COTS outbreaks have been a major cause of coral loss on the reef; they can kill up to 90% of corals on affected reefs.
  • AIMS found COTS on 27 surveyed reefs in both 2025 and 2026.
Quick check
?How can an algal bloom lead to coral loss on the Great Barrier Reef?
Mission check

?A site at 20° S has clear water, a mean temperature of 26 °C and a salinity of 35 parts per thousand, but the sea floor is 60 m deep. No reef-building corals grow there. Which condition is missing?

Reefs grow only where warm, salty, clear and shallow water coincide. On the Great Barrier Reef, natural stresses (heat, acidity, fresh water, algal blooms feeding starfish) decide how healthy that growth stays.

Mission 4: The Great Barrier Reef is called well managed. So why is its outlook still one of deterioration?

  • 3.1.6.5 named reef: human impacts (drainage basin schemes, onshore development, desalination, pollution, tourism, fishing)

Mission 4

The Great Barrier Reef

  • Off the coast of Queensland, north-east Australia: the Marine Park covers 344,400 km², with about 3,000 coral reefs.
  • World Heritage since 1981.
  • Reefs grow in warm, clear, shallow, sunlit, salty water: corals depend on algae living in their tissue that need light.
  • The last mission covered the four natural factors in its health: water temperature, acidity, salinity and algal blooms. This mission adds what people do to it, and what its future looks like.
Quick check
?A reef has clean, clear water and no fishing, but after a summer of record sea temperatures its corals turn white. What is happening?
Mission 4

Human impact: the drainage basin

  • 35 major catchments drain into the Reef region.
  • Grazing land is "the main contributor of fine sediment and particulate nitrogen"; sugarcane is "the primary source of excess nutrients (dissolved) and pesticides".
  • Large drainage basin schemes (land clearing, irrigation, dams) change how much water, sediment and nutrient reach the coast.
  • "Poor water quality continues to affect many coastal and inshore areas", though it has "continued to improve slowly" since 2019.
Mission 4

Human impact: onshore development, desalination and pollution

  • Reef water quality has declined since European settlement "due to coastal development and agricultural activities".
  • Pollution sources include urban areas, sewage treatment plants, aquaculture, industry and ports.
  • Desalination plants return brine (saltier, sometimes warmer, water) to the sea: a local salinity stress near outfalls.
Mission 4

Human impact: tourism and fishing

  • Tourism: the Reef generates $7.9 billion a year in tourism; it contributes $9 billion to Australia's economy and supports 77,000 jobs (a study for the Great Barrier Reef Foundation).
  • Impacts: anchors, boat traffic, trampling; managed by zoning and permits.
  • Fishing: "fishing activities continue to have a high impact on the Region's values".
  • Zoning sets which activities are allowed, banned or need a permit in each zone.
Quick check
?UNESCO reports 115,000 km² of "no-take" and "no-entry" zones in the Great Barrier Reef; the Marine Park covers 344,400 km². What share is closed to taking, to the nearest whole per cent?
Mission 4

Recent condition

  • Hard coral cover, northern GBR: 39.8% (2024) to 30.0% (2025), "the largest annual decline recorded for this region since monitoring began". Southern GBR: 38.9% to 26.9%.
  • AIMS: "the mass coral bleaching event was the primary source of coral mortality".
  • 2026: northern cover recovered to 35.1%, helped by monsoon cloud and cooler water; southern cover was 26.4%.
Mission 4

Future prospects

  • AIMS: mass bleaching events "are now occurring with increasing frequency, while recovery periods are decreasing".
  • IPCC: coral reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C of warming, and by more than 99% at 2 °C.
  • Reef Authority (2024): the outlook "remains one of future deterioration due largely to climate change".
  • Yet the Region "continues to be well-managed overall".
Mission 4

Local control, global threat

  • What managers control: fishing (zoning), tourism (permits), runoff (farm practice), crown-of-thorns culling.
  • What they cannot: ocean heat and acidity, driven by global CO₂ emissions.
  • Local actions build resilience: healthier reefs recover faster between bleaching events. But they cannot stop the heat itself.
  • Exam move: in a "future prospects" answer, separate local pressures (manageable) from global ones (not), then judge which dominates.
Mission check

?A Caribbean reef has strict no-fishing zones and has cut farm runoff. Yet its coral cover keeps falling after repeated marine heatwaves. Why is its outlook still poor?

The Great Barrier Reef is well managed locally, but its outlook is set globally: heat stress (six mass bleaching events since 2016) and acidification. Local action on fishing, tourism and runoff builds resilience; only cutting emissions removes the main threat.

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.6 a distinctive local ecosystem (e.g. heathland, dune system): characteristics; flora and fauna adaptations to climate, soil and soil moisture

Mission 5

The case: Murlough, County Down

  • A dune system on Dundrum Bay, estimated at up to 6,000 years old.
  • Owned by the National Trust; managed as Ireland's first nature reserve since 1967.
  • Protected as an ASSI and a Special Area of Conservation.
  • "The largest and most important dune heath site in Northern Ireland".
Mission 5

Characteristics: a complete sequence

  • From the sea inland: foredunes → yellow dunes → grey dunes → dune grassland → acid dune heath and scrub → saltmarsh.
  • Older dunes are leached: calcium removed, soil more acidic, so heather and bell heather dominate.
  • Rich in insects: 21 butterfly species (71% of Northern Ireland's), 213 moth species (48%).
  • Home to the marsh fritillary, a butterfly "threatened throughout Europe".
Mission 5

Abiotic, biotic and the system

  • Abiotic: mobile and fixed sand, wind, salt spray, leached acidic soil, a fairly dry climate (possibly a rain shadow of the Mourne Mountains).
  • Biotic: marram, heather, grasses, insects, birds, rabbits, grazing cattle and ponies.
Mission 5

Adaptations to climate, soil and soil moisture

  • Marram thrives where sand is moving and dominates dune building; shifting dunes need active sand movement.
  • Sand couch and lyme-grass are "salt-tolerant, sand-binding grasses".
  • Dune soils drain freely; Murlough "is rather dry" and wet slack communities "are not well developed".
  • Heather dominates the acidic, leached older dunes.
  • Fauna: heat-loving insects need bare sand that warms quickly; solitary bees and wasps dig nesting burrows in it.
Quick check
?Why are patches of bare sand important for Murlough's insects?
Mission 5

Local factors in ecological change

  • Planned introduction: sea buckthorn, "not native to Ireland", introduced by the Downshire family in the 1890s to stabilise the dunes.
    • Its thorny thickets shade out native plants; in storms it can leave "a sheer cliff rather than a smooth slope".
    • Only sycamore survives its shade; "sycamore woodland now stands where once had been sand dune".
  • Agriculture (grazing): without grazing, "Bracken and coarse grasses can quickly take over and ultimately woody species may become dominant".
  • Unplanned introductions: species that arrive by accident rather than by planting.
  • Urban and recreational change: cleaning amenity beaches removes the strandline that traps sand for new dunes; heavy trampling causes "loss of cover and soil erosion".
Mission 5

Impacts: how is Murlough doing?

  • DAERA 2025/26 condition data for Murlough ASSI:
    • all five dune habitat features and the saltmarsh: unfavourable (last assessed 2011);
    • rare dune plants such as carline thistle and viper's-bugloss: unfavourable (2022);
    • grass/heath and coastal moth invertebrate assemblages: favourable (2022).
  • Designation has not kept every feature in good condition.
Mission 5

Management and conservation

  • Grazing: low-intensity grazing by cattle and ponies keeps open areas and a diverse sward; rabbits help keep the sward short.
  • Holding succession at the heath stage by management creates a plagioclimax.
  • Removing the introduction: a five-year project, part-funded by NIEA, to clear about 4 ha of sea buckthorn, working in winter before bird nesting.
  • Access: boardwalk restoration to protect the dunes; "sensitive management of access" where trampling prevents recovery.
  • Law: as an ASSI, changes in grazing and burning need consent.
Quick check
?Why does the reserve use grazing rather than leaving the dunes untouched?
Mission 5

Sustainable development: challenges and opportunities

  • Challenges: invasive scrub, under- or over-grazing, visitor pressure on fragile dunes, and coastal erosion (a post-storm erosion closure of the beach section of a trail).
  • Opportunities: grazing as conservation; volunteers and public funding (NIEA) for restoration; visitors who value wildlife.
  • Sustainability means keeping the dynamic dunes and heath for future visitors and wildlife while allowing access.
Mission check

?A town-edge heathland has rhododendron (planted on a Victorian estate) spreading into it, birch saplings invading since grazing stopped, and a popular path widening into bare ground. Which plan best fits the Murlough evidence?

At Murlough, sea buckthorn planted in the 1890s to fix the dunes became an invasive that shades out native species, so it is being removed; grazing, access management and ASSI protection keep the dune heath as a managed plagioclimax.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Ranchers, miners and river families share the Amazon. Why does forest change hit each so differently?

Make a guess: Ranchers, miners and river families share the Amazon. Why does forest change hit each so differently?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.7 case study of a specified region experiencing ecological change: nature of and reasons for the change; how the community's economic, social and political character reflects its ecological setting; how the community responds
Mission 6

The region: the Brazilian Amazon

  • The rainforest of Brazil's Legal Amazon, drained by the Amazon river system.
  • Amazonia holds roughly 16,000 tree species.
  • Brazil's space institute, INPE, has measured clearing here every year since 1988.
  • Ecological setting: forest, rivers and floodplains. Each supports a different way of life.
Mission 6

The nature of the change

  • Clearing: forest turned to pasture and cropland.
  • Degradation: selective logging affected an area 60–123% as large as clear-felling in 1999–2002.
  • Carbon: south-eastern Amazonia now acts as a net carbon source.
  • Drought: in October 2023 the river at Manaus fell to its lowest level since 1902; one lake reached 41°C through its whole depth.
Mission 6

Reasons for the change

  • Farming: across the tropics, 90–99% of clearing in 2011–15 was driven by agriculture. In South America, soy spread mostly onto pasture first cleared for cattle.
  • Access: nearly 95% of clearing was within 5.5 km of a road or 1 km of a river.
  • Mining: garimpo (small-scale gold mining) area grew about 1,200%, 1985–2022.
  • Climate: longer dry seasons plus clearing increase stress and fire.
Quick check
?Which pair of factors best explains where Amazon clearing happens?
Mission 6

Economic character reflects the setting

  • A frontier economy built on land and resources: cattle, soy, timber, gold, hydropower.
  • Land cleared for cattle later becomes soy fields; in 2025, 73.6% of China's soybean imports by weight came from Brazil.
  • Mining's local gains are "limited" and "tied to fluctuations in global mineral prices".
  • So incomes depend on converting forest and on world prices.
Mission 6

Social character reflects the setting

  • River communities live from fishing on the floodplains. At Belo Monte dam, resettlement and river change "undermined livelihoods for fisheries-dependent households".
  • The 2023 drought had "unprecedented" social and ecological impacts in the central Amazon, including the deaths of 209 river dolphins.
  • Impaired river transport was the main socio-economic impact, cutting access to healthcare, drinking water and markets.
  • Illegality: at least 77% of 2022 gold-mining sites showed explicit signs of being illegal.
Mission 6

Political character reflects the setting

  • The forest is a contested national and global resource.
  • Federal tools: law enforcement, limits on credit for farmers who clear, and new protected areas.
  • State politics can pull the other way: in January 2026 traders left the soy moratorium after a Mato Grosso state law.
  • Global: the UN Climate Change Conference, COP30, was held in Belém, an Amazon city, in November 2025.
Quick check
?Which statement best links a community's character to its ecological setting?
Mission 6

Responses: protected areas and Indigenous lands

  • Protected areas shelter 54% of the Brazilian Amazon's remaining forest.
  • Indigenous lands, strictly protected areas and sustainable-use reserves all held back clearing in 1997–2008.
  • New protected areas caused 37% of the fall in clearing from 2004 to 2006.
Mission 6

Responses: supply chains and policy

  • Soy Moratorium (from 2006): grain traders agreed not to buy soy grown on recently cleared land.
  • It prevented about 18,000 km² (± 9,000) of clearing in 2006–16, helped by land registers and satellite monitoring.
  • Together, enforcement, soy and beef supply-chain deals, credit limits and protected areas, plus falling demand for new clearing, contributed to a 70% fall in clearing.
  • In 2026 major traders left the moratorium; environmentalists said this "effectively ended" it.
Mission 6

Outcome so far

  • INPE's estimate for August 2025 to July 2026: 3,335 km² cleared, the lowest since 1988.
  • The year before: 5,731 km².
  • The 2025–26 value is an estimate, to be consolidated later.
Quick check
?Clearing fell from 5,731 km² (2024–25) to 3,335 km² (2025–26). What is the percentage fall?
Mission 6

How well is the community responding?

  • Strengths: protected areas and enforcement work when funded; combined policies cut clearing by about 70%.
  • Costs: the protected-area network costs Brazil about US$147 billion (± 53 billion, net present value) in forgone profits and investment.
  • Fragility: supply-chain deals depend on companies and state politics.
  • Risk: by 2050, 10–47% of Amazon forests could face combined disturbances that may trigger ecosystem transitions.
  • Exam move (AQA "To what extent"): judge success separately for each group: ranchers, miners, river families, Indigenous peoples.
Mission check

?A government in another forest region expands protected areas, including Indigenous lands, and limits farm credit for anyone who clears forest. Which pair of effects is best supported?

A community's economic, social and political character grows from how it uses its ecological setting. In the Amazon, ranching, mining, fishing and Indigenous communities face the same change but respond in different, sometimes opposing, ways.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Seres move through seral stages from pioneers to a climatic climax; each stage changes soil, light and water so new species win.
  • Lithosere (bare rock, e.g. 33-year chalk study) and hydrosere (Esthwaite North Fen: reed-swamp advance and alder carr) both lead towards oak-dominated temperate deciduous woodland (Oxford: ~5 °C Jan, ~18 °C Jul, ~680 mm).

Mission 2

  • Sub-climax: succession halted by a natural factor (waterlogging, poor soil, exposure). Plagioclimax: held by human activity (burning, grazing, mowing).
  • Heather moorland: created by forest clearance, kept by patch burning (about every 15 years on dry heath) and grazing for sheep and grouse; without fire it becomes birch and pine woodland. England's 2021 Regulations require a licence to burn on deep peat (> 40 cm) on designated sites.

Mission 3

  • Reef-builders need water above 18 °C (best 23–29 °C), salinity 32–42 ppt, clear light to about 30 m; so reefs lie in shallow tropical and subtropical seas, about 35° N–35° S.
  • Great Barrier Reef: heat (bleaching; six mass events since 2016, the latest in 2025), acidity (fewer carbonate ions), fresh water from floods, and nutrient-fed blooms that boost crown-of-thorns starfish.

Mission 4

  • Natural factors (water temperature, acidity, salinity, algal blooms) are joined by human ones.
  • Human factors: catchment runoff, coastal development and pollution, desalination brine, tourism and fishing. The future depends mostly on global warming.

Mission 5

  • Murlough: a dune system up to 6,000 years old with a full dune sequence (a psammosere) from foredunes to acid dune heath; rich insect fauna (71% of NI butterflies); dry, leached, low-nutrient sands.
  • Change: planned introduction (sea buckthorn, 1890s), grazing change (bracken and scrub), beach cleaning and trampling. Management: grazing, buckthorn removal (4 ha), boardwalks, ASSI consent.

Mission 6

  • The Brazilian Amazon is changing through clearing, degradation and drought, driven by cattle and soy, roads, mining, dams and a lengthening dry season.
  • Responses include protected areas and Indigenous lands, enforcement, credit limits and the Soy Moratorium; clearing fell to its lowest since 1988 in 2025–26 (INPE estimate), but the moratorium has ended and tipping-point risks remain.
