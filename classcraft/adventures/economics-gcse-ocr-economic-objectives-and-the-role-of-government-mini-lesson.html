Mission 1

Mission 1: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 1

Measuring output: GDP

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year.
  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP over time.
  • Nominal GDP uses the prices of that year, so it rises when prices rise.
  • Real GDP removes the effect of rising prices. It shows whether more is really being produced.
  • GDP per capita (per person) = GDP ÷ population. It is a better guide to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose" — not: "GDP rose", when you mean the economy produced more.
Mission 1

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP800 bn856 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)800 bn824 bn
Population40 m40.4 m
  • Nominal growth = (856 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 7%. Part of that is just higher prices.
  • Real growth = (824 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 3%.
  • Real GDP per capita: 800 bn ÷ 40 m = 20,000 → 824 bn ÷ 40.4 m = 20,396.
  • Growth per capita = (20,396 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = about 2%.
  • The population grew, so output per person grew less than total output.
Mission 1

Small rates add up

  • Growth builds on growth: each year's rise is on top of last year's.
  • Output per person doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year.
  • At 9% a year, output per person would double in about 8 years.
  • Recent and historical data: look at the trend over many years, not one year's figure.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X: real GDP 1,200 bn, population 300 million. Country Y: real GDP 400 bn, population 50 million. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Two kinds of growth on a PPC

  • A PPC shows the most an economy can produce with all its resources fully used.
  • Point A inside the curve: some workers and machines are unemployed (spare capacity).
  • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth. The economy produces more by using spare capacity.
  • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth. The economy can produce more.
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
Mission 1Graph lab

Spending and capacity

Illustrative data. Start: demand for goods = 460, productive capacity = 500, so there is spare capacity. Output = 460; price level = 100.

  • Takeaway: extra spending raises output only while there is spare capacity. After that, it mostly pushes up prices. Lasting growth needs capacity to grow too.
Quick check
?Which of these is most likely to shift a country's PPC outwards?
Mission 1

Causes of growth

  • Investment: more and better capital (machines, factories, roads).
  • Technology: new methods and inventions let each worker produce more.
  • Workforce: more workers, e.g. migration or a higher retirement age.
  • Education and training: more skilled workers produce more (human capital).
  • Natural resources: new discoveries, such as oil or minerals.
  • Government policy: stable rules, good infrastructure, support for research.
Mission 1

When growth turns negative

  • A recession: a period when real GDP falls. Firms cut jobs, and incomes fall.
  • Historical data: in the 2008 recession, the UK economy shrank by more than 6%. It took five years to get back to its pre-recession size (ONS).
  • Most years real GDP rises, but not every year. Growth is not guaranteed.
Mission 1

Reading growth figures

  • Illustrative data. A country's real GDP growth over five years: 2.1%, 1.8%, 0.5%, −0.4%, 1.2%.
  • Growth slowed in years 2 and 3: output still rose, just more slowly.
  • Real GDP fell only in year 4 (negative growth).
  • Say: "growth slowed" — not: "the economy shrank", when the rate is still above zero.
Mission 1

Benefits and costs of growth

BenefitsCosts
Higher incomes and living standardsPollution and emissions
More jobsUsing up resources such as oil and forests
More tax revenue for schools and hospitalsGains can go mostly to some people
Less povertyPrices may rise if spending grows faster than capacity
  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. This helped lift almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
Quick check
?A country's real GDP grows fast for ten years. Over the same years, its rivers become badly polluted and its forests are cut down. Which statement is best?
Mission 1Evaluate

Is growth always good?

It depends on:

  • Who gains: if most of the extra income goes to a few, most people gain little.
  • The environment: cleaner technology can let output grow with less damage.
  • How rich the country is: extra growth matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country.
Mission check

?A small country has no spare capacity: its workers and factories are all fully used. Its government wants output to keep rising for the next ten years. Which plan is most likely to work?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Mission 2: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 2

Who counts as unemployed?

  • Unemployed (the ILO measure): without a job, have looked for work in the last 4 weeks, and can start within 2 weeks.
  • Employed: did at least one hour of paid work in the week.
  • Labour force (economically active) = employed + unemployed.
  • Economically inactive: not working and not looking, e.g. students, retired people, full-time carers. They are not unemployed.
  • In the UK the survey is the Labour Force Survey (ONS).
Mission 2

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 32.4 m, unemployed 1.6 m, inactive 16 m.
  • Labour force = 32.4 + 1.6 = 34 m.
  • Unemployment rate = 1.6 ÷ 34 × 100 = 4.7%.
  • Divide by the labour force, not the whole population: 1.6 ÷ 50 = 3.2% would be wrong.
Mission 2

The Claimant Count

  • Claimant Count: the number of people claiming benefits because they are unemployed (Jobseeker's Allowance, or Universal Credit while required to look for work).
  • It comes from benefit records, so it is quick and cheap to collect.
  • But it misses jobless people who cannot or do not claim. And under Universal Credit it includes some people in low-paid work.
  • So the two measures can differ a lot (ONS):
    • 2015: Claimant Count 0.80 m; ILO unemployment 1.78 m
    • 2021: Claimant Count 2.28 m; ILO unemployment 1.54 m
  • Say: "the ILO measure counts people looking for work" — not: "it counts people on benefits".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country has 48 million people of working age. 28.8 million are employed, 1.2 million are unemployed, and the rest are economically inactive. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 2

Types of unemployment

  • Frictional: people between jobs, searching for the right one. Usually short.
  • Seasonal: jobs that disappear at certain times of year: farm work, beach and ski resorts, building in winter.
  • Structural: a mismatch between people's skills or location and the jobs on offer, because some industries decline while others grow. Often long-lasting and concentrated in particular regions.
    • Technological unemployment, where machines or software replace a skill, is a type of structural unemployment.
  • Cyclical: too little spending in the economy, in a downturn or recession. Firms sell less, so they need fewer workers.
Mission 2

Never zero

  • Even in a boom, some people are always between jobs (frictional) and some have the wrong skills or live in the wrong place (structural).
  • So full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • In a recession, cyclical unemployment is added on top, and the rate rises fast:
    • US unemployment rose from 5.0% in December 2007 to 10.0% in October 2009 (BLS).
    • UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS).
  • When spending recovers, cyclical unemployment falls. Frictional and structural unemployment remain.
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 2

Causes of unemployment

  • Too little spending (recession): cyclical.
  • Changes in what is produced: industries such as coal mining or traditional manufacturing decline: structural.
  • New technology replaces some jobs: structural.
  • Workers can't easily retrain or move: lack of skills, high housing costs, family ties: structural.
  • Time to find a match: frictional.
  • Time of year: seasonal.
  • Unemployment is often regional: when a big employer closes, a whole town can be hit.
Mission 2

The costs of unemployment

  • For people (individuals and families): lost income; skills fade the longer they are out of work; stress and worse health.
  • For communities (regions): shops and services close when local incomes fall; young people move away; crime and social problems can rise.
  • For the whole economy:
    • lost output: goods and services that could have been produced are lost for ever; the economy is inside its PPC
    • for the government: less income tax and sales taxes such as VAT, more spent on benefits, so more borrowing
    • for firms: fewer customers, though it is easier to recruit workers
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?Unemployment in a country rises from 4% to 7%. Which is the most likely effect on the government's budget?
Mission 2Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: a short search between jobs does little harm; long spells cut skills and income.
  • Who and where: job losses packed into one town or group hit harder than the same rise spread thinly.
  • The response: benefits, retraining and quick action to raise spending reduce the lasting damage.
Quick check
?Who is counted as unemployed on the ILO measure?
Mission check

?During a boom, a region has thousands of job vacancies for software developers and nurses. Yet 6% of its labour force is unemployed, most of them former factory workers whose factories closed. What kind of unemployment is this mainly, and what would help most?

Extra spending cures cyclical unemployment. Mismatches of skills or place, and time spent between jobs, remain even in a boom.

Mission 3

Income and wealth

  • Income: money received over a period of time (a flow), e.g. per week or per year.
  • Types of income:
    • wages and salaries: from working
    • rent: from owning property or land
    • interest: from savings
    • profit and dividends: from owning a business or shares
    • benefits: payments from the government (a transfer, not earned)
  • Wealth: the value of what a person owns at a point in time (a stock): a house, savings, shares, a pension.
  • Net wealth = value of assets − debts (such as a mortgage or loan).
  • Wealth can create income (rent, interest, dividends), and saved income builds wealth.
Mission 3

Worked example: same income, different wealth

  • Illustrative data. Two families each have an income of 40,000 a year.
Family AFamily B
House300,000400,000
Second flat (rented out)0200,000
Savings5,00050,000
Mortgage (debt)250,0000
  • Family A: 300,000 + 5,000 − 250,000 = 55,000 net wealth.
  • Family B: 400,000 + 200,000 + 50,000 = 650,000 net wealth: almost 12 times as much.
  • B's flat also earns rent, so B can save more and grow its wealth faster.
  • Say: "income is a flow, wealth is a stock" — not: "a high income means you are wealthy".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A household owns a house worth 200,000 and has 20,000 in savings. It owes 150,000 on its mortgage and 10,000 on a car loan. What is its net wealth?
Mission 3

How unequal is the UK?

  • Wealth in Great Britain (ONS, April 2018 to March 2020):
    • the wealthiest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth
    • the poorest 50% of households held 9%
  • Wealth is shared out more unequally than income (ONS).
  • Taxes and benefits reduce income inequality: incomes after taxes and benefits are more equal than incomes from work and savings alone (ONS).
  • Say: "the distribution of income" — not: "the amount of income". It is about who gets what share.
Mission 3

Why incomes and wealth differ

  • Skills and qualifications: scarce skills that firms value (doctors, engineers) earn more.
  • Type of job and hours: full-time or part-time; some jobs pay more for risk or responsibility.
  • Age: earnings usually rise with experience, then fall in retirement.
  • Where you live: wages and house prices differ between regions.
  • Inheritance: some people inherit houses, land or savings.
  • Owning assets: wealth earns rent, interest and dividends; the gap can widen over time.
  • Discrimination: unfair treatment by gender, ethnicity or disability can lower pay (illegal in many countries, but it still happens).
  • Bargaining power: workers in trade unions or with rare skills can win higher pay.
Mission 3

Consequences of inequality

  • Arguments that some inequality helps: higher pay rewards skill, effort and risk; it gives people an incentive to train, work and start businesses.
  • Arguments that large inequality harms:
    • relative poverty: some people cannot afford what most take for granted
    • gaps in health and education between rich and poor
    • less social mobility: children from poorer families find it harder to get on
    • possible social tension and crime
  • Economists disagree about how much inequality is too much. It depends on its causes and on what people value: fairness, freedom and rewards for effort.
Quick check
?Two friends earn exactly the same wage for 30 years. One inherits a house and rents it out; the other inherits nothing. What is most likely to happen to the gap in their wealth?
Mission 3

Progressive taxes: worked example

  • Progressive tax: people with higher incomes pay a larger share of their income in tax.
  • Proportional: everyone pays the same share. Regressive: people with lower incomes pay a larger share.
  • Illustrative income tax:
    • first 10,000: no tax
    • income from 10,000 to 40,000: taxed at 20%
    • income above 40,000: taxed at 40%
  • Income 20,000: tax = 20% × 10,000 = 2,000. Share of income = 10%.
  • Income 60,000: tax = 20% × 30,000 + 40% × 20,000 = 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000. Share = 23.3%.
  • The higher earner pays a bigger share: the tax is progressive, so it narrows the gap after tax.
  • Taxes on spending, such as VAT, are often regressive: poorer households spend a larger share of their income.
Quick check
?Use the illustrative tax bands from the worked example (0% on the first 10,000; 20% from 10,000 to 40,000; 40% above 40,000). How much tax is paid on an income of 100,000?
Mission 3

Other ways to narrow the gap

  • Benefits: payments to people on low incomes, unemployed, disabled or retired.
  • Free or cheap education and health care: give poorer children and families the same chances; better skills raise future earnings.
  • Trade-offs:
    • high taxes and benefits may reduce the incentive to work, save or invest (economists disagree on how much)
    • education works slowly: the benefits take years to show
Mission check

?A government needs to raise more tax revenue. It also wants to narrow the gap between rich and poor households. Which change does both?

A progressive tax takes a larger share from higher incomes, so it raises revenue and narrows the gap at the same time.

Mission 4

What inflation is

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level: prices on average, not one price.
  • Rate of inflation: the % rise in the general price level over a year.
  • Inflation means each unit of money buys less: its purchasing power falls.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level (inflation below 0%).
  • Disinflation: inflation falls, but stays above 0%. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 6% to 3%, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 4

Measuring inflation: the CPI

  • CPI (Consumer Prices Index): measures the average change in the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households.
  • The basket holds hundreds of items: food, clothes, rent, energy, transport, phones, haircuts.
  • Each item is weighted by how much households spend on it. Fuel matters more than matches.
  • Prices are collected every month. The index starts at 100 in a base year.
  • Inflation rate = % change in the CPI over 12 months.
  • The UK government sets the Bank of England a CPI inflation target of 2%.
Mission 4

Worked example: a weighted index

  • Illustrative data. A basket with three groups. Index for each group this year (base year = 100).
GroupWeightPrice index
Food20110
Transport30105
Housing50102
  • Multiply each index by its weight: 20 × 110 = 2,200; 30 × 105 = 3,150; 50 × 102 = 5,100.
  • Add them and divide by the total weight: 10,450 ÷ 100 = 104.5.
  • So prices rose 4.5% since the base year.
  • Food prices rose most (10%), but food has a small weight, so it pulls the average up less.
Mission 4

Worked example: inflation and prices

  • Illustrative data. CPI last year = 120; CPI this year = 126.
  • Inflation rate = (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 5%.
  • Effect on a price: a meal costing 40 last year costs about 40 × 1.05 = 42 this year, if its price rose in line with inflation.
  • Say: "the CPI rose by 6 points, which is 5%" — not: "inflation was 6%".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's CPI rises from 150 to 156 over a year. What is the rate of inflation?
Mission 4

Real and nominal

  • Nominal value: measured in money, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation; what the money can actually buy.
  • Real change ≈ nominal change − inflation.
  • Illustrative data:
    • Pay rises 3%; inflation is 5%. Real pay falls by about 2%: the worker can buy less.
    • Savings of 1,000 earn 2% interest: 1,020 next year. Inflation is 5%, so the 1,020 buys about 3% less than the 1,000 did.
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when the money amount rose.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 520, a rise of 4%. Inflation over the same year is 3%. What has happened to her real pay?
Mission 4

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull inflation: total spending grows faster than the economy can produce, so prices are pulled up.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • Causes:
    • consumers spend more (rising confidence, cheap borrowing, low interest rates)
    • government spends more or cuts taxes
    • foreign buyers want more of our exports
  • Most likely when firms are already near full capacity: they cannot produce much more, so they raise prices.
Mission 4

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push inflation: firms' costs rise, and they push prices up to protect their profits.
  • Causes:
    • dearer raw materials and energy (e.g. world oil and gas prices)
    • higher wages, if they rise faster than productivity
    • dearer imports, e.g. when the exchange rate falls
    • higher indirect taxes, such as VAT
  • Real case: UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, with energy and food prices the biggest contributors (ONS).
Quick check
?Which of these is most likely to cause demand-pull inflation?
Mission 4

Who gains and who loses?

  • Consumers: if prices rise faster than incomes, they can buy less.
  • People on fixed incomes (e.g. some pensions, benefits that are not raised): lose purchasing power.
  • Savers: lose if the interest rate is below inflation; their savings buy less.
  • Borrowers: gain if the interest rate they pay is below inflation, because the real value of what they owe falls.
  • Lenders: lose for the same reason: they are repaid with money that buys less.
  • Producers: costs rise; if inflation is higher than in other countries, exports become less competitive.
  • Government: tax revenue rises as prices and pay rise, but so does the cost of pensions, benefits and public-sector pay.
Mission 4

Costs for the whole economy

  • Menu costs: firms must keep changing prices: price lists, menus, websites.
  • Shoe-leather costs: people and firms spend time and effort managing money to protect its value (e.g. moving savings around).
  • Uncertainty: when inflation is high or unpredictable, firms are less confident about future costs and prices, so they may invest less.
  • Low and stable inflation (around 2%) is seen as healthy: it is easy to plan around.
Mission 4

Too much, or too little

  • Hyperinflation: extremely high inflation. Zimbabwe's official annual inflation rate reached 231,000,000% in July 2008. In 2009, people stopped using the local currency.
  • Deflation, prices falling, can also be harmful:
    • people delay spending, hoping prices will fall further, so firms sell less and cut jobs
    • the real value of debts rises, so borrowers lose and lenders gain
    • savers gain: their money buys more
  • Example: in Japan, consumer prices fell in 9 of the 14 years from 1999 to 2012.
Quick check
?Inflation turns out much higher than anyone expected. Who is most likely to gain?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Last year, Amira's pay rose by 4%. Her savings account paid 1% interest. Inflation was 6%. Which statement is correct?

A pay rise smaller than inflation is a cut in real pay. Money only matters for what it can buy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Economic growth is a rise in real GDP. GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity; potential growth shifts the PPC out.
  • Growth comes from investment, technology, more and better-skilled workers, resources and good policy.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment and may not be shared fairly.

Mission 2

  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Two UK measures: the ILO measure (Labour Force Survey) and the Claimant Count.
  • Frictional: between jobs. Seasonal: time of year. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little spending.
  • Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Unemployment costs people, communities and the whole economy.

Mission 3

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Net wealth = assets − debts.
  • Incomes differ with skills, jobs, age, region, inheritance and owned assets; wealth gaps can grow over time.
  • Progressive taxes, benefits and free education and health care narrow the gap, with trade-offs.

Mission 4

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured by the CPI.
  • Inflation rate = % change in the CPI over a year.
  • Real change ≈ nominal change − inflation.
  • Demand-pull: spending outruns what firms can produce. Cost-push: firms' costs rise.
  • Inflation hurts savers, lenders and people on fixed incomes; it helps borrowers. Deflation does the opposite.
