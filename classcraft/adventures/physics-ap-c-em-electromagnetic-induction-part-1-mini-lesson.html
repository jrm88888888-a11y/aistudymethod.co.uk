Mission 1

Mission 1: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Make a guess: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Mission 1

Magnetic flux

  • Magnetic flux: how much field passes through an area. Think: number of field lines through it.
  • ΦB = B·A (uniform field), A ⟂ to the surface.
  • Sign: positive if B is along the area vector, negative if opposite.
  • Unit: weber. 1 Wb = 1 T·m².
  • Plane of the loop parallel to the field: zero flux.
Quick check
?A square wire loop with sides of 0.20 m is in a uniform magnetic field of magnitude 0.40 T. The plane of the loop makes an angle of 30° with the direction of the field. What is the magnitude of the magnetic flux through the loop?
Mission 1

Faraday's law

  • An emf is induced whenever the magnetic flux through the loop changes: move a magnet, move the loop, change B, change the area, or turn the loop.
  • Steady flux, however large: no emf.
  • Size: emf = rate of change of flux (× N for a coil of N turns).
  • ε = −dΦB/dt; for N turns, |ε| = N|dΦB/dt|.
  • On a ΦB–t graph for one turn, |ε| = slope; for N turns, |ε| = N × slope.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², is ⟂ to a 0.50 T field. The field falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s.
  • Change in flux per turn = 0.50 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. For 200 turns: N ΔΦB = 200 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.040 Wb.
  • emf = 0.040 ÷ 0.10 = 0.40 V (while the field is falling).
  • Same change in 0.050 s: twice the emf. Once the field stops changing: zero.
Quick check
?A square wire loop with sides of 0.15 m lies in a horizontal plane in a uniform vertical magnetic field. The field's magnitude increases steadily from 0.20 T to 0.60 T in 0.12 s. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the loop?
Mission 1

Lenz's law

  • The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that produces it.
  • N pole pushed in: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole, repelling it.
  • N pole pulled out: the near end becomes an S pole, attracting it back.
  • The minus sign in Faraday's law expresses this.
  • Method: is the flux through the loop growing or shrinking? The induced field points against a growth, with a decrease. Then the right-hand rule gives the current.
Mission 1

Lenz's law is energy conservation

  • Suppose the induced current helped the change: the magnet would be pulled in faster, the current would grow, and so on. Energy from nothing.
  • So the induced current must oppose the motion. You must do work against that force.
  • That work becomes electrical energy, then thermal energy in the circuit.
  • Open circuit: an emf, but no current, so no opposing force.
  • A solid metal sheet or pipe acts like many loops: changing flux drives eddy currents around inside it, and they oppose the motion too.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a stationary square loop in the plane of the page, inside a region of uniform magnetic field directed into the page. The arrowheads show the direction of the current induced in the loop. Which change is taking place?
Mission 1

Flux and emf with calculus

  • Non-uniform field: ΦB = ∫B·dA. Choose strips (B varies with x) or rings (B varies with r) so B is constant across each.
  • ε = −dΦB/dt: find ΦB as a function of time, then differentiate.
  • Example: B = βt through a fixed loop of area A: |ε| = Aβ, constant.
  • Example: ΦB = βt² per turn, N turns: |ε| = 2Nβt, growing with time.
Quick check
?A square loop with sides of 0.20 m lies in the xy-plane, with one side along the y-axis and the opposite side at x = 0.20 m. A magnetic field perpendicular to the plane of the loop has magnitude B = (5.0 T/m²)x² and does not vary with y. What is the magnitude of the magnetic flux through the loop?
Mission check

?A thick aluminum plate swings as a pendulum through the gap between the poles of a strong magnet, and stops after a few swings. An identical plate with many deep slots cut into it swings for much longer. Which explains the difference?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, so currents flow around the pipe. By Lenz's law their field opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it; its lost energy heats the copper.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Make a guess: Move a metal rod or spin a coil near a magnet, and a voltage appears. What decides how big it is?

Mission 2

A moving rod makes an emf

  • A rod moves at right angles to a uniform field. Its free electrons move with it, so the magnetic force pushes them along the rod.
  • Charge piles up at one end: an emf across the rod.
  • Flux view: in time Δt the rod sweeps area (length × vΔt), so the flux it cuts is B × length × vΔt.
  • ε = Bℓv (B, ℓ and v all at right angles).
  • Moving along the field, or along its own length: no emf.
Mission 2

Worked example: rod on rails

  • Rod 0.20 m long slides at 3.0 m/s on rails joined by a 1.5 Ω resistor. B = 0.50 T, at right angles to the rails' plane.
  • emf: 0.50 × 0.20 × 3.0 = 0.30 V.
  • Current: 0.30 ÷ 1.5 = 0.20 A (rod and rails of negligible resistance).
  • Force on the rod: B × I × length = 0.50 × 0.20 × 0.20 = 0.020 N, opposing its motion.
Quick check
?A metal rod slides at a constant 2.0 m/s along two parallel horizontal conducting rails that are 0.25 m apart. The rails are joined at one end by a 0.50 Ω resistor; the rod and rails have negligible resistance. A uniform 0.40 T magnetic field is perpendicular to the plane of the rails. At the instant the rod is 0.50 m from the resistor, what is the current in the resistor?
Quick check
?Use Lenz's law to predict before the next screen. A metal rod rests across two horizontal conducting rails that are joined at one end by a resistor, in a uniform vertical magnetic field. A student gives the rod a brief push along the rails and lets go. Friction and air resistance are negligible. Which claim about the rod's motion after the push, with its justification, is correct?
Mission 2

Why the rod is braked

  • The induced current is in the field, so the rod feels a force B × I × length.
  • Lenz's law: that force opposes the motion. Let go, and the rod slows down.
  • Constant speed needs a push equal to the magnetic force: F = IℓB = B²ℓ²v/R.
  • Energy: work done by the push = thermal energy in the resistor.
  • Example, the rod on rails above: 0.020 N × 3.0 m/s = 0.060 W = I²R = 0.20² × 1.5.
Mission check

?An aircraft with a wingspan of 40 m flies horizontally at 250 m/s. The vertical component of the Earth's magnetic field there is 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ T. What emf is induced between the wingtips?

The emf is the rate of cutting flux: emf = B × length × v. Stronger field, longer conductor or faster motion all give more. A spinning coil works the same way: the flux through it keeps changing.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A magnet can slow a moving metal loop without touching it. What decides how hard it brakes?

Make a guess: A magnet can slow a moving metal loop without touching it. What decides how hard it brakes?

Mission 3

Faraday's law with calculus

  • ε = −dΦB/dt, with ΦB = ∫B · dA. For N turns: ε = −N dΦB/dt.
  • Changing B, fixed area: |ε| = A dB/dt.
  • Changing area, fixed B: |ε| = B dA/dt. Rod on rails: dA/dt = ℓv, so |ε| = Bℓv.
  • Both changing: ΦB = BA, so dΦB/dt = A dB/dt + B dA/dt (product rule).
Quick check
?Two long, parallel conducting rails a distance ℓ apart are joined at one end by a conducting bar, and a conducting rod slides along the rails at constant speed v, completing a rectangular circuit. At time t the rod is a distance d + vt from the joined end, where d is a constant. A uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the plane of the circuit has magnitude B = βt, where β is a positive constant. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the circuit at time t?
Mission 3

A loop crossing a field boundary

  • F = ∫I dℓ × B: only segments inside the field feel a force.
  • Entering: flux rising, current flows; force on the leading side opposes the motion.
  • Fully inside (uniform B): flux constant, no current, no force.
  • Leaving: flux falling, current reverses; force on the trailing side still opposes the motion.
  • Force size B²ℓ²v/R: bigger with faster flux change, smaller with more resistance. A cut loop: no current, no force.
  • A coil spinning in a field: the forces give a torque opposing the rotation.
Quick check
?The figure shows a square conducting loop at rest, with half of it inside a region of uniform magnetic field; the field is increasing, and the data are labeled. What is the magnitude of the net magnetic force on the loop at the instant shown?
Mission 3

Newton's second law for the rod

  • Coasting rod on horizontal rails, mass m: m dv/dt = −B²ℓ²v/R.
  • Solve it: the speed decays exponentially; it never reverses.
  • Falling rod on vertical rails: m dv/dt = mg − B²ℓ²v/R.
  • Terminal speed when the forces balance: vT = mgR/(B²ℓ²).
  • Example: m = 0.020 kg, R = 2.0 Ω, B = 0.50 T, ℓ = 0.40 m, g = 10 m/s². vT = (0.020 × 10 × 2.0)/(0.50² × 0.40²) = 10 m/s.
Quick check
?A conducting rod of mass m and length ℓ slides on frictionless horizontal rails connected by a resistor of resistance R, in a uniform vertical magnetic field of magnitude B; all other resistances are negligible. At time t = 0 the rod has speed v0, and no horizontal force other than the magnetic force acts on it. Which expression gives the rod's speed v at time t?
Mission check

?A conducting rod slides at constant speed along conducting rails in a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to the plane of the rails. The rails are connected by a resistor, and all other resistances are negligible. The magnetic force on the rod is 0.60 N. The experiment is repeated with the magnetic field doubled, the rod's speed tripled and a resistor of twice the resistance. What is the magnitude of the magnetic force on the rod now?

The braking force is B²ℓ²v/R: it grows with how fast the flux changes (B and v) and shrinks with resistance. No current, no braking.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Switch off a big electromagnet and a spark jumps the switch gap. Where does that large voltage come from?

Make a guess: Switch off a big electromagnet and a spark jumps the switch gap. Where does that large voltage come from?

Mission 4

Inductance

  • Current in a coil makes a magnetic field, and so a total flux NΦB through its own N turns.
  • Change the current: NΦB changes, so an emf is induced in the coil itself (Faraday).
  • By Lenz's law that emf opposes the change in current: not the current itself.
  • Inductance: L = NΦB/I. Unit: henry, 1 H = 1 V·s/A = 1 Wb/A.
  • A coil with significant L is an inductor. Straight wires: L ≈ 0.
Quick check
?In the circuit shown, the bulbs B1 and B2 are identical and the inductor is ideal. When switch S is closed, B2 lights at once while B1 brightens gradually; a long time later, B1 and B2 are equally bright. Which claim about the inductor do these observations support?
Mission 4

ε = −L dI/dt

  • Total flux NΦB = LI, with L constant.
  • Faraday: ε = −d(NΦB)/dt = −L dI/dt.
  • Minus sign: the emf acts against the change. Current rising: emf opposes it. Current falling: emf tries to keep it going.
  • Steady current: dI/dt = 0, so no emf across an ideal inductor.
  • Read dI/dt as the slope of an I–t graph.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • L = 0.20 H. Current rises steadily from 0 to 3.0 A in 15 ms.
  • dI/dt = 3.0/0.015 = 200 A/s.
  • |ε| = L|dI/dt| = 0.20 × 200 = 40 V, opposing the rise.
  • Switch off: 3.0 A falls to zero in 10 μs. |ε| = 0.20 × 3.0/(1.0 × 10⁻⁵) = 6.0 × 10⁴ V.
Quick check
?The diagram shows an ideal inductor of inductance L between points X and Y. The current in the direction shown is I = I0 + βt², where I0 and β are positive constants. What is the potential difference VY − VX at time t?
Mission 4

Inductance of a solenoid

  • Long solenoid: B = μ0NI/ℓ, uniform inside.
  • Total flux: NΦB = N × BA = μ0N²AI/ℓ.
  • So L = μ0N²A/ℓ. With a core: Lsol = μcore N²A/ℓ.
  • L depends only on the geometry and core: not on the current, the battery or the circuit.
  • Example: N = 500, ℓ = 0.25 m, radius 2.0 cm. A = π(0.020)² = 1.26 × 10⁻³ m². L = (4π × 10⁻⁷)(500²)(1.26 × 10⁻³)/0.25 = 1.6 mH.
Quick check
?Three long air-core solenoids have these properties. Solenoid 1: N turns, length ℓ, cross-sectional area A. Solenoid 2: 2N turns, length 2ℓ, area A. Solenoid 3: N turns, length ℓ/2, area A/2. Which ranks their inductances L1, L2 and L3?
Mission 4

Energy stored in an inductor

  • Building up the current, the source works against the inductor's emf: power = LI dI/dt.
  • Total work from 0 to I: ∫LI dI = UL = ½LI².
  • The energy is stored in the magnetic field. In an air-core solenoid: UL = (B²/2μ0) × volume.
  • When the current falls, the field gives the energy back: to a resistor (as thermal energy) or to a capacitor.
  • Conservation: energy released by the inductor = energy gained elsewhere.
Quick check
?An ideal inductor of inductance 0.50 H, connected only to a resistor, carries a current of 4.0 A. The current then decreases to 2.0 A. How much energy does the resistor dissipate while the current decreases?
Mission check

?The graph shows the current I in an ideal inductor of inductance 0.040 H as a function of time t. What is the magnitude of the emf induced across the inductor at t = 9.0 ms?

The emf depends on how fast the current changes, not on its size: |ε| = L|dI/dt|. Switching off a big current in microseconds gives a huge emf, enough to spark across the gap.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ΦB = ∫B·dA; unit Wb = T·m².
  • Faraday: |ε| = |ΔΦB/Δt|; no change, no emf.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change; otherwise energy would be created.

Mission 2

  • A rod on rails: ε = Bℓv; the magnetic force on the induced current opposes the motion.

Mission 3

  • ε = −dΦB/dt, including changing B and changing area together.
  • Forces act only on loop segments inside the field and always oppose the motion; F = B²ℓ²v/R.
  • Newton's second law: a coasting rod slows exponentially; a falling rod reaches vT = mgR/(B²ℓ²).

Mission 4

  • An inductor opposes changes in its current: ε = −L dI/dt. Solenoid: L = μN²A/ℓ.
  • Energy stored in its magnetic field: UL = ½LI².
