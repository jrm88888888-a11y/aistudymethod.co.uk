Mission 1

Buddha images

  • The earliest Buddhist art showed no figure of the Buddha: only symbols (footprints, an empty seat, the Bodhi tree, the wheel).
  • Images appear around the 1st century CE (Gandhara, Mathura).
  • Mudras (hand gestures) carry meaning: earth-touching (the awakening), meditation, turning the wheel (teaching), fearlessness.
  • An image is often consecrated before use, e.g. an "eye-opening" ceremony in Sri Lanka.
  • Role: a reminder of the Buddha's qualities and a focus for the mind; treated with respect (shoes off, feet never pointed at it).
  • Theravada images are almost all of Gautama Buddha: the one who taught the path in this age.
  • Mahayana shrines add other buddhas and bodhisattvas (Amitabha, Avalokitesvara/Guanyin, Tara).
Mission 1

Stupas

  • A stupa is a mound that holds relics: after the Buddha's death, his remains were divided and enshrined (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, DN 16).
  • Emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE) is said to have built many; Sanchi (India) is a famous early example.
  • Names by region: chedi (Thailand), dagoba (Sri Lanka), pagoda (East Asia).
  • Devotees walk around it clockwise, offer flowers and lamps, and recollect the Buddha.
  • For Theravada, stupas and images keep Gautama at the centre: the historical teacher whose relics are honoured.
Mission 1Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana views of worship

  • Theravada: the Buddha has attained parinibbana: he does not hear or respond.
  • So devotion is respect and recollection (buddhanussati): it calms the mind, inspires practice and makes merit.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): honours paid to the Buddha bear fruit, though he does not accept them, as a fire that has gone out still helps those who make their own fire.
  • Many lay Theravadins also seek blessing and protection, e.g. through paritta (protective chanting).
  • Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas are active now in many worlds and can respond to devotion.
  • E.g. Lotus Sutra ch. 25: calling on Avalokitesvara (Guanyin) brings help; Pure Land: reliance on Amitabha's vow.
  • Tibetan devotion: prostrations, mantras, prayer wheels; devotion also trains the mind.
Mission 1

Dana: giving

  • Dana: generosity, giving. The first of the ten perfections (Theravada) and of the six perfections (Mahayana).
  • Three kinds of gift: material things; fearlessness (protection, safety); the Dhamma (teaching), counted highest.
  • Lay people give food to monks on the morning alms round, and robes at Kathina after the rains retreat.
  • Intention matters: giving with a glad mind before, during and after the gift.
  • Giving, with chanting and study, is how many lay Buddhists live the path day to day.
Mission 1

Punya: merit

  • Punya (Sanskrit; Pali puñña): merit, the good kamma produced by wholesome actions.
  • Three bases of making merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Merit brings happiness, a better rebirth and good conditions for practice; it carries over into future lives.
  • Beyond merit: the awakened one is "above good and evil, above the bondage of both" (Dhammapada 412, Müller): no longer making kamma at all.
Mission 1

Sharing merit

  • Theravada: after giving, people share (dedicate) the merit, often to dead relatives, while monks chant; water is poured from a small jug into a bowl.
  • Source: the Tirokudda Sutta (Khuddakapatha 7, paraphrase): departed relatives wait outside the walls; gifts to the Sangha dedicated to them help them.
  • Kamma is personal: "by oneself one is purified … no one can purify another" (Dhammapada 165, Müller).
  • So many Theravada teachers explain: the dead (or living) person rejoices in the good deed (anumodana) and so makes merit of their own. Sharing merit is itself generous, so the giver loses nothing.
  • Mahayana: parinamana: bodhisattvas turn over their vast merit to all beings; practice ends with a dedication of merit.
  • The debate: can merit pass between people when kamma is personal? Theravada answers through rejoicing; Mahayana through the bodhisattva's compassion.

Mission 2

Two goals: better rebirth and nirvana

  • Nirvana (Pali nibbana): the end of greed, hatred and delusion, and so of rebirth. The ultimate goal.
  • Better rebirth: a happier human life or a heaven realm, gained by merit (punya): giving, keeping precepts, devotion. A proximate goal.
  • Many lay Buddhists in Theravada countries aim mainly at better rebirth; many monastics aim at nirvana.
  • Anthropologist Melford Spiro, Buddhism and Society (1970), called these nibbanic and kammatic Buddhism (plus "apotropaic": protective ritual).
  • Most Buddhists see them as stages on one road, not rivals: a good rebirth gives the chance to practise; merit supports the path.
Mission 2

The arhat, "worthy one"

  • Arhat (Pali arahant): "worthy one": a person who has reached nirvana in this life by following the Buddha's teaching.
  • Greed, hatred and delusion are destroyed; no new karma is made; at death there is no further rebirth (parinirvana).
  • Standard canonical description (paraphrase): birth is ended, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done.

> "There is no suffering for him who has finished his journey, and abandoned grief, who has freed himself on all sides, and thrown off all fetters." (Dhammapada 90, Müller 1881)

  • In Theravada the arhat is the ideal of the Buddha's disciple; the Buddha himself is also an arahant, but one who found the path unaided.
  • Your spec writes arahant, nibbana, parinibbana.
Mission 2

The ten fetters

  • Progress is measured by breaking ten fetters (samyojana) that bind beings to rebirth:
  • Lower five: 1 belief in a permanent self · 2 doubt (in the Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) · 3 clinging to rites and rituals as liberating in themselves · 4 sensual desire · 5 ill will.
  • Higher five: 6 craving for fine-material existence · 7 craving for immaterial existence · 8 conceit ("I am") · 9 restlessness · 10 ignorance.
  • "Fetters" in Müller's Dhammapada 90 are these ties.
Mission 2

The four stages of awakening

  • 1 Stream-winner (sotapanna, "stream-enterer"): breaks fetters 1–3. At most seven more lives, none in the lower realms; unshakeable confidence in the Three Refuges.
  • 2 Once-returner (sakadagami): fetters 4–5 weakened. One more human birth.
  • 3 Non-returner (anagami): fetters 4–5 broken. Reborn in the Pure Abodes, a heaven realm, and reaches nirvana there.
  • 4 Arhat: fetters 6–10 broken. Full awakening in this life.
  • Each stage has a path moment and a fruit: "four pairs, eight kinds of person", the noble Sangha honoured in the refuge chant.
Mission 2Read the source

The first step

> "Better than sovereignty over the earth, better than going to heaven, better than lordship over all worlds, is the reward of the first step in holiness." (Dhammapada 178, Müller 1881)

  • "The first step in holiness" = the fruit of stream-entry (sotapatti-phala).

Mission 2

Renunciation and the monastic path

  • Theravada emphasises renunciation: leaving home for the monastic life gives the best conditions to reach nirvana.
  • Richard Gombrich (b. 1937), Theravada Buddhism: A Social History (1988): the Theravada tradition has held that the full path is normally lived by monastics, while lay people support them and make merit; but nirvana is not barred to lay people, and the canon records lay disciples who reached the early stages, some even arhatship.
  • Gombrich stresses that the Buddha ethicised karma: what matters is intention, not ritual (cf. fetter 3).
  • Today: forest monasteries (e.g. the Thai Forest tradition) and lay meditation movements (e.g. in Myanmar, from the 20th century) both make awakening a live aim, not only a distant ideal.
Mission 2

Gunaratana: insight breaks the fetters

  • Bhante Henepola Gunaratana (b. 1927), Sri Lankan monk, founder of the Bhavana Society (West Virginia, USA), Mindfulness in Plain English (1991) and Beyond Mindfulness in Plain English (2009).
  • He teaches the stages as practical: fetters are weakened by mindfulness and broken by insight (vipassana), supported by concentration (jhana).
  • Stream-entry is a realistic aim for sincere practitioners, lay or monastic, today (paraphrase).
  • This links the four stages to meditation practice in Mission 6–b.

Mission 3

The only surviving Nikaya school

  • After the Buddha's death the Sangha split into early schools, traditionally eighteen: the Nikaya schools.
  • The first split (c. 4th century BCE) was between the Sthaviras ("elders") and the Mahasanghikas ("great community").
  • Theravada ("teaching of the elders") descends from the Sthavira line.
  • All the other Nikaya schools died out; Theravada is the only one that survives.
  • Today: Sri Lanka, Myanmar, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia, and communities worldwide.
  • Do not call it "Hinayana" ("lesser vehicle"): a Mahayana term that Theravadins reject.
Mission 3

The Pali canon and Sri Lanka

  • Mahinda, son of the emperor Ashoka, brought the Dhamma to Sri Lanka in the 3rd century BCE (tradition).
  • The Tipitaka ("three baskets") in Pali was first written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE.
  • Vinaya (monastic rules) · Sutta (discourses) · Abhidhamma (systematic analysis).
  • Buddhaghosa (5th century CE) wrote the great commentaries and the Visuddhimagga ("Path of Purification").
  • Theravada sees itself as preserving the Buddha's teaching unchanged; scholars debate how far that is so.
  • Your anthology suggests Thanissaro Bhikkhu's translations; paraphrase, not quotation.
Mission 3

No three vehicles

  • Mahayana sorts paths into three vehicles (yana): disciples, solitary Buddhas, bodhisattvas; and ranks its own "great vehicle" above a "lesser" one.
  • The Lotus Sutra adds: the three are provisional; really there is one vehicle.
  • Theravada rejects this scheme: it does not accept the Mahayana sutras as the Buddha's word, nor that the arahant's goal is a lower or provisional one.
  • It does recognise three kinds of awakened being (disciple arahant, paccekabuddha, sammasambuddha), but all reach the same nibbana.
  • Some writers also use "three vehicles" for Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana; Theravadins reject being placed as the "lesser" one in that scheme too.

Mission 3

Images and stupas

  • Gautama is central: he is the Buddha of this age, the one who found and taught the path.
  • Buddha images (Buddha rupa): in every temple and many homes. Buddhists bow, offer flowers, light, incense; recite the Three Refuges.
  • New images are consecrated (in Sri Lanka, an "eye-opening" ceremony).
  • Stupas (Sri Lanka: dagoba; Pali cetiya): domed monuments holding relics of the Buddha or saints. Devotees walk around them clockwise.
  • Examples: Shwedagon (Yangon, Myanmar); Ruwanwelisaya (Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka); the Temple of the Tooth (Kandy) houses a tooth relic.
  • Images and stupas are reminders of the Buddha's qualities, and focus devotion.
Mission 3

Gombrich: gone, yet present

  • Richard Gombrich (b. 1937), Oxford Indologist; Precept and Practice (1971); Theravada Buddhism: A Social History (1988).
  • In Sri Lankan villages he found Buddhists saying the Buddha is gone: in nibbana, he cannot hear or act.
  • Yet they treat his image and relics with deep feeling, as if he were present.
  • His terms: cognitively (in belief) the Buddha is absent; affectively (in feeling) he is present.
  • Not a contradiction: devotion trains the devotee's mind (respect, joy, gratitude) and makes merit.
  • Gombrich also described "Protestant Buddhism": 19th–20th century lay-led reform in Sri Lanka, stressing texts, meditation and reason.

Mission 3

Nibbana and parinibbana

  • Nibbana: the "blowing out" of greed, hatred and delusion. The goal of Theravada practice.
  • Reached in this life: the arahant still has a body and feelings, but no craving. Nibbana "with remainder".
  • Parinibbana: final nibbana at death. No further rebirth; nibbana "without remainder".
  • The Buddha reached nibbana at 35 under the Bodhi tree, and parinibbana at about 80, at Kusinara.
  • What the arahant is after death is one of the questions the Buddha left unanswered: "exists", "does not exist" do not apply.
Mission 3

Renunciation

  • Nekkhamma (renunciation): letting go of sense pleasures and worldly ties, following the Buddha's own going forth.
  • The monastic Sangha is at the centre: monks follow the Vinaya (227 rules in the Theravada code).
  • Going forth (pabbajja) as a novice, then higher ordination (upasampada).
  • In Thailand and Myanmar many young men ordain for a short time.
  • The lay community supports monks with food (dana), and gains merit; monks give teaching.
  • Renunciation is a matter of the mind as well: lay people can practise it, and some can become stream-enterers or more.
Mission 3Read the source

Four stages to arahant

> "Better than sovereignty over the earth, better than going to heaven, better than lordship over all worlds, is the reward of the first step in holiness." (Dhammapada 178, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "first step in holiness" translates sotapatti: becoming a stream-enterer.
  • Stream-enterer (sotapanna): has broken the first three of ten fetters: belief in a fixed self, doubt, clinging to rites. At most seven more lives.
  • Once-returner (sakadagami): sense desire and ill will weakened. One more human birth.
  • Non-returner (anagami): sense desire and ill will ended. Reborn in the Pure Abodes, where awakening is completed.
  • Arahant: the last five fetters gone (including conceit and ignorance). Free.

Q1 Why might entering the stream be worth more than going to heaven?

Show answer

Heaven ends; the stream-enterer is certain to reach nibbana.

Mission 3

Gunaratana: practice for everyone

  • Bhante Henepola Gunaratana (b. 1927), Sri Lankan Theravada monk; founded the Bhavana Society in West Virginia, USA; Mindfulness in Plain English (1991).
  • He teaches vipassana (insight) meditation in clear, plain steps, for lay people as well as monks.
  • Mindfulness, he teaches, is the tool that sees impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self directly in one's own experience.
  • He keeps meditation inside the whole Noble Eightfold Path: ethics and wisdom, not technique alone.
  • So distinctive Theravada teachings (the three marks, the arahant path) become practice today.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is Theravada the original Buddhism?

  • Claim: Theravada is the original form of Buddhism.
  • For: Only surviving Nikaya school; the Pali canon is an early, carefully preserved collection.
  • For: Its focus on Gautama, the arahant path and renunciation matches what the early texts describe.
  • Against: Theravada too developed: Abhidhamma, Buddhaghosa's commentaries, relic cults, modern lay meditation.
  • Against: Other early schools had their own canons (some survive in Chinese); none is simply "the original".
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as "original": texts, practice or goal? How much change is faithful development?
  • Gombrich stresses Theravada's continuity with early Buddhism, while showing how it changed in society.

Mission 4

The "Great Vehicle"

  • Mahayana: "Great Vehicle". Its followers aim at full Buddhahood for the sake of all beings, through the bodhisattva path.
  • Some Mahayana texts called other paths Hinayana, "lesser vehicle". This is a polemical term: do not use it for Theravada today.
  • Today Mahayana is the largest Buddhist tradition, across China, Taiwan, Korea, Japan, Vietnam, Tibet and Mongolia (the Tibetan forms are often called Vajrayana).
  • Mahayana was a movement, not at first a separate school: Mahayana monks lived under the same monastic rules (Vinaya) as others, sometimes in the same monasteries.
  • Mahayana Buddhists accepted the new sutras as the Buddha's word (buddhavacana). Traditional accounts say they were taught by the Buddha but kept until people were ready, or revealed in meditation by Buddhas now teaching in other worlds; some also held that whatever agrees with the Dhamma is the Buddha's word.
  • Your spec writes nibbana, arahant; Mahayana texts use Sanskrit nirvana, arhat.
Mission 4

The context: India, c. 2nd century BCE to 1st century CE

  • After Ashoka (d. c. 232 BCE): Buddhism spread across India and beyond, with many monastic schools (the first split, between Sthaviras and Mahasanghikas, is traditionally placed about a century after the Buddha's death).
  • North-west India: Indo-Greek, Saka and Kushan rulers; trade along the Silk Road; mixed cultures. The first images of the Buddha appear (Gandhara and Mathura, c. 1st century CE).
  • Writing: scriptures begin to be written down (the Pali Canon in Sri Lanka, 1st century BCE), allowing new texts to spread and be honoured as books.
  • Devotion (bhakti) grows across Indian religion; stupas become great pilgrimage centres.
  • New sutras: the first Mahayana sutras (e.g. the Perfection of Wisdom in 8,000 Lines) date from around the 1st century BCE; some were translated into Chinese by Lokaksema around 179 CE.
  • Your spec: "its development and context in the second century BCE and the first century CE".
Mission 4

Why did it emerge? Competing theories

  • Lay and stupa cult: Hirakawa Akira (1915–2002) linked Mahayana to lay devotees at stupas. Now widely questioned: early texts are mostly by and for monks.
  • Forest ascetics: many early sutras praise strict forest renunciants seeking Buddhahood (e.g. Jan Nattier, A Few Good Men, 2003, on the Ugrapariprccha). A renewal movement, not a lay rebellion.
  • Cult of the book: Gregory Schopen (b. 1947): early Mahayana groups centred on honouring a sutra as the Buddha's presence.
  • Visionary experience: meditation in which practitioners met Buddhas of other worlds and received teaching (e.g. the Pratyutpanna Samadhi Sutra).
  • Doctrinal reasons: the wish to follow the Buddha's own path (bodhisattva, not only arhat); grief at the Buddha's absence; deeper reflection on emptiness.
  • A. L. Basham (1914–1986): Mahayana grew from gradual change within Indian religion: the bodhisattva idea developed from the Jataka tales, encouraged by the wider rise of devotion; he allowed that ideas from north-west India's mixed cultures may have contributed (paraphrase).

Mission 4

Key Mahayana ideas

  • Bodhisattva ideal for all: aim at full Buddhahood out of compassion, not only personal liberation.
  • Emptiness (shunyata): all things lack fixed own-being; developed in the Perfection of Wisdom sutras and by Nagarjuna (–b).
  • Skilful means (upaya): the Buddha adapts his teaching to his hearers.
  • Many Buddhas in many worlds, now: e.g. Amitabha, and great bodhisattvas such as Avalokiteshvara who can be called on for help.
  • Buddha nature: every being can become a Buddha.
  • Merit transfer: merit can be shared for the good of all (see Mission 1).
Mission 4

A bigger idea of the Buddha

  • Theravada: Gautama was a human being who found the path and taught it. After his parinibbana he is beyond reach; Buddhists honour him as teacher and example. One Buddha per age.
  • Mahayana: there are countless Buddhas in countless worlds, active now; the Buddha's life is far longer than it appeared (Lotus Sutra ch. 16).
  • The trikaya ("three bodies") later explains this: the Dharma-body (ultimate reality), the enjoyment-body (Buddhas in pure lands) and the emanation-body (Gautama's earthly life) (see Mission 5).
  • Gautama is still central in both traditions: his images and stupas are honoured everywhere, but in Mahayana he is one among many Buddhas.

Mission 4

Buddha nature in every person

  • Tathagatagarbha: "womb (or embryo) of the Buddha": every being has the potential to become a Buddha, already present but hidden.
  • The Tathagatagarbha Sutra compares it to gold buried in filth or a precious statue wrapped in rags (paraphrase). The Mahayana Mahaparinirvana Sutra says all beings have Buddha nature.
  • Central to Chan/Zen, Tibetan and much East Asian Buddhism: awakening means uncovering what is already there.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935): the basic nature of mind is clear and luminous; anger and craving are adventitious, so they can be removed. Because all beings share this nature, all deserve compassion and respect (paraphrase of his teaching).
  • Debate: some scholars (e.g. Japan's "Critical Buddhism", 1980s) argue Buddha nature sounds like a permanent self, against anatta; defenders reply it is emptiness seen as potential, not a self.
Mission 4

Mahayana across Asia

  • China (from the 1st century CE, via the Silk Road): Chan, Pure Land, Tiantai, Huayan.
  • Korea (4th century): Seon (Chan) dominant; the Jogye Order.
  • Japan (6th century, via Korea): Tendai, Shingon, Pure Land (Jodo-shu, Jodo Shinshu), Zen (Rinzai, Soto), Nichiren (–31).
  • Vietnam (early centuries CE): Thien (Chan) and Pure Land, often combined.
  • Tibet (7th–8th centuries): Vajrayana: Nyingma, Kagyu, Sakya, Gelug.
  • Each school adapted to its culture: e.g. Chan absorbed Daoist simplicity; Tibetan Buddhism absorbed elements of Bon.
  • Images and stupas: Gautama's images remain central everywhere; the stupa becomes the pagoda in East Asia and the chorten in Tibet.

Mission 4Evaluate

Faithful development or a new religion?

  • Claim: Mahayana is a faithful development of the Buddha's teaching.
  • For: It grew within monastic communities under the same Vinaya; its core (dukkha, anatta, the path) is shared.
  • For: Upaya is already visible in the Pali Canon: the Buddha adapted teaching to each hearer.
  • Against: Its sutras appeared centuries after the Buddha; Theravada does not accept them as his words.
  • Against: Many active Buddhas, Buddha nature and a "lesser vehicle" reshape the goal and the founder.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as the Buddha's word (buddhavacana): origin or agreement with the Dhamma? Does upaya allow any change? How do Mahayana Buddhists themselves see it?

Mission 5

Theravada: the Buddha as teacher

  • Siddhartha Gautama was a human being who, by his own effort, became awakened: a sammasambuddha, who found the path alone and taught it.
  • He is unique in this age: there is one Buddha at a time. Earlier Buddhas came before him; Metteyya will come after.
  • He had extraordinary powers and qualities, but he was not a god and did not create the world.
  • At death he entered parinirvana: no further rebirth. He cannot be reached as a person now.
  • What remains is the Dhamma, his teaching, and the Sangha that keeps it.
  • Edexcel uses Pali forms: parinibbana, Dhamma.
Mission 5Read the source

"Only preachers"

> "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller 1881)

  • In the Pali canon the Buddha tells the sick monk Vakkali, who longed to see him: whoever sees the Dhamma sees me (Samyutta Nikaya 22.87, paraphrase).

Q1 What does Dhammapada 276 say a Buddha does, and what must the follower do?

Show answer

The Buddha shows the way; the follower must make the effort.

Q2 What does the Vakkali story suggest the Buddha's true "body" is?

Show answer

His teaching, the Dhamma: a seed of the later idea of a Dharma body.

Mission 5

Mahayana: the Buddha extended

  • Context: Mahayana took shape between about the 2nd century BCE and the 1st century CE in India.
  • Many Buddhas: countless Buddhas teach now in countless world-systems, such as Amitabha in his Pure Land in the west.
  • The Buddha's life as a display: the Lotus Sutra says Shakyamuni became awakened countless ages ago; his birth and death were skilful means.
  • Earlier, a school called the Lokottaravada ("transcendentalists") already taught that the Buddha's life was supramundane: he only seemed to eat, sleep and fall ill.
  • So "Buddha" comes to mean more than one man: awakening itself, present everywhere.

Mission 5

The three bodies

  • Trikaya: "three bodies" of a Buddha. Developed in Mahayana, set out fully by the Yogacara school (c. 4th century CE).
  • Dharmakaya: the truth or Dharma body: awakening itself, formless, the same in all Buddhas.
  • Sambhogakaya: the enjoyment or bliss body: a glorious form in the Pure Lands, seen by advanced bodhisattvas.
  • Nirmanakaya: the emanation or transformation body: a form that appears in the world, such as Siddhartha.
  • A common teaching image: the moon in the sky (truth body), its light (enjoyment body), its reflections in many pools (emanation bodies).
Quick check

Mission 5

Buddha nature: Basham and the Dalai Lama

  • Buddha nature (tathagatagarbha, "the womb or seed of the Buddha"): every being has the potential, or already the nature, of a Buddha. Widely taught in East Asia and Tibet.
  • It links to the Dharmakaya: the truth body is present, hidden, in all beings.
  • A. L. Basham (1914–1986), The Wonder That Was India (1954): as a historian, he presents Mahayana as a development within Indian religion, with the Buddha increasingly honoured as a divine saviour (paraphrase).
  • 14th Dalai Lama (Tenzin Gyatso, b. 1935): teaches that the mind's basic nature is clear light; its stains can be removed, so every being can become a Buddha (paraphrase of a constant theme in his teaching).
  • Gautama stays central in Mahayana too: as Shakyamuni his image stands in temples, often beside Amitabha or great bodhisattvas, and relics are kept in stupas (in East Asia, pagodas).
Mission 5Diversity

Two concepts of Buddha

  • Who: Theravada — Gautama, a human being who awakened · Mahayana — awakening itself, appearing as many Buddhas.
  • How many now: Theravada — one per age · Mahayana — countless, in many worlds.
  • After death: Theravada — parinirvana; not reachable as a person · Mahayana — still present through the three bodies.
  • Relationship: Theravada — follow his teaching and example · Mahayana — also devotion, visualisation, Pure Land hope.
  • Shared: the Buddha is not a creator god; his awakening and his Dhamma are the heart of practice.
Mission 5Evaluate

Development or departure?

  • Claim: The trikaya doctrine departs from the Buddha's own teaching.
  • For: The Pali texts present a human teacher who is gone at death; heavenly Buddha-bodies are new.
  • For: The doctrine can make the Buddha look like a god, against his teaching of self-effort (Dhammapada 276).
  • Against: It develops seeds in the Pali texts: the Dhamma as the Buddha's body (Vakkali); the Buddha's marvellous powers.
  • Against: It answers a real question: how can a Buddha's compassion simply end at death?
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to early texts; coherence; what the doctrine does for practice.

Mission 6

Meditation on the path

  • Pali bhavana: "cultivation" or "bringing into being". Meditation is mind-training, not just sitting.
  • The Eightfold Path falls into three trainings: sila (ethics), samadhi (meditation, concentration), panna (wisdom).
  • The samadhi group: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration (the 6th, 7th and 8th factors).
  • The trainings support each other: ethics steadies the mind; a steady mind sees clearly; clear seeing deepens ethics.
  • Meditation shapes moral development: a mind trained in calm and kindness acts less from greed and hatred.
Mission 6

Two kinds of meditation

  • Samatha: calm, tranquillity. The mind settles on one object and becomes still.
  • Vipassana: insight, clear seeing. The mind looks into how things really are (see Mission 7).
  • An early text pairs them (AN 2.30, paraphrase): calm develops the mind and lets go of passion; insight develops discernment and lets go of ignorance.
  • Many Theravada teachers, following Buddhaghosa: calm suppresses the defilements for a while; insight uproots them.
  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
Mission 6

How samatha is practised

  • Choose one object: the breath (anapanasati, mindfulness of breathing), a coloured disc (kasina), a recollection of the Buddha, or loving kindness.
  • Sit upright and still; return the mind each time it wanders.
  • Counting breaths (e.g. one to ten) is a common start.
  • Five hindrances block calm: sensual desire, ill will, sloth and torpor, restlessness and worry, doubt.
  • As they fade, a steady mental image or sign (nimitta) may arise and absorption becomes possible.

Mission 6

The jhanas

  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
  • 1st jhana: applied and sustained thought, rapture (piti), happiness (sukha), one-pointedness.
  • 2nd: thought falls away; rapture and happiness remain, with inner confidence.
  • 3rd: rapture fades; happiness and mindful equanimity remain.
  • 4th: beyond pleasure and pain; pure equanimity and mindfulness.
  • Beyond these lie four formless states (e.g. infinite space; nothingness).
  • Right concentration (the 8th factor) is defined in the suttas as the four jhanas (SN 45.8).

Mission 6

Metta bhavana: loving kindness

  • Metta: loving kindness, a warm wish for the welfare of beings. Bhavana: cultivation.
  • Metta is one of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abodes"): loving kindness, compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita), equanimity (upekkha).
  • The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8, paraphrase): as a mother protects her only child with her life, cultivate a boundless heart towards all beings.
  • Method (Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga IX): begin with oneself, then a dear person, a neutral person, a hostile person, and break down the barriers between them.
  • Many Western groups (e.g. Triratna) teach five stages: self, good friend, neutral, difficult, all beings.
  • Metta is a samatha practice that can lead to jhana; it is also the antidote to ill will.

Mission 6

Thanissaro Bhikkhu: calm and insight together

  • Thanissaro Bhikkhu (b. 1949), American monk of the Thai Forest Tradition; abbot of Metta Forest Monastery, California.
  • The Path of Concentration & Mindfulness (1997) and "One Tool Among Many" (1997), paraphrased:
  • In the suttas, samatha and vipassana are qualities developed together, not two separate techniques.
  • Right mindfulness leads into right concentration: the jhanas are part of the path, not a detour.
  • He criticises teaching insight methods that skip jhana.
Mission 6Diversity

Personalised practice

  • Buddhaghosa lists forty meditation subjects and matches them to temperaments (Visuddhimagga III):
  • Greedy temperament: reflection on the body's unattractiveness.
  • Hating temperament: the four brahmaviharas (e.g. metta).
  • Deluded or speculative temperament: mindfulness of breathing.
  • Faithful temperament: recollection of the Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.
  • Traditionally a teacher (kalyanamitta, "good friend") chooses the subject for the student.
  • Traditions differ: Theravada stresses breath and the brahmaviharas; Tibetan schools add visualisation; Zen stresses sitting itself (see Mission 7).
Mission 6Evaluate

"Calm is only a preparation; insight is what frees the mind."

  • For: the Buddha learned deep formless states from his teachers Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta, then left them: calm alone did not end suffering (MN 26).
  • For: AN 2.30: only insight removes ignorance, the root of samsara.
  • For: many Theravada commentators say calm only suppresses defilements; insight uproots them.
  • Against: the Buddha awakened from the fourth jhana (the deepest of four absorptions, beyond pleasure and pain) and recalled childhood jhana as "the path" (MN 36).
  • Against: right concentration is the four jhanas: an essential factor, not an extra (SN 45.8).
  • Against: the American monk Thanissaro Bhikkhu argues that calm and insight develop together; metta also shapes moral character.
  • Judgement criteria: What do the earliest texts say? Can insight be stable without calm? Is ethics changed by calm as well as by insight?

Mission 7

Vipassana: clear seeing

  • Vipassana (Pali; Sanskrit vipashyana): insight, "seeing clearly".
  • The meditator watches experience closely: breath, sensations, feelings, thoughts.
  • Aim: to see directly the three marks of existence:
  • Anicca: everything changes; nothing stays.
  • Dukkha: nothing conditioned can fully satisfy.
  • Anatta: no fixed, lasting self is found anywhere.
  • Seeing this weakens craving and ends ignorance: insight leads towards Nirvana (nibbana).
  • Samatha steadies the mind; vipassana uses that steadiness to see (see Mission 6).
Mission 7Read the source

Foundations of mindfulness

The Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10; also Digha Nikaya 22), paraphrase: a meditator is mindful of four things, seeing each arise and pass away:

  • Body (breathing, posture, parts of the body).
  • Feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral).
  • Mind (e.g. a mind with or without anger).
  • Dhammas (mental qualities and teachings: the hindrances, the Four Noble Truths).

"'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller)

"Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Mission 7

Right mindfulness

  • Sati: mindfulness; the word also carries the sense of remembering (keeping the Dhamma in mind).
  • Right mindfulness is the 7th factor of the Eightfold Path.
  • It runs through all practice:
  • In ethics: noticing anger before it becomes harsh speech; remembering the precepts.
  • In samatha: noticing when the mind wanders and bringing it back.
  • In vipassana: watching things arise and pass.
  • In daily life: walking meditation, eating, working with full attention.
  • "Right" matters: mindfulness joined to wisdom and good intention, not attention for any purpose.

Mission 7

Ways of practising insight

  • Mahasi Sayadaw (1904–1982), Burma: mentally noting each experience ("rising", "falling" of the abdomen; "hearing", "thinking").
  • S. N. Goenka (1924–2013), in the tradition of Sayagyi U Ba Khin (1899–1971): ten-day silent courses; three days of breath awareness, then scanning bodily sensations with equanimity; metta on the last day.
  • The commentaries describe two routes: the vehicle of calm (jhana first, then insight) and the vehicle of pure insight (insight with only "momentary" concentration, without first reaching jhana). Teachers debate which is closer to the suttas.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), "Two Styles of Insight Meditation" (2005), paraphrase: the same technique can be practised in two ways:
  • As a naturalistic discipline: it brings calm, equanimity and some insight, but stays within conditioned existence.
  • Within faith and right view (the Four Noble Truths, kamma and rebirth): only then, he argues, does insight aim at final release, Nibbana.
Mission 7

Joseph Goldstein: insight for lay people

  • Joseph Goldstein (b. 1944), American teacher; co-founder of the Insight Meditation Society, Barre, Massachusetts (1975).
  • Trained in India and Burma-based lineages (including the Mahasi method).
  • Works: The Experience of Insight (1976); One Dharma (2002); Mindfulness: A Practical Guide to Awakening (2013).
  • His ideas (paraphrase): the Satipatthana is a direct path to freedom; mindfulness of each moment reveals impermanence and not-self; awakening is freedom from greed, hatred and delusion.
  • Insight practice is open to lay people in ordinary life, not only to monastics.
  • He sets meditation alongside generosity and ethics as supports of the path.
Mission 7

Zazen: just sitting

  • Zazen: Japanese, "seated meditation"; central to Zen.
  • Posture: lotus or half-lotus, back upright, eyes half-open, hands in an oval mudra.
  • Soto Zen (Dogen, 1200–1253): shikantaza, "just sitting": no object, no goal-seeking. Sitting itself expresses the Buddha nature already present.
  • Rinzai Zen: zazen often with a koan (a paradoxical question) to break through ordinary thinking towards satori or kensho (awakening, seeing one's nature).
  • Unlike samatha it has no chosen object; unlike vipassana it does not analyse experience into parts.

Mission 7

Meditation in lived Buddhism

  • Purpose: Enlightenment (awakening); on the way, calm, kindness and wisdom grow.
  • For centuries, mainly monks, especially forest monks, meditated intensively.
  • Many lay Buddhists in Asia practise mainly through chanting (refuges, precepts, protective paritta), giving (dana) and study, and keeping precepts.
  • From c. 1900, Burmese teachers (e.g. Ledi Sayadaw, then Mahasi and U Ba Khin) taught insight meditation to lay people; today it is central for many lay and Western Buddhists.
  • So the "relative importance" of meditation differs between lay and monastic life, and between schools.
Mission 7Evaluate

"Meditation is the most important element of Buddhist practice."

  • For: the Buddha awakened in meditation; only insight removes ignorance.
  • For: Dhammapada 372: knowledge needs meditation; together they bring one near Nirvana.
  • For: many modern lay and Western Buddhists put meditation at the centre of practice.
  • Against: ethics (sila) is the first training; meditation built on bad conduct goes wrong.
  • Against: many lay Buddhists practise mainly through giving, chanting and precepts; Pure Land Buddhists rely on Amida's vow.
  • Against: the trainings depend on each other, so none is "most" important; wisdom is the goal and meditation a means.
  • Judgement criteria: Important for whom (monastic, lay, Theravada, Pure Land)? Measured by the goal or by daily life? Can one training stand without the others?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Puja, images and stupas focus devotion on the Buddha. Theravada: he does not respond; devotion trains the mind and makes merit. Mahayana: buddhas and bodhisattvas can respond.
  • Dana makes punya (merit), which can be shared.

Mission 2

  • Better rebirth (through merit) is a near goal; nirvana is the ultimate goal; Spiro called these kammatic and nibbanic Buddhism.
  • Four stages break ten fetters: stream-winner (1–3), once-returner (4–5 weakened), non-returner (4–5 broken), arhat (all ten).
  • Theravada emphasises renunciation (Gombrich); insight meditation breaks the fetters (Gunaratana).

Mission 3

  • Theravada: the only surviving Nikaya school; Pali Tipitaka; rejects the Mahayana scheme of three vehicles and the label "Hinayana".
  • Gautama is central: images and stupas recall him; Gombrich: absent in belief, present in feeling.
  • Goal: nibbana, then parinibbana; renunciation and the monastic Sangha; four stages (stream-enterer, once-returner, non-returner, arahant); Gunaratana teaches insight meditation in plain terms.

Mission 4

  • Mahayana ("Great Vehicle") emerged gradually in India, c. 2nd century BCE to 1st century CE, through new sutras, devotion and the bodhisattva ideal; scholars debate why.
  • Mahayana widens the concept of Buddha: many Buddhas active now; Gautama one among them (trikaya in Mission 5).
  • Buddha nature in every being (the Dalai Lama); Mahayana took different forms in China, Korea, Japan, Vietnam and Tibet.

Mission 5

  • Theravada: Gautama was a human being who found the path, taught it, and entered parinirvana; one Buddha per age.
  • Mahayana: countless Buddhas; the trikaya: Dharmakaya (truth body), Sambhogakaya (enjoyment or heavenly body), Nirmanakaya (emanation or earthly body).
  • Buddha nature: every being has the nature of a Buddha (the Dalai Lama's teaching on the clear mind).

Mission 6

  • Meditation (bhavana) is the samadhi training of the Eightfold Path: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • Samatha calms the mind (breath, kasinas, metta) and can lead to the jhanas; vipassana gives insight (see Mission 7).
  • Metta bhavana, personalised subjects and debate about how calm relates to insight show the variety of Buddhist practice.

Mission 7

  • Vipassana is insight into the three marks (anicca, dukkha, anatta); right mindfulness (sati) runs through all practice.
  • Styles differ: Mahasi noting, Goenka body-scanning, and Zen zazen (just sitting, or with koans).
  • Two vehicles (calm first or pure insight); Bodhi: the same technique practised naturalistically or within faith and right view.
