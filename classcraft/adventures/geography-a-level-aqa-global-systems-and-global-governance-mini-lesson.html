Mission 1

Globalisation is flows

  • Products: world merchandise exports rose from $3.46 trillion (1990) to $24.62 trillion (2024).
  • Services: $0.88 trillion to $8.88 trillion over the same years.
  • Capital: foreign direct investment into low- and middle-income countries was $462 billion in 2024.
  • Labour: 304 million international migrants in 2024, nearly double the 1990 figure; they sent $665 billion in remittances to low- and middle-income countries in 2024.
  • Information: internet users rose from 15.6% of the world's people (2005) to 73.6% (2025).
Quick check
?World merchandise exports were $3.46 trillion in 1990 and $24.62 trillion in 2024. How many times as large were they in 2024?
Mission 1

Global marketing and production patterns

  • Global marketing: selling one brand worldwide, adapted to local tastes (glocalisation). KOF even counts McDonald's restaurants and IKEA stores per person as cultural globalisation.
  • Production: firms split production across countries (global production networks).
  • Distribution: container ports handled 547.1 million TEU (twenty-foot containers) in 2010 and 913.3 million in 2024.
  • Consumption: the same goods and media reach consumers worldwide; tastes converge but also adapt locally.
Mission 1

Measuring globalisation: the KOF index

  • A globalisation index combines many indicators into one score, so countries can be ranked and tracked.
  • The KOF Globalisation Index (ETH Zurich) covers 195 countries, 1970–2023, using 42 variables.
  • Three dimensions: economic, social and political.
  • Each splits into de facto (actual flows, such as trade or migrants) and de jure (rules that enable flows, such as tariffs or trade agreements).
Quick check
?Which of these would count as a de jure measure of globalisation?
Mission 1

The 2025 ranking

  • 1st Netherlands, 2nd Switzerland, 3rd Belgium, 4th United Kingdom (2023 data, published December 2025).
  • Of 195 countries, the USA ranks 28th, China 82nd and India 94th; Somalia is last.
  • On economic globalisation alone, the USA ranks 67th.
  • On political globalisation, the USA ranks 14th.
Mission 1

Why small, open economies top the table

  • Most KOF variables are ratios: trade in goods is "Exports and imports of goods (% of GDP)".
  • Trade as a share of GDP, 2024: Netherlands 154.0%, USA 25.0%.
  • The DHL index explains why: large economies' "large internal markets reduce the relative share of international flows".
  • Some variables are counts: embassies are an "absolute number", which favours large, powerful states, hence the USA's high political rank.
Mission check

?Singapore's trade is far smaller in dollars than the USA's, yet Singapore ranks 1st on KOF economic globalisation and the USA 67th. Why?

The same explains the hook. KOF mostly measures flows relative to size (% of GDP, per person). The Netherlands' trade was 154% of its GDP in 2024; the USA's 25%. The USA trades huge amounts in total, but its home market is so large that international flows are a small share of it.

Mission 2

A "shrinking world"

  • Time–space convergence: places get closer in travel or communication time as technology improves.
  • Cost–space convergence: moving goods and information gets cheaper.
  • Time–space compression (David Harvey): faster flows speed up economic and social life.
  • The world is not smaller; the friction of distance falls.
Mission 2

The 19th century: steam, rail and telegraph

  • Steamships: by the late 1830s they were regularly crossing the Atlantic; the Suez Canal's shortcut to Asia made them viable on long routes.
  • Railways: the Stockton and Darlington opened in 1825; almost a million kilometres of rail had been built by 1913.
  • Inland transport costs fell by over 90% between 1800 and 1910.
  • Telegraph: the first successful transatlantic message, August 1858, cut Europe–North America communication from ten days "to a matter of minutes".
Quick check
?What did the 1858 transatlantic telegraph shrink most?
Mission 2

The 20th century: jets and containers

  • Jet aircraft cut intercontinental journeys from days by sea to hours by air.
  • Air: by 1980 the real cost of airfreight had fallen to about a quarter of its level at the start of the Second World War. Air passengers rose from 310 million (1970) to 4,456 million (2019).
  • Containerisation: standard steel boxes move ship to train to truck without unpacking.
  • A study of trade flows, 1962–1990, found "economically large" effects, supporting "containerization being a driver of 20th century economic globalization".
  • Container port traffic: 547 million TEU (2010) to 913 million (2024).
Quick check
?Air passengers rose from 310.4 million (1970) to 4,455.7 million (2019). How many times the 1970 figure is the 2019 figure?
Mission 2

The 21st century: ICT

  • "Thanks to fibre optic cables, satellites and digital technology, the cost of overseas telecommunications is approaching zero".
  • More than 600 active and planned submarine cables; over 1.5 million km in service in early 2026.
  • Internet users: 15.6% of people (2005), 73.6% (2025, estimate).
  • Mobile phones, social networking and electronic banking move information and money instantly: time–space compression.
Mission 2

Financial and security systems

  • Financial systems: Swift, "the world's leading provider of secure financial messaging services", links "over 11,000 financial institutions". Capital can cross borders in seconds.
  • Security systems: secure messaging, encryption and payment systems make firms trust distant partners.
  • Security of routes: physical security matters too. UNCTAD: average voyages lengthened from 4,831 miles (2018) to 5,245 (2024), "as security concerns redraw the map of global shipping".
Mission 2

Management systems and trade agreements

  • Management and information systems: barcodes, tracking and supply-chain software let firms run just-in-time production across many countries.
  • Trade agreements lower tariffs and set shared rules. As of 1 October 2026, 388 regional trade agreements were in force.
Mission 2

Connected supply chains are exposed

  • A 2023 drought cut Panama Canal transits: daily crossings fell to 27 in January 2024, against a long-term average of 38.
  • Carriers had to reroute "through the Suez Canal and the Strait of Magellan and around the Cape of Good Hope".
  • About 34% of global seaborne exports of oil pass through the Strait of Hormuz.
Quick check
?What does the Panama Canal drought show about global supply chains?
Mission 2

The world shrinks unevenly

  • Internet use: 94% in high-income countries, 23% in low-income countries; 85% in urban areas, 58% in rural areas.
  • Exporting a container cost $3,204 from a landlocked developing country and $1,268 from a transit country (World Bank figures for 2014).
  • Technology shrinks the world most for rich, urban, coastal places.
Mission check

?A firm in a landlocked low-income country can now email buyers in Europe and be paid in seconds. Its goods still travel by road to a foreign port. Has the world "shrunk" for this firm?

The world has shrunk in time and cost: 14 times as many air passengers in 2019 as in 1970, and about three-quarters of people online. But it has shrunk unevenly (94% online in rich countries, 23% in poor ones), and physical geography and conflict still bite: drought at Panama, security concerns at sea.

Mission 3

An interdependent world

  • Interdependence: places rely on each other, so a change in one affects others.
  • Economic: when oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz were disrupted in 2026, IEA members released stocks.
  • Political: NATO members treat an attack on one as an attack on all.
  • Social: migrants and their families link places through money, ideas and visits.
  • Environmental: 195 Parties adopted the Paris Agreement in 2015, because emissions anywhere warm everywhere.
Mission 3

The volume of world trade

  • World merchandise exports: $3.46 trillion (1990); $24.62 trillion (2024).
  • World service exports: $0.88 trillion (1990); $8.88 trillion (2024).
  • 2024 goods by value: manufactures 68.8%, fuels 11.2%, food 9.1%, ores and metals 4.0%.
  • Values are in current dollars, so part of the rise is inflation.

Mission 3

The pattern of trade and investment

  • China's share of world goods exports: 3.8% (2000), 14.5% (2024). USA: 12.1% to 8.4%.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI), 2025: $1.6 trillion worldwide; developed economies about $723 billion, developing economies $901 billion, of which developing Asia $644 billion.
  • The least developed countries received $43 billion: just 2.7%.
  • The top 20 host economies took more than 80% of global inflows.
Mission 3

Remittances: money sent home

  • Remittances: money that migrants send to their families.
  • To low- and middle-income countries: $608 billion (2023), $665 billion (2024), $704 billion (2025).
  • Largest totals, 2024: India $137.7 billion, Mexico $67.9 billion, Philippines $40.3 billion.
  • As a share of GDP, 2024: Tajikistan 47.2%, Tonga 39.4%, Nicaragua 26.6%, Nepal 26.0%.
Mission 3

Flows that promote stability and growth

  • Money: to low- and middle-income countries in 2023, remittances ($608 billion) exceeded FDI ($408 billion) and aid ($252 billion).
  • Remittances go straight to households: food, schooling, health care, housing.
  • People: migrants fill labour shortages in host economies.
  • Ideas and technology: returning migrants bring skills, contacts and new practices.
Mission 3

Flows that cause inequality and injustice

  • Costs: sending money cost an average of 6.36% of the amount sent in Q3 2025; the UN target is "less than 3 per cent" by 2030.
  • Uneven investment: the least developed countries got 2.7% of world FDI in 2025.
  • Dependence: 95 of 142 developing countries depended on commodities for over 60% of merchandise exports in 2019–21.
  • Loss of workers: source places lose working-age people, often the most skilled.

Mission check

?A student says: "World trade is worth trillions, so trade is always the flow that matters most." Which response is best supported by this mission?

The hook's inflow was remittances: 47.2% of Tajikistan's GDP in 2024. Globally, trade is far larger, but for Tajikistan, Tonga or Nepal, money from migrants is huge relative to the size of the economy. These unequal flows build interdependence, and they bring both gains and costs.

Mission 4

Three kinds of power

  • Superpower: projects influence across the whole world, in several dimensions at once.
  • Emerging power: rapidly gaining economic and political weight, but not yet global in every dimension.
  • Regional power: dominates its own world region, with limited reach beyond it.
  • Power is relational: it shows in who can set the rules of global systems and who must follow them.
Mission 4

One economy, two sizes

  • Market exchange rates (nominal), 2025: USA $30.86 trillion, 26.0% of world GDP; China $19.63 trillion, 16.6%.
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP), 2025: China $41.26 trillion; USA $30.86 trillion.
  • PPP adjusts for price levels: better for comparing output volume. Nominal shows what output buys on world markets.
  • Per person (PPP), 2025: USA $90,267; China $29,369.
Quick check
?Using the 2025 PPP figures (China $41.26 trillion; USA $30.86 trillion), how many times as large as the USA's economy was China's?
Mission 4

Financial and political power

  • The dollar: 56.70% of the world's allocated foreign-exchange reserves in 2026 Q2; the renminbi 2.11%.
  • The veto: China, France, Russia, the UK and the USA are permanent Security Council members; non-procedural decisions need "the concurring votes of the permanent members".
  • IMF votes: the USA holds 16.49%, China 6.08%. Quota changes need 85% of votes, so the USA alone can block them.
  • These are structural advantages: rules written after 1945 still favour some states.
Mission 4

Military power

  • Military spending, 2025: USA $954 billion, 33% of the world total; China an estimated $336 billion, about 12%.
  • The 32 NATO members together spent $1,581 billion: 55% of the world total.
  • Military reach also depends on alliances and bases, not just budgets.
Mission 4

From hard to soft power

  • Hard power coerces or pays: military force, threats, sanctions, tariffs.
  • Soft power (Joseph Nye) attracts: culture, values, universities, media.
  • Between them sit economic tools that both reward and pressure, such as loans, investment and market access.
  • Brand Finance's 2026 soft-power survey: USA 1st (74.9 out of 100, down 4.6 points), China 2nd (73.5). It measures reputation, not actual flows.
Quick check
?Where does a government offering large infrastructure loans to other countries sit on the hard–soft spectrum?
Mission 4

Drivers and responders

  • Drivers set the rules: veto holders, the largest IMF shareholders, issuers of reserve currencies.
  • Responders work within those rules: most states hold small IMF vote shares and no veto.
  • Responders can still resist: by joining blocs, or by using new lenders and partners.
  • A World Bank study found that transport projects under China's Belt and Road Initiative (BRI) could raise world trade by 1.7–6.2%, but some economies "could suffer a further medium-term deterioration in their outlook for debt sustainability".
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows unequal power relations in global systems?
Mission 4

Weighing the USA and China

  • USA: leads on nominal GDP, the dollar, military spending, alliances and soft power; holds a veto.
  • China: leads on PPP GDP and goods exports; holds a veto; builds new networks such as the BRI.
  • China's GDP per person (PPP) and military spending are each about a third of the USA's (our calculation).
  • Judgement: the USA is an actual superpower in every dimension; China a potential one, strongest economically.
Mission check

?A newspaper claims: "India's population is now the world's largest, so India is about to become a superpower." Which response is best supported by this mission?

The superpower question turns on which measure you use. At PPP China's economy is larger; at market rates, and in finance, military reach and soft power, the USA leads. The USA is the actual superpower; China the leading potential one.

Mission 5

The tools of market access

  • Tariff: a tax on imports. Quota: a limit on the quantity imported. Subsidy: government money to domestic producers.
  • All three make it harder for foreign producers to sell.
  • The WTO sets the rules: 166 members, "representing 98 per cent of world trade".
Mission 5

Agreements and blocs

  • Trade agreements cut tariffs between partners; trade blocs go further (e.g. the EU single market).
  • 388 regional trade agreements were in force on 1 October 2026.
  • Inside a bloc, partners dominate: intra-EU trade ranged from 28% (Cyprus) to 83% (Luxembourg) of each member's goods trade in 2025.
  • Outsiders face the bloc's common external tariff.

Access depends on development level

Tariff escalation

  • "Higher import duties on semi-processed products than on raw materials, and higher still on finished products".
  • It "discourages the development of processing activity in the countries where raw materials originate".
  • Result: smaller economies export beans and fibre; value is added elsewhere.

Mission 5

Subsidies: cotton

  • In 2003 four African countries, Benin, Burkina Faso, Chad and Mali, the Cotton Four, raised cotton at the WTO.
  • WTO members agreed in 2005 that "all forms of export subsidies for cotton will be eliminated by developed countries in 2006".
  • They also called for "trade distorting domestic subsidies for cotton production" to be reduced "more ambitiously".
  • Subsidies in large economies lower world prices that smaller producers receive.
Mission 5

Impacts on well-being

  • Economic: cotton supports "hundreds of millions of people" through its value chain.
  • Agreements can be withdrawn: the USA ended Ethiopia's AGOA preferences from 1 January 2022.
  • The decision threatened Ethiopia's textile industry, which supplies global fashion brands.
  • Social: jobs, wages and school fees in export sectors depend on access.

Mission 5

Your life

  • The UK says its DCTS "benefits UK businesses and consumers by reducing the import cost of thousands of products".
  • Coffee, cut flowers and vegetables are among Ethiopia's top exports: goods on UK shelves.
  • Tariffs, quotas and preferences shape prices you pay, choice in shops, and jobs and incomes for producers abroad.
Mission check

The two farmers in the opening differ because of where they farm. Development level and agreements decide access; the importer can take it away, and access then decides incomes, jobs and the prices consumers pay.

Mission 6

How TNCs organise production

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) operates in more than one country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm builds or buys a business abroad and keeps a lasting say in running it.
  • Outsourcing: contracting another firm to do part of the work. Offshoring: moving work to another country, owned or contracted.
Mission 6

Apple: outsourcing at global scale

  • Headquarters: Cupertino, California; about 166,000 full-time equivalent employees (September 2025).
  • "A significant majority" of manufacturing is done by outsourcing partners located mainly in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Partners "primarily located in Asia" carry out final assembly of "substantially all" its hardware.
  • Apple keeps design, software, services and marketing.
Mission 6

Apple: markets, gains and risks

  • Net sales, year to September 2025: $416.2 billion; Greater China $64.4 billion, a large new market.
  • Gain: these arrangements "can lower operating costs".
  • Risk: they "reduce the Company's direct control over production and distribution".
  • Tariffs and trade controls "can materially adversely affect the Company's business and supply chain".

Mission 6

Toyota: spatial organisation

  • Headquarters in Japan. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 overseas manufacturing organisations in 27 countries and regions.
  • Vehicle production, year to March 2026 (thousands): Japan 4,149; North America 2,050; Asia 1,802; Europe 815; other 477; total 9,293.
  • Employees, 31 March 2026: 390,927, of whom 207,137 in Japan, 66,331 in Asia, 65,411 in North America and 24,741 in Europe.
Mission 6

Toyota: production and linkages

  • The Toyota Production System: Just in Time, where "each process produces only what is needed for the next process in a continuous flow", and Jidoka, stopping work when a problem occurs.
  • Low stocks need reliable suppliers close to the plant: strong local linkages.
  • In Asia, Toyota has been "developing relationships with local suppliers".
  • Building near customers lets it match local costs to local sales, reducing currency risk.
Quick check

Mission 6

Toyota: trading and marketing

  • Sells in about 200 countries and regions; 9.6 million vehicles in the year to March 2026.
  • Glocalisation: Toyota designs for local "road conditions, climate, and values", an approach it calls "Best in town".
  • Share of vehicles sold that were made in the same region: North America 74.6%, Europe 69.0%, Asia 96.4%.
  • Bulky goods such as cars cost a lot to ship, so building near buyers pays; small, light goods such as phones can be assembled in a few places and shipped worldwide.
  • Building inside a market reduces exposure to tariffs: Toyota notes that "in 2025, a significant increase in tariffs on exports to the United States" was announced.
Mission 6

Toyota's impacts on host countries

  • Jobs: 183,790 employees outside Japan in March 2026: 47.0% of the total (our calculation).
  • Output and linkages: 2.05 million vehicles built in North America in the year to March 2026; local suppliers grow around plants.
  • Skills: the Toyota Production System spreads to local managers and suppliers.
  • Costs: key decisions and profits are controlled from Japan; plants are exposed to trade policy, such as the 2025 US tariff increases.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): weigh impacts by country and over time; name the plant region and give a dated figure.

The backlash is real but partial: UK regions more exposed to Chinese imports backed Leave more, and US–China tariffs rose to 126.1% before falling back. Globalisation's gains and losses fall on different places, which is why the judgement must be conditional.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Globalisation is flows of products, services, capital, labour and information, all growing since 1990.
  • Global marketing and global production, distribution and consumption tie these flows to brands and supply chains.
  • Indices such as KOF combine many variables; because most are ratios to GDP, small, open economies top the table.

Mission 2

  • Factors: financial, transport, security, communications, management and information systems, and trade agreements.

Mission 3

  • Places are interdependent economically, politically, socially and environmentally; unequal flows of money, people, ideas and technology bring both growth and inequality.
  • Trade has grown (services faster than goods) and its pattern has shifted towards China; FDI is concentrated in a few hosts.

Mission 4

  • Superpower, emerging and regional powers differ in the reach and breadth of their power; measure each dimension with dated figures and name the measure (PPP or nominal).
  • Unequal power relations: rule-setters (veto, IMF votes, reserve currency) drive global systems; others respond or resist within them.

Mission 5

  • Market access is set by tariffs, quotas, subsidies, agreements and blocs; preferences give least developed countries tariff-free access that others lack, but importers can withdraw them (Ethiopia, AGOA, 2022).
  • Tariff escalation and rich-country subsidies keep smaller economies exporting raw goods; these rules affect well-being abroad and prices at home.

Mission 6

  • TNCs organise production through FDI (Toyota: plants in 27 countries and regions, 47% of staff outside Japan) or outsourcing (Apple: partners mainly in Asia); both create linkages, new markets and risks.

Mission 7

  • Soy is crushed for meal and oil; China's rising meat diet drives its soybean imports, now mostly from Brazil.
  • Market access (China's lower tariff on beans; the 2018 tariff on US soybeans) shapes who supplies, who processes and whose land changes.

Mission 8

  • Norms become laws and institutions (GATT 1948 → WTO 1995); the UN (1945) and Bretton Woods bodies (1944) still regulate global systems.
  • They promote stability and growth, but unequal power (veto; USA 16.49% vs the 15% blocking share) can widen inequality; global rules work through national and local scales.

Mission 9

  • Post-1945 agencies such as the IMF can restore growth and stability (Sri Lanka 2023–24), but conditions raise costs for households and votes follow quotas.

Mission 10

  • Global commons are shared by all and open to overuse; the right to sustainable development must be balanced with protection.
  • Antarctica (to the Convergence) is a cold, dry, high, ice-covered continent with a krill-based ocean ecosystem. It is threatened by climate change, fishing and whaling, potential mining, tourism and research, and is vulnerable to pressures from outside.

Mission 11

  • Treaty (1959) and Protocol (1991): peace, science, frozen claims and an open-ended mining ban, enforced by inspection and consensus; IWC moratorium (1982) weakened by objections and withdrawal; CCAMLR has the strongest compliance tools.
  • UNEP and NGOs (ASOC, IAATO) monitor, advise and lobby without votes; governance affects scientists, tourists and wildlife in Antarctica, and sea level, ozone science and food chains elsewhere.

Mission 12

  • World trade's share of GDP more than doubled to 2008, then stalled; regions that lost from import competition backed nationalist choices more.
  • Weigh benefits (poverty fall, integration) against costs (inequality, concentrated losses, conflict, embodied emissions) for named places.
