Mission 1

Mission 1: What do Christians believe happens after we die, and why don't they all agree?

Make a guess: What do Christians believe happens after we die, and why don't they all agree?

Mission 1

Death is not the end

  • Christians believe in eternal life: life with God that death cannot end.
  • The reason: Jesus rose from the dead. Christians believe his resurrection is the first of many.
  • The creeds: "I believe in … the resurrection of the body, and the life everlasting" (Apostles' Creed).
  • Belief about the future shapes life now: hope, comfort and responsibility.
Mission 1Read the source

"I am the resurrection" (John 11)

  • Context: Jesus' friend Lazarus has been dead four days. His sister Martha meets Jesus (John 11:17–20).
  • Martha believes Lazarus will rise "in the resurrection at the last day" (John 11:24).
  • "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies." (John 11:25)
  • "Whoever lives and believes in me will never die." (John 11:26)

Q1 What does Jesus claim about himself?

Show answer

He is the source of resurrection and life.

Q2 What does Jesus promise to those who believe in him?

Show answer

They will live, even though they die.

Mission 1Read the source

A new kind of body

  • Paul: "Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruit of those who are asleep." (1 Corinthians 15:20): Jesus' resurrection is the first of a harvest.
  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable." (1 Corinthians 15:42)
  • "It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:44)
  • Like a seed and the plant it becomes: the same person, transformed (1 Corinthians 15:36–38).
  • Q: What does "spiritual body" mean here? (A real, transformed body raised by God's Spirit, not a ghost.)
Mission 1

Judgement

  • Christians believe all people will be judged by God through Christ.
  • Matthew 25:31–46: the sheep and the goats: people are judged by how they treated the hungry, thirsty, stranger, sick and prisoner.
  • Most Christians hold that judgement looks at faith and how it was lived; they differ on the balance.
Quick check
?In the parable of the sheep and the goats (Matthew 25:31–46), what decides whether people are judged as "sheep"?
Mission 1

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: being with God for ever in perfect happiness. Catholics call it the beatific vision: seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12).
  • Hell: being cut off from God for ever.
  • "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life." (Matthew 25:46)
  • Catholic teaching: hell is a state of self-exclusion: a person's own final choice to refuse God's love. God predestines no one to hell (CCC 1033, 1037).
Mission 1

Purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: purgatory is a state of purification after death for those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully holy. All in purgatory will reach heaven.
  • Catholics pray for the dead and offer Mass for them (2 Maccabees 12:46; 1 Corinthians 3:15 "saved, but as through fire").
  • Most Protestants reject purgatory: Jesus' death is enough; scripture does not teach it.
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead, but do not define purgatory as Catholics do.
Quick check
?Which statement about purgatory is accurate?
Mission 1

Why it matters now

  • Hope and comfort: death is not the final word; Christian funerals speak of "sure and certain hope of the resurrection" (Book of Common Prayer).
  • Responsibility: judgement means how people live matters (Matthew 25).
  • Value of life: each person is made for eternal life with God.
  • Catholic practice: prayers and Masses for the dead; All Souls' Day (2 November); the last rites prepare the dying.
  • Some Christians warn against being good only to reach heaven: love should be its own reason.
Mission 1Evaluate

"A loving God would not send anyone to hell."

  • Reasons for: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4). Eternal punishment for limited wrong seems unjust. Universalists and some theologians hope all will be saved.
  • Reasons against: Jesus speaks of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46; Luke 16). Love respects free will: people may finally refuse God. Catholic teaching: God sends no one; hell is self-exclusion (CCC 1033). Justice for victims requires that evil be judged.
  • Another view: annihilation: the unsaved cease to exist, with no endless suffering.
  • Judge it by: Does "send" describe what hell is? How should Jesus' warnings be read: literally or as pictures? Can love and justice both be fully satisfied?
Mission check

?At a funeral the priest says: "We pray for Maria, that God will cleanse her of any remaining sin, so that she may soon see God face to face." Which tradition's belief is this prayer expressing?

Christians share hope in resurrection, judgement and eternal life with God. They differ on purgatory, on what heaven and hell are like, and on who will finally be saved.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If God judges everyone at death, why do Catholics believe in a second judgement at the end of time?

Make a guess: If God judges everyone at death, why do Catholics believe in a second judgement at the end of time?

Mission 2

The four last things

  • Catholic teaching names four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell.
  • Death: the end of earthly life; the soul is separated from the body. It is the end of the time in which a person can choose for or against God.
  • Judgement: each life is seen in the light of Christ.
  • Heaven: eternal life with God.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God.
  • Purgatory is not one of the four: it is a stage on the way to heaven.
  • The hope behind them: Jesus' resurrection (see Mission 1). Death is real, but not the last word.
Mission 2

Two judgements

  • Particular judgement: at the moment of death, each soul is judged by Christ, and goes to heaven (perhaps through purgatory) or to hell (CCC 1021–1022, paraphrase).
  • Final (Last) judgement: at the end of time, when Christ returns (the Parousia). The dead are raised; all humanity is judged together, body and soul (CCC 1038–1041, paraphrase).
  • "We must all be revealed before the judgement seat of Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:10).
  • Why two? The particular judgement concerns the individual. The final judgement makes public the meaning of all of history, and includes the body, and every effect a life has had on others.
  • The final judgement does not overturn the first; it completes it.

Mission 2Read the source

"Saved, but as through fire"

"If any man's work is burnt, he will suffer loss, but he himself will be saved, but as through fire." (1 Corinthians 3:15)

  • Paul pictures each life as a building on the foundation of Christ (3:11). Some materials survive the "fire" of the Day; some burn (3:12–14).
  • Catholic reading: some people die in God's friendship but are not yet fully purified. Purgatory is their final cleansing before heaven (CCC 1030–1031, paraphrase).
  • Purgatory is not a second chance and not a lesser hell: all in purgatory are saved.
  • The living can help by prayer and Masses for the dead (2 Maccabees 12:45;).

Q1 In 3:15, does the person whose work burns end up saved or lost?

Show answer

"He himself will be saved."

Q2 What is the foundation every life is built on, in 3:11?

Show answer

Jesus Christ.

Mission 2Read the source

The Rich Man and Lazarus

"There was a certain rich man … living in luxury every day. A certain beggar, named Lazarus, was taken to his gate, full of sores" (Luke 16:19–20).

  • Both die. Lazarus is carried to "Abraham's bosom"; the rich man is "in torment" (16:22–23).
  • "Between us and you there is a great gulf fixed" (16:26): after death, the choice made in life stands.
  • "If they don't listen to Moses and the prophets, neither will they be persuaded if one rises from the dead" (16:31).
  • Catholic significance: judgement follows death at once (the brothers are still alive: a sign of particular judgement, CCC 1021); ignoring the poor is a serious failure of love; people already have God's word (Moses and the prophets) to guide them, and a miracle would not persuade those who ignore it; a hint of the Resurrection.
  • Q: What does the "great gulf" suggest about choices after death? (They can no longer be changed.)
Mission 2Read the source

The Unforgiving Servant

  • Peter asks how often he must forgive. Jesus: "until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:21–22).
  • A king cancels a servant's huge debt. That servant then has a fellow servant, who owes him little, thrown into prison.
  • The king: "Shouldn't you also have had mercy on your fellow servant, even as I had mercy on you?" (18:33)
  • "So my heavenly Father will also do to you, if you don't each forgive your brother from your hearts" (18:35).
  • Catholic significance: God's mercy is vast, but refusing mercy to others closes the heart to it. Judgement reveals whether we have received love and passed it on.
  • Q: What is the servant condemned for? (Refusing to show the mercy he had received.)

Mission 2

Resurrection of the body, not just a surviving soul

  • "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. … It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:42, 44)
  • Catholics believe the soul survives death; but the full hope is the resurrection of the body: the whole person, body and soul, reunited and transformed at the end of time, like the risen Jesus.
  • Popular belief often imagines only a soul floating free for ever, or ghosts. Catholic teaching: humans are a unity of body and soul; a soul alone is not a complete human being.
  • Significance: the body matters (care for the sick, respect for the dead, burial).
Mission 2

Heaven and hell

  • Heaven: perfect, eternal union with God, seeing God "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12): the beatific vision.
  • Not a place in the sky, but a state of complete happiness and love with God, Mary and the saints (CCC 1023–1029, paraphrase).
  • Hell: eternal self-exclusion from communion with God, by freely and finally refusing God's love (CCC 1033, paraphrase).
  • Its chief pain is separation from God, for whom humans were made (CCC 1035, paraphrase).
  • Jesus warns: "These will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life" (Matthew 25:46).
  • The question: how can hell fit with God's unconditional love and universal mercy? (the later screen; also.)
Mission 2Evaluate

"Catholics should hope that everyone will be saved."

  • Reasons for: God "desires all people to be saved" (1 Timothy 2:4); CCC 1037: God predestines no one to hell, and the Church prays daily for mercy for all; the medieval mystic Julian of Norwich: a hidden "Great Deed" will make all things well; the theologian Hans Urs von Balthasar (1905–1988, Dare We Hope "That All Men Be Saved"?, 1986) argued Catholics may hope, though not know, that hell is empty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus' own warnings of eternal punishment (Matthew 25:46) and the "great gulf" (Luke 16:26); Catholic teaching affirms that hell is real and eternal (CCC 1035); love cannot be forced, so free creatures may refuse God for ever; Julian herself accepted that some are condemned; critics say universal hope weakens the urgency of conversion.
  • Judge it by: Is there a difference between hoping something and teaching it? Can God's mercy override human freedom? How should Jesus' warnings be read: as predictions or as urgent calls?
Mission check

?Sam says: "If my nan was judged by Christ the moment she died, a Last Judgement at the end of time is pointless. Her verdict is already known." Which reply best reflects Catholic teaching?

Death, judgement, heaven and hell frame Catholic hope: each life judged at death, all creation renewed at the end, with purgatory and prayer in between.

Mission 3: Who decides what Catholics believe, and why are they trusted to do it?

Make a guess: Who decides what Catholics believe, and why are they trusted to do it?

Mission 3

Scripture, Tradition, Magisterium

  • Scripture: the Bible, the written Word of God.
  • Tradition: the faith handed on by the apostles in the Church's life, creeds, worship and teaching: "hold the traditions which you were taught by us, whether by word or by letter" (2 Thessalonians 2:15).
  • Magisterium (Latin magister, teacher): the Church's living teaching office.
  • Catholic teaching: the three are so joined that none stands without the others (Dei Verbum 10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God; it serves it.
Mission 3

The living teaching office

  • Who: the pope and the bishops in communion with him (CCC 100, paraphrase).
  • Function: to interpret Scripture and Tradition authentically, and apply them to new questions.
  • Why "living": new questions (medicine, technology, the economy) need answers faithful to the old truths.
  • Importance: it keeps Catholics united in one faith across the world and the centuries.
  • Catholics believe the Holy Spirit guides it: "he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13).
Mission 3

Apostolic succession

  • Jesus to Peter: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). "Feed my sheep." (John 21:17)
  • To the apostles: "Whoever listens to you listens to me" (Luke 10:16).
  • Catholics believe the apostles appointed successors by the laying on of hands: "commit the same things to faithful men who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2).
  • Catholics believe bishops are the successors of the apostles, and the pope, Bishop of Rome, is the successor of Peter.
  • So, Catholics believe, their teaching authority comes from Christ, through an unbroken line.
Quick check
?According to Catholic teaching (CCC 100), who makes up the Magisterium?
Mission 3

Conciliar and pontifical

  • Conciliar: teaching given by the bishops of the world gathered in an ecumenical council with the pope.
  • Model: the Council of Jerusalem: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us" (Acts 15:28).
  • The Catholic Church counts 21 ecumenical councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–65).
  • Pontifical (from pontifex, a title of the pope): teaching given by the pope himself.
  • Forms: encyclicals (letters to the whole Church, e.g. Laudato Si', 2015, on care for creation), apostolic exhortations (e.g. Evangelii Gaudium, 2013).
  • Catholics believe both carry authority because both are the voice of the successors of the apostles.
Mission 3

Ordinary and extraordinary

  • Extraordinary magisterium: a solemn definition of a doctrine as part of the faith.
  • By an ecumenical council (e.g. Nicaea, 325, on the Son being of one substance with the Father).
  • Or by the pope speaking ex cathedra ("from the chair" of Peter) on faith or morals, as defined at the First Vatican Council (1870). Clear examples: the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption (1950).
  • Ordinary magisterium: the everyday teaching of popes and bishops: encyclicals, letters, catechisms, preaching.
  • When the bishops worldwide, united with the pope, teach a matter of faith or morals as definitive, the ordinary and universal magisterium is also held to be infallible (Lumen Gentium 25, paraphrase).
  • Other ordinary teaching asks for Catholics' respectful assent ("religious submission of mind and will").
Mission 3Read the source

Evangelium Vitae 65

Pope John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae ("The Gospel of Life", 1995), paragraph 65 (paraphrase):

  • Euthanasia is an act or omission that, by its nature and intention, causes death in order to end all suffering.
  • It is different from refusing aggressive medical treatment that is out of proportion to any hope of benefit, and from pain relief.
  • The pope, "in harmony with" his predecessors and in communion with the bishops, confirms that euthanasia is "a grave violation of the law of God".
  • This teaching rests on natural law and Scripture, is handed on by Tradition and is "taught by the ordinary and universal Magisterium".

Q1 Which forms of magisterium does EV 65 bring together?

Show answer

Pontifical: the pope confirms; ordinary and universal: what the bishops worldwide already teach.

Q2 Is EV 65 an ex cathedra definition?

Show answer

No: it is the pope solemnly confirming the ordinary universal teaching, not a new definition.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The Magisterium is the most important source of authority for Catholics."

  • For: CCC 100 gives it alone the task of authentic interpretation. Without it, people read the Bible in conflicting ways. Its authority comes from Christ through the apostles (Luke 10:16). It answers new questions Scripture does not address directly.
  • Against: it is not above the Word of God but serves it (Dei Verbum 10): Scripture is primary. Conscience is where God's voice echoes within each person (Gaudium et Spes 16, paraphrase). The three sources depend on each other, so none is "most" important.
  • Other Christians: most Protestants hold Scripture alone as the final authority (Luther, 1521: unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason). The Orthodox accept councils and Tradition but not the pope's universal authority.
  • Judge it by: Can authority be ranked if the sources depend on each other? What prevents division? What role is left for conscience?
Mission check

?A new technology raises a moral question that the Bible never mentions. According to Catholic teaching, how would the Church give authoritative guidance on it?

For Catholics, authority is not one person's opinion: the pope and bishops, as successors of the apostles, serve and interpret the Word handed on to them.

Mission 4

What is an ecumenical council?

  • An ecumenical council is a gathering of the bishops of the whole world, with the Pope, to teach and decide for the whole Church.
  • Its teaching is part of the conciliar Magisterium (see Mission 3): the Church's teaching authority exercised together.
  • Catholics count 21 such councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), called "Vatican II" because it was the second council held at the Vatican (in St Peter's Basilica), after Vatican I (1869–1870).
  • Earlier councils mostly met to settle a dispute or answer a challenge (e.g. Trent, 1545–1563, after the Reformation).
  • Vatican II was different: no single crisis. Its aim was renewal: John XXIII's word was aggiornamento, Italian for "bringing up to date".
Mission 4

The story, 1959–1965

  • 25 January 1959: Pope John XXIII surprises the Church by announcing a council.
  • 11 October 1962: the Council opens. Over 2,000 bishops attend, from every continent.
  • 3 June 1963: John XXIII dies. The new Pope, Paul VI, continues the Council.
  • Four autumn sessions (1962, 1963, 1964, 1965), with drafts revised between them.
  • Every document was debated and voted on by the bishops, then approved by the Pope.
  • 8 December 1965: the Council closes, having approved 16 documents: 4 constitutions, 9 decrees, 3 declarations.

Mission 4

The four key documents

The four constitutions are the Council's most important teaching.

  • Dei Verbum ("The Word of God", 1965): how God reveals himself; Scripture and Tradition.
  • Lumen Gentium ("Light of the nations", 1964): what the Church is.
  • Sacrosanctum Concilium ("This sacred Council", 1963): the liturgy (the Church's worship).
  • Gaudium et Spes ("Joy and hope", 1965): the Church in the modern world.
  • Each takes its name from its first words in Latin.
Mission 4

Dei Verbum: God speaks

  • God reveals himself above all in Jesus Christ.
  • Revelation is passed on through Scripture and Tradition, which flow from the same divine source and form one deposit of faith (DV 9–10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God but serves it: it teaches only what has been handed on (DV 10, paraphrase).
  • Easy access to the Bible should be open to all; Catholics are urged to read it (DV 22, 25).
  • For Catholic living: Bible study groups, Scripture in the vernacular, a longer selection of readings at Mass.
Mission 4

Lumen Gentium: what the Church is

  • The Church is a mystery: a visible community and a spiritual reality, a sign of union with God (LG 1).
  • It is the People of God (ch. 2) before it is a hierarchy: all the baptised share in Christ's work.
  • The hierarchy (ch. 3): bishops, as successors of the apostles, with the Pope as head; collegiality.
  • The laity (ch. 4) and a universal call to holiness (ch. 5): holiness is for everyone, not just priests and religious.
  • Mary (ch. 8) and the Church's hope of heaven.
  • For Catholic living: lay readers, catechists and parish councils; every Catholic called to be a saint.
Mission 4

Sacrosanctum Concilium: worship renewed

  • The liturgy is the summit towards which the Church's activity is directed and the source of its strength (SC 10, paraphrase).
  • Aim: the full, active participation of the whole people (SC 14), not just the priest.
  • Permitted the vernacular (local language) alongside Latin (SC 36); this soon became normal practice.
  • More Scripture in worship; simpler, clearer rites.
  • For Catholic living: Mass in English, the people saying the responses, and later the priest usually facing the people across the altar (introduced by guidelines in 1964, after the Council's first document).
Quick check

Mission 4

What changed?

  • Worship: Mass in the vernacular; altar facing the people; lay readers and ministers of Holy Communion.
  • Laity: parish councils; lay people as catechists, chaplains and teachers of the faith.
  • Other Christians: ecumenism (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964): dialogue and prayer for unity.
  • Other religions: Nostra Aetate (1965): the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions (NA 2, paraphrase); a new relationship with Jews.
Mission 4Diversity

One Council, different readings

  • The Church's official teaching receives the Council as the work of the Holy Spirit, and it shapes parish life everywhere.
  • Renewal in continuity: Benedict XVI (2005) argued that the Council must be read in continuity with earlier teaching, not as a break.
  • "Spirit of the Council": some Catholics want reforms to go further (e.g. more lay and women's leadership) and appeal to the Council's spirit of openness.
  • Traditionalists: some prefer the older Latin Mass; a small group (the Society of St Pius X, founded by Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, 1970) rejects parts of the Council's teaching, e.g. on religious liberty and ecumenism.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Vatican II was the most important event in the Catholic Church in the last 100 years."

  • Reasons for: it changed the experience of every Catholic every Sunday (SC); it gave lay people a new role (LG); it opened dialogue with other Christians, other religions and science (GS 36, Nostra Aetate).
  • Reasons against: its teaching is not new faith but "aggiornamento", so the deeper continuity matters more; some say later developments (e.g. the global growth of the Church in Africa and Asia, or the papacy of John Paul II) shaped today's Church as much; traditionalist critics see some changes as loss.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "important": doctrine, daily practice, or numbers affected? Did the Council change the faith or how it is lived? Whose experience of the Church do you measure?
Mission check

?A parishioner who remembers Mass in the 1950s says: "Back then we watched the priest and listened to Latin. Now we all join in, in English." Which explanation of the change best fits the history of the Second Vatican Council?

Vatican II renewed how the Church worships, understands itself and meets the modern world, through bishops consulting, debating and voting together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Because Jesus rose, Christians believe in resurrection, judgement, and heaven or hell; 1 Corinthians 15 describes a transformed "spiritual body".
  • Catholics believe in purgatory and pray for the dead; most Protestants reject it (39 Articles, XXII).
  • Views differ: places or states; eternal punishment, annihilation or universal salvation.

Mission 2

  • Four last things: death, judgement, heaven, hell. Particular judgement at death; final judgement at Christ's return, when bodies rise.
  • Purgatory (1 Corinthians 3:15): final purification of the saved, helped by prayer.
  • Matthew 18 and Luke 16 link judgement to mercy shown; resurrection of the body, not only survival of the soul.

Mission 3

  • Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium (pope and bishops in communion with him) belong together; the Magisterium serves the Word of God (CCC 100).
  • Apostolic succession: Catholics believe bishops succeed the apostles and the pope succeeds Peter.
  • Magisterium is conciliar (councils, e.g. Nicaea 325, Vatican II) or pontifical (popes, e.g. encyclicals); it can be ordinary (everyday) or extraordinary (solemn definitions).

Mission 4

  • Vatican II (1962–1965), called by John XXIII and closed by Paul VI, sought renewal (aggiornamento) of the Church's life.
  • Its four key documents: Dei Verbum (revelation), Lumen Gentium (the Church), Sacrosanctum Concilium (liturgy), Gaudium et Spes (the Church in the modern world).
