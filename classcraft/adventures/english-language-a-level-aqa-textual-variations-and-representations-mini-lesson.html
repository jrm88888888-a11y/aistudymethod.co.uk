Language in context · mode, field, tenorScreen 2

Mode is a continuum

  • Typical speech: spontaneous, interactive, ephemeral, shared context (this, over there), non-fluency.
  • Typical writing: planned, edited, permanent, self-contained, denser noun phrases.
  • Many texts sit between. A group chat is written but synchronous (near real time), interactive and informal, with speech-like ellipsis and emoji for tone.
  • A court transcript records speech, but in a formal, rule-bound setting, kept as a permanent record.

Function, and Halliday's three kinds of meaning

  • Function: what a text is for: to inform, persuade, instruct, entertain, or keep a relationship going (phatic talk: hiya, how's things?).
  • Most texts have several: Text B questions a witness, tests evidence and creates a record.
  • Halliday linked context to three metafunctions: field shapes ideational meaning (content), tenor shapes interpersonal meaning (relationships), and mode shapes textual meaning (organisation).

Mission checkScreen 9

?A group chat and a court transcript: why do they look nothing alike?

Their whole context differs. The chat is fast, synchronous typing between friends about a meet-up; the transcript records formal, rule-bound talk between unequal participants about evidence, kept as a permanent record. Mode, field, tenor and function each leave traces, so strong answers link features to the factor that best explains them.

Textual variations · representationsScreen 1

What a representation is

  • A representation is a version of a person, group, place or event, built from language choices. It re-presents reality; it never simply copies it.
  • Every text selects (what goes in), foregrounds (what comes first) and omits (what is left out).
  • Choices are shaped by audience, purpose, genre, mode and context.
  • Texts also position readers: they invite us to see things one way and to adopt a role (visitor, worried resident).
  • Writers construct identities too: for themselves, their readers and the people they describe.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A brochure and a crime report describe one town. Which is true?

Both are true in their details and both are partial. A representation is built from choices of what to include, foreground and leave out, and of words and grammar, all shaped by audience and purpose. Your job in Paper 1 is not to decide which text is true but to analyse how each constructs its version of the place.

Mission 3

Textual variation · comparing textsScreen 1

Compare through context

  • Compare how texts use language, not just what they say, and explain each difference by context.
  • Mode: spoken, written or multimodal; spontaneous or crafted.
  • Audience and purpose (Edexcel: field, function, audience): who it's for and what it does.
  • Genre and time: conventions and period style.
  • Make connections: similarities count as much as differences.

Mode, audience and time

  • Mode is a continuum: Bird's letter is written but personal; the vlog is spoken but planned, and multimodal.
  • In the vlog, images do the describing, so language points: deixis (this, that ridge).
  • Bird writes to one known reader; the vlogger addresses a mass audience as individuals (guys, you): synthetic personalisation.
  • Time: Bird's impersonal one, formal lexis (crystalline), long sentences, semicolons; the vlog's informal gonna and vague like.

Textual variation · comparing textsScreen 8

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 1, Section A: two texts, one contemporary and one older, linked by topic. Q1 and Q2 analyse each text (25 marks each); Q3 compares them (20 marks), rewarding connections informed by linguistic concepts and methods (AO4).
Mission checkScreen 9

?An 1873 letter and a vlog today describe one lake. What changed?

The context changed, and the language changed to fit it. Bird's written letter has to paint the lake in words for one distant reader, in 19th-century formal style; the vlog is spoken, multimodal and addresses a mass audience, so it points at what the camera shows. Some things stayed, such as the facts and the sense of wonder.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Mode, field, tenor (or audience) and function (or purpose), plus genre, describe a text's context.
  • Mode is a continuum: electronic texts such as group chats blend features of speech and writing.
  • Halliday: field shapes ideational meaning, tenor interpersonal meaning, mode textual meaning.
  • AQA: audience, purpose, genre, mode.
  • A representation is a constructed version of reality: texts select, foreground and omit, and position readers.
  • Analyse it through lexis and connotation, naming, agency (passives), modification, presupposition, discourse structure and address.
  • Paper 1 Section A: analyse two linked texts, one contemporary and one older (25 + 25), then compare them (20).
  • Compare how texts use language, and explain each difference or similarity through mode, audience, purpose, genre and time.
  • Mode is a continuum: written texts can feel immediate; spoken texts can be planned and multimodal.
  • Strong comparisons connect a shared or contrasting feature, give precise evidence from both texts and explain it by context.

Key quotations to learn

  • I have found a dream of beauty at which one might look all one's life and sigh (Bird, Letter I, opening) impersonal one and evaluative metaphor: formal written style conveying awe
  • Lake Tahoe is before me (Bird, Letter I) present tense and deixis: a written text creating immediacy
  • a sheet of water twenty-two miles long by ten broad (Bird, Letter I) precise noun phrase doing the describing that a camera would do
