Mission 1

He loves to learn. So what is Blake's schoolboy protesting about?

Make a guess: He loves to learn. So what is Blake's schoolboy protesting about?

Eduqas anthology · "The Schoolboy"Screen 1

Blake and the poem

  • William Blake (1757–1827) was a London poet, painter and engraver who printed his poems as hand-coloured plates.
  • "The Schoolboy" first appeared in Songs of Innocence (1789). In later copies of Songs of Innocence and of Experience Blake moved it into Experience.
  • Innocence: joy, freedom, trust. Experience: sorrow, control, adult systems. This poem travels from the first to the second.
  • The speaker is a child, not Blake himself.

The poem stanza by stanza

StanzaWhat happens

Eduqas anthology · "The Schoolboy"Screen 5

Form and the caged bird

  • Six five-line stanzas rhyming ABABB (morn / tree / horn / me / company). The extra fifth line lingers, like a sigh.
  • Most lines have four beats: "I LOVE to RISE in a SUMmer MORN"; the fifth line is usually shorter.
  • Stanza 4 is built from rhetorical questions: the speaker is pleading, not stating.
  • Extended metaphors: the child as a caged bird (stanza 4), then as a young plant whose buds are nipped off (stanza 5) and a harvest that never comes (stanza 6).

?He loves to learn. So what is Blake's schoolboy protesting about?

The boy loves learning in the open; what he protests against is schooling that cages a child "born for joy". Blake's plant and season metaphors warn the parents that a crushed childhood means a winter, not a harvest, in adult life. Many readers see the poem's move into Experience as fitting: adult systems close in on innocence.

Mission 2

Why does a poem about daffodils end on a couch?

Make a guess: Why does a poem about daffodils end on a couch?

Eduqas anthology · "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud"Screen 1

Where the poem came from

  • 15 April 1802: William Wordsworth (1770–1850) and his sister Dorothy walk by Ullswater; her journal describes the daffodils there.
  • Written 1804, published 1807. In 1815 he added a stanza and changed some words: your anthology prints this 1815 version.
  • He said his wife Mary wrote the "inward eye" lines.
  • The speaker is "lonely", yet the real walk was shared: poets shape experience.

The cloud and the couch

Eduqas anthology · "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud"Screen 3

The poem stanza by stanza

StanzaWhat happens
1Wandering alone, he suddenly sees a "crowd", a "host", of "golden" daffodils, fluttering and dancing by the lake.
2Added in 1815: like stars on "the milky way", they stretch in a "never-ending line": ten thousand at a glance.
3The waves dance, but the daffodils beat them "in glee". He can't help being "gay" (then: cheerful) in such "jocund" (merry) company. He hardly guessed what "wealth" the sight gave him.
4Now, lying on his couch, the daffodils "flash" into his mind, and his heart dances with them.

Eduqas anthology · "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud"Screen 5

Form: from then to now

  • Four six-line stanzas: a quatrain then a rhyming couplet, ABABCC (cloud / hills / crowd / daffodils / trees / breeze).
  • Iambic tetrameter: eight syllables, four beats: i WAN · dered LONE · ly AS · a CLOUD. The steady beat suits a poem about dancing.
  • Stanzas 1–3 are in the past tense, outdoors; stanza 4 shifts to the present ("For oft"), indoors. The turn makes memory the poem's end point.
  • The last couplet closes on the heart that "dances", echoing the flowers.

?Why does a poem about daffodils end on a couch?

The couch is the point. Stanzas 1–3 show a sight in the past; stanza 4 shows its "wealth": years later, alone and idle, the daffodils flash back and his heart dances again. For Wordsworth, nature's effect on the mind lasts long after the moment.

Mission 3

It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

Make a guess: It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 1

Context in brief

  • Elizabeth Barrett (1806–1861) was a famous poet when Robert Browning wrote to her in January 1845; he first visited her that May.
  • They married secretly on 12 September 1846 and left for Italy. Her father never forgave her and cut her off.
  • Sonnets from the Portuguese (44 sonnets) were written during the courtship and published in her Poems, 1850, framed as if translations because they were so personal.
Extract Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 2

Sonnet 29 (whole poem)

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 3

What she says

  • Lines 1–4: her thoughts of him grow like wild vines over a tree, until only "straggling green" is seen and the tree is hidden.
  • Lines 5–8: "Yet": she refuses to have thoughts "instead of thee". She wants the real man.
  • Lines 8–11: she commands the tree to shake off the vines: "Renew thy presence".
  • Lines 12–14: when he is with her, she needn't think of him: she is "too near thee".
Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 4

Form: a Petrarchan sonnet

  • 14 lines of iambic pentameter: five beats a line (ten syllables, plus an unstressed extra one in the "thee" lines).
  • Octave rhymed ABBAABBA (bud / tree / see / wood…), then a sestet rhymed CDCDCD (bare / insphere thee / everywhere…).
  • The sestet's D rhymes are all feminine and all end on "thee": insphere thee, hear thee, near thee. He is at the end of every one.
  • The turn comes early, at "Yet" in line 5, and the sestet then turns thought into action.

Love poetry · Sonnet 29 ('I think of thee!')Screen 6

Language: from thoughts to presence

  • The extended metaphor runs through the poem: vines, leaves, tree, boughs, trunk, shadow.
  • "O my palm-tree": a direct, adoring apostrophe; a palm is tall, strong and upright.
  • Exclamations and dashes ("I think of thee!—", "dearer, better!") show urgent feeling.
  • "A new air": being near him feels like breathing again.
  • "Thee" and "thy": intimate, and the language of the older sonnet tradition.

?It opens 'I think of thee!' and ends 'I do not'. Why?

The opening and ending don't contradict each other. Her thoughts of him grow like wild vines that hide the real man; she wants them swept away so he is present. When he is near, she doesn't need to think of him at all: "I am too near thee."

Mission 4

Cast off for her cousin, she ends with a 'gift'. Who has won?

Make a guess: Cast off for her cousin, she ends with a 'gift'. Who has won?

Conflict and anthology poetry · "Cousin Kate"Screen 1

Rossetti and the "fallen woman"

  • Christina Rossetti (1830–1894) published "Cousin Kate" in Goblin Market and Other Poems (1862).
  • Victorian society called a woman who had sex outside marriage "fallen": shamed and shut out, while the man often kept his good name. This is a double standard.
  • From 1859 to 1870 Rossetti volunteered at a church home for women who had been prostitutes.
  • Class matters too: the speaker is a "cottage maiden"; the man is "a great lord".

Conflict and anthology poetry · "Cousin Kate"Screen 5

Form, voice and the turn

  • Six eight-line stanzas. Lines alternate four and three beats, like a ballad, a traditional form for stories of love and betrayal.
  • In each stanza the even lines share one rhyme (air / fair / hair / care).
  • A dramatic monologue: one speaker addresses a silent Kate ("O Lady Kate, my cousin Kate"), then her son.
  • Antithesis: "I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing".
  • The turn: "Yet I've a gift you have not got," and the poem ends with the speaker holding the power.

The last stanza gives the speaker power: her "fair-haired son" is the heir the lord "would give lands" for. But it is a bitter win ("my shame, my pride"), and both women's lives were decided by the lord and judged by the neighbours. Strong answers weigh the triumph against what it cost.

Mission 5

Buried far from home under strange stars. Does Hodge belong now?

Make a guess: Buried far from home under strange stars. Does Hodge belong now?

Eduqas anthology · "Drummer Hodge"Screen 1

Hardy, Hodge and the Boer War

  • The Second Boer War began in October 1899: Britain against two Boer republics in South Africa.
  • Thomas Hardy (1840–1928) published the poem on 25 November 1899, weeks into the war, as "The Dead Drummer".
  • "Hodge" was a stock name for a farm labourer. Hodge comes from "Wessex", Hardy's name for the south-west English countryside he wrote about.
  • Army drummers were often boys.

Eduqas anthology · "Drummer Hodge"Screen 5

Form and structure

  • Three six-line stanzas rhyming ABABAB (rest / found / crest / around / west / mound).
  • Lines alternate eight and six syllables, four beats then three, the rhythm of hymns and ballads: a simple song for a simple soldier.
  • Each stanza moves time: present, past, future. Each ends on the stars: "constellations", "Strange stars", "His stars eternally".
  • South African words (kopje, veldt, Karoo) feel as foreign to the reader as the land did to Hodge.

Mission 6

A sonnet full of happy memories. Why does the last word say 'pain'?

Make a guess: A sonnet full of happy memories. Why does the last word say 'pain'?

Eduqas anthology · "I Shall Return"Screen 1

McKay and the poem

  • Claude McKay was born in rural Jamaica in 1890 (some sources say 1889) and published Songs of Jamaica in 1912.
  • In 1912 he left for the USA to study. There he met harsh racism; "If We Must Die" (1919) answered the racist violence of that year.
  • "I Shall Return" was collected in Harlem Shadows (1922), an early book of the Harlem Renaissance of Black writing and art.
  • The poem never names Jamaica: it speaks of "native life" and "village dances".

Eduqas anthology · "I Shall Return"Screen 5

A sonnet of promise

  • A Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a couplet, ABAB CDCD EFEF GG (return / eyes / burn / skies).
  • Mostly iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five beats: i SHALL re TURN a GAIN · i SHALL re TURN.
  • Sonnets are often love poems; this one is a love poem to a homeland.
  • Repetition: "I shall return" opens each quatrain and the couplet, six times in all. "shall" is firm future: a vow, but still only a hope.
  • The couplet gives the reason, the turn: "pain".

Owen · DisabledScreen 1

Owen, 1917

  • Owen served in France until he was invalided home in 1917.
  • At Craiglockhart hospital, Edinburgh, among men like the poem's soldier, he wrote "Disabled" (revised 1918).
  • Recruits had to be 18 to enlist, 19 to serve abroad.
  • Owen was killed on 4 November 1918. His Poems (1920) name their subject: "War, and the pity of War".

Owen · DisabledScreen 5

Structure: then and now

  • The poem cuts between present (chair, park, institutions) and past (town, football, joining up).
  • Returns are marked: "Now he will never feel again", "Now he is old", then "a few sick years in Institutes".
  • Contrast: once "carried shoulder-high" after matches; now he waits for someone to "put him into bed".
  • Loose rhyme (dark / park; hymn / him); uneven stanzas.
  • It ends on a repeated question, "Why don't they come?": he can't get to bed alone.

?Why does "Disabled" keep jumping between then and now?

Owen sets the young man's past (dancing, football, the cheers as he left) against his present in the chair, so every return to "now" shows what the war took. The structure also exposes how he was drawn in by vanity and cheering, which makes the pity at the end, and the waiting for bed, harder to bear.

Recap

Big ideas

  • "The Schoolboy" moves from a joyful summer morning to the misery of school, then pleads with the parents in images of caged birds, nipped buds and lost harvests.
  • Five-line ABABB stanzas, rhetorical questions and extended metaphors carry the protest; the child loves learning, but not a schooling that cages him.
  • Pair it with poems about youth, freedom or nature, and compare ideas and methods together.
  • Based on a walk with Dorothy by Ullswater (15 April 1802); published 1807, revised 1815 (the version in your anthology).
  • Simile and personification make the speaker a drifting cloud and the daffodils a dancing crowd; ABABCC sestets in iambic tetrameter keep a dancing beat.
  • The shift from past to present, outdoors to the couch, shows that the poem's real gift is memory.
  • Pair it with other poems of nature, memory or joy, comparing ideas and methods together.
  • A Petrarchan sonnet: an extended metaphor of vines and tree, an early turn ("Yet") and a sestet of commands.
  • Every sestet D rhyme ends on "thee": the beloved's real presence is the goal.
  • Pair it with "Cousin Kate" or "I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud".
  • A cottage girl, seduced and discarded by a lord who then marries her cousin, speaks to Kate in a ballad-like dramatic monologue (1862).
  • Similes ("like a silken knot", "like a glove") and antithesis ("dust" / "gold") expose the lord's power and society's double standard.
  • The turn ("Yet I've a gift") gives her a bitter victory: a son and heir, "my shame, my pride".
  • Pair it with anthology poems on love, judgement or family, comparing ideas and methods together.

  • Foreign words and personified, "strange-eyed" stars show a land and sky Hodge never understood but now belongs to for ever.
  • Pair it with other poems on war, home or belonging, comparing ideas and methods together.
  • McKay left Jamaica for the USA in 1912; "I Shall Return" appeared in Harlem Shadows (1922), early in the Harlem Renaissance.

Key quotations to learn

  • I love to rise in a summer morn, (Stanza 1, line 1) Joy and freedom: the world before school.
  • O it drives all joy away! (Stanza 2, line 2) The turn: school destroys joy.
  • Under a cruel eye outworn, (Stanza 2, line 3) The teacher as a watching, worn-out eye.
  • How can the bird that is born for joy / Sit in a cage and sing? (Stanza 4, lines 1–2) The central metaphor: school as a cage.
  • When the blasts of winter appear? (Stanza 6, line 5) The last line: a ruined childhood leads to a bleak adulthood.
  • I wandered lonely as a cloud (Line 1) Simile: the solitary, drifting speaker.
  • When all at once I saw a crowd, (Line 3) Sudden sight; flowers as a lively "crowd".
  • They flash upon that inward eye (Stanza 4) Memory arrives suddenly, as an inner vision.
  • Which is the bliss of solitude; (Stanza 4) Being alone, now, is a joy, not loneliness.
  • And dances with the daffodils. (Last line) The heart joins the flowers' dance in memory.
  • my thoughts do twine and bud (Line 1) Starts the extended metaphor: thoughts as growing vines.
  • the straggling green which hides the wood (Line 4) Her thoughts hide the real man.
  • I will not have my thoughts instead of thee (Line 6) The turn: she wants him, not imagination.
  • Renew thy presence (Line 8) Her commands begin, leading into the sestet.
  • I do not think of thee—I am too near thee. (Line 14) The resolution: presence beats thought.
  • He wore me like a silken knot, (Stanza 2) Simile: she is an ornament to be worn.
  • He changed me like a glove; (Stanza 2) Simile: used and replaced.
  • The neighbours call you good and pure, / Call me an outcast thing. (Stanza 4) Society's double standard.
  • Even so I sit and howl in dust, / You sit in gold and sing: (Stanza 4) Antithesis: two fates, one man.
  • Yet I've a gift you have not got, (Stanza 6) The turn: the speaker's power.
  • My fair-haired son, my shame, my pride, (Stanza 6) A bitter, mixed victory.
  • They throw in Drummer Hodge, to rest (Stanza I, line 1) A rough, unceremonious burial.
  • Uncoffined—just as found: (Stanza I, line 2) No coffin, no ceremony: an ordinary death.
  • Strange stars amid the gloam. (Stanza II, line 6) The foreignness Hodge never understood.
  • Yet portion of that unknown plain / Will Hodge for ever be; (Stanza III, lines 1–2) In death he becomes part of the land.
  • And strange-eyed constellations reign / His stars eternally. (Stanza III, lines 5–6) Alien stars rule him for ever: the last irony.
  • I shall return again; I shall return (Line 1) The vow, repeated: determination and longing.
  • To laugh and love and watch with wonder-eyes (Line 2) Childlike joy: home restores innocence.
  • That bathe the brown blades of the bending grasses, (Line 6) Alliteration: lingering, gentle memory.
  • Stray melodies of dim remembered runes. (Line 12) Memories are fading: urgency.
  • To ease my mind of long, long years of pain. (Line 14) The turn: return as a cure for exile.
  • "He sat in a wheeled chair, waiting for dark," (Line 1) Opens in a passive, waiting present
  • "All of them touch him like some queer disease." (Stanza 2) Simile: others now recoil from him
  • "Poured it down shell-holes till the veins ran dry," (Stanza 3) Metaphor: blood and youth wasted far from home
  • "Smiling they wrote his lie; aged nineteen years." (Stanza 4) Recruiters complicit in his underage enlistment
  • "Some cheered him home, but not as crowds cheer Goal." (Stanza 5) Homecoming without glory, against the football crowd
  • "Why don't they come?" (Last line) Repeated question: helpless and forgotten
