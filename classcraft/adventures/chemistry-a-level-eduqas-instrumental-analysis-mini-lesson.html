Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does carbon dioxide trap heat, while nitrogen, most of the air, does not?

Make a guess: Why does carbon dioxide trap heat, while nitrogen, most of the air, does not?

Mission 1

Molecular ion and fragments

  • In the spectrometer a molecule loses one electron: the molecular ion, M⁺. Its m/z = Mr.
  • M⁺ is the peak at highest m/z (apart from tiny isotope peaks). It can be small.
  • M⁺ can break into a fragment ion (detected) and a neutral piece (not detected).
  • The tallest peak is the base peak. It is often a fragment.
  • Mass lost = Mr − m/z of the fragment. Common losses: 15 CH3, 17 OH, 29 C2H5 or CHO, 31 OCH3 or CH2OH.
Mission 1

Worked example: ethanol

  • Ethanol, CH3CH2OH: Mr = 46, so M⁺ at m/z 46.
  • Base peak at m/z 31: mass lost 46 − 31 = 15, a CH3.
  • So the ion is CH2OH⁺: the C–C bond broke.
  • m/z 29: mass lost 46 − 29 = 17, an OH, leaving C2H5⁺.
  • Each fragment ion carries the + charge; the lost piece is neutral.
Quick check
?Which statement about the molecular ion peak in the mass spectrum of an organic compound is correct?
Quick check
?Methyl butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COOCH3 (Mr = 102), gives a peak at m/z 71. Which neutral fragment was lost to form this ion?
Mission 1

Radiation and molecular motion

  • Infrared radiation makes covalent bonds vibrate more: they stretch and bend.
  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches the gap between two levels.
Mission 1

Reading an IR spectrum

  • Each bond absorbs IR at its own wavenumber, in cm⁻¹ (= 1 ÷ wavelength in cm).
  • The spectrum plots % transmittance against wavenumber, 4000 cm⁻¹ on the left. Absorptions are dips.
  • Above about 1500 cm⁻¹: bands you can assign to bonds.
  • Below about 1500 cm⁻¹: the fingerprint region, unique to each compound.
  • Match the whole spectrum with a database spectrum: same fingerprint, same compound.

Mission 1

Worked example: alcohol, ketone or acid?

  • Butan-1-ol: broad dip near 3300 (O–H), no C=O.
  • Butanone: strong sharp dip near 1715 (C=O), nothing broad above 3000.
  • Butanoic acid: C=O near 1710 plus a very broad O–H from 2500 to 3300, overlapping C–H.
  • C–O (1000–1300) can't decide: alcohols, acids and esters all have it.
Quick check
?Compound Q, C3H6O, absorbs strongly at 1725 cm⁻¹ and does not absorb between 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission 1

Greenhouse gases

  • The Earth's surface emits infrared. Greenhouse gases absorb it, then re-emit it in all directions, some back down.
  • A molecule absorbs IR only if a vibration changes its dipole.
  • CO2 is non-polar, but its asymmetric stretch and bend change the dipole: it absorbs IR.
  • N2 and O2: stretching never makes them polar, so they don't absorb IR.
  • CO2, H2O and CH4 (C=O, O–H and C–H bonds) absorb IR. More of them in the air: more IR trapped, linked to global warming.
Mission 1

IR at work

  • Breathalysers: IR passes through a breath sample. Absorption by ethanol's C–H bonds (near 2950 cm⁻¹) measures how much ethanol is present.
  • Not the O–H band: breath is full of water vapour, which also absorbs there.
  • More absorption → more ethanol in the breath.
  • Pollution monitors measure CO and NO from car exhausts at their own IR wavenumbers.
Mission check

?Compound X has its molecular ion at m/z 60 and a fragment peak at m/z 45. Its IR spectrum shows a strong absorption at 1710 cm⁻¹ and a very broad absorption from 2500 to 3000 cm⁻¹. What is X?

Every IR band is a bond vibration absorbing infrared. CO2 traps heat for the same reason: its bonds vibrate and absorb the infrared the Earth gives off.

Mission 2

Mission 2: An MRI scanner images your body by detecting hydrogen nuclei. What can the same effect tell a chemist about a molecule?

Make a guess: An MRI scanner images your body by detecting hydrogen nuclei. What can the same effect tell a chemist about a molecule?

Mission 2

What ¹H NMR shows

  • In a strong magnetic field, ¹H nuclei absorb radio waves.
  • H atoms in different environments absorb at different frequencies: one signal per environment.
  • Position: chemical shift, δ in ppm, measured from TMS at δ = 0.
  • δ depends on what is nearby: an O atom or a C=O moves a signal to higher δ.
  • The area under a signal (shown by the integration trace) is proportional to the number of H in that environment.
Mission 2

Counting environments

  • H atoms are equivalent if symmetry or free rotation makes them interchangeable. Equivalent H share one signal.
  • All three H on one CH3 group are equivalent.
  • 1,2-Dichloroethane, ClCH2CH2Cl: one signal (4H).
  • 2-Methylpropane, (CH3)3CH: two signals, areas 9 : 1.
Quick check
?Which describes the ¹H NMR spectrum of 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH?
Mission 2

Worked example: ethanol, low resolution

  • Low resolution: one peak per environment, no splitting.
  • CH3CH2OH has three H environments: three signals, areas 3 : 2 : 1.
  • δ 3.7, 2H: the CH2, on the carbon bonded to O (high δ).
  • δ 2.6, 1H: the O–H (its position varies with concentration and solvent).
  • δ 1.2, 3H: the CH3 (an alkyl CH3, nudged up slightly by the O two carbons away).
Quick check
?How many signals does the low-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of 3-methylbutan-1-ol, (CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH, show, and in what ratio of areas?
Calculate
?A compound, C5H12O, gives three signals in its ¹H NMR spectrum. Their integration traces rise by 4.5 cm, 1.0 cm and 0.5 cm. How many H atoms are in the environment of the 4.5 cm signal?
Mission 2

Splitting: the n + 1 rule

  • High resolution: each signal can split into several close peaks.
  • A signal is split by H atoms on adjacent carbons: n neighbouring H → n + 1 peaks.
  • 0 → singlet, 1 → doublet, 2 → triplet, 3 → quartet.
  • Equivalent H don't split each other. An O–H usually gives a singlet and doesn't split its neighbours.
  • Splitting counts the neighbours' H; the group's own H count comes from the area.
Mission 2

Worked example: ethanol, high resolution

  • δ 1.2, 3H, triplet: CH3 next to a CH2 (2 neighbours → 3 peaks).
  • δ 3.7, 2H, quartet: CH2 next to a CH3 (3 neighbours → 4 peaks).
  • δ 2.6, 1H, singlet: O–H.
  • A 3H triplet with a 2H quartet is the sign of an ethyl group, CH3CH2–.
Quick check
?In a high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum, one signal is split into a quartet. What does this show about the structure?
Quick check
?In the high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of 3-methylbutan-1-ol, (CH3)2CHCH2CH2OH, what splitting does the 6H signal at δ 0.9 show?
Mission check

?Which describes the high-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum of methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3?

¹H NMR shows how many kinds of H (signals), how many of each (areas), what is nearby (shift) and how many H next door (splitting).

Mission 3

Mission 3: Only about one carbon atom in a hundred is magnetic. How can that be enough to map every carbon in a compound?

Make a guess: Only about one carbon atom in a hundred is magnetic. How can that be enough to map every carbon in a compound?

Mission 3

Why ¹³C NMR works

  • Only about 1.1% of carbon atoms are ¹³C. A ¹³C nucleus behaves like a tiny magnet, so it gives an NMR signal; ¹²C is not magnetic and gives none.
  • A sample has billions of molecules, so every carbon position holds a ¹³C atom in some of them.
  • Many scans are added together to build up the weak signal.
  • One peak per carbon environment. δ in ppm from TMS (δ = 0), range about 0–220.
  • Spectra are proton decoupled: every peak is a single line. No splitting to read.
  • Peak heights are not used to count carbons.
  • So ¹³C spectra are simpler than ¹H spectra: count the peaks, read their positions.
Mission 3

Counting carbon environments

  • Carbons are equivalent if symmetry makes them interchangeable. They share one peak.
  • Propan-1-ol, CH3CH2CH2OH: 3 peaks.
  • Propan-2-ol, (CH3)2CHOH: the two CH3 are equivalent: 2 peaks.
  • Propanone, CH3COCH3: 2 peaks (CH3 and C=O).
  • Benzene: all six carbons equivalent: 1 peak.
Quick check
?Which alcohol, C4H10O, gives only two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum?

Mission 3

Worked example: ethyl ethanoate

  • CH3COOCH2CH3: four different carbons → 4 peaks.
  • δ 171: C=O of an ester (160–185).
  • δ 60: the CH2 bonded to O (50–90).
  • δ 21: the CH3 bonded to C=O (20–50).
  • δ 14: the CH3 of the ethyl group (5–40).
Quick check
?Compound R, C3H6O, gives two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum, at δ 206 and δ 31. What is R?
Mission 3

Predicting a spectrum

  • Draw the full structure. Label each carbon.
  • Group equivalent carbons: number of peaks = number of environments.
  • For each environment, look up its δ range.
  • Methyl propanoate, CH3CH2COOCH3: 4 peaks.
  • C=O ester 160–185 · O–CH3 50–90 · CH2 next to C=O 20–50 · CH3 5–40.
Calculate
?How many peaks are in the ¹³C NMR spectrum of 1,4-dimethylbenzene?
Mission 3

¹³C and ¹H together

  • ¹³C: how many kinds of carbon, and what each is bonded to.
  • ¹H: how many kinds of H, and how many of each (areas).
  • Together they narrow the structure fast.
  • Propan-1-ol and propan-2-ol: ¹³C gives 3 v 2 peaks; ¹H gives 4 v 3 signals (2 : 2 : 3 : 1 v 6 : 1 : 1).
Quick check
?Compound T, C3H8O, gives two peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum. Its low-resolution ¹H NMR spectrum has three signals with areas 6 : 1 : 1. What is T?
Mission check

?A hydrocarbon, C5H12, gives four peaks in its ¹³C NMR spectrum. Which isomer is it?

Only 1 in 100 carbons is ¹³C, but billions of molecules mean every position shows up. One peak per environment maps the carbon skeleton.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • MS: M⁺ gives Mr; mass lost (Mr − fragment m/z) gives the piece that broke off.
  • IR: broad O–H near 3300, an alcohol; strong C=O near 1700; C=O plus a very broad O–H, a carboxylic acid.
  • Microwave ↔ rotation; infrared ↔ vibration; UV/visible ↔ electrons. IR is absorbed only when a vibration changes the dipole.

Mission 2

  • Signals = H environments; area = relative number of H; δ = what is nearby; n neighbouring H → n + 1 peaks.

Mission 3

  • ¹³C NMR: one peak per carbon environment; equivalent carbons share a peak.
  • δ shows what each carbon is bonded to: C=O highest (160–220), C–O 50–90, alkyl lowest.
  • No splitting (proton decoupled), and peak heights don't count carbons.
