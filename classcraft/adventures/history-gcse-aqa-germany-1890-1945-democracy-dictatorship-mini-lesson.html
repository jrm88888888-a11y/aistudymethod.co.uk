Germany, 1890–1945

This mini-lesson walks you through the whole AQA Germany, 1890–1945 period study: Kaiser Wilhelm II's Germany, the impact of the First World War, the founding and struggles of the Weimar Republic, the rise of the Nazis, how Hitler consolidated power, and life in Nazi Germany up to 1945.

Kaiser's Germany · 1890+

Kaiser Wilhelm II's Germany

In 1890 Germany was ruled by Kaiser Wilhelm II. It was a young, powerful nation, growing fast — but power was not truly democratic.

  • Germany was booming industrially — coal, steel and new industries made it a great economic power.
  • There was a parliament, the Reichstag, elected by men — but the Kaiser controlled the army, foreign policy and could ignore it. Prussian militarism was strong.
  • As factories grew, so did the working class and the socialist SPD (Social Democratic Party), which the Kaiser distrusted.

Weltpolitik: Wilhelm II wanted Germany to be a world power with an empire and a great navy. This naval race with Britain raised tension and helped push Europe towards war.

The First World War · 1914–18

The impact of the First World War

Germany entered the First World War in 1914 confident of quick victory. Instead came four years of exhausting total war (1914–1918).

  • By 1918 Germany was starving and defeated — blockaded, its people hungry and its armies collapsing.
  • In the German Revolution of November 1918, sailors mutinied and unrest spread across the country.
  • On 9 November 1918 the Kaiser abdicated and fled; a republic was declared. The armistice ending the fighting was signed on 11 November 1918.

Misconception alert: Germany was not occupied or invaded in 1918 — its army was beaten in the field and at home, and it asked for the armistice. This mattered later, because some Germans wrongly claimed the army had been "betrayed" rather than defeated.

Weimar Republic · 1919

A new democracy is born

Out of defeat came Germany's first real democracy — the Weimar Republic, founded in 1919 (named after the town of Weimar, where the new constitution was written).

  • Friedrich Ebert (of the SPD) became the first President.
  • The Weimar Constitution (1919) was very democratic: all men and women over 20 could vote, using proportional representation.
  • Article 48 let the President rule by emergency decree in a crisis — a power that would later be badly abused.

Strength or weakness? Proportional representation was fair, but it produced many small parties and weak coalition governments — making the Republic hard to govern in a crisis.

Weimar Republic · 1919

The Treaty of Versailles

The new Republic's first act was to accept the peace terms. The Treaty of Versailles was signed on 28 June 1919 — and Germans hated it.

  • Germany lost land, its armed forces were slashed, and it had to pay huge reparations.
  • The "War Guilt" clause forced Germany to accept blame for the war — a deep humiliation.
  • Enemies of the Republic spread the "stab in the back" myth — the lie that the army had been betrayed at home by the "November Criminals" (the politicians who signed the armistice and the treaty).

The Weimar politicians were blamed for a defeat that was not their fault. This resentment poisoned the Republic from the start and was later exploited by the Nazis.

Weimar Republic · 1919–1923

Early threats from left and right

The young Republic was attacked from both extremes in its first years:

  • Spartacist uprising (January 1919) — communists led by Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht tried to seize power; it was crushed (with help from the right-wing Freikorps).
  • Kapp Putsch (1920) — right-wing nationalists tried to overthrow the government; it collapsed when workers went on strike.
Weimar Republic · 1923

The crisis of 1923

1923 was Weimar's worst year. Germany fell behind on reparations, so in January 1923 French and Belgian troops occupied the Ruhr (Germany's industrial heartland) to seize goods as payment.

  • The government told workers to go on strike (passive resistance) — but then printed money to pay them.
  • This triggered hyperinflation (1923): prices spiralled out of control and money became worthless — a loaf of bread ended up costing billions of marks.
  • Savings were wiped out; many middle-class Germans lost everything and never forgave the Republic.

Misconception alert: hyperinflation was mainly caused by the government printing too much money, not just by reparations. It ruined savers — but people on fixed debts or with foreign money could actually benefit.

Weimar recovery · 1924–1929

The Stresemann years

From late 1923, Gustav Stresemann helped steer Germany into recovery — the "golden years":

  • Rentenmark (1923) — a new, stable currency ended hyperinflation.
  • Dawes Plan (1924) — US loans and easier reparation payments; the Young Plan (1929) reduced reparations further.
  • Locarno Pact (1925) and joining the League of Nations (1926) improved relations with former enemies.
  • Culture flourished — the Bauhaus design movement, bold new art, cinema and nightlife.

How stable was it? Recovery depended heavily on American loans. When those loans were suddenly recalled after 1929, the "golden years" collapsed.

Rise of the Nazis · 1921–1923

Hitler and the early Nazi Party

Adolf Hitler became leader of the Nazi Party (NSDAP) in 1921. It was a small, nationalist, anti-communist party with its own private army, the SA (brownshirts).

  • In November 1923, amid the chaos of hyperinflation, Hitler attempted the Munich (Beer Hall) Putsch — a bid to seize power in Bavaria.
  • It failed: police stopped it, Nazis were killed, and Hitler was arrested and jailed.
  • In prison he wrote Mein Kampf, setting out his ideas. He decided to seek power legally, through elections, in future.

Turning point: the failed Putsch taught Hitler a lesson — the Nazis would win power by votes and propaganda, not by force. This shaped everything that followed.

Rise of the Nazis · 1929–1932

Depression and the Nazi surge

The Wall Street Crash (October 1929) plunged Germany into the Great Depression. US loans were recalled, businesses collapsed and unemployment soared to around 6 million by 1932.

Hitler's message — jobs, national pride, blaming Versailles, the "November Criminals" and the Jews — won millions of desperate voters. By July 1932 the Nazis were the largest party in the Reichstag.

Rise of the Nazis · 1933

Hitler becomes Chancellor

Being the largest party did not automatically make Hitler leader. He was appointed by President Hindenburg on 30 January 1933.

  • Conservative politicians (like von Papen) thought they could use and control Hitler in a coalition — "box him in".
  • They were badly wrong: Hitler used the powers of the Chancellorship to destroy democracy from within.

Misconception alert: Hitler was not elected as dictator by a majority — the Nazis never won over 50% in a free election. He was appointed Chancellor in a political deal, then seized total power afterwards.

Dictatorship · 1933–1934

Consolidation of power

In under two years Hitler turned the Chancellorship into a total dictatorship:

  • Reichstag Fire (27 February 1933) — the parliament building burned down; Hitler blamed the communists and used it to arrest opponents and suspend civil rights.
  • Enabling Act (March 1933) — let Hitler make laws without the Reichstag, the legal basis of his dictatorship.
  • Night of the Long Knives (30 June 1934) — Hitler purged the SA, killing its leader Ernst Röhm and rivals.
  • When President Hindenburg died (August 1934), Hitler merged the roles of President and Chancellor to become Führer; the army swore a personal oath to him.

Nazi Germany · control

The police state

The Nazis controlled Germany through terror and propaganda. Opposition was dangerous:

  • The SS and Gestapo (secret police) spied on and arrested opponents; concentration camps (the first, Dachau, opened in 1933) held them.
  • Goebbels ran propaganda — radio, film, posters and mass rallies — while censorship silenced anything the Nazis disliked.
Nazi Germany · society

Young people and women

The Nazis tried to control every part of life, especially the young and women:

  • Youth: boys joined the Hitler Youth and girls the League of German Maidens (BDM); schools taught Nazi ideas and racial theory.
  • Women: the Nazi ideal was "Kinder, Küche, Kirche" ("children, kitchen, church") — women were encouraged to leave work, stay home and have large families.

The aim was total loyalty: raise obedient Nazi children and boost the "Aryan" birth rate. Not everyone conformed — some youth resisted (see later).

Nazi Germany · economy

Jobs, roads and rearmament

The Nazis promised to end unemployment — and largely did, though at a cost:

  • Public works — building projects, including the famous autobahns (motorways), created jobs.
  • Rearmament — rebuilding the army and weapons industry (breaking Versailles) put millions back to work.
  • Economists like Hjalmar Schacht managed the early recovery; the Four Year Plan (1936) under Göring aimed to make Germany ready for war and self-sufficient (autarky).

At what price? Trade unions were banned and workers lost the right to strike or bargain. "Full employment" was real, but it came with lost freedom and a war economy.

Nazi Germany · persecution

Persecution and antisemitism

At the heart of Nazi ideology was violent antisemitism and racism. Persecution of Jewish people grew steadily worse:

  • Nuremberg Laws (1935) — stripped Jews of German citizenship and banned marriage between Jews and other Germans.
  • Kristallnacht (9–10 November 1938) — the "Night of Broken Glass": Jewish shops, homes and synagogues were attacked across Germany; many Jews were killed or arrested.
  • During the war, Jews were forced into ghettos and then murdered in the Holocaust / "Final Solution".

The Nazis also persecuted many other groups they saw as "undesirable" — this racism was central to the regime, not an accident of it.

Nazi Germany · opposition

Opposition and resistance

Terror made opposition dangerous, but some Germans did resist the Nazis:

  • The White Rose — students in Munich who secretly handed out anti-Nazi leaflets; their leaders were executed.
  • The Edelweiss Pirates — groups of young people who rejected the Hitler Youth and clashed with the Nazis.
  • The July 1944 Bomb Plot — army officers led by Claus von Stauffenberg planted a bomb to kill Hitler; it failed, and the plotters were executed.

Misconception alert: opposition existed but was small and heavily punished — it never came close to overthrowing the regime. Terror, propaganda and some genuine support kept the Nazis in power.

Nazi Germany · religion

The Nazis and the Church

The Nazis were wary of the Churches, which offered people a rival source of loyalty:

  • Hitler signed a Concordat (1933) with the Catholic Church, agreeing not to interfere — but soon broke it.
  • He tried to unite Protestants into a pro-Nazi "Reich Church".
  • Some clergy resisted — the Confessing Church, led by figures like Martin Niemöller, opposed Nazi control; Niemöller was sent to a concentration camp.

The Nazis wanted loyalty to the state above religion — but never fully controlled the Churches, which remained one of the few surviving independent institutions.

The end · to 1945

War and the fall of the Third Reich

Nazi rearmament and aggression led to the Second World War (1939–45). War transformed life inside Germany:

  • At first victories brought loot and confidence; later, total war, rationing and Allied bombing brought hardship and ruin.
  • Under cover of war the Nazis carried out the Holocaust — the systematic murder of around six million Jews.
  • As Germany faced total defeat, Hitler died in April 1945; Germany surrendered soon after, ending the Nazi dictatorship.

The story that began with a proud Kaiserreich in 1890 ended in 1945 with Germany defeated, devastated and occupied — the full arc from democracy to dictatorship to ruin.

Exam skill

Sources and interpretations

In the exam you handle sources and interpretations about Germany. Two quick habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A Nazi poster and an anti-Nazi leaflet about the same event will disagree — because the Nazis used propaganda to control opinion.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know (dates, events, motives).

Interpretations differ: some historians stress terror as the key to Nazi control, others stress propaganda or genuine support. Being aware that interpretations vary — and why — is exactly what the exam rewards.

Causation

Why did democracy fail?

No single cause — but a chain of pressures destroyed Weimar democracy and let Hitler take power:

The Depression was the trigger, but Weimar's deep weaknesses — resentment of Versailles, fragile coalitions and powerful enemies on the right — made it vulnerable long before 1929.
