Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do Christians call the day Jesus died "Good" Friday?

Make a guess: Why do Christians call the day Jesus died "Good" Friday?

Mission 1

Lent: forty days

  • Lent: the 40 days of preparation before Easter (Sundays are not counted in the West).
  • It recalls Jesus' 40 days in the wilderness, fasting and tempted, before his ministry (Matthew 4:1–11, paraphrase).
  • In the early Church, Lent was when new believers prepared for baptism at Easter.
  • A season of repentance (turning back to God) and self-discipline. Purple in church.
  • Significance: Christians share Jesus' struggle so as to share his victory at Easter.
Mission 1

Shrove Tuesday and Ash Wednesday

  • Shrove Tuesday: to be "shriven" was to confess sins and be forgiven. Rich foods were used up: pancakes.
  • Ash Wednesday: the first day of Lent.
  • Ashes (from burning last year's palm crosses) are marked in a cross on the forehead, with words such as:

> "For you are dust, and you shall return to dust." (Genesis 3:19)

  • Or: "Repent, and believe the Good News" (based on Mark 1:15).
  • Meaning: human life is short; turn back to God.
  • Catholics fast on Ash Wednesday (and Good Friday). Anglicans, Lutherans and Methodists also often hold Ash Wednesday services.
Mission 1

Keeping Lent today

  • Jesus names three practices: giving to the poor, prayer, fasting (Matthew 6:1–18, paraphrase).
  • Giving up a luxury (chocolate, social media), or taking up something good: extra prayer, volunteering.
  • Charity: Lent appeals, e.g. CAFOD's Family Fast Day: eat simply and give the money saved.
  • Catholics often pray the Stations of the Cross on Fridays; many churches run Lent study groups.
  • Orthodox Great Lent: a stricter fast (usually no meat, dairy or wine) from Clean Monday.
  • Some evangelical and free churches do not keep Lent formally; Christians are free to fast at any time.

Mission 1

Palm Sunday and Maundy Thursday

  • Palm Sunday: Jesus rides into Jerusalem on a donkey; crowds wave branches and shout "Hosanna!" (Matthew 21:1–11, paraphrase).
    • Customs: processions with palms; palm crosses given out; the Passion is read, looking ahead to the cross.
  • Maundy Thursday: the Last Supper (Matthew 26:17–29). Jesus gives bread and wine as his body and blood; Christians see this as the first Eucharist.
    • "Maundy" from Latin mandatum, "commandment": love one another (John 13:34).
    • Foot-washing: priests or ministers wash people's feet, following Jesus:

> "If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet." (John 13:14)

  • That night: the altar is stripped bare; people keep watch, remembering Jesus' prayer and arrest in Gethsemane.
Mission 1

Good Friday and Holy Saturday

  • Good Friday: Jesus' crucifixion (Matthew 27:45–55: darkness at noon; Jesus' cry; the Temple curtain torn; a centurion says he was the Son of God, paraphrase).
  • Why "Good"? The word: "good" here is an old English sense meaning "holy" (some say it came from "God's Friday", but dictionaries doubt this).
  • Christians also explain the name by what they believe his death achieved: salvation, forgiveness and reconciliation with God.
  • Customs: a bare church; veneration of the cross; the Stations of the Cross (14 scenes of the way to Calvary); a three-hour service (noon to 3 p.m.); walks of witness by churches together; fasting; hot cross buns.
  • Catholic churches celebrate no Mass on Good Friday.
  • Holy Saturday: quiet waiting at the tomb. After dark, the Easter Vigil begins:
    • a new fire; the Paschal candle is lit and carried into the dark church: "The light of Christ";
    • readings from creation to the resurrection; baptisms; the first Eucharist of Easter.
Mission 1

Easter Sunday

  • Women find the tomb empty. The angel says:

> "He is not here, for he has risen, just like he said." (Matthew 28:6)

  • Easter celebrates Jesus' resurrection: the most important festival of the Christian year.
  • Customs: sunrise services outdoors (the women came at dawn); flowers; white and gold; "Alleluia" sung again after Lent; feasting.
  • Eggs: new life breaking out, like the empty tomb. Orthodox Christians dye them red.
  • Orthodox Pascha: a midnight service; candles lit from one flame; "Christ is risen!" "He is risen indeed!"
  • Origins: linked to the Jewish Passover (Pascha); its date is the first Sunday after the first full moon after the spring equinox. Orthodox churches calculate it by the older (Julian) calendar, so their Easter is often later.

Mission 1Read the source

"If Christ has not been raised"

Some Christians in Corinth said there is no resurrection of the dead (1 Corinthians 15:12). Paul answers:

> "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith also is in vain." (1 Corinthians 15:14)

> "But now Christ has been raised from the dead. He became the first fruit of those who are asleep." (1 Corinthians 15:20)

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22)

  • "The last enemy that will be abolished is death." (1 Corinthians 15:26)
  • First fruit: the first part of the harvest, a promise that the rest will follow.

Q1 What would be lost if Christ had not been raised, according to Paul?

Show answer

Preaching and faith would be "in vain": empty.

Q2 What does "first fruit" promise to believers?

Show answer

That they too will be raised, as Jesus was.

Mission 1

Easter in Britain today

  • Public holidays: Good Friday, and Easter Monday (except in Scotland); school holidays around them.
  • Churches are fuller at Easter; many hold walks of witness and Passion plays in public places.
  • Commercial Easter: chocolate eggs, bunnies, cards and adverts; for many people a spring holiday.
  • Differing feelings: some Christians regret that chocolate eggs eclipse the cross; others use eggs and hot cross buns to tell the story.
  • For Christians: the most important festival; every Sunday is a "little Easter", recalling the resurrection.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Easter is more important than Christmas for Christians."

  • For: Paul makes the resurrection the foundation of faith: without it, "your faith also is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14); Christians believe Jesus' death and resurrection bring salvation.
  • For: Easter has the longest preparation (Lent) and season (fifty days); Sunday worship recalls it every week; the Orthodox call it the "Feast of Feasts".
  • Against: without the Incarnation there would be no death and resurrection; Christmas shows God's love in becoming human.
  • Against: Christmas reaches more people in Britain today, so it may matter more for sharing the faith.
  • Another view: they are one story; ranking them splits what belongs together.
  • Judge it by: Which belief is foundational? Importance in belief, or in practice? Importance for Christians, or for society?
Mission check

?In some churches, the bare wooden cross venerated on Good Friday is covered with fresh flowers on Easter morning. A visitor asks why Christians treat the cross, a tool of execution, as a sign of hope. Which answer best fits Christian belief?

Good Friday is "good" because, for Christians, the cross brings salvation and leads to Easter: Lent prepares, Holy Week remembers, and Easter celebrates new life.

Mission 2: Nobody knows Jesus' birthday, so why is 25 December one of the biggest days of the Christian year?

Make a guess: Nobody knows Jesus' birthday, so why is 25 December one of the biggest days of the Christian year?

Mission 2

The church year

  • Christians mark the life of Jesus in a yearly cycle: the church year (liturgical year).
  • In Western churches it begins with Advent, four Sundays before Christmas.
  • Order: Advent → Christmas → Epiphany → Lent → Holy Week → Easter → Ascension → Pentecost → Ordinary Time.
  • Fixed feasts keep their date (Christmas, 25 December); movable feasts depend on Easter.
  • Colours in church mark the season: purple for preparation, white or gold for celebration, red for the Spirit and martyrs, green for Ordinary Time.
  • Significance: the whole story of salvation is retold every year, so believers live it, not just learn it.
Mission 2

Advent: getting ready twice

  • Advent (Latin adventus, "coming"): the four weeks before Christmas.
  • Christians prepare for two comings of Jesus:
    • his birth in Bethlehem, celebrated at Christmas;
    • his Second Coming at the end of time, as judge and king.
  • Advent wreath: a candle lit each Sunday; in many churches a fifth, white candle for Christ on Christmas Day.
  • Advent services: carol services and an Advent procession moving from darkness to light.
  • Prayer, fasting and charity: Catholics are encouraged to go to confession; many churches collect food and gifts for those in need.
  • The Orthodox keep a Nativity Fast of 40 days before Christmas.
  • Advent calendars began as a way of counting the days in prayer.
Mission 2Read the source

Two birth accounts

  • Luke 2:1–20 (paraphrase): a census sends Joseph and Mary to Bethlehem; Jesus is born and laid in a manger; angels announce to shepherds a Saviour, "Christ the Lord", and sing "Glory to God in the highest".
  • Matthew 2:1–12 (paraphrase): wise men (Magi) from the east follow a star and ask in Jerusalem where the king of the Jews is born; King Herod is troubled; they find the child and bring gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Luke stresses the poor and lowly: shepherds are first to hear.
  • Matthew stresses Jesus as king, recognised by non-Jews from far away.

Q1 Which Gospel tells of the shepherds, and which of the Magi?

Show answer

Luke: shepherds. Matthew: Magi.

Q2 Does Matthew say how many Magi came?

Show answer

No. "Three" is a tradition based on the three gifts.

Mission 2

Christmas celebrates the Incarnation

  • Incarnation: Christians believe God the Son became a human being in Jesus. "The Word became flesh" (John 1:14).
  • Christmas celebrates that God came close to humanity, as a vulnerable baby.
  • The Bible gives no date for Jesus' birth. 25 December was chosen centuries later.
  • Significance:
    • God shares human life, so every human life has dignity;
    • joy and thanksgiving for God's gift;
    • giving to others, especially the poor, as Jesus was born poor.
  • Say: "Christmas celebrates the Incarnation" — not: "Christmas celebrates the resurrection".
Mission 2

How Christians celebrate Christmas

  • Midnight Mass (Catholic) or Midnight Communion (Anglican): the first celebration of Christmas, late on Christmas Eve.
  • Christmas Day services; Orthodox churches that follow the old (Julian) calendar celebrate on 7 January.
  • Carol services, often Nine Lessons and Carols: Bible readings from the Fall to the birth of Jesus.
  • Crib (nativity scene): begun by St Francis of Assisi in 1223.
  • Christingle: an orange (the world), a candle (Jesus, the Light of the World), a red ribbon (his blood), fruits (God's gifts).
  • Nativity plays, giving presents, family meals, and helping the lonely and homeless.

Mission 2

Where 25 December came from

  • The first record of Christians celebrating Jesus' birth on 25 December: Rome, AD 336.
  • Why that date? Historians give two main theories:
    • it replaced a Roman winter festival of the "Unconquered Sun";
    • it was calculated: Jesus was believed to have been conceived on 25 March, so born nine months later.
  • Many Eastern Christians first celebrated the birth on 6 January.
  • 1647: the Puritan Parliament banned Christmas in England as unbiblical; it returned in 1660.
  • 1840s: Christmas trees, cards and Dickens' A Christmas Carol (1843) shape the modern British Christmas.
Mission 2

Epiphany: Christ shown to the world

  • Epiphany (Greek, "appearing" or "revealing"): 6 January, the twelfth day after Christmas.
  • Western churches: the visit of the Magi. Jesus is revealed as Saviour of all nations, not only the Jews.
  • The gifts are traditionally read as signs: gold for a king, frankincense for God, myrrh for his death.
  • Eastern churches (Theophany): Jesus' baptism; priests bless water, sometimes throwing a cross into the sea or a river.
  • Customs:
    • Chalking the doors: "20 + C + M + B + 26" in blessed chalk: the year and the Magi's traditional names, or Christus mansionem benedicat, "May Christ bless this house";
    • King cake: a cake with a small figure or bean hidden inside; the finder is "king" for the day;
    • in Spain and Latin America, children receive gifts on 6 January (Three Kings' Day).
Quick check
?At Epiphany some Christian families write "20 + C + M + B + 26" above their front door in blessed chalk. Why?
Mission 2

Christmas in Britain today

  • A public holiday: 25 and 26 December. Many people who rarely attend church go at Christmas.
  • Schools hold nativity plays and carol services; the monarch's Christmas broadcast often speaks of faith.
  • Social significance: families gather, people give to charity, churches open their doors for lunches and night shelters.
  • Commercialisation: shopping, advertising and Father Christmas can take centre stage; many people celebrate without the religious meaning.
  • For Christians it remains a holy day: worship, the Incarnation, and generosity to others.
Mission 2Diversity

Different ways and different attitudes

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican: the liturgical year in full; a night service (Midnight Mass for Catholics); a season of feasting from Christmas to Epiphany.
  • Many Protestant churches: carol services, nativity plays, preaching on the birth; less emphasis on the liturgical calendar.
  • Traditional Quakers: every day is equally holy, so no special festivals (many Quakers today mark Christmas informally).
  • Some Reformed churches keep only what the Bible commands, so they do not keep Christmas as a church feast.
  • Jehovah's Witnesses (who reject the Trinity) do not celebrate Christmas.
  • Feelings about commercialisation: some Christians object ("keep Christ in Christmas"); others see an opportunity: carols, generosity and open doors draw people in.
Quick check
?Which Christians have traditionally not celebrated Christmas as a special religious festival?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Christmas has lost its religious meaning in Britain today."

  • For: spending, adverts and Father Christmas dominate; most people do not go to church at Christmas.
  • For: many families celebrate with no mention of Jesus; for some, it is mainly a holiday.
  • Against: carol services and Midnight Masses are among the fullest services of the year.
  • Against: nativity plays, charity appeals and church lunches keep the story and its values public.
  • Against: for Christians the meaning is unchanged: the Incarnation is celebrated whatever the shops do.
  • Judge it by: Meaning for whom: Christians, or society as a whole? Can commercial and religious Christmas exist side by side? Are generosity and family gatherings themselves religious values?
Mission check

?In a country where 25 December is an ordinary working day, a small church holds its Christmas service on the nearest Sunday, with a crib and readings from Luke 2. Some members worry it is "on the wrong day". Which statement best explains why the service still keeps the meaning of Christmas?

Christmas is about the Incarnation, prepared for in Advent and shown to the world at Epiphany; the date is tradition, the meaning is the heart.

Make a guess: Why do some churches put out their great Easter candle forty days after Easter?

Mission 3

After Easter: the fifty days

  • Easter is not one day but a season of fifty days (Eastertide).
  • Day 40: Ascension. Jesus appeared to his disciples for forty days, then was taken up to heaven (Acts 1:3, 9, paraphrase).
  • Day 50: Pentecost (Greek, "fiftieth"). The Holy Spirit comes on the apostles (Acts 2).
  • Pentecost was already a Jewish festival, Shavuot: a harvest feast that also remembers the giving of the Law at Sinai.
  • The Paschal candle, lit at the Easter Vigil, burns at services throughout the season.
Mission 3

Ascension Day

  • A Thursday, forty days after Easter. Jesus is taken up and a cloud hides him; angels say he will return in the same way (Acts 1:9–11, paraphrase).
  • Meaning for Christians:
    • Jesus' earthly work is complete; he reigns with the Father ("seated at the right hand", in the Creeds);
    • he takes human nature into heaven;
    • he will come again;
    • he promises the Holy Spirit and sends the disciples out.
  • Customs:
    • extinguishing the Paschal candle after the Gospel: Jesus' bodily presence on earth has ended (an older custom; many Catholic churches now keep it lit until Pentecost);
    • processions, such as English parishes "beating the bounds" at Ascensiontide;
    • an all-night vigil, and nine days of prayer to Pentecost, waiting for the Spirit as the apostles did.
  • A public holiday in some countries (e.g. France, Germany); in others it is moved to the following Sunday.
Mission 3Read the source

Wind and fire

Before ascending, Jesus promises:

> "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, …" (Acts 1:8)

  • Acts 2:1–4 (paraphrase): on the day of Pentecost the disciples are together in one place. A sound like a rushing wind fills the house. Something like tongues of fire rests on each of them. They are filled with the Holy Spirit and speak in other languages.
  • Peter preaches to the crowd; about three thousand are baptised that day (Acts 2:41, paraphrase).

Q1 Which two signs of the Spirit can be seen or heard?

Show answer

Wind and fire; also the speaking in other languages.

Q2 Why is Pentecost called the "birthday of the Church"?

Show answer

The Spirit gives the disciples power to preach, and the Church grows from that day.

Mission 3

Celebrating Pentecost

  • Red vestments and decorations: the fire of the Spirit. Some worshippers wear red.
  • Whitsun ("White Sunday"): the old English name, from the white robes of those baptised that day.
  • Baptisms and confirmations: receiving the Spirit.
  • Whit walks: processions of witness by churches, especially in north-west England.
  • Kneeling Prayers: on Pentecost evening, Orthodox Christians kneel for the first time since Easter, while the priest says long prayers for the Spirit. Churches are often decorated with green branches.
  • Pentecostal and charismatic churches stress the Spirit's gifts today, such as speaking in tongues and healing.

Mission 3

How festivals have changed

  • Holy days into holidays: in the UK, the Whit Monday holiday became a fixed late-May bank holiday (1971).
  • Some feasts are moved to Sunday, so that more people can attend.
  • Shops open on Sundays and feast days; fewer people keep fasts such as Lent strictly.
  • Online services: many churches have streamed festival worship since 2020.
  • Harvest: from fresh produce to tins for food banks.
  • Commercial pressure: Christmas and Easter are heavily marketed in many countries; Halloween (All Hallows' Eve, the night before All Saints) is now mainly a secular event.
  • Societies with many faiths and none: Christian festivals sit alongside others.
Mission 3Diversity

How Christians feel about the changes

  • Welcome them: adapting is how the Church has always reached people; online worship lets the housebound join in.
  • Concerned: holy days become days off; the meaning gets lost; preparation (fasting) disappears.
  • Keep the tradition: Orthodox and traditional Catholic communities often keep the old dates, fasts and services.
  • Use them as opportunities: e.g. some churches hold "light parties" at Halloween, or carol services in shopping centres.
  • Most Christians agree the meaning matters more than the form; they disagree on how much the form can change without losing it.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"Ascension and Pentecost matter as much as Christmas and Easter."

  • For: the story of salvation is incomplete without them: Jesus reigns, and the Spirit empowers the Church.
  • For: Pentecost is the Church's "birthday"; the Ascension is in both Creeds; the Orthodox count both among their Great Feasts.
  • Against: Easter is the foundation: without the resurrection there is nothing to ascend from.
  • Against: in practice, far fewer people attend; Ascension is often moved to a Sunday; society barely notices them.
  • Another view: they are one season: Ascension and Pentecost complete Easter rather than compete with it.
  • Judge it by: Importance in belief or in practice? For Christians or for society? Does the Spirit's work today make Pentecost most relevant?
Mission check

?In a German village, a Thursday forty days after Easter is a public holiday. At church the large candle lit at Easter is put out after the Gospel, and the parish begins nine days of prayer leading up to the Sunday ten days later. Which belief are these customs expressing?

Easter's fifty days end in Ascension and Pentecost: Jesus goes to the Father, and the Spirit comes to the Church.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Holy Week moves from Palm Sunday (entry) and Maundy Thursday (Last Supper) to Good Friday (the cross); Easter celebrates the resurrection, the most important Christian festival.
  • Lent: forty days of prayer, fasting and giving, from Ash Wednesday, recalling Jesus in the wilderness.

Mission 2

  • Christmas celebrates the Incarnation: God the Son born as Jesus (Luke 2: shepherds; Matthew 2: Magi); the date 25 December is a 4th-century tradition.
  • Advent prepares for Jesus' birth and his Second Coming, with prayer, wreaths, services and charity.
  • Epiphany (6 January) celebrates Christ revealed to all nations: the Magi in the West, Jesus' baptism in the East.

Mission 3

  • Ascension (day 40) celebrates Jesus returning to the Father; Pentecost (day 50) celebrates the Spirit's coming with wind and fire, the Church's birthday.
  • Festivals have changed (holidays, Sundays, streaming, commerce); Christians differ on whether this is loss or opportunity.
