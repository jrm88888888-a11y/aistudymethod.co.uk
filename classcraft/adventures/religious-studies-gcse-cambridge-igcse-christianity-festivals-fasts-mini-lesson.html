Festivals and fasts

This mini-lesson covers Christianity Topic C4 — Festivals and fasts in the Cambridge IGCSE 0490 syllabus: Advent, Christmas, Epiphany, Lent, Holy Week and Easter, Ascension and Pentecost — and, for each one, the syllabus wants three things: the religious reasons for observing it (origins, purpose, symbolism), its social significance, and the different ways it is observed. It ends with changes and commercialism.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Christians disagree with one another about many of these things, so watch for the Different views flag — that is what part (c) of every exam question rewards. Press Start when you are ready.

The Christian year

How the year is shaped

The Christian calendar is not a random list of holidays. It is a retelling of the life of Christ, year after year, and it moves in a fixed order:

Advent → Christmas → Epiphany → Lent → Holy Week → Easter → Ascension → Pentecostfast → feast → fast → feast: each celebration is prepared for by a season of self-denial

Notice the rhythm. Each great feast is preceded by a fast: Advent prepares for Christmas, Lent prepares for Easter. The point is that joy is deepened by preparation — you cannot properly feast if you have never gone without.

Fixed and moveable. Christmas is always 25 December (and 7 January for churches still using the older Julian calendar, such as many Orthodox). Easter moves, and everything attached to it moves too — Ash Wednesday, Holy Week, Ascension (40 days after Easter) and Pentecost (50 days after Easter).

C4.7 · Advent

Advent: waiting twice over

Advent (from Latin adventus, “coming”) begins four Sundays before Christmas and opens the Christian year. It looks in two directions at once, and the syllabus expects both:

  • Backwards — preparing to celebrate Jesus’ birth at Christmas.
  • Forwards — preparing for the Second Coming, when Christians believe Christ will return. This is why Advent readings are often about judgement, and why the mood is watchful rather than merely festive.

How it is observed: a season of fasting, prayer and charity in the more traditional churches (purple vestments; no flowers). The Advent wreath has four candles, one lit each Sunday, plus a central white candle on Christmas Day. Advent calendars, carol services and Nine Lessons and Carols are widely popular. Orthodox Christians keep a longer Nativity Fast of forty days.

Different ways of observing: most children now meet Advent as a chocolate calendar, and many churches sing carols throughout December. Traditionalists object that this collapses the fast into the feast and empties Advent of its meaning; others reply that anything which keeps the story in public view is worth having.

C4.2 · Christmas

Christmas: the Incarnation

Christmas celebrates the Incarnation — God taking on human form. That is the religious reason, and it should be the first thing you write. The Gospel accounts tell of the birth in Bethlehem, laid in a manger because there was no guest room, with the announcement to the shepherds (Luke 2:1–20); John states the meaning: “The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us” (John 1:14).

  • Midnight Mass — the Eucharist celebrated as Christmas Day begins, in Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and many other churches.
  • Other services — a Christmas morning family service; Crib services for children, with a Nativity scene; carol services; the crib or presepio set up in the church.
  • Symbolism — light in darkness (candles, a star), gift-giving recalling the gifts of the Magi and God’s gift of his Son, evergreens standing for eternal life.

Social significance vs religious reason. The syllabus splits these deliberately. Socially, Christmas brings families together, drives huge charitable giving, and marks the calendar even for the non-religious. Religiously, none of that is the point — the point is that God became human. A strong answer keeps the two apart and then relates them.

C4.3 · Epiphany

Epiphany: Christ revealed to the world

Epiphany (from the Greek for “manifestation” or “showing”), kept on 6 January in the Western churches, celebrates the revelation of Christ to the world — that is, to the Gentiles, beyond Israel.

  • The visit of the Magi (Matthew 2:1–12) — wise men from the East follow a star to the infant Jesus and offer gold (for a king), frankincense (for God) and myrrh (used to anoint the dead — a shadow of the crucifixion falling across the nativity). They are the first non-Jews to worship Jesus, which is precisely why the feast matters: the good news is for everyone.
  • The King cake — a cake shared on Epiphany with a bean or figurine hidden inside; whoever finds it is “king” for the day. Kept in France, Spain, Latin America and New Orleans, among other places.
  • Chalking the doors — the year and the letters C M B are chalked over the doorway, standing for the traditional names of the Magi (Caspar, Melchior, Balthasar) and for the Latin blessing Christus mansionem benedicat — “may Christ bless this house”.

Different ways of observing: in much of Spain and Latin America, Epiphany — not Christmas Day — is when children receive their presents, from the Three Kings. In many Orthodox churches Epiphany (Theophany) commemorates the baptism of Jesus instead, with the great blessing of the waters. Same feast, different emphasis.

C4.6 · Lent

Lent: forty days in the wilderness

Lent is the forty-day fast leading up to Easter. Its origin is the forty days Jesus spent in the wilderness, fasting and being tempted, before he began his ministry (Matthew 4:1–11). Its purpose is preparation for Easter.

  • Ash Wednesday opens Lent. Worshippers come forward and are marked on the forehead with a cross of ashes — traditionally made by burning the previous year’s palm crosses — with words recalling mortality and repentance. The symbolism is exact: last year’s triumph, burnt, becomes this year’s penitence.
  • Fasting and giving up — the syllabus names three things Christians give up: luxuries, money (given away instead) and time (given in service of others). Chocolate and alcohol are the cliché; almsgiving and volunteering are the substance. Catholics abstain from meat on Fridays in Lent; Orthodox Christians keep a strict fast from meat, dairy, fish, wine and oil.
  • In churches — purple vestments; no flowers; the word “Alleluia” is not sung. Lent courses and study groups are common.

Different views: many evangelical and Pentecostal Christians keep no Lenten fast at all, arguing it is a human tradition not found in the New Testament and that Christians should live self-denyingly all year, not for six weeks. Those who keep it reply that a discipline with a beginning and an end is one people actually manage.

C4.1 · Holy Week

Holy Week, day by day

The final week is the heart of the Christian year. Learn it as five days with five customs.

  • Palm Sunday — celebrates Jesus’ triumphal entry into Jerusalem on a donkey, with crowds spreading branches and shouting “Hosanna!” (Mark 11:1–11). Custom: a procession, often outdoors, and the giving out of palm crosses. But the reading in church is the whole Passion narrative — the day deliberately looks ahead to the end of the week.
  • Maundy (Holy) Thursday — commemorates the Last Supper and the betrayal in Gethsemane. Customs: a Eucharist / Holy Communion service, and foot-washing, re-enacting John 13:1–17. (“Maundy” comes from the Latin mandatum, “commandment” — Jesus’ new commandment to love one another.) The altar is then stripped bare.
  • Good Friday — commemorates the crucifixion and death of Jesus (Mark 15). Customs: solemn services from noon to three; the Stations of the Cross; services of ecumenical witness, in which the different churches of a town walk together behind a cross through the streets. No Eucharist is celebrated in many traditions; the church is stark and unadorned.
  • Holy Saturday — the day of waiting, and the beginning of the resurrection celebrations. Custom: the Easter Vigil after dark — a new fire is kindled, the Paschal candle is lit from it and carried into a dark church, readings trace salvation history, and converts are baptised.
  • Easter Sunday — celebrates the resurrection. Customs: sunrise services (often outdoors, echoing the women arriving at the tomb at dawn), processions, bells, flowers, “Alleluia” restored, and feasting — the Lenten fast is over. Orthodox Christians greet one another: “Christ is risen!” — “He is risen indeed!”

C4.4 · Ascension

Ascension: forty days after Easter

Ascension Day falls on the Thursday forty days after Easter. It celebrates Jesus going up into heaven: he blesses the disciples and is taken up out of their sight, and two men in white ask why they stand looking into the sky (Acts 1:9–11; Luke 24:50–53).

  • Meaning — the risen Christ is no longer present in one place, at one time. His work on earth is complete; he is exalted, and the disciples are now to be his witnesses. It is also the hinge into Pentecost: he leaves so that the Spirit may come.
  • Customs — the Paschal candle, lit since the Easter Vigil, is extinguished after the Gospel, marking the end of Christ’s visible presence. Processions, sometimes out of doors or up a hill. An all-night vigil in some traditions. In parts of England the old custom of “beating the bounds” — walking the parish boundary — is kept.

Social significance: Ascension Day is a public holiday in several European countries, but it is barely noticed in Britain and has no commercial life at all — a useful contrast for C4.8. It is the clearest example of a festival that is religiously major and socially minor.

C4.5 · Pentecost

Pentecost: the coming of the Spirit

Pentecost (Whitsun) falls fifty days after Easter and celebrates the coming of the Holy Spirit upon the twelve apostles. The account is in Acts 2:1–4: a sound like a violent wind filled the house, what seemed to be tongues of fire came to rest on each of them, and they began to speak in other tongues — so that pilgrims from every nation heard the message in their own language.

  • Meaning — often called the birthday of the Church. The frightened disciples become bold public preachers; the good news begins to cross every language barrier.
  • Customs — red is worn by clergy and congregation, for the fire of the Spirit. In the Orthodox churches, the Kneeling Prayers service is held: the congregation kneels for the first time since Easter. Confirmations and baptisms are often held at Pentecost. In Britain the day was long called Whitsun (“White Sunday”), from the white robes of the newly baptised, and was marked by Whit walks.

Different emphases: for Pentecostal and charismatic Christians, Pentecost is not just a memory but a pattern to be repeated — they expect the same gifts today. Other Christians read Acts 2 as a unique, once-for-all event that founded the Church.

C4.8 · change

Changes to festivals and fasts

The syllabus closes the topic by asking how these observances are changing, and how Christians feel about it. Handle it fairly — there are serious arguments on both sides.

  • Commercialisation — Christmas is a vast retail season, with decorations in shops from September and advertising built around Santa Claus rather than the Incarnation. Easter has become chocolate eggs and a bank holiday weekend. Even Lent is marketed (“give up X, download our app”).
  • Weakening of fasts — Advent and Lent are widely kept in a much lighter form than a century ago; the Orthodox fasting rules are strict but not universally observed.
  • New forms — online carol services, live-streamed Midnight Mass, church food-bank appeals at Christmas, “Real Advent Calendars” that tell the story alongside the chocolate.

How Christians feel — both ways. Many are dismayed: they say the religious meaning has been buried under consumption, that a season of fasting has become a season of spending, and that children now know Santa better than the shepherds. Others are more relaxed: they point out that Christmas trees, eggs and King cakes were always partly cultural; that generosity and family reunion are hardly un-Christian; and that a commercial Christmas still puts the nativity story in front of millions who would otherwise never hear it. A minority — some Puritans historically, some evangelicals today — would say the festivals should never have become national holidays in the first place.

Exam skill · part (c)

Answering the 8-mark evaluation

The part (c) question is worth 8 of the 20 marks and is marked on evaluation. Take this:

“Christmas has lost its religious meaning.” Discuss this statement.
How to build the answer

One view (agree): for most people in many countries Christmas is now a shopping season and a family holiday. Santa, not the Incarnation, is the central figure; church attendance on 25 December is a fraction of the population; Advent, the fast, has collapsed into six weeks of parties.

Another view (disagree): Midnight Mass and carol services are the best-attended services of the year, drawing in people who never otherwise come. The nativity story is still told in every primary school. Charitable giving peaks. And the generosity and family reconciliation that the season produces are themselves Christian values, even when nobody names them.

Judgement: commit and justify. E.g. “‘Lost’ is too absolute. The religious meaning has been obscured in public life while being fully preserved in the worship of the Church — so the statement describes the high street accurately and the church inaccurately.”

Structure that scores: a clear view, a clear counter-view, real evidence for each, and a judgement that is reasoned rather than merely asserted. Naming denominations — Orthodox fasting, Quaker non-observance, Pentecostal practice — is what pushes an answer to the top level.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Advent: preparation for the Second Coming and for Christmas · fasting, prayer, charity · Advent wreath

Christmas: the Incarnation (Luke 2:1–20; John 1:14) · Midnight Mass, crib services · light, gifts, evergreens

Epiphany: Christ revealed to the world · the Magi (Matthew 2:1–12) · King cake · chalking the doors (C M B)

Lent: Jesus’ forty days in the wilderness (Matthew 4:1–11) · Ash Wednesday · giving up luxuries, money or time

Holy Week: Palm Sunday (procession, palm crosses; Mark 11:1–11) · Maundy Thursday (Eucharist, foot-washing; John 13:1–17) · Good Friday (Stations of the Cross, ecumenical witness; Mark 15) · Holy Saturday (Easter Vigil, Paschal candle) · Easter Sunday (sunrise services, processions, feasting)

Ascension: forty days after Easter (Acts 1:9–11) · Paschal candle extinguished · processions, all-night vigil

Pentecost: fifty days after Easter · wind, tongues of fire, speaking in tongues (Acts 2:1–4) · red vestments · Kneeling Prayers

Change: commercialism at Christmas and Easter · weakening fasts · and Christians who are dismayed, relaxed, or opposed to the festivals altogether
