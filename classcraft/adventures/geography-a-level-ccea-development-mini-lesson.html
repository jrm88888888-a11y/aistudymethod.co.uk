Mission 1

Mission 1: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Make a guess: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.3a.i problems in defining "development"
  • AS2.3a.ii explain and evaluate two economic, two social and two composite measures of development
Mission 1

Why "development" is hard to define

  • Development means improvement in people's lives, but people disagree on what counts: income, health, education, rights, the environment.
  • Whose values? Measures chosen by richer countries may not match local priorities.
  • Averages hide gaps between regions, groups, women and men.
  • It changes over time: early measures used income alone; later ones added health, education and gender.
Mission 1

Single and composite measures

  • A single indicator measures one thing (life expectancy).
  • A composite index combines several (the HDI).
  • Measures can be economic, social or environmental. Each answers a different question.
Mission 1

Economic measures

  • GNI per capita (PPP): income per person, adjusted for what money buys locally. 2023: Guyana $46,959, Sri Lanka $12,616.
  • Economic growth: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 and 19.3% in 2025; oil and gas "have contributed to rapid GDP growth since 2019".
  • Economic sector balance: the share of output or jobs in primary, secondary and tertiary activity. A shift into oil changes Guyana's balance.
  • Limits: averages hide inequality; income can jump before lives change.
Mission 1

Social measures

  • Life expectancy at birth (2023): Guyana 70.2, Sri Lanka 77.5 years.
  • Mean years of schooling (2023): Guyana 8.7, Sri Lanka 10.8.
  • Infant mortality (deaths before age 1 per 1,000 live births, WHO estimates, 2024): Guyana 23.2, Sri Lanka 5.2.
  • Guyana's infant mortality estimate has a wide range: 12.8 to 42.8.
Quick check
?Guyana's infant mortality rate (23.2) is about how many times Sri Lanka's (5.2)?
Mission 1

A composite: the HDI

  • Three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (expected and mean years of schooling), a decent standard of living (GNI per capita, PPP).
  • Each becomes an index from 0 to 1 between fixed goalposts, e.g. life expectancy 20–85 years, income $100–$75,000 (logged).
  • HDI = geometric mean of the three: (I_health × I_education × I_income)^(1/3).
Quick check
?Guyana's 2023 dimension indices are health 0.772, education 0.652, income 0.929. Their ordinary average is 0.784, but UNDP publishes an HDI of 0.776. Why is the published value lower?
Mission 1

A second composite: the GII

  • The Gender Inequality Index measures disadvantage for women in three dimensions, from 0 (equal) to 1:
    • reproductive health: maternal mortality, adolescent births
    • empowerment: parliamentary seats, secondary education
    • labour market: labour force participation.
  • 2023: Denmark 0.003 (rank 1), Yemen 0.838 (rank 172), world 0.455.
  • Sri Lanka: 0.367 (rank 93); women held 5.3% of its parliamentary seats.
Mission 1

Evaluating the measures

  • Validity: does it measure what it claims? GNI says little about health; seat shares say little about real power.
  • Reliability: are the data accurate, current and comparable? Estimates come with ranges; the GII's maternal mortality data refer to 2020, its other indicators to 2023.
  • Built-in judgements: income above $75,000 adds nothing to the HDI, a ceiling nine countries exceeded in 2023.
  • Coverage: the HDI ranks 193 countries, the GII only 172.
Quick check
?Which is a problem of validity rather than reliability?
Mission 1

Exam move: two of each

  • Economic: GNI per capita (PPP); economic growth or sector balance.
  • Social: life expectancy; infant mortality (or years of schooling).
  • Composite: HDI; GII.
  • For each: say what it measures, give a located figure, then one strength and one limit. End with a judgement: several measures together beat any one.
Mission check

?Country P has three times Country Q's income per person, but lower life expectancy and fewer years of schooling. Their HDIs are equal. Why?

Sri Lanka scores higher on health (77.5 years against 70.2) and schooling (10.8 years against 8.7), and its infant mortality is far lower. The HDI logs income and takes a geometric mean, so Guyana's income of $46,959 against $12,616 cannot make up the gap: both score 0.776.

Mission 2

Mission 2: The world halved extreme poverty five years early. So why did it need a new set of goals?

Make a guess: The world halved extreme poverty five years early. So why did it need a new set of goals?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.3b.i aims of the Millennium Development Goals; evaluate the impact of any two (here MDG 1, poverty and hunger, and MDG 4, child mortality) as a means of improving global development
  • AS2.3b.ii aims of the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development and how the Global Goals build on the MDGs
Mission 2

The eight MDGs

1. Eradicate extreme poverty and hunger 2. Achieve universal primary education 3. Promote gender equality and empower women 4. Reduce child mortality 5. Improve maternal health 6. Combat HIV/AIDS, malaria and other diseases 7. Ensure environmental sustainability 8. Develop a global partnership for development

All "by the target date of 2015".

Mission 2

The aim: measurable targets

  • A "blueprint agreed to by all the world's countries and all the world's leading development institutions".
  • Each goal had numbered targets against a 1990 baseline:
  • Target 1.A: "Halve, between 1990 and 2015, the proportion of people whose income is less than $1.25 a day".
  • Target 4.A: "Reduce by two thirds, between 1990 and 2015, the under-five mortality rate".
Mission 2

MDG 1: poverty

  • People in extreme poverty: 1.9 billion (1990) → 836 million (2015).
  • Developing regions: "nearly half" (1990) → 14% (2015).
  • "The target of reducing extreme poverty rates by half was met five years ahead of the 2015 deadline".
  • But about 795 million people were still undernourished.
Mission 2

MDG 1: uneven progress

  • Eastern Asia: 61% → 4%; Southern Asia: 52% → 17% (1990 → 2015).
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: "More than 40 per cent" still in extreme poverty in 2015.
  • The UN: "progress has been uneven across regions and countries, leaving significant gaps".
Quick check
?Southern Asia's extreme poverty rate fell from 52% (1990) to 17% (2015). Did it meet the MDG 1 target for that rate?
Mission 2

MDG 4: child mortality

  • Under-five deaths per 1,000 live births: 90 → 43 (1990 → 2015), "a decline of more than half".
  • Deaths of children under five: 12.7 million (1990) → almost 6 million (2015).
  • Sub-Saharan Africa: 179 → 86 per 1,000, still "the world's highest" rate.
  • Target 4.A (two-thirds) was missed globally.
Quick check
?To meet Target 4.A, the world's under-five mortality rate had to fall by two-thirds from 90 per 1,000. What was the target rate for 2015?
Mission 2

Evaluating the MDGs' impact

  • Strengths: clear, measurable targets; global data; big falls in poverty and child deaths.
  • UN view: the MDGs "produced the most successful anti-poverty movement in history" (Ban Ki-moon, 2015). That is the UN's claim, not proof of cause.
  • Limits: much of the poverty fall came from Eastern Asia; sub-Saharan Africa missed targets; MDG 4 was missed globally.
  • Design: the goals aimed at developing countries; environment was one goal of eight.
Mission 2

The 2030 Agenda: the Global Goals

  • World leaders met at UN Headquarters, 25–27 September 2015.
  • 17 Sustainable Development Goals and 169 targets.
  • Aim: "to end poverty and hunger everywhere".
  • Balance "the three dimensions of sustainable development: the economic, social and environmental".
Mission 2

How the Global Goals build on the MDGs

  • In the UN's words, they "seek to build on the Millennium Development Goals and complete what these did not achieve".
  • Halve → end: from halving poverty to ending it "everywhere".
  • Developing countries → all countries: the Agenda "is accepted by all countries and is applicable to all".
  • Averages → everyone: "no one will be left behind".
  • 8 goals → 17: environment, inequality and other themes widened.
Quick check
?Which change best shows how the Global Goals tried to "complete what these did not achieve"?
Mission 2

Where the Global Goals stand in 2025

  • 139 of 169 targets could be assessed.
  • "only 35 per cent show adequate progress".
  • "18 per cent of targets have regressed below 2015 baseline levels".
  • About 808 million people expected in extreme poverty in 2025 (measured at an updated poverty line, so not comparable with the MDG $1.25 figure).
Mission check

?A country halved its extreme poverty rate between 1990 and 2015, meeting the MDG 1 target. Most of its remaining poor live in one remote region. Which feature of the 2030 Agenda responds most directly to this?

The MDGs worked as measurable targets: poverty halved early and child deaths fell by more than half, but unevenly, and some targets were missed. The Global Goals keep targets but aim to finish the job: end, not halve, for all countries and with no one left behind.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Uganda now gets more money from foreign investors than from aid. Is that good for its development?

Make a guess: Uganda now gets more money from foreign investors than from aid. Is that good for its development?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.3b.iii explain the different roles that globalisation and aid can have in influencing development in LEDCs, with one national LEDC case study (e.g. Uganda)
Mission 3

Uganda: the LEDC case

  • Landlocked, on the equator; population 45.9 million (2024).
  • A low-income country and a UN Least Developed Country since 1971.
  • GNI per capita: $1,120 (2025, Atlas method).
  • Agriculture: 24.8% of GDP and about 72% of the labour force.
  • Africa's largest refugee host: over 1.9 million refugees in 2026.
Mission 3

Types of aid

  • Bilateral: from one government to another (e.g. the UK).
  • Multilateral: pooled through international bodies (e.g. World Bank, UN agencies).
  • NGO aid: from charities, often funded by donations and by governments.
  • Tied aid: must be spent on goods or services from the donor country.
  • Uganda received $2.26bn of net aid (ODA) in 2023: 4.7% of GNI, down from 8.4% in 2020.
Mission 3

Multilateral aid: the World Bank

  • The World Bank's IDA lends to Uganda on "highly concessional terms, with no interest".
  • New IDA loans (from July 2025) are repaid over 40 years with an 11-year grace period.
  • Portfolio (September 2026): 18 projects worth $4.62bn, from social protection to energy.
  • A multi-donor trust fund ($76.47m) pools money from seven donors, including the UK, Ireland and the EU.
  • Role: large, long-term infrastructure and services; but loans add to debt.
Mission 3

Bilateral aid through NGOs and the UN

  • The UK suspended budget support to Uganda's government in 2013 and gives no funding directly to it.
  • UK programmes are run by UN, NGO and private-sector partners.
  • Refugee programme BRAER (2018–24, £210m): nearly 800,000 people received food assistance through cash transfers in one year.
  • UK support helped set up a Senior Citizens Grant reaching about 440,000 Ugandans over 80.
  • Role: reaching the poorest and refugees directly, bypassing government budgets.
Quick check
?A 40-year World Bank IDA credit with no interest, funding a social protection programme in Uganda, is an example of what?
Mission 3

Limits of aid

  • Volatile: net aid was $3.09bn in 2020 and $2.16bn in 2022.
  • Tied aid can raise costs and send contracts to the donor's firms.
  • Conditions: donors choose channels; the UK's 2013 suspension moved its aid away from government systems.
  • Debt: the World Bank reports "limited access to concessional financing"; interest payments absorb "nearly one-third of tax revenue".
Mission 3

Globalisation: foreign direct investment and oil

  • Net FDI inflows: $0.54bn (2010) → $3.12bn (2024).
  • Oil was discovered near Lake Albert in 2006. Two oilfield projects, Tilenga and Kingfisher, are being developed with TotalEnergies and CNOOC (China).
  • EACOP pipeline: 1,443 km (296 km in Uganda, 1,147 km in Tanzania), from Hoima to Tanga port in Tanzania; owned by TotalEnergies 62%, Uganda's and Tanzania's state oil companies 15% each, CNOOC 8%.
  • A Ugandan refinery has first call on 60,000 barrels a day; the rest is for export. Exports are planned for early 2027.
Quick check
?Uganda received $2.26bn net aid in 2023 and $3.12bn net FDI in 2024. Roughly how many times larger was FDI?
Mission 3

Globalisation: trade and remittances

  • Top foreign-exchange earners: gold, coffee, tourism and remittances.
  • Exports rose from 11.6% of GDP (2023) to 21.4% (2025).
  • As an LDC, Uganda can use the EU's Everything But Arms scheme: duty- and quota-free access for almost all products.
  • The UK's DCTS also covers UN LDCs.
  • Remittances from Ugandans abroad: $1.43bn in 2024.
Mission 3

Different roles

  • Aid: health, refugees, social protection, long-term infrastructure; reaches people markets miss.
  • FDI and trade: capital, technology and export earnings; concentrated in sectors such as oil.
  • Growth was an estimated 6.4% in FY2025–26; the World Bank projects 7.4% then 9.2% as oil starts (projections).
  • The World Bank warns Uganda "will need more than oil", including higher farm productivity and better jobs.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Explain"): give each a distinct role, with Ugandan evidence for each, then say how they interact.
Quick check
?Which is a development role that aid has played in Uganda but FDI in oil is unlikely to play?
Mission check

?A minister in another LEDC says: "Foreign investment now exceeds aid, so we can end all aid." Using Uganda, which response is best supported?

In Uganda, aid and globalisation play different roles: aid reaches the poorest, refugees and public services; FDI and trade bring capital and export earnings, concentrated in oil. Development depends on using both and broadening growth beyond oil.

Mission 4

Mission 4: BRICS, MINT, "emerging markets": what makes a poorer country's economy take off?

Make a guess: BRICS, MINT, "emerging markets": what makes a poorer country's economy take off?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.3c.i describe and explain "emerging markets" in the context of LEDCs
  • AS2.3c.ii how and why BRICS and MINT economies have grown; one national BRICS or MINT case study
Mission 4

What is an emerging market?

  • An emerging market is a lower- or middle-income economy (an LEDC in CCEA's terms) that is growing fast and integrating into world trade and finance.
  • The IMF scores non-advanced economies on five measures: nominal GDP (weight 0.40), population, GDP per person, share of world trade and share of world external debt (0.15 each).
  • This identified 20 emerging markets, holding 34% of world nominal GDP.
  • Since 2000, average incomes per person in emerging markets have more than doubled.
Mission 4

BRICS and MINT

  • BRICS: originally Brazil, Russia, India and China (2006); South Africa joined in 2011.
  • Six more joined in 2024–25: Egypt, Ethiopia, Indonesia, Iran, Saudi Arabia and the UAE.
  • MINT: Mexico, Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey. A label for a second wave, not a formal organisation.
  • Labels differ: Nigeria is not on the IMF's list of 20.
Quick check
?Which best describes an emerging market?
Mission 4

How much have they grown?

  • Share of world GDP (PPP), 2000 → 2024:
    • China 7.5% → 19.1%; India 4.3% → 7.8%; Russia 2.0% → 3.5%; Brazil 3.2% → 2.4%; South Africa 0.8% → 0.5%.
  • The five together: 17.9% → 33.3%.
  • Growth is uneven: China and India rose; Brazil's and South Africa's shares fell.
Mission 4

Why do emerging markets grow?

  • Opening up: lower tariffs and welcome to foreign direct investment (FDI) bring firms, capital and technology.
  • Workforce: a large, growing working-age population (a "demographic dividend").
  • Structural change: workers move from farming to higher-output industry and services.
  • Resources: some (e.g. Brazil, Russia) grew with world demand for commodities; growth then follows commodity prices.
Quick check
?Between 2000 and 2024, Brazil's share of world GDP (PPP) fell from 3.2% to 2.4% while China's rose from 7.5% to 19.1%. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 4

India: growth at a glance

  • National case: India (a BRICS founder).
  • Real GDP growth averaged about 6.2% a year from 2000 to 2024 (calculated from annual rates).
  • GDP per person: US$443 (2000) → US$2,592 (2024), current US$.
  • The worst year in the period was 2020 (−5.8%), the pandemic.
Mission 4

Why India grew: opening up

  • Average tariff on imports (weighted): 56.4% in 1990, 27.0% in 1992, 4.6% in 2022.
  • FDI inflows: US$0.24bn (1990) → US$27.1bn (2024).
  • Trade rose from 15.5% of GDP (1990) to 45.9% (2024).
  • Market reforms in the early 1990s made India far more open.
Mission 4

Why India grew: services and people

  • Service exports: US$16.7bn (2000) → US$374.9bn (2024).
  • 47.4% of service exports in 2024 were ICT (software, IT support).
  • Working-age (15–64) share of the population: 57.4% (1990) → 68.2% (2024): more workers per dependant.
Quick check
?India's GDP per person rose from US$443 (2000) to US$2,592 (2024). By what factor did it multiply?
Mission 4

Limits to India's growth

  • Agriculture still employed 42.4% of workers in 2024.
  • GDP per person (US$2,592) remains far below China (US$13,293) or the USA (US$86,170) in 2024.
  • FDI inflows peaked at US$64.4bn in 2020, then fell to US$27.1bn in 2024: investment has not kept rising.
  • Exam move (CCEA "Explain"): give each reason for growth, a figure that shows it, and how it led to growth.
Mission check

?Country X has cut its tariffs, attracted foreign factories and has a fast-growing young workforce. Its GDP grows 6% a year, but most people still farm and income per person is low. Is it an emerging market?

An emerging market is defined by rapid growth and integration, not by wealth. BRICS and MINT economies grew by opening up, using large workforces and shifting to industry and services, as India's data show, but growth is uneven within and between them.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In 1991 India's economy almost stalled. Three decades later it is one of the world's largest. What changed?

Make a guess: In 1991 India's economy almost stalled. Three decades later it is one of the world's largest. What changed?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • AS2.3c.ii how and why BRICS and MINT economies have grown, with one national case study of a BRICS or MINT country (here India, a BRICS country)
Mission 5

India: a BRICS emerging economy

  • BRICS: originally "Brasil, Russia, India, and China in 2006"; South Africa joined in 2011; six more members in 2024–25.
  • India is one of 20 emerging markets identified by the IMF's method.
  • Population 1.46 billion (2025); World Bank income group: lower middle income.
  • Big, still poor per person, but growing fast: a classic emerging economy.
Mission 5

How fast has India grown?

  • GDP (current US$): $270 billion (1991) → $3.96 trillion (2025), about 14.6 times larger (our calculation).
  • GDP per person: $306 → $2,702 (calculated ratio 8.8×).
  • Real growth averaged about 6.3% a year from 1992 to 2025 (compound average calculated from annual rates).
  • 2025: real growth 7.6%.
Quick check
?GDP rose about 14.6 times from 1991 to 2025, but GDP per person rose only about 8.8 times. Why the difference?
Mission 5

How: a changing economy

  • Jobs in agriculture: 63.1% (1991) → 41.6% (2025).
  • Services: 37.8% (1991) → 49.3% (2025) of GDP.
  • Trade: 17.0% (1991) → 46.3% (2025) of GDP.
  • Growth has come with a shift from farming to services and much deeper links to world markets.
Mission 5

Why 1: opening up

  • 1991 was a crisis year (real growth 1.1%); India then cut barriers to trade and investment.
  • Average tariff (weighted): 56.4% (1990) → 27.0% (1992) → 4.6% (2022).
  • Foreign direct investment inflows: $3.6 billion (2000) → $64.4 billion (2020); $40.8 billion in 2025.
  • Cheaper imports and foreign capital brought technology, firms and jobs.
Quick check
?Which change best shows that India opened up its economy after 1991?
Mission 5

Why 2: services and IT

  • India's ICT service exports: $5.0 billion (2000) → $200.3 billion (2025), about 40 times larger (our calculation).
  • ICT made up 47.4% of India's service exports in 2024.
  • Global firms use Indian services and suppliers: Apple's outsourcing partners are "located primarily in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam".
  • Growth was led by services, not only factories.
Mission 5

Why 3: people

  • Working-age share (15–64): 57.6% (1991) → 68.4% (2025): a demographic dividend, more workers per dependant.
  • Adult literacy: 48.2% (1991) → 76.3% (2022).
  • A large, younger, more educated workforce also forms a growing consumer market.
Mission 5

Why 4: state policy and global links

  • Make in India (September 2014) aimed to raise manufacturing to "25% of the GDP by 2020".
  • Result: manufacturing was 15.1% of GDP in 2014 and 13.5% in 2025, so the target was missed.
  • Foreign manufacturers invest too: Toyota Kirloskar Motor began in India in December 1999.
  • BRICS links: India chairs BRICS in 2026 for the fourth time.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains India's growth since 1991?
Mission 5

Growth is uneven

  • Inequality: in 2022–23, "22.6% of national income went to just the top 1%".
  • Poverty: multidimensional poverty 14.96% (2019–21); 19.28% rural vs 5.27% urban.
  • Jobs: 41.6% of workers still in agriculture in 2025.
  • Infrastructure "has not kept pace with both economic growth and population growth" (JICA).
Mission check

?A MINT country's GDP has grown fast for 20 years. Which set of evidence would best show that its growth followed the same path as India's after 1991?

India grew by opening up after 1991, building IT and other services, attracting investment and drawing on a larger, more educated workforce. The gains are real but uneven, which is why emerging economies remain "emerging".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • "Development" is multidimensional and value-laden, so no single measure captures it. Know two economic, two social and two composite measures, with a strength and a limit for each.
  • Economic (GNI per capita, growth, sector balance), social (life expectancy, infant mortality, schooling) and composite (HDI, GII) measures can rank the same countries differently.

Mission 2

  • MDGs (8 goals, target 2015): MDG 1 halved extreme poverty early (1.9 billion → 836 million) but unevenly; MDG 4 cut under-five mortality from 90 to 43 per 1,000, short of two-thirds.
  • The 2030 Agenda (17 goals, 169 targets) builds on them: end not halve, all countries, no one left behind; in 2025 only 35% of assessed targets showed adequate progress.

Mission 3

  • Uganda (LDC, low income): aid is bilateral (UK, via NGOs and the UN since 2013), multilateral (World Bank IDA) and NGO; net aid was $2.26bn (4.7% of GNI) in 2023.
  • Globalisation: FDI has risen to $3.12bn (2024), led by oil (Tilenga, Kingfisher, EACOP); trade preferences (EBA, DCTS) and remittances also matter; the World Bank says growth needs more than oil.

Mission 4

  • Emerging markets are lower- or middle-income economies growing fast and integrating into world trade and finance; the IMF identifies 20, with 34% of world nominal GDP. BRICS is a formal group (now enlarged); MINT is a label.
  • India grew by about 6.2% a year (2000–2024) as tariffs fell, FDI and services exports rose and the working-age share grew; but 42% of workers still farm and income per person remains low.

Mission 5

  • How: India's GDP rose about 14.6× in current dollars (1991–2025), with real growth averaging about 6.3% a year, a shift from farming to services and much more trade.
  • Why: opening up after 1991 (tariffs, FDI), ICT services, a demographic dividend and better education, with state policy (Make in India, target missed) and global links. Limits: inequality, rural poverty, infrastructure.
