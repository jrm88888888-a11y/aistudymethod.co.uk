Mission 1

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.

Mission 1

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
  • You need to read and interpret a Lorenz curve, not draw one.
Mission 1

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • United States: 41.5 (2019, World Bank).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.

Mission 1

Why is income unequal?

  • In competitive markets, each factor earns the value of its marginal product (its MRP).
  • Factors that produce more, or are scarcer, earn more. This alone creates income differences.
  • Other sources: taxes, human capital, social capital (networks), inheritance, discrimination, access to credit, mobility, bargaining power.
  • US, 2025 (BLS): median weekly earnings 1,578 dollars with a bachelor's degree, 966 dollars with a high school diploma.
  • Unemployment rates: 2.8% and 4.3% for the same groups.
  • So income and unemployment differ widely across groups, as well as across countries.
Mission check

?In country P, the Gini coefficient for household income is 0.30 and for household wealth is 0.65. (Illustrative data.) What can you conclude?

A Gini coefficient measures how evenly something is shared, not how much there is: here wealth is shared less evenly than income.

Mission 2

Two meanings of poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a line that is fixed in real terms (it rises only with prices).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income of the same society.
    • Measured against a line that moves with the median income, e.g. 60% of median household income.
  • Median: the middle income when all households are lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Absolute poverty asks: can you survive? Relative poverty asks: can you take part in normal life where you live?
  • Say: "relative poverty is low income compared with the median" — not: "a milder form of absolute poverty".
Mission 2

Poverty lines in practice

  • International poverty line (World Bank): $3.00 a day per person, at 2021 prices, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).
    • PPP: adjusts for what money buys in each country, so $3.00 means the same basket everywhere.
    • It is an absolute line. It replaced $2.15 a day (2017 prices) in June 2025.
    • Higher lines for richer countries: $4.20 and $8.30 a day.
  • US: the official poverty rate uses fixed income thresholds by family size, raised each year with prices. In 2024, about $32,130 a year for a family of four.
  • A fixed threshold raised only with prices is an absolute line.
Mission 2

Case: who is poor in the US?

  • Poverty rates vary across and within groups and countries.
  • US official poverty rate, 2024: 10.6% of people (35.9 million).
  • By age: under 18: 14.3% · 18–64: 9.6% · 65 and older: 9.9%.
  • By group: Asian: 7.5% · Hispanic: 15.0%.
  • Across countries: around 1 in 10 people in the world live below the World Bank's $3.00 a day line (2025).
  • Why rates differ: earnings and skills, number of earners per household, age, family size, and government transfers.
Quick check
?US official poverty rates in 2024 were: under 18, 14.3%; 18 to 64, 9.6%; 65 and older, 9.9%; all people, 10.6%. Which statement does the data support?
Mission 2

What changes the poverty count?

  • Poverty rates change over time as well as across groups.
  • Absolute poverty falls when the real incomes of the poor rise.
  • Relative poverty falls only when the poor's incomes rise faster than the median.
  • So a recession can cut relative poverty: the median falls while benefits and pensions hold steady.
  • And fast growth can raise relative poverty, if gains go mostly to middle and high earners.
  • Say: "relative poverty fell because the median fell" — not: "the poor became better off".
Mission check

?Over ten years, the real incomes of the poorest fifth of a country's households rise by 15%. Median real household income rises by 40%. Which outcome is most likely?

Absolute poverty tracks the real incomes of the poor. Relative poverty tracks the gap between the poor and the median, so it can rise while almost everyone gets richer.

Mission 3

What counts as discrimination?

  • Labor market discrimination: workers who are equally productive are treated differently in pay, hiring or promotion because of a characteristic such as gender or ethnicity.
  • Kinds:
    • employer discrimination: an employer prefers not to hire or promote one group
    • statistical discrimination: an employer judges an individual by a group average (e.g. "this group quits more often"), not by the person
    • occupational crowding: one group is kept out of some jobs and pushed into others
    • customer or co-worker discrimination: employers respond to others' prejudice
  • Before the labor market: unequal access to education and training lowers a group's skills before they apply for a job.
  • Say: "discrimination: equal productivity, unequal treatment" — not: any difference in average pay between groups.
Mission 3

Discrimination on a graph

  • Take the labor market for one group, group B.
  • An employer who discriminates acts as if group B's workers produce less than they do.
  • So the demand for B's labor (MRP = D) shifts left: D1 → D2.
  • The supply of B's labor has not changed: the workers are just as willing and able to work.
  • New equilibrium: lower wage (W1 → W2) and lower employment (L1 → L2) for group B.
  • On the graph: W (wage) against Q (quantity of labor); W1 → W2, Q1 → Q2.
  • The wage falls by less than the shift: some of B's workers leave this market rather than work for less.

Mission 3

Worked example: the pay gap

  • Pay gap = (men's median pay − women's median pay) ÷ men's median pay × 100.
  • US, 2023 (BLS, full-time wage and salary workers): women's median weekly earnings 1,005 dollars, men's 1,202 dollars.
    • (1,202 − 1,005) ÷ 1,202 × 100 = 16.4% gap; women earn 83.6% of men's median.
  • A gap is not all discrimination. It also reflects occupation, hours, experience and career breaks for childcare.
  • But those choices can themselves be shaped by discrimination and social norms.
Quick check

Mission 3

Real world: testing for discrimination

  • How can economists separate discrimination from differences in skills?
  • Field experiment: send employers job applications that are identical except for one thing.
  • US, published 2004 (Bertrand and Mullainathan): nearly 5,000 résumés sent to over 1,300 job adverts in Boston and Chicago.
  • Each résumé was randomly given a name that sounded white or one that sounded Black.
  • Résumés with white-sounding names received 50% more callbacks.
  • Same qualifications, so the difference measures discrimination in hiring, not productivity.
Mission 3

Who loses, and what can be done?

  • Workers in the group: lower pay and fewer jobs; less reason to invest in their own skills.
  • Firms that discriminate pay more for favored workers than for equally productive others, so their costs are higher.
  • The economy: talent is misallocated, so output is lower.
  • Discrimination is one source of income inequality, alongside differences in human capital, inheritance and bargaining power.
  • Policies:
    • laws: Equal Pay Act 1963; Civil Rights Act 1964 (Title VII bans employment discrimination by race, color, religion, sex and national origin)
    • childcare support and paid family leave; name-blind hiring
Mission 3Explain

Show discrimination on a graph

  • Draw W (vertical) and Q (horizontal) for one group's labor market.
  • Employers who discriminate act as if the group's MRP is lower: D = MRP shifts left.
  • Supply is unchanged. Show the wage falling W1 → W2 and employment falling Q1 → Q2.
  • Lower wages and fewer jobs for the group widen income inequality.
Mission check

?In a country's computing industry, many employers start acting as if workers over 50 are less productive than younger workers, although tests show they are just as productive. What happens in the market for older computing workers?

Discrimination works through demand: employers value a group's work as if it were worth less, so pay and jobs both fall.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 2

  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed real line for basic needs. Relative poverty: below a share (often 60%) of the median.
  • Poverty rates vary across countries and, within a country, by age and group.

Mission 3

  • Discrimination: equally productive workers treated differently because of who they are.
  • On a graph, it shifts the demand for the group's labor left: lower wage and lower employment.
  • Discrimination is one source of income inequality.
