Unit 7 · Planning

Business plans and corporate objectives

A business plan sets out the idea, the market, the marketing and operations plan, the management team and — crucially — the financial forecasts (cash flow, break-even, funding required).

  • Why write one: it is required by lenders and investors; it forces the entrepreneur to research and quantify assumptions; it becomes a benchmark to monitor against.
  • Limits: it is built on forecasts that may be wrong, it can create false confidence, and it dates quickly in a dynamic market.

The hierarchy runs: mission (why we exist) → corporate aims → corporate objectives (SMART, whole-business) → functional objectives (marketing, HR, operations, finance) → tactics.

Strategy vs tactics: strategy is the long-term plan to achieve the corporate objectives — expensive and hard to reverse. Tactics are short-term, cheap, reversible actions (a two-week price promotion). Do not confuse them in an answer.

Unit 7 · SWOT

SWOT analysis

HelpfulHarmful
InternalStrengths — brand, patents, skilled staff, cashWeaknesses — high gearing, ageing plant, narrow range
ExternalOpportunities — new markets, technology, a rival failingThreats — new entrants, recession, regulation

Strengths and weaknesses are internal and controllable; opportunities and threats are external and come straight out of PESTLE and Porter.

The classic error: putting "recession" in weaknesses or "our strong brand" in opportunities. Ask: is it inside the business, or outside it?

Unit 7 · Ansoff

Ansoff's matrix

Ansoff's matrix maps growth strategies against risk, using two axes: existing/new products and existing/new markets.

Existing productsNew products
Existing marketsMarket penetration — lowest risk: sell more to current customers (loyalty schemes, promotion)Product development — new products for known customers; needs R&D
New marketsMarket development — existing products into new segments or countriesDiversification — highest risk: new product, new market

Judgement: risk rises as you move away from the top-left cell — but so does the potential reward. Diversification spreads risk across markets, yet the firm has neither product nor market knowledge, which is why so many diversifications fail.

Unit 7 · Porter

Porter's generic strategies

Porter argued a firm must choose a source of competitive advantage (low cost or differentiation) and a scope (broad market or narrow niche):

  • Cost leadership — be the lowest-cost producer in the whole market. Needs scale, efficiency and tight cost control; margins survive a price war.
  • Differentiation — offer something the market values as unique (brand, design, service, quality) and charge a premium.
  • Cost focus and differentiation focus — the same two strategies applied to a narrow niche.

Stuck in the middle: Porter warned that a firm that is neither the cheapest nor meaningfully different has no competitive advantage at all — the most common cause of long-run failure.

Unit 7 · Porter

Porter's five forces

Five forces determine how profitable an industry is:

  • Threat of new entrants — low if barriers (capital cost, brand, patents, economies of scale, regulation) are high.
  • Bargaining power of buyers — high if buyers are few, large and can switch easily (supermarkets vs farmers).
  • Bargaining power of suppliers — high if suppliers are few, or the input is unique.
  • Threat of substitutes — a different way of meeting the same need (streaming replaced DVDs).
  • Competitive rivalry — the intensity of competition, driven by the other four.

Use it to explain why margins are thin in an industry, and what a firm must do — build barriers, differentiate, lock in customers, integrate vertically — to protect them.

Unit 7 · Core competences

Core competences and strategic fit

A core competence (Hamel and Prahalad) is something the business does that is valuable to customers, difficult to imitate and transferable to new products or markets — for example an engineering firm's miniaturisation expertise.

Strategy should build on core competences rather than chase every opportunity. Ask three questions of any proposed strategy:

  • Suitability — does it fit the external environment and our objectives?
  • Feasibility — do we have the finance, capacity and skills?
  • Acceptability — will shareholders and other stakeholders accept the risk and the returns?

Unit 7 · Advantage

Competitive advantage and why strategies fail

Competitive advantage is an edge that lets a firm outperform rivals — and it must be sustainable: valuable, rare, hard to imitate and supported by the organisation.

Why strategies fail:

  • Strategic drift — the environment changes but the strategy does not, until the gap becomes a crisis.
  • Poor implementation — a good strategy with no resources, no communication and no buy-in.
  • Stuck in the middle — neither cheapest nor different.
  • Over-optimistic forecasts and ignoring rivals' likely responses.

Exam edge: the best evaluation of any strategy asks three things — is it suitable (fits the environment), feasible (we have the money, skills and capacity) and acceptable (the risk and return satisfy stakeholders)?
