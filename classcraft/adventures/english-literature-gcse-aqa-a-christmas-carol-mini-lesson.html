Mission 1

Why does Scrooge's whole life change in a single night?

Make a guess: Why does Scrooge's whole life change in a single night?

A Christmas Carol · structureScreen 1

A ghost story in five staves

  • A stave is a verse of a song. Dickens calls his chapters staves because the book is a carol.
  • It is a novella (a short novel), published on 19 December 1843.
  • Chronological structure: one night, three spirits, Scrooge's past, present and future in order.
  • Circular structure: Stave 5 returns to the people and places of Stave 1.
  • Dickens didn't invent Christmas: carols and feasts were already old. His book helped shape how it was celebrated.
A Christmas Carol · structureScreen 2

What happens in each stave

StaveDickens's titleWhat happens
1Marley's GhostChristmas Eve. Scrooge rejects his nephew Fred, two charity collectors and a carol singer, and grudges his clerk Bob Cratchit a day off. Marley's ghost, in chains, warns him three spirits will come.
2The First of the Three SpiritsThe Ghost of Christmas Past: Scrooge as a lonely schoolboy, his sister Fan, Fezziwig's party, Belle ending their engagement, Belle's later happy family.
3The Second of the Three SpiritsThe Ghost of Christmas Present: London on Christmas morning, the Cratchits' dinner and Tiny Tim, miners, a lighthouse, a ship, Fred's party, then two children: Ignorance and Want.
4The Last of the SpiritsThe silent Ghost of Christmas Yet To Come: businessmen joke about a death, thieves sell the dead man's things, a couple in debt to him are relieved, the Cratchits mourn Tiny Tim, Scrooge sees his own grave.
5The End of ItChristmas morning: a prize turkey for the Cratchits, a large donation pledged to the charity collector, dinner at Fred's, a pay rise for Bob. Tiny Tim does not die.
A Christmas Carol · structureScreen 3

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Paper 1 Section B: an extract, then the novella as a whole; usually 30 marks (AO1, AO2, AO3).
  • The exam is closed book, so learn quotations. Essay practice is for class.

Mission 2

Does Scrooge change overnight, or was the change already in him?

Make a guess: Does Scrooge change overnight, or was the change already in him?

A Christmas Carol · ScroogeScreen 1

Scrooge in Stave 1

  • Dickens builds Scrooge from cold: frost on his head, "his thin lips blue", an office he "iced" in summer.
  • A list of -ing words (present participles) piles up greed: "squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching".
  • He is cut off from people: "solitary as an oyster".
  • His words match: "Bah!" and "Humbug!" to Fred; "Nothing!" to the collectors.
  • Write 'Dickens presents Scrooge as…': he is a construct, built to change.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Does Scrooge change overnight, or was the change already in him?

Both, and the best answers argue it that way. The night is fast, but Dickens shows the change growing in stages: tears for his younger self in Stave 2, concern for Tiny Tim in Stave 3, pleading in Stave 4. Scrooge was "flint" that could still make fire, and in the end he chooses to change.

Mission 3

Tiny Tim barely speaks. Why does the book end on his words?

Make a guess: Tiny Tim barely speaks. Why does the book end on his words?

A Christmas Carol · the CratchitsScreen 1

Who the Cratchits are

  • Bob Cratchit: Scrooge's clerk, on fifteen shillings a week, copying letters in a cold "Tank".
  • His family: Mrs Cratchit, Martha (an apprentice at a milliner's), Belinda, Peter, two younger children and Tiny Tim, who has a crutch and an iron frame.
  • Their poverty is shown, not told: a "twice-turned gown", a borrowed collar, "Two tumblers, and a custard-cup without a handle."
  • Dickens presents them as the novella's model of Christmas: warmth without wealth.

A Christmas Carol · the CratchitsScreen 4

What Tiny Tim is for

  • He says little, but his "God bless us every one!" in Stave 3 states the novella's message: blessing for all.
  • The Ghost's warning is conditional: "If these shadows remain unaltered by the Future, the child will die."
  • Stave 4: the family grieve quietly; Bob cries, "My little, little child!"
  • Stave 5: Tim "did NOT die", and Scrooge becomes "a second father".
  • Some readers find Tim sentimental; others argue Dickens uses pathos on purpose, to move readers to act.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Tiny Tim barely speaks. Why does the book end on his words?

Tim speaks little, but he carries the message. His blessing on "every one" sums up what Scrooge learns: care for all, not just yourself. Dickens made Tim's life depend on whether Scrooge changes, so ending on his words, with Tim alive, shows that the change was real.

A Christmas Carol · the ghostsScreen 4

Christmas Past and Christmas Present

  • Past carries a bright light. Scrooge forces its cap down, but "he could not hide the light": memory can't be put out.
  • Present stands for plenty and generosity. His torch blesses dinners, "To a poor one most."
  • His scabbard holds no sword and is rusted: many readers see a sign of peace.
  • He ages through one night; his life "ends to-night".
  • Under his robe he hides two children: Ignorance and Want.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does the last ghost never say a single word?

Its silence makes Scrooge do the work. The earlier spirits explain; this one only points, so Scrooge has to read the signs, work out that the dead man is himself, and decide whether the future "May be" changed. The silence also makes death feel unknown and frightening.

Mission 5

Who should help the poor: the law, charity or Scrooge himself?

Make a guess: Who should help the poor: the law, charity or Scrooge himself?

A Christmas Carol · social responsibilityScreen 1

Whose business?

  • Social responsibility: the idea that everyone owes care to others, not just the law.
  • Scrooge, Stave 1: the poor are "not my business".
  • Fred, Stave 1: Christmas makes people see the poor as "fellow-passengers to the grave".
  • Marley's ghost, Stave 1: "Mankind was my business."
  • Dickens sets these views side by side; the novella tests which one Scrooge will learn.

A Christmas Carol · social responsibilityScreen 4

Why 1843?

  • 1798: Thomas Malthus argued that population grows faster than food. Scrooge's "surplus population" echoes this cold way of counting the poor.
  • February 1843: the Children's Employment Commission's second report described children's working lives. Dickens planned a pamphlet for poor children, then chose to write a story instead.
  • September 1843: Dickens visited a ragged school (a free school for destitute children) in London.

Mission 6

Scrooge pays his taxes. Why isn't that enough for Dickens?

Make a guess: Scrooge pays his taxes. Why isn't that enough for Dickens?

A Christmas Carol · the Poor LawScreen 1

The New Poor Law, 1834

  • The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 grouped parishes into Unions, each with a workhouse.
  • Able-bodied poor people were meant to get help only by entering it.
  • Less eligibility: life inside was kept no better than the poorest labourer's, to deter people from applying.
  • Inmates were split by age and sex, so families were separated. Work included stone-breaking and picking oakum.

A Christmas Carol · the Poor LawScreen 4

What Scrooge's words reveal

  • Scrooge names prisons, the Treadmill (a prison punishment) and the Union workhouses together: to him, the poor and criminals belong in the same places.
  • He pays taxes for these "establishments" and thinks that ends his duty.
  • The gentleman's answer: "Many can't go there; and many would rather die."
  • Dickens knew poverty: in 1824 his father was jailed for debt, and twelve-year-old Charles worked in a blacking factory.
A Christmas Carol · the Poor LawScreen 5

The words come back

  • Stave 3: Scrooge asks if Ignorance and Want have "no refuge or resource".
  • The Spirit answers, "turning on him for the last time with his own words": "Are there no prisons?" … "Are there no workhouses?"
  • This structural echo turns Scrooge's careless words into a judgement on him: applied to two starving children, they sound cruel.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Scrooge pays his taxes. Why isn't that enough for Dickens?

Paying for the system isn't the same as caring. Scrooge's taxes support prisons, the Treadmill and workhouses that the poor feared more than death, so he treats poverty as a crime. Dickens throws his words back at him in Stave 3 to show how cruel that attitude is.

Mission 7

Fog, frost, fire: why does the weather change as Scrooge does?

Make a guess: Fog, frost, fire: why does the weather change as Scrooge does?

A Christmas Carol · languageScreen 1

A story that stands for more

  • An allegory is a story where characters or objects stand for ideas: Ignorance and Want; spirits of Past, Present and Future.
  • Dickens's preface hopes the book will "raise the Ghost of an Idea".
  • It is also realistic: real places (Cornhill, Camden Town), real wages.
  • So read on two levels: the literal story and what it stands for. Symbols (cold, fog, fire, light) link them.
A Christmas Carol · languageScreen 2

Dickens's toolkit

MethodExampleEffect
Simile"solitary as an oyster"Shut tight against others
List"squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous"Greed piles up, word on word
Pathetic fallacythe fog "came pouring in"The setting mirrors gloom
Narrator's voice"as close to it as I am now to you"A storyteller at your elbow, as if reading aloud
Symbolcold and dark against fire and lightColdness = selfishness; warmth = kindness
  • Pathetic fallacy means weather or nature reflecting human feeling.

A Christmas Carol · languageScreen 6

The weather turns

  • Stave 5: "No fog, no mist; clear, bright, jovial, stirring, cold; cold, piping for the blood to dance to".
  • It is still cold, but now the cold is lively, not deadening. The world hasn't changed much: Scrooge's view of it has.
  • An extended contrast across the novella: fog and frost (Stave 1), fire and light (Staves 2–3), darkness (Stave 4), clear sun (Stave 5).
Write itScreen 7
?Short analysis, ungraded.

In 3–4 sentences, explain how Dickens uses weather and temperature to show Scrooge's change. Use one quotation from Stave 1 and one from Stave 5.

Model answer

In Stave 1 Dickens uses the metaphor "The cold within him froze his old features" to suggest that Scrooge's meanness has become part of his body. The fog that comes "pouring in at every chink and keyhole" makes his world dark and shut in. In Stave 5 there is "No fog, no mist", and the cold is "piping for the blood to dance to". The weather is still cold: what has changed is Scrooge, so the cold now feels lively instead of deadening.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 8

?Fog, frost, fire: why does the weather change as Scrooge does?

The weather is part of the allegory. Dickens makes fog and frost reflect Scrooge's coldness in Stave 1, and in Stave 5 the day is still cold, but "clear, bright, jovial": Scrooge has changed, so the cold now feels alive.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A Christmas Carol is a novella in five staves (verses of a carol), published in December 1843.
  • Its structure is chronological (past, present, future in one night) and circular (Stave 5 echoes Stave 1).
  • AQA prints an extract and asks about the whole novella (usually 30 marks).
  • In Stave 1 Dickens builds Scrooge from cold imagery, a list of greedy -ing words and similes of hardness and isolation.
  • The change is gradual: memory (Stave 2), sympathy (Stave 3), fear and pleading (Stave 4), action (Stave 5).
  • Interpretations differ on whether the change is believable; the strongest answers use the staged structure as evidence.
  • Dickens presents the Cratchits as poor but loving, a model of Christmas warmth without wealth.
  • Their poverty is shown through detail (clothes, shoes, the pawnbroker), and Dickens asks readers to respect them, not blame them.
  • Tiny Tim's fate is conditional on Scrooge changing; the Ghost turns Scrooge's "surplus population" words against him.
  • Dickens uses the ghost-story genre for a moral purpose: each spirit has a role in Scrooge's change.
  • Marley's chain is a symbol of sins chosen "link by link"; Past brings memory, Present brings plenty and a warning.
  • The silent last spirit makes Scrooge interpret the future himself, and his question ("Will be" or "May be") shows hope.
  • Scrooge says the poor are "not my business"; Marley's ghost answers "Mankind was my business".
  • Context (1843): Malthus's "surplus population", the Children's Employment Commission report and Dickens's ragged-school visit help explain his purpose.
  • Ignorance and Want are an allegory: society's neglected children, a warning that society must act.
  • The 1834 New Poor Law made the workhouse the main help for the able-bodied poor, with harsh conditions and families separated.
  • Scrooge links prisons, the Treadmill and the workhouses, and thinks paying for them ends his duty; the gentleman replies that many "would rather die".
  • The Spirit's echo of "Are there no workhouses?" in Stave 3 turns Scrooge's words against him. Link context to a line, never bolt it on.
  • A Christmas Carol is an allegory and a realistic story: read it on both levels.
  • Dickens's methods: similes, lists, pathetic fallacy, the narrator's voice, and symbols of cold against warmth.
  • The weather follows Scrooge: fog and inner cold in Stave 1, a lively cold in Stave 5.

Key quotations to learn

  • "Marley was dead: to begin with." (Stave 1) The opening line: sets up the ghost story and the narrator's chatty voice.
  • "nothing wonderful can come of the story I am going to relate" (Stave 1) The narrator explains why Marley's death must be certain.
  • "I will live in the Past, the Present, and the Future." (Stave 4 (repeated in Stave 5)) The three-spirit structure becomes Scrooge's promise.
  • "It's all right, it's all true, it all happened." (Stave 5) Scrooge insists the night was real.
  • "God bless Us, Every One!" (Stave 5 (last line)) The ending returns to Tiny Tim's words from Stave 3.
  • "solitary as an oyster" (Stave 1) Shut off from others, but an oyster can hold something precious.
  • "Hard and sharp as flint, from which no steel had ever struck out generous fire" (Stave 1) Hard, yet able to spark: hints at change.
  • "I should like to have given him something" (Stave 2) The first regret for how he treats others.
  • "I am not the man I was." (Stave 4) He claims the change before he wakes.
  • "I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year." (Stave 4) His promise: a lasting change, not one day.
  • "as good a friend, as good a master, and as good a man, as the good old city knew" (Stave 5) The narrator's verdict on the new Scrooge.
  • "God bless us every one!" (Stave 3) Tim's blessing for all: the novella's message.
  • "But, they were happy, grateful, pleased with one another, and contented with the time" (Stave 3) Warmth outweighs poverty.
  • "If these shadows remain unaltered by the Future, the child will die." (Stave 3) Tim's fate depends on change.
  • "My little, little child!" (Stave 4) Bob's grief in the possible future.
  • "Spirit of Tiny Tim, thy childish essence was from God!" (Stave 4) The narrator presents Tim as holy and good.
  • "to Tiny Tim, who did NOT die, he was a second father" (Stave 5) Proof of Scrooge's change.
  • "I wear the chain I forged in life" (Stave 1) Sin as a chain we make ourselves.
  • "Your reclamation, then. Take heed!" (Stave 2) Christmas Past's purpose: to win Scrooge back.
  • "he could not hide the light" (Stave 2) Memory and truth can't be put out.
  • "To any kindly given. To a poor one most." (Stave 3) Christmas Present's generosity favours the poor.
  • "The Phantom slowly, gravely, silently, approached." (Stave 4) Slow, solemn, silent: the dread of the unknown future.
  • "Are these the shadows of the things that Will be, or are they shadows of things that May be, only?" (Stave 4) Scrooge hopes the future can change.
  • "It's not my business" (Stave 1) Scrooge denies responsibility.
  • "decrease the surplus population" (Stave 1) Echoes Malthus; returns in Stave 3.
  • "Mankind was my business." (Stave 1) Marley reverses Scrooge's idea of business.
  • "fellow-passengers to the grave" (Stave 1) Fred: rich and poor share one fate.
  • "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want." (Stave 3) Allegory of society's neglected children.
  • "They are Man's" (Stave 3) Society is responsible for them.
  • "Are there no prisons?" (Stave 1 (echoed in Stave 3)) Scrooge links the poor with criminals.
  • "And the Union workhouses?" (Stave 1) Points to the 1834 system.
  • "I help to support the establishments I have mentioned" (Stave 1) He thinks taxes end his duty.
  • "Many can't go there; and many would rather die." (Stave 1) The fear of the workhouse.
  • "Are there no workhouses?" (Stave 3) The Spirit turns his words on him.
  • "The cold within him froze his old features" (Stave 1) Metaphor: meanness as inner cold.
  • "a squeezing, wrenching, grasping, scraping, clutching, covetous, old sinner!" (Stave 1) A list that piles up greed.
  • "The fog came pouring in at every chink and keyhole" (Stave 1) Pathetic fallacy: gloom invades.
  • "Darkness is cheap, and Scrooge liked it." (Stave 1) Darkness as a symbol of meanness.
  • "as close to it as I am now to you, and I am standing in the spirit at your elbow" (Stave 2) The narrator speaks to the reader.
  • "No fog, no mist; clear, bright, jovial, stirring, cold" (Stave 5) The changed world of the changed man.
