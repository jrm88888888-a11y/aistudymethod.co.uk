Environmental Challenges

This mini-lesson covers Eduqas Theme 3 — Environmental Challenges: how humans use, modify and damage ecosystems, using the tropical rainforest and hot semi-arid grasslands as examples, the causes and consequences of desertification, and how ecosystems and drylands can be sustainably managed.

3.2.1 Using ecosystems

How humans use & modify ecosystems

An ecosystem is a community of plants and animals interacting with each other and their physical environment. People change ecosystems to obtain the resources they need:

  • Food — clearing land for farming crops and grazing livestock, and fishing.
  • Energy — collecting fuelwood, growing biofuels, and building dams for hydroelectric power.
  • Water — extracting groundwater and river water for irrigation, industry and homes.

Key idea: using an ecosystem is not automatically harmful — the problem comes when use becomes unsustainable, taking resources faster than the ecosystem can recover, so it becomes damaged.

3.2.2 Ecosystems under threat

Two ecosystems damaged by people

Eduqas expects you to know two real examples where human activity degrades an ecosystem:

  • Tropical rainforest — cleared (deforested) for logging, cattle ranching, soya and palm-oil plantations, mining and new roads.
  • Hot semi-arid grassland / savanna — degraded by over-grazing, over-cultivation and cutting trees for fuelwood, which strips the soil.

Rainforest under threat

Why rainforest loss matters

The tropical rainforest is the most biodiverse ecosystem on Earth, so deforestation has effects at both a local and a global scale:

Key idea: rainforests are described as the "lungs of the planet" because they store carbon and release oxygen — so their loss is both a local biodiversity problem and a global climate problem.

Drylands under threat

How grasslands are degraded

In hot semi-arid grasslands the vegetation is sparse and the soil is fragile. Several human activities strip it away:

  • Over-grazing — too many cattle, goats or sheep eat the grass faster than it can regrow, leaving bare soil.
  • Over-cultivation — growing crops on the same land year after year exhausts the soil's nutrients.
  • Deforestation for fuelwood — cutting trees and shrubs for firewood removes the roots that bind the soil together.

The result — soil degradation: with no plant cover, the topsoil is blown away by wind or washed off by rare heavy rain (erosion). The land loses its fertility, and biodiversity falls both locally and, as productive land is lost worldwide, globally.

3.2.3 Sustainable management

Managing the rainforest sustainably

We conserve ecosystems because of their biodiversity, the resources and medicines they provide, and their role in regulating climate. Several strategies let people use the rainforest without destroying it:

  • Selective logging — only certain mature trees are felled, so the forest can recover.
  • Agro-forestry — growing crops among the trees, keeping tree cover in place.
  • Ecotourism — small-scale, low-impact tourism that gives local people an income for protecting the forest.
  • Protected areas & national parks, plus debt-for-nature swaps (debt cancelled in return for conservation) and community forestry.
  • International agreements such as REDD+ (rewarding countries for keeping forests) and CITES (controlling trade in endangered species).

Sustainable means meeting today's needs without stopping future generations from meeting theirs — using the forest so it is still there for the future.

3.4 Desertification

What is desertification?

Desertification is the process by which fertile, productive land turns into desert-like land. It happens on the fringes of hot deserts — for example the Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara:

Key idea: desertification is caused by a combination of physical (climate) factors and human actions working together — not by one cause alone.

Model answer
  • Locally, clearing trees destroys habitats so biodiversity falls, as many plant and animal species lose their home.
  • With no tree roots to hold it, the thin forest soil is quickly washed away by heavy rain, so the land loses its fertility.
  • Globally, burning and removing the trees releases stored carbon dioxide into the atmosphere, which adds to climate change.

Human causes

Desertification: human causes

A rising population needs more food, fuel and water from fragile land. This pressure produces the human causes of desertification:

Consequences: crops fail and animals die, so food and water become scarce, people can fall into poverty, and some are forced to migrate — while the bare, degraded land spreads.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Using ecosystems: humans change ecosystems for food, energy and water; harm comes when use is unsustainable.

Rainforest damage: deforestation for logging, ranching, soya, palm oil, mining & roads → local loss of biodiversity & soil, and global CO₂ release.

Dryland damage: over-grazing, over-cultivation & fuelwood cutting strip the soil in hot semi-arid grasslands.

Sustainable rainforest management: selective logging, agro-forestry, ecotourism, protected areas, debt-for-nature swaps, community forestry, REDD+ & CITES.

Desertification: productive land turning to desert on the fringes of hot deserts (e.g. the Sahel).

Physical causes: unreliable rainfall, high evapotranspiration, drought, sinking dry air under high pressure.

Human causes: over-grazing, over-cultivation, fuelwood collection, over-use of water, population pressure.

Dryland management: local NGO methods (drip irrigation, "magic stones"/bunds, drought-tolerant crops, agroforestry) & international schemes (the Great Green Wall).
