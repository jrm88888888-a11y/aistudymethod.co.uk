Mission 1

Mission 1: If everyone lived like the average person in Qatar, humanity would use up a whole year's worth of nature by 4 February. If everyone lived like the average person in India, the year's supply would not run out before the year ended. What is being measured?

Make a guess: If everyone lived like the average person in Qatar, humanity would use up a whole year's worth of nature by 4 February. If everyone lived like the average person in India, the year's supply would not run out before the year ended. What is being measured?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.1 the concept of ecological footprint
Mission 1

Ecological footprint

  • Ecological footprint: how much biologically productive land and water a person, population or activity needs.
  • It counts the area needed to grow food, timber and fibres, to hold buildings and roads, and to absorb carbon dioxide.
  • It is measured in global hectares (gha): hectares of world-average productivity.
Mission 1

Biocapacity: nature's supply

  • Biocapacity: how much the land and sea can regrow and supply each year.
  • Footprint = demand. Biocapacity = supply.
  • When humanity's demand is bigger than the supply, that is overshoot.
Quick check
?What does biocapacity measure?
Mission 1

Carbon is the biggest part

  • The carbon part of the footprint is the area of forest needed to absorb CO₂ from burning fossil fuels.
  • It makes up 61% of humanity's ecological footprint.
  • Halving CO₂ from fossil fuels would move Earth Overshoot Day later by three months.
Mission 1

Earth Overshoot Day

  • The date when humanity has used all the nature that Earth can regrow that year.
  • In 2026 it fell on 30 July: humanity used nature as fast as 1.73 Earths could supply.
  • The first overshoot day, as now calculated, was 31 December 1972.
Quick check
?Humanity uses 1.73 Earths. The overshoot day falls after 365 ÷ (number of Earths) days. On about which day of the year does it fall?
Mission 1

Countries compare

  • A Country Overshoot Day is the date Earth Overshoot Day would fall if everyone lived like that country's people.
  • 2026 dates: Qatar 4 February; United States 14 March; United Kingdom 22 May; China 27 May.
  • India has no overshoot day: if everyone lived like the average Indian, humanity would use 75% of Earth's biocapacity.
  • Pattern: high-income countries overshoot earliest. Of the 13 countries with no overshoot day, none is high income.
Mission 1

Three scales

  • Individual: an online calculator estimates your own footprint.
  • National: per person, or for the whole country.
  • Global: humanity's total against Earth's biocapacity.
  • A country with a small footprint per person but a huge population can still have a large total. Say which you mean.
Mission 1

What it cannot show

  • Its authors say it is "not a full assessment of sustainability".
  • It does not measure how badly land is damaged, and it cannot capture every kind of consumption, so some demand is left out.
  • Fresh water is tracked by a separate measure, the water footprint.
  • Exam move: define the ecological footprint, give an example (Qatar vs India), then say one limitation.
Mission check

?In 2026 the United Kingdom's Country Overshoot Day was 22 May and China's was 27 May. What do these two dates show?

The dates measure the ecological footprint against biocapacity. If everyone lived like Qatar's residents, a year's supply would be gone by 4 February; like the UK's, by 22 May; like India's, only 75% would be used. Carbon is 61% of humanity's footprint.

Mission 2

Mission 2: China imported about ten times as many soybeans in 2023 as in 2000. In 2025, nearly three in four came from Brazil. Why does what people eat in China matter to land in Brazil?

Make a guess: China imported about ten times as many soybeans in 2023 as in 2000. In 2025, nearly three in four came from Brazil. Why does what people eat in China matter to land in Brazil?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.1 links between global interdependence, consumerism and ecosystem destruction in the TRF and one other biome (e.g. palm oil, biofuel)
Mission 2

Changing diets

  • As incomes rise and people move to cities, diets change: more meat, dairy and processed food.
  • Meat supply per person in mainland China: 3.3 kg (1961) → 73.5 kg (2023).
  • Viet Nam: 41.0 kg (2010) → 60.5 kg (2023).
  • This is consumerism: rising demand for more, and more varied, goods.
Quick check
?Which change is most likely to raise a country's demand for animal feed?
Mission 2

From diet to soybeans

  • The US Department of Agriculture calls China "the world's largest importer of soybeans".
  • It links this to "dietary shifts toward more animal proteins", creating "a strong demand for soybean meal used for livestock feed".
  • China's soybean imports: about 10.4 million tonnes (2000) → 103.4 million tonnes (2023).
  • In 2025 Brazil supplied 73.6%, the USA 15.0% and Argentina 7.1%.
  • This is interdependence: diets in one country depend on land in another.
Mission 2

Soy and the savanna

  • South America's soybean area more than doubled: 26.4 → 55.1 million ha (2000–2019).
  • "Most soybean expansion occurred on pastures originally converted from natural vegetation for cattle production".
  • But "9% of forest loss was converted to soybean by 2016", and nearly half of soy-driven deforestation was in Brazil's Cerrado.
  • The Cerrado is a tropical savanna biome.
Mission 2

Palm oil and the rainforest

  • Indonesia and Malaysia produce more than 80% of the world's palm oil.
  • About 68% of palm oil goes into food, 27% into industrial and consumer products, and 5% into biofuels.
  • In Southeast Asia, 45% of sampled oil palm plantations were on land that was forest in 1989.
  • The forests still suitable for new plantations hold many mammal and bird species "at risk of extinction".
Quick check
?Which statement best links consumers to ecosystem loss?
Mission 2

Exam move: build the chain

  • Link each step: consumerism (more meat, more processed food) → global interdependence (trade, TNCs) → ecosystem destruction (clearing).
  • Name two biomes: tropical rainforest (palm oil, Southeast Asia) and tropical savanna (soy, Cerrado).
  • Use one figure for each.
Mission check

?Between 2010 and 2023, meat supply per person in Viet Nam rose by almost half. Why might this matter to farmland on another continent?

Rising incomes mean more meat; livestock eat soybean meal. China's soybean imports rose about tenfold, and most came from Brazil, where soy spread onto old pasture and into the Cerrado: global interdependence.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Transporting the world's fruit and vegetables releases almost twice as much greenhouse gas as growing them. So is "buy local" the answer?

Make a guess: Transporting the world's fruit and vegetables releases almost twice as much greenhouse gas as growing them. So is "buy local" the answer?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.1 consumerism's environmental impacts via agri-business and transport (food miles, out-of-season produce)
Mission 3

Consumerism and food

  • Consumerism: buying more goods, and expecting choice all year round.
  • In UK shops, food from all over the world is on sale in every season.
  • In 2025 the UK imported £15,049 million of fruit and vegetables and exported £967 million.
  • Meeting this demand needs long supply chains: farms, packhouses, ships, lorries and planes.
Mission 3

Food miles

  • Food miles: the distance food travels from where it is grown to where it is eaten.
  • One study estimates that moving food produces about 3.0 billion tonnes of CO₂-equivalent a year: about 19% of all emissions from the food system.
  • The other 81% comes from the rest of the food system, such as farming and land use.
Mission 3

Out-of-season produce

  • In 2023 UK farms produced 16% of the fresh fruit and 53% of the fresh vegetables supplied in the UK (by value).
  • The fruit share is low partly because of "consumer demand for tropical and out-of-season fruit which cannot be sourced domestically or from Europe".
  • The UK's largest fresh-fruit suppliers in 2023 were Spain (16%) and South Africa (13%).
Mission 3

Tomatoes through the year

  • In summer, the Netherlands was the largest supplier of imported tomatoes, when UK production is also at its greatest.
  • In winter, UK and Dutch supply fell and was replaced mainly by Spain and Morocco.
  • So the source of the same food changes with the season.
Quick check
?Why do UK shops sell South African fruit in the British winter?
Mission 3

Where transport matters most

  • For fruit and vegetables, freight contributes 36% of food-miles emissions: "almost twice the amount of greenhouse gases released during their production".
  • The study's authors call for more plant-based diets "coupled with more locally produced items, mainly in affluent countries".
  • But for most foods, what is produced and how matter more: impacts "can vary 50-fold among producers of the same product".
Mission 3

Seasonal beats local, except by air

  • Defra: producing food out of season "can substantially increase the GHG footprint"; importing from where it is in season is "often preferable".
  • Field crops have a "significantly lower" warming effect than crops from heated greenhouses.
  • So "'Seasonal' is … a more important criteria than 'local'", "except for air freighting food, as this adds considerably to its carbon footprint".
Quick check
?In January, which tomato is likely to have the lower carbon footprint for a UK shopper?
Mission 3

Agri-business

  • Agri-business: farming run as a large-scale business, often linked to processors and supermarkets.
  • Typical features: large fields of one crop, machinery, fertilisers and pesticides, heated glasshouses, and contracts to supply shops all year.
  • Agriculture produced about 12% of the UK's greenhouse gas emissions in 2022.
Mission 3

Agri-business and the environment

  • Water pollution: in England only 45% of groundwater bodies were classed as good in 2019; nitrate is the most common cause of failure.
  • Energy: growing tomatoes, cucumbers and peppers under glass needs heating; when energy costs soared, many growers delayed or reduced planting.
  • Exporting countries: imports have impacts where food is grown, with trade-offs in land use and water use.
Quick check
?Which is an environmental impact of agri-business rather than of transport?
Mission check

?A UK school canteen wants to cut the carbon footprint of its salads in January. Which plan best fits the evidence?

Not simply. Transport is about 19% of food-system emissions, but for fruit and vegetables it outweighs growing them. Defra finds that seasonal matters more than local, except for air-freighted food. How food is grown (agri-business, heated glasshouses, fertiliser) also shapes its impact.

Mission 4

Mission 4: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

Make a guess: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.1 environmental impacts of waste disposal, including e-waste effects on people and environment
Mission 4

Where waste goes

  • Disposal options: landfill, incineration (burning), recycling, export, or dumping.
  • Only about 9% of all plastic waste had been recycled (estimate cited in 2018).
  • About 80% had been landfilled or had ended up in the environment.
Mission 4

The waste hierarchy

  • Five steps, "ranked according to environmental impact". Best first: 1. Prevention: use less material; keep products longer. 2. Preparing for re-use: check, clean, repair, refurbish. 3. Recycling: turn waste into a new product, including composting. 4. Other recovery: e.g. incineration with energy recovery. 5. Disposal: landfill, and incineration without energy recovery.
Quick check
?A council sends black-bag rubbish to a plant that burns it and uses the heat to generate electricity. Where is this on the waste hierarchy?
Mission 4

Impacts of landfill and burning

  • Landfill: rotting waste gives off methane, a greenhouse gas; rainwater seeps through and carries pollutants (leachate) into soil and groundwater.
  • Incineration: releases gases and ash that must be cleaned and stored.
  • Leakage: plastic that escapes ends up in rivers and seas, harming wildlife.
  • About 80% of all plastic waste had been landfilled or had leaked into the environment.
Mission 4

Exporting waste, and China's ban

  • "Upward of half of the plastic waste intended for recycling has been exported".
  • China had taken a cumulative 45% of the world's plastic waste imports since 1992.
  • In 2017 China announced a policy "permanently banning the import of nonindustrial plastic waste".
  • Imports reported (thousand tonnes), 2016 → 2018: China 8,801.6 → 51.4 (UN Comtrade, all plastic scrap, weight estimated; a study using the same UN database gives 7.35 million tonnes for 2016); Malaysia 287.7 → 872.5; Thailand 69.5 → 552.7; Türkiye 152.9 → 424.6.
Quick check
?Malaysia imported 287.7 thousand tonnes of plastic waste in 2016 and 872.5 thousand tonnes in 2018. About how many times as much did it import in 2018?
Mission 4

E-waste: a fast-growing problem

  • E-waste: thrown-away electrical and electronic items, from phones to fridges.
  • 2022: a record 62 billion kg, about 7.8 kg per person.
  • Only 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled.
  • Europe generated 17.6 kg per person and recycled 42.8%; in Africa, less than 1% was documented as formally recycled.
Mission 4

E-waste: effects on people and the environment

  • An estimated 3.3 billion kg went from high-income to middle- and low-income countries "through uncontrolled and undocumented transboundary movements" (2022).
  • People: the WHO says informal e-waste recycling "may have several adverse health effects"; children and pregnant women are "particularly vulnerable".
  • Environment: "Lead is a common substance released into the environment when e-waste is recycled, stored or dumped".
  • Burning cables and dumping in open ground pollute air, soil and water.
Quick check
?Why is informal e-waste recycling especially harmful?
Mission 4

Moving up the hierarchy

  • Circular economy: "a system where materials never become waste and nature is regenerated".
  • International rules: Basel Convention controls on plastic waste took effect on 1 January 2021; from 1 January 2025 all e-waste needs "prior informed consent".
  • The EU bans plastic waste exports to non-OECD countries from 21 November 2026.
Mission check

?A country stops accepting imports of used electronics. What is the most likely result for the countries that used to send them?

China banned most plastic waste imports (announced 2017), so its imports fell to 51.4 thousand tonnes while Malaysia's tripled. Waste moves unless it is reduced or reused: the top of the waste hierarchy.

Mission 5

Mission 5: England has had three droughts in five years, yet UK records show part of the year getting wetter. How can both be true?

Make a guess: England has had three droughts in five years, yet UK records show part of the year getting wetter. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.2 short- and long-term effects of climate change on people, economy and environment
  • 8.1.2 effects of climate change in the UK
  • 8.1.2 alternative futures for these locations (e.g. extreme-event frequency, changing seasons)
Mission 5

The UK is already warmer

  • 2025 was the UK's warmest year in a series from 1884; the last four years are all in the top five.
  • 2016–2025 was 1.33 °C warmer than 1961–1990.
  • July 2022: 40.3 °C at Coningsby, Lincolnshire: the UK's first temperature above 40 °C.
  • The Met Office: without human-induced climate change, 40 °C in the UK "would be virtually impossible".
Mission 5

Changing weather patterns

  • Wetter winters: October–March in 2016–2025 was 13% wetter than in 1961–1990.
  • More very wet months: October–March months with double the old average rainfall have about doubled.
  • More heat extremes: in Greater London, days over 30 °C and nights over 18 °C have more than quadrupled since 1961–1990.
  • Higher seas: UK sea level is up about 20 cm since 1901; two-thirds of that rise came in the last three decades.
Mission 5

Changing seasons

  • Summers swing to drought: England had droughts in 2022, 2025 and 2026.
  • July 2026 was the driest July in 190 years.
  • 2025 was the UK's sunniest year (from 1910), with its sunniest spring.
  • Living things respond: in 2025, some UK trees and shrubs had their highest seed yields on record (since 2001), likely linked to the warm, dry, sunny spring and summer.
  • Snow is set to fade: lying snow is projected to fall by almost 100% over much of the UK by 2100.
Quick check
?England's winters are getting wetter, yet it has had three droughts in five years. Which explanation fits the evidence best?
Mission 5

Effects on people

  • Health: summer 2022's heatwaves caused an estimated 2,803 excess deaths among people aged 65+ in England, the highest since 2004.
  • Water: in August 2026, 45 million people lived in a drought area; over 27 million were under hosepipe bans.
  • Homes: rising seas threaten coasts; heavier summer downpours are projected to increase surface water flooding.
Mission 5

Effects on industry

  • Farming: in 2026 farmers reported their earliest harvests in 20 years, with lower yields; poor grass growth meant feeding winter stocks early.
  • Irrigation: 1,534 irrigation restrictions were in place in East Anglia.
  • Water companies: hosepipe bans in eight company areas; the government plans nine new reservoirs and transfer schemes.
  • Transport: 26 canal closures because of low water.
Mission 5

Effects on the environment

  • Rivers: in August 2026, only 14% of river flows were normal; some rivers reached 27–28 °C, about 2 °C higher than expected.
  • Wildlife: breeding birds, amphibians and white-clawed crayfish were affected.
  • Wildfire: 245 wildfires on protected sites (SSSIs) by August 2026.
  • Seas: 297 marine heatwave days around north-west Europe in 2025, a record.
Quick check
?Which effect of the 2026 drought was mainly an effect on the economy?
Mission 5

Short-term and long-term effects

  • Short term (days to months): heatwave deaths, hosepipe bans and canal closures, wildfires.
  • Long term (decades): rising seas, wetter winters and drier summers, wildlife that has "not fully recovered from previous droughts".
  • Exam move: for each effect, say who or what is affected (people, economy, environment) and whether it is short or long term.
Mission 5

Alternative futures

  • The future depends on how much the world emits. UKCP18 projects the UK's climate under low and high emission scenarios.
  • Seasons: warmer, wetter winters and hotter, drier summers.
  • Hot summers: the chance of a summer as hot as 2018 could rise to about 50–60% by mid-century.
  • Extremes: under high emissions, hot spells (30 °C+ for two days or more) rise from about 0.2 a year to 4.1 a year by 2070.
  • Sea level, London, by 2100: 0.29–0.70 m (low emissions); 0.53–1.15 m (high emissions).
Quick check
?Under UKCP18, London's sea level by 2100 is very likely to rise 0.29–0.70 m with low emissions and 0.53–1.15 m with high emissions. What does this show?
Mission check

?A farmer in eastern England is planning for the 2050s. Which pattern of rainfall should she plan for, based on UK projections?

Rain is shifting towards winter (the October–March half-year is 13% wetter than in 1961–1990), while hot, dry summers bring droughts like those of 2022, 2025 and 2026. When summer rain does come, UKCP18 projects heavier downpours, so drought and flash flooding can hit the same farm.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Rotterdam to Yokohama is about 11,200 nautical miles via Suez, but about 6,500 over the top of the world. Will melting ice make the Arctic a new shipping highway?

Make a guess: Rotterdam to Yokohama is about 11,200 nautical miles via Suez, but about 6,500 over the top of the world. Will melting ice make the Arctic a new shipping highway?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.2 short- and long-term effects of climate change on people, economy and environment
  • 8.1.2 effects in one contrasting environment (the Arctic)
  • 8.1.2 alternative futures for this location (e.g. extreme-event frequency, changing seasons)
Mission 6

The fastest-warming region

  • Since 1979 the Arctic has warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • Snow-covered sea ice reflects up to 90% of sunlight; open ocean reflects only 6%.
  • As ice melts, darker sea absorbs more heat, which melts more ice: a positive feedback.
Mission 6

Shrinking summer sea ice

  • In September 2026, Arctic sea ice shrank to 4.60 million km², tied for the 10th-lowest on record.
  • The record low was 3.39 million km², in 2012.
  • The September minimum is shrinking by 11.8% per decade compared with the 1981–2010 average.
Quick check
?Why does melting sea ice speed up Arctic warming?
Mission 6

Effects on the environment

  • Arctic June snow cover on land shrank by about 13% per decade from 1967 to 2018, a loss of about 2.5 million km².
  • Satellites show the tundra "greening" as shrubs spread.
  • Permafrost (frozen ground) temperatures have risen to record highs since the 1980s.
Mission 6

Effects on people

  • Shishmaref, Alaska, a village on a barrier island: summer sea ice has become "much more unpredictable" since the 1980s.
  • About 7 m of the island washes into the sea each year; residents have voted three times to relocate.
  • Saami reindeer herders report thawing ground and "rotten spring vegetation" in summer grazing areas since the mid-1990s.
Mission 6

Effects on the economy

  • Shipping: ships entering the Arctic Polar Code area rose 37% from 2013 to 2023; the distance they sailed rose 111%.
  • Oil and gas: the USGS estimated about 90 billion barrels of undiscovered oil and 1,669 trillion cubic feet of gas north of the Arctic Circle, about 84% of it offshore.
  • These are estimates of undiscovered resources, not proven reserves.
  • Less sea ice could make some of them easier to reach; burning them would add to warming.
Quick check
?Rotterdam to Yokohama is 11,200 nautical miles via Suez and 6,500 across the Arctic. By what percentage is the Arctic route shorter?
Mission 6

Short-term and long-term effects

  • Short term (seasons and years): longer ice-free shipping seasons; less predictable ice for hunters and villages.
  • Long term (decades and centuries): permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 billion tonnes of carbon, almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere.
  • Carbon lost when permafrost thaws cannot be recovered for centuries. Its release would add to warming: another positive feedback.
Quick check
?Which is a long-term effect of climate change in the Arctic?
Mission 6

Alternative futures

  • Changing seasons: the IPCC projects the Arctic is likely to be practically ice-free in September at least once before 2050, under all five of its illustrative emissions scenarios.
  • Vegetation: shrubs and trees are projected to cover 24–52% of the tundra by 2050.
  • Ice stays in winter: the Arctic Council expects sea ice to be present for most of each year this century.
  • Could it recover? Summer sea ice changes roughly in step with global temperature; the IPCC finds "no tipping point", so losses are potentially reversible if warming stops.
Quick check
?Which future for the Arctic Ocean is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?A shipping firm plans weekly container sailings from Europe to East Asia across the Arctic from 2030. Which plan is best supported by the evidence?

The Arctic route is about 42% shorter, and shipping there is growing (ships up 37%, distance sailed up 111%, 2013–2023). But sea ice is expected for most of each year this century, so for now it is a seasonal route, not a new highway.

Mission 7

Mission 7: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Make a guess: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.1.2 role of individuals and government in adopting new technologies and lifestyles to cut emissions
  • 8.1.2 differing values and attitudes about these changes
  • 8.1.2 local, national and international responses to climate change
Mission 7

Two kinds of response

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes of climate change, by cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewable energy, planting forests).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to the effects of climate change to reduce harm (sea walls, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Responses happen at international, national and local scales, and by individuals.
Mission 7

The Paris Agreement, 2015

  • Adopted on 12 December 2015; in force from 4 November 2016.
  • Aim: hold warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels", and try to limit it to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), and updates it every five years.
  • 194 Parties. The United States' withdrawal took effect on 27 January 2026.
Mission 7

Who cuts, and who pays?

  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • The least developed countries produced less than 0.4% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
  • Finance: rich countries' goal of US$100 billion a year was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion). In 2024 a new goal was set: US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Fund for responding to Loss and Damage helps developing countries that are particularly vulnerable.
Quick check
?What is a nationally determined contribution (NDC)?
Mission 7

Is Paris working?

  • Strengths: almost every country has joined; every Party must set and update targets.
  • Limits: targets are self-chosen. UNEP (2025): if all NDCs are met, warming this century of 2.3–2.5 °C; current policies alone, up to 2.8 °C.
  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C). The Paris goal is judged over decades, not one year.
  • The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂; 2025 emissions were about 38.1 billion tonnes from fossil fuels plus 4.1 billion from land-use change.
Quick check
?The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂. In 2025, about 38.1 billion tonnes came from fossil fuels and 4.1 billion from land-use change. At 2025 levels, about how many years would the budget last?
Mission 7

A developed country: the UK

  • Government: the Climate Change Act 2008 made cuts a legal duty: at least 80% by 2050 (from 1990), raised to 100% (net zero) in 2019.
  • It set five-year carbon budgets and an independent Committee on Climate Change.
  • Results: 2025 emissions were 54% below 1990 (provisional); the last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Hardest to cut: domestic transport, 31% of net emissions in 2025.
Mission 7

Local action, individuals and technology

  • Technology: from 2010 to 2019, the cost of solar energy fell by 85%, wind by 55% and lithium-ion batteries by 85%.
  • Local: in Germany, 220,000 members of 951 energy cooperatives have invested about €3.6 billion in renewable energy.
  • Individuals: choices about travel, home heating, diet and what to buy change a person's carbon footprint.
  • UK organisations and individuals: the Energy Saving Trust advises householders to insulate, because "about a third of all the heat lost in an uninsulated home escapes through the walls".
  • Government sets the rules that make the low-carbon choice easier: standards, taxes and grants.
Mission 7

Different values and attitudes

  • In a 2024 UNDP survey of 77 countries, 80% of people wanted their government to take stronger climate action.
  • Among large emitters, support ranged from 66% (United States, Russia) to 93% (Italy).
  • In small island developing states, 71% said they were more worried than the year before, against 53% worldwide.
  • Views differ with what people stand to lose: homes from rising seas, or jobs in coal and oil.
Quick check
?Which is a local response to climate change?
Mission check

?Country X is a low-income country with tiny historical emissions and worsening coastal floods. Under the Paris Agreement, what is expected of it, and from others?

The agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries take the lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still fall short: UNEP projects 2.3–2.8 °C.

Mission 8

Mission 8: Since 1970 the world has lost about 22% of its wetlands. Can a plan for a whole river basin bring a wetland back?

Make a guess: Since 1970 the world has lost about 22% of its wetlands. Can a plan for a whole river basin bring a wetland back?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.2.1 strategies to restore habitats damaged by human activity, including wetland restoration
Mission 8

Wetlands under pressure

  • Wetlands: land covered by shallow water or waterlogged for much of the year, e.g. marshes, swamps, bogs, floodplains.
  • Since 1970 about 22% of the world's wetlands have been lost, and the loss continues at 0.52% a year.
  • Causes: draining for farms and building, pollution, dams and canals that change water flows, and climate change.
  • Wetlands store flood water, store carbon and clean water.
Mission 8

How to restore a damaged habitat

  • Give the water back: block drains, remove or open dams and sluices, release water at the right times.
  • Restore the river's shape: refill straight canals and reconnect old bends, so water spreads onto the floodplain again.
  • Cut pollution: treat run-off from farms and towns.
  • Remove invasive species and replant native plants.
  • Protect the site by law, e.g. listing it under the Ramsar Convention on wetlands (1971).
Quick check
?A marsh dried out after farmers dug ditches to drain it. Which strategy would do most to restore it?
Mission 8

Re-meandering a river: the Kissimmee, Florida

  • The Kissimmee River "once meandered for 103 miles" across a wide floodplain.
  • After floods in 1947, it was dug into a straight canal, 30 feet deep, in the 1960s.
  • This "destroyed much of a floodplain-dependent ecosystem".
  • Restoration filled in 22 miles of the canal and reconnected old river channels, restoring 44 miles of the historic river and 20,000 acres of wetlands; construction was completed in July 2021.
Mission 8

A whole-basin plan: the Everglades

  • Water once "flowed slowly from the Kissimmee River to Florida Bay", forming a "River of Grass".
  • Canals and levees drained land for farms and homes. Results: polluted water; in drier areas, peat that burns in wildfires; in wetter areas, lost tree islands.
  • The Comprehensive Everglades Restoration Plan (2000) aims to restore the "quantity, quality, timing, and distribution" of water.
  • It has 68 parts, including a reservoir to store and clean water from Lake Okeechobee.
Mission 8

Water for wetlands: the Murray–Darling, Australia

  • The Murray–Darling Basin Plan aims to recover 2,750 GL of water a year for the environment, about a 20% cut in water for farms and towns.
  • The water recovered has "improved river flows, connectivity, and ecosystem and biodiversity outcomes".
  • But the Plan will not be fully implemented on time or within budget, and buying water back too fast puts pressure on farming communities.
Quick check
?Why can the Everglades only be restored by managing the whole basin?
Mission 8

Weighing up restoration

  • Environmental: habitats, wildlife and flood storage return, but slowly.
  • Economic: costly; the Everglades plan has received billions of dollars.
  • Social: farmers may lose land or water; other people gain cleaner water, fewer floods and places to visit.
  • Restoration works best when the whole basin is planned together.
Quick check
?Which statement best weighs the Murray–Darling Basin Plan?
Mission check

?A river's floodplain marshes were drained for farming and the river was straightened. A plan is drawn up to restore them. Which plan is most likely to work?

Wetlands depend on water from the whole basin. The Kissimmee and Everglades plans and the Murray–Darling Plan show restoration is possible, but slow, costly and contested.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Make a guess: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 8.2.1 managing tourism to reduce its impacts on the environment and water resources of two contrasting environments, incl. tropical coastline/coral reefs
  • 8.2.1 making tourism more sustainable and conservation-positive; ecotourism, ethical tourism and responsible travel
Mission 9

Mass tourism and carrying capacity

  • Mass tourism: very large numbers of tourists visiting the same places, often on package holidays.
  • Carrying capacity: the most visitors a place can take before unacceptable damage occurs.
  • Damage can be environmental (coral, wildlife, litter), cultural (residents pushed out, traditions sold as shows) or felt as overcrowding.
Mission 9

Impacts of mass tourism

  • Economic +: jobs and income. The industry body WTTC estimates tourism supported 366 million jobs worldwide in 2025. Economic −: low-paid seasonal work; profits leak abroad; rising prices for residents.
  • Cultural +: money to restore heritage; interest in local crafts. Cultural −: in Venice, UNESCO reports homes replaced by tourist accommodation.
  • Environmental +: entry fees can fund protected areas. Environmental −: Maya Bay's coral was damaged by thousands of daily visitors.
Quick check
?In a historic city, flats are turned into holiday lets and long-term residents move away. What kind of impact is this?
Mission 9

Maya Bay, Thailand: a tropical coast

  • A bay on the uninhabited island of Phi Phi Leh, in a national park in Krabi province.
  • It became world-famous after the 1999 film The Beach. At its peak in 2017, almost 5,000 people a day visited.
  • It closed in June 2018, "when it was determined the area's coral and other marine life needed time to recover" from the damage caused by thousands of daily tourists.
  • Thailand is upper-middle income.
Mission 9

Managing Maya Bay

  • Closure: announced for four months from 1 June 2018, then extended; a plan to reopen on 1 January 2022 came after about three and a half years.
  • A new jetty on the far side of the island: boats must land there, not in the bay. Experts advised this was the only way for the coral to keep recovering.
  • Controlled daily numbers, a marked swimming area, and coral and beach trees replanted.
  • Dispersal: Thailand's tourism authority promoted Krabi's other islands while the bay was shut.
Mission 9

Venice, Italy: an urban lagoon

  • UNESCO: "The exceptionally high tourism pressure on the city of Venice has resulted in a partial functional transformation".
  • About 53,000 residents lived in the historic centre in 2018; on average 66,800 visitors and tourists were there each day (year not stated).
  • That is about 1.26 visitors per resident every day. Day-trippers alone: almost 22 million a year.
  • Italy is high income.
Mission 9

Managing Venice

  • Cruise ships: UNESCO's World Heritage Committee urged Italy in 2014 to ban large ships from the lagoon. In 2021 the Italian government banned large cruise ships from the Venice Lagoon.
  • Access fee: on set dates in 2026, day visitors to the historic city had to pay a fee unless exempt. The 2026 trial ended; from 27 July the fee no longer applied.
  • Both aim to cut pressure on the lagoon and the city without stopping tourism.
Quick check
?Which measure most directly protected Maya Bay's coral after it reopened?
Mission 9

Who can make tourism more sustainable?

  • Sustainable tourism takes "full account of its current and future economic, social and environmental impacts", meeting the needs of visitors, industry, environment and host communities (UN Tourism).
  • Individuals: follow site rules, keep off coral, visit out of season, use locally owned businesses.
  • Organisations: Thailand's national parks department and tourism authority (Maya Bay); UNESCO pressing Italy over cruise ships (Venice).
  • Governments: Italy's ban on large cruise ships (2021) and Venice's access fee (city council); Thailand closing the bay and limiting daily numbers.
Mission 9

Ecotourism, ethical tourism, responsible travel

  • Ecotourism: "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education"; it should "provide direct financial benefits for conservation".
  • Ethical tourism: choosing trips that treat workers, communities and wildlife fairly.
  • Responsible travel: the visitor's own choices, such as respecting rules and local culture and cutting waste.
  • Conservation-positive example: in Rwanda, from 2017, 10% of national park tourism revenue was to go to communities next to the parks.
Quick check
?Which of these is an action by individuals to make tourism more sustainable?
Mission 9

Evaluating strategies at different scales

  • Site (local): closure and a jetty let Maya Bay's coral and land ecosystems recover, but income from the site stopped while it was shut.
  • City: Venice's access fee raises money and may deter some day-trippers, but it was a trial and can exclude poorer visitors.
  • National: Italy's cruise-ship ban protects the lagoon, but moves ships and their spending elsewhere.
  • Global: UNESCO and UN Tourism set principles; they rely on governments to act.
  • Water resources: tourists, hotels and pools add demand for fresh water and produce waste water. Ways to manage this: water-saving fittings, treating waste water before it is released, and capping visitor numbers.
  • Exam move: evaluate each strategy by who gains, who loses, and whether it protects the place long term.
Mission check

?A national park caps a famous mountain trail at a fixed number of walkers a day, even though many more want to come and would pay. Why would it turn paying visitors away?

Maya Bay passed its carrying capacity: almost 5,000 visitors a day in 2017 damaged its coral. Closing it, then reopening with a jetty and controlled daily numbers, let it recover. Venice faces the same pressure in a city, managed by a cruise-ship ban and an access fee.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ecological footprint: the productive land and sea needed for what we consume and to absorb our carbon, in global hectares.
  • Humanity uses about 1.73 Earths. High-income countries overshoot earliest; carbon is the largest part.

Mission 2

  • Consumption changed as incomes and cities grew.
  • Consumerism and interdependence link shoppers to the loss of rainforest (palm oil) and savanna (soy).

Mission 3

  • Consumerism brings out-of-season produce from around the world: food miles are about 19% of food-system emissions, and more for fruit and vegetables.
  • "Seasonal beats local, except by air"; agri-business adds impacts through fertiliser, energy and water use.

Mission 4

  • The waste hierarchy ranks prevention, re-use, recycling, other recovery and disposal, best first.
  • Landfill, burning and dumping harm soil, water and air; informal e-waste recycling releases toxins such as lead and harms workers' health.

Mission 5

  • The UK is warmer (2016–2025: +1.33 °C on 1961–1990), with wetter winters, more heat extremes, rising seas and summer droughts.
  • Effects reach people (heat deaths, water bans), industry (farming, water, transport) and the environment (rivers, wildlife, wildfire).
  • Alternative futures depend on emissions: e.g. London's sea level by 2100, 0.29–0.70 m (low) or 0.53–1.15 m (high).

Mission 6

  • The Arctic is warming nearly four times as fast as the globe; summer sea ice is shrinking.
  • Effects: environment (snow, tundra, permafrost), people (Shishmaref, Saami herders), economy (shipping, oil and gas), short and long term.
  • Alternative futures: ice-free Septembers are likely before 2050, winter ice remains, and the outcome depends on how much the world warms.

Mission 7

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm.
  • Paris (2015): every Party sets an NDC; aim well below 2 °C, ideally 1.5 °C. Strength: nearly universal. Weakness: self-chosen targets that fall short.
  • Action happens locally (cooperatives), nationally (laws) and internationally (Paris); people's views differ with what they stand to lose.

Mission 8

  • Wetlands are lost to drainage, dams, pollution and climate change; about 22% lost since 1970.
  • Restore them by giving back water, reconnecting rivers, cutting pollution and protecting sites; plan across the whole basin.

Mission 9

  • Mass tourism brings jobs and income but can damage environments and push out residents: positive and negative economic, cultural and environmental impacts.
  • Maya Bay (Thailand, upper-middle income; tropical coast and coral): closure, jetty, daily limits. Venice (Italy, high income; urban lagoon): cruise-ship ban, access fee.
  • Ecotourism, ethical tourism and responsible travel can make tourism conservation-positive.
