Mission 1

How to apply situation ethics

  • 1. The situation: what exactly is happening, and what is at stake?
  • 2. The people: who is affected? (personalism: persons, not rules, come first.)
  • 3. The rules: what guidance does the community's experience give? (useful, not binding.)
  • 4. Love's verdict: which choice brings the most agape to everyone affected? (pragmatism, relativism, with love as the one absolute.)
  • Fletcher's answers can differ from case to case, by design.
  • Application to theft and lying; to life-and-death issues: –.
Mission 1

Fletcher's own case: Mrs Bergmeier

  • From Fletcher's book (paraphrase): at the end of the Second World War, a German mother is held in a Soviet camp in Ukraine. Her husband and children, back in Berlin, are searching for her.
  • Only the sick and the pregnant are being released. She asks a guard to make her pregnant; she is released and reunited with her family.
  • The family welcome the baby, and love him for what he did for them.
  • Fletcher asks: did she do right? The rule against adultery is set beside the good of her husband, her children and the new child.
  • Fletcher's point is the question, not a fixed answer: legalism cannot even see the dilemma.

Mission 1

Situation ethics and utilitarianism

  • Similar: both are teleological and consequentialist; both judge acts case by case; both reject fixed rules as final.
  • Fletcher borrows the utilitarian procedure: an "agapeic calculus", seeking the greatest amount of neighbour welfare for the largest number of neighbours (paraphrase).
  • Different: the good maximised is agape, not pleasure or happiness.
  • Different: Fletcher's starting point is a faith commitment (positivism); utilitarianism claims to rest on observable facts about pleasure and pain.
Mission 1Read the source

Barclay's critique

William Barclay (1907–1978), Scottish New Testament scholar, Ethics in a Permissive Society (1971), chapter 4 (paraphrase):

  • Agrees: love is central; rigid legalism is wrong; morality needs freedom.
  • Extreme cases: most of Fletcher's examples are extraordinary. Ordinary life needs ordinary rules; hard cases do not show we can do without law.
  • Too much freedom: deciding everything alone, with no ready answer, is a burden most people are not ready to carry.
  • People need law: as a child is trained by habit, people need rules; without love, freedom easily becomes licence.
  • Grace is missing: Fletcher says little about God's power to change people.

Q1 What does Barclay accept from Fletcher?

Show answer

Love is central; legalism is wrong.

Q2 Why does he think law is still needed?

Show answer

Most people do not want total freedom; rules train us, and freedom without love becomes licence.

Mission 1

Is Fletcher's agape really religious?

  • Religious, yes: agape comes from the New Testament (1 Corinthians 13; Luke 10); positivism means accepting "God is love" by faith.
  • Not really: the method never needs God; love could mean "wanting the best for the person involved". A humanist could use it unchanged.
  • Fletcher himself later left Christian belief and was named Humanist of the Year by the American Humanist Association (1974).
  • Individualistic and subjective? Without absolute rules, each person decides alone; two people may reach opposite "loving" answers.
  • Reply: love is not whatever I feel; it is judged by its effects on others, and community rules still guide.
Mission 1Diversity

How churches responded

  • Catholic: Pope Pius XII criticised "situation ethics" in 1952, and the Holy Office (1956) forbade it in Catholic teaching; Veritatis Splendor (1993) teaches that some acts are always wrong.
  • Protestant critics: Paul Ramsey, Deeds and Rules in Christian Ethics (1967): love itself needs rules ("rule agapism").
  • Liberal Protestants: Robinson and others welcomed the stress on persons over codes.
  • Evangelicals: generally reject it: scripture's commands are God's revealed will, not optional guides.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is situation ethics a helpful method?

  • Claim: Situation ethics is a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Person-centred; flexible in hard cases; avoids legalism's cruelty; one clear principle; rooted in Jesus' ethic of love.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; love's verdict is subjective; it can seem to justify adultery or killing (Barclay; Pius XII).
  • Against: It may promote immoral behaviour by those who rationalise their wishes as "love"; it may be humanism with Christian words.
  • Judgement criteria: Can ordinary people use it day to day? Is agape the only intrinsic good? Does it keep what religious rules protect? Does it need God?
Quick check

Mission 2

Character, not rules

  • Most theories ask: "What should I do?" Virtue ethics asks: "What kind of person should I be?"
  • It is agent-centred: it judges character, then acts as expressions of character.
  • A virtue (Greek aretē, "excellence") is a settled good quality of character: courage, honesty, generosity.
  • A good person reliably does good acts, for the right reasons, and wants to.
  • Source: Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics.
Mission 2

Eudaimonia and the human function

  • Every action aims at some good. The highest good is chosen for its own sake: eudaimonia.
  • Eudaimonia: flourishing, living well and doing well. Not a passing feeling of happiness.
  • Function argument (Book I): the function (ergon) of a human is what humans alone do: activity guided by reason.
  • So eudaimonia is rational activity in accordance with virtue, over a complete life (one fine day does not make a happy life).
  • It also needs friends and some external goods: health, enough wealth, a community.
Mission 2

Two kinds of virtue

  • Intellectual virtues: excellences of thinking, learned by teaching and experience.
  • The key one for ethics is phronesis (practical wisdom): seeing what to do in this situation. Others include sophia (wisdom) and technē (skill).
  • Moral virtues: excellences of character (courage, temperance, generosity, truthfulness), formed by habit.
  • Greek ēthos (character) comes from ethos (habit): Aristotle's own wordplay in Book II.
Mission 2Read the source

We become what we do

  • "And so by doing just acts we become just, and by doing acts of temperance and courage we become temperate and courageous." (Nicomachean Ethics II.1, Peters trans.)
  • Virtues are not in us by nature: a stone that naturally falls cannot be trained to rise. But we are able to acquire them.
  • Builders learn by building, lyre-players by playing. Character is trained the same way.
  • So upbringing makes "a great difference, or rather all the difference." (II.1, Peters trans.)

Q1 According to Aristotle, which comes first: just acts or a just character?

Show answer

Just acts, repeated until they become a settled character.

Q2 Why does the stone example matter?

Show answer

Mission 2

The golden mean

  • A moral virtue is a mean between two vices: one of deficiency, one of excess.
  • Courage: cowardice (deficiency of confidence) · courage · rashness (excess).
  • Temperance: insensibility · temperance · self-indulgence.
  • Generosity: meanness · generosity · prodigality (wasteful giving).
  • Truthfulness about oneself: mock-modesty · truthfulness · boastfulness.
  • The mean is relative to us: a meal too little for Milo the wrestler is too much for a beginner.
  • Feeling and acting "at the right times, about the right things, towards the right people, for the right end, and in the right way" (II.6, paraphrase).
Mission 2

Where there is no mean

  • "But it is not all actions nor all passions that admit of moderation; … as malevolence, shamelessness, envy, and, among acts, adultery, theft, murder." (II.6, Peters trans.)
  • These are wrong in themselves: there is no right amount of theft.
  • The mean is not an average: sometimes it lies nearer one extreme (courage is nearer rashness than cowardice).
  • So it is hard to be good: anyone can get angry; being angry with the right person, to the right degree, at the right time, is rare (II.9, paraphrase).
  • Say: "the mean is the right response, judged by reason" — not: "the mean is always a moderate amount".
Quick check
?A friend is publicly humiliated and Jo feels angry. Which response fits Aristotle's mean for anger?
Mission 2

Practical wisdom and role models

  • The mean is fixed "as determined by reason, i.e. by the reason by which the prudent man would determine it." (II.6, Peters trans.)
  • The phronimos (practically wise person) sees what each situation needs. Moral virtue and phronesis need each other.
  • Applying it: ask "What would a virtuous person do here?" (applied to theft and lying in Mission 4).
  • Rules of thumb help, but ethics is not an exact science (Book I): it fits the case.
Mission 2Worked answer

Explaining the mean

Task: explain Aristotle's doctrine of the mean.

  • Point: moral virtue is a settled state, a mean between a vice of deficiency and a vice of excess.
  • Evidence: courage lies between cowardice and rashness (NE II.7).
  • Develop: the mean is relative to us and found by practical wisdom; some acts (theft, adultery, murder) have no mean.
  • Link: this shows virtue ethics is about trained judgement, not mechanical rules.

Mission 3

Anscombe: the revival

  • G. E. M. Anscombe (1919–2001), "Modern Moral Philosophy" (Philosophy, 1958).
  • Modern ethics uses a "moral ought" that only makes sense in a law conception of ethics: a law needs a lawgiver (God).
  • Many modern thinkers have dropped belief in a divine lawgiver but kept the language of law: the "ought" has lost its meaning.
  • Her proposal: stop doing moral philosophy until we have an adequate philosophy of psychology, and return to Aristotle's concepts: virtue, flourishing, human nature.
  • She criticised consequentialism (her word) for allowing anything if the outcome is good enough.
  • (Anscombe was a Catholic; she thought the divine-law framework true, but argued the revival on Aristotelian grounds.)
Mission 3

Virtue ethics applied

  • Ask: what would a virtuous person do, and what would doing this make me?
  • Abortion: Rosalind Hursthouse (b. 1943, "Virtue Theory and Abortion", 1991) sets aside rights and asks whether a decision is callous, light-minded, or wise and responsible.
  • Euthanasia: compassion and justice may pull different ways; Foot (1977) argued that charity could favour some cases while justice limits them.
  • Animals: is intensive farming cruel, and what does it do to our character?
Quick check
?A virtue ethicist is asked whether a doctor should help a terminally ill patient to die. Which approach is most typical of virtue ethics?
Mission 3

Three challenges

  • Not a practical guide: "be virtuous" does not say what to do in a dilemma. Reply: the philosopher Rosalind Hursthouse: virtue terms give rules ("do what is honest, not what is cruel"), plus role models.
  • Cultural relativism: virtues differ by culture: Homeric warriors prized pride; Christians prize humility. Reply: Aristotle and the philosopher Philippa Foot ground virtues in shared human nature and needs.
  • Virtues used for immoral acts: a brave and loyal bank robber. Reply: Aristotle: virtue includes a right end chosen by practical wisdom; Philippa Foot: courage in a wrongful act does not operate as a virtue.
  • Also: circularity (a virtuous act is what a virtuous person does, and vice versa); moral luck in upbringing; whose role model?
  • Strengths: whole person; motives and emotions count; flexible; explains moral growth and education.
Quick check
?A hospice nurse must decide whether to tell a dying patient a painful truth. She asks, "What would a virtuous nurse do?" Her colleague replies: "That tells me nothing: honesty says tell her, kindness says wait." Which challenge to virtue ethics is the colleague raising?
Mission 3Evaluate

Is virtue ethics useful when faced with a moral dilemma?

  • Claim: Virtue ethics gives real help when facing a moral dilemma.
  • For: it attends to the particular case; practical wisdom sees what rules miss.
  • For: role models and virtue rules ("be honest, be kind") give guidance.
  • Against: in a true dilemma two virtues clash (honesty vs kindness); "do what the virtuous person would do" may not say which wins.
  • Against: in a crisis, the person without a formed character gets little immediate help.
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as "useful": a decision procedure, or a better decider? Is any theory more useful in real dilemmas?
Mission check

?For years a company director shows great patience, self-control and nerve while secretly defrauding pensioners. A critic says this proves that virtues can serve immoral ends. Which reply would Aristotle most likely give?

For Aristotle, a "virtue" aimed at a bad end is not a virtue at all; critics still ask whether that reply makes the theory hard to use in real dilemmas. Next: virtue ethics in action on theft and lying.

Mission 4

The cases

  • Theft: taking what belongs to another without consent. From shoplifting for fun to taking food in a famine.
  • Lying: saying what you believe to be false, intending to deceive. From a lie for gain to a "white lie" about a present, to a lie that protects someone.
  • Breaking a promise: failing to do what you freely bound yourself to do.
  • Adultery: a married person's sexual relationship with someone else: a broken promise of faithfulness (marriage vows).
  • Three theories: natural moral law (Aquinas), situation ethics (Fletcher), virtue ethics (Aristotle).
Mission 4

Natural law on lying

  • Aquinas (Summa Theologiae II-II, q.110): speech exists naturally to express what is in the mind. To say what you think false goes against the purpose of speech.
  • So every lie is a sin, even a helpful one. Aquinas ranks them: helpful and jocose (joking) lies are lesser (venial) sins; mischievous lies, which harm, are grave.
  • You may keep silent or prudently withhold the truth; you may not assert a falsehood.
  • Primary precept: live in an ordered society, which depends on trust.
  • Catholic teaching today agrees (Catechism 2482–2486); it adds that no one is bound to reveal the truth to someone without a right to know it (2489).
  • Proportionalists (e.g. Bernard Hoose) argue a lie may be justified for a proportionate reason.
Mission 4

Natural law on theft

  • "You shall not steal." (Exodus 20:15)
  • Theft breaks the precept of ordered society and the virtue of justice: it takes what is due to another.
  • But Aquinas (q.66, a.7): in urgent need, when there is no other remedy, taking what is necessary to survive is not theft: in need, all things are common (paraphrase).
  • Basis: God gave the earth's goods for everyone; private property serves that purpose.
  • Catholic teaching: the universal destination of goods (Catechism 2402–2408).
Quick check
?In a famine, a father with no other way to feed his children takes bread from a rich man's full store. What does Aquinas say?
Mission 4

Situation ethics

  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), Situation Ethics (1966): only love (agape) is intrinsically good; rules are guidelines.
  • Theft: if taking food or medicine is the most loving act for the people involved, love requires it. Stealing for fun is unloving and wrong.
  • Lying: a lie told in love to protect a person can be right; a lie for selfish gain is wrong.
  • Personalism: people matter more than rules. Pragmatism: it must actually work. Relativism and positivism (faith in love as the norm).
  • Fletcher uses rules as "illuminators", not "directors" (paraphrase).
  • Same act, different verdict: it depends on the situation and on love.
Mission 4

Virtue ethics

  • Aristotle: theft is among acts with no mean (NE II.6): it is always unjust.
  • Truthfulness is a virtue: the mean between boastfulness and mock-modesty about oneself. Lying for gain is a vice.
  • Virtue ethicists ask: what would an honest, just and kind person do? And what will this act make me?
  • One lie makes the next easier: habits form character.
  • White lies: an honest and kind person may use tact (choosing what to say) without deceiving; virtue ethicists differ on whether a kind lie is ever virtuous.
  • Practical wisdom fits the response to the case.
Quick check
?Ola's aunt asks whether Ola likes the jumper she knitted, which Ola thinks is ugly. Which response fits virtue ethics best?
Mission 4Diversity

Four cases, three theories

  • Stealing food in a famine: natural law: not theft in urgent need · situation ethics: right if most loving · virtue ethics: justice and compassion; a mean? theft has none, but is this theft?
  • Shoplifting for fun: all three: wrong (against justice; unloving; a vice).
  • Lying to protect someone hiding: natural law: never lie, but may stay silent or withhold the truth · situation ethics: lie if most loving · virtue ethics: courage and justice; virtue ethicists divide.
  • White lie about a gift: natural law: a lesser (venial) sin · situation ethics: may be loving · virtue ethics: tact without deceit.
  • Note: the theories often agree in ordinary cases and diverge in hard ones.
Mission 4

What is conscience? (recap)

  • Aquinas: synderesis (an inborn grasp that good is to be done, evil avoided) + conscientia (reason applying it to a case). Conscience can err; you must still follow it, though ignorance you could have avoided is blameworthy.
  • Newman (1875): "Conscience is the aboriginal Vicar of Christ" (Letter to the Duke of Norfolk): the voice of God in the soul.
  • Freud: the superego (internalised parental and social rules) produces guilt; not necessarily a guide to what is right.
  • Fletcher: conscience is a verb, not a noun: the activity of deciding in the situation, not a store of rules (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Conscience at work: lies, promises, adultery

  • Lies: for Aquinas, a well-formed conscience recognises lying as against reason; guilt after a lie is conscience judging the act.
  • Freud: guilt over a "harmless" lie may simply be the superego; it does not prove the lie was wrong.
  • Broken promises: a promise binds; conscience weighs it against new duties (e.g. a promised meeting vs stopping to help at an accident).
  • Adultery: breaks the marriage covenant ("You shall not commit adultery", Exodus 20:14); for Aquinas, against reason and the good of family.
  • Fletcher's case: Mrs Bergmeier, in a Soviet camp, chose pregnancy by a guard to be released to her family (Situation Ethics, 1966): Fletcher asks whether this "sacrificial adultery" could be loving.
  • Many Christians reject Fletcher's conclusion; humanists judge adultery by trust, honesty and harm.
Quick check
?Ravi feels intense guilt after telling a harmless white lie, because his parents taught him that "lying is always wicked". Which thinker would say his guilt does not show the lie was wrong?
Mission 4Evaluate

Which theory deals best with theft and lying?

  • Natural law, for: clear and stable; protects trust; yet flexible on theft in urgent need.
  • Natural law, against: "never lie" even to protect someone seems harsh; the silence/lie distinction can look thin.
  • Situation ethics, for: responds to real people in real situations; allows protective lies and theft in need.
  • Situation ethics, against: easy to rationalise selfish lies as "loving"; hard to predict outcomes.
  • Virtue ethics, for: looks at character and habit; explains why small lies matter.
  • Virtue ethics, against: may not give a clear answer in a dilemma.
  • Judgement criteria: Which handles the hard cases? Which protects trust? Which is usable in the moment? Which fits a religious believer's sources?
Mission 4Worked answer

Applying a theory

Task: explain how situation ethics might be applied to lying.

  • Principle: only love is intrinsically good; rules are guidelines (Fletcher, 1966).
  • Apply: a lie that protects a vulnerable person may be the most loving act (personalism); a lie for personal gain is unloving.
  • Contrast: natural law forbids every lie (Aquinas, ST II-II q.110), though it allows silence.
  • Conscience: for Fletcher, conscience is the act of deciding in the situation, not a fixed rule.
  • AO1 tip: name the theory's principle, apply it to the issue, and use a specific example.
Mission check

?Priya promised her late grandmother never to sell the family home. Now only its sale can pay for her brother's life-saving treatment. Which response would Joseph Fletcher most likely give?

The same promise, lie or theft can be judged three different ways: by the purpose of the act, by love in the situation, or by what it shows and shapes in character.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Applying situation ethics: the situation, the people, the rules as guides, then love's verdict.
  • Close to utilitarianism in method (an "agapeic calculus"), but the good is agape. Critics: Barclay (extreme cases; the need for law; too much freedom), Catholic teaching (Pius XII, Veritatis Splendor), Ramsey; is agape really religious, and is the method subjective?

Mission 2

  • Virtue ethics asks what kind of person to be.
  • For Aristotle, the goal is eudaimonia, flourishing through rational activity in accordance with virtue.
  • Moral virtues are formed by habit; each is a mean between deficiency and excess, relative to us; some acts have no mean.
  • Practical wisdom (phronesis) finds the mean.

Mission 3

  • Anscombe (1958) sparked the revival.
  • Challenges: not a practical guide; cultural relativism; virtues used for immoral acts; each has replies.

Mission 4

  • Natural law: every lie is wrong (silence allowed); theft is wrong, but taking what you need in urgent need is not theft.
  • Situation ethics: love decides; protective lies and theft in need can be right. Virtue ethics: honesty, justice and kindness; theft has no mean.
  • Conscience: Aquinas (reason applying synderesis), Newman (voice of God), Freud (superego), Fletcher (a verb); applied to lies, promises and adultery.
