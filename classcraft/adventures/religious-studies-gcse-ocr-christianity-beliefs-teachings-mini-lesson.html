Christianity: Beliefs & Teachings

This mini-lesson works through the OCR GCSE topic Christianity — Beliefs and Teachings: the nature of God and the Trinity, God as Creator, the person and work of Jesus Christ (incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection, ascension), sin and salvation, and beliefs about the afterlife.

The big picture

What this topic covers

OCR's Christianity: Beliefs and Teachings asks you to know and explain the central beliefs Christians hold, and why they matter. Five big ideas run through it:

  • The nature of God — one God who is a Trinity: omnipotent, loving and just.
  • Creation — God as Creator, and the role of the Word (Logos).
  • Jesus Christ — the incarnation, crucifixion, resurrection and ascension.
  • Salvation — sin, atonement, grace, and being reconciled to God.
  • The afterlife — resurrection, judgement, heaven and hell.

Spec focus: the specification asks you to describe these beliefs, explain their significance for Christians, and show awareness of diversity between Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Trinity — the one God existing as three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Incarnation — God becoming human in the person of Jesus Christ.
  • Resurrection — Jesus rising from the dead on the third day.
  • Atonement — restoring the relationship between God and humanity through Jesus' death.
  • Salvation — being saved from sin and brought back to God, given by God's grace.

Common mix-up: the Trinity is still one God, not three gods; and the incarnation (God becoming human) is different from the resurrection (rising from death). Keep the words precise (more on each soon).

The Trinity

One God in three persons

Christianity is monotheistic: there is one God. Christians teach that this one God is a Trinity — Father, Son (Jesus Christ) and Holy Spirit — as set out in the Nicene Creed. Each person is fully God, yet there is only one God. God is described as omnipotent, loving and just, and as Father and Creator.

Scripture: Christians affirm one God — "the Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Mark 12:29) — while baptising "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19), which points to the Trinity.
Watch out

The Trinity is not three gods

A very common exam error is to say the Trinity means Christians believe in three gods. This is wrong. The Trinity is the teaching that the one God exists as three persons — Father, Son and Holy Spirit — who are distinct but share one divine nature (one "substance").

Say it like this: "Christianity is monotheistic. The Trinity means one God in three persons — not three separate gods." That single sentence protects you from the most common mistake in this topic.

Creation

God as Creator and the Word

Christians teach that God created everything. Genesis 1 describes God creating the universe over six "days" and calling it "good". John's Gospel adds that God created through the Word (Logos) — later identified with Jesus — showing the Son was involved in creation.

Scripture: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1) "In the beginning was the Word... and the Word was God... Through him all things were made." (John 1:1–3)

Jesus Christ

The incarnation — God become human

Christians believe Jesus is the Son of God and that in him God became human — the incarnation. Jesus is understood as fully God and fully human at the same time: not half-and-half, but truly both natures in one person.

Scripture: "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us." (John 1:14) The angel tells Mary her son "will be called the Son of God" (Luke 1:35).

Because Jesus is fully human, Christians believe he understands human life and suffering; because he is fully God, they believe his death and resurrection have the power to save. The incarnation is why Christians celebrate Christmas.

Jesus Christ

Crucifixion, resurrection, ascension

Christians teach three key events at the heart of their faith:

  • Crucifixion — Jesus was put to death on a cross. Christians believe he died willingly for the sins of humanity (Good Friday).
  • Resurrection — on the third day Jesus rose from the dead, defeating death (Easter Sunday).
  • Ascension — forty days later Jesus returned to God the Father in heaven.

The resurrection is the most important belief for most Christians: it shows Jesus is the Son of God and gives them hope of life after death.

Careful: the crucifixion (his death) and the resurrection (rising to life) are different events — don't merge them. Both are needed for salvation.

Sin & salvation

Sin, atonement and grace

Christians teach that humans are separated from God by sin — wrongdoing that breaks the relationship with God. Many Christians also teach original sin: a tendency to sin inherited from the first humans (Adam and Eve).

The good news is salvation — being saved from sin and reconciled to God. This happens through atonement: Jesus' death and resurrection "at-one" humanity with God again. Christians believe salvation is a gift of God's grace (undeserved love), not something people can earn on their own.

Diversity: Protestants often stress salvation "by grace through faith". Catholics teach that grace works through both faith and good works, including the sacraments. Both agree salvation begins with God's grace, made possible by Christ.

The afterlife

Judgement, heaven and hell

Christians believe in life after death. Most teach the resurrection of the dead and a judgement, where God assesses how people have lived and responded to him.

  • Heaven — eternal life in the presence of God.
  • Hell — separation from God (understood by some as a literal place, by others as a state of being apart from God).
  • Purgatory — in Catholic teaching, a state of purification before entering heaven (Orthodox and Protestant Christians generally do not accept purgatory).

Why it matters: belief in judgement and an afterlife encourages many Christians to live morally, love others and follow Jesus now, in the hope of eternal life with God.

A key pair

Fully God and fully human

A central — and easily muddled — teaching about Jesus is that he is both natures at once:

  • Fully God — Jesus is the divine Son, one with the Father; this gives his death and resurrection the power to save.
  • Fully human — Jesus was born, grew hungry and tired, felt pain and died; this means he truly shares human experience.

Christians hold both together: Jesus is not half-God and half-human, and not God pretending to be human — he is truly and completely both. This is central to the incarnation.

Watch out: saying Jesus was "just a good man" or "only God in disguise" both miss the teaching — the belief is fully God AND fully human at the same time.

Why it matters

How these beliefs shape lives

Exam questions often ask about the significance of these beliefs. They are not just ideas — they shape how Christians live:

  • Believing God is Creator can lead to stewardship — caring responsibly for the world.
  • Belief in the incarnation shows Christians that God understands human life and values every person.
  • The crucifixion and resurrection give hope of forgiveness and life after death, inspiring worship at Easter.
  • Salvation by grace encourages gratitude, faith and (for many Christians) good works and love of neighbour.
  • Belief in judgement and the afterlife motivates Christians to live morally and follow Jesus now.

Recap

The beliefs to know

Nature of God: one God, the Trinity — Father, Son, Holy Spirit; omnipotent, loving, just.

Creation: God is Creator (Genesis 1), making all things through the Word/Logos (John 1).

Incarnation: God became human in Jesus — fully God and fully human, the Son of God.

Jesus' work: crucifixion, resurrection (Easter) and ascension to the Father.

Salvation: sin (incl. original sin), atonement, and being saved by God's grace.

Afterlife: resurrection, judgement, heaven, hell — and purgatory (Catholic teaching).
