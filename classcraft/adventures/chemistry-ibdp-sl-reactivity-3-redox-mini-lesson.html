Mission 1

Oxidation states: the rules

  • An uncombined element is 0: Fe, O₂, Cl₂.
  • A monatomic ion's state is its charge: Fe³⁺ is +3.
  • Usually O is −2 and H is +1. F is −1 in all its compounds.
  • In compounds, group 1 metals are +1; group 2 metals, +2.
  • The states add up to the charge on the species: 0 for a compound.
  • Write the sign first: +3, not 3+.
Mission 1

Worked examples

  • SO₄²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H₂S: 2(+1) + x = 0, so S is −2.
  • P₂O₇⁴⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −4, so 2x = +10 and each P is +5.
  • Sulfur runs from −2 to +6: most non-metals have several states.

Mission 1

Oxidation states in names

  • A Roman numeral in a name gives the oxidation state of the element before it.
  • FeCl₂ is iron(II) chloride; FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride.
  • Transition elements have variable states: Cu₂O is copper(I) oxide; CuO is copper(II) oxide.
  • Oxyanions too: ClO₃⁻ is chlorate(V); SO₃²⁻ is sulfate(IV); SO₄²⁻ is sulfate(VI).
Quick check

Mission 1

Oxidizing and reducing agents

  • The oxidizing agent takes electrons, so it is itself reduced.
  • The reducing agent gives electrons, so it is itself oxidized.
  • Agents are reactants, never products.
  • Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Zn: 0 → +2, oxidized: the reducing agent.
  • Cu²⁺: +2 → 0, reduced: the oxidizing agent.
Quick check

Mission 1

Half-equations

  • A half-equation shows the oxidation or the reduction alone, with the electrons written in.
  • Oxidation: electrons are products. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Reduction: electrons are reactants. Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
  • Both atoms and charge must balance.
Mission 1

Balancing in acid: worked example

Cr₂O₇²⁻ → Cr³⁺ in acidic solution:

  • Balance Cr: Cr₂O₇²⁻ → 2Cr³⁺.
  • Balance O with H₂O: add 7H₂O on the right.
  • Balance H with H⁺: add 14H⁺ on the left.
  • Balance charge with e⁻: left +12, right +6, so add 6e⁻ on the left.
  • Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O. Check: 2 Cr each fall by 3, so 6e⁻.
Quick check
?Which half-equation correctly shows the conversion of MnO₄⁻(aq) into Mn²⁺(aq) in acidic solution?
Mission 1

Combining half-equations

  • Scale so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained, then add and cancel them.
  • Oxidation: 2I⁻ → I₂ + 2e⁻. Multiply by 3 to give 6e⁻.
  • Add to the dichromate half-equation; cancel 6e⁻:
  • Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6I⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O + 3I₂
  • Check charge: left −2 + 14 − 6 = +6; right 2(+3) = +6.

Mission 2

Metals: the trend in ease of oxidation

  • Metals react by losing electrons: they are oxidized, so they act as reducing agents.
  • Down a group, oxidation gets easier: outer electrons are further out and more shielded.
  • Towards the left of a period it gets easier too: Na > Mg > Al.
  • So K is oxidized more readily than Na, and Na more readily than Mg.
Quick check

Mission 2

Halogens: the opposite trend

  • Halogens react by gaining electrons: X₂ + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. They are oxidizing agents.
  • Reduction gets easier up group 17: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂.
  • A smaller atom holds an added electron closer to the nucleus, with less shielding.
  • A halogen oxidizes the halide ions below it: Cl₂ + 2I⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + I₂.
  • Br₂ with Cl⁻: no reaction.

Mission 2

Metal displacement reactions

  • A metal oxidized more readily displaces a less readily oxidized metal from its ions.
  • Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: copper coats the zinc; the blue colour fades.
  • Cu + Zn²⁺: no reaction.
  • Exams give you the data. Rank the metals by which displaces which.
  • The ion of the least readily oxidized metal is the strongest oxidizing agent in the set.
Quick check
?A strip of nickel is placed in Cu²⁺(aq), and a coating of copper forms. Another strip of nickel is placed in Zn²⁺(aq), and nothing happens. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Reactive metals with dilute acids

  • A reactive metal reduces H⁺ to hydrogen gas and is itself oxidized.
  • Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen. This is redox, not neutralization.
  • Mg + 2HCl → MgCl₂ + H₂. Ionic: Mg + 2H⁺ → Mg²⁺ + H₂.
  • Zn + H₂SO₄ → ZnSO₄ + H₂.
  • Each H₂ needs 2e⁻: count the electrons the metal loses.
  • Metals oxidized less readily than hydrogen, such as Cu, Ag and Au, do not react.

Mission 3

Anode and cathode

  • Anode: the electrode where oxidation happens.
  • Cathode: the electrode where reduction happens.
  • This holds in every electrochemical cell. Their signs depend on the type of cell.
Mission 3

Building a primary (voltaic) cell

  • A voltaic cell turns the chemical energy of a spontaneous redox reaction into electrical energy.
  • Two half-cells: zinc in Zn²⁺(aq); copper in Cu²⁺(aq).
  • Joined by an external circuit (wire) and a salt bridge, e.g. KNO₃(aq).
  • Zinc is oxidized more readily. Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu.
Mission 3

Which way do electrons and ions go?

  • Electrons flow through the wire, from the anode (Zn) to the cathode (Cu).
  • So in a voltaic cell the anode is negative; the cathode is positive.
  • Electrons never pass through the solutions or the salt bridge.
  • Salt bridge: anions move to the anode half-cell, where Zn²⁺ is building up.
  • Cations move to the cathode half-cell, where Cu²⁺ is being used up.
  • This keeps both solutions neutral, so the current keeps flowing.

Mission 3

Primary, secondary and fuel cells

  • Primary cell: single use. Its reaction cannot practically be reversed.
  • Secondary cell: rechargeable. A charger's current drives the reaction backwards.
  • Charging reverses each whole half-equation: products become reactants, electrons included.
  • Fuel cell: fuel (e.g. H₂) and O₂ are fed in continuously; it never needs recharging.
  • A cell stores chemical energy, not electricity.
Mission 3

Charging, worked: the lithium-ion cell

  • Discharge, negative electrode (oxidation): LiC₆ → C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻.
  • Discharge, positive electrode (reduction, Co +4 → +3): CoO₂ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiCoO₂.
  • Charging reverses both: C₆ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiC₆ and LiCoO₂ → CoO₂ + Li⁺ + e⁻.
  • During charging, Li⁺ ions move back into the graphite.
Quick check

Mission 4

The electrolytic cell

  • An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction.
  • A DC power supply is connected to two electrodes in an electrolyte.
  • Anode: joined to the positive terminal. Cathode: joined to the negative terminal.
  • Oxidation is still at the anode; reduction at the cathode.

Mission 4

How the current is carried

  • In the wires and electrodes: electrons.
  • In the electrolyte: ions moving. Cations go to the cathode; anions go to the anode.
  • In a solid ionic compound the ions are held in the lattice: no conduction.
  • Molten (or dissolved), the ions are free to move. No electrons pass through the melt.
Mission 4

Products from a molten salt: worked example

Molten lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂(l), with inert electrodes:

  • Cathode (−): Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb(l). Molten lead collects.
  • Anode (+): 2Br⁻ → Br₂(g) + 2e⁻. Brown vapour.
  • Overall: PbBr₂(l) → Pb(l) + Br₂(g).
  • A molten binary salt gives the metal at the cathode and the non-metal at the anode.

Mission 5

Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols

  • Find the carbon bonded to the –OH. Count the carbons attached to it.
  • Primary: one (methanol: none). Ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH.
  • Secondary: two. Propan-2-ol, CH₃CH(OH)CH₃.
  • Tertiary: three. 2-methylbutan-2-ol, (CH₃)₂C(OH)CH₂CH₃.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols have an H on that carbon; tertiary alcohols have none.
Mission 5

What oxidation gives

  • [O] stands for the oxidizing agent; you need not name it.
  • Primary → aldehyde → carboxylic acid, in two steps:
  • CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O, then CH₃CHO + [O] → CH₃COOH.
  • Secondary → ketone: CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ + [O] → CH₃COCH₃ + H₂O.
  • Tertiary: not oxidized under the same conditions; there is no H on the C–OH carbon.
Quick check
?Which of these is a secondary alcohol?
Mission 5

Distillation or reflux?

  • The aldehyde boils lowest: it has no O–H, so no hydrogen bonding between its molecules.
  • Distillation: the aldehyde boils off as it forms, so it is not oxidized further.
  • Reflux: a vertical condenser returns vapour to the flask, so the aldehyde stays and becomes the acid.
  • For the aldehyde: distil, with the alcohol in excess. For the acid: reflux, with excess oxidizing agent.

Mission 5

Reduction: adding hydrogen to C=O

  • Reducing a carbonyl compound or acid is a gain of hydrogen. [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • The reducing agent supplies hydride ions, H⁻: hydrogen with two electrons, which adds to the C=O carbon.
  • Acid → aldehyde: CH₃COOH + 2[H] → CH₃CHO + H₂O.
  • Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH₃CHO + 2[H] → CH₃CH₂OH.
  • Overall: CH₃COOH + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂OH + H₂O.
  • Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH₃COCH₃ + 2[H] → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃.

Mission 5

Hydrogenation

  • Hydrogenation: H₂ adds across C=C or C≡C, over a heated nickel catalyst.
  • It lowers the degree of unsaturation: a reduction (gain of hydrogen).
  • One H₂ per π bond: C=C takes 1 H₂; C≡C takes 2 H₂. The σ bond stays.
  • CH₃CH=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₃.
  • CH₃C≡CCH₃ + 2H₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₃, via but-2-ene.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Oxidation is loss of electrons, a rise in oxidation state. The oxidizing agent is itself reduced.
  • In acid, balance O with H₂O, H with H⁺, then charge with e⁻.
  • Metals are oxidized more readily down a group; halogens are reduced more readily up group 17.
  • Reactive metal + dilute acid → salt + hydrogen.
  • Anode = oxidation. Voltaic: anode negative. Electrolytic: anode positive. Electrons travel only in wires.
  • Molten salt: ions carry the current; metal at the cathode, non-metal at the anode.
  • Primary alcohol → aldehyde → acid; secondary → ketone; tertiary is not oxidized. Reduction and hydrogenation add hydrogen.
