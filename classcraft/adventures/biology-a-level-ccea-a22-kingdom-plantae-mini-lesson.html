Mission 1

Mission 1: Mosses and ferns both spread by spores. Why can a fern stand tall while a moss stays low and damp?

Make a guess: Mosses and ferns both spread by spores. Why can a fern stand tall while a moss stays low and damp?

Mission 1

Three problems on land

  • Water holds a plant up and stops it drying. On land, a plant must solve three problems:
  • Water loss: air dries tissues out.
  • Support: air does not hold a plant up.
  • Dispersal: spores or seeds must survive dry air.
  • Three plant groups solve them to different degrees:
  • Moss → fern → flowering plant (angiosperm).
  • This mission: moss and fern.: angiosperms.
Mission 1

A moss up close

  • Multicellular, but not differentiated into true leaves, stems and roots.
  • It has leaf-like and stem-like parts, but no vascular tissue (no xylem, no phloem).
  • No waterproof cuticle and no stomata on its leafy parts.
  • Stomata occur only on the spore capsule.
  • Rhizoids: thin threads at the base, for attachment.
Mission 1

Why a moss is tied to damp places

  • Rhizoids do not penetrate deeply into soil.
  • So mosses live only where water and ions are close to the surface.
  • Water enters across the whole plant surface; with no cuticle, it is also lost that way.
  • Support by turgor only: no vascular tissue, so no woody support. Mosses stay short.
  • Spores germinate only in moist conditions and are only partly resistant to drying.
  • Say: "rhizoids anchor the moss" — not: "moss roots absorb water".
Quick check

Mission 1

A fern: true organs and plumbing

  • Greater adaptation to land than a moss.
  • Well differentiated: true roots, stems (often an underground rhizome) and leaves (fronds).
  • A vascular system: xylem carries water and ions; phloem carries sugars.
  • Roots reach deeper water and anchor the plant.
  • Waterproof cuticle on the leaves.
  • Stomata with guard cells that open and close, so water loss is controlled.
Mission 1

A fern: support, and one weakness left

  • Support by turgor, plus woody (lignified) xylem and other strengthening tissue in the vascular bundles.
  • So ferns can grow taller than mosses and lift fronds into the light.
  • But dispersal is still by spores:
  • spores germinate only in moist conditions;
  • spores are only partly resistant to drying.
  • So most ferns still grow best in damp, shaded places.

Mission 1Practical

Studying plant specimens

  • Living moss, hand lens: find rhizoids, leaf-like parts, and a capsule on its stalk.
  • Moss leaf, microscope: a sheet mostly one cell thick; no stomata, no veins of xylem.
  • Fern frond: find sori (spore clusters) on the underside.
  • Prepared slide, transverse section of fern rhizome: find the vascular bundles and the lignified xylem.
  • Compare photographs of mosses and ferns in their habitats.
  • Calculation: magnification = image size ÷ actual size. Example: a cell 4 mm wide in a drawing at ×200 is 4 ÷ 200 = 0.02 mm = 20 µm.
Quick check

Mission check

?A student finds an unfamiliar small, low-growing plant. Under the microscope it has no xylem or phloem, no waxy cuticle or stomata on its leaf-like parts, and thin rhizoids at its base. Which prediction about its habitat, with its reason, is correct?

Roots, xylem, cuticle and stomata let a fern stand taller and resist drying. Spores still keep both mosses and ferns close to damp ground.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Flowering plants cover dry lands where few mosses or ferns grow. What lets them leave the damp behind?

Make a guess: Flowering plants cover dry lands where few mosses or ferns grow. What lets them leave the damp behind?

Mission 2

Everything a fern has, and more

  • Angiosperm = flowering plant.
  • It keeps the fern's land features:
  • true roots, stems, leaves; xylem and phloem;
  • waterproof cuticle; stomata with guard cells;
  • support by turgor and lignified xylem.
  • Three upgrades, many more highly evolved than in ferns:
  • wood · xerophytic adaptations · seeds that resist drying.
Mission 2

Wood: xylem built up year on year

  • In trees, new xylem is added each year.
  • Built-up, lignified xylem = wood.
  • Wood is strong and stiff: it supports a tall trunk without relying on turgor.
  • Height lifts leaves above competitors, into the light.
  • The outer, younger wood still carries water and ions up from the roots.
  • Ferns make no wood.
Quick check
?Which statement best explains how wood lets a tree grow far taller than a moss?
Mission 2

Xerophytes: cut water loss

  • Xerophyte: a plant adapted to dry places.
  • Leaf curvature: rolled leaves trap moist air over the stomata.
  • Reduced surface area: small or few leaves.
  • Thick cuticle: less water lost through the leaf surface.
  • Hairs: trap a layer of moist air.
  • Sunken stomata: in pits that hold moist air.
  • Moist air outside the stomata → smaller water potential gradient → less transpiration.
  • Example: marram grass (sand dunes): rolled leaf, hairs, sunken stomata.
Mission 2

Xerophytes: find and keep water

  • Succulent tissue: stores water in swollen stems or leaves.
  • Deep roots: reach water far below the surface.
  • Spines: leaves reduced to spines, so less surface for water loss; also deter animals that would eat the stored water.
  • Example: cactus: succulent stem, spines, thick cuticle.
  • Say: "lowers the water potential gradient" — not: "stops transpiration".
Quick check
?In a transverse section of a marram grass leaf, the leaf is rolled inwards. The stomata lie on the inner surface, in pits, among many hairs. Which pair of features both reduce water loss by trapping moist air next to the stomata?
Mission 2

Seeds beat spores in dry air

  • Mosses and ferns: spores, single cells, only partly resistant to drying.
  • Angiosperms: seeds, with an embryo, a food store and a tough outer coat.
  • The coat lets seeds withstand desiccation.
  • Seeds can wait until conditions are moist, and then germinate.
  • So angiosperms can spread into habitats that are dry for much of the year.
  • Record: a date palm seed about 2,000 years old germinated (Sallon et al., 2008).

Mission 2Practical

Studying angiosperm specimens

  • Prepared slide, transverse section of a marram grass leaf: find the rolled shape, hairs, sunken stomata and thick cuticle.
  • Prepared slide, transverse section of a woody stem: count the rings of xylem (wood).
  • Seeds: peel the coat from a soaked broad bean; find the embryonic root and shoot, and the two cotyledons (food store).
  • Compare photographs of a cactus, a marram grass dune and a tree.
  • Calculation: % change in mass = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a dry bean of 1.20 g weighs 2.28 g after soaking: (2.28 − 1.20) ÷ 1.20 × 100 = +90%. Water uptake starts germination.
Quick check

Mission check

?A plant grows on a dry, rocky hillside. It has woody stems, small leaves with a thick cuticle and sunken stomata, and seeds with hard coats. A student says: "It can't be a flowering plant: flowering plants need moist conditions to germinate, and this habitat is dry." Which response is correct?

Angiosperms keep everything a fern has, then add wood, xerophytic features and seeds that can wait out the dry.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Moss: no true organs or vascular tissue, no waterproof cuticle, stomata only on the capsule, shallow anchoring rhizoids, turgor support, spores: tied to moist places.
  • Fern: true roots, stems and leaves, vascular tissue, cuticle and stomata, xylem support; but still spores, so still mostly in damp places.

Mission 2

  • Angiosperms keep the fern's roots, vascular tissue, cuticle, stomata and xylem support, and add wood (in trees), xerophytic adaptations and seeds that withstand desiccation.
  • Xerophytic features: leaf curvature, reduced surface area, thick cuticle, hairs, sunken stomata, succulent tissue, deep roots, spines.
