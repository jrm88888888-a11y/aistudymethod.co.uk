Mission 1

Amitabha and his vow

  • Amitabha ("Infinite Light"; also Amitayus, "Infinite Life"); Japanese Amida.
  • The Larger Sukhavativyuha Sutra (paraphrase): long ago a monk, Dharmakara, made 48 vows to create a perfect land for all beings, and practised for aeons to fulfil them.
  • He became the Buddha Amitabha: so, Pure Land Buddhists believe, the vows are now in force.
  • The 18th vow ("Primal Vow"), paraphrase: anyone who sincerely trusts, longs to be born in his land and calls his name, even ten times, will be born there (except those guilty of the gravest offences).
  • Attendants: the bodhisattvas Avalokitesvara (compassion) and Mahasthamaprapta (wisdom).
Mission 1

Sukhavati: the Pure Land

  • Sukhavati, the "Land of Bliss", lies in the west.
  • Three core texts: the Larger and Smaller Sukhavativyuha Sutras and the Contemplation Sutra (visualising Amitabha and his land).
  • Described as jewelled, fragrant, filled with the sound of the Dhamma; no hells or animal births.
  • Purpose: perfect conditions to hear the Dhamma and reach enlightenment easily, from where one returns to help others.
  • Readings differ: a real place after death; a symbol of the awakened mind; or (for Shinran) birth there is itself awakening.
  • The nembutsu: "Namu Amida Butsu", "I take refuge in Amida Buddha" (namu = homage to, or refuge in).
Mission 1

Pure Land in China

  • Huiyuan (334–416): a society at Mount Lu (402) meditated on Amitabha, aiming at birth in the Pure Land.
  • Tanluan (476–542): stressed other-power: birth depends on Amitabha's vow, not one's own strength.
  • Daochuo (562–645): we live in the age of the decline of the Dharma (Chinese mofa, Japanese mappō), when few can awaken by their own effort.
  • Shandao (613–681): vocal recitation of the name is the main practice.
  • Nianfo (Chinese), nembutsu (Japanese): "mindfulness of the Buddha", which came to mean calling his name.
  • Pure Land practice spread across many Chinese schools; it was rarely a separate school.

Mission 1

Honen and Jodo-shu

  • Honen (1133–1212), a Tendai monk, despaired of awakening by his own effort.
  • In 1175, reading the Chinese master Shandao, he turned to the exclusive nembutsu (senju nembutsu); this marks the founding of Jodo-shu (Pure Land school).
  • Work: Senchakushu (1198): in the age of mappō (the age of the Dharma's decline, when few can awaken by their own effort), choose the one practice Amida chose in his vow: reciting his name.
  • Practice: recite the nembutsu constantly, with faith in the vow, until death; tradition says he recited it tens of thousands of times a day.
  • Honen himself kept monastic precepts; his school spread to all classes, including women and the poor.
Mission 1

Shinran and Jodo Shinshu

  • Shinran (1173–1263), Honen's disciple; exiled in 1207; married and called himself "neither monk nor layman".
  • Jodo Shinshu ("True Pure Land school"), today one of Japan's largest Buddhist denominations.
  • Key idea: shinjin, true entrusting, is given by Amida's other-power (tariki), not produced by the believer.
  • At the moment of shinjin, birth in the Pure Land is settled, in this life.
  • So the nembutsu is an expression of gratitude, not a practice that earns birth.
  • Tannisho ch. 3 (paraphrase): if even a good person is born in the Pure Land, how much more an evil person.
  • No monastic precepts; clergy marry; no rites for worldly benefits.
Mission 1Diversity

Jodo-shu and Jodo Shinshu compared

  • Founder: Honen · Shinran.
  • Role of the nembutsu: the practice of reliance, repeated constantly · thanksgiving after shinjin.
  • When birth is settled: at death, met by Amida (deathbed practice matters) · at the moment of shinjin, in this life.
  • Self-power: recitation is still the believer's act, in reliance on the vow · all self-power given up; even faith is Amida's gift.
  • Clergy: monastic ordination and precepts (though most priests now marry) · married clergy from the start, no precepts.
  • Head temples: Chion-in, Kyoto · Nishi and Higashi Hongwanji, Kyoto.
  • Both: refuge in Amida alone; both rely on the Primal Vow.

Two scholars

  • Shinko Mochizuki (1869–1948), A Doctrinal History of Chinese Pure Land (Japanese, 1942; English trans. Leo Pruden, 1999–2002), paraphrase:
  • Chinese Pure Land grew gradually through many teachers, from meditation and visualisation (Huiyuan) to vocal recitation (Shandao).
  • It was practised within many schools (e.g. Tiantai, later Chan), not as one sect.
  • W. T. de Bary (1919–2017), ed., The Buddhist Tradition in India, China and Japan (1969), paraphrase:
  • Presents Pure Land as a religion of faith that opened salvation to ordinary lay people.
  • In Japan, Honen and Shinran made it a radical simplification: one practice, or faith alone, for an age of decline.

Mission 1

Continuity and change

  • Roots in earlier teaching: recollection of the Buddha (buddhanussati) is in the Pali canon; Mahayana taught many buddhas, bodhisattva vows and the transfer of merit.
  • Changes:
  • Reliance on another's power, set against early texts:

"Self is the lord of self, who else could be the lord?" (Dhammapada 160, Müller)

"You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller)

  • Goal: birth in a Pure Land first, rather than Nibbana in this life.
  • Community: married clergy and lay-centred temples (Shinshu).
  • Modernity: Jodo Shinshu temples in America (from 1899) adopted Sunday services and hymns.
  • Gradual (Mochizuki) or radical (de Bary)? Both views can be argued.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Pure Land Buddhism is an 'easy' path."

  • For: a classic image (attributed to Nagarjuna): the difficult path is walking overland; the easy path is sailing in a boat.
  • For: no need for long meditation, scholarship or monastic life: open to farmers, women, the poor.
  • For: in the age of mappō, it suits people as they really are.
  • Against: Shinran: true entrusting is the hardest thing of all, because it means giving up every trace of self-power.
  • Against: Honen's constant recitation, kept up for life, is demanding.
  • Against: "easy" describes Amida's provision, not the believer's effort: Pure Land Buddhists see it as compassion meeting people's limits, not laziness.
  • Judgement criteria: Easy to do, or easy to believe? Does "easy" mean less Buddhist? Does it fit the Buddha's teaching on effort?

Mission 2

From Chan to Zen

  • Zen is the Japanese form of the Chinese Chan school; both words come from Sanskrit dhyana, "meditation".
  • Tradition traces Chan to Bodhidharma (5th–6th century), an Indian monk said to have sat facing a wall for nine years.
  • Huineng (638–713), the "Sixth Patriarch", taught sudden awakening: our Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly.
  • Two lineages reached Japan: Rinzai (brought by Eisai, 1141–1215) and Soto (brought by Dōgen, 1200–1253).
  • Zen is a Mahayana school: it builds on Buddha nature and emptiness.
  • Your named scholar W. T. de Bary (1919–2017) edited The Buddhist Tradition in India, China and Japan (1969), which traces this route through primary sources.
Mission 2Read the source

A transmission outside the scriptures

A four-line verse, attributed to Bodhidharma but first found in Song-dynasty China (11th–12th centuries), sums up Zen's self-understanding (rendering of the Chinese):

> A special transmission outside the scriptures;

> not founded on words and letters;

> pointing directly to the human mind;

> seeing into one's nature and becoming Buddha.

  • The Flower Sermon (paraphrase; also first recorded in Song China): the Buddha silently holds up a flower; only Mahakashyapa smiles. The Buddha says he has passed the true Dharma to him, beyond words.
  • So Zen claims a mind-to-mind transmission, teacher to student, in an unbroken lineage.

Q1 What does "pointing directly" leave out?

Show answer

Study of doctrine and long explanation: the teacher points to the student's own mind.

Q2 Why does the Flower Sermon need Mahakashyapa?

Show answer

Mission 2

Zazen: seated meditation

  • Zazen = "seated meditation", the heart of Zen practice.
  • Posture: cross-legged (full or half lotus) or kneeling, back upright, eyes half-open, hands in the cosmic mudra (an oval below the navel).
  • Attention rests on posture and breath; thoughts are let come and go without being followed.
  • Done in a zendo (meditation hall), often for long periods; walking meditation (kinhin) between sittings.
  • Intensive retreats (sesshin) may involve many hours of zazen a day for a week.
Mission 2

Koan: a puzzle reason cannot solve

  • A koan is a story, question or saying from an earlier master, set by a teacher.
  • Famous examples: a monk asks Zhaozhou whether a dog has Buddha nature; he answers "Mu" ("no" / "nothing"). Hakuin (1686–1769): "What is the sound of one hand?"
  • Collections: the Blue Cliff Record (1125) and the Gateless Gate (Mumonkan, 1228; "Mu" is its first case).
  • The student holds the koan in zazen until ordinary reasoning is exhausted; in private interview (dokusan) the teacher tests the response.
  • The answer is not a clever explanation but a direct expression of insight.

Mission 2Diversity

Rinzai and Soto

  • Rinzai (from the Chinese master Linji, d. 866): koan practice, often in a graded curriculum (systematised in the line of Hakuin); vigorous effort; aims at a breakthrough to satori (awakening); sitters face into the hall.
  • Soto (from the Chinese Caodong line): shikantaza, "just sitting", with no object or goal; sitters face the wall.
  • Dōgen: practice and realisation are one. Zazen is not a means to a later awakening; sitting is itself the expression of Buddha nature.
  • Both use both: Dōgen commented on many koans; Rinzai monks sit for long hours.
  • Shared: lineage, the teacher's authority, monastic discipline, daily work and chanting.
Mission 2

Zen and scripture in practice

  • "Not founded on words" is not "no words". Zen monasteries chant the Heart Sutra daily; Huineng is said to have awakened on hearing the Diamond Sutra.
  • The image often used: a sutra is a finger pointing at the moon. Do not mistake the finger for the moon.
  • Stories of Zen iconoclasm: a master burns his sutra commentaries; Linji says "If you meet the Buddha, kill the Buddha" (paraphrase): cling to no image, not even of the Buddha.
  • Authority lies in the lineage of awakened teachers, who confirm a student's insight (dharma transmission).
  • Zen has a large literature of its own: koan collections, records of masters, Dōgen's Shōbōgenzō.
Mission 2

What changed from earlier Buddhism?

  • Continuity: meditation at the centre; the goal is awakening; monastic life and precepts; the Buddha's warning that reciting texts is not enough:

> "The thoughtless man, even if he can recite a large portion (of the law), but is not a doer of it, has no share in the priesthood…" (Dhammapada 19, Müller 1881)

  • Changes: sudden rather than gradual awakening; Buddha nature already present; authority in a teacher lineage more than in texts; paradox and the koan; manual work as practice (a Chan saying: a day without work, a day without food).
  • Chinese culture shaped Chan: Daoist love of naturalness and paradox is often noted.
  • Spec 4.1c: weigh which changes are gradual developments of Mahayana and which are radical.

Mission 3

Sangharakshita and a new movement

  • Sangharakshita (Dennis Lingwood, 1925–2018): a Londoner who stayed in India after wartime service and was ordained a Theravada monk (1949–50).
  • In India for about twenty years he studied with Tibetan and other teachers and worked with Dr B. R. Ambedkar's movement, which led many Dalits to convert to Buddhism (from 1956).
  • He returned to England in 1964 and founded the Friends of the Western Buddhist Order (FWBO, 1967) and the Western Buddhist Order (1968).
  • Renamed Triratna Buddhist Community in 2010: triratna = "Three Jewels".
  • Aim: a Buddhism for the modern West, drawing on all schools (Theravada, Mahayana, Vajrayana) but tied to none.
Mission 3

Going for refuge is central

  • For Sangharakshita, the central act of Buddhist life is going for refuge to the Three Jewels: Buddha, Dharma (Pali dhamma), Sangha.
  • Everything else (meditation, ethics, study, ritual) expresses that commitment.
  • He spoke of levels of refuge: cultural (born into it), provisional, effective (a real, life-shaping commitment), and real (awakening has begun to transform the person).
  • Ordination in the Triratna Order is the recognition of effective going for refuge.
  • Summary phrase used by the movement: "commitment is primary, lifestyle secondary."
Mission 3

One order: neither monastic nor lay

  • Traditional Buddhism (especially Theravada) divides the sangha into monastics and lay supporters.
  • Triratna has one Order: men and women, celibate or with partners, living in communities or families, all ordained in the same way.
  • Members take a new name; men are Dharmacharis, women Dharmacharinis; they wear a white kesa (stole) at ceremonies.
  • Order members do not take the monastic Vinaya; they take ten ethical precepts.
  • This is Triratna's idea of the unity of the sangha: no split by lifestyle or gender in the Order.
  • Wider community: Friends (anyone attending) and Mitras ("friends" who have made a commitment to practise with Triratna).

Mission 3

Precepts, not the Vinaya

  • Spec: "the rejection of specific rules within the context of the sangha".
  • Order members do not take the Vinaya rules of monks and nuns; Sangharakshita judged many rules tied to ancient Indian conditions.
  • Instead they take ten precepts covering body (no killing, stealing, sexual misconduct), speech (no false, harsh, frivolous or divisive speech) and mind (no greed, hatred, false views).
  • Each has a positive form: e.g. loving-kindness, generosity, contentment, truthfulness.
  • Some members choose celibacy (anagarikas); that is a personal vow, not a higher rank.
  • Women and men receive the same ordination: a contrast with traditions where full ordination for women was lost (see Mission 6).
Mission 3

Spreading the dhamma

  • Spec: "the importance of spreading the message of Buddhism".
  • Public centres in cities (e.g. the London Buddhist Centre, Bethnal Green, 1978): classes in mindfulness of breathing and metta bhavana (loving-kindness).
  • The "new society": residential communities, and team-based "right livelihood" businesses run by members.
  • Western culture used as a resource: Sangharakshita drew on poets and artists (e.g. Blake) to express the dhamma.
  • India: Triratna's Indian wing works among Ambedkarite Buddhists, a large share of its members.
  • Members have founded projects such as Breathworks, mindfulness for pain and illness.

  • Vajragupta, an Order member, The Triratna Story (2010): tells the movement's history from within. He presents it as a response to Western needs: Sangharakshita stripped away cultural forms to get back to what is essential (refuge, ethics, meditation, friendship) while keeping links to all Buddhist traditions; he also records mistakes and lessons learned (paraphrase).
  • James William Coleman, sociologist, The New Buddhism: The Western Transformation of an Ancient Tradition (2001): describes a Western "new Buddhism" that is lay-centred, focused on meditation, more egalitarian between men and women, and draws on many schools (paraphrase).

Mission 3Diversity

How have others responded?

  • Welcome: many value its accessible teaching, its classes and retreats, and its work in India; it is one of Britain's largest convert Buddhist movements.
  • Traditional questions: some Theravada and other monastics ask whether an Order without Vinaya ordination or a recognised ordination lineage is a sangha in the traditional sense.
  • Concerns about eclecticism: taking parts from every school may lose the depth that each lineage carries.
  • Controversy: from 1997, and again from 2016, reports alleged sexual exploitation of young men by Sangharakshita and by some senior members in the 1970s–80s. Sangharakshita expressed regret; Triratna apologised, reviewed its past and introduced safeguarding and ethics processes.
  • Some former members criticised a culture of pressure in its early communities; the movement says it has changed.
  • Spec 4.2b: "the response of other Buddhist traditions to these developments".
Mission 3Evaluate

Has Triratna succeeded in a new culture?

  • Claim: Triratna has successfully applied Buddhist teaching in Western culture.
  • For: many centres, retreat houses and members; strong growth in India; projects such as Breathworks.
  • For: its core (refuge, ethics, meditation, friendship) is traditional; only the form is new.
  • For: equal ordination for women answers a Western value without abandoning Buddhism.
  • Against: without the Vinaya and a traditional lineage, some say it is a new religion, not a new form of an old one.
  • Against: scandals damaged trust and raised questions about authority with too few checks.
  • Against: critics say drawing on every school loses the depth and safeguards each lineage carries.
  • Judgement criteria: Success measured by numbers, faithfulness to the dhamma, or ethical health? Who judges: Triratna, other Buddhists, outsiders?

Mission 4

Two ways of living the dhamma

  • Buddhism has two main ways of life: monastic (monks and nuns of the Sangha) and lay (householders).
  • Monastics are celibate: no sexual activity at all. For a monk, sexual intercourse is the first parajika ("defeat"): it ends his monkhood.
  • Lay Buddhists keep the third precept: to refrain from sexual misconduct (Pali kamesu micchacara). It does not ask for celibacy.
  • Behind both: craving (tanha) and attachment lead to suffering. Monastics cut the ties; lay people try to hold them without clinging.
  • Your specification uses the Pali forms: dhamma, nibbana.
Mission 4Read the source

Fetters of family

Dhammapada (Müller translation, 1881):

> "Wise people do not call that a strong fetter which is made of iron, wood, or hemp; far stronger is the care for precious stones and rings, for sons and a wife." (Dhammapada 345)

> "'These sons belong to me, and this wealth belongs to me,' with such thoughts a fool is tormented. He himself does not belong to himself; how much less sons and wealth?" (Dhammapada 62)

Q1 What kind of "fetter" is the verse warning about?

Show answer

Not family itself, but the anxious care and possessive attachment that bind the mind.

Q2 Which Buddhist teaching does verse 62 rely on?

Show answer

Anatta: not even the self is "mine", so sons and wealth cannot be owned.

  • Many lay Buddhists read these verses as a warning against clinging, not a ban on family love.
Mission 4

Celibacy: why, and who

  • The Buddha's example: Siddhattha left his wife Yasodhara and baby son Rahula to seek awakening (the Great Renunciation).
  • Celibacy frees time and mind for meditation, and removes a strong source of craving.
  • Theravada (Thailand, Sri Lanka, Myanmar): monks are celibate; many Thai men ordain temporarily, then marry.
  • Japan: Shinran (1173–1263), founder of Jodo Shinshu, married openly; a government decree of 1872 allowed all monks to marry. Today most Japanese temple priests are married.
  • Tibet and Korea: Tibetan ngakpas (lay tantric practitioners) may marry; Korea's Taego order has married clergy, the Jogye order does not.
  • So different Buddhists disagree about whether a religious specialist must be celibate.

Mission 4

Marriage: a social contract

  • The Buddha gave no wedding rite; in Buddhist countries marriage is a civil and family matter, not a sacrament.
  • In Theravada countries, monks traditionally do not conduct weddings; a couple may invite monks to chant blessings (paritta) and receive food, making merit.
  • Marriage is valued as a setting for kindness, faithfulness and generosity: the third precept protects trust.
  • Divorce is also a civil matter. No Buddhist text forbids it; many Buddhists accept it when a marriage causes harm.
Mission 4Read the source

The Sigalovada Sutta

Digha Nikaya 31 (paraphrase): the Buddha tells a young layman, Sigala, to "worship the six directions" by keeping duties to six groups. Two pairs:

  • Parents and children (the east). Children support their parents, keep the family tradition and make offerings for them after death. Parents keep children from wrong, train them for a living, help them marry well and pass on their inheritance.
  • Husband and wife (the west). The husband respects his wife, does not look down on her, is faithful, gives her authority in the home and provides for her. The wife manages the home well, is hospitable to both families, is faithful, guards their earnings and works skilfully.

Q1 What makes these duties reciprocal?

Show answer

Each side owes something to the other; neither has only rights.

Q2 Which duty supports the third precept?

Show answer

Faithfulness, owed by both husband and wife.

Mission 4

Men and women: dhamma, history, today

  • Dhamma: the Buddha taught that women can reach nibbana (the Therigatha preserves verses of awakened nuns). Mother and father are honoured as a child's first teachers.
  • History: Theravada societies gave men the monastic route; women made merit mainly by supporting monks and sons. In Thailand, a son's ordination is believed to bring merit to his mother.
  • T Bartholomeusz (Tessa Bartholomeusz, 1958–2001, Women under the Bo Tree, 1994), paraphrase: in colonial and modern Sri Lanka, Buddhist revivalists praised women as devoted wives and mothers, while some women chose a renunciant life as ten-precept "lay nuns" outside that ideal.
  • N S Salgado (Nirmala S. Salgado, Buddhist Nuns and Gendered Practice, 2013), paraphrase: Western ideas of "freedom" and "equality" can misread how renunciant women themselves understand family ties and their practice.
  • Contemporary: in many Western and urban Asian sanghas, men and women share household and teaching roles; lay women lead meditation groups and organisations.
Mission 4Evaluate

Are Buddhist family roles fair to women?

  • Claim: Buddhist teaching on family life treats men and women fairly.
  • For: The Sigalovada Sutta gives reciprocal duties; the wife has authority in the home.
  • For: The dhamma teaches that women can reach nibbana; mothers are honoured.
  • Against: History gave men the monastic path and greater status; women's merit came largely through supporting men.
  • Against: Bartholomeusz and Salgado show women's roles were shaped by revivalist and later Western ideals, not only by the dhamma.
  • Judgement criteria: Is fairness the same as having identical roles? Should dhamma be judged apart from history? Whose voices count: texts, institutions or women themselves?

Mission 5

Two different questions

  • Q1: Can a woman reach awakening (Nirvana; become an arhat, Pali arahant)?
  • Q2: Can a woman become a fully enlightened Buddha (samma-sambuddha), who rediscovers and teaches the Dharma for a whole age?
  • Many texts answer yes to Q1 and no, not in a female body to Q2.
  • Keep two more levels apart: theoretically (what the teaching allows) and realistically (whether women get ordination, teachers, education and respect).
  • Pali forms on your lines: nibbana, dhamma, arahant.
Mission 5

Early texts: women can awaken

  • When Ananda asks whether women who go forth can reach the fruits of the path up to arahantship, the Buddha says yes (Cullavagga X.1; see Mission 6).
  • Therigatha ("verses of the elder nuns"): about 73 poems by early nuns describing their awakening, e.g. Patacara, Kisa Gotami.
  • Soma and Mara (Samyutta Nikaya 5.2, paraphrase): Mara taunts that a woman's "two-finger wisdom" cannot reach the goal. Soma replies: what does being a woman matter, when the mind is concentrated and insight arises? Only someone who thinks "I am a woman" or "I am a man" is Mara's target.
  • So the earliest teaching treats insight, not sex, as what liberates.

Mission 5Read the source

The Dragon King's daughter

Lotus Sutra, "Devadatta" chapter (paraphrase):

  • Manjushri says the eight-year-old daughter of the dragon king Sagara is wise and close to awakening. A bodhisattva doubts it: the Buddha himself practised for countless ages.
  • Shariputra objects: a woman's body cannot be a vessel for the Dharma; women face five obstacles (they cannot be Brahma, Indra, Mara, a wheel-turning king, or a Buddha).
  • She offers the Buddha a precious jewel, which he accepts at once. She says she will become a Buddha even faster than that.
  • Instantly her body becomes male; he completes the bodhisattva practices and becomes a Buddha in a southern world, teaching all beings.

Q1 Which of Shariputra's assumptions does the story overturn?

Show answer

That a young, female, non-human being cannot reach Buddhahood, or only after ages.

Q2 Why do some readers call the story limiting?

Show answer

She still becomes male before Buddhahood.

Mission 5

Other Mahayana voices

  • Lotus Sutra overall: all beings possess the potential for Buddhahood (One Vehicle); the Buddha also predicts Buddhahood for his aunt Mahapajapati and his former wife Yasodhara (ch. 13 in Kumarajiva).
  • Vimalakirti Sutra, ch. 7: a goddess changes Shariputra into a woman's form to show that "female" and "male" have no fixed essence: all things are neither male nor female (emptiness).
  • Srimaladevi Sutra: Queen Srimala teaches the Dharma with the Buddha's approval.
  • Tara (Tibetan Buddhism): vows to reach and to teach awakening in a female body.
  • Pure Land: Amida's vows include one that women who wish it will no longer be reborn as women (often read as freeing them from social disadvantage).
  • So Mahayana is not one view: some texts affirm women fully; some still need a male body at the end.

Mission 5

Family life and the roles of men and women

  • In the dhamma: marriage is a lay, social matter; there is no Buddhist marriage sacrament. The Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31, paraphrase) sets mutual duties:
  • Husband to wife: respect, courtesy, faithfulness, giving her authority in the home, providing for her.
  • Wife to husband: managing the household well, hospitality to relatives, faithfulness, protecting the family's wealth, diligence.
  • In history: wealthy laywomen such as Visakha were leading patrons of the early Sangha; men dominated monastic leadership.
  • In contemporary society: in Thailand and Myanmar many young men ordain temporarily (merit often dedicated to their mothers); Western Buddhist groups usually treat men and women as equal teachers and leaders.

Mission 5

Rita Gross: Buddhism after patriarchy

  • Rita M. Gross (1943–2015), American scholar and practitioner; Buddhism After Patriarchy (1993).
  • History: Buddhist institutions have been androcentric (male-centred); women's stories were often forgotten.
  • Doctrine: core teachings (anatta, emptiness, buddha-nature) give no basis for fixed gender roles; the Dharma is "neither male nor female".
  • So: Buddhism's commitment to ending suffering for all, and its teaching that gender is empty, make it inherently feminist at its core.
  • Reconstruction: recover women's history; train and recognise women teachers; change institutions to fit the teaching.
Mission 5

Salgado and Bartholomeusz: whose feminism?

  • Tessa Bartholomeusz (d. 2001), Women under the Bo Tree (1994): history of Sri Lankan lay nuns (dasa sil mata, "ten-precept mothers") from the late 19th century; women created a respected religious life without full ordination, amid colonial and nationalist change.
  • Nirmala S. Salgado, Buddhist Nuns and Gendered Practice (2013): warns against judging nuns by Western liberal feminist ideas of rights and equality; many dasa sil matas value renunciation and do not seek higher ordination; their own understanding should come first.
  • Contrast with Gross: Gross reconstructs Buddhism using feminist values; Salgado asks whether those values fit Asian nuns' own aims.
  • Your spec: feminist approaches "with reference to the ideas of N S Salgado and T Bartholomeusz".
Mission 5Evaluate

Does Buddhism align with feminism?

  • Claim: Buddhism is inherently feminist.
  • For: anatta and emptiness: no fixed male or female self; women's awakening affirmed (Therigatha, Soma); Gross.
  • For: modern movements (Sakyadhita, bhikkhuni revival) draw on the Buddha's own founding of the nuns' order.
  • Against: texts barring a female Buddha (e.g. MN 115) and the eight garudhammas (see Mission 6) place women below men.
  • Also against: the Dragon King's daughter becomes male; and "feminism" is itself a Western framework that may not fit Buddhist renunciants (Salgado).
  • Criteria: Teaching or institutions? Early texts or later ones? Whose feminism, and whose voices count: scholars, monks, nuns, laywomen?

Mission 6

The fourfold assembly

  • The Buddha's community has four parts: bhikkhus (monks), bhikkhunis (nuns), upasakas (laymen), upasikas (laywomen).
  • Bhikkhuni (Pali) / bhikshuni (Sanskrit): a fully ordained nun, who keeps the nuns' Vinaya (311 rules in the Theravada Vinaya; 348 in the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya used in East Asia).
  • Full ordination (upasampada) is given by a quorum of the Sangha; for nuns the Vinaya requires a dual ordination: by bhikkhunis, then by bhikkhus.
  • Below full ordination: novices (samaneri) and probationers (sikkhamana).
  • Your spec's term: the ordained sangha.
Mission 6Read the source

Mahapajapati Gotami asks to go forth

Cullavagga X.1 (also Anguttara Nikaya 8.51), paraphrase:

  • Mahapajapati Gotami, the Buddha's aunt and foster mother, asks him three times to let women go forth. Three times he refuses.
  • She and many Sakyan women shave their heads, put on robes and walk to Vesali. She stands outside, feet swollen, in tears.
  • Ananda asks: are women able to reach stream-entry and the further fruits, up to arahantship? The Buddha: they are.
  • Ananda reminds him she nursed him as a baby. The Buddha agrees, if she accepts eight weighty rules. She accepts them "like a garland".
  • The text adds a prediction: because women have gone forth, the true Dhamma will last 500 years instead of 1,000.

Q1 What reason does Ananda's question give for admitting women?

Show answer

They can reach the same goal as men.

Q2 Which parts of the story do critics see as later additions?

Show answer

The eight rules and the 500-year prediction.

Mission 6

The eight garudhammas

Pali garudhamma ("weighty rules"), paraphrased:

  • 1. A nun, even of 100 years' standing, bows to a monk ordained that day.
  • 2. Nuns do not spend the rains retreat where there are no monks.
  • 3. Every fortnight nuns ask the monks for the observance day and for instruction.
  • 4. At the end of the rains, nuns invite criticism from both Sanghas.
  • 5. A nun guilty of a serious offence undergoes discipline before both Sanghas.
  • 6. A probationer trained for two years seeks full ordination from both Sanghas.
  • 7. A nun must never abuse or revile a monk.
  • 8. Nuns may not admonish monks; monks may admonish nuns.
  • Effect: the nuns' order depends on and ranks below the monks' order.
Mission 6

Origins, controversies, and the role of men

  • Traditional readings: the rules protected women in ancient Indian society and kept the Sangha respected; Mahapajapati accepted them freely.
  • Critical readings (e.g. Bhikkhu Analayo; Bhikkhu Sujato; Ute Hüsken): rule 6 assumes a probationer stage that did not yet exist; some rules duplicate lesser offences found elsewhere in the Vinaya; the 500-year prediction did not come true. So the rules and the prediction may be later additions by a male monastic community.
  • Today: some nuns accept them as a training in humility; others reject or reinterpret rule 1.
  • Role of men in the sangha: bhikkhus give ordination, instruction and discipline; in Theravada countries they lead rituals and receive most lay support; in Thailand and Myanmar many men ordain temporarily, which brings their families merit.
  • Significance of Mahapajapati: the first bhikkhuni and the model for every later nun; her story is the main source in debates today.

Mission 6

How the line was broken, and where it survives

  • Ordination needs living bhikkhunis (dual ordination). Once the last nuns die, who can ordain new ones?
  • Sri Lanka: the Theravada bhikkhuni order dies out by about the 11th century, after war and invasion; Myanmar by about the 13th century. Thailand has no record of a bhikkhuni order.
  • 433 CE: Sri Lankan nuns travel to China and help give the dual ordination at Nanjing: the Chinese lineage descends partly from Sri Lanka.
  • Mahayana lineages continue unbroken today in China, Taiwan, Korea and Vietnam, under the Dharmaguptaka Vinaya.
  • Tibet: the full bhikshuni ordination of its own Vinaya (Mulasarvastivada) was never established; women ordain as novices. Some have taken full ordination from East Asian nuns. Separately, in Bhutan, 2022, over 140 women received full ordination.
  • Thailand today: a 1928 order of the Supreme Patriarch forbids Thai monks to ordain women; the Thai Sangha still applies it.

Mission 6

The late 1990s revival

  • 1987: Sakyadhita ("Daughters of the Buddha"), the International Association of Buddhist Women, founded at Bodh Gaya (Karma Lekshe Tsomo, Ayya Khema, Chatsumarn Kabilsingh and others). Aims: unite Buddhist women, education, research, and full ordination where women want it.
  • 1996, Sarnath: ten Sri Lankan women receive full ordination with Korean bhikkhunis and Sri Lankan monks.
  • 1998, Bodh Gaya: an international ordination led by the Taiwanese order Fo Guang Shan includes Theravada women.
  • From 1998, Sri Lanka: bhikkhuni ordinations held in Sri Lanka itself (Dambulla), now continuing in Sri Lanka's own Sangha.
  • 2009, Australia: Ajahn Brahm's monastery ordains four bhikkhunis; his monastery is removed from the Thai forest lineage that trained him.
Mission 6

Dhammananda

  • Bhikkhuni Dhammananda (Chatsumarn Kabilsingh, b. 1944): Thai scholar of Buddhist philosophy; co-founder of Sakyadhita.
  • Her mother, Voramai Kabilsingh, took full ordination in Taiwan (1971).
  • She becomes a novice in Sri Lanka (2001) and a bhikkhuni (2003): widely described as the first Thai woman fully ordained as a Theravada bhikkhuni; abbess of Songdhammakalyani monastery near Bangkok.
  • Her arguments (paraphrase):
  • The Buddha himself founded the bhikkhuni Sangha; Buddhism needs all four assemblies.
  • The 1928 order binds Thai monks, not Sri Lankan ones; it is not a rule of the Vinaya.
  • Ordination gives women education, dignity and teaching roles, and protects poor rural women from exploitation.
  • The Thai Sangha does not recognise her ordination.
Mission 6Diversity

The ordination debate

  • For revival:
  • The Buddha ordained women and affirmed their awakening.
  • Dharmaguptaka and Theravada Vinayas descend from close early schools; and Sri Lankan nuns founded the Chinese line.
  • Some (e.g. Bhikkhu Bodhi; Bhikkhu Analayo) argue monks alone may ordain nuns when no nuns exist, as at the very start.
  • Against revival:
  • The Vinaya requires dual Theravada ordination; once broken, only a future Buddha could restart it.
  • East Asian nuns follow a different Vinaya school; mixing lineages is invalid.
  • Harmony of the Sangha and respect for senior monks' rulings.
  • Another voice: some mae chi and dasa sil matas prefer their freedom from monks' authority and do not want full ordination (see Salgado).

Mission 6Evaluate

Why does the treatment of women differ so widely?

  • Claim: Differences in how Buddhist women are treated come more from culture than from Buddhist teaching.
  • For: the same tradition varies by country: thilashin respected in Myanmar, mae chi often marginal in Thailand; secular rights in Thailand moved faster than religious ones.
  • For: where lineages survived (Taiwan, Korea), nuns lead large, educated communities.
  • Against: the Vinaya itself (dual ordination, garudhammas) creates the barrier, whatever the culture.
  • Against: some women's choices (dasa sil mata, mae chi) reflect their own religious aims, not only culture.
  • Criteria: Text, law, or practice? Whose authority: senior monks, scholars, or women themselves? Does restoring ordination serve all women, or only some?

The same Vinaya meets different histories and authorities: that is why ordination is open in Taiwan and Sri Lanka but blocked in Thailand.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Amitabha's Primal Vow promises birth in the Pure Land (Sukhavati) to all who entrust themselves and call his name; the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu", is going for refuge in Amida.
  • Honen (Jodo-shu): constant recitation in reliance on the vow. Shinran (Jodo Shinshu): shinjin is Amida's gift; nembutsu is gratitude.
  • Debates: is it an "easy" path, and is it a gradual development or a radical change from the Buddha's teaching of self-effort?

Mission 2

  • Zen (Chinese Chan) teaches that Buddha nature is already present and can be seen directly in a sudden awakening (satori), passed on mind to mind in a teacher lineage.
  • Rinzai stresses koans and breakthrough; Soto stresses shikantaza, with practice and realisation one (Dōgen).
  • Scripture, ritual and words remain, but as pointers: a "special transmission outside the scriptures".

Mission 3

  • Sangharakshita founded Triratna (FWBO, 1967) as a Buddhism for the West, drawing on all schools.
  • Going for refuge is central: one Order of men and women, any lifestyle; ten precepts, not the Vinaya.
  • It spreads the dhamma through centres, communities, right livelihood and work in India; responses range from welcome to doubts about lineage and serious concern over past abuse.

Mission 4

  • Monastics are celibate; lay Buddhists keep the third precept (no sexual misconduct). Some schools, especially in Japan, have married clergy.
  • Marriage is a civil matter; the Sigalovada Sutta sets reciprocal duties for husbands, wives, parents and children.
  • Bartholomeusz and Salgado show women's family and renunciant roles shaped by history as well as dhamma.

Mission 5

  • Early texts affirm women's awakening (arahantship); texts such as MN 115, and later Theravada, say a Buddha cannot be female.
  • Mahayana is mixed: the Dragon King's daughter attains Buddhahood instantly but becomes male first; Vimalakirti and Tara deny or transcend fixed gender.
  • Salgado and Bartholomeusz question Western feminist frames and highlight Sri Lankan lay nuns; Gross argues Buddhism is feminist at its core.

Mission 6

  • Mahapajapati Gotami became the first bhikkhuni after Ananda's plea; the eight garudhammas placed nuns under monks, and critics question whether they are original.
  • The Theravada line died out (Sri Lanka by the 11th century); Mahayana lines survive.
  • Eight- and ten-precept women (mae chi, thilashin, dasa sil mata) filled the gap with very different status.
