Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Make a guess: Your body temperature wobbles all day. Why is that a sign that control is working, not failing?

Mission 1

A steady inside

  • Homeostasis: keeping the internal environment within limits, whatever happens outside.
  • Internal environment = blood and tissue fluid around the cells.
  • Regulated variables include core temperature, blood pH, blood glucose and the water potential of the blood.
  • IB calls the last one the blood's osmotic concentration.
  • Each variable fluctuates around a set point. It is never perfectly constant.
Mission 1

Why the limits matter

  • Temperature too high: enzymes denature. Too low: too few successful collisions, so reactions slow.
  • pH away from the optimum: ionic and hydrogen bonds in the enzyme alter, so the active site changes shape. Fewer enzyme–substrate complexes form.
  • Blood glucose too low: cells lack respiratory substrate. The brain is hit first.
  • Blood glucose too high: blood water potential falls, so water leaves cells by osmosis.
  • Say: "lower water potential" — not: "the blood is more concentrated with water".
Mission 1

The control loop

  • Stimulus: a change in a variable.
  • Receptor detects it.
  • Coordinator (e.g. hypothalamus, pancreas) compares it with the set point.
  • Effector (a muscle or a gland) makes the response.
  • The parts are linked by nerves (fast) or hormones in the blood (slower, longer-lasting).
  • Say: "receptors detect; effectors respond" — never swap them.

Mission 1

Negative feedback

  • Negative feedback: the response reverses the change.
  • Variable rises above the set point → response lowers it.
  • Variable falls below the set point → response raises it.
  • So it works from both sides, and the variable oscillates around the set point.
  • The response starts only after a change, so small swings are normal.
  • Say: "negative feedback opposes the change" — not: "negative feedback lowers the level".
Quick check
?During a long run, a person's blood glucose concentration falls to 3.5 mmol L⁻¹. Glucagon secretion increases and the concentration returns to 5 mmol L⁻¹. Which description of this sequence is correct?
Mission 1

Exercise: feedback in action

  • Working muscles respire faster: more CO2, more heat.
  • CO2 dissolves to form carbonic acid, so blood pH falls.
  • Chemoreceptors detect it: in the medulla (brainstem) and in the aorta and carotid arteries.
  • Baroreceptors in the aorta and carotid artery walls monitor blood pressure.
  • The medulla sends more impulses to the diaphragm and intercostal muscles (faster ventilation) and to the heart (higher heart rate and stroke volume).
  • More CO2 is removed; pH returns towards its set point: negative feedback.
Quick check
?During a run, a student's blood carbon dioxide concentration rises, blood pH falls from 7.40 to 7.32 and ventilation rate rises from 12 to 30 breaths per minute. Which statement correctly links these changes?
Mission 1

Positive feedback

  • Positive feedback: the response increases the original change.
  • The loop runs until an end point removes the stimulus.
  • It cannot hold a set point, so it is not used to keep variables steady. That is why homeostasis uses negative feedback.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "positive feedback is the helpful kind".
Quick check
?Why are homeostatic variables controlled by negative feedback rather than by positive feedback?
Mission check

?Two hormones help regulate blood calcium in mammals. When blood calcium falls below normal, the parathyroid glands secrete parathyroid hormone (PTH), which raises it. When blood calcium rises above normal, the thyroid secretes calcitonin, which lowers it. Which statement is correct?

New variable, same logic: a response that reverses the change is negative feedback, whichever way it pushes.

Mission 2

Mission 2: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Make a guess: You eat a sugary snack, yet your blood sugar barely changes for long. What pulls it back?

Mission 2

Glucose in, glucose out

  • Cells need a steady supply of glucose as respiratory substrate. The brain depends on it most.
  • Raises blood glucose: digesting and absorbing carbohydrate; the liver releasing glucose from glycogen or making new glucose.
  • Lowers blood glucose: respiration (e.g. exercise); cells storing glucose as glycogen or fat.
  • Control: two pancreatic hormones, insulin and glucagon, by negative feedback.
Mission 2

The pancreas: two tissues

  • Most of the pancreas is exocrine: clusters of cells (acini) secrete digestive enzymes into a duct.
  • Scattered among them: the islets of Langerhans, the endocrine tissue.
  • β (beta) cells secrete insulin. α (alpha) cells secrete glucagon.
  • Islet cells secrete straight into capillaries: no duct.
  • The islet cells are both the receptors and the source of the hormones: no signal from the brain is needed.
Quick check
?Blood glucose concentration rises after a meal. What happens next?
Mission 2

Glucose too high: insulin

  • β cells detect the rise and secrete insulin into the blood.
  • Insulin binds receptors on target cells: liver, muscle and adipose (fat) cells.
  • In muscle and adipose cells, more glucose transporter channel proteins are put into the cell-surface membrane, so they take up more glucose.
  • Insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fat.
  • Cells respire more glucose.
  • Blood glucose falls → β cells secrete less insulin: negative feedback.
  • Say: "insulin makes cells take up glucose" — not: "insulin breaks down glucose".
Mission 2

Glucose too low: glucagon

  • α cells detect the fall and secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon binds receptors on liver cells.
  • It activates enzymes that convert glycogen → glucose.
  • Glucose leaves the liver cells; blood glucose rises → α cells secrete less glucagon.
Quick check
?Which option correctly pairs a hormone with its effect on target cells?
Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, liver cells cannot break down glycogen. Their glucagon receptors work normally. Which prediction for a person with this condition after an overnight fast is best supported?

Feedback needs every link: sensor, hormone, receptor and effector. Break the effector and the hormone shouts into silence.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Make a guess: Two people both have high blood sugar, but only one lacks insulin. How can that be?

Mission 3

Two types of diabetes

  • Diabetes mellitus: blood glucose stays too high because the insulin loop fails.
  • Type 1: the immune system destroys the β cells (autoimmune). Little or no insulin. Often starts in childhood; onset is usually quick.
  • Type 2: insulin is made, but target cells respond less to it: insulin resistance. Later, β cells may secrete less. Usually develops slowly in adults.
  • Type 2 risk factors: obesity (especially around the abdomen), physical inactivity, a high-sugar, energy-dense diet, increasing age, family history.
  • Type 1 is not caused by diet or lifestyle.
  • Say: "in type 2, target cells respond less to insulin" — not: "type 2 is a milder type 1".
Quick check
?A lean 12-year-old develops very high blood glucose within a few weeks; antibodies against pancreatic islet cells are found in the blood and blood insulin is very low. A 55-year-old with obesity has had slowly rising fasting blood glucose for years, with a high blood insulin concentration. Which interpretation is best supported?
Mission 3

Treatment and prevention

  • Type 1: insulin by injection or pump, matched to carbohydrate eaten and exercise. Regular blood glucose monitoring.
  • Type 2: first diet (less sugar and refined carbohydrate), exercise and weight loss: these improve insulin sensitivity.
  • Then drugs, e.g. metformin (the liver releases less glucose; cells respond better). Insulin is added later if needed.
  • Prevention of type 2: healthy body mass, regular activity, a balanced diet. Type 1 cannot be prevented this way.

Mission check

?In a strain of mice, the liver, muscle and adipose cells have no working insulin receptors. Their β cells are normal. Which prediction after a meal is best supported?

High glucose with high insulin means the message is sent but not received: the signature of insulin resistance.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why is an egg released in the middle of the cycle, and not at the start or the end?

Make a guess: Why is an egg released in the middle of the cycle, and not at the start or the end?

Mission 4

The hormones and where they come from

  • Hypothalamus → GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) → anterior pituitary.
  • Anterior pituitary → FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and LH (luteinising hormone) → ovary.
  • Ovary → oestrogen (mainly oestradiol) and progesterone → uterus lining (endometrium), hypothalamus and pituitary.
  • Ovary: a follicle grows and releases an egg; what is left becomes the corpus luteum.
  • Two linked cycles: ovarian (follicle → ovulation → corpus luteum) and uterine (lining shed → rebuilt → maintained). Together: the menstrual cycle.
Mission 4

One cycle, step by step

  • 1: Days 1–5, menstruation: the endometrium is shed. Progesterone is low, so FSH rises → follicles develop.
  • 2: Growing follicle secretes more and more oestradiol → endometrium thickens.
  • 3: About day 14: high oestradiol → LH surge (with a smaller FSH peak) → ovulation.
  • 4: Corpus luteum secretes progesterone (and oestradiol) → endometrium maintained; FSH and LH held low.
  • No pregnancy: corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → menstruation. FSH rises again.
  • Days are for a typical 28-day cycle; real cycles vary.
Mission 4

Two kinds of feedback

  • Negative feedback: low to moderate oestradiol, and progesterone, inhibit GnRH, FSH and LH. This stops new follicles developing in the second half.
  • Positive feedback: high, sustained oestradiol stimulates GnRH and LH release → more oestradiol → the LH surge → ovulation.
  • The fall in progesterone lifts the inhibition → FSH rises → a new cycle.
  • Say: "positive feedback: the response increases the stimulus" — not: "positive feedback is the good kind".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the feedback control operating in the days just before ovulation?
Mission 4

Hormones in IVF

  • 1: A drug suspends the woman's normal secretion of FSH and LH: her own cycle is switched off.
  • 2: Daily injections of FSH make many follicles develop at once: superovulation.
  • 3: An injection of hCG (which acts like LH) matures the eggs; they are collected from the follicles about 36 hours later, before ovulation.
  • 4: Eggs are fertilised in the laboratory; one or two embryos are placed in the uterus. Progesterone keeps the endometrium ready.
  • Why switch the natural cycle off? So that it cannot trigger ovulation early: the clinic controls the timing.
Quick check
?IVF treatment begins with a drug that suspends the woman's own secretion of FSH and LH, followed by daily injections of FSH. Why is this done?
Mission check

?The combined contraceptive pill contains an oestrogen and a progestogen (a progesterone-like hormone). Taken daily, it keeps both at a steady, moderate level. Why does ovulation not happen?

The egg comes mid-cycle because that is when oestradiol is high enough to flip feedback from negative to positive. Keep it steady and the flip never comes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Make a guess: Your brain has separate parts with separate jobs. So how do they add up to one thinking mind?

Mission 5

Where the main parts are

  • Cerebrum: the largest part, on top. Two cerebral hemispheres, left and right.
  • Its outer layer, the cortex, is folded grey matter.
  • Cerebellum: at the back, below the cerebrum.
  • Medulla oblongata: in the brain stem, where the brain joins the spinal cord.
  • Hypothalamus: small, at the base of the brain in the centre, just above the pituitary gland.
Mission 5

What each part does

  • Cerebral hemispheres: conscious thought, voluntary movement, interpreting the senses, language, learning and memory.
  • Cerebellum: coordinates skeletal muscle contraction: smooth, accurate movement; balance and posture.
  • Medulla oblongata: involuntary control of heart rate and breathing.
  • Hypothalamus: homeostasis, e.g. body temperature and water balance; controls the pituitary gland.
  • Say: "the cerebrum starts a movement; the cerebellum coordinates it" — not: "the cerebellum decides to move".
Quick check
?Which statement describes the role of the cerebellum in the control of body movements?
Mission 5

The brain as an integrator

  • Many inputs arrive at once: sight, hearing, touch, body position.
  • The brain combines them, with memory of past experience.
  • Result: one coordinated decision and response, not one response per sense.
  • Learning and memory are functions of the brain.
  • Say: "practised movements are stored in the brain" — not: "they are stored in the muscles".
Quick check
?A tennis player watches the ball, hears it hit the opponent's racket, senses the position of her own arm and remembers where the opponent usually aims. What is the role of her brain in producing her return shot?
Mission check

?After a stroke, a patient can plan to reach for a cup and knows exactly where it is. But her arm moves jerkily and overshoots, and she sways when she stands. Which part of the brain is most likely damaged?

One movement, two regions: the cerebrum decides, the cerebellum makes it smooth. Damage one, and you see which job was lost.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Homeostasis keeps internal variables within limits around a set point; negative feedback reverses a change from either side, so small oscillations are normal.
  • Positive feedback amplifies a change until an end point; it drives processes such as birth or ripening, not steady states.

Mission 2

  • High glucose → β cells → insulin → cells take up glucose and make glycogen; low glucose → α cells → glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen. Both are negative feedback.

Mission 3

  • Type 1: autoimmune loss of β cells, no insulin, treated with insulin. Type 2: target cells respond less to insulin, treated first by diet, exercise and weight loss.
  • World diabetes prevalence nearly doubled from 1980 to 2014 (WHO); a rise in a risk factor alongside is a correlation, not proof.

Mission 4

  • FSH grows follicles; rising oestradiol thickens the endometrium, then flips to positive feedback for the LH surge and ovulation; progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the lining and holds FSH and LH down.
  • In IVF, the natural cycle is suspended and FSH injections cause superovulation.

Mission 5

  • Cerebral hemispheres (conscious thought, voluntary movement, memory), cerebellum (coordination, balance), medulla (heart rate, breathing), hypothalamus (homeostasis, pituitary).
  • The brain combines many inputs with memory into one response.
