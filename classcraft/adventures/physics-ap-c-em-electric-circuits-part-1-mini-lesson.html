Mission 1

Mission 1: A lamp glows while charge flows through it. Does any of the charge get used up on the way?

Make a guess: A lamp glows while charge flows through it. Does any of the charge get used up on the way?

Mission 1

Charge and current

  • Charge, q, in coulombs (C). One electron: −e, with e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C: tiny.
  • 1 C is the charge of about 6.25 × 10¹⁸ electrons.
  • Current: the rate of flow of charge through a cross-section.
  • I = dq/dt (for a steady current, I = Δq/Δt)
  • 1 A = 1 C/s. So 1 C is the charge passing a point when 1 A flows for 1 s.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A charger supplies a steady 1.5 A for 20 minutes.
  • Δt = 20 × 60 = 1200 s.
  • Δq = IΔt = 1.5 × 1200 = 1800 C.
  • Number of electrons = 1800 ÷ (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 1.1 × 10²².
Quick check
?In an electron beam in a vacuum tube, 1.5 × 10¹⁵ electrons pass through a cross-section of the beam in 8.0 s at a steady rate. What is the magnitude of the current in the beam? Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
Mission 1

What actually moves

  • Metals: free electrons, already present throughout the wire.
  • No potential difference: carriers move fast but randomly. Net flow through any cross-section: zero, so no current.
  • Connect a battery: its emf makes the carriers already in the wire drift. The battery does not supply the charge.
Quick check
?A copper wire carries a current directed toward the right. Which statement describes the motion of the charge carriers that make up this current?
Mission 1

Which way is the current?

  • Conventional current: the direction positive charge would move. Outside the battery: from + to −.
  • In metals, electrons move the opposite way.
  • Current is a scalar with a direction along its conductor: it does not add as a vector.
Mission 1

Drift velocity

  • Under a potential difference, the electrons' random motion gets a slow average drift velocity, vd, along the wire.
  • In time Δt, carriers within a length vdΔt pass a cross-section of area A: charge nqAvdΔt.
  • I = nqvdA (n: carriers per unit volume).
  • Copper: n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ free electrons per cubic meter; A = 1.0 mm²; I = 2.0 A:
  • vd = 2.0 ÷ (8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.0 × 10⁻⁶) = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m/s.
  • A lamp still lights at once: the field is set up all round the circuit almost instantly, and carriers everywhere drift together.
Quick check
?A wire consists of two sections made of the same metal and joined end to end. The diameter of section 2 is half the diameter of section 1. The wire carries a steady current, which is the same in both sections. What is the ratio v2/v1 of the drift speed of the free electrons in section 2 to that in section 1?
Mission 1

Charge is conserved

  • Charge cannot be created, destroyed or used up.
  • In a steady current, charge cannot build up anywhere.
  • So the current into a lamp equals the current out of it. Same all round a single loop.
  • The lamp transfers energy, not charge.
  • Junction rule: ΣIin = ΣIout, a consequence of conservation of charge.
  • Example: 3.0 A in; 1.2 A out along one branch → 1.8 A out along the other.
Quick check
?Several wires meet at a junction in a circuit with steady currents. A student measures a current of 1.20 A in one wire leading into the junction, and currents of 0.70 A and 0.40 A in two wires leading out of it. The ammeters are ideal and read correctly. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?A copper wire has a thick section X joined to a thin section Y, and there is a steady current in the wire. How do the currents IX and IY in the two sections compare?

Charge is never used up. As much charge leaves a lamp each second as enters it; what the lamp takes is energy.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Make a guess: A battery is marked one and a half volts, a lamp six. What exactly does a volt count?

Mission 2

Potential difference

  • Potential difference ΔV: the change in electric potential energy per unit charge between two points.
  • ΔV = ΔUE/q
  • 1 volt = 1 J/C: 1 J transferred for each coulomb passing between the points.
Mission 2

Energy in a lamp

  • Charge passing through a lamp or resistor transfers electrical energy to internal energy and light.
  • The charge comes out; the energy stays behind.
  • Worked example: potential difference across a lamp 6.0 V; 3.0 C passes through.
  • ΔUE = qΔV = 3.0 × (−6.0) = −18 J: the system's electric potential energy falls by 18 J, which becomes internal energy and light.
  • ΔUE depends only on the start and end points, not on the path taken.
Quick check
?As 60 C of charge passes through a small electric fan, 360 J of energy is transferred from electrical energy to other forms in the fan. What is the potential difference across the fan?
Quick check
?Predict first. A cell has an emf of 1.5 V. What does this mean?
Mission 2

Electromotive force (emf)

  • A cell or supply does work on the charge: energy from another store (chemical, light) becomes electrical energy.
  • emf: energy transferred to electrical energy per unit charge passing through the source.
  • Symbol ε: the potential difference a battery maintains, which drives charge round the circuit.
  • emf is measured in volts, like potential difference. It is not a force.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes how a battery maintains a potential difference between its terminals?
Mission 2

Where emf comes from

  • Chemical cell: reactions push positive and negative charge apart, to opposite terminals. Chemical energy → electrical.
  • A cell does not store charge or make new charge. It gives energy to charge that is already in the circuit.
Quick check
?For two points A and B in an electric field, VB − VA = −50 V. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A 12 V battery moves 60 C of charge through a starter motor. How much energy does it transfer to that charge?

A volt counts joules per coulomb. A 1.5 V cell gives each coulomb 1.5 J; a 12 V battery gives each coulomb 12 J.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Make a guess: Double the potential difference across a wire. When does the current exactly double?

Mission 3

Resistance

  • Resistance: how strongly a component opposes the flow of charge.
  • R = ΔV/I, written in Ohm's law as I = ΔV/R.
  • Unit: the ohm, 1 Ω = 1 V/A.
  • Same potential difference: bigger R → smaller current.
Quick check
?A resistor sealed in plastic is placed in an insulated cup of water and connected to an ideal battery. While there is a steady current in the resistor, the temperature of the water rises. Which statement correctly describes where the energy that warms the water comes from?
Mission 3

Where resistance comes from

  • The potential difference sets up an electric field in the wire. Free electrons are accelerated by it (opposite to the field: they are negative)…
  • …then collide with the vibrating positive ions of the lattice, giving them energy.
  • Speed up, collide, speed up again: a steady average drift.
  • Ions gain energy → vibrate more → the conductor warms: the heating effect of a current.
  • The energy comes from the charges' electrical energy. No charge is used up.
Mission 3

Ohm's law

  • Ohmic: the same resistance at every current. Assume the temperature, and so R, stays constant unless a question says otherwise (a real metal's resistance rises as it warms).
  • Ohmic I–V graph: a straight line through the origin, same in both directions.
  • R = V/I defines resistance for any component, at any point. Ohm's law is the special case where R stays constant.
  • Unless a question says otherwise, treat a lightbulb as a resistor of constant resistance.
Mission 3

Worked example: is it ohmic?

  • Readings: ΔV = 2.0 V, I = 0.40 A and ΔV = 6.0 V, I = 1.2 A.
  • R = ΔV/I: 2.0 ÷ 0.40 = 5.0 Ω; 6.0 ÷ 1.2 = 5.0 Ω. Same → consistent with ohmic.
  • Graph of I (vertical) against ΔV (horizontal): slope = 1.2 ÷ 6.0 = 0.20 A/V = 1/R.
  • With ΔV on the vertical axis, the slope is R itself.
Quick check
?A student measures the current I in a resistor for six values of the potential difference ΔV across it. The graph shows the data and the student's best-fit line. Which value of the resistor's resistance is best supported by the data?
Mission check

?Two components are tested. Component P: ΔV = 2.0 V, I = 0.40 A; 4.0 V, 0.80 A; 6.0 V, 1.2 A. Component Q: 2.0 V, 0.50 A; 4.0 V, 0.80 A; 6.0 V, 1.0 A. Which is ohmic over this range?

The current doubles exactly when the resistance stays the same: an ohmic resistor kept at constant temperature.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Make a guess: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Mission 4

Resistivity

  • Longer wire → more collisions in the way: R ∝ length.
  • Thicker wire → more paths side by side: R ∝ 1/A.
  • R = ρℓ/A.
  • Resistivity ρ depends only on the material (and its temperature), not on its shape.
  • Unit: Ω·m.
  • Copper: 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω·m. Constantan: 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω·m.
Quick check
?A uniform copper wire is cut into two pieces of equal length. How do the resistance and the resistivity of one piece compare with those of the original wire?
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Constantan wire: length 0.80 m, diameter 0.30 mm, ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω·m. Find R.
  • Radius = 0.15 mm = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m. Halve the diameter first.
  • A = πr² = π × (1.5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m².
  • R = ρ × length ÷ A = (4.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.80) ÷ (7.07 × 10⁻⁸) = 5.5 Ω.
  • Trap: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², not 10⁻³ m².
  • Stretch a wire to twice the length: its volume is unchanged, so A halves. The resistance becomes 2 ÷ ½ = 4 times as large.
Quick check
?A student makes a resistor from nichrome wire of diameter 1.5 mm. Nichrome has a resistivity of 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m. What length of the wire is needed for the current in it to be 2.4 A when the potential difference across it is 3.0 V?
Mission 4

Resistance against length

  • Same wire, different lengths: R against length is a straight line through the origin.
  • Slope = R ÷ length = ρ/A.
  • So ρ = slope × A.
  • Unit check: (Ω/m) × m² = Ω·m.
Mission 4

Hotter metal, more resistance

  • Heat a metal: the ions vibrate more.
  • Free electrons collide with them more often.
  • The number of free electrons hardly changes.
  • So a metal's resistivity, and resistance, rise with temperature.
Quick check
?A copper wire is connected across an ideal battery. The wire is then cooled from 60 °C to 20 °C; its length and cross-sectional area change by negligible amounts. How do the wire's resistance and the current in it change as it cools?
Mission check

?Wire X has resistance RX. Wire Y is made of the same material as X, is three times as long as X, and has twice the diameter of X. Both wires have uniform cross sections. What is the ratio RY/RX?

Stretching a wire to twice its length keeps its volume, so its area halves. R = ρ(2L)/(A/2) = 4 × the resistance, with the same resistivity.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A rod conducts less well toward one end. Where along it is the electric field strongest?

Make a guess: A rod conducts less well toward one end. Where along it is the electric field strongest?

Mission 5

Current density, the vector

  • Current density J = nqvd: current per unit area, pointing the way positive charge flows (n: carriers per unit volume; vd: drift velocity).
  • Negative carriers drifting one way give J the other way. Opposite carriers moving oppositely add.
  • Current through a surface is the flux of J: I = ∫J·dA.
  • Uniform J perpendicular to area A: I = JA.
  • A slanted surface: more area, smaller normal component. Same current.
Quick check
?A beam of protons moving at 3.0 × 10⁶ m/s has a circular cross section of radius 5.0 cm, over which the protons are spread uniformly, with 2.0 × 10⁹ protons per cubic meter. What is the current in the beam? (e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission 5

The field inside a conductor

  • A current-carrying wire is not in electrostatic equilibrium. There is a field inside.
  • E = ρJ (ρ: resistivity): for the same J, a poorer conductor needs a bigger field.
  • Uniform wire: E = ρI/A, the same all along. ΔV = Eℓ = I(ρℓ/A), so R = ρℓ/A follows.
  • Field and potential: E = −dV/dx. A steep V(x) graph means a strong field.
Mission 5

When J depends on r

  • J(r) varies with distance r from the axis. Split the cross-section into rings: radius r, width dr.
  • Ring area dA = 2πr dr; J is constant on it.
  • I = ∫0ᴿ J(r) 2πr dr.
  • Worked example: J = J0(1 − r²/R²), J0 = 2.0 × 10⁶ A/m², R = 1.0 mm.
  • I = 2πJ0(R²/2 − R²/4) = πJ0R²/2 = π × 2.0 × 10⁶ × (1.0 × 10⁻³)² ÷ 2 = 3.1 A.
  • Not J0πR²: J falls to zero at the surface.
Quick check
?A long, straight wire of radius R carries a current whose density is directed along the wire and has magnitude J(r) = J0r/R, where r is the distance from the wire's axis and J0 is a positive constant. What is the current in the wire?
Mission 5

When ρ varies along the length

  • Slices of length dℓ are in series: each has resistance ρ(ℓ) dℓ/A. Add them:
  • R = ∫ρ(ℓ) dℓ/A. On a ρ–ℓ graph: (area under the graph) ÷ A.
  • Worked example: ρ = ρ0(1 + x/ℓ), ρ0 = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ Ω·m, ℓ = 2.0 m, A = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m².
  • R = (ρ0/A)(ℓ + ℓ/2) = 3.0 Ω. With 6.0 V across it: I = 2.0 A.
  • Series, same A: J is the same everywhere, 2.0 × 10⁶ A/m².
  • So E = ρJ grows along the rod: 2.0 V/m at x = 0, 4.0 V/m at x = ℓ. Strongest where ρ is largest.
Quick check
?The graph shows the resistivity ρ of the material of a rod as a function of the distance x from one end. The rod is 0.50 m long and has a uniform cross-sectional area of 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m². What is the resistance of the rod between its ends?
Mission 5

Series or side by side?

  • Materials in series along the current (one after another): same I, same A → same J. E = ρJ differs.
  • Materials side by side (core and outer layer, between the same two end plates): same ΔV over the same length → same E. J = E/ρ differs.
  • Side by side: add each region's current, I = Σ JᵢAᵢ = Σ (E/ρᵢ)Aᵢ.
  • Decide which quantity is shared before writing any equation.
Quick check
?A long wire of length ℓ consists of a solid core of radius a and resistivity ρ, surrounded by a tightly fitting layer of a different metal, of resistivity ρ/4, that extends from r = a to r = 2a, where r is the distance from the wire's axis. Each end of the wire is attached to a thick metal plate, and the two plates are held at a potential difference ΔV. What is the current in the wire?
Mission check

?A wire made of two segments of different metals joined end to end has the same cross-sectional area throughout and carries a steady current. The graph shows the electric potential V as a function of position x along the wire. What is the resistivity ρ2 of segment 2 in terms of the resistivity ρ1 of segment 1?

In series the current density is the same all along, so E = ρJ is strongest where the material conducts worst: the steepest part of the V(x) graph.

Make a guess: One dead bulb can put out a whole string of lights, yet home lamps work on their own. Why?

Mission 6

Closed, open, short

  • Charge flows only round a closed loop.
  • A gap anywhere in a loop (open circuit): no current in that loop.
  • Short circuit: a wire straight across a component. No potential difference across it, so no current through it.
Mission 6

Circuit diagrams

  • A circuit diagram shows what connects to what, not where things sit.
  • Conventional current flows from + to − round the external circuit.
  • A diagonal arrow through a symbol = variable component.
  • Ideal wires have no resistance. An ideal cell has no internal resistance.
  • Circuits may also contain capacitors and inductors.
Quick check
?Three circuits are each made from an identical cell of negligible internal resistance, a resistor and a lamp. In circuit 1 the three form a single closed loop. Circuit 2 is the same loop with an open switch added. Circuit 3 is the same loop as circuit 1 with a wire of negligible resistance connected directly across the lamp's terminals. Which ranks the currents I1, I2 and I3 in the lamps of circuits 1, 2 and 3?
Mission 6

Two conservation laws

  • Junction: total current in = total current out. Charge is conserved: none piles up.
  • Closed loop: sum of emfs = sum of potential differences. Energy is conserved: each coulomb gains in the cell what it loses in the components.
  • Junction rule: ΣIin = ΣIout. Loop rule: ΣΔV = 0 round any closed loop.
  • Current is not used up. Energy is transferred.
Quick check
?A 9.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance is connected to three resistors in series. The potential differences across two of them are 2.0 V and 4.5 V. What is the potential difference across the third resistor?
Mission 6

Series and parallel

  • Series: one path. Same current. ΔV values add: ΔV = ΔV1 + ΔV2.
  • Parallel: same ΔV across each branch. Currents add: I = I1 + I2.
  • Series: Req = ΣRi. Parallel: 1/Req = Σ(1/Ri).
  • Parallel: more paths, so the total is less than the smallest branch resistance.
  • Home lamps are in parallel: each has the full supply potential difference, whatever the others do.
Mission 6

Where the formulas come from

  • Series, same I. Energy: ΔV = ΔV1 + ΔV2.
  • So IReq = IR1 + IR2 → Req = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel, same ΔV. Charge: I = I1 + I2.
  • So ΔV/Req = ΔV/R1 + ΔV/R2 → 1/Req = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
Quick check
?Resistors of 3.0 Ω, 6.0 Ω and 8.0 Ω are connected in parallel. What is the equivalent resistance of the combination?
Mission 6

Worked example

  • 6.0 V supply, negligible internal resistance. A 1.0 Ω resistor in series with 3.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in parallel.
  • Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 → 2.0 Ω.
  • Total: 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0 Ω. Supply current: 6.0 ÷ 3.0 = 2.0 A.
  • Potential difference across the pair: 2.0 × 2.0 = 4.0 V. Across the 1.0 Ω: 2.0 V. Sum: 6.0 V.
  • Branch currents: 4.0 ÷ 3.0 = 1.3 A; 4.0 ÷ 6.0 = 0.67 A. Sum: 2.0 A.
Mission 6

Meters

  • Ammeter: in series. Ideal: zero resistance, so it does not change the current.
  • Voltmeter: in parallel, across the component. Ideal: infinite resistance, so it draws no current.
  • Treat meters as ideal unless told otherwise.
  • Real meters change the circuit a little: a real ammeter adds resistance; a real voltmeter draws some current.
Quick check
?A student connects an ideal voltmeter in series with a resistor of resistance R and an ideal battery of emf ε, forming a single loop. Which describes the voltmeter reading and the current in the resistor?
Mission check

?Three identical lamps are connected to a supply of negligible internal resistance: lamp A is in series with lamps B and C, which are in parallel with each other. The filament of lamp C breaks. What happens to the brightness of lamps A and B? (Assume each lamp's resistance stays the same.)

Parallel branches share the same potential difference, so each lamp works on its own. In series, one break stops the current in the whole loop.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current = rate of flow of charge; 1 A = 1 C/s.
  • I = dq/dt.
  • Conventional current: the way positive charge would move; electrons in metals move the other way.
  • Charge is conserved: the current into a component equals the current out.
  • At a junction: total current in = total current out.
  • I = nqvdA.

Mission 2

  • Potential difference = energy transferred per unit charge; 1 V = 1 J/C.
  • ΔV = ΔUE/q; in a circuit ΔUE = qΔV.
  • A battery's emf ε is the potential difference it maintains by separating charge chemically.

Mission 3

  • I = ΔV/R. Ohmic: constant R; slope of I against ΔV is 1/R.
  • Resistance comes from collisions of free electrons with vibrating lattice ions, which also heat the conductor.

Mission 4

  • R = ρℓ/A: resistivity is a property of the material and typically rises with temperature.
  • Hotter metal: ions vibrate more, more collisions, more resistance.

Mission 5

  • J = nqvd (direction of positive flow); I = ∫J·dA, using rings 2πr dr for J(r); E = ρJ.
  • ρ varying along a rod: R = ∫ρ dℓ/A; same J throughout, so E is largest where ρ is largest. Side-by-side materials share E instead.

Mission 6

  • Junction: current in = current out (charge). Loop: emfs = sum of potential differences (energy).
  • Junction rule ΣIin = ΣIout; loop rule ΣΔV = 0.
  • Series: same I, ΔV values add, Req = ΣRi. Parallel: same ΔV, currents add, 1/Req = Σ(1/Ri).
  • Ideal ammeter: zero resistance, in series. Ideal voltmeter: infinite resistance, in parallel.
