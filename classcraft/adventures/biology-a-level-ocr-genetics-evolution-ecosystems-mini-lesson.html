Mission 1: Change one letter in a gene: does the protein always change?

Make a guess: Change one letter in a gene: does the protein always change?

Mission 1

What is a gene mutation?

  • A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Many arise as errors during DNA replication that are not corrected.
  • The changed DNA is transcribed into changed mRNA. That may change the polypeptide.
  • Larger changes, to whole chromosomes, are chromosome mutations.
Mission 1

Three ways to change a sequence

  • (1) Substitution: one base replaced by another. Same length.
  • (2) Deletion: a base removed. One shorter.
  • (3) Insertion: a base added. One longer.
  • Compare the sequences base by base, and count their lengths.
Quick check
?Part of the base sequence of a gene is GAT TCA GGC ATT before a mutation and GAT TCG GCA TT after it. Which type of gene mutation has occurred?
Mission 1

A substitution: three possible outcomes

  • A substitution changes one codon only. The reading frame is untouched.
  • Outcome 1: the new codon codes for the same amino acid. No change. The code is degenerate.
  • Outcome 2: the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid changes.
  • Outcome 3: the new codon is a stop codon. The polypeptide is cut short.
  • You may also see: silent (1), missense (2), nonsense (3).
Mission 1

Worked example: four mutations, one gene

  • Code table: AUG Met · GCA, GCU Ala · UGG Trp · AAG Lys · GAG Glu · CUU Leu · CAU His · GGA Gly · AGC Ser · UGA stop.
  • Normal mRNA: AUG GCA UGG AAG CUU → Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu.
  • (a) GCA → GCU: Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu. No change.
  • (b) AAG → GAG: Met–Ala–Trp–Glu–Leu. One amino acid changed.
  • (c) UGG → UGA: Met–Ala–stop. Two amino acids only.
  • (d) Delete the G of codon 2: AUG CAU GGA AGC UU… → Met–His–Gly–Ser… Every codon after the deletion changes.
Quick check
?In mRNA the codons GCU, GCC, GCA and GCG all code for alanine. A base substitution in a gene changes the codon GCU in its mRNA to GCC. What is the effect on the polypeptide?
Mission 1

Deletions and insertions: the frame shifts

  • The ribosome reads in threes from the start codon. There are no gaps or commas.
  • Delete one base and every later base moves up one place: a frameshift.
  • Inserting one base does the same.
  • Every downstream codon is regrouped. Codons before the mutation are unchanged.
  • A new stop codon usually appears early. The polypeptide almost always stops working.
  • Three bases added or removed: the frame is kept. One amino acid is gained or lost (a neighbour may change too).
  • Say: "frameshift: all downstream triplets change" — not: "one base, one amino acid".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, strain A has a deletion of one nucleotide in codon 10 of a gene that has 300 codons. Strain B has a deletion of the three adjacent nucleotides that make up codon 10 of the same gene. Which prediction about the polypeptides translated from the two genes is best supported?
Mission 1

A real point mutation: sickle cell

  • Gene: β-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin.
  • DNA coding strand: GAG → GTG. mRNA: GAG → GUG.
  • Amino acid 6: glutamic acid (charged) → valine (non-polar).
  • At low oxygen, these haemoglobin molecules stick together into long fibres.
  • Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, rigid, and block capillaries.
  • One base out of hundreds. Two copies of this allele: sickle cell anaemia.
Quick check
?In the allele that causes sickle cell anaemia, the mRNA codon for amino acid 6 of the beta chain of haemoglobin is GUG instead of GAG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG codes for valine. What is the effect of this mutation on the protein?
Mission 1

Harmful, neutral or beneficial?

  • Harmful: the protein no longer works, or works worse.
  • Neutral: no detectable effect on the organism. Silent mutations are usually neutral.
  • Beneficial: the new protein gives an advantage.
  • The environment decides: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic helps only where that antibiotic is present.
  • Say: "silent: same amino acid sequence" — not: "silent: no effect on the phenotype". That is neutral.
Mission check

?A gene codes for a polypeptide of 400 amino acids. A single base substitution changes the fifth codon of its mRNA from UAC to UAA. In the code table, UAC codes for tyrosine and UAA is a stop codon. What is the most likely effect?

One letter can do nothing, change one amino acid, or stop the whole chain. It depends on which codon it creates.

Mission 2

One genome, many cell types

  • Almost every cell in a body carries the same genome.
  • Each cell uses only some of its genes. Which ones decides what the cell becomes.
  • Gene expression: transcription → translation → the protein does its job.
  • Transcription is the main switch. No transcription: no mRNA, no protein.
Quick check
?Developing red blood cells produce large amounts of the beta chain of haemoglobin, but liver cells produce none, although both cell types contain the gene. Which statement explains the difference?
Mission 2

Transcription factors: the switches

  • Transcription factors: proteins that bind specific base sequences in DNA and control transcription.
  • In eukaryotes, RNA polymerase cannot start alone. Transcription factors bind the promoter first; then RNA polymerase binds.
  • Activators stimulate transcription. Repressors inhibit it.
  • Each cell type has its own mix of transcription factors, so its own set of genes is on.
  • Say: "the promoter is a DNA sequence; transcription factors are proteins that bind it" — not: "the promoter is a protein".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical species of yeast, protein P represses gene G by binding to a DNA sequence next to the promoter of gene G. A mutation changes this DNA sequence so that protein P can no longer bind to it. Which prediction about cells with this mutation is correct?
Mission check

?In a plant, high temperature activates transcription factor T. T binds to the same short base sequence beside the promoters of three genes that protect cells from heat. A mutation changes the part of protein T that binds DNA, so T can no longer bind. What happens in mutant plants at high temperature?

Same genes, different switches: in a skin cell or a heat-stressed leaf, transcription factors decide which genes are read.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Make a guess: Bacteria make the enzymes for milk sugar only when it is there. How do they know when to start?

Mission 3

Enzymes made only when needed

  • The bacterium Escherichia coli can feed on the sugar lactose.
  • β-galactosidase hydrolyses lactose → glucose + galactose.
  • Lactose permease carries lactose into the cell.
  • No lactose: the cell makes almost none of these enzymes. Lactose added: they appear within minutes.
  • Making them only when lactose is present saves energy and amino acids.
Mission 3

The lac operon

  • Operon: a group of genes transcribed together, under the control of one promoter and one operator.
  • Promoter: where RNA polymerase binds.
  • Operator: where a repressor protein binds.
  • Structural genes: lacZ (β-galactosidase) and lacY (lactose permease).
  • A separate regulatory gene, lacI, codes for the repressor. It is transcribed all the time.
Mission 3

Lactose lifts the block

  • (1) No lactose: the repressor binds the operator. RNA polymerase cannot transcribe the genes.
  • (2) Lactose present: lactose (as allolactose) binds the repressor. Its shape changes; it falls off the operator.
  • (3) RNA polymerase binds the promoter and transcribes the genes → enzymes made → lactose used.
  • Lactose used up → the repressor binds the operator again → genes off.
  • Say: "lactose binds the repressor" — not: "lactose binds the DNA".
Quick check
?Escherichia coli bacteria growing in a medium without lactose are transferred to a medium containing lactose. Within minutes they begin to synthesise beta-galactosidase, the enzyme that hydrolyses lactose. What causes this change in gene expression?

?The diagram shows a model of an inducible operon in a hypothetical species of bacterium when the inducer is absent. In a mutant, a change in the operator prevents the repressor from binding to it. Which prediction about genes 1, 2 and 3 in the mutant is best supported by the model?
Mission check

?A mutant strain of E. coli makes a lac repressor that still binds the operator normally, but can no longer bind lactose. What happens when these bacteria are given lactose?

The switch is the repressor's shape. A repressor that cannot bind lactose never lets go, so the genes stay locked off.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A gene has already been copied into a message. Can the cell still stop its protein being made?

Mission 4

Control after transcription

  • Transcription is not the last switch.
  • After it, the mRNA can be edited, blocked or broken down early.
  • Three levels of control: transcriptional (lac operon, transcription factors), post-transcriptional (editing primary mRNA), post-translational (activating proteins, e.g. by cAMP).
Mission 4

Splicing: introns out, exons joined

  • In eukaryotes, transcription makes primary mRNA (pre-mRNA): exons and introns.
  • Splicing removes the introns and joins the exons → mature mRNA.
  • This is the editing of primary mRNA: removal of introns to produce mature mRNA.
  • Only mature mRNA leaves the nucleus to be translated.
  • Say: "introns are removed from the mRNA" — not: "introns are cut out of the gene".
Mission 4

cAMP switches proteins on

  • Post-translational control: a protein that already exists is activated. No new transcription.
  • A hormone (e.g. adrenaline or glucagon) binds a receptor on the cell surface.
  • The enzyme adenylyl cyclase makes cyclic AMP (cAMP) from ATP.
  • cAMP binds the regulatory subunits of protein kinase A (PKA). They change shape and release the catalytic subunits: PKA is now active.
  • Active PKA phosphorylates target enzymes, e.g. starting glycogen breakdown in liver cells.
  • Fast: seconds, because the proteins are already there.
Quick check
?Glucagon binds to liver cells and glycogen breakdown starts within seconds. A drug that blocks transcription does not stop this response. Which explanation fits?
Mission check

?In a mutant liver cell, the regulatory subunits of protein kinase A cannot bind cAMP. All other proteins are normal. What happens when glucagon binds to the cell?

Post-translational control depends on shape: no cAMP binding, no shape change, no active enzyme.

Make a guess: An early embryo is a ball of identical cells. How does it know where to build a head and a tail?

Mission 5

The body plan problem

  • A zygote divides by mitosis into many unspecialised, genetically identical cells.
  • They must become different in the right places: head at one end, tail at the other.
  • Body plan: the arrangement of body parts along the body's axes, such as head to tail.
  • The genes that set up body plans code for transcription factors. Each one switches on many other genes.
Mission 5

Homeobox genes: an ancient toolkit

  • Homeobox: a 180-base-pair sequence found in some genes.
  • It codes for a 60-amino-acid homeodomain, which binds DNA. So homeobox genes code for transcription factors.
  • Homeobox sequences in plants, animals and fungi are similar and highly conserved.
  • Why: almost any change disrupts development and is harmful, so it is selected against.
  • Hox genes: a group of homeobox genes in animals that control the body plan along the head-to-tail axis.
Mission 5

Hox genes in order, head to tail

  • Hox genes lie in clusters on chromosomes.
  • Their order along the chromosome matches the order of the body regions they control, head to tail.
  • Each Hox gene switches on the genes that build its region, e.g. legs on a thoracic segment.
  • Mutations can turn one body part into another: Antennapedia flies grow legs where antennae should be; Ultrabithorax mutant flies grow a second pair of wings.
  • Fruit flies have 8 Hox genes, in one cluster split into two parts; humans have 39, in four clusters.
Quick check
?Homeobox sequences from a fruit fly, a mouse and a yeast are clearly similar, although these species last shared an ancestor hundreds of millions of years ago. What is the best explanation?
Mission 5

Mitosis builds, apoptosis sculpts

  • Mitosis adds cells. Apoptosis (programmed cell death) removes them in a controlled way.
  • Apoptosis: enzymes break down the cytoskeleton and DNA → the cell shrinks and breaks into membrane-bound fragments → phagocytes engulf them. Contents are not released, so no inflammation.
  • Examples: cells between the developing fingers and toes die → separate digits. A tadpole's tail is removed.
  • Hox genes help regulate both: where cells divide and where they die.
  • Body form comes from the balance of mitosis and apoptosis.
Mission 5

Genes that respond to stimuli

  • Genes regulating the cell cycle and apoptosis respond to stimuli.
  • Internal stimuli: growth factors, hormones, DNA damage.
  • External stimuli: stress, e.g. heat, shortage of nutrients, radiation.
  • Example: DNA damage → protein p53 builds up → the cell cycle stops for repair, or apoptosis if the damage is too great.
  • Say: "apoptosis is a controlled, programmed death" — not: "the cell dies because it is injured".
Quick check
?During the development of a human hand, cells in the tissue between the developing fingers receive chemical signals that cause them to undergo apoptosis, and the fingers become separate. Which statement best describes apoptosis?
Mission check

?In chick embryos, the cells between the developing toes die by apoptosis. In duck embryos far fewer of these cells die, and the feet keep their webbing. Which explanation is best?

Same toolkit, different form: shift where cells die and a chick's foot becomes a duck's.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Substitutions change one codon: same amino acid (degenerate code), one different amino acid, or an early stop. Deletions and insertions not in multiples of three shift the frame and change every downstream codon.

Mission 2

  • Every cell carries the same genome; cell types differ in which genes are transcribed.
  • Transcription factors bind specific DNA sequences to switch genes on or off.

Mission 3

  • Lac operon: with no lactose, the repressor sits on the operator and blocks transcription; lactose binds the repressor, it lets go, and the genes for lactose use are transcribed.

Mission 4

  • Splicing removes introns from primary mRNA; cAMP activates protein kinase A, switching existing enzymes on.

Mission 5

  • Homeobox genes code for DNA-binding transcription factors; Hox genes, in head-to-tail order, control the body plan.
  • Body form is sculpted by the balance of mitosis and apoptosis, controlled by genes that respond to internal and external stimuli.
