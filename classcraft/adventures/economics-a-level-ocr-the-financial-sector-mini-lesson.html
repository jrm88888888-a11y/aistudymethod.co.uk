Mission 1

Mission 1: When a bank lends you money, whose money are you actually borrowing?

Make a guess: When a bank lends someone 10,000, where does that money come from?

Mission 1

What banks do

  • Commercial (retail) banks:
    • Accept deposits and keep them safe.
    • Make loans to households and firms.
    • Run payments: cards, transfers.
  • They channel savings into investment: firms borrow to build and expand.
  • Borrowers pay interest: the price of credit. Banks charge more for riskier loans.
  • Collateral: an asset the bank can take if a loan is not repaid (e.g. a house for a mortgage).
Mission 1

A bank's balance sheet

  • Liabilities: what the bank owes.
    • Customers' deposits (the bank owes them on demand or at a set date).
    • Borrowing from other banks.
  • Assets: what the bank owns or is owed.
    • Reserves: cash and deposits at the central bank. Most liquid, earn least.
    • Liquid assets: e.g. short-term government bills.
    • Loans to customers. Least liquid, earn most.
    • Investments such as bonds.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities. It absorbs losses.
Mission 1

Loans create deposits

  • When a bank makes a loan, it credits the borrower's account with a new deposit.
  • That deposit is new money: no one else's balance went down.
  • Example (illustrative): a bank lends 10,000 to a café owner.
    • Assets: loans +10,000.
    • Liabilities: deposits +10,000.
    • Broad money rises by 10,000 at once.
  • When loans are repaid, deposits fall: money is destroyed.
  • What limits lending? Demand for loans, the risk of non-payment, the interest rate set by the central bank, and capital and liquidity rules.
  • Say: "banks create deposits when they lend" — not: "banks just lend out savers' money".
Mission 1

The money multiplier model

  • A textbook model of how deposits spread through the banking system.
  • Fractional reserve banking: banks keep only a fraction of deposits as reserves and lend the rest.
  • Worked example (illustrative): reserve ratio 10%; a new cash deposit of 1,000.
    • Round 1: keep 100; lend 900. The 900 is spent and deposited in another bank.
    • Round 2: keep 90; lend 810.
    • Round 3: keep 81; lend 729 … and so on.
  • Maximum total deposits = 1,000 × (1 ÷ 0.10) = 10,000.
  • Bank credit multiplier = 1 ÷ reserve ratio = 10.
Mission 1Graph lab

Rounds of lending

Illustrative data. A new deposit of 1,000 enters the banking system.

  • Takeaway: a lower reserve ratio means more of each deposit is lent on, so deposits grow further.
Mission 1

Why the multiplier overstates

  • The simple multiplier assumes banks lend every penny of excess reserves and every loan comes back as a deposit.
  • In reality:
    • Banks may keep excess reserves, e.g. when they fear losses.
    • People and firms hold some cash instead of deposits.
    • Lending depends on demand for loans and on capital rules.
  • Real world:
    • The Fed's required reserve ratio has been 0% since March 2020.
    • Central banks today mostly set interest rates and supply the reserves banks need.
  • So the multiplier is a maximum, not a forecast.
  • Link to prices (quantity theory): if money grows faster than real output, inflation may follow.
Quick check
?Two economies both have a reserve ratio of 10%. In each, the central bank buys government bonds from banks, adding 1,000 to the banks' reserves. In Economy A, banks lend all their excess reserves, and every loan comes back as a deposit. In Economy B, banks keep some extra reserves, and people keep part of their loans as cash. Using the multiplier model, which is correct?
Mission check

?A bank holds 1,000,000 of its customers' savings deposits. It then agrees a new loan of 50,000 to a firm and credits the firm's account. What happens at once to the savers' balances and to the money supply?

A bank loan is not someone else's savings passed on. It is a new deposit, so it is new money.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If your savings grow every year, how can you still end up able to buy less?

Make a guess: If your savings grow every year, how can you still end up able to buy less?

Mission 2

Nominal and real

  • You save 1,000 for one year at an interest rate of 5% (illustrative data).
  • After a year you have 1,050. That 5% is the nominal interest rate: the rate in money terms, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Over the same year, inflation is 3%. A basket that cost 1,000 now costs 1,030.
  • 1,050 ÷ 1,030 = 1.019. You can buy only about 2% more than a year ago.
  • That 2% is the real interest rate: the rise in what your savings can buy.
  • Say: "the real interest rate fell" — not: "interest rates fell", when the nominal rate stayed the same and only inflation rose.
Mission 2

The Fisher relation

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Named after the economist Irving Fisher.
  • OCR: this is not what your course calls the "Fisher equation". That name means Fisher's equation of exchange, MV = PT, which links the money supply to the price level.
  • Example: nominal 5%, inflation 3% → real ≈ 5 − 3 = 2%.
  • If inflation is higher than the nominal rate, the real rate is negative: savings buy less each year.
  • The exact answer here is 1.94%, not 2%. The subtraction is close when rates are low, and it is the form you use in calculations.
  • Say: "real interest rate = nominal rate minus inflation" — not: "nominal rate minus the interest".
Quick check
?A bank pays a nominal interest rate of 6% on savings. Inflation over the year is 8%. What is the real interest rate?
Mission 2

When real rates go negative

  • Real-world data, euro area, October 2022:
  • The European Central Bank's deposit rate was 0.75%. Euro area inflation was 10.6%.
  • Real rate on that deposit ≈ 0.75 − 10.6 = −9.85%.
  • Nominal rates had started to rise, but real rates were still deeply negative.
  • Borrowers care about the real rate too: a negative real rate means a debt shrinks in real terms.
  • So a rise in the nominal rate is not always "tighter" money: it depends on what happens to inflation.
Mission 2

Expected and actual inflation

  • Lenders set the nominal rate before they know the inflation rate.
  • A lender wants a real return of 3% and expects inflation of 2%. It charges 3 + 2 = 5% nominal (illustrative data).
  • Inflation turns out to be 6%.
  • Real rate actually earned = 5 − 6 = −1%, not the 3% planned.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders: the money repaid buys less than both expected.
  • Unexpectedly low inflation does the reverse.
Quick check
?A firm borrows at a fixed nominal rate of 7% for one year. When the loan is agreed, both sides expect inflation of 3%. Actual inflation turns out to be 1%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Theory 1: loanable funds

  • Classical economists: the interest rate is the price of borrowing saved funds.
  • Supply of loanable funds: saving by households, firms and government. A higher real rate rewards saving, so supply slopes up.
  • Demand for loanable funds: borrowing for investment (and by government and consumers). A lower real rate makes more projects worthwhile, so demand slopes down.
  • The real interest rate settles where saving = borrowing.
  • More saving (supply shifts right) → the real rate falls.
  • More borrowing (demand shifts right) → the real rate rises.
  • Mainly a long-run theory of the real rate.
Mission 2

Theory 2: Keynes's liquidity preference

  • Keynes (1936): the interest rate is the price of giving up liquidity (holding money instead of bonds).
  • People demand money for three motives:
  • Transactions motive: to pay for everyday spending. Rises with income.
  • Precautionary motive: for emergencies. Rises with income and uncertainty.
  • Speculative motive: to hold money instead of bonds when bond prices are expected to fall. Higher when interest rates are low.
  • So the demand for money (liquidity preference, LP) slopes down against the interest rate: holding money costs the interest you give up.
  • The money supply is set by the central bank: a vertical line.
  • The nominal interest rate settles where money demand = money supply.

  • Takeaway: with a fixed money supply, a rise in the demand for money raises the nominal interest rate.
Quick check
?In Keynes's liquidity preference theory, investors come to expect interest rates to rise soon. The money supply does not change. What happens?
Mission 2

Why there is not one interest rate

  • Different loans carry different rates. Illustrative data, one year:

Central bank policy rate4%
Government (10-year bond)4.5%

Small firm9%

  • Risk premium: lenders charge more where default is more likely.
  • Time: longer loans usually pay more (more can go wrong; money is tied up).
  • Security: a loan backed by a house (collateral) is cheaper than an unsecured one.
  • All rates tend to move with the policy rate set by the central bank.
Mission 2Evaluate

Which theory explains interest rates?

It depends on:

  • Time period: loanable funds fits the long-run real rate; liquidity preference fits short-run nominal rates.
  • The central bank: in practice, most central banks set a short-term policy rate and supply whatever money is demanded at it. Market forces then matter more for long-term rates.
  • Banks: banks create deposits when they lend, so lending is not limited to past saving (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Expectations: both theories depend on expected inflation and expected future rates, which can change quickly.
Mission check

?A central bank raises its nominal policy rate from 3% to 5%. Over the same period, inflation rises from 2% to 7%. What happened to the real policy rate?

A higher nominal rate can still mean a lower real rate, if inflation rises by more.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Make a guess: If money has a price, what makes that price go up or down?

Mission 3

Holding money has a cost

  • You can keep your wealth as money (cash, current accounts) or as interest-bearing assets such as bonds.
  • Money is the most liquid asset: you can spend it at once.
  • But money pays little or no interest.
  • So the opportunity cost of holding money is the interest you give up.
  • The higher the nominal interest rate, the more it costs to hold money.
Mission 3

The demand for money

  • At a high nominal interest rate, people hold less money and more bonds.
  • At a low rate, holding money costs little, so they hold more.
  • So money demand (MD) slopes down against the nominal interest rate.
  • A change in the interest rate → a movement along MD.
  • MD shifts right when people need more money for spending: a higher price level or higher real output (more transactions).
  • This is Keynes's liquidity preference curve (see Mission 2): transactions, precautionary and speculative motives.
Mission 3

Money supply and equilibrium

  • The money supply (MS) is set by the central bank. It does not depend on the interest rate.
  • So MS is a vertical line.
  • Equilibrium: the nominal interest rate where the quantity of money demanded = the money supply.
  • Illustrative data: MS = 600 (billion); MD gives an equilibrium rate of 4%.
  • Say: "money demand shifted right" — not: "money supply increased", when people simply want to hold more money.
Mission 3

Getting back to equilibrium

  • Bond prices and interest rates move in opposite directions.
  • Step 1: rate 5%, above equilibrium. People want to hold only 500 of the 600: a surplus of money of 100.
  • They use spare money to buy bonds → bond prices rise → interest rates fall back to 4%.
  • Step 2: rate below equilibrium → a shortage of money.
  • People sell bonds to get money → bond prices fall → interest rates rise.
  • The nominal rate adjusts; the money supply stays where the central bank put it.
Quick check
?In the money market, the nominal interest rate is below its equilibrium level. What happens next?
Mission 3Graph lab

Prices up, rate?

  • Takeaway: a higher price level raises money demand, so with a fixed money supply the nominal rate rises.
Mission 3

The central bank moves the money supply

  • To raise the money supply, the central bank buys government bonds (open market operations or quantitative easing). It pays with new money.
  • MS shifts right: MS1 → MS2. The nominal rate falls (illustrative data: 600 → 700, rate 4% → 3%).
  • To reduce the money supply, it sells bonds: MS shifts left, the rate rises.
  • Say: "an increase in the money supply lowers the nominal interest rate" — not: "lower interest rates increase the money supply" (wrong direction of cause).
Quick check
?Real output grows strongly, so people make more transactions. The central bank leaves the money supply unchanged. What happens in the money market?
Mission 3Evaluate

How useful is this model?

It depends on:

  • What the central bank targets: most central banks set a policy interest rate and supply whatever money is demanded at it. Then the money supply is not fixed.
  • Stable money demand: if money demand shifts a lot (new payment methods, panics), a fixed money supply gives an unstable rate.
  • Very low rates: near zero, extra money may not cut rates further. Real-world data: the European Central Bank's deposit rate was at or below 0% from July 2012 to September 2022.
  • The link to spending: a lower rate only raises AD if firms and households want to borrow.
Mission check

?After a wave of online payment fraud, people decide to hold more cash. Money demand rises by 50 billion at every interest rate. The central bank wants to keep the nominal interest rate unchanged. What should it do?

To hold the rate steady, a central bank must supply the extra money people want to hold.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why can a country full of oil, copper or cocoa still stay poor for decades?

Make a guess: Why can a country full of oil, copper or cocoa still stay poor for decades?

Mission 4

The poverty cycle

  • A poverty cycle (poverty trap): low income causes the things that keep income low.
  • Low income → low saving → low investment → little capital per worker → low productivity → low income.
  • A second loop: low income → poor health and education → low human capital → low productivity → low income.
  • Each arrow is a link. Break one link, and the cycle can turn into a growth path.
Quick check
?In a poverty cycle, low incomes lead to low saving. Why does low saving keep incomes low in the future?
Mission 4

The savings gap: Harrod-Domar

  • Harrod-Domar model: growth depends on saving and on how productive new capital is.
  • Growth rate = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio.
  • Capital-output ratio: units of capital needed for 1 more unit of output a year.
  • Worked example (illustrative data): savings ratio 12%, capital-output ratio 4.
  • Growth = 12 ÷ 4 = 3% a year.
  • Target growth 6%: savings needed = 6 × 4 = 24% of income.
  • Savings gap = 24% − 12% = 12% of income. Aid, FDI or borrowing abroad may fill it.
  • Income from oil, copper or cocoa raises growth only if it is saved and invested productively at home.
  • Limits: saving may not become productive investment; the model ignores technology, human capital and institutions; the capital-output ratio is not fixed.
Quick check
?A country saves 20% of its income. Its capital-output ratio is 5. According to the Harrod-Domar model, what is its growth rate?
Mission check

?A country saves 15% of its income and grows at 3% a year. Aid and new savings schemes raise the savings ratio to 25%, but growth stays at 3%. What does the Harrod-Domar model suggest has happened?

More saving raises growth only if it becomes productive investment.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Where does the money businesses borrow come from, and what decides its price?

Make a guess: Where does the money businesses borrow come from, and what decides its price?

Mission 5

A market for saving and borrowing

  • Savers lend their unspent income (through banks and bond markets). Borrowers use it, mostly for investment in new capital.
  • The market for loanable funds brings them together.
  • Its price is the real interest rate: what lenders earn and borrowers pay in purchasing power (see Mission 2).
  • Supply slopes up: a higher real rate rewards saving.
  • Demand slopes down: at a lower real rate, more investment projects are worth borrowing for.
  • Equilibrium: real interest rate r1, quantity of loanable funds Q1.
  • If the rate is above r1, there is a surplus of funds: lenders compete and the rate falls. Below r1, a shortage pushes it up.
Mission 5

National saving

  • The supply of loanable funds comes from national saving.
  • National saving = private saving + public saving.
  • Public saving = tax revenue − government spending. A budget deficit is negative public saving.
  • Illustrative data (billion): private saving 900, taxes 600, government spending 700.
  • Public saving = 600 − 700 = −100.
  • National saving = 900 + (−100) = 800.
  • Say: "a budget deficit reduces public saving" — not: "a deficit is the same as the national debt".
Quick check
?Private saving is 500 billion. The government collects 300 billion in taxes and spends 350 billion. What is national saving?

Mission 5Graph lab

The government borrows more

Start: real interest rate 6%, loanable funds 500 (billion), all borrowed for private investment (illustrative data).

  • Takeaway: government borrowing raises the real rate, and some private investment is priced out.
Mission 5

Crowding out

  • In the lab, a deficit of 100 raises the real rate from 6% to 7%.
  • Total borrowing rises to 550, but the government takes 100 of it.
  • Private investment falls from 500 to 450: it is crowded out.
  • Less private investment today → a smaller capital stock → slower long-run growth.
  • How much crowding out? It depends on how far the rate rises, and how sensitive investment is to it.
Quick check
?The government gives firms a tax credit for each new machine they buy (an investment tax credit). Other things equal, what happens in the loanable funds market?
Mission 5

Saving, investment and growth

  • Saving supplies the funds for investment, and investment builds the capital that raises output.
  • Harrod-Domar model: growth rate = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio.
  • Illustrative data: a country saves 24% of its income; it takes 4 units of capital to make 1 more unit of output a year.
  • Growth = 24 ÷ 4 = 6% a year.
  • Low incomes → low saving → low investment → low growth: a savings gap that foreign aid, FDI or borrowing may fill.
  • Limits: saving may not reach productive investment; the model ignores technology, human capital and institutions (see Mission 4).
Mission 5Evaluate

How well does loanable funds explain interest rates?

It depends on:

  • Interest sensitivity: if saving barely responds to the real rate, the supply curve is steep.
  • Bank lending: banks create deposits when they lend, so new lending is not limited to past saving (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, firms may want to invest little, so government borrowing may crowd out little.
  • Open economies: capital inflows can stop the real rate rising much.
  • Central banks: they set short-term rates directly; the model fits long-term real rates better.
Mission check

?Worried about their jobs, households decide to save a larger share of their income. Other things equal, what happens in the loanable funds market?

More saving lowers the real interest rate and raises the quantity of funds borrowed for investment.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Banks create money when they lend: a loan creates a matching deposit.
  • The multiplier model: maximum deposits = new reserves × 1 ÷ reserve ratio. It overstates because of idle reserves and cash leakages.

Mission 2

  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation; it can be negative.
  • Unexpected inflation helps borrowers and hurts lenders.
  • Loanable funds explains the real rate by saving and borrowing; liquidity preference explains the nominal rate by money demand and money supply.

Mission 3

  • The opportunity cost of holding money is the interest given up, so money demand slopes down.
  • Money supply is vertical, set by the central bank; the nominal interest rate is where they meet.
  • MD shifts with the price level and real output; MS shifts with central bank action.

Mission 4

  • A poverty cycle: low income → low saving and human capital → low investment and productivity → low income.
  • Harrod-Domar: growth = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio; a savings gap limits growth.

Mission 5

  • Loanable funds: supply = national saving (private + public), demand = borrowing for investment; price = real interest rate.
  • A budget deficit raises the real rate and can crowd out private investment.
  • Harrod-Domar: growth = savings ratio ÷ capital-output ratio.
