Electric current

Current is a flow of charge

An electric current is a flow of electric charge around a circuit. It only flows if there is a complete loop with no gaps, driven by a cell or battery.

  • Current is measured in amperes (amps), symbol A.
  • You measure current with an ammeter, connected in series (in the loop).
  • The bigger the current, the more charge flows past each point every second.

Watch out: current is not used up by a lamp. The same current flows all the way round a single loop — the lamp transfers energy, but it does not "eat" the current.

Direction of current

Which way does current go?

Inside a metal wire the tiny particles that actually move are electrons, and they carry a negative charge. But long before electrons were discovered, scientists agreed on a direction to draw current in.

We use conventional current: it flows from the positive (+) terminal of the cell, round the circuit, to the negative (−) terminal.

Common mistake: conventional current and electron flow point in opposite directions. For KS3 circuits, always draw conventional current from + to −.

Potential difference

Potential difference

Potential difference (p.d.), also called voltage, tells you how much energy the cell gives to each bit of charge flowing round the circuit, and how much energy the charge passes on to each component.

  • Potential difference is measured in volts, symbol V.
  • You measure it with a voltmeter, connected in parallel with (across) the component.
  • A bigger p.d. across the same component drives a bigger current through it.

Ratings

Battery and bulb ratings

A cell is labelled with its voltage rating, such as 1.5 V. Join cells + to − to make a battery: their voltages add.

A lamp (bulb) is labelled with the voltage it is designed for, such as 3 V. Match it to the battery:

  • Same voltage: the lamp glows normally.
  • Battery voltage too low: the lamp is dim.
  • Battery voltage too high: it is too bright and may blow (its thin wire overheats and breaks).

Resistance

Resistance slows the flow

Resistance is how much a component opposes the flow of current. It is measured in ohms (Ω).

  • The higher the resistance, the smaller the current (for the same voltage).
  • Long thin wires have more resistance than short thick wires.
  • A resistor gets warm because it transfers energy as current flows through it.
more resistance → less currentfor the same potential difference (voltage)

Think of it like a slide: the p.d. is like the height of the slide (how much energy each person gets on the way down), resistance is a rough, narrow slide that slows you down, and current is how many people get down per second.

Resistance

Calculating resistance

Resistance is the ratio of potential difference to current. Measure the p.d. across a component and the current through it, then divide:

resistance = p.d. ÷ currentR = V ÷ I  ·  resistance is measured in ohms (Ω)
Worked example

A resistor has a p.d. of 6 V across it and a current of 0.5 A through it.

R = V ÷ I = 6 ÷ 0.5 = 12 Ω

Common mistake: dividing the wrong way round. It is volts ÷ amps, never amps ÷ volts.

Conductors & insulators

Low resistance, high resistance

Conductors, such as copper and other metals, have a very low resistance, so current flows through them easily. Insulators, such as plastic, rubber and glass, have a very high resistance, so almost no current flows.

  • 1 m of copper connecting wire: less than 0.1 Ω.
  • A plastic ruler, end to end: more than 1 000 000 000 Ω (a thousand million ohms).

Why cables are copper inside plastic: the copper carries the current; the plastic stops it reaching you.

Series circuits

Series: one single loop

In a series circuit all the components are joined in one single loop, so there is only one path for the current.

  • The current is the same at every point in the loop.
  • The cell's voltage is shared between the components.
  • Add more lamps → each is dimmer, because the voltage is split further.
  • If one lamp breaks, the loop is broken and they all go out.

Common mistake: the current is not "used up" as it goes round a series circuit — it is exactly the same everywhere in the single loop.

Parallel circuits

Parallel: separate branches

In a parallel circuit the components are on separate branches, so the current has more than one path to take.

  • Each branch gets the full voltage of the cell, so lamps stay bright.
  • The current splits at a junction. Where the branches meet again, the branch currents add up to the current from the cell.
  • If one branch breaks, the others keep working.

Why homes use parallel: you can switch one light off without the rest going out, and each device gets full mains voltage.

Parallel circuits

Currents add where branches meet

In a parallel circuit, the current from the cell splits at a junction. Where the branches meet again, the branch currents add up to the current in the main line.

0.5 A = 0.3 A + 0.2 Amain line = branch 1 + branch 2

Common mistake: the current does not always split equally. Here the two lamps have different resistances, so their branch currents are different.

Static electricity

Charge by rubbing

Static electricity is a build-up of charge on an object that isn't flowing. When you rub two insulators together, tiny electrons are transferred from one to the other.

  • The object that gains electrons becomes negatively charged.
  • The object that loses electrons is left positively charged.
  • Rubbing does not make new charge — it just separates positive and negative charges.
Forces between charges

Like charges repel

Charged objects push and pull on each other without touching:

  • Like charges repel — two + charges (or two − charges) push apart.
  • Opposite charges attract — a + and a − charge pull together.

The region around a charge where it can push or pull is called an electric field. We draw it with field lines pointing away from + and towards −.

Common mistake: "opposites attract" is right, but like charges repel — two negatives do not attract each other.

Magnetism

Poles: attract and repel

Every magnet has two ends called poles: a north (N) pole and a south (S) pole. The rule is like static charge:

  • Unlike poles attract — N and S pull together.
  • Like poles repel — N and N (or S and S) push apart.
  • Magnets attract magnetic materials: iron, steel, nickel and cobalt.

Common mistake: just like charges, like poles repel. Two north poles do not attract each other.

Magnetic fields

The field around a bar magnet

A magnetic field is the region around a magnet where it can affect magnetic materials or other magnets. We draw it with field lines that always go out of the N pole and into the S pole.

Earth & the compass

The Earth is a giant magnet

The Earth has its own magnetic field, as if a huge bar magnet sat inside it. A compass contains a tiny magnet that is free to turn.

  • The needle lines up with the Earth's field, so its N end points roughly north.
  • A compass is a handy way to detect and map a magnetic field.
  • Near a bar magnet, the compass needle points along the field lines.
Electromagnets

Magnetism from electricity

When a current flows through a wire it makes a magnetic field. Wind the wire into a coil around an iron core and you get an electromagnet — a magnet you can switch on and off.

You can make an electromagnet stronger by: adding more turns of wire, using a bigger current, or adding an iron core.

Uses: scrapyard cranes (switch off to drop the metal), electric bells, loudspeakers, motors, and maglev trains.

Electric motors

How a motor turns

A wire carrying a current makes its own magnetic field. Put the wire between the poles of a magnet and the two fields interact: the wire feels a force.

In a DC motor, a coil of wire sits between the poles of a magnet. Current flows one way along one side of the coil and the opposite way along the other. So one side is pushed up, the other down, and the coil turns.

Keeping it spinning: DC (direct current) flows one way only. Every half turn, a switching part reverses the current in the coil, so it keeps turning the same way.

There is only one loop (one path), so the charge cannot go anywhere else. The same amount of charge passes every point each second, so the current is the same everywhere. It is not used up by the components.

Recap

The big ideas

Current: flow of charge, measured in amperes (A) with an ammeter (in series).

Potential difference: the energy given to the charge, measured in volts (V) with a voltmeter (in parallel).

Ratings: cells joined + to − add their voltages; match a lamp's voltage rating to the battery.

Resistance: opposes current — more resistance means less current.

R = V ÷ I: resistance in ohms (Ω) = p.d. in volts ÷ current in amps. Conductors: low resistance; insulators: very high.

Series: one loop; current the same everywhere; voltage shared.

Parallel: separate branches; each gets full voltage; current splits.

Junctions: branch currents add up to the main-line current.

Static: rubbing separates charge; like charges repel, opposites attract.

Magnetism: like poles repel; field lines go N → S outside the magnet; electromagnets can be switched on/off.

Motors: a current-carrying coil in a magnetic field feels a force, so it turns.
