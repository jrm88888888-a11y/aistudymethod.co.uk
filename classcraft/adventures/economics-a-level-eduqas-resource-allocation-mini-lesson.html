Mission 1

No one is in charge

  • Every day, millions of buyers and sellers decide what to buy, make and sell.
  • No one gives the orders. Yet shops are mostly stocked with things people want.
  • The price mechanism: changes in prices, driven by demand and supply, decide how scarce resources are used.
  • It answers the basic questions: what to produce, how, and for whom.
  • Prices do three jobs: signalling, incentive and rationing.
Mission 1

Signalling

  • A price change is a signal: it carries information.
  • A rising price says: buyers want more than is on offer here. The good has become scarcer.
  • A falling price says: there is too much on offer. Use resources elsewhere.
  • Buyers and sellers need not know why the price changed (a drought, a fashion, a new use). The price alone tells them how to react.
Mission 1

Incentive (and the role of profit)

  • A higher price makes producing the good more profitable.
  • Firms already in the market produce more: quantity supplied rises (a movement along S).
  • Call this an extension of supply.
  • In time, new firms enter (market supply shifts right), and resources (workers, land, machines) move into the industry.
  • A falling price cuts profit. Firms produce less, and resources move out to better-paid uses.
  • Profit is the reward that drives this. Losses are the warning to leave.
  • So profit is the engine of resource allocation in a free market: it pulls resources to where buyers value them most.
  • Say: "the higher price gives existing firms an incentive to extend supply" — not: "the higher price shifts the supply curve".
Mission 1

Rationing

  • Scarce goods must be shared out somehow.
  • A higher price cuts quantity demanded: some buyers buy less, or stop buying.
  • Call this a contraction of demand.
  • The good goes to those willing and able to pay the price.
  • Price rationing removes a shortage without queues, lotteries or ration books.
  • But "able to pay" depends on income. Price rationing is not the same as giving goods to those who need them most.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls", for a movement along D.

Mission 1

One shock, three jobs: Europe's gas, 2022

  • 2022: Russia cut pipeline gas supplies to Europe. Wholesale gas prices soared.
  • Signal: the price told every user and supplier that gas was now very scarce.
  • Incentive: suppliers shipped in more liquefied natural gas from other countries, by sea.
  • Rationing: factories and households cut their use.
  • Real data: EU gas use, August 2022 to March 2023, was 17.7% lower than the average for those months in 2017–2022 (Eurostat, 2023). The EU had set a voluntary target of 15%.
  • Caution: a mild winter and government campaigns to save energy also played a part. Price was not the only cause.
Mission 1

Product markets and factor markets

  • Product market: households buy goods and services; firms sell.
  • Factor market: firms buy (hire) land, labour and capital; households sell.
  • They are linked. Demand for a factor is derived demand: it comes from demand for what the factor makes.
  • Example: demand for electric cars rises → car prices and profits rise → firms want more battery engineers → engineers' wages rise.
  • Higher wages signal workers to train for and move into that job.
  • Income earned in factor markets is what households spend in product markets.

Mission 1Evaluate

How well does the price mechanism allocate resources?

Strengths

  • Uses information spread across millions of people. No planner could gather it all (Hayek, 1945).
  • Automatic: signals and incentives work without orders.
  • Rewards efficiency and new ideas through profit.

Limits. It depends on …

  • Income distribution: price rations by ability to pay, so the poorest can be priced out of essentials.
  • Market failure: prices miss external costs and benefits (pollution), and public goods may not be provided at all.
  • Information and competition: monopoly power or poor information distort the signals.
  • Speed of response: factors move slowly (new skills, new mines), so prices can swing sharply.
  • Judge: a powerful way to coordinate choices, but its results are only as good as the prices it works with.
Mission check

?A new type of battery greatly increases demand for lithium. The price of lithium rises sharply. Which statement matches each function of price correctly?

One price change does three jobs at once: it informs, it rewards, and it shares out what is scarce.

Mission 2

Product markets and factor markets

  • Product markets: goods and services. Households buy, firms sell.
  • Factor markets: land, labour, capital, enterprise. Firms buy, households sell.
  • The two are linked: firms hire factors because they want to make products.
  • So a change in a product market spreads to the factor markets that serve it, and back again through incomes.
Mission 2

Joint demand: goods used together

  • Joint demand: goods bought together, such as games consoles and games, or printers and ink. Also called complements.
  • Step 1: price of printers falls → extension of demand for printers.
  • Step 2: more printers in use → demand for ink shifts right.
  • Step 3: ink price and quantity rise.
  • The price of a complement is a shift factor of demand.
  • Say: "demand for ink increases (shifts)" — not: "the demand for printers shifts" (a change in a good's own price is a movement).
Mission 2

Competitive demand: goods that replace each other

  • Competitive demand: goods that are substitutes, such as coffee and tea, or train and coach travel.
  • Price of coffee rises → contraction of demand for coffee → demand for tea shifts right.
  • The closer the substitutes, the bigger the shift.
Quick check
?The price of printers falls sharply. What happens in the market for printer ink?
Mission 2

Derived demand

  • Derived demand: demand for something because of what it helps to produce.
  • Demand for factors is derived from demand for products.
  • More homes wanted → more demand for bricklayers, bricks and building land.
  • So a rise in demand in a product market shifts demand in a factor market.
Quick check
?More people want to eat in restaurants, so the demand for chefs rises. What kind of demand for chefs is this?

Mission 3

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 3

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 3

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
Mission 3

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 3

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 3

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 3Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?
Mission check

?Many workers say they should save more for retirement but do not start. In one scheme, workers sign up now to put part of each future pay rise into their pension; nothing leaves today's pay. Many more workers saved more under the scheme. Which bias does the design best get round?

People who mean well today often fail tomorrow; designs that commit the future self can close the gap.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The price mechanism allocates scarce resources with no one in charge: prices signal, give incentives and ration.
  • Product and factor markets are linked: demand for factors is derived from demand for goods.
  • It works well when prices reflect all costs and benefits; it struggles with market failure and unequal incomes.

Mission 2

  • Joint demand (complements) move together; competitive demand (substitutes) move in opposite directions.
  • Derived demand links product markets to factor markets.

Mission 3

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.
