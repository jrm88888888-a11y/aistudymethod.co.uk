Mission 1

Three ways to be a couple

  • Le mariage: a civil ceremony at the town hall (la mairie). Only the civil marriage is legally valid; a religious wedding may follow it, never replace it.
  • Le PACS (pacte civil de solidarité, since 1999): a contract between two adults, of the same or different sex. Simpler to sign and to end than a marriage.
  • L'union libre (also le concubinage): living together with no legal tie.
  • 2025: 251,000 marriages, of which about 7,000 between two people of the same sex.
  • 2024: 197,200 PACS (about 10,400 same-sex; latest year available). Marriage is still more common.
  • New trends: living together before marrying is now the norm; some couples stay together without living together (les couples non cohabitants).
  • se marier (avec) – to get married (to) · se pacser – to sign a PACS · vivre en couple – to live as a couple
Mission 1

Laws that changed the family

  • 1975: divorce by mutual consent (le divorce par consentement mutuel) becomes possible.
  • 1999: the PACS is created.
  • 2013: le mariage pour tous – same-sex couples can marry and adopt (law of 17 May 2013). The bill was fiercely debated, with large demonstrations both for and against.
  • 2017: a divorce by mutual consent normally no longer needs a judge: the spouses sign an agreement drawn up by their lawyers.
  • 2021: medically assisted reproduction (la PMA) is opened to female couples and single women (bioethics law of 2 August 2021).
  • Pattern: the law has followed changes in how people live, and each change was debated.

Mission 1

New family shapes

  • INSEE (France's statistics office), 2020, families with children under 18:
  • One in four is a single-parent family (une famille monoparentale): 25%, double the 1990 share (12%). About four in five are headed by the mother.
  • 9% are blended families (des familles recomposées): a couple living with at least one child from a previous relationship.
  • Children under 18: 67% live with both parents, 22% in a single-parent family, 11% in a blended family.
  • Une famille homoparentale: a family with two parents of the same sex.
  • un beau-père / une belle-mère – a stepfather / stepmother (also father-/mother-in-law)
  • un demi-frère / une demi-sœur – a half-brother / half-sister (everyday French also uses it for a stepbrother or stepsister)
  • Watch out: the adjectives agree with famille: une famille monoparentale / recomposée, des familles monoparentales / recomposées. Une famille nombreuse = a large family (3+ children), not a numerous one.
Mission 1

Fewer babies, born later

  • 2025: 645,000 births and 651,000 deaths. For the first time since the Second World War, France had more deaths than births.
  • Fertility: 1.56 children per woman (1.61 in 2024). The generations are no longer fully replaced (about 2.1 needed).
  • Women have children later: average age at childbirth 31.2.
  • Marriage and children are no longer linked: about six births in ten (59.7% in 2024) are to unmarried parents.
  • Across the French-speaking world: in Quebec, over four couples in ten (42.7% in 2021) live in union libre, against 16.9% in the rest of Canada.

Mission 1

Grandparents, parents and children

  • People live longer: life expectancy in 2025, 85.9 for women and 80.3 for men. Many children know their great-grandparents.
  • Grandparents often look after grandchildren (garder les petits-enfants) and help adult children with money or housing.
  • La génération pivot (sandwich generation): adults in their 50s and 60s who support their children and also care for ageing parents. Un(e) aidant(e) – an unpaid carer.
  • Un EHPAD – a care home for dependent older people. Whether to move a parent there is a common family dilemma.
  • Concerns: le fossé des générations (the generation gap) over screens, values and work; young adults who stay longer at home because of the cost of housing; conflict after separation (custody, la garde alternée – shared custody).
  • s'entendre bien avec – to get on well with · se disputer – to argue · soutenir – to support

Mission 1

La famille traditionnelle est-elle en crise ? Argue both sides

  • Arguments made by those who see a crisis or a loss:
  • Les séparations fragilisent les enfants. – Separations make children more vulnerable.
  • Le mariage protégeait mieux les conjoints et les enfants. – Marriage protected spouses and children better.
  • La baisse de la natalité fait craindre pour l'avenir des retraites. – The falling birth rate raises fears about future pensions.
  • Arguments made by those who see change, not crisis:
  • La famille n'a pas disparu, elle s'est diversifiée. – The family has not disappeared; it has diversified.
  • Ce qui compte, c'est l'amour et la stabilité, pas la forme de la famille. – What matters is love and stability, not the shape of the family.
  • Les liens entre générations restent très forts. – Ties between generations remain very strong.
  • Balance: Plutôt qu'une crise, on assiste à une transformation. – Rather than a crisis, we are seeing a transformation.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Eight young people in ten in France get the bac. So why do so many students feel under pressure?

Make a guess: Eight young people in ten in France get the bac. So why do so many students feel under pressure?

Mission 2

From 3 to 18

  • L'école maternelle (3–6): compulsory from age 3 since 2019.
  • L'école élémentaire (6–11): from CP to CM2.
  • Le collège (11–15): from la sixième to la troisième, ending with the diplôme national du brevet.
  • Le lycée (15–18): la seconde, la première, la terminale. Three tracks (voies): general, technological, vocational (professionnelle).
  • Since 2020, young people aged 16–18 must be in education, training or work.
  • Watch out: le collège is a secondary school for 11–15-year-olds, not a college. Year groups count down: la sixième is the first year of collège.
  • un(e) collégien(ne) · un(e) lycéen(ne) · un(e) étudiant(e) – a student in higher education only
Mission 2

The bac

  • Le baccalauréat ends lycée and opens the door to higher education.
  • Since the 2021 reform: 40% continuous assessment (le contrôle continu), 60% final exams.
  • Final exams: French (in première), philosophy, two specialist subjects (les spécialités), and le grand oral, a 20-minute oral.
  • New: since June 2026, pupils in the general and technological tracks also sit a two-hour maths exam in première (it counts from the 2027 bac).
  • 2025 results: 91.6% passed (general track 96.2%, technological 90.9%, vocational 83.9%).
  • 80.1% of a generation now hold the bac.
  • Une mention – a distinction (assez bien, bien, très bien). Marks are out of 20; 10 is a pass.
  • Watch out: passer le bac = to sit the bac. To pass it: réussir le bac or avoir le bac.

Mission 2

After the bac

  • Students apply on Parcoursup (since 2018), a national platform: up to 10 wishes (vœux); each course ranks applicants.
  • L'université: licence (3 years), master (+2), doctorat (+3). Open to bac holders; selection for some courses.
  • 2026–27 fees for French and EU students: €178 a year for a licence, €255 for a master. Non-EU students can be charged €2,902 / €3,950; universities may exempt some of them (a May 2026 decree caps these exemptions).
  • Les grandes écoles (engineering, business, Sciences Po …): selective, often after two years of classes préparatoires and a competitive exam (un concours).
  • Shorter, job-focused courses: BTS (2 years), BUT (3 years).
  • L'alternance: study combined with paid work in a company (l'apprentissage).
Mission 2

Student issues

  • La précarité étudiante: many students struggle with rent, food and transport.
  • Housing: student halls run by the CROUS are cheap but too few; private rents in big cities are high.
  • Les bourses sur critères sociaux: means-tested grants.
  • Since 4 May 2026, a meal in a CROUS university restaurant costs €1 for every student (before, only grant holders and students in difficulty).
  • Many students work alongside their studies (un job étudiant), which can hurt results.
  • Other worries: stress over Parcoursup, choosing a path at 15–17, dropping out in the first year (le décrochage).
  • joindre les deux bouts – to make ends meet · avoir recours à l'aide alimentaire – to turn to food aid

Mission 2

Life at school: today's debates

  • Phones: banned in écoles and collèges (2018 law), and in lycées since September 2026 (law of 24 August 2026).
  • Bullying (le harcèlement scolaire) has been a criminal offence since 2022. Schools run the pHARe anti-bullying programme.
  • Workload: long school days and heavy terminale timetables; debate about homework and stress.
  • Equality of opportunity: OECD PISA studies regularly find that in France pupils' results depend more on their social background than in the average OECD country.
  • Repeating a year (le redoublement) is now rare.
  • Religion at school and laïcité: see another lesson.

Mission 2

Faut-il sélectionner à l'entrée de l'université ? Argue both sides

  • Arguments made by supporters of selection:
  • La sélection oriente les étudiants vers des filières où ils ont des chances de réussir. – It steers students to courses where they can succeed.
  • Elle réduit l'échec en première année. – It reduces first-year failure.
  • Arguments made by opponents:
  • Le bac doit rester la clé d'entrée à l'université. – The bac should remain the key to university.
  • La sélection renforce les inégalités sociales. – Selection reinforces social inequality.
  • Les algorithmes de Parcoursup manquent de transparence. – Parcoursup's algorithms lack transparency.
  • Balance: Tout dépend de la manière dont on accompagne les étudiants. – It all depends on how students are supported.

Mission 3

Working life in France

  • Les 35 heures: the legal working week since the Aubry laws (1998 and 2000). Hours beyond it are overtime (les heures supplémentaires), paid extra.
  • Les congés payés: five weeks of paid holiday (the first two weeks were won in 1936, the fifth in 1982).
  • Le SMIC: the national minimum wage, raised every year in line with prices.
  • Retirement: the 2023 reform began raising the minimum age from 62 to 64. In December 2025 Parliament suspended it: the legal age stays at 62 years and 9 months until January 2028.
  • Pôle emploi became France Travail in 2024: the public job centre.
  • travailler à temps plein / à temps partiel – to work full-time / part-time
Mission 3

Contracts and the changing work scene

  • Un CDI (contrat à durée indéterminée) – a permanent contract. Un CDD (à durée déterminée) – a fixed-term contract. L'intérim – temping.
  • Young people often start with a CDD, an internship (un stage) or work-study (l'alternance).
  • Part-time jobs for students: un job d'été, du baby-sitting, la restauration rapide.
  • Remote work (le télétravail) spread after 2020, mainly in office jobs.
  • Platform work (l'ubérisation): delivery riders and drivers, often self-employed (auto-entrepreneurs), with fewer protections.
  • Attitudes: many workers say they look for meaning (le sens du travail) and a better work–life balance (l'équilibre vie professionnelle – vie privée).
  • Watch out: un stage = an internship or work placement (it can also be a short training course), never a theatre stage.

Mission 3

The right to strike

  • A constitutional right: the 1946 Preamble, still in force, says « Le droit de grève s'exerce dans le cadre des lois qui le réglementent. »
  • Strikers are not paid for strike days. In public services, unions must give 5 days' notice (un préavis).
  • Minimum service: since 2007, transport staff must say 48 hours ahead whether they will strike, so users can be told which services will run; since 2008, primary schools must look after pupils during a teachers' strike.
  • Key movements:
  • May 1968: a general strike by millions; the accords de Grenelle raise the minimum wage by 35%.
  • 1995: weeks of transport strikes against a social-security and pensions plan.
  • 2019–2020: a long rail and metro strike against a pensions reform.
  • 2023: 14 national days of strikes and marches against raising the pension age to 64.
  • faire grève (no article) · être en grève · un(e) gréviste · un syndicat – a trade union · une manifestation – a protest march (not a "manifestation"). Protest in depth.

Mission 3

Gender equality at work

  • INSEE, private sector, 2024:
  • On average, women earn 21.8% less than men over the year.
  • At the same working time, the gap is about 14%.
  • In the same job with the same employer, it is 3.6%.
  • Why the gap: more women work part-time; jobs and sectors differ (care, education); careers slow after children; few women at the top (le plafond de verre – the glass ceiling).
  • Laws: equal pay for equal work (1972); 40% women on the boards of large companies (Copé-Zimmermann law, 2011); a gender-equality index that companies with 50+ staff must publish (phased in 2019–2020); 30% women among senior executives of large firms by 2026, 40% by 2029 (Rixain law, 2021).

  • Supporters: C'est souvent le seul moyen de se faire entendre. – It is often the only way to be heard.

  • Critics: Il faut recruter selon les compétences, pas selon le sexe. – Recruitment should be by skills, not sex.

Mission 4

The right to strike

  • 1864: going on strike stops being a crime.
  • 1884: trade unions become legal (loi Waldeck-Rousseau).
  • 1946: the right to strike is written into the Preamble of the Constitution: « Le droit de grève s'exerce dans le cadre des lois qui le réglementent. » It still applies today.
  • Limits:
  • In public services, unions must give five days' notice (un préavis de grève).
  • Since 2007, transport staff must say 48 hours ahead if they will strike.
  • Since 2008, primary schools must offer a minimum childcare service on strike days.
  • Strikers are not paid for the days they strike.
  • faire grève / être en grève – to strike / be on strike (no article) · un(e) gréviste – a striker
  • Watch out: une manifestation = a demonstration, a protest march. Manifester = to demonstrate; un(e) manifestant(e) = a protester.
Mission 4

The unions: few members, real power

  • Main union confederations: CGT, CFDT, FO, CFE-CGC, CFTC; also UNSA, Solidaires and FSU (strong in education).
  • Membership is low: about 10% of employees (DARES, 2019): about 18% in the public sector, 8% in the private sector.
  • Influence is much wider than membership:
  • unions negotiate agreements by sector that cover almost all private-sector employees;
  • workplace elections measure their weight. Since 2017 the CFDT has been ahead of the CGT in the private sector;
  • they call strikes and days of protest (journées de mobilisation).
  • L'intersyndicale: all the main unions acting together, as in 2023.
  • Your turn: "The unions called a day of action."

Mission 4

Do strikes work? Some victories

  • 1936: a wave of strikes and factory occupations under the Front populaire government. The accords de Matignon bring pay rises; new laws give two weeks' paid holiday and the 40-hour week.
  • May 1968: student protests, then a general strike by millions of workers. The Grenelle talks (les accords de Grenelle) end with the minimum wage raised by 35%.
  • 1995: weeks of public-sector strikes. The government drops its pension reform for public-sector special schemes.
  • 2006: students and unions protest against the CPE, a contract making it easier to dismiss under-26s. The government abandons it in April 2006, although Parliament had passed it.
  • 2018: the gilets jaunes (yellow vests), organised online without unions, occupy roundabouts. The fuel-tax rise is cancelled and about €10 billion of measures follow.
  • 2024: farmers block motorways; the government drops a planned tax rise on farm diesel.
Mission 4

... and the limits: pensions, 2023

  • The reform: legal retirement age from 62 to 64.
  • January–June 2023: 14 national days of action called by the united unions.
  • 16 March 2023: the government uses article 49.3 of the Constitution. The bill passes without a final vote in the National Assembly, unless a motion of censure brings the government down. The motion fails by 9 votes.
  • 14 April 2023: the Constitutional Council approves the core of the law. It is promulgated.
  • Afterwards: the 2024 elections left no majority in the Assembly. In October 2025 the government proposed suspending the age rise to avoid censure. In December 2025 Parliament voted it: the age is frozen at 62 years and 9 months until January 2028, after the 2027 presidential election.
  • Protest supporters call this a delayed victory. Others see it as a parliamentary deal.

Mission 4

Who holds power: the street or the ballot box?

  • Representative view: voters elect a President and a Parliament; they decide. Prime Minister Jean-Pierre Raffarin (2003): « Ce n'est pas la rue qui gouverne. »
  • Social view: between elections, strikes and protests let citizens correct decisions they never voted for. The Constitution protects both rights.
  • Other players:
  • the Constitutional Council, which can strike out a law;
  • the référendum d'initiative partagée: in 2023 opponents tried to force a referendum on pensions. The Council rejected both requests.
  • Counting the crowd is political too. 7 March 2023: 1.28 million marchers selon le ministère de l'Intérieur, 3.5 million selon la CGT.
  • selon la police / selon les organisateurs – according to the police / organisers
Mission 4

Attitudes: argue both sides

  • For strikes and protests (arguments supporters make):
  • La grève est souvent le seul moyen de se faire entendre. – Striking is often the only way to be heard.
  • Sans les grèves de 1936, les salariés n'auraient pas obtenu les congés payés. – Without the 1936 strikes, workers would not have won paid holidays.
  • Against (arguments critics make):
  • Les usagers paient le prix d'un conflit qui ne les concerne pas. – Service users pay the price of a dispute that is not theirs.
  • Une minorité ne devrait pas pouvoir bloquer tout un pays. – A minority should not be able to block a whole country.
  • Les grèves coûtent cher à l'économie. – Strikes are costly for the economy.
  • Attitudes depend on the sector and the cause. In 2023 polls repeatedly found most French people opposed to the reform, even when transport was disrupted.

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Couples: mariage (251,000 in 2025), PACS (since 1999; 197,200 in 2024), union libre. Key laws: 1975, 1999, 2013, 2017, 2021.
  • Family shapes (INSEE 2020): 25% single-parent, 9% blended; six births in ten outside marriage; fertility 1.56 in 2025.
  • The family has diversified: argue "crisis" vs "transformation" with evidence.

Mission 2

  • Maternelle (compulsory from 3) → élémentaire → collège (brevet) → lycée (bac). Passer = sit; réussir / avoir = pass.
  • Bac 2025: 91.6% pass; 80.1% of a generation. Parcoursup since 2018; licence €178 a year.
  • Student issues: précarité, housing, grants, €1 CROUS meals for all since May 2026; phones banned up to lycée since September 2026.

Mission 3

  • 35-hour week (1998/2000), five weeks' paid holiday, SMIC; pension age held at 62 years 9 months until January 2028.
  • Right to strike in the 1946 Preamble; key movements 1968, 1995, 2019–20, 2023. Faire grève, être en grève depuis + present.
  • Pay gap (INSEE 2024): 21.8% overall, about 14% at equal hours, 3.6% in the same job; laws 1972, 2011, 2019 index, 2021.

Mission 4

  • Right to strike: legal since 1864, in the Constitution since 1946; notice rules in public services and transport.
  • Unions: about 10% membership but wide influence (CGT, CFDT, FO…); united intersyndicale in 2023.
  • Effective? Victories in 1936, 1968, 1995, 2006, 2018; the 2023 reform passed (49.3), then the age rise was suspended in December 2025.
