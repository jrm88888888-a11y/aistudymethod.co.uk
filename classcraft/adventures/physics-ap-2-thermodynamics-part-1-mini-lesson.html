Mission 1

Mission 1: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Make a guess: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Mission 1

Pressure and the mole

  • P = F/A, F the force perpendicular to the surface. 1 Pa = 1 N/m².
  • A gas pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches.
  • 1 mole: 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms or molecules). This is Avogadro's number, N0: n = N/N0.
  • Molar mass M: the mass of one mole. n = total mass ÷ M.
Quick check
?A cylinder contains 0.64 kg of oxygen gas, which consists of O2 molecules. The molar mass of oxygen atoms is 16 g/mol, and N0 = 6.02 × 10²³ per mole. How many oxygen molecules are in the cylinder?
Quick check
?Predict first. Students measure the pressure of three samples of gas, each sealed in a rigid container, at several temperatures between 0 °C and 100 °C. The samples are of different gases and different amounts, and each behaves as an ideal gas. For each sample they plot pressure against temperature in °C and get a straight line. They then extend each best-fit line to zero pressure. What do they find?
Mission 1

Absolute zero by extrapolation

  • Fixed volume of gas: measure pressure at several temperatures, 0–100 °C.
  • Plot pressure against θ in °C: a straight line.
  • Extend the line to zero pressure: it meets the axis at about −273 °C.
  • Every gas, any amount: the same intercept.
  • This is absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C.
  • T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15 (often + 273).
Mission 1

The ideal gas equation

  • PV = nRT = NkB T
  • R = 8.31 J/(mol·K) (molar gas constant).
  • kB = R/N0 = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K (Boltzmann constant).
  • SI units only: pressure in Pa, V in m³, T in K.
Mission 1

Worked example: a gas cylinder

  • Rigid cylinder of helium: V = 0.020 m³, pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa, θ = 20 °C → T = 293 K.
  • Moles: n = PV/RT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(8.31 × 293) = 1.6 mol.
  • Molecules: N = PV/(kB T) = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 293) = 9.9 × 10²³.
  • Warmed to 60 °C (333 K), V fixed: pressure ∝ T, so the pressure = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 333/293 = 2.3 × 10⁵ Pa.
Quick check
?A rigid steel tank contains an ideal gas at 27°C and a pressure of 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa. A valve is opened until half of the gas has escaped, and then the gas remaining in the tank is heated to 327°C. What is the pressure of the gas now?
Mission 1

Reading gas graphs

  • P against V, constant T: a curve (PV = constant). Hotter gas: curve farther from the origin. Each curve is an isotherm.
  • PV against P, constant T: horizontal line.
  • V against T (constant P) and P against T (constant V): straight lines through 0 K.
  • On P–V axes, the product PV at a point is proportional to T (fixed amount of gas).
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows three states, A, B and C, of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Which ranks the temperatures of the gas in the three states, from highest to lowest?
Mission check

?A sealed, rigid container holds an ideal gas at 7 °C. The gas is heated to 287 °C. How does its new pressure compare with its starting pressure?

Pressure ∝ T in kelvin. Every gas line points to the same zero: 0 K = −273 °C, absolute zero.

Mission 2

The ideal gas model

  • A very large number of particles (atoms or molecules) in rapid, random motion.
  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • Collisions with each other and the walls are perfectly elastic: no kinetic energy lost.
  • Collision time is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  • No forces between particles except during collisions.
  • An ideal gas is a model. Real gases only approximate it.
Quick check
?Careful measurements show that at very high pressures a real gas no longer obeys PV = nRT closely. Which reasoning, based on the assumptions of the ideal-gas model, best supports this observation?
Mission 2

Gas atoms hitting the walls

  • Gas atoms move randomly, colliding with each other and with the walls. Every collision conserves momentum (atoms + wall system).
  • Smooth wall, elastic collision: the component parallel to the wall is unchanged; the perpendicular component reverses.
  • Change in the atom's momentum: |Δp| = 2mv⊥, directed away from the wall. The wall gets an equal and opposite impulse.
  • About 10²⁷ hits on each square meter every second → a steady average force. Pressure P = total perpendicular force ÷ area.
  • Pressure exists throughout the gas, not only at the walls: atoms strike any surface placed inside it.
  • Pressure depends on how hard and how often the atoms hit. Both grow with the atoms' speed.
  • Smaller volume (T fixed): same speeds, but hits on each square meter are more frequent → pressure rises.
Quick check
?The diagram shows, from above, a helium atom of mass 6.64 × 10⁻²⁷ kg that strikes a fixed, smooth wall of its container with speed 1.20 × 10³ m/s and rebounds elastically with the same speed. The angles are marked on the diagram. What is the magnitude of the change in the atom's momentum during the collision?
Mission 2

Temperature measures mean kinetic energy

  • Kavg = (3/2)kB T = ½mvrms²
  • Mean translational kinetic energy per molecule ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Translational: moving from place to place. Molecules such as N2 also spin; that energy is extra.
  • Same temperature → same mean translational kinetic energy, whatever the gas.
  • In this model, as T → 0 K this mean kinetic energy → 0: absolute zero.
  • kB = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K, the Boltzmann constant.
Quick check
?A rigid, sealed container holds an ideal gas. The absolute temperature of the gas is doubled. Using the kinetic model, in which the pressure comes from atoms colliding with the walls, by what factor is the pressure of the gas multiplied?
Mission 2

Worked example: root-mean-square speed

  • rms speed: square every speed, take the mean, then the square root.
  • vrms = √(3kB T/m)
  • Speeds 300, 400, 500 m/s: mean = 400; rms = √((300² + 400² + 500²)/3) = 408 m/s. Not the same.
  • Nitrogen, N2, at 300 K.
  • m = 0.028 kg/mol ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg.
  • Mean translational kinetic energy = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.
  • rms speed = √(3kB T/m) = √(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 ÷ 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 517 m/s.
Mission 2

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

  • Number of molecules per unit speed interval against speed: no molecules at zero speed; a peak; a long tail to high speeds.
  • Not symmetric: most probable speed < mean speed < rms speed.
  • N2 at 300 K: about 420, 480, 520 m/s.
  • Higher temperature: peak moves to higher speed, becomes lower and broader.
  • Same number of molecules: the area under the curve stays the same.
Quick check
?The graph shows the distributions of atomic speeds for two samples, 1 and 2, of the same ideal gas. The two samples contain the same number of atoms. Which claim about the temperatures of the samples, with its reasoning, is correct?
Mission 2

Internal energy of an ideal gas

  • Internal energy: total random kinetic energy of the particles + total potential energy from the forces between them.
  • U = kinetic energy of the atoms + potential energy of their configuration.
  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles → no potential energy. U is all kinetic.
  • Monatomic: U = (3/2)nRT = (3/2)NkB T
  • U depends only on T (for a fixed amount). Same T → same U, whatever the volume.
Mission 2

When is a real gas nearly ideal?

  • Low pressure and low density: molecules far apart, so their own volume is negligible.
  • High temperature (well above boiling point): fast molecules; attractive forces have little effect.
  • At high pressure, molecular volume is no longer negligible.
  • Near liquefying, attractions pull molecules back from the walls: pressure lower than PV = nRT predicts.
Mission check

?A container holds a mixture of hydrogen (H2) and oxygen (O2) at one temperature. An oxygen molecule has 16 times the mass of a hydrogen molecule. What is (rms speed of the hydrogen molecules)/(rms speed of the oxygen molecules)?

Temperature fixes the mean translational kinetic energy. For air (mostly N2) at room temperature that means rms speeds near 500 m/s, faster than a passenger jet.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Make a guess: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Quick check
?A 0.20 kg copper block at 80 °C is lowered into a large tank containing 200 kg of water at 20 °C. The water has far more internal energy than the block. Which statement describes the net thermal energy transfer?
Mission 3

Which way does energy flow?

  • Temperature decides the direction: net energy transfer is always from higher to lower temperature.
  • Not "more energy to less": a hot cup can lose energy to a huge cold bath.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. An object does not "contain heat".
  • Two systems are in thermal contact if energy can pass between them by thermal processes. Heating: energy in; cooling: energy out.
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net energy transfer between objects in contact. They are at the same temperature.
Mission 3

Why hot to cold? Collisions

  • At the boundary, atoms of the two systems collide.
  • In a collision, energy is most likely to pass from the higher-energy atom to the lower-energy one. Not in every collision.
  • Over many collisions: a net transfer from the hotter system to the colder one.
  • The most probable final state: both at the same temperature. Then transfers each way balance: thermal equilibrium.
Quick check
?Sample P is warmed from 10 °C to 30 °C. Sample Q is warmed from 290 K to 310 K. Which statement about the two temperature changes is correct?
Mission 3

Internal energy

  • Internal energy = total random kinetic energy + total potential energy of the particles.
  • Kinetic energy: random motion (vibration in solids; moving about in liquids and gases).
  • Potential energy: from the forces between particles, so it depends on their configuration (separation).
  • The energies are randomly distributed: particles have a range of energies.
  • Not included: the kinetic energy of the whole object moving, or its gravitational potential energy.
  • Heat it → internal energy rises, and (unless it is changing phase) its temperature rises.
Quick check
?A sealed container of ideal gas rests on a level table. The container is heated, and the temperature of the gas increases. Which statement describes the gas and the container after the heating?
Mission check

?A thermometer at room temperature, 20 °C, is placed in a cup of water at 70 °C. Its reading rises for a while and then stays steady. Why does the steady reading give the temperature of the water?

Metal and wood left in the same room reach thermal equilibrium with it: same temperature.

So the colder feeling is not telling you the temperature.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A metal bench and a wooden bench sit in the same cold park. Why does the metal one feel colder?

Make a guess: A metal bench and a wooden bench sit in the same cold park. Why does the metal one feel colder?

Mission 4

Three ways energy moves

  • Conduction: energy passed through a material, particle to particle. The material stays put.
  • Convection: a warm fluid (liquid or gas) moves and carries its energy with it.
  • Radiation: electromagnetic waves, mostly infrared at everyday temperatures. Needs no medium.
  • Net energy transfer is always from hotter to colder, by itself. It stops when the temperatures are equal: thermal equilibrium.
  • "Cold" is not a thing that flows. Something cools because it loses energy.
Quick check
?A vacuum flask has double glass walls with a vacuum between them. Which statement explains how the vacuum layer helps keep hot coffee in the flask hot?
Quick check
?One end of a glass rod is held in a flame. How does the molecular model explain conduction of energy along the rod?
Mission 4

Conduction: the particle picture

  • Hot end: particles vibrate with more kinetic energy on average.
  • They collide with slower neighbors and pass kinetic energy on, and so on along the material.
  • In any one collision, energy is most likely to pass from the higher-energy atom to the lower-energy one, not in every collision. Over many collisions the net flow is hot → cold.
  • Metals conduct far better: they also have free electrons that carry energy quickly through the lattice.
Mission 4

The rate of conduction

  • Q/Δt = kAΔT/L
  • A: area of the face. L: thickness. ΔT: temperature difference across it.
  • k: thermal conductivity, W/(m·K). A property of the material alone, set by how its atoms are arranged and interact.
  • Typical k: copper ~400 · steel ~50 · glass ~1 · wood ~0.1 · still air ~0.025.
  • More area, bigger temperature difference, thinner layer → faster flow.
Mission 4

Worked example: a window

  • Single-glazed window: area 1.5 m², glass 4.0 mm thick, k = 0.80 W/(m·K).
  • Inner face of the glass 6.3 °C, outer face 5.7 °C.
  • ΔT = 0.60 K, L = 4.0 × 10⁻³ m.
  • Q/Δt = (0.80 × 1.5 × 0.60)/(4.0 × 10⁻³) = 180 W.
  • Only 0.6 K across the glass, yet 180 W flows: glass is thin. Most of the insulation comes from the thin layers of still air on each side.
Quick check
?A brick wall has area 12 m² and thickness 0.20 m; its thermal conductivity is 0.60 W/(m·K). Its inner face is at 18 °C and its outer face at 3 °C. What is the rate of energy transfer through the wall by conduction?
Mission 4

Convection

  • Fluid near a heater warms and expands: same mass, more volume, so lower density.
  • Cooler, denser fluid around it sinks and pushes the warm fluid up (buoyancy).
  • The warm fluid cools, becomes denser, sinks again: a convection current.
  • Only in fluids: solid particles cannot flow.
  • Not "heat rises". Warm fluid rises, because it is less dense.
Mission 4

Radiation

  • Every object above absolute zero emits electromagnetic radiation.
  • Hotter objects emit far more, and at shorter wavelengths.
  • The only mechanism that crosses a vacuum: how the Sun's energy reaches Earth.
  • Dull, dark surfaces are good emitters and good absorbers; shiny surfaces are poor at both.
Quick check
?In some refrigerators the cooling unit is at the top of the cabinet, not the bottom. Why does this cool the whole cabinet well?
Mission check

?Two slabs, P and Q, have the same area and the same thickness, and the same temperature difference is held across each. P is copper (k = 400 W/(m·K)) and Q is glass (k = 0.80 W/(m·K)). Which statement about the rates of energy transfer by conduction is correct?

The benches are at the same temperature. Steel's much larger k means it conducts energy away from your warm hand far faster, so your skin cools quickly and the metal feels colder.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Extrapolating pressure against θ (°C) to zero pressure gives absolute zero, −273 °C.
  • PV = nRT = NkBT, N = nN0.

Mission 2

  • Ideal gas: many particles in random motion, negligible volume, elastic collisions, no forces except in collisions.
  • Pressure comes from momentum changes as particles hit a surface.
  • Mean translational kinetic energy per particle ∝ absolute temperature: Kavg = (3/2)kB T. Same T, same mean translational kinetic energy; lighter particles move faster.
  • Maxwell–Boltzmann curve: hotter → peak at higher speed, lower and broader.
  • Ideal gas internal energy is all kinetic: U = (3/2)nRT for a monatomic gas.

Mission 3

  • Net energy transfer goes from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium (same temperature, no net transfer).
  • Internal energy = random kinetic energy + potential energy of the particles' configuration. T (K) ≈ temperature in °C + 273; temperature changes are the same in K and °C.
  • Changing an object's internal energy does not change the motion of the object as a whole.

Mission 4

  • Conduction, convection, radiation; net transfer is always hotter → colder. Only radiation crosses a vacuum.
  • Q/Δt = kAΔT/L; k is a property of the material.
  • Convection: warm fluid expands, becomes less dense and is pushed up by denser fluid.
