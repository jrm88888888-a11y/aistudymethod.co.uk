Mission 1

Mission 1: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 1

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
Mission 1

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • λ = h/p, where p is the momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: Eₖ = p²/2m, so p = √(2mEₖ) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the electron as non-relativistic. What is the de Broglie wavelength of the electron?
Quick check
?Predict first. In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a potential difference of a few kilovolts pass through a thin film of graphite and strike a fluorescent screen, where bright concentric rings are seen. How does this experiment provide evidence for the wave nature of matter?
Mission 1

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 1

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√V. Four times the p.d. halves λ.
Quick check
?Predict first. Electrons are sent towards a double slit at such a low rate that only one electron is in the apparatus at any time. Each electron is recorded as a single dot on a detector screen. At first the dots seem to be scattered at random, but after many thousands of electrons they form a pattern of bright and dark fringes. What do these results show about each electron?
Mission 1

One electron at a time

  • Electrons sent through a double slit so slowly that only one is in the apparatus at a time.
  • Each arrives as a single dot: particle-like.
  • After thousands of dots, interference fringes appear.
  • So each electron is detected at a point, but wave interference sets where it is likely to land.
Mission 1

Where is the first minimum?

  • A beam of particles scattered by an object of size D (for example a nucleus) gives a diffraction pattern.
  • First minimum of intensity near sin θ ≈ λ/D. This is an estimate, not a data-booklet equation: it has the same form as the booklet's single-slit θ = λ/b (a round object gives about 1.22λ/D).
  • Measure θ, know λ = h/p → estimate D.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → the minimum moves to a smaller angle.
Quick check
?A beam of high-energy electrons is scattered by the nuclei in a thin target. The first minimum of scattered intensity is at 40.0° to the beam, where sin θ ≈ λ/D. The momentum of the electrons is then doubled. At what angle is the first minimum now?
Mission 1

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A cricket ball at 30 m s⁻¹: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission check

?A beam of electrons and a beam of protons are each accelerated from rest through 100 V and aimed at the same crystal, whose rows of atoms are 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Which beam shows clear diffraction?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. They diffract when it is close to the size of the gap, as with the spacing of carbon atoms.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Make a guess: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Mission 2

Two ways to measure a nucleus

  • Alpha particles: closest approach gives an upper limit on R.
  • Raise the alpha energy: at a few tens of MeV, scattering departs from Rutherford's prediction. The closest approach there ≈ R.
  • Electrons: not affected by the strong force. Their diffraction by nuclei gives a more accurate R.
Mission 2

Electron diffraction by nuclei

  • High-energy electrons (hundreds of MeV): λ ≈ 10⁻¹⁵ m, about the size of a nucleus.
  • Beam through a thin target; count scattered electrons at each angle.
  • Intensity falls with angle, with a first minimum (a dip, not zero).
  • First minimum: sin θ ≈ λ/D, D = diameter.
Quick check
?In alpha-particle scattering, the numbers scattered through large angles agree with Rutherford's prediction, which assumes that only the electric repulsion acts, at low energies. At kinetic energies of a few tens of MeV the measured numbers fall below the prediction. What is the reason?
Mission 2

Radius and nucleon number

  • Measured radii fit one rule:
  • R = R0A1/3, A = nucleon number. R0 = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ m (the Fermi radius, in your data booklet).
  • From data: R against A1/3 is a straight line through the origin.
  • 8 times the nucleons → 2 times the radius. 27 times → 3 times.
  • Typical radii: about 2.7 fm (carbon-12) to about 7.4 fm (uranium-238). 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Gold-197: A = 197.
  • A1/3 = 1971/3 = 5.82.
  • R = 1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.82 = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • About 7 fm: roughly 20 000 times smaller than the gold atom.
Quick check
?A nucleus of lead-208 has proton number 82 and nucleon number 208. Using R = R0A1/3 with R0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, what is the radius of this nucleus?
Quick check
?A carbon-12 nucleus contains 12 nucleons, each of mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. R0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. What is the density of the nucleus?
Mission 2

Every nucleus has the same density

  • Volume: V = ⁴⁄3πR³ = ⁴⁄3πR0³A. So V ∝ A.
  • Mass: m ≈ A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. So m ∝ A.
  • Density = m/V: the A cancels. The same for every nucleus.
  • ρ = 3 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ ÷ (4π × (1.20 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • Nucleons are packed at a fixed spacing, like molecules in a drop of liquid.
Mission 2

Nucleus versus atom

  • Solids, made of atoms: about 10³ to 2 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³ (gold: 1.9 × 10⁴).
  • Nuclear matter: 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, about 10¹³ times denser.
  • Why: the nucleus is 10⁴–10⁵ times narrower than the atom, so its volume is about 10¹² to 10¹⁵ times smaller, yet it holds nearly all the mass.
  • A teaspoon (5 cm³) of nuclear matter: 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.3 × 10¹⁷ ≈ 1 × 10¹² kg, about a billion tonnes.
Mission check

?A neutron star has a mass of 2.8 × 10³⁰ kg. Model it as a sphere of nuclear matter, with the same density as any nucleus. Estimate its radius.

Nuclear matter has the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, in any amount. A whole star of it is only about 14 km in radius, and a teaspoon of it has a mass of about 10¹² kg: a billion tonnes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can't the electron in a hydrogen atom orbit at any distance it likes?

Make a guess: Why can't the electron in a hydrogen atom orbit at any distance it likes?

Mission 3

Bohr's picture

  • Hydrogen: one electron in a circular orbit around one proton.
  • The electric attraction provides the centripetal force.
  • Classical physics allows any radius, so any energy.
  • Classical physics also says an orbiting (accelerating) charge radiates and spirals in. Real atoms do not.
Mission 3

Bohr's rule

  • Bohr (1913): the electron's angular momentum L = mvr is quantised.
  • mvr = nh/2π, n = 1, 2, 3, …
  • Only certain radii obey this, so only certain orbits, each with its own energy.
  • In an allowed orbit the electron does not radiate. It emits only when it jumps between orbits.
Mission 3

Why the rule? Standing waves

  • de Broglie: the electron has a wavelength λ = h/mv.
  • Allowed orbit: a whole number of wavelengths fits around it, a standing wave.
  • 2πr = nλ.
  • Substitute λ = h/mv: 2πr = nh/mv → mvr = nh/2π. Bohr's rule.
  • Any other radius: the wave fails to join up with itself. Not allowed.
Quick check
?In the Bohr model of hydrogen, the angular momentum of the electron in the n = 2 orbit is 2.11 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. What is its angular momentum in the n = 4 orbit?
Quick check
?Predict first. In the Bohr model, the energy levels of hydrogen are given by E = −13.6/n² eV. Which statement about these energy levels is correct?
Mission 3

The energy levels

  • Bohr's rule gives the hydrogen levels: E = −13.6/n² eV.
  • n = 1: −13.6 eV (ground state). n = 2: −3.40 eV. n = 3: −1.51 eV. n = 4: −0.85 eV.
  • As n → ∞, E → 0: the electron is free. Ionisation energy from n = 1: 13.6 eV.
  • The levels get closer together as n increases.
Mission 3

Worked example: a red line

  • Transition n = 3 → n = 2.
  • E3 = −13.6/9 = −1.51 eV. E2 = −13.6/4 = −3.40 eV.
  • ΔE = −1.51 − (−3.40) = 1.89 eV = 1.89 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • λ = hc/ΔE = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 3.02 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 6.58 × 10⁻⁷ m: red light.
Quick check
?In the Bohr model, the energy levels of hydrogen are E = −13.6/n² eV. What is the wavelength of the photon emitted when the electron makes a transition from n = 4 to n = 3? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 3

Successes and limits

  • Success: predicted the hydrogen lines and the 13.6 eV ionisation energy.
  • Limits: fails for atoms with more than one electron. Gives no reason for its own rule beyond the standing-wave picture.
  • Electrons do not really follow orbits. Quantum theory replaced the model.
  • Still useful: the first model to explain discrete energy levels.
Mission check

?In the Bohr model of hydrogen, the electron in the n = 3 state moves in a circular orbit of radius 4.77 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. What is the speed of the electron? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and mₑ = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.

Only radii with mvr = nh/2π are allowed: a whole number of electron wavelengths fits round each. So the radii, and the energies, come in steps.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Scatter X-rays off electrons and their wavelength gets longer. Why would light do that?

Make a guess: Scatter X-rays off electrons and their wavelength gets longer. Why would light do that?

Mission 4

Compton's experiment

  • 1923: X-rays of one known wavelength aimed at graphite. Its outer electrons are almost free.
  • A detector measured the wavelength of X-rays scattered at different angles θ.
  • Result: a scattered component with a longer wavelength.
  • The shift grew with the angle.
  • Wave theory predicted the same wavelength at every angle. It could not explain the shift.
Quick check
?Predict first. When an X-ray photon is scattered by a free electron, the scattered photon has a longer wavelength than the incident photon. What is the reason?
Mission 4

A photon–electron collision

  • Treat the X-ray as a stream of photons.
  • Each photon has energy E = hf = hc/λ and momentum p = h/λ (= E/c).
  • One photon hits one electron at rest. The electron recoils, gaining kinetic energy.
  • Energy and momentum of the photon–electron system are conserved.
  • The photon keeps less energy → lower f → longer λ. Its speed is still c.
Quick check
?Predict first. Photons are Compton scattered by free electrons. For which scattering angle θ is the increase in the wavelength of the photons greatest?
Mission 4

The Compton shift

  • λf − λi = Δλ = (h/me c)(1 − cos θ): λi incident, λf scattered.
  • θ = angle between the incident and scattered photon directions.
  • h/me c = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ (9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 3.00 × 10⁸) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • θ = 0°: no shift. θ = 90°: Δλ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m. θ = 180°: largest, 4.85 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • The shift does not depend on the incident wavelength.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • X-rays, λ = 2.00 × 10⁻¹¹ m, scattered through 90° by free electrons at rest.
  • Δλ = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² × (1 − cos 90°) = 2.43 × 10⁻¹² m.
  • λf = 2.00 × 10⁻¹¹ + 0.243 × 10⁻¹¹ = 2.24 × 10⁻¹¹ m.
  • Electron's kinetic energy = photon energy lost = hc/λi − hc/λf.
  • = 9.95 × 10⁻¹⁵ − 8.87 × 10⁻¹⁵ = 1.08 × 10⁻¹⁵ J.
Mission 4

Why it matters

  • Photoelectric effect: light delivers energy in quanta, hf.
  • Compton scattering: each photon also carries momentum h/λ and collides with one electron, like a particle.
  • Additional evidence that light is made of photons.
  • Why X-rays? Δλ is about 10⁻¹² m whatever λ is. For visible light (λ ≈ 5 × 10⁻⁷ m) that is about 5 millionths of λ, too small to measure.
Quick check
?A student suggests repeating Compton's experiment with visible light of wavelength 500 nm instead of X-rays. Why would the Compton shift be very hard to detect?
Mission check

?An X-ray photon of wavelength 3.00 × 10⁻¹¹ m is scattered straight back (θ = 180°) by a free electron initially at rest. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the kinetic energy of the recoiling electron?

The photon hands energy to the recoiling electron. Less energy means a lower frequency, so a longer wavelength.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).

Mission 2

  • R = R0A1/3, R0 = 1.20 fm (data booklet); nuclear radii are a few fm.
  • V ∝ A and m ∝ A, so all nuclei have the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³: about 10¹³ times that of ordinary matter.
  • Electron diffraction (first minimum) measures R more accurately than alpha scattering.

Mission 3

  • Bohr: mvr = nh/2π, so only certain orbits; hydrogen levels E = −13.6/n² eV.
  • The model explains hydrogen's lines but fails for atoms with more than one electron.

Mission 4

  • A photon has energy hc/λ and momentum h/λ. Scattered by a free electron, it loses energy, so its wavelength increases.
  • Δλ = (h/me c)(1 − cos θ): zero at 0°, largest at 180°, independent of λ.
  • Compton scattering shows that photons carry momentum: more evidence for the particle nature of light.
