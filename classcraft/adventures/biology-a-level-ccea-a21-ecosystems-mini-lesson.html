Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Make a guess: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Mission 1

Four ways numbers change

  • Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove them.
  • Change in population = (births + immigrants) − (deaths + emigrants).
  • Numbers change seasonally (e.g. breeding season, winter food shortage) and from year to year (weather, food supply).
Quick check
?A newt population starts the year at 500. During the year there are 120 births, 80 deaths, 15 immigrants and 35 emigrants. What is the population at the end of the year?
Mission 1

Exponential growth

  • Few individuals, plentiful resources, weak limiting factors (little competition, predation or disease).
  • Births exceed deaths by a constant proportion of the population.
  • So the number added grows each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … A J-shaped curve.
  • Each individual does not breed faster. There are simply more individuals breeding.
  • The maximum rate a population can grow at is its biotic potential.
Mission 1

Sigmoid growth and carrying capacity

  • Real populations slow down and level off: an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
  • Carrying capacity: the largest population the environment's resources can support sustainably.
  • Limiting resources: food, water, light, space, nest sites, mineral ions.
  • As numbers rise, resources per individual fall. Births fall; deaths rise. At the plateau, births ≈ deaths.
  • Births and deaths do not stop at the plateau: they balance.
  • Phases: lag → exponential (log) → stationary → decline (if a non-renewable resource runs out, or waste builds up).
  • Environmental resistance: all the factors that stop a population reaching its biotic potential.
Quick check
?Yeast was grown in a sealed flask of glucose solution and cell counts were made every four hours. After 24 hours the count stopped rising. Which explanation for the plateau is correct?
Mission 1

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

  • Density-dependent: affects a larger proportion of the population as it gets denser.
    • Competition for limited resources, predation, disease and parasites spread faster when crowded.
  • Density-independent: affects a similar proportion whatever the density.
    • Frost, drought, flood, fire, storms.
  • Biotic factors are usually density-dependent; abiotic factors such as weather are usually density-independent.
  • Say: "a storm kills the same proportion" — not: "density-dependent because it killed so many".
Quick check
?A storm kills 30% of a seabird colony. Over the following years, the colony returns to its former size. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 1

r-selected and K-selected species

  • r-selected: small, short-lived, mature early, many offspring, little parental care.
    • Unstable habitats. Numbers boom, overshoot and crash: a J-shaped curve that falls. Aphids, annual weeds.
  • K-selected: large, long-lived, mature late, few offspring, much parental care.
    • Stable habitats. Numbers level off near carrying capacity: a sigmoid curve. Elephants, oaks.
  • Most species lie between the two extremes.
Mission 1Practical

Growth of a yeast population

  • Assessed practical task: investigate the growth of a yeast population using a haemocytometer.
  • Culture medium: water, glucose (energy), a nitrogen source (e.g. yeast extract), mineral ions. Constant temperature.
  • Sample at fixed times. Mix first; dilute if crowded. Count on a haemocytometer: each small square is 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm, 0.1 mm deep = 0.004 mm³.
  • Example: 52 cells in 5 squares → 52 ÷ (5 × 0.004) = 2600 cells per mm³ (2.6 × 10⁶ per cm³). Multiply by any dilution factor.
  • Count cells touching two chosen sides only. Methylene blue stains dead cells.
  • Plot log10(cell number) against time. A straight line = exponential growth; the line bending over shows the stationary phase.

Mission check

?In 1911, 25 reindeer were introduced to St Paul Island, Alaska, where they had almost no predators. By 1938 there were about 2000; by 1950 only 8 remained, after the lichens they ate in winter had been destroyed. How should this be interpreted in relation to the sigmoid growth model?

Growth stops because each individual's share of the resources shrinks. Destroy the resources and the carrying capacity itself falls.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Make a guess: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Mission 2

Two kinds of competition

  • Competition: individuals use the same resource, and it is in limited supply.
  • Intraspecific: between members of the same species. They need exactly the same food, light, water, space, nest sites and mates.
  • Interspecific: between different species whose needs overlap.
  • No fight is needed. Seedlings shading each other are competing.
  • Say: "competition for a limited resource" — not: "competition means fighting".
Quick check
?Why does competition occur between members of the same population?
Mission 2

What competition does to growth curves

  • Intraspecific competition rises with density. It is what bends the curve into a plateau at carrying capacity.
  • Interspecific, grown together: each species may level off lower than when alone.
  • Or one keeps rising while the other falls to zero: competitive exclusion (Gause's Paramecium).
  • Remove a competitor: the other population rises to a new, higher plateau. It does not grow for ever.
  • Competition is a −/− interaction: both species lose resources to the other.

Mission check

?A forester plants beech seedlings very close together in a cleared plot with no other plant species. Over ten years, most of the seedlings die and the survivors are tall and spaced out. Which explanation is best?

Same species, same needs, same limited light: that is why competition within a species is so fierce.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Make a guess: How can losing a few otters turn a forest of seaweed into bare rock?

Mission 3

Predator and prey numbers rise and fall together

  • Prey plentiful → more predators survive and breed. Predator numbers rise, a little later.
  • More predators → more prey eaten. Prey numbers fall.
  • Less food → predators starve and breed less. Predator numbers fall.
  • Fewer predators → prey recover. The cycle repeats.
  • The predator peak lags the prey peak: breeding and starving take time.
  • Case study: snowshoe hare and Canada lynx, Canada. Fur-trade records show a cycle of about 10 years.
  • On a growth curve, predation keeps prey oscillating below the level food alone would allow.
  • Effects pass down a food chain. Where sea otters were hunted out, the urchins they eat multiplied and grazed kelp forests to bare rock.
Quick check
?Fur records from the Hudson's Bay Company show snowshoe hare and Canada lynx numbers rising and falling in cycles of about ten years, with each peak in lynx numbers occurring one to two years after a peak in hares. Which explanation is correct?

Mission 3

Living together: mutualism and other symbioses

  • Mutualism (+/+): both partners benefit.
  • Predation, parasitism and grazing are +/−; mutualism is +/+.
Quick check
?When sea temperatures rise, hard corals expel their zooxanthellae, turn white and often die of starvation within weeks. Why does losing the algae starve the coral?
Mission 3

Biological control of pests

  • Pest: an organism that harms crops, livestock or people, causing economic loss.
  • Biological control: use a natural predator or competitor to keep the pest below damaging levels.
  • Predator: the mite Phytoseiulus persimilis eats red spider mites on glasshouse crops.
  • Competitor: the fungus Phlebiopsis gigantea, painted on fresh pine stumps, takes them over before the root-rot fungus Heterobasidion can.
  • Advantages over chemicals: specific to the pest; no residues in food or food chains; pests rarely become resistant; agent may breed and persist.
  • Effective agent: specific, breeds fast, finds pests well, survives local conditions, does not become a pest itself.
  • It reduces the pest; it rarely eliminates it. Predator and pest settle at low numbers.
Quick check
?Predatory mites are released into a glasshouse to control red spider mites. Pest numbers fall to a low level but never reach zero. Why?
Mission check

?Illustrative scenario. In a woodland, owls eat voles, and voles eat tree seedlings. A disease kills most of the owls. What is the most likely effect on the tree seedlings over the next few years?

Remove a predator and the effect skips down the food chain: otters, urchins and kelp; owls, voles and seedlings.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Make a guess: Bare rock can turn into forest without anyone planting a single tree. How does that happen?

Mission 4

What succession is

  • Succession: change in the species making up a community, over time.
  • Primary succession: starts on bare ground with no soil: rock, cooled lava, sand, land left by a melting glacier.
  • Secondary succession: starts where soil remains after the community is destroyed: fire, flood, wind damage, felling, abandoned farmland.
  • Primary succession is relatively slow. Secondary is faster and predictable, because soil, seeds and roots remain.
Mission 4

Pioneers on bare rock

  • Pioneer species colonise first: e.g. lichens, then mosses.
  • Their features: spores spread by wind; survive drought, heat and cold; need almost no soil.
  • Lichens help weather the rock surface.
  • When pioneers die, decomposers turn them into humus.
  • Rock particles + humus = the first thin soil.
Mission 4

Each stage paves the way, then gives way

  • Deeper soil holds more water and nutrients: the environment is less hostile.
  • New species can now establish: grasses and ferns, then shrubs, then trees.
  • Taller plants shade out the earlier ones.
  • Each stage changes the environment so it suits other species better, and often suits itself less.
  • Say: "the pioneers changed conditions, which allowed other species to establish" — not: "pioneers prepare the ground for the next species".
Quick check
?Alder trees colonising ground exposed by a retreating glacier have nitrogen-fixing bacteria in their root nodules. Within decades the soil nitrogen content rises and spruce trees, which cannot grow on the bare ground, replace the alders. Which statement about the cause of this change is correct?
Mission 4

What rises as succession proceeds

  • Soil depth, humus, water and mineral content.
  • Plant size: mosses → shrubs → trees.
  • Primary production (biomass made per m² per year).
  • Species diversity and food-web complexity: more niches.
Quick checkRead the data

Mission 4

The climax community

  • Climax community: the final, stable community, in equilibrium with its environment.
  • It persists until a disturbance; its populations still fluctuate.
  • Usually dominated by a few large species. In most of lowland Britain and Ireland: deciduous woodland (e.g. oak).
  • Climatic climax: set by the climate, e.g. woodland in the lowlands.
  • Biotic climax: held at an earlier stage by a living factor, e.g. grassland kept short by grazing sheep or rabbits.
Mission 4

Succession held back

  • Human activity can stop succession before the climax.
  • Grazing livestock eat tree and shrub seedlings: grassland or moorland persists.
  • Grazing-held grassland is a biotic climax.
Mission check

?On a coastal sand-dune system, marram grass covers the young dunes nearest the sea. On older dunes further inland, marram is scarce and the dunes are covered by mosses, low shrubs and other grasses, growing in a thin layer of soil. Which explanation is correct?

On rock or on sand, pioneers change the ground they live on, and their changes let others take over. That is how bare ground becomes forest.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Make a guess: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Mission 5

Energy in, energy out

  • Sunlight is the source of energy for almost every ecosystem.
  • Producers (plants, algae) capture light and fix carbon dioxide from the air or water into organic compounds.
  • That energy is now chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • It passes along food chains when one organism eats another.
  • At every step, respiration releases energy that leaves as heat. Heat cannot be captured again.
  • Decomposers feed on dead matter and faeces, and respire too.
  • So energy flows through an ecosystem, one way. It must be resupplied all the time.
  • Say: "energy is transferred, and lost as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 5

Food chains and food webs

  • Food chain: one feeding pathway, e.g. grass → rabbit → fox.
  • Each arrow points from the food to the feeder: the direction energy and biomass move.
  • Food web: many linked chains in one community.
  • This is a grazing food chain: it starts with a living producer.
  • Say: "the arrow means 'is eaten by'" — not: "the arrow means 'eats'".
Quick check
?What do the arrows in a food web represent?
Mission 5

Trophic levels

  • A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain.
  • Level 1: producers. Level 2: primary consumers (herbivores). Level 3: secondary consumers. Level 4: tertiary consumers.
  • An animal with a varied diet sits at different levels in different chains.
  • Count from the producer: the producer is not a consumer.
  • Detritivores and decomposers also count as trophic levels: they feed on dead matter from every level.
Quick check
?In a garden, blackbirds eat berries from shrubs and also eat snails, which feed on the leaves of the same shrubs. Which trophic level or levels does the blackbird occupy?
Mission 5

Feeding on the dead

  • Dead leaves, dead bodies and faeces still hold chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • Decomposers (many fungi and bacteria) are saprotrophs: they secrete enzymes onto dead matter, digest it outside their cells, then absorb the products.
  • Detritivores (e.g. earthworms, woodlice) ingest dead matter and digest it inside.
  • Dead matter → detritivores and decomposers is a detritus food chain.
  • Decomposers respire the carbon compounds: CO2 returns to the air; energy leaves as heat.
  • Say: "faeces still contain energy" — not: "faeces are energy-free waste".
Quick check
?A woodlouse and a fungus are both feeding on the same pile of dead leaves. How do their ways of feeding differ?
Mission check

?In autumn, an oak tree drops its leaves. Over the next year, fungi and bacteria break the leaves down. What happens to the carbon and to the energy that were in the leaves?

Matter goes round; energy goes through. Sunlight in, heat out: that is why every food chain needs the sun, or another outside source, all the time.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Make a guess: A caterpillar eats a whole leaf. Why does so little of that leaf end up as caterpillar?

Mission 6

Gross and net primary production

  • Producers use light energy to fix CO2 into sugars.
  • Most of the sugar is respired by the plant itself. The rest builds other molecules: the plant's biomass.
  • Gross primary production (GPP): the chemical energy stored by photosynthesis in plant biomass, in a given area or volume.
  • R: the energy the plants lose in respiration.
  • Net primary production: NPP = GPP − R.
  • NPP is what is left for plant growth and reproduction, and for consumers and decomposers.
  • Say: "producers capture and store energy" — not: "producers make energy".
Mission 6

Production is a rate

  • The biomass present now is a stock: e.g. g m⁻² or kJ m⁻².
  • Productivity is the rate of production: per area per unit time.
  • e.g. kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Primary production: by producers. Secondary production: by consumers.
  • Say: "productivity is a rate" — not: "productivity is the biomass present".
Mission 6

Worked example: NPP

A grassland (illustrative data): GPP = 24 000 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹. Plant respiration, R = 14 400 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.

  • Step 1: NPP = GPP − R = 24 000 − 14 400 = 9 600 kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹.
  • Step 2: share of GPP left as NPP = 9 600 ÷ 24 000 × 100 = 40%. The plants respired 60%.
  • Keep the units: per area, per year, at every step.

Mission 6

Where a consumer's food goes

  • Energy in the food eaten (ingested): I.
  • Part is never absorbed: lost in faeces (egestion).
  • Part of what is absorbed leaves in urine (excretion, e.g. urea).
  • Most of the absorbed energy is respired (R) and lost as heat.
  • Only the rest becomes new biomass: the consumer's net production.
  • Example (illustrative): a bullock eats grass holding 100 kJ; 64 kJ is lost in faeces and urine; R = 32 kJ. Net production = 100 − (64 + 32) = 4 kJ.
Quick check
?A caterpillar ingests leaf material containing 100 kJ of energy. It egests 50 kJ in faeces, releases 35 kJ as heat through cell respiration and stores 15 kJ in new biomass. What happens to the energy in the faeces?
Mission 6

Why so much is lost

  • Not eaten: roots, wood, bones. Dead remains go to decomposers.
  • Not digested: lost in faeces. Cellulose is hard to digest, so herbivores lose much more this way than carnivores.
  • Excreted: nitrogenous waste such as urea, in urine.
  • Respired: every organism, plants included, respires; most absorbed energy ends up as heat.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) respire far more to keep warm, so less of their food becomes biomass.
  • Plants fix only a few per cent of the sunlight reaching them: much is reflected, passes through, or is the wrong wavelength; CO2 and temperature can limit photosynthesis.
  • Say: "energy is lost to the surroundings as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Quick check
?Why is much less energy available to the secondary consumers in a food chain than to the primary consumers?

Mission check

?A wood mouse eats seeds containing 500 kJ of energy. 100 kJ is lost in faeces and urine, and 380 kJ is released in respiration. What percentage of the energy it ate is available to an owl that eats the mouse?

Like the caterpillar, the mouse loses most of its food in waste and as heat from respiration. Only the small remainder becomes body, and only that can feed the next level.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Make a guess: Why are top predators so rare, and why does eating lower in a food chain feed more people?

Mission 7

Less at every level

  • Each trophic level passes on only a small share of its energy: often around 10%, measured from about 5% to 20%.
  • After 3 or 4 transfers (4 or 5 levels), too little is left to support another population.
  • So top predators are few, and each needs a large area to find enough food.
  • Eat or harvest lower in a chain and fewer transfers come between the producers and you: more of their energy reaches you as food.
  • Say: "less energy reaches each higher level" — not: "top predators hold the most energy".
Quick check
?Food chains in most ecosystems have no more than four or five trophic levels. What is the reason for this restriction?
Mission 7

Three kinds of pyramid

  • Pyramid of numbers: organisms per area at each level. Can be inverted: one oak tree feeds thousands of caterpillars.
  • Pyramid of biomass: dry mass per area (g m⁻²) at one moment. Usually upright. In some seas it is inverted: a small, fast-dividing stock of phytoplankton feeds a larger stock of zooplankton.
  • Pyramid of energy: energy flow per area per year (kJ m⁻² yr⁻¹). Never inverted: each level receives less than the level below.
  • Usefulness: numbers ignores size; biomass is a snapshot that ignores turnover and season; energy is most accurate but hardest to measure.
  • Decomposers are not drawn as a bar.
Quick check
?In a model grassland ecosystem the dry biomass per square metre is: producers 800 g, primary consumers 100 g, secondary consumers 15 g, tertiary consumers 2 g. Samples of dry biomass from each level release similar amounts of energy per gram when burned. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 7

Farming: send more energy to us

  • Farmers cannot make more sunlight fall on a field. They steer more of the energy their crops capture into human food.
  • Simplify the food web: less energy flows into chains that do not end in humans.
  • Herbicides kill weeds, which compete with the crop for light, water and ions.
  • Insecticides and fungicides kill pests and pathogens that would eat or infect the crop.
  • Fertilisers remove mineral shortages, raising primary productivity.
  • Each extra trophic level loses most of its energy, so producing animal products costs much more crop energy than using crops directly.
Mission 7

Farming: cut respiratory losses

  • Livestock respire much of their food away. Cut that loss, and more becomes meat, milk or eggs.
  • Confinement in pens or sheds: less movement, so less energy respired.
  • Warm housing: less energy spent keeping warm (they are endotherms).
  • This improves the respiration/production ratio: less respired for each unit of new biomass.
  • Harvest young: growing animals convert more of their food into biomass.
  • High-energy feeds (e.g. silage) and high-protein feeds speed growth.
  • These methods raise animal-welfare and disease-spread concerns; questions may ask you to weigh them against the gains.
  • Say: "less energy is lost as heat in respiration" — not: "the animals make more energy".
Quick check
?Young cattle are kept in warm sheds with little room to move. Why does this raise the share of their food energy that becomes meat?
Mission check

?In a lake, the main food chain is phytoplankton → zooplankton → small fish → pike. A fishery could harvest either the small fish or the pike. Which harvest can be the larger each year, and why?

Every step up a chain loses most of its energy, mainly as heat. That keeps top predators rare, and it is why shorter chains, and lower respiratory losses, give more food.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plentiful resources: exponential growth. As resources per individual shrink, births fall and deaths rise until they balance at the carrying capacity.
  • Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease) push numbers back towards carrying capacity; density-independent factors (weather) knock them about.

Mission 2

  • Intraspecific competition: same species, identical needs; it levels a population off at carrying capacity and cuts each individual's breeding success.
  • Interspecific competition lowers both species' numbers or excludes one; removing a competitor raises the other to a new, higher plateau.

Mission 3

  • Predator and prey numbers cycle, with the predator peak lagging; predation is density-dependent and lowers the prey's numbers.
  • Mutualism is +/+; predation, grazing and parasitism are +/−.

Mission 4

  • Pioneers colonise bare ground and change it; each stage makes the environment suit other species, until a stable climax community forms.
  • Grazing, mowing, burning or drainage can hold succession back; conservation often manages this deliberately.

Mission 5

  • Energy enters as light (or, for chemoautotrophs, from oxidising inorganic substances), passes along food chains in carbon compounds, and leaves as heat; it is never recycled.
  • Trophic level is a feeding position; one species can occupy several. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.

Mission 6

  • NPP = GPP − R; productivity is a rate per area per time; efficiency = energy (or biomass) at a level ÷ that at the level below × 100.
  • Consumers lose energy in faeces, urine and, above all, as heat from respiration; only new biomass passes on.

Mission 7

  • Most energy is lost at each transfer, so higher levels hold less energy and biomass, and chains rarely exceed four or five levels. Harvesting lower in a chain gives more food energy.
  • Pyramids of numbers and biomass can be inverted; pyramids of energy never are.
  • Farming increases food output by simplifying food webs and cutting respiratory losses of livestock.
