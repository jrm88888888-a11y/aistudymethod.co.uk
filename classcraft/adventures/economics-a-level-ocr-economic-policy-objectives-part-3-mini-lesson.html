Mission 1

Developed, emerging, developing

  • Developed economies: high income per head; most output from services; deep financial markets (e.g. UK, US, Japan).
  • Emerging economies: middle income; growing fast; moving from farming to industry and services; opening to trade and investment (e.g. China, India, Brazil, Indonesia).
  • Developing economies: lower income per head; many people in farming; high poverty (e.g. Ethiopia, Malawi).
  • The lines are blurry: groups such as the IMF and World Bank use different definitions.
  • Compare with more than one measure: GDP per head, HDI, literacy, life expectancy.
Mission 1

From BRICs to BRICS

  • BRICs: Brazil, Russia, India, China. Term coined by economist Jim O'Neill at Goldman Sachs in 2001, predicting their fast growth.
  • South Africa joined the political group in 2010, making BRICS. The group has taken in more members since 2024.
  • Their records since 2000 differ a lot: China and India grew fast; Brazil, Russia and South Africa grew more slowly, with booms and slumps tied to commodity prices.
  • Say: "emerging economy" — not "developing" or "poor" for all of them.

Mission 1

Worked example: nominal to real

  • Nominal GDP uses current prices. Real GDP removes the effect of inflation.
  • Illustrative data: nominal GDP grows 12% in a year; inflation is 5%.

1. Quick estimate: real growth ≈ 12 − 5 = 7%.

2. Exact: 1.12 ÷ 1.05 = 1.0667 → real growth 6.7%.

  • To compare countries, also use PPP: it adjusts for different price levels, so a dollar buys the same basket everywhere.
  • Say: "real GDP per head at PPP" for living standards — not: "GDP" alone.
Mission 1

Why China and India grew so fast

  • Catch-up: a poor economy can copy existing technology, so returns to new capital are high.
  • Structural change: workers moved from low-productivity farming to factories and services.
  • High saving and investment, especially in China: infrastructure, factories, housing.
  • Opening up: China's reforms from 1978 and entry to the WTO in 2001; India's reforms from 1991. Trade and FDI rose.
  • Different paths: China led in manufacturing exports; India grew more through services such as IT.
  • Result: the number of people in China living on under 1.90 dollars a day fell by close to 800 million over 40 years (World Bank, 2022).

Mission 1

Winners and losers for the UK and others

  • Opportunities:
    • bigger export markets, e.g. for financial services, education, cars, machinery
    • cheaper imports: lower prices and higher real incomes for consumers
    • investment flows both ways
  • Threats:
    • competition for UK manufacturing → job losses in some regions (structural unemployment)
    • higher world demand pushes up prices of oil, metals and food
  • Also: economic and political influence in global decisions shifts towards the emerging economies.
  • Overall effect: debated; it differs by sector, region and skill group.

Mission 1Evaluate

Will the fast growth last, and who gains?

It depends on:

  • Catch-up slows as the gap narrows: China's growth rate has fallen since the 2010s.
  • Demographics: China's workforce is ageing; India and much of Africa have young populations.
  • Debt and imbalances: high borrowing for property and infrastructure can end in a slowdown.
  • Inequality and environment: growth can widen gaps and add pollution.
  • Data quality: some statistics are disputed; PPP estimates are revised.
  • For the UK: whether firms and workers move into sectors where the UK has comparative advantage.

Mission 2

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.

Mission 2

Absolute and relative poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a fixed line, e.g. the World Bank's international poverty line per person per day.
  • Relative poverty: income well below the typical income in that society.
    • UK measure: household income below 60% of median income.
  • As average incomes rise, absolute poverty can fall while relative poverty stays the same.
  • Measuring poverty in more depth: Mission 3.
Mission 2

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
Mission 2

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • UK disposable household income: Gini 32.9 on the 0–100 scale (financial year ending 2024, ONS).
  • Great Britain household wealth: Gini about 0.6, or 60 on the 0–100 scale (2018–2020, ONS). The richest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth; the poorest half held 9%.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.
Mission 2

Worked example: from quintiles to a Lorenz curve

  • Illustrative data. Income shares of each fifth (quintile) of households, poorest first: 5%, 10%, 15%, 25%, 45%.
  • Step 1: add the shares up as you go (cumulative).
    • 5 → 5 + 10 = 15 → 15 + 15 = 30 → 30 + 25 = 55 → 55 + 45 = 100.
  • Step 2: plot (20, 5), (40, 15), (60, 30), (80, 55), (100, 100). Start at (0, 0).
  • Step 3: join the points; draw the line of equality for comparison.
  • A quantile ratio compares groups: richest fifth ÷ poorest fifth = 45 ÷ 5 = 9.

Mission 2

Why is income unequal?

  • Human capital: education and skills raise productivity and pay.
  • Ownership of wealth: land, property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends; inheritance passes them on.
  • Inequality of opportunity: family background affects schooling, contacts and health.
  • Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and others.
  • Power: bargaining power of unions, employers with monopsony power, top executives.
  • Tax and benefit policy: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; regressive taxes widen them.
  • Globalisation and technological change: raise the pay of scarce skills; some routine jobs move abroad or are automated.
Mission 2Evaluate

How much inequality is a problem?

It depends on:

  • Values. Fairness is a normative judgement; people disagree on how equal outcomes should be.
  • Causes. Inequality from effort and skill is viewed differently from inequality from inheritance, luck or discrimination.
  • Mobility. Wide gaps matter more if children of poor families stay poor.
  • Living standards of the poorest. Rising inequality with rising absolute incomes differs from falling incomes at the bottom.
  • Measurement. Income or wealth? Before or after taxes and benefits? Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income.
Mission check

?In country P, the Gini coefficient for household income is 0.30 and for household wealth is 0.65. (Illustrative data.) What can you conclude?

A Gini coefficient measures how evenly something is shared, not how much there is: here wealth is shared less evenly than income.

Mission 3

Two meanings of poverty

  • Absolute poverty: income too low to pay for basic needs: food, clean water, shelter, clothing.
    • Measured against a line that is fixed in real terms (it rises only with prices).
  • Relative poverty: income far below the typical income of the same society.
    • Measured against a line that moves with the median income, e.g. 60% of median household income.
  • Median: the middle income when all households are lined up from lowest to highest.
  • Absolute poverty asks: can you survive? Relative poverty asks: can you take part in normal life where you live?
  • Say: "relative poverty is low income compared with the median" — not: "a milder form of absolute poverty".
Mission 3

Worked example: the line moves

  • Illustrative data. Nine households, weekly income:
    • 150 · 220 · 280 · 340 · 400 · 460 · 520 · 650 · 900
  • Median = 400. Relative line = 0.6 × 400 = 240.
  • Below 240: two households (150, 220). Relative poverty rate = 2/9 = 22%.
  • Ten years later, in real terms: the bottom three gain 2%; everyone else gains 20%.
    • New median = 400 × 1.2 = 480. New line = 0.6 × 480 = 288.
    • Bottom three now: 153 · 224.4 · 285.6. All below 288.
    • Relative poverty rate = 3/9 = 33%.
  • Absolute line fixed at 200 (real terms): one household below it before (150), one after (153).
  • Everyone got richer. Relative poverty rose; absolute poverty did not.
Quick check
?Over 20 years, the real income of every household in a country doubles, including the median household's. The poverty lines are 60% of the median (relative) and a fixed basket of basic needs (absolute). What happens?
Mission 3

Poverty lines in practice

  • International poverty line (World Bank): $3.00 a day per person, at 2021 prices, adjusted for purchasing power parity (PPP).
    • PPP: adjusts for what money buys in each country, so $3.00 means the same basket everywhere.
    • It is an absolute line. It replaced $2.15 a day (2017 prices) in June 2025.
    • Higher lines for richer countries: $4.20 and $8.30 a day.
  • UK: relative low income = below 60% of median household income, before or after housing costs (BHC/AHC).
  • The UK also uses an "absolute low income" line: 60% of the 2010/11 median, held fixed in real terms.
Mission 3

Case: the numbers

  • World, below the international line:
    • 1990: around 2.3 billion people.
    • 2025: around 831 million: about 1 in 10 people.
  • UK, 2023/24, median income £650 a week (BHC). Line: 0.6 × 650 = £390 a week.
    • Relative low income: 17% of people BHC; 21% AHC.
    • Children: 31% AHC. Pensioners: 16% AHC.
  • These figures are for a couple with no children. So the UK relative line is about £28 a day each (390 ÷ 7 ÷ 2): far above the World Bank's $3.00 a day.
  • One country can cut absolute poverty fast while relative poverty barely moves.
Mission 3

Causes of poverty

  • Individuals and households:
    • unemployment, low pay, part-time or insecure work
    • low human capital: few skills or qualifications
    • sickness, disability, caring duties, old age
    • large families or one earner (e.g. lone parents)
    • discrimination (gender, ethnicity, disability) and unequal opportunity
    • owning little wealth or property: no income from rent, interest or profit
  • Economy and government:
    • recession, rising prices of essentials (food, energy, rents)
    • changes to taxes and benefits
    • conflict, weak institutions, climate shocks (low-income countries)
Mission 3

What changes the poverty count?

  • Absolute poverty falls when the real incomes of the poor rise.
  • Relative poverty falls only when the poor's incomes rise faster than the median.
  • So a recession can cut relative poverty: the median falls while benefits and pensions hold steady.
  • And fast growth can raise relative poverty, if gains go mostly to middle and high earners.
  • Say: "relative poverty fell because the median fell" — not: "the poor became better off".
Quick check
?During a recession, median household income falls by 5% in real terms. State pensions are held constant in real terms, and most pensioners' income comes from the state pension. What is most likely to happen to measured relative poverty among pensioners?
Mission check

?Over ten years, the real incomes of the poorest fifth of a country's households rise by 15%. Median real household income rises by 40%. Which outcome is most likely?

Absolute poverty tracks the real incomes of the poor. Relative poverty tracks the gap between the poor and the median, so it can rise while almost everyone gets richer.

Mission 4

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 4

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 4

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1950s–60s: UK data fitted the curve well. Many thought governments could choose a point on it.
  • 1970s: UK inflation averaged about 24% in 1975 (RPI), while unemployment also rose.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outwards.
  • Two reasons: oil-price supply shocks, and workers and firms coming to expect high inflation.
Mission 4

Expectations shift the short-run curve

  • Milton Friedman (1968) and Edmund Phelps (1967): the trade-off is only temporary.
  • Adaptive expectations: people expect inflation to be what it was recently.
  • (1) Point A: unemployment at its natural rate, inflation 2%, expected inflation 2%.
  • (2) AD rises. Workers' money wages rise, but prices rise faster than they expected. Real wages fall, firms hire: move along SRPC1 to B (lower unemployment, higher inflation).
  • (3) Workers now expect the higher inflation and bargain for higher money wages. Real wages recover, firms stop the extra hiring: unemployment returns to the natural rate at C. SRPC1 → SRPC2: each SRPC is drawn for one expected inflation rate.
  • Result: same unemployment, higher inflation.
Mission 4

The long-run Phillips curve

  • Join points A and C: the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • In the long run there is no trade-off: pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation.
  • The natural rate of unemployment: unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium. It is frictional plus structural unemployment.
  • NAIRU: the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. Below it, inflation keeps rising. It is often treated as the same as the natural rate.
  • Vertical LRPC = vertical LRAS: the classical (neo-classical) view.
  • Keynesian AS (reverse-L) suggests a trade-off can last when there is spare capacity; the vertical LRPC matches the neo-classical LRAS.
Mission 4

Worked example: chasing low unemployment

  • Illustrative data. Natural rate = 5%. Expected inflation starts at 2%.
  • Short-run rule: inflation = expected inflation + (5 − unemployment rate).
  • Year 1: government holds unemployment at 3%. Inflation = 2 + 2 = 4%.
  • Year 2: people now expect 4%. Inflation = 4 + 2 = 6%.
  • Year 3: expect 6%. Inflation = 6 + 2 = 8%.
  • Each year the SRPC shifts up. Holding unemployment below the NAIRU makes inflation accelerate.
  • Stop pushing: unemployment returns to 5% with inflation stuck at 8%.
  • Bringing inflation back down needs unemployment above 5% for a time: at 7%, inflation = 8 − 2 = 6%.

Mission 4

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favourable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
  • A change in expected inflation shifts the SRPC.
  • A change in the natural rate shifts the LRPC. Better skills matching, job search help or more flexible labour markets move it left. Long spells of unemployment that erode skills (hysteresis) can move it right.
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?
Mission 4Evaluate

How useful is the Phillips curve?

It depends on:

  • Time horizon: a short-run trade-off may exist.
  • In the long run, if expectations adapt, it disappears.
  • Supply shocks: they shift the SRPC, so past data may not predict the future.
  • Measurement: the NAIRU cannot be observed. It is estimated, and it changes over time.
  • Credible inflation targets: if people trust the central bank, expectations stay near target. The curve can look flat.
  • UK evidence: in 2019 unemployment was 3.8%, yet CPI inflation was only 1.8%.
  • Global factors (cheap imports, energy prices) also drive inflation, not just domestic unemployment.
Mission check

?A sudden rise in world energy prices hits an economy. Over a year, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which best describes this on a Phillips curve diagram?

A lower-unemployment, higher-inflation deal exists only along one short-run curve. Supply shocks can shift that curve.

Mission 5

The big four objectives

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks likeWhy it matters
Economic growthSteady, sustainable rise in real GDPHigher living standards; more tax revenue

  • UK: the Bank of England's inflation target is 2% (CPI), set by the government.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 5

More objectives

  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one economy this year: real GDP growth 2.1%, unemployment 4.0%, inflation 6.5% (target 2%), current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is furthest from being met?
Mission 5

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve (see Mission 4).
Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.

Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 5

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Mission 5

UK performance and other economies

  • UK, last 20 years:
    • Growth: the 2008–09 recession cut real GDP by more than 6%; growth afterwards was slower than before 2008, with weak productivity growth.
    • Unemployment: fell below 4% in 2019, low by historical standards.
    • Inflation: mostly near the 2% target, then a peak of 11.1% in October 2022.
    • Current account: in deficit every year since 1984.
  • Emerging economies often grow faster: China's growth averaged over 9% a year from 1978 (World Bank). They are catching up, adopting existing technology and moving workers out of farming.
  • Developing economies may grow fast or slowly, and are more exposed to commodity prices, conflict and weak institutions.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 5Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.
Mission check

?An economy is already close to full capacity. The government cuts income tax to give growth a boost. Which outcome is most likely in the short run?

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Emerging economies: middle income, fast growth, structural change, opening up. BRICs (2001) → BRICS.
  • Index = value ÷ base × 100; real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. Use real GDP per head at PPP to compare.
  • Their growth brings opportunities (markets, cheaper imports) and threats (competition, dearer commodities) to the UK and others.

Mission 2

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 3

  • Absolute poverty: below a fixed real line for basic needs. Relative poverty: below a share (often 60%) of the median.
  • Growth cuts absolute poverty more reliably than relative poverty; a recession can even cut relative poverty.

Mission 4

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
  • With adaptive expectations, the SRPC shifts up whenever unemployment is held below the natural rate. The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU).

Mission 5

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.
