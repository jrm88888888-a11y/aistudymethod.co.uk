Mission 1

What is urbanization?

  • Urbanization: the growth in the share of a population living in urban areas.
  • But countries define "urban" differently: the threshold can be as low as 200 people (Denmark) or as high as 50,000 (Japan).
  • So "how urban is a country?" depends on who is counting, and how.
Mission 1

One world, two answers

  • Adding up countries' own definitions: 58% of the world's people were urban in 2025.
  • The UN also applies one rule everywhere:
    • city: at least 50,000 people and at least 1,500 people per km²;
    • town: at least 5,000 people and at least 300 people per km².
  • By that rule, 45% lived in cities and 36% in towns in 2025: 81% together.
Quick check
?Why does the UN's single rule give a much higher urban share than adding up countries' own figures?
Mission 1

Site

  • Site: the physical features of the land a city is built on: water supply, flat land, a harbor, a defensible hill or island.
  • The Aztecs built their capital, Tenochtitlan, "on a small island in the Lake of Texcoco": easy to defend, with water all around.
  • The Spanish built Mexico City "on the ruins of the prehispanic city" and began to drain the lake.
Mission 1

Situation

  • Situation: a city's location relative to other places, routes and resources.
  • Istanbul has a "strategic location on the Bosphorus peninsula between the Balkans and Anatolia, the Black Sea and the Mediterranean".
  • That situation helped make it the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire and then the Ottoman Empire.
Quick check
?Which statement describes a city's situation rather than its site?
Mission 1

Site and situation over time

  • A good site explains why a city began; situation often explains why it grew and what its function became (port, capital, market).
  • Sites can become problems as cities grow: Mexico City's lake was drained, and UNESCO lists "excessive ground-water extraction" among threats to Xochimilco's chinampas, farm plots built in the old lake.
  • Situation can change: new railways, ports or airports can make a place more, or less, central.
Mission 1

What drives urbanization?

  • Transportation and communication: link cities to markets and let people and goods move to them.
  • Population growth: natural increase within cities.
  • Migration: people move from rural areas to cities for jobs and services.
  • Economic development: industrial and service jobs cluster in cities.
  • Government policies: investment, special zones, or changing which places count as urban.
Mission 1

A driver at work: Shenzhen

  • Government policy: Shenzhen, "a small backward border town", was designated China's first special economic zone in 1980.
  • It had 17.98 million residents by the end of 2024.
Quick check
?A country reclassifies several large villages as towns. Nobody moves, and no new jobs arrive, yet its urban share rises. Which driver of urbanization is at work?
Mission check

?Country A counts any place with 2,000 people as urban; Country B counts only places with 20,000 or more. Both report that they are 50% urban. What can you conclude?

How urban the world is depends on the definition: 58% by countries' own rules, 81% in cities or towns by the UN's single rule (2025). Site, situation, migration, natural increase and policy then explain where cities grow.

Mission 2

Three engines of city growth

  • Urbanization: a rising share of people living in urban areas.
  • Cities grow by:
    • natural increase (births minus deaths in the city)
    • migration into the city, mostly rural-to-urban
    • reclassification: rural places redrawn or relabeled as urban
Mission 2

Shenzhen

  • Established March 1979; China's first special economic zone (SEZ) from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents by the end of 2024.
  • Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city"; average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
  • Jobs in the SEZ drew young workers to the city.
Mission 2

Where China's urban growth came from

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase
  • These are national figures; the sources give no breakdown for Shenzhen itself.
Quick check
?Which engine supplied the most urban growth in China between 2010 and 2020?
Mission 2

Megacities and metacities

  • Megacity: a city of more than 10 million people.
  • Metacity: a "massive conurbation of more than 20 million people", a term from UN-Habitat.
  • The first metacity was Tokyo, which passed 20 million in the mid-1960s.
  • Megacities: 8 in 1975 → 33 in 2025, 19 of them in Asia.
Mission 2

The biggest cities today

  • Largest cities, 2025 (UN):
    • Jakarta (Indonesia): nearly 42 million
    • Dhaka (Bangladesh): almost 37 million
    • Tokyo (Japan): 33 million
  • All three are metacities by UN-Habitat's 20-million threshold.
  • Caution: the UN's 2025 figures define a "city" as any continuous area of at least 1,500 people per km², so they are larger than many older, boundary-based counts.
Mission 2

Core, semiperiphery and periphery

  • World-system theory splits countries into core (wealthy, high-tech), semiperiphery and periphery (poorer, lower-wage).
  • In the 1950s the world's largest cities were New York and Tokyo, both in countries now in the core.
  • Tokyo is projected to fall from 3rd to 7th largest by 2050; Dhaka may become the largest by mid-century.
  • New megacities projected by 2050: Addis Ababa, Dar es Salaam, Hajipur (India) and Kuala Lumpur, all outside the core.
  • Exam move: for "explain", link the pattern to a process: young populations and rural-to-urban migration drive megacity growth outside the core.
Quick check
?Why are new megacities appearing mostly in periphery and semiperiphery countries?
Mission check

?Dar es Salaam (Tanzania) is projected to pass 10 million people by 2050. Where will its new residents most likely come from?

The UN names natural increase and reclassification as the main drivers of city growth, with internal migration making a "visible contribution" in sub-Saharan Africa. Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city", and across China 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration. The same engines now drive megacity growth mostly in periphery and semiperiphery countries.

Mission 3

Moving out: three processes

  • Suburbanization: people, then shops and offices, move from the central city to the suburbs.
  • Sprawl: "unplanned, low-density, uncontrolled expansion of urban areas".
  • Decentralization: jobs and services spread out from the downtown to many suburban centers.
  • Drivers: cars and highways, cheaper land, bigger homes and lots, newer schools and services.
Mission 3

Detroit: the Motor City shrinks

  • Detroit city population, US census:
    • 1910: 465,766
    • 1950: 1,849,568 (5th-largest US city)
    • 1990: 1,027,974
    • 2010: 713,777
    • 2020: 639,111
Quick check
?By about what percentage did Detroit city's population fall from 1950 (1,849,568) to 2020 (639,111)?
Mission 3

Jobs left, people followed

  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963.
  • Deindustrialization: factory jobs left the central city as firms automated or moved to cheaper sites.
  • Households that could afford to move, often younger and better-paid, left first, many for the suburbs.
  • Result for the city: a smaller tax base. Median household income (2020–2024): Detroit $39,938; USA $80,734.
Mission 3

Decentralization: offices leave downtown

  • Office space in the Detroit metro area, 1999:
    • Detroit's downtown (core city): 21.2%
    • other central cities: 9.3%
    • suburbs: 69.5%
  • One suburban office cluster, Southfield, had more office space than downtown Detroit: "Detroit is the only central city in the study that is surpassed in size by a single suburban office cluster".
Mission 3

Edge cities

  • Edge city (Joel Garreau, 1991): a new suburban business center that:
    • has 5 million+ sq ft of office space and 600,000+ sq ft of retail space
    • has more jobs than bedrooms
    • is seen by people as one place
    • "Were nothing like a 'city' as recently as thirty years ago"
  • US example: Tysons Corner, outside Washington, DC.
Mission 3

Exurbs

  • Exurbs: communities on the urban fringe where at least 20% of workers commute to jobs in an urbanized area, with low housing density and fast population growth.
  • The typical exurban census tract has 14 acres of land per home; the typical US tract, 0.8 acres.
  • Detroit metro, 2000: about 289,000 exurban residents, 6.5% of its 4.45 million people; Brookings lists four exurban counties there, including Livingston County.
Mission 3

Boomburbs

  • Boomburbs: "large, rapidly growing, incorporated communities of more than 100,000 residents that are not the biggest city in their region".
  • Examples: Mesa (Arizona), the biggest; Plano (Texas); Naperville (Illinois).
  • They have housing, shops and offices, but no large downtown.
Quick check
?A fast-growing incorporated suburb of 250,000 people near Phoenix has malls and office parks but no traditional downtown, and is not the largest city in its metro area. What is it?
Mission 3

New challenges of sprawl

  • Land: worldwide, built-up area grew almost twice as fast as population, so built-up area per person rose from 43 m² to 63 m²; about 60% of land converted to urban use since 1970 was farmland.
  • From 2000 to 2020, cities sprawled outward up to 3.7 times faster than they grew upward.
  • Car dependence: longer commutes, more traffic and emissions.
  • Costs: new roads, pipes and schools at the edge, while the central city loses taxpayers.
Mission check

?Between 1950 and 2010, a Midwestern central city lost population while its metropolitan area kept growing. Which explanation fits best?

Deindustrialization plus suburbanization: Detroit lost about 65% of its 1950 population by 2020 as factory jobs went (140,000 lost in 1947–1963) and people and offices moved to suburbs, edge cities and exurbs.

Mission 4

The urban hierarchy

  • An urban hierarchy ranks settlements by size and by the functions they provide.
  • Bottom: villages and small towns with everyday services. Middle: regional and national centers.
  • Top: world cities, which serve and connect the global economy.
  • Rank depends on what a city does for places far away, not only on its size.
Mission 4

What makes a world city

  • World cities concentrate advanced producer services: banking and finance, law, accounting, advertising and consulting, which help firms work across borders.
  • They host headquarters of transnational corporations, stock exchanges and major airports.
  • The GaWC research network ranks cities as "nodes in the world city network (i.e. enabling corporate globalisation)".
  • So world cities do not just experience globalization: they drive it.
Mission 4

Ranking world cities

  • GaWC (Loughborough University) ranks cities by the office networks of 175 leading advanced-service firms across 785 cities (2024).
  • Alpha++: London and New York.
  • Alpha+: Hong Kong, Beijing, Singapore, Shanghai, Paris, Dubai, Tokyo and Sydney.
  • 49 cities reach one of the four Alpha levels.
Quick check
?What does the GaWC ranking measure?
Mission 4

Size is not status

  • UN, 2025: Jakarta nearly 42 million people, Dhaka almost 37 million, Tokyo 33 million.
  • GaWC, 2024: Tokyo Alpha+, Jakarta Alpha, Dhaka Gamma+.
  • A megacity has more than 10 million people. A world city is defined by its global functions. A city can be one without the other.
Mission 4

Networks link cities

  • When a bank or law firm opens offices in many cities, it links them: deals, data and staff move between offices.
  • These links form a network, with world cities as the best-connected nodes.
  • Money, information, people and goods flow along the links: through stock exchanges, airports, ports and data centers.
Mission 4

The network changes

  • In 2000 GaWC rated Shanghai Alpha−, Beijing Beta+ and Dubai Beta. By 2024 all three were Alpha+.
  • Shenzhen, a special economic zone since 1980, was Alpha− in 2024.
  • Caution: the 2000 study used 100 firms in 315 cities, the 2024 study 175 firms in 785 cities, so ranks are not exactly comparable.
Mission 4

How world cities mediate global processes

  • Mediate: global flows pass through world cities, and decisions there shape distant places.
  • A loan arranged in London or New York can fund a factory or port on another continent.
  • A firm in Mumbai (Alpha) can reach global investors through banks and law firms with offices there.
  • Unevenly: in Africa, only Johannesburg reaches an Alpha level (Alpha−); Cairo and Nairobi are Beta, Lagos Beta−.
Quick check
?Which is the best evidence that a city is a world city?
Mission check

?Imagine City P, with 25 million people but few offices of global banks, law firms or advertisers, and City Q, with 2 million people that hosts the regional headquarters of many such firms. Which ranks higher in the world urban hierarchy, and why?

World cities sit at the top of the urban hierarchy because they concentrate advanced producer services and headquarters that link the world economy. Tokyo (Alpha+) outranks larger Dhaka (Gamma+) for this reason: networks, not numbers, make a world city.

Mission 5

Size and spacing of cities

  • Urban hierarchy: cities ranked by size and importance. Few large cities, more medium ones, many small towns.
  • Relative size: how big each city is compared with the others.
  • Spacing: large cities lie far apart; small towns lie close together.
  • Interdependence: smaller places rely on larger ones for some services, and the reverse.
Mission 5

The rank-size rule

  • Rank-size rule: the nth largest city is about 1/n the size of the largest.
  • 2nd largest ≈ half the largest; 3rd ≈ one third; 4th ≈ one quarter.
  • A country that fits the rule has a smooth hierarchy: many cities of steadily falling size.

Mission 5

The primate city

  • Primate city: a country's largest city, more than twice the size of the second, and dominant in its economy, government and culture.
  • Bangkok had about 18.18 million people in 2025, and no other Thai city is on the UN's list of cities above 5 million. So Bangkok is more than 3.6 times the size of any other Thai city (18.18 ÷ 5).
  • From 2015 to 2025 Bangkok grew nearly 2% a year; Thailand as a whole, 0.2%.
Mission 5

Primacy is not fixed

  • USA (UN 2025, city areas defined by density): New York 13.92 million, Los Angeles 12.74 million. The 2nd city is 92% of the 1st: no primate city.
  • Mexico: Mexico City is its only city above 5 million, but its share of Mexico's people fell from 18% (2000) to 13% (2025) as smaller cities grew.
  • Primacy grows where jobs, investment and government concentrate in one city; it weakens as other cities grow.
Quick check
?In an imaginary country, the largest city has 6 million people, the second 1.2 million and the third 0.9 million. Which description fits?
Mission 5

The gravity model

  • Gravity model: interaction between two places is proportional to their populations multiplied, divided by the distance squared.
  • Interaction = (Population A × Population B) ÷ distance².
  • Bigger places interact more; distance reduces interaction fast. Used to predict trips, phone calls, trade and migration between cities.
Quick check
?City A has 2 million people. City B has 1 million and is 100 km from A. City C has 4 million and is 200 km from A. Using the gravity model, which pair interacts more?
Mission 5

Christaller's central place theory

  • Walter Christaller (1933): towns are central places that serve the people around them, their market area.
  • Threshold: the minimum number of customers a service needs to survive.
  • Range: the maximum distance people will travel for it.
  • On a flat, evenly settled plain, market areas form hexagons: they cover all the land with no gaps or overlaps.
Mission 5

Christaller's hierarchy, and its limits

  • Low-order services (grocery store, gas station): low threshold, short range → many small places, close together.
  • High-order services (major hospital, airport, sports stadium): high threshold, long range → few large places, far apart, each serving many small ones: interdependence.
  • Limits: real land is not flat or evenly settled; rivers, roads, borders and governments distort the pattern.
Mission check

?An imaginary country has one huge port city that holds the national government, most large firms and the main airport. Its next city is one-sixth as large. A neighboring country's city sizes fall smoothly: the 2nd city is half the 1st, the 3rd a third. Which statement explains the difference best?

Bangkok is a primate city: power, jobs and investment concentrate there. US cities follow the rank-size rule more closely. The gravity model and central place theory explain how cities interact and why large cities are fewer and farther apart.

Mission 6

Bid-rent theory

  • Bid-rent theory (William Alonso, 1964): land users bid for each site according to how much they gain from accessibility.
  • Accessibility is highest in the central business district (CBD), so land values peak there.
  • Values fall with distance from the CBD.
  • Each use has its own bid-rent curve; the highest bidder wins each site.
Mission 6

Tokyo's land-value gradient

  • Japan's highest commercial land value (January 1, 2026): Ginza 4-chome, ¥67.1 million per m².
  • Average residential values per m², 2026:
    • Chiyoda ward (center): ¥3,631,400
    • Setagaya ward: ¥779,900
    • Hachioji: ¥131,300
    • Ome (western edge of Tokyo): ¥98,600
Quick check
?Ginza's value (¥67,100,000 per m²) is about how many times Ome's average (¥98,600 per m²)?
Mission 6

How bid rent sorts land uses

  • Commercial users (shops, offices) gain most from being central, so they outbid others in the CBD.
  • Industry and high-density housing take the next ring; low-density housing sits further out, where land is cheap.
  • Values rise again at secondary peaks: train stations, highway junctions, suburban business centers.
  • So: tall buildings and dense land use at the center; larger plots at the edge.
Mission 6

Burgess concentric-zone model

  • Ernest Burgess, The City (University of Chicago Press, 1925), based on Chicago.
  • A city expands "radially from its central business district" in rings:
    • I CBD ("The Loop")
    • II Zone in transition: invaded by business and light manufacturing
    • III Workers' homes, close to their work
    • IV Residential zone: better-off housing
    • V Commuters' zone: suburbs "within a thirty- to sixty-minute ride" of the CBD
  • Status of housing rises with distance from the center.
Mission 6

Hoyt sector model

  • Homer Hoyt, for the US Federal Housing Administration (1939), using rent data from many American cities.
  • Land uses grow outward in sectors (wedges) along transportation routes.
  • Hoyt found "a wedge or radial development of the high rent area extending in a sector almost from the center of the city to the periphery".
  • Industry follows railroads and rivers in its own sector; low-income housing lies next to it.
Quick check
?In a city, high-income housing runs in a wedge from downtown along a scenic lakeshore boulevard, while factories line a railroad in another wedge. Which model fits best?
Mission 6

Harris and Ullman multiple-nuclei model

  • Chauncy Harris and Edward Ullman, "The Nature of Cities" (1945).
  • A city grows around several nuclei (centers), not one CBD: e.g. the CBD, an industrial district, a university, an airport, an outlying business district.
  • Similar activities cluster (they benefit from being together).
  • Some activities repel each other (heavy industry and high-income housing).
  • Fits car-based cities, where outlying centers grow.
Quick check
?A metro area has a downtown, a separate cluster of offices and malls about 12 miles away, an airport district and a university town. Which model best describes it?
Mission 6

Strengths and limitations

  • Strength: simple patterns that help explain where land uses and income groups locate.
  • Limits: built from US cities, 1920s–1940s; static; assume flat land.
  • They underplay physical geography (hills, coasts, floodplains), planning and zoning, government policy and car-era sprawl.
  • Bid rent assumes one center; real cities have many value peaks.
  • Models for cities in other world regions, and the galactic city model, come in the next mission.
Quick check
?A city squeezed onto a narrow coastal plain between mountains and the sea has grown into a long strip along the shore. Which limitation of the classic models does it show best?
Mission check

?In a US city, office towers crowd the downtown core, while warehouses and big-box stores sit on large lots near a highway at the edge of the city. What best explains this pattern?

Bid rent explains Ginza and Ome: central accessibility lets commercial users outbid everyone, so Ginza's value is about 680 times Ome's average. Burgess, Hoyt, and Harris and Ullman describe the patterns this sorting creates.

Mission 7

Recap: models from North America

  • Burgess (concentric zones): rings around the CBD; wealthier people farther out.
  • Hoyt (sector): wedges along transport routes; a high-rent sector grows outward.
  • Harris and Ullman (multiple nuclei): several centers, each drawing related land uses.
  • Bid-rent: land is most expensive at the center; only offices and shops can pay the most.
  • In these models, wealthier households tend to live toward the edge.
Mission 7

The galactic city

  • In the galactic city model (geographer Peirce Lewis), the old downtown is no longer the main center.
  • Offices, malls and housing are scattered in nodes along freeways and beltways, like stars in a galaxy.
  • Edge cities, suburban downtowns, grow at freeway junctions.
  • It describes many US metro areas built around the car.
Quick check
?A metro area has its biggest office parks and malls at freeway interchanges miles from downtown, linked by beltways. Which model fits best?
Mission 7

The Latin American city model

  • Published by Ernst Griffin and Larry Ford in 1980; Ford revised it in 1996.
  • CBD and market at the core, around the old colonial plaza.
  • Spine: a grand commercial avenue running out from the CBD, lined with offices, shops and theaters.
  • Elite residential sector follows the spine; a mall may sit at its far end.
Mission 7

Rings outside the spine

  • Zone of maturity: older, solid housing with good services.
  • Zone of in situ accretion: mixed housing, slowly improved by residents.
  • Zone of peripheral squatter settlements: informal homes built by new migrants, with the fewest services.
  • Disamenity sector: the poorest areas, often on steep slopes, by rail lines or near dumps.
  • So in this model the rich live near the center and the poorest at the edge: the reverse of Burgess.
Quick check
?In the Latin American city model, where do new migrants from rural areas most often build homes?
Mission 7

The Southeast Asian city model

  • Based on T. G. McGee's 1967 study of Southeast Asian primate cities; the model is centered on the old colonial port zone.
  • The focus is the port zone, not a single CBD.
  • Instead of one CBD: a government zone, a Western commercial zone, and an alien commercial zone (historically, Chinese merchants).
  • Around them: mixed land use, new suburbs, squatter areas, market gardening and new industrial estates at the edge.
Mission 7

The African city model

  • Associated with geographer Harm de Blij.
  • Three CBDs side by side: a colonial CBD (offices, banks), a traditional CBD (local businesses) and a market zone (often open-air).
  • Around them: ethnic and mixed neighborhoods, a mining and manufacturing zone.
  • At the edge: informal satellite townships.
Quick check
?A city has three central business areas side by side: one built under colonial rule, one of local businesses, and one large open-air market. Which model fits?
Mission 7

What the world models share

  • A colonial past shapes the core in all three.
  • Informal settlements at the edge, where migrants build their own homes.
  • In 2022, 1.12 billion people lived in slums. More than 85% of them lived in three regions: Eastern and South-Eastern Asia (362 million), Central and Southern Asia (334 million) and sub-Saharan Africa (265 million).
  • Models simplify: real cities mix features, and they change over time.
Mission check

?A Latin American city has grown for 60 years. The city has slowly extended water, sewers and paved roads outward from the old center, while rural migrants keep arriving. Away from the spine, what pattern of housing does the Griffin–Ford model predict, moving from the center to the edge?

Services and investment spread outward over time, and new migrants arrive at the edge, so housing quality falls from center to edge. Wealth follows the spine from the colonial core: the reverse of North American models, where wealth moved to the suburbs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Site = the physical land a city is built on (Tenochtitlan's island); situation = its position relative to other places (Istanbul between seas and continents). Both shape a city's origin, function and growth.
  • Urbanization is driven by transportation and communication, population growth, migration, economic development and government policies, and how much there is depends on how "urban" is defined.

Mission 2

  • Megacity: 10 million+; metacity: 20 million+ (UN-Habitat). Megacities rose from 8 (1975) to 33 (2025).
  • The largest and fastest-growing cities are increasingly in periphery and semiperiphery countries, driven by natural increase, rural-to-urban migration and reclassification.

Mission 3

  • Suburbanization, sprawl and decentralization moved homes and jobs outward, creating edge cities, exurbs and boomburbs.
  • New challenges: farmland loss, car dependence, costly infrastructure at the edge, and shrinking central-city tax bases.

Mission 4

  • World cities top the urban hierarchy and drive globalization by concentrating advanced producer services and headquarters.
  • They are nodes in global networks that move money, information, people and goods, and decisions made in them shape distant places.

Mission 5

  • Rank-size rule (nth city ≈ 1/n of the largest) vs primate city (largest more than twice the second): two patterns of city size.
  • Gravity model (population × population ÷ distance²) and Christaller's central place theory (threshold, range, hexagonal market areas, hierarchy) explain interaction, spacing and interdependence.

Mission 6

  • Bid-rent theory: land values fall with distance from the CBD; the use that gains most from accessibility outbids the rest.
  • Burgess (rings), Hoyt (sectors along routes), Harris and Ullman (multiple nuclei): all based on early-20th-century US cities.

Mission 7

  • Galactic city: scattered nodes and edge cities along freeways; the old downtown no longer dominates.
  • Latin American (spine, elite sector, peripheral squatters), Southeast Asian (port zone, separate commercial zones) and African (three CBDs, edge townships) models show colonial cores and informal edges.
