Mission 1

What finance does for the real economy

  • The financial sector: banks, insurers, pension funds, markets.
  • It channels savings to firms and households that want to borrow and invest.
  • It runs the payments system: wages, card payments, bills.
  • It spreads and prices risk: insurance, diversified funds.
  • It turns short-term deposits into long-term loans (maturity transformation).
  • UK: financial services were about 9% of economic output in 2023, a large share for a rich economy.
  • A large financial sector brings jobs, tax and exports, but also bigger risks if it fails.
Mission 1

Two ways a bank fails

  • A bank's assets: loans, bonds, reserves. Its liabilities: deposits and other borrowing.
  • Capital (equity) = assets − liabilities: the owners' stake that absorbs losses.
  • Insolvency: loans go bad, assets fall below liabilities, capital is wiped out.
  • Illiquidity: the bank is solvent but cannot turn loans into cash fast enough when depositors withdraw (a run).
  • The two feed each other: rumours of losses start a run; selling assets in a hurry causes losses.
  • Say: "capital is the owners' funding that absorbs losses" — not: "capital is cash the bank keeps in the vault".

Reading a bank's ratios

Illustrative data, Bank A (billions):

  • Leverage ratio = capital ÷ total assets = 6 ÷ 100 = 6%.

  • Liquidity: liquid assets ÷ deposits = (5 + 15) ÷ 85 = 23.5%.
  • Stress: 8% of loans go bad. Loss = 0.08 × 80 = 6.4, more than capital of 6. Bank A is insolvent.
  • More capital = a bigger cushion. More liquid assets = more time in a run.

Mission 1

Systemic risk

  • Systemic risk: the risk that trouble at one bank spreads to the whole financial system.
  • Banks lend to each other, so one failure means losses for others (contagion).
  • Fire sales: a bank dumps assets, prices fall, other banks' assets lose value.
  • Panic: depositors run from banks that look similar.
  • Lending shrinks (a credit crunch), so firms cannot borrow; output and jobs fall.
  • These costs fall on people outside the bank: a negative externality, a reason to regulate.
Mission 1

Moral hazard and too big to fail

  • Moral hazard: being protected from the cost of a risk makes people take more risk.
  • If a bank is too big to fail, its owners and lenders expect a rescue.
  • So profits from risky lending stay private, while big losses fall on taxpayers.
  • Lenders charge such banks less, so they can grow even bigger and riskier.
  • Deposit insurance and the lender of last resort help to prevent runs, but can add to moral hazard.
  • Say: "moral hazard: behaviour changes after protection is given" — not: "adverse selection", which is hidden information before a deal (see Mission 2).

Mission 1

The crisis of 2007–08

  • (1) 2000s: US house prices boom. Lenders sell sub-prime mortgages to borrowers with weak credit.
  • (2) Mortgages are bundled into securities and sold worldwide; banks borrow heavily (high leverage) to buy them.
  • (3) From 2006, US house prices fall. Defaults rise; nobody knows which banks hold the losses.
  • (4) August 2007: banks stop lending to each other. September 2007: run on Northern Rock (UK). 15 September 2008: Lehman Brothers goes bankrupt.
  • (5) Governments rescue banks: in 2008–09 the UK government took large stakes in RBS and Lloyds Banking Group.
  • An asset bubble in housing, fed by cheap credit, burst.
Mission 1

From bank losses to the real economy

  • Banks with less capital cut lending to rebuild their ratios: the lending collapse.
  • Firms cancel investment; households cut spending; AD falls.
  • UK real GDP fell by just over 6% from early 2008 to mid-2009: at the time, the deepest UK recession since the Second World War.
Mission 1

Who regulates UK finance

  • Before 2013 the FSA, the Bank of England and the Treasury shared the job; the gaps were blamed for missing the risks.
  • Since April 2013:
  • Financial Policy Committee (FPC), at the Bank of England: watches risks to the whole system (macroprudential), e.g. sets extra capital buffers when credit grows fast.
  • Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England: supervises the safety of individual banks and insurers (microprudential).
  • Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate: regulates how firms treat customers and keeps markets honest.
  • The Bank of England is still lender of last resort: it lends to solvent banks short of cash.
Mission 1

The rules after the crisis

  • More capital (Basel III): banks must hold core capital of at least 4.5% of risk-weighted assets, plus a 2.5% buffer, and more for the biggest banks.
  • Leverage ratio: capital of at least 3% of total assets, with no risk weights (UK: 3.25% for large banks).
  • Liquidity coverage ratio: enough liquid assets to survive 30 days of stressed outflows.
  • UK ring-fencing (from 2019): big banks separate everyday deposits from riskier investment banking.
  • Stress tests: regulators test whether banks could survive a severe recession.
  • Bail-in: losses fall on a failing bank's owners and lenders first, not taxpayers.

Mission 1

Why regulating finance is hard

  • Global finance: banks and money move across borders; a crisis abroad can hit home banks.
  • Regulatory arbitrage: activity moves to where rules are lighter, or to shadow banks (funds and lenders outside bank rules).
  • Information: regulators know less than banks; risks look small until they are not.
  • Regulatory capture: regulators may come to see things the industry's way.
Mission 1Evaluate

Do tighter rules make us safer?

  • For: more capital and liquidity mean banks absorb losses and survive runs; fewer rescues; less moral hazard.
  • Against: capital costs banks more than deposits, so loans may be dearer and scarcer, slowing investment and growth.
  • Against: risk can move to shadow banks, where it is harder to see.
  • It depends on: how high the requirements are; whether rules are international; whether regulators can spot risks in time; and whether a failing bank can really be wound down without a rescue.
Mission check

?A country's government announces that it will cover every loss of its three largest banks, with no new rules on how much capital they hold. What does economic theory predict?

Protection from losses changes behaviour. Because bank failures spread to the whole economy, governments protect banks, and then regulate them to limit the extra risk this invites.

Mission 2

Finance and the real economy

  • The real economy: the production of goods and services, jobs and incomes.
  • The financial sector serves it: it moves savings to borrowers, runs payments, and shares out risk.
  • It works well when prices of assets and loans reflect real risks and returns.
  • When finance misprices risk, money flows to the wrong uses, and a crash can cut lending, spending and jobs.
  • So failures in financial markets matter far beyond the banks.
Mission 2

Asymmetric information

  • Asymmetric information: one side of a deal knows more than the other.
  • Borrowers know more about their ability to repay than lenders.
  • Sellers of financial products know more than buyers: complex terms, hidden fees.
  • Adverse selection (before the deal): high interest rates attract the riskiest borrowers.
  • Moral hazard (after the deal): someone protected from risk takes more of it (see Mission 1).
  • UK example: payment protection insurance (PPI) was mis-sold with loans for years; banks paid about 38 billion pounds in compensation by 2020.
  • 2000s US: lenders who sold mortgages on to investors had little reason to check borrowers; buyers of the bundles could not see the risk.
Quick check
?A lender sells each mortgage it makes to investors within weeks, earning a fee every time. The investors cannot check each borrower. What market failure does this create?
Mission 2

Externalities: risk that others pay for

  • When a bank takes risks, it weighs its own costs and gains (MPC, MPB).
  • A crash also hurts third parties: firms that lose credit, workers who lose jobs, taxpayers who fund rescues.
  • So the marginal social cost of risky lending is above the private cost: MSC > MPC.
  • The market lends at Q1 (MPB = MPC). The social optimum is Q* (MSB = MSC).
  • Too much risky lending: the shaded triangle is the welfare loss.
Mission 2

Speculation and bubbles

  • Speculation: buying an asset to sell it later at a higher price, not to use it.
  • A bubble: an asset's price rises far above the value its future income can justify.
  • Rising prices draw in buyers who expect more rises; demand shifts right; prices rise again.
  • When expectations turn, everyone tries to sell; the price crashes.
  • Real-world: the US technology share index (the Nasdaq Composite) peaked in March 2000, then fell by about 78% by October 2002.
  • 2000s: a US housing bubble, fed by easy credit, burst from 2006 and set off the 2007–08 crisis (see Mission 1).
  • Say: "a rise in expected future prices shifts demand to the right" — not: "higher prices increase demand".

Mission 2

Herding and animal spirits

  • Herding: people copy what others are doing, assuming the crowd knows something.
  • For a fund manager, being wrong with everyone else is safer for their career than being wrong alone.
  • Animal spirits (Keynes, 1936): waves of optimism and fear drive decisions, not careful calculation.
  • Long booms make people forget risk: borrowing and debt build up while things look safe.
Mission 2

When finance fails, the real economy pays

  • Credit: banks with losses cut lending; firms cancel investment.
  • Debt: households with big debts cut spending when house prices fall.
  • Wealth effect: falling asset prices make people feel poorer, so consumption falls.
  • Confidence: fear spreads, and firms and households wait.
  • AD falls, output and jobs fall: a financial crash becomes a recession.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can regulators stop bubbles?

  • For acting: lean against booms with macroprudential tools. UK example: from 2014 the FPC limited the share of new mortgages at 4.5 times income or more to 15%.
  • For acting: better information rules and fines reduce mis-selling and rigging.
  • Against: bubbles are hard to spot in time; rising prices may reflect real changes.
  • Against: rules can push risk to less regulated lenders; and popping a bubble can cause the crash it aims to avoid.
  • It depends on: how quickly a bubble can be recognised; how much of it is funded by borrowing; and whether rules cover all lenders, not just banks.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: the price of a rare collectible digital token rises by 300% in a year. Nothing about how it can be used has changed. Most buyers say they bought it because they expect to sell it at a higher price. Which explanation and risk fit best?

When buyers buy because they expect prices to rise, expectations can drive a market far from real value, and then into a crash.

Mission 3

What financial markets do

  • Financial markets link savers (who have spare money) with borrowers (who need it).
  • Their roles:
    • Help people save: a safe place and a return.
    • Lend to households and firms: mortgages, business loans.
    • Make exchange easy: payments for goods and services.
    • Provide forward markets: fix a price today for a deal in the future.
    • Provide a market for equities (shares), so firms can raise money.
  • Benefit to the real economy: savings flow to investment, which raises productive capacity and growth.
Mission 3

Money, capital and foreign exchange markets

  • Money market: short-term lending and borrowing (under a year), e.g. bills between banks and firms.
  • Capital market: long-term finance through shares and bonds.
    • Primary market: new shares or bonds are sold, raising money for the issuer.
    • Secondary market: existing ones are traded between investors (e.g. the stock exchange).
  • Foreign exchange (forex) market: currencies are bought and sold.
  • Forward market example (illustrative): an exporter expects to receive 100,000 euros in 3 months. It agrees a fixed exchange rate today, so a fall in the euro cannot hurt it.
Mission 3

Debt versus equity

  • Debt: borrowed money that must be repaid with interest.
    • Loans from banks.
    • Bonds: a borrower (government or firm) sells an IOU that pays fixed yearly interest and repays the face value at maturity.
  • Equity: shares give part-ownership of a firm.
    • Shareholders may get dividends if the firm makes a profit; nothing is guaranteed.
    • If a firm fails, lenders are paid before shareholders.
  • So: shares usually carry more risk but a higher expected return.
  • Say: "a firm raises equity finance by selling shares" — not: "a firm borrows by selling shares".
Quick check

Mission 3

Liquidity, return and risk

  • Every asset can be judged on three things:
    • Liquidity: how easily it turns into spending power.
    • Rate of return: what it earns (interest, dividends, price gains).
    • Risk: how far that return could vary, or be lost.
  • Cash and current account deposits: most liquid, little or no return, low risk.
  • Bonds: less liquid, fixed interest, prices can change.
  • Shares: higher expected return, more risk.
  • Opportunity cost of holding money = the interest forgone by not holding bonds or savings instead.
    • When interest rates rise, holding money costs more, so people hold less of it.
Mission 3

How bonds work

  • A bond has:
    • Face value: what is repaid at the end, e.g. 1,000.
    • Coupon: the fixed yearly interest, e.g. 5% of 1,000 = 50 a year.
    • Maturity: the date it is repaid, e.g. in 10 years.
  • After it is sold, a bond can be traded. Its market price can change; its coupon cannot.
  • Suppose new bonds now pay more interest.
    • The old bond still pays only 50 a year.
    • No one will pay 1,000 for it when a new 1,000 bond pays more.
    • Its price must fall until it offers a similar return.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.

Mission 3

Regulation and the real economy

  • Benefits of the financial sector to the real economy:
    • Channels savings into investment.
    • Spreads and prices risk (insurance, forward contracts).
    • Makes payments fast and cheap.
  • Why regulate:
    • Asymmetric information: savers cannot judge how risky a bank is.
    • Systemic risk: one failure can spread (2008).
    • Moral hazard: banks may take more risk if they expect to be rescued.
  • UK regulators: the PRA (bank safety), the FCA (how firms treat customers), the FPC (risks to the whole system).
  • Too little regulation risks crises; too much can raise costs and reduce lending.

Mission 4

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: £1 = $1.25. So $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system, the government or central bank sets the rate; a change is a devaluation or revaluation (see Mission 6).
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 4

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate £1 = $1.30.
  • A £500 phone sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans cost a UK buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = £50.
  • The pound appreciates to $1.50. Phone: 500 × 1.50 = $750. Jeans: 65 ÷ 1.50 = £43.33.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 4

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.

Mission 4

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Much of the trading is for financial investment and speculation, not to pay for goods.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand = exports + capital inflows. Supply = imports + capital outflows.
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 4

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • US central bank (the Federal Reserve): top of its target range for interest rates 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission 4Evaluate

How far can you predict a currency?

  • It depends on:
    • several factors at once: interest rates may rise while growth slows; the effects can offset
    • expectations: speculators act on what they expect, so a rate can move before anything else changes
    • relative, not absolute: what matters is a country's interest rates, inflation and growth compared with other countries'
    • time horizon: day to day, financial flows dominate; over years, trade and competitiveness matter more
  • A central bank may also intervene (see Mission 6).

Mission 5

Who wants a currency, and who sells it?

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand for the home currency comes from foreigners who need it to:
    • buy the country's exports of goods and services
    • buy its financial assets: bonds, shares, bank deposits (portfolio investment)
    • set up or buy firms there (inward FDI)
  • Supply of the home currency comes from residents who sell it to buy imports and foreign assets.
  • Financial flows are huge and move in seconds. News about interest rates hits the currency within minutes.
Mission 5

Money flows toward the higher real interest rate

  • Investors compare the return on assets in different countries.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • Illustrative data:
Nominal rateInflationReal rate
Country A5%4%1%
Country B3%1%2%
  • A has the higher nominal rate, but B has the higher real rate.
  • Other things equal, financial capital flows out of A and into B.
  • Demand for B's currency rises: B's currency appreciates.
  • Large, fast, short-term flows chasing returns are called hot money.
  • Say: "capital flows toward the higher real interest rate" — not: toward the higher nominal rate.

Mission 5

From a rate decision to the whole economy

  • The central bank cuts Bank Rate:
  • Real interest rates fall in the short run → financial capital flows out → the currency depreciates.
  • Exports become cheaper abroad; imports more expensive at home → net exports rise → AD rises.
  • Effects:
    • output rises and unemployment falls (if there is spare capacity)
    • inflation rises: more expensive imports, and AD closer to capacity
    • the current account tends to improve, after a time lag
  • A rate rise works the other way: appreciation, lower net exports, lower AD.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): the central bank creates money to buy bonds. Bond yields fall, so investors move money abroad: the currency tends to depreciate.
Quick check
?A central bank raises its policy interest rate by 1 percentage point. Inflation expectations do not change. Which chain of effects is most likely?
Mission 5

Beyond interest rates

  • Relative inflation: faster inflation at home makes exports less competitive. Demand for the currency falls; it depreciates.
  • Relative growth: faster growth draws in FDI and portfolio investment (currency up), but also raises imports (currency down).
  • Confidence: investors avoid countries with political or financial risk.
  • Speculation: if traders expect a currency to rise, they buy it now, and it rises.
  • Safe haven: in a crisis, investors buy currencies seen as safe.
  • Central bank intervention: buying the home currency with foreign reserves raises demand for it; selling it lowers its value.
  • Trade: rising exports raise demand for the currency; rising imports raise its supply.
  • Real case: in 2011, during the euro-area debt crisis, money poured into the Swiss franc. The Swiss National Bank set a minimum exchange rate of 1.20 francs per euro and sold francs to defend it.
  • On 15 January 2015 it dropped the minimum rate and cut its interest rate to −0.75%. The franc rose sharply against the euro.
  • The rate cut alone would pull the franc down. Ending the sales of francs outweighed it.
Quick check
?Investors expect Country K's currency to fall next month. Many sell it today. What is the most likely result?
Mission 5

Case: the yen and the dollar in 2022

  • In 2022 the US central bank (the Federal Reserve) raised its target for the federal funds rate from 0–0.25% to 4.25–4.50%.
  • The Bank of Japan kept its short-term policy rate at −0.1%.
  • The gap in interest rates widened, and funds flowed toward US assets.
  • Real returns rose too: the yield on 10-year inflation-protected US government bonds went from −0.97% (3 January) to 1.73% (20 October).
  • Yen per US dollar: 115.27 on 3 January 2022; 149.82 on 20 October 2022.
  • Worked numbers:
    • Value of the dollar in yen: (149.82 − 115.27) ÷ 115.27 × 100 = +30.0%
    • Value of the yen in dollars: 1 ÷ 115.27 = 0.008675; 1 ÷ 149.82 = 0.006675. Change = −23.1%
  • A 30% rise in one currency is not a 30% fall in the other.
  • Japan's authorities intervened as the yen fell: on 22 September, 21 October and 24 October 2022 they sold dollars and bought yen.
Mission 5Evaluate

Does a higher interest rate always lift the currency?

It depends on:

  • Real, not nominal: if inflation rises faster than the rate, the real return falls.
  • Expectations: markets often price in a rise before it happens. A rise smaller than expected can make a currency fall.
  • Other countries: if foreign central banks raise rates too, the gap may not change.
  • Confidence and risk: a rate rise to defend a currency in a crisis may be read as a sign of trouble.
  • Other forces: trade flows, growth, speculation and safe-haven moves can outweigh the rate change.
  • So: a rate rise tends to cause appreciation, other things equal. In practice other things are rarely equal.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Country R's central bank keeps its nominal interest rate at 4%, while R's inflation rate rises from 2% to 5%. Interest rates and inflation abroad do not change. What is the most likely effect on R's currency?

Investors look through the headline rate to the real return, so inflation can move a currency even when the central bank stands still.

Mission 6

Three exchange rate systems

  • Floating: demand and supply in the currency market set the rate (see Mission 4). A rise is an appreciation, a fall a depreciation.
  • Fixed (a peg): the central bank commits to one rate against another currency or a basket. A deliberate cut in the official rate is a devaluation; a deliberate rise, a revaluation.
  • Managed float: the rate floats, but the central bank steps in to limit or steer its movements.
  • A managed float is also called a dirty float.
  • Real examples: the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at about 7.80 since 1983; the euro floats; Singapore manages its currency against a basket of its trading partners' currencies.
  • Say: "the pound depreciated" for a floating currency — not: "the pound was devalued". Devaluation is a policy decision under a fixed rate.

Mission 6

Holding a fixed rate

  • Step 1: the market for the currency clears at the peg.
  • Step 2: demand for the currency falls (D1 → D2). At the peg, supply now exceeds demand: the market rate would fall below the peg.
  • Step 3: the central bank closes the gap. It buys its own currency, paying with foreign currency reserves. This adds to demand: D2 → D3, back to the peg.
  • Other tools: raise interest rates (attracts financial inflows, so more demand for the currency); exchange controls (limits on buying foreign currency); borrowing reserves from abroad.
  • Pressure the other way (demand rises): the central bank sells its own currency and builds up reserves, or cuts interest rates.

Mission 6

The effective (trade-weighted) exchange rate

  • One currency has many exchange rates. They often move in different directions.
  • The effective exchange rate (trade-weighted index) is a weighted average of them, with weights from each partner's share of trade.
  • Base year = 100. Each partner's rate is an index.
  • Illustrative data:

  • Say: "the index rose to 103.5" — not: "the currency is worth 103.5".

It depends on:

  • Credibility and reserves: on 16 September 1992 the UK raised interest rates from 10% to 12% in one day to hold the pound in Europe's Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), then left the system that evening.
  • Where shocks come from: a floating rate absorbs shocks from abroad; a peg imports the anchor country's interest rates, which may not suit.
  • Openness and trade links: a small, open economy trading mainly with one partner (Hong Kong, with the US dollar) gains more from a peg.
  • Inflation history: a peg can import credibility, but if inflation stays higher than the anchor's, the currency becomes overvalued.

Mission 7

Prices across the border

  • A firm sets its price in its own currency. The exchange rate decides what that price is abroad.
  • Illustrative data: the pound falls from 1.50 to 1.20 dollars: a 20% depreciation.
  • UK export priced £10: in the US, it was 10 × 1.50 = $15; now 10 × 1.20 = $12. Cheaper abroad.
  • US import priced $30: in the UK, it was 30 ÷ 1.50 = £20; now 30 ÷ 1.20 = £25. Dearer at home.
  • Memory aid: SPICED — Strong Pound, Imports Cheap, Exports Dear. A weak pound does the reverse.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates from 2.00 to 1.50 units of foreign currency. A firm keeps the price of its export at 50 in its own currency. What does a foreign buyer now pay?
Mission 7

Net exports and AD

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad → export volumes rise. Imports cost more at home → import volumes fall.
  • So net exports (X − M) tend to rise, and AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2).
  • Step 1: AD1 meets SRAS below full capacity.
  • Step 2: AD1 → AD2: real GDP rises, the price level rises, unemployment tends to fall.
  • Step 3: imported raw materials and parts cost more, so SRAS shifts left (SRAS1 → SRAS2): more inflation, less of the output gain.
  • An appreciation does the reverse: net exports fall, AD shifts left.
  • A central bank interest rate cut tends to weaken the currency (see Mission 5): a second channel from monetary policy to AD.
  • Say: "a depreciation shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".

Mission 7

The Marshall-Lerner condition

  • A depreciation makes each import dearer. Whether spending on imports falls depends on how much the import volume falls.
  • Marshall-Lerner condition: starting from balanced trade, a depreciation improves the trade balance if PEDx + PEDm > 1 (absolute values: the price elasticities of demand for exports and for imports).
  • Illustrative data: exports = imports = 100 (home currency). The currency depreciates by 10%.
  • Short run: PEDx = 0.3, PEDm = 0.4. Sum = 0.7 < 1.
    • Exports: 100 × (1 + 0.1 × 0.3) = 103.
    • Imports: 100 × 1.1 × (1 − 0.1 × 0.4) = 105.6.
    • Trade balance: 103 − 105.6 = −2.6. It worsens.
  • Long run: PEDx = 0.8, PEDm = 0.7. Sum = 1.5 > 1.
    • Exports 108, imports 110 × 0.93 = 102.3, balance +5.7. It improves.
  • The depreciation also worsens the terms of trade: import prices rise in home currency while export prices do not.
  • Say: "the sum of the elasticities must exceed 1" — not: "each elasticity must exceed 1".
Quick check
?A country starts with balanced trade. The price elasticity of demand for its exports is −0.6 and for its imports −0.3. Its currency depreciates. Other things equal, what happens to its trade balance?
Mission 7Evaluate

Does a weaker currency help?

It depends on:

  • Elasticities and time: buyers are slow to switch, so volumes change little at first; the gain may take years.
  • Spare capacity: with output below its full employment level, more AD raises output and jobs; near full capacity, mostly inflation.
  • Import content of exports: in global supply chains, dearer imported parts eat into the price advantage.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design and reliability may matter more than price.
  • Why the currency fell, and others' response: a fall caused by lost confidence may also deter investment; rivals may devalue too (competitive devaluation).
Mission check

?Investors abroad move funds into a country, and its currency appreciates sharply. Other things equal, which effect of the appreciation is most likely in the short run?

An appreciation is the mirror image of a depreciation: net exports and AD fall, and imported inflation eases.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Banks fail through insolvency (losses wipe out capital) or illiquidity (a run); capital and liquidity ratios measure the cushions.
  • Bank failures spread (systemic risk), and expected rescues encourage risk (moral hazard, too big to fail): two reasons to regulate.
  • After 2007–08: FPC, PRA and FCA, Basel III capital and liquidity rules, ring-fencing and stress tests; regulation has costs and limits.

Mission 2

  • Financial markets fail through asymmetric information, externalities (crashes hurt third parties), moral hazard, and speculation and bubbles.
  • Expectations, herding and animal spirits can shift demand for assets, so prices overshoot and then crash.
  • Failures in finance spread to the real economy through credit, debt, wealth and confidence.

Mission 3

  • Financial markets link savers and borrowers; money markets are short-term, capital markets long-term.
  • Debt (loans, bonds) must be repaid with interest; equity (shares) gives ownership and uncertain dividends.
  • Bond prices and market interest rates move in opposite directions.

Mission 4

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 5

  • Capital flows toward the higher real interest rate, so a rise in a country's real rate tends to make its currency appreciate.
  • Monetary policy works through the exchange rate: a rate cut → depreciation → higher net exports and AD.
  • Inflation, growth, confidence, speculation, safe-haven demand and intervention also move currencies.

Mission 6

  • Floating: market sets the rate (appreciation/depreciation). Fixed: the central bank holds it (devaluation/revaluation). Managed: it floats, with intervention.
  • A peg is held by buying or selling the currency with reserves, and by interest rates.
  • Effective exchange rate = trade-weighted average of a currency's exchange rate indices.

Mission 7

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad, imports more expensive at home, net exports and AD rise. Appreciation: the reverse.
  • The price level rises (AD and dearer inputs); importers, consumers and travellers lose, exporters gain.
  • Marshall-Lerner: a depreciation improves the trade balance only if PEDx + PEDm > 1.
