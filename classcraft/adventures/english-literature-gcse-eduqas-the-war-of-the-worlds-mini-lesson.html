The War of the Worlds · structure and the narratorScreen 5

Other people's stories

  • Book 1, Chapters 14, 16 and 17 follow the narrator's brother in London: "My younger brother was in London when the Martians fell at Woking." (Chapter 14)
  • Chapter 15, "What Had Happened in Surrey", is pieced together "from the conflicting accounts".
  • Newspaper reports and telegrams are quoted inside the story.
  • Two strands run at the same time: a parallel narrative. In Book 2, Chapter 1 he admits, "I have wandered so much from my own adventures".

The War of the Worlds · structure and the narratorScreen 7

How it begins and ends

  • In Chapter 1 the narrator watches Mars through Ogilvy's telescope; the novel ends in the Epilogue with him in his study, still uneasy: "the stress and danger of the time have left an abiding sense of doubt and insecurity in my mind".
  • Back home in Book 2, Chapter 9 he finds his unfinished paper on moral progress, stopped mid-sentence: "In about two hundred years," I had written, "we may expect——"
  • The broken sentence is ironic: confident predictions about progress were cut off by events.

The War of the Worlds · the narratorScreen 1

Who is he?

  • Unnamed, married, living at Maybury near Woking; "a professed and recognised writer on philosophical themes" (Book 2, Chapter 7).
  • Before the invasion he was writing papers on "the probable developments of moral ideas as civilisation progressed" (Book 1, Chapter 1).
  • Without a name he works as an everyman: any educated reader could be in his place.
  • Remember: the narrator is a construct, not Wells himself. Write Wells presents the narrator as….

A confession

  • In Book 2, Chapter 7 he judges himself for the curate's death: "I stood my trial, my only trial, for that moment of wrath and fear."
  • He insists on honesty: "There were no witnesses—all these things I might have concealed."
  • He leaves the verdict to us: "the reader must form his judgment as he will."
  • Some readers find this confessional voice trustworthy; others notice that he also excuses himself as "the creature of a sequence of accidents".

Mission 3

The Martians are monsters. So why do they look like our future?

Make a guess: The Martians are monsters. So why do they look like our future?

The War of the Worlds · the MartiansScreen 1

The first sight

  • Book 1, Chapter 4: the crowd expects "a man emerge"; instead "a big greyish rounded bulk, the size, perhaps, of a bear" rises from the cylinder.
  • The narrator piles up details in a long list, and uses a semantic field of disgust and decay.
  • He alludes to the Gorgon (Greek myth: a monster with snakes for hair) for the tentacles.
  • He admits his reaction: "I was overcome with disgust and dread."

The War of the Worlds · the MartiansScreen 5

Brains in machines

  • Fighting-machines: tripods "higher than many houses" carrying the Heat-Ray (Book 1, Chapter 10).
  • Handling-machines: crab-like, so quick that one "seemed infinitely more alive than the actual Martians" (Book 2, Chapter 2).
  • The Martians themselves "were heads—merely heads", with no digestion. They feed by injecting the blood of living creatures, which the narrator refuses to describe in full.
  • They do not sleep, have no sex, and (he believes) share thoughts without sound.
The War of the Worlds · the MartiansScreen 6

A glimpse of our future?

  • In Book 2, Chapter 2 the narrator recalls a writer who predicted, in 1893, that humans would evolve into brains and hands.
  • He concludes: "To me it is quite credible that the Martians may be descended from beings not unlike ourselves".
  • Wells is teasing himself: his own essay The Man of the Year Million appeared in the Pall Mall Budget in November 1893, and the narrator calls its tone "foolish, facetious".
  • The warning: intellect without a body becomes "a mere selfish intelligence".

Mission checkScreen 8

?The Martians are monsters. So why do they look like our future?

The narrator suggests the Martians may have evolved from beings like us, losing body and gaining brain, as Wells had joked humans might in his 1893 essay. So the monsters are also a warning: intelligence without feeling, and humanity's possible future.

Mission 4

Earth's mightiest invaders are killed by germs. Is that a cheat?

Make a guess: Earth's mightiest invaders are killed by germs. Is that a cheat?

The War of the Worlds · science and evolutionScreen 1

Darwin in the novel

  • Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859): natural selection. In the struggle for existence, living things with useful variations survive and pass them on.
  • Wells trained in biology under T. H. Huxley, Darwin's leading champion, at the Normal School of Science from 1884.
  • Book 1, Chapter 1: "life is an incessant struggle for existence, and it would seem that this too is the belief of the minds upon Mars."

The War of the Worlds · science and evolutionScreen 4

Planted from the start

  • Book 1, Ch 1: life on old, cooling Mars has "hardened their hearts" in the struggle to survive.
  • Book 2, Ch 2: Mars has no germs. Micro-organisms "have either never appeared upon Mars or Martian sanitary science eliminated them ages ago."
  • Book 2, Ch 6: bacteria kill the red weed: "the red weed rotted like a thing already dead."
  • This foreshadowing answers the charge of deus ex machina (an outside force suddenly solving the plot).

The War of the Worlds · science and evolutionScreen 6

Science that sounds real

  • Verisimilitude (seeming true): real names ("Men like Schiaparelli", the Lick Observatory, Nature), exact figures ("140,000,000 miles") and technical detail: the Heat-Ray's "polished parabolic mirror" (Book 1, Ch 6).
  • Yet he admits doubt: the germ explanation is only "almost as a proven conclusion" (Epilogue).
  • Irony: Ogilvy says "The chances against anything manlike on Mars are a million to one".

Mission checkScreen 8

?Earth's mightiest invaders are killed by germs. Is that a cheat?

It isn't a cheat on the novel's own terms. Wells sets up the struggle for existence in Chapter 1, notes in Book 2 that Mars has no micro-organisms, and describes the red weed dying of bacteria two chapters before the end, so the Martians' deaths follow from natural selection. Some readers still find it sudden: the strongest answers explain how it is prepared.

Mission 5

Why would a British novel make Britain the conquered country?

Make a guess: Why would a British novel make Britain the conquered country?

The War of the Worlds · empire and invasionScreen 1

Invasion stories

  • In 1897 Britain ruled the largest empire in the world. The artilleryman calls it "the greatest power in the world" (Book 2, Ch 7).
  • Invasion literature: George Chesney's The Battle of Dorking (1871) imagined Germany invading England, and many imitations followed.
  • Wells keeps the form (real Surrey and London places destroyed) but changes the enemy.
  • Book 1, Ch 8: the cylinder made less stir than "an ultimatum to Germany would have done".
Extract The War of the Worlds · empire and invasionScreen 2

The narrator asks us not to judge too quickly

The War of the Worlds · empire and invasionScreen 3

The Tasmanians: the facts

  • From British settlement in 1803, violence, disease and forced removal (to Flinders Island, 1830s) devastated Tasmania's Aboriginal people. Many historians call it genocide; others debate the term.
  • "Entirely swept out of existence" repeats a 19th-century myth: Aboriginal Tasmanian communities live on today.
  • "Inferior races" and "in spite of their human likeness" are racist 1890s language: quote them only to analyse them.
  • Wells recalled the idea may have begun in a talk about Tasmania.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why would a British novel make Britain the conquered country?

Wells borrows the popular invasion story but makes the invader Mars, not Germany, and has his narrator compare the Martians to European colonisers. British readers at the height of empire are put in the place of the conquered. Many readers see this as a critique of imperialism; others note that the narrator still uses the racist language of his time.

Make a guess: When the Martians come, what breaks first: buildings or people?

The War of the Worlds · society in collapseScreen 1

Business as usual

  • Book 1, Ch 8: the narrator is struck by "the dovetailing of the commonplace habits of our social order" with the start of its fall.
  • On Friday night "people were dining and supping"; trains run; newspapers print rumours.
  • People at a garden gate laugh at the narrator's news (Ch 7). At Byfleet an old man argues for his orchids (Ch 12).
  • This complacency makes the later collapse more shocking.

People under pressure

  • The curate loses his faith's comfort: "Why are these things permitted?" (Book 1, Ch 13).

  • Miss Elphinstone keeps her nerve and drives the pony; her sister-in-law panics and calls for "George".

The War of the Worlds · society in collapseScreen 5

The artilleryman's new order

  • On Putney Hill (Book 2, Ch 7) he plans an underground society in the drains, sneering at "damn little clerks".
  • He would keep only the "able-bodied, clean-minded", saying others "ought to die": the cruelty he fears, turned on his own kind.
  • His tunnel is "scarcely ten yards long": the narrator sees "the gulf between his dreams and his powers".
  • Many readers see this as satire of brutal survival-of-the-fittest talk.

People break first. Wells shows routine carrying on at first, then fear spreading until police, railways and property rights fail, while much of London still stands empty. Some characters, like Miss Elphinstone and the brother, keep their courage, which makes the collapse of others more striking.

Mission 7

What did a reader in 1897 see in this story that we miss?

Make a guess: What did a reader in 1897 see in this story that we miss?

The War of the Worlds · contexts and key quotationsScreen 1

The first readers

  • Serialised in Pearson's Magazine, April to December 1897; published as a book by Heinemann in 1898.
  • The places were real: Wells lived in Woking from 1895 and cycled the roads he destroys.
  • Readers knew Darwin (On the Origin of Species, 1859), invasion stories and the Empire.
  • So the novel felt close to home: a real Surrey commuter town, a familiar fear, the latest science.
The War of the Worlds · contexts and key quotationsScreen 2

Mars in the news

  • In 1877 the astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli mapped lines on Mars he called canali ("channels"). In English this became "canals", which suggested builders.
  • In 1895 Percival Lowell argued in Mars that intelligent beings had dug them.
  • The novel names Schiaparelli, who "failed to interpret the fluctuating appearances of the markings they mapped so well" (Book 1, Ch 1).
  • Wells flips the usual hope: men "fancied there might be other men upon Mars, perhaps inferior to themselves".

Model answer

In the Epilogue the narrator admits, "To them, and not to us, perhaps, is the future ordained." The cautious "perhaps" and the inverted word order put "them" before "us", so the Martians come first and humanity second. For readers raised on Darwin's idea that species rise and fall, and on the belief that Britain led the world's progress, this was unsettling: the future might belong to another, more advanced species.

Mission checkScreen 8

?What did a reader in 1897 see in this story that we miss?

First readers met real Surrey towns, the latest Mars science and Darwin's struggle for existence, in the familiar shape of an invasion story. Wells turned it round, so the world's leading empire became the conquered, and its confidence in progress and safety was shaken.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The novel is a first-person retrospective account by an unnamed survivor, in two books (17 and 10 chapters).
  • Parallel strands (the brother in London, the reconstructed Chapter 15) widen the view; quoted papers and reports make it feel like a true record.
  • Your question is built on a printed extract: know where it sits in the two books and how the narration shapes it.
  • The unnamed narrator is an everyman: an educated writer whose confidence is broken by the invasion.
  • He moves from overconfidence and war fever to terror, violence, humility and lasting unease.
  • His confessional first-person voice invites us to judge him; readers disagree about how far to trust it.
  • The first description (Book 1, Ch 4) piles up contradictory details and a semantic field of disgust: the Martians are hard to put into words.
  • They are "heads—merely heads" that live through machines, feed on blood and feel no fatigue.
  • The narrator suggests they may descend from beings like us, which turns horror into a warning about human evolution.
  • Darwin's natural selection and "struggle for existence" run from the first chapter to the last.
  • The ending is foreshadowed: Mars has no micro-organisms (Book 2, Ch 2) and the red weed rots from bacteria (Book 2, Ch 6).
  • Verisimilitude (real names, figures, technical detail) makes the science fiction feel like a report, while the narrator admits what is unproven.
  • Context counts in your answer: link Darwin to a specific line, not as a separate paragraph.
  • Invasion literature (from The Battle of Dorking, 1871) was popular; Wells keeps the form but makes the invader Martian.
  • The narrator compares the Martians to colonisers (the Tasmanians, the dodo, missionaries), so British readers see conquest from below.
  • Handle the Tasmanian passage factually: the narrator repeats a 19th-century myth; Aboriginal Tasmanians survive today.
  • Complacency first (Book 1, Ch 7–8), then panic: by Monday the social order is "liquefaction".
  • Characters show different responses: the curate's despair, the gold-clutching man, Miss Elphinstone's courage, the artilleryman's brutal dream.
  • Water and body imagery turn crowds into forces of nature.
  • First published as a serial in Pearson's Magazine (1897), then as a book (1898), set in real places Wells knew.
  • Contexts to link: Darwin and natural selection; empire and invasion literature; the Mars "canals" debate; Victorian confidence in progress.
  • Use context to explain a line or method, never as a separate paragraph of history.
  • Context (AO3) is assessed in your question: build it into your analysis of the extract.

Key quotations to learn

  • "No one would have believed in the last years of the nineteenth century" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Hindsight from the very first line.
  • "The storm burst upon us six years ago now." (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") The narrator writes years after the events.
  • "My younger brother was in London when the Martians fell at Woking." (Book 1, Ch 14 "In London") The switch to a parallel strand.
  • "It was the beginning of the rout of civilisation, of the massacre of mankind." (Book 1, Ch 17 "The Thunder Child") The low point that closes Book One.
  • "In about two hundred years," I had written, "we may expect——" (Book 2, Ch 9 "Wreckage") Confident prophecy cut off: irony about progress.
  • "an abiding sense of doubt and insecurity in my mind" (Book 2, Ch 10 "The Epilogue") The ending leaves him unsettled, not triumphant.
  • "Suddenly, like a thing falling upon me from without, came—fear." (Book 1, Ch 5 "The Heat-Ray") Fear as an outside force; an honest admission.
  • "I wanted to be in at the death." (Book 1, Ch 10 "In the Storm") War fever: excitement before he understands.
  • "a professed and recognised writer on philosophical themes" (Book 2, Ch 7 "The Man on Putney Hill") His status, which the invasion humbles.
  • "There were no witnesses—all these things I might have concealed." (Book 2, Ch 7 "The Man on Putney Hill") The confessional voice.
  • "a sense of dethronement, a persuasion that I was no longer a master" (Book 2, Ch 6 "The Work of Fifteen Days") His view of humanity's place changes.
  • "an abiding sense of doubt and insecurity in my mind" (Book 2, Ch 10 "The Epilogue") He ends unsettled, not heroic.
  • "intellects vast and cool and unsympathetic" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Our first idea of them: mind without feeling.
  • "vital, intense, inhuman, crippled and monstrous" (Book 1, Ch 4 "The Cylinder Opens") Contradictory list: too strange for one word.
  • "There was something fungoid in the oily brown skin" (Book 1, Ch 4 "The Cylinder Opens") Disgust; the clever invaders seem like low life.
  • "They were heads—merely heads." (Book 2, Ch 2 "What We Saw from the Ruined House") Brain without body.
  • "a mere selfish intelligence, without any of the emotional substratum of the human being" (Book 2, Ch 2 "What We Saw from the Ruined House") The warning in their evolution.
  • "how repulsive our carnivorous habits would seem to an intelligent rabbit" (Book 2, Ch 2 "What We Saw from the Ruined House") Turns the judgement back on us.
  • "life is an incessant struggle for existence" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Darwin's phrase sets up the whole conflict.
  • "The chances against anything manlike on Mars are a million to one" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Scientific confidence, ironically wrong.
  • "the red weed rotted like a thing already dead" (Book 2, Ch 6 "The Work of Fifteen Days") Rehearses the Martians' fate.
  • "slain, after all man’s devices had failed, by the humblest things that God, in his wisdom, has put upon this earth" (Book 2, Ch 8 "Dead London") Science and religious language together.
  • "By the toll of a billion deaths man has bought his birthright of the earth" (Book 2, Ch 8 "Dead London") Natural selection as an inheritance.
  • "it is by no means a proven conclusion" (Book 2, Ch 10 "The Epilogue") The narrator's scientific caution (about the Martians' ignorance of decay).
  • "ready to welcome a missionary enterprise" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Mocks the imperial idea of "civilising" others.
  • "Are we such apostles of mercy as to complain if the Martians warred in the same spirit?" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") The rhetorical question that turns judgement on the reader.
  • "So some respectable dodo in the Mauritius might have lorded it in his nest" (Book 1, Ch 7 "How I Reached Home") The narrator as a doomed species.
  • "It’s bows and arrows against the lightning, anyhow" (Book 1, Ch 12 "What I Saw of the Destruction of Weybridge and Shepperton") The technology gap of colonial wars, reversed.
  • "crippled the greatest power in the world" (Book 2, Ch 7 "The Man on Putney Hill") Britain's power humbled.
  • "the fear and empire of man had passed away" (Book 2, Ch 6 "The Work of Fifteen Days") Human "empire" itself overthrown.
  • "the dovetailing of the commonplace habits of our social order" (Book 1, Ch 8 "Friday Night") Routine carries on as the threat grows.
  • "What good is religion if it collapses under calamity?" (Book 1, Ch 13 "How I Fell in with the Curate") Faith tested by disaster.
  • "that swift liquefaction of the social body" (Book 1, Ch 16 "The Exodus from London") Society melts.
  • "a boiling stream of people, a torrent of human beings rushing northward" (Book 1, Ch 16 "The Exodus from London") The crowd as a natural force.
  • "As they grew hungry the rights of property ceased to be regarded." (Book 1, Ch 17 "The Thunder Child") Law and property give way.
  • "the gulf between his dreams and his powers" (Book 2, Ch 7 "The Man on Putney Hill") The artilleryman's plan exposed.
  • "minds that are to our minds as ours are to those of the beasts that perish" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Humans placed lower on an evolutionary scale.
  • "failed to interpret the fluctuating appearances of the markings they mapped so well" (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Links to Schiaparelli and the canals debate.
  • "It seemed so safe and tranquil." (Book 1, Ch 1 "The Eve of the War") Complacency just before the landing.
  • "this war has taught us pity—pity for those witless souls that suffer our dominion" (Book 2, Ch 7 "The Man on Putney Hill") Pity for animals under human rule; some readers extend it to colonised peoples.
  • "it has robbed us of that serene confidence in the future which is the most fruitful source of decadence" (Book 2, Ch 10 "The Epilogue") Complacency named as the real danger.
  • "To them, and not to us, perhaps, is the future ordained." (Book 2, Ch 10 "The Epilogue") Humanity may not be evolution's winner.
