Mission 1

Qur'an and Hadith on men and women

  • Same worth: both are God's servants and trustees (khalifa); in the Qur'an, Adam and his wife both disobey and both repent: "Our Lord! We have wronged ourselves." (Qur'an 7:23, Pickthall). Eve is not blamed alone.
  • Hadith: women are the "twin halves" of men (paraphrase; Sunan Abu Dawud, no. 236); the best of you are those best to their families (paraphrase; Jami' al-Tirmidhi, no. 3895); "your mother" is named three times before "your father" as most deserving of kindness (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5971).
  • Different roles (traditional view): men must maintain (nafaqa) wife and family; so in law, shares and duties differ. "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them" (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall).
  • Contested texts: a hadith calls women "deficient in intellect and religion" (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 304). Traditional scholars link it to rules on witness and exemption from prayer during menstruation; many modern Muslims read it as context-bound or question how it is used.
  • So most Muslims hold equal worth before God; they disagree on how far legal differences follow (feminist readings).
Mission 1

Marriage and divorce in law (Surah 4)

  • Nikah is a contract: offer and acceptance, witnesses (required by most Sunni schools), and the bride's consent (a hadith requires it: Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5136). Most scholars today hold that a forced marriage is invalid; some classical schools let a father marry off a daughter who had never married.
  • Wali (guardian): most schools require the bride's guardian; the Hanafi school allows an adult woman to contract her own marriage.
  • Mahr: "give unto the women (whom ye marry) free gift of their marriage portions" (Qur'an 4:4, Pickthall). It belongs to the wife.
  • Polygyny: up to four wives, on condition of justice (4:3); yet "Ye will not be able to deal equally between (your) wives" (Qur'an 4:129, Pickthall). Many modern scholars and states treat monogamy as the norm; some states ban or restrict polygyny.
  • Divorce: permitted but discouraged. The husband's talaq with a waiting period ('idda, 2:228), revocable twice (2:229); the wife's khul' (she returns the mahr); or a judge's dissolution for harm. First, arbiters from both families should try to reconcile (4:35).

Mission 1

Religion, culture and change

  • Empire: British India codified Muslim family law ("Anglo-Muhammadan law"), fixing one reading of the schools.
  • Immigration: from the 1950s men came to work; families followed. Second- and third-generation British Muslims often sort "what is Islam" from "what is my grandparents' culture".
  • Secular rights: votes for women (1918/1928), the Equality Act 2010, and forced marriage made a crime (2014) shape the setting in which British Muslims live.
  • Changing roles: many Muslim women now study and work; many couples share earning and childcare; scholars debate whether the husband's duty of maintenance changes when both earn.
  • Responses differ: some see this as a return to the rights Islam first gave; others fear secular norms will erode the family.
Mission 1

Celibacy, marriage and sexuality

  • Celibacy: sex belongs only within marriage; chastity before marriage is required. But lifelong celibacy is not a religious ideal: "whoever turns away from my sunnah is not of me" (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 5063, on men who vowed never to marry).
  • Same-sex relationships: the traditional view, held by most scholars, forbids same-sex sexual acts, citing the people of Lot (7:80–81; 26:165–166). Many scholars distinguish feelings (not sinful in themselves) from acts.
  • A minority of Muslims, e.g. Scott Siraj al-Haqq Kugle, Homosexuality in Islam (2010), read the Lot story as about rape and inhospitality. LGBT Muslim groups exist in Britain (e.g. Imaan).
  • Transgender matters: classical law already discussed intersex people (khuntha). Iran permits gender-reassignment surgery under rulings by Ayatollah Khomeini (first in the 1960s; best known from the mid-1980s); many Sunni scholars permit surgery only to resolve intersex conditions.
  • UK law allows same-sex marriage (2013 Act) but does not require mosques to perform it.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are women and men equal in Islam?

  • Claim: Islam teaches full equality between men and women.
  • For: equal creation and reward (4:1, 33:35, 16:97); the Qur'an gave women property, inheritance, mahr and consent in the 7th century; early women's leadership (Khadijah, 'A'isha).
  • For: many differences in practice come from culture, not revelation.
  • Against: legal texts give different shares and rights (4:11; men "a degree above", 2:228; talaq mostly in the husband's hand).
  • Against: "equal but different" may still mean unequal in power: critics within and outside Islam argue this.
  • Judgement criteria: Does equality mean identical rights, or equal worth with different roles? Are legal rules fixed or tied to 7th-century conditions? Which counts more: text, history or today's practice?

Mission 2

Equal before God

  • Muslims believe men and women come from one soul: "created you from a single person, created, of like nature, His mate" (Qur'an 4:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Same duties of faith, same reward: "If any do deeds of righteousness,- be they male or female - and have faith, they will enter Heaven" (Qur'an 4:124, Yusuf Ali).
  • Qur'an 33:35 names believing men and women, side by side, ten times over, with one promise: forgiveness and great reward.
  • So most Muslims agree on spiritual equality. The debate is about roles in family and society.
Mission 2

Women in early Islam

  • Khadijah: a trader who employed Muhammad, proposed marriage, and was the first to accept his message.
  • Aisha: a leading teacher of the first generation; she narrated over two thousand hadith, and senior Companions brought her questions of law.
  • Umm Salamah: hadith collections report she asked why the Qur'an spoke of men and not women; 33:35 followed (al-Nasa'i, Ahmad; Tirmidhi reports the question from Umm 'Umara).
  • Nusaybah bint Ka'b defended the Prophet at Uhud (625).
  • Women prayed in the Prophet's mosque. Hadith: do not stop the women servants of God from the mosques of God (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).

Mission 2

Surah 4: family law

  • Surah 4 is called al-Nisa', "Women". Much of it is family law.
  • Marriage: a contract; the bride's consent; the mahr (dower) is hers (4:4). Up to four wives, but "if ye fear that ye shall not be able to deal justly (with them), then only one" (Qur'an 4:3, Yusuf Ali).
  • Kindness: "live with them on a footing of kindness and equity" (Qur'an 4:19, Yusuf Ali).
  • Inheritance: women inherit in their own right; a son's share is twice a daughter's (4:11). Classical reason: the man must support the family; her share is hers alone.
  • Conflict and divorce: arbiters from both families (4:35); a settlement if the husband is cruel (4:128); divorce rules in 2:228–232.
Mission 2Read the source

Qur'an 4:34

> "Men are the protectors and maintainers of women, because Allah has given the one more (strength) than the other, and because they support them from their means." (Qur'an 4:34, Yusuf Ali)

  • The verse goes on: if a husband fears nushuz (Yusuf Ali: "disloyalty and ill-conduct") from his wife: admonish her; then separate beds; then a word Yusuf Ali renders "beat them (lightly)" (paraphrase).
  • Pickthall renders the opening "Men are in charge of women".
  • Key Arabic words: qawwamun (maintainers; in charge), faddala (has given more, or preferred), daraba (to strike; it has many other senses).

Q2 Why does the translation matter here?

Show answer

Mission 2

Classical readings

  • Most classical scholars read qawwamun as the husband's authority and duty to provide and protect, within the household.
  • They read the three steps as a ladder of last resort, and limited the third:
  • Not on the face, not to injure. Ibn Abbas is reported as saying: with a tooth-stick or the like (al-Tabari's commentary).
  • Aisha reported that the Prophet never struck a woman or a servant (Muslim).
  • A hadith: the best of you are those best to their wives (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • Many jurists (e.g. al-Shafi'i) said that not striking at all is better.
  • So even traditional readings restricted the verse. The modern question is whether it gives permission at all.

Mission 2

Amina Wadud

  • Amina Wadud (b. 1952), American Muslim scholar: Qur'an and Woman: Rereading the Sacred Text from a Woman's Perspective (1992); Inside the Gender Jihad (2006).
  • Method: read each verse in its first context, alongside the whole Qur'an, guided by its universal principles (justice, the oneness of God).
  • Qawwamun is not built-in superiority: it depends on the husband actually providing ("because they support them").
  • Nushuz is also used of husbands (4:128), so it means a breakdown of marital harmony, not a wife's disobedience alone.
  • The striking clause restricted a violent practice in 7th-century Arabia; the Qur'an's direction is towards ending it.
  • In 2006 she wrote that she now says "no" to applying the clause literally at all (paraphrase).
  • In 2005 she led a mixed congregation in Friday prayer in New York: most scholars objected; others supported her.
Mission 2

Bint al-Shati'

  • Aisha Abd al-Rahman (1913–1998), pen-name Bint al-Shati' ("daughter of the shore"), Egyptian professor of Arabic literature and Qur'anic studies.
  • One of the first modern women to publish a commentary (tafsir) on the Qur'an: al-Tafsir al-Bayani (The Literary Explanation of the Qur'an, 1962–69).
  • Method: collect every use of a word in the Qur'an; let the Qur'an explain itself; reject later legends and forced "scientific" readings.
  • She wrote lives of the women of the Prophet's household, bringing them into the story of Islam.
  • She held that the Qur'an gave women rights to education, property and consent, but criticised Western feminism, keeping distinct family roles.
  • Pioneering, because a woman now interpreted the text itself. Wadud's method shares this habit of reading the Qur'an through itself.
Mission 2Diversity

Four ways of reading 4:34

  • Traditionalist: husband's leadership is a duty of care; the striking clause stands but is hedged by the Prophet's example.
  • Reformist (feminist exegesis): Wadud; Asma Barlas, Believing Women in Islam (2002): the Qur'an is not patriarchal; patriarchal readings came from its interpreters.
  • Re-translation: Laleh Bakhtiar, The Sublime Quran (2007), renders daraba "go away from them".
  • Critical: some Muslim scholars (e.g. Ayesha Chaudhry, Domestic Violence and the Islamic Tradition, 2013) argue that the pre-modern tradition did permit striking, and modern readers must face that honestly (paraphrase).

Mission 2Evaluate

"Feminist readings of the Qur'an are faithful to the text."

  • For: they read the Qur'an through itself, like much classical tafsir. They follow its own principles of justice (4:135) and mercy between spouses (30:21). They recover women of early Islam.
  • For: Bint al-Shati' read the Qur'an through itself in the same way.
  • Against: classical scholars across all schools read the verse otherwise for a thousand years. Re-defining daraba is linguistically disputed. Saying "no" to a verse (Wadud, 2006) goes beyond interpretation.
  • Another view: all reading is shaped by its age; the classical readings were themselves interpretations by men of their time.
  • Judge it by: Who has authority to interpret? Does the reading fit the Arabic? Does it fit the whole Qur'an? Does it serve justice in practice?
  • AO2 tip: build a line of argument; weigh the reasons, then reach your own reasoned judgement.

Mission 3

Words for the veil

  • Hijab: literally "screen" or "curtain". Today usually a headscarf covering hair and neck.
  • Khimar: head-covering (24:31). Jilbab: outer garment or cloak (33:59).
  • Niqab: face-veil, eyes visible. Burqa: full covering with a mesh over the eyes. Chador: Iranian full-length cloak.
  • Awra: the parts of the body to be covered in public.
  • Hanafi and Maliki scholars, the Ja'fari Shi'a school and many others: a woman covers all but face and hands. Many Hanbali and some Shafi'i scholars require the face to be covered too.
  • A small number of modern scholars argue the headscarf itself is not obligatory.
Mission 3Read the source

Modesty for men and women

> "Say to the believing men that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty: that will make for greater purity for them" (Qur'an 24:30, Yusuf Ali)

> "And say to the believing women that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty; that they should not display their beauty and ornaments except what (must ordinarily) appear thereof; that they should draw their veils over their bosoms" (Qur'an 24:31, Yusuf Ali)

  • 33:59 asks the Prophet's wives, daughters and the believing women to draw their outer garments around them, "that they should be known (as such) and not molested" (Qur'an 33:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • A hadith: modesty (haya') is a branch of faith (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).

Mission 3

Empire and Qasim Amin

  • Context: Britain occupied Egypt from 1882. Its consul-general Lord Cromer (1841–1917), in Modern Egypt (1908), called the veil and seclusion the main obstacle to Egypt's progress. In England he led opposition to votes for women.
  • Qasim Amin (1863–1908), Egyptian lawyer and judge, studied in France: Tahrir al-Mar'a (The Liberation of Woman, 1899); al-Mar'a al-Jadida (The New Woman, 1900).
  • His argument: face-veiling and seclusion are customs, not required by Shari'a. They keep women uneducated, so the nation stays backward. The veil is a barrier to achievement.
  • He called for girls' education and limits on easy divorce and polygamy. He did not call for abandoning modest dress.
  • Your spec: "the veil seen as a barrier to achievement".
Mission 3Diversity

Other Muslim responses

  • Talat Harb (1867–1941), The Education of Woman and the Veil (1899): the veil is Islamic, and protects the family and society.
  • Malak Hifni Nasif (1886–1918): women's education, yes; but unveiling should not be forced on women by men; reform men's conduct first.
  • Huda Sha'rawi (1879–1947): removed her face-veil in public at Cairo station in 1923; founded the Egyptian Feminist Union (1923).
  • So early Muslim feminism was plural: some unveiled; some kept the veil and fought for education and rights.
  • Your spec: "other Muslim views on this issue".
Mission 3

Leila Ahmed

  • Leila Ahmed (b. 1940), Egyptian-American, Harvard: Women and Gender in Islam: Historical Roots of a Modern Debate (1992).
  • Colonial feminism: Cromer used women's rights to justify empire. Amin's book, she argues, repeated that contempt for Egyptian culture (paraphrase).
  • So the veil became a symbol in a fight between West and East, not only a piece of cloth.
  • Two voices in early Islam: an ethical, egalitarian vision in the Qur'an, and an androcentric legal tradition built later. Muslim women can reclaim the first (paraphrase).
  • In A Quiet Revolution (2011) she finds that many American Muslim women chose hijab as a sign of faith, identity and activism for justice.

Mission 3

States and the veil

  • Unveiling by law: Iran under Reza Shah banned the veil in public (1936). Turkey's secular republic discouraged it and later banned headscarves in universities and state offices (lifted in stages, 2010–2013).
  • Rights by secular law: Tunisia's Code of Personal Status (1956) banned polygamy and gave women judicial divorce.
  • Veiling by law: after the 1979 revolution, Iran made hijab compulsory (law of 1983). Protests followed the death of Mahsa Amini in the custody of the morality police (2022).
  • Bans in Europe: France banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (2004) and face-coverings in public (2010).
  • Britain: no ban. Research (e.g. Afshar, the later screen) finds many younger British Muslim women choose hijab for themselves.
  • Your spec: "the rights given to women by secular governments".
Mission 3

Haleh Afshar

  • Haleh Afshar (1944–2022), Iranian-born British professor of politics and women's studies, University of York; Baroness Afshar from 2007.
  • Islam and Feminisms: An Iranian Case-Study (1998): women in the Islamic Republic used Islamic arguments to win back rights in education, work and family law.
  • Islamic feminism: women can claim equality from within their faith, not only from secular feminism.
  • Her research with young British Muslim women who cover: many chose it themselves, sometimes against family wishes, as faith and as identity (paraphrase).
  • She opposed compulsion both ways: forced veiling in Iran and forced unveiling by bans.

Mission 3Diversity

Why Muslim women cover, or do not

  • Obedience: a duty from God (24:31, 33:59), held by most scholars.
  • Identity: being visibly Muslim, especially as a minority.
  • Freedom: some say covering means being judged by mind, not body.
  • Protest: against Western consumer culture, or against a regime (Iran before 1979).
  • Not covering: some Muslim women hold that modesty is in conduct, not in a scarf; others face pressure or danger if they cover.
  • Culture or religion? Burqa, chador and niqab differ by region: culture shapes how Islam is lived.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The veil is a symbol of oppression."

  • For: some Muslim reformers saw veiling and seclusion as barriers to education and public life. Where the law forces it (Iran), it is imposed by the state. Family pressure is real for some women.
  • For: Amin (1899) and Sha'rawi (1923) made exactly this case.
  • Against: many women choose it freely (Afshar). Bans also take away choice.
  • Against: Ahmed (2011) found the same; calling it oppression can repeat colonial feminism (Cromer).
  • Another view: the cloth has no single meaning: oppression or resistance depends on choice and context.
  • Judge it by: Who chose it? Who is speaking for the women? Is the meaning religious, cultural or political? Does a ban or a law help women?

Mission 4

Knowledge as a duty

  • Hadith: seeking knowledge ('ilm) is an obligation on every Muslim (paraphrase; Sunan Ibn Majah, no. 224).
  • "O my Lord! advance me in knowledge." (Qur'an 20:114, Yusuf Ali)
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Revival of the Religious Sciences, Book of Knowledge: medicine and arithmetic are fard kifaya, a communal duty: someone in every community must master them, or all are at fault.
  • Khalifa (2:30): humans are trustees of the earth; understanding creation helps them care for it.
  • So Muslims could see science as a duty required by the Qur'an, not only an interest.
Mission 4

Worship needed science

  • Qibla: facing Makkah from Spain or India needs spherical trigonometry.
  • Prayer times: shift with the sun and season; mosque timekeepers (muwaqqit) built tables and instruments.
  • Calendar: a lunar calendar needs prediction of the new crescent (Ramadan, Hajj).
  • Inheritance: the Qur'an's shares (4:11–12) need careful arithmetic; al-Khwarizmi's algebra book includes inheritance problems.
  • So religious practice gave scholars real problems to solve.

Mission 4

The translation movement

  • Under the Abbasid caliphs (from 750), Baghdad became a centre of learning.
  • Greek, Persian, Syriac and Sanskrit works on medicine, mathematics, astronomy and philosophy were translated into Arabic (8th–10th centuries).
  • The House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Hikma), associated with caliph al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833), linked libraries and translators.
  • Translators included Christians such as Hunayn ibn Ishaq (809–873), translator of Galen; Jews and Sabians also took part.
  • Paper, learned from China, spread from Samarkand to Baghdad in the late 8th century and made books cheaper.
  • Scholars checked, criticised and extended the texts: Arabic became the international language of science.
Mission 4

Mathematics

  • Al-Khwarizmi (c. 780–c. 850), The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing (c. 820): systematic solving of equations. "Al-jabr" in the title gave us algebra; his name gave algorithm.
  • He also explained the Indian decimal numerals with zero; Europe later learned them in Arabic form (hence "Arabic numerals").
  • Trigonometry: sine and tangent tables developed for astronomy and the qibla (e.g. al-Battani, d. 929).
  • 'Umar al-Khayyam (1048–1131): geometric solutions of cubic equations.
Mission 4

Medicine

  • Hadith: God has not sent down a disease without sending down its cure (paraphrase; Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Medicine, no. 5678). Seeking cures is part of trusting God.
  • Hospitals (bimaristan) in Baghdad, Damascus and Cairo treated patients free, with wards and teaching.
  • Al-Razi (c. 865–925): clinical case notes; first clear account distinguishing smallpox from measles.
  • Ibn Sina (980–1037), The Canon of Medicine (c. 1025): a systematic medical encyclopaedia, used in European universities for about five centuries.
  • Al-Zahrawi (c. 936–1013): surgery, with drawings of instruments.
  • Ibn al-Nafis (1213–1288): described the passage of blood through the lungs (pulmonary circulation).
Mission 4

Astronomy and optics

  • Observatories: Maragha (founded 1259) and Samarkand (1420s, under Ulugh Beg).
  • Nasir al-Din al-Tusi (1201–1274) and Ibn al-Shatir (1304–1375), timekeeper of the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus, built models of planetary motion that appear in mathematically similar form in Copernicus. How they reached him is still debated.
  • Al-Biruni (973–c. 1050): measured the radius of the Earth.
  • Ibn al-Haytham (c. 965–c. 1040), Book of Optics: sight happens when light enters the eye; he tested claims with controlled experiments, so he is often called a pioneer of experimental method.

Mission 4

Did Muslim science decline after al-Ghazali?

  • The old "decline" story (e.g. Ernest Renan, 1883): orthodoxy, led by al-Ghazali, killed science after the 11th century.
  • Historians now: major work came after al-Ghazali: Maragha (1259), Ibn al-Nafis, Ibn al-Shatir, Samarkand (1420s). George Saliba, Islamic Science and the Making of the European Renaissance (2007).
  • Later slowdowns had many causes: the Mongol sack of Baghdad (1258), shifts in trade and wealth, late adoption of printing.

Mission 4Evaluate

Was the medieval achievement driven by Islam?

  • Claim: the medieval Muslim contribution to science shows that Islam itself encourages science.
  • For: the Qur'an calls nature "signs" and commands reflection; worship needed astronomy and mathematics; hadith on knowledge and cures.
  • For: many scientists were also religious scholars (Ibn al-Shatir a mosque timekeeper; Ibn al-Nafis wrote on law and hadith).
  • Against: much built on Greek, Indian and Persian learning; Christians, Jews and Sabians took part; courts, not mosques, paid for much of it.
  • Against: some scholars distrusted philosophy; science later slowed in the same societies.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "Islam" its texts, its scholars or its civilisation? Does motivation matter, or only results? What else explains the later slowdown?
  • Skill tip: in an AO1 answer on science as a Qur'anic duty, use one Qur'anic verse and one historical example.

Mission 5: Does the Qur'an predict the Big Bang, and must a Muslim reject evolution?

Mission 5

Modern science meets Islam

  • Modern European science reached Muslim lands in the 19th century, often with colonial power (Napoleon in Egypt, 1798; British India).
  • It stressed evidence and reason, natural laws, deep time and evolution.
  • Muslim reformers welcomed it as their own heritage returning:
  • Sayyid Ahmad Khan (1817–1898), founder of the Aligarh college (1875): the Word of God (Qur'an) and the Work of God (nature) cannot contradict.
  • Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838/9–1897): answering Ernest Renan (1883), he argued Muslims must master modern science.
  • Others feared science came bundled with materialism and Western dominance.
  • Science also became a stimulus to ethical thinking: new powers (e.g. genetics) raised new questions.
Mission 5Read the source

Creation in the Qur'an

> "Have not those who disbelieve known that the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them, and we made every living thing of water?" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall)

> "Then turned He to the heaven when it was smoke" (Qur'an 41:11, Pickthall)

> "We have built the heaven with might, and We it is Who make the vast extent (thereof)." (Qur'an 51:47, Pickthall)

  • Creation took "six Days" (7:54), but "a Day with Allah is as a thousand years of what ye reckon" (Qur'an 22:47, Pickthall): many Muslims read the days as ages.

Q1 Which verse suggests the heavens and earth began as one thing?

Show answer

21:30: "of one piece, then We parted them".

Q2 Why do some modern translators render 51:47 as "We are expanding it"?

Show answer

The Arabic musi'un can mean "making wide": a reading that fits an expanding universe.

Mission 5

Big Bang, Steady State, oscillating universe

  • Big Bang: the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago from a hot, dense state and is expanding.
  • Many Muslims welcome it: a beginning fits God as Originator (2:117); 21:30 and 41:11 seem to describe a single mass and "smoke".
  • Steady State (Hoyle, Bondi and Gold, 1948): an eternal universe with no beginning, matter created continually. Most Muslims see a universe without a beginning as clashing with creation, though some link continual creation to God's constant sustaining (50:15 "a new creation").
  • Oscillating universe: endless cycles of expansion and collapse. Some cite "As We began the first creation, We shall repeat it" (21:104); but endless cycles with no first beginning would clash with creation.
  • Some Muslims warn against tying the Qur'an to any theory, since theories change.

Mission 5Diversity

Responses to Darwin

  • Muslims have debated Darwin since the 1870s. Three broad positions today:
  • Rejection (creationism): species, and above all humans, were created directly; evolution is seen as materialist. Best-known: "Harun Yahya" (pen name of Adnan Oktar), Atlas of Creation (2006).
  • Evolution, except humans: plants and animals evolved, but Adam was created directly from clay (15:28; 3:59). Sometimes called "Adamic exceptionalism".
  • Full acceptance: evolution is God's way of creating, as rain is His way of growing crops; "He created you by (divers) stages" (Qur'an 71:14, Pickthall). E.g. the astrophysicist Nidhal Guessoum, Islam's Quantum Question (2011).
  • Common ground: all three agree that God is Creator and that humans have a purpose as khalifa (2:30). They differ on how God created.

The making of a human

> "Verily We created man from a product of wet earth; Then placed him as a drop (of seed) in a safe lodging; Then fashioned We the drop a clot, then fashioned We the clot a little lump, then fashioned We the little lump bones, then clothed the bones with flesh, and then produced it as another creation. So blessed be Allah, the Best of creators!" (Qur'an 23:12–14, Pickthall)

  • The Arabic 'alaqa (here "clot") can also mean "something that clings" or "leech-like".

Q1 How many stages of development does the passage list after the "drop"?

Show answer

Four: clot, little lump, bones, bones clothed with flesh; then "another creation".

Q2 Why do some Muslims see this passage as a scientific sign, and others as a call to wonder?

Show answer

Its staged account resembles embryology; others say its purpose is to make people praise "the Best of creators".

Mission 5

Maurice Bucaille

  • Maurice Bucaille (1920–1998), French physician, The Bible, the Qur'an and Science (1976).
  • His claims (paraphrase): the Qur'an contains no statement that contradicts established modern science, while the Bible does (e.g. Genesis' chronology).
  • It contains statements no 7th-century person could have known: embryo stages (23:12–14), life from water (21:30), the heavens as smoke (41:11), widening of the heaven (51:47), a barrier between two seas (55:19–20).
  • So the Qur'an must be from God.
  • His book was hugely popular; it helped inspire a genre of "scientific miracles of the Qur'an" (i'jaz 'ilmi).
Mission 5

Critics of Bucaille; Nasr's view

  • Reading in: modern meanings are read back into Arabic words ('alaqa, musi'un) that earlier commentators understood differently.
  • Changing science: if faith is tied to today's theory, what happens when the theory changes?
  • Selective: verses that seem not to fit are passed over.
  • Purpose: the Qur'an calls itself guidance (2:2), not a science textbook (e.g. Ziauddin Sardar's critique of "Bucaillism").
Mission 5Diversity

Science in Islam today

  • Science is neutral; adopt it: follow evidence; the Qur'an gives values, science gives facts (in the line of Sayyid Ahmad Khan; many Muslim scientists).
  • Scientific miracles: modern discoveries confirm the Qur'an (Bucaille; popular preaching).
  • Islamise knowledge: rebuild the sciences on Islamic principles (Isma'il al-Faruqi, Islamization of Knowledge, 1982).
  • Reject parts: accept technology but reject theories seen as contradicting scripture (e.g. human evolution).
Mission 5

Genetic engineering

  • Principles: life is a trust (amana) from God; humans are khalifa; public benefit (maslaha); a hadith forbids causing harm or returning harm (paraphrase; Sunan Ibn Majah, no. 2340); "whoso saveth the life of one, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall).
  • Warning verse: Satan vows that people "will change Allah's creation" (Qur'an 4:119, Pickthall): used against needless alteration of human nature.
  • Widely permitted: gene therapy on body cells to treat disease (it is seeking a cure).
  • Widely forbidden: human reproductive cloning and germline changes for enhancement ("designer babies"): they confuse lineage (nasab) and alter creation.
  • Debated: GM crops (benefit vs unknown harm); embryo research before ensoulment (many scholars allow it with limits; others oppose).

Mission 5Evaluate

Is Islam compatible with modern science?

  • Claim: Islam and modern science are fully compatible.
  • For: the Qur'an commands study of creation (3:190); a beginning fits the Big Bang; many Muslim scientists see no conflict; a rich history (see Mission 4).
  • For: God works through causes; evolution or expansion can be God's method.
  • Against: a literal Adam created from clay clashes with common descent; Steady State-type eternity clashes with creation.
  • Against: modern science's naturalism (only natural causes count) can clash with belief in God's constant action and miracles.
  • Judgement criteria: Compatible with the Qur'an, or with a reading of it? With scientific results, or with naturalism as a worldview? Does reading verses as predictions help or harm faith?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Qur'an teaches spiritual equality: one soul (4:1), the same reward (33:35, 16:97); Adam and his wife sin and repent together.
  • Traditional law gives different duties: men maintain; inheritance shares differ (4:11); divorce routes differ (talaq, khul', judicial). Consent and mahr belong to the woman.
  • Marriage is the norm; most scholars forbid same-sex acts while some Muslims reread the texts; views on transgender matters vary (Iran vs many Sunni scholars).

Mission 2

  • Most Muslims agree men and women are spiritually equal (4:1, 4:124, 33:35); debate is about family roles.
  • Classical scholars read 4:34 as household authority with striking as a limited last step; feminist readers (Wadud) read the role as conditional and the striking clause as a restriction on its way to ending.
  • Bint al-Shati' pioneered women's tafsir and a method of reading the Qur'an through itself.

Mission 3

  • Qur'an 24:30–31 and 33:59 ask modesty of men and women; most scholars require women to cover all but face and hands.
  • Amin (1899) called the veil a barrier to achievement; Ahmed shows how empire made the veil a symbol ("colonial feminism").
  • Afshar: Islamic feminism and women's own choice, against compulsion in either direction.

Mission 4

  • The Qur'an calls creation "signs" and urges reflection (3:190, 88:17); hadith make seeking knowledge a duty; al-Ghazali made medicine and arithmetic communal duties.
  • Medieval Muslim scholars translated and extended Greek, Persian and Indian learning: algebra (al-Khwarizmi), medicine (al-Razi, Ibn Sina, Ibn al-Nafis), astronomy and optics (al-Tusi, Ibn al-Shatir, Ibn al-Haytham).
  • Historians now reject the idea that science stopped after al-Ghazali.

Mission 5

  • Modern science arrived with colonial power; reformers (Sayyid Ahmad Khan, al-Afghani) welcomed it as compatible with Islam.
  • Big Bang: many welcome a beginning (21:30, 41:11, 51:47); most Muslims see Steady State and endless cycles as clashing with creation.
  • Darwin: rejection, evolution with a human exception, full acceptance.
  • Bucaille: the Qur'an anticipated science; critics: reading in, changing theories.
  • Genetic engineering: therapy to cure is widely permitted; cloning and enhancement widely forbidden (4:119, maslaha, nasab).
