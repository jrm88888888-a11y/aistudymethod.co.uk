Mission 1

Mission 1: Sunni Muslims list six core beliefs and Shi'a Muslims five roots. Do they believe different things?

Make a guess: Sunni Muslims list six core beliefs and Shi'a Muslims five roots. Do they believe different things?

Mission 1

Iman: faith in what?

  • Iman means faith: the beliefs that make a Muslim a believer.
  • Islam (submission) is lived out in practice, such as the Five Pillars; iman is what a Muslim holds in the heart and mind.
  • Sunni Muslims (around 87–90% of Muslims) summarise iman as the six articles of faith.
  • Shi'a Muslims summarise the foundations as the five Usul ad-Din: "roots" or "principles of religion".
  • Both lists come from the Qur'an and the teaching of the Prophet Muhammad.
Mission 1Read the source

Believe in Allah, His angels, His books…

"Any who denieth Allah, His angels, His Books, His Messengers, and the Day of Judgment, hath gone far, far astray." (Qur'an 4:136, Yusuf Ali)

  • A fuller report in Sahih Muslim's Book of Faith, the Hadith of Jibril (1:1), adds faith in the divine decree, good and bad (paraphrase).

Q1 Which five beliefs does Qur'an 4:136 name?

Show answer

Allah, angels, books, messengers, the Day of Judgement.

Q2 Which sixth article comes from the Hadith of Jibril?

Show answer

Al-Qadr: God's decree, or predestination.

Mission 1

The six articles of faith (Sunni)

1. Tawhid: Allah is one; nothing is like Him.

2. Mala'ikah (CIE: Malaʾikah): angels, Allah's messengers and servants.

3. Kutub: the holy books: Scrolls, Torah, Psalms, Gospel and, finally, the Qur'an.

4. Risalah: prophethood; Allah sends messengers, ending with Muhammad.

5. Akhirah: the Day of Judgement and life after death.

6. Al-Qadr (CIE: Qadar): predestination; Allah knows and decrees all things.

Mission 1

Where the articles came from, and why

  • History: five articles are named together in the Qur'an (4:136, 2:177). The sixth, al-Qadr, is in the Hadith of Jibril and in verses such as "all things have We created in proportion and measure" (54:49).
  • Early Sunni scholars set them out in short creeds (aqidah, "what is firmly held").
  • Purpose: to define what a believer must hold, so that Muslims everywhere share one faith.
  • They also shape life: believing in Akhirah and angels makes a Muslim accountable; believing in Qadr brings trust in Allah.

Mission 1

The five roots of Usul ad-Din (Shi'a)

1. Tawhid (OCR: Al-Tawhid): the oneness of Allah.

2. 'Adl (OCR: Al-Adl; also Adalat): divine justice.

3. Nubuwwah (OCR: Al-Nubuwwah; CIE: Risalah): prophethood.

4. Imamah (OCR: Al-Imamah): the Imams, the Prophet's chosen successors.

5. Mi'ad (OCR: Al-Ma'ad): the Day of Judgement and the Resurrection.

  • They are roots: the rest of religious practice (the "branches", furu' ad-Din, such as prayer and fasting) grows from them.
  • Many Shi'a scholars teach that each believer should accept the roots through their own understanding, not just by copying others.
Mission 1

What makes the roots Shi'a: justice and Imamah

  • 'Adl / Adalat: Allah is perfectly just and never does wrong; good and evil are real, and reason can recognise them. "Whoever works righteousness benefits his own soul; whoever works evil, it is against his own soul: nor is thy Lord ever unjust (in the least) to His Servants." (Qur'an 41:46, Yusuf Ali)
  • So humans are free and responsible, and Allah's judgement is fair.
  • Imamah: after the Prophet, Allah appointed Imams from his family (the Ahl al-Bayt), beginning with Ali, to guide the community without error.
  • Sunni view: leaders (caliphs) were chosen by the community; leadership is important, but not an article of faith.
  • More on the Imams.
Mission 1Diversity

Side by side

  • Shared: Tawhid; prophethood; the Day of Judgement and resurrection.
  • Shi'a also believe in angels, the holy books and Allah's decree: these sit inside the roots (e.g. books and angels as part of prophethood and revelation), not as separate roots.
  • Sunni also believe Allah is just (al-'Adl is one of His names), but justice is not a separate article.
  • Distinctive to Sunni lists: al-Qadr as a named article.
  • Distinctive to Shi'a roots: 'Adl and Imamah.
  • So the two lists are mostly shared beliefs, arranged differently; the real division is over Imamah (who leads after the Prophet) and how far stress falls on justice and human freedom.

Mission 1

Living by the articles and roots

  • Tawhid: only Allah is worshipped; the Shahadah begins with it.
  • Angels and Akhirah: every word and deed is recorded and will be judged, so Muslims try to act well even when unseen.
  • Books and prophets: the Qur'an is recited daily; the Prophet's Sunnah is a model for life.
  • Al-Qadr: trust in Allah; saying insha'Allah ("if Allah wills"); patience in hardship.
  • 'Adl: a duty to work for justice, since Allah is just.
  • Imamah: Twelvers follow a senior scholar's guidance and remember the Imams (e.g. Husayn at Ashura); Isma'ilis follow the Imam of the time.
  • Different views: some Muslims stress belief, others practice, as the heart of faith; Sufis stress the inner life of the heart.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The differences between Sunni and Shi'a beliefs matter more than the similarities."

  • For: Imamah is a root of faith for Shi'a Muslims, and it shapes leadership, law and devotion. Historically, the division over leadership has run deep. 'Adl leads to different ideas of human freedom.
  • Against: Tawhid, prophethood, the Qur'an and the Day of Judgement are shared. Both believe in angels, books and Allah's decree, just listed differently. Both pray, fast, give zakah and make Hajj (Sunni Muslims count these among the Five Pillars, Shi'a Muslims among the branches, furu' ad-Din), and both belong to the ummah (the worldwide Muslim community).
  • Another view: "more important" depends on the question: for daily worship the similarities dominate; for authority and leadership the differences do.
  • Judge it by: Which beliefs shape daily life most? Are the differences about what is believed or how it is listed? How do Muslims themselves describe the relationship?
Mission check

?A classmate says: "Shi'a Muslims don't believe in angels or holy books, because those are not in the five roots." Which reply is most accurate?

The six articles and five roots are two ways of summarising one faith. The deepest difference is the Imamah.

Mission 2

A house on five pillars

  • The Prophet taught that Islam is built on five: the Shahadah, prayer, zakah, Hajj and fasting in Ramadan (Sahih al-Bukhari, Book of Faith, paraphrase).
  • The pillars are ibadah (worship): acts of obedience that put iman (faith) into practice.
  • They are duties (fard) for every adult Muslim who is able, with exemptions (e.g. illness, poverty).
  • They bind the ummah together: the same words, prayers, fast and pilgrimage across the world.
Mission 2

The Five Pillars

1. Shahadah (CCEA: Shahada): declaring faith in one God and His messenger.

2. Salah (OCR: Salat): prayer five times a day.

3. Zakah (OCR, CCEA: Zakat): giving 2.5% of savings above a minimum to those in need.

4. Sawm: fasting from dawn to sunset in the month of Ramadan.

5. Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah once in a lifetime, if able (see Mission 6).

Mission 2

The Shahadah

  • Arabic: lā ʾilāha ʾillā-llāh, muḥammadur-rasūlu-llāh
  • Meaning: "There is no god but God (Allah). Muhammad is the messenger of God."
  • Part 1: Tawhid: rejecting every false god, including money, fame or the self.
  • Part 2: Risalah: accepting Muhammad as the final messenger and following his example.
  • Usually said with "I bear witness that…" (ashhadu): a personal testimony.
Mission 2Read the source

Allah bears witness

"There is no god but He: That is the witness of Allah, His angels, and those endued with knowledge, standing firm on justice. There is no god but He, the Exalted in Power, the Wise." (Qur'an 3:18, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Who bears witness that there is no god but Allah, in 3:18?

Show answer

Allah Himself, the angels and people of knowledge.

Q2 How does 3:18 connect with the Shahadah?

Show answer

A Muslim who says the Shahadah joins the same witness, and witnesses to it personally.

Mission 2

The Shahadah in a Muslim's life

  • Birth: the adhan, containing the Shahadah, is whispered into a newborn's right ear: the first words heard.
  • Becoming Muslim: saying it sincerely, with belief in the heart, usually before witnesses.
  • Every day: in the adhan and in every salah (the tashahhud); many repeat it in dhikr.
  • Death: Muslims hope it will be their last words; it is said to and by the dying.
  • Sincerity: words alone are not enough; the intention (niyyah) and belief must be real, and life should show it.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Shahadah is the most important pillar of Islam."

  • For: it is the foundation: without belief in Allah and His messenger, other acts have no meaning. Saying it sincerely makes a person Muslim. It contains Tawhid, the heart of Islam.
  • Against: a building needs all its pillars. Many Muslims teach that salah is the first deed judged on the Day of Judgement. Words without action may be empty: faith is shown in practice.
  • Shi'a view: belief belongs to the roots; among the branches, salah is often called the most important act.
  • Judge it by: Can belief and practice be separated? What makes someone a Muslim, and what keeps them one? Does "important" mean first, or most demanding?
Mission check

Mission 3

Makkah around 570 CE

  • Makkah: a trading town in a dry valley in western Arabia, ruled by the Quraysh tribe.
  • At its centre stood the Ka'bah. Muslims believe Ibrahim and Isma'il built it for the worship of the one God.
  • By the 6th century it was filled with idols of many tribal gods, and pilgrims to them brought trade.
  • Muslims call this time Jahiliyyah: the "age of ignorance".
  • Tribe and family decided who was protected; the poor, orphans and slaves had few rights.
Mission 3

An orphan called al-Amin

  • Muhammad was born in Makkah around 570 CE, into the Hashim clan of the Quraysh.
  • His father died before he was born; his mother died when he was about six.
  • He was raised by his grandfather, then by his uncle Abu Talib.
  • He worked as a shepherd, then as a trader on caravan journeys to Syria.
  • People called him al-Amin: "the trustworthy". Makkans left valuables with him for safe keeping.
  • He worked for Khadijah, a respected merchant; she proposed marriage, and they married when he was 25.
  • When the clans argued over who should replace the sacred Black Stone in the rebuilt Ka'bah, he set it on a cloak so every clan leader could lift it together.

Mission 3Read the source

"Read!": the first revelation

  • Around 610 CE, aged 40, Muhammad often went to pray and reflect in the cave of Hira' on a mountain near Makkah.
  • One night in Ramadan, Muslims believe, the angel Jibril (Gabriel) came to him and commanded him to read or recite.

> "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot. Read: And thy Lord is the Most Bounteous, Who teacheth by the pen, Teacheth man that which he knew not." (Surah 96:1–5, Pickthall)

Q1 Who does the passage say is the source of human knowledge?

Show answer

God, "thy Lord", who teaches by the pen.

Q2 Why might Muslims see "Read!" as a fitting first word of the Qur'an?

Show answer

The word Qur'an means "recitation"; the message is to be read and passed on.

Mission 3

After the cave: fear, comfort, a calling

  • Muhammad ran home shaking and asked to be wrapped up. Khadijah comforted him: God would not disgrace a man so kind and honest (paraphrase of a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari).
  • Her Christian cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal said the same angel who came to Moses had come to him.
  • After a pause, revelations continued for the rest of his life: about 23 years.
  • Significance: for Muslims, this is the start of Risalah in its final form: God speaks again to humanity, through a final messenger.
  • For Muslims, this shows the message came from God, not from Muhammad: he was afraid and did not seek it.
  • Many Muslims remember the night in Ramadan as Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power.
Mission 3

The first Muslims

  • For about three years Muhammad taught quietly, among family and friends.
  • Khadijah: the first person to accept Islam.
  • Ali ibn Abi Talib: his young cousin, raised in his house, about 10 years old.
  • Abu Bakr: a close friend and merchant; many Sunni sources call him the first adult free man to believe.
  • Zayd ibn Harithah: a freed slave whom Muhammad had adopted.

Mission 3

Why Makkah's leaders turned against him

  • From about 613 Muhammad preached in public. Most Quraysh leaders opposed him.
  • Religion: "God is one" attacked the idols, and the ways of their ancestors.
  • Money: pilgrims to the idols at the Ka'bah brought trade to Makkah.
  • Power: if all believers are equal before God, the tribe's leaders lose their status.
  • Justice: the message demanded care for the poor and orphans, and condemned cheating and greed.
  • Judgement: many mocked the idea that the dead would rise to be judged.
  • Learn these five as the reasons for his persecution in Makkah.
Mission 3

Ten hard years

  • Persecution: Bilal, an Abyssinian slave, was tortured with a rock on his chest; he kept repeating "One, One". Sumayyah was killed for her faith.
  • c. 615: some Muslims fled to Abyssinia (Ethiopia), where a Christian king protected them.
  • c. 616–619: a boycott of the Prophet's clan: no trade or marriage with them.
  • 619, the Year of Sorrow: Khadijah and Abu Talib both died. Without his uncle, the Prophet lost his clan's protection.
  • He sought support in Ta'if, and was driven out with stones.
  • c. 620: the Night Journey and Ascension (Isra' and Mi'raj): Muslims believe he was taken to Jerusalem and to heaven, and the five daily prayers were given.

Mission 3

The Hijrah, 622 CE

  • People from Yathrib, a town about 450 km north, accepted Islam and promised to protect the Prophet (the pledges of Aqabah).
  • Muslims moved there in small groups. The Quraysh plotted to kill him: "they plot, but Allah (also) plotteth" (Surah 8:30, Pickthall).
  • Ali slept in the Prophet's bed to fool the attackers. The Prophet and Abu Bakr hid in the cave of Thawr for three days: "when they two were in the cave… Grieve not. Lo! Allah is with us." (Surah 9:40, Pickthall)
  • They reached Quba near Yathrib, where the first mosque was built, then entered the town.
  • Yathrib became Madinat al-Nabi, "the city of the Prophet": Madinah.
  • Hijrah means "migration". The migrants are the Muhajirun; the Madinans who welcomed them are the Ansar ("helpers").
Mission 3

Why the Hijrah still matters

  • Calendar: the Islamic calendar counts from 622: year 1 AH (anno Hegirae, "in the year of the Hijrah"). The caliph Umar chose it.
  • Why not his birth or the first revelation? The Hijrah began the first Muslim community (ummah) living by God's guidance.
  • Turning point: from a persecuted minority to a community with its own mosque, leaders and laws.
  • Example: Muslims see it as a model of sacrifice: leaving home, wealth and family for God.
  • Brotherhood: the Ansar shared homes and wealth with the Muhajirun; many Muslims point to this when they welcome refugees today.
  • Makkah's place in history: the Prophet's birthplace, the place of persecution, and the start of the hijrah.
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Hijrah was more important than the first revelation."

  • For: the Hijrah turned believers into a community (ummah) with a mosque, leaders and laws. Islam survived and spread from Madinah. The calendar starts there.
  • Against: without the revelation there would be no Islam to migrate for. Hira' is the start of the Qur'an, the source of all later guidance. Many Muslims mark Laylat al-Qadr as "better than a thousand months" (Surah 97:3).
  • Another view: the events cannot be ranked: the Prophet's life is one whole, and Muslims follow his whole example (Sunnah).
  • Judge it by: Important for what: belief, community or history? What do Muslims remember and celebrate today? Could either have happened without the other?
Mission check

The Hijrah turned a persecuted group into a community. That is why the Islamic calendar begins there. A later mission: Madinah, and the Seal of the Prophets.

Mission 4

Madinah: the first Muslim community

  • 622: the Prophet arrives in Madinah after the Hijrah (see Mission 3).
  • The mosque: the Prophet's Mosque is built beside his home: a place for prayer, teaching, meeting and shelter for the poor. (The very first mosque was built at Quba, on the way.)
  • Brotherhood: each Muhajir (migrant) is paired with an Ansari (helper) to share home and work.
  • The Charter of Madinah: Muslims, Jewish tribes and others agree to defend the city together; each group keeps its own religion.
  • Prayer: Bilal gives the first adhan (call to prayer). In 624 the qiblah (direction of prayer) changes from Jerusalem to the Ka'bah in Makkah.
  • The Prophet is now prophet, judge and leader of a community.
Mission 4

From Badr to the return to Makkah

  • Permission to fight back in self-defence is given after the Hijrah (Surah 22:39).
  • 624 Badr: a small Muslim force defeats a larger Makkan army.
  • 625 Uhud: a defeat; 627 the Trench: Madinah survives a siege.
  • 628 Hudaybiyyah: Muslims set out to make pilgrimage but are stopped. The Prophet agrees a ten-year truce, even on terms many companions thought unfair.
  • 630: after the truce is broken, the Prophet enters Makkah with little fighting, pardons most former enemies, and clears the idols from the Ka'bah.
  • Many Arabian tribes then accept Islam.

Mission 4

The last sermon and the Prophet's death

  • 632: the Prophet leads the Farewell Pilgrimage (Hajj). On the plain of Arafat he gives his last sermon (paraphrase of the main points):
    • Every Muslim's life, property and honour are sacred.
    • Interest on loans (riba) and old blood feuds are abolished.
    • Treat women well: husbands and wives have rights over each other.
    • All people descend from Adam: no Arab is better than a non-Arab, nor white than black, except by piety and good action.
    • Keep the prayer, the fast, zakah and Hajj; Muslims are brothers to one another.
  • June 632: he dies in Madinah, in the house of his wife Aisha, and is buried there; the place is now inside the Prophet's Mosque.
Mission 4

The Seal of the Prophets

> "Muhammad is not the father of any man among you, but he is the messenger of Allah and the Seal of the Prophets; and Allah is ever Aware of all things." (Surah 33:40, Pickthall)

  • A seal closes and completes a document. Muslims understand the Seal of the Prophets (Khatam an-Nabiyyin) as:
  • Final: no prophet will come after him.
  • Complete: the Qur'an completes God's guidance; it does not need adding to.
  • Confirming: he brings the same message as all earlier prophets, especially Tawhid (one God), and so fulfils their teaching.
  • Universal: earlier prophets came to their own peoples; he was sent "unto all mankind" (Surah 34:28, Pickthall).
  • Implications: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims agree that any later claim to be a prophet is false. The Shi'a Imams are guides, not prophets: they bring no new revelation.
Mission 4Read the source

A messenger, not more

> "O Prophet! Lo! We have sent thee as a witness and a bringer of good tidings and a warner. And as a summoner unto Allah by His permission, and as a lamp that giveth light." (Surah 33:45–46, Pickthall)

  • He is fully human. Muslims honour him, but never worship him; they add "peace be upon him" after his name.

Q1 Name two roles 33:45–46 gives the Prophet.

Show answer

Any two: witness, bringer of good news, warner, one who calls to God, a lamp giving light.

Q2 Why does a "lamp" suit a messenger rather than God?

Show answer

A lamp gives light that comes from elsewhere: he passes on God's guidance.

Mission 4

A role model: the Sunnah

> "Verily in the messenger of Allah ye have a good example for him who looketh unto Allah and the Last Day, and remembereth Allah much." (Surah 33:21, Pickthall)

  • His way of life is the Sunnah; reports of it are the Hadith.
  • Qualities that made him a good prophet: honesty (al-Amin), patience under persecution, mercy, humility, courage and justice.
  • Examples Muslims learn: he mended his own clothes and shoes, helped at home, ate simply, was gentle with children and animals, and forgave enemies at Makkah.
  • Today: Muslims copy his prayer and fasting, his greetings and manners, and his care for the poor.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Muhammad is the most important prophet in Islam."

  • For: he is the Seal: the final, complete message, sent to all people. The Qur'an is the only revealed book Muslims believe is unchanged. His Sunnah guides daily life and is named in the Shahadah.
  • Against: "We make no distinction between any of them" (2:136). All prophets taught the same Tawhid. Ibrahim is father of the prophets and is blessed in every salah; Adam was the first.
  • Another view: no distinction in faith (all are true messengers), but a difference in role: "some of whom We have caused to excel others" (2:253).
  • Judge it by: Important in what way: truth, role or example? Does "final" mean "greatest"? What does the Shahadah suggest?
Mission check

?A new group claims its founder received a fresh revelation from God in 2020, adding to the Qur'an. How would most Sunni and Shi'a Muslims respond?

For Muslims, the Seal means the message is complete. That is why Muslims look to the Qur'an and the Prophet's example, not to new prophets.

Mission 5

632: who leads now?

  • The Prophet died in June 632. He had been prophet, judge and leader of the Muslim community (ummah).
  • No new prophet would come (see Mission 4). But who would lead?
  • One answer: the community chooses the best-qualified Muslim. Those who followed this became the Sunni Muslims: "people of the Sunnah and the community".
  • Another answer: God had already chosen a leader from the Prophet's family, through the Prophet: his cousin and son-in-law Ali. His supporters were the Shi'at Ali, "the party of Ali": Shi'a Muslims.
  • Your course spells these Shi'ah, 'Umar, 'Uthman and 'Ali.
  • At first the question was about leadership. Over time it shaped belief, law and worship.
Mission 5

Abu Bakr chosen

  • While Ali and the Prophet's family prepared his burial, leaders of Madinah met at the Saqifah (a meeting hall).
  • Umar pledged loyalty (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr; most of those present followed. Abu Bakr became the first caliph (khalifah: successor).
  • Sunni view: a right choice by consultation (shura): Abu Bakr was the Prophet's closest friend, his companion in the cave, and led the prayers in the Prophet's last illness.
  • Shi'a view: the meeting ignored the Prophet's choice. Ali and the family were absent.
  • Ali later gave his pledge to Abu Bakr: Sunni sources present this as agreement; Shi'a sources, as accepting what he could not change, for the sake of unity.
Mission 5

Ghadir Khumm: two readings

  • March 632: returning from the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (ghadir) of Khumm, took Ali's hand and said: for whoever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla (paraphrase of a hadith in Sunni and Shi'a collections).
  • Mawla can mean "master, leader" or "friend, ally".
  • Shi'a reading: "master": a public appointment of Ali as leader after him. This is nass: explicit designation of the next Imam, by God's command through the Prophet.
  • Sunni reading: "friend": the Prophet answered complaints about Ali and told Muslims to love and support him; it was not about the caliphate.
  • Shi'a Muslims celebrate the day as Eid al-Ghadir.

Mission 5

The caliphs: Abu Bakr and Umar

  • Abu Bakr (632–634):
    • Defeated tribes that broke away or refused zakah (the Ridda wars), keeping Arabia united.
    • Ordered the first written collection of the Qur'an.
    • Named Umar to follow him.
  • Umar (634–644):
    • Islam spread to Syria, Jerusalem (637), Iraq, Persia and Egypt.
    • Began the Hijri calendar; set up a register (diwan) to pay soldiers and support the poor; took the title "Commander of the Faithful".
    • Known for strict justice and a simple life. Killed in 644; he left a council (shura) of six to choose the next caliph.
  • Sunni view: both were rightly chosen, and continued the Prophet's work faithfully.
Mission 5

The caliphs: Uthman and Ali

  • Uthman (644–656):
    • Made the standard written copy of the Qur'an and sent it to the main cities.
    • Further expansion; the first Muslim navy.
    • Criticised for appointing relatives (the Umayyad clan) as governors; killed by rebels in Madinah in 656.
  • Ali (656–661):
    • Ruled from Kufa (Iraq) amid the first civil war (fitna): the Battle of the Camel (656); Siffin (657) against Mu'awiya, governor of Syria.
    • Remembered for justice, learning and eloquence; his sermons and letters are gathered in Nahj al-Balaghah.
    • Killed in 661 by an extremist (a Kharijite) in the mosque at Kufa.
  • After Ali, Mu'awiya founded the Umayyad dynasty. In 680 Ali's son Husayn was killed at Karbala.
  • Sunni: the four are the Rightly-Guided Caliphs (al-Khulafa' al-Rashidun). Shi'a: Ali is the first rightful Imam; he advised the first three, but they were not the Prophet's appointed successors.

Mission 5

Two traditions today

  • Sunni Muslims (about 87–90% of Muslims): leadership by the best-qualified, chosen by the community; authority in the Qur'an, the Sunnah and the scholars.
  • Shi'a Muslims (about 10–13%): the imamate (Imamah): leadership belongs to Imams from the Prophet's family, appointed by God through nass, beginning with Ali.
  • Role of the Imam (Shi'a): to guide the community without error, to interpret the Qur'an, to keep the Prophet's teaching pure.
  • Significance: for Shi'a Muslims, Imamah is a root of faith (Usul ad-Din); Shi'a groups include Twelvers (the largest), Isma'ilis and Zaydis (whose view of the imamate is less strict).
  • Shared: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet; prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj (Sunni Muslims call these acts, with the Shahadah, the Five Pillars). Sunni Muslims also love the Prophet's family: every salah sends blessings on "Muhammad and the family of Muhammad".
  • The Shi'ah belief in the Twelve Imams is taught in an earlier lesson.

Mission 5Evaluate

"The split between Sunni and Shi'a Muslims was only about politics."

  • For: it began with a practical question: who leads the community? Both share the Qur'an, the Prophet, prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj. Ali himself worked with the first three caliphs.
  • Against: for Shi'a Muslims the leader is chosen by God, and Imamah is a root of faith. The two traditions developed different hadith collections, schools of law and practices (e.g. Ashura). Readings of the Qur'an (33:33) and Hadith (Ghadir Khumm) differ.
  • Another view: it began as a question of authority, which in Islam is both religious and political, so the two cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: Is choosing a leader a religious question in Islam? How much do the differences affect worship and belief today? How do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims describe the split themselves?

Mission 6

Hajj: the fifth pillar

  • Hajj is the pilgrimage to Makkah, in the month of Dhul-Hijjah (8th–13th days), one of the Five Pillars.
  • Who must go: every adult Muslim, once in a lifetime, if able: healthy in body and mind, able to pay after debts and family needs, and able to travel safely.
  • "Pilgrimage to the House is a duty unto Allah for mankind, for him who can find a way thither." (Qur'an 3:97, Pickthall)
  • Schools differ on whether a woman must travel with a male relative (mahram); Saudi rules now let women travel in approved groups.
Mission 6

Origins: Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il

  • Muslims believe Allah told Ibrahim (Abraham) to leave his wife Hajar and their baby son Isma'il in the dry valley of Makkah.
  • Hajar asked whether Allah had commanded this; when Ibrahim said yes, she replied that then Allah would not abandon them (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • When the water ran out, she ran seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, searching.
  • Water burst from the ground near Isma'il: the spring of Zamzam. People came to settle by it.
  • Later Ibrahim and Isma'il built the Ka'bah as a house for the worship of the One God (2:127), and Ibrahim called people to pilgrimage (22:27).
  • Over centuries the Ka'bah filled with idols. In 630 CE the Prophet Muhammad cleared them; his Farewell Hajj in 632 set the pattern Muslims follow today.

Mission 6

Ihram: purity and equality

  • Ihram is the sacred state a pilgrim enters before Hajj, at a boundary point (miqat) outside Makkah.
  • First a full bath (ghusl), then the intention (niyyah) and the talbiyah: "Labbayk Allahumma labbayk" ("Here I am, O Allah, here I am").
  • Men: two plain white unstitched cloths. Women: ordinary modest clothes, often white; face and hands uncovered.
  • Rules: no perfume; no cutting hair or nails; no sexual relations; no hunting; men keep their heads uncovered; no arguing: "no lewdness nor abuse nor angry conversation on the pilgrimage" (Qur'an 2:197, Pickthall).
  • Significance: rich and poor look the same: equality before Allah. The white cloths are like the burial shroud, a reminder of death and Judgement. The rules focus the mind on Allah.

Mission 6

Tawaf: circling the Ka'bah

  • The Ka'bah: a cube-shaped building in the centre of the Masjid al-Haram (Sacred Mosque), covered in a black cloth (kiswah) embroidered with Qur'an verses. It is the qiblah for every Muslim's prayer.
  • Tawaf: walking around the Ka'bah seven times anticlockwise, starting at the corner of the Black Stone, which pilgrims kiss, touch or point to.
  • After tawaf, two rak'ahs of prayer near the Station of Abraham (2:125).
  • Significance: Allah at the centre of life; the whole ummah moving as one; following Ibrahim and the Prophet.
  • Not worship of the building: Muslims worship only Allah. The Ka'bah is a place and a direction, not an object of worship.
Mission 6

Sa'y and Zamzam

  • Sa'y: walking seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwa, now inside a long covered gallery. Men hurry along one marked stretch, as Hajar ran.
  • "(the mountains) As-Safa and Al-Marwah are among the indications of Allah." (Qur'an 2:158, Pickthall)
  • Zamzam: pilgrims drink from the well, and many bring its water home as a gift.
  • Significance: Hajar's trust in Allah and her effort: Muslims learn to rely on Allah and still act. Allah provides for those who turn to Him.
  • A woman's search is at the heart of every pilgrim's Hajj.

Mission 7

The days of Hajj

  • 8 Dhul-Hijjah: in ihram, pilgrims go to Mina, a valley of tents outside Makkah, and pray there.
  • 9th: Arafat: the standing, from midday to sunset. At night, Muzdalifah.
  • 10th: back to Mina: stoning the largest pillar, sacrifice, shaving or cutting the hair. Then to Makkah for tawaf (and sa'y). Muslims worldwide celebrate Id-ul-Adha.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three pillars each day.
  • Before leaving Makkah: a farewell tawaf around the Ka'bah.
Mission 7

Arafat: the heart of Hajj

  • On the 9th, pilgrims stand (wuquf) on the plain of Arafat from midday until sunset: praying, reading the Qur'an, and asking Allah for forgiveness.
  • The Prophet said, "Hajj is Arafat" (hadith, paraphrase): a pilgrim who misses the standing has not completed Hajj.
  • The Mount of Mercy (Jabal al-Rahmah) rises from the plain. Near it the Prophet gave his Farewell Sermon in 632 CE: all people equal, no Arab better than a non-Arab except by piety; lives and property sacred; women to be treated kindly (paraphrase).
  • Some traditions say Adam and Hawwa (Eve) were reunited and forgiven here.
  • Significance: a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: millions standing before Allah, in plain white, waiting for mercy.
Mission 7

Muzdalifah: a night under the sky

  • After sunset pilgrims move to Muzdalifah, between Arafat and Mina.
  • They pray Maghrib and Isha together and sleep on the ground in the open air, rich and poor alike.
  • "when ye press on in the multitude from 'Arafat, remember Allah by the sacred monument" (Qur'an 2:198, Pickthall)
  • They collect small pebbles for the stoning at Mina.
  • Significance: simplicity, equality and trust in Allah after the day of standing.
Mission 7

Mina: stoning, sacrifice, shaving

  • Stoning (rami): pilgrims throw seven pebbles at the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah) on the 10th, and at all three on the following days. Today the pillars are tall walls reached by a multi-storey bridge.
  • Muslim tradition says Shaytan tempted Ibrahim three times here to disobey Allah's command to sacrifice his son, and Ibrahim drove him away with stones.
  • Significance: pilgrims reject evil and temptation in their own lives.
  • Sacrifice: a sheep, goat or camel is sacrificed (today usually arranged with a voucher) and the meat goes to the poor. This recalls the ram Allah gave Ibrahim in place of his son.
  • Hair: men shave the head or cut it short; women cut a small lock. Most ihram rules now end.
  • Tawaf al-ifadah: a further tawaf (and sa'y) at the Ka'bah, then back to Mina for the remaining days.

Mission 7

Benefits: on Hajj and for life

  • Forgiveness: the Prophet said a pilgrim whose Hajj is accepted returns free of sin, like a newborn (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Closeness to Allah: days given entirely to prayer and remembrance.
  • Equality and ummah: people of every nation, colour and class in the same white cloth. Malcolm X (1925–1965) wrote after his Hajj in 1964 that he had eaten and prayed with pilgrims of every colour, and saw true brotherhood (paraphrase).
  • Patience and self-control: heat, crowds and no arguing (2:197).
  • For life afterwards: many return determined to pray more, give more and avoid sin; the title Hajji or Hajjah is a reminder to live up to it.
Mission 7

Challenges

  • Cost: travel, accommodation and approved packages are expensive; many save for years.
  • Physical: summer heat in recent years, long walks, illness, crowds. Deadly crushes have happened (e.g. at Mina in 2015); Saudi authorities have rebuilt the Jamarat bridge and use crowd control.
  • Quotas: Saudi Arabia limits pilgrims from each country, so many wait years.
  • Spiritual: keeping patience and focus while tired, crowded and uncomfortable.
  • Commercialisation: luxury hotels overlooking the Ka'bah; some Muslims fear this undermines equality.
  • Effects of the journey: tiredness and illness for some; for many, a deep sense of peace and renewal.

Mission 7

Umrah: the lesser pilgrimage

  • Umrah: a shorter pilgrimage to Makkah that can be made at any time of year.
  • Same as Hajj: ihram, tawaf, sa'y, then shaving or cutting the hair.
  • Different: no fixed dates; no Arafat, Muzdalifah, Mina, stoning or required sacrifice; takes hours, not days.
  • Status: Hajj is a pillar. Scholars differ on Umrah: Hanafi and Maliki scholars call it a strongly recommended Sunnah; Shafi'i, Hanbali and Shi'a scholars hold it obligatory once for those able.
  • Many pilgrims perform Umrah and then Hajj in one journey.
  • Different attitudes: many Muslims make Umrah often, especially in Ramadan, when the Prophet said its reward equals a Hajj (hadith, paraphrase). Others warn that repeated Umrahs cost money that could feed the poor.
Mission 7

Makkah and Madinah

  • Makkah: birthplace of the Prophet (c. 570 CE); where revelation began; where early Muslims were persecuted; left in the Hijrah of 622.
  • Madinah: where the Hijrah ended; the first Muslim community was set up; the first mosque, Quba', was built; the Prophet died in 632.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sunni Muslims summarise iman as six articles: Tawhid, angels, books, prophets, Akhirah, al-Qadr.
  • Shi'a Muslims hold five roots of Usul ad-Din: Tawhid, 'Adl (Adalat), Nubuwwah, Imamah, Mi'ad; they also believe in angels, books and Allah's decree.

Mission 2

  • Islam is "built on five": Shahadah, Salah, Zakah, Sawm and Hajj, the acts of worship (ibadah) that hold up a Muslim's life.
  • The Shahadah declares Tawhid and Risalah; it must be said sincerely, and it marks a Muslim's life from birth to death.

Mission 3

  • Muhammad (born c. 570 in Makkah) was an orphan, a trader and known as al-Amin, "the trustworthy".
  • In 610, in the cave of Hira', Muslims believe Jibril brought the first revelation: "Read!" (Surah 96:1–5).
  • The Quraysh persecuted the Muslims for religious, economic and political reasons; in 622 the Prophet made the Hijrah to Madinah, year 1 of the Islamic calendar.

Mission 4

  • In Madinah the Prophet built the first community: mosque, brotherhood, charter; he returned to Makkah in 630 and died in Madinah in 632, after his last sermon.
  • Muslims believe Muhammad is the Seal of the Prophets (33:40): the final prophet, confirming all earlier prophets and sent to all people.
  • His Sunnah makes him the model for Muslim life (33:21); traditions differ on how his teaching was passed on.

Mission 5

  • In 632 the question "who leads?" had two answers: chosen by the community (Sunni: Abu Bakr, then Umar, Uthman, Ali) or appointed by God from the Prophet's family (Shi'a: Ali, by nass at Ghadir Khumm).
  • The four Rightly-Guided Caliphs united Arabia, spread Islam, standardised the Qur'an and faced the first civil war.

Mission 6

  • Hajj (fifth pillar) is required once in a lifetime of every adult Muslim who is able (Qur'an 3:97).
  • Its origins: Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il; Hajar's search between Safa and Marwa; Zamzam; Ibrahim and Isma'il building the Ka'bah (2:125–127).
  • At Makkah: ihram (equality, purity), tawaf (seven circuits anticlockwise), sa'y (seven walks between Safa and Marwa), Zamzam.

Mission 7

  • 8–13 Dhul-Hijjah: Mina → Arafat (standing until sunset; Mount of Mercy) → Muzdalifah (night, pebbles) → Mina (stoning, sacrifice, hair) → tawaf.
  • Benefits: forgiveness, equality, ummah, a changed life; challenges: cost, crowds, heat, quotas, commercialisation.
  • Umrah can be made any time and has no Arafat or Mina; Madinah holds the Prophet's Mosque and grave and the Quba' mosque.
