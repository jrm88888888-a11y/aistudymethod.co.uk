Mission 1

Arriving in Britain

  • 1950s–60s: men from Gujarat and Punjab come to work in mills, factories and transport; families follow.
  • 1967–72: East African Asians, mostly Gujarati Hindus, leave Kenya and Uganda (Idi Amin's expulsion, 1972); many arrive with business and professional skills.
  • 1980s–90s: Tamil Hindus from Sri Lanka (civil war); later skilled migrants from across India, and Nepalis.
  • Main centres: London (Brent, Harrow), Leicester, Birmingham, Bradford, Coventry.
  • 2021 Census (England and Wales): just over 1 million Hindus, 1.7% of the population.
Mission 1

Crossing the black water

  • Kala pani ("black water"): some dharma texts listed sea travel among acts that caused loss of caste purity, especially for Brahmins.
  • Gandhi's caste council outcasted him for sailing to London (1888).
  • So for centuries few Brahmin teachers went West. Westerners learned "Hinduism" mostly from texts and colonial scholars, not living practice.
  • That helped build a Western image of Hinduism as Sanskrit scriptures and philosophy (see Mission 2).
  • The rule faded in the 20th century; monks such as Vivekananda (1893) and later gurus travelled freely.
  • Your spec: the "historic prohibition on travel outside of India for Brahmins".

Mission 1

From front rooms to mandirs

  • Worship began at home: a shrine in the front room or a kitchen shelf.
  • Then rented halls for festivals, then converted buildings (churches, chapels, schools) as mandirs.
  • Then purpose-built stone mandirs, e.g. the BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir, Neasden (1995).
  • Diversity: regional (Gujarati, Punjabi, Tamil, Bengali); sampradayas (Swaminarayan, Pushtimarg, ISKCON, Sathya Sai, Shaiva temples); caste associations.
  • Many mandirs serve everyone: several deities under one roof, English used for children.
Mission 1

Daily practice in a non-Hindu society

  • Time: work and school timetables leave less time for daily puja; festivals move to the nearest weekend.
  • Priests: often trained in India; fewer can explain rituals in English.
  • Food: vegetarianism and fasting are harder at school, work and university.
  • Language: children may not speak Gujarati, Tamil or Hindi; mandirs run classes and use English.
  • Public festivals: Leicester's Diwali lights are often described as among the largest outside India; Navaratri dances (garba) fill halls.
Mission 1

Funerals and the law

  • Tradition: an open-air pyre, ideally by a river, lit by the eldest son, soon after death; ashes into a holy river.
  • UK practice: cremation in a crematorium (Cremation Act 1902); a son may press the button or view the committal.
  • Davender Ghai case (Court of Appeal, 2010): an open-air pyre is lawful if inside a structure with walls and an opening to the sky.
  • Ashes: some families fly them to the Ganges; others use river sites in Britain (e.g. on the River Soar in Leicester), following Environment Agency guidance.
  • Delays for death registration or inquests can clash with the wish for quick cremation.
  • Your spec: "traditions affected by legal/cultural concerns, for example funeral traditions".

Mission 1

Varna and ashrama abroad

  • Varna as a division of work hardly fits British jobs; jati (caste community) survives mainly in marriage and caste associations (e.g. Lohana, Patidar).
  • Caste discrimination in Britain is debated: the Equality Act 2010 allowed caste to be added by order; the government decided in 2018 not to do so. Tribunals have treated some caste cases as race discrimination.
  • Ashramas: student and householder stages fit British life; vanaprastha and sannyasa (withdrawal, renunciation) are rare; grandparents often become the household's religious teachers.
  • Many young British Hindus reject caste, or see it as cultural, not religious.
  • Your spec: "varna and ashrama in relation to society and social values".
Mission 1

India and sacred sites outside India

  • India stays central: pilgrimage to Varanasi, Vrindavan, Tirupati; ashes to the Ganges; family and guru links.
  • Can sacred sites grow outside India? Some say yes: a place becomes holy through deities installed by ritual (prana pratishtha), saints and devotion.
  • UK examples: Bhaktivedanta Manor (Hertfordshire), a pilgrimage centre at Janmashtami; Neasden mandir; Skanda Vale (Wales), a multi-faith Hindu-based ashram.
  • Some say no: true tirthas are tied to the sacred land of India and its rivers.
Mission 1

Freedom of religion as a human right

  • European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9 (1950): freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including worship and practice; in UK law through the Human Rights Act 1998.
  • It may be limited by law where necessary for public safety, order, health or others' rights.
  • Skanda Vale (2007): the temple bull Shambo tested positive for bovine TB; the courts allowed the Welsh government to slaughter him despite Article 9.
  • Bhaktivedanta Manor: a long planning dispute over public worship ended when a new access road was approved (1996).
  • Effect on Hindu thought: Hindus present themselves as one faith community among others, alongside churches, mosques and gurdwaras.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Traditional Hinduism cannot be fully practised in a non-Hindu society."

  • For: no sacred rivers or tirthas; funeral rites must change; varnashrama barely fits; priests and languages from India; time pressures.
  • Against: home puja, festivals and mandir life thrive; the law protects religious freedom; sacred sites can be created; India itself has adapted constantly.
  • Another view: "traditional" was never one thing; adaptation (e.g. weekend festivals) is itself traditional, since dharma depends on time and place.
  • Judge it by: which practices are essential? Whose tradition (Gujarati, Tamil, Punjabi)? Which generation?

Mission 2

What makes a Hindu identity?

  • No creed, founder or single scripture to sign up to. So identity is built from several strands.
  • Belief: Brahman, deities, karma, rebirth, dharma.
  • Practice and worship: home puja, mandir, festivals, samskaras (life rites).
  • Lifestyle and conduct: vegetarianism, ahimsa, family duty, respect for elders.
  • Belonging: family, region (Gujarati, Punjabi, Tamil), caste, language, sampradaya.
  • A person may be strong in some strands and weak in others: "practising", "cultural" and "spiritual but not religious" Hindus.
Mission 2

How the West has seen Hinduism

  • Orientalists admired ancient texts: William Jones (1746–1794) translated Sanskrit works; Max Müller (1823–1900) edited the Rig Veda and The Sacred Books of the East.
  • Missionaries attacked "idol worship", caste and sati as signs a religion needed replacing.
  • Result: a split image: a noble ancient philosophy vs a "degenerate" popular religion.
  • 1960s on: the Beatles at Rishikesh (1968), gurus in the West, Hinduism as mystical and exotic.
  • Scholar Richard King (Orientalism and Religion, 1999) argues Western writers shaped "Hinduism" around Vedanta texts and overlooked lived practice (paraphrase).
  • Your spec: "non-Hindu perceptions of Hinduism".
Mission 2

Popular culture

  • Yoga: a global fitness practice, often cut off from its Hindu roots and goal (moksha).
  • "Karma": used to mean instant payback or luck, not the law of action and rebirth.
  • Images: deities printed on shoes, swimwear and drinks have drawn Hindu protests.
  • Film and TV: Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom (1984) was criticised for its picture of Kali worship; the Simpsons character Apu for stereotyping South Asians.
  • Positive visibility: Diwali in Trafalgar Square; Bollywood; Holi-style colour runs (sometimes criticised as stripped of meaning).

Mission 2

Hindu responses: correcting and changing

  • Challenge stereotypes: Hindu Council UK, Hindu Forum of Britain and youth groups complain about misuse of images and correct media errors; mandirs hold open days.
  • Explain in Western terms: "one God in many forms", "Hinduism is a way of life", "sanatana dharma".
  • Changes through Western influence: emphasis on texts and philosophy; monotheistic explanations; reform of practices criticised by outsiders (Brahmo Samaj, neo-Vedanta–).
  • Some Hindus ask whether these explanations are faithful or are themselves shaped by Western expectations.
  • Gandhi became the best-known face of Hinduism in the West (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

Two responses: preserve or renew

  • Secularisation: religion loses influence in society and becomes a private choice; science, law and personal choice replace it as public authorities.
  • Preserve existing sects: e.g. Brahmo Samaj UK, continuing Ram Mohan Roy's reformed, image-free worship of one God. It offers a rational, ethical Hinduism suited to a secular society, but stays small.
  • New forms: ISKCON: converts, strict rules, strong community; offers what secularisation removes: authority, moral code, belonging.
  • Other growth: Swaminarayan (BAPS) and other sampradayas with youth programmes, volunteering and large mandirs.
  • Some Hindus respond by adapting: Hinduism as "a way of life", compatible with science and personal choice.

Mission 2

Generations and identity

  • First generation: identity through region, caste, language and village links; religion taken for granted.
  • Second and third generations: "British Hindu"; more choice; less ritual knowledge, more questions ("why do we do this?").
  • University Hindu societies (e.g. the National Hindu Students' Forum) and youth wings build a shared Hindu identity across regions and castes.
  • Some young people stress dharma as lifestyle: vegetarianism, ethics, yoga, service.
  • Others become cultural Hindus: festivals and family, little belief or practice.
Mission 2Evaluate

"There is one clear Hindu identity in Britain."

  • For: common festivals and values; national bodies speak for "British Hindus"; the Census groups 1 million people as Hindu; young people build a shared identity.
  • Against: deep regional, linguistic, caste and sampradaya differences; ISKCON converts and Gujarati elders may share little; many are "cultural" Hindus.
  • Judge it by: which strands of identity (belief, practice, belonging) count most? Is "Hindu" a religion, a culture, or both?

Mission 3

A life in four places

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), born in Porbandar, Gujarat, into a Vaishnava family; his mother was deeply devout, with Jain neighbours and friends.
  • London (1888–1891): trained as a lawyer; first read the Bhagavad Gita (in Edwin Arnold's English) with Theosophist friends.
  • South Africa (1893–1914): campaigned for Indians' rights; first satyagraha campaigns; founded communal farms (Phoenix, Tolstoy Farm).
  • India (1915–1948): led the independence movement; Salt March (1930); Quit India (1942); independence and Partition (1947).
  • Shot on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for concessions to Muslims and Pakistan.
  • Called Mahatma ("great soul") and Bapu ("father").
Mission 3

Satyagraha: holding firmly to truth

  • Satyagraha = satya (truth) + agraha (firm grasp): "truth-force" or "soul-force". Coined in South Africa (c. 1906–08).
  • Not "passive resistance": it is active, but never violent.
  • Aim: not to defeat the opponent but to convert them, by accepting suffering rather than inflicting it.
  • Methods: non-cooperation, civil disobedience, strikes, boycotts of foreign cloth, fasting.
  • Rules: openness (tell the authorities in advance), discipline, no hatred.
  • Example: the Salt March (1930): about 390 km to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.
  • Your spec: the social and political importance of his work.
  • Why non-violent? Gandhi believed all life is one and that "Truth is God", so harming an opponent harms oneself and betrays truth.
Mission 3

Swaraj: rule over yourself

  • Swaraj = self-rule. For Gandhi it meant two things: India governing itself, and each person governing their own desires.
  • Hind Swaraj (1909, written in Gujarati): Indian freedom would be empty if India simply copied Western industrial civilisation.
  • Swadeshi ("of one's own country"): use local goods; the spinning wheel (charkha) and homespun cloth (khadi) became symbols of self-reliance.
  • Ideal: self-governing, simple village communities.
  • Spiritual teaching and politics are one: freedom begins with self-discipline.
Mission 3Diversity

Who claims Gandhi?

  • Most Hindus in India: "Father of the Nation"; his birthday (2 October) is a national holiday.
  • Hindu nationalists: some honour his patriotism and cow protection; others, like Godse, blamed him for Partition and for favouring Muslims.
  • Orthodox Hindus of his day: opposed his temple-entry campaigns as breaking purity rules.
  • Dalit thinkers: Ambedkar saw him as a defender of caste in a reformer's clothes.
  • Background: Gandhi called untouchability a sin and campaigned for temple entry, but defended varna; the Dalit leader B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) said caste itself must go.
  • The non-Hindu world: an inspiration for Martin Luther King Jr (Montgomery 1955–56), the US civil rights movement and others; the UN marks 2 October as the International Day of Non-Violence.
  • So the best-known Hindu is not accepted by all Hindus as speaking for Hinduism; he drew on Tolstoy, Ruskin, the Sermon on the Mount and Jain ethics as well as the Gita.

Mission 4

Diversity is not pluralism

  • Diversity: the fact that many religions (or many traditions) exist side by side.
  • Pluralism: a view about that fact: many religions are valid paths to the truth.
  • Exclusivism: only one religion leads to the truth or to liberation.
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth; others contain part of it.
  • Inside Hinduism itself: Vaishnavas, Shaivas, Shaktas, Smartas and many local traditions live together without one creed or authority.

Mission 5

Stridharma: a woman's duty

  • Dharma is duty by one's place in life; stridharma is the dharma of women (stri, woman).
  • Classical texts set it mainly within the household: serving husband and family, keeping rituals of the home, raising children.
  • The ideal of the pativrata: a wife devoted to her husband, as Sita (Ramayana) and Savitri (Mahabharata) are remembered.
  • Texts were written by men, mostly Brahmin, for an ideal society; lived practice varied by region, jati and era.
  • Example: Tryambakayajvan's Stridharmapaddhati (18th century, Thanjavur), a rulebook for wives.
  • Many Hindu women also hold religious roles of their own: fasts (vratas) for family welfare, household puja, singing bhajans.
Mission 5Read the source

Two voices in the Manusmriti

Manusmriti 3.56 (paraphrase): where women are honoured, the gods rejoice; where they are not honoured, all rites bring no reward.

Manusmriti 5.147–148 and 9.3 (paraphrase): a woman should never act independently; she is protected by her father in childhood, her husband in youth and her sons in old age.

Q1 What do the two passages share?

Show answer

Q2 Why can both feminists and traditionalists quote the Manusmriti?

Show answer

  • The Manusmriti is smriti (remembered tradition), not shruti; many Hindus treat it as a product of its time.
Mission 5

Marriage at the centre

  • Vivaha (marriage) is one of the samskaras (life-cycle rites) and opens the grihastha (householder) stage.
  • Traditionally a sacred, lifelong bond, not a contract: the couple take seven steps (saptapadi) around the sacred fire.
  • Kanyadan: the father gives his daughter in marriage; she joins her husband's family.
  • For women, classical texts treat marriage as their main samskara, in place of the student initiation boys receive.
  • Children: the householder's duty includes continuing the family and its rites.
  • Many Hindu marriages are arranged (by families, with the couple's consent); "love marriages" are increasingly common.
Mission 5

Sons and daughters

  • Sons: traditionally perform the parents' funeral rites (antyeshti) and the ancestral offerings (shraddha); the eldest son lights the pyre.
  • Manusmriti 9.138 explains putra (son) as one who saves his father from a hell called put (paraphrase): a basis of son preference.
  • Sons usually stay in the family home and care for parents in old age.
  • Daughters: honoured as Lakshmi in the home, but often called "another's wealth" (paraya dhan), since they leave at marriage.
  • Today many daughters perform last rites, and inheritance law gives daughters equal shares (see Mission 6).
Mission 5

Dowry and social issues

  • Stridhan ("woman's wealth"): gifts that belong to the bride herself; recognised in the dharmashastras.
  • Dowry (dahej): payments demanded from the bride's family by the groom's family: a social custom, not a scriptural duty.
  • Banned by the Dowry Prohibition Act 1961, yet still widespread; dowry demands can lead to harassment and dowry deaths.
  • Son preference has led to sex-selective abortion; sex-determination tests are banned (1994 Act).
  • Hindu voices against dowry include reform movements (Arya Samaj), many gurus and temple leaders, and women's groups.
  • Discussion point: how far do these social issues arise from religious teaching, and how far from culture?

Mission 5

Divorce: different views

  • Traditional view (classical dharmashastras): marriage is indissoluble, for this life and beyond; a widow does not remarry.
  • But texts disagree: Narada Smriti 12.97 (paraphrase) lets a woman take another husband if hers is lost, dead, has renounced the world, is impotent, or is outcaste.
  • Many jatis always allowed separation and remarriage by local custom.
  • Hindu Marriage Act 1955: divorce on set grounds; divorce by mutual consent added in 1976.
  • Many Hindus today accept divorce as a lesser evil than a harmful marriage; others see it as against dharma and seek reconciliation through families.
  • Divorce still carries stigma, often heavier for women.
Mission 5

Changing roles of men and women

  • Education and work: Hindu women now work in every profession; many families value daughters' education as highly as sons'.
  • Religious roles: women priests (pujaris) trained in some places (e.g. by the Shankar Seva Samiti and Jnana Prabodhini in Pune); women gurus (e.g. Anandamayi Ma, 1896–1982; Mata Amritanandamayi, b. 1953).
  • Roles of men: more shared housework and child care in many families; but the provider role and son preference persist.
  • Diaspora: British Hindu women lead temple committees and teach in Hindu schools.
  • Many Hindus reinterpret stridharma as mutual duties of partners; traditionalists see the old roles as complementary, not unequal.

Mission 6

An encounter with empire

  • From the late 1700s, Britain ruled more and more of India: new laws, missionary schools, Western ideas of rights and reason.
  • Missionaries and officials often attacked Hindu practice, especially the treatment of women.
  • Hindu reformers answered in two ways at once: they used Western arguments and went back to Hindu texts to show that harmful customs were not true dharma.
  • Later, India's secular state (from 1947) gave women rights by law, outside religion.
  • So change came from three sources: reformers within, colonial rule, and the secular state. How much from each is debated.
Mission 6

Sati

  • Sati: a widow burning herself, or being burned, on her husband's funeral pyre. Named after the goddess Sati, though in her story she was not a widow.
  • Never universal: practised mainly among some higher castes in some regions; cases rose in early 19th-century Bengal.
  • Defenders praised it as the supreme act of a pativrata (devoted wife); opponents called it murder, often under family pressure.
  • Banned in Bengal in 1829 (Governor-General William Bentinck), after Hindu reformers' campaigns. Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833) argued from Hindu law books that sati was not required.
  • A case in Rajasthan in 1987 (Roop Kanwar) led to the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act 1987, which also bans glorifying sati.
Mission 6

Ram Mohan Roy

  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), Bengali scholar of Sanskrit, Persian and English; founded the Brahmo Sabha (1828), later the Brahmo Samaj.
  • Campaigned against sati in tracts (1818, 1820) and petitions; supported the 1829 ban and defended it in England before the Privy Council; he died in Bristol in 1833.
  • His method: argue from the shastras. The texts do not command sati; they praise a widow's chaste, disciplined life (paraphrase).
  • Also argued for women's property rights (1822), against polygamy, and for women's education.
  • Significance: he made the case for women's rights as a Hindu, from inside the tradition.
Mission 6Read the source

Roy's appeal to Manu

Manusmriti 5.157–160 (paraphrase): after her husband's death a widow should live a pure, self-controlled life; a virtuous wife who remains chaste after his death reaches heaven, even without a son.

  • Roy cited verses like these: Manu, the highest authority among the law books, praises a widow who lives, not one who dies.
  • Opponents cited other texts (later smritis and Puranas) that praised sati.

Q1 How could this passage be used against sati?

Show answer

It praises a widow's life of discipline, with heaven as its reward, not death on the pyre.

Q2 What does Roy's method show about reform in Hinduism?

Show answer

Reform can work by choosing which texts are authoritative and how to read them.

Mission 6

Widows, daughters, education

  • Widows faced shaved heads, white clothes, poverty and a ban on remarriage in many high-caste families.
  • Ishwar Chandra Vidyasagar (1820–1891) argued from the Parashara Smriti for widow remarriage: Hindu Widows' Remarriage Act 1856.
  • Education: Savitribai Phule (1831–1897) opened a girls' school in Pune (1848) with Jyotirao Phule; the Arya Samaj founded girls' schools; Pandita Ramabai (1858–1922) opened a home for widows (1889).
  • Child marriage was restricted by the Child Marriage Restraint Act (1929).
  • Daughters: rights to inherit came later.

Mission 6

Rights from a secular state

  • The Constitution (1950): equality before the law (Article 14), no discrimination by sex (Article 15); every adult woman can vote.
  • The Hindu Code (1955–56): Hindu Marriage Act (monogamy, divorce); Hindu Succession Act (daughters inherit); adoption and guardianship laws. Ambedkar resigned as Law Minister in 1951 partly because the bill was delayed.
  • 2005: daughters gain equal rights in ancestral family property.
  • Laws on dowry (1961), sex selection (1994), domestic violence (2005).
  • Outside religion: education, jobs and cities changed roles of men and women; many men now share work once called women's.
  • Some Hindus welcomed this as true dharma; others saw state interference in religious law.
Mission 6

Manushi and Madhu Kishwar

  • Manushi: founded in Delhi in 1978 by Madhu Kishwar (b. 1951) and Ruth Vanita; a journal "about women and society".
  • Work: reporting violence against women, dowry deaths and unfair laws; campaigns for women's land and property rights; giving ordinary women space to speak.
  • Later, as Manushi Sangathan, it also campaigned for poor workers such as Delhi's street vendors.
  • Kishwar's essay "Why I do not call myself a feminist" (1990): Western feminist labels may not fit Indian women's lives (paraphrase).
  • She drew on Indian culture as a resource, e.g. reading Sita as a woman of moral strength who challenged Rama's injustice (1997).
  • Significance: feminism with Indian and Hindu resources, not only against them.
Mission 6

Caste and the feminist movement

  • Dalit feminists: mainstream Indian feminism was led mostly by upper-caste women and missed caste.
  • Gopal Guru, "Dalit Women Talk Differently" (1995): Dalit women face caste and gender together.
  • National Federation of Dalit Women (1995).
  • Uma Chakravarti (1993): "Brahmanical patriarchy": caste purity was protected by controlling women's marriage and sexuality.
  • Savitribai Phule is honoured by many as an early Dalit-Bahujan feminist: a woman from a Shudra jati teaching girls of all castes.
  • Discussion: who speaks for women: the same question as for Dalits.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is feminism an issue in Hinduism today?

  • Claim: Feminism is still a live issue in Hinduism today.
  • For: dowry, son preference and widow stigma persist; some texts limit women's independence.
  • For: Dalit feminists show women are still unequal within caste.
  • Against: law gives equal rights; many Hindu women lead, teach and serve as priests.
  • Against: Kishwar and others say Hindu culture gives women resources, so a Western-style feminism is not needed.
  • Judgement criteria: issue in teaching, or in culture? Which women: urban or rural, upper-caste or Dalit? Measured by law or by daily life?

Mission 6

Change from inside or outside?

  • Inside: Roy argued from the shastras; bhakti saints gave women voice.
  • Vidyasagar argued from the Parashara Smriti for widow remarriage.
  • Kishwar drew on Sita as a woman of moral strength.
  • Outside: British law (1829 sati ban; 1856 widow remarriage), the secular Constitution and Hindu Code, economic change.
  • Both: most reforms needed Hindu arguments and state law.
  • Impact on men: new laws changed fathers' and husbands' duties (shared property, no dowry, divorce), and many men's roles at home.

Mission 7

From exclusion to movement

  • Dalits faced exclusion from temples, wells, schools and many jobs.
  • Modern Dalit movements turned that exclusion into organised action.
  • Jyotirao Phule (1827–1890), from a Shudra (gardener) jati, and Savitribai Phule (1831–1897): opened a school for girls in Pune (1848) and later schools for untouchable children.
  • Phule founded the Satyashodhak Samaj ("Truth-Seekers' Society", 1873): against Brahmin priestly control and caste.
  • Two big questions run through every movement: reform Hinduism or leave it? and who leads: Dalits or others?
Mission 7

B. R. Ambedkar

  • Bhimrao Ramji Ambedkar (1891–1956), born into the Mahar community (Dalit) in western India.
  • Studied at Columbia University (New York) and the London School of Economics; lawyer and economist.
  • Mahad satyagraha (1927): Dalits drank from a public tank closed to them; the same year Ambedkar's followers publicly burned the Manusmriti.
  • Annihilation of Caste (1936): caste is rooted in the shastras, so reform must reject their authority over society (paraphrase).
  • Chaired the drafting committee of India's Constitution (1947–50): Article 17 abolished untouchability.
  • Slogan attributed to him: "Educate, agitate, organise."
Mission 7

Gandhi on untouchability and varna

  • Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948) called untouchability a sin and a blot on Hinduism.
  • Renamed untouchables Harijans ("people of God"); weekly paper Harijan (1933); founded the Harijan Sevak Sangh (1932).
  • Defended varna as a division of duty, not of worth: each does the work of his birth without pride or contempt (paraphrase of his 1930s writings).
  • Rejected the hierarchy and the idea of pollution; did his own cleaning of latrines to show labour has dignity.
  • Change should come from caste Hindus' change of heart (temple entry, shared wells), not from splitting Hindu society.
  • In later life he backed inter-caste marriage more openly.
Mission 7Read the source

One's own duty

Bhagavad Gita 18.47 (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "Better thine own work is, though done with fault, / Than doing others' work, ev'n excellently."

  • Gandhi read this as svadharma: do your own inherited work, and no work is lower than another.
  • Ambedkar's reply in Annihilation of Caste: work fixed by birth is the caste system; dignity cannot be given while choice is taken away (paraphrase).

Q2 Why might Ambedkar see the same verse as part of the problem?

Mission 7

1932: the Poona Pact

  • 1932: the British Communal Award gave the "Depressed Classes" separate electorates (Dalits electing their own representatives). Ambedkar supported this.
  • Gandhi, in Yerwada jail, began a fast unto death against it: separate electorates would divide Hindus permanently.
  • Under great pressure, Ambedkar signed the Poona Pact (24 September 1932): reserved seats in joint electorates instead (more seats, but chosen by all voters).
  • Ambedkar later argued that joint electorates let caste Hindus pick "safe" Dalit candidates.
  • The question at the heart of it: who speaks for Dalits, and who chooses their speakers?
Mission 7

Leaving Hinduism: Ambedkar's conversion

  • 1935, Yeola: Ambedkar declared that although he was born a Hindu he would not die a Hindu (paraphrase of a widely reported speech).
  • 14 October 1956, Nagpur: Ambedkar and hundreds of thousands of followers took Buddhist vows. He died weeks later (6 December 1956).
  • His Buddhism (often called Navayana, "new vehicle") stresses equality, reason and social liberation.
  • A path of exit: leave a system whose texts rank you, rather than reform it.
  • Many Dalits today are Buddhist; others are Hindu, Sikh, Christian, Muslim, or Ravidassia.
Mission 7

The Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP)

  • Founded 1984 by Kanshi Ram (1934–2006): "Bahujan" = "the majority of the people": Dalits, other backward classes and minorities together.
  • Aim: political power for the excluded, not favours from others.
  • Mayawati (b. 1956): first Dalit woman Chief Minister of an Indian state (Uttar Pradesh), four times; won a majority in 2007 by also reaching Brahmin voters.
  • Built memorials to Ambedkar and Dalit heroes: pride and visibility.
  • Other gains in public life and business: K. R. Narayanan, India's first Dalit President (1997–2002); the Dalit Indian Chamber of Commerce and Industry (founded 2005).
  • Critics: a party can win power without ending everyday discrimination; the BSP's vote has fallen since 2012.

Mission 7

Dalit education

  • Education was the first key in every movement: Phule's schools (1848 onwards); Ambedkar's People's Education Society (1945) and Siddharth College, Bombay (1946).
  • Since 1950: reserved places and scholarships for Scheduled Caste students in colleges and universities.
  • Results: a Dalit middle class, writers, scholars, officials.
  • Problems remain: drop-out rates, discrimination on campus, unequal schools.
  • Religious and cultural attitudes matter: where untouchability lingers, children may be seated apart or given lower expectations.
Mission 7

Dalit Solidarity Networks

  • National Campaign on Dalit Human Rights (NCDHR, India, 1998).
  • Dalit Solidarity Network UK (DSN-UK, 1998) and the International Dalit Solidarity Network (IDSN, Copenhagen, 2000): campaign for caste to be treated as a human-rights issue.
  • The UN treats caste as discrimination based on descent (CERD General Recommendation 29, 2002).
  • UK debate: the Equality Act 2010 allowed caste to be added as a protected characteristic; Parliament required it in 2013; the government decided in 2018 not to (relying on case law).
  • Many Dalit groups supported a law; several Hindu organisations opposed it, saying it would stigmatise Hindus and caste discrimination is rare.
  • Networks let Dalits speak for themselves on a world stage, with allies.
Mission 7Evaluate

How best can entrenched discrimination be ended?

  • Claim: Political and legal power has done more for Dalits than religious reform.
  • For: the Constitution, reservations and laws (Ambedkar) changed what is legal; the BSP brought real power.
  • For: reform from within depended on caste Hindus' goodwill, which was slow (Gandhi's temple-entry work).
  • Against: laws are poorly enforced where social and religious attitudes do not change.
  • Against: religious change (bhakti saints, conversion to Buddhism) gave dignity and identity that votes cannot.
  • Judgement criteria: Who leads: Dalits or others? Change in law, or in daily life? Measured by what: education, income, safety, dignity?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Migration from the 1950s (Gujarat, Punjab, East Africa, Sri Lanka) built a diverse British Hindu community of about 1 million (2021).
  • Practice adapts to law and culture: home puja, weekend festivals, crematoria, river sites for ashes; caste survives mainly in marriage.
  • Article 9 protects religious freedom, within limits (Shambo, 2007).
  • Sacred sites may develop outside India.

Mission 2

  • Western images of Hinduism swing between admired philosophy and exotic stereotype; popular culture borrows yoga, karma and deities, often without meaning.
  • Secularisation pushes religion into private life.
  • Hindus respond by preserving sects (Brahmo Samaj UK), new forms (ISKCON) and adapting.
  • Hindu identity is built from belief, practice, lifestyle, worship and belonging, so British Hindu identity is both shared and diverse.

Mission 3

  • Gandhi (1869–1948) joined religion and politics: Truth is God; all life is one; so non-violence (ahimsa) and satyagraha.
  • Hindus, Dalits and the wider world judge him differently: Father of the Nation, defender of caste, or global model of non-violence.

Mission 4

  • Diversity is a fact; pluralism, inclusivism and exclusivism are views about it.
  • Ishtadeva, Rig Veda 1.164.46 and Gita 4:11 support openness; Gita 9:23 and Radhakrishnan show ranking (inclusivism).

Mission 5

  • Stridharma sets women's duty within the household; the Manusmriti both honours women and limits their independence.
  • Marriage and children, the value placed on sons, and dowry give rise to social issues that many Hindus see as culture rather than teaching.
  • Hindus hold different views on divorce, from indissoluble marriage to divorce as a lesser evil.

Mission 6

  • Under British rule, reformers such as Ram Mohan Roy argued from the shastras against sati and for women's rights; laws followed (1829 sati ban; 1856 widow remarriage).
  • The secular state and the Hindu Code gave women legal rights; Manushi and Madhu Kishwar sought women's rights with Indian cultural resources.
  • Dalit feminists add caste to gender.

Mission 7

  • Gandhi opposed untouchability but kept varna as duty and sought a change of heart; Ambedkar saw caste as rooted in the shastras and sought its end, through law and finally Buddhism.
  • The Poona Pact (1932) and the BSP (1984) turn on one question: who speaks for Dalits and who holds power.
  • Education and solidarity networks (NCDHR, DSN-UK, IDSN) carry the movement into law and world forums, including the UK caste-law debate.
