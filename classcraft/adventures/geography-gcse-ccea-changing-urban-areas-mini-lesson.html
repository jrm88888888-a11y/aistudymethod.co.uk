Mission 1

Mission 1: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Make a guess: A square metre of land in Ginza, central Tokyo, is valued at ¥67.1 million. A square metre of housing land in Ome, also in Tokyo, averages ¥98,600. Why the gap?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2B.1 characteristics and location of CBD, inner city, suburbs and rural–urban fringe
  • 2B.1 interpret aerial photographs and maps (incl. OS) to identify settlement functions and land use zones
Mission 1

Four zones of a city

  • CBD (central business district): the city centre.
  • Inner city: the ring around the CBD, usually the oldest housing and industry.
  • Suburbs: newer housing further out.
  • Rural–urban fringe: the edge, where town meets countryside.
Mission 1

What each zone looks like

  • CBD: shops, offices, banks; tall buildings; little housing; busy roads, main stations.
  • Inner city: terraced houses and flats, old factories and warehouses; some redeveloped.
  • Suburbs: semi-detached and detached houses with gardens; curving roads, cul-de-sacs; parks.
  • Rural–urban fringe: retail parks, business parks, golf courses, sewage works, farmland.
Quick check
?A zone has semi-detached houses with gardens, curving streets and a small parade of shops. Which zone is it?
Mission 1

Accessibility and land value

  • Accessibility: how easy a place is to reach. It is highest in the CBD, where roads and railways meet.
  • Many land users want accessible sites, so they compete for them, and land values rise.
  • Shops and offices gain most from being central, so they can pay the most.
  • Values fall with distance from the centre, with smaller peaks at stations and main roads.
Mission 1

Tokyo's land values

  • Japan's highest commercial land value on 1 January 2026: Ginza 4-chome, central Tokyo, ¥67.1 million per m².
  • Average housing land values per m², 2026:
    • Chiyoda ward (central): ¥3,631,400
    • Setagaya ward: ¥779,900
    • Hachioji (west Tokyo): ¥131,300
    • Ome (far west Tokyo): ¥98,600
Quick check
?About how many times greater is Ginza's value (¥67,100,000 per m²) than Ome's (¥98,600 per m²)?
Mission 1

Locational needs

  • Shops need many passing shoppers, so central or busy sites.
  • Offices need access for workers and clients.
  • Industry needs large, cheap, flat sites with road links, so it moves to the edge.
  • Housing needs space and quiet; most people can afford only cheaper land further out.
  • Planning (zoning) also decides what may be built where.
Quick check
?A firm wants to build a large warehouse that needs lorry access. Where will it most likely locate?
Mission 1

Settlement function

  • Function: the main job or purpose of a settlement.
  • Examples: market town (trade), port, resort (tourism), industrial, administrative (council, government), dormitory or commuter settlement.
  • Large settlements have many functions; one may dominate.
Mission 1

Clues on an OS map

  • Symbols and labels: PO (post office), PH (pub), TH (town hall), places of worship, stations, tourist information.
  • CCEA uses 1:50 000 OS maps.
  • CBD: roads and railways converge; dense buildings; town hall, station, bus station.
  • Suburbs: curving roads and cul-de-sacs; schools, playing fields.
  • Fringe: large buildings by main roads, golf courses, fields.
  • Function clues: harbour and quays (port); beach, caravan sites, museums (resort); works or large sheds by railways (industry).
Mission 1

Clues on an aerial photograph

  • Vertical photos look straight down (like a map); oblique photos look down at an angle.
  • Look at building size, height and density, roof types, green space and car parks.
  • Tall, tightly packed buildings: CBD. Rows of long, narrow roofs: terraced inner city. Houses with gardens: suburbs. Big flat roofs with car parks: retail or business park.
Quick check
?A map square shows a harbour, quays, a ferry terminal and a railway ending at the waterfront. What is the settlement's main function?
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a plot beside the main station in the city centre costs far more per square metre than a plot of the same size near the city edge. Why?

Land values fall with distance from the most accessible point, the CBD. Shops and offices outbid housing and industry there, which is why each zone of a city has its own land use.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can an inner-city area be both run-down and too expensive for the people who grew up there?

Make a guess: How can an inner-city area be both run-down and too expensive for the people who grew up there?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2B.2 MEDC inner-city housing issues, poor-quality housing and gentrification
  • 2B.2 MEDC inner-city cultural mix issues, ethnic and religious tensions, language barriers
Mission 2

The MEDC inner city

  • The inner city is the ring of old housing and former industry around the city centre (CBD).
  • In many UK cities it was built in the 1800s as factory workers' housing. Some was later cleared for council estates.
  • Today it is often the most mixed part of a city: old and new, rich and poor, many cultures.
Mission 2

Poor-quality housing

  • Old or badly maintained homes can be damp, cold and expensive to heat, and some are overcrowded.
  • Example: the Heygate estate, Southwark, London: 1,212 homes, completed in 1974 for about 3,000 people.
  • A late-1990s survey found its buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
Mission 2

Knock it down or do it up?

  • The Heygate was demolished by July 2014. A plan approved in January 2013 included 3,000 new homes.
  • In 2005, the 595 council tenants still there were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in Southwark.
  • Across London, 50 estates were regenerated in a decade: the number of homes "almost doubled", but there was "a net loss of some 8,000 social rented homes".
Quick check
?What does the London-wide evidence on 50 regenerated estates show?
Mission 2

Gentrification

  • Gentrification: wealthier people move into a run-down area, renovate homes, and prices and rents rise.
  • New York: researchers classed 15 of the 22 neighbourhoods that were low-income in 1990 as gentrifying, meaning rents rose faster than in the median area.
  • Average rent change, 1990 to 2010–2014: gentrifying areas 34.3%; whole city 22.1%; low-income areas that did not gentrify 13.2%.
Mission 2

Winners and losers

  • Winners: home owners, landlords, new residents, new shops and cafés.
  • Losers: renters on low incomes, who face higher rents.
  • At the Heygate, some residents say "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor" in the decision to demolish.
  • Limits of the evidence: the New York researchers "cannot directly observe if low-income residents are moving out".
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows that a neighbourhood is gentrifying?
Mission 2

Cultural mix: language barriers

  • Inner cities often house many recent migrants, so many languages are spoken.
  • England and Wales, 2021 census: 1.5% of residents (880,000) could not speak English well, and 0.3% (161,000) could not speak it at all.
  • London had the lowest share of people with English as their main language: 78.4%.
  • A language barrier makes it harder to find work, see a doctor or support children at school. Interpreters and English classes help.
Mission 2

Cultural mix: ethnic and religious tensions

  • Most mixed areas are peaceful, but tension can grow where groups compete for housing and jobs, live apart, or face prejudice.
  • Police in England and Wales recorded 137,550 hate crimes in the year to March 2025. Outside London's Metropolitan Police area, race hate crimes rose 6% and religious hate crimes 3%.
  • The Home Office warns that, because recording has improved, the figures "do not provide reliable trends in hate crime since 2014".
  • Belfast: "peace walls" separate mainly Catholic and mainly Protestant neighbourhoods. The Northern Ireland Executive committed to "remove all Interface structures by 2023"; gates still operate at 17 locations.
Quick check
?A new arrival in an inner-city area cannot read letters from the doctor or the school. Which issue is this?
Mission check

?Over ten years, old warehouses and terraces near a city centre become flats and cafés. Rents rise faster than the city average, and many long-time renters move further out. What best describes this?

An inner-city area can be run-down (old, poor-quality buildings) yet sit on valuable land near the centre. Redevelopment and gentrification raise rents: in New York's gentrifying areas, rents rose 34.3% against 22.1% citywide, and London's estate regeneration brought a net loss of some 8,000 social rented homes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: In 2003 London began charging drivers to enter its centre. By 2007 traffic was still lower, but congestion was back where it started. How?

Make a guess: In 2003 London began charging drivers to enter its centre. By 2007 traffic was still lower, but congestion was back where it started. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2B.2 MEDC inner-city traffic issues: congestion (air quality, journey time), public transport (cost, efficiency), parking (cost, availability)
Mission 3

What is congestion?

  • Congestion: extra journey time caused by too much traffic for the road space.
  • Transport for London (TfL) measures excess delay: minutes per km above the free-flow time at night.
  • Congestion depends on traffic and on road capacity (the space available).
Mission 3

Why inner cities jam

  • Many journeys head for the centre at the same time: the morning and evening peaks.
  • Old inner-city streets are narrow; parked cars, deliveries and road works take space.
  • London is an MEDC city: its inner-city traffic problems are typical of MEDC cities.
  • In London, TfL reported "a sustained rise in congestion in all parts of London during the years leading up to the pandemic".
Mission 3

Impacts: journey time and air quality

  • Journey time: London's average bus speed was 9.3 mph in 2023/24, 1% slower than the year before; TfL blamed "increasing traffic congestion" in part.
  • Air quality: road transport is the largest single source of fine particles (PM2.5) emitted within London, about 30%.
  • Stop-start traffic burns more fuel per km, so more pollution.
  • Over 600,000 Londoners live with a lung condition.
Quick check
?Traffic stays the same, but a lane is closed for road works. What happens to excess delay?
Mission 3

Public transport: cost and efficiency

  • Efficiency: buses stuck in traffic are slow and unreliable (London: 9.3 mph). Bus lanes and trains avoid the jams.
  • Capacity: one rail line can move huge numbers: the Elizabeth line carried about 660,000 journeys a day by July 2024.
  • Cost to users: if fares cost more than driving and parking, people keep using cars.
  • Cost to taxpayers: new lines and cheap fares need public money.
Mission 3

Parking: cost and availability

  • Availability: old inner-city streets have few spaces; residents, shoppers and workers compete for them.
  • Drivers circling to find a space add to congestion.
  • Cost: high charges in the centre discourage driving but can hurt shops and low-income drivers.
  • Responses: residents' permit zones, multi-storey car parks, park-and-ride sites at the city edge.
Quick check
?Which problem is about the efficiency of public transport?
Mission 3

London's congestion charge

  • February 2003: a £5 daily charge to drive into central London.
  • July 2005: raised to £8. February 2007: the zone was extended westwards.
  • Aims: reduce congestion, improve buses, make car journey times more reliable, and help goods deliveries.
Mission 3

What happened?

  • Excess delay in the original zone (minutes per km) against a "representative" 2002 level of 2.3:
    • 2003: 1.6 · 2004: 1.6 · 2005: 1.8 · 2006: 2.1 · 2007: 2.3.
  • Traffic stayed lower: in 2007 there were 21% fewer vehicles with four or more wheels than in 2002.
Quick check
?By what percentage did excess delay fall from 2002 (2.3 minutes per km) to 2003 (1.6 minutes per km)?
Mission 3

Why the benefit faded

  • TfL: road space was given to "pedestrian, cyclist and bus priority measures" and to street improvement schemes.
  • Road works also rose sharply, mainly "infrastructure replacement by the utility companies".
  • So capacity for cars fell: less traffic, but less road to share.
  • Exam move: judge success by more than one measure: traffic (lasting fall) versus congestion (gain gone by 2007).
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a two-lane main road becomes one car lane plus a bus lane. Car traffic falls by a tenth, but car journeys now take longer than before. How?

Congestion is traffic relative to capacity. London's charge cut traffic for good (21% fewer four-wheeled vehicles in 2007), but road space went to buses, cyclists and pedestrians and road works rose, so by 2007 congestion was back to 2002 levels.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Make a guess: Two neighbourhoods on two continents: both were demolished and redeveloped. Who decides what replaces a neighbourhood?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2B.3 evaluate one MEDC urban planning scheme regenerating inner-city housing, employment, transport and environment
Mission 4

Urban regeneration

  • Urban regeneration: improving a run-down urban area: new or improved homes, jobs, transport and environment.
  • Reasons: poor-quality housing, derelict land, few jobs, crime or a poor reputation, and rising land values nearby.
  • Decided by councils, developers and landowners, with residents consulted or not.
  • CCEA calls this an urban planning scheme.
Mission 4

The Heygate estate, London

  • A council estate at Elephant and Castle in Southwark, completed in 1974, originally housing about 3,000 people.
  • 1,212 homes: 1,020 council-owned and 192 privately owned.
  • A late-1990s survey found the buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
  • In a 1998 consultation, 96% of responses favoured a major regeneration scheme.
Mission 4

Why regenerate? Two views

  • Southwark Council: "the combination of housing management challenges and regeneration opportunities" justified demolition.
  • Some residents: "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • The redevelopment "has been the subject of a two-decade long controversy".
Quick check
?A survey found the Heygate buildings "structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment". What does this suggest?
Mission 4

Main features: Elephant Park

  • Demolition of the Heygate was completed in July 2014.
  • The plan approved in January 2013 included 3,000 new homes, 50 retail spaces, offices and public spaces, and a new energy centre.
  • "Nearly half of the new development is public space, including one of central London's largest new parks".
  • Mature trees on the estate were kept where possible.
Mission 4

Housing: who gained?

  • In 2005, all 595 remaining council tenants were given top priority to move; 99% chose to stay in Southwark, and all were offered the right to return.
  • Leaseholders were offered compensation under the statutory code.
  • After the 2008 financial crisis, contracts were renegotiated in 2010: affordable housing rose from 11% to 25%.
  • Across London, 50 estates were regenerated in a decade: total homes almost doubled, but there was a net loss of some 8,000 social rented homes.
Quick check
?If 25% of Elephant Park's 3,000 new homes were affordable, how many is that, and how does it compare with the 1,020 council homes on the Heygate?
Mission 4

Employment

  • New jobs: 50 retail spaces and office space in the plan.
  • In 2016 the developer, Lendlease, opened a Construction Skills Centre at Elephant Park. It "aims to train 1,000 local residents annually".
  • Southwark Council says it "has already helped nearly a thousand people find work on the construction site".
  • Building jobs end when building ends; shop and office jobs last longer.
Mission 4

Transport and environment

  • Transport before: the Heygate "was initially designed for car owners", with elevated walkways ("streets in the sky").
  • Transport after: the scheme "aims to better connect the community with surrounding areas and make it more pedestrian-friendly". This is an aim, not a measured result.
  • Environment: a large new park and public spaces, mature trees kept, and a new energy centre.
Mission 4

Evaluating the scheme

  • Housing: 3,000 new homes, but most are not affordable (25% affordable), and tenants had to move out first. Southwark says that by 2025 at least 1,693 new affordable homes would have been built in the wider Elephant and Castle area since 2001.
  • Employment: new shops, offices and training; some jobs only last while building goes on.
  • Transport: better walking routes planned; little measured evidence in the sources.
  • Environment: clear gains: park, trees, energy centre.
  • Exam move: "Evaluate" means judge each aim, say who gained and who lost, and give an overall verdict.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest criticism of the scheme?
Mission 4

A contrast: Otodo-Gbame, Lagos

Amnesty International reports:

  • Over 30,000 residents of this waterfront community were forcibly evicted in three operations between November 2016 and April 2017.
  • All 75 evictees it interviewed said they had received no prior written notice.
  • The evictions went ahead despite court orders telling the state to wait.
  • A luxury housing estate is being built on part of the land and water area.
  • The government gave varying reasons, from denying responsibility to preventing crime.
Mission 4

So who decides?

  • Power over land: governments, landowners and developers make the key decisions.
  • Residents' influence depends on protections: secure tenure, rehousing rights, compensation, court orders that are obeyed.
  • Resident ballots: since 18 July 2018, landlords seeking Greater London Authority (GLA) funding for estate regeneration that demolishes social homes must win a residents' ballot.
Mission check

?In a made-up city, a council and a developer plan to replace an old market and rented flats beside a new station. The tenants have short leases and there is no ballot. Who is most likely to decide what replaces the neighbourhood?

Power over land decides. At the Heygate, the council redeveloped amid two decades of controversy, with rehousing priority for tenants; in Lagos, Amnesty reports evictions of over 30,000 people without notice. Since 2018, London requires resident ballots for GLA-funded schemes that demolish social homes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2B.3 location, rapid growth and characteristics of shanty towns in one LEDC city (Mumbai)
Mission 5

Mumbai: a fast-growing city

  • Mumbai is on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea.
  • Census 2011: 12,442,373 people in Greater Mumbai.
  • The UN, counting the whole built-up city, gives 15.95 million in 2000 and 20.20 million in 2025: a rise of about 4.25 million in 25 years (our calculation).
  • In 2025 it was the most densely populated city of a million or more in the world: about 27,000 people per km².
  • India is an LEDC, so Mumbai is an LEDC city.
Mission 5

Why people move to Mumbai

  • Cities grow by natural increase (more births than deaths) and migration.
  • Push from rural areas: low farm incomes, few jobs, drought, poor services.
  • Pull to the city: jobs, wages, schools and hospitals.
  • Since the 1960s, M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
Mission 5

Slums: unplanned housing

  • A slum is crowded housing that lacks basic services such as water, toilets and drains; much of it is built without permission.
  • Census 2011: 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • Praja Foundation, citing the same census, gives 41.84%.
  • CCEA calls these areas shanty towns.
Mission 5

Where the slums are

  • Slums grow on land others do not want or cannot use: beside railways, on marshy or flood-prone ground, next to landfill, at the city edge.
  • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%).
  • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%).
  • M/East contains "Asia's second largest and oldest open land-fill".
  • Formal housing dominates where land is most valuable.
Quick check
?Two sources, both using the 2011 census, give Mumbai's slum share as 52.5% and 41.84%. What is the best explanation?
Mission 5

Life in M/East

Praja Foundation, citing a 2017 study by the charity Apnalaya:

  • The landfill still receives about 4,500 tonnes of garbage a day.
  • Infant mortality: 66 per 1,000 live births, against a national average of 41.
  • Average age at death: 39.
  • Air and groundwater are contaminated; basic amenities "are conspicuous by their absence".
  • Average family income in its slum clusters: Rs 7,802 a month.
Quick check
?Which M/East figure is the clearest sign of poor health and living conditions?
Mission 5

Challenges of rapid growth

  • Housing: about half the city lived in slums at the 2011 census.
  • Water, sanitation and energy: in M/East, families won legal water and electricity connections only after organising; many homes share or lack toilets.
  • Waste and pollution: about 4,500 tonnes a day to M/East's open landfill; air and groundwater contaminated.
  • Services and transport: schools, clinics, roads and trains struggle to keep up with demand.
  • Jobs: high unemployment and informal work (jobs with no contract, sick pay or pension).
Mission check

?In a made-up city, Ward A sits by the port and business district, where land is expensive. Ward B is at the edge, beside the city's landfill; people cleared from central slums were resettled there, and new migrants keep arriving. Which ward will have the higher slum share, and why?

Land values and history explain the gap: D ward 10.0% in slums, M/East 84.9%. Cheap marginal land beside a landfill, resettlement from inner-city slums and new migrants concentrated slums in M/East.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cities have four broad zones: CBD, inner city, suburbs and rural–urban fringe. Accessibility, land values and locational needs explain where each land use goes.
  • OS map symbols and aerial-photo clues (building size, density, roofs, green space) reveal a settlement's functions and zones.

Mission 2

  • MEDC inner cities: poor-quality housing (e.g. the Heygate) and gentrification, which brings investment but raises rents.
  • Cultural mix: diversity, with language barriers and, in places, ethnic and religious tensions (e.g. Belfast's interface barriers).

Mission 3

  • Congestion depends on traffic and road capacity. It lengthens journeys and worsens air quality. Public transport (cost, efficiency) and parking (cost, availability) shape how many people drive.
  • London's congestion charge cut traffic, but congestion returned as road space was reallocated.

Mission 4

  • Regeneration replaces run-down areas with new homes, jobs and spaces. The Heygate became Elephant Park: 3,000 homes, a large park and new shops, but fewer affordable homes than the council homes it replaced.
  • Those with power over land decide; residents' protections (tenure, rehousing rights, ballots) shape who benefits.

Mission 5

  • Mumbai grew fast through migration and natural increase. About half its people lived in slums in 2011, concentrated where land was cheap and marginal, as in M/East.
  • Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, services) and challenges (housing, water, waste, pollution, informal work).
