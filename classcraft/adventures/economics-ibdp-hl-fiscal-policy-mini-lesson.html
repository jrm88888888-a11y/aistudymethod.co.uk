Mission 1

Mission 1: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Make a guess: If a government spends 1 billion, why can the country's income end up growing by much more?

Mission 1

One injection, many rounds

  • The government spends 100 (million) on a new bridge. An injection.
  • Builders earn 100. They spend some of it and withdraw the rest (saving, tax, imports).
  • Illustrative data: 80% of each extra unit of income is spent on domestic output.
  • Round 1: 100 → round 2: 80 → round 3: 64 → round 4: 51.2 → …
  • Each person's spending is someone else's income.
  • The rounds add up to 500: national income rises by 5 times the injection.
  • Multiplier effect: a change in an injection causes a larger final change in national income.
  • It works in reverse too: a cut in an injection reduces income by more than the cut.
Mission 1Graph lab

Re-spend more

  • Takeaway: the more of each round is re-spent, the bigger the multiplier.
Mission 1

The multiplier formula

  • Marginal propensity to consume (MPC): the share of each extra unit of income that is spent. MPC = ΔC ÷ ΔY. On the first screen of this mission it was 0.8.
  • Marginal propensity to save (MPS): the share of extra income saved.
  • Multiplier (k) = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • Closed economy, no government: 1 − MPC = MPS, so k = 1 ÷ MPS.
  • Final change in national income = k × change in injection.
  • Example: MPC = 0.8. k = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. A rise of 100 in investment → national income rises by 500.
  • Say: "real national income rises by k times the injection" — not: "the injection is multiplied at once". It takes many rounds.
Quick check
?In a closed economy with no government, the MPC is 0.75. Investment rises by 40 billion. If there is spare capacity, by how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 1

Saving, tax and imports: MPW

  • Out of each extra unit of national income, three withdrawals: saving, tax, imports.
  • MPS, MPT, MPM: the shares of extra income saved, paid in tax and spent on imports (marginal propensities to save, tax and import).
  • MPW = MPS + MPT + MPM. Here MPC = the share spent on domestic output, so MPC + MPW = 1.
  • k = 1 ÷ MPW = 1 ÷ (MPS + MPT + MPM).
  • Illustrative data: MPS = 0.1, MPT = 0.2, MPM = 0.2.
EconomyWithdrawalsk
Closed, no governmentMPS = 0.11 ÷ 0.1 = 10
Closed, with government0.1 + 0.2 = 0.31 ÷ 0.3 = 3.33
Open, with government0.1 + 0.2 + 0.2 = 0.51 ÷ 0.5 = 2
  • Exports rise by 30 billion in the open economy: national income rises by 2 × 30 = 60 billion.
  • More withdrawals → a smaller multiplier.
Quick check
?An open economy has MPS = 0.15, MPT = 0.25 and MPM = 0.1 (illustrative data). Government spending rises by 20 billion. By how much does national income finally rise?
Mission 1

The multiplier on the AD/AS diagram

  • Step 1: an injection rises (say G). AD shifts right by the first-round amount: AD1 → AD2.
  • Step 2: the multiplier adds further rounds: AD2 → AD3.
  • Step 3: with upward-sloping AS, part of the extra demand raises the price level. Real output rises by less than the full shift.
  • Near full capacity, most of the effect is on prices, not real output.
Mission 1

How big in reality?

  • Real-world multipliers are estimated, not observed. Estimates vary widely.
  • IMF (2012): forecasts for 2010–11 had assumed fiscal multipliers of about 0.5. Its own estimate for the years after the 2008 crisis was higher: about 0.9 to 1.7.
  • Multipliers tend to be larger in deep recessions with spare capacity.
  • They tend to be smaller near full capacity, in very open economies, or if interest rates rise in response.
  • Economists still disagree on the size: it matters for debates on stimulus and austerity.
Mission 1Evaluate

How strong is the multiplier?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: near full capacity, extra demand mostly raises prices.
  • Withdrawals: the more of each round that is saved, taxed or spent on imports, the smaller the multiplier.
  • Confidence: worried households may save extra income.
  • Time lags: the rounds take months to work through.
  • Crowding out: government borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment.
  • Size: a small injection in a large economy barely shows.
Quick check
?A large car factory closes, and investment in the region falls sharply. What happens to national income, other things equal?
Mission check

?Two economies get the same rise in government spending. In Economy X, households spend much of any extra income on imports. In Economy Y, they buy mostly home-produced goods. Saving and tax rates are the same. Which statement is correct?

The more of each round that is withdrawn (saving, tax, imports), the smaller the multiplier.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Make a guess: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Mission 2

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 2

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
Mission 2

Types of tax

  • Direct tax: on income or wealth, paid straight to the government (income tax, corporation tax).
  • Indirect tax: on spending, collected by sellers (VAT, excise duties).
  • Illustrative data, two people earning 20,000 and 80,000 a year:
TypeLower earner paysHigher earner paysShare of income
Progressive2,00020,00010% → 25%: rises with income

Regressive3,0006,00015% → 7.5%: falls with income
  • Indirect taxes are often regressive: lower earners spend a larger share of income.
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a smaller share of a higher income" — not: "the rich pay less tax".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a worker earning 30,000 a year pays 3,000 in tax. A manager earning 60,000 a year pays 6,000. What kind of tax is this?
Mission 2

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a deflationary (recessionary) gap: real output and employment rise; the price level rises a little.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close an inflationary gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes (see Mission 1).

  • Takeaway: because of the multiplier, a rise in spending smaller than the gap can close it, if prices stay steady.

Mission 2

Two views: Keynesian vs monetarist and new classical

  • Keynesian view: AS is flat when there is lots of spare capacity.
    • In a deep recession, expansionary fiscal policy raises real output and jobs with little inflation.
    • Near full capacity, it mainly raises the price level.
  • Monetarist / new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output.
    • In the short run, more G raises output and the price level.
    • In the long run, wages and costs adjust: SRAS shifts left. Output returns to potential, at a higher price level.
    • Government borrowing may push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • So the two views disagree on how far fiscal policy can raise output for long.
Mission 2

Fiscal policy on the supply side

  • Some fiscal measures also shift LRAS right:
    • capital spending on infrastructure, broadband, transport
    • spending on education, training and health
    • tax incentives for investment or research
  • These raise productive capacity: more output with less pressure on prices.
  • But they take years, and the first effect is on AD (spending now).
  • The same works the other way: supply-side policies that involve spending also raise AD in the short run.
Mission 2

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilisers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
  • Political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; tax rises and spending cuts are not.
  • Sustainable debt: high debt raises interest costs and may limit room for future stimulus.
Mission 2

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • UK 2008–09: VAT was cut from 17.5% to 15% from December 2008 to December 2009, to support spending during the recession. Its effect is debated: some spending was brought forward rather than added.
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary (see Mission 1).
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?
Mission 2Evaluate

How effective is fiscal policy?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, extra demand raises output; near capacity it mostly raises prices.
  • Size of the multiplier: high saving or imports weaken the effect.
  • Time lags: stimulus can arrive too late.
  • Confidence and debt: if people expect future tax rises to repay debt, they may save more. If markets doubt debt is sustainable, interest rates may rise.
  • Crowding out: borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment (stronger near full capacity).
Mission check

?A government spots a recession and plans a large spending programme. It takes 18 months to pass and start, and by then the economy is already recovering strongly. What is the main risk?

Fiscal policy can lift a slump, but its effect depends on timing and on how much spare capacity is left.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Make a guess: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Mission 3

Competing for the same savings

  • To finance a deficit, the government borrows: it sells bonds to savers, banks and pension funds.
  • Firms also borrow to invest. Both compete for the same pool of saving.
  • More government borrowing can push up interest rates and so reduce private investment.
  • Crowding out: an increase in government spending or borrowing that reduces private sector spending.
Mission 3

The loanable funds market

  • Supply of loanable funds: from saving. Higher real interest rates encourage more saving.
  • Demand for loanable funds: from borrowers: firms' investment plus government borrowing.
  • Illustrative data (billion): at a real interest rate of 4%, saving = private investment = 500.
  • The government borrows an extra 100. Demand shifts right by 100: D1 → D2.
  • New equilibrium: real interest rate 5%, total lending 550.
  • At 5%, private investment falls to 450. 50 is crowded out.
  • The other 50 comes from extra saving drawn out by the higher rate.
  • Say: "private investment falls" — not: "the government takes firms' money". The higher price of borrowing does the work.

  • Takeaway: the less saving responds to interest rates, the more crowding out.

Mission 3

Short run and long run

Short run:

  • A higher real interest rate cuts interest-sensitive spending: business investment, new homes, cars bought on credit.
  • This offsets part of the rise in AD from the extra government spending.

Long run:

  • Less private investment → the capital stock grows more slowly.
  • So productive capacity grows more slowly: a smaller rightward shift of LRAS (the PPC grows less).
  • Exception: if the government borrows to build public capital (roads, broadband, training), total capital may still grow.
Quick check
?At full employment, a government increases its deficit spending, financed by borrowing. Saving responds only slightly to interest rates. Which is the most likely long-run effect?
Mission 3

Debt, interest and credit ratings

  • A large debt means large interest payments every year. That money cannot pay for schools, health or lower taxes: debt interest crowds out other public spending.
  • If lenders worry about repayment, they demand a higher yield on government bonds. Agencies may downgrade the credit rating.
  • Government bond yields are a benchmark: when they rise, firms' and households' borrowing costs tend to rise too, deepening crowding out.
  • Example: Greece's rating was cut to "junk" in April 2010, and its borrowing costs soared.
Mission 3

When crowding out is small, or turns to crowding in

Crowding out is smaller when:

  • The economy is in a deep recession: spare capacity, high saving, weak private demand for loans. Extra borrowing raises interest rates very little.
  • The central bank holds interest rates down (rate cuts, QE).
  • Foreign lenders supply funds at little extra cost.

Crowding in (a Keynesian view): government spending raises incomes and sales. Firms then invest more to meet demand (the accelerator).

  • Public capital (roads, research, broadband) can make private investment more profitable.
  • Example: Japan's gross government debt is over 200% of GDP, yet its 10-year bond yield was held near 0% for several years from 2016. The Bank of Japan's bond buying explains part of this.
Mission 3Evaluate

How big is crowding out?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: large near full employment; small in a deep recession.
  • Saving and investment responses: if saving barely responds to interest rates, or investment is very sensitive to them, crowding out is larger.
  • Monetary policy and openness: a central bank holding rates down, or inflows of foreign saving, reduce it.
  • What the money buys: public capital can crowd in private investment; spending on current items is less likely to.
  • Confidence: if high debt makes lenders nervous, yields rise and crowding out grows.
  • The evidence is contested. Monetarist and new classical economists stress crowding out; Keynesian economists stress crowding in during recessions.
Quick check
?In which situation is crowding out likely to be smallest?
Mission check

?An economy is at full employment. The government cuts its budget deficit, so it borrows 100 billion less each year. The supply of loanable funds slopes upward. What happens in the loanable funds market?

Crowding out works in reverse: less government borrowing lowers the real interest rate and lets private investment rise, by less than the cut.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Make a guess: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Mission 4

Two ways the budget changes

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending. E.g. a budget that cuts income tax or funds new roads.
  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and transfer spending that happen on their own as the economy moves through the trade cycle (also called the business cycle). No new decision is needed.
  • Tax rates and benefit rules stay the same. What changes is how much income, spending and unemployment there is.
  • IB: the two main automatic stabilisers are progressive taxes and unemployment benefits.
Mission 4

How stabilisers work

In a recession:

  • Incomes fall → income tax paid falls. Spending falls → sales taxes (VAT) collected fall.
  • With a progressive tax, tax falls proportionally more than income.
  • Unemployment rises → unemployment benefit payments rise.
  • So disposable income falls by less than income. Consumption, and AD, fall by less.

Illustrative data:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first 10,000 · 20% from 10,000 to 50,000 · 40% above 50,000.
  • Income falls from 60,000 to 40,000 (−33%). Tax falls from 12,000 to 6,000 (−50%).
  • In a boom, the reverse: tax rises faster than income and benefit spending falls. This slows the rise in AD.
  • Say: "automatic stabilisers reduce the size of fluctuations" — not: "they stop recessions".

Mission 4

Stabilisers and the deficit

  • Lower tax revenue + higher benefit spending = a bigger deficit in a recession, with no new decision.
  • In a boom the deficit shrinks (or a surplus grows) automatically.
  • 2008–09 example: UK borrowing rose to about 10% of GDP in 2009–10. Part was automatic (falling tax receipts, rising benefit claims). Part was discretionary, e.g. a temporary VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% (December 2008 to December 2009).
Mission 4

Automatic vs discretionary: strengths and limits

Automatic stabilisers:

  • Fast: no time lag for recognising the problem or passing a law.
  • Work in both directions: they cushion recessions and cool booms.
  • But partial: they reduce fluctuations; they do not remove them.

Discretionary policy:

  • Can be larger and targeted (e.g. at infrastructure or at low-income households).
  • But faces time lags: recognising the problem, deciding, then putting it into effect. It may arrive after recovery has begun.
  • Can face political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; reversing them in a boom is not.
Mission 4Evaluate

How much can stabilisers do?

It depends on:

  • Progressivity and benefits: steeper tax rates and fuller unemployment benefits give a bigger cushion. Countries with larger public sectors tend to have stronger stabilisers.
  • Size of the shock: in a deep recession stabilisers cushion only part of the fall; discretionary policy may be needed too.
  • Debt and rules: high debt or strict rules on the actual deficit can force governments to work against the stabilisers.
  • Incentives: high marginal tax rates and generous benefits may reduce incentives to work, a long-run cost to weigh.
  • Confidence: if households save rather than spend, even well-designed support raises AD less.
Mission check

?Country A has a progressive income tax and pays unemployment benefits. Country B charges every adult the same fixed amount of tax and pays no unemployment benefits. Both suffer the same fall in exports. Neither government takes any new decision. Which statement is correct?

Automatic stabilisers cushion AD in a downturn, and the price is a larger cyclical deficit.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Make a guess: How can a government raise the most its economy can produce, and why does it take so long?

Mission 5

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 5

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Quick check
?Which policy is most clearly aimed at the supply side of the economy?
Mission 5

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.

  • Market-based: free markets and incentives work better with fewer barriers.
  • Interventionist: markets under-provide some things (positive externalities, public goods), so the government steps in.
Mission 5

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 5

Labour market diagrams

  • Minimum wage: set above the equilibrium wage, labour supplied exceeds labour demanded: unemployment (Ls − Ld).
  • Removing or lowering it: employment moves towards Le. Evidence on how many jobs this adds is mixed.
  • Benefit reform and flexibility: more people seek work at each wage, so labour supply shifts right: employment rises.
Mission 5

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 5

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.

Mission 5

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 5Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
  • Budget, equity, environment. Tax cuts and spending both strain the government budget; looser rules may weaken worker or environmental protection.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An injection raises national income by more than itself, through rounds of spending.
  • k = 1 ÷ (1 − MPC).
  • With saving, tax and imports: k = 1 ÷ MPW.

Mission 2

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.
  • Crowding out may limit it too.

Mission 3

  • Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and the real interest rate; private investment falls.
  • Crowding out is usually partial: higher rates also draw out extra saving.
  • Short run: interest-sensitive spending falls. Long run: slower capital accumulation and growth.
  • It is smaller in a deep recession, and public capital can crowd in private investment.

Mission 4

  • Discretionary policy = a deliberate decision. Automatic stabilisers = tax and transfers that change with the cycle, with no decision.
  • In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: AD falls by less, the deficit rises.

Mission 5

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.
