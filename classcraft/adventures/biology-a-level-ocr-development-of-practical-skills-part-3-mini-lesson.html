Mission 1

Mission 1: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Make a guess: How can you measure the length of a cell you can only see through a microscope?

Mission 1

Cell theory

  • Cell theory: all living organisms are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the smallest unit that carries out all the processes of life.
  • New cells arise only from existing cells, by division.
Mission 1

Units for tiny things

  • 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres).
  • 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres).
  • mm → µm: × 1000. µm → nm: × 1000.
  • Whole cells: µm. Organelle detail, viruses, molecules: nm.
  • A red blood cell is about 7.5 µm across; a ribosome about 25 nm.
  • Say: "1 mm = 1000 µm" — not: "1 mm = 100 µm".
Mission 1

The magnification formula

  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size
  • Rearrange: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Rearrange: image size = actual size × magnification.
  • Put both sizes in the same unit first.
  • Magnification has no unit: write ×2000.
  • Your specification writes it: magnification = image size / object size.
  • Check: the actual size must be smaller than the image. If yours is bigger, you multiplied.
Mission 1

Worked example: size and magnification

Actual size of a chloroplast in a micrograph at ×4000. The image is 24 mm long.

  • Convert: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
  • Actual size = 24 000 ÷ 4000 = 6 µm.

Magnification of a mitochondrion drawn 45 mm long. Its actual length is 1.5 µm.

  • Convert: 45 mm = 45 000 µm.
  • Magnification = 45 000 ÷ 1.5 = ×30 000.
Quick check
?In a photomicrograph taken at a magnification of ×2000, a red blood cell measures 15 mm across. What is the actual width of the cell?
Mission 1

Scale bars

  • A scale bar is a line on an image labelled with the actual length it represents.
  • Magnification = measured bar length ÷ bar label (same units).
  • Example: a bar 20 mm long labelled 5 µm. 20 000 µm ÷ 5 µm = ×4000.
  • Then use the formula for anything else in that image.
  • To draw a bar: drawn length = label × magnification.
  • Say: "measure the bar with a ruler" — not: "the label is the bar's length".
Mission 1

Practical: calibrating an eyepiece graticule

  • Eyepiece graticule: a scale in the eyepiece. Its divisions have no fixed size.
  • Stage micrometer: a slide with a real scale, often 1 mm in 100 divisions of 10 µm.
  • Line up the two scales. Example at the ×10 objective: 25 graticule divisions = 250 µm. So 1 division = 10 µm.
  • Change the objective, recalibrate. At ×40: 10 divisions = 25 µm. So 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Measure: a cell spans 18 divisions at ×40. 18 × 2.5 µm = 45 µm.
  • PAG1 (Microscopy): use an eye piece graticule and stage micrometer.
Quick check
?With the ×40 objective lens in place, 40 divisions of an eyepiece graticule line up exactly with 100 µm on a stage micrometer. Using the same objective, a cell spans 44 graticule divisions. What is the actual length of the cell in millimetres?
Mission 1

Making and viewing a slide

  • Temporary mount: a thin layer of cells (e.g. onion epidermis) in a drop of water or stain; lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
  • Stains add contrast, not detail. Iodine stains starch blue-black; methylene blue stains cells blue, nuclei most strongly.
  • Focus at low power first, with the coarse then the fine adjustment.
  • Switch to high power. Refocus with the fine adjustment only.
  • Say: "the stain increases contrast" — not: "the stain makes the image sharper".
Mission 1

Scientific drawings and low-power plans

  • Sharp pencil; single, continuous lines; no shading or colouring.
  • Draw what you see, in correct proportions; large enough to fill half the page.
  • Label lines ruled, not crossing; labels outside the drawing. Annotations add notes on function.
  • Low-power plan: tissue outlines only, no individual cells.
  • Drawing magnification = drawing size ÷ actual size. Example: a privet leaf 300 µm thick (30 divisions × 10 µm) drawn 90 mm thick: 90 000 ÷ 300 = ×300.
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a pollen grain 60 mm across. A scale bar on the micrograph is 20 mm long and is labelled 10 µm. What is the actual diameter of the pollen grain?

You never measure the cell directly. You measure the image, then use a calibrated scale (graticule or scale bar) to convert it.

Mission 2

Magnification is not resolution

  • Magnification: how many times larger the image is than the object.
  • Resolution: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate.
  • Resolution is limited by the wavelength used. Visible light (about 400–700 nm) gives a limit of about 200 nm.
  • Electrons have a far shorter wavelength, so electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.
  • Enlarging past the resolution limit gives a bigger blur, not more detail.
  • Say: "higher resolution shows more detail" — not: "higher magnification shows more detail".
Quick check
?Ribosomes are about 25 nm across. They cannot be seen with a light microscope but are clearly visible in electron micrographs. What is the reason?
Mission 2

Three microscopes, three ways to make an image

  • Light (optical) microscope: light passes through a thin specimen; glass lenses focus it.
  • Transmission electron microscope (TEM): a beam of electrons passes through a very thin section. Electromagnets focus it.
  • In a TEM, parts stained with heavy metals scatter electrons and look dark. The image is a flat slice of internal structure.
  • Scanning electron microscope (SEM): an electron beam is scanned across the surface of a specimen coated in metal.
  • In an SEM, electrons given off or scattered back from the surface build a 3D image of the surface only.

  • Colours on electron micrographs are false colours added by computer.

Mission 2

Reading micrographs

  • Light micrograph (photomicrograph): coloured by stains; whole cells and tissues. Only structures larger than about 0.2 µm, such as nuclei and chloroplasts.
  • TEM micrograph: a flat slice. Shows membranes inside organelles, e.g. the folds inside a mitochondrion.
  • SEM micrograph: a 3D-looking surface with shadows; no internal detail.
  • First find the scale bar or the magnification.
  • Next missions: naming the organelles you see.
Mission 2

Laser scanning confocal microscope

  • The specimen is stained with fluorescent dyes.
  • A laser scans the specimen point by point; the dyes give out light.
  • A pinhole blocks light from out-of-focus layers, so each image is a sharp, thin optical section.
  • Sections at different depths are combined into a 3D image.
  • Works on living cells and thick specimens. Resolution is still limited by light: close to a light microscope's.
Mission 2

Differential staining

  • Differential staining uses two or more stains, so different components or cell types take up different colours.
  • Gram stain: crystal violet, then a counterstain (safranin). Gram-positive bacteria stay purple; Gram-negative bacteria look red or pink.
  • Haematoxylin and eosin (tissue sections): nuclei stain blue-purple; cytoplasm stains pink.
  • So one slide can separate cell types, or nuclei from cytoplasm, at a glance.
Mission check

?A virus particle is about 100 nm across. A student photographs a stained sample through a light microscope at ×1500, then enlarges the photo 20 times: a total magnification of ×30 000. At this magnification a 100 nm particle would be 3 mm across. Which conclusion is correct?

Enlarging an image never adds detail. Detail is set by resolution, and resolution is set by the wavelength used.

Mission 3

Rules for a scientific drawing

  • Sharp pencil. Clear, continuous single lines. No shading or colouring.
  • Draw only what you see, in the right proportions. Use most of the space.
  • Label lines: ruled, no arrowheads, touching the structure, never crossing.
  • Labels outside the drawing, aligned neatly.
  • Annotation = label + a short note: what the structure does, or a feature you can see.
  • Title: specimen, section (TS or LS), magnification or a scale bar.
Mission 3

Plan diagram or cell drawing?

  • Plan diagram (low power): outlines of tissue regions only. No individual cells.
  • Draw each tissue layer in the right proportion of the whole.
  • High-power drawing: a few representative cells, with the detail you see (walls, nuclei, chloroplasts).
  • Leaf TS, top to bottom: upper epidermis (cuticle) → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (air spaces) → lower epidermis (stomata).
  • In each vein: xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower.

Mission 3

Stem and root in section

  • Stem TS: epidermis → cortex → a ring of vascular bundles → pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: phloem on the outside, xylem on the inside.
  • A cap of sclerenchyma fibres sits outside the phloem: support.
  • Root TS: epidermis (root hairs) → wide cortex → a central vascular cylinder.
  • In the root, xylem forms a central star; phloem lies between its arms.

Mission 3Practical

Sections of a stem

  • PAG1 / PAG2: draw stained plant sections showing xylem and phloem; dissect stems lengthways and across to show xylem.
  • Cut thin transverse sections with a sharp blade, cutting away from you. Keep them in water.
  • Stain, e.g. toluidine blue: lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) turn blue-green; other walls purple.
  • Low power: plan diagram. High power: draw a few cells of each type.
  • Xylem vessels: wide, empty, lignified, inner side. Sclerenchyma: narrow lumens, thick walls, outside the phloem. Phloem: small, thin-walled cells between.
  • Stand a stem in dye solution, then cut across and along: the dyed strands are the xylem.
Mission 3

Worked example: magnification of a drawing

  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size, both in the same units.
  • A cell spans 12 eyepiece graticule divisions. At this objective, 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Step 1: actual length = 12 × 2.5 = 30 µm.
  • Step 2: the drawing is 60 mm long = 60 000 µm.
  • Step 3: magnification = 60 000 ÷ 30 = × 2000.
  • Scale bar instead: a 10 µm bar is drawn 10 × 2000 = 20 000 µm = 20 mm long.
  • Say: "draw a 10 µm scale bar at its magnified length" — not: "draw it 10 mm long".
Mission check

Leave out the cells, keep the proportions, and label with care: that is what turns a sketch into evidence.

Mission 4

Before you cut

  • Tools: scalpel, scissors, forceps, blunt seeker, mounted needle, dissecting board.
  • Cut away from yourself and your fingers, on the board.
  • Keep blades sharp: a blunt blade needs more force and slips.
  • Gloves on; wash hands afterwards; disinfect the board.
  • Blades into a sharps bin; tissue disposed of as instructed.
  • Ethics: organs usually come from a butcher, so no animal is killed for the lesson.
  • Anyone who objects can use models, photographs or video for the same observations.
Mission 4

Find your way round an organ

  • Orient first. Heart: the coronary artery runs diagonally across the front (ventral) surface.
  • Identify by structure, position and connections: not by how dark something looks.
  • Push a blunt seeker into each vessel. Which chamber does it lead to?
  • Artery: thick, elastic wall that stays open. Vein: thin wall that collapses flat.
  • Record: labelled drawing (see Mission 3) or photo with a ruler in view. Measure, e.g. wall thickness.
  • Say: "the left ventricle wall is thicker because it generates higher pressure to pump blood round the whole body" — not: "because it holds more blood".
Mission 4Practical

Dissections each board names

  • PAG2: mammalian heart; fish gills and/or insect tracheae; kidney; stems (in Mission 3).
  • Same routine every time: orient → identify → record → measure.
  • Organ detail: heart in a later lesson; gills and tracheae in a later lesson; kidney in a later lesson.
Quick check
?A student dissects a sheep's heart and finds that the wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 4

Reading a stained section

  • A section is a thin slice. A tube cut across (TS) looks like a ring; cut along (LS), two parallel strips (its walls).
  • A common stain, H&E: haematoxylin colours nuclei blue-purple; eosin colours cytoplasm and fibres pink.
  • Identify tissue by cell shape, the number and position of nuclei, arrangement and striations.
  • Stain colour alone does not identify a tissue.
  • Draw a plan first (see Mission 3); then a few cells at high power.
Mission 4

Three kinds of muscle

  • Skeletal: long, unbranched fibres; striated; many nuclei at the edge of each fibre.
  • Cardiac: branched cells joined by intercalated discs; striated; one or two central nuclei.
  • Smooth: spindle-shaped cells; no striations; one central nucleus.
  • Skeletal muscle detail (sarcomeres, sliding filaments): a later lesson.
Quick check
?A photomicrograph shows long, unbranched, cylindrical fibres with regular cross-striations. Each fibre has many nuclei, lying just beneath its surface. Which tissue is this?
Mission 4

Blood films

  • PAG1: examine and draw cells in blood smears.
  • Red blood cells: most numerous; no nucleus; pale centre (biconcave).
  • Neutrophil (polymorph): nucleus with 2–5 lobes.
  • Lymphocyte: a large round nucleus filling most of the cell.
  • Monocyte: the largest white cell; kidney-shaped nucleus.
  • Draw a few of each type at high power, to scale with the red cells.
Mission 4

Sections each board names

  • Liver: rows of hepatocytes running out from a central vein, with sinusoids between.
  • Kidney cortex: glomeruli inside Bowman's capsules, among sections of tubules.
  • Pancreas: pale-staining islets of Langerhans among darker-staining enzyme-secreting acini.
  • Exchange surfaces: alveoli lined by squamous epithelium; trachea with ciliated epithelium, goblet cells, cartilage.

Structure, position and connections, never shade or size alone: that is how you name what you find, whether cut open or sliced thin.

Mission 5

PCR: copy one stretch of DNA

  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies one chosen stretch of DNA, in a tube.
  • It needs:
    • the template DNA sample
    • two primers: short single strands of DNA
    • free DNA nucleotides
    • a heat-stable DNA polymerase
  • The usual enzyme is Taq polymerase.
  • The cycle repeats, about 25–35 times. Each cycle doubles the target.
Mission 5

One cycle, three steps

  • 1 Denature, about 95 °C. Hydrogen bonds between bases break. The strands separate.
  • 2 Anneal, about 55 °C. Primers bind by complementary base pairing to each end of the target, one on each strand.
  • 3 Extend, about 72 °C. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer, building new complementary strands.
  • The new strands become templates in the next cycle.
  • Say: "heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands" — not: "heat breaks the DNA into nucleotides".
Mission 5

Why primers? Why a heat-stable enzyme?

  • DNA polymerase can only add to an existing strand. Primers give it a start.
  • The two primers sit either side of the target. Only the DNA between them is amplified.
  • Primers also stop the separated strands re-pairing at once.
  • Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs.
  • It is not denatured at 95 °C, so it works through every cycle. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first cycle.
  • 72 °C is close to Taq's optimum temperature.
  • Say: "primers anneal to complementary sequences" — not: "primers copy the DNA" or "primers separate the strands".
Quick check
?Why is Taq polymerase, rather than a DNA polymerase from human cells, used in the polymerase chain reaction?
Mission 5

Worked example: how many copies?

  • Every molecule is copied in every cycle, so the number doubles each cycle.
  • Copies after n cycles = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Example: 1 molecule, 30 cycles.
    • 1 × 2³⁰ = 1 073 741 824
    • About 1.1 × 10⁹ copies: over a billion.
  • Example: 5 molecules, 3 cycles: 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 5 × 2³ = 40.
  • This assumes every strand is copied each cycle. In a real tube, copying slows in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.
  • Say: "multiply by 2ⁿ" — not: "multiply by 2 × n".
Quick check
?A PCR reaction starts with 3 double-stranded DNA molecules of the target section. How many double-stranded molecules of the target are present after 4 complete cycles?
Mission 5

Gel electrophoresis: sorting by length

  • DNA samples go into wells at one end of a gel. A voltage is applied across the gel.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups). Every fragment moves towards the positive electrode.
  • The gel is a mesh. Shorter fragments slip through more easily and travel further.
  • Fragments of the same length travel the same distance: they form one band.
  • A ladder of fragments of known length runs in one lane, for comparison.
  • The same method separates proteins in a gel.
  • Say: "shorter fragments travel further" — not: "longer fragments travel further because they carry more charge".
Quick check
?Three DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1200 base pairs are loaded into the same well of a gel and a voltage is applied. Which statement describes the result?
Mission 5Practical

Running a DNA gel

  • PAG6: separation of biological compounds by electrophoresis.
  • Method:
    • Cast an agarose gel with a comb; remove the comb to leave wells.
    • Cover the gel with buffer. Wells at the negative end.
    • Load each sample with a dense loading dye. Load a ladder in one lane.
    • Run at a set voltage until the dye nears the far end. Stain to show the bands.
  • Keep the same for every lane: gel, voltage, run time. The ladder is the reference.
  • Measure each band's distance from its well. Compare with the ladder to estimate length.

Mission 5

What PCR and gels are used for

  • Forensics: amplify DNA from a hair, a drop of blood or skin cells, then compare it (see Mission 6).
  • Paternity and relationships: compare amplified regions between people, animals or plants.
  • Diagnosing infections: detect a pathogen's DNA or RNA, even at low levels.
  • Genetic testing: amplify a gene, then test it for a particular allele.
  • Ancient and degraded DNA: amplify the little that survives.
  • Before sequencing: make enough copies of a gene to read its base sequence.

Mission 6

Repeats that vary between people

  • Much of the genome does not code for proteins. Some of it is tandem repeats: a short sequence repeated end to end, e.g. GATA GATA GATA.
  • Forensic profiles use short tandem repeats (STRs): units of 2–6 bases.
  • The number of repeats at a locus varies between people. More repeats: a longer fragment.
  • You inherit one allele at each locus from each parent: one or two bands per locus.
  • Many loci, many alleles at each: the chance that two people share all of them is very low. Identical twins are the exception.
Mission 6

Making a profile

  • The method is called DNA profiling.
  • 1 Extract DNA from the sample.
  • 2 Amplify several repeat loci by PCR. Primers bind either side of each repeat.
  • 3 Separate the fragments by length: electrophoresis.
  • 4 Detect the bands: labelled primers or labelled probes make them visible. Compare samples.
Quick check
?Two people have different alleles at one short tandem repeat locus. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, their bands for this locus are in different places. What difference between the alleles does the gel detect?
Mission 6

Worked example: why test many loci?

  • Illustrative: suppose 1 in 10 unrelated people share a profile at one locus by chance.
  • Loci on different chromosomes are inherited independently. Multiply the chances.
  • 2 loci: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1 in 100.
  • 10 loci: (1/10)¹⁰ = 1 in 10¹⁰: about one person in 10 000 000 000.
  • Real casework: the UK system tests 16 repeat loci; the US core set has 20.
  • Relatives share more alleles, so their chance of matching is higher.
  • Say: "the chance of a coincidental match is very low" — not: "the profile proves the suspect did it".
Quick check
?A forensic laboratory compares a sample from a crime scene with a suspect's DNA using 20 different markers rather than one. Why does this make the result more reliable?
Mission 6

Reading a profile

  • Forensic match: the sample could come from a person only if every band matches.
  • One band different: that person is excluded.
  • Paternity: each band in a child came from the mother or the father.
    • Cross out the child's bands that match the mother.
    • Every band left must be in the father's profile.
    • The father has other bands the child did not inherit: that is expected.
  • Matching bands are consistent with a relationship. Different bands exclude one.
Quick check
?A DNA profile was prepared for a mother, her child and a man who may be the father. The fragment lengths (in base pairs) in each profile were: mother 100, 220, 260, 340; child 100, 150, 220, 300; man 150, 180, 300, 330. What can be concluded?
Mission 6

Uses: identity and relationships

  • Forensics: link a sample from a scene to a person, or exclude a suspect.
  • Paternity and family relationships; identifying human remains.
  • Animals and plants: confirm the parents of a pedigree animal; identify a crop variety; trace the source of illegally traded wildlife.
Mission 6

Uses: health and populations

  • Some repeats cause disease when they are too long. In Huntington's disease, a CAG repeat in one gene is measured by PCR and electrophoresis:
    • 26 or fewer repeats: normal; 40 or more: the disease develops.
  • Markers close to a disease allele are inherited with it: they help estimate disease risk in a family.

Mission 7

Chemical messengers from the tips

  • Plants coordinate growth with chemical signals: plant hormones.
  • Made in growing regions (shoot and root tips); they move to other tissues and act there.
  • No glands, no blood: they move from cell to cell or in the vascular tissue.
  • The best known: auxin. The main natural auxin is IAA (indoleacetic acid).
  • A tropism is a directional growth response to a directional stimulus: towards it = positive; away from it = negative.
  • Range of tropisms: phototropism (light), geotropism (gravity), hydrotropism (water), chemotropism (chemicals), thigmotropism (touch).
Mission 7

Auxin makes cells longer

  • IAA acts in the region just behind the tip.
  • It promotes cell elongation, not cell division.
  • IAA stimulates proton pumps: H⁺ ions are secreted into the cell wall (the apoplast).
  • The wall's pH falls. Cross-links between cellulose molecules loosen.
  • The cell takes up water by osmosis; turgor pressure stretches the loosened wall.
  • Say: "the cross-links loosen and the wall stretches" — not: "the acid dissolves the cellulose".
  • More elongation on one side than the other → the organ bends.
Quick check
?Which statement describes how auxin promotes the elongation of a plant cell?
Mission 7

Bending towards the light

  • Light from one side (lateral light) is detected at the shoot tip.
  • IAA is redistributed to the shaded side. It is not destroyed by light.
  • Shaded-side cells elongate more than lit-side cells.
  • Unequal growth bends the shoot towards the light: positive phototropism.
  • Takes hours to days: it is growth, not movement.
  • Say: "the shaded side elongates more" — not: "the lit side grows towards the light".
Quick check
?A seedling is grown in a box with a window on its left side only. After two days the shoot has curved towards the window. Which statement explains the curvature?
Mission 7

Roots and shoots respond in opposite ways

  • Root cells are far more sensitive to IAA than shoot cells.
  • A concentration that stimulates shoot cells inhibits root cells.
  • Seedling on its side: IAA collects on the lower side of both shoot and root.
  • Shoot: lower side elongates more → bends up: negative gravitropism.
  • Root: lower side elongation inhibited → upper side grows more → bends down: positive gravitropism.
  • OCR calls this geotropism.
  • Roots in lateral light: IAA moves to the shaded side, inhibits elongation → root bends away from light: negative phototropism.

  • Shoot growth is promoted over a range where root growth is inhibited.
  • Positive = promoted; negative = inhibited (not "shrinks").
Quick check
?A seedling is laid on its side. IAA builds up on the lower side of both its root and its shoot, reaching about 10⁻⁶ mol dm⁻³. The upper sides have about 10⁻¹⁰ mol dm⁻³. Using the table on the last screen, how will the root and shoot respond?
Mission 7Practical

IAA concentration and root growth (OCR PAG11)

  • Serial dilution: 1 cm³ of 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ IAA + 9 cm³ distilled water → 10⁻⁴; repeat down to 10⁻⁸.
  • Control: distilled water only.
  • 10 germinated seedlings per dish, on filter paper soaked in one solution. Measure root lengths.
  • Keep in the dark at 25 °C for 48 h (no phototropism). Measure again.
  • Calculate mean change in length, % change compared with control, and standard deviation.
  • Safe and ethical: goggles (IAA is an irritant); use only as many seedlings as needed; dispose of them properly.

Worked example (illustrative data): increase in root length / mm

  • Water: 12, 14, 11, 13, 15 → mean 13.0, SD 1.6
  • 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³: 6, 8, 5, 7, 9 → mean 7.0, SD 1.6
  • % change = (7.0 − 13.0) ÷ 13.0 × 100 = −46%
  • Mean ± 1 SD: water 11.4–14.6 mm; IAA 5.4–8.6 mm. The ranges do not overlap: the difference is likely to be real, not chance.
Mission 7

Apical dominance: the evidence

  • Apical dominance: the shoot tip (apex) stops side (lateral) buds from growing.
  • Thimann and Skoog (1933–34), broad bean:
  • Intact plant → lateral buds stay dormant.
  • Apex removed → lateral buds grow.
  • Apex removed + agar block with auxin on the cut stem → buds stay dormant.
  • Apex removed + plain agar block → buds grow (control: agar alone has no effect).
  • Conclusion: auxin from the apex inhibits lateral bud growth.
  • Later work: auxin acts indirectly, e.g. by lowering the supply of cytokinin to the buds.
Mission check

?A thin plastic sheet is pushed down through the tip of a shoot so that it separates the half facing a lamp from the shaded half. The plastic stops any chemical crossing from one half to the other. The shoot is lit from one side for a day. It grows upwards without bending. What is the best explanation?

Bending is just unequal elongation: block the hormone gradient and the bend disappears.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnification = image size ÷ actual size; same units first (1 mm = 1000 µm = 1 000 000 nm).
  • Calibrate the eyepiece graticule with a stage micrometer for every objective; a scale bar gives the magnification of a micrograph.

Mission 2

  • Magnification makes an image bigger; resolution decides how much detail it holds, and is limited by wavelength (light about 200 nm).
  • TEM: thin sections, internal detail, highest resolution; SEM: 3D surfaces; both need dead specimens in a vacuum.

Mission 3

  • Scientific drawings: clear continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, true proportions; annotations add function or features.
  • Plan (block) diagrams show tissue regions, not cells: leaf, stem with a ring of bundles (xylem inside, phloem outside).
  • In a root, xylem forms a central star with phloem between its arms.
  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size; scale bars are drawn at their magnified length.

Mission 4

  • Dissect safely (cut away, sharp blade, board, gloves) and identify by structure, position and connections; record and measure.
  • In stained sections, identify tissue by cell shape, nuclei, arrangement and striations, not stain colour.

Mission 5

  • PCR repeats denature, anneal and extend; primers mark the target and each cycle doubles it (starting number × 2ⁿ).
  • Taq polymerase survives 95 °C; annealing about 55 °C; extension about 72 °C.
  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments travel further.

Mission 6

  • Repeat loci vary in length between individuals; PCR copies them and a gel sorts them by length.
  • Chances at independent loci multiply, so more loci make a chance match less likely.
  • One band that cannot be explained excludes; matching bands are consistent with, not proof of, identity or relationship.

Mission 7

  • Plant hormones are made in growing regions and move to other tissues; auxin (IAA) promotes cell elongation, and unequal elongation bends an organ.
  • Phototropism: auxin moves to the shaded side; shaded cells elongate more; the shoot bends towards light.
  • The same IAA concentration that stimulates shoot cells inhibits root cells: shoots grow up, roots grow down.
