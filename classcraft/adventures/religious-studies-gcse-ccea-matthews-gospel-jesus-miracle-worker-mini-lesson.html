Mission 1: A frightened woman, a desperate father, a blind beggar: what did their faith have to do with healing?

Make a guess: A frightened woman, a desperate father, a blind beggar: what did their faith have to do with healing?

Mission 1

Healing as a sign

  • Mark calls them mighty works (6:5). Many Christians see them as signs that God's Kingdom has come near (1:15).
  • The prophets pictured that time: "Then the eyes of the blind will be opened, and the ears of the deaf will be unstopped." (Isaiah 35:5)
  • Mark's healings show Jesus' compassion and his authority: he acts by his own word and touch.
  • They often call for faith: trust in Jesus and in God's power.
  • They often break a barrier: sickness, uncleanness, death, being pushed aside.
Mission 1

Jesus heals many

  • In Simon Peter's house, Jesus takes Simon's mother-in-law by the hand; the fever leaves and she serves them (1:29–31).
  • At sunset (the Sabbath over), the whole town brings the sick and possessed to the door (1:32–33).
  • "He healed many who were sick with various diseases and cast out many demons." (Mark 1:34)
  • He does not let the demons speak, "because they knew him" (1:34): part of what scholars call the Messianic secret (in Mark, Jesus often orders silence about who he is).
  • Next morning he leaves to pray and to preach elsewhere (1:35–38): healing serves his message, not fame.
Mission 1

The paralysed man

  • Capernaum: the house is packed. Four friends dig through the roof and lower their friend on his mat (2:1–4).
  • "Jesus, seeing their faith, said to the paralytic, 'Son, your sins are forgiven you.'" (Mark 2:5)
  • Some scribes think: only God can forgive sins; this is blasphemy (2:6–7).
  • Jesus heals the man so "that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (2:10).
  • The man walks out carrying his mat; everyone glorifies God (2:12).
  • Faith here is the friends' faith, shown in action.
  • Matthew's version (Matt 9:1–7) is shorter: no roof; Jesus adds "Son, cheer up!" (Matt 9:2).
Mission 1

Jairus' daughter

  • Jairus, a leader of the synagogue, falls at Jesus' feet: his little daughter is dying (5:22–23).
  • On the way, a delay (the woman, next screen). Then news: "Your daughter is dead." (5:35)
  • Jesus: "Don't be afraid, only believe." (Mark 5:36)
  • He takes only Peter, James and John and the parents. Mourners laugh at "not dead, but … asleep" (5:39–40).
  • "Talitha cumi!" (Aramaic: "Girl, I tell you, get up!"). She gets up and walks; she is twelve (5:41–42).
  • He orders silence and tells them to give her something to eat (5:43).
  • In Matthew (9:18–26) the ruler says from the start that his daughter "has just died" (Matt 9:18).
Mission 1

The woman with a haemorrhage

  • Bleeding for twelve years; she has spent everything on doctors and grown worse (5:25–26).
  • Under the Law, her bleeding made her ritually unclean (Leviticus 15:25), cut off from worship and touch.
  • She thinks: "If I just touch his clothes, I will be made well." (Mark 5:28) She is healed at once.
  • Jesus stops: "Who touched my clothes?" She comes forward, "fearing and trembling", and tells the whole truth (5:30–33).
  • He calls her "Daughter": healed in body, and welcomed back in public.
  • Mark sandwiches her story inside Jairus' story: a leader and an outcast, both twelve years, both "daughters".

Mission 1

Bartimaeus

  • Leaving Jericho, on the way to Jerusalem. A blind beggar sits by the road (10:46).
  • He shouts: "Jesus, you son of David, have mercy on me!" (Mark 10:47) Son of David: a title for the Messiah.
  • The crowd tells him to be quiet; he shouts louder (10:48). Persistent faith.
  • He throws off his cloak (perhaps all he owns) and comes (10:50).
  • "Rabboni, that I may see again." Jesus: "Go your way. Your faith has made you well." (10:51–52)
  • He "followed Jesus on the way" (10:52): he becomes a disciple, on the road to the cross.
  • He sees clearly, while the disciples, just before, have misunderstood (10:35–45).
Mission 1

The paralysed hand, and Matthew's versions

  • Mark 3:1–6: a synagogue on the Sabbath; a man with a withered hand. Opponents watch to accuse Jesus.
  • Jesus asks: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm?" (Mark 3:4) He heals; the Pharisees plot with the Herodians (3:6).
  • Matthew 12:9–14 adds a sheep in a pit: "Of how much more value then is a man than a sheep!" (Matthew 12:12)
  • Matthew's healings are often shorter than Mark's and stress Jesus' authority and the faith of those who ask.
  • The healings were controversial: they raised questions about the Law, forgiveness and who Jesus is (more in Mission 3).

Mission 1Diversity

What is faith in Mark?

  • Trust in Jesus' power: the woman and Jairus believe he can heal (5:28, 5:36).
  • Faith that acts: the friends break a roof (2:4); Bartimaeus will not be silenced (10:48).
  • Faith of others: the man is healed through his friends' faith (2:5), the girl through her father's.
  • Faith that struggles: "I believe. Help my unbelief!" (9:24, a later mission).
  • Unbelief limits: at Nazareth "he could do no mighty work there", and "marvelled because of their unbelief" (6:5–6).
Mission 1Diversity

Healing miracles today

  • Literal: real events; Jesus has God's power over sickness and death (Catholic, Orthodox, many Protestants).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic churches hold healing services and pray for healing with laying on of hands.
  • Liberal readings: healing of mind and body together; the stories mainly teach inclusion and wholeness.
  • Christian witness: hospitals, hospices and medical missions continue Jesus' care.
  • Non-religious views: stories grew in retelling; some illnesses may improve through belief (the placebo effect); Humanists value the compassion but not the miraculous claim.
Mission 1Evaluate

"In Mark's healing miracles, faith matters more than the miracle itself."

  • Reasons for: Jesus says "your faith has made you well" (5:34; 10:52). Bartimaeus' healing ends in following. Unbelief at Nazareth limits him (6:5–6). The woman gains more than health: a name, "Daughter".
  • Reasons against: the miracles show who Jesus is: only God forgives sins (2:7–10). Jesus heals where no faith is mentioned (1:34). The girl is dead: she can have no faith. The healings are signs of the Kingdom (Isaiah 35:5–6).
  • Non-religious view: if the miracles did not happen, the stories still show compassion and courage, but no special power.
  • Judge it by: Does faith cause the healing or receive it? Is Mark's main point who Jesus is, or how to respond to him? Does it matter whether the events are historical?

Mission 2

Who was pushed aside, and why?

  • People with skin disease ("leprosy"): under the Law they were unclean and lived apart: "He shall dwell alone. His dwelling shall be outside of the camp." (Leviticus 13:46)
  • Tax collectors: collected money for Herod or Rome, often took extra; seen as collaborators and dishonest.
  • "Sinners": people seen as not keeping God's Law.
  • Gentiles (non-Jews): outside God's covenant; many Jews avoided eating with them to keep purity laws.
  • Reasons were not only prejudice: purity laws protected holiness, worship and the community's identity under foreign rule.
  • Illness was often linked with sin, so the sick could be blamed as well as excluded.
Mission 2

The man with leprosy

  • He kneels and begs: "If you want to, you can make me clean." (Mark 1:40)
  • Jesus, "moved with compassion", touches him: "I want to. Be made clean." (Mark 1:41)
  • Touching made a person unclean; here the touch makes the leper clean.
  • Jesus sends him to the priest with the offering "which Moses commanded" (1:44): he respects the Law and restores the man to the community.
  • He is told to say nothing, but spreads the news; now Jesus must stay outside the towns (1:45). He takes the outsider's place.
  • Matthew 8:1–4 tells the same story more briefly: the man "worshipped him" and is cleansed at once (Matt 8:2–3).

The call of Levi

  • Levi, son of Alphaeus, sits at his tax office by the lake (2:14).
  • Jesus says, "Follow me." "And he arose and followed him." (Mark 2:14)
  • Then Jesus eats in Levi's house with "many tax collectors and sinners" (2:15).
  • Eating together meant acceptance and friendship.
  • Scribes and Pharisees object: "Why is it that he eats and drinks with tax collectors and sinners?" (Mark 2:16)

Mission 2

The Syro-Phoenician woman

  • Near Tyre, in Gentile territory. A woman, "a Greek, a Syrophoenician by race", begs Jesus to free her daughter (7:24–26).
  • Jesus: "Let the children be filled first, for it is not appropriate to take the children's bread and throw it to the dogs." (Mark 7:27)
  • Children = Israel; dogs = Gentiles, a common insult.
  • She answers: "Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children's crumbs." (Mark 7:28)
  • "For this saying, go your way." Her daughter is freed, healed at a distance (7:29–30).
  • Readings: Jesus tests her faith · his mission was to Israel first, then to all · some scholars think the woman changes Jesus' mind.
  • An outsider three times over: a Gentile, a woman, the mother of a possessed child.
Mission 2

The Roman officer's servant

  • Capernaum. A centurion (Roman officer, a Gentile and part of the occupying army) asks Jesus to heal his paralysed servant (Matt 8:5–6).
  • "Lord, I'm not worthy for you to come under my roof. Just say the word, and my servant will be healed." (Matthew 8:8)
  • He understands authority: he gives orders and they are obeyed (8:9).
  • Jesus is amazed: "I haven't found so great a faith, not even in Israel." (Matthew 8:10)
  • Many "from the east and the west" will share the Kingdom feast (8:11): the Kingdom is open to Gentiles.
  • Catholics echo his words before Communion at every Mass.

Mission 2Diversity

Significance for Christians today

  • Inclusion: churches welcome people others reject: refugees, prisoners, people with HIV, the homeless.
  • Care for the sick: The Leprosy Mission (founded 1874) still treats leprosy; St Damien of Molokai lived and died among leprosy patients in Hawaii.
  • Different emphases: some Christians stress welcome first; others stress the call to repentance that comes with it (1:15; 2:17).
  • Non-religious view: Humanists share the value of equal dignity for all, based on shared humanity, not on God.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Christians should welcome everyone without conditions, as Jesus did."

  • Reasons for: Jesus touched the leper (1:41), ate with sinners (2:15–16), healed a Gentile's daughter (7:29); he praised a Roman's faith (Matt 8:10); "you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40).
  • Reasons against: Jesus calls people to "repent" (1:15; 2:17): welcome leads to change; he sends the leper to the priest, keeping the Law; welcoming a person is not approving everything they do.
  • Another view: "without conditions" may mean no one is excluded from the invitation, while following still asks a response.
  • Judge it by: What is the difference between welcome and approval? Does Jesus' practice set limits? How do churches balance welcome with their teaching?

Mission 3

Who were the authorities?

  • Scribes: experts who copied, taught and interpreted the Law of Moses.
  • Pharisees: a respected movement of devout Jews who applied the Law carefully to daily life, especially purity and the Sabbath.
  • Herodians: supporters of Herod's family, who ruled under Rome.
  • Chief priests: ran the Temple in Jerusalem; with elders and scribes they formed the Sanhedrin (council).
  • Romans: the occupying power; the Gospels present only the governor as able to order an execution (John 18:31).
  • In Mark, these groups clash with Jesus over authority: who speaks for God?
Mission 3

The first conflict: forgiving sins

  • A paralysed man is lowered through a roof. Jesus says: "Son, your sins are forgiven you." (Mark 2:5)
  • Scribes think: "Why does this man speak blasphemies like that? Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:7)
  • Blasphemy: insulting God, or claiming what belongs only to God.
  • Jesus heals the man "that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins" (2:10).
  • For Christians this shows Jesus' divine authority. For the scribes, it was a serious offence.
  • Matthew 9:3: "This man blasphemes."
  • It opens a chain of five disputes (2:1–3:6) that ends in a plot to kill him (3:6).

Mission 3

The Sabbath in the Jewish scriptures

  • "Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy." (Exodus 20:8) No work on the seventh day (20:9–10).
  • It recalls God resting after creation (Genesis 2:2–3) and freedom from slavery in Egypt (Deuteronomy 5:15).
  • The Sabbath is a gift and a sign of the covenant, a day of rest, joy and worship.
  • Jewish teachers debated what counted as work; later rabbis listed 39 kinds, including reaping.
  • Jewish teaching holds that saving a life overrides the Sabbath.
  • So the question was not whether to keep the Sabbath, but how.
Mission 3

Grain on the Sabbath

  • Walking through fields on the Sabbath, the disciples pick ears of grain (2:23).
  • Pharisees: "Why do they do that which is not lawful on the Sabbath day?" (Mark 2:24) Picking counted as reaping.
  • Jesus recalls David, who in need ate the holy bread kept for priests (2:25–26; 1 Samuel 21:1–6).
  • "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27)
  • "Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath." (Mark 2:28)
  • Human need comes first; and Jesus claims authority over how the Sabbath is kept.
Mission 3

The withered hand

  • A synagogue on the Sabbath. A man with a withered hand. Opponents watch "that they might accuse him" (3:1–2).
  • Jesus asks: "Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm? To save a life or to kill?" (Mark 3:4) They are silent.
  • Angry and grieved at their "hardening of their hearts", he heals the hand (3:5).
  • "The Pharisees went out, and immediately conspired with the Herodians against him, how they might destroy him." (Mark 3:6)
  • Irony: they plan to kill on the Sabbath, while he restores.
  • Matthew 12:9–14 adds a sheep in a pit: "Therefore it is lawful to do good on the Sabbath day." (Matthew 12:12)
  • The Pharisees' view had reason: the hand was not life-threatening and could wait a day.

Mission 3

The Temple and the plot

  • Jerusalem, days before Passover. In the Temple courts, Jesus drives out traders and overturns the money changers' tables and the dove-sellers' seats (11:15–16).
  • "Isn't it written, 'My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations'? But you have made it a den of robbers!" (Mark 11:17; Isaiah 56:7; Jeremiah 7:11)
  • The trading filled the Court of the Gentiles, the one place non-Jews could pray.
  • Readings: a cleansing (reform of worship) or a prophetic sign of judgement on the Temple (Mark frames it with the cursed fig tree, 11:12–21; see 13:2).
  • "The chief priests and the scribes heard it, and sought how they might destroy him." (Mark 11:18)
  • The plot: not at the feast, "because there might be a riot amongst the people" (14:1–2). Judas goes to the chief priests, who promise him money (14:10–11).
Mission 3

Taxes to Caesar

  • Pharisees and Herodians try to "trap him with words" (12:13): "Is it lawful to pay taxes to Caesar, or not?" (12:14)
  • A trap: "yes" angers nationalists; "no" is treason against Rome.
  • He asks for a denarius: "Whose is this image and inscription?" "Caesar's." (12:16)
  • "Give to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's." (Mark 12:17)
  • Many Christians read: obey and pay the state, but humans, made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), belong to God.
  • Discipleship today: Christians are good citizens in a pluralist society, but some, like the apostles (Acts 5:29), refuse a law that goes against God.

Mission 3

Why conflict drives Mark's story

  • Conflict grows: questions (2:7) → watching to accuse (3:2) → a plot (3:6) → the leaders seek to destroy him (11:18) → arrest and trial (14).
  • Each clash is about authority: to forgive, over the Sabbath, over the Temple.
  • The conflicts show who Jesus is (Son of Man, with God's authority) and why he died.
  • They prepare readers for the cross, which Jesus foretells three times (8:31; 9:31; 10:33–34).
  • Fairness: these were debates among Jews; Jesus and his disciples were Jews; crowds of Jews followed him.
  • Christian churches today reject blaming the Jewish people for Jesus' death (Catholic Church, Nostra Aetate, 1965).
Mission 3Evaluate

"The authorities were right to see Jesus as a threat."

  • Reasons for: he claimed authority belonging to God (2:7–10); he disrupted the Temple at Passover, risking a riot and Roman force; crowds followed him; leaders were responsible for keeping peace with Rome.
  • Reasons against: he kept the Sabbath and the Law, and debated as other teachers did; his healings did good; the plot came from fear of losing power (11:18).
  • He did not call for revolt ("Give to Caesar…", 12:17).
  • Many Christians: he was a threat to sin and injustice, not to God's Law; the conflict fulfilled God's purpose in his death.
  • Judge it by: A threat to what: to God's Law, to peace, to their power? Does the answer depend on who Jesus was? Can we judge first-century leaders by today's standards?

Mission 4

The Kingdom of Heaven

  • Matthew usually says "Kingdom of Heaven" where Mark and Luke say "Kingdom of God". Many Jews used "Heaven" in place of "God" out of reverence: the meaning is the same.
  • The Kingdom is God's rule: wherever God's will is done.
  • It is both present (begun in Jesus' ministry) and future (completed at the end of the age).
  • Jesus teaches it through parables: everyday stories with a spiritual meaning.
  • Parables invite listeners to think, and they divide listeners: "He who has ears to hear, let him hear." (Matthew 13:9)
Mission 4

The Sower (13:1–9, 18–23)

  • A farmer scatters seed. It lands on four kinds of ground. Jesus explains it himself:
  • Path: people hear "the word of the Kingdom" but do not understand; the evil one snatches it away.
  • Rocky ground: quick joy, no root; they fall away under persecution.
  • Thorns: worries and "the deceitfulness of riches" choke the word.
  • Good soil: they hear, understand and bear fruit, "some one hundred times as much, some sixty, and some thirty" (13:23).
  • Then: why many rejected Jesus, and hope that the harvest would still be huge.
  • Today: Christians ask which soil they are; churches keep sharing the message even when most do not respond.
Mission 4

The Weeds (13:24–30, 36–43)

  • An enemy sows darnel (a weed that looks like wheat when young) among a farmer's wheat.
  • The servants want to pull it up. The master says no: "Let both grow together until the harvest" (13:30).
  • Jesus explains: the field is the world; good seed, the children of the Kingdom; weeds, the children of the evil one; harvest, the end of the age (13:38–39).
  • Then: why evil still exists if the Kingdom has come; judgement belongs to God at the end, not to people now.
  • Today: patience and humility: Christians should not try to judge who is "in" and who is "out".

Mission 4

The Lost Sheep (18:10–14)

  • A shepherd leaves the ninety-nine and searches the hills for one that has wandered off.
  • When he finds it, he rejoices more over it than over the ninety-nine.
  • "Even so it is not the will of your Father who is in heaven that one of these little ones should perish." (Matthew 18:14)
  • In Matthew, the story is told to disciples about care for "little ones": vulnerable or straying members of the community (18:10).
  • Luke tells it to Pharisees about sinners (Luke 15:3–7).
  • Today: pastoral care for those who drift from faith; Christians value every individual, not only the majority.

The Workers in the Vineyard

Workers hired at dawn agree to a denarius (a day's wage). Others are hired at nine, noon, three and five. All are paid a denarius. The first group grumble.

"Friend, I am doing you no wrong. Didn't you agree with me for a denarius?… Or is your eye evil, because I am good? So the last will be first, and the first last." (Matthew 20:13, 15–16)

Show answer

A denarius: what they received.

Q2 According to the landowner, what is the real reason for the complaint?

Show answer

Mission 4

Grace, not wages

  • The landowner stands for God; the denarius, for salvation or eternal life.
  • God's grace: a free gift, not a wage earned by hours worked.
  • Then: latecomers (tax collectors, sinners, Gentiles) receive the same welcome as those who had long kept the Law.
  • Nobody is cheated: the first workers get exactly what they agreed.
  • Today: Christians should not resent newcomers or late converts; no one "earns" a better place with God.
  • Some Christians also apply it to work: enough to live on for every worker. This is an application, not the parable's main point.
Mission 4

The Wedding Feast (22:1–14)

  • A king gives a wedding feast for his son. The invited guests refuse; some kill his servants.
  • The king sends servants to the roads to invite everyone, "both bad and good" (22:10).
  • One guest has no wedding clothes and is thrown out.
  • "For many are called, but few chosen." (Matthew 22:14)
  • Then: the feast is the Kingdom; the refusing guests, those who rejected Jesus' call; the new guests, outsiders.
  • The wedding clothes: accepting the invitation means changing one's life: repentance and good deeds.
  • Today: the invitation is open to all, but it asks for a real response, not just turning up.

Mission 4Diversity

Different readings

  • Weeds: Augustine (354–430) read the field as the Church: a mixed body of saints and sinners until the end. Others, following 13:38, read it as the world.
  • Wedding Feast: early Christians often read the refusing guests as the Jewish people. Since the Second Vatican Council (Nostra Aetate, 1965) and similar Protestant statements, churches reject blaming the Jewish people as a whole.
  • Judgement: some Christians stress the warnings (fire, outer darkness) as real; others read them as picture language about losing God.
  • Kingdom now or later: some stress present action (justice, care); others the future Kingdom at Christ's return. Most hold both.
  • Workers: Catholic and Protestant Christians agree that salvation is God's gift. Catholics also stress cooperating with grace through faith and good works.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Matthew's Kingdom parables are more about judgement than about God's love."

  • Reasons for: the Weeds and the Wedding Feast end in fire, "weeping and gnashing of teeth" (13:42) and outer darkness; the Sower shows most seed lost; "few chosen" (22:14).
  • Reasons against: the Lost Sheep shows God searching for one; the Workers shows extravagant generosity; the Wedding Feast invites "both bad and good".
  • Another view: judgement and love belong together: the invitation is generous because the stakes are real.
  • Judge it by: Which parables did Matthew place where, and to whom? Is a warning itself an act of love? Do the endings outweigh the openings?

Mission 1

  • Mark's healings are signs of God's Kingdom and show Jesus' compassion and authority, even over sin and death.
  • Faith in Mark is trust that acts (the friends, the woman, Jairus, Bartimaeus) and becomes following.
  • Christians read the healings literally, as wholeness and inclusion, or as continuing in prayer, sacraments and medicine; non-religious people doubt the miraculous.

Mission 2

  • First-century purity laws, politics and fear of sin pushed aside lepers, tax collectors, sinners and Gentiles.
  • Jesus touched, ate with and healed them, out of compassion and because the Kingdom is for all who respond.

Mission 3

  • Scribes, Pharisees, Herodians and chief priests clashed with Jesus over his authority: to forgive sins, over the Sabbath, over the Temple.
  • "The Sabbath was made for man": Jesus kept the Sabbath but put human need first; the Temple protest led straight to the plot.
  • The conflicts explain why Jesus died; Christians read them as debates among Jews, not as blame on the Jewish people.

Mission 4

  • Matthew's five parables picture the Kingdom of Heaven: God's rule, present now and complete at the end of the age.
  • Sower and Weeds: different responses, and good and evil together until God's judgement.
  • Lost Sheep, Workers, Wedding Feast: God seeks the lost, gives generously, and invites all to respond.
