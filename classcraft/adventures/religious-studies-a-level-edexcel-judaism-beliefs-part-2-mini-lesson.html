Mission 1

She'ol: the place of the dead

  • In most of the Tenakh, the dead go down to She'ol: a dark, silent place under the earth.
  • Everyone goes there, righteous and wicked alike: Jacob says he will "go down to the grave to my son mourning" (Genesis 37:35, JPS 1917; Hebrew She'ol).
  • It is a place of shades, cut off from praise: "For in death there is no remembrance of Thee; in the nether-world who will give Thee thanks?" (Psalm 6:5, JPS 1917)
  • No reward, no punishment: "there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave" (Ecclesiastes 9:10, JPS 1917).
  • So the early focus is this life: blessing, long life, children, land.
  • Edexcel pairs She'ol with Gehenna, a later idea.

Saul and Samuel at En-dor

1 Samuel 28:11–20 (JPS 1917). Before a battle, God no longer answers Saul. He has banned those who call up the dead, but now visits a woman at En-dor.

> "I see a godlike being coming up out of the earth." (1 Samuel 28:13, JPS 1917)

> "And Samuel said to Saul: 'Why hast thou disquieted me, to bring me up?'" (1 Samuel 28:15, JPS 1917)

  • Samuel repeats God's judgement on Saul and adds: "to-morrow shalt thou and thy sons be with me" (1 Samuel 28:19, JPS 1917).
  • The dead exist, keep their identity and memory, and are below.
  • Calling them up is forbidden (Deuteronomy 18:10–11).

Mission 1

The soul

  • Nefesh: life, a living being. God breathed "the breath of life; and man became a living soul" (Genesis 2:7, JPS 1917).
  • Ruach: spirit, wind; neshamah: breath, later the highest level of the soul.
  • At death: "the dust returneth to the earth as it was, and the spirit returneth unto God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7, JPS 1917).
  • Each morning Jews pray: the soul God gave is pure, and God will one day take it and restore it (Talmud Berakhot 60b, paraphrase).
  • The rabbis held body and soul together: a lame man and a blind man rob an orchard together, and are judged together (Sanhedrin 91a–b, paraphrase).
  • Edexcel: the nature of the soul: God-given, pure, surviving death.
Mission 1

Resurrection of the dead

> "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917)

  • Also: "Thy dead shall live, my dead bodies shall arise" (Isaiah 26:19, JPS 1917); Ezekiel's dry bones (Ezekiel 37), first about the nation's revival.
  • The Pharisees taught resurrection; the Sadducees denied it.
  • The Mishnah: whoever says resurrection is not taught in the Torah has no share in the world to come (Sanhedrin 10:1, paraphrase).
  • Daily Amidah: God "revives the dead" (mechayeh ha-metim).
  • Thirteen Principles: principle 13, resurrection of the flesh.
  • Modern attitudes: Orthodox Jews affirm a literal, bodily resurrection (one reason for burial, not cremation); many Progressive Jews do not.
Mission 1

Judgement, olam ha-ba, Gan Eden, Gehinnom

  • Judgement: each soul is judged after death; the final judgement follows the resurrection.
  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come": the realm of souls after death, or the renewed world after resurrection (sources use both).
  • "This world is like a vestibule before the world to come" (Pirkei Avot 4:16, paraphrase): life now prepares for it.
  • Gan Eden (paradise): the reward of the righteous.
  • Gehinnom (Gehenna): named after the valley of Hinnom, outside Jerusalem (Jeremiah 7:31); a place of purification, mostly for at most twelve months (Mishnah Eduyot 2:10).
  • So mourners say Kaddish for eleven months, not twelve.
  • "The righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come" (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Immortality of the soul and the Pittsburgh Platform

  • Later Jewish thought stressed the immortal soul: medieval philosophers (Saadia, Maimonides) and Moses Mendelssohn, whose Phaedon (1767) argued for immortality as endless moral growth.
  • Pittsburgh Platform (American Reform, 1885), paragraph 7:
  • "We reassert the doctrine of Judaism that the soul is immortal" (Pittsburgh Platform, 1885).
  • It rejects "the beliefs both in bodily resurrection and in Gehenna and Eden (Hell and Paradise) as abodes for everlasting punishment and reward" (Pittsburgh Platform, 1885).
  • Reform prayer books changed "who revives the dead" to "who gives life to all" (mechayeh ha-kol); recent ones offer both.
  • Today: Orthodox Jews hold resurrection and an immortal soul; many Reform and Liberal Jews hold immortality only, or leave the question open.
Mission 1

Afterlife and the justice of God

  • The Tenakh promises reward in this life for keeping the covenant (Deuteronomy 11:13–14).
  • But experience disagrees: "Wherefore doth the way of the wicked prosper?" (Jeremiah 12:1, JPS 1917).
  • Daniel's hope of resurrection arose in a time of persecution, when the faithful were dying for their faith (2nd century BCE, on many scholars' dating).
  • Link: if God is just, wrongs not put right in this life must be put right beyond it.
  • The rabbis: the reward for mitzvot is not in this world (Kiddushin 39b, paraphrase).
  • Tension: Avot 1:3 says serve God not for reward; many Progressive Jews put God's justice into human action in this world.
Mission 1

Reincarnation in Kabbalah

  • Gilgul (gilgul neshamot, "rolling of souls"): in some kabbalistic thought, a soul may return in another body.
  • Found in the Bahir and the Zohar; developed by Isaac Luria (1534–1572) in Safed, recorded by Hayyim Vital.
  • Purpose: to repair (tikkun) what was left undone, or fulfil mitzvot not yet completed.
  • Living Judaism: a bedtime prayer in many prayer books forgives wrongs done "in this gilgul or another".
  • Rejected by Saadia Gaon (882–942) as foolishness; most Reform Jews do not hold it.
  • Elie Kaplan Spitz, a Conservative rabbi, Does the Soul Survive? (2000): moved from doubt to accepting the soul's survival and past lives, drawing on Jewish mysticism and the case histories of Brian Weiss (b. 1944, Many Lives, Many Masters, 1988), a psychiatrist whose patients described past lives under hypnosis (paraphrase).
  • Critics: memories under hypnosis can be suggested or drawn from forgotten sources.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are beliefs about the afterlife still relevant for Jews today?

  • Claim: Beliefs about judgement and the afterlife remain relevant for Jews today.
  • For: They are in the daily liturgy (Amidah, morning blessings) and in mourning (Kaddish, burial).
  • For: They answer the problem of God's justice when the righteous suffer.
  • Against: The Tenakh is mostly silent; Judaism stresses this world and the mitzvot.
  • Against: Pittsburgh and many Progressive Jews reject bodily resurrection and literal Gehenna and Eden.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to belief or to practice? To which Jews? Does "relevant" require being literal?

Mission 2

Made in the image of God

  • "And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them." (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917)
  • Tzelem Elohim ("image of God"): not a body shape (God has none) but human reason, moral freedom, creativity and the ability to relate to God.
  • Every person has equal and infinite worth: humanity began with one person, so "whoever saves a single life saves a whole world" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5, paraphrase).
  • So how a person treats others is part of how they honour God.
Mission 2

Two inclinations

  • Every person has a yetzer hatov (good inclination) and a yetzer hara (evil, or selfish, inclination).
  • The Torah: "the imagination of man's heart is evil from his youth" (Genesis 8:21, JPS 1917). Hebrew yetzer = "imagination", "inclination".
  • The yetzer hara is not evil in itself: a midrash says without it no one would build a house, marry or have children (Genesis Rabbah 9:7).
  • The task is to direct it, not destroy it. "Love the LORD thy God with all thy heart": with both inclinations (Mishnah Berakhot 9:5).
  • Torah and the mitzvot train the yetzer hatov; it is said to mature at bar/bat mitzvah age.
  • No original sin: each person is born free to choose. "Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).

Mission 2

Purpose 1: obedience

  • "what doth the LORD thy God require of thee, but to fear the LORD thy God, to walk in all His ways, and to love Him, and to serve the LORD thy God with all thy heart and with all thy soul" (Deuteronomy 10:12, JPS 1917).
  • "fear God, and keep His commandments; for this is the whole man." (Ecclesiastes 12:13, JPS 1917)
  • Obedience means keeping the 613 mitzvot of the covenant: ritual and ethical.
  • The mitzvot make ordinary life holy: eating, working, resting, family, business.
  • Obedience is an act of love, not just duty: Jews bless God "who has made us holy with his commandments".
Mission 2

Purpose 2: repairing the world

  • Tikkun olam: "repair of the world".
  • In prayer: the Aleinu hopes "to perfect the world under the kingdom of the Almighty" (paraphrase).
  • In Mishnah law: some rulings are made "for the sake of tikkun ha-olam" (social order) (Mishnah Gittin 4).
  • In Kabbalah: Isaac Luria (1534–1572): at creation, divine light was scattered in sparks; each mitzvah helps gather them, repairing the world.
  • Today: especially in Reform and Liberal Judaism, social justice and environmental work.
  • Humans, made in God's image, are partners with God in completing creation.
Mission 2

Three modern voices

  • Y Buxbaum (Yitzhak Buxbaum), Jewish Spiritual Practices (1990), paraphrase: the purpose of life is to serve God in everything, to be always aware of God, and to make all one's deeds "for the sake of Heaven" (Mishnah Avot 2:12). "I have set the LORD always before me" (Psalm 16:8, JPS 1917).
  • E Kaplan-Spitz (Elie Kaplan Spitz, Conservative rabbi), Does the Soul Survive? (2000), paraphrase: each soul has a task; life is an opportunity for the soul to grow and help repair the world; he takes seriously the kabbalistic idea of gilgul (reincarnation).
  • B L Weiss (Brian L. Weiss, b. 1944, psychiatrist), Many Lives, Many Masters (1988), paraphrase: from patients' reported past lives, he argues souls return to learn love and compassion; life is a school for the soul.
  • Weiss is not a Jewish theologian; his work is used to compare with kabbalistic ideas of the soul's journey. Many Jews reject reincarnation.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is obedience the most important purpose of life?

  • Claim: For Jews, obedience to God's commandments is the most important purpose of life.
  • For: The Torah sums up life as keeping the commandments (Deuteronomy 10:12–13; Ecclesiastes 12:13).
  • For: Tikkun olam happens through the mitzvot, so obedience comes first.
  • Against: Many Reform and secular Jews put repairing the world first; ritual obedience is optional for them.
  • Against: Love of God and neighbour may be the deeper aim; obedience is the means.
  • Against: Buxbaum: the purpose is constant awareness of God; Kaplan Spitz and Weiss: the soul's growth.
  • Judgement criteria: Can the purposes be ranked, or do they depend on each other? Which Jewish movement's view is weighed? Is "most important" about time, value, or what God commands?

Mission 3

A tractate about character

  • Pirkei Avot ("Chapters of the Fathers"; also "Ethics of the Fathers"): a tractate of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE).
  • Unlike the rest of the Mishnah, it has almost no law: it collects sayings of the sages about wisdom, study, character and how to treat others.
  • It sits in the order Nezikin ("damages"): civil and criminal law, courts and judges.
  • Why there? Many explain: judges and those who apply law need good character. The Talmud says whoever wants to be pious should keep the laws of Nezikin; another sage says, "the matters of Avot" (Bava Kamma 30a, paraphrase).
  • Many communities study a chapter of Avot on Shabbat afternoons in the summer.
  • Your spec calls God "the Almighty" and spells "gemiluth chasadim".
Mission 3Read the source

Avot 1:1

Pirkei Avot 1:1 (paraphrase):

  • Moses received the Torah at Sinai and handed it on to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • They said three things: Be careful in judgement. Raise up many students. Make a fence around the Torah.

Show answer

Extra rules that keep people well away from breaking a commandment.

Mission 3

What the world stands on

  • Simeon the Righteous (1:2): the world stands on three things: Torah (study), avodah (worship; once the Temple service) and gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness).
  • Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel (1:18): the world endures on three things: justice, truth and peace, citing "execute the judgment of truth and peace in your gates" (Zechariah 8:16, JPS 1917).
  • So the chapter begins with study and worship and ends with justice in society.
  • Ethics is not an extra: it holds the world up.

Mission 3

Sayings of the sages

All paraphrases from Avot 1:

  • Antigonus (1:3): do not serve God like servants working for a reward.
  • Joshua ben Perachiah (1:6): get yourself a teacher, gain a friend, and judge everyone favourably.
  • Hillel (1:12): be a disciple of Aaron: love peace, pursue peace, love people and bring them near to Torah.
  • Hillel (1:14): If I am not for myself, who will be for me? If I am only for myself, what am I? If not now, when?
  • Shammai (1:15): make study a fixed habit; say little and do much; greet everyone cheerfully.
  • Simeon (1:17): not study but action is the main thing.
Mission 3

Law, ethics and the nature of Torah

  • Torah is more than the written text: Avot 1 assumes an oral Torah passed through teachers, so interpretation is part of revelation.
  • Authority by transmission: the chain names each link; the order shows authority passing from prophets to sages.
  • Law: "a fence around the Torah" grounds rabbinic rules that protect commandments (e.g. not handling tools on Shabbat).
  • Ethics: most sayings concern character: humility, generosity, fair judgement, peace.
  • Interpretation: study must lead to action (1:17); motive matters (1:3).
Mission 3

Torah and values: Kellner

  • M Kellner (Menachem Kellner, b. 1946), "Jewish ethics", in P. Singer (ed.), A Companion to Ethics (Blackwell, 1991): Anthology extract (1). Paraphrase:
  • Judaism does not treat ethics as a separate subject: right action is taught by God's revealed Torah, written and oral, covering every part of life.
  • Jewish thinkers have still asked whether there is a morality the Torah assumes. The rabbis praise acting beyond the letter of the law (lifnim mi-shurat ha-din).
  • G Robinson (Essential Judaism, 2000), paraphrase: Jewish values are lived through daily mitzvot, not held as abstract principles.
  • Avot is a key example: law from the Mishnah's civil order, taught with the character it needs.
Mission 3

The Noahide Code

  • The rabbis teach that God gave all humanity, through Noah, seven laws (Talmud, Sanhedrin 56a):
  • Set up courts of justice; do not blaspheme; do not worship idols; no sexual immorality; no murder; no theft; do not eat flesh torn from a living animal (cf. Genesis 9:4).
  • Universal values: non-Jews who keep these are "righteous among the nations" and have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2; Maimonides).
  • Jewish distinctiveness: Jews are bound by 613 mitzvot as the covenant people; others need not convert to be righteous.
  • So most Jewish communities do not actively seek converts.

Love, justice, holiness

> "Ye shall be holy; for I the LORD your God am holy." (Leviticus 19:2, JPS 1917)

> "thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:18, JPS 1917)

> "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20, JPS 1917)

  • God as the source of moral values: Jewish teaching is that humans are to imitate God's qualities. The Talmud (Shabbat 133b, paraphrase): as God is gracious and merciful, so you be gracious and merciful.

Mission 3

Gemilut chasadim

  • Gemilut chasadim: acts of loving-kindness, one of the three pillars (Avot 1:2).
  • Greater than charity (Sukkah 49b, paraphrase): charity uses money, kindness uses body and money; charity is for the poor, kindness for rich and poor; charity is for the living, kindness for the living and the dead.
  • Examples: visiting the sick (bikur cholim), hospitality, comforting mourners, burying the dead, free loans.
  • Imitating God: God clothed Adam, visited Abraham, comforted Isaac, buried Moses: Jews are to do the same (Sotah 14a, paraphrase).
  • Significance: Avot sets it beside Torah and worship, so kindness is a religious duty, not an optional extra.

Is ethics, not law, the heart of Torah?

  • Claim: Pirkei Avot shows that ethics, not law, is the heart of Torah.
  • For: most sayings are about character; action and kindness hold the world up (1:2, 1:17, 1:18).
  • For: Hillel's teachings stress love of people and peace.
  • Against: Avot is part of the Mishnah's legal order; "a fence around the Torah" strengthens law.
  • Against: Kellner: in Judaism ethics is learned through revealed law, not apart from it.

Mission 4

Pluralistic Judaism

  • Migration made Britain multi-faith: Jews arrived from 1656 (mostly Sephardi), then in large numbers from Eastern Europe (1881–1914) and as refugees from Nazism (1930s).
  • Jewish Britain is itself diverse: Orthodox (United Synagogue; Haredi communities; Sephardi), Masorti, Reform, Liberal, and many secular Jews.
  • Pluralistic Judaism: accepting that these different Jewish movements are each legitimate ways of being Jewish.
  • Implications within Judaism: shared bodies (e.g. the Board of Deputies, the Jewish Leadership Council) work across movements, but most Orthodox authorities do not recognise non-Orthodox rabbis or conversions.
  • Implications beyond Judaism: a community used to living with internal difference may find it easier to respect other faiths.
Mission 4

Inclusivism inside Judaism: Sacks and Persoff

  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), Chief Rabbi 1991–2013, One People? (1993): inclusivism: all Jews belong to the one covenant people, even if Orthodox halakhah alone is authoritative (paraphrase).
  • So: love and respect for every Jew, without recognising every movement's theology.
  • Tensions: in 1996 Sacks did not attend the funeral of the Reform rabbi and Auschwitz survivor Hugo Gryn; a leaked letter criticising Gryn caused a public row (1997).
  • Meir Persoff, Another Way, Another Time: Religious Inclusivism and the Sacks Chief Rabbinate (2010): a critical history; he argues inclusivism often gave way to pressure from the religious right, leaving Progressive Jews excluded (paraphrase).
  • Question for evaluation: is inclusivism genuine pluralism, or polite exclusivism?

Mission 4

Three attitudes to other faiths

  • Exclusivism: only one religion is true or leads to God.
  • Inclusivism: one religion holds the fullest truth, but others contain truth and their followers can be "saved" or righteous.
  • Pluralism: many religions are equally valid responses to God.
  • Rabbinic Judaism is often described as unusual: it is exclusive about the Torah's truth for Jews, but inclusive about salvation: the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2).
  • Many Orthodox Jews: inclusivist. Many Reform and Liberal Jews: closer to pluralism. Some Haredi Jews: exclusivist about truth, wary of any religious contact.
Mission 4

The Noahide Code and universal values

  • The rabbis teach seven Noahide laws for all humanity (Sanhedrin 56a): courts of justice; no blasphemy, idolatry, sexual immorality, murder, theft, or eating flesh from a living animal (full study).
  • Non-Jews who keep them are righteous; they need not convert. So Judaism does not seek converts.
  • Maimonides: Islam is strict monotheism; he counted Christian worship as idolatry for Jews (paraphrase). Menachem Meiri (1249–c. 1315): Christians and Muslims are nations "bound by the ways of religion", not idolaters (paraphrase).
  • Jewish distinctiveness: the 613 mitzvot for Jews; universal values for all.
  • Gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness) are owed to everyone; God's love, justice and holiness are the source of these values (Leviticus 19).

Mission 4

John Hick's pluralism and Jewish replies

  • John Hick (1922–2012), God and the Universe of Faiths (1973), An Interpretation of Religion (1989).
  • A Copernican revolution: put God (the Real), not one religion, at the centre; the faiths circle it (paraphrase).
  • The Real as it is in itself is beyond any one tradition; each faith is a culturally shaped response to it.
  • Test: does a faith transform people from self-centredness to Reality-centredness? Many do equally well.
  • Jewish replies: Progressive Jews may welcome the respect it shows. Many Orthodox Jews say it reduces revelation at Sinai to one human response among many, and empties the covenant of meaning.
  • General critique: pluralism itself overrules what each faith says about itself (e.g. Gavin D'Costa).

Mission 4Diversity

Interfaith dialogue

  • Reform: the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) saw Christianity and Islam as helping spread monotheism; strong support for dialogue and joint action for tikkun olam.
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964): cooperate on shared moral and social concerns, but no debate about the content of faith (paraphrase).
  • Sacks, The Dignity of Difference (2002): one God made a diverse world; difference is a blessing (paraphrase). Some Haredi rabbis objected, and he revised passages in a 2003 edition.
  • Haredi: many avoid interfaith dialogue altogether.
  • Examples: the Council of Christians and Jews (founded 1942); Jewish–Muslim projects; Catholic Nostra Aetate (1965) and the Jewish statement Dabru Emet (2000).
  • The Messiah in dialogue: Christians believe Jesus is the Messiah; most Jews say the messianic age has not come. Dialogue can respect the difference.
Mission 4

Freedom of religious expression

  • Freedom of religion is a human right in European law: European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9 (1950); UK Human Rights Act 1998. Israel's Declaration of Independence (1948) promises freedom of religion.
  • Jews, long a persecuted minority, strongly support it: for themselves and for others.
  • Live issues: shechitah (kosher slaughter) banned in Denmark (2014) and parts of Belgium (2019); a German court ruled against infant brit milah (Cologne, 2012) before Germany passed a law protecting it; wearing a kippah safely in public.
  • Jewish and Muslim groups often defend shechitah and halal, and circumcision, together.
  • Limits: most Jews support free speech but want protection from incitement and antisemitism.
  • In Israel: debates about the Orthodox Chief Rabbinate's control of marriage and about non-Orthodox prayer at the Western Wall.

Mission 4Evaluate

Can Judaism be truly pluralist?

  • Claim: Judaism can accept other faiths as equally valid without losing its own identity.
  • For: the teaching that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come means non-Jews need not become Jews; Micah 4:5 can be read pluralistically.
  • For: Reform and Liberal Judaism practise pluralism and keep a strong Jewish identity.
  • Against: chosenness and Sinai claim a unique revelation; full pluralism seems to dissolve it.
  • Against: even within Judaism, most Orthodox bodies do not recognise other movements' rabbis or conversions.
  • Persoff: Sacks' inclusivism often gave way to pressure from the religious right.
  • Judgement criteria: pluralism about salvation or about truth? Does respect require equal validity? Which movement?

Mission 5

Rashi of Troyes

  • Rashi = Rabbi Shlomo Yitzchaki (1040–1105), of Troyes, in Champagne, northern France.
  • Studied in the great Rhineland academies of Mainz and Worms, then led his own school in Troyes.
  • Wrote commentaries on almost the whole Tanakh and almost the whole Babylonian Talmud.
  • Context: in 1096 crusaders massacred the Jewish communities of Speyer, Worms and Mainz, where he had studied.
  • His comments at times answer Christian readings of scripture or reflect Israel's suffering among the nations.
  • Your spec calls God "the Almighty" in places; this mission writes "God".
Mission 5

Peshat: Rashi's method

  • Rashi's goal: the plain sense (peshat) of each verse, in its context.
  • In his own words (on Genesis 3:8): he has come only for the plain meaning, and for such aggadah as "settles" the words of the verse (paraphrase).
  • Close reading: he notices a strange word, a repetition, a gap, and asks the question a careful reader would ask.
  • Forensic exegesis: grammar, context and parallels as evidence.
  • He uses midrash selectively: only where it explains the text.
  • He glosses hard words in Old French, written in Hebrew letters (la'azim).
Mission 5Read the source

Rashi on Genesis 1:1

> "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Why begin with creation? Rashi asks: a book of law could have begun with the first commandment to Israel (Exodus 12:2).
  • Answer (paraphrase): so that if the nations call Israel robbers for taking the land, Israel can reply that the whole earth belongs to God, who gives it to whom He wills.
  • What does the grammar say? The verse does not teach the order of creation: water is already there in verse 2.
  • Read it as: "In the beginning of God's creating the heaven and the earth, when the earth was unformed… God said: Let there be light" (paraphrase).

Mission 5

Rashi and the Talmud page

  • Rashi's Talmud commentary goes word by word: he explains terms, supplies missing steps and shows where each argument begins and ends.
  • Without it, few could study the Aramaic Talmud alone; with it, the Talmud became open to every student.
  • Where he died before finishing (e.g. tractate Makkot), his pupils completed the work.
  • Printed editions place Rashi in the inner column beside the text; the complete Venice edition of Daniel Bomberg (1520–23) fixed this layout.
  • The first dated printed Hebrew book was Rashi's Torah commentary (Reggio di Calabria, 1475).
Mission 5

His pupils and the tosafot

  • Rashi had no sons; his daughters married scholars, and his grandsons led the next generation.
  • Rashbam (Samuel ben Meir, c. 1085–c. 1158) pressed peshat further, sometimes against his grandfather's readings.
  • Rabbeinu Tam (Jacob ben Meir, c. 1100–1171) led the tosafists.
  • Tosafot ("additions"): 12th–14th-century comments from France and Germany, printed in the outer column.
  • Method: compare passages across the whole Talmud, find contradictions, resolve them by fine distinctions; often question Rashi.
  • A practical trace: Rashi and Rabbeinu Tam disagreed on the order of passages in tefillin; some Jews put on a second pair, "Rabbeinu Tam tefillin".

Mission 5

Judah Halevi: poet of Zion

  • Judah Halevi (c. 1075–1141): physician and Hebrew poet of Muslim and Christian Spain.
  • Wrote love and wine poems, but above all piyyutim: religious poems for Sabbaths and festivals, many still in Sephardi prayer books.
  • Songs of Zion: longing for the Land of Israel: his heart is in the East, while he is at the edge of the West (paraphrase).
  • His ode "Zion, will you not ask…" is recited among the laments (kinot) of Tisha B'Av.
  • In 1140 he left Spain for the Land of Israel; he reached Egypt and died in 1141. A legend says he died at the gates of Jerusalem.
  • Unity of God and Israel: in his poems Israel's exile is the exile of a beloved, still bound to God.
Mission 5

The Kuzari

  • The Kuzari (Arabic, completed c. 1140): "The Book of Refutation and Proof in Defence of the Despised Faith".
  • Frame: the king of the Khazars dreams that his intentions please God, but his actions do not.
  • He questions a philosopher, a Christian scholar and a Muslim scholar, then a rabbi.
  • The rabbi begins with the God of Abraham, Isaac and Israel who brought Israel out of Egypt, not with proofs of a creator.
  • Why? Revelation at Sinai, before the whole people, is more certain than any philosopher's argument.
  • The Christian and the Muslim both point back to Israel's history, so the king asks the Jew; he ends by accepting Judaism.
Mission 5

The God of Abraham, not of Aristotle

  • Rejects Aristotle: the philosopher's God does not know individuals or want anything; no one could pray to such a God.
  • Halevi's line (paraphrase): Greek wisdom bears flowers, but no fruit.
  • Religious experience: God is known by experience and love, as Abraham knew God, not only by reasoning.
  • Prophecy: the highest human state; a special divine influence rests on Israel, most fully in the Land of Israel.
  • Divine word in nature and Law: the commandments are God's prescriptions, which reason alone could not discover.
  • Israel among the nations is like the heart among the organs: most exposed to sickness, most alive (Kuzari II, paraphrase).

Mission 5

Authority and influence (Potok, Halkin)

  • Nature of their authority: neither was a prophet or a king; their authority came from learning and from being accepted by the community.
  • Rashi: printed beside the Torah and Talmud, studied from childhood; the starting point of almost all later commentary.
  • Halevi: a voice of experience and love of Zion; later read by kabbalists, Hasidim and religious Zionists (e.g. Rav Kook).
  • Chaim Potok (1929–2002), Wanderings (1978): sets figures like Rashi and Halevi within Jewish history as a meeting with surrounding cultures (paraphrase).
  • Hillel Halkin (b. 1939), Yehuda Halevi (2010): reads the journey to Zion, chosen over an honoured life in Spain, as the key to Halevi's life and poetry (paraphrase).
Mission 5Evaluate

Is Rashi the most important medieval Jewish commentator?

  • Claim: Rashi is the most important medieval Jewish commentator.
  • For: Universal use: printed with every Chumash and Talmud; first commentary taught to children.
  • For: His clarity made the Talmud open to all, and the tosafot built on his work.
  • Against: Maimonides shaped law and philosophy more deeply; Ibn Ezra and Rashbam were stricter about peshat.
  • Against: Rashi explains texts; others (Halevi, Maimonides) asked why to believe at all.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance for whom (students, legal scholars, philosophers)? Breadth of influence or depth of ideas? Then or now?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Tenakh's She'ol became, in later Judaism, judgement, olam ha-ba, Gan Eden, Gehinnom and the resurrection of the dead (Daniel 12:2).
  • At En-dor (1 Samuel 28), Samuel comes up out of the earth: the dead live on, shadowy, in She'ol.
  • Orthodox Jews affirm bodily resurrection (principle 13); the Pittsburgh Platform (1885) kept only the immortal soul.
  • Belief in an afterlife is tied to God's justice; some kabbalists teach gilgul (reincarnation).

Mission 2

  • Humans are made in God's image, with infinite worth, free will and two inclinations.
  • Purposes of life include obedience to the mitzvot and tikkun olam; Jews weigh them differently.
  • Buxbaum, Kaplan Spitz and Weiss give modern accounts of purpose.

Mission 3

  • Pirkei Avot, a Mishnah tractate in Nezikin, opens with the chain of transmission and collects the sages' ethical sayings.
  • The world stands on Torah, worship and gemilut chasadim (1:2), and endures on justice, truth and peace (1:18).
  • The Noahide Code gives universal values; in Jewish teaching, love, justice and holiness reflect God's own qualities.

Mission 4

  • Judaism usually combines unique truth (the Torah for Israel) with open salvation (the righteous of the nations, through the Noahide laws).
  • Orthodox views range from Haredi caution to Soloveitchik's limited cooperation and Sacks' "dignity of difference"; Reform and Liberal Jews lean to pluralism.
  • Pluralistic Judaism and freedom of religious expression: internal diversity, Sacks' inclusivism and its critics, and shared campaigns for religious freedom in a secular society.

Mission 5

  • Rashi (1040–1105) explained the Tanakh and Talmud through peshat, close reading and selected midrash; his pupils and the tosafot built on and challenged him.
  • Judah Halevi (c. 1075–1141): poet of Zion; in the Kuzari, faith rests on Israel's history and experience, not Aristotle's proofs.
