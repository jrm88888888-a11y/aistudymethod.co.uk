Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a Catholic Bible have more books than a Protestant one?

Make a guess: Why does a Catholic Bible have more books than a Protestant one?

Mission 1

A library, not a single book

  • "Bible" comes from the Greek ta biblia: "the books".
  • Written by many people over more than 1,000 years, in Hebrew, some Aramaic, and Greek.
  • Two parts: the Old Testament (before Jesus) and the New Testament (Jesus and the first Christians).
  • Testament means covenant: an agreement between God and people.
  • Christians believe it has one unity: the story of God and humanity, from creation to new creation.
  • How many books? 66 in Protestant Bibles, 73 in Catholic Bibles, more in Orthodox Bibles.
Mission 1

The Old Testament

  • Law (the Pentateuch: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy): creation, the patriarchs, Moses and the commandments.
  • History (e.g. Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings): Israel in the promised land, its kings and exile.
  • Writings (e.g. Psalms, Proverbs, Job): poetry, prayers and wisdom.
  • Prophets (e.g. Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos): God's messengers call people back to God and speak of hope to come.
  • The Jewish Tanakh has the same core books in a different order: Torah, Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).
Mission 1

The New Testament

  • Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John): the life, death and resurrection of Jesus. "Gospel" means "good news".
  • Acts: history of the early Church, from the Ascension to Paul in Rome.
  • Letters (epistles): 21 letters to churches and leaders, 13 under Paul's name; teaching on belief and life.
  • Revelation: visions of God's final victory, written in symbols (apocalyptic).
  • Almost all churches accept the same 27 New Testament books.

Mission 1

Different kinds of writing

  • The Bible contains many literary forms (types of writing):
  • Law: commands and rules for life and worship (Leviticus).
  • History: accounts of events, told to show God at work (Kings, Acts).
  • Prophecy: God's message through a prophet, often as poems and visions (Amos).
  • Poetry and wisdom: songs, prayers and sayings (Psalms, Proverbs).
  • Gospel: proclaiming Jesus as good news, not a modern biography.
  • Letter: real letters to real churches (Romans).
  • Apocalyptic: symbols and visions about the end (Revelation, part of Daniel).
  • Catholic teaching: to find what God means, readers must notice the literary form the human author used (Dei Verbum 12, paraphrase).

Mission 1

How the Bible came together

  • Oral tradition first: stories, laws and songs were passed on by word of mouth, then written down and edited over centuries.
  • The Septuagint: a Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures (from the 3rd century BCE), used by many early Christians.
  • New Testament: Paul's letters come first (c. 50–60 CE); the Gospels c. 65–100 CE.
  • Canon (Greek "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books.
  • Tests used: link to an apostle, use in worship across the churches, agreement with the faith handed down.
  • 367 CE: Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, lists the 27 New Testament books; councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) confirm them.
  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit guided this whole process.
Mission 1

The Apocrypha and the Council of Trent

  • Some books were in the Greek Septuagint but not in the Hebrew scriptures: e.g. Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees.
  • Catholics call them deuterocanonical ("second canon"); Protestants call them the Apocrypha ("hidden").
  • Reformers (16th century) followed the Hebrew list; Luther printed these books in a separate section (1534).
  • Council of Trent, 8 April 1546: the Catholic Church declared these books fully part of scripture, with equal authority. Total: 73 books.
  • Church of England (39 Articles, Article VI): the Church reads them "for example of life and instruction of manners" but not to establish doctrine.
  • Orthodox Bibles include them, and a few more (e.g. 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151).
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

"The deuterocanonical books should be part of the Bible."

  • Reasons for: they were in the Septuagint, the Greek scripture of many early Christians. Church Fathers such as Augustine used them as scripture. Councils at Hippo and Carthage included them; Trent confirmed them. 2 Maccabees 12:46 supports praying for the dead.
  • Reasons against: they are not in the Hebrew scriptures. Jerome (c. 347–420) distinguished them from the Hebrew books. Protestants point out that the New Testament never quotes them directly as scripture. Reformers saw some teaching in them (e.g. on prayers for the dead) as unsupported elsewhere.
  • Judge it by: Who decides the canon: the Church, the Jewish tradition, or scripture's own witness? Which early list carries most weight? Does it change central Christian beliefs?

Catholic, Protestant and Orthodox Bibles share a core, but differ on the books found only in the Greek Septuagint.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Mission 2

Revelation: God makes himself known

  • Revelation: Christians believe God shows people what they could not find out alone: who God is and what God wants.
  • General revelation: through the world, nature and conscience: open to everyone.
  • Special revelation: through particular events and people: the prophets, the scriptures, and above all Jesus, the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
  • Christians believe the Bible records and passes on God's revelation: it is the Word of God.
Mission 2

Inspiration

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God guided the human writers.
  • Most Christians believe the writers used their own language, style and culture, while God worked through them.
  • So, for most Christians, the Bible is both fully human words and the Word of God.
  • Christians differ on what follows: is every detail without error, or the message God intended?
Mission 2Read the source

Catholic teaching

  • Dei Verbum (Second Vatican Council, 1965), chapter 3 (paraphrase):
  • God is the author of scripture; the human writers are true authors too, using their own powers (DV 11).
  • Scripture teaches without error the truth God wanted written for the sake of our salvation (DV 11).
  • To understand it, readers must look at what the human author meant, including the literary form (DV 12).
  • CCC 105–108 (paraphrase): God inspired the human authors (105–106); the books teach the truth for our salvation (107); Christianity is not "a religion of the book" but of the living Word, Jesus (108).
  • Catholics read scripture with Tradition, guided by the Magisterium (the Church's teaching authority).
  • Q: According to Dei Verbum, what kind of truth is scripture guaranteed to teach? (The truth God wanted written for our salvation.)
Quick check
?What does it mean to say the Bible is inspired?
Mission 2Diversity

Three views of the Bible's authority

  • The literal Word of God: every word is from God and true in every detail, including history and science (e.g. creation in six 24-hour days). Held by fundamentalist Christians and some evangelicals.
  • The inspired (revealed) Word of God: God's message comes through human writers; some parts are symbolic or reflect their time. Held by most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants.
  • A source of guidance and teaching: written by people inspired by their faith in God; a wise and treasured guide, open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Implications: views on creation and science, on women's roles in church, on ethical questions.

Mission 2

The Bible among other authorities

  • Many Protestants: sola scriptura: scripture alone is the final authority; preaching explains it.
  • Catholics: Scripture and Tradition together form one sacred deposit of God's Word, interpreted by the Magisterium (Dei Verbum 10).
  • Orthodox: scripture lives within Holy Tradition: the Creeds, the councils, the liturgy.
  • Anglicans often speak of scripture, tradition and reason; Methodists add experience.
  • Quakers: the Inner Light (God's Spirit within) guides; the Bible is valued but tested by the Spirit.
Mission 2

The Bible in daily life

  • Formal worship: set readings (a lectionary) at Mass or Communion; psalms sung; a sermon explains the readings.
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1662) sets daily Bible readings and psalms for Morning and Evening Prayer.
  • Informal worship: Bible study groups, house churches, preaching in free churches.
  • Home and private worship: daily reading and prayer. "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105)
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, reading plans and apps, commentaries.
  • Comfort and guidance: e.g. Psalm 23 at funerals; looking to the Bible when making decisions.
Quick check
?Josh uses an app that gives him a short Bible passage each morning with a few lines explaining it and a prayer. What is he using?
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Bible is the only authority Christians need."

  • Reasons for: the Bible is "God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16). Protestant sola scriptura: Church teaching and tradition can err, but God's Word does not. Every Christian can read it for themselves.
  • Reasons against: the Bible needs interpreting, and Christians disagree on what it means. The Church chose which books belong in it (see Mission 1). Catholics: Scripture, Tradition and Magisterium belong together. Modern questions (e.g. IVF, AI) are not addressed directly; reason and conscience are needed.
  • Another view: many Protestants hold scripture as the highest authority, but still value creeds, reason and experience.
  • Judge it by: Can a text interpret itself? Who decides between interpretations? How do Christians handle questions the Bible does not mention?
Mission check

?An architect plans a cathedral. Over many years, different stonemasons carve it, each in their own style. The finished building still follows the architect's plan. Which Christian belief about the Bible does this picture best?

Most Christians believe the Bible is God's Word through human words. They differ on how literally to read it and on who interprets it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Who decides what Catholics believe, and why are they trusted to do it?

Make a guess: Who decides what Catholics believe, and why are they trusted to do it?

Mission 3

Scripture, Tradition, Magisterium

  • Scripture: the Bible, the written Word of God.
  • Tradition: the faith handed on by the apostles in the Church's life, creeds, worship and teaching: "hold the traditions which you were taught by us, whether by word or by letter" (2 Thessalonians 2:15).
  • Magisterium (Latin magister, teacher): the Church's living teaching office.
  • Catholic teaching: the three are so joined that none stands without the others (Dei Verbum 10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God; it serves it.
Mission 3

The living teaching office

  • Who: the pope and the bishops in communion with him (CCC 100, paraphrase).
  • Function: to interpret Scripture and Tradition authentically, and apply them to new questions.
  • CCC 100: the task of interpreting the Word of God authentically is entrusted to the Magisterium alone (paraphrase).
  • Why "living": new questions (medicine, technology, the economy) need answers faithful to the old truths.
  • Importance: it keeps Catholics united in one faith across the world and the centuries.
  • Catholics believe the Holy Spirit guides it: "he will guide you into all truth" (John 16:13).
Mission 3

Apostolic succession

  • Jesus to Peter: "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18). "Feed my sheep." (John 21:17)
  • To the apostles: "Whoever listens to you listens to me" (Luke 10:16).
  • Catholics believe the apostles appointed successors by the laying on of hands: "commit the same things to faithful men who will be able to teach others also" (2 Timothy 2:2).
  • Catholics believe bishops are the successors of the apostles, and the pope, Bishop of Rome, is the successor of Peter.
  • So, Catholics believe, their teaching authority comes from Christ, through an unbroken line.
Quick check
?According to Catholic teaching (CCC 100), who makes up the Magisterium?
Mission 3

Conciliar and pontifical

  • Conciliar: teaching given by the bishops of the world gathered in an ecumenical council with the pope.
  • Model: the Council of Jerusalem: "It seemed good to the Holy Spirit, and to us" (Acts 15:28).
  • The Catholic Church counts 21 ecumenical councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–65).
  • Pontifical (from pontifex, a title of the pope): teaching given by the pope himself.
  • Forms: encyclicals (letters to the whole Church, e.g. Laudato Si', 2015, on care for creation), apostolic exhortations (e.g. Evangelii Gaudium, 2013).
  • Catholics believe both carry authority because both are the voice of the successors of the apostles.
Quick check
?In 1965, bishops from around the world, meeting with the pope at the Second Vatican Council, approved a document on the Church in the modern world. Which kind of magisterium is this?
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Magisterium is the most important source of authority for Catholics."

  • For: CCC 100 gives it alone the task of authentic interpretation. Without it, people read the Bible in conflicting ways. Its authority comes from Christ through the apostles (Luke 10:16). It answers new questions Scripture does not address directly.
  • Against: it is not above the Word of God but serves it (Dei Verbum 10): Scripture is primary. Conscience is where God's voice echoes within each person (Gaudium et Spes 16, paraphrase). The three sources depend on each other, so none is "most" important.
  • Other Christians: most Protestants hold Scripture alone as the final authority (Luther, 1521: unless convinced by Scripture and plain reason). The Orthodox accept councils and Tradition but not the pope's universal authority.
  • Judge it by: Can authority be ranked if the sources depend on each other? What prevents division? What role is left for conscience?
Mission check

?A new technology raises a moral question that the Bible never mentions. According to Catholic teaching, how would the Church give authoritative guidance on it?

For Catholics, authority is not one person's opinion: the pope and bishops, as successors of the apostles, serve and interpret the Word handed on to them.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why did a meeting of bishops in the 1960s change what Sunday Mass looks like in every parish?

Make a guess: Why did a meeting of bishops in the 1960s change what Sunday Mass looks like in every parish?

Mission 4

What is an ecumenical council?

  • An ecumenical council is a gathering of the bishops of the whole world, with the Pope, to teach and decide for the whole Church.
  • Its teaching is part of the conciliar Magisterium (see Mission 3): the Church's teaching authority exercised together.
  • Catholics count 21 such councils, from Nicaea (325) to the Second Vatican Council (1962–1965), called "Vatican II" because it was the second council held at the Vatican (in St Peter's Basilica), after Vatican I (1869–1870).
  • Earlier councils mostly met to settle a dispute or answer a challenge (e.g. Trent, 1545–1563, after the Reformation).
  • Vatican II was different: no single crisis. Its aim was renewal: John XXIII's word was aggiornamento, Italian for "bringing up to date".
Mission 4

The story, 1959–1965

  • 25 January 1959: Pope John XXIII surprises the Church by announcing a council.
  • 11 October 1962: the Council opens. Over 2,000 bishops attend, from every continent.
  • 3 June 1963: John XXIII dies. The new Pope, Paul VI, continues the Council.
  • Four autumn sessions (1962, 1963, 1964, 1965), with drafts revised between them.
  • Every document was debated and voted on by the bishops, then approved by the Pope.
  • 8 December 1965: the Council closes, having approved 16 documents: 4 constitutions, 9 decrees, 3 declarations.
  • Christians from other Churches (Orthodox, Anglican, Protestant) were invited as observers.

Mission 4

The four key documents

The four constitutions are the Council's most important teaching.

  • Dei Verbum ("The Word of God", 1965): how God reveals himself; Scripture and Tradition.
  • Lumen Gentium ("Light of the nations", 1964): what the Church is.
  • Sacrosanctum Concilium ("This sacred Council", 1963): the liturgy (the Church's worship).
  • Gaudium et Spes ("Joy and hope", 1965): the Church in the modern world.
  • Each takes its name from its first words in Latin.
Mission 4

Dei Verbum: God speaks

  • God reveals himself above all in Jesus Christ.
  • Revelation is passed on through Scripture and Tradition, which flow from the same divine source and form one deposit of faith (DV 9–10, paraphrase).
  • The Magisterium is not above the Word of God but serves it: it teaches only what has been handed on (DV 10, paraphrase).
  • Easy access to the Bible should be open to all; Catholics are urged to read it (DV 22, 25).
  • For Catholic living: Bible study groups, Scripture in the vernacular, a longer selection of readings at Mass.
Mission 4

Lumen Gentium: what the Church is

  • The Church is a mystery: a visible community and a spiritual reality, a sign of union with God (LG 1).
  • It is the People of God (ch. 2) before it is a hierarchy: all the baptised share in Christ's work.
  • The hierarchy (ch. 3): bishops, as successors of the apostles, with the Pope as head; collegiality.
  • The laity (ch. 4) and a universal call to holiness (ch. 5): holiness is for everyone, not just priests and religious.
  • Mary (ch. 8) and the Church's hope of heaven.
  • For Catholic living: lay readers, catechists and parish councils; every Catholic called to be a saint.
Mission 4

Sacrosanctum Concilium: worship renewed

  • The liturgy is the summit towards which the Church's activity is directed and the source of its strength (SC 10, paraphrase).
  • Aim: the full, active participation of the whole people (SC 14), not just the priest.
  • Permitted the vernacular (local language) alongside Latin (SC 36); this soon became normal practice.
  • More Scripture in worship; simpler, clearer rites.
  • For Catholic living: Mass in English, the people saying the responses, and later the priest usually facing the people across the altar (introduced by guidelines in 1964, after the Council's first document).

Mission 4

Gaudium et Spes: the Church in the world

  • Opens: the joys and hopes, griefs and anxieties of people today, especially the poor, are those of Christ's followers too (GS 1, paraphrase; more in an earlier lesson).
  • Human dignity, the common good, family, culture, economics, peace (GS 78).
  • For Catholic living: Catholics can welcome science (e.g. the Big Bang, evolution) as studying God's creation; social action is part of faith.
Mission 4

What changed?

  • Worship: Mass in the vernacular; altar facing the people; lay readers and ministers of Holy Communion.
  • Laity: parish councils; lay people as catechists, chaplains and teachers of the faith.
  • Other Christians: ecumenism (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964): dialogue and prayer for unity.
  • Other religions: Nostra Aetate (1965): the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions (NA 2, paraphrase); a new relationship with Jews.
Mission 4Diversity

One Council, different readings

  • The Church's official teaching receives the Council as the work of the Holy Spirit, and it shapes parish life everywhere.
  • Renewal in continuity: Benedict XVI (2005) argued that the Council must be read in continuity with earlier teaching, not as a break.
  • "Spirit of the Council": some Catholics want reforms to go further (e.g. more lay and women's leadership) and appeal to the Council's spirit of openness.
  • Traditionalists: some prefer the older Latin Mass; a small group (the Society of St Pius X, founded by Archbishop Marcel Lefebvre, 1970) rejects parts of the Council's teaching, e.g. on religious liberty and ecumenism.
  • Other Christians: many Protestants and Anglicans welcomed the vernacular, Bible reading and dialogue.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Vatican II was the most important event in the Catholic Church in the last 100 years."

  • Reasons for: it changed the experience of every Catholic every Sunday (SC); it gave lay people a new role (LG); it opened dialogue with other Christians, other religions and science (GS 36, Nostra Aetate).
  • Reasons against: its teaching is not new faith but "aggiornamento", so the deeper continuity matters more; some say later developments (e.g. the global growth of the Church in Africa and Asia, or the papacy of John Paul II) shaped today's Church as much; traditionalist critics see some changes as loss.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "important": doctrine, daily practice, or numbers affected? Did the Council change the faith or how it is lived? Whose experience of the Church do you measure?
Mission check

?A parishioner who remembers Mass in the 1950s says: "Back then we watched the priest and listened to Latin. Now we all join in, in English." Which explanation of the change best fits the history of the Second Vatican Council?

Vatican II renewed how the Church worships, understands itself and meets the modern world, through bishops consulting, debating and voting together.

Mission 5: If every church building closed tomorrow, would the Church still exist?

Make a guess: If every church building closed tomorrow, would the Church still exist?

Mission 5

The Church is people

  • In the New Testament, "church" translates the Greek ekklesia: an assembly, people called together.
  • The first Christians had no church buildings. They met in homes and in the Temple courts (Acts 2:46).
  • So, for Christians, the Church (capital C) is the community of everyone who follows Jesus.
  • A church (small c) is a building, or one local congregation.
  • Two pictures from the Bible describe this community: the Body of Christ and the People of God.
Mission 5Read the source

One body, many members

"For as the body is one and has many members, and all the members of the body, being many, are one body; so also is Christ." (1 Corinthians 12:12)

  • Paul wrote to Christians in Corinth who were divided and competing over whose gifts mattered most.
  • The foot cannot say it is not part of the body because it is not a hand; the ear cannot leave because it is not an eye (12:15–16, paraphrase).
  • "The eye can't tell the hand, 'I have no need for you'" (12:21). The weaker-looking parts are necessary (12:22).
  • "When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it. When one member is honoured, all the members rejoice with it." (12:26)
  • "Now you are the body of Christ, and members individually." (12:27)

Q1 What two mistakes does Paul warn against?

Show answer

Thinking you don't belong; thinking others aren't needed.

Q2 What should happen when one member suffers?

Show answer

All share it, and care for that member: 12:25–26.

Mission 5

Many gifts, one Spirit

  • 1 Corinthians 12:1–11: the Holy Spirit gives each Christian gifts (Greek charismata).
  • Paul's list: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, telling spirits apart, tongues and their interpretation.
  • Each gift is given "for the profit of all" (12:7): for building up the community, not for showing off.
  • The same Spirit gives them all (12:4, 11). No gift makes someone a better Christian.
  • So the body works because members are different, not in spite of it.

Mission 5

Romans 12: one body in Christ

  • "For even as we have many members in one body, and all the members don't have the same function, so we, who are many, are one body in Christ, and individually members of one another" (Romans 12:4–5).
  • Verse 6: "having gifts differing according to the grace that was given to us".
  • Gifts named next: prophecy, serving, teaching, encouraging, giving, leading, showing mercy (12:6–8).
  • "Members of one another": Christians belong to each other, not only to God.
  • Paul stresses humility: no one should think of themselves more highly than they ought (12:3, paraphrase).
  • In other letters (Ephesians 1:22–23; Colossians 1:18) Christ is called the head of the body.
Mission 5

The People of God

  • In the Old Testament God makes a covenant with Israel: "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:6).
  • The New Testament applies this to the Church: "you are a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God's own possession" (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Christians who "were not a people" are "now … God's people" (1 Peter 2:10).
  • Lumen Gentium (Vatican II, 1964), chapter 2, "The People of God": God chose to save people not as isolated individuals but as one people (paraphrase, LG 9).
  • The People of God is a pilgrim people: still on a journey towards God's Kingdom.
  • Body of Christ stresses unity with Christ; People of God stresses all the baptised together, laity as much as clergy.

Mission 5

The body in action: Acts 2

  • After Pentecost, the first Christians "continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42)
  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44)
  • They sold possessions and shared "according as anyone had need" (2:45).
  • They met daily, ate together "with gladness", and praised God (2:46–47).
Mission 5Diversity

What is "the Body"?

  • All Christians: Christ is the head; believers are the members; the Spirit unites them.
  • Catholic: the Church is a visible, ordered community; the one Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church, led by the pope and bishops (Lumen Gentium 8, paraphrase). Elements of holiness are found in other Churches too.
  • Catholic: the Eucharist builds the body: "we, who are many, are one body; for we all partake of the one loaf of bread" (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Orthodox: the Church is most fully the Body when it gathers for the Divine Liturgy around its bishop.
  • Many Protestants: the true Body is all genuine believers everywhere, known fully to God (the invisible Church), seen in local congregations.
  • 1B-3.5: some see denominations as different parts of one body; others see the divisions as wounds to the body.
Mission 5

Why it matters today

  • Belonging: every baptised person has a place and a gift; no one is a spectator.
  • Lay ministry: since Vatican II, Catholics stress the laity's part: readers, catechists, parish councils, charity work.
  • Care: "When one member suffers, all … suffer" (1 Corinthians 12:26): visiting the sick, food banks, praying for persecuted Christians.
  • Unity: Christians work together across denominations (ecumenism); Catholic teaching sees all the baptised as joined to Christ.
  • Mission: a pilgrim People of God looks outward, inviting others in.
  • 1B-3.5: the image also helps Christians explain why a growing, worldwide Church can be so diverse yet one.
Quick check
?A newspaper headline says: "Church closes: village loses its church." Many Christians would say the headline is only half right. Why?
Mission 5Evaluate

"The Church is the people, not the building."

  • Reasons for: ekklesia means assembly; the early Church met in homes (Acts 2:46); "where two or three are gathered together in my name, there I am" (Matthew 18:20); the Body of Christ is made of people (1 Corinthians 12:27).
  • Reasons against (the building matters too): it is the centre of worship and of the local community; Catholics and Orthodox believe Christ is present in the Eucharist and the tabernacle there; consecrated buildings, icons and altars help people pray; a visible building witnesses to the area.
  • Another view: "people and building": the building serves the people and expresses their faith.
  • Judge it by: Could the Church survive without buildings (history says it once did)? What is lost without a sacred space? Does the answer differ between denominations?
Mission 5Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the Church as the Body of Christ is important for Catholics today. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: every member has a gift. Source: "Now you are the body of Christ, and members individually" (1 Corinthians 12:27). Development: so lay Catholics take active roles, as readers or catechists, not only the priest.
  • Reason 2: members care for each other. Development: "When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it" (12:26), so parishes visit the sick and support those in need.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two developed reasons and one source of wisdom and authority, named precisely.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Bible is a library: Old Testament (law, history, writings, prophets) and New Testament (gospels, Acts, letters, Revelation).
  • The canon formed over centuries; the 27 New Testament books were agreed by the late 4th century.
  • Catholic Bibles have 73 books; Protestant Bibles have 66; Orthodox Bibles more.
  • The Council of Trent (1546) confirmed the Catholic list of 73 books.

Mission 2

  • Christians believe God reveals himself, and the Bible is the inspired ("God-breathed") record of that revelation (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Views of authority: literal Word, inspired Word (most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants), or a human guide inspired by faith.
  • The Bible is used in worship, at home, through reading aids and groups, for comfort and moral guidance.

Mission 3

  • Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium (pope and bishops in communion with him) belong together; the Magisterium serves the Word of God (CCC 100).
  • Apostolic succession: Catholics believe bishops succeed the apostles and the pope succeeds Peter.
  • Magisterium is conciliar (councils, e.g. Nicaea 325, Vatican II) or pontifical (popes, e.g. encyclicals); it can be ordinary (everyday) or extraordinary (solemn definitions).

Mission 4

  • Vatican II (1962–1965), called by John XXIII and closed by Paul VI, sought renewal (aggiornamento) of the Church's life.
  • Its four key documents: Dei Verbum (revelation), Lumen Gentium (the Church), Sacrosanctum Concilium (liturgy), Gaudium et Spes (the Church in the modern world).

Mission 5

  • The Church (ekklesia) is the community of Christians; a church building is where they gather.
  • 1 Corinthians 12: one body, many members, many gifts from one Spirit; every member needed, all share suffering and joy.
  • Romans 12:4–6 and 1 Peter 2:9–10: "members of one another" and one People of God; Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants understand the Body in different ways.
