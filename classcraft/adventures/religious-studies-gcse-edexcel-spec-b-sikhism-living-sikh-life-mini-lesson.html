Make a guess: Why does a Sikh place of worship have a kitchen, a throne and a flag you can see from far away?

Mission 1

The door of the Guru

  • Gurdwara means "door (or gateway) of the Guru".
  • Any building where the Guru Granth Sahib is installed with honour is a gurdwara.
  • Guru Nanak's community at Kartarpur met in a dharamsal (place of faith); later such places came to be called gurdwaras.
  • Three purposes: to house the Guru; to gather the sangat (community); to serve anyone who comes.
  • Through this "door", Sikhs believe they come into the Guru's presence and closer to God.
Mission 1

Outside: flag and doors

  • Nishan Sahib: a tall flagpole wrapped in cloth, with a triangular saffron (or blue) flag bearing the Khanda symbol.
  • It tells travellers: here is a gurdwara, with shelter, food and welcome for all.
  • The flag and its cloth are renewed every year at Vaisakhi.
  • Four doors: the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar has doors on all four sides.
  • Meaning: people from every direction, of every caste and every faith, are welcome.
  • Most local gurdwaras keep the idea by opening their doors to all, even if the building has one entrance.
  • On entering: shoes off, head covered, hands washed; no tobacco or alcohol.
Mission 1

The prayer hall

  • The main hall is the diwan hall (court); the Guru Granth Sahib is its sovereign.
  • Takht: the raised throne on which the Guru Granth Sahib rests, higher than everyone in the room.
  • Palki: the ornate canopied structure, like a royal carriage, around the takht.
  • Manji sahib: the small stand on which the scripture lies, with cushions and cloths (rumalas).
  • Chanani: the canopy above it, a sign of honour.
  • Chaur: the whisk waved over the scripture by the reader or a sewadar, as over a monarch.
  • Everyone sits on the floor at the same level: no seats of honour. Often men and women sit on different sides, by custom.
  • No statues and no pictures to bow to: Sikhs bow (matha tekna) only to the Guru Granth Sahib, and may leave an offering.
Mission 1

Sach khand and the langar hall

  • Sach khand ("realm of truth"): the room where the Guru Granth Sahib is laid to rest each night (sukhasan) and brought out at dawn (prakash).
  • Its name links to the highest stage of the soul's journey to God.
  • Langar hall: a kitchen and dining hall where a free meal is served to anyone.
  • Volunteers cook, serve and wash up. People sit in rows on the floor (pangat), side by side.

Mission 1Read the source

Rules and praise for the Guru's door

Rahit Maryada, chapters 4–6 (summary):

  • A Sikh should join the sangat at the gurdwara, where Gurbani is heard and understood.
  • No one of any country, religion or caste is barred from entering for darshan (a sight of the Guru).
  • A Nishan Sahib should stand at every gurdwara. The Guru Granth Sahib is opened every morning and closed at night.
  • No images, idols or ritual lamps; everyone sits without distinction of caste.
  • Ch. 5: kirtan uses only Gurbani (and set approved writings). Ch. 6: how the daily hukam is taken.

Guru Granth Sahib 1391 (paraphrase): a hymn in praise of the Guru says the sight of the Guru's door drives away the darkness of ignorance, and the true congregation (sat sangat) is exalted.

Mission 1

What happens there: the gurdwara's role

  • Worship: morning and evening prayers; kirtan (hymns sung with harmonium and tabla); katha (talks); Ardas (standing prayer); the day's hukamnama.
  • Karah prashad: a sweet offering (flour, sugar, ghee) given to everyone equally at the end.
  • Ceremonies: naming, Amrit initiation, weddings (Anand Karaj), funeral prayers, Akhand Paths.
  • Learning: Punjabi and Gurmukhi classes, kirtan lessons, Sikh history, gatka (martial art).
  • Community: langar every day, help for elderly and new arrivals, food banks, shelter in emergencies.
  • In Britain many gurdwaras hold the main gathering on Sunday, when most people are free; daily prayers continue all week.
  • Role: the gurdwara is where Sikhs meet the Guru and the sangat, and practise sewa.

Mission 1Diversity

How important is the gurdwara today?

  • Essential: the Guru Granth Sahib is present there, and Sikhs need the sangat to grow spiritually. Many visit daily.
  • Helpful, not essential: God is everywhere; nam japna and Nitnem happen at home and at work. Some Sikhs visit mainly on Sundays and festivals.
  • Keep features as they are: e.g. langar eaten sitting on the floor, as a sign of equality; services in Punjabi.
  • Adapt features: e.g. chairs for elderly or disabled people; English translations on screens for young Sikhs and visitors.
  • Sikhs disagree on how far adaptation should go; all keep the Guru Granth Sahib at the centre.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The gurdwara is the most important part of Sikh life."

  • Reasons for: the Guru Granth Sahib is there; worship with the sangat; langar and sewa happen there; ceremonies that mark life's stages take place there.
  • Reasons against: the Gurus taught that God is everywhere; remembering God's Name in daily life (GGS 305) matters more than a building. Honest work and sharing happen at home and at work. Sikhs without a gurdwara nearby can still live as Sikhs.
  • Another view: the gurdwara and daily life support each other: what Sikhs learn and practise there shapes the rest of the week.
  • Judge it by: Is "Sikh life" mainly worship, or the whole of life? What did the Gurus stress: place, people, or practice?

Question: Explain two features of a gurdwara and why they are important to Sikhs.

  • Feature 2: the langar hall. Development: everyone eats the same free meal sitting in rows, which puts into practice the Sikh belief that all people are equal before God.

Mission 2

Amritsar: the pool of nectar

  • Amritsar (Punjab, India) means "pool of nectar".
  • Guru Ram Das, the fourth Guru, began the city in the 1570s and had a large pool (sarovar) dug.
  • Guru Arjan, the fifth Guru, completed the pool and built a gurdwara in the middle of it.
  • Traders and craftworkers settled; the city grew into a centre of both faith and business.
Mission 2

Building the Harmandir Sahib

  • Harmandir Sahib: "temple of God". Often called the Golden Temple; also Darbar Sahib.
  • Building began in 1588. Sikh tradition says the Muslim Sufi saint Mian Mir was invited to lay the foundation stone: a sign of openness.
  • In 1604 Guru Arjan installed the newly compiled Adi Granth (the scripture, later the Guru Granth Sahib) inside; Baba Buddha became its first reader (granthi).
  • Destroyed in the 1700s by Afghan invaders and rebuilt by the Sikhs.
  • Maharaja Ranjit Singh covered the upper storeys in gold in the early 1800s: hence "Golden Temple".
Mission 2

Features and their meaning

  • Four doors, one on each side: open to people of every direction, caste and religion.
  • Built lower than the land around it: visitors step down to enter, a lesson in humility.
  • Stands in the sarovar, reached by a causeway from a gateway (Darshani Deori).
  • A marble walkway (parikrama) runs round the pool.
  • Inside: the Guru Granth Sahib, and kirtan (hymns from it) sung from early morning to late at night.
  • Facing the causeway: the Akal Takht. Nearby: the huge langar hall.

Mission 2

A pilgrim's day

  • Before dawn: the Guru Granth Sahib is carried in a palanquin (palki) from the Akal Takht and opened in the Harmandir Sahib (prakash).
  • Pilgrims cover their heads, remove shoes and wash their feet.
  • Many bathe (ishnan) in the sarovar, then walk the parikrama clockwise.
  • They queue to bow (matha tekna) before the Guru Granth Sahib and listen to kirtan.
  • They receive karah prashad.
  • They do sewa (selfless service): cleaning the walkway, minding shoes, washing dishes.
  • Langar: volunteers serve a free vegetarian meal to a huge number of people every day, all sitting on the floor in rows (pangat), whatever their wealth, caste or religion.
  • At night: the Guru Granth Sahib is carried back to the Akal Takht to rest (sukhasan).
Mission 2Read the source

The true congregation

  • Guru Granth Sahib 1391 (paraphrase): the true congregation (sat sangat) is sublime; those who keep their minds on the Guru there become humble and are absorbed in God's love.
  • Guru Granth Sahib 4 (Japji, paraphrase): pilgrimage, self-discipline, kindness and charity bring only a little merit. Real cleansing comes from hearing and loving God's Name, in the shrine within.

Q1 According to GGS 1391, what does joining the sangat do for a person?

Show answer

It makes them humble and filled with God's love.

Q2 Does GGS 4 say pilgrimage is wrong?

Show answer

No: it gives "a little merit", but the inner pilgrimage of the Name matters most.

Mission 2Diversity

Should Sikhs go on pilgrimage?

  • Pilgrimage is not compulsory in Sikhism. Guru Nanak criticised the belief that bathing at holy places washes away sin.
  • Reasons many Sikhs visit: to see where the Gurus lived and taught; to feel the history; to join a huge sangat; to do sewa; to renew faith.
  • Other historical gurdwaras: Nankana Sahib (Guru Nanak's birthplace, Pakistan); Kartarpur (where he died); Anandpur Sahib (where the Khalsa began); the five takhts.
  • Some Sikhs stress the inner journey: God is everywhere; the local gurdwara and daily Nam Japna matter more than travel.
  • Some believe bathing in the sarovar brings healing; others call this superstition the Gurus rejected.
  • For Sikhs in Britain, a visit can be a link with family roots in Punjab.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Every Sikh should visit the Harmandir Sahib."

  • Reasons for: it is the place the Gurus built and the scripture was first installed; the sangat, kirtan and langar there show Sikh values on a vast scale; it strengthens identity, especially for Sikhs far from Punjab.
  • Reasons against: the Gurus taught that God is everywhere and that outward pilgrimage brings "a little merit"; cost and travel are not possible for all; daily Nam Japna and sewa at the local gurdwara matter more.
  • Another view: a visit is a gift, not a duty: worthwhile if it deepens inner devotion.
  • Judge it by: What did the Gurus teach about outward ritual? Does the experience change how a person lives? Is "should" a duty or an encouragement?

Mission 3

What is langar?

  • Langar: the free meal served in every gurdwara, and the kitchen where it is made.
  • Anyone may eat: any faith or none, any caste, rich or poor, man or woman.
  • People sit side by side in rows (pangat) and eat the same simple food.
  • It is cooked, served and cleared away by volunteers.
  • Two Sikh ideas meet here: equality of all people, and sewa (selfless service).
Mission 3

Beginnings: Guru Nanak

  • The true bargain (Sacha Sauda): sent to trade, the young Nanak used the money to feed hungry holy men. Sikhs see the spirit of langar here.
  • At Kartarpur (c. 1522), Nanak's community farmed together, worshipped together and ate together.
  • Sharing food from honest work became part of the Sikh way: vand chhakna (sharing with others).
  • In a society divided by caste, where many would not eat with "lower" castes, eating together was a radical act.
Mission 3

Mata Khivi and Guru Angad

  • Guru Angad (2nd Guru) kept the langar going at Khadur.
  • His wife, Mata Khivi, ran the langar: she organised the kitchen and served everyone who came.
  • She is remembered for her generous care and for the rich rice pudding (kheer) she served.
  • Many Sikhs see her as a model of women's leadership in sewa from the earliest days.
Mission 3Read the source

Guru Granth Sahib 967

Guru Granth Sahib 967 (paraphrase): the bards Satta and Balwand praise Mata Khivi as a good woman who gives cool, leafy shade to all. She shares out the bounty of the Guru's langar; the rice pudding with ghee is as sweet as amrit (nectar).

  • The same hymn describes the Guru's langar of the Word (Shabad) as running constantly, its supplies never running out.

Q1 Which two things does the hymn say the langar gives?

Show answer

Food for the body, and the Guru's Word for the soul.

Q2 What image shows Khivi's care for everyone?

Show answer

Cool shade that shelters all.

Mission 3

Guru Amar Das: first langar, then the Guru

  • Guru Amar Das (3rd Guru, at Goindwal) made a rule: "first pangat, then sangat" (first eat together in the rows, then join the congregation).
  • Everyone who came to see him had to eat langar first, sitting on the floor with everyone else.
  • Sikh tradition says even the Mughal Emperor Akbar sat in the rows and ate langar before meeting the Guru.
  • The message: before the Guru, an emperor and a beggar are equal.

Mission 3

How langar works

  • Food: simple vegetarian dishes (e.g. dal, vegetables, chapatis, rice pudding), so people of any faith or diet can eat.
  • Cooking: volunteers prepare it with heads covered and hands washed; many recite "Waheguru" or Gurbani as they work.
  • Serving: people sit in rows on the floor; volunteers walk the rows serving; all receive the same.
  • Manners: take what you will eat, waste nothing, receive with thanks.
  • Funding: donations of food and money, including daswandh (giving a tenth of income).
  • Clearing up: washing dishes and floors is honoured sewa, done by doctors and teachers alongside everyone else.
Mission 3

Langar as sewa: tan, man, dhan

  • Sikhs serve with body, mind and wealth (tan, man, dhan).
  • Tan (body): chopping, cooking, serving, washing up.
  • Man (mind): organising, planning the rota, remembering God while working, welcoming guests.
  • Dhan (wealth): giving money, flour, vegetables, gas and equipment.
  • Sewa is done without seeking reward or praise. Sikhs believe it helps to defeat haumai (ego) and to become gurmukh (God-centred).
  • Purposes of langar: equality, humility, sharing, hospitality, and serving God by serving people.

Mission 3

Langar today

  • Every gurdwara serves langar; the largest, at the Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar, feeds very large numbers every day.
  • Langar for the homeless: Sikh groups in many British cities serve free hot food on the streets.
  • Disaster relief: Sikh charities set up langar after floods, earthquakes and wars, and for refugees.
  • During the Covid-19 pandemic, many gurdwaras cooked and delivered meals to people self-isolating and to health workers.
  • Significance: langar takes the gurdwara's equality and sewa into the wider world.
Mission 3Diversity

Different practices and views

  • Floor or chairs? Many Sikhs insist on sitting on the floor, as the Gurus taught, a sign of equality. Some gurdwaras provide tables and chairs, especially for elderly and disabled people. The Akal Takht has ruled that langar should be eaten on the floor; practice still varies.
  • Simple or rich food? Some say langar should stay simple, so that no one competes to impress; others serve festive dishes on special days.
  • Inside or outside? Some stress langar in the gurdwara with the sangat; others see street langar for the homeless as the truest form.
  • All agree: langar is free, open to everyone, and given as sewa.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Langar is the most important form of sewa."

  • Reasons for: it involves tan, man and dhan at once; it goes back to Guru Nanak and was made central by Guru Amar Das; it feeds people and teaches equality every day.
  • Reasons against: sewa takes many forms: caring for the sick, teaching, cleaning shoes at the gurdwara, giving daswandh. Bhai Kanhaiya's care for the wounded is also held up as sewa. Some sewa outside the gurdwara (e.g. disaster relief) reaches people langar in a gurdwara cannot.
  • Another view: the attitude matters more than the task: any service done humbly, without wanting praise, is true sewa.
  • Judge it by: Who benefits, and how many? Does the server's attitude matter more than the act? What did the Gurus themselves practise?

Mission 4

What nam japna means

  • Nam: God's Name, meaning God's nature and presence in everything, not just a word.
  • Japna: to repeat, recite and meditate on.
  • Nam japna (meditating on God's Name) is the first of the three things Guru Nanak taught every Sikh to do:
  • Nam japna (remember God), kirat karni (earn an honest living), vand chhakna (share with others).
  • Your spec writes Nam Japna; it is also called nam simran (remembering the Name).
Mission 4

Ways of nam japna

  • Repeating "Waheguru" ("Wonderful Lord"), the name most Sikhs use for God, silently or aloud, often in rhythm with the breath.
  • Some Sikhs use a mala (string of prayer beads) to keep focus; many do not.
  • Reciting and singing Gurbani: the hymns of the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Amrit vela: the early hours before dawn, which Sikhs see as the best time.
  • In the gurdwara: the sangat sings kirtan and chants "Waheguru" together in simran sessions.
  • In daily life: remembering God while working, travelling or cooking, not only at set times.
Mission 4

Why it matters

  • Sikhs believe the mind is pulled away from God by haumai (ego) and the five thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride).
  • Nam japna turns the mind back to God, making a person gurmukh (God-centred) rather than manmukh (self-centred).
  • It brings inner peace and the courage to face hardship (chardi kala, rising spirits).
  • Sikhs believe it prepares the soul for mukti (liberation), which comes by God's grace (nadar).
  • It is not magic: the aim is a changed heart, shown in honest work and sharing.
Mission 4Read the source

Through the Name, pain is removed and the ignorant become wise. Nanak is saved through the Name; other rituals only please other people.

Mission 4

Prayer at home: Nitnem

  • Nitnem: the daily set prayers, read from a gutka (prayer book), after bathing, with head covered.
  • Morning (amrit vela): Japji Sahib (Guru Nanak), Jaap Sahib and Tav-Prasad Savaiye (Guru Gobind Singh). Many add Chaupai Sahib and Anand Sahib.
  • Evening: Rehras Sahib.
  • Night, before sleep: Kirtan Sohila.
  • Some families also gather to read, sing kirtan, or take a hukamnama if the Guru Granth Sahib is kept at home.
  • Role: it shapes the day around God, from waking to sleeping, and teaches children the prayers.
Mission 4

Different prayers, different purposes

  • Nitnem: praise and remembrance at fixed times every day.
  • Ardas: a standing prayer of petition and thanks, said at the end of worship and before or after any important task (a journey, an exam, a meal).
  • Simran: personal, silent remembrance of God's Name at any time.
  • Optional banis: e.g. Sukhmani Sahib (Guru Arjan), read for peace of mind.
  • Why have different prayers? Each meets a need: to praise, to ask, to thank, to stay calm, to remember God through the whole day.
Mission 4

Kirtan and Ardas in the gurdwara

  • Kirtan: singing Gurbani to music (harmonium, tabla), led by ragis. Guru Nanak sang his hymns with Bhai Mardana.
  • Sikhs believe singing and hearing Gurbani is itself nam japna: the mind is absorbed in the Guru's word.
  • Ardas: the sangat stands facing the Guru Granth Sahib, hands joined.
  • It remembers God, the Ten Gurus, the Guru Granth Sahib, and Sikhs who gave their lives for their faith.
  • It ends with a prayer for all: "by your will, may all be blessed" (sarbat da bhala).

Mission 4Diversity

Sikh and Christian prayer at home

  • Similar: both pray at home, alone and as families; both praise, thank and ask God; both use set prayers and free personal prayer.
  • Similar: repetition helps focus: Sikhs repeat "Waheguru"; many Catholics pray the Rosary; Orthodox Christians repeat the Jesus Prayer.
  • Different: Sikh Nitnem is a fixed pattern for every day (morning, evening, night). Most Christians are encouraged to pray daily, without one required pattern (though clergy and some lay people keep a set daily office).
  • Different: Sikh prayers are the Gurus' hymns (Gurbani); Christians most often pray in their own words or with the Lord's Prayer (Matthew 6:9–13), addressing God as Father, through Jesus.
  • Different: Christians often say grace before meals; Sikhs often say Ardas before eating, especially in langar.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Nam japna is the most important Sikh practice."

  • Reasons for: it is the first of Guru Nanak's three teachings; GGS 305 makes remembering God the mark of a true Sikh; it overcomes haumai and leads towards mukti.
  • Reasons against: Guru Nanak taught three duties together: nam japna without honest work and sharing would be empty. Sewa and langar express faith in action. For Khalsa Sikhs, keeping Amrit and the Five Ks is also central.
  • Another view: the practices are one: honest work and sewa done while remembering God are nam japna in daily life.
  • Judge it by: Can inner devotion and outward action be separated? What did the Gurus say makes someone a Sikh?

Mission 5

An unbroken reading

  • Akhand Path: a continuous, unbroken (akhand) reading (path) of the whole Guru Granth Sahib, from the first page to the last.
  • All 1,430 angs, read aloud, without stopping: it takes about 48 hours.
  • A team of trained readers (pathis) takes turns, often about two hours each.
  • At each change, the new reader joins mid-line so that not a word is missed.
Mission 5

Beginning and end

  • Beginning: karah prashad is prepared; six verses of Anand Sahib are sung; Ardas is said; a hukamnama is taken; the reading starts.
  • During: the sangat comes and goes to listen at all hours; langar is served throughout.
  • End (bhog): the final pages are read; Anand Sahib is sung; Ardas is said; a hukamnama is taken; karah prashad and langar are shared.
  • Often kirtan is sung at the bhog, and the family who arranged the path thank the sangat.
Mission 5Read the source

Rahit Maryada, chapter 7

Rahit Maryada, ch. 7 (summary):

  • An Akhand Path, taking about 48 hours, may be held in times of hardship or of joy.
  • The reading must be clear and correct. Reading so fast that listeners cannot follow is disrespect to the Guru.
  • The family or sangat should take part themselves: a reader must not be left reading with no one listening.
  • Placing a water pot, coconut or lamp beside the reading as a ritual is against Sikh teaching (Gurmat).
  • The start (Anand Sahib, Ardas, hukamnama) and the end (final pages, Anand Sahib, Ardas, karah prashad) are set out.

Mission 5

Where did it come from?

  • Its exact origin is not recorded.
  • Many Sikhs link it to the 18th century, when Sikhs were persecuted and often scattered. Groups would read the whole scripture in one continuous sitting, so that the complete Guru's word could be heard when they could gather.
  • Some traditions trace continuous readings back to the time of Guru Gobind Singh.
  • Since then it has become part of Sikh life worldwide: in gurdwaras every week and in homes.
Mission 5

When is an Akhand Path held?

  • Joy: a birth, a wedding, moving into a new home, opening a business, success in exams.
  • Sorrow or hardship: after a death, during illness, at a time of trouble.
  • Festivals and gurpurbs: timed so that the bhog falls on the morning of the festival day.
  • Anniversaries: e.g. of a family member's death.
  • It may be held in a gurdwara or, if a clean, separate room is ready, at home.
Mission 5

Why it matters

  • For the community: the sangat hears the whole of the Guru's word together; many share the sewa (reading, listening, cooking langar, giving money); it unites families and gurdwaras at key moments.
  • For the individual: a way to thank God in joy and find comfort in sorrow; hearing Gurbani steadies the mind (nam japna); some Sikhs train to read, a deep act of devotion.
  • How Sikhs take part: reading a turn, listening at any hour (even through the night), cooking or serving langar, donating, attending the bhog.
Mission 5Diversity

Akhand Path and other complete readings

  • Sahaj Path (easy reading): a complete reading at a slower pace, over days, weeks or months, at home or in the gurdwara, with time to understand.
  • Saptahik Path: a complete reading spread over seven days.
  • Some Sikhs see the Akhand Path as a powerful offering of the whole community.
  • Others worry that paying readers who read with no one listening, or holding one only for good luck, turns it into ritual: the Rahit Maryada warns against both.
  • Many say what matters is listening and living by the words, whichever kind of reading is chosen.

Mission 5Evaluate

"An Akhand Path is the best way for Sikhs to mark an important event."

  • Reasons for: the whole Guru's word is heard; the sangat shares the sewa; it fits joy and sorrow alike; it ends with Ardas, hukamnama and langar, bringing together worship, guidance and equality.
  • Reasons against: a Sahaj Path gives more time to understand the words; nam japna and sewa can mark events every day; if no one listens, it becomes ritual; it costs time and money not every family has.
  • Another view: the occasion matters less than the attitude: any act that turns the family to the Guru marks the event well.
  • Judge it by: Is hearing every word or understanding the words more important? Who takes part? Does it change how people live afterwards?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gurdwara = door of the Guru: Nishan Sahib and the open-door idea outside; takht, palki, chanani, chaur and rumalas inside; sach khand and langar hall.
  • Rahit Maryada chs 4–6 set the rules (open to all, Nishan Sahib, no images); GGS 1391 praises the Guru's door and the sat sangat.

Mission 2

  • Guru Ram Das began Amritsar; Guru Arjan built the Harmandir Sahib (1588–1604) and installed the scripture there.
  • Four doors (open to all) and a lower level (humility); pilgrims bathe, walk the parikrama, hear kirtan, do sewa and eat langar.

Mission 3

  • Langar began with Guru Nanak's sharing, grew with Mata Khivi and Guru Angad, and was made a rule of equality by Guru Amar Das ("first pangat, then sangat").
  • GGS 967 praises Mata Khivi's langar and the Guru's langar of the Word.
  • Langar is sewa in action (tan, man, dhan), done humbly; today it reaches far beyond the gurdwara.

Mission 4

  • Nam japna (meditating on God's Name) is the first of Guru Nanak's three duties; repeating "Waheguru", Gurbani and kirtan turn the mind from ego to God.
  • Prayer at home: Nitnem (morning, evening, night), Ardas, simran; GGS 305 calls a Sikh to rise early and remember God all day.
  • GGS 1142: the Name is the true pilgrimage; compare Sikh Nitnem with Christian prayer at home.

Mission 5

  • Akhand Path: unbroken reading of all 1,430 angs, about 48 hours, by relays of readers; starts and ends (bhog) with Anand Sahib, Ardas, hukamnama and karah prashad.
  • Rahit Maryada ch. 7: in joy or hardship; clear reading; family and sangat must listen; no ritual objects.
  • Held for births, weddings, deaths and gurpurbs; Sahaj Path is a slower complete reading.
