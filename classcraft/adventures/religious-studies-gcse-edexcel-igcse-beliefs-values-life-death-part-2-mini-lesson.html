Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do so many religions make marriage a promise before God, not just a legal contract?

Make a guess: Why do so many religions make marriage a promise before God, not just a legal contract?

Mission 1

What is marriage?

  • Marriage: a legal union of two people, publicly promising to share their lives.
  • Religious view: also a sacred commitment: a covenant (a binding promise before God), a sacrament (Catholic, Orthodox) or a contract blessed by God (Islam).
  • UK law: a wedding can be civil (register office, approved venue) or religious.
  • Same-sex couples have been able to marry in Great Britain since 2014 (more in Mission 3).
  • Civil partnership: a legal union with almost the same rights, without the word "marriage".
Mission 1

Christian purposes of marriage

  • Lifelong, faithful union: "a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his wife, and they will be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24)
  • Purposes: mutual love, help and comfort; a faithful sexual relationship; having and bringing up children; a stable base for family and society.
  • It reflects God's love: "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (Ephesians 5:25).

Mission 1

The Christian wedding

  • Welcome and purpose: the minister explains why marriage matters.
  • Readings: often 1 Corinthians 13 ("Love is patient and is kind", 13:4).
  • Vows: promises before God for life: love, comfort, faithfulness.
  • Rings: an unbroken circle: love without end.
  • Declaration and blessing: the couple are pronounced married and blessed; the register is signed.
  • Differences in practice: Catholics often include a Nuptial Mass; Orthodox couples are crowned; Quakers marry in a meeting for worship, with no minister.

Mission 1Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: marriage (nikah) is a contract and a sunnah of the Prophet; offer and acceptance by both, a mahr (gift to the bride, Qur'an 4:4), and witnesses; many Sunni schools also require the bride's guardian (wali).
  • "He created for you helpmeets from yourselves that ye might find rest in them, and He ordained between you love and mercy." (Qur'an 30:21, Pickthall)
  • Judaism: marriage is kiddushin ("making holy") and a mitzvah; "It is not good for the man to be alone" (Genesis 2:18).
  • Jewish wedding: under a chuppah (canopy: the new home); the ketubah (marriage contract setting the husband's duties); a ring; the Seven Blessings; breaking a glass (remembering the Temple's destruction).
Mission 1Diversity

Hindu, Sikh and Buddhist traditions

  • Hinduism: marriage (vivaha) is a sacred samskara (life ritual) beginning the grihastha (householder) ashrama; it supports dharma, family and society.
  • Wedding: the couple circle the sacred fire; saptapadi (seven steps), each a promise.
  • Sikhism: the Anand Karaj ("blissful union"): the couple walk four times round the Guru Granth Sahib as the Lavan of Guru Ram Das are sung.
  • Buddhism: marriage is a social contract, not a religious duty; monks may give blessings. The Sigalovada Sutta gives five duties each for husband and wife: respect, faithfulness, care, sharing responsibility, kindness. Some see celibacy (monastic life) as the higher path.
Mission 1

Arranged, assisted, forced

  • Arranged marriage: families introduce and help choose a partner; both must consent. Common in some Hindu, Sikh and Muslim families in Britain.
  • Assisted marriage: the couple choose, with family support and introductions.
  • Forced marriage: one or both do not consent. Every religion here condemns it; it has been a crime in England and Wales since 2014.
  • Since 2023, no one under 18 may marry in England and Wales.
  • Supporters say arranged marriages unite families and share values; critics worry about family pressure.
Mission 1Diversity

Cohabitation and civil partnership

  • Cohabitation: a couple living together in a sexual relationship without marrying.
  • Catholic teaching: not acceptable; sex belongs within marriage.
  • Many Protestants: prefer marriage, but some accept committed cohabitation, often as a step towards it.
  • Muslims, Orthodox Jews, most Hindus and Sikhs: not acceptable.
  • Buddhists: no fixed rule; what matters is avoiding harm (the Third Precept).
  • Humanists: a personal choice; commitment, honesty and care matter more than the legal form.
  • Civil partnership: since 2005 for same-sex couples; since 2019 for opposite-sex couples too (England and Wales). Some churches allow ceremonies; many do not.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Marriage is no longer important today."

  • Reasons for: cohabitation is common and widely accepted; many marriages end in divorce; commitment does not need a certificate; Humanists see it as one choice among several.
  • Reasons against: marriage is a public, lifelong promise; legal protection for partners and children; for many believers it is a sacrament or covenant before God; many non-religious people still choose to marry.
  • Judge it by: Important to whom: the couple, children, society, God? Does a public promise change a relationship? Are religious and legal importance different?

Mission 2: Many religions call sex a gift from God. So why do they say it belongs only in marriage?

Make a guess: Many religions call sex a gift from God. So why do they say it belongs only in marriage?

Mission 2

Made sexual, male and female

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • In Genesis, God blesses them: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28). Jews and Christians read this as showing that sexuality is part of God's good creation.
  • Genesis 2: the man and woman become "one flesh" (2:24); they "were both naked, and they were not ashamed" (2:25).
  • Human sexuality: our sexual identity, attraction and relationships. Religions see it as a gift with a purpose.
Mission 2

Marital, unitive, procreative

  • Traditional Christian teaching, set out most fully by the Catholic Church, gives sex three purposes:
  • Marital: sex belongs within marriage, the lifelong, faithful commitment it expresses.
  • Unitive: sex unites the couple in love: body and person.
  • Procreative: sex is open to new life.
  • Many Protestants stress the unitive purpose more, and accept that not every act must be open to children.

  • "for you were bought with a price. Therefore glorify God in your body" (1 Corinthians 6:20)

Mission 2Diversity

Christian and non-religious views on sex before marriage

  • Catholic and Orthodox: sex before marriage is wrong; all are called to chastity.
  • Evangelical Protestants: sex is for marriage only (Hebrews 13:4: "Let marriage be held in honour amongst all, and let the bed be undefiled").
  • Church of England: official teaching places sex within marriage; many Anglicans accept sex in a committed, loving relationship.
  • Liberal Christians: what matters is love, commitment and no exploitation (situation ethics).
  • Humanists: sex is a personal choice between consenting adults; what matters is consent, honesty, respect and responsibility (e.g. avoiding pregnancy and infection when not wanted).
Mission 2Diversity

Other religions on sex

  • Islam: sex is a gift within marriage, with rights for both spouses; outside marriage it is zina: "come not near unto adultery. Lo! it is an abomination and an evil way." (Qur'an 17:32, Pickthall)
  • Judaism: Orthodox Jews keep sex for marriage; many Reform and Liberal Jews focus on love, commitment and respect.
  • Hinduism: kama (pleasure) is a proper aim of life within dharma, in the grihastha (householder) stage; students (brahmacharya) stay celibate.
  • Sikhism: sex only within marriage; kaam (lust) is one of the five "thieves" (vices).
  • Buddhism: the Third Precept: avoid sexual misconduct, meaning sex that causes harm or breaks trust. Many lay Buddhists accept sex in a loving, committed relationship.

Mission 2

Celibacy

  • Celibacy: choosing not to marry or have sex, often for religious reasons.
  • Christian roots: Jesus speaks of some who give up marriage "for the Kingdom of Heaven's sake" (Matthew 19:12); Paul: "it is good for them if they remain even as I am" (1 Corinthians 7:8).
  • Catholic: priests of the Latin Church, monks and nuns are celibate, giving themselves wholly to God and others.
  • Orthodox: priests may marry before ordination; bishops and monks are celibate. Protestant ministers may marry.
  • Buddhism: Theravada monks and nuns are celibate; in Japan, Jodo Shinshu priests (and many Zen priests) marry.
  • Hinduism: celibacy in the student stage and for sannyasins. Islam, Judaism and Sikhism: marriage is the norm; lifelong celibacy is not encouraged (all but one of the Sikh Gurus married).

Mission 2

Adultery

  • Adultery: sex between a married person and someone who is not their spouse.
  • Condemned in every tradition here: "You shall not commit adultery." (Exodus 20:14)
  • Jesus goes further: lust in the heart is already adultery (Matthew 5:28).
  • Islam: zina (17:32). Buddhism: the Third Precept. Hinduism and Sikhism: breaks the marriage promise.
  • Why it is seen as wrong: it breaks vows made before God and partner, betrays trust, harms children and families.
  • Humanists also see it as wrong because it breaks trust and causes harm, though most say it is a private matter, not for the law.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Sex should only take place within marriage."

  • Reasons for: Genesis 2:24 and 1 Corinthians 6: sex expresses a lifelong union; marriage gives security for any children; Catholic teaching: sex should be unitive and procreative within marriage; Islam forbids zina.
  • Reasons against: many loving, committed couples are not married; liberal Christians and Humanists focus on love, consent and responsibility; Buddhist ethics asks about harm, not marital status.
  • Judge it by: Is marriage the only setting for total commitment? Should religious teaching or personal choice decide? What best protects people from harm?
Mission check

?A charity runs a sex-education talk. Which statement could be agreed by Catholics, Muslims, Orthodox Jews and Humanists?

Religions disagree about where sex belongs, but share values such as faithfulness, respect and consent.

Mission 3: Two people who love each other can marry in law. Why do believers reading the same texts still disagree?

Make a guess: Two people who love each other can marry in law. Why do believers reading the same texts still disagree?

Mission 3

Words and the law

  • Sexuality / sexual orientation: who a person is attracted to. Heterosexual: the opposite sex. Homosexual (gay, lesbian): the same sex. Bisexual: both.
  • Civil partnership: legal union, not called marriage. UK-wide since 2005 (Civil Partnership Act 2004).
  • Same-sex marriage: England and Wales from 2014 (Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013); Scotland 2014; Northern Ireland 2020.
  • No religious group is forced to hold same-sex weddings. Groups may opt in; the Church of England and Church in Wales are barred by the Act unless the law changes.
  • Cohabitation: a couple living together without marrying.
Mission 3

The traditional Christian view

  • Marriage is a lifelong union of one man and one woman: "they will be one flesh" (Genesis 2:24); Jesus repeats it (Mark 10:6–8).
  • Sex belongs only within that marriage, where it unites the couple and can create life.
  • Texts read as forbidding same-sex acts: Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27; 1 Corinthians 6:9–10; 1 Timothy 1:10.
  • Most who hold this view separate orientation (not a sin) from sexual acts (sinful outside man–woman marriage).
  • So: same-sex attracted Christians are called to celibacy; same-sex marriage is not a true marriage in God's eyes.
Mission 3Read the source

Two ways of reading

"You shall not lie with a man as with a woman. That is detestable." (Leviticus 18:22, WEBBE)

  • Traditional reading: a moral law, repeated in the New Testament (1 Corinthians 6:9–10), so it still applies.
  • Liberal reading: part of a holiness code that also bans wearing two kinds of material (Leviticus 19:19); it addresses acts in an ancient setting, not loving, faithful partnerships.

Show answer

Q2 How would a traditionalist reply?

Show answer

The moral ban is repeated in the New Testament; food and fabric laws are not.

Mission 3Diversity

The range of Christian views

  • Catholic and Orthodox: acts sinful; persons respected; no same-sex marriage.
  • Many evangelicals: same view, from the authority of the Bible's plain sense.
  • Church of England: does not marry same-sex couples; since 2023 its Prayers of Love and Faith can be used to pray God's blessing on them in ordinary services.
  • Have opted in to same-sex weddings: Quakers, United Reformed Church (2016), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church (2021, where a local church agrees), Church of Scotland (2022, ministers who opt in).
  • Liberal Christians: "God is love" (1 John 4:8); God made people as they are; faithful, loving relationships reflect God whatever the couple's sex.

Mission 3Diversity

Judaism and Islam

  • Orthodox Jews: Leviticus 18:22 forbids the act; marriage is between a man and a woman. Many stress welcome and dignity for gay Jews (UK Chief Rabbi's guidance for schools, 2018).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews: read Leviticus in context; every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27). Their synagogues in Britain hold same-sex weddings.
  • Most Muslims: same-sex acts are forbidden. The Qur'an tells of the people of Lut: "Lo! ye come with lust unto men instead of women." (Qur'an 7:81, Pickthall)
  • Marriage (nikah) is between a man and a woman; sex belongs only within it (Qur'an 23:5–6).
  • Many Muslims distinguish feelings from actions, and reject abuse of gay people.
  • A minority of Muslims (e.g. the UK group Imaan) read the Lut story as condemning violence, not loving relationships. Most scholars reject this reading.
Mission 3Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: no single authority. Many see marriage and the householder stage (grihastha ashrama), with its duty to have children, as the right path, so same-sex relationships clash with householder dharma.
  • Others point to old texts that speak of a "third nature" (tritiya prakriti) and stories of gods who change sex. India decriminalised gay sex in 2018; its Supreme Court declined to create same-sex marriage in 2023.
  • Buddhists: no official view. The Third Precept forbids sexual misconduct: sex that harms, exploits or betrays. Some count same-sex acts as misconduct; many Western Buddhists (e.g. Triratna) do not, if the relationship is loving and faithful.
  • Sikhs: the Lavan (wedding hymns) see married life between husband and wife as the ideal; nine of the ten Gurus married. In 2005 the head (Jathedar) of the Akal Takht told Sikhs to oppose same-sex marriage. Some Sikhs stress that God's light is in every person.

Mission 3

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: morality is about wellbeing and consent, not divine commands. A loving relationship between consenting adults harms no one.
  • Equality: the same rights for all couples. Humanists UK campaigned for the 2013 Act.
  • Atheists hold a range of views, but there is no religious reason against same-sex marriage.
  • Religious response: the state may change the law, but it cannot change what marriage is in God's design; the Act protects freedom of religion by letting faiths opt out.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Religious believers should accept same-sex marriage."

  • Reasons for: God is love (1 John 4:8); everyone is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); faithful love is a good that churches already praise; the law treats all couples equally.
  • Reasons against: scripture defines marriage as man and woman (Genesis 2:24, Mark 10:6–8); texts forbid same-sex acts; marriage is open to children; churches must follow God, not changing culture.
  • Another view: accept civil same-sex marriage in law, but not as religious marriage.
  • Judge it by: How should ancient texts apply today? What is marriage for? Does respecting people mean approving every relationship?
Mission 3Worked answer

Two beliefs that differ

Question: Explain two contrasting beliefs in contemporary British society about same-sex relationships.

  • Belief 1: many Catholics believe same-sex sexual acts are wrong. Development: the Catechism teaches that sex is for a married man and woman and open to new life, so they call gay people to chastity, while respecting them.
  • Belief 2: many liberal Christians and Humanists believe same-sex relationships are as good as heterosexual ones. Development: for liberal Christians "God is love" (1 John 4:8); for Humanists, consenting adults harm no one, so same-sex couples should marry.

Mission 4

Ending a marriage

  • Divorce: the legal ending of a marriage. Separation: living apart while still married.
  • Annulment: a declaration that a marriage was never valid in the first place.
  • Remarriage: marrying again while a former spouse is still alive.
  • Law in England and Wales: since April 2022, no-fault divorce (Divorce, Dissolution and Separation Act 2020). The only ground: the marriage has broken down irretrievably; no one has to be blamed.
  • Common reasons: adultery, abuse, addiction, money worries, growing apart.
  • Breakdown affects children, the wider family, faith communities and society (cost, housing, wellbeing).
Mission 4

What Jesus taught

  • Marriage is God's work: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8; Genesis 2:24).
  • Matthew 19:1–12: Pharisees ask whether divorce is allowed. Jesus says Moses allowed it "because of the hardness of your hearts" (Matthew 19:8), but it was not God's plan.
  • In Matthew, one exception: sexual immorality (Matthew 19:9; 5:32). Mark has no exception (Mark 10:11–12).
Mission 4Read the source

"What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart." (Matthew 19:6)

Mission 4Diversity

Other Christians

  • Orthodox: marriage is meant for life, but out of mercy (economy) the Church allows divorce and up to three marriages; a second wedding has a penitential tone.
  • Church of England: since 2002 (General Synod), divorced people may remarry in church at the priest's discretion.
  • Catholics: no remarriage in church while a former spouse lives, unless the first marriage is annulled (declared never valid).
  • Methodists and many Protestants: divorce is a sad last resort; God forgives; remarriage can be a fresh start.
  • Some evangelicals: divorce only for adultery (Matthew 19:9) or desertion by an unbelieving spouse (1 Corinthians 7:15).
Mission 4

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, Situation Ethics, 1966): do the most loving (agape) thing. If staying together causes lasting harm, divorce may be the loving act.
  • Humanists and atheists: marriage is a human commitment; if it has failed, divorce can be best for adults and children; remarriage is fine.
  • They support no-fault divorce: less conflict, less blame.
Mission 4Evaluate

Sanctity of vows or compassion?

  • Sanctity of vows: the promise was made before God for life; Jesus taught against divorce (Matthew 19:6); stability for children; easy divorce weakens commitment.
  • Compassion: no one should stay in abuse or misery; God forgives and offers new starts; children can suffer more from conflict; Moses allowed divorce because of human weakness (Matthew 19:8).
  • Another view: both matter: work hard to save the marriage, but allow separation or divorce when it truly fails.
  • Judge it by: Is a vow unbreakable or a commitment to try? Whose welfare counts most? Does allowing divorce show mercy or weaken marriage?
Mission 4Diversity

Islam

  • Divorce is allowed as a last resort; a hadith calls it the most disliked of permitted things (Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, try to reconcile: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk." (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall)
  • Talaq (divorce by the husband), then iddah: a waiting time of three monthly cycles, to allow reconciliation and check for pregnancy (Qur'an 2:228).
  • "Divorce must be pronounced twice and then (a woman) must be retained in honour or released in kindness." (Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall)
  • A wife can seek divorce by khul' (returning her dowry) or through a judge or Shari'ah council.
Mission 4Diversity

Judaism

  • Divorce is allowed, though sad. Deuteronomy 24:1: the husband "writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • The get (bill of divorce) is given by the husband before a Beth Din (rabbinic court). The wife must accept it freely.
  • Orthodox: a civil divorce is not enough; without a get neither may remarry in Orthodox Judaism. A woman whose husband refuses is an agunah ("chained" woman).
  • Reform: divorce and remarriage accepted; a get is encouraged but the movement's own procedures help where a husband refuses.
Mission 4Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: marriage is a sacred samskara, ideally binding beyond this life; divorce was not part of traditional Hindu law.
  • Buddhists: marriage is a social contract, not a sacrament; no religious ban on divorce. Reduce suffering; act with compassion (karuna).
  • Sikhs: the Anand Karaj joins two souls; divorce is strongly discouraged and families and the sangat try to reconcile the couple. Civil divorce is reluctantly accepted.

Mission 4Worked answer

Vows and compassion

Question: "Religious people should never divorce." Evaluate this statement.

  • For: Catholics believe a valid marriage cannot be dissolved (CCC 2382); Jesus said "What therefore God has joined together, don't let man tear apart" (Matthew 19:6). Vows before God are sacred.
  • Against: Muslims, Jews and most Protestants allow divorce as a last resort; staying in abuse is not loving (situation ethics); God forgives.
  • Weighing: the Catholic view rests on the sacrament being permanent; the other view rests on compassion for real suffering. Which is stronger depends on whether a vow can be broken by the other partner's behaviour.

Mission 5

Types of family

  • Nuclear: two parents and their children.
  • Extended: wider relatives (grandparents, aunts, cousins) living together or closely involved.
  • Single-parent: one parent raising the children.
  • Blended (step-family): parents with children from earlier relationships.
  • Your spec calls a blended family reconstituted.
  • Same-sex parents: two mothers or two fathers.
  • Polygamous: one person with more than one spouse. Illegal in the UK (bigamy); legal in some countries.
  • Also adoptive and foster families.
Mission 5

What families are for

  • Procreation: having children: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28).
  • Stability: a secure, loving place to grow up.
  • Protection of children: care, safety, food, home.
  • Education: values and, for believers, educating children in a faith.
  • Care across generations: children care for ageing parents: "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12).
  • Families are the building block of society: where people first learn love, duty and forgiveness.
  • Children have rights (e.g. UN Convention on the Rights of the Child, 1989): care, education, a voice.

Mission 5Diversity

Christians and new family types

  • Catholic: every child deserves a mother and father; a child is a gift, not a right (CCC 2378, paraphrase). So the Church opposes same-sex couples adopting, and surrogacy; it rejects IVF for every couple, married or not (CCC 2376–2377).
  • Catholics still welcome and support single-parent, blended and same-sex-parent families in parish life.
  • Liberal Protestants (many Anglicans, Methodists, URC): what matters is love, stability and care, not the family's shape.
  • Polygamy: Christians teach monogamy: "the two will become one flesh" (Mark 10:8).
  • Humanists: judge a family by how well it cares for children, not its structure; same-sex couples and single people have an equal right to raise children.
Mission 5Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: the family is the foundation of the ummah (worldwide community of Muslims); procreation and raising Muslim children strengthen it.
  • "We have commended unto man kindness toward parents." (Qur'an 46:15, Pickthall)
  • Extended families are common. Polygyny: Qur'an 4:3 allows up to four wives only if treated with equal justice; most Muslims are monogamous, and UK Muslims follow UK law.
  • Most Muslims do not accept same-sex parenting; single parents are supported by the wider family.
  • Judaism: "Lo, children are a heritage of the LORD; the fruit of the womb is a reward." (Psalm 127:3, JPS 1917)
  • The home is a small sanctuary (mikdash me'at): Shabbat meals, festivals, teaching children (Deuteronomy 6:7). Orthodox Jews hold the man–woman ideal; Reform Jews welcome same-sex parents.
Mission 5Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: the householder stage (grihastha) is for marriage and raising children; joint (extended) families are common. Purposes: procreation, security, education.
  • Buddhists: the Sigalovada Sutta (Digha Nikaya 31) lists five duties each way. Parents: keep children from wrong, guide them to good, train them for work, find a suitable spouse, hand over the inheritance in time.
  • Children: support their parents, do their duties, keep up the family line, be worthy of the inheritance, make offerings for them after death.
  • Sikhs: family life (grihasth) is the path to God; nine of the ten Gurus married, and they rejected living as hermits.

?Which teaching about the family is correctly matched to its tradition?
Mission 5Evaluate

"The nuclear family is the best kind of family."

  • Reasons for: Catholic teaching and many Christians see married mother and father as God's plan (Genesis 2:24); stability; a child's link to both birth parents.
  • Reasons against: extended families give more support (many Hindu, Muslim and Sikh families); single, blended and same-sex-parent families can be equally loving; Humanists judge by care, not shape.
  • Another view: the ideal and the reality differ; communities should support every family.
  • Judge it by: What do children most need? Is there evidence that structure, or quality of care, matters most? Should religious ideals shape law?
Mission 5Worked answer

Purpose with a source

Question: Explain two reasons why the family is important for Christians. In your answer you must refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Reason 1: it is where children are brought up in the faith. Source: fathers should "nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4). Development: prayer, worship and values are learned at home first.
  • Reason 2: for Catholics, the family is the domestic church. Development: Familiaris Consortio teaches the family forms a community of love and serves society, so a strong family builds a strong Church and society.

?A council asks local faith leaders: "Should we fund support groups for every kind of family, including single-parent and same-sex-parent families?" Which response fits Catholic teaching most closely?

Faiths share the purposes of family (life, love, protection, faith); they differ on which structures fulfil them best.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians see marriage as a lifelong union before God (Genesis 2:24; Mark 10:6–9); Catholics as a sacrament, unitive and procreative.
  • Other faiths: nikah contract (Islam), kiddushin (Judaism), samskara (Hinduism), Anand Karaj (Sikhism), social contract (Buddhism).
  • Cohabitation and civil partnership: accepted by some, rejected by others; free consent is essential to any marriage.

Mission 2

  • Many Christians, especially Catholics, see sexuality as God's good gift, meant for marriage: marital, unitive and procreative.
  • Views on sex before marriage range from forbidden (Catholic, Islam, Orthodox Judaism) to accepted when loving and responsible (liberal believers, Humanists); adultery is condemned across traditions.

Mission 3

  • UK law allows civil partnership (2005) and same-sex marriage (2014; NI 2020); faiths can opt in or out.
  • Traditional Christians, Catholics, Orthodox Jews and most Muslims forbid same-sex acts while calling for respect; liberal Christians, Reform Jews, many Western Buddhists and Humanists accept same-sex relationships.
  • In Dharmic traditions there is no single view: householder dharma, the Third Precept and the 2005 Akal Takht edict are key points.

Mission 4

  • Divorce ends a marriage in law; annulment declares it was never valid. Catholics accept annulment, not remarriage after divorce; most other Christians allow remarriage.
  • The key debate: sanctity of vows (Matthew 19:6) vs compassion (Matthew 19:8).
  • Situation ethics and Humanists put compassion and the welfare of those involved first.
  • Islam (talaq, iddah, khul'), Judaism (get, agunah) and, reluctantly, Dharmic traditions allow divorce as a last resort.

Mission 5

  • Types: nuclear, extended, single-parent, blended, same-sex parents. Purposes: procreation, stability, protecting and educating children.
  • Christians: Ephesians 6:1–4; Catholics: domestic church (Familiaris Consortio), child as gift, not right.
  • Polygamous families too. Other faiths: ummah (46:15), Psalm 127, grihastha, Sigalovada Sutta.
  • Sikhs: family life (grihasth) as the path to God.
