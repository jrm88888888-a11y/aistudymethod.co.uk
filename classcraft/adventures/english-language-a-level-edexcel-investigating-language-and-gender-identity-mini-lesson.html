Language diversity · language and genderScreen 2

Lakoff and her critics

  • Language and Woman's Place (1973 article, 1975 book) listed features of 'women's language': hedges ('sort of'), tag questions ('isn't it?'), empty adjectives ('divine'), super-polite forms, rising intonation on statements.
  • Her method was mainly introspection: her own and acquaintances' speech, and TV.
  • Holmes (1984): women used more facilitative tags (inviting others in); men more modal tags (checking facts).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): in US trials these features appeared in low-status witnesses of both sexes: powerless language.
Language diversity · language and genderScreen 3

Testing the interruption claim

Zimmerman and West (1975)Recorded 31 conversations around a US university campus; in the 11 between a man and a woman, men made almost all of the 48 interruptions (46)
Their conclusionMen deny women equal status as speakers: dominance
ProblemsSmall campus sample; and does every cut-in count? A supportive overlap that agrees is not a grab for the floor
Later evidenceJames and Clarke's (1993) review found little consistent difference between the sexes; power and context matter more
  • To test the claim you must define an interruption, choose comparable contexts and code every cut-in the same way.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Do men interrupt more? How would you even test it?

The answer depends on what you count. Zimmerman and West found men made almost all the interruptions in their 1975 sample, but later studies found little consistent difference once supportive overlaps were separated from floor grabs and contexts were compared. A fair test defines an interruption, compares like settings and looks at power as well as gender.

From topic to question

  • Start with an area of interest, then narrow it to a research question your own data can answer.
  • A good question is specific, answerable from the data and often comparative (two groups, two settings).
  • You may add a hypothesis, a prediction to test, but your conclusion can reject it.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 3

Collecting data

  • Choose the type: spoken, written, multimodal, word lists, or attitudes (surveys, interviews).
  • Collect enough to see patterns, and keep the conditions comparable.
  • Observer's paradox (Labov, 1972): we want to study how people speak when not being observed, but we can only find out by observing them. Reduce it by recording for longer and using later sections.
  • Transcribe spoken data with a key (pauses, overlaps); give speakers labels.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 4

Choosing methods

  • Quantitative: count features and compare (e.g. number of questions per speaker per hour), shown in tables or charts.
  • Qualitative: close analysis of examples in context, using the language levels and frameworks (e.g. politeness, turn-taking).
  • Strong investigations combine both: counts show the pattern, examples explain it.
  • Evaluate your method: sample size, what you couldn't control, what you'd do next.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 5

Ethics

  • Informed consent: tell people what you're studying and get their permission, spoken or written.
  • Under 16: you also need a parent's or carer's permission.
  • Right to withdraw: anyone can pull out, even after agreeing.
  • Anonymity: no real names or identifying details; use labels (Speaker A, Parent 1). Keep recordings secure.
  • No secret recording: BAAL guidance calls deception unacceptable. If knowing the aim would change how people speak, give the general purpose, or explain straight after and delete the data if anyone refuses.

Mission checkScreen 9

?You've recorded your family at dinner. What question could it answer?

A focused, comparative question the recording can actually answer, such as how two speakers' questions differ in form and function. Choose data that fits the question, combine counting with close analysis, reduce the observer's paradox, and get informed consent before you record.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Four approaches: deficit (Lakoff), dominance (Zimmerman and West, Spender), difference (Tannen, Coates), diversity (Cameron, Butler). Each has evidence and critics.
  • Lakoff's features were challenged by Holmes (functions of tags) and O'Barr and Atkins (powerless language, linked to status not sex).
  • Claims about interruption depend on definitions and context; later reviews found little consistent sex difference.
  • Narrow a topic into a specific, answerable, often comparative research question; a hypothesis is optional and can be rejected.
  • Match data and method to the question: combine quantitative counts with qualitative analysis, and evaluate your method's limits. Reduce the observer's paradox.
  • Ethics: informed consent (parents too for under-16s), right to withdraw, anonymity, no secret recording.
