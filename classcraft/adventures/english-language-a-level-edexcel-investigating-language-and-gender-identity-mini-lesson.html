Investigating Language: Language and Gender Identity

How does language relate to gender and identity? This topic covers the classic models, their critiques, and the modern view that gender is performed through language.

You will meet Lakoff, Zimmerman & West, Tannen, O'Barr & Atkins, and social-constructionist ideas. Press Start.

Gender · deficit

The deficit model: Lakoff

Robin Lakoff (1975) proposed the deficit model: she claimed women's language contained features such as hedges, tag questions, empty adjectives (lovely, divine), intensifiers and super-polite forms, implying it was tentative or weak.

Critique: Lakoff's list was based largely on introspection, not systematic data, and assumes male language as the norm. Later research challenged it.

Gender · dominance

The dominance model

The dominance model sees differences as reflections of male social power.

  • Zimmerman & West found men interrupted far more than women in mixed-sex conversation.
  • Dale Spender argued language itself is man-made and embeds male advantage.

Attribute: interruptions study = Zimmerman & West; man-made language = Spender.

Gender · difference

The difference model

The difference model treats male and female styles as different but equal, learned as subcultures.

  • Deborah Tannen proposed contrasts such as report talk (status, information) vs rapport talk (connection), and other pairs like advice vs understanding.
  • This avoids ranking one style above another.

Critique: the difference model can overstate a gender binary and downplay power and context.

Gender · identity

Gender as performance (social constructionism)

Modern approaches see gender as something we do, not something we simply have:

  • Judith Butler — gender is performative, produced through repeated acts, including language.
  • Deborah Cameron — challenged the myth that men and women communicate in fundamentally different ways, stressing context and diversity within genders.
  • Identity is also shaped by communities of practice (Eckert & McConnell-Ginet).

Evaluation: social constructionism moves beyond a fixed binary, treating gendered language as flexible and context-dependent.
