Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians call God all-powerful, all-loving and a fair judge. How can one God be all three?

Make a guess: Christians call God all-powerful, all-loving and a fair judge. How can one God be all three?

Mission 1

One God

  • Christianity is monotheistic: there is only one God.
  • It inherits this from Judaism: "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Christians believe God is greater than any human idea of God.
  • So they use several words, each naming one attribute (quality) of the one God.
  • (How one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit: a later mission.)
Mission 1

Power and knowledge

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful. Nothing that can be done is beyond God.
  • The creeds call God Almighty.
  • Genesis 1: God only speaks, and things exist. That is power without effort.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing. God knows every thought, past, present and future.
  • Significance: Christians trust God in prayer, because God both knows the need and can act.
Mission 1

Loving, just, forgiving

  • Benevolent (omni-benevolent): all-loving; God wills only good for creation.
  • Just: God is fair. Wrongdoing matters to God, and God judges rightly.
  • Christians hold these together: God's justice means wrong is real; God's love offers a way back.
Mission 1

Beyond time, beyond the world, close by

  • Eternal: without beginning or end; not limited by time.
  • Transcendent: beyond and outside the created world; not part of nature.
  • Significance: a transcendent God is worshipped with awe; an immanent, personal God is prayed to as a friend.

Mission 1

God in Genesis 1–3

  • Creator: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)
  • Eternal and transcendent: God is there before anything else, and stands outside what is made.
  • Omnipotent: "God said, 'Let there be light,' and there was light." (Genesis 1:3)
  • Benevolent: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • Significance for Catholics: everything that exists depends on God and is good; humans respond with praise and trust.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If Genesis says the world was made in six days, how can many Christians also accept the Big Bang?

Make a guess: If Genesis says the world was made in six days, how can many Christians also accept the Big Bang?

Mission 2

God the Creator

  • Christians believe God made everything that exists: the whole universe.
  • God made it out of nothing (Latin ex nihilo): no material existed before.
  • God made it freely, by speaking: "God said… and there was" (Genesis 1).
  • God saw it was "very good" (Genesis 1:31): creation is good.
  • So Genesis shows God as Creator, omnipotent, transcendent (outside creation), eternal and benevolent.

Mission 2

Father, Word and Spirit at work

  • Father: the source; the one who creates (Genesis 1:1).
  • Word (the Son): the one through whom all things were made (John 1:3), heard in "God said" (Genesis 1:3).
  • Spirit: God's life-giving power, "hovering" over the waters (Genesis 1:2).
  • Belief in the Trinity shaped how Christians read Genesis 1:1–3: early writers such as Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202) called the Son and the Spirit the "two hands" of God in creation.
  • Jewish readers do not see the Trinity here: the Hebrew ruach can mean "spirit", "breath" or "wind".

Mission 2

Two accounts, side by side

  • First account (Genesis 1:1–2:3): six days, then God rests on the seventh. God speaks; humans, male and female together, come last, in God's image. God is transcendent.
  • Second account (from Genesis 2:4): no days. "The LORD God" forms the man from dust and breathes life into him, plants a garden, brings the animals, then makes the woman. God is close and personal.
  • Different order, different style, different picture of God.
  • Most Christians read them as two complementary accounts, each teaching something true about God and humans.

Mission 2Diversity

Literal or metaphorical?

  • Literal: some Christians, often evangelical or fundamentalist, read Genesis 1 as six 24-hour days; the earth is thousands of years old (young-earth creationism).
  • Day-age: others read each "day" as a long age; the order still matters.
  • Metaphorical (non-literal): Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and many Protestant Christians read Genesis as teaching truths about God and humans, not a scientific timetable.
  • Literary type: Genesis 1 reads like a hymn: repeated refrains, days 1–3 forming spaces, days 4–6 filling them.
  • Catholic teaching: scripture teaches without error the truth God wished to reveal for our salvation (Dei Verbum 11, 1965, paraphrase).
  • Augustine himself warned Christians not to read Genesis in ways that contradict what can be known of nature (The Literal Meaning of Genesis, c. 401–415).
Quick check
?A Christian says: "The six days of Genesis 1 are not 24-hour days. They are a poetic picture showing that God made everything in good order." Which approach is this?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Christian beliefs about creation conflict with science."

  • Reasons for: a literal reading gives six days and a young earth; science gives an age of billions of years. In Genesis 1, plants appear before the sun. Some creationists reject evolution.
  • Reasons against: most Christians read Genesis metaphorically: science asks how, Genesis asks who and why. The Big Bang theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Catholic priest and physicist. A universe with a beginning fits belief in creation out of nothing.
  • Judge it by: Does it depend on how Genesis is read? Can science answer "why is there anything at all?" Do literal readers speak for all Christians?
Mission check

?A Christian astronomer studies light from galaxies billions of years old. She says, "God created all of this." Which belief, held by many Christians, lets her hold both views?

Read as a picture of who made the world and why, the six days need not clash with a universe billions of years old.

Mission 3

Made in the image of God

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • Latin: imago Dei, "image of God".
  • Not a physical likeness: Christians believe God has no body.
  • It means humans share, in a small way, some of God's qualities: reason, free will, conscience, creativity, and the ability to love and to know God.
  • Male and female are made in God's image equally.
  • So humans are unique: no other creature is described this way.
Mission 3Read the source

Rule, breathe, keep

"God said to them, 'Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth.'" (Genesis 1:28)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

"The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15)

Q1 Which verse stresses ruling? Which stresses caring?

Show answer

1:28; 2:15.

Q2 What does 2:7 suggest humans are made of?

Show answer

Earth, dust, and God's own breath.

Mission 3

The image of God in practice

  • Christians draw these beliefs from the image of God:
  • Dignity: every person has equal worth, whatever their age, ability, wealth or background.
  • Free will: humans can choose; so they are responsible for their choices.
  • Sanctity of life: Catholics believe human life is holy, a gift from God, and must be protected from conception to natural death.
  • Stewardship: as God's image, humans should care for creation as God would.
  • For Catholics these shape views on abortion, euthanasia, poverty and the environment (later missions).

Mission 3

Dominion or stewardship?

  • Dominion: rule or authority over nature (Genesis 1:26, 28).
  • Historian Lynn White (1907–1987) argued in 1967 that reading dominion as a licence to exploit helped cause the ecological crisis.
  • Stewardship: looking after something that belongs to someone else, and answering to them for it.
  • "The earth is the LORD's" (Psalm 24:1): humans are not the owners.
  • Most Christians today read dominion as stewardship: rule as God would, like a good shepherd, not a tyrant.
Quick check

Mission 3

Caring for creation is loving your neighbour

  • Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): care for "our common home".
  • Pollution and climate change hit the poorest hardest, and harm future generations.
  • So caring for the environment is a concrete way to "love your neighbour" (Mark 12:31).
  • Local: parishes and schools cut energy and waste, plant gardens, recycle.
  • National: Christians campaign and write to MPs for fair climate policies.
  • Global: agencies such as CAFOD and Caritas support sustainable farming and clean water.
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"Being made in God's image means humans matter more than the rest of creation."

  • Reasons for: only humans are made in God's image (Genesis 1:27) and given dominion (1:28). Jesus says people are worth more than many sparrows (Matthew 10:31). Many Christians believe human life is sacred in a way other life is not (the sanctity of life).
  • Reasons against: God called each part of creation "good" before humans existed (Genesis 1:10–25). God's covenant after the Flood is with "every living creature" (Genesis 9:10). Stewardship means more responsibility, not more importance.
  • Judge it by: Does "special" mean "more important" or "more responsible"? Can something matter to God without being in God's image? Who speaks for future generations?
Mission check

"Rule" in Genesis is read by most Christians as care: the earth is God's, and humans answer for it.

Mission 4

Why paint the faith?

  • Christian painting expresses belief in pictures: for those who make it and those who look.
  • The artist may paint as an act of prayer and offer skill to God.
  • The observer may learn, pray, or be moved to awe.
  • Teaching: Pope Gregory the Great (c. 600) said pictures let those who cannot read "read" the faith on the walls (paraphrase).
  • Devotion: an image focuses prayer.
  • Beauty: Catholics believe beauty reflects God.
  • A fresco is painted on wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall.
Mission 4

The Sistine Chapel ceiling

  • Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564): Italian sculptor, painter and poet.
  • Pope Julius II asked him to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, the Pope's chapel in the Vatican (1508–1512).
  • Down the centre: nine scenes from Genesis, from God separating light from darkness to the story of Noah.
  • The Creation of Adam (c. 1511–12) is one of the central scenes.
  • The chapel is still used: cardinals elect a new pope there.
Mission 4

Reading the Creation of Adam

  • Left: Adam lies on bare earth, strong and beautiful, but limp: his hand hangs, waiting.
  • Right: God, shown as a powerful elderly man, sweeps through the sky, carried by angels in a billowing red cloak.
  • Centre: their fingers almost touch. The gap is the moment just before life passes from God to Adam.
  • Adam's pose roughly mirrors God's: humans are made in God's likeness.
  • Under God's arm, a young woman looks towards Adam: often read as Eve, not yet created; some read her as Wisdom or Mary.
  • Some modern viewers see the red cloak as shaped like a human brain: perhaps God gives a mind as well as life (one interpretation).
Mission 4Read the source

Made in God's image

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

Mission 4

A contrast: Burne-Jones, The Days of Creation

  • Edward Burne-Jones (1833–1898), English artist; The Days of Creation (six panels, 1870–76; Harvard Art Museums).
  • Each panel is one day of Genesis 1. An angel holds a glass globe showing that day's work; the angels from earlier days stand behind, so the panels build up.
  • The sixth day (Adam and Eve) has an angel making music in praise.
  • God is never shown. God is known through what is made: "The heavens declare the glory of God." (Psalm 19:1)
  • Contrast: Michelangelo shows one dramatic moment, God in human form, and the human body at the centre. Burne-Jones shows creation as a calm, ordered whole, God unseen and transcendent, humans as the last part of a sequence.
  • Both express God as creator and creation as good.
Mission 4Evaluate

"How far does the Creation of Adam express Catholic beliefs?"

  • Expresses them well: God as active, powerful creator (omnipotent); God comes from heaven to earth (transcendent, beyond the created world, yet personal); life as God's gift; Adam's likeness to God (imago Dei, "the image of God") and the beauty of the human body (dignity); Eve already in God's plan.
  • Falls short: Catholic teaching is that God is spirit (John 4:24), neither man nor woman; the old man is a symbol (cf. "Ancient of Days", Daniel 7:9), and some Christians think God should not be pictured at all. Genesis 2:7 has breath, not a touch. The painting shows the body, not reason, free will or the soul, which Catholics see as central to God's image.
  • Judge it by: Can a picture ever show an invisible God? Does a symbol have to be literal to be true? Which Catholic beliefs about humans matter most?
Mission 4

The Last Judgement

  • Michelangelo, 1536–1541, on the altar wall of the Sistine Chapel, for Popes Clement VII and Paul III.
  • Centre top: Christ as judge, arm raised; Mary beside him.
  • Around: saints and martyrs, many holding the instrument of their death (e.g. St Bartholomew with his skin; St Lawrence with a gridiron).
  • Below centre: angels blow trumpets and hold books: a small book of the saved, a large book of the damned.
  • Left: the dead rise from graves, some pulled up to heaven.
  • Right: the damned are dragged down; at the bottom, a ferryman and a judge from Dante's Inferno receive them.
Mission 4

Themes of the Last Judgement

  • Christ the judge: "When the Son of Man comes in his glory… Before him all the nations will be gathered" (Matthew 25:31–32).
  • Resurrection of the body: "the trumpet will sound and the dead will be raised" (1 Corinthians 15:52): bodies, not just souls.
  • Heaven and hell: Catholics believe each person's choices have eternal results.
  • Mercy and intercession: Mary and the saints are close to Christ.
  • Hope and warning: the saved are lifted up by others; the priest saying Mass beneath it is reminded of what is at stake.
  • Controversy: many figures were nude; after the Council of Trent, Daniele da Volterra painted cloths over some (1565). Some saw nudity as fitting (we rise as God made us); others as unfitting for a chapel.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If humans are made "in God's image", what follows for how Catholics treat human life?

Make a guess: If humans are made "in God's image", what follows for how Catholics treat human life?

Mission 5

Two accounts, two pictures

  • Genesis has two creation accounts. Catholics read them as complementary, both inspired.
  • Genesis 1:1–2:3: God is transcendent: God speaks, and the world exists in an ordered six days, then rests. Humans come last, male and female together, in God's image.
  • Genesis 2:4–24: "the LORD God" is close and personal: God forms the man from dust, breathes life into him, plants a garden, brings the animals to be named, makes the woman.
  • Both show: God is Creator; creation is good; humans have a unique dignity and a task in creation.
Mission 5Read the source

Image and breath

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

"The LORD God formed man from the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul." (Genesis 2:7)

Q1 Who is made in God's image in Genesis 1:27?

Show answer

Both male and female: all humans, equally.

Q2 What two parts of a human does Genesis 2:7 describe?

Show answer

A body from the earth, and life breathed in by God: Catholics see this as body and soul.

Mission 5

What "image of God" means

  • Imago Dei: Latin for "image of God". Not a physical likeness: God has no body.
  • Catholics believe humans share in God's life in a special way: reason, free will, conscience, and the ability to love and relate to God.
  • The Catechism: each human being is "not just something, but someone" (CCC 357).
  • The image is damaged by sin (Genesis 3), but never lost.
Mission 5

What follows from the image

  • In Catholic teaching, the image of God leads to four things:
  • Free will: God gives humans real choice (Genesis 2:16–17), so they are responsible for what they do.
  • Stewardship: "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15) Humans care for creation on God's behalf.
  • Dignity: every person has equal worth, whatever their race, sex, ability or wealth.
  • Sanctity of life: human life is sacred (holy) because it comes from God; only God is Lord of life (CCC 2258, paraphrase).

Mission 5

Michelangelo's Creation of Adam

  • A fresco on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, Vatican; painted by Michelangelo (1475–1564) for Pope Julius II, 1508–1512.
  • One of nine scenes from Genesis along the centre of the ceiling.
  • Adam reclines on bare earth: a perfect body, but limp, not yet fully alive.
  • Michelangelo shows God as a powerful elderly figure, surrounded by angels, rushing towards Adam.
  • Their fingers almost touch: the moment life passes from God to Adam.
  • Adam's pose mirrors God's: the image of God.
  • God's other arm surrounds a figure often read as Eve, not yet created, or as Wisdom.
  • Catholic beliefs shown: God as Creator and giver of life; life as gift; human dignity; a personal relationship between God and humans.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The Creation of Adam expresses Catholic beliefs about creation well."

  • Reasons for: shows God as active, powerful Creator; life as gift from God; Adam's mirroring pose expresses imago Dei; the near-touch shows closeness and relationship; commissioned for the Pope's chapel and treasured by the Church.
  • Reasons against: God has no body, yet is shown as an old man (the Catechism says God is neither man nor woman, CCC 239); Genesis 2:7 has God breathe, not touch; Genesis 1:27 has male and female made together, but Adam appears alone; Orthodox tradition generally, and many Protestants, avoid picturing God the Father.
  • Another view: art uses symbols; Catholic viewers know the figure is a picture of God's action, not of God's appearance.
  • Judge it by: Does it match Genesis? Does it express key Catholic beliefs? Can any image show a God who has no body?
Mission 5

Natural law

  • Creation is good and ordered (Genesis 1:31): it has purposes built into it.
  • Humans can use reason to discover these purposes and so know how to live well. This is natural law.
  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I–II, q. 94: the first rule is that "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided".
  • Basic goods include: preserving life, having and educating children, living in society, seeking truth about God.
  • Paul: even people without the Bible show "the work of the law written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15).
  • Sanctity of life: preserving life is a basic good, so deliberately taking innocent life is always wrong in Catholic teaching.
Mission 5

Science and faith: Gaudium et Spes 36

  • Second Vatican Council (1962–65); Gaudium et Spes ("Joy and Hope"), 1965.
  • §36: science has its own proper methods and freedom (the autonomy of earthly affairs).
  • Research done honestly and morally never truly conflicts with faith, because "earthly matters and the concerns of faith derive from the same God" (GS 36).
  • It regrets past attitudes among some Christians that set science against faith (its footnote points to the Galileo affair).
  • Influence: the Big Bang theory was first proposed by a Catholic priest, Georges Lemaître (1927); Catholic teaching accepts that evolution can be compatible with faith, while holding that God creates each human soul.

Mission 5

Before birth: Luke 1:44

Elizabeth, pregnant with John the Baptist, greets Mary, who is carrying Jesus:

"For behold, when the voice of your greeting came into my ears, the baby leapt in my womb for joy!" (Luke 1:44)

  • Catholic reading: the unborn John already responds to Christ; he is already someone.
  • So Catholic teaching holds that the image of God, and human dignity, begin at conception, and that human life must be protected absolutely from that moment (CCC 2270, paraphrase).
  • Influence: Catholic teaching holds that abortion is gravely wrong; Catholics also support pregnant women and families in need.
  • Other views: some Christians (e.g. many Anglicans and Methodists) accept abortion in limited circumstances; many non-religious people stress the woman's right to choose.
Mission 5

At the end of life: care of the dying

  • Euthanasia: an action or omission intended to cause death to end suffering. Catholic teaching holds it is always wrong: life is God's gift (Evangelium Vitae 65, 1995, paraphrase).
  • But Catholics need not use every possible treatment: stopping over-burdensome or "extraordinary" treatment is allowed (CCC 2278).
  • Pain relief is right, even if it may shorten life, provided death is not intended (CCC 2279).
  • Palliative care and the hospice movement (Cicely Saunders, a Christian, founded St Christopher's Hospice, London, 1967) express the sanctity of life: care, not killing.
  • Other views: Humanists UK and some Christians support assisted dying for those terminally ill, stressing autonomy and compassion.
Mission check

?A Catholic charity runs a night shelter that turns no one away, including people with criminal records. Which Catholic belief from Genesis best explains this?

Because every person is made in God's image, Catholics believe human dignity cannot be lost or earned: it is given.

Mission 6

A library, not a single book

  • "Bible" comes from the Greek ta biblia: "the books".
  • Written by many people over more than 1,000 years, in Hebrew, some Aramaic, and Greek.
  • Two parts: the Old Testament (before Jesus) and the New Testament (Jesus and the first Christians).
  • Testament means covenant: an agreement between God and people.
  • Christians believe it has one unity: the story of God and humanity, from creation to new creation.
  • How many books? 66 in Protestant Bibles, 73 in Catholic Bibles, more in Orthodox Bibles.
Mission 6

The Old Testament

  • Law (the Pentateuch: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy): creation, the patriarchs, Moses and the commandments.
  • History (e.g. Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings): Israel in the promised land, its kings and exile.
  • Writings (e.g. Psalms, Proverbs, Job): poetry, prayers and wisdom.
  • Prophets (e.g. Isaiah, Jeremiah, Amos): God's messengers call people back to God and speak of hope to come.
  • AQA names three key forms: law, history and prophecy.
  • The Jewish Tanakh has the same core books in a different order: Torah, Nevi'im (Prophets), Ketuvim (Writings).
Mission 6

The New Testament

  • Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John): the life, death and resurrection of Jesus. "Gospel" means "good news".
  • Acts: history of the early Church, from the Ascension to Paul in Rome.
  • Letters (epistles): 21 letters to churches and leaders, 13 under Paul's name; teaching on belief and life.
  • Revelation: visions of God's final victory, written in symbols (apocalyptic).
  • Almost all churches accept the same 27 New Testament books.

Mission 6

Different kinds of writing

  • The Bible contains many literary forms (types of writing):
  • Law: commands and rules for life and worship (Leviticus).
  • History: accounts of events, told to show God at work (Kings, Acts).
  • Prophecy: God's message through a prophet, often as poems and visions (Amos).
  • Poetry and wisdom: songs, prayers and sayings (Psalms, Proverbs).
  • Gospel: proclaiming Jesus as good news, not a modern biography.
  • Letter: real letters to real churches (Romans).
  • Apocalyptic: symbols and visions about the end (Revelation, part of Daniel).
  • Catholic teaching: to find what God means, readers must notice the literary form the human author used (Dei Verbum 12, paraphrase).

Mission 6

How the Bible came together

  • Oral tradition first: stories, laws and songs were passed on by word of mouth, then written down and edited over centuries.
  • The Septuagint: a Greek translation of the Jewish scriptures (from the 3rd century BCE), used by many early Christians.
  • New Testament: Paul's letters come first (c. 50–60 CE); the Gospels c. 65–100 CE.
  • Canon (Greek "measuring rod"): the agreed list of books.
  • Tests used: link to an apostle, use in worship across the churches, agreement with the faith handed down.
  • 367 CE: Athanasius, Bishop of Alexandria, lists the 27 New Testament books; councils at Hippo (393) and Carthage (397) confirm them.
  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit guided this whole process.
Mission 6

The Apocrypha and the Council of Trent

  • Some books were in the Greek Septuagint but not in the Hebrew scriptures: e.g. Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees.
  • Catholics call them deuterocanonical ("second canon"); Protestants call them the Apocrypha ("hidden").
  • Reformers (16th century) followed the Hebrew list; Luther printed these books in a separate section (1534).
  • Council of Trent, 8 April 1546: the Catholic Church declared these books fully part of scripture, with equal authority. Total: 73 books.
  • Orthodox Bibles include them, and a few more (e.g. 3 Maccabees, Psalm 151).

Mission 6Evaluate

"The deuterocanonical books should be part of the Bible."

  • Reasons for: they were in the Septuagint, the Greek scripture of many early Christians. Church Fathers such as Augustine used them as scripture. Councils at Hippo and Carthage included them; Trent confirmed them. 2 Maccabees 12:46 supports praying for the dead.
  • Reasons against: they are not in the Hebrew scriptures. Jerome (c. 347–420) distinguished them from the Hebrew books. Protestants point out that the New Testament never quotes them directly as scripture. Reformers saw some teaching in them (e.g. on prayers for the dead) as unsupported elsewhere.
  • Judge it by: Who decides the canon: the Church, the Jewish tradition, or scripture's own witness? Which early list carries most weight? Does it change central Christian beliefs?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe in one God (monotheism), described by many attributes: omnipotent, loving (benevolent), just.
  • Genesis 1–3 shows God as Creator, eternal, transcendent, omnipotent and good.

Mission 2

  • Christians believe God created everything out of nothing: the Father creates, through the Word (the Son, John 1:1–3), with the Spirit giving life (Genesis 1:1–3).
  • Genesis has two accounts (1:1–2:3 and from 2:4) with different pictures of God; some Christians read them literally, many metaphorically.

Mission 3

  • Humans are made in God's image (imago Dei, Genesis 1:27): this gives every person equal dignity, free will and responsibility.
  • Genesis 1:28 gives dominion; Genesis 2:15 asks humans to "cultivate and keep" the garden. Most Christians read dominion as stewardship.
  • Catholics see care for creation as love of neighbour, carried out locally, nationally and globally.

Mission 4

  • Michelangelo's Creation of Adam (Sistine Chapel, c. 1511–12): God as creator; life as God's gift; humans in God's image (Genesis 1:27); how far it fits Catholic belief is debated.
  • Burne-Jones's The Days of Creation contrasts: creation as an ordered whole, God unseen. The Last Judgement (1536–41) shows Christ as judge, the resurrection of the body, heaven and hell.

Mission 5

  • Genesis 1 and 2 show God as Creator and humans as made in God's image, with reason, freedom and the ability to love.
  • Imago Dei grounds free will, stewardship, equal dignity and the sanctity of life.
  • Natural law: human reason can read God's good order in creation, and it supports the sanctity of life.

Mission 6

  • The Bible is a library: Old Testament (law, history, writings, prophets) and New Testament (gospels, Acts, letters, Revelation).
  • The canon formed over centuries; the 27 New Testament books were agreed by the late 4th century.
  • Catholic Bibles have 73 books; Protestant Bibles have 66; Orthodox Bibles more.
  • The Council of Trent (1546) confirmed the Catholic list of 73 books.
