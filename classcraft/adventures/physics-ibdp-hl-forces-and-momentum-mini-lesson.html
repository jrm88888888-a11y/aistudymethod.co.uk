Mission 1

Mission 1: With air resistance ignored, which forces still act on a ball once it has left your foot?

Make a guess: With air resistance ignored, which forces still act on a ball once it has left your foot?

Mission 1

A force is an interaction

  • A force is a push or pull by one object on another. Unit: the newton, N.
  • Every force has a source: name it as "force on X by Y".
  • Force is a vector: size and direction.
  • No object exerts a net force on itself. No source, no force.
Mission 1

Weight

  • Weight: the gravitational force on an object by Earth (or another planet or moon).
  • Fg = mg
  • Mass (kg): how much matter. Same everywhere. Weight (N): changes with g.
  • g = F/m: gravitational field strength, the force per kilogram, in N kg⁻¹. Same value as the free-fall acceleration in m s⁻².
  • Weight acts at the centre of gravity. It needs no contact: a field force.
  • Electric and magnetic forces are field forces too.
Quick check
?A space probe weighs 4900 N on Earth, where g = 9.8 N kg⁻¹. It lands on Mars, where g = 3.7 N kg⁻¹. What is the probe's mass on Mars?
Mission 1

Normal force, friction, tension

  • Normal force FN: the component of the contact force perpendicular to the surface.
  • Friction: parallel to the surface. Opposes sliding, or the tendency to slide, between the surfaces.
  • Friction symbol: Ff.
  • Tension: the pull of a string, rope or cable, along it, away from the object.
  • Ideal string (light, does not stretch): the same tension all along it, and on both sides of an ideal pulley.
Mission 1

Forces from fluids

  • Drag (air resistance): opposite to the velocity; grows with speed.
  • Drag symbol: Fd.
  • Buoyancy Fb: upward force from a fluid a body is in.
  • Thrust: the driving force of an engine.
Quick check
?A ball has been thrown straight up and is still rising. Air resistance is not negligible; the buoyancy of the air is negligible. Which list gives all the forces acting on the ball, and no others?
Mission 1

Drawing a free-body diagram

  • One object only. Show every force on it, by something else.
  • Label each arrow with what exerts it. Arrow length shows size.
  • Never draw a "force of motion" or the object's own push on something else.
  • For a particle, start each arrow on a dot.
  • For an extended body, start each arrow where the force acts (weight at the centre of gravity). This matters for moments.
Mission 1

From diagram to resultant

  • Add all the forces as vectors: the resultant (net) force.
  • Resolve each force along two perpendicular axes, then add components.
  • A sledge is pulled by a rope: tension 50 N at 30° above the horizontal; friction 30 N.
  • Horizontal: 50 cos 30° − 30 = 43.3 − 30 = 13.3 N forwards.
  • Vertical: the rope's 50 sin 30° = 25 N upwards, plus the normal force, balances the weight.
Quick check
?A crate is given a push and then slides down a rough ramp on its own. Which list gives all the forces acting on the crate while it slides, and no others?
Mission check

?A hockey puck is hit by a stick and then slides across level ice. Friction and air resistance are negligible. Which forces are exerted on the puck while it slides, after it has left the stick?

Same for the kicked ball: once the foot has gone, only Earth pulls on it (its weight). Nothing pushes it along its path.

Make a guess: Can something be moving fast when all the forces on it cancel out?

Mission 2

Newton's first law

  • A body stays at rest or moves with constant velocity unless a resultant force acts on it.
  • Moving things do not stop "naturally". Friction and drag stop them.
  • No resultant force needed to keep moving; one is needed to change velocity.
Quick check
?A space probe is so far from any star or planet that the forces exerted on it are negligible. Its engines fire briefly and then shut off. Which statement describes the probe's motion after the engines have shut off?
Mission 2

Equilibrium

  • Resultant force: the vector sum of all the forces on a body.
  • Translational equilibrium: resultant force zero → at rest or at constant velocity.
  • A skydiver whose drag equals her weight falls at constant velocity: her terminal velocity. She is in equilibrium.
Mission 2

On a slope

  • Take axes along the slope and perpendicular to it.
  • Weight resolves into mg sin θ down the slope and mg cos θ into the slope.
  • A 5.00 kg block rests on a rough slope at 30.0°. Equilibrium: friction = mg sin θ, normal force = mg cos θ.
  • g = 9.8 m s⁻²: friction = 24.5 N up the slope · normal force = 42.4 N
Quick check
?A block is held at rest on a ramp by a light string that is parallel to the ramp and tied to a post at the top, as shown in the diagram. The ramp is inclined at 37° above the horizontal, friction between the block and the ramp is negligible, and the gravitational force on the block is 50 N. Use sin 37° = 0.60 and cos 37° = 0.80. What is the tension in the string?
Quick check
?A hanging basket of mass 3.0 kg is held at rest by two ropes: one horizontal, and one at 55° above the horizontal. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the tension in the rope at 55°?
Mission check

?Which of the following objects is in translational equilibrium?

Yes. Moving fast or slow, an object with constant velocity has zero net force on it.

A skydiver at terminal velocity falls very fast, typically around 50 m s⁻¹, with zero resultant force: drag balances weight. Zero resultant force means constant velocity, not zero velocity.

Mission 3: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Make a guess: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Mission 3

Newton's second law

  • F = ma: resultant force = mass × acceleration (constant mass).
  • The acceleration is in the direction of the resultant force, not necessarily the velocity.
  • 1 newton: the resultant force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻². 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻².
  • Same force: a ∝ 1/m. Same mass: a ∝ F.
Quick check
?A crate slides to the left across a level floor with negligible friction. At one instant, two students push on it horizontally: one pushes to the left with a force of 20 N, and the other pushes to the right with a force of 30 N. Which statement about the crate at this instant is correct?
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A 1200 kg car: driving force 3000 N forwards, resistive forces 600 N backwards.
  • Resultant force = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N forwards.
  • a = F/m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2.0 m s⁻² forwards.
  • From rest, after 5.0 s: v = 0 + 2.0 × 5.0 = 10 m s⁻¹.
  • Always use the resultant force, never just the driving force.
Quick check
?A loaded trolley of mass 8.0 kg moves along a straight, level track. The graph shows the trolley's velocity v as a function of time t. What is the magnitude of the resultant force exerted on the trolley between t = 0 and t = 2.0 s?
Mission 3

Connected bodies

  • Whole system first: only external forces count. Internal pulls and pushes cancel in pairs.
  • Then one object on its own: this gives the internal force (tension, or a contact force).
  • A 0.80 kg block on a smooth table is pulled by a string over an ideal pulley to a 0.20 kg hanging mass.
  • System: resultant = 0.20 × 9.8 = 1.96 N; total mass 1.00 kg → a = 1.96 ÷ 1.00 = 1.96 m s⁻².
  • Block alone: T = 0.80 × 1.96 = 1.6 N: less than the hanging weight, because the hanging mass accelerates.
Quick check
?Block X (2.0 kg) and block Y (4.0 kg) rest on a frictionless horizontal surface, joined by a light string. A horizontal force of 24 N pulls X away from Y, and both accelerate together. What is the tension in the string?
Mission 3

Force and momentum

  • Momentum p = mv.
  • F = Δp/Δt: resultant force = rate of change of momentum.
  • Constant mass: Δp/Δt = mΔv/Δt = ma. Same law.
  • F = ma is for one body of constant mass. F = Δp/Δt also handles mass that keeps arriving or leaving: sand falling onto a conveyor belt; a rocket and its exhaust, taken together.
Quick check
?Water leaves a hose at 0.50 kg s⁻¹ with a horizontal speed of 12 m s⁻¹. It hits a vertical wall and stops dead, without bouncing back. What horizontal force does the water exert on the wall?
Mission check

?A student exerts different constant resultant forces on a trolley on a level track and measures the trolley's acceleration each time. The graph shows the results. A block is then fixed to the trolley so that the mass of the trolley–block system is twice the mass of the trolley, and the experiment is repeated with the same resultant forces. Which describes the new graph of acceleration against resultant force?

Same force, a ∝ 1/m. A 1200 kg car has 40 times the mass of a 30 kg wheelbarrow, so the same push (ignoring friction) gives it 1/40 of the acceleration.

Mission 4

Forces come in pairs

  • If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.
  • The two forces in a pair are always:
    • the same type (both gravitational, both contact, …)
    • on different bodies
    • along the same line, at the same time.
Quick check
?A hand pushes block L to the right, and L pushes a smaller block S ahead of it. Both blocks speed up to the right along a level surface with negligible friction. The diagram shows a student's representation of the forces that L and S exert on each other: FL on S is the force exerted on S by L, and FS on L is the force exerted on L by S. Which statement about the student's diagram is correct?
Mission 4

Finding the partner

  • Name the force: "on X by Y". Its partner is "on Y by X".
  • Book on a table. Weight = force on book by Earth → partner: force on Earth by book, upwards.
  • Normal force on book by table → partner: push on table by book, downwards.
  • Weight and normal force on the book are not a pair: both act on the book, and they are different types.
Quick check
?A lamp hangs at rest from a cord fixed to the ceiling. The cord exerts an upward force on the lamp. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with this force?
Quick check
?A horse pulls a cart along a level road, and both speed up from rest. A student argues: "The cart pulls back on the horse with a force equal in magnitude to the horse's pull on the cart, so these forces cancel and the cart cannot speed up." Which statement correctly identifies the flaw in the argument?
Mission 4

Pairs never cancel

  • The two forces act on different bodies, so they never add to zero on either one.
  • Horse pulls cart; cart pulls back on horse, equally. The cart still speeds up:
    • on the cart: horse's pull > friction on the cart.
    • on the horse: ground's forward push on its hooves > cart's pull back.
  • Equal forces, different masses → different accelerations (a = F/m).
Quick check
?A steel paper clip lies at rest on a wooden table. A magnet held near it pulls it with a small force, but friction from the table stops it moving. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with the magnet's pull on the clip?
Mission check

?Trolley 1 rolls along a level track and collides with trolley 2. Trolley 1 has three times the mass of trolley 2. Force sensors record the force exerted on trolley 1 by trolley 2 and the force exerted on trolley 2 by trolley 1; the graph shows the data, with the direction of trolley 1's initial motion as positive. Friction is negligible. How does the change in velocity of trolley 2 during the collision compare with that of trolley 1?

Truck and car feel forces of the same size at every instant. The car and its occupants get a much larger acceleration from the same size of force, because the car's mass is much smaller. That is why the car comes off worse.

Quick check

Mission 5

Which way does friction act?

  • Friction acts parallel to the surfaces in contact.
  • It opposes the relative motion of the two surfaces (or the slipping that would happen without it).
  • Relative means: relative to the other surface, not to the ground.
  • Box on a belt that moves faster than the box: the box slips backwards relative to the belt, so friction on the box points forwards.
Mission 5

Static friction

  • Surfaces not sliding relative to each other: static friction.
  • It is only as large as it needs to be, to stop slipping.
  • Push a heavy crate with 10 N: friction is 10 N. Push with 30 N: friction is 30 N.
  • It has a maximum:
  • Ff ≤ μs FN
  • Push beyond the maximum: the surfaces start to slide.
Mission 5

Sliding friction

  • Surfaces sliding: dynamic friction, Ff = μd FN.
  • μ has no unit: it is a ratio of two forces. It depends on the pair of materials.
  • Usually μs > μd: harder to start sliding than to keep sliding.
  • To a good approximation, sliding friction does not depend on speed.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Box on a level floor. Normal force on it: 200 N. μs = 0.50; μ for sliding = 0.40.
  • Maximum static friction = 0.50 × 200 = 100 N.
  • Push 60 N: no sliding. Friction = 60 N (not 100 N).
  • Push just over 100 N: the box starts to slide.
  • Sliding: friction = 0.40 × 200 = 80 N.
  • Keep pushing with 100 N: resultant force = 100 − 80 = 20 N forwards. The box speeds up.
Quick check
?The diagram shows the forces acting on a block at rest on a rough slope. The coefficient of static friction between the block and the slope is 0.70. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the magnitude of the frictional force Ff on the block?
Quick check
?A block rests on a level table. A student pulls it with a horizontal force that increases slowly from zero, and a sensor records the friction force exerted on the block by the table. The block starts to slide when the pull reaches 12 N. The graph shows the friction force as a function of the pull. Which claim does the graph support?
Mission 5

The normal force is not always mg

  • Friction depends on the normal force: the push perpendicular to the surface.
  • Level floor, no other vertical forces: normal force = mg.
  • Slope at angle θ: normal force = mg cos θ. Less friction than on the level.
  • Pulled by a rope angled upwards: the rope takes some of the weight. Normal force = mg − T sin θ.
  • Always find the normal force first, from the forces perpendicular to the surface.
Quick check
?A block of mass 4.0 kg slides down a rough plane inclined at 25° to the horizontal. The coefficient of dynamic friction between the block and the plane is 0.20. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the magnitude of the frictional force on the block?
Mission check

?A book lies on the flat bed of a pickup truck. The truck speeds up from rest along a straight, level road, and the book does not slide on the bed. Which statement about friction on the book is correct?

Yes. Friction opposes slipping between the surfaces. The bed tends to move forwards under the book, so friction on the book points forwards and speeds it up, just like a box on a faster belt.

Mission 6: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 6

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • FH = −kx. k is the spring constant.
  • Unit of k: N m⁻¹. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
Quick check
?In a lab, a student models a spring as a light spring that obeys Hooke's law. Which statement describes how the spring's force behaves in this model?
Mission 6

A restoring force

  • The minus sign: the spring's force is opposite to its displacement from equilibrium.
  • Stretched to the right: the spring pulls left. Compressed to the left: it pushes right.
  • Always towards the equilibrium (natural length) position: a restoring force.
  • At the natural length the spring exerts no force.
Quick check
?A spring of spring constant k = 40 N m⁻¹ has a natural length of 10.0 cm. One end is fixed and a student pulls the other end so that the length of the spring becomes 13.0 cm. Taking the direction in which the spring is stretched as positive, what is the elastic restoring force FH that the spring exerts on the student's hand?
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N m⁻¹.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 6

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = k.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by a light spring that obeys Hooke's law, as a function of the spring's length L, from its natural length to a length of 20 cm. What is the spring constant of the spring?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

Mission 7

Drag

  • Drag: the resistive force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). Air resistance is drag in air.
  • It acts opposite to the velocity relative to the fluid.
  • It increases with speed.
  • It also grows with cross-sectional area, a less streamlined shape, and the fluid's density.
Mission 7

Falling with drag

  • At release: v = 0, so drag = 0.
  • In air (buoyancy negligible), the only force is the weight.
  • Speed rises → drag rises → resultant force (weight − drag) falls.
  • When drag = weight: resultant force zero.
  • The object then falls at a constant terminal velocity.
  • v–t graph: starts as steeply as the no-drag line, then curves and levels off at vT.
Quick check
?A small steel ball is released from rest in air and falls until it reaches terminal velocity. How does its acceleration change during this time?
Mission 7

Worked example: a falling ball

  • A 0.20 kg ball falls. At one instant the drag on it is 0.80 N.
  • Weight = 0.20 × 9.8 = 1.96 N. Resultant = 1.96 − 0.80 = 1.16 N downwards.
  • a = 1.16 ÷ 0.20 = 5.8 m s⁻²: less than g.
  • It keeps speeding up until drag = weight, about 2.0 N. Then a = 0: terminal velocity.
Mission check

?Two paper cones have the same size and shape. A second cone is pushed inside one of them, doubling its mass without changing its outside shape. Both are dropped from a high balcony. Which statement about their terminal velocities is correct?

Drag grows with speed until it balances the weight. Falling raindrops reach this balance at under 10 m s⁻¹, not the 140 m s⁻¹ they would reach falling 1 km without air.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every force is exerted on one object by another; a free-body diagram shows only the forces on the chosen object, each with its source.
  • Weight = mg (Earth's pull); normal force ⟂ surface; friction ∥ surface; tension along the string.

Mission 2

  • Zero resultant force ⇔ constant velocity (including at rest): translational equilibrium.
  • On a slope, resolve along and perpendicular to the surface: mg sin θ and mg cos θ.

Mission 3

  • F = ma (constant mass): use the resultant force; the acceleration is in its direction.
  • F = Δp/Δt; for constant mass it is F = ma.

Mission 4

  • Third-law pair: same type, equal size, opposite direction, on two different bodies; so the pair never cancels.

Mission 5

  • Friction acts parallel to the surfaces and opposes their relative slipping, so it can point forwards (a box on a faster belt, a walking foot).
  • Static: Ff ≤ μs FN, as large as needed. Dynamic: Ff = μd FN. Usually μs > μd.

Mission 6

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: gradient of the force–extension graph = k, in N m⁻¹.
  • The minus sign: the spring force is a restoring force, always towards the equilibrium position.

Mission 7

  • Drag grows with speed. Falling, the resultant force and acceleration fall to zero when drag = weight: terminal velocity.
