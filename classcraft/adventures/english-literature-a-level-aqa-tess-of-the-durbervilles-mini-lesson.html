Echoes across the Phases

  • Parallels: Tess sleeps in The Chase (XI) and on the stone at Stonehenge (LVIII). Each time, others decide her fate while she sleeps.
  • Mistiming: Angel fails to dance with her (II), then returns from Brazil too late (LV): "It is too late," she tells him.
  • Irony: the Phase called "Fulfilment" ends with a black flag over Wintoncester gaol.
  • The Phases also shape a love story: meeting (3), marriage (4), separation (5), brief reunion (7).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Hardy cuts Tess's life into seven 'Phases'. Why not just chapters?

Each Phase ends at a turning point, and each title names Tess as the world sees her ("Maiden No More", "The Woman Pays"). The structure is part of Hardy's argument: it shows a woman labelled stage by stage, and ends on the bitter irony of "Fulfilment".

Mission 2

Does Tess act, or is she only acted upon?

Make a guess: Does Tess act, or is she only acted upon?

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · TessScreen 1

Between two worlds

  • Tess is the eldest child of a poor, feckless Marlott family. After the horse Prince dies (IV), she feels she must repair the loss.
  • She has passed the Sixth Standard and "spoke two languages" (III): dialect at home, standard English outside.
  • Raymond Williams argued that Tess is best read not as a peasant but as an educated member of the rural working class, caught between worlds.
  • Write Hardy presents Tess as…: she is a construct.

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · TessScreen 5

Readings of Tess

  • Penny Boumelha argued that Tess can't be read as a stable, consistent character: she is complex and contradictory.
  • Boumelha also noted that Tess is often asleep, dreaming or silent at the crises (The Chase, the arrest), as if most herself where the narrator can't reach her.
  • Test it: Tess sleeps at Stonehenge, but wakes to say "I am ready" (LVIII).
Tess of the D'Urbervilles · TessScreen 6

Tess and love

  • Love through the ages includes love and sex, social conventions and taboos, approval and disapproval.
  • Tess loves Angel "for ever—in all changes, in all disgraces" (XXXV); he cannot love her past.
  • Compare Hardy's poem "The Ruined Maid" (pre-1900 anthology): 'Melia, "ruined", wins fine clothes and town speech. Its comic irony questions the same label Tess carries.

Both men remake Tess

  • Alec makes her Eve, the temptress: "You temptress, Tess" (XLVI); "You are Eve, and I am the old Other One" (L).
  • Angel idealises her, then reverses: "the woman I have been loving is not you" (XXXV).

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · Alec and AngelScreen 5

Critics on the two men

  • D. H. Lawrence, in Study of Thomas Hardy (written 1914, published 1936), read Angel as loving an idea of woman rather than Tess's living self; Alec, for all his wrongs, at least responds to her as a body.
  • Penny Boumelha argued that Hardy's revisions blackened both Alec and Angel, sharpening the case for Tess.
  • Test them: Hardy gives Angel "a hard logical deposit" (XXXVI) that blocks his acceptance of Tess.

Darwin and an indifferent Nature

  • Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) described a natural world without design. Hardy called himself one of its earliest acclaimers.
  • The narrator mocks Wordsworth's faith in "Nature’s holy plan" (III).
  • Love depends on timing, not providence: "the man to love rarely coincides with the hour for loving" (V).
  • So "fate" may be Nature's indifference, plus human choices.

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · nature and rural changeScreen 4

Does Nature reflect Tess?

  • At Talbothays the lovers walk at dawn "as if they were Adam and Eve" (XX): pastoral and Eden.
  • But Tess first stands in that valley "like a fly on a billiard-table" (XVI): tiny, of no consequence.
  • When Tess feels guilty, the narrator says her fancy reads reproach into the winter winds (XIII): Nature itself does not judge.
  • So Hardy uses pathetic fallacy and then questions it.
Tess of the D'Urbervilles · nature and rural changeScreen 5

A countryside in crisis

  • The agricultural depression (about 1873–1896): cheap prairie grain from America; wheat prices roughly halved; labourers left for towns.
  • Hardy's essay 'The Dorsetshire Labourer' (Longman's Magazine, July 1883) described labourers moving farm to farm each year.
  • In the novel, Old Lady-Day brings "a fever of mobility" (LI), and old village families are forced out.
  • Flintcomb-Ash belongs to an absent owner (XLIII).

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · nature and rural changeScreen 8

Reading rural change

  • Some readers see an elegy for a rural world invaded from outside: "Modern life stretched out its steam feeler" (XXX) to the dairy's milk train.
  • Raymond Williams instead read Tess as an educated member of the rural working class, so the pressures come from within a changing rural society too.
  • The narrator's own verdict on migration: "the tendency of water to flow uphill when forced by machinery" (LI).
  • Love is shaped by place: Talbothays makes love possible, Flintcomb-Ash tests it.
Mission checkScreen 9

?Tess thrives at one farm, suffers at another. Why does place matter?

Hardy's places do double work. Talbothays and Flintcomb-Ash carry Tess's feelings through season and landscape, and they also show real economic change: dairy and arable farming, absent owners, migration and machines. Strong answers link the imagery to that context.

Mission 6

Hardy's subtitle calls Tess 'A Pure Woman'. Why did that shock?

Make a guess: Hardy's subtitle calls Tess 'A Pure Woman'. Why did that shock?

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · purity and moralityScreen 1

The double standard

  • Victorian convention treated a woman's sexual "fall" as permanent, a man's as forgivable.
  • The law matched it: under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 a husband could divorce for adultery alone; a wife had to prove adultery plus another wrong, such as cruelty.
  • In the novel, Tess forgives Angel's past; Angel can't forgive hers (XXXIV–XXXV).
  • Tess's own bitter verdict: "Once victim, always victim—that’s the law!" (XLVII).

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · purity and moralityScreen 4

The Chase: what the text leaves out

  • In Chapter XI Alec leaves Tess resting on a heap of leaves in the fog, returns, and finds her asleep. The narrative then cuts away to the narrator's questions; the act itself is never narrated.
  • Later, Tess says she was "dazed" by him; the narrator says she "succumbed to adroit advantages he took of her helplessness", then briefly yielded (XII).
  • Readers and critics have long debated whether Hardy presents rape or seduction. The gap is deliberate.
Tess of the D'Urbervilles · purity and moralityScreen 5

How revisions shaped the debate

  • Serial (1891): a mock marriage replaced the seduction.
  • First edition (1891): Alec gives Tess a drugging drink on the ride; Hardy removed it in 1892.
  • Hardy wrote in 1891 that his manuscript had told a plain seduction.
  • Mary Jacobus argued that Tess's purity is partly a construct, built up in revision to answer objections.
  • So your edition matters: check it.
Tess of the D'Urbervilles · purity and moralityScreen 6

"A Pure Woman"

  • Hardy added the subtitle after reading the final proofs (1912 note).
  • His 1892 preface: objectors see only the "artificial and derivative meaning" of the word and ignore "the meaning of the word in Nature".
  • So purity means nature and intention, not sexual history. In Brazil, Angel reaches the same idea: a character's true history lies "among things willed" (XLIX).
Tess of the D'Urbervilles · purity and moralityScreen 7

Linking purity

  • Love and sex; social conventions and taboos; approval and disapproval.
  • Hardy's own poem "The Ruined Maid" (pre-1900 anthology) treats the label comically. 'Melia replies: "O didn’t you know I’d been ruined?" said she.
  • Compare: the poem's ruined maid prospers in town; Tess, with the same label, is destroyed.

?Hardy's subtitle calls Tess 'A Pure Woman'. Why did that shock?

The subtitle challenged the double standard. Society called a woman with Tess's history "fallen" for ever; Hardy calls her pure, defining purity by her nature, intentions and will. The Chase is left deliberately unnarrated, and Hardy's revisions show how carefully he shaped that challenge.

Mission 7

Make a guess: Hardy's narrator defends Tess and stares at her. Can it do both?

Tess of the D'Urbervilles · narrator and criticsScreen 1

One narrator, several voices

  • A third-person omniscient narrator, but not neutral: he calls Tess "our heroine" (XI) and defends her.
  • He moves between close-up on Tess's body and essay-like generalisation: "In the ill-judged execution of the well-judged plan of things" (V).
  • He uses irony: "Justice" in scare quotes (LIX).
  • The narrator is a construct; Hardy's prefaces are a separate voice.

How does Hardy's narrator present Tess in Chapter V? Use one critic, and test their view against the text.

Model answer

Hardy's narrator explains why Alec's "eyes" "rivet themselves" on Tess, but his own phrasing, "a luxuriance of aspect, a fulness of growth", lingers on her body too. Penny Boumelha argues that the narrator's gaze is erotic and possessive, and this passage supports her. Yet the narrator also protects Tess: she is "innocently" unaware, and her "attribute" is called "a disadvantage", which shifts blame to the watcher. The narrator both looks and defends.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Seven titled Phases chart Tess's life from Marlott to Wintoncester; each ends at a turning point, and the titles name her as others judge her.
  • The novel exists in versions: the censored 1891 Graphic serial, the 1891 three volumes, and the 1912 Wessex Edition quoted here.
  • In Section C, compare how Tess and your poems shape a love story in time.
  • Hardy presents Tess between two worlds: educated, bilingual, but bound to a poor family and a rural economy.
  • She acts (refuses, baptises, confesses, strikes, chooses), but almost always under pressure; critics such as Boumelha stress how hard she is to pin down.
  • Link Tess's love and its "disgraces" to your anthology, e.g. "The Ruined Maid".
  • Alec (new money, sensual, the "tragic mischief") and Angel (clergyman's son, idealist) look like opposites.
  • Both turn Tess into an image (Eve; a goddess, then "another woman") and judge her by it; Angel's double standard is exposed when Tess forgives his past and he can't forgive hers.
  • Compare their kinds of love with poems of possession and idealisation.
  • Hardy's narrator offers fate (ancestry, "It was to be"), chance (Prince, the letter) and human causes, and invites us to weigh them.
  • Darwin's indifferent Nature replaces providence; the last paragraph's "Justice" and "sport" turn fate's language against society.
  • Mistimed love links Tess to poems of love and time.
  • Each place in Tess's Wessex is a season, a mood and an economy: lush Talbothays, "starve-acre" Flintcomb-Ash.
  • Hardy uses pathetic fallacy and also questions it: Nature is often indifferent ("like flies").
  • Context: the agricultural depression (about 1873–1896), yearly migration at Lady Day and machines like the threshing "tyrant".
  • Place shapes love.
  • The double standard, in custom and in law, judged a woman's sexual past for ever and a man's lightly; Angel's reaction dramatises it.
  • The Chase is deliberately unnarrated; readers debate rape or seduction, and Hardy's revisions (serial, 1891, 1892, 1912) changed the evidence. Jacobus sees Tess's purity as partly built in revision.
  • "A Pure Woman" defines purity by Nature and will: a provocation to "the ordinances of civilization".
  • Compare "The Ruined Maid".
  • Hardy's omniscient narrator is partisan: he defends "our heroine", generalises like an essayist and uses irony ("Justice").
  • He also dwells on Tess's body ("a luxuriance of aspect") and makes her a "tissue" others mark: Boumelha and Silverman examine that gaze.
  • Lawrence (Angel loves an idea), Williams (educated rural working class), Jacobus (purity built in revision): use critics to open questions, then test them.

Key quotations to learn

  • It was to be. (Phase 1, Chapter XI (narrator, quoting Tess's people)) Fatalism closes Phase the First.
  • An immeasurable social chasm was to divide our heroine’s personality thereafter from that previous self of hers (Phase 1, Chapter XI) The Phase break as a social split.
  • Almost at a leap Tess thus changed from simple girl to complex woman. (Phase 2, Chapter XV) The turn from Phase 2 to the Rally.
  • It is too late (Phase 7, Chapter LV (Tess to Angel)) Mistiming shapes the plot.
  • It is as it should be (Phase 7, Chapter LVIII (Tess at Stonehenge)) Tess's calm acceptance near the end.
  • spoke two languages (Phase 1, Chapter III (narrator)) Tess between dialect and standard English, two worlds.
  • Did it never strike your mind that what every woman says some women may feel? (Phase 2, Chapter XII (Tess to Alec)) Tess claims her own feelings against a stereotype.
  • My eyes were dazed by you for a little, and that was all. (Phase 2, Chapter XII (Tess to Alec)) Tess's own account; central to debates about The Chase.
  • I love you for ever—in all changes, in all disgraces, because you are yourself. (Phase 5, Chapter XXXV (Tess to Angel)) Her constancy against his judgement.
  • I am ready (Phase 7, Chapter LVIII (Tess at Stonehenge)) Her last act is a choice of calm.
  • tragic mischief (Phase 1, Chapter V (narrator, of Alec)) Alec as the agent of the tragedy.
  • She was no longer the milkmaid, but a visionary essence of woman (Phase 3, Chapter XX (narrator)) Angel's idealising.
  • Call me Tess (Phase 3, Chapter XX (Tess to Angel)) Tess resists being turned into a type.
  • the woman I have been loving is not you (Phase 5, Chapter XXXV (Angel)) Angel's love was for an image.
  • You temptress, Tess; you dear damned witch of Babylon (Phase 6, Chapter XLVI (Alec)) Alec blames Tess for his own desire.
  • a hard logical deposit, like a vein of metal in a soft loam (Phase 5, Chapter XXXVI (narrator, of Angel)) Why Angel cannot accept Tess.
  • A blighted one. (Phase 1, Chapter IV (Tess to Abraham)) Tess's fatalism, just before Prince dies.
  • a morality good enough for divinities (Phase 1, Chapter XI (narrator)) The narrator rejects inherited guilt.
  • the man to love rarely coincides with the hour for loving (Phase 1, Chapter V (narrator)) Chance and timing in love.
  • “Justice” was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess. (Phase 7, Chapter LIX (narrator)) Fate's language used to indict human law.
  • As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods; / They kill us for their sport. (Hardy's 1892 preface, quoting King Lear (Gloucester)) Hardy's defence of the ending.
  • like a fly on a billiard-table (Phase 3, Chapter XVI (narrator)) Tess dwarfed by the valley.
  • as if they were Adam and Eve (Phase 3, Chapter XX (narrator)) Talbothays as Eden.
  • starve-acre place (Phase 5, Chapter XLIII (narrator, after Marian)) Flintcomb-Ash's poverty.
  • the red tyrant that the women had come to serve (Phase 6, Chapter XLVII (narrator)) The machine rules the workers.
  • He was in the agricultural world, but not of it. (Phase 6, Chapter XLVII (narrator, of the engine-man)) Machinery as an outsider.
  • the tendency of water to flow uphill when forced by machinery (Phase 6, Chapter LI (narrator)) Forced rural migration.
  • It was they that were out of harmony with the actual world, not she. (Phase 2, Chapter XIII (narrator)) The narrator sides with Tess against convention.
  • She had been made to break an accepted social law, but no law known to the environment (Phase 2, Chapter XIII (narrator)) Social law versus Nature.
  • succumbed to adroit advantages he took of her helplessness (Phase 2, Chapter XII (narrator)) Key evidence in the Chase debate.
  • Once victim, always victim—that’s the law! (Phase 6, Chapter XLVII (Tess to Alec)) The double standard in Tess's own words.
  • the meaning of the word in Nature (1892 preface (Hardy)) Hardy's defence of "pure".
  • our heroine (Phase 1, Chapter XI (narrator)) The narrator takes Tess's side.
  • a luxuriance of aspect, a fulness of growth (Phase 1, Chapter V (narrator)) The narrator's gaze on Tess's body.
  • this beautiful feminine tissue, sensitive as gossamer, and practically blank as snow as yet (Phase 1, Chapter XI (narrator)) Tess as a surface others mark.
  • Poor wounded name! My bosom as a bed / Shall lodge thee. (Epigraph (from Shakespeare, The Two Gentlemen of Verona)) The narrator as Tess's protector.
  • a novel is an impression, not an argument (1892 preface (Hardy)) Hardy's reply to his critics.
  • a disrespectful phrase about the Immortals (1892 preface (Hardy, on a reviewer)) Contemporary reception of the ending.
