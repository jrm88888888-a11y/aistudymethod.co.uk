Mission 1

Justice at the heart of revelation

  • 'Adl and qist: justice, fairness, putting things in their right place.
  • Muslims believe God sent prophets with scripture and "the Balance (of Right and Wrong), that men may stand forth in justice" (Qur'an 57:25, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good, and liberality to kith and kin" (Qur'an 16:90, Yusuf Ali).
  • The poor: zakah is their right, not a favour (9:60;). Surah 107 links denying the Judgement with failing to feed the poor.
  • The oppressed (mustad'afun): the Qur'an asks why believers do not act for "those who, being weak, are ill-treated (and oppressed)" (Qur'an 4:75, Yusuf Ali).

Mission 1

Enjoining good, forbidding wrong

  • Al-amr bil-ma'ruf wal-nahy 'an al-munkar: "enjoining what is right, and forbidding what is wrong" (Qur'an 3:104, Yusuf Ali); also 3:110 and 9:71 (believing men and women).
  • Hadith: whoever sees a wrong should change it with his hand; if he cannot, with his tongue; if he cannot, with his heart, the weakest faith (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Fard kifaya: a communal duty. If enough people do it, the rest are released. ("Let there arise out of you a band of people", 3:104.)
  • Protecting rights (classical limits): no spying ("spy not on each other", 49:12); no breaking into homes; act only on wrongs that are open and agreed; never cause a greater harm.
  • Historic office: the muhtasib (market inspector) checked weights, prices and public conduct.

Mission 1

Jihad and mercy

  • Jihad: "striving" in God's way.
  • Greater jihad: the struggle against one's own nafs (lower self). A well-known report calls it greater than fighting; scholars of hadith judge its chain weak, but the idea is widespread.
  • Lesser jihad: armed struggle, for defence and against oppression, within limits: "Fight … those who fight you, but do not transgress limits" (Qur'an 2:190, Yusuf Ali).
  • Mercy: "We sent thee not, but as a Mercy for all creatures" (Qur'an 21:107, Yusuf Ali).
  • At the conquest of Makkah (630) the Prophet pardoned his former persecutors, echoing Joseph's words to his brothers: "This day let no reproach be (cast) on you" (Qur'an 12:92, Yusuf Ali).
  • Hadith: the merciful are shown mercy by the Most Merciful (Abu Dawud, Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
Mission 1

Qisas, diya and maslaha

  • Qisas: equal retribution for murder and injury (2:178, 5:45). "In the Law of Equality there is (saving of) Life to you" (Qur'an 2:179, Yusuf Ali): it deters and ends blood feuds.
  • Diya: compensation paid to the victim's family instead.
  • Forgiveness is praised: remitting retaliation "is an act of atonement for himself" (Qur'an 5:45, Yusuf Ali). The victim's family chooses.
  • Maslaha: public interest. Al-Ghazali (al-Mustasfa): a true maslaha protects the five aims of Shari'a: faith, life, intellect, family line, property.
  • Examples: Abu Bakr gathering the Qur'an into one book; Umar suspending the penalty for theft in a year of famine.
  • Balance: individual rights (the family's choice in qisas) and public interest (order, safety) are weighed together.

Mission 1

Ali Shari'ati: religion as revolution

  • Ali Shari'ati (1933–1977), Iranian sociologist; doctorate in Paris (1964), where he read Frantz Fanon and debated existentialists and Marxists.
  • Marxist influence: history as a struggle between oppressors and oppressed (he read Cain and Abel this way). But he rejected Marxist materialism: faith drives liberation.
  • Red Shi'ism of Ali (protest, martyrdom, justice) vs Black Shi'ism of the Safavids (state religion, passive mourning, waiting).
  • Criticism: of religion serving power, in the caliphs' official Sunnism and in apolitical, clerical Shi'ism.
  • Husayn at Karbala: a model of active resistance to tyranny, not only grief.
  • Lectures at the Husayniyeh Ershad, Tehran (1967–72); imprisoned (1973–75); died in England in 1977. His ideas inspired many young revolutionaries of 1979.
Mission 1

Abdal Hakim Murad: the forgotten revolution

  • Abdal Hakim Murad (Timothy Winter, b. 1960), Cambridge scholar; founding Dean of Cambridge Muslim College.
  • Essay "Islamic Spirituality: the Forgotten Revolution" (1990s): modern Islamist revivalism is outward, angry and political; it has forgotten the purification of the soul (tazkiyah) (paraphrase).
  • Anger without inner change produces extremism and fails the oppressed.
  • Traditionalist Sufi approach: renew the heart through the classical spiritual tradition; then just action follows.
  • "Activism within": the first struggle is with the self (the greater jihad).
  • Support often cited: "Verily never will Allah change the condition of a people until they change it themselves (with their own souls)." (Qur'an 13:11, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 1

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberationist: reading the Qur'an from the side of the poor and oppressed, and acting to change unjust structures, not only to give charity.
  • Farid Esack (b. 1959), South Africa: fought apartheid with the Call of Islam movement, alongside Christians and others; Qur'an, Liberation and Pluralism (1997): God sides with the oppressed (paraphrase).
  • Asghar Ali Engineer (1939–2013), India: Islam and Liberation Theology (1990): Islam began as a movement for the weak against the rich of Makkah (paraphrase).

Mission 2

Jihad means striving

  • Jihad: from j-h-d, "to strive, to make an effort". Not the word for "holy war".
  • "And those who strive in Our (cause),- We will certainly guide them to our Paths" (Qur'an 29:69, Yusuf Ali).
  • Forms of jihad: of the self (nafs); of the tongue and pen (speaking truth, teaching); of wealth (giving); and armed struggle.
  • "Strive in His cause as ye ought to strive" (Qur'an 22:78, Yusuf Ali).
  • Tradition: greater jihad (al-jihad al-akbar): struggle with the self; lesser jihad (al-jihad al-asghar): armed struggle.
Mission 2

Greater jihad: the nafs

  • Nafs: the self, the ego with its desires.
  • Three states named from the Qur'an: the soul inciting to evil (12:53); the self-reproaching soul, conscience (75:2); the soul at peace (89:27).
  • Greater jihad: lifelong effort to move the self towards peace with God: prayer, fasting, honesty, controlling anger and greed.
  • The Prophet is reported to have said that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for God (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).
  • A popular report calls returning from battle a return "from the lesser jihad to the greater" (al-Bayhaqi; paraphrase; many scholars grade it weak).

Mission 2

Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time

  • Makkah (610–622): persecution, but no fighting; patience and preaching.
  • 622: the hijrah to Madinah. Permission to fight comes because the Muslims "are wronged" (22:39).
  • Battles: Badr (624), Uhud (625), the Trench (627).
  • 628: Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace accepted on hard terms.
  • 630: Makkah taken almost without fighting; a general amnesty.
  • Jurists made lesser jihad a communal duty of the ummah (fard kifaya), led by legitimate authority; it becomes an individual duty if the land is invaded.
  • OCR: lesser and greater jihad are both commanded, within the call to justice and to enjoin good and forbid wrong (3:104).
Mission 2

Conditions of lesser jihad

  • Just cause: defence, or ending oppression of the weak (4:75), never forcing faith: "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a recognised leader, not by individuals.
  • Right intention: for God and justice, not gain or revenge.
  • Last resort and proportion: "do not transgress limits" (2:190).
  • Protect non-combatants: the Prophet forbade killing women and children (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase); Abu Bakr told his commanders not to kill the old or children, cut fruit trees or destroy buildings (Malik, Muwatta; paraphrase).
  • Stop when the enemy seeks peace (8:61); treat prisoners humanely.

Mission 2

Terrorism is not jihad

  • Terrorism: violence against civilians to spread fear for political goals.
  • Mainstream scholars across Sunni and Shi'a Islam reject calling it jihad: no legitimate authority, civilians targeted, suicide forbidden (4:29).
  • "If any one slew a person - unless it be for murder or for spreading mischief in the land - it would be as if he slew the whole people" (Qur'an 5:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Examples: the Amman Message (2004–05); Tahir-ul-Qadri's Fatwa on Terrorism and Suicide Bombings (2010); scholars' open letter to the leader of ISIS (2014).
  • Groups that use the word for terror are a small minority, condemned by most Muslim bodies; many of their victims are Muslims.

Mission 3

Justice and the aims of punishment

  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good" (Qur'an 16:90, Yusuf Ali); "when ye judge between man and man, that ye judge with justice" (Qur'an 4:58, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muslims believe crimes wrong God, the victim and society.
  • Aims of punishment in fiqh: retribution (justice for the wronged), deterrence, protection of society, reform and repentance.
  • Final judgement belongs to God; earthly punishment is limited and needs strict proof.
Mission 3

Hudud: fixed limits

  • Hudud (singular hadd, "limit"): penalties fixed by Qur'an or Sunna, seen as God's right; a judge may not change them.
  • Examples: theft (5:38); unlawful sexual relations (24:2); false accusation of unchastity (24:4); armed robbery and spreading terror (hiraba, 5:33); drinking alcohol (from the Sunna).
  • Strict proof: e.g. four eyewitnesses for adultery (24:4); confession can be withdrawn; theft only above a minimum value and not from need.
  • Legal maxim: "avert the hudud by doubts" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase). Caliph Umar is reported to have suspended the theft penalty in a famine.
  • Today: few states apply hudud; many Muslims and scholars question whether they can be applied justly now.
Mission 3

Qisas and diya

  • Qisas: equal retribution for murder or deliberate injury: "life for life, eye for eye" (5:45). It limits revenge: one life for one life, never more (17:33).
  • The victim's family chooses: qisas; diya (compensation, "blood money"); or forgiveness.
  • Diya is also due for accidental killing (4:92), with an act of expiation.
  • A court must try the case and carry out any sentence: never private revenge.
  • Forgiveness is praised: "if a person forgives and makes reconciliation, his reward is due from Allah" (Qur'an 42:40, Yusuf Ali).
  • OCR: qisas (retribution) and diya (restitution).

Mission 3

Ta'zir and maslaha

  • Ta'zir: discretionary punishments set by a judge or the state for offences without a fixed penalty: e.g. fraud, bribery, drug dealing, cases where hadd proof fails.
  • Can be fines, prison, community penalties; aimed at reform and deterrence.
  • Guided by maslaha (public interest): laws should protect the five essentials named by al-Ghazali (1058–1111): religion, life, intellect, lineage, property.
  • So the state can make new laws (traffic, cyber-crime) that serve the shari'a's purposes.
  • OCR: maslaha balances individual rights and public interest.
Mission 3

Enjoining good: a communal duty

  • "Let there arise out of you a band of people inviting to all that is good, enjoining what is right, and forbidding what is wrong" (Qur'an 3:104, Yusuf Ali).
  • Al-amr bil-ma'ruf wal-nahy 'an al-munkar is a fard kifaya: a duty on the community, fulfilled when some carry it out.
  • Hadith: whoever sees a wrong should change it with the hand, if unable then with the tongue, if unable then in the heart (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Individual rights limit it: "spy not on each other" (49:12); al-Ghazali taught that a wrong must be openly visible, not found by searching homes.
  • Enforcement by force belongs to lawful authority, not to individuals or vigilante groups.

Mission 4

Madinah: the model

  • In Madinah (622–632) the Prophet was prophet, judge, law-giver and commander.
  • Constitution of Madinah: one community (ummah) above tribe; Jewish tribes kept their religion; disputes referred to God and His Messenger.
  • Religious: prayer, zakah and fasting set the rhythm of society. Moral: justice, care for the poor. Political: treaties, defence, consultation.
  • So most Muslims hold that Islam began with no split between religion and state. A minority disagree: Ali Abd al-Raziq (Islam and the Foundations of Governance, 1925) argued the Prophet's mission was spiritual, not the founding of a state.
  • The debate: is Madinah a blueprint to copy, or a set of principles (justice, consultation, accountability) to apply in new forms?
Mission 4Read the source

Authority and consultation

> "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you. If ye differ in anything among yourselves, refer it to Allah and His Messenger" (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali)

> "who (conduct) their affairs by mutual Consultation" (Qur'an 42:38, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Prophet was told: "consult them in affairs (of moment)" (Qur'an 3:159, Yusuf Ali).
  • Shura: consultation. Ulu al-amr: "those charged with authority": rulers, scholars, or both? Interpreters differ.

Mission 4

Sunni theory: a contract

  • After the Prophet, Abu Bakr was chosen by companions and given the bay'ah (pledge of allegiance).
  • Al-Mawardi (974–1058), al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya (The Ordinances of Government): the imamate (caliphate) is a contract ('aqd) between the caliph and "those who loose and bind" (qualified electors).
  • Caliph's duties: protect the faith, enforce the law, judge, defend the borders, collect zakah, appoint honest officials.
  • Complementary roles: the ruler holds power and enforces; the ulama interpret Shari'a and advise. Neither replaces the other.
  • Obedience: most Sunni scholars counselled obeying even a flawed ruler who keeps the prayer, to avoid civil strife (fitnah). Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328): government must serve Shari'a (al-Siyasa al-Shar'iyya).
Mission 4

Shi'a theory: the Imam and the ulama

  • Imam: chosen by God, designated by the previous Imam, infallible (ma'sum). The supreme authority in religion and politics.
  • For Twelvers, the twelfth Imam went into Occultation (from 874; the Greater Occultation from 941).
  • In his absence, the ulama act as his general deputies: they judge, teach, receive khums, guide believers.
  • Quietist view: no state is fully legitimate before the Imam returns; scholars guide but do not rule (e.g. Ayatollah Sistani, b. 1930, Najaf).
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just senior jurist should govern on the Imam's behalf. Basis of Iran's 1979 constitution.

Mission 4

Western codes and secularism

  • In the 19th–20th centuries many reformers identified secularism with modernisation and progress.
  • Ottoman Empire: French-based commercial and penal codes (1850s); the Mecelle (1869–76) codified Hanafi civil law.
  • Turkey: Atatürk abolished the sultanate (1922) and the caliphate (1924), adopted the Swiss civil code (1926), and made the state secular.
  • Egypt: European-style courts (1875, 1883); the civil code of al-Sanhuri (1949) blended French law with Shari'a principles.
  • Iran: Reza Shah (ruled 1925–41) moved law and education away from the clergy.
  • Family law usually stayed Shari'a-based: marriage, divorce, inheritance.
Mission 4

Opposition: God's law, not the West's

  • Critics linked secularism with Western imperialism and moral decline.
  • Hasan al-Banna (1906–1949) founded the Muslim Brotherhood (Egypt, 1928): Islam as a complete way of life, including the state.
  • Abul A'la Mawdudi (1903–1979), Jamaat-e-Islami (1941): sovereignty (hakimiyyah) belongs to God alone; an Islamic state is a "theo-democracy": elected, but bound by Shari'a.
  • Sayyid Qutb (1906–1966), Milestones (1964): societies that replace God's law with human law live in jahiliyyah (ignorance).
  • The Iranian Revolution (1979) put opposition to the secular Shah into power.
  • Many Muslims oppose secularism without endorsing these movements.
Mission 4

Shari'a in modern Muslim states

  • Saudi Arabia: the Qur'an and Sunnah are the constitution (Basic Law, 1992); Hanbali law applied by judges.
  • Iran: Islamic Republic; elected president and parliament under the Supreme Leader and the Guardian Council.
  • Egypt: "principles of Islamic Shari'a are the principal source of legislation" (constitution, from 1980).
  • Pakistan: Islamic Republic (1956); a Federal Shariat Court reviews laws (1980).
  • Turkey: secular constitution. Indonesia: largest Muslim-majority democracy; not an Islamic state (Pancasila), though Aceh applies Shari'a by-laws.
  • Your spec: "the status of the Shari'a in modern Muslim states".

Mission 4

Law schools and democracy

  • Incompatible: law-making belongs to God alone; majority votes cannot permit what God forbids (e.g. Hizb ut-Tahrir, banned in the UK since 2024; Qutb).
  • Compatible: shura (42:38), the bay'ah as consent, and the accountability of rulers make Islam friendly to democracy (e.g. Khaled Abou El Fadl, Islam and the Challenge of Democracy, 2004; Rached Ghannouchi, Tunisia).
  • In between: Mawdudi's theo-democracy; Iran's elected bodies checked by jurists.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Islam is compatible with democracy."

  • For: shura (42:38, 3:159); the first caliphs chosen and pledged to; elections in Indonesia, Pakistan and Tunisia (from 2011); British Muslims vote and stand for office.
  • For: the contract theory of al-Mawardi.
  • Against: sovereignty belongs to God (Mawdudi, Qutb); a majority could vote against Shari'a; the Prophet's authority came from revelation, not votes.
  • Another view: it depends on what "democracy" means: majority rule within fixed moral limits is found in many democracies.
  • Judge it by: Who makes law? Who interprets Shari'a? Can minorities be equal citizens? Is Madinah a blueprint or a set of principles?

Hijra: migrating for faith

  • Hijra (migration): in 622 CE the Prophet and his followers moved from Makkah to Madinah to practise Islam freely. The Islamic calendar counts from it.
  • Earlier (c. 615) some Muslims fled to Abyssinia, where a just Christian king protected them: a model for living as a minority under a non-Muslim ruler.

> "Was not Allah's earth spacious that ye could have migrated therein?" (Qur'an 4:97, Pickthall)

> "Whoso migrateth for the cause of Allah will find much refuge and abundance in the earth" (Qur'an 4:100, Pickthall)

  • Classical jurists divided the world into dar al-Islam (abode of Islam) and dar al-harb (abode of war); some added dar al-'ahd (abode of treaty).
  • Many held: if you cannot practise Islam where you live, migrate. If you can practise freely, staying is allowed (some Shafi'i jurists said it may even be better).

Q1 According to 4:97, what is wrong with staying where you are oppressed?

Show answer

You could have migrated somewhere you could live your faith.

Q2 Why is Abyssinia important for European Muslims today?

Show answer

It shows Muslims living faithfully under a just non-Muslim ruler.

Mission 5

Da'wa: inviting to Islam

  • Da'wa (invitation, mission): sharing Islam with others.

> "Call unto the way of thy Lord with wisdom and fair exhortation, and reason with them in the better way." (Qur'an 16:125, Pickthall)

  • Limited by "There is no compulsion in religion" (Qur'an 2:256, Pickthall): conversion must be free.
  • Methods: good example (often seen as the most effective); mosque open days; literature and street stalls; online teaching.
  • Many converts in Europe describe study and friendship, not preaching, as what drew them.
  • Debate: is da'wa in a secular society a duty, or a source of tension when it seems pushy?
  • Tariq Ramadan (b. 1962) calls Europe dar al-shahada (abode of witness): a place to bear witness to Islam by how Muslims live.
Mission 5

Minority jurisprudence

  • Fiqh al-aqalliyyat (minority jurisprudence): applying Shari'a for Muslims who live as a minority.
  • Developed from the 1990s by Taha Jabir al-Alwani (1935–2016, USA) and Yusuf al-Qaradawi (1926–2022), through the European Council for Fatwa and Research (founded 1997).
  • Principles: public good (maslaha); necessity (darura); ease (God "desireth not hardship for you", Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall); local custom ('urf).
  • Respecting the law of the land: citizenship is a covenant; "keep the covenant" (Qur'an 17:34, Pickthall). Muslims obey local law unless it forces them to sin.
  • Example: the Council allowed a conventional mortgage for a family's first home where no Islamic option exists (necessity).
  • Critics: some traditional scholars: it waters down Shari'a; Ramadan and others: Muslims are citizens, not a "minority" needing special law.

Mission 5

A European Muslim identity

  • Multiculturalism: policies that recognise and support different cultural and religious groups within one state.
  • "Euro-Islam" (Bassam Tibi, b. 1944, from 1992): an Islam that accepts secular democracy, pluralism and individual rights, against what he calls "ghetto Islam".
  • Ramadan: Muslims can be fully European and fully Muslim: faithful to principles, flexible in culture.
  • Mustafa Cerić (b. 1952), Grand Mufti of Bosnia-Herzegovina 1993–2012, A Declaration of European Muslims (2006), paraphrase:
  • European Muslims accept the social contract: the rule of law, democracy, human rights and tolerance.
  • Islam is not foreign to Europe: Bosnian Muslims are native Europeans.
  • He asks Europe to recognise Islam in its institutions and to protect Muslims from discrimination; Muslims must reject terrorism.
  • OCR spellings: hijra, da'wa, Shari'a.
Mission 5

After 9/11: multiculturalism under fire

  • Events: 11 September 2001; Madrid (2004) and London (7 July 2005) bombings; the Danish cartoons (2005–06); Charlie Hebdo (2015).
  • Laws: France banned conspicuous religious symbols in state schools (2004) and face coverings in public (2010); Swiss voters banned new minarets (2009).
  • Opponents of multiculturalism: Angela Merkel (2010) and David Cameron (Munich, 2011) said state multiculturalism had failed and called for shared national values.
  • Supporters reply: multiculturalism is not the cause of extremism; exclusion and discrimination push young people away.
  • Muslims across Europe condemned the attacks, yet often felt treated as suspects.
Mission 5

What is Islamophobia?

  • Term made widely known by the Runnymede Trust report Islamophobia: A Challenge for Us All (1997).
  • It contrasted closed views of Islam (one unchanging bloc; separate from other cultures; inferior; an enemy; manipulative) with open views (diverse; interacting; open to fair criticism).
  • APPG on British Muslims (2018) definition: Islamophobia is rooted in racism and targets "expressions of Muslimness or perceived Muslimness".
  • Debate: critics say "Islamophobia" may chill legitimate criticism of religion and prefer "anti-Muslim hatred"; supporters say it names a real pattern of prejudice.
  • Todd Green (The Fear of Islam, 2015) traces such fears back through European history, long before 9/11.

Mission 5

Bias, misrepresentation, inaccuracy

  • Bias: choosing which stories to tell. Studies of UK newspapers (e.g. Cardiff University, 2008) found most stories about British Muslims framed them as a threat or a problem.
  • Misrepresentation: the actions of a few presented as typical; "Islamist" and "Muslim" blurred.
  • Inaccuracy: false claims, sometimes later corrected by the press regulator (IPSO).
  • Politics: politicians' language about veils, migration or "no-go areas" can make prejudice seem respectable.
  • Online: algorithms reward outrage; organised hate campaigns; groups such as Tell MAMA record anti-Muslim incidents.
  • Edward Said (1935–2003), Covering Islam (1981): Western media present "Islam" as one threatening thing.

Mission 5Evaluate

Does Islam permit life as a minority?

  • Claim: Islam fully allows Muslims to live as a minority in non-Muslim lands.
  • For: the Abyssinian precedent; jurists who allowed residence where Islam can be practised freely; 17:34 on keeping covenants; minority jurisprudence; Ramadan's "abode of witness".
  • Against: the classical ideal is life under Shari'a; 4:97 warns those who stay where faith is hard; some scholars see minority fiqh as compromise.
  • Against: practice is sometimes limited (veil bans, minaret ban).
  • Judge it by: Can Islam be practised freely? Is Shari'a mainly state law or personal duty? Does the old dar al-Islam / dar al-harb map fit a world of citizens?
Mission 5Evaluate

How far does the media shape perceptions of Islam?

  • Claim: The media is the main cause of negative Western perceptions of Islam.
  • For: most people meet Islam mainly through news; studies show threat-framing; online outrage spreads fast; Said's argument.
  • Against: real events (terror attacks) shape fear whatever the coverage; older history (crusades, empire) shaped attitudes before modern media (Green).
  • Against: personal contact with Muslim neighbours and colleagues changes views; media also produces positive portrayals.
  • Judge it by: What evidence links coverage to attitudes? Which media: press, TV, online? Is Islam represented accurately today?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Justice ('adl) is central: 57:25, 4:135, 5:8; the poor have a right to zakah; the Qur'an calls for action for the oppressed (4:75).
  • Enjoining good is a communal duty (fard kifaya) within limits; qisas, diya and maslaha balance rights and public interest; mercy marks the Prophet's conduct.
  • Shari'ati: revolutionary "red Shi'ism"; Murad: the forgotten inner revolution; liberationists (Esack, Engineer) side with the poor.

Mission 2

  • Jihad = striving: greater (against the nafs) and lesser (armed, under conditions).
  • Lesser jihad in the Prophet's time was a response to persecution and attack, limited by the Qur'an (22:39, 2:190, 8:61) and the Prophet's rules.

Mission 3

  • Three categories: hudud (fixed, strict proof), qisas (retribution, with diya or forgiveness by the family), ta'zir (discretion, guided by maslaha).
  • Enjoining good and forbidding wrong is a fard kifaya, limited by individual rights (49:12).

Mission 4

  • Madinah united religion and state; Muslims debate whether it is a blueprint or a set of principles (4:59, 42:38).
  • Sunni theory: a contract (al-Mawardi), ruler and ulama in complementary roles; Shi'a theory: the Imam, then the ulama in his absence (quietism or wilayat al-faqih).
  • Modern states range from secular to Islamic; Muslims disagree whether Islam and democracy fit together.

Mission 5

  • Hijra, the Abyssinian precedent, da'wa and minority jurisprudence give Muslims ways to live faithfully as a minority, within the law of the land.
  • European Islam (Tibi, Ramadan, Cerić's 2006 Declaration) argues Muslims can be fully European; opponents of multiculturalism grew louder after 9/11.
  • Islamophobia (Runnymede, 1997) involves closed views of Islam; bias, misrepresentation and inaccuracy in politics, media and online shape perceptions.
