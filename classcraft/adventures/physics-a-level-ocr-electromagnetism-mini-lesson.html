Mission 1

Mission 1: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Mission 1

Where magnetic fields come from

  • A magnetic field is produced by moving charges (an electric current) and by permanent magnets.
  • It exerts forces on moving charges, currents and magnetic materials (iron, steel).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Mission 1

Field lines

  • Direction of a field line: the way the north end of a compass needle points.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Field lines are closed loops: outside a magnet they run N → S; inside, S → N.
  • Bar magnet: lines leave the N pole, curve around, enter the S pole, and continue back through the magnet.
Quick check
?Point C is at the centre of a bar magnet, inside it. Point D is just outside the magnet, beside its centre. How does the magnitude of the magnetic field at C compare with that at D?
Mission 1

Field of a long straight wire

  • Field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current; curled fingers give the field direction.
  • Lines spread out with distance: the field gets weaker away from the wire.
  • End-on views: ⊙ current out of the page → field anticlockwise; ⊗ into the page → clockwise.
Quick check
?A long, straight, vertical wire carries a steady current upwards. Point P is 5.0 cm due east of the wire, level with it. What is the direction of the magnetic field of the wire at P?
Mission 1

Flat coil and solenoid

  • Flat coil: the circles around each part of the wire combine. At the centre the field is along the axis.
  • Solenoid (long coil): inside, straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines (uniform field). Outside, the pattern of a bar magnet.
  • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows around the turns; the thumb points along the field inside, towards the coil's N end.
Quick check
?You look at the left end of a long solenoid. Seen from there, the current in its turns flows clockwise. Which end of the solenoid is its north end?
Mission check

?A flat horizontal circular coil carries a steady current that flows clockwise when viewed from above. What is the direction of the magnetic field at the centre of the coil?

Same rule, new shape. Around a straight wire the field circles the current, so a compass needle just below a wire laid along it swings towards lying across the wire, tangent to a circle centred on it: fully across if the wire's field is much stronger than Earth's. Reverse the current and it swings the other way.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Make a guess: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Mission 2

The motor effect

  • A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force.
  • The force is at right angles to both the current and the field.
  • No force when the current is parallel to the field.
  • Reverse the current or the field: the force reverses.
Mission 2

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field (N → S); seCond finger = Current (+ → −); thuMb = Motion (force).
Quick check
?A straight wire lies in the plane of the page and carries a current towards the right of the page. A uniform magnetic field in the plane of the page is directed towards the top of the page. What is the direction of the force on the wire?
Mission 2

How big?

  • F = BIL sin θ
  • θ = angle between the current and the field. Largest at 90°, zero at 0°.
  • The length is the length inside the field.
  • B is in tesla (T).
Mission 2

Magnetic flux density

  • B is the magnetic flux density.
  • Rearranged (current ⟂ field): B = F/(IL): force per unit current per unit length.
  • 1 tesla: the field in which a wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field feels 1 N on each metre.
  • 1 T = 1 N A⁻¹ m⁻¹.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A 4.0 cm length of wire carries 2.5 A at right angles to a 0.15 T field.
  • Force = 0.15 × 2.5 × 0.040 = 0.015 N.
  • Turn the wire to 30° to the field: F = 0.015 × sin 30° = 0.0075 N.
  • Backwards: 0.012 N on a 5.0 cm wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles. B = force ÷ (current × length) = 0.012 ÷ (3.0 × 0.050) = 0.080 T.
Quick check
?A straight wire 0.40 m long carries a current of 3.0 A. Only 0.25 m of the wire lies inside a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.080 T, and the wire makes an angle of 30° with the direction of the field. What is the magnitude of the force on the wire?
Mission 2

Measuring B with a balance

  • A U-shaped magnet sits on a top-pan (digital) balance. A stiff wire is clamped across the gap, at right angles to the field, not touching the magnet.
  • Current on: the wire is pushed one way, so (Newton's third law) the magnet is pushed the other way. The reading changes by Δm.
  • F = Δm × g.
  • Vary I: plot F against I. Straight line through the origin; gradient = BL, so B = gradient ÷ L.
  • Zero the balance with the current off; reverse the current and average to cancel stray effects.
Quick check
?In a current-balance experiment, 5.0 cm of a horizontal wire lies at right angles to the field between the poles of a magnet resting on a balance. With no current the balance reads 152.46 g. With a current of 3.0 A it reads 152.10 g. Take g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹. What is the magnetic flux density between the poles?
Mission check

?A long straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. It is in a uniform horizontal magnetic field that points due north. In which direction is the magnetic force on the wire?

The force is at right angles to both the current and the field, and its size is B × current × length when they are perpendicular: so a wire between the poles is pushed sideways, out of the gap or into it, never towards a pole.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Make a guess: A magnetic field can steer a beam of charged particles. Can it also make them go faster?

Mission 3

Force on a moving charge

  • A current is moving charge, so a single moving charge in a field also feels a force.
  • F = BQv (velocity ⟂ field).
  • Zero force on a charge at rest, or moving along the field.
  • Example: an electron at 3.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹ at right angles to a 0.20 T field: F = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 3.0 × 10⁶ × 0.20 = 9.6 × 10⁻¹⁴ N.
Mission 3

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule, with the second finger along the conventional current: the direction a positive charge moves.
  • For an electron, point the second finger opposite to its motion.
  • The force is always ⟂ to the velocity, so it does no work: the speed and kinetic energy never change.
Quick check
?An electron moves towards the right of the page, in the plane of the page, through a uniform magnetic field directed into the page. What is the direction of the magnetic force on the electron at that instant?
Mission 3

Circular paths

  • Only the direction changes. Uniform field, v ⟂ B: a circle.
  • The magnetic force is the centripetal force: magnetic force = mv²/r.
  • r = mv/(Bq)
  • Faster or heavier → bigger circle. Stronger field or more charge → tighter circle.
  • Time for one orbit: T = 2πr/v = 2πm/(Bq).
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A proton (m = 1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ kg, q = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) at 2.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹, at right angles to a 0.50 T field.
  • Radius: r = mv/(Bq) = (1.673 × 10⁻²⁷ × 2.0 × 10⁶) ÷ (0.50 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹) = 0.042 m.
  • Period: T = 2πm/(Bq) = 1.3 × 10⁻⁷ s.
  • Double the field: r halves to 0.021 m, and T halves too, to 6.6 × 10⁻⁸ s.
Quick check
?A proton moves in a circle in a uniform magnetic field that is perpendicular to its velocity. A second proton moves at twice the speed of the first, perpendicular to the same field. Compared with the first proton, what are the radius of the second proton's circle and the time it takes to complete one revolution?
Quick check
?In a velocity selector, uniform electric and magnetic fields act at right angles to each other and to the velocity of the particles, so that the electric and magnetic forces on a proton are in opposite directions. Protons moving at speed v0 pass straight through undeflected. Gravity is negligible. Which statement about this velocity selector is correct?
Mission 3

Crossed fields

  • In both E and B fields, a charge feels two independent forces: EQ (any speed) and BQv (depends on speed).
  • E and B at right angles, both ⟂ to the beam, forces opposite. Straight through only if EQ = BQv: v = E/B. This is a velocity selector.
  • Faster particles: magnetic force wins. Slower: electric force wins.
  • Example: plates 1.0 cm apart, 400 V, B = 0.050 T. E = 4.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹; v = E/B = 8.0 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹.
Mission check

?Two protons enter two separate regions at the same speed. Region 1 contains only a uniform electric field; region 2 contains only a uniform magnetic field. Each proton enters at right angles to the field lines. Gravity is negligible. A short time later, which statement is correct?

The magnetic force is always at right angles to the velocity. It changes direction, never speed, so a magnetic field alone can bend a beam but never accelerate it to a higher energy. That is why particle accelerators use electric fields to speed particles up; magnetic fields only steer them.

Mission 4

Make a guess: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Mission 4

Magnetic flux

  • Magnetic flux: how much field passes through an area. Think: number of field lines through it.
  • ϕ = BA cos θ, θ = angle between B and the normal to the area.
  • Unit: weber. 1 Wb = 1 T m².
  • Plane of the loop parallel to the field: zero flux.
Mission 4

Flux linkage

  • A coil of N turns: the same flux passes through every turn.
  • Flux linkage = Nϕ.
Quick check
?A flat rectangular loop measures 6.0 cm by 5.0 cm. It is in a uniform magnetic field of 0.25 T, with the plane of the loop at 20° to the field lines. What is the magnetic flux through the loop?
Mission 4

Faraday's law

  • An emf is induced whenever the flux linkage changes: move a magnet, move the coil, change B, change the area, or turn the coil.
  • Steady flux, however large: no emf.
  • Size: emf = rate of change of flux linkage.
  • E = −Δ(Nϕ)/Δt, E = induced emf
  • On a flux linkage–time graph, size of the emf = gradient. On a graph of the flux through one turn, size of the emf = N × gradient.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • A coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², is ⟂ to a 0.50 T field. The field falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s.
  • Change in flux per turn = 0.50 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. Change in flux linkage = 200 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.040 Wb.
  • emf = 0.040 ÷ 0.10 = 0.40 V (while the field is falling).
  • Same change in 0.050 s: twice the emf. Once the field stops changing: zero.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the magnetic flux through each turn of a coil of 200 turns varies with time t. The coil is held at rest. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the coil between t = 0.50 s and t = 0.70 s?
  • (The graph labels the flux Φ; your data sheet writes it ϕ.)
Mission 4

Lenz's law

  • The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that produces it.
  • N pole pushed in: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole, repelling it.
  • N pole pulled out: the near end becomes an S pole, attracting it back.
  • The minus sign in Faraday's law expresses this.
Mission 4

Lenz's law is energy conservation

  • Suppose the induced current helped the change: the magnet would be pulled in faster, the current would grow, and so on. Energy from nothing.
  • So the induced current must oppose the motion. You must do work against that force.
  • That work becomes electrical energy, then thermal energy in the circuit.
  • Open circuit: an emf, but no current, so no opposing force.
  • A solid metal sheet or pipe acts like many loops: changing flux drives eddy currents around inside it, and they oppose the motion too.
Quick check
?The south pole of a bar magnet is pulled away from one end of a coil whose ends are joined. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A thick aluminium plate swings as a pendulum through the gap between the poles of a strong magnet, and stops after a few swings. An identical plate with many deep slots cut into it swings for much longer. Which explains the difference?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, so currents flow around the pipe. By Lenz's law their field opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it; its lost energy heats the copper.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Mission 5

Seeing induction: magnet and coil

  • Coil joined to a centre-zero galvanometer (or data logger). Push a magnet in: the needle flicks.
  • Magnet at rest, even deep inside: no reading.
  • Pull it out, or use the other pole: the needle flicks the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster motion, stronger magnet, more turns.
Mission 5

Linked coils

  • Two coils on one iron core. Coil 1 has a switch and a cell; coil 2 has a meter.
  • Switch on: the current in coil 1 grows, its flux grows, and coil 2's flux linkage changes: a flick.
  • Steady current: steady flux, zero emf. Switch off: a flick the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster change of current, more turns on coil 2, an iron core (more of coil 1's flux links coil 2).
  • An alternating current in coil 1 gives a continuous alternating emf in coil 2: the principle of the transformer.
Quick check
?According to Faraday's law of induction, the magnitude of the emf induced in a coil with a fixed number of turns is proportional to which quantity?
Mission 5

Demonstrating Lenz's law

  • Hanging aluminium ring. Push a magnet towards it: the ring moves away. Pull the magnet back: the ring follows.
  • Ring with a slit: no complete circuit, no current, no movement. The emf alone exerts no force.
  • Galvanometer and coil: find which way current makes an N pole at the coil's end (test with a cell). Push an N pole in: the coil's near end becomes N.
  • Each time, the induced current opposes the change. To keep the magnet moving you must do work: that is where the electrical energy comes from.
Quick check
?Some trains brake with strong magnets held just above the steel rail. The magnets move along the rail with the train and never touch it. Where does the train's kinetic energy go, and how does the braking force change as the train slows down?
Mission 5

A coil turning in a field

  • Rectangular coil, N turns, area A, in a uniform field B. θ = angle between B and the normal to the coil.
  • ϕ = BA cos θ per turn, so flux linkage Nϕ = NBA cos θ.
  • Coil face-on to the field (θ = 0): flux linkage largest, BAN. Edge-on (θ = 90°): zero.
  • Example: N = 50, A = 3.0 × 10⁻³ m², B = 0.20 T. Largest flux linkage = 0.030 Wb. At θ = 60°: 0.030 × cos 60° = 0.015 Wb.
Quick check
?A flat rectangular coil of 100 turns measures 4.0 cm by 5.0 cm. It starts with its plane perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of strength 0.40 T and is then rotated through 60° in 0.050 s, about an axis perpendicular to the field. What is the magnitude of the average emf induced during the rotation?
Mission 5

Search coil and oscilloscope

  • Search coil: a small flat coil with many turns and a known area.
  • Put it in an alternating field (e.g. inside a solenoid or between coils fed with a.c.). Its flux linkage alternates, so it gives an alternating emf.
  • The oscilloscope shows the peak emf, which is proportional to the peak flux linkage (fixed frequency).
  • Move the coil to map how B varies: along a solenoid's axis, or with distance from a wire. Keep the coil's face ⟂ to the field.
Quick check
?A search coil is connected to an oscilloscope and placed in a uniform alternating magnetic field. With the normal to the coil parallel to the field, the peak emf is 12 mV. The coil is turned so that its normal makes an angle of 60° with the field. What is the new peak emf?
Mission 5

Reading flux-linkage graphs

  • Size of emf = gradient of the flux linkage–time graph. The induced emf opposes the change, so the graph draws it as minus the gradient.
  • Example: flux linkage rises steadily from 0 to 0.060 Wb in 0.20 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.20 = 0.30 V.
  • Flux linkage then stays at 0.060 Wb: emf = 0.
  • Flux linkage falls to 0 in 0.10 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.10 = 0.60 V, in the opposite direction.
  • Steeper gradient → bigger emf. Flat → none, whatever the value.
Mission check

?Two coils share an iron core. The current in coil 1 rises steadily from 0 to 2.0 A in 0.40 s, stays at 2.0 A for a while, then falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s. How does the emf induced in coil 2 compare in the three intervals?

An emf appears only while flux linkage is changing. Switching on coil 1 changes coil 2's flux linkage for an instant: a flick. Once the current is steady, nothing changes, and the reading falls back to zero.

Mission 6

The a.c. generator

  • A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform field. Slip rings and brushes connect it to the circuit.
  • As it turns, the flux linkage keeps changing, so an emf is induced (Faraday). It reverses every half turn: alternating.
  • Coil plane perpendicular to the field: flux linkage greatest, momentarily not changing. emf = 0.
  • Coil plane parallel to the field: flux linkage zero but changing fastest. emf greatest.
  • Larger B, coil area or number of turns: larger peak emf.
  • Turn it twice as fast: flux linkage changes twice as fast. Peak emf doubles; period halves.
  • A metal rod moved across a field works the same way: as it moves, the flux it sweeps through changes, so an emf appears across it.
Quick check
?A coil turns at a steady rate in a uniform magnetic field, making one full turn in a time T. At t = 0 the plane of the coil is perpendicular to the field. At which of these times is the size of the induced emf greatest?
Quick check
?A coil rotating at 50 revolutions per second in a uniform magnetic field produces an alternating emf with a time period of 20 ms. The rotation rate is then changed to 1800 revolutions per minute. What is the new time period of the emf?
Mission check

?A simple a.c. generator's coil turns 5.0 times per second, and the emf has a peak value of 3.0 V. The coil is rewound with half as many turns and is then turned 15 times per second. Which describes the new emf?

The emf is the rate of change of flux linkage. Stronger field, bigger coil, more turns or faster turning all give more; for a moving rod, a stronger field, a longer rod or a faster speed.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A transformer changes the voltage of a supply with no moving parts. How does it do it?

Make a guess: A transformer changes the voltage of a supply with no moving parts. How does it do it?

Mission 7

How a transformer works

  • Two coils, primary and secondary, wound on one soft iron core. No wire joins them.
  • a.c. in the primary makes an alternating magnetic flux in the core.
  • The core carries that flux through the secondary.
  • Changing flux linkage in the secondary induces an alternating emf (Faraday's law).
  • Steady dc: steady flux, no output (except briefly at switch-on and switch-off).
Mission 7

The turns ratio

  • Same flux in both coils, so the same emf per turn.
  • Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.
  • Step-up: Ns > Np, so Vs > Vp. Step-down: Ns < Np, so Vs < Vp.
  • Ideal transformer (100 % efficient): IsVs = IpVp. Step the pd up and the current steps down.
  • For an ideal transformer: Ns/Np = Vs/Vp = Ip/Is.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • 230 V to 12 V. Primary: 2300 turns.
  • Ns = Np × Vs/Vp = 2300 × 12/230 = 120 turns.
  • Ideal; output current 2.0 A. Output power = 12 × 2.0 = 24 W.
  • Input current = 24/230 = 0.10 A.
Mission 7

Investigating a transformer

  • Two coils on a C-core, low-voltage a.c. supply on the primary.
  • Keep Np fixed; change Ns. Measure Vs with an a.c. voltmeter (or an oscilloscope).
  • Plot Vs against Ns: a straight line through the origin, gradient Vp/Np.
  • Add a load; measure Ip and Is too, to compare IsVs with IpVp.
Quick check
?A transformer has 400 turns on its primary coil and 1600 turns on its secondary coil. The primary is connected to a 12 V a.c. supply. What is the pd across the secondary?
Mission 7

Why real transformers waste energy

  • Coil resistance: I²R heating in the windings. Use thick copper wire.
  • Eddy currents: the changing flux induces currents in the iron core, which heat it. Make the core from thin laminations, insulated from each other.
Quick check
?A transformer's laminated core is replaced by a solid iron core of the same shape and size. What is the main effect?
Mission check

?A transformer has 2000 turns on its primary coil and 100 turns on its secondary coil. What is the secondary pd (i) when the primary is on a 230 V a.c. supply, and (ii) a few seconds after the primary is switched to a 230 V battery?

An alternating current makes a changing flux in the core, which induces an emf in the secondary. The turns ratio sets the size: Vs/Vp = Ns/Np.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets; field lines are closed loops, closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: circles, direction by the right-hand grip rule. Solenoid: uniform inside, bar-magnet pattern outside.

Mission 2

  • Force on a current in a field: at right angles to both; zero when parallel.
  • Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • F = BIL sin θ.
  • 1 T: 1 N on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field.

Mission 3

  • F = BQv for v ⟂ B.
  • The force is ⟂ to v, so speed and kinetic energy never change.
  • v ⟂ B gives a circle: r = mv/(Bq) = p/(Bq); the period does not depend on speed.
  • Crossed fields pass undeflected only particles with v = E/B.

Mission 4

  • ϕ = BA cos θ; unit Wb = T m².
  • Faraday: emf = rate of change of flux linkage; no change, no emf.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change; otherwise energy would be created.

Mission 5

  • Induced emf only while flux linkage changes: faster change, stronger field or more turns give more emf.
  • Lenz's law in practice: the induced current opposes the change, so work must be done; a cut ring has emf but no current and no force.
  • Turning coil: Nϕ = NBA cos θ, largest face-on, zero edge-on.

Mission 6

  • An a.c. generator: emf greatest when the coil plane is parallel to the field; faster turning gives a bigger, more frequent emf.

Mission 7

  • A transformer needs a changing flux: a.c. in, a.c. out. Vs/Vp = Ns/Np; ideal: IsVs = IpVp.
  • Losses: coil heating, and eddy currents (so the core is laminated).
