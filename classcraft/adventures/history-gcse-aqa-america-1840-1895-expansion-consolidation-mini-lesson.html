America, 1840–1895

This mini-lesson walks you through the AQA America, 1840–1895 wider world depth study: the pull of Manifest Destiny and migration west, the life of the Plains Indians, the Indian Wars, the Civil War and Reconstruction, and how the West was settled and consolidated by 1895.

Expansion · the big idea

Manifest Destiny

Manifest Destiny was the widely held belief that white Americans had a God-given right and duty to spread across the whole continent, from the Atlantic to the Pacific.

It drove settlers to migrate west from the 1840s and shaped US policy:

  • It was used to justify taking land, including land already lived on by Native Americans.
  • It fed a sense that the West was empty, waiting to be "civilised" and farmed.
  • It powered the migration along trails such as the Oregon Trail.

Why it matters: Manifest Destiny is the underlying cause that ties the whole period together — it explains migration, the clash with the Plains Indians, and the drive to settle the West.

Expansion · moving west

Trails, gold and the Mormons

From the 1840s thousands of Americans made the long, dangerous journey west. Different groups had different reasons:

  • Oregon Trail settlers — families seeking farmland travelled west by wagon train from the 1840s.
  • The California Gold Rush (1848–49) — the discovery of gold drew a flood of "Forty-Niners" hoping to strike it rich.
  • The Mormons — fleeing persecution, they migrated under Brigham Young to Salt Lake, Utah, from 1847.

The Donner Party (1846–47) shows how deadly the journey could be: trapped by snow in the mountains, many died and survivors resorted to cannibalism. The trails were hard, but the pull west was stronger.

Plains Indians · way of life

Life on the Great Plains

Native American peoples such as the Sioux (Lakota) and Cheyenne had lived on the Great Plains for generations. Their way of life was shaped by the land:

  • Nomadic: they followed the buffalo herds, living in portable tipis rather than fixed towns.
  • Dependence on the buffalo: the buffalo gave food, clothing, shelter (hides for tipis), tools and more — almost every part was used.
  • Beliefs: they saw themselves as part of nature, with a deep spiritual link to the land and animals; land was not something to be owned and sold.
  • Tribal structure: bands and tribes led by chiefs and councils, with warrior societies for defence and hunting.

Clash of cultures: Manifest Destiny and the settlers pushing west directly threatened this way of life. Settlers wanted to own and fence land the Plains Indians used freely.

Plains Indians · the buffalo

The buffalo — everything depended on it

For the Plains Indians the buffalo was the centre of life. Destroying the buffalo was, in effect, destroying their way of life.

Deliberate destruction: as railroads and settlers spread, the buffalo were slaughtered in huge numbers. This was partly deliberate — killing the buffalo forced the Plains Indians onto reservations.

Expansion · settling the land

The Homestead Act and the railroad

In 1862 the US government passed two laws that opened up the West to settlers:

  • Homestead Act (1862) — offered settlers 160 acres of free public land if they farmed it for five years, encouraging families to move onto the Plains.
  • Pacific Railroad Act (1862) — supported the building of a railway across the continent.

The First Transcontinental Railroad was completed in 1869 at Promontory Summit, Utah, linking East and West. It brought settlers, moved goods and cattle, and sped up the destruction of the buffalo.

Impact on the Plains Indians: the railroad and homesteaders cut through their hunting grounds, fenced the open land, and made their nomadic way of life almost impossible.

Conflict · the Indian Wars

The Indian Wars

As settlers pushed onto their land, the Plains Indians fought back. Decades of conflict followed, broken by treaties that were often broken again:

  • Fort Laramie Treaties (1851 and 1868) — attempts to set boundaries for tribal lands; later broken as settlers pushed in.
  • Little Crow's War (1862) and the Sand Creek Massacre (1864) — early, brutal clashes.
  • Red Cloud's War (1866–68) — a rare Native American success, closing the Bozeman Trail.

Pattern to remember: settlers wanted land → treaties were made → treaties were broken (often when gold or railways were involved) → war followed. The US Army usually won in the end.

Conflict · 1876

The Battle of the Little Bighorn

The most famous battle of the Indian Wars was the Battle of the Little Bighorn on 25 June 1876 — sometimes called "Custer's Last Stand".

  • A large force of Sioux and Cheyenne, led by Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse, destroyed Custer's 7th Cavalry. Custer and his men were killed.
  • It was a stunning Native American victory — but it shocked and angered the US public.
  • The army then hit back hard, and Native American resistance was steadily crushed.

Misconception alert: the Little Bighorn was a Native American victory, not a defeat — but it did not save them. It made the US government even more determined, and resistance collapsed within a few years.

Conflict · the end of resistance

Reservations, the Dawes Act and Wounded Knee

By the 1880s the Plains Indians had been forced onto reservations — often poor land, far smaller than their old hunting grounds — and their culture was under attack:

  • Dawes Act (1887) — broke up tribal reservations into individual family plots, aiming to force Native Americans to farm and abandon their tribal way of life.
  • Wounded Knee Massacre (1890) — US soldiers killed around 250 Sioux, mostly unarmed men, women and children. It marked the end of Native American armed resistance.

End point: Wounded Knee (1890) is usually seen as the moment the "Indian Wars" ended and the frontier was "closed" — the West had been consolidated under US control.

The Civil War · causes

Why the Civil War broke out

Alongside westward expansion, the USA was splitting apart over deep differences between North and South:

The key causes were slavery, states' rights, and the economic differences between the industrial North and the slave-farming South. When anti-slavery candidate Abraham Lincoln was elected president in 1860, Southern states began to secede.

The Civil War · 1861–1865

The war and its turning points

The American Civil War (1861–1865) was fought between the Union (North) and the Confederacy (South):

  • Southern states seceded and formed the Confederacy in 1861; the war began at Fort Sumter in April 1861.
  • The Emancipation Proclamation (1863) declared enslaved people in the rebel states free, making the war clearly about ending slavery.
  • The Battle of Gettysburg (1863) was the war's great turning point, a major Union victory.
  • Confederate general Robert E. Lee surrendered at Appomattox in April 1865, ending the war. Days later Lincoln was assassinated.

The Union won. Slavery was on its way to abolition, and the question now was how to rebuild the defeated South — that is Reconstruction.

Reconstruction · 1865–1877

Rebuilding the South

Reconstruction (1865–1877) was the effort to rebuild the defeated South and decide the rights of the freed slaves. Three amendments to the Constitution aimed to secure those rights:

  • 13th Amendment (1865) — abolished slavery.
  • 14th Amendment (1868) — gave citizenship to former slaves.
  • 15th Amendment (1870) — gave Black men the right to vote.

The Freedmen's Bureau helped former slaves with food, schools and legal help.

Key link: Reconstruction is what the Civil War made necessary — winning the war ended slavery, but the fight over the rights of freed African Americans had only just begun.

Reconstruction · the backlash

The KKK and the end of Reconstruction

Many white Southerners bitterly resisted the new rights of African Americans:

  • The Ku Klux Klan (KKK), formed in 1865/1866, used terror and violence to intimidate Black voters and their white supporters.
  • When Reconstruction ended in 1877 and federal troops left the South, states brought in "Jim Crow" laws enforcing racial segregation.

Misconception alert: the amendments gave rights on paper, but in practice Reconstruction's gains were rolled back. Segregation and discrimination against African Americans continued in the South for decades after 1877.

The West · cattle & cowboys

Cowboys, cattle drives and cow towns

After the Civil War the cattle industry boomed as beef from Texas was driven north to the railroads:

  • Cattle drives moved huge herds along routes like the Chisholm Trail to "cow towns" such as Abilene and Dodge City, where they met the railway.
  • Joseph McCoy helped create the cow-town system by building stockyards at Abilene.
  • Cattle grazed freely on the open range — until barbed wire and homesteaders fenced the land.

Law and order: booming cow towns were often lawless. Sheriffs and marshals were appointed to keep order, though it was a slow, uneven process.

The West · conflict on the land

Homesteaders vs ranchers

As more people settled, rival groups clashed over the land itself:

  • Homesteaders wanted to fence off farms; ranchers wanted the open range for their cattle.
  • These tensions led to range wars, such as the Johnson County War (1892) in Wyoming, where big ranchers clashed with small settlers.

Consolidation: by the 1890s barbed wire, railways, farms and law had tamed the "Wild West". The open frontier was closing and the West was being consolidated as part of the modern USA.

Exam skill

Sources and interpretations

In the exam you handle sources and interpretations about the West. Two quick habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A settler's diary and a Native American account of the same event will disagree — because each had very different experiences and points of view.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know (dates, events, motives).

Interpretations differ: some see westward expansion as heroic pioneering; others see it as the destruction of Native American peoples. Being aware that interpretations vary — and why — is exactly what the exam rewards.

Consolidation

How the West was consolidated

By 1895 the USA had been transformed — expanded across the continent and consolidated into a single, modern nation:

By 1895 the frontier was gone, Native American resistance had ended at Wounded Knee (1890), and the USA was one nation stretching from coast to coast — at a heavy cost to Native Americans.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Expansion: Manifest Destiny; Oregon Trail (1840s); Mormons to Utah (1847); California Gold Rush (1848–49).

Plains Indians: nomadic, buffalo-dependent, deeply spiritual; clashed with settlers over land.

Settling the land: Homestead Act & Pacific Railroad Act (1862); Transcontinental Railroad completed (1869).

Indian Wars: Sand Creek (1864); Little Bighorn (1876); Dawes Act (1887); Wounded Knee (1890) ended resistance.

Civil War: 1861–1865; caused by slavery, states' rights, economics; Gettysburg (1863); Lee surrenders (1865).

Reconstruction (1865–77): 13th (1865), 14th (1868), 15th (1870) Amendments; KKK; Jim Crow segregation follows.
