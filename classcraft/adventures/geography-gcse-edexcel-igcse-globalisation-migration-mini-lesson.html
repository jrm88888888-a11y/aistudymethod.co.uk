Mission 1

Mission 1: In 2024 one inflow of money into Tajikistan equalled almost half of its GDP. Which flows matter most, and for which places?

Make a guess: In 2024 one inflow of money into Tajikistan equalled almost half of its GDP. Which flows matter most, and for which places?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.1a rise of the global economy (growth of production and commodity chains) and the factors encouraging it: trade, foreign investment, aid, labour, modern transport and information technologies
Mission 1

The rise of the global economy

  • World goods exports: $0.85 trillion (1975) → $24.62 trillion (2024), in current dollars.
  • Trade (exports plus imports) rose from 31.9% to 56.7% of world GDP (1975–2024).
  • So trade grew faster than the world economy: production became global.
  • In 2024, manufactured goods made up 68.8% of world goods exports.
Mission 1

Commodity chains

  • A commodity chain links every stage of a product, from raw material to the shopper:
    • produce (farm, mine) → process (factory) → transport → sell (shop) → consume.
  • Stages are often in different countries.
  • Example: in 2025, 73.6% of China's soybean imports came from Brazil; in China the beans are crushed for soybean meal for livestock feed.
Quick check
?In a chocolate commodity chain, cocoa is grown in Côte d'Ivoire, ground in the Netherlands and made into bars in the UK. What does this show?
Mission 1

Trade and foreign investment

  • Trade: lower barriers and global rules let goods and services cross borders more cheaply.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business in another country, e.g. a TNC's factory.
  • Global FDI reached $1.6 trillion in 2025.
  • Developing economies received $901 billion of it.
Mission 1

Aid and labour

  • Aid: grants and cheap loans to poorer countries, e.g. for roads and ports that link them to trade.
  • Labour: people move to work abroad, and send money home: remittances.
  • To low- and middle-income countries in 2023:
    • remittances $608 billion
    • FDI $408 billion
    • aid $252 billion.
Quick check
?In 2023, low- and middle-income countries received $608 billion in remittances and $252 billion in aid. How many times as large were remittances?
Mission 1

Modern transport

  • Containers: standard steel boxes moved between ship, train and lorry. Port traffic: 547 million TEU (2010) → 913 million TEU (2024).
  • Air: passengers rose from 310 million (1970) to 4,456 million (2019).
  • Cheaper, faster transport lets each stage of a commodity chain sit wherever it is cheapest.
Mission 1

Information technologies

  • Internet users: 15.6% of the world's people (2005) → 73.6% (2025).
  • ICT services (software, data, telecoms) grew from 5.3% to 14.8% of world service exports (2000–2024).
  • ICT lets firms manage factories and suppliers worldwide in real time, and lets migrants send money home quickly.
Quick check
?A clothing firm in London orders shirts from a factory in Bangladesh by email, tracks the shipment online and pays the same day. Which factor mainly makes this possible?
Mission 1

Which flow matters most?

  • Globally, goods trade ($24.62 trillion in 2024) is far bigger than remittances, FDI or aid.
  • Nationally, it depends on the place. Remittances as a share of GDP, 2024: Tajikistan 47.2%, Tonga 39.4%, Nepal 26.0%.
  • India received the most remittances in total: $137.7 billion in 2024.
  • Sending money is costly: on average 6.36% of the amount sent (2025).
Mission check

?Remittances to Tonga equalled 39.4% of its GDP in 2024. Which conclusion is best supported?

The flow equal to almost half of Tajikistan's GDP was remittances (47.2% in 2024). Globally, trade is the biggest flow; for some countries, money from migrant workers matters most.

Mission 2

Mission 2: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Make a guess: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.1b roles of the WTO, IMF and TNCs in creating a more globalised economy
Mission 2

Debt relief

  • Many poor countries owed more than they could repay. Repayments took money from schools and hospitals.
  • The IMF and World Bank started the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996; in 2005 another scheme added 100% relief on some debts.
  • To qualify, countries need a track record of reform and a poverty reduction strategy carried out for at least a year.
  • Rwanda is one of the countries that completed HIPC; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • + frees money for services. − strict conditions; countries can fall into debt again.
Mission 2

Rescue loans: Sri Lanka

  • In 2022 Sri Lanka stopped paying its foreign debts: "the external default in 2022" (IMF).
  • Its economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022. Inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
  • March 2023: the IMF approved a four-year loan of about US$3 billion.
  • Conditions (conditionality) included higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy really costs).
  • Results: inflation fell "to around 4 percent in 2023", and the economy grew 5.0% in 2024.
Mission 2

Who sets the terms?

  • The government negotiates conditions with IMF staff; the IMF's Executive Board approves the loan.
  • Votes follow each country's quota, roughly its economic weight.
  • Big decisions, such as changing quotas, need 85% of the votes. The USA holds 16.49%, enough to block them.
  • Creditors also had to accept less: governments, Chinese state banks and private bondholders.
  • Alternatives: grants from donors, or loans from newer banks such as the BRICS countries' New Development Bank, which funds projects rather than rescues.
Mission 2

The WTO, IMF and TNCs in a global economy

  • WTO: "the only international organization dealing with the rules of trade between nations"; a forum for trade talks and for settling trade disputes; 166 members, 98% of world trade.
  • IMF: 191 members; lends to countries that cannot pay for imports or debts, with conditions, so trade and payments keep flowing, as with Sri Lanka in 2023.
  • TNCs: firms that produce and sell in many countries. Foreign direct investment, mostly by TNCs, reached $1.6 trillion in 2025.
  • Together they lower barriers, keep money flowing and spread production worldwide: a more globalised economy.
Quick check
?Two countries disagree over whether a tariff on imported steel breaks trade rules. Which organisation handles the dispute?
Quick check
?What does conditionality mean for an IMF loan?
Mission check

?A low-income country asks to have its debts to the IMF and World Bank cancelled. Who sets the conditions it must meet?

Sri Lanka's government agreed conditions with the IMF; the IMF's Executive Board, where votes follow quotas, approved them, and creditors had to accept less. Alternatives include grants, debt relief and newer lenders such as the New Development Bank.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Make a guess: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.1b roles of the WTO, IMF and TNCs in a more globalised economy (TNC part)
  • 8.2a costs and benefits for TNC host countries
Mission 3

Transnational corporations

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) is a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Its headquarters is in its home country; a country where it sets up is a host country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business abroad and runs it.
  • Outsourcing: a company pays another firm to do part of its work, such as making its products.
Mission 3

How TNCs organise themselves

  • Headquarters, design and research usually stay in the home country: Apple is based in Cupertino, California.
  • Making the product can happen anywhere. Apple's manufacturing is mostly done by outsourcing partners in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
  • By spreading production, trade and investment across borders, TNCs help create a more globalised economy.
Mission 3

Why locate in many countries?

  • Lower costs: Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs".
  • Near customers: in the year to March 2026 Toyota made 73.9% of the vehicles it sold outside Japan in factories outside Japan. Cars are big and heavy to ship.
  • Local supplies and money: buying parts locally matches local costs to local sales.
  • Avoiding trade barriers: Toyota notes a "significant increase" in US tariffs, including on cars, announced in 2025; making cars inside a market avoids them.
  • Government incentives such as tax breaks also attract TNCs.
Quick check
?Which is an example of FDI rather than outsourcing?
Mission 3

Apple in India: outsourcing

  • India is one of the main countries where Apple's outsourcing partners make its products.
  • The partner firms own and run the factories and employ the workers; Apple designs the products and sells them worldwide.
  • Apple says outsourcing lowers costs but "reduce[s] the Company's direct control over production".
  • Because partners work in six countries, production can shift between them.
Mission 3

Toyota in India: FDI

  • Toyota Kirloskar Motor has made cars in India since December 1999.
  • Models include the Innova, Fortuner and Urban Cruiser Hyryder, some as hybrids.
  • A Toyota group company, Toyota Kirloskar Auto Parts, has made axles and transmissions in India since 2002.
  • Toyota invests in its own factories and in nearby suppliers: its Just in Time system needs parts to arrive as they are needed.
Mission 3

FDI into India

  • Foreign direct investment into India (World Bank):
    • 2000: $3.6 billion · 2020: $64.4 billion (peak) · 2024: $27.1 billion · 2025: $40.8 billion
  • FDI grew hugely after 2000, but it rises and falls from year to year.
  • Worldwide, FDI reached $1.6 trillion in 2025.
Mission 3

Advantages for the host country

  • Jobs and wages in factories, and in partner and supplier firms.
  • Skills and technology: workers learn new methods (for example Toyota's production system).
  • Multiplier effect: suppliers (such as parts makers) and local services grow too.
  • Exports and taxes: goods made in India are sold abroad, and firms pay tax.
Mission 3

Disadvantages for the host country

  • Profits flow back to the home country.
  • Top jobs (design, research, management) often stay at headquarters.
  • Footloose: contracts and investment can move. FDI into India fell by about 58% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Competition: local firms may struggle against large TNCs.
  • Working conditions and the environment depend on how well laws are enforced.
Quick check
?Why might outsourcing jobs be less secure for a host country than FDI factories?
Mission check

?Company P makes heavy washing machines. Company Q designs small wireless earbuds. Which strategies are they most likely to use?

Apple keeps design and services and outsources assembly to partners in Asia, including India: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, with its own factories in India since 1999, building cars near customers. Both bring jobs and skills to host countries, but profits and top jobs mostly stay at home, and contracts and investment can move.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Some move abroad for a better job; others flee for their lives. What pushes people, and what pulls them?

Make a guess: Some move abroad for a better job; others flee for their lives. What pushes people, and what pulls them?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.1c social, economic and political push/pull factors for migration types (medical, sport, tourism, economic) over 50 years
  • 8.2b impacts of voluntary, forced, national, international and rural–urban migration on different groups
Mission 4

Moving across borders

  • International migration: moving away from where you usually live "and across an international border".
  • Emigration: leaving a country (seen from the country left behind).
  • Immigration: arriving in a new country to live (seen from the country of arrival).
Mission 4

Who is who?

  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or higher pay. This is voluntary: the person chooses to go.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for their race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion. This is forced migration.
  • Asylum seeker: has asked another country for protection but the claim is not yet decided; "every recognized refugee is initially an asylum seeker".
  • About 9 million people were asylum seekers awaiting a decision at the end of 2025.
Quick check
?Amira leaves her country because she is threatened with prison for her political views. She is granted protection in another country. What is she?
Mission 4

Push and pull

  • Push factors drive people away: few jobs, low wages, poor schools or healthcare, conflict, persecution, natural disasters.
  • Pull factors draw people towards a place: jobs, higher wages, safety, family already there, education, healthcare.
  • Sort them into economic (jobs, wages), social (family, schools, health care) and political (war, persecution, government rules).
Mission 4

Example: Indians working abroad

  • In 2024 the UK was home to about 1.04 million people born in India.
  • Indian migrants abroad send money home (remittances): about $0.43 billion in 1975, about $150.7 billion in 2025. That is roughly 350 times as much in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation (our calculation).
  • Pull: jobs and higher pay abroad. Push: fewer well-paid jobs at home. Family networks make the move easier.
Quick check
?Which of these is a pull factor?
Mission 4

Impacts: migrants, origin, destination

  • Migrants: higher income and new skills, but may face separation from family, poor housing or exploitation.
  • Origin country: gains remittances (India: about $150.7 billion in 2025); loses young, skilled workers (brain drain).
  • Destination: fills labour shortages. About 190,000 of 1.35 million NHS staff in England reported a non-British nationality (March 2021). Population growth also adds demand for housing and services.
Mission 4

Types of migration over 50 years

  • Economic: money sent home to India rose about 350-fold between 1975 and 2025, in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation: a sign of how important work abroad has become.
  • Medical: health workers move to fill gaps; Indian, Filipino and Nigerian are the most common non-EU nationalities in the NHS. Some patients also travel abroad for treatment.
  • Sport: top players and coaches move for higher pay and bigger leagues.
  • Tourism: short-term moves, made easier by cheaper flights and rising incomes.
  • Political rules shape all of these, e.g. the UK's 2021 points-based system.
Mission 4

Different migrations, different impacts

  • Voluntary (chosen, e.g. for work): migrants usually gain income; families at home gain remittances.
  • Forced (fleeing war or persecution): people often lose homes, jobs and savings; neighbouring host countries face sudden pressure.
  • National (inside a country, e.g. rural–urban): cities gain workers; villages lose young people.
  • International: adds remittances and skills, but also brain drain and border costs.
Quick check
?Many young doctors leave a country to work abroad. Which is the most likely negative impact on the origin country?
Mission check

?Maria, a nurse, leaves the Philippines for a hospital job in the UK at higher pay. She sends money home every month. Which statement is correct?

Economic migrants move by choice, pulled by jobs and pay; refugees are forced out by persecution. Both change their origin and destination countries.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Make a guess: Within a year of August 2017, nearly 725,000 people had fled from Myanmar into Bangladesh. Why do so many people flee at once, and what happens where they arrive?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.2b impacts of voluntary, forced, national, international and rural-urban migration on different groups
Mission 5

Who is who

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but has "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
Quick check
?A family flees fighting and crosses into a neighbouring country, where they ask for protection. Their claim has not yet been decided. What are they?
Mission 5

The Rohingya: long-term causes

  • Origin: northern Rakhine State, Myanmar. Destination: Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh.
  • A UN fact-finding mission found: "Most Rohingya have become de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • Their travel "has long been restricted", affecting rights "to food, health and education".
  • These conditions left them vulnerable before 2017.
Mission 5

The trigger

  • 25 August 2017: an armed group (ARSA) attacked a military base and "up to 30 security force outposts".
  • Security forces responded with what the authorities called "clearance operations".
  • The UN mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
  • Push: persecution and violence. Pull: safety across the nearest border.
Mission 5

Challenges for the refugees

  • 31 October 2026: 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh.
  • Most live in "33 highly congested camps in Cox's Bazar District".
  • They depend on aid: the 2025 response plan required US$455.6 million.
  • Safe from violence, but crowded camps, dependence on aid and no clear way home.
  • Impact on the migrants: safety, but dependence and an uncertain future.
Mission 5

Challenges for Bangladesh, the destination

  • Scale and time: about 1.2 million refugees at the end of 2025; the UN found returns "not possible" in 2018.
  • Cost and land: aid must be raised every year, and camps are so crowded that "some 35,000 refugees relocated to Bhasan Char", an island.
  • Host communities: the 2025–26 response plan covers the needs of "Rohingya refugees and host communities".
  • Host area (Cox's Bazar), UNDP 2018:
    • about 4,818 acres of forest reserve destroyed (Forest Department estimate)
    • wages for unskilled work in Teknaf and Ukhiya depressed by about 20%, because Rohingya work for less
    • so poverty rose by 2.73 (Teknaf) and 2.63 (Ukhiya) percentage points.
  • Law: Bangladesh is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention (146 states are).
Quick check
?Which is a challenge for Bangladesh, the destination country, rather than for the refugees themselves?
Mission 5

Different migrations, different impacts

  • Voluntary, international: Indians abroad sent home US$150.7 billion in 2025. Families gain income; the origin loses young workers.
  • Voluntary, rural–urban: migrants gain jobs in cities; cities face crowded housing and services.
  • Forced, national: 9.1 million IDPs inside Sudan, end of 2025; they lose homes and work but stay in their own country.
  • Forced, international: Rohingya refugees gain safety; Bangladesh carries a long-term cost.
Quick check
?Which impact fits voluntary international migration best?
Mission check

?By the end of 2025, 2.8 million people had fled Sudan to other countries, and 9.1 million were displaced inside Sudan. Which statement is correct?

The Rohingya fled persecution and violence (a political push) to the nearest safety. Bangladesh now hosts 1,208,018 refugees (31 Oct 2026), mostly in crowded camps funded by aid: a long-term challenge for refugees and host alike.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Make a guess: Tourism makes up about a fifth of the Maldives' economy. Yet in 2022 most people working in its resorts were not Maldivian. Who really gains from a resort?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.2c positive and negative impacts of global tourism, including mass tourism, on the environment, economy and people of destination areas
Mission 6

A growing global industry

  • About 1.52 billion international tourist arrivals were recorded in 2025, almost 60 million more than in 2024.
  • The industry body WTTC estimates travel and tourism was 9.8% of the world economy in 2025 and supported 366 million jobs, about 1 in 9 jobs.
  • WTTC speaks for tourism businesses and counts indirect effects, so its totals are at the high end.
Mission 6

Benefits of tourism

  • Economy: jobs, foreign currency, taxes for the government, and new airports and roads.
  • Multiplier effect: wages and spending circulate locally, so each tourist's money supports more local jobs.
  • People and environment: money for services and conservation. In Rwanda, from 2017, 10% of national park tourism revenue was to be shared with communities next to the parks.
Mission 6

Problems of tourism

  • Leakage: money that leaves the destination: profits to foreign owners, imported food and fuel, commission to foreign booking sites.
  • Jobs: often seasonal, low-paid, and taken by migrant workers.
  • Environment: crowds damage fragile places. Thailand closed Maya Bay in 2018 so its coral could recover from damage caused by thousands of daily visitors.
  • People: crowding, rising prices, and culture turned into a product to sell.
Quick check
?Which is an example of leakage?
Mission 6

Case study: the Maldives

  • An upper-middle-income country of small islands in the Indian Ocean.
  • 2,246,516 tourist arrivals in 2025; tourism was 20.3% of GDP. The World Bank calls it "the largest economic sector".
  • 179 resorts, 16 hotels, 973 guest houses and 168 safari boats in 2025.
Mission 6

Enclave tourism

  • Enclave tourism: tourists stay inside a self-contained resort and spend almost all their money there.
  • Example: a resort on its own island, where guests sleep, eat, shop and take trips without leaving the resort.
  • At the end of 2022, resorts employed 55,874 people: 59% foreign workers and 41% Maldivian. Women were 11% of resort workers; Maldivian women 2%.
  • Consequences: high leakage, few links to local businesses, and few jobs for local people, especially women.
Mission 6

Over-dependence on tourism

  • When one industry dominates, a shock to tourism hits the whole economy.
  • Tourist arrivals in the Maldives: 1,702,887 in 2019; 555,494 in 2020.
  • The World Bank warns of "potential external shocks to tourism".
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, by what percentage did tourist arrivals in the Maldives fall from 2019 to 2020?
Quick check
?Which judgement about tourism in the Maldives is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?A luxury resort on a private island in a lower-income country is owned by a foreign hotel chain. It imports most of its food, recruits most staff from abroad, and its guests rarely leave the resort. Who gains most from each dollar a guest spends?

Several groups gain, unequally. In the Maldives, tourism is about a fifth of GDP, but 59% of resort jobs in 2022 went to foreign workers and only 2% to Maldivian women. Enclave tourism and leakage limit local gains; over-dependence adds risk.

Make a guess: From 1 January 2021, EU citizens needed points to get a UK work visa. By the year to March 2023, UK net migration had peaked at 944,000. How far can governments control global interactions?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.3a how geopolitical relationships between countries help manage trade, migration and tourism

Mission 7

Geopolitical relationships

  • Geopolitical relationships: the agreements and ties between governments.
  • They set the rules for three big flows: goods (trade), people who move to live (migration) and visitors (tourism).
  • Trade blocs vary in depth: a free trade area removes tariffs between members; a single market also allows free movement of services, capital and people, as in the EU.
Mission 7

Managing trade

  • The EU–UK Trade and Cooperation Agreement was signed on 30 December 2020 and applied from 1 January 2021.
  • It gives zero tariffs and zero quotas on goods that meet its rules of origin.
  • In Africa, trading under the African Continental Free Trade Area began on 1 January 2021, aiming "to eliminate trade barriers and boost intra-Africa trade".
Mission 7

Managing migration: the Common Travel Area

  • The Common Travel Area (CTA) links the UK, Ireland, the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man.
  • British and Irish citizens can move freely, live, work and study in either country.
  • It "pre-dates both British and Irish membership of the EU and is not dependent on it", so it continued after Brexit.
Mission 7

Managing tourism: visas and ETAs

  • Governments decide who may visit, and on what terms.
  • Most visitors to the UK need an electronic travel authorisation (ETA) or a visa.
  • An ETA allows a stay of up to 6 months for tourism or visiting family, and costs £20.
  • Visitors from Europe, the USA, Australia and Canada usually need an ETA rather than a visa: easier rules for countries with close ties.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a geopolitical relationship managing migration?
Mission 7

The UK's points-based system

  • On 1 January 2021 free movement with the EU ended and a points-based system began.
  • "EU and non-EU citizens will be treated equally".
  • Workers needed 70 points: for a job at the right skill level, English, and a salary threshold (generally £25,600).
  • There was no general route for low-skilled work. The stated aim was to "reduce overall migration numbers".
Mission 7

What happened next

  • UK net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023.
  • In December 2023 the government said it would raise the salary threshold for overseas workers from £26,200 to £38,700 and stop overseas care workers bringing dependants.
  • Net migration fell to 171,000 in the year to December 2025 (provisional).
  • In that year, of non-EU+ immigrants, 47% came to study, 23% to work and 14% to seek asylum.
Mission 7

Three approaches in one country

  • Open: free movement with the EU (until the end of 2020); the CTA with Ireland (still in place).
  • Selective: points for skills, English and salary, the same for EU and non-EU workers (from 2021).
  • Restrictive: higher salary thresholds and limits on dependants (from 2024).
  • Exam move: name the country, date each approach and compare its aim with what the data show.
Quick check
?Net migration fell from 944,000 (year to March 2023) to 171,000 (year to December 2025, provisional). By about what percentage did it fall?
Mission 7

How much control?

  • The 2021 rules changed who could come: EU citizens lost free movement, while non-EU workers could apply on equal terms.
  • Numbers rose at first, partly because of demand from employers and students, and asylum flows.
  • They fell after the 2024 tightening, though the 2025 figures are provisional.
Mission check

?Country Z ends free movement with its neighbours and brings in a points system open to workers from every country. Two years later, immigration has risen. What best explains this?

Partly. In the UK, ending free movement changed who could come, but net migration still peaked at 944,000 (year to March 2023); after tighter rules it fell to 171,000 (2025, provisional). Geopolitical agreements such as the TCA, the CTA and ETAs shape trade, migration and tourism; they rarely switch flows off.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Make a guess: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.3c making tourism more sustainable: individuals, organisations and governments in a named emerging or developing country (and a developed one)
Mission 8

Rwanda: a low-income country

  • The World Bank classes Rwanda as a low-income country. Income per person (GNI per capita) was US$1,150 in 2025.
  • Its mountain gorillas live in Volcanoes National Park, part of the Virunga Massif, shared with DR Congo and Uganda.
  • In Edexcel's terms Rwanda is a developing country.
Mission 8

Sustainable tourism and ecotourism

  • Sustainable tourism takes full account of its economic, social and environmental impacts, now and in the future, for visitors, businesses, the environment and host communities.
  • Ecotourism: "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education".
  • One of its principles: provide direct money for conservation.
  • Not every nature holiday is ecotourism.
Quick check
?Which trip best fits the definition of ecotourism?
Mission 8

High value, low volume

  • On 6 May 2017 the Rwanda Development Board (RDB) raised the gorilla permit from US$750 to US$1,500.
  • RDB called this "high-value, low-volume": fewer visitors, each paying more.
  • Visits are limited: at most 8 visitors per group, and one hour with the gorillas.
  • Visitors who also spent 3 days in Rwanda's other national parks got a 30% discount.
Mission 8

Sharing the money

  • Since 2005, a revenue-sharing scheme has given part of park income to communities living next to the parks.
  • In 2017 the communities' share rose from 5% to 10% of park tourism revenue.
  • Since 2005 it has funded nearly 1,300 projects worth more than 23 billion Rwandan francs: schools, health centres and water supplies.
Quick check
?Communities receive 10% of park tourism revenue. How much of one US$1,500 permit is that?
Mission 8

Protecting the environment

  • Mountain gorillas in the Virunga Massif rose from as few as 242 in 1981 to 480 in 2010 and 604 in 2016.
  • The world total was about 1,063 in the 2019 census results.
  • Tourism pays for protection, and gives local people jobs as rangers, wardens and porters, so they gain from the forest standing.
  • Careful: these counts come before the 2017 price rise. They show recovery under long-term protection, not the effect of the new price.
Mission 8

Problems and trade-offs

  • Access: a foreign visitor's permit (US$1,500) costs more than Rwanda's average income per person (US$1,150). Rwandan and East African citizens pay a promotional US$200.
  • Wildlife: diseases caught from people remain a threat to gorillas.
  • Land: the park is being expanded; the first phase affects 547 households, who are being moved to a new village.
  • Dependence: tourism fell from about 28% of export earnings in 2019 to about 11% in 2020. A sector that depends on travel is easily hit by global shocks.
Mission 8

Who makes tourism sustainable?

  • Government: RDB sets prices, limits group sizes and shares revenue with communities.
  • Organisations: groups such as the African Wildlife Foundation support the park and its neighbours; The International Ecotourism Society sets out ecotourism principles.
  • Individuals (responsible tourists):
    • keep to group sizes, time limits and the rangers' rules;
    • do not trek when ill: human diseases can infect gorillas;
    • hire local guides and porters, and buy local food and crafts;
    • respect local customs; take litter home.
Quick check
?Which action is most typical of a responsible tourist on a gorilla trek?
Mission check

?A national park in another low-income country plans to double its entry fee. Which plan is most likely to make its tourism more sustainable?

Rwanda chose high value, low volume: a US$1,500 permit, small groups and 10% of park revenue shared with neighbouring communities. Gorillas were already recovering under protection. The trade-offs: access, disease risk, relocation and dependence on travel.

Mission 9: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Make a guess: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel International GCSE Geography (4GE1)

  • 8.3c approaches to make tourism more sustainable from individuals, organisations and governments in a named developed and a named emerging or developing country
Mission 9

Mass tourism and carrying capacity

  • Mass tourism: very large numbers of tourists visiting the same places, often on package holidays.
  • Carrying capacity: the most visitors a place can take before unacceptable damage occurs.
  • Damage can be environmental (coral, wildlife, litter), cultural (residents pushed out, traditions sold as shows) or felt as overcrowding.
Mission 9

Impacts of mass tourism

  • Economic +: jobs and income. The industry body WTTC estimates tourism supported 366 million jobs worldwide in 2025. Economic −: low-paid seasonal work; profits leak abroad; rising prices for residents.
  • Cultural +: money to restore heritage; interest in local crafts. Cultural −: in Venice, UNESCO reports homes replaced by tourist accommodation.
  • Environmental +: entry fees can fund protected areas. Environmental −: Maya Bay's coral was damaged by thousands of daily visitors.
Quick check
?In a historic city, flats are turned into holiday lets and long-term residents move away. What kind of impact is this?
Mission 9

Maya Bay, Thailand: a tropical coast

  • A bay on the uninhabited island of Phi Phi Leh, in a national park in Krabi province.
  • It became world-famous after the 1999 film The Beach. At its peak in 2017, almost 5,000 people a day visited.
  • It closed in June 2018, "when it was determined the area's coral and other marine life needed time to recover" from the damage caused by thousands of daily tourists.
  • Thailand is upper-middle income. Use it as your named emerging country.
Mission 9

Managing Maya Bay

  • Closure: announced for four months from 1 June 2018, then extended; a plan to reopen on 1 January 2022 came after about three and a half years.
  • A new jetty on the far side of the island: boats must land there, not in the bay. Experts advised this was the only way for the coral to keep recovering.
  • Controlled daily numbers, a marked swimming area, and coral and beach trees replanted.
  • Dispersal: Thailand's tourism authority promoted Krabi's other islands while the bay was shut.
Mission 9

Venice, Italy: an urban lagoon

  • UNESCO: "The exceptionally high tourism pressure on the city of Venice has resulted in a partial functional transformation".
  • About 53,000 residents lived in the historic centre in 2018; on average 66,800 visitors and tourists were there each day (year not stated).
  • That is about 1.26 visitors per resident every day. Day-trippers alone: almost 22 million a year.
  • Italy is high income. Use it as your named developed country.
Mission 9

Managing Venice

  • Cruise ships: UNESCO's World Heritage Committee urged Italy in 2014 to ban large ships from the lagoon. In 2021 the Italian government banned large cruise ships from the Venice Lagoon.
  • Access fee: on set dates in 2026, day visitors to the historic city had to pay a fee unless exempt. The 2026 trial ended; from 27 July the fee no longer applied.
  • Both aim to cut pressure on the lagoon and the city without stopping tourism.
Quick check
?Which measure most directly protected Maya Bay's coral after it reopened?
Mission 9

Who can make tourism more sustainable?

  • Sustainable tourism takes "full account of its current and future economic, social and environmental impacts", meeting the needs of visitors, industry, environment and host communities (UN Tourism).
  • Individuals: follow site rules, keep off coral, visit out of season, use locally owned businesses.
  • Organisations: Thailand's national parks department and tourism authority (Maya Bay); UNESCO pressing Italy over cruise ships (Venice).
  • Governments: Italy's ban on large cruise ships (2021) and Venice's access fee (city council); Thailand closing the bay and limiting daily numbers.
Quick check
?Which of these is an action by individuals to make tourism more sustainable?
Mission check

?A national park caps a famous mountain trail at a fixed number of walkers a day, even though many more want to come and would pay. Why would it turn paying visitors away?

Maya Bay passed its carrying capacity: almost 5,000 visitors a day in 2017 damaged its coral. Closing it, then reopening with a jetty and controlled daily numbers, let it recover. Venice faces the same pressure in a city, managed by a cruise-ship ban and an access fee.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The global economy grew through trade, investment, aid, labour migration, container transport and ICT.
  • Commodity chains spread production stages across countries.
  • Which flow matters most depends on scale: trade globally; remittances for places such as Tajikistan, Tonga and Nepal.

Mission 2

  • Lenders (the IMF and World Bank) set the conditions for rescue loans and debt relief; voting follows quotas.
  • The WTO sets trade rules, the IMF supports countries in crisis, and TNCs spread investment: together they globalise the economy.

Mission 3

  • TNCs keep headquarters and design at home and spread production abroad, by outsourcing (Apple) or FDI (Toyota).
  • For India: jobs, skills, exports and suppliers; but profits leave, top jobs stay away, and FDI rises and falls.

Mission 4

  • Migration is caused by push and pull factors; economic migrants choose to move, refugees are forced to flee persecution; asylum seekers are waiting for a decision.
  • Impacts fall on migrants, origin (remittances, brain drain) and destination (workers, demand for services).

Mission 5

  • Refugees cross a border fleeing persecution; IDPs do not. Rohingya: statelessness, then the 2017 "clearance operations".
  • Challenges: congested, aid-dependent camps (refugees); land, money and time (Bangladesh).

Mission 6

  • Tourism brings jobs, foreign currency, taxes and infrastructure (benefits), but also leakage, low-paid seasonal work, environmental damage and crowding (problems).
  • Maldives: 20.3% of GDP; 59% of resort workers foreign in 2022; arrivals fell 67.4% in 2020.

Mission 7

  • Geopolitical relationships manage trade (EU–UK TCA: zero tariffs and quotas), migration (CTA) and tourism (ETAs and visas).
  • The UK (named country) has used open, selective and restrictive approaches; rules changed who came more than how many.

Mission 8

  • Ecotourism protects nature, benefits local people and educates visitors.

Mission 9

  • Mass tourism brings jobs and income but can damage environments and push out residents: positive and negative economic, cultural and environmental impacts.
  • Maya Bay (Thailand, upper-middle income; tropical coast and coral): closure, jetty, daily limits. Venice (Italy, high income; urban lagoon): cruise-ship ban, access fee.
