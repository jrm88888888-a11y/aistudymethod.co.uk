The Spoken Language Endorsement

This mini-lesson walks you through the Spoken Language Endorsement of Edexcel International GCSE English Language A: a formal presentation on a topic you choose, delivered to an audience in Standard English, followed by questions — graded Pass, Merit or Distinction and reported separately from your 9–1 grade.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (spotting Standard English, choosing structure, naming delivery techniques) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

What the endorsement is

A separate, spoken assessment

The Spoken Language Endorsement is an optional, separate assessment alongside the written exams — it is not one of the numbered written components. You prepare and deliver a presentation — a speech or talk on a topic of your choice — to a live audience, then answer questions about it.

  • It is prepared and lasts no longer than about 10 minutes.
  • You may use brief notes or slides — but you must not read from a full script.
  • You must use Spoken Standard English.
  • It is graded Not Classified / Pass / Merit / Distinction.
  • The grade is reported separately on your certificate — it does not change your 9–1 grade.

Why it matters: being able to plan, deliver and defend ideas aloud is a real-world skill — universities and employers value it. Treat it as its own project, not an afterthought.

Planning your presentation

Give it a clear shape

A strong presentation has a clear structure the audience can follow. Plan three parts and signpost as you move between them:

Signposting = little phrases that tell the audience where you are: "There are three reasons…", "Turning now to…", "Finally…". They make a talk easy to follow without any slides.

Worked example · openings

A strong vs a weak opening

The first 20 seconds set the tone. Compare these two openings for a talk titled "Why every teenager should learn to cook":

❌ Weak: "Um, so, my presentation is about cooking, I guess. Cooking is quite good and yeah, I'm going to talk about it now."
✅ Strong opening

"Last week I watched a classmate spend £6 on a sandwich he could have made for 80p. Today I'll argue that every teenager should learn to cook — for their health, their wallet, and their independence. Let me take each in turn."

What makes it strong: a hook (the anecdote), a clear statement of the topic, and a roadmap of the three points to come — all in Standard English, no filler.

Standard English & register

Speak in Standard English

Standard English is the widely-understood form used in formal settings — correct grammar and full, complete vocabulary, without slang or non-standard forms. It is what the endorsement expects, especially for Merit and Distinction.

Standard-English fix

❌ Non-standard: "We was gonna do it but like it weren't gonna work, innit."

✅ Standard English: "We were going to attempt it, but it was clear the plan would not work."

Register is how formal you sound. A presentation needs a formal register: full sentences, precise words, no text-speak. You can still be warm and lively — formal doesn't mean robotic.

Misconception buster

Standard English ≠ a "posh" accent

A very common myth: that you must sound "posh" to do well. Not true.

  • Standard English is about grammar and word choice — not the region or accent you speak with.
  • You can keep your own accent completely and still use Standard English.
  • What examiners want is clear, grammatically correct, formal speech — "we were", not "we was"; "going to", not "gonna".

Bottom line: fix the grammar and drop the slang, but keep your voice. Standard English is a dialect, not an accent.

Delivery technique

How you say it matters

The endorsement rewards engaging delivery, not just good words. Use your voice and your body deliberately:

Pace & pause: nerves make people rush. Slow down slightly, and pause after important sentences — silence gives your idea room to land.

Engaging the audience

Techniques that pull people in

Distinction-level talks use a range of strategies to engage the audience. Weave a few of these in:

  • Rhetorical question — "Have you ever wondered why…?"
  • Direct address — speaking to "you", the audience.
  • Rule of three — "faster, cheaper, greener".
  • Anecdote or vivid example — a short real story.
  • Statistic or striking fact — used sparingly for impact.

Match technique to purpose: a rhetorical question opens a topic; the rule of three makes a point memorable; an anecdote builds connection. Coming up: you'll match each technique to what it does.

Responding to questions

Handling the Q&A

After your talk, the audience asks questions — and responding well is part of the grade. Higher grades want you to answer formally and in detail, still in Standard English.

  • Listen to the whole question before answering.
  • Acknowledge it — "That's a good point…" — then respond.
  • Answer in full sentences, giving a reason or example.
  • If you're unsure, it's fine to say so calmly and give your best reasoning.

Plan for questions. The Q&A isn't random luck — think in advance about what people might ask (objections, "what about…?", why you chose this topic) and prepare a sentence or two for each.

Misconception buster

Two more myths to drop

Myth 1: "The questions are a surprise you can't prepare for." You absolutely can — anticipate likely questions and rehearse short answers. Being ready shows confidence.

Myth 2: "If my content is good I don't need signposting." Even brilliant ideas lose an audience without clear guiding phrases. A presentation needs signposting so listeners always know where they are.

Confidence > nerves: pausing to think before you answer is not a weakness — a considered, full-sentence answer beats a rushed one every time.

The grading criteria

Pass · Merit · Distinction

There are no marks — examiners award a grade using set criteria. You must meet all the criteria for a level to be given it. The ladder rises across the same four skills: ideas & vocabulary, structure & audience, Standard English, and responding to questions.

Not Classified is given if the criteria for a Pass aren't met. A learner must satisfy all criteria at a level to be awarded that level.

Recap

The endorsement in a nutshell

Format: a prepared presentation (≤ ~10 min) + questions from the audience

Structure: hook & roadmap → signposted main points → strong close

Language: Spoken Standard English & formal register (accent is fine)

Delivery: pace, pause, emphasis, eye contact, gestures, engaging techniques

Q&A: listen, acknowledge, answer formally in full sentences — plan ahead

Grading: Not Classified / Pass / Merit / Distinction, reported separately
