Mission 1: Most of Earth's carbon is locked away in rock. How does any of it get back into the air?

Mission 1

Carbon: the building block of life

  • "Carbon is the chemical backbone of life on Earth": every sugar, protein and cell is built on carbon chains.
  • Natural world: plants fix carbon into sugars; animals eat it and breathe it out; shells and skeletons store it as calcium carbonate.
  • Humans: fossil fuels "provide energy that fuels our global economy"; limestone is used to make concrete.
  • Carbon is never made or destroyed here: it moves between stores by fluxes.
Mission 1

Where the carbon is

  • Unit: 1 petagram (Pg) = 1 gigatonne (Gt) = 10¹⁵ g.
  • Atmosphere: about 589 PgC before industry; 828 PgC in 2011.
  • Vegetation: 450–650 PgC; litter and soils: 1,500–2,400 PgC; permafrost: about 1,700 PgC.
  • Ocean: dissolved inorganic carbon about 38,000 PgC; marine life only about 3 PgC. The ocean holds "about 50 times more carbon than the atmosphere".
  • Rocks: "Most of Earth's carbon" is stored in rocks.
Mission 1

Geological carbon

  • "Most of Earth's carbon" is geological, stored in rocks: in carbonate rocks (limestone, chalk) built from shells, and as organic carbon from buried plants and plankton in shale and coal.
  • Fossil fuel reserves (AR5): gas 383–1,135, oil 173–264, coal 446–541 PgC.
  • These are slow-domain stores: turnover times of "10,000 years or longer".
  • Natural exchange with the fast cycle is "relatively small (<0.3 PgC yr–1)".

Mission 1

Photosynthesis and respiration

  • Photosynthesis: plants "move carbon from the atmosphere into the biosphere". Gross primary production is about 123 ± 8 PgC a year.
  • Respiration (plants, animals, microbes) returns much of it to the air.
  • The fast cycle moves 1,000 to 100,000 million tonnes of carbon a year.
  • Evidence: CO₂ at Mauna Loa falls each northern summer and rises each winter, "driven by Northern Hemisphere summer vegetation growth … and winter decay".
Mission 1

Decomposition and soil carbon

  • Dead leaves, roots and animals become litter and soil organic matter.
  • Decomposition by microbes releases carbon "back into the atmosphere or soil".
  • Cold or waterlogged ground slows decomposition, so carbon builds up as peat and in permafrost.
  • Arctic and boreal permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, "almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere".
Mission 1

Combustion

  • Combustion oxidises carbon to CO₂ quickly.
  • Natural: wildfires burn vegetation and peat.
  • Human: burning fossil fuels; projected at 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025.
  • Fossil burning takes carbon from a slow store and adds it to the fast cycle within hours.
Mission 1

Sequestration in the oceans and sediments

  • Solubility pump: CO₂ "is more than twice as soluble in cold polar waters than in warm equatorial waters"; cold, dense water sinks and carries it to depth.
  • Biological pump: plankton fix carbon near the surface; "perhaps 30%" of it sinks into deeper waters, and "only about 0.1% reaches the seafloor".
  • Calcifying plankton build calcium carbonate shells; these sink, but "below about 4 km" virtually none accumulates on the seafloor.
  • What reaches the seafloor can be buried in sediments and, over millions of years, become rock.
Quick check

Mission 1

The slow cycle: weathering and outgassing

  • CO₂ dissolves in rain to form carbonic acid, which dissolves rock (chemical weathering) and releases calcium and other ions.
  • Rivers carry the ions to the ocean, where calcium combines with bicarbonate to form calcium carbonate: shells, then limestone.
  • Volcanic outgassing returns rock carbon to the air: volcanoes emit 130–380 million tonnes of CO₂ a year.
  • Scale: the slow cycle moves 10–100 million tonnes of carbon a year; rock weathering about 0.3 and volcanism about 0.1 PgC a year.
  • Timescale: carbon "takes between 100-200 million years" to move round the slow cycle.
Quick check

Mission check

?A limestone hill could return its carbon to the air by two routes: (1) natural, as the rock is eventually carried down and heated beneath a volcano; (2) human, as it is quarried and heated to make cement. Which statement is best supported?

Rock carbon returns to the air mainly by volcanic outgassing, a slow-cycle flux of only about 0.1 PgC a year, balanced over millions of years by chemical weathering and carbonate formation. Human extraction and burning move rock carbon far faster.

Mission 2

The ocean as a carbon store

  • The ocean holds about 50 times as much carbon as the atmosphere.
  • Carbon moves quickly between surface water and air, but may be stored for centuries in the deep ocean.
  • Store: holds carbon. Sink: takes up more than it releases. Source: releases more than it takes up.
Mission 2

The solubility pump and thermohaline circulation

  • CO₂ is more than twice as soluble in cold polar water as in warm equatorial water.
  • In the far North Atlantic, surface water loses heat, becomes "cooler and denser", and sinks to the bottom.
  • Thermohaline circulation is driven by density, controlled by temperature (thermo) and salinity (haline). It carries dissolved carbon into the deep ocean, out of contact with the air.
Mission 2

The biological pump: phytoplankton

  • Phytoplankton take up CO₂ in sunlit surface water by photosynthesis and turn it into organic matter.
  • Perhaps 30% of that matter sinks into deeper water; only about 0.1% reaches the seafloor sediments.
  • A model study estimated that the biological pump causes about 80% of the vertical gradient in dissolved inorganic carbon, and the solubility pump about 20%.
Mission 2

The carbonate pump

  • Many plankton "extract dissolved carbonate ions from seawater" to build calcium carbonate shells (tests); coccolithophores are the dominant group.
  • When they die, some shells "sink down into the deep oceans", carrying carbon with them.
  • Below about 4 km, the water is so corrosive that "virtually no calcium carbonate material can accumulate on the seafloor": shells dissolve, and their carbon stays in deep water.
  • Thermohaline circulation then keeps that deep water away from the surface for centuries.
Quick check
?Why do cold high-latitude waters, such as the far North Atlantic, take up so much CO₂?
Mission 2

A sink under strain

  • 2015–2024: the ocean took up 3.2 ± 0.4 billion tonnes of carbon a year, 29% of total CO₂ emissions; land took 21% and the atmosphere kept 50%.
  • The ocean sink has been "stagnant since 2016".
  • Models estimate climate change cut the sink by 0.20 GtC a year (−7.1%) in 2015–2024: warmer water dissolves less CO₂.
Mission 2

Ocean acidification

  • Dissolved CO₂ forms carbonic acid, releasing hydrogen ions. The ocean's average pH is now around 8.1; surface pH has fallen by 0.1 units over the industrial era.
  • Extra hydrogen ions bond with carbonate ions, leaving fewer carbonate ions for corals and shellfish to build skeletons and shells.
  • IPCC: it is "virtually certain" that human-caused CO₂ emissions, such as from burning fossil fuels, are the main driver of current surface ocean acidification.
Quick check
?The pH scale is logarithmic: a fall of 1 unit means 10 times as many hydrogen ions. Surface pH has fallen by 0.1. By about what percentage has hydrogen-ion concentration risen?
Mission 2

Coral reefs under two pressures

  • Bleaching is caused by heat stress: from early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area; mass bleaching was recorded in at least 83 countries and territories.
  • Acidification slows reef growth, and is projected to "amplify the adverse effects of warming".
  • Critical threshold: reefs are projected to decline by a further 70–90% at 1.5 °C of warming, and by more than 99% at 2 °C.
Mission 2

The Great Barrier Reef: recent data

  • Hard coral cover, northern GBR: 39.8% (2024) → 30.0% (2025), the largest annual decline recorded there since monitoring began.
  • AIMS: "the mass coral bleaching event was the primary source of coral mortality".
  • 2026: northern cover recovered to 35.1%, helped by monsoon cloud and cooler water.
  • Coral loss means habitat loss: biodiversity falls as reef-dependent fish and invertebrates lose shelter and food.
Mission 2

Who depends on healthy oceans?

  • Food: aquatic foods gave about 17% of the world's animal protein in 2019, but 50% or more in Cambodia, Ghana, Mozambique, Bangladesh, Indonesia, Sierra Leone and some small island developing states.
  • Coastal protection: reefs reduce the wave energy reaching coastlines by 97%; 197 million people can receive risk-reduction benefits from reefs.
  • Tourism: reef-based tourism earns income in many coastal and island economies.
  • Threats to ocean health hit hardest where people have fewest alternatives: often developing regions.
Quick check
?Which community is most vulnerable to reef loss?
Mission check

?In 2007 an Oregon oyster hatchery lost huge numbers of larvae. The cause was seawater with low pH and low carbonate saturation. How is this linked to the ocean taking up CO₂?

Corals face the same chemistry. The ocean sink slows warming, but absorbed CO₂ forms carbonic acid: surface pH has fallen by 0.1, leaving fewer carbonate ions for skeletons. Add heat-driven bleaching, and the IPCC projects a further 70–90% loss of reefs at 1.5 °C.

Mission 3

The atmosphere as an energy system

  • Energy arrives as shortwave radiation from the Sun and leaves as longwave (infrared) radiation from Earth.
  • IPCC global averages: 340 W/m² of sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere; 100 W/m² is reflected straight back to space.
  • Of the rest, 80 W/m² is absorbed by the atmosphere and 160 W/m² by the surface.
Mission 3

The outgoing side

  • The surface emits 398 W/m² of longwave radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases and clouds absorb much of it and send 342 W/m² back down (back radiation).
  • 239 W/m² of longwave leaves the top of the atmosphere.
  • Incoming slightly exceeds outgoing: an imbalance of 0.7 W/m², so the climate system is gaining heat.
Quick check
?340 W/m² of sunlight arrives and 100 W/m² is reflected. What is Earth's planetary albedo?
Mission 3

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Greenhouse gases let most sunlight through but absorb outgoing longwave radiation and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface.
  • "Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas, and carbon dioxide (CO2) is the second-most important one".
  • To emit about 240 W/m², a surface would need to be about −19 °C; the actual global mean is about 14 °C.
  • The natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C (our calculation) and makes Earth habitable.
Mission 3

Carbon dioxide and methane

  • Global average CO₂: 425.62 ppm in 2025; methane: 1,936 ppb.
  • Before the Industrial Revolution, CO₂ was 280 ppm or less.
  • IPCC: in 2019, CO₂ was higher than at any time in at least 2 million years; methane and nitrous oxide higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years.
  • More of these gases absorbs more outgoing longwave radiation: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
Mission 3

Greenhouse effect, temperature and precipitation

  • The greenhouse effect sets how warm the surface is, and so how much water evaporates.
  • Warmer air can hold more water vapour, itself a greenhouse gas: a positive feedback.
  • More evaporation means more energy and moisture for precipitation, so changes in CO₂ and methane alter where and how much it rains as well as temperature.
Mission 3

Photosynthesis and soil carbon

  • Photosynthesis on land and in the ocean (phytoplankton) draws CO₂ from the air.
  • 2015–2024: the ocean took up 29% of total CO₂ emissions and land 21%; about 50% stayed in the atmosphere.
  • The yearly CO₂ cycle at Mauna Loa is driven by Northern Hemisphere vegetation: summer growth "reduces atmospheric carbon dioxide", winter decay increases it.
  • Soil carbon (organic matter) holds water and nutrients and keeps soil structure, so it underpins soil health and plant productivity.
Mission 3

Fossil fuels unbalance the stores

  • Burning fossil fuels moves carbon from a slow geological store to the atmosphere: 38.1 billion tonnes of fossil CO₂ projected for 2025.
  • In 2025, atmospheric CO₂ was about 52% above pre-industrial levels.
  • Climate: the IPCC's best estimate of human-caused warming from 1850–1900 to 2010–2019 is 1.07 °C. Ecosystems: the ocean has absorbed enough CO₂ to lower its pH by 0.1 units, "a 30% increase in acidity".
  • Hydrological cycle: heavy precipitation has become more frequent and intense over most land areas since the 1950s.
Quick check
?Why does only about half of the CO₂ we emit stay in the atmosphere?
Mission 3

Volcanic forcing: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere.
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight": less sunlight reached the surface.
  • Over the next 15 months, average global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C; measurable cooling lasted almost two years.
Mission check

?A large tropical eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What happens to the global energy balance, and for how long?

Pinatubo put about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. Droplets scattered incoming sunlight, changing the incoming side of the energy balance, and global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over 15 months. The droplets fell out within about two years, so the cooling was temporary.

Primary and secondary energy

  • Primary energy: energy as found in nature: coal, crude oil, natural gas, uranium, sunlight, wind, flowing water, biomass.
  • Secondary energy: energy converted from primary sources before use: electricity, petrol and diesel refined from crude oil.
  • Renewable (flows: sun, wind, water) vs non-renewable (stocks: fossil fuels, uranium).
  • Domestic vs foreign: energy produced at home vs imported.
Mission 4

The world mix, 2025

  • Total energy supply passed 600 exajoules in 2025, up 1.7% on 2024.
  • Fossil fuels "continued to expand in absolute terms" and supplied 86% of total energy supply.
  • All major energy sources reached "all-time highs for the second consecutive year".
  • Renewables were "the largest source of TES growth for the first time outside of a recession".

Contrasting mixes: USA and France

  • Method: these shares use the substitution method, which counts non-fossil electricity as the fossil fuel it replaces.
Mission 4

Why the USA and France differ

  • Physical availability: in 2024 the USA produced 9,977 TWh of oil and used 9,949; it produced 10,330 TWh of gas and used 9,022.
  • France produced 1.2% of the oil it used in 2015, the last year its production is listed: oil and gas are foreign energy for France.
  • Technology and cost: France generates most of its power from nuclear and was a net electricity exporter in 2024 (net imports −90 TWh).
  • Perception, development and environmental priorities decide which options a country accepts: nuclear in France; cheap domestic oil and gas in the USA.
Mission 4

Where fossil fuels are produced and used

  • 2024 oil (our calculation):
    • the Middle East produced 31.0% of the world's oil but used 9.5%
    • Asia Pacific used 38.1% but produced 7.6%.
  • Europe used about 4.9 times as much oil as it produced, and produced 42% of the gas it used.
  • The mismatch comes from geology: oil and gas fields are where they are; demand is where people and industry are. Trade bridges the gap.

Mission 5

Energy pathways and chokepoints

  • An energy pathway is the route energy takes from producer to consumer: a pipeline, shipping lane or power line.
  • A chokepoint is a narrow point on a pathway with few alternatives.
  • In 2025, an average of 20 million barrels a day of crude oil and oil products passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • Asia is "normally the destination for the vast majority of the oil and gas that flows through the Strait".
Mission 5

Suez and the Red Sea

  • The Suez Canal "normally passes about 15 percent of global maritime trade volume".
  • After attacks on shipping in the Red Sea, Suez trade volume "dropped by 50 percent year-over-year in the first two months of 2024".
  • Ships diverted around the Cape of Good Hope, adding "10 days or more on average" to deliveries.
  • A chokepoint does not have to close to cause harm: delay and extra cost spread along supply chains.
Mission 5

The 2026 Hormuz disruption

  • "The conflict in the Middle East that began on 28 February 2026 has impeded oil flows through the Strait of Hormuz".
  • Flows averaged 2.7 million barrels a day in March, April and May.
  • The North Sea Dated crude price "reached an all-time high of $144 per barrel – more than double its pre-war levels".
  • Responses: IEA members released 400 million barrels of emergency stocks; Saudi Arabia and the UAE used pipelines that bypass the strait.
Quick check

Mission 5

From energy shock to food shock

  • The UN's SDG Report 2026: disruptions in the Strait of Hormuz "affected the movement of key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels".
  • This drove up "production and transportation costs across food systems".
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
  • The link is the water–food–energy nexus: farming runs on fuel and fertiliser.
Mission 5

Russian gas to Europe

  • Gas reaches Europe by pipeline and as liquefied natural gas (LNG) by ship.
  • Russia's share of EU pipeline gas imports dropped "from around 40% in 2021 to around 6% in 2025".
  • Counting pipeline gas and LNG together, Russia supplied around 12% of EU gas imports in 2025.
  • Russian LNG is harder to replace: it still made up 17.3% of EU LNG imports in the second quarter of 2026, down from 21.2% in the first quarter of 2021.
  • A pathway built for decades of trade was largely closed by politics within four years.
Mission 5

Energy players

  • Producer governments and OPEC: OPEC was formed in 1960 to "coordinate and unify petroleum policies among Member Countries". Including the UAE, it produced 35% of world crude oil in 2025; the UAE then left OPEC on 1 May 2026.
  • Consumer governments: the IEA "was founded in 1974 to ensure the security of oil supplies"; its members hold and release emergency stocks.
  • TNCs: Nord Stream AG, which built gas pipelines from Russia to Germany, is 51% owned by a Gazprom Group company, with stakes held by Wintershall Dea (15.5%), ENGIE (9.0%), E.ON and Gasunie.
  • Consumers: their demand, and how much they cut it when prices rise, decides how much must flow.

Mission 6

What makes a fuel "unconventional"?

  • Oil or gas that will not simply flow to a well, or lies in extreme places.
  • Tar (oil) sands: sand mixed with thick bitumen; mined, or heated with steam underground.
  • Shale gas: methane trapped in tight shale; released by fracking (water, sand, chemicals crack the rock).
  • Oil shale: rock containing kerogen, a solid organic material, which must be heated to yield oil.
  • Deep-water oil: below deep ocean, e.g. Brazil's pre-salt, found more than 7,000 m down (water, rock and salt).

  • 6.9b adaptation strategies (water management, resilient agriculture, land-use planning, flood management): costs and risks
  • 6.9c mitigation (carbon taxation, renewable switching, energy efficiency, afforestation, CCS, solar radiation management) needs problematic global agreement and national action
Mission 12

Mitigation and adaptation

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes: cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewables, carbon pricing, forests, carbon capture).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to actual or expected climate to moderate harm or use opportunities (flood defences, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Mitigation needs global action; adaptation is mostly local, but poorer places need help to pay for it.
Mission 12

Global agreements

  • UNFCCC (Convention): adopted 9 May 1992, in force 1994.
  • Kyoto Protocol: adopted 1997, in force 2005; binding targets for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition only.
  • Limits: the USA signed but never ratified; Canada withdrew with effect from 15 December 2012.
  • Paris Agreement: adopted 12 December 2015, in force 4 November 2016; US withdrawal took effect 27 January 2026.
Mission 12

The Paris Agreement

  • Goal: "well below 2°C" and pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), on a five-year cycle, with a global stocktake.
  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • Strength: near-universal. Weakness: targets are self-chosen.
Mission 12

The gap

  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C); 2025 was 1.44 °C. Paris is judged over decades, not single years.
  • UNEP (2025): full NDCs → 2.3–2.5 °C this century; current policies → up to 2.8 °C.
  • Carbon budget left for 1.5 °C: 170 billion tonnes CO₂. 2025 emissions: 38.1 Gt fossil + 4.1 Gt land-use change.

Mission 12

Who pays? Sources far from impacts

  • Least developed countries: under 0.4% of fossil CO₂ (1850–2019).
  • Deaths from floods, droughts and storms were 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions (2010–2020).
  • Finance: the US$100 billion goal was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion); new goal US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Loss and Damage Fund assists developing countries particularly vulnerable to climate change. These are goals and funds, not delivered outcomes.
Quick check
?What is the main argument for high-emitting countries funding loss and damage in poorer ones?
Mission 12

Adaptation strategies: costs and risks

  • Water management: on-farm water management, storage and irrigation reduce vulnerability, but irrigation can deplete groundwater and salinise soil.
  • Flood management: early warnings plus levees have cut deaths; land-use planning (no-build zones) and wetland restoration reduce risk further.
  • Resilient agriculture: cultivar improvements, agroforestry, diversification.
  • Costs: the Dutch Delta Programme has about €29 billion for 2015–2050 against costs of about €38 billion.
  • Risk: seawalls protect now but can lock in exposure: maladaptation.
Mission 12

Mitigation: the toolkit

  • Carbon tax / trading: covered over 20% of global emissions by 2020, but prices too low for deep cuts; the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) cut emissions from covered sectors about 47% from 2005.
  • Renewables and efficiency: 2010–2019 unit costs fell 85% (solar) and 55% (wind).
  • CCS captures CO₂ at power stations or factories and stores it underground; direct air capture removes CO₂ already in the air.
  • Carbon capture: Iceland's Mammoth direct-air-capture plant is designed for up to 36,000 t CO₂ a year; it captured 675 t in the first half of 2026.
  • Afforestation and forest offsets: most of 26 projects studied did not significantly reduce deforestation.
  • Solar radiation management: does not reduce greenhouse gases; a sudden stop would cause rapid climate change.
Mission 12

Why global agreement is hard

  • Emissions mix globally: a country that cuts bears the cost; benefits are shared (the free-rider problem).
  • Major emitters have left agreements: Canada (Kyoto), the USA (Paris).
  • Carbon leakage: firms may move production to countries with weaker rules.
  • Pledges add up to 2.3–2.5 °C, not 1.5 °C.

The UN agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still point to 2.3–2.8 °C.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Stores (PgC): ocean ~38,000 ≫ soils 1,500–2,400 and permafrost ~1,700 > atmosphere ~828 (2011) > vegetation 450–650; most carbon of all is in rock.
  • Fast cycle: photosynthesis, respiration, decomposition, combustion, ocean exchange (≈123 PgC a year by photosynthesis). Slow cycle: weathering, carbonate formation, burial, outgassing (fractions of a PgC a year, over millions of years).

Mission 2

  • The ocean stores carbon through the solubility pump, the biological pump and the carbonate pump, with thermohaline circulation carrying it to depth.
  • Absorbing CO₂ makes the ocean more acidic; with heat-driven bleaching, reefs near a critical threshold, threatening food, coastal protection and income, above all in developing regions.

Mission 3

  • Earth's energy balance: 340 W/m² in, 100 reflected, 239 longwave out; the natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C. Forcings, natural or human, shift the balance.
  • Photosynthesis and the ocean absorb about half of our CO₂; fossil fuels unbalance the stores.

Mission 4

  • Primary energy is used as found; secondary energy (electricity, refined fuels) is converted. Mixes differ: France leans on nuclear; the USA on its own oil and gas.
  • Energy use per person and per unit of GDP vary widely; oil and gas are produced in a few regions and used in others, so trade fills the gap.

Mission 5

  • Energy pathways (pipelines, shipping lanes, power lines) pass through chokepoints such as Hormuz and Suez; disruption raises costs far away.
  • OPEC, the IEA, TNCs, governments and consumers each secure supplies differently; Russian gas to Europe shows a pathway disrupted.

Mission 6

  • Tar sands (Canada: 158.9bn barrels, royalties; tailings, emissions +529%), shale gas (USA: 78% of dry gas, largest LNG exporter; methane, tremors, England moratorium) and deep-water oil (Brazil pre-salt; Deepwater Horizon 2010).
  • Evaluate benefits (reserves, revenue, security) against costs (carbon lock-in, fragile environments, local risk), and say who gains and who pays.

Mission 7

  • Supply strategies: oil and gas exploration, nuclear, renewables. UK electricity moved from 72% coal (1990) to 0% coal and 36% wind and solar (2025).
  • The UK has decoupled: GDP 185.9, energy 74.4, CO₂ 47.6 (1990 = 100). Brazil's biofuels show growth, food-land pressure and uncertain carbon savings.

Mission 8

  • Interventions either cut emissions (efficiency, fuel shifts, carbon pricing) or store carbon (forests, CCS, DAC). Capture is tiny today: about 50 Mt a year against 38.1 Gt emitted.
  • SRM masks warming without removing CO₂. Global agreement remains hard.

Mission 9

  • Amazonia stores 150–200 Pg C and recycles much of its own rain; clearing for food, minerals and energy turns local gains in streamflow into basin-wide losses of rainfall and carbon.
  • Droughts (2005, 2010, 2023) show the store is vulnerable; restoration, better farming and protection help both cycles, but at small scale so far.

Mission 10

  • Impacts: agriculture drives 90–99% of tropical deforestation; clearance removes the biomass nutrient store, so latosols are leached and food webs simplified.
  • Responses: protection (conservation units, indigenous lands, payments), forest expansion (Vietnam, China, Costa Rica). The Kuznets curve and forest transition describe a possible turn, but displacement abroad and mixed evidence make it contested.

Mission 11

  • Water and carbon stores regulate climate; they link through the atmosphere (vapour, CO₂), ocean, cryosphere and vegetation.
  • Arctic warming is amplified by positive feedbacks (ice–albedo, water vapour, permafrost carbon); some dampen it (radiation to space, CO₂ fertilisation), but sinks become less effective.
  • Future warming depends on socio-economic choices and on feedbacks and tipping points whose timing is uncertain.

Mission 12

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm. Kyoto bound few countries; Paris includes almost everyone but relies on self-set NDCs.
  • Every adaptation and mitigation option has costs and risks; global agreement is hard because of free-riding, leakage and withdrawals.
