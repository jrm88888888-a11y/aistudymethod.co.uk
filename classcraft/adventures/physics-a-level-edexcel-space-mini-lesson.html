Mission 1

Mission 1: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Make a guess: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Mission 1

Luminosity

  • Luminosity, L: the total power a star emits, in watts.
  • Stars are close to black bodies.
  • L = σAT⁴, with A = 4πR² the star's surface area.
  • Same temperature, twice the radius → 4 × the luminosity.
Mission 1

What we receive

  • The star's power spreads over a sphere of area 4πd² at distance d.
  • Intensity, I: the power received per unit area, in W m⁻².
  • I = L/(4πd²)
  • Inverse-square law: twice as far → ¼ as much per square metre.
  • A faint star may be weak, or just far away.
Quick check
?A star has 40 times the Sun's luminosity and is 25 ly from the Earth. Take the Sun's luminosity = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W and 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. What is the intensity of the star's light at the Earth?
Mission 1

Stellar parallax

  • Observe a nearby star twice, six months apart: from opposite sides of Earth's orbit.
  • It shifts against the very distant background stars.
  • Parallax angle p: half the total shift. It is the angle the Earth–Sun distance (1 AU) subtends at the star.
  • Closer star → bigger p.
  • tan p = (1 AU)/d. For small angles, in radians: d = (1 AU)/p.
  • Only works for nearby stars: further away, p is too small to measure.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A star has a parallax angle of 0.20 arcseconds.
  • p = 0.20/3600 = 5.56 × 10⁻⁵ degrees × π/180 = 9.70 × 10⁻⁷ rad.
  • d = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m (1 AU) ÷ 9.70 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.5 × 10¹⁷ m.
Quick check
?Over six months, a nearby star's position against the distant stars shifts by a total angle of 0.0800 arcseconds. The radius of the Earth's orbit is 1.50 × 10¹¹ m. How far away is the star?
Mission 1

Standard candles

  • A standard candle: an object whose luminosity is known.
  • Examples: Cepheid variables (pulsation period → luminosity); type Ia supernovae (similar peak luminosity).
  • Measure the intensity I received at the Earth.
  • Rearrange I = L/(4πd²): d = √(L/(4πI)).
  • Works far beyond parallax: to other galaxies.
Quick check
?A Cepheid variable in a distant star cluster has a luminosity of 1.5 × 10³⁰ W. The intensity of its light at the Earth is 2.0 × 10⁻¹² W m⁻². What is its distance from the Earth?
Mission check

?At the Earth, the intensity of star A's light is 4 times that of star B's. Star B is 3 times as far from the Earth as star A. What is the ratio (luminosity of A) ÷ (luminosity of B)?

Brightness alone cannot tell you a star's luminosity. Measure its distance too; then L = 4πd² × (power received per square metre).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Make a guess: Why will the Sun end as a small, fading ember, while far heavier stars blow themselves apart?

Mission 2

A star in balance

  • Gravity pulls every layer of a star inwards.
  • Pressure pushes outwards: hot gas (gas pressure) and radiation (radiation pressure).
  • Fusion in the core keeps the core hot, so the pressure is maintained.
  • Balanced: the star is stable, neither collapsing nor expanding. Huge forces, zero resultant.
  • Birth: a cloud of dust and gas collapses under gravity. It heats up as it shrinks: a protostar.
  • Once the core is hot and dense enough, hydrogen fuses into helium. The pressure stops the collapse: a main-sequence star.
Mission 2

The HR diagram

  • Luminosity (vertical, log scale, in units of the Sun's) against surface temperature (horizontal, log scale).
  • Temperature decreases to the right: hot blue stars on the left, cool red stars on the right.
  • Main sequence: diagonal band, upper left to lower right. About 90% of stars.
  • Red giants: upper right. Red supergiants: across the top.
  • White dwarfs: lower left.
Quick check
?The HR diagram shows the main sequence, the Sun and a star X. Which statement about star X is correct?
Mission 2

What position tells you

  • Same temperature, more luminous → bigger (more surface area).
  • Upper right: cool, yet very luminous → huge.
  • Lower left: hot, yet dim → tiny.
  • On the main sequence, mass sets the position: more massive stars are higher, hotter and more luminous.
  • More massive → burns fuel much faster → shorter main-sequence life.
Mission 2

Mass decides the path

  • Core hydrogen runs out → core contracts and heats up; outer layers expand and cool.
  • Low mass (like the Sun): main sequence → red giant → outer layers drift off as a planetary nebula → core left as a white dwarf.
  • High mass (more than roughly 8 times the Sun's mass): main sequence → red supergiant → supernova → neutron star, or a black hole for the most massive.
  • More mass → stronger gravity → hotter core → heavier elements fused.
  • A white dwarf has no fusion: it slowly cools and fades.
Mission 2

Paths on the HR diagram

  • A star does not slide along the main sequence as it ages.
  • Sun-like star: up and to the right (red giant) → leftwards as the core is exposed → down to the white-dwarf region → fades downwards and to the right as it cools.
  • Massive star: from the upper main sequence, across to the right (red supergiant). After the supernova, the remnant (a neutron star or black hole) is not plotted on the HR diagram.
Quick check
?Astronomers observe a glowing shell of gas expanding gently away from a very hot, very small star at its centre: a planetary nebula. Which describes the central star?
Mission check

?Astronomers find a neutron star at the centre of the expanding remains of a supernova. What can be concluded about the star that produced it, compared with the Sun?

The Sun has too little mass. Its core will never get hot enough to fuse heavy elements, and its leftover core is too small to collapse further: a white dwarf that fades.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Make a guess: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Quick check
?Predict first. Imagine a wavefront diagram of the sound from a source moving at constant speed through still air towards a stationary observer X. Each wavefront is drawn as a circle centred on the point where the source was when that wavefront was emitted. Compared with the waves the same source emits when it is at rest, how do the waves reaching X differ?
Mission 3

A moving source

  • A source emits wavefronts at a steady frequency. Each spreads out from where the source was when it was emitted.
  • Moving source: wavefronts bunched ahead (shorter λ), spread out behind (longer λ).
  • Wave speed through the air is unchanged. So ahead: higher f; behind: lower f.
  • Approaching: higher pitch. Receding: lower pitch. Constant velocity → constant (shifted) pitch.
  • Faster relative motion → bigger shift. Same velocity as the listener → no shift.
Mission 3

A moving observer

  • Source still: wavefronts are evenly spaced circles. λ unchanged.
  • Observer moving towards the source meets wavefronts more often → higher f.
  • Moving away → meets them less often → lower f.
  • What matters is the relative velocity of source and observer.
Mission 3

Doppler shift of light

  • Same effect for light and all EM waves.
  • Receding source: λ longer → red shift. Approaching: λ shorter → blue shift.
  • For v ≪ c:
  • Δf/f = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c
  • Red shift z = Δλ/λ; z = v/c for v ≪ c.
  • v is the radial velocity: the part along the line of sight.
  • For galaxies, Hubble's law v = H0d then gives the distance.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A hydrogen line: 486.1 nm in the lab, 487.6 nm in a star's spectrum.
  • Δλ = 487.6 − 486.1 = 1.5 nm. Longer → moving away.
  • v = c × Δλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 1.5 ÷ 486.1 = 9.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹ (about 0.3% of c).
  • v ≪ c, so the approximation is fine.
Quick check
?A spectral line of hydrogen has a wavelength of 656.3 nm when measured in the laboratory. A galaxy is moving directly away from Earth at 3.00 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. At what wavelength is this line observed in the spectrum of the galaxy?
Mission check

?A calcium absorption line has a wavelength of 393.4 nm in the laboratory. In the spectrum of a distant galaxy the same line is at 401.3 nm. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the galaxy's velocity relative to Earth?

Approaching: waves bunched, higher f; receding: stretched, lower f. The size of the shift grows with the relative speed, so measuring it measures the speed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Make a guess: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Mission 4

Red shifts of distant galaxies

  • Almost every distant galaxy is red-shifted: almost all are receding.
  • The further away, the larger the red shift and the faster they recede.
  • Not because we are at the centre: space is expanding everywhere.
Mission 4

Hubble's law

  • Plot recession speed (from red shift) against distance (measured independently, e.g. from stars of known luminosity).
  • Distant galaxies: a straight line through the origin. Speed ∝ distance.
  • v = H0d
  • H0, the Hubble constant: the gradient of the graph.
  • Twice as far → receding twice as fast.
  • Nearby galaxies scatter about the line: their own motions through space matter more.
Mission 4

Worked example: how far?

  • A galaxy's red shift gives v = 6.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.
  • d = v/H0 = 6.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.5 × 10²⁴ m.
  • In light-years: 2.5 × 10²⁴ ÷ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 2.6 × 10⁸ ly.
  • The light we see left that galaxy about 260 million years ago.
Quick check
?A galaxy at a distance of 1.2 × 10²⁴ m recedes from the Earth at 2800 km s⁻¹. What value of the Hubble constant does this give?
Mission 4

Units of H0

  • Astronomers quote H0 in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹: extra recession speed (km s⁻¹) for each megaparsec of distance.
  • 1 Mpc = 10⁶ pc = 3.09 × 10²² m.
  • H0 ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ → 70 × 10³ m s⁻¹ ÷ 3.09 × 10²² m = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.
  • To go back: multiply by 3.09 × 10²² m, then divide by 10³ m km⁻¹.
  • SI unit: s⁻¹ (speed ÷ distance).
Quick check
?A value of the Hubble constant is 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. Take 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m. What is this in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹?
Mission 4

The age of the universe

  • Run the expansion backwards: every galaxy was once in the same place as every other.
  • Time for a galaxy to cover distance d at speed v: d/v.
  • Hubble's law: d/v = 1/H0, the same for every galaxy.
  • t ≈ 1/H0
  • H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ → t = 4.2 × 10¹⁷ s ÷ 3.16 × 10⁷ s per year = 1.3 × 10¹⁰ years.
  • Only an estimate: it assumes the galaxies' speeds have never changed.
Quick check
?A measured value of the Hubble constant is 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. What is the estimated age of the universe? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)
Mission check

?Galaxy P is 2.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 5.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Galaxy Q is 6.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 1.56 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹. Assuming each has always moved at its present speed, how long ago was each at our position? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)

Yes: every galaxy gives about the same distance ÷ speed, so they were all together about 1/H0 ago: roughly 13 to 15 billion years.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Where in the universe did the Big Bang happen?

Make a guess: Where in the universe did the Big Bang happen?

Mission 5

Space itself expands

  • Galaxies are not flying apart through space. Space-time itself is expanding, carrying them apart.
  • Picture dots on a balloon being blown up: every dot moves away from every other.
  • From any galaxy, the others recede with v ∝ d. Every observer sees Hubble's law.
  • The Big Bang was not an explosion at one point into empty space. Expansion began everywhere at once.
  • Galaxies themselves do not expand: gravity holds them together.
Quick check
?As the universe expands, which of these distances increases?
Mission 5

How old? How will it end?

  • Age ≈ 1/H0, so it depends on H0. Methods disagree: about 67 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ from the CMB, about 73 from Cepheids and supernovae (some other distance methods give about 70).
  • 1/H0 then gives about 14.5 or 13.4 billion years. Why the values differ is still unexplained.
  • Fate: depends on the mean density of the universe compared with a critical density.
  • Denser than critical: gravity eventually halts the expansion and it contracts. Less dense: it expands for ever.
  • Exactly critical: the expansion slows, approaching zero but never reversing.
  • Dark matter: unseen mass, inferred from how galaxies rotate. Its amount affects the mean density, so it affects the predicted fate.
  • Since 1998, observations of distant supernovae show the expansion is accelerating: another reason the question is still open.
Quick check
?Surveys suggest that visible matter makes up about 5% of the critical density, and that including dark matter raises the mean density to about 30% of the critical density. Ignoring the observed acceleration, what does this predict?
Mission check

?Galaxies A and B lie in opposite directions in our sky, each at the same distance d from the Milky Way. Each recedes from us at speed v. What would an astronomer in galaxy A measure for the recession speed of galaxy B?

The Big Bang happened everywhere. Every galaxy sees the others rushing away; none is at the centre.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Luminosity is a star's total power output: L = 4πR²σT⁴.
  • Intensity I = L/(4πd²): a faint star may be weak or distant.
  • Parallax for nearby stars; standard candles (known L) for distant ones.

Mission 2

  • Stable star: gravity in, gas and radiation pressure out, kept up by fusion.
  • HR diagram: luminosity up, temperature decreasing to the right; main sequence, giants upper right, white dwarfs lower left.
  • Mass decides the end: low mass → planetary nebula + white dwarf; high mass → supernova + neutron star or black hole.

Mission 3

  • Approaching → higher f, shorter λ; receding → lower f, longer λ; bigger relative speed → bigger shift.
  • Light, v ≪ c: Δλ/λ ≈ v/c; red shift means moving away.

Mission 4

  • Hubble's law: v = H0d; 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m converts km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ to s⁻¹.
  • Age of the universe ≈ 1/H0, roughly 13–15 billion years (assumes constant expansion).

Mission 5

  • The Big Bang was the beginning of the expansion of space-time everywhere, not an explosion at a point.
  • The age (from H0) and the fate (from the mean density, including dark matter) are still debated.
