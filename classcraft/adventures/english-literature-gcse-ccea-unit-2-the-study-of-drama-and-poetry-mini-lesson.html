Mission 1

Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Make a guess: Two war poems, a century apart. How do you compare, not list?

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Mission 2

Why squeeze a love story into exactly fourteen lines?

Make a guess: Why squeeze a love story into exactly fourteen lines?

Poetry skills · form, metre and rhymeScreen 1

Counting the beat

  • Metre is the pattern of stressed (DUM) and unstressed (da) syllables in a line.
  • An iamb is da-DUM. Five iambs make iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five stresses, close to the rhythm of speech.
  • A trochee is DUM-da. The Witches in Macbeth chant in trochaic tetrameter: four stresses, often with the last weak syllable dropped, so it sounds like a spell.
  • Poets vary the pattern. When the beat changes, ask why.

Poetry skills · form, metre and rhymeScreen 3

Two shapes of sonnet

  • A sonnet: 14 lines, usually iambic pentameter. Petrarch (14th-century Italy) made it the form for love poems; Wyatt brought it into English in the early 1500s.
  • Petrarchan: an octave (ABBAABBA) then a sestet (CDECDE or CDCDCD). The volta (turn) usually comes at line 9.
  • Shakespearean: three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) and a couplet (GG). The turn comes at line 9, the couplet, or both.
  • Barrett Browning's Sonnet 43, "How do I love thee? Let me count the ways", uses the Petrarchan rhyme scheme.

Mission 3

'This is a metaphor' earns almost nothing. What makes it analysis?

Make a guess: 'This is a metaphor' earns almost nothing. What makes it analysis?

Literature skills · method and effectScreen 1

Spotting is not analysing

  • Feature-spotting names a technique: 'This is a simile.' It shows you know a term, not that you understand the writing.
  • Examiners reward analysis: how a writer's language, form and structure create meanings and effects (AO2).
  • Analysis answers three questions: What has the writer done? How does it shape what we see or feel? Why does the writer want that here?
  • The term is a tool. The effect is the point.

Mission 4

Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

Make a guess: Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 1

An essay is an argument

  • Marks follow the argument, not how many quotations you use.
  • A thesis is your answer to the question in one or two sentences: how the writer presents something and why (what the text suggests).
  • Each paragraph proves one part of the thesis; quotations are its evidence.
  • This mission teaches the thesis and the plan. Full essay practice is for class.
Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 2

Unpack the question first

Example: How does Dickens present Scrooge's change in A Christmas Carol?

  • Circle the focus (Scrooge's change) and the command: how does Dickens present means methods and purpose, not just what happens.
  • Check the scope: an extract, the whole text, or both? If the question starts from an extract, plan how you'll move out to the whole text.
  • Ask so what? Why might Dickens want readers to watch this change?

Essay skills · thesis and argumentScreen 5

From thesis to plan

  • Break the thesis into 3–4 steps; each step becomes a paragraph.
  • Write each step as a topic sentence that argues, not a plot point.
  • Under each: a short quotation or two, the method, and context only where it explains the point.
  • Order the steps so the argument builds.

For the question How does Shakespeare present ambition in Macbeth?, write a thesis (one or two sentences) and three topic sentences, one for each step of your argument.

Model answer

Thesis: Shakespeare presents ambition as a desire that promises greatness but destroys whoever acts on it, warning his audience against breaking the natural order. Step 1: At first Macbeth's ambition is a secret wish he can barely admit, even to himself. Step 2: Lady Macbeth turns that wish into action by attacking his sense of manhood. Step 3: Once king, Macbeth finds his reward is fear and sleeplessness, so ambition proves empty.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two essays, same quotations; one scores double. What's different?

The difference is the argument. The stronger essay starts with a thesis that answers how and why, plans 3–4 steps that prove it, and uses each quotation as evidence for a step. The weaker one uses the same quotations to retell the story or list techniques.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.
  • Metre is the pattern of stresses: iambic pentameter (da-DUM ×5) and trochaic tetrameter (DUM-da ×4); count syllables and stresses before you name it.
  • A Petrarchan sonnet has an octave and a sestet with a volta at line 9; a Shakespearean sonnet has three quatrains and a couplet.
  • Ballads tell stories in ballad metre; dramatic monologues give one speaker and a silent listener; free verse drops regular metre and rhyme. Always say what the form does.
  • Naming a technique earns little on its own: explain what it makes the reader see or feel, and why the writer wants that (AO2).
  • Zoom in on one precise word or phrase, embed short quotations, and offer a second reading with 'perhaps' or 'could'.
  • Write about the writer: "Dickens presents…", "Dickens uses…".
  • A thesis answers the question in one or two sentences: how the writer presents something, and why.
  • A plan breaks the thesis into 3–4 steps, each with a topic sentence that argues and a quotation as evidence.
  • Unpack the question first (focus, command, scope), and use context only where it explains a point. Essay writing itself is practised in class.
