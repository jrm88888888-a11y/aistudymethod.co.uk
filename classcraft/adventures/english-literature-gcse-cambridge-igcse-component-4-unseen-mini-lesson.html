Paper 4: Unseen

The Unseen paper is 1 hour 15 minutes, 25 marks, 25% of the qualification. You answer one question from a choice of two: one is based on a passage of literary prose, the other on a poem or extract from a poem. Both require a critical commentary on the text printed on the paper — a text you have never seen before.

The paper

What the Unseen paper asks

1 hour 15 minutes, 25 marks. You answer one question from a choice of two — both carry equal marks.

  • One question is based on a passage of literary prose — for example an extract from a novel or short story.
  • The other is based on a poem or an extract from a poem.
  • Both require you to write a critical commentary on the text printed in the question paper, demonstrating an appreciation of the text.
  • Cambridge advises spending around 20 minutes reading the questions and planning your answer — leaving about 55 minutes to write.

The four assessment objectives (equally weighted, 25% each): AO1 show detailed knowledge of the content of literary texts, supported by reference to the text; AO2 understand the meanings of literary texts and their contexts, and explore texts beyond surface meanings; AO3 recognise and appreciate ways in which writers use language, structure and form to create and shape meanings and effects; AO4 communicate a sensitive and informed personal response.

The good news: nothing can be revised wrongly here, because there is nothing to revise. The Unseen rewards a method — and a method can be practised until it is automatic.

Method

The first ten minutes

Twenty minutes of reading and planning is a gift. Use it in stages.

  • Read once, for the whole. No pen. Just find out what happens and how it made you feel.
  • Write one sentence: this text is about... If you cannot, read again — everything else depends on this.
  • Read again with a pen. Mark the shifts: where the tone changes, where the pace changes, where the language suddenly intensifies.
  • Find the turn. Almost every unseen text has one — the moment it stops being one thing and becomes another. Your commentary is usually built around it.
  • Plan four claims. Each must be an idea about the text, with the evidence that proves it.

Trust your first reaction, then interrogate it. If a passage made you uneasy, the analytical question is simply: which words did that to me? That is where your commentary starts.

Unseen poetry

Reading an unseen poem

Work through these in order, and you will always have something to say.

  • Voice — who is speaking, to whom, and why now? What do they want, and what are they avoiding?
  • Situation — what is literally happening? Establish this before you interpret anything.
  • Form — regular or free? Does the shape on the page suggest control, or its absence?
  • Structure — where does it turn? What is the poem doing at the start that it is not doing at the end?
  • Imagery — look for patterns: a run of images from the same world (weather, machinery, childhood) is the poem thinking.
  • Sound — rhythm, rhyme, enjambment, caesura, sibilance, harshness. Read it in your head and listen.
  • The ending — poems put their weight at the end. Never leave the last line unaccounted for.

Unseen prose

Reading an unseen prose passage

Prose extracts reward exactly the same discipline, with a different toolkit.

  • Narrative voice — first or third person? How close are we to the character mind? Do we trust the narrator?
  • Characterisation — what do we learn, and how: from speech, from gesture, from what the character notices, from what they refuse to say?
  • Setting — how does the place carry mood, power or history? What is the weather doing, and to whom?
  • Pace and structure — where does the passage slow down? A writer who devotes a paragraph to a doorknob is telling you where to look.
  • Withholding — what are we not told? Suspense in prose is almost always a matter of managed ignorance.

Sentence structure is evidence. A run of long, subordinated sentences that suddenly gives way to three words is a decision — and it usually marks the moment the passage is really about.

Writing it

Structuring a commentary

A commentary is an argument, not a running description. Do not simply walk down the text saying what each line does.

A strong shape

Introduction: one or two sentences — what the text is doing, and the claim your commentary will prove.

Four or five paragraphs, each organised around an idea (the voice, the turn, the imagery pattern, the ending) rather than around a line number.

Conclusion: the last turn of the argument — what the whole text finally amounts to.

Line-by-line is the commonest weakness. It produces a list of observations with no argument. Organise by idea, and let the evidence come from wherever in the text it lives.

Practice

How to get better at unseens

The Unseen is the most trainable paper in the qualification, because it is pure method.

  • Do one a week — a poem one week, a prose extract the next. Twenty minutes of annotation is enough; you do not always need to write the essay.
  • Always write the one-sentence summary first. Candidates who misread the situation write brilliant analysis of the wrong poem.
  • Build a personal glossary of terms you can actually use — and never use one you cannot explain the effect of.
  • Read poems aloud. Half of what you need to notice is audible.

Do not panic at difficulty. An unseen text that resists you is not a disaster — the struggle is the material. Write about the ambiguity: a commentary that explores what is genuinely uncertain, and grounds that uncertainty in the words, is a sophisticated one.

Terminology

Terminology that earns its keep

Technical terms are useful only when they help you say something you could not say otherwise. Learn a small set, and learn what each one does.

  • Enjambment — meaning runs past the line end. Effect: momentum, spillage, a feeling that cannot be contained.
  • Caesura — a strong pause within the line. Effect: hesitation, a mind stopping.
  • Sibilance — repeated s sounds. Effect: can be soothing, or a hiss of menace — the context decides, and you must say which.
  • Semantic field — a cluster of words from one world. Effect: colours the whole passage.
  • Free indirect style — third-person narration coloured by a character thoughts. Effect: closeness without trust.

The rule: never write the name of a device unless the next clause explains its effect here. A term without an effect is a word, not an argument.

Openings

How to open a commentary

Do not open by naming devices, and do not open by summarising. Open with the reading you are about to prove.

Two openings

Weak: This poem uses many techniques such as metaphor, alliteration and enjambment to create effects.

Strong: The poem looks like an elegy and behaves like an accusation: every image of tenderness is placed where a reproach should be, and by the final stanza the speaker cannot tell the difference between grieving and blaming.

The strong opening does three jobs: it says what the text is doing, it takes a position, and it tells the examiner what the next 800 words will prove.

Recap

The big ideas to know

The paper: 1 hour 15 minutes · 25 marks · one question from a choice of two (one prose, one poetry)

The task: a critical commentary demonstrating an appreciation of the text

Time: spend around 20 minutes reading the questions and planning your answer

Poetry method: voice · situation · form · structure and turn · imagery · sound · ending

Prose method: narrative voice · characterisation · setting · pace and structure · what is withheld

Above all: trust the words on the page — the unseen is a test of reading, not of recall
