Topic-Based Essay (Coursework)

This is an optional route. OCR History A is option-based, but Y100 is compulsory for every candidate. It is the non-exam assessment: a 3,000–4,000 word essay worth 20% of the A-level, on a topic of your own choice — which may arise out of content studied elsewhere in the course. It carries synoptic assessment. This lesson teaches the skills; the content is yours.

Y100 is the only part of the A-level where you choose the question. It is also the part where the difference between a student of history and a historian becomes visible: independent research, primary evidence weighed for value, and a genuine engagement with what academic historians have argued.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Look out for the Interpretations flags — A-level History is judged on how well you weigh competing arguments. Press Start when you're ready.

The brief · what Y100 actually is

An independently researched enquiry

OCR describes Y100 as an opportunity to bring together the skills developed across the whole course in an independently researched enquiry of your own choosing. Three things follow, and they matter.

  • It is an essay, not a project. 3,000–4,000 words, arguing a case. Not a survey, not a report, not a narrative.
  • The topic is yours — and, unlike some boards, OCR permits it to arise out of content studied elsewhere in the course. That is a real advantage: you may go deeper into something your Unit Group 1, 2 or 3 work opened up, rather than starting from nothing.
  • It is synoptic. It should draw together historical understanding, the handling of evidence, and the evaluation of interpretations — the three things the whole A-level has been training you to do.

Twenty per cent of your grade, and the only 20% you control completely. It is written over months, not in ninety minutes, with the books open. There is no excuse for a weak one — and examiners know it.

Choosing · the question

The three tests every question must pass

Most weak coursework is weak because the question was wrong, and no amount of good writing rescues it. Test your question against three criteria before you commit.

  • 1. Is it contested? Can you name at least two academic historians who genuinely disagree about it? If the answer is only 'my textbook says', find another question. A question with no debate gives you nothing to evaluate.
  • 2. Are the sources reachable? Can you actually get at primary material — printed document collections, digitised archives, newspapers, Hansard, memoirs, visual and material sources, your local record office? A brilliant question you cannot evidence is worthless.
  • 3. Is it answerable in 4,000 words? 'What caused the First World War?' is a career. 'How far was British naval policy responsible for the deterioration of Anglo-German relations, 1898–1914?' is an essay.

Shape the question so it forces a judgement. Use the formulations the exam uses: 'To what extent…', 'How far…', or a quoted judgement followed by 'Assess the validity of this view.' A question beginning 'Describe' or 'What happened when' cannot reach the top band, because it does not require an argument.

Contested · evidenced · answerableFail any one of the three and the essay is capped before you start.

Research · working like a historian

Finding the debate and the evidence

Independent research is a method, not a mood. Here is the method.

  • Start with a serious survey, then abandon it. A good academic overview gives you the shape of the field — and, far more importantly, a bibliography. Mine the footnotes. That is how professional historians find their reading, and it is how you will find your two disagreeing historians.
  • Prefer journal articles for the historiography. They are short, they state a thesis in the first page, and they usually argue explicitly against a named opponent. That is your interpretations material, handed to you.
  • Go to the primary material early. Do not spend three months reading historians and then bolt sources on at the end. The sources should shape your argument, not decorate it.
  • Keep a full running bibliography from day one, with page numbers. Reconstructing citations at midnight in March is how honest students commit accidental plagiarism.

What counts as an academic historian? A monograph from a university press; a peer-reviewed journal article; a scholarly edition. Not a revision guide, not a school textbook, not an uncredited website. The distinction is not snobbery — it is the difference between an argument advanced with evidence and a summary written for exams.

Skill · using primary sources

Value, not 'bias'

The single most common failure in coursework is treating primary sources as illustrations rather than evidence.

  • A source is evidence for something. Ask what this particular document is good evidence for — which may not be what it appears to be about. A propaganda poster is weak evidence of public opinion and excellent evidence of what the state wanted public opinion to be.
  • Interrogate the situation, not the label. 'Written by a communist, therefore biased' earns nothing. 'Written by a Party official for internal circulation in 1937, when failure to report success could be fatal, so its production figures are almost certainly inflated — and the inflation is itself evidence of how the system worked' is history.
  • Use more than one type. Official records, private papers, press, visual and material culture, and where relevant quantitative material. Different types fail in different ways, and playing them against each other is what makes an argument robust.
  • Weigh: who made it, when, for whom, why; what they could and could not know; what they had reason to conceal; and how it sits against everything else you know.
Provenance + purpose + your own knowledgeSay what the source can tell you — and what it cannot.

Skill · using historians

Explain the disagreement, don't just report it

'Historian A says X; historian B says Y; I agree with A.' That is the coursework of a candidate who will not get an A.

  • Explain why they disagree. Usually it is one of three things: they used different evidence; they asked different questions; or they wrote in different times, when different answers were thinkable, fundable or publishable.
  • Locate the historian in time. A historian of the Soviet Union writing in 1970 could not read the archives that opened after 1991. That is a limitation, not a failing — and identifying it is a high-order skill. A historian of empire writing in 1950 worked in a culture that had not yet been forced to ask the questions that now seem obvious.
  • Test them against your own evidence. This is the move that separates the top band. Take the interpretation, take the primary material you have gathered, and ask honestly: does it hold? Where does it strain?
  • Then judge — and say what would have changed your mind. Historians who cannot name their own counter-evidence are not being rigorous; they are being lawyers.

Short quotations, always footnoted. Long block quotations from historians eat your word count and do your thinking for you. Paraphrase the argument accurately, cite the page, and spend your words on evaluating it.

Writing · the architecture

How to build 4,000 words

Structure by argument, never by source type and never by chronology.

  • Introduction (~400 words): the question; why it is genuinely contested; the historians you will engage; and your line, stated in one sentence. Do not withhold your argument for suspense — this is not a novel.
  • Body (~3,000 words): four or five sections, each built around a factor or a strand of the debate. Inside each: your own analysis, primary evidence weighed for value, and the historians engaged where they actually bear on the point.
  • Counter-argument: give the strongest case against you its best form. An essay that defeats a straw man has proved nothing; an essay that defeats a serious objection has proved a great deal.
  • Conclusion (~500 words): a substantiated judgement that answers the question actually asked, acknowledging its limits.
  • Footnotes and bibliography throughout. They demonstrate your range, validate your reading, protect you from plagiarism — and universities notice students who can do this properly.

Word count discipline: at 3,000–4,000 words you have room for roughly four substantial sections. That means choosing — three factors argued deeply beat six mentioned shallowly, every single time.

Integrity · the rules that matter

Independence, malpractice and honest work

Coursework carries an authentication requirement. Take it seriously — the penalties are severe and irreversible.

  • The work must be your own. Your teacher may explain the requirements, discuss the topic in general terms, and monitor your progress. They may not plan it for you, redraft it for you, or hand you a reading list of ready-made sources.
  • Cite everything. Every quotation, every paraphrased argument, every statistic. Not because you might be caught, but because a claim without a source is not history.
  • Extensive verbatim copying is malpractice, even from sources you have cited. Paraphrase properly — which means putting the idea into your own analytical framework, not swapping a few words.
  • Generative AI does not count as your work. Using it to write, restructure or research your essay is malpractice, and it produces confidently invented citations — which is the fastest way to be caught.

The honest truth about coursework: the students who do best are not the ones who found a clever shortcut. They are the ones who chose a question they actually wanted the answer to, and then spent four months finding it out.

Worked example · from interest to title

Watch a question get better

The difference between a weak coursework mark and a strong one is usually decided in the first fortnight.

Attempt 1 — the enthusiasm

'The Cold War.' Not a question. Not answerable. Nothing to argue.

Attempt 2 — a question, but unmanageable

'Who was to blame for the Cold War?' A real debate — but it is a whole historiographical field, and 4,000 words cannot do it.

Attempt 3 — the title that works

'To what extent was the Berlin Blockade of 1948–49 a defensive response to Western policy? Assess the validity of this view.' Now it is contested (orthodox, revisionist and post-revisionist historians answer it differently), evidenced (published diplomatic documents, the Foreign Relations of the United States series, contemporary press, memoirs), and bounded — one crisis, argued deeply.

Notice the shape of the improvement. Each version got narrower in scope and sharper in argument. That is always the direction of travel. If your title has got broader, you are going the wrong way.

Recap

The rules and the skills, in one place

The brief: 3,000–4,000 words · 20% of the A-level · your own choice of topic · synoptic · independently researched

The question: Contested by academic historians · evidenced by reachable primary sources · answerable in 4,000 words · forces a judgement

Primary sources: Weigh for value, not 'bias' — provenance, purpose, what the source cannot tell you · use more than one type

Historians: Explain why they disagree: different evidence, different questions, different eras · then test them against your evidence

Structure: Four or five analytical sections by factor · counter-argument at its strongest · substantiated judgement

Integrity: Your own work · full footnotes and bibliography · verbatim copying and AI-generated text are malpractice
