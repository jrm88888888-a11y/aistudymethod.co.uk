The perfect tense

The Perfekt is how Germans talk about the past in speech and writing — "I played", "I went". It has two parts: an auxiliary (haben or sein) in second position, and a past participle parked at the very end. This lesson builds both halves and shows exactly when to pick sein.

What it does

The everyday past tense

English has "I played" and "I have played". German mostly uses one tense — the Perfekt — for both, especially when speaking:

  • Ich habe gestern Tennis gespielt. → I played tennis yesterday.
  • Wir haben einen Film gesehen. → We watched / have watched a film.

The pattern never changes: conjugate the auxiliary for the person, and send the participle to the end. Only these two pieces do all the work.

The frame

haben (or sein) + participle at the end

Think of the sentence as a bracket: the auxiliary opens it in slot 2, the participle closes it at the end. Everything else sits inside:

subjectauxiliary… middle …participle
Ichhabegestern Pizzagegessen
Wirsindins Kinogegangen

The participle is patient: however long the middle, it waits at the very end. This "verb bracket" is one of German's signature structures.

Building participles · weak verbs

Regular: ge- … -t

For a regular (weak) verb, wrap the stem in ge- and -t:

ge- + stem + -tspielen → gespielt · machen → gemacht · kaufen → gekauft
  • spielen → gespielt (played)
  • machen → gemacht (done/made)
  • hören → gehört (heard)
  • arbeiten → gearbeitet (worked — extra -e- after -t)

Most verbs are weak, so this is your default. If the stem ends in -t or -d, add an -e- for sound: gearbeitet.

Building participles · strong verbs

Strong: ge- … -en (learn each one)

Strong verbs end in -en and often change their stem vowel. There's no shortcut — memorise the high-frequency ones:

infinitiveparticiple
essen (to eat)gegessen
trinken (to drink)getrunken
sehen (to see)gesehen
sprechen (to speak)gesprochen
lesen (to read)gelesen
nehmen (to take)genommen

Nearly all take haben: Ich habe … gegessen / getrunken / gesehen / gesprochen. These six alone cover a huge share of what you'll write about food and free time.

Choosing the auxiliary

haben or sein?

Most verbs take haben. Use sein for verbs of movement from A to B or a change of state:

haben

Most verbs, especially with an object: essen, trinken, sehen, spielen, machen.

sein

Movement / change: gehen, fahren, kommen, fliegen, bleiben, werden, sein.

  • Ich bin nach Berlin gefahren. — I travelled to Berlin.
  • Wir sind ins Kino gegangen. — We went to the cinema.

Two odd ones to memorise: bleiben (to stay) and sein (to be) also take sein: Ich bin geblieben, ich bin gewesen.

Three "no-ge- or split-ge-" groups

When ge- moves or vanishes

Three tidy exceptions to the ge- rule:

  • -ieren verbs take no ge-: telefonieren → telefoniert, studieren → studiert.
  • Inseparable prefixes (be-, ver-, er-, ent-) take no ge-: besuchen → besucht, verkaufen → verkauft.
  • Separable verbs tuck ge- in the middle: aufräumen → aufgeräumt, fernsehen → ferngesehen.

Spot the pattern: if a verb already starts with an unstressed prefix or ends in -ieren, it skips the ge-.

Recap

Your perfect-tense toolkit

The frame: auxiliary (haben/sein) in slot 2 + past participle at the end.

Regular participles: ge- + stem + -t (gespielt, gemacht).

Strong participles: ge- + … + -en, learn each (gegessen, getrunken, gesehen, gesprochen, gegangen, gefahren).

sein for movement/change: gehen, fahren, kommen, bleiben, werden, sein. Everything else takes haben.

No/split ge-: -ieren (telefoniert) and inseparable prefixes (besucht) skip ge-; separable verbs split it (aufgeräumt).
