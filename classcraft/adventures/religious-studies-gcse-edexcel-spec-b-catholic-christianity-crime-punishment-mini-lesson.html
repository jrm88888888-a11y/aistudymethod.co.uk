Mission 1

Mission 1: Can you forgive someone and still want them punished?

Make a guess: Can you forgive someone and still want them punished?

Mission 1

Four key words

  • Peace: not just the absence of war or conflict, but harmony, security and justice. Hebrew shalom and Arabic salaam mean wholeness and well-being.
  • Justice: fairness; giving each person what is due to them; putting right what is wrong.
  • Forgiveness: letting go of resentment and the wish for revenge towards someone who has wronged you.
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so that former enemies can live or work together again.
  • They link: justice is often needed for lasting peace; forgiveness opens the way to reconciliation.
Quick check

Mission 1

Christian teaching on forgiveness

  • In the Lord's Prayer: "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors." (Matthew 6:12, WEBBE)
  • "If you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." (Matthew 6:14, WEBBE)
  • Peter asks how often to forgive: "until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22, WEBBE): without limit.
  • Jesus on the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34, WEBBE)
  • God has given Christians "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18, WEBBE). Catholics receive forgiveness in the sacrament of Reconciliation.
  • Forgiveness does not cancel justice: most Christians say a forgiven offender may still rightly face punishment.
Mission 1Read the source

The peace Christ gives

"Peace I leave with you. My peace I give to you; not as the world gives, I give to you. Don't let your heart be troubled, neither let it be fearful." (John 14:27, WEBBE)

  • Jesus says this at the Last Supper, before his arrest.
  • The parable of the unforgiving servant ends: "So my heavenly Father will also do to you, if you don't each forgive your brother from your hearts" (Matthew 18:35, WEBBE).

Q1 How is Christ's peace different from the world's peace, according to John 14:27?

Show answer

It is an inner peace, free from fear, not just the end of fighting.

Q2 What does Matthew 18:35 suggest about the link between being forgiven and forgiving?

Show answer

Those who refuse to forgive others cannot expect God's forgiveness.

Mission 1

Justice and human dignity

  • "Let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24, WEBBE)
  • "What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8, WEBBE)
  • Micah 3 condemns rulers and judges who take bribes and "hate the good" (paraphrase).
  • Imago Dei: "God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE): every person has equal dignity.
  • Victims: justice matters for victims; it recognises the wrong done to them.
  • Non-religious: Humanists value justice and human rights on the basis of shared humanity and reason (e.g. the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948).
Mission 1

Peace and peacemaking for Christians

  • "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (Matthew 5:9, WEBBE)
  • In the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:1–16) Christians are also the "salt of the earth" and "light of the world": they work for peace in public.
  • Prince of Peace (Isaiah 9:6): a title Christians give Jesus.
  • At his arrest Jesus stops the violence and heals the ear of the high priest's servant (Luke 22:47–53): "he touched his ear and healed him" (Luke 22:51, WEBBE).
  • Christians differ: Quakers and some others are pacifist; most churches accept force as a last resort.
  • Peacemaking groups: Pax Christi (Catholic), Fellowship of Reconciliation, Corrymeela (Northern Ireland).
Mission 1Diversity

Peace and justice in other traditions

  • Islam: "Be ye staunch in justice, witnesses for Allah, even though it be against yourselves" (Surah 4:135, Pickthall). Believers answer the foolish with "Peace" (Surah 25:63, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: justice must be proportionate: "eye for eye, tooth for tooth" (Deuteronomy 19:21, JPS 1917), applied by courts; the rabbis read it as fair compensation, not literal injury. "Though your sins be as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (Isaiah 1:18, JPS 1917).
  • Peace comes first, even in war: "proclaim peace unto it" (Deuteronomy 20:10, JPS 1917). The world stands on "justice, truth and peace" (Pirkei Avot 1:18, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5, Müller).
  • The chapter on happiness (Dhammapada 197–208) opens: "Let us live happily then, not hating those who hate us!" (Dhammapada 197, Müller)
  • The just person leads others "not by violence, but by law and equity" (Dhammapada 256, 257, Müller).
  • The Buddhist Peace Fellowship (founded 1978, USA) applies these teachings to peace and justice work.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-harm) and shanti (peace); Gandhi's non-violent struggle for justice.
  • The thunder's "Da, Da, Da" teaches self-control, giving and compassion (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.2, paraphrase). Inner peace: "the will governed sets the soul at peace" (Bhagavad Gita 2.64–66, Arnold).
  • Sikhism: sarbat da bhala (the welfare of all); defend the oppressed; justice for all, regardless of caste or religion.
Quick check
?"Repel the evil deed with one which is better, then lo! he, between whom and thee there was enmity (will become) as though he was a bosom friend." (Surah 41:34, Pickthall) What does this verse teach?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Forgiveness should always be offered, whatever the wrong."

  • Reasons for: Jesus teaches forgiveness without limit (Matthew 18:22) and forgives from the cross; forgiveness frees the victim from bitterness; it can break cycles of revenge.
  • Also for: Buddhists teach that "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5).
  • Reasons against: some wrongs, such as genocide, seem unforgivable; many Jews teach that only the victim can forgive a wrong done to them, so no one can forgive on behalf of the murdered; forgiveness offered too easily can excuse wrong or deny victims justice.
  • Another view: forgiveness may be a goal, but it cannot be forced; it is different from excusing, forgetting or letting off punishment.
  • Humanists: forgiveness is often good for everyone, but it is a free choice, not a duty.
  • Judge it by: Is forgiveness possible for every wrong? Who has the right to forgive? Can forgiveness and justice go together?
Mission check

?A teenager smashes the window of Nadia's shop during a riot. Nadia, a Christian, says she forgives him, but she still supports his being taken to court. Her friend says: "If you really forgave him, you would drop the charges." How would most Christians respond to the friend?

Forgiveness, justice, peace and reconciliation are different things that work together. You can forgive and still want justice done.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Should prisoners be treated well, even when they have done terrible things?

Make a guess: Should prisoners be treated well, even when they have done terrible things?

Mission 2

Prison

  • Prison: taking away an offender's liberty for a set time.
  • Aims served: protection (keeps dangerous people off the streets), retribution, deterrence, and, with education and courses, reformation.
  • Criticisms: overcrowding; drugs and violence inside; prisoners may learn more crime; families suffer; many people reoffend after release.
  • Most religious believers accept prison but say prisoners must be treated with dignity and given the chance to change.
  • Humanists agree: prison should be humane and focused on rehabilitation.
Mission 2Read the source

Caring for prisoners

"I was in prison and you came to me." (Matthew 25:36)

"Remember those who are in bonds, as bound with them" (Hebrews 13:3)

  • "Open your mouth, judge righteously, and serve justice to the poor and needy." (Proverbs 31:9)
  • Surah 76:8: the righteous "feed with food the needy wretch, the orphan and the prisoner, for love of Him" (Pickthall).
  • Surah 76:1–12 promises reward to those who do this for Allah alone.

Q1 In Matthew 25, whom is a person really visiting when they visit a prisoner?

Show answer

Jesus: "I was in prison".

Q2 What does Surah 76:8 say about prisoners?

Show answer

Feeding them is a righteous act done for love of Allah.

Mission 2

Prison reformers and chaplains

  • John Howard (1726–1790), The State of the Prisons (1777): inspected prisons and campaigned for clean, safe, healthy conditions.
  • Elizabeth Fry (1780–1845), a Quaker: visited women in Newgate Prison from 1813; set up schooling, sewing work and Bible reading; campaigned for reform.
  • Prison chaplains: faith leaders in every UK prison; they support prisoners of all faiths and none, lead worship, and help with grief, guilt and release.
  • Prison Fellowship (Christian): volunteers visit, pray with prisoners and run courses such as Sycamore Tree (offenders learn about the harm done to victims).
  • Street Pastors (Christian): volunteers patrol town centres at night, helping vulnerable people and calming trouble.
  • Muslim Chaplains Association: trains and supports Muslim chaplains. Jewish Care and synagogues support offenders' families and people in need.
Mission 2Diversity

Teachings on treating offenders

  • Buddhism: "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129, Müller)
  • Dhammapada 129–140: harming the innocent brings great suffering back on the one who does it; treat others as you would be treated.
  • Hinduism: Mahabharata 13.117 (paraphrase): ahimsa, non-violence, is the highest dharma; cruelty harms the one who does it.
  • Judaism: Deuteronomy 19:15–21: no one is convicted on one witness; "At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, shall a matter be established" (19:15): a fair trial. Teshuvah means no one is beyond change.
  • Sikhism: Guru Granth Sahib 245 (your spec: the chance of reform and seeking God): offenders can turn back to God; sewa and the sangat help.
  • Islam: treat prisoners humanely (Surah 76:8); justice must be fair to all.
Mission 2

Fair trial, human rights and torture

  • Human rights protect suspects and prisoners too: the right to a fair trial (innocent until proven guilty; a lawyer; an independent judge) and, for serious cases in England and Wales, trial by jury.
  • Torture: deliberately causing severe pain to punish, to frighten, or to force information or a confession.
  • "No one shall be subjected to torture or to cruel, inhuman or degrading treatment or punishment." (Universal Declaration of Human Rights, Article 5, 1948)
  • Catholic teaching (Catechism 2297, paraphrase): torture to extract confessions, punish, frighten or satisfy hatred is "contrary to respect for the person and for human dignity". Vatican II lists torture among acts that poison society (Gaudium et Spes 27, 1965).
  • The Catechism (2298) also admits that in the past the Church did not protest against cruel practices, and now works for their abolition.
  • Other traditions: most believers oppose torture, though some argue it could be allowed to save many lives. Muslims: "Verily we have honoured the Children of Adam" (Qur'an 17:70, Pickthall). Jews: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), and Jewish law did not accept a person's confession against himself (Talmud, Sanhedrin 9b). Buddhists and Hindus: ahimsa (non-harm). Sikhs: God's light is in every person, so all deserve respect.
  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1966): do the most loving thing in each situation. Some argue it could allow torture for a greater good (e.g. to stop a bomb that would kill many). Others answer that torture can never be an act of love.
Quick check
?Police hold a suspect who may know where a bomb is hidden. An officer suggests torturing him to find out. What is the Catholic Church's teaching?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Torture can never be justified, even to save lives."

  • Reasons for: every person keeps human dignity (imago Dei; Catechism 2297); human rights law forbids it absolutely (UDHR Article 5); information gained under torture is often unreliable; it brutalises those who use it.
  • Reasons against: a utilitarian or situationist may argue that saving many lives outweighs harm to one guilty person ("the greater good"); a government has a duty to protect innocent citizens; refusing may cost many lives.
  • Replies: critics say the "ticking bomb" case is rare in reality and that, once allowed, torture spreads; defenders answer that the dignity and lives of the potential victims count too.
  • Judge it by: Are some acts always wrong (absolutist), or does it depend on results (relativist)? Who decides who is guilty?
Mission check

?A government plans to save money by letting guards beat prisoners who misbehave and by closing all prison education classes. Which response best fits the teaching of most Christian churches?

Punishment takes away liberty, not humanity: most religious traditions insist offenders keep their dignity and their chance to change.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two people do the same thing for opposite reasons. Can one be right and the other wrong?

Make a guess: Two people do the same thing for opposite reasons. Can one be right and the other wrong?

Mission 3

Good, evil, sin, crime

  • Morality: ideas of right and wrong that guide how people act.
  • Good: what is right, kind and beneficial. Evil: what is wrong, harmful or against God's will.
  • Sin: an act (or thought) against God's will or religious law. A religious idea.
  • Crime: an act against the law of the state, which the state can punish.
  • Some acts are both (murder, theft). Some sins are not crimes (most lies, greed). Some crimes are not seen as sins by everyone (breaking an unjust law in protest).
Mission 3

Intentions and actions

  • An intention is the reason or aim behind an action.
  • The same act can have good or evil intentions: a gift given out of love, or to bribe.
  • Christianity: "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7)
  • Islam: the Prophet taught that actions are judged by their intentions (niyyah) (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: actions are called skilful or unskilful, not sinful; the Buddha taught that intention (cetana) is what makes karma. "If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him" (Dhammapada 1, Müller).
  • Most traditions judge both: a good intention does not make every act right.
Mission 3Read the source

Good deeds rewarded

"for I was hungry and you gave me food to eat. I was thirsty and you gave me drink. I was a stranger and you took me in." (Matthew 25:35)

  • "you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me." (Matthew 25:40)
  • Islam: the righteous "feed with food the needy wretch, the orphan and the prisoner, for love of Him" (Qur'an 76:8, Pickthall) and are rewarded with "a Garden" (76:12).
  • Judaism: God tells Moses, "I will make all My goodness pass before thee" (Exodus 33:19, JPS 1917): goodness belongs first to God, who is gracious and merciful.
  • Hinduism: good actions in line with dharma bring good karma; evil actions bring suffering in this or a future life (Bhagavata Purana, book 4, is your named source).

Mission 3Diversity

What makes an act wrong?

  • Christianity: going against God's will shown in the Bible, the teaching of Jesus (love God and neighbour) and conscience. Salvation is by God's grace, not earned.
  • Islam: doing the will of Allah (Shari'ah) is good; disobeying it is sin. Every deed is weighed: "whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then" (Qur'an 99:7, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: keeping or breaking the mitzvot (commandments); people have free will to choose good.
  • Hinduism: acting against dharma (duty), harming others (against ahimsa, non-violence); bad karma delays moksha (liberation).
  • Sikhism: living by God's will, not ego; ahimsa and service; good karma and God's grace lead to mukti (liberation). Your named sources: Guru Granth Sahib 1, 1241.
  • Buddhism: actions are skilful (kusala) or unskilful (akusala), not "sinful"; unskilful acts come from greed, hatred and ignorance and break the Five Precepts; skilful acts bring good karma and lead towards nibbana (nirvana).
  • Humanist: wrong acts cause unnecessary harm to wellbeing; judged by reason and empathy.
Quick check
?A Buddhist says lying is unskilful rather than sinful. What is the main reason for this choice of word?
Mission 3

Can causing suffering ever be good?

  • Sometimes suffering is caused for good ends: surgery, a vaccine injection, punishment to protect society.
  • Joseph tells his brothers: "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20).
  • But: causing suffering for pleasure, revenge or profit is evil in every tradition.
Mission 3Evaluate

"It can never be good to cause suffering."

  • Reasons for: suffering is the clearest sign of harm; Buddhists and Hindus value ahimsa (non-violence); Jesus taught love of enemies; once one hurt is justified, others may follow.
  • Reasons against: surgery and medicine cause pain to heal; just punishment protects others; Romans 5:3–4: suffering can build character; some believe God brings good out of evil (Genesis 50:20).
  • Judge it by: Does the intention matter more than the result? Is the suffering chosen by the person (as in surgery) or imposed? Is there another way to reach the good?
Mission check

?Two drivers each pull a stranger from a crashed car, and both break the stranger's arm. One acted to save a life; the other wanted to be filmed as a hero. How would most religious believers judge this?

Religions judge both what we do and why we do it; good results do not make every motive good.

Mission 4: Why do people commit crimes, and is breaking the law always the same as doing wrong?

Make a guess: Why do people commit crimes, and is breaking the law always the same as doing wrong?

Mission 4

Crime and sin

  • A crime breaks the law of the state. The state punishes it (courts, fines, prison).
  • A sin breaks God's law or a religious teaching. It damages a person's relationship with God.
  • Many acts are both: murder and theft are crimes and sins ("You shall not murder", "You shall not steal": Exodus 20:13; Exodus 20:15).
  • Some sins are not crimes in the UK: e.g. lying to a friend, adultery, greed.
  • Some crimes may not be sins: e.g. breaking an unjust law out of conscience.
Mission 4

Why do people commit crime?

  • Poverty: stealing to survive or to feed a family.
  • Upbringing: parents or friends who see crime as normal; lack of care or guidance.
  • Mental health problems: an illness may affect judgement.
  • Addiction: to drugs, alcohol or gambling; crime pays for the habit, or happens under the influence.
  • Greed: wanting more than you need (fraud, theft of luxuries).
  • Hate: anger at a person or a group; it can lead to violence.
  • Opposition to an unjust law: breaking a law as a protest (civil disobedience).
Quick check
?Which act would many Christians call a sin, even though it is not a crime in the UK?
Mission 4

Views about people who break the law

  • Most religious believers: condemn the crime, not the person. Every offender is still made in God's image, or has the same worth as anyone.
  • Causes such as poverty, addiction or mental illness call for compassion and help, though most believers say people remain responsible for free choices.
  • Greed and hate: harder to excuse; they come from the person's own choices, and many faiths name them as roots of wrongdoing.
  • Unjust law: Christians are taught to respect the state ("Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities", Romans 13:1). But "We must obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29).
  • Martin Luther King (1929–1968), a Baptist minister, broke segregation laws peacefully and accepted the penalty; many Christians see this as right.
  • Christian groups: Prison Fellowship visits prisoners and runs courses; Street Pastors patrol town centres at night to help vulnerable people and calm trouble (more in Mission 2).
  • Humanists: look at causes and evidence; tackle poverty, addiction and mental illness; protest against unjust laws through peaceful, democratic means where possible.
Mission 4Read the source

Mission 4

Justice

  • Justice: fairness; each person given what is due; the law applied equally to rich and poor.
  • Why it matters for victims: their suffering is recognised; harm is put right where possible; they can feel safe.
  • Micah 3:1–12: the prophet attacks rulers "who abhor justice, and pervert all equity" (Micah 3:9), and leaders who "judge for bribes" (Micah 3:11).
  • Micah 6:8: "What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?"
  • Catholics: justice protects the dignity of every person; the Church supports victims as well as offenders.
  • Non-religious views (atheist, Humanist): justice matters because human wellbeing matters and societies need fair rules, regardless of religion. Many believers agree, and add that justice comes from God's own nature.
Mission 4Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: "Lo! Allah enjoineth justice and kindness, and giving to kinsfolk, and forbiddeth lewdness and abomination and wickedness." (Qur'an 16:90, Pickthall)
  • Surah 16:91–92 add: keep your promises and oaths. Crime is also a distraction from Allah: it turns life away from worship.
  • Surah 4:135: "Be ye staunch in justice, witnesses for Allah, even though it be against yourselves" (Pickthall): justice even against your own family, rich or poor.
  • The Muslim Chaplains Association supports Muslims in prison; Mosaic mentors young people to tackle causes of crime.
  • Judaism: justice is a duty: "Justice, justice shalt thou follow" (Deuteronomy 16:20, JPS 1917).
  • Deuteronomy 19:19–21: a false witness receives the penalty he planned for another, "so shalt thou put away the evil from the midst of thee" (JPS 1917).
  • Deuteronomy 11: keeping God's commandments brings blessing, turning from them brings harm. Jewish Care and synagogues support people and families in need, tackling causes of crime.
Mission 4Diversity

Buddhism, Hinduism, Sikhism

  • Buddhism: crime grows from the three poisons: greed, hatred, ignorance.
  • "By oneself the evil is done, by oneself one suffers" (Dhammapada 165, Müller). Dhammapada 160–165: each person is responsible for their own actions.
  • Dhammapada 256–257: a person is not just who acts "by violence", but one who leads others "by law and equity" (Müller). Prison Dharma Network / Prison Mindfulness teaches meditation in prisons.
  • Hinduism: crime breaks dharma (duty) and brings bad karma.
  • Bhagavad Gita 14.16–18 (paraphrase): rajas (passion) produces greed and tamas (darkness) produces ignorance and delusion; these qualities drive wrong action. BAPS charities work on causes such as poverty and addiction.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.2 (paraphrase): the teaching "da, da, da": self-control, giving, compassion, the virtues that make a just society.
  • Sikhism: the Five Thieves (lust, anger, greed, attachment, pride) lead people away from God and into wrongdoing.
  • Guru Granth Sahib 61 is your spec's named source on the Five Thieves; Guru Granth Sahib 274 on justice. The Sikh Welfare Awareness Team (SWAT) feeds and supports homeless and vulnerable people.
Quick check
?A Buddhist teacher says that most crimes grow from a few roots inside the person. Which three would she name?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Someone who steals because they are poor should not be punished."

  • Reasons for: poverty limits real choice. Society shares the blame for poverty. Christians: Jesus showed mercy (John 8:11). Help with the cause (food, work) may stop it happening again.
  • Reasons against: theft still harms a victim, who deserves justice. Most poor people do not steal, so it is still a choice. The commandment "You shall not steal" (Exodus 20:15) makes no exception for need. Excusing it may encourage more crime.
  • Middle positions: a lighter or different sentence (community service, support) that takes need into account.
  • Judge it by: Is the person responsible? What does the victim deserve? Which response best prevents future crime?
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two religious beliefs about people who commit crimes because of poverty.

  • Belief 1: Condemn the crime, not the person. Source: Jesus told the woman, "Neither do I condemn you … sin no more" (John 8:11). Development: so Christians offer offenders mercy and a fresh start, while still saying the act was wrong.
  • Belief 2: Society must tackle the cause. Source: "Allah enjoineth justice and kindness, and giving to kinsfolk" (Qur'an 16:90). Development: so Muslims give zakah and support projects that reduce poverty, the cause of the crime.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two developed points, plus a source of wisdom and authority.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Peace (harmony, shalom, salaam), justice (fairness), forgiveness (letting go of revenge) and reconciliation (restoring relationships) are linked but different.
  • Christians are taught to forgive without limit (Matthew 6:12–14, 18:21–35) and to act justly (Micah 6:8, Amos 5:24); human dignity rests on the image of God; forgiveness does not cancel justice.

Mission 2

  • Treatment of criminals: prison and community service; most believers want dignity and reform.
  • Matthew 25:36, Hebrews 13:3, Surah 76:8: care for prisoners. Reformers (Howard, Fry), chaplains, Prison Fellowship.
  • Street Pastors help vulnerable people in town centres at night.
  • Fair trial and freedom from torture are human rights; Catholic teaching condemns torture (Catechism 2297).

Mission 3

  • Sin is against God's will; crime is against the law of the state; some acts are both.
  • Absolute morality: some acts always wrong; relative morality: depends on the situation.
  • Most traditions judge intentions as well as actions; conscience, scripture and religious law guide believers.

Mission 4

  • Crime breaks the law of the state; sin breaks God's law. Many acts are both; some are only one.
  • Reasons for crime: poverty, upbringing, mental health, addiction, greed, hate, opposition to unjust law. Believers condemn the crime, not the person.
  • Justice matters to every tradition: Micah 3 and 6, Surah 4:135, Dhammapada 256–257, Deuteronomy 19:19–21.
