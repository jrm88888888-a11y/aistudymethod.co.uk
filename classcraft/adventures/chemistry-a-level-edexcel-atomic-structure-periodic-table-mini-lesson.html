Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Almost all the mass is in a tiny, dense nucleus of protons and neutrons.
  • Electrons surround the nucleus. The atom is about 100 000 times wider than its nucleus.

Mission 1

Reading the nuclear symbol

  • In ᴬZ X, Z = atomic (proton) number = number of protons. It fixes the element.
  • A = mass number = protons + neutrons. So neutrons = A − Z.
  • Neutral atom: electrons = Z. Positive ion: Z − charge. Negative ion: Z + charge.
  • ⁵⁶26Fe³⁺: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
  • ¹⁶8O²⁻: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons. The number of protons never changes.
  • Molecules and polyatomic ions: add up the atoms. ¹H2¹⁶O: 10 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
Calculate
aHow many neutrons are in one atom of ²⁷13Al?
bHow many electrons are in one ²⁷13Al³⁺ ion?
cHow many electrons are in one ³¹15P³⁻ ion?
dHow many electrons are in one hydroxide ion, OH⁻? (Z: H 1, O 8.)
Quick check
?An ion X³⁺ contains 23 electrons and 30 neutrons. Which nuclear symbol represents this ion? (Each symbol is written with its superscript first and its subscript second.)
Mission 1

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so different masses.
  • Chlorine-35: 17 p, 18 n. Chlorine-37: 17 p, 20 n.
  • Same electron configuration → same chemical properties.
  • Different mass → different physical properties: density, rate of diffusion.
Quick check
?Chlorine (atomic number 17) has isotopes with mass numbers 35 and 37. Pure samples of ³⁵Cl2 gas and ³⁷Cl2 gas are compared at the same temperature and pressure. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Masses on the carbon-12 scale

  • Atoms are too light to weigh in grams, so we compare them with ¹/12 of the mass of one carbon-12 atom.
  • Relative isotopic mass: the mass of an atom of an isotope compared with ¹/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • Relative atomic mass, Aᵣ: the weighted mean mass of an atom of an element compared with ¹/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
  • On this scale ¹²C is exactly 12. Relative masses have no units.
Mission 1

A weighted mean

  • Aᵣ = Σ(relative isotopic mass × % abundance) ÷ 100.
  • Boron: ¹⁰B 19.9% (10.01), ¹¹B 80.1% (11.01). (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199.2 + 881.9) ÷ 100 = 10.81.
  • Chlorine: ³⁵Cl 75.8% (34.97), ³⁷Cl 24.2% (36.97). (34.97 × 75.8 + 36.97 × 24.2) ÷ 100 = 35.45.
  • So 35.45 is an average: no single atom has it. It sits nearer 35 because ³⁵Cl is about 3 times as common.
  • Working back: call one abundance x% and the other (100 − x)%, then solve for x.
Calculate
?Lithium is 7.59% ⁶Li (relative isotopic mass 6.015) and 92.41% ⁷Li (7.016).
  • Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium to three significant figures.
Calculate
?Gallium has two isotopes, ⁶⁹Ga (relative isotopic mass 68.926) and ⁷¹Ga (70.925). Its relative atomic mass is 69.723. Calculate the percentage abundance of ⁶⁹Ga to three significant figures.
Mission 1

Where the abundances come from

  • A mass spectrometer gives the data. For an element, each peak from a 1+ ion is one isotope.
  • x-axis: m/z = the isotope's relative mass. Peak height = its abundance.
  • Neon: m/z 20 (90.48%), 21 (0.27%), 22 (9.25%).
  • Aᵣ = (19.992 × 90.48 + 20.994 × 0.27 + 21.991 × 9.25) ÷ 100 = 20.18.
  • The tallest peak is the most common isotope, not the Aᵣ.
Mission 1

Relative molecular and formula mass

  • Relative molecular mass, Mᵣ: for simple molecules. Add up the Aᵣ values.
  • Relative formula mass: the same sum, for giant structures (ionic compounds).
  • You don't need to define these, only calculate them.
  • H2O: 2(1.0) + 16.0 = 18.0. CaCO3: 40.1 + 12.0 + 3(16.0) = 100.1.
Mission check

?Bromine's relative atomic mass is 79.90, almost exactly halfway between 79 and 81. What does this show?

Same reason as chlorine: isotopes. Aᵣ is a weighted mean of whole-ish isotopic masses, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Make a guess: How can you weigh atoms that are far too small for any balance?

Mission 2

What a mass spectrometer tells you

  • It turns particles into positive ions and sorts them by mass-to-charge ratio, m/z.
  • For a 1+ ion, m/z = the ion's relative mass.
  • For an element: one peak per isotope. Peak height = relative abundance.
  • So it gives each isotope's mass and abundance, and from these the element's mean atomic mass.
  • You only need to read spectra, not explain how the machine works.
Mission 2

Aᵣ from relative peak heights

  • Heights are often scaled so the tallest peak is 100. Then they don't add up to 100.
  • Aᵣ = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Boron: m/z 10 (height 24.8), m/z 11 (height 100).
  • (10 × 24.8 + 11 × 100) ÷ (24.8 + 100) = 1348 ÷ 124.8 = 10.8.
  • Sense check: the answer lies between the smallest and largest m/z, nearer the tallest peak.
Quick check
?The mass spectrum of element E shows three peaks from singly charged atomic ions: m/z 24 (relative abundance 100), m/z 25 (relative abundance 12.7) and m/z 26 (relative abundance 13.9). What is the relative atomic mass of E, calculated from these data?
Mission 2

Chlorine gas: three molecular peaks

  • Cl2 gives Cl⁺ peaks at m/z 35 and 37 (3 : 1) and Cl2⁺ peaks at m/z 70, 72, 74.
  • ³⁵Cl is about ¾ of the atoms, ³⁷Cl about ¼. Two atoms pair up at random.
  • 70 (³⁵Cl–³⁵Cl): ¾ × ¾ = 9/16.
  • 72 (³⁵Cl–³⁷Cl): 2 × ¾ × ¼ = 6/16. The × 2: the mixed pair forms two ways.
  • 74 (³⁷Cl–³⁷Cl): ¼ × ¼ = 1/16.
  • Peak heights 9 : 6 : 1.
Quick check
?Bromine atoms are about 50% ⁷⁹Br and 50% ⁸¹Br. Which describes the Br2⁺ molecular-ion peaks in the mass spectrum of bromine?
Mission 2

Molecules: the molecular ion

  • A molecule that loses one electron is the molecular ion, M⁺.
  • The M⁺ peak is at the highest m/z (ignore a tiny peak one unit above it). Its m/z = Mᵣ.
  • The other peaks are fragments (organic analysis lesson).
Quick check
?The electron-impact mass spectrum of a liquid has peaks at m/z 15, 29, 31, 45 and 46, plus a very small peak at 47. Which compound could it be? (Aᵣ: H 1.0, C 12.0, O 16.0)
Mission check

?The mass spectrum of gallium shows two peaks from 1+ ions: m/z 69 (height 100) and m/z 71 (height 66.4).
What is the relative atomic mass of gallium given by these data?

You never weigh one atom. The spectrometer sorts ions by m/z and counts them; masses × abundances give the average. (The data-table value, 69.72, uses precise isotopic masses rather than whole-number m/z.)

Mission 3

Mission 3: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Make a guess: How much energy does it take to pull an electron out of an atom?

Mission 3

From the convergence limit to kJ mol⁻¹

  • Background: this shows where an IE value comes from. You won't be asked to calculate it.
  • Hydrogen's lines for falls to n = 1 crowd together to a convergence limit: a fall from n = ∞ to n = 1.
  • Run it backwards: the same energy removes the electron completely. That is ionisation.
  • f = 3.29 × 10¹⁵ Hz. E = hf = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.29 × 10¹⁵ = 2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J per atom.
  • × 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ = 1.31 × 10⁶ J mol⁻¹ = 1310 kJ mol⁻¹ (measured: 1312).
Mission 3

Defining ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻. Always gaseous; always endothermic (positive).
  • Second: Na⁺(g) → Na²⁺(g) + e⁻. Each further one is a successive ionisation energy.
  • Define the second too: one mole of electrons removed from one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the second ionisation energy of calcium?
Mission 3

What sets the size of an IE

  • The electron is held by its attraction to the nucleus. Stronger attraction → higher IE.
  • Nuclear charge: more protons → stronger attraction.
  • Distance (atomic radius): electron further out → weaker attraction.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel the outer electron, so it feels less of the nuclear charge.
  • Sub-shell: in the same shell, a p electron is higher in energy than an s electron, so easier to remove.
Mission 3

The pattern across the periodic table

  • Across a period: IE generally rises. Nuclear charge increases; electrons enter the same shell, with similar shielding; the radius shrinks.
  • Period 3: Na 496 → Ar 1521 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Down a group: IE falls. Each new shell puts the outer electron further away and more shielded; this outweighs the extra protons.
  • Peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at group 1: the evidence for shells.
  • Two small dips in each period (e.g. Be → B, N → O) come from sub-shells: a later mission.
Quick check
?How does the first ionisation energy generally change across Period 3 from sodium to argon, and why?
Mission 3

Group 2: IE and reactivity

  • First IE falls down Group 2: Be 900 · Mg 738 · Ca 590 · Sr 550 · Ba 503 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Each step down adds a shell: the outer s electrons are further out and more shielded. Evidence for shells.
  • Group 2 metals react by losing two electrons, so 1st + 2nd IE matter.
  • 1st + 2nd: Mg 2188 · Ca 1735 · Sr 1614 · Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Less energy needed to form M²⁺ → reactivity increases down the group.
Mission 3

Successive IEs reveal shells and the group

  • Successive IEs always rise: each electron is pulled from an ion that is more positive than the last.
  • A big jump marks the first electron taken from a shell nearer the nucleus, less shielded.
  • Magnesium: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ mol⁻¹. Jump after 2 → 2 outer electrons → Group 2.
  • Plot log10(IE) against electrons removed: the jumps stand out. For Mg they split the 12 electrons 2, 8, 2: three shells.
  • Count the electrons before the first big jump = outer electrons.
  • Three outer electrons → aluminium's group (13 in 1–18 numbering; Group 3 or III on some tables).
Quick check
?The first four successive ionisation energies of an element X are 578, 1817, 2745 and 11 577 kJ mol⁻¹. How many electrons does an atom of X have in its outer shell?
Mission check

?Element X is in Period 3.
Its first six ionisation energies are 1012, 1907, 2914, 4964, 6274 and 21 268 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement identifies X and explains the large sixth value?

How much energy? It depends on how strongly the nucleus attracts that electron: nuclear charge, distance and shielding. Removing an electron from an inner shell takes far more.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Make a guess: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Mission 4

Three factors explain every trend

  • Nuclear charge: more protons, a stronger pull on the outer electrons.
  • Distance: outer electrons in a higher shell are further away, so pulled less.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel outer ones, reducing the pull they feel.
  • Across a period: nuclear charge rises, same shell, similar shielding → stronger pull.
  • Down a group: a new shell adds distance and shielding → weaker pull, despite more protons.
Mission 4

Atomic radius

  • Across a period, atomic radius decreases. Down a group, it increases.
  • Na: 11 protons. Cl: 17 protons. Both have 10 inner electrons and outer electrons in shell 3.
  • Chlorine's six extra electrons join the same shell. Electrons in one shell shield each other poorly.
  • So the outer shell feels a much larger net pull and is drawn closer: Cl is smaller.
Mission 4

Ionic radius

  • Positive ions are smaller than their atoms: Na → Na⁺ loses the whole outer shell.
  • Negative ions are larger than their atoms: Cl → Cl⁻ has the same 17 protons but more electron repulsion.
  • Down a group, ions with the same charge get larger: Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺; F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻.
  • Isoelectronic ions share a configuration; more protons → smaller ion.
  • N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺: all 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, with 7 to 13 protons.
Quick check
?The ions Na⁺, F⁻, Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ all have the electron configuration 1s² 2s² 2p⁶. Which lists them in order of decreasing ionic radius?
Mission 4

First ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across a period it generally increases: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • Down a group it decreases: the outer electron is further away and more shielded.
  • Small dips (Mg → Al, P → S) are explained in a later lesson.
  • Recall the plot of first IE against atomic number: peaks at the noble gases, sharp drops at Li, Na, K.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energy of magnesium is lower than that of beryllium. Which explains this?
Mission 4

Electron affinity

  • Background here; you'll use it in Born–Haber cycles.
  • First electron affinity: the enthalpy change when each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms gains one electron, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • Energy is usually released: the nucleus attracts the incoming electron.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → more energy released.
  • Down a group: further away, more shielded → less energy released.
  • Many exceptions. Cl (−349 kJ mol⁻¹) releases more than F (−328 kJ mol⁻¹): fluorine's small, crowded atom repels the newcomer.
Mission 4

Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Pauling scale, no units. Fluorine is highest, 4.0.
  • Increases across a period and decreases down a group: the same three factors.
  • Period 3: Na 0.9 → Cl 3.0.
  • Not electron affinity: that is measured on isolated gaseous atoms.
Quick check
?Fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. Which explains this?
Mission 4

Melting points across Periods 2 and 3

  • Period 3: m.p. rises Na → Mg → Al, peaks at Si, then drops sharply.
  • Na, Mg, Al: giant metallic. More delocalised electrons and smaller, more highly charged ions → stronger metallic bonding → m.p. rises.
  • Si: giant covalent. Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds → highest m.p.
  • P4, S8, Cl2, Ar: simple molecular (Ar: atoms). Melting overcomes only weak London forces.
  • S8 > P4 > Cl2 > Ar: more electrons per molecule → stronger London forces.
  • Period 2 shows the same pattern: Li, Be metallic; B, C giant covalent; N2, O2, F2, Ne simple molecular.
  • Boiling temperatures follow the same pattern for the same reasons.
Quick check
?Why does silicon have the highest melting point of the Period 3 elements?
Mission 4

All the trends on one map

  • Across a period → radius decreases; IE and electronegativity increase.
  • Down a group → radius increases; IE and electronegativity decrease.
  • The pattern repeats in each period: this is periodicity.
  • Metallic character increases down a group and towards the left.
Mission check

?An argon atom has seven more electrons than a sodium atom, yet argon's first ionisation energy is about three times sodium's. Which explains this?

Same rule as chlorine vs sodium. Across a period, more protons pull the same shell in tighter.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Z = protons (fixes the element); A = protons + neutrons; ions differ only in electrons.
  • Isotopes: same protons, different neutrons. Aᵣ is their abundance-weighted mean, so it is rarely a whole number.

Mission 2

  • A mass spectrum of an element: one peak per isotope; m/z = isotopic mass; height = abundance.
  • Mean mass = Σ(m/z × height) ÷ Σ(heights).
  • Cl2: m/z 70, 72, 74 in a 9 : 6 : 1 ratio.
  • Molecules: M⁺ (highest m/z) gives Mᵣ.

Mission 3

  • First IE: 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • IE rises across a period, falls down a group. Electrons before the big jump in successive IEs = outer electrons.

Mission 4

  • Nuclear charge, distance and shielding explain radius, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → smaller atoms that hold electrons more tightly.
  • Melting points across a period follow structure: giant metallic → giant covalent → simple molecular.
