Spoken English

Speaking well is a skill you can learn — not something you're just born with. This mini-lesson shows you how to plan a presentation, use Standard English and the right register, deliver with confidence, and take part in discussion and debate.

Why spoken English matters

Speaking is a skill you build

Every day you speak to teachers, friends and family — but speaking effectively means being understood and being convincing. The KS3 National Curriculum expects you to:

  • use Standard English confidently in formal and informal situations;
  • give short speeches and presentations, expressing ideas clearly;
  • take part in formal debates and structured discussions;
  • listen and respond to others, and adapt your tone for the audience.

Good news: these are all learnable techniques — a nervous speaker who plans and practises will beat a confident one who doesn't, every time.

Planning & structuring

Give your talk a clear shape

A strong presentation has three parts. The audience can only hear your talk once, so you must signpost — tell them where you are going and where you have been.

Signposting means signalling your structure out loud: "Firstly… secondly… finally… to sum up…". It helps a listener follow you even though they can't re-read.

Model example

A weak vs a strong opening

Compare two openings for a talk on why schools should have longer breaks:

✗ Weak opening

"Um, so, I'm going to talk about, like, breaks and stuff. Breaks are good I think. Yeah."

No hook, no signpost, lots of fillers ("um", "like"), no sense of what's coming.

✓ Strong opening

"Imagine a school where students return to class focused and ready to learn. Today I'll argue that longer breaks would improve both our behaviour and our results. I'll give you three reasons."

A vivid hook, a clear line of argument, and a signpost ("three reasons") — the audience knows exactly where they're going.

Standard English

What is Standard English?

Standard English is the widely-understood form of English used in formal writing and speech — with agreed grammar and vocabulary. It is what you'd use in a job interview, a debate, or a presentation to people you don't know well.

  • Standard: "We were going to the shop." · "I did my homework." · "There weren't any left."
  • Non-standard: "We was going…" · "I done my homework." · "There weren't none left."
Big misconception: Standard English is not a "posh accent". You can speak Standard English in any accent — a Geordie, Scouse or Cornish speaker can all use perfect Standard English. Accent is how words sound; Standard English is about grammar and word choice.
Register & audience

Formal ↔ informal register

Register is how formal or informal your language is. You adapt it to your audience and purpose — chatting with a friend is different from presenting to the head teacher.

Formal doesn't mean stiff — it means avoiding slang and contractions where they'd be too casual, and choosing precise words. A debate needs a fairly formal register; a group chat with mates can be informal.

Model example

A Standard-English fix

Here's a sentence from a class debate, "fixed" into Standard English for a formal register:

✗ Before

"We ain't got no reason to change the uniform, it's well comfy and that."

✓ After (Standard English, formal)

"There is no strong reason to change the uniform: it is comfortable and practical."

What changed: the double negative ("ain't got no") became "there is no"; the slang ("well comfy", "and that") became precise, standard vocabulary. The point is the same — the register is now right for a debate.

Delivery skills

How you say it matters

Even brilliant ideas fall flat if they're mumbled. Delivery is about your voice and your body language:

  • Pace — don't rush; a slightly slower pace sounds confident and is easier to follow.
  • Pause — a short silence before a key point makes the audience listen.
  • Emphasis — stress the important words so your meaning lands.
  • Eye contact — look at your audience, not your notes or the floor.
  • Posture & gesture — stand tall; use natural hand movements, not fidgeting.

Try this: read the sentence "This. Changes. Everything." aloud with a pause after each word. Notice how the pauses add power — that's emphasis and pause working together.

Discussion & debate

Making a point that counts

In a debate or structured discussion, a good contribution isn't just an opinion — it has a point, a reason, and some evidence or an example:

Point + Reason + Evidencestate your view → say why → back it up with a fact or example
Example

Point: "Break times should be longer."

Reason: "Because students concentrate better after a proper rest."

Evidence: "A study found short breaks improved test scores in the afternoon."

This works whether you're agreeing, disagreeing or building on what someone else said.

Building & challenging

Taking turns in a debate

A discussion is a conversation, not a competition to talk loudest. You listen, then build on or politely challenge what others said:

Building on: "Building on Sam's point, I'd add that…" · Challenging politely: "I see what you mean, but have you considered…?"

Active listening

Listening is part of speaking well

Speaking effectively includes listening. You can't build on or challenge an idea you didn't really hear. Active listening means:

  • Focus on the speaker — don't plan your reply while they're still talking.
  • Show you're listening — nod, make eye contact, don't interrupt.
  • Summarise — "So you're saying that…" to check you understood.
  • Respond to what they actually said, not what you assumed.
Misconception: "The best speaker is the one who talks most." In a structured discussion the strongest contributors summarise and build on others — that only works if you listen first.

Adapting tone & register

Read the room

The same idea should be said differently depending on audience and purpose. Good speakers adapt their tone — friendly, serious, persuasive, or calm — to fit the moment.

Same message, three audiences

To a friend (informal): "Come on, the new library's actually class, you'll love it."

To the class (semi-formal, persuasive): "The new library gives us a quiet place to study — I think we should all use it."

To the head teacher (formal): "The new library has already improved study conditions, and I'd recommend extending its opening hours."

Notice the message stays the same — only the register and tone shift to suit who's listening and why you're speaking.

Performing scripts & poetry

Improvise, rehearse, perform

The curriculum also asks you to improvise, rehearse and perform play scripts and poetry for dramatic effect. Many delivery skills carry straight over:

  • Rehearsal — practising makes delivery smooth and confident.
  • Expression — vary your voice to show emotion (angry, hopeful, sad).
  • Pace and pause — a well-timed pause in a poem creates tension.
  • Volume and emphasis — stress the words that carry the meaning.

Performing a scripted line and delivering a presentation both reward the same thing: rehearsed, expressive, deliberate delivery rather than a flat read-through.

Common misconceptions

Myth-busting

  • Myth: "Standard English means a posh accent." → No — it's about grammar and word choice; you can use it in any accent.
  • Myth: "A presentation just needs good content." → No — without signposting and clear structure, listeners get lost.
  • Myth: "Speaking well means talking the most." → No — listening, summarising and building on others are part of speaking well.
  • Myth: "Being nervous means I'll be bad." → No — planning, rehearsal and pauses turn nerves into a controlled, confident delivery.

Recap

The skills to remember

Structure: introduction → main points → conclusion, with signposting.

Standard English: agreed grammar & vocabulary — not a posh accent.

Register: slide between formal and informal for your audience & purpose.

Delivery: pace, pause, emphasis, eye contact, posture.

Debate: Point + Reason + Evidence; build on and challenge politely.

Listening: focus, summarise, respond — part of speaking well.
