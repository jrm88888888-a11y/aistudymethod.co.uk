Talking about people

Family, friends and yourself come up in every speaking and writing task. To talk about people you need the pronouns in all three cases (ich–mich–mir), the possessives (mein, dein, sein…), the verb gefallen for "to like", the dative after words like mit, and the handy man ("one / you / people").

Who is doing it?

The subject pronouns

These replace the person doing the action (the nominative). Note that sie does triple duty:

ichI
duyou (one friend)
er / sie / eshe / she / it
wirwe
ihryou (a group of friends)
sie / Siethey / you (polite)

The three "sie": sie = "she", sie = "they", and Sie (always capital) = polite "you". The verb ending tells them apart: sie ist (she is) vs sie sind (they are).

Accusative pronouns

When the person is on the receiving end of the action (the direct object), use the accusative pronoun:

nominativeaccusative

Only "er" really changes shape → ihn. Ich liebe ihn (I love him), Er sieht mich (He sees me), Wir besuchen euch (We're visiting you).

Dative pronouns and gefallen

The dative marks the person to/for whom something happens. It's also the form used with "liking" verbs:

nominativedative
ichmir (to me)
dudir (to you)
er / sie / esihm / ihr / ihm
wir / ihr / sieuns / euch / ihnen

German says "to like" backwards with gefallen: the thing you like is the subject, and you go in the dative:

  • Der Film gefällt mir. — I like the film. (lit. "the film pleases to-me")
  • Die Musik gefällt ihr. — She likes the music.

The possessives

These say whose it is. Watch the pair that trips everyone up: sein = "his", ihr = "her":

meinmy
deinyour (du)
seinhis / its
ihrher / their
unserour
euer / Ihryour (ihr / polite)

sein vs ihr: sein Vater = "his father", ihr Vater = "her father". The word matches the owner, not the noun.

Endings on the possessive

They behave like "ein"

Every possessive takes the same endings as ein / kein. So in the accusative, a masculine object adds -en:

gendernominativeaccusative
masculinemein Brudermeinen Bruder
femininemeine Schwestermeine Schwester
neutermein Kindmein Kind
pluralmeine Elternmeine Eltern

Just like cluster 1: only the masculine changes in the accusative. Ich liebe meinen Bruder, but Ich liebe meine Schwester.

Dative for people

mit, bei, zu + the dative

A group of everyday prepositions always take the dative. With people this is where you'll meet the dative possessive endings -em (m/n) and -er (f):

  • Ich gehe ins Kino mit meinem Vater. — with my father (m → -em).
  • Ich wohne bei meiner Mutter. — at my mother's (f → -er).
  • Wir fahren zu unseren Freunden. — to our friends (pl → -en, +n on the noun).

Learn the dative-trigger set: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu — all followed by the dative, every time.

The handy word "man"

man is an all-purpose subject meaning "one / you / people / they" in general. It always takes the er/sie/es verb form:

  • In Deutschland spricht man Deutsch. — In Germany people speak German.
  • Hier kann man schwimmen. — You can swim here.
  • Man muss viel lernen. — One has to study a lot.

Don't confuse man (the pronoun, one n) with der Mann (a man, double n, capital). They sound alike but are different words.

Recap

Your people toolkit

Pronouns: nominative ich/du/er…, accusative mich/dich/ihn…, dative mir/dir/ihm…. Only er shifts to ihn / ihm.

gefallen = to like, backwards: the thing is the subject, the person is dative — Der Film gefällt mir.

Possessives: mein, dein, sein (his), ihr (her), unser… — take ein-endings; masculine object → meinen.

Dative prepositions: aus, bei, mit, nach, seit, von, zu → -em (m/n), -er (f).

man = one/you/people; always the er/sie/es verb form (not der Mann!).
