Mission 1

Mission 1: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Make a guess: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Mission 1

Equilibrium: where the curves cross

  • Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • At that price the market clears: every unit offered is bought, every buyer willing to pay it gets one.
  • No pressure for the price to change, unless something else changes.
  • Label it P1 and Q1 (or Pe and Qe).
  • In this model: many buyers and many sellers, none big enough to set the price alone (a competitive market).
Mission 1

Price too high: excess supply

  • At a price above Pe, quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
  • This is excess supply, or a surplus.
  • Size of the surplus = Qs − Qd at that price.
  • Signs: unsold stock piling up, discounts, "clearance" sales.
  • Only Qd is actually sold: firms cannot sell more than buyers want.
  • Market forces: sellers cut the price to shift their stock.
Mission 1

Price too low: excess demand

  • At a price below Pe, quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
  • This is excess demand, or a shortage.
  • Size of the shortage = Qd − Qs at that price.
  • Signs: queues, waiting lists, "sold out", resale above the original price.
  • Only Qs is actually bought: buyers cannot buy what is not supplied.
  • Market forces: buyers compete, so the price is bid up.
  • A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Scarcity is always present; a shortage exists only when the price is below equilibrium.

Mission 1

Market forces: movements, not shifts

  • Surplus → price falls → quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls → until Qd = Qs.
  • Shortage → price rises → quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises → until Qd = Qs.
  • These are movements along the curves. Neither curve shifts: the price is on the axis, so it cannot shift its own curve.
  • Say: "the price falls, so quantity demanded increases and quantity supplied decreases" — not: "demand increases and supply decreases".
  • Market forces drive the price to equilibrium; the competitive equilibrium clears the market.

Mission 2

A shift starts a chain

  • Something other than the good's own price changes. One curve shifts.
  • Example: incomes rise and the good is normal. Demand shifts right, D1 → D2.
  • At the old price P1 there is now excess demand (a shortage).
  • The price is bid up. Suppliers respond by moving along S.
  • New equilibrium: higher price P2, higher quantity Q2.
  • Say: "an increase in demand causes an increase in quantity supplied" — not: "an increase in supply".
Mission 2

  • Demand shifts: price and quantity move the same way.
  • Supply shifts: price and quantity move opposite ways.
  • Always ask first: which curve does the event shift? Usually only one.
  • A supply shift right means more is offered at every price, not a move "down" the graph.

Mission 2

How far? It depends on elasticity

  • Same leftward shift of supply, two demand curves:
  • Price inelastic demand (drawn steeper through the starting point): large price rise, small fall in quantity.
  • Price elastic demand (drawn flatter through the same point): small price rise, large fall in quantity.
  • Slope is only a drawing guide here: elasticity is about percentage changes.
  • The same logic applies to supply: inelastic supply turns a demand shift mainly into a price change.
  • So markets with inelastic demand and supply have volatile prices. Example: farm commodities, where a harvest cannot be changed until next season.
  • Elasticity in full: (demand) (supply).

Mission 3

Mission 3: When you would gladly pay more than the price, where does that extra value go?

Make a guess: When you would gladly pay more than the price, where does that extra value go?

Mission 3

Consumer surplus

  • Willingness to pay: the most a buyer would pay for a unit.
  • You would pay 15 for a concert ticket. It costs 10. You gain 5 that you never pay.
  • Consumer surplus = what buyers are willing to pay − what they actually pay.
  • The demand curve shows willingness to pay for each unit.
  • On a graph: the area below the demand curve and above the price, up to the quantity bought.
Mission 3

Producer surplus

  • Each seller has a minimum price they would accept for a unit: its extra cost.
  • The supply curve shows that minimum price for each unit.
  • The supply curve reflects the firm's marginal cost.
  • Producer surplus = price received − minimum price the seller would accept.
  • On a graph: the area above the supply curve and below the price, up to the quantity sold.
  • Say: "producer surplus" — not: "profit". It ignores fixed costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: four buyers would each pay at most 12, 10, 8 and 6 for one unit of a good. The market price is 8. What is the total consumer surplus in this market?
Mission 3

Worked example: measuring the areas

  • Illustrative data. Demand meets the price axis at 16. Supply meets it at 4.
  • Equilibrium: price 12, quantity 40 units a week.
  • Both areas are triangles: ½ × base × height.
  • Consumer surplus = ½ × 40 × (16 − 12) = 80.
  • Producer surplus = ½ × 40 × (12 − 4) = 160.
  • Total economic surplus = 80 + 160 = 240.
  • Height is always measured from the price line.
Mission 3

Total surplus is largest at equilibrium

  • Total economic surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.
  • Up to Qe, each unit is worth more to buyers than it costs to make: trading it adds surplus.
  • Beyond Qe, a unit costs more to make than buyers value it: trading it would reduce surplus.
  • So, with no market failure, a competitive market maximizes total surplus at equilibrium.
  • This is allocative efficiency.
  • Lost total surplus from producing the wrong quantity is a deadweight loss.
Mission 3

Worked example: deadweight loss

  • Same market (illustrative data). Output is held at 30, not 40.
  • At Q = 30, buyers value the 30th unit at 13 (demand); it costs 10 (supply).
  • The units from 30 to 40 are not traded. The lost triangle is the deadweight loss.
  • Deadweight loss = ½ × (40 − 30) × (13 − 10) = 15.
  • Total surplus falls from 240 to 225.
  • Interventions in an efficient market (taxes, price controls) create a deadweight loss like this.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a diagram, demand meets the price axis at 60 and supply meets it at 15. The market clears at a price of 30 and a quantity of 150. What is total surplus at equilibrium?
Mission 3

When demand or supply shifts

  • Build the new equilibrium first, then redraw the areas.
  • Supply rises (costs fall): price falls, quantity rises.
    • Consumer surplus rises: existing buyers pay less, and new buyers join.
    • Producer surplus: the price is lower, but costs are lower and more units sell. With a parallel shift it rises too.
  • Demand rises: price and quantity rise.
    • Producer surplus rises: a higher price on more units.
    • Consumer surplus is measured under the new demand curve. With a parallel shift it rises too, even though price rose.
  • Demand or supply falls: the reverse.
  • Say: "an increase in supply" (a shift) — not: "an extension of supply" (a movement along the curve).

  • Takeaway: a parallel rise in demand raises both surpluses; the higher price is more than offset by more units and a higher demand curve.

?Illustrative case: a city caps the number of taxi licenses. Fewer rides take place than at the free-market equilibrium, and fares rise. Compared with the free-market equilibrium, what happens to total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus)?

The extra value buyers keep is consumer surplus. Moving surplus from buyers to sellers is a transfer; trades that never happen are a deadweight loss.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Make a guess: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Mission 4

What a free market gets right

  • Each point on the demand curve shows the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit to buyers.
  • Each point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost (MC) of one more unit to sellers.
  • At competitive equilibrium, P = MB = MC.
  • Consumer surplus: what buyers would pay minus what they do pay.
  • Producer surplus: what sellers receive minus the least they would accept.
  • Consumer + producer surplus = total economic surplus.
  • If there is no market failure, equilibrium makes this total as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
Mission 4

Market equilibrium vs the social optimum

  • Buyers and sellers weigh only their private benefits and costs: market equilibrium is where MPB = MPC.
  • Society counts all benefits and costs: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods society values most; the value of the last unit equals its full cost, MSB = MSC (for a single firm: P = MC).
  • No outside effects: MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the market reaches the social optimum.
  • Outside effects: the market equilibrium and the social optimum are at different quantities.
  • Say: "the market equilibrium is not the socially optimum position" — not: "the market is not in equilibrium". A market can be in equilibrium and still be inefficient.
Quick check
?Which condition shows that a market produces the allocatively efficient quantity?
Mission 4Graph lab

Too much, too little

Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. The social optimum is Q = 50.

  • Takeaway: any quantity other than MSB = MSC creates a welfare loss, whether output is too high or too low.
Mission 4

Worked example: measuring the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q; MSC = 20 + Q (price in $, quantity in units per week).
  • Optimum: 120 − Q = 20 + Q, so Q = 50, value 70.
  • Output is 65 instead. At Q = 65: MSC = 20 + 65 = 85; MSB = 120 − 65 = 55.
  • Each extra unit from 50 to 65 costs society more than it is worth.
  • Deadweight loss = triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q = 50 to 65.
  • Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (65 − 50) × (85 − 55) = ½ × 15 × 30 = 225.
  • Total surplus at the optimum = ½ × 50 × (120 − 20) = 2,500. At 65 it falls to 2,500 − 225 = 2,275.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. A single seller restricts output to Q = 40. What is the welfare loss?
Mission 4

What market failure means

  • Market failure: the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, so the market outcome is not the social optimum.
  • The result is too much or too little of a good, and a welfare loss.
Mission 4

Sources of market failure

SourceWhat goes wrongMission
Negative externalitiesCost to third parties: overproduction
Positive externalitiesBenefit to third parties: underproduction
Public goodsFree riders: often no market at all
  • Information gaps (asymmetric information): buyers or sellers misjudge value.
  • Market power (monopoly): output restricted, price above MC.
Mission 4

Private, external, social

  • Externality: a cost or benefit to a third party, not paid for or received through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. At the margin: MSB = MPB + MEB.
  • External cost from production (e.g. pollution): MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces.
  • External benefit from consumption (e.g. vaccination): MSB above MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • Either way: a welfare (deadweight) loss triangle that points to the social optimum.
  • Externalities arise when property rights are not clearly defined, or when transaction costs of bargaining are too high for the parties to agree a deal.
Mission 4

Market power

  • A monopoly maximizes profit where MR = MC.
  • It charges the price on the demand curve at that output: P > MC.
  • So buyers value the next unit more than it costs to make, but it is not produced: underproduction.
  • Result: a welfare (deadweight) loss, even with no externalities.
  • The firm equates its private MB (MR) and MC: rational for the firm, inefficient for society.
Quick check
?In a region, a factory's smoke harms nearby farms' crops, and there is no way to charge the factory for this. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Why governments step in

  • Cost-benefit analysis: policymakers compare the benefits and costs of a policy, including external ones.
  • A policy that makes MSB = MSC removes the deadweight loss.
  • Intervention can also fail: a tax or rule set at the wrong level can create a new welfare loss.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a competitive market for bread has no externalities and settles at 1,000 loaves a day. The government pays bakers to raise output to 1,200 loaves a day. What happens to total surplus from bread?

With no market failure, equilibrium is already the efficient quantity. Moving away from it in either direction loses surplus.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd), the price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs), the price rises. Both adjust by movements along the curves.
  • Calculate Qs − Qd: positive is a surplus, negative a shortage.

Mission 2

  • A shift creates excess demand or supply at the old price; the price then changes and the market moves along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Demand shifts move price and quantity the same way; supply shifts move them opposite ways. Two shifts at once leave one result uncertain.
  • The less elastic the curves, the more the price moves.

Mission 3

  • Consumer surplus: below demand, above price. Producer surplus: above supply, below price.
  • Total economic surplus is maximized at equilibrium; less output means a deadweight loss.

Mission 4

  • At the social optimum MSB = MSC and total surplus is as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
  • Market failure (for example externalities and public goods) leaves output above or below that point, causing a welfare loss.
  • Deadweight loss = ½ × base × height between MSB and MSC.
