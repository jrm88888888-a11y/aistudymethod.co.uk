Mission 1

The Going Forth

  • At 29, after the Four Sights, Siddhattha leaves the palace: the Great Going Forth.
  • Later biographies: he leaves at night on his horse Kanthaka with Channa, glances at his sleeping wife Yasodhara and newborn son Rahula, then cuts off his hair and puts on an ascetic's robe.
  • The Buddha's own account is plainer (Majjhima Nikaya 26, paraphrase): while still young and black-haired, he shaved his head and went forth, though his mother and father wept.
  • He becomes a samana (wandering seeker), seeking what is "beyond ageing and death".
Mission 1

Teachers and austerities

  • He studies with two meditation teachers (Majjhima Nikaya 26):
  • Alara Kalama: the meditative state of "nothingness".
  • Uddaka Ramaputta: the state of "neither perception nor non-perception".
  • He masters both, but they do not end suffering: when the meditation ends, the problem returns.
  • Then about six years of extreme austerity with five companions: holding the breath, eating almost nothing.
  • His own memory (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase): his spine stood out like a string of beads; when he touched his belly he felt his backbone.
  • Austerity did not bring awakening either.
Mission 1

The Middle Way

  • He remembers a childhood moment: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he entered calm, joyful meditation (jhana) without effort (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Insight: that joy is not to be feared; it may be the path. But a starved body cannot reach it.
  • He eats again. Later lives name the giver: Sujata, who offers milk-rice.
  • His five companions leave him, thinking he has given up.
  • The Middle Way: neither sensual indulgence (the palace) nor self-torment (the austerities). Taught in his first sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).

Mission 1

Mara: the night of the Awakening

  • At Bodh Gaya, he sits under a tree (later called the Bodhi tree) and resolves not to rise until he is awake.
  • Mara ("death", "the killer"), lord of desire and death, tries to stop him: storms and armies, his daughters as temptation, then a challenge to his right to the seat.
  • Readings of Mara:
  • A real being: in traditional cosmology, a powerful deity of the realm of desire.
  • Inner forces: the Sutta Nipata lists Mara's ten "armies" as sensual desire, discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth, fear, doubt, hypocrisy, gain and fame, and pride (Sutta Nipata 3.2, paraphrase).
  • Both readings agree: awakening means overcoming craving, fear and doubt.
Mission 1

Touching the earth

  • Mara demands: who will witness your right to sit here?
  • Siddhattha says nothing. He touches the earth with the fingers of his right hand.
  • The earth itself witnesses the good deeds of his past lives (the perfections he built over many births).
  • In South-East Asian tradition the earth goddess (Thai: Phra Mae Thorani) wrings water from her hair and floods Mara's army away.
  • Bhumisparsha mudra (earth-touching gesture): seated, left hand in the lap, right hand over the right knee, fingers to the ground. One of the most common Buddha images, especially in Thailand and Myanmar.
  • Meaning: his authority comes from his own effort across lives, and the earth, not a god, is witness.
Mission 1Read the source

Three knowledges

The Buddha describes the three watches of that night (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase):

  • First watch: he recalls his past lives: names, families, joys and sufferings, over many ages.
  • Second watch: with the "divine eye" he sees beings dying and being reborn according to their karma.
  • Third watch: he knows dukkha, its cause, its ending and the path to its ending. The "taints" are destroyed: he is awake (Buddha).

The Dhammapada gives words for that moment, addressed to craving as the builder of the house of rebirth:

> "But now, maker of the tabernacle, thou hast been seen; thou shalt not make up this tabernacle again." (Dhammapada 153–154, Müller 1881)

Mission 1

Teaching career and parinibbana

  • At first he hesitates: the truth is subtle. The god Brahma Sahampati asks him to teach (Majjhima Nikaya 26).
  • Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath): first sermon to the five companions, the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11). Kondañña understands first.
  • The sangha grows: monks, then nuns (Mahapajapati Gotami), and lay followers of every class.
  • About 45 years of teaching across the Ganges plain.
  • At 80, at Kusinara, he dies: the parinibbana (final passing away, with no further rebirth).
  • Last words (Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrase): all conditioned things decay; strive on with diligence.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can Awakening be put into words?

  • Claim: The Buddha's Awakening is an experience beyond language.
  • For: the Buddha hesitated to teach because the Dhamma is subtle and beyond the reach of reasoning alone (Majjhima Nikaya 26, paraphrase).
  • For: nibbana is described mostly by what it is not (unborn, unconditioned) or by images (the house-builder seen).
  • For: William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902): mystical states have ineffability: the subject "says that it defies expression".
  • Against: the Buddha taught for 45 years; the three knowledges and the Four Noble Truths give a clear structure.
  • Against: the Pali texts call it knowledge (ñana) with content, not only a feeling. James too says mystical states are noetic (they give knowledge).
  • Judgement criteria: Is a description the same as the experience? Can words point the way even if they cannot contain it? Do Theravada and Zen answer differently?

Mission 2

Training rules, not commandments

  • Each precept is a sikkhapada: a training rule (literally a "step of training").
  • The formula is spoken in the first person: "I undertake the training rule to abstain from …" (sikkhapadam samadiyami).
  • No god gives them and no one enforces them on lay people: they are undertaken voluntarily, usually after going for refuge.
  • Breaking one is not a sin against God. It is akusala action with karmic results; the response is to recognise it, resolve again and retake the precept.
  • They describe how an awakened person naturally behaves; for everyone else they are aspirations to train towards.
Mission 2

The five precepts

  • I undertake the training rule to abstain from:
  • 1 Killing or harming living beings (panatipata).
  • 2 Taking what is not given (adinnadana).
  • 3 Sexual misconduct (kamesu micchacara).
  • 4 False speech (musavada).
  • 5 Intoxicating drinks and drugs that cause heedlessness (sura-meraya-majja-pamadatthana).
  • Lay Buddhists in Theravada countries often chant them in Pali at the temple, after the three refuges.
  • The fifth protects the other four: an intoxicated mind breaks precepts more easily.
Mission 2

The positive side

  • Each restraint has a positive quality to grow:
  • 1 not harming → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion.
  • 2 not stealing → generosity (dana).
  • 3 no sexual misconduct → contentment and faithfulness.
  • 4 no false speech → truthfulness.
  • 5 no intoxicants → mindfulness and clarity.
  • This pairing is widely taught today (e.g. the Triratna "positive precepts"; Thich Nhat Hanh's Five Mindfulness Trainings, which reword the precepts as commitments).
  • So the precepts are about building character, not only avoiding acts (kusala roots).

Mission 2

Eight and ten precepts

  • Eight precepts: kept by many lay Buddhists on uposatha (observance) days and on retreat. The third becomes celibacy, and three more are added: no food after midday; no entertainment or adornment; no high or luxurious beds.
  • Ten precepts (dasa sila): kept by novices (samaneras and samaneris), and by some lay women renunciants (e.g. dasa sil matas in Sri Lanka).
  • Ten = the eight, with entertainment and adornment as separate rules (7 and 8), plus 10: not accepting gold and silver (money).
  • Fully ordained monks (bhikkhus) keep the Patimokkha: 227 rules in the Theravada Vinaya.
Mission 2Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana

  • Theravada: the five precepts are chanted at almost every lay ceremony; monastics keep the Vinaya closely; the precepts are mainly restraints.
  • Mahayana: many keep the bodhisattva precepts, e.g. the Brahmajala Sutra (Chinese, c. 5th century CE): 10 major and 48 minor precepts. They add positive duties (to help, protect and teach others) and, in East Asia, a ban on eating meat.
  • Intention and compassion may weigh more than the letter: some Mahayana texts allow a bodhisattva to break a precept through skilful means to prevent greater harm (see Mission 7).
  • Japan: Jodo Shinshu priests have married since its founder Shinran (1173–1263); from 1872 the government permitted marriage for all clergy. Many Japanese priests marry today.
  • Peter Harvey, An Introduction to Buddhist Ethics (2000): the precepts are shared by all schools, but Mahayana places them within the bodhisattva's aim of benefiting all beings.
  • Bodhi: in Theravada the precepts are the indispensable base of the path, never to be outgrown.

Mission 3

The Middle Way

  • In his first sermon (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11) the Buddha names two extremes to avoid (paraphrase):
  • Indulgence in sense pleasures: low, worldly, unprofitable.
  • Self-mortification (harsh asceticism): painful, unprofitable.
  • Between them, the Middle Way leads to calm, insight, awakening and nirvana. The Buddha says that Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • It mirrors his life: palace luxury, then six years of harsh ascetic practice, including extreme fasting, then awakening after accepting food (see Mission 1).
  • The path is the fourth Noble Truth: magga, the way to the end of dukkha.
Mission 3

Eight factors, three trainings

  • Each factor is samma: "right" in the sense of complete, fitting, skilful; not "right" as opposed to a rule broken.
  • Wisdom (panna): 1 right view (understanding the Four Noble Truths) · 2 right intention (renunciation, goodwill, harmlessness).
  • Ethics / morality (sila): 3 right speech · 4 right action · 5 right livelihood.
  • Meditation (samadhi): 6 right effort · 7 right mindfulness · 8 right concentration.
  • The nun Dhammadinna teaches that the path is included in the three trainings, not the reverse: each factor sorts under one training (Culavedalla Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 44).
  • Practice usually begins with ethics, which steadies the mind for meditation, which ripens into wisdom; but the list opens with view.
  • The eight are not steps in a row: they are developed together, each training supporting the others.

Mission 3

Speech, action, livelihood

  • Defined in the Magga-vibhanga Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 45.8) (paraphrase):
  • Right speech: no lying, no divisive speech, no harsh speech, no idle chatter.
  • Right action: no killing, no taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct.
  • Right livelihood: giving up wrong ways of earning a living. Lay followers should not trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poison (Vanijja Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.177).
  • These overlap with the five precepts (see Mission 2). Practice today: a Buddhist may refuse work in an arms factory, avoid gossip at work, or choose a caring profession.
Mission 3

Metta, karuna, khanti

  • Metta (loving-kindness): goodwill to all beings without exception. The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8) compares it to a mother protecting her only child with her life (paraphrase).
  • Karuna (compassion): the wish that beings be free from suffering. Metta and karuna are two of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abidings"), with sympathetic joy and equanimity.
  • Khanti (patience, forbearance): not repaying harm with hatred. In the simile of the saw, even someone being cut apart by bandits who gives way to hate "is not following my teaching" (paraphrase, Kakacupama Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 21).

> "The Awakened call patience the highest penance, long-suffering the highest Nirvana" (Dhammapada 184, Müller 1881)

  • These qualities express right intention and are trained in meditation.

Mission 3Diversity

One path, different settings

  • Theravada: the path leads to arahantship. Ethics, meditation and wisdom are often taught in that order (Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga, 5th century CE, is structured as sila, samadhi, panna).
  • Mahayana: the path sits inside the bodhisattva way; the six perfections (giving, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom) map onto the three trainings. Karuna stands beside wisdom as one of the two wings of awakening.
  • Zen: the path is lived in daily tasks and zazen rather than taught as a list; Pure Land: many rely on Amida's vow more than on their own effort.
  • Contemporary: the path is taught in the West as a practical ethic for lay people; Engaged Buddhists apply right livelihood and right action to social issues.

Mission 4

Meditation on the path

  • Pali bhavana: "cultivation" or "bringing into being". Meditation is mind-training, not just sitting.
  • The Eightfold Path falls into three trainings: sila (ethics), samadhi (meditation, concentration), panna (wisdom).
  • The samadhi group: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration (the 6th, 7th and 8th factors).
  • The trainings support each other: ethics steadies the mind; a steady mind sees clearly; clear seeing deepens ethics.
Mission 4

Two kinds of meditation

  • Samatha: calm, tranquillity. The mind settles on one object and becomes still.
  • Vipassana: insight, clear seeing. The mind looks into how things really are (see Mission 5).
  • An early text pairs them (AN 2.30, paraphrase): calm develops the mind and lets go of passion; insight develops discernment and lets go of ignorance.
  • Many Theravada teachers, following Buddhaghosa: calm suppresses the defilements for a while; insight uproots them.
  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
Mission 4

The jhanas

  • Jhana (Sanskrit dhyana): a state of deep absorption reached through samatha.
  • 1st jhana: applied and sustained thought, rapture (piti), happiness (sukha), one-pointedness.
  • 2nd: thought falls away; rapture and happiness remain, with inner confidence.
  • 3rd: rapture fades; happiness and mindful equanimity remain.
  • 4th: beyond pleasure and pain; pure equanimity and mindfulness.
  • Beyond these lie four formless states (e.g. infinite space; nothingness).
  • Right concentration (the 8th factor) is defined in the suttas as the four jhanas (SN 45.8).
  • Seen as a religious experience: joy, stillness and clarity, but not the same as Nirvana.
Mission 4

Metta bhavana: loving kindness

  • Metta: loving kindness, a warm wish for the welfare of beings. Bhavana: cultivation.
  • Metta is one of the four brahmaviharas ("divine abodes"): loving kindness, compassion (karuna), sympathetic joy (mudita), equanimity (upekkha).
  • The Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8, paraphrase): as a mother protects her only child with her life, cultivate a boundless heart towards all beings.
  • Method (Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga IX): begin with oneself, then a dear person, a neutral person, a hostile person, and break down the barriers between them.
  • Many Western groups (e.g. Triratna) teach five stages: self, good friend, neutral, difficult, all beings.
  • Metta is a samatha practice that can lead to jhana; it is also the antidote to ill will.

Mission 5

Vipassana: clear seeing

  • Vipassana (Pali; Sanskrit vipashyana): insight, "seeing clearly".
  • The meditator watches experience closely: breath, sensations, feelings, thoughts.
  • Aim: to see directly the three marks of existence:
  • Anicca: everything changes; nothing stays.
  • Dukkha: nothing conditioned can fully satisfy.
  • Anatta: no fixed, lasting self is found anywhere.
  • Seeing this weakens craving and ends ignorance: insight leads towards Nirvana (nibbana).
  • Samatha steadies the mind; vipassana uses that steadiness to see (see Mission 4).
Mission 5Read the source

Foundations of mindfulness

The Satipatthana Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 10; also Digha Nikaya 22), paraphrase: a meditator is mindful of four things, seeing each arise and pass away:

  • Body (breathing, posture, parts of the body).
  • Feelings (pleasant, unpleasant, neutral).
  • Mind (e.g. a mind with or without anger).
  • Dhammas (mental qualities and teachings: the hindrances, the Four Noble Truths).

"'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity." (Dhammapada 277, Müller)

"Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Show answer

Q2 What does verse 372 say about meditation and wisdom?

Show answer

Mission 5

Right mindfulness

  • Sati: mindfulness; the word also carries the sense of remembering (keeping the Dhamma in mind).
  • Right mindfulness is the 7th factor of the Eightfold Path.
  • It runs through all practice:
  • In ethics: noticing anger before it becomes harsh speech; remembering the precepts.
  • In samatha: noticing when the mind wanders and bringing it back.
  • In vipassana: watching things arise and pass.
  • In daily life: walking meditation, eating, working with full attention.
  • "Right" matters: mindfulness joined to wisdom and good intention, not attention for any purpose.

Mission 6

What ahimsa means

  • Ahimsa: non-harming, non-violence towards all living beings. The word is shared with Hindu and Jain traditions.
  • In Buddhism it is expressed in the first precept: to abstain from killing or harming living beings (see Mission 2).
  • It covers all sentient beings: humans, animals, insects.

> "All men tremble at punishment, all men fear death; remember that you are like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 129, Müller 1881)

  • The reason given is empathy: put yourself in the other's place.
  • "Nor cause slaughter": ahimsa covers causing others to kill, not only killing yourself.
Mission 6

Why harm matters: four reasons

  • Karma: harming from hatred or greed is akusala and brings suffering to the one who harms.
  • Rebirth: all beings circle in samsara; an animal may have been a human, even one's mother, in a past life (Samyutta Nikaya 15.14–19, paraphrase).
  • Compassion (karuna) and loving-kindness (metta): the wish that all beings be well (see Mission 3).
  • Interdependence: harming others damages the web of conditions one also depends on.
  • Ahimsa is applied with intention in view: deliberate harm weighs most.
Mission 6

Animals as fellow beings

  • The animal realm is one of the six realms of rebirth; animals suffer, fear and seek happiness.
  • The Jataka tales tell of the Buddha's past lives, many as animals, often acting with great compassion.
  • The Buddha rejected animal sacrifice: in the Kutadanta Sutta (Digha Nikaya 5) he describes an ideal sacrifice in which no animals are killed (paraphrase).
  • Practices today: releasing animals (life release, common in East Asia and Tibet); caring for temple animals; avoiding pesticides where possible.
  • Hard cases: pests, animal experiments for medicine, farming. Buddhists weigh intention, need and degree of harm, and come to different conclusions.

Mission 6Diversity

Meat-eating

  • Theravada: monks eat what they are given on almsround. The Buddha allows meat that is pure in three respects: the monk has not seen, heard or suspected that the animal was killed for him (Jivaka Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 55, paraphrase).
  • Pali texts show the Buddha himself accepting meat offered as alms, e.g. pork from the householder Ugga (Anguttara Nikaya 5.44, paraphrase).
  • When Devadatta demanded that monks be made vegetarian, the Buddha refused to make it a rule (Vinaya, Cullavagga VII, paraphrase). Many Theravada lay people eat meat; some are vegetarian.
  • East Asian Mahayana: monastics are vegetarian. The Lankavatara Sutra (ch. 8) and the bodhisattva precepts of the Brahmajala Sutra forbid eating meat, since all beings have been one's relatives.
  • Tibetan: meat is widely eaten, partly because of climate. The 14th Dalai Lama (b. 1935) encourages vegetarianism and tried it himself, but health problems led doctors to advise some meat.

Mission 7

The ideal: non-violence

  • Ahimsa (see Mission 6) rules out deliberate killing of humans most of all.

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller 1881)

> "Victory breeds hatred, for the conquered is unhappy." (Dhammapada 201, Müller 1881)

  • War is driven by the three poisons: greed (land, wealth), hatred, delusion (us and them).
  • The greatest victory is over oneself: one who conquers himself is "the greatest of conquerors" (Dhammapada 103, Müller 1881).
  • Monks may not go to watch an army in the field, stay with it more than three nights, or visit its drills and parades (Vinaya, Pacittiya 48–50, paraphrase); serving royal soldiers may not be ordained without release (Mahavagga I.40, paraphrase).
Mission 7Read the source

The Yodhajiva Sutta

Samyutta Nikaya 42.3 (paraphrase):

  • Yodhajiva ("warrior"), the headman of a village of professional soldiers, tells the Buddha his teachers taught that a warrior who dies fighting is reborn among the "devas (gods) of those slain in battle". What does the Buddha say?
  • Twice the Buddha declines to answer and tells him to leave the question.
  • Asked a third time, he answers: in battle the warrior's mind is already low and misdirected, set on the thought that these beings should be struck down and destroyed.
  • If he dies like that, he is reborn not in heaven but in hell, in the hell "of those slain in battle"; if he holds the wrong view, hell or the animal womb.
  • Yodhajiva weeps: not at the Buddha's words, but because he was deceived by his teachers for so long. He takes refuge.

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 7

The Buddha's example

  • The Rohini river: the Sakyas (the Buddha's own clan) and the Koliyas are about to fight over water in a drought. The Buddha asks which is worth more, water or the warriors' blood; they make peace (Jataka and Dhammapada commentaries, paraphrase).
  • Vidudabha's army: three times the Buddha sits in thin shade on the road where the king will march against the Sakyas; three times the king turns back. The fourth time, the Buddha does not intervene: the Sakyas' karma ripens (Dhammapada commentary, paraphrase).
  • War between kings: told of a victory, the Buddha says that the killer begets a killer and the conqueror a conqueror (Samyutta Nikaya 3.15, paraphrase).
  • Lessons drawn: mediate where possible; see conflict's causes; but violence has karmic consequences on all sides.
Mission 7

Mahayana: compassion and skilful means

  • Upayakausalya Sutra (Skill in Means; Mahayana, early centuries CE) (paraphrase): a ship's captain (the Buddha in a past life) learns that a robber plans to kill 500 merchants. Out of compassion for the merchants, and for the robber, who would suffer terribly for the crime, he kills the robber and accepts the karmic cost himself.
  • Some Mahayana thinkers draw from this that killing with pure compassion, to prevent greater harm, may be justified in extreme cases.
  • Lotus Sutra, chapter 20: the bodhisattva Never Disparaging bows to everyone, saying they will become Buddhas; when people beat him with sticks and stones, he steps back and bows again (paraphrase): non-retaliation rooted in respect for every being's Buddha nature.
  • So Mahayana sources point both ways: radical non-violence, and exceptional compassionate force.
Mission 7

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD (nuclear, chemical, biological) kill indiscriminately: civilians, children, animals, and future generations through radiation and pollution.
  • So almost all Buddhist voices oppose them: they breach ahimsa on a vast scale, and no intention can be precise enough to justify them.
  • Josei Toda, second president of Soka Gakkai (Japanese Nichiren lay movement), issued a Declaration Against Nuclear Weapons in 1957; Soka Gakkai International still campaigns for abolition.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama (Nobel Peace Prize 1989) calls for the elimination of nuclear weapons and for "inner disarmament" as the root of outer disarmament; in 1987 he proposed Tibet as a zone of ahimsa.
  • A debated question: can possessing WMD as a deterrent be justified if the intention is to prevent war? Most Buddhist leaders say no; some argue it reduces suffering.

Buddhist teaching on war centres on intention: hatred in battle is akusala, and even compassionate force carries karmic weight. Buddhists differ on whether that ever permits fighting.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Siddhattha went forth, tried extreme austerity, then took the Middle Way; he taught for about 45 years and reached parinibbana at Kusinara.
  • Under the Bodhi tree he faced Mara, touched the earth as witness, and gained three knowledges: past lives, karma and rebirth, the ending of dukkha.

Mission 2

  • The precepts are training rules (sikkhapada), undertaken voluntarily, not commandments; breaking one is akusala and is met by renewed resolve.
  • Five for lay people, eight on observance days, ten (dasa sila) for novices; each restraint has a positive quality to grow.

Mission 3

  • The Middle Way avoids indulgence and self-mortification; the Buddha identifies it with the Noble Eightfold Path, the fourth Noble Truth.
  • Eight factors in three trainings: wisdom (view, intention), ethics (speech, action, livelihood), meditation (effort, mindfulness, concentration); practised together, not one by one.
  • Metta, karuna and khanti are the qualities the path cultivates; Keown argues ethics is part of the goal, not just a raft.

Mission 4

  • Meditation (bhavana) is the samadhi training of the Eightfold Path: right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • Samatha calms the mind (breath, kasinas, metta) and can lead to the jhanas; vipassana gives insight (see Mission 5).

Mission 5

  • Vipassana is insight into the three marks (anicca, dukkha, anatta); right mindfulness (sati) runs through all practice.

Mission 6

  • Ahimsa (non-harming) is the principle of the first precept: do not kill, nor cause killing (Dhammapada 129).
  • Theravada allows meat that is not seen, heard or suspected to be killed for oneself; East Asian Mahayana monastics are vegetarian; Tibetans often eat meat; the Dalai Lama encourages vegetarianism.

Mission 7

  • The ideal is non-violence: hatred ceases by love (Dhammapada 5); the Yodhajiva Sutta denies heaven to warriors who die with killing in mind.
  • The Buddha's example: mediation (Rohini river), but no protection from karma; rulers' duty to protect sits in tension with ahimsa (DN 26, Ashoka).
