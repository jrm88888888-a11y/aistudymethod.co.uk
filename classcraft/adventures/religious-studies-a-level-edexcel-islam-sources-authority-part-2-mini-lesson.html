Mission 1: If the Qur'an came in pieces over 23 years, how did it become one book?

Make a guess: If the Qur'an came in pieces over 23 years, how did it become one book?

Mission 1

What Muslims believe the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": a text meant to be heard and recited.
  • Muslims believe it is kalam Allah, the speech of God, not the words of Muhammad.
  • Revealed in Arabic, through the angel Jibril, to Muhammad, over about 23 years (610–632 CE).
  • Muhammad is the recipient and messenger, not the author.
  • It is the final revelation, confirming and completing earlier scriptures.
Mission 1

Wahy: revelation through Jibril

  • Wahy is the revelation God gives to a prophet.
  • Jibril is the medium: "for he brings down the (revelation) to thy heart by Allah's will" (Qur'an 2:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • "With it came down the spirit of Faith and Truth- To thy heart and mind, that thou mayest admonish. In the perspicuous Arabic tongue." (Qur'an 26:193–195, Yusuf Ali)
  • How it came (Bukhari, paraphrase of Aisha's report): sometimes like the ringing of a bell, the hardest form for him; sometimes the angel came as a man and spoke.
  • Companions saw him sweat on a cold day as revelation came.
Mission 1

Revealed in stages

  • "(It is) a Qur'an which We have divided (into parts from time to time), in order that thou mightest recite it to men at intervals: We have revealed it by stages." (Qur'an 17:106, Yusuf Ali)
  • Makkans asked why it did not come "all at once"; the reply: "that We may strengthen thy heart thereby" (Qur'an 25:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Reasons Muslims give: to strengthen the Prophet; to answer events as they happened (asbab al-nuzul, occasions of revelation); to make memorising easy; to bring in laws step by step.
  • And the Night of Power? Many scholars, following Ibn Abbas, say the whole Qur'an came down to the lowest heaven that night, then to Muhammad in stages.

Mission 1

The structure of the Qur'an

  • 114 surahs (chapters), each made of ayat (verses; ayah also means "sign").
  • Order: al-Fatiha first, then roughly longest to shortest. Not chronological: the first revealed (96) comes near the end.
  • Every surah but Surah 9 opens with the basmala: "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Yusuf Ali).
  • Divided into 30 juz' (parts) for reading across a month.
  • Muslims believe the order of verses was set by the Prophet as Jibril directed; each Ramadan Jibril reviewed the revealed Qur'an with him.
  • Scholars differ on whether the order of surahs came from the Prophet or from the companions.

Mission 1

Memorised and written in the Prophet's lifetime

  • Revelation was first memorised: companions who learned it all were called huffaz (singular hafiz).
  • Scribes, such as Zayd ibn Thabit, wrote verses down as they came: on palm stalks, flat stones, shoulder bones, leather and parchment.
  • Muslims believe the Prophet checked the recitation, and Jibril reviewed it with him each Ramadan.
  • At his death (632) there was no single bound copy: revelation had continued almost to the end.
Mission 1

Abu Bakr and Uthman

  • Abu Bakr (caliph 632–634): many huffaz were killed at the battle of Yamamah. Urged by Umar, he asked Zayd ibn Thabit to gather the written pieces, checked against memory and witnesses.
  • The result, suhuf (sheets), passed to Umar, then to his daughter Hafsah, a widow of the Prophet.
  • Uthman (caliph 644–656), c. 650: Muslims in distant provinces recited differently. A committee led by Zayd copied Hafsah's sheets into a standard mushaf (written copy) in the Quraysh dialect.
  • Copies went to the main cities; other copies were destroyed. Today's Qur'an follows this Uthmanic text.
  • Later, dots and vowel signs were added to make the script easier to read. Recognised readings (qira'at) remain, e.g. Hafs (most widespread) and Warsh (North and West Africa).
Mission 1Diversity

Sunni, Shi'a and academic views

  • Sunni account: as on the earlier screen; the Uthmanic text is complete and exact.
  • Shi'a accounts add that Ali compiled his own copy after the Prophet's death, in order of revelation with explanations.
  • Mainstream Twelver Shi'a scholars, e.g. Abu al-Qasim al-Khoei (1899–1992), al-Bayan fi Tafsir al-Qur'an, hold today's text is complete; some argue it was already gathered in the Prophet's lifetime. Early reports alleging missing verses are rejected by most.
  • Academic views: most historians accept a fixed text by the mid-7th century. John Wansbrough (1928–2002), Quranic Studies (1977), argued for a later formation; early manuscripts have since weakened that view.
  • In 2015 the University of Birmingham reported two Qur'an leaves radiocarbon-dated to 568–645 CE.

Mission 1Evaluate

How far is the Qur'an's compilation a human work?

  • Claim: The Qur'an as Muslims have it today is the product of human compilation.
  • For: People chose scribes, gathered sheets, standardised dialect, burned other copies, added dots and vowels.
  • For: Different readings (qira'at) and early variant manuscripts (e.g. the Sana'a palimpsest) show a history.
  • Against: The words are believed revealed by God; humans gathered, they did not compose. Memorisation by huffaz checked every copy.
  • Against: The process was early and public, under the Prophet's own scribes, within about 20 years of his death.
  • Judgement criteria: Is gathering the same as authoring? What does 15:9 promise: the words or every manuscript? What can historical evidence show about divine origin?

Mission 2: Why do Muslims who don't speak Arabic still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic?

Make a guess: Why do Muslims who don't speak Arabic still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic?

Mission 2

An Arabic Qur'an

  • "We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an, in order that ye may learn wisdom." (Qur'an 12:2, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims believe the Arabic words and sounds themselves are revealed, not only the ideas.
  • I'jaz (inimitability): Muslims hold no human could produce its like: "they could not produce the like thereof" (Qur'an 17:88, Yusuf Ali).
  • So Muslims everywhere learn to recite in Arabic, even without understanding every word. Salah is always in Arabic.
  • Arabic unites a worldwide ummah: the same words in Jakarta, Lagos and Leeds.
Mission 2

Translation as interpretation

  • Every translation must choose between meanings, so every translation is an interpretation.
  • Example: khalifah (2:30) can be rendered "vicegerent", "successor" or "steward": each suggests a different role for humans.
  • So many Muslims call an English version a translation of the meaning. Pickthall titled his The Meaning of the Glorious Koran (1930).
  • Translations are valued for understanding and for sharing the message, but they are not used in salah and are not "the Qur'an" itself.
  • Trustworthy interpretation: tafsir (commentary) by qualified scholars, using the Arabic, the hadith and the context of each revelation.

Mission 2

Treating the mushaf with respect

  • "Which none shall touch but those who are clean" (Qur'an 56:79, Yusuf Ali). Most scholars take this to mean wudu (ritual washing) before touching an Arabic copy.
  • Some read 56:79 as describing angels and the heavenly book; they still urge respect.
  • A copy is kept high, often wrapped, nothing placed on top; read on a stand (rehal); never put on the floor or taken into a toilet.
  • Worn-out copies are buried or burned respectfully, never thrown away.
  • Many treat translations with respect but with fewer rules, as they are not the Arabic text.
Mission 2

Recited, memorised, remembered

  • Tilawah (recitation) with tajwid: precise rules of pronunciation and rhythm. A skilled reciter is a qari.
  • Hifz: memorising the whole Qur'an; a hafiz is honoured in family and mosque.
  • Al-Fatiha is recited in every rak'ah (unit) of salah.
  • Ramadan: "the (month) in which was sent down the Qur'an" (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali). Many read the whole Qur'an across the month; in many mosques it is recited in full in the night prayers (tarawih).
  • Laylat al-Qadr: in the last ten nights, Muslims pray through the night; some stay in the mosque (i'tikaf).
  • Recited at births, weddings, sickness and funerals; written as calligraphy in mosques and homes.

Surah al-Fatiha

> "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. Praise be to Allah, the Cherisher and Sustainer of the worlds; Most Gracious, Most Merciful; Master of the Day of Judgment. Thee do we worship, and Thine aid we seek. Show us the straight way," (Qur'an 1:1–6, Yusuf Ali)

  • Verse 7 asks for the way of those whom God has blessed, not of those who earn wrath or go astray.
  • Called Umm al-Kitab ("mother of the Book"): the whole Qur'an in miniature.
  • Declaration of Allah: merciful, Lord of all worlds, Judge.
  • Submission: the believer worships God alone and depends on Him alone.

Mission 2

Al-Baqarah: the Qur'an in summary

  • Surah 2, al-Baqarah ("the Cow"): the longest surah (286 verses), Madinan.
  • Opens: "This is the Book; in it is guidance sure, without doubt, to those who fear Allah;" (Qur'an 2:2, Yusuf Ali)
  • Three kinds of people: believers, those who reject faith, hypocrites (2:1–20).
  • Prophets: Adam as khalifah (2:30); Musa and the Children of Israel; Ibrahim and Isma'il building the Ka'bah (2:127).
  • Practice: qiblah (2:144), fasting (2:183), Hajj, charity, family law, debts.
  • Belief: the Throne Verse (2:255); "no compulsion in religion" (2:256).
  • Submission: it ends with believers saying: "We hear, and we obey" (Qur'an 2:285, Yusuf Ali).
  • Anthology extract 1 is Yusuf Ali's translation of Surah 1 and the opening of Surah 2.

Mission 2

The first source of authority

  • Muslims hold the Qur'an is the first and highest authority for belief, worship, law and morals.
  • It is the first source of shari'a; everything else is tested against it (see Mission 3).
  • Its words are God's own speech; to obey it is to submit to God.
  • Created or uncreated? Sunni theology (Ash'ari, Hanbali) holds the Qur'an is God's uncreated speech; the Mu'tazila, and most Twelver Shi'a theologians, hold it was created. (The caliph al-Ma'mun tried to impose the "created" view in the mihna, 833.)
  • Both sides agree it is fully God's word and fully authoritative.
Mission 2Diversity

Nasr and Malik on the Qur'an's authority

  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), Ideals and Realities of Islam (1966).
  • Nasr (paraphrase): the Qur'an is the central sacred reality of Islam; its Arabic sounds, letters and recitation carry divine presence (baraka). Its role is closer to Christ's in Christianity than to the Bible's. Its authority is complete and timeless; traditional interpretation guards it.
  • Fazlur Rahman Malik (1919–1988), Islam (1966), Major Themes of the Qur'an (1980), Islam and Modernity (1982).
  • Rahman (paraphrase): the Qur'an is wholly God's word and, as it came through the Prophet's heart, also his word in a sense. Its authority lies above all in its moral principles: a "double movement" from specific rulings back to their purpose in context, then forward to the present.
  • Contrast: Nasr stresses the sacred text as given; Rahman stresses principles applied today. Both accept full authority.
Mission 2

Qur'an, Sunnah and Hadith

  • "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger" (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • Sunnah: the Prophet's way: what he said, did and approved. It explains the Qur'an.
  • Example: "be steadfast in prayer" (Qur'an 2:43, Yusuf Ali) does not say how; the times, movements and number of rak'ahs come from the Sunnah.
  • Hadith: the reports through which the Sunnah is known.
  • Order: Qur'an first; mainstream scholars hold that sound hadith explain the Qur'an and do not contradict it.
  • Diversity: Shi'a Muslims also receive the teachings of the Imams through their hadith; a small minority ("Qur'anists") reject hadith as a source of law.

Mission 2Evaluate

Can the Qur'an ever be translated accurately?

  • Claim: The message of the Qur'an can be translated accurately.
  • For: Meanings can be carried across: millions of Muslims understand their faith through translations.
  • For: Careful translations with notes (e.g. Yusuf Ali) give context and alternatives.
  • Against: Arabic words carry many meanings at once (e.g. khalifah); translators must choose.
  • Against: Sound, rhythm and rhyme are part of the revelation (i'jaz); they are lost in any other language.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "the message" the meaning or the words? Accurate enough for what: understanding, law, or worship?
  • For relevance today, compare Nasr (timeless text) and Rahman (principles applied anew).

Mission 3

Shari'a and fiqh

  • Shari'a: literally "the path to the water": God's way for human life.
  • "Then We put thee on the (right) Way of Religion: so follow thou that (Way), and follow not the desires of those who know not." (Qur'an 45:18, Yusuf Ali) The Arabic word is shari'ah.
  • It covers worship (prayer, fasting) and dealings (family, trade, food, crime): much more than criminal law.
  • Fiqh ("deep understanding"): the human effort to understand and apply the shari'a. Scholars can err; the shari'a, as God's will, cannot.
  • So Muslims distinguish the ideal (God's shari'a) from law in practice (the jurists' fiqh).
  • Edexcel spelling: Shari'ah.
Mission 3

Scriptural sources: Qur'an and Sunnah

  • Qur'an: the first source, God's word. Some rulings are clear (no pork, 5:3; fasting in Ramadan, 2:185); many verses give principles (justice, 4:58; consultation, 42:38).
  • Sunnah: the Prophet's example, known through hadith. It explains and adds detail: the Qur'an commands prayer; the Sunnah shows how to pray.
  • "So take what the Messenger assigns to you, and deny yourselves that which he withholds from you." (Qur'an 59:7, Yusuf Ali)
  • Hadith are graded by chain and text: sahih (sound), hasan (good), da'if (weak).
  • Shi'a Muslims include the teachings of the Imams within the Sunnah.
Mission 3

Non-scriptural sources

  • Ijma (consensus): agreement of the qualified scholars of an age on a ruling. Once reached, Sunnis treat it as binding ("my community will not agree on error", Ibn Majah; paraphrase).
  • Qiyas (analogy): extending a ruling from a case in the texts to a new case that shares its cause ('illah).
  • 'Urf (custom): local practice accepted where it does not contradict the texts (e.g. customary amounts of dowry). Malik also gave weight to the practice ('amal) of Madinah.
  • Other tools: istihsan (juristic preference, Hanafi), maslahah (public interest, Maliki).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists: Qur'an, Sunnah (with the Imams), ijma (valid if it reflects the Imam's view) and 'aql (reason); they reject qiyas.
Mission 3Read the source

From wine to every intoxicant

> "They ask thee concerning wine and gambling. Say: 'In them is great sin, and some profit, for men; but the sin is greater than the profit.'" (Qur'an 2:219, Yusuf Ali)

> "O ye who believe! Intoxicants and gambling, (dedication of) stones, and (divination by) arrows, are an abomination,- of Satan's handwork: eschew such (abomination), that ye may prosper." (Qur'an 5:90, Yusuf Ali)

  • Many scholars see stages: 2:219 (more harm than benefit), 4:43 (do not pray intoxicated), 5:90 (avoid completely).
  • A hadith: "Every intoxicant is khamr, and every khamr is forbidden" (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Qiyas: original case: wine · cause ('illah): intoxication · new case: cannabis, cocaine, other drugs · ruling: haram.

Mission 3

Al-Shafi'i: a method for law

  • Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820), al-Risala ("The Epistle").
  • Before him, regional scholars (Kufa, Madinah) mixed hadith, local practice and personal opinion (ra'y) in different ways.
  • He set out an order of sources: Qur'an → Sunnah → ijma → qiyas.
  • A sound hadith binds, even if reported by a single chain, because the Sunnah explains the Qur'an (2:151: "Scripture and Wisdom", Yusuf Ali).
  • He limited free opinion and istihsan: reasoning must be tied to a text through qiyas.
  • So he is called the founder of usul al-fiqh (the roots, or method, of jurisprudence).
Mission 3

The law schools (madhhabs)

  • Hanafi: Abu Hanifa (699–767), Kufa; wide use of reasoning and istihsan; Turkey, Central and South Asia (most British Muslims of South Asian heritage).
  • Maliki: Malik ibn Anas (c. 711–795), Madinah; al-Muwatta; the practice of Madinah; North and West Africa.
  • Shafi'i: al-Shafi'i; Egypt, East Africa, South-East Asia.
  • Hanbali: Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), Baghdad; text first, cautious about reasoning; Saudi Arabia, Qatar.
  • Ja'fari: Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), the sixth Imam; Twelver Shi'a; Iran, Iraq.
  • They agree on essentials; they differ in detail (e.g. hand position in prayer). Each recognises the others as legitimate.
  • Taqlid: ordinary Muslims follow a school or scholar; ijtihad: qualified jurists derive rulings within its framework.
Mission 3

The five rulings (ahkam)

  • Fard / wajib (obligatory): rewarded if done, sin if left: salah, fasting in Ramadan.
  • Mustahabb / mandub (recommended): rewarded if done, no sin if left: extra prayers, sadaqah.
  • Mubah (permitted): neutral: most foods, ordinary work.
  • Makruh (disliked): better avoided, no sin if done: e.g. wasting water in wudu; coming to the mosque after eating raw garlic.
  • Haram (forbidden): sin if done: alcohol and gambling (5:90), pork (5:3).
  • Halal: lawful, everything not haram. Modesty: lower the gaze and guard modesty (24:30–31); dress rules derive from these verses and the Sunnah.
  • Killing and eating animals: halal meat is slaughtered with God's name (6:121), a swift cut and draining of blood; scholars differ on pre-stunning.
  • Makruh applied: smoking was long called makruh; as its harm became known, many scholars ruled it haram (harm to self is forbidden).
  • Edexcel: the "Akham (ahkam) pentad".

Mission 3

Interpretation and authority beyond the texts

  • Ulama (scholars), muftis issuing fatwas (non-binding legal opinions), and the schools carry authority beyond the texts.
  • Was the "gate of ijtihad" closed after about the 10th century? A common Sunni view said yes; Wael Hallaq (b. 1955) argues jurists never stopped independent reasoning. Twelver Shi'a always kept ijtihad open.
  • S. H. Nasr (b. 1933), Ideals and Realities of Islam (1966): the shari'ah is divine, the ideal pattern for every human act; its principles do not change, though applications do (paraphrase).
  • Fazlur Rahman Malik (1919–1988), Islam and Modernity (1982): a "double movement": from the Qur'an's specific rulings back to their moral aims, then forward to apply those aims today (paraphrase).
Mission 3Evaluate

Divine law or human interpretation?

  • Claim: Shari'a as Muslims practise it is more human interpretation than divine law.
  • For: fiqh is human; the schools disagree; rulings change (smoking); applying old rulings to new cases needs interpretation.
  • For: ijma and qiyas are human methods; states have codified and reshaped the law.
  • Against: the sources and principles are divine; scholars discover rulings, they do not invent them.
  • Rahman's double movement supports "for"; Nasr's view supports "against".
  • Against: the core (prayer, fasting, the main prohibitions) is agreed across all schools.
  • A further view: the limits of human understanding can lead to tolerance of diversity within Muslim practice.
  • Criteria: Where is the line between shari'a and fiqh? Does diversity weaken authority or show its depth?

Where the texts are silent, scholars reason from them: the method, not a new revelation, reaches new cases.

Mission 4

Why five categories?

  • Muslims believe the shari'a covers all of life: worship, family, trade, food, manners.
  • As fiqh developed, jurists needed a clear way to say how strongly God wants or forbids an act.
  • The answer: al-ahkam al-khamsa, the five rulings, a scale from "must" to "must not".
  • The scale guides intention and reward, not only law courts.
  • Edexcel calls this the ahkam pentad; spelling Shari'ah.
  • S. H. Nasr stresses the Shari'ah as the divine pattern for every act of life; Fazlur Rahman (F. R. Malik) urged reading rulings in the light of the Qur'an's moral purposes (both paraphrased).
Mission 4

The scale of five

  • Fard (also wajib): obligatory. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is a sin.
  • Mustahab (also mandub, sunna): recommended. Doing it is rewarded; leaving it is no sin.
  • Mubah: permitted and neutral. No reward, no sin.
  • Makruh: disliked. Avoiding it is rewarded; doing it is no sin, but it is discouraged.
  • Haram: forbidden. Doing it is a sin; avoiding it for God's sake is rewarded.
  • Halal means "permitted": in a broad sense everything not haram; Eduqas uses halal for the middle category.
Mission 4Read the source

Do not invent the haram

> "O ye who believe! make not unlawful the good things which Allah hath made lawful for you, but commit no excess" (Qur'an 5:87, Yusuf Ali)

> "But say not - for any false thing that your tongues may put forth,- 'This is lawful, and this is forbidden,' so as to ascribe false things to Allah." (Qur'an 16:116, Yusuf Ali)

  • Jurists drew a principle: in everyday matters, the default is permission; a ban needs evidence.

Show answer

Mission 4

Fard and mustahab: examples

  • Fard: the five daily salah; fasting in Ramadan; zakah; Hajj if able; honesty; caring for parents.
  • Fard 'ayn: on each person (salah). Fard kifaya: on the community; if some do it, the rest are free (funeral prayer, training doctors).
  • Mustahab: extra (sunna) prayers; fasting on Mondays and Thursdays; sadaqah; saying bismillah before eating; using the miswak (tooth-stick).
  • Greeting with salam is mustahab; replying to it is obligatory.
  • These categories show a Muslim where the floor is and how to go above it.
Mission 4

Mubah, makruh, haram: examples

  • Mubah: most daily choices: which halal food, which job, what colour to wear.
  • Makruh: wasting water in wudu; eating raw garlic or onion before going to the mosque (Bukhari; paraphrase); many classical jurists put smoking here.
  • Divorce is lawful but discouraged: reported as the most hated of permitted things to God (Abu Dawud; paraphrase; chain debated).
  • Haram: alcohol and gambling (5:90); pork, blood and carrion (2:173); theft; riba; lying; murder.
  • Killing animals for food: halal if slaughtered in God's name with a swift cut to the throat and the blood drained; whether pre-stunning is acceptable is debated.
  • Modesty: covering the body as required is fard; immodest display is haram; how much must be covered, and whether a face veil is required, varies among schools and scholars.
Mission 4Diversity

Same scale, different placements

  • Hanafi jurists split the top: fard (proved by certain evidence) and wajib (by less certain evidence), e.g. the witr prayer is wajib for Hanafis, strongly recommended for other schools.
  • Hanafis also split makruh tahrimi (close to haram) and makruh tanzihi (mildly disliked).
  • Twelver Shi'a jurists use the same five: wajib, mustahabb, mubah, makruh, haram.
  • The same act may sit in different places: e.g. prawns are halal for most schools, makruh or forbidden for many Hanafis.
  • Each believer follows their school or, for Twelver Shi'a, a senior scholar (marja') (taqlid: following a qualified scholar or school).

The five categories let Muslims tell a duty from a recommendation, and a sin from a dislike, so they can aim higher without fear of losing everything.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Qur'an: God's speech (kalam Allah), revealed through Jibril (wahy) to Muhammad in Arabic, in stages, 610–632 (2:97, 17:106).
  • Makkan surahs: short, tawhid and judgement; Madinan: longer, law and community. 114 surahs, al-Fatiha first, then roughly by length.
  • Memorised and written in the Prophet's life; gathered under Abu Bakr (Zayd); standardised under Uthman (c. 650); "We will assuredly guard it" (15:9).

Mission 2

  • Muslims believe the Arabic words are revealed (12:2) and inimitable (17:88); translation is interpretation, so salah stays in Arabic.
  • Treated with respect (56:79: wudu, kept high, never on the floor); recited with tajwid, memorised by huffaz, read through in Ramadan.
  • Al-Fatiha declares God and submission; al-Baqarah summarises guidance, belief and practice. Nasr: sacred text; Rahman (Malik): moral principles.

Mission 3

  • Shari'a is God's way; fiqh is the human understanding of it. Sources: Qur'an and Sunnah, then ijma, qiyas and custom (Twelvers: reason instead of qiyas).
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820) set the order of sources; five schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali, Ja'fari) apply them.
  • Every act falls under five rulings: obligatory, recommended, permitted, disliked (makruh), forbidden (haram).

Mission 4

  • Fard, mustahab, mubah (halal), makruh, haram: a scale of how strongly God wills an act, linked to reward and sin.
  • Most of life is permitted; bans need evidence (5:87, 16:116); necessity and intention matter.
  • Schools differ at the edges (Hanafi fard/wajib; prawns; smoking); Shi'a fiqh uses the same five.
