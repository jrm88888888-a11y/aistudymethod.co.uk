Mission 1

Mission 1: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Make a guess: Christians call the Bible "the Word of God", but people wrote it. How can it be both?

Mission 1

Revelation: God makes himself known

  • Revelation: Christians believe God shows people what they could not find out alone: who God is and what God wants.
  • General revelation: through the world, nature and conscience: open to everyone.
  • Special revelation: through particular events and people: the prophets, the scriptures, and above all Jesus, the Word made flesh (John 1:14).
  • Christians believe the Bible records and passes on God's revelation: it is the Word of God.
Mission 1

Inspiration

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Inspired (Greek theopneustos, "God-breathed"): God guided the human writers.
  • Most Christians believe the writers used their own language, style and culture, while God worked through them.
  • So, for most Christians, the Bible is both fully human words and the Word of God.
  • Christians differ on what follows: is every detail without error, or the message God intended?

Mission 1Diversity

Three views of the Bible's authority

  • The literal Word of God: every word is from God and true in every detail, including history and science (e.g. creation in six 24-hour days). Held by fundamentalist Christians and some evangelicals.
  • The inspired (revealed) Word of God: God's message comes through human writers; some parts are symbolic or reflect their time. Held by most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants.
  • A source of guidance and teaching: written by people inspired by their faith in God; a wise and treasured guide, open to reinterpretation. Held by many liberal Christians.
  • Implications: views on creation and science, on women's roles in church, on ethical questions.
Quick check
?A Christian says: "Genesis is God's message, but the six days are a poetic way of saying God made everything." Which view of the Bible is she most likely to hold?
Mission 1

The Bible among other authorities

  • Many Protestants: sola scriptura: scripture alone is the final authority; preaching explains it.
  • Catholics: Scripture and Tradition together form one sacred deposit of God's Word, interpreted by the Magisterium (Dei Verbum 10).
  • Orthodox: scripture lives within Holy Tradition: the Creeds, the councils, the liturgy.
  • Anglicans often speak of scripture, tradition and reason; Methodists add experience.
  • Quakers: the Inner Light (God's Spirit within) guides; the Bible is valued but tested by the Spirit.
  • Its meaning, purpose and truth: Christians agree the Bible's purpose is to show God and the way of salvation; they differ on whether its truth includes every historical detail.
Mission 1

The Bible in daily life

  • Formal worship: set readings (a lectionary) at Mass or Communion; psalms sung; a sermon explains the readings.
  • Informal worship: Bible study groups, house churches, preaching in free churches.
  • Home and private worship: daily reading and prayer. "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105)
  • Bible reading aids: daily reading notes, reading plans and apps, commentaries.
  • Fellowship meetings: small groups meeting in homes to read, discuss and pray.
  • Comfort and guidance: e.g. Psalm 23 at funerals; looking to the Bible when making decisions.
Quick check
?Josh uses an app that gives him a short Bible passage each morning with a few lines explaining it and a prayer. What is he using?
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Bible is the only authority Christians need."

  • Reasons for: the Bible is "God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:16). Protestant sola scriptura: Church teaching and tradition can err, but God's Word does not. Every Christian can read it for themselves.
  • Reasons against: the Bible needs interpreting, and Christians disagree on what it means. The Church chose which books belong in it. Catholics: Scripture, Tradition and Magisterium belong together. Modern questions (e.g. IVF, AI) are not addressed directly; reason and conscience are needed.
  • Another view: many Protestants hold scripture as the highest authority, but still value creeds, reason and experience.
  • Judge it by: Can a text interpret itself? Who decides between interpretations? How do Christians handle questions the Bible does not mention?
Mission check

?An architect plans a cathedral. Over many years, different stonemasons carve it, each in their own style. The finished building still follows the architect's plan. Which Christian belief about the Bible does this picture best?

Most Christians believe the Bible is God's Word through human words. They differ on how literally to read it and on who interprets it.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can a worship song with drums and guitars be as holy as an ancient chant?

Make a guess: Can a worship song with drums and guitars be as holy as an ancient chant?

Mission 2

What counts as "contemporary"?

  • Contemporary worship music: songs written from the 1960s onwards in current musical styles.
  • Typical features: simple, repeated choruses; everyday language; guitars, keyboard, drums; words on a screen; a worship band leading.
  • Worship songs often speak directly to God ("you", "I", "we").
  • Modern hymns keep verses and doctrine but use modern tunes, e.g. Keith Getty and Stuart Townend, "In Christ Alone" (2001).
  • Also contemporary, but quiet: Taizé chants from the ecumenical Taizé community in France: short lines repeated many times as a prayer.
Mission 2

Graham Kendrick

  • Graham Kendrick (born 1950): English songwriter, from a Baptist background; one of the pioneers of British worship songs.
  • "The Servant King" (1983), also known as "From heaven you came": Jesus leaves heaven to serve, and suffers in Gethsemane; it echoes Philippians 2:6–8.
  • "Meekness and Majesty" (1986): Jesus fully God and fully human.
  • "Shine, Jesus, Shine" (1987): Christ as light of the world, asked to fill the nation.
  • Co-founded March for Jesus (1987): Christians singing in city streets as public witness.
  • His songs are in Catholic and Protestant hymn books and sung in schools.
Mission 2

Casting Crowns

  • Casting Crowns: American Christian rock band formed in 1999 by Mark Hall, a youth pastor, with students from his church.
  • Hall kept working as a youth pastor while the band toured: the songs come from church life.
  • "Who Am I": wonder that the God of the universe knows and loves each person (compare Psalm 8:4).
  • "Praise You in This Storm": trusting and praising God in suffering, even when prayers seem unanswered.
  • "If We Are the Body": challenges churches that ignore outsiders; Christians as the Body of Christ must welcome them.
  • Heard more on Christian radio, at concerts and at youth events than sung at Mass.

Mission 2

Charismatic worship and tongues

  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship stresses the gifts (charismata) of the Holy Spirit, as at Pentecost (Acts 2:4).
  • Long "times of worship": singing flows from song to song; people raise hands, clap, pray aloud.
  • Speaking in tongues (glossolalia): praying or singing in a language the speaker has not learned (or a language of prayer no one speaks), believed to be given by the Spirit. Paul: "I will sing with the spirit, and I will sing with the understanding also" (1 Corinthians 14:15).
  • In Ireland: the Elim Pentecostal Church began in Monaghan in 1915; many independent and charismatic churches today.
  • Many Presbyterians welcome lively music but are cautious about tongues in public worship: "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Catholics too: the Catholic Charismatic Renewal began in 1967 and has prayer groups in many parishes.
Mission 2

Where and when it is used

  • Protestant churches: in many evangelical, charismatic and Pentecostal churches, the band-led time of worship is the heart of the service.
  • Catholic parishes: often at youth and school Masses, Confirmation, prayer groups; worship songs sit alongside hymns and the Responsorial Psalm.
  • Other settings: youth festivals and large Catholic youth events, Christian concerts and radio, school assemblies, street marches, streaming at home.
  • Why: to reach young people, to let people pray in their own musical language, to share faith with outsiders.
Mission 2Diversity

The debates

  • For contemporary music: everyday language people understand; emotional and personal; draws young people and newcomers; the Bible calls for "a new song" (Psalm 96:1); early Christians wrote new hymns.
  • Against (or cautious): some lyrics are thin on doctrine, or focus on "I" more than "we"; repetition can feel shallow; can feel like a concert, with the band performing; can push out the Church's older treasury of music.
  • Orthodox churches keep unaccompanied singing; Quakers often use no music at all.
  • Most churches mix old and new.
Quick check
?Which is a criticism some Christians make of contemporary worship songs?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Contemporary worship songs are better than traditional hymns for worship today."

  • Reasons for: understood by everyone; personal and heartfelt; attract young people and newcomers; a "new song" (Psalm 96:1); continues what the first Christians did, who wrote new hymns.
  • Reasons against: traditional hymns carry richer doctrine in many verses; tested over centuries; shared by all generations; less dependent on a band's skill; less risk of performance.
  • Another view: both are needed; the question is which helps this congregation pray on this occasion.
  • Judge it by: Do the words express Christian belief accurately? Can the whole congregation join in? Does it suit the occasion?
Mission check

?A hospital chapel holds a Sunday service. One week a nurse leads a guitar worship song; the next week the chaplain uses an old chant. Most Christians who use both would say music is fit for worship when…

Christians judge worship music by what it says and how it helps people pray; old and new can both serve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe God reveals himself, and the Bible is the inspired ("God-breathed") record of that revelation (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Views of authority: literal Word, inspired Word (most Catholics, Orthodox and many Protestants), or a human guide inspired by faith.
  • The Bible is used in worship, at home, through reading aids and groups, for comfort and moral guidance.

Mission 2

  • Contemporary worship music uses current styles, simple repeated choruses and bands; modern hymns keep doctrine with new tunes.
  • Charismatic and Pentecostal worship stresses the Spirit's gifts, including speaking in tongues; others are more cautious.
