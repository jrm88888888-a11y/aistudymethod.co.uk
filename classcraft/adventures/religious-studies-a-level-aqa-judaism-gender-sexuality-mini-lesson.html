Mission 1

The home: a small sanctuary

  • Judaism is lived as much at home as in the synagogue: Shabbat meals, festivals, kashrut, prayer, study.
  • The home is often called a mikdash me'at ("small sanctuary"): the table like the Temple altar.

> "and thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children, and shalt talk of them when thou sittest in thy house" (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917)

  • Parents are the first teachers of Torah; the Pesach seder is built around children's questions.
  • Shalom bayit (peace in the home) is a high value: family harmony is a religious goal.
Mission 1

Traditional roles

  • Orthodox Judaism teaches distinct but complementary roles for men and women.
  • Women are exempt from most time-bound positive mitzvot (e.g. tefillin, fixed public prayer) (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:7); a common explanation is their role in the home and with children.
  • Three mitzvot traditionally linked with women (Mishnah Shabbat 2:6): niddah (family purity), separating challah from dough, and lighting Shabbat candles.
  • Jewish identity passes through the mother in Orthodox and Masorti Judaism.
  • Men: public prayer in a minyan, Torah study as a lifelong duty, leading services.

A woman of valour

> "She openeth her mouth with wisdom; and the law of kindness is on her tongue." (Proverbs 31:26, JPS 1917)

  • Eshet chayil ("woman of valour", Proverbs 31:10–31) is sung by many husbands and families at the Friday night table.
  • The woman in the poem runs a household, trades, buys a field and teaches with wisdom.

Mission 1

Why roles have changed

  • Society outside religion: women's education, paid work and leadership became normal in the 20th century.
  • Secular rights: UK votes for women (1918, equal in 1928); Sex Discrimination Act 1975; Equality Act 2010. Religious bodies have some exemptions, but expectations changed.
  • Different understandings of Torah and Halakhah:
  • Orthodox: halakhah is divine and binding; change happens only within it.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): Torah reflects its time; equality is a Jewish value that can override old rules.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhah binding but developing; many egalitarian rulings since the 1970s.
  • Interpretations of equality: equal dignity with different roles (Orthodox), or equal roles (Progressive)?

Mission 1

Women and Torah education

  • The Mishnah (Sotah 3:4) records a debate: Ben Azzai says a man should teach his daughter Torah; Rabbi Eliezer opposes teaching daughters Torah (paraphrase).
  • For centuries most women learned practical halakhah and prayer at home, not Talmud in yeshivah.
  • Sarah Schenirer (1883–1935) founded the Bais Yaakov schools for Orthodox girls in Kraków (1917), with rabbinic approval.
  • Today: advanced women's Torah study centres (e.g. Drisha in New York, Nishmat and Matan in Jerusalem); Haredi girls' schools teach Bible and law but usually not Talmud.
  • Progressive Judaism: identical Jewish education for girls and boys, including bat mitzvah with Torah reading.
Mission 1

Agunah: the chained woman

> "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand, and sendeth her out of his house" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917)

  • Jewish divorce needs a get: a document the husband gives freely to his wife, before a beth din (rabbinic court).
  • A civil divorce alone does not end a Jewish marriage in Orthodox law.
  • Agunah (plural agunot): a "chained" woman whose husband refuses to give a get, or has disappeared.
  • She cannot remarry in Orthodox law; children of a new relationship would be mamzerim, restricted in whom they may marry.
  • A man without a get faces fewer consequences: the system is unequal.
Mission 1

Breaking the chains

  • Prenuptial agreements: the husband agrees in advance to pay daily support until he gives a get (e.g. used by the United Synagogue and in the USA).
  • UK law: the Divorce (Religious Marriages) Act 2002 lets a civil court delay the final divorce until a religious divorce is given.
  • Pressure: some batei din name refusers publicly; Israel's rabbinical courts can impose sanctions, even prison.
  • Masorti: a clause in the ketubah (marriage contract) committing both to the beth din (the "Lieberman clause", 1954); in some cases annulment.
  • Reform and Liberal: civil divorce, or their own egalitarian get, so the problem largely does not arise.

Mission 1

Minyan: who counts?

  • Minyan: a quorum of ten adults needed for parts of public prayer (e.g. Kaddish, Kedushah, Torah reading).
  • Orthodox: ten men over bar mitzvah; women may attend but do not count. A mechitzah (partition) separates seating.
  • Masorti / Conservative: a 1973 ruling allowed women to count; most UK Masorti communities now count women, some do not.
  • Reform and Liberal: women and men count equally; mixed seating.
  • Women's prayer groups and partnership minyanim (from 2002) let Orthodox women read Torah and lead some parts of the service, while ten men still form the minyan.
Mission 1

The Jewish Orthodox Feminist Alliance

  • JOFA, founded 1997 in New York by Blu Greenberg (b. 1936); a UK branch followed.
  • Aim: expand women's roles within halakhah. Greenberg's motto: where there is a rabbinic will, there is a halakhic way.
  • On agunot: promotes prenuptial agreements, supports International Agunah Day (the Fast of Esther), campaigns against get refusal.
  • On minyan: supports women's prayer groups and partnership minyanim; women's Torah reading and leadership in ritual.
  • Critics: many Orthodox rabbis say partnership minyanim break with tradition; some feminists say working within halakhah leaves women unequal.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can women be equal to men in Judaism?

  • Claim: Women can be fully equal to men in Judaism.
  • For: Progressive Judaism already gives equal roles; women in the home are central to Jewish life.
  • For: JOFA and Masorti show change within halakhah is possible (prenups, partnership minyanim).
  • Against: in Orthodox law only men give a get or form a minyan: structural inequality remains.
  • Against: if equality means identical roles, much of traditional Judaism must change; many women choose distinct roles.
  • Judgement criteria: equal dignity or equal roles? Who decides: halakhah, the community, women themselves?

Mission 2

What is a rabbi?

  • Rabbi: "my teacher". A scholar of Torah and halakhah, not a priest: no sacrifices, no special holiness.
  • Roles today: teaching, deciding questions of halakhah, preaching, pastoral care, leading a community.
  • Semikhah: ordination, a certificate that the holder may teach and decide halakhah.
  • A knowledgeable adult Jew can lead prayers without being a rabbi (in Orthodox practice, a man leads); the question is who may hold authority.
  • So the debate is about authority, tradition and equality, not about holiness.
Mission 2

Regina Jonas

  • Regina Jonas (1902–1944), Berlin. Studied at the Liberal rabbinical college in Berlin.
  • Her thesis (1930) asked: can a woman hold rabbinic office according to halakhic sources? She argued yes.
  • Ordained privately in December 1935 by Rabbi Max Dienemann (Liberal): the first woman rabbi.
  • Taught, preached and gave pastoral care as Nazi persecution grew.
  • Deported to Theresienstadt in 1942, where she kept up rabbinic work; murdered at Auschwitz in 1944.
  • Largely forgotten until her papers were found in an archive in 1991.
Mission 2

From 1972: women rabbis today

  • 1972: Sally Priesand, ordained by the American Reform seminary (Hebrew Union College).
  • 1975: Jackie Tabick, the first British woman rabbi (Leo Baeck College, Reform).
  • 1977: Julia Neuberger (b. 1950), ordained at Leo Baeck College; rabbi of the South London Liberal Synagogue, the first British woman to lead her own congregation; later a member of the House of Lords (2004) and Senior Rabbi of West London Synagogue (2011–2020).
  • 1985: Amy Eilberg, first Conservative (Masorti) woman rabbi.
  • 2009: Sara Hurwitz, first graduate of the Orthodox women's programme later called Yeshivat Maharat (New York).
  • Today women serve as rabbis across Reform, Liberal, Reconstructionist and Masorti Judaism.

Mission 2

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • Second-wave feminism (1960s–70s) asked why women were shut out of public life; many leading secular feminists were Jewish (e.g. Betty Friedan).
  • Jewish women asked the same of Judaism: no minyan, no aliyah, no ordination, no get without the husband.
  • 1971–72: Ezrat Nashim, a group of Conservative Jewish women, issued a "Call for Change": count women in the minyan, let them be witnesses, rabbis and cantors.
  • Rachel Adler, "The Jew Who Wasn't There" (1971): halakhah treats women as outside the full community (paraphrase;).
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: rethinking God-language, Torah and history from women's experience (Plaskow).
  • Impact: new rituals (naming ceremonies for girls, bat mitzvah, women's Rosh Chodesh groups), women scholars, and equal-rights campaigns beyond the synagogue.

Judge and king

> "Now Deborah, a prophetess, the wife of Lappidoth, she judged Israel at that time." (Judges 4:4, JPS 1917)

> "one from among thy brethren shalt thou set king over thee" (Deuteronomy 17:15, JPS 1917)

  • Maimonides, Laws of Kings 1:5 (paraphrase): from "king" (not "queen"), the rabbis learned that only men are appointed to positions of authority (serarah) in Israel.

Mission 2

Orthodox objections

  • Authority: Maimonides' rule against women in serarah.
  • Obligation: women are exempt from time-bound mitzvot like fixed prayer, so they cannot fulfil men's obligation as prayer leaders.
  • Community rules: a woman does not count in a minyan, and traditionally does not serve as a witness.
  • Modesty: concerns about kol isha (a woman's singing voice) and tzniut.
  • Tradition: no precedent in rabbinic history; fear of copying Reform.
  • Many Orthodox women answer: the roles are different but of equal dignity; the home and teaching are already leadership.
Mission 2

Orthodox women leaders today

  • Yoatzot halakhah (halakhic advisers), trained from 1997 at Nishmat in Jerusalem: answer women's questions on family purity laws.
  • Maharat title (from 2009); some graduates use Rabba or Rabbi. Rabbi Avi Weiss led this.
  • Opposition: the Rabbinical Council of America (2010, 2015) and the Orthodox Union (2017) ruled against ordaining or appointing women as clergy.
  • UK: Dina Brawer became the first British Orthodox woman ordained (2018, through Yeshivat Maharat); the United Synagogue does not appoint women rabbis.
  • Open Orthodox supporters: the modern rabbi is mainly a teacher and pastor, not the ancient serarah.

Responses across movements

  • Haredi: reject women rabbis and feminist change as against Torah; strong honour for women's home role.
  • Modern Orthodox: many welcome women scholars and advisers but stop at ordination; a minority (Open Orthodox) ordain women.
  • Masorti / Conservative: ordain women since 1985 (JTS decision 1983), arguing from halakhic development.

Mission 3

Sex as a mitzvah

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is holy, not shameful; marriage is called kiddushin, "holiness".
  • Procreation: "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917) is counted as the first mitzvah. The school of Hillel (Mishnah Yevamot 6:6): at least a son and a daughter.
  • Companionship and pleasure: a husband owes his wife onah, her "conjugal rights" (Exodus 21:10, JPS 1917): a duty to her, not to himself.
  • So sex has value even when children are not possible (e.g. after menopause).
  • Holiness also brings boundaries: when, with whom and how.

Mission 3

Celibacy and marriage

  • "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Genesis 2:18, JPS 1917): marriage is the norm.
  • Judaism has no celibate clergy: rabbis are expected to marry.
  • Talmud (Yevamot 62b–63b, paraphrase): a man without a wife lives without joy, blessing or goodness.
  • One sage, Ben Azzai, stayed unmarried to study Torah; the Talmud records him as an exception, and criticised.
  • Single people are full members of the community; marriage is valued, not forced.
  • Marriage is a covenant; divorce (with a get) is allowed when it fails.

Mission 3

Prohibited relations

  • Leviticus 18 and 20 list the arayot ("nakedness"): forbidden relations.
  • They include incest, adultery, sex during niddah (a woman's monthly period and the days after), male–male intercourse ("Thou shalt not lie with mankind, as with womankind", Leviticus 18:22, JPS 1917) and bestiality.
  • Gilui arayot (forbidden relations) is one of three sins a Jew must die rather than commit (Sanhedrin 74a).
  • Sex before marriage: forbidden in Orthodox halakhah; many progressive Jews accept committed relationships before marriage.
Mission 3Diversity

Same-sex relationships

  • Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 forbids male–male intercourse; rabbis also forbid sex between women (a lesser, rabbinic ban). Orientation itself is not a sin; acts are what halakhah addresses. Same-sex marriage is not recognised.
  • Orthodox responses vary: a 2010 US "Statement of Principles" (many Modern Orthodox rabbis) urged welcome and dignity for gay Jews; the UK Chief Rabbi's guide for Orthodox schools (2018) addresses LGBT+ pupils' wellbeing.
  • Masorti / Conservative (US): since 2006 allows gay and lesbian rabbis; since 2012 same-sex ceremonies.
  • Reform and Liberal: full equality: same-sex marriages, LGBT+ rabbis; Leviticus 18:22 read as tied to its ancient context, or outweighed by the dignity of every person made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
Mission 3

Transgender matters

  • Many Orthodox authorities: a person's halakhic sex is fixed by the body at birth; surgery raises bans (e.g. on castration, Leviticus 22:24; Deuteronomy 22:5 on dress). Pastoral responses differ.
  • A minority Orthodox view: Rabbi Eliezer Waldenberg (1915–2006) held that after surgery a person's halakhic sex can change in some respects.
  • Reform and Liberal: affirm transgender and non-binary Jews; e.g. the US Union for Reform Judaism resolution (2015); liturgy for transition.
  • Question for all: how ancient categories (male, female) apply to modern medicine.
  • Spec: "transgender matters".

Mission 3

Rachel Adler

  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Jewish feminist theologian; Engendering Judaism (1998).
  • 1972, as an Orthodox writer: defended niddah and mikveh as a cycle of death and rebirth; later (1993) rejected her own essay, saying purity laws harm women.
  • Argues halakhah must be rebuilt with women's voices as full partners.
  • Brit Ahuvim ("lovers' covenant"): a marriage ceremony based on a partnership contract, not on the traditional act of acquisition (kinyan) in which the man acquires the wife.
  • It is open to same-sex couples.
  • Critics (Orthodox): rejects the halakhic form of kiddushin.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is halakhah on sex relevant today?

  • Claim: Halakhah on sex is relevant in the 21st century.
  • For: It values sex as holy, protects the wife's rights (onah) and builds respect and boundaries into marriage.
  • For: Many Orthodox couples say niddah renews intimacy each month.
  • Against: Laws on same-sex relations and gender clash with modern equality; LGBT+ Jews may feel excluded.
  • Against: Most progressive Jews do not keep niddah; Adler argues purity language harms women.

Mission 4

Why Jewish feminism arose

  • 1960s–70s: second-wave feminism changed women's roles in work, law and education. Many Jewish women asked why their religious lives had not changed too.
  • Their questions: women could not be counted in a minyan, lead most prayer, be rabbis, or end their own marriages.
  • 1971: Rachel Adler, then Orthodox, published "The Jew Who Wasn't There": women in halakhah treated like those exempt from many commandments.
  • 1972: the group Ezrat Nashim presented a "Call for Change" to Conservative rabbis.
  • Jewish feminist theology goes further: it asks how texts, memory and images of God were shaped by men, and how they could include women.
  • Some feminists, such as Judith Plaskow, argue that women were left unaddressed even in the story of Sinai. Rachel Adler's answer, for marriage, is to rebuild from within Jewish sources rather than leave them.
Mission 4

Traditional marriage: acquisition and the agunah

  • Jewish marriage begins with kiddushin. Classical halakhah describes it as kinyan (acquisition): "a woman is acquired in three ways", by money, by document or by intercourse (Mishnah Kiddushin 1:1, paraphrase).
  • Today: the groom gives a ring before witnesses; the ketubah sets out his duties to his wife.
  • Divorce: only the husband can give a get (bill of divorce): "he writeth her a bill of divorcement, and giveth it in her hand" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • If he refuses, or disappears, she cannot remarry under halakhah: she is an agunah ("chained"; plural agunot).
  • Your spec uses the term agunot untranslated.
  • Defenders: kinyan is a legal form, not ownership of a person; the ketubah protects wives.
Mission 4

Adler: Brit Ahuvim, the lovers' covenant

  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Engendering Judaism: An Inclusive Theology and Ethics (1998), chapter 5.
  • Her diagnosis (paraphrase): a marriage built on acquisition makes one partner the subject and the other the object. The agunah problem follows from that.
  • Her proposal: Brit Ahuvim ("covenant of lovers"), a marriage between two subjects.
  • She builds it from a different part of halakhah: the law of partnership (shutafut), used for business partners.
  • In the ceremony both partners place a symbolic item of value into a shared bag and lift it together: a partnership, not a purchase.
  • Both make promises; neither acquires the other.

Mission 4

Dissolving and reconfiguring

  • Dissolution: a partnership can be ended by the partners. So a Brit Ahuvim can be dissolved without a husband's get: no one can be left an agunah.
  • Adler also proposes a ritual for ending, so that a broken covenant is marked honestly before the community.
  • Inclusive: the ceremony works for same-sex couples as well as for a woman and a man.
  • Reconfiguring the tradition (paraphrase): Adler does not invent from nothing. She reads Jewish sources again and rebuilds from materials already inside them.
  • Adler's view is one of several Jewish views on marriage and same-sex relationships: Orthodox teaching keeps marriage as kiddushin between a man and a woman.
Mission 4Diversity

Orthodox and Progressive responses

  • Orthodox: Torah and halakhah are given by God, so change must come within halakhah. Responses to agunot: prenuptial agreements that put pressure on a husband to give a get; rabbinic courts using sanctions.
  • Conservative: the Lieberman clause (1954) added to the ketubah lets a rabbinic court act if a husband refuses a get.
  • Reform and Liberal: egalitarian worship, women rabbis, inclusive God-language, women's names (the matriarchs) in the Amidah. Many accept civil divorce or use egalitarian marriage documents.
  • Reconstructionist and Renewal: most open to Plaskow's new midrash and to ceremonies like Brit Ahuvim.
  • Critics of Plaskow and Adler: a covenant "reshaped" by each generation may stop being the covenant given at Sinai.

Mission 5

How science challenges religion

  • Modern science asks for evidence and reason: claims are tested, not accepted on authority.
  • Some discoveries seem to clash with a plain reading of Genesis:
  • The Big Bang (from the 1920s–60s): the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Evolution (Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859): life developed over billions of years by natural selection.
  • Genetics: humans can now read and edit DNA, raising new ethical questions.
  • Jewish responses range from rejection of some findings to full acceptance with a re-reading of Torah.
  • Your spec's three strands: evidence and reason; specific discoveries; science as a stimulus to Jewish ethical thinking.
Mission 5

Maimonides: study nature, love God

  • Maimonides (1138–1204), Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 2:2 (paraphrase): contemplating God's works and seeing God's boundless wisdom in them leads to love and awe of God (full study in Mission 6).
  • So studying nature is a religious duty, not a threat.

> "The heavens declare the glory of God, and the firmament showeth His handiwork" (Psalm 19:1, JPS 1917, where it is verse 2)

  • He also taught: where reason proves something, a Torah verse that seems to contradict it should be read figuratively (Guide for the Perplexed II.25, paraphrase).
  • Many Jews today use this to welcome science: discovering how nature works shows more of God's wisdom.
Mission 5Read the source

Bereshit

> "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Bereshit: "in the beginning", the first word of the Torah and the Hebrew name of Genesis.
  • Rashi (1040–1105), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): the plain sense is "At the beginning of God's creating the heaven and the earth…"; the verse does not set out to teach the order of creation.
  • Nachmanides (Ramban, 1194–1270), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): God created from absolute nothing a tiny substance, no bigger than a mustard seed, from which everything else came.
  • Creatio ex nihilo (creation from nothing): God alone is eternal; the world had a beginning (Saadia Gaon; Maimonides).

Mission 5Diversity

Responses to evolution

  • Rejection (many Haredi Jews): six literal days; species created separately. Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902–1994), the Lubavitcher Rebbe, argued in letters that science offers theories about the distant past, not proof.
  • Embrace through mysticism: Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine: evolution, as gradual ascent towards perfection, fits kabbalistic teaching (paraphrase).
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science explains how things work; religion asks what they mean (paraphrase). Rabbi Natan Slifkin (b. 1975), The Challenge of Creation (2006): Torah and evolution are compatible; some Haredi rabbis banned his books in 2004–05.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): accept evolution fully; Genesis teaches values (one Creator, a good world, human dignity), not biology.
  • Most Jewish movements found evolution less of a crisis than many Christian groups did: the Oral Torah had long allowed non-literal readings.
Mission 5

How old is the universe?

  • The Jewish calendar counts years from creation: Rosh Hashanah 2026 began the year 5787.
  • Science: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old; the Earth about 4.5 billion.
  • Jewish responses:
  • Literal: the world is under 6,000 years old; it was made mature (with apparent age), so evidence of age does not show real age (Schneerson's view).
  • Earlier worlds: a midrash says God "built worlds and destroyed them" before this one (Bereshit Rabbah 3:7, paraphrase); Rabbi Israel Lipschutz (1782–1860) linked it to fossils.
  • Day = age: yom (day) can mean a long period; the calendar counts from Adam, not from the first moment.
  • Relativity: Gerald Schroeder (next screen).
Mission 5

Gerald Schroeder: six days and 15 billion years

  • Gerald Schroeder (b. 1938), MIT-trained physicist living in Jerusalem; Genesis and the Big Bang (1990), The Science of God (1997).
  • Einstein's relativity: time passes at different rates in different frames of reference.
  • The six days are told from the perspective of creation's beginning; the universe has since stretched by a factor of about a million million (10¹²).
  • So six days "then" equal about 15 billion years "now". Each day is half the one before: about 8, 4, 2, 1, ½ and ¼ billion years.
  • From Adam, time is counted in human terms: hence about 5,800 years.
  • Critics: scientists say the calculation uses relativity loosely; some religious critics say it reads modern science into Torah (when science changes, so must the reading).

Mission 5

Genetic engineering: permission to heal

  • Gene therapy (fixing faulty genes), embryo screening and GM crops: science as a stimulus to Jewish ethical thinking.
  • The Torah requires a person who injures another to "cause him to be thoroughly healed" (Exodus 21:19, JPS 1917).
  • The Talmud reads this as permission for the doctor to heal (Bava Kamma 85a, paraphrase): medicine is not interfering with God.
  • Pikuach nephesh (saving life) overrides almost every other mitzvah, so research that could cure disease is widely seen as a duty.
  • Humans as God's partners in creation: improving the world (Genesis 2:15: to "dress it and to keep it", JPS 1917).
  • Example: Dor Yeshorim (founded 1983) confidentially tests young Orthodox Jews for genetic diseases such as Tay-Sachs before matches are made.
Mission 5Diversity

Where are the limits?

  • Therapy vs enhancement: most rabbis support curing disease; many oppose "designer" changes for looks or intelligence.
  • Embryos: many authorities allow screening embryos (PGD) to avoid serious disease, since an embryo outside the womb has lower status (Rabbi Moshe Tendler, 1926–2021); others urge caution (Rabbi J. David Bleich, b. 1936). (More in an earlier lesson.)
  • Mixing kinds: Leviticus 19:19 forbids breeding "with a diverse kind". Most authorities say it covers cross-breeding and grafting, not gene splicing, so GM foods are generally treated as kosher; a minority disagree.
  • Playing God / eugenics: memories of Nazi racial science make many Jews wary of "improving" humans.
  • Progressive Jews: support research, with strong regulation and fair access.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is Judaism compatible with science?

  • Claim: Jewish belief is fully compatible with scientific thinking.
  • For: Maimonides made studying nature a path to loving God; reason can reshape how a verse is read.
  • For: Rashi and the Oral Torah never required a literal, scientific Genesis; Kook and Sacks welcome evolution.
  • Against: many Haredi Jews reject evolution and an old universe; the Slifkin ban shows real conflict.
  • Against: Schroeder-style harmonising may change Torah to fit each new theory (critics' view).
  • Judgement criteria: Which Jews? Compatible with scientific findings, or with science's method (evidence over authority)? Does science answer the questions Judaism asks?

The clash is about how to read Genesis, not whether God created. Jews divide over evolution and the age of the world, but almost all welcome science as the study of God's works.

Mission 6

Three great works

  • Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204): rabbi, philosopher and court physician in Fustat (Old Cairo).
  • Commentary on the Mishnah (Arabic, c. 1168): explains each Mishnah; includes the Thirteen Principles.
  • Mishneh Torah (Hebrew, c. 1180): a complete code of Jewish law, in 14 books.
  • Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic, c. 1190): philosophy for a Jew torn between Torah and reason.
  • One aim runs through all three: to know God truly, and to live by the Torah with understanding.
Mission 6Read the source

Nature reveals God's glory

Maimonides, Mishneh Torah, Foundations of the Torah 2:2 (paraphrase):

  • How does a person come to love and fear God?
  • By contemplating God's great and wonderful works and creatures, and seeing in them God's wisdom, which has no limit.
  • At once the person loves, praises and longs to know God, as David said:

> "My soul thirsteth for God, for the living God" (Psalm 42:2, JPS 1917)

  • Then, reflecting more, the person draws back in awe, knowing how small and dark they are before God's knowledge:

> "When I behold Thy heavens, the work of Thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which Thou hast established; What is man, that Thou art mindful of him?" (Psalm 8:3–4, JPS 1917)

Mission 6

Science as a path to God

  • For Maimonides, studying nature (physics, astronomy, biology) is not a distraction from religion: it is a commandment, part of loving God.
  • The rabbis' "Account of Creation" (Ma'aseh Bereshit) he identifies with physics; the "Account of the Chariot" (Ma'aseh Merkavah) with metaphysics.
  • He used the best science of his day (Aristotle's, Ptolemy's astronomy) and wrote medical works himself.
  • Glory of God: the order of nature shows God's wisdom: "How manifold are Thy works, O LORD! In wisdom hast Thou made them all" (Psalm 104:24, JPS 1917).
  • Today: many Jews see scientific study as a way to wonder at creation; some worry that science replaces wonder with explanation (see Mission 5).
  • AQA: link Maimonides to science's emphasis on evidence and reason.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The home is a "small sanctuary"; Orthodox Judaism teaches complementary roles, Progressive Judaism equal roles; society, secular rights and readings of Torah drive the debate.
  • An agunah is "chained" when a husband refuses a get; remedies include prenups, the UK 2002 Act and court pressure; Reform and Liberal Jews avoid the problem.
  • Minyan: Orthodox counts ten men; most Masorti and all Reform and Liberal communities count women; Orthodox feminists (JOFA) seek change within halakhah.

Mission 2

  • Regina Jonas (ordained 1935, murdered 1944) was the first woman rabbi; Reform ordained Sally Priesand in 1972, Britain's Jackie Tabick and Julia Neuberger followed (1975, 1977), Masorti in 1985, a small Orthodox stream from 2009.
  • Jewish feminism grew from second-wave feminism and Jewish women's exclusion from minyan, ordination and divorce rights, and has changed ritual, scholarship and leadership.
  • Orthodox objections rest on Maimonides' rule on authority, minyan and witness laws; Reform rests on equality as a Jewish value.

Mission 3

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is a mitzvah: for procreation, and for closeness and pleasure (onah, the wife's right).
  • Celibacy is not an ideal: marriage is the norm, and rabbis marry.
  • Orthodox Jews forbid same-sex acts while many stress welcome for gay Jews; Reform and Liberal Jews affirm same-sex marriage.
  • Reform and Liberal Jews affirm transgender Jews; Rachel Adler rebuilds marriage as a partnership (Brit Ahuvim).

Mission 4

  • Adler: traditional marriage rests on acquisition, which leaves agunot; Brit Ahuvim is a partnership of equals that the partners can dissolve.

Mission 5

  • Science's evidence-based method and discoveries (Big Bang, evolution, genetics) challenge literal readings of Genesis; Jewish responses run from rejection (many Haredim) to full acceptance (Progressive), with Kook, Sacks and Slifkin in between.
  • Bereshit: Rashi says Genesis 1 does not teach the order of creation; Ramban's tiny first substance and creatio ex nihilo fit a universe with a beginning; Schroeder uses relativity to make six days equal about 15 billion years.
  • Genetic engineering: healing is permitted and a duty (Exodus 21:19; pikuach nephesh), with limits on enhancement and wariness of eugenics.

Mission 6

  • Contemplating nature reveals God's wisdom and glory, leading to love and then awe.
