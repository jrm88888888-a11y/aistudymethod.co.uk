Mission 1

Two kinds of question

  • Science asks how: what happened, in what order, by what processes. It answers from evidence that can be tested.
  • Religion often asks why: is there a purpose? Who, if anyone, is behind it?
  • A creation account (creation story) is a sacred text about the beginning of the world and its meaning.
  • Many religious people say the two kinds of question fit together. Others think one side has to give way.
  • The key word is interpretation: how a sacred text is read decides whether it clashes with science.
Mission 1Read the source

Genesis 1

"In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)

"God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)

  • Genesis 1: God creates by speaking, over six days, then rests on the seventh (Genesis 2:1–3). Humans come last.
  • Genesis 2 (from 2:4): a second account. God forms the man from dust before plants and animals, then the woman.

Mission 1

The Big Bang

  • Scientists' best account: about 13.8 billion years ago the universe was extremely hot and dense, and it has been expanding and cooling ever since.
  • Evidence: distant galaxies are moving away from us (red shift); the cosmic microwave background, faint radiation left over from the early universe.
  • Stars, then galaxies and planets, formed over billions of years. Earth formed about 4.5 billion years ago.
  • The theory was first proposed by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Belgian Catholic priest and physicist, in 1927 and 1931.
  • Lemaître kept science and faith distinct: the theory describes the physical beginning; it neither proves nor disproves God.

Mission 1Diversity

Christians read Genesis differently

  • Literal reading (creationists, e.g. some evangelical Christians): Genesis is accurate history; God made everything in six days; many say the earth is only thousands of years old (young earth). The Big Bang is rejected or reinterpreted.
  • Non-literal reading (most Catholics, Anglicans, Orthodox, many Protestants): Genesis is true about who and why, not a science report. The "days" can be stages or a poetic framework.
  • These Christians see the Big Bang as how God created: science and faith are compatible.
  • Shared belief: God is Creator; the universe depends on God and is good; humans have a special place in it.
  • Catholic and most other Christian teaching: God creates ex nihilo (out of nothing). Science studies how the universe develops; it cannot explain why there is something rather than nothing.

Mission 1Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: Allah is the one Creator of everything. The Qur'an speaks of creation in six days (Qur'an 7:54), read by many Muslims as six periods.
  • "Have not those who disbelieve known that the heavens and the earth were of one piece, then We parted them" (Qur'an 21:30, Pickthall). Many Muslims see this as fitting the Big Bang.
  • Judaism: God created the universe (Genesis 1–2; the Torah is the first part of the Tenakh).
  • Orthodox Jews: Genesis is God's word; some read it literally, many read the days as stages; the Jewish calendar counts years from creation.
  • Reform Jews: Genesis teaches meaning and values; the Big Bang and an ancient universe are accepted.
Mission 1Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: the universe goes through endless cycles of creation, preservation and dissolution (for many Hindus, Brahma, Vishnu and Shiva are three aspects of Brahman; Vaishnavas and Shaivas see Vishnu or Shiva as supreme). Many Hindus say a cyclical, vast universe fits modern cosmology.
  • Sikhism: no step-by-step creation story like Genesis; Waheguru (God) created everything by the divine will (hukam). The Mool Mantar (Guru Granth Sahib 1) calls God Karta Purakh, the Creator.
  • Buddhism: no creator God and no single creation story. World-systems arise, decay and are reborn in cycles, without a known first beginning.
  • The Buddha refused to answer whether the universe is eternal: such questions do not lead to the end of suffering (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63: the parable of the poisoned arrow).
Mission 1

Does science count against God?

  • Atheists and Humanists (non-religious people who live by reason and human values) point out that the universe can be explained by science without God.
  • Argument: if physics can describe the universe from its earliest moments, a Creator is an unnecessary extra. Creation stories were early attempts to explain what science now explains.
  • Many add that a literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence.
  • Religious replies:
    • The Big Bang needs a cause: why is there a universe at all? (the First Cause argument)
    • The laws of physics are finely balanced for life; some see design in this.
    • Science explains how, not why: it cannot rule out a Creator.
Quick check
?Many Buddhists say that the question "How did the universe begin?" is not important to answer. Why?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Science has shown that God did not create the universe."

  • Reasons for: the Big Bang explains the universe's development by physical laws. A literal six-day creation conflicts with the evidence. Explanations that need fewer causes are often preferred (no need for God).
  • Reasons against: science explains how the universe developed, not why there is anything at all. The Big Bang suggests a beginning, which fits belief in creation (Lemaître; creation ex nihilo). Many scientists are believers.
  • Other views: Buddhists see the question as unimportant; many Hindus and Sikhs see the universe's vastness as fitting their scriptures.
  • Judge it by: Do science and religion answer the same question? Does a beginning need a cause? Does it depend on how scripture is read?
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two religious beliefs about the origins of the universe. Refer to sacred writings or another source of religious belief and teaching.

  • Belief 1: Christians believe God created the universe out of nothing (ex nihilo). Source: "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth" (Genesis 1:1). Development: so many Christians see the universe as depending on God, and the Big Bang as the way God's creation began.
  • Belief 2: Some Christians read Genesis literally. Development: they believe the world was made in six days, so they reject the Big Bang where it conflicts with Genesis.

Mission 2

Why the world has value

  • For many religious people the world has value because God made it and called it "very good" (Genesis 1:31).
  • Awe and wonder: the deep amazement people feel at nature's size, beauty and order. Many believers see God in it.
  • "The heavens declare the glory of God." (Psalm 19:1)
  • The opposite idea: the world as a commodity, something to be bought, sold and used up for profit.
  • Non-religious people may also feel awe and value the world for its own sake and for future generations.
Mission 2Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

"God said to them, 'Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth.'" (Genesis 1:28)

"The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15)

"The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness; the world, and those who dwell in it." (Psalm 24:1)

Q2 If "the earth is the LORD's", who owns the world?

Mission 2

Dominion or stewardship?

  • Dominion: rule or authority over creation (Genesis 1:28).
  • Some have read dominion as permission to use nature without limit. The historian Lynn White (1967) blamed this reading for part of the ecological crisis.
  • Stewardship: humans look after the world on God's behalf and must answer to God for it.
  • Most Christians and Jews today read dominion as stewardship: responsible rule, like a good king who cares for his people.
  • Responsibility: a duty to care for the world for others now and for future generations.

Mission 2

Use and abuse of the environment

  • Pollution: harmful substances in air, water or land (exhaust fumes, plastic waste, chemical run-off, oil spills).
  • Global warming: burning fossil fuels releases greenhouse gases, which trap heat and change the climate.
  • Natural resources: materials from the earth. Non-renewable (coal, oil, gas, metals) can run out; renewable (sun, wind, water) are replaced naturally.
  • Sustainability (sustainable development): meeting today's needs without stopping future generations meeting theirs.
  • Responses: recycling, renewable energy, conservation, campaigning, changing what people buy and eat.
Mission 2

Christian responses

  • Stewardship means acting: Christians support recycling, renewable energy, conservation and campaigns for climate justice.
  • Catholic teaching: humans' dominion over creation is not absolute; it must respect the good of others, including future generations (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2415, paraphrase).
  • Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): care for "our common home"; the poor suffer most from environmental damage (paraphrase and short phrase).
Mission 2Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: humans are khalifah (vicegerents, trustees) on earth: "I will create a vicegerent on earth" (Qur'an 2:30, Yusuf Ali).
  • Allah set the mizan (balance) in creation: "That ye exceed not the measure" (Qur'an 55:8, Pickthall).
  • Fitra: the natural state of creation and of human nature, in harmony with Allah's will (Qur'an 30:30).
  • On the Day of Judgement (akhirah) Muslims believe they will answer for how they used the earth.
  • Judaism: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • Bal tashchit (do not destroy): even in war, do not cut down fruit trees (Deuteronomy 20:19); extended by the rabbis to all needless waste.
  • Tikkun olam (repairing the world) and gemilut hasadim (acts of loving kindness) include care for the environment.
Mission 2Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: Brahman is present in all things; harming nature harms the sacred. Ahimsa (non-violence) extends to the environment.
  • Examples: the Bishnoi community's protection of trees; the Chipko movement (1970s), villagers hugging trees to stop logging.
  • Sikhism: God is present in creation; air is the Guru, water the father, and earth the great mother (Japji, closing verse, Guru Granth Sahib 8, paraphrase).
  • Many gurdwaras and Sikh groups (e.g. EcoSikh) run environmental projects; sewa (selfless service) includes caring for the earth.
  • Buddhism: everything is interdependent (pratitya samutpada, dependent arising): harming nature harms all beings.
  • "Because he has pity on all living creatures, therefore is a man called Ariya." (Dhammapada 270, Müller)
  • In Buddhist teaching, the First Precept (do not harm living beings) and the Second Precept (do not take what is not given) apply to nature and its resources.

Mission 2

Non-religious views

  • Humanists and many atheists see no Creator, but argue humans have strong reasons to protect the planet.
  • This is the only world we have, and there is no afterlife; the well-being of people and animals now and in future depends on it.
  • They rely on scientific evidence about climate change and pollution.
  • Some non-religious thinkers give nature value in itself; others value it for its use to humans and other sentient beings.
Quick check
?Why would most Humanists say people have a duty to protect the environment?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Humans have the right to use the earth's resources as they wish."

  • Reasons for: Genesis 1:28 gives dominion; human needs (food, homes, jobs, escaping poverty) matter. Some argue technology will solve shortages.
  • Reasons against: "The earth is the LORD's" (Psalm 24:1): humans are stewards, not owners. Humanists: future generations have interests too. Pollution and climate change harm the poorest most (Laudato Si').
  • Other traditions agree: Muslims are khalifah, not owners; bal tashchit, ahimsa and the Precepts forbid needless harm.
  • Judge it by: What does "dominion" mean? Do future generations have rights? Do animals and nature have value in themselves?
Mission 2Worked answer

Different beliefs

Question: Explain two different religious beliefs about humans' relationship with the natural world.

  • Belief 1: Most Christians believe humans are stewards. Development: God put the man in Eden "to cultivate and keep it" (Genesis 2:15), so they recycle, save energy and support climate campaigns.
  • Belief 2: Buddhists believe all things are interdependent. Development: harming nature harms all beings, so they keep the First and Second Precepts by avoiding harm and waste.

Mission 3

Where animals stand

  • Animal rights: the view that animals have moral claims of their own, such as not to be made to suffer or killed for human benefit.
  • Animal welfare: animals may be used, but must be treated well and spared needless suffering.
  • Sentience: being able to feel pain and pleasure. Most people agree many animals are sentient.
  • Christianity, Islam and Judaism teach that humans have a special place (image of God; khalifah) but must care for animals as God's creatures.
  • Hinduism, Sikhism and Buddhism stress that all beings have a soul or can be reborn, so harming animals has spiritual consequences.
Mission 3

Animal experimentation

  • Using live animals in scientific research: testing new medicines and vaccines, studying diseases, checking chemicals for safety.
  • Supporters: it has helped develop treatments that save many lives; the law requires new medicines to be tested before human trials.
  • Opponents: it causes suffering; animals' bodies differ from humans'; alternatives exist (cell cultures, computer models).
  • UK law: the Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986 licenses research and requires suffering to be kept as low as possible. Testing cosmetics on animals is banned in the UK.
  • The 3Rs: Replace animals where possible, Reduce the number used, Refine methods to lessen suffering.
Mission 3

Animals for food

  • Most people in the UK eat meat. Questions raised: factory farming (intensive farming, large numbers in small spaces), animal welfare, and environmental cost.
  • Vegetarians eat no meat or fish; vegans eat no animal products at all.
  • Many religions have food laws: halal (Islam), kosher (Judaism), no beef for most Hindus, no ritually slaughtered meat for Khalsa Sikhs.
  • Reasons for a meat-free diet: compassion, ahimsa, health, the environment.
  • Reasons people eat meat: scripture allows it, nutrition, tradition and culture.

Christianity and animals

"Every moving thing that lives will be food for you." (Genesis 9:3)

"A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10)

  • Catechism of the Catholic Church 2415–2418 (paraphrase): animals are God's creatures, entrusted to human stewardship. Humans may use them for food and clothing. Medical and scientific experiments are acceptable within reasonable limits if they help to care for or save human lives. It is against human dignity to make animals suffer or die needlessly.

Mission 3Diversity

Christian views

  • Most Christians (including Catholic teaching): animal experiments are acceptable when necessary for human health, with suffering kept low; testing cosmetics is wrong because it is not necessary.
  • Some Christians oppose most or all experiments: animals are part of God's good creation, and causing pain for human gain is not stewardship. The Anglican theologian Andrew Linzey (born 1952) argues that animals have God-given rights ("theos-rights").
  • On food: most Christians eat meat (Genesis 9:3; Peter's vision in Acts 10:9–16). Some, e.g. many Seventh-day Adventists and Christian Vegetarian groups, choose a meat-free diet to care for creation.
Mission 3Diversity

Islam and Judaism

  • Islam: animals are Allah's creation: "they (all) shall be gathered to their Lord" (Qur'an 6:38, Yusuf Ali). As khalifah, humans must not be cruel.
  • Hadith tell of a man forgiven for giving water to a thirsty dog, and a woman punished for starving a cat (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Meat is allowed if it is halal: the animal is slaughtered quickly, Allah's name is spoken, and the blood drained. Pork is forbidden.
  • Many Muslim scholars allow experiments that are necessary for human health, with suffering kept as low as possible; testing for cosmetics is widely opposed.
  • Judaism: tza'ar ba'alei chayim: the duty not to cause animals unnecessary suffering. "Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn" (Deuteronomy 25:4, JPS 1917).
  • Kashrut (food laws): only certain animals, killed by shechita (a swift cut by a trained shochet). Many Jews accept medical experiments for saving human life (pikuach nefesh), with minimal pain.

Mission 3Diversity

Hinduism, Sikhism, Buddhism

  • Hinduism: every living being has an atman; humans may be reborn as animals. Ahimsa leads many Hindus to vegetarianism; the cow is especially respected and beef is avoided.
  • Sikhism: God's light is in all creatures. Langar (the gurdwara's free kitchen) is vegetarian, so anyone can eat. The Sikh code (Rehat Maryada) forbids kutha meat (ritually slaughtered).
  • Sikh views on meat differ: many amritdhari Sikhs are vegetarian; others eat jhatka meat (killed with one blow). Kabir's verse criticises killing animals (Guru Granth Sahib 1375); Guru Nanak calls arguments over eating meat foolish (Guru Granth Sahib 1289) (paraphrases).
  • Buddhism: the First Precept: do not harm or kill living beings. "All men tremble at punishment, all men love life; remember that thou art like unto them, and do not kill, nor cause slaughter." (Dhammapada 130, Müller)
  • Many Mahayana Buddhists are vegetarian. In Theravada, monks may eat meat offered to them if the animal was not killed for them. Most Buddhists oppose experiments that cause suffering.
Mission 3

Singer and speciesism

  • Peter Singer (born 1946), Australian philosopher, Animal Liberation (1975): a utilitarian (someone who judges actions by how much suffering and happiness they produce) who argues for equal consideration of interests.
  • What matters morally is the ability to suffer, not species. (Jeremy Bentham made this point in 1789.)
  • Speciesism (a term coined by Richard Ryder, 1970): favouring the interests of one's own species just because it is one's own, like racism or sexism.
  • Singer urges vegetarianism and opposes most experiments. An experiment is only justified if we would accept it on a human with similar capacities (paraphrase).
Quick check

Mission 3Evaluate

"Animal experiments are never right."

  • Reasons for: animals suffer (sentience; Singer). Ahimsa and the First Precept forbid harm. Many results do not transfer to humans; alternatives are growing. Some Christians (Linzey) see it as abuse of God's creatures.
  • Reasons against: many medicines and vaccines depended on it. Humans have a special status (imago Dei, khalifah), and saving human life comes first (pikuach nefesh; CCC 2417). Utilitarians may judge that the benefit to many outweighs the harm.
  • Judge it by: Is human life more valuable than animal life? Can suffering be reduced enough? Are alternatives good enough? Does the purpose (medicine or cosmetics) matter?

For most believers the questions are need and suffering; for Singer, whether species should count at all.

Mission 4

Two shapes, and the question of the soul

  • Afterlife: existence of some kind after death.
  • Linear view (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life → death → judgement → eternal destiny.
  • Cyclical view (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): birth → death → rebirth, again and again (samsara), shaped by karma (actions and their results), until liberation.
  • Soul: the non-physical, spiritual part of a person; for many believers, the real self that survives death.
  • Immortality of the soul: the soul cannot die; the body is its temporary home (an idea found in Plato and in many religions).
  • Physicalism: a person is nothing more than a physical body and brain; when the brain dies, the person ends. Most atheists and Humanists hold this.
  • Why believe in a soul? Sense of a self beyond the body, conscience, religious teaching. Why not? Mind changes when the brain is damaged; no soul has been detected.
Mission 4

Christian beliefs: judgement, heaven and hell

  • Death is not the end: Christians believe the risen Jesus has defeated death.
  • Judgement: God judges each person, through Christ; faith and how people lived both matter (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Heaven: eternal life with God. Many Christians, including Catholic teaching, see it as a state of being with God more than a place in the sky.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God. Catholic teaching describes it as a person's own final rejection of God.
  • At the end of time Christ will return, the dead will be raised, and there will be a Last Judgement.

Mission 4

Resurrection of the body or survival of the soul?

  • Survival of the soul (popular belief, and the Greek idea of immortality): the soul floats free of the body for ever; the body was only a shell.
  • Resurrection of the body (Catholic and mainstream Christian teaching): the whole person is raised, body and soul, by God.
  • Catholic teaching: at death the soul is separated from the body and meets God; at the end of time it is reunited with a transformed, glorified body.
  • Not resuscitation: Lazarus (John 11) came back to ordinary life and later died again; resurrection means a new, deathless life.
  • Some liberal Christians see resurrection as spiritual only.

Mission 4

Islam: akhirah

  • Akhirah: life after death, one of the key Muslim beliefs (Sunni and Shi'a alike). This life is a test.
  • At death the soul is taken by the angel of death; it waits in barzakh (a barrier) until the Day of Resurrection (Qur'an 23:100).
  • Day of Judgement: all are raised; each person's deeds are weighed (Qur'an 101:6–9).
  • Jannah (paradise) for the faithful who did good; Jahannam (hell) for those who rejected God and did evil. God is merciful and just.
  • Muslims differ on whether descriptions of paradise and hell are literal or point to realities beyond words.
Mission 4

Judaism: this world and the world to come

  • The Tenakh says little about the afterlife; Judaism focuses on living this life by the mitzvot.
  • Early books speak of sheol: a shadowy place of the dead.
  • Later books and the rabbis teach resurrection: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917)
  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come": the age after the Messiah, or the life of the soul after death.
  • Orthodox Jews affirm bodily resurrection. Many Reform Jews speak instead of the soul's survival, or of living on through deeds and memory.
Mission 4

Hinduism: atman, samsara, moksha

  • Atman: the eternal self or soul in every living being. It is never born and never dies.
  • At death the atman passes into a new body: reincarnation (transmigration).
  • "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones … / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Bhagavad Gita 2:22, Arnold 1885).
  • Karma decides the next birth. Samsara is the cycle; moksha is release from it.
  • Advaita Hindus: in moksha the atman realises it is one with Brahman. Many Vaishnava Hindus: the soul lives for ever in loving devotion to God (Vishnu or Krishna).
Mission 4

Sikhism: the soul's journey to mukti

  • The soul (atma) is a spark of God. It passes through many life forms (transmigration, samsara), shaped by karma.
  • A human birth is rare and precious: the chance to become close to God.
  • Mukti: liberation from rebirth; the soul merges with Waheguru. It comes through God's grace (nadar), remembering God's name (naam simran), service (sewa) and living as a gurmukh (God-centred), not manmukh (self-centred).
  • Many Sikhs see heaven and hell as states of being close to or far from God, more than places.
Mission 4

Buddhism: rebirth without a fixed soul

  • Anatta: there is no permanent, unchanging self. A person is five ever-changing skandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness).
  • Everything is impermanent (anicca), including "me".
  • So rebirth, not reincarnation: no soul moves on, but a stream of kamma-energy continues, like a flame lit from another flame.
  • Rebirth may be in one of six realms: gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. None is permanent.
  • Nibbana (Sanskrit nirvana) ends rebirth: the fires of greed, hatred and ignorance go out.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Belief in life after death makes this life more valuable."

  • Reasons for: life is a test or preparation; how we live is judged (Matthew 25; Qur'an 99:7–8: "whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then"). Karma means every act counts. Human life is rare and precious (Sikh, Buddhist, Hindu). Hope gives comfort and courage to care for the dying.
  • Reasons against: focus on the next life may lessen effort in this one. Humanists: because this is our only life, it is all the more precious; people live on through memory and good done.
  • Judge it by: Does hope of reward make good acts less generous? Which view better motivates care for others? Can value come without an afterlife?

Mission 5

Good, evil, sin, crime

  • Morality: ideas of right and wrong that guide how people act.
  • Good: what is right, kind and beneficial. Evil: what is wrong, harmful or against God's will.
  • Sin: an act (or thought) against God's will or religious law. A religious idea.
  • Crime: an act against the law of the state, which the state can punish.
  • Some acts are both (murder, theft). Some sins are not crimes (most lies, greed). Some crimes are not seen as sins by everyone (breaking an unjust law in protest).
Mission 5

Absolute or relative?

  • Absolute morality: some acts are always right or wrong, whatever the situation or culture. "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13)
  • Relative morality: what is right depends on the situation, culture or consequences.
  • Absolutists value clear, fair rules; relativists value flexibility and compassion.
  • Many religious believers hold both: some absolute rules, with care for each situation.
Quick check
?Which statement is an example of relative morality?
Mission 5

Intentions and actions

  • An intention is the reason or aim behind an action.
  • The same act can have good or evil intentions: a gift given out of love, or to bribe.
  • Christianity: "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart." (1 Samuel 16:7)
  • Islam: the Prophet taught that actions are judged by their intentions (niyyah) (Sahih al-Bukhari 1, paraphrase).
  • Buddhism: actions are called skilful or unskilful, not sinful; the Buddha taught that intention (cetana) is what makes karma. "If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him" (Dhammapada 1, Müller).
  • Most traditions judge both: a good intention does not make every act right.
Mission 5Diversity

What makes an act wrong?

  • Christianity: going against God's will shown in the Bible, the teaching of Jesus (love God and neighbour) and conscience. Salvation is by God's grace, not earned.
  • Islam: doing the will of Allah (Shari'ah) is good; disobeying it is sin. Every deed is weighed: "whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then" (Qur'an 99:7, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: keeping or breaking the mitzvot (commandments); people have free will to choose good.
  • Hinduism: acting against dharma (duty), harming others (against ahimsa, non-violence); bad karma delays moksha (liberation).
  • Sikhism: living by God's will, not ego; ahimsa and service; good karma and God's grace lead to mukti (liberation). Your named sources: Guru Granth Sahib 1, 1241.
  • Buddhism: actions are skilful (kusala) or unskilful (akusala), not "sinful"; unskilful acts come from greed, hatred and ignorance and break the Five Precepts; skilful acts bring good karma and lead towards nibbana (nirvana).
  • Humanist: wrong acts cause unnecessary harm to wellbeing; judged by reason and empathy.
Quick check
?A Buddhist says lying is unskilful rather than sinful. What is the main reason for this choice of word?
Mission 5

Sin and crime: types and consequences

  • Types of crime: against the person (assault), against property (theft), against the state (terrorism), hate crimes.
  • Types of sin (Catholic): mortal (grave, chosen knowingly) and venial (less serious).
  • Islam: major sins (e.g. shirk, murder) and minor sins.
  • Consequences: crime → punishment by courts; sin → separation from God, bad karma, need for repentance and forgiveness.
  • Social attitudes: many acts seen as sins (most lying, adultery) are not crimes in Britain; religious believers differ on whether the law should enforce morality.

Religions judge both what we do and why we do it; good results do not make every motive good.

Mission 6

Soul or just a body?

  • Soul: a non-physical part of a person: the true self that may survive death.
  • Immortality: the soul lives on after the body dies.
  • Dualism: a person is body and soul. Plato (Phaedo) and René Descartes (1596–1650) argued the mind or soul is distinct from the body.
  • Physicalism: the real world is only physical; the mind is the working of the brain; nothing survives death.
  • Why some believe in a soul: scripture and tradition; the sense of a self beyond the body; religious and near-death experiences.
  • Why others do not: brain damage changes personality and memory; no scientific evidence of a soul.
Mission 6Diversity

Human nature in six religions

  • Christianity: body, spirit and soul (many Christians simply speak of body and soul); made "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27); God "breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul" (Genesis 2:7).
  • Islam: the body, the nafs (self, with its desires) and the qalb (heart, the centre of faith); God "breathed into him of My Spirit" (Qur'an 15:29, Pickthall).
  • Judaism: the unity of body and soul: a person is one whole, made in God's image; both matter to God.
  • Hinduism: the body is temporary; the atman (eternal self) is reborn; the body-mind is made of three gunas: sattva (goodness), rajas (passion), tamas (dullness).
  • Sikhism: the body, the man (mind) and the atman (soul), a spark of God's light within.
  • Buddhism: anatta (no permanent self); a person is five changing khandhas (body, feelings, recognition, motivation, consciousness); all is anicca (impermanent) and marked by dukkha (suffering).
Quick check
?Which religion teaches that there is no permanent, unchanging self?
Mission 6

Why humans do wrong: Abrahamic faiths

  • Christianity: Original Sin: since Adam and Eve's disobedience (Genesis 3), humans are inclined to sin. "for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23).
  • Paul: "the good which I desire, I don't do" (Romans 7:19). Greed: "the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10).
  • Islam: humans are born good (fitrah), but Shaytan (Iblis) tempts them and the nafs can incline to evil. The gravest sins: shirk (treating anything as equal to God) and kufr (unbelief).
  • Doing the will of Allah is good; going against it is sin.
  • Judaism: each person has a yetzer ha-tov (inclination to good) and a yetzer ha-ra (inclination to evil) and must choose: "sin crouches at the door ... you are to rule over it" (Genesis 4:7).
Mission 6

Why humans do wrong: Dharmic faiths

  • Buddhism: the three poisons: greed, hatred and ignorance (avijja); mental defilements (kilesa) drive unskilful action and karma.
  • "There is no fire like passion, there is no shark like hatred, there is no snare like folly, there is no torrent like greed." (Dhammapada 251, Müller)
  • Hinduism: avidya (ignorance of the true self) and maya (illusion: taking the changing world as ultimate) lead to selfish desire and bad karma.
  • Sikhism: haumai (ego, self-centredness) makes a person manmukh (self-willed) instead of gurmukh (God-centred); maya distracts from God.
  • Can they be overcome? Yes, in each faith: grace and forgiveness (Christianity, Islam), teshuvah (Judaism), the Eightfold Path (Buddhism), yoga and devotion (Hinduism), God's grace and remembering God's name (Sikhism).

Mission 6

Free will, determinism, predestination

  • Free will: humans can genuinely choose between options.
  • Determinism: every event, including every choice, is caused by earlier events (genes, upbringing, brain chemistry).
  • Hard determinists: so we are not truly free. Libertarians: we are free; causes influence but do not force. Compatibilists: we are free when we act on our own wishes, even if they have causes.
  • Predestination: God has decided in advance who will be saved.
  • Christian views differ: John Calvin (1509–1564), Institutes of the Christian Religion (1536): God predestines some to salvation. Catholic and Methodist teaching: God offers grace to all; humans freely accept or reject it. "I have set before you life and death ... Therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19).
  • Hinduism and Buddhism: past karma shapes present conditions, but present choices are free. Sikhism: God's hukam (will) orders all, yet humans choose their deeds.
Mission 6

Al-Qadr and free will

  • Al-Qadr (predestination): God knows, records and wills all that happens. "Lo! We have created every thing by measure." (Qur'an 54:49, Pickthall)
  • Yet humans are free and responsible: "whosoever will, let him believe, and whosoever will, let him disbelieve" (Qur'an 18:29, Pickthall).
  • Muslims believe that on the Day of Judgement (Yawm al-Din), each person answers for their own choices.
  • Sunni Muslims count al-Qadr among the six articles of faith. A common Sunni teaching: God creates all acts; humans acquire responsibility for them by choosing.
  • Shi'a teaching stresses God's justice ('adl): a just God would not punish what humans could not avoid, so human choice is real.
  • Many Muslims say: God's knowledge of a choice does not force it.
Quick check
?Which statement best summarises how most Muslims hold al-Qadr and free will together?
Mission 6

Responsibility and punishment

  • If we are free, we can be held responsible, and punishment can be fair.
  • If our choices are determined, is punishment still fair? Some say: punish to protect and reform, not for retribution.
  • Many legal systems already reduce or remove blame for some people: young children (below the age of criminal responsibility) and people with severe mental illness.
  • Religious views: most teach humans are accountable to God for free choices (judgement, karma), while God is merciful.
  • Calvinists answer: those not chosen still freely sin and are justly judged.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Hatred and war are inevitable."

  • Reasons for: Original Sin and the three poisons describe a deep human tendency; every age of history has had war; evolution may have made humans aggressive to rivals.
  • Reasons against: "hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5, Müller); religions teach that people can be changed by grace, discipline and enlightenment; humans also evolved to cooperate; many societies keep peace for long periods.
  • Humanist view: war is a human choice; reason, education and human rights can reduce it.
  • Judge it by: Is human nature fixed? Does "always happened" prove "must happen"? Can whole societies, not just individuals, overcome hatred?

Many believers hold that God knows our choices without forcing them, so freedom and responsibility remain.

Mission 7

One God or many?

  • Theism: belief in God or gods.
  • Monotheism: one God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism).
  • Polytheism: many gods, each with their own powers (e.g. ancient Greek and Norse religion).
  • Many Hindus worship many deities but see them as forms of one ultimate reality, Brahman; so "polytheist" is often a poor label for Hinduism.
  • Why one God? One ordered universe suggests one source; revelation (the Shema; Surah 112); a single ultimate source of goodness.
  • Why many? Different forces of nature and needs of life; family and cultural tradition; the divine may show itself in many forms.
Mission 7

The "omni" words

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing: past, present, future, and every thought.
  • Omnipresent: present everywhere at once. "If I ascend up into heaven, you are there." (Psalm 139:8)
  • Omnibenevolent: all-loving and all-good.
  • These are ways of saying God has no limits.
Mission 7

Beyond the world, and within it

  • Transcendent: beyond and outside the universe, space and time; greater than anything humans can imagine. "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8).
  • Immanent: present and active within the world and in people's lives.
  • A transcendent God inspires awe and worship; an immanent God is close enough to pray to.
  • Most Jews, Christians and Muslims hold both together: God is not part of the world, yet is never absent from it.
  • The Qur'an: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall), yet "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall).
Mission 7

Personal or impersonal? Knowable or not?

  • Personal: God can be known as someone: who loves, speaks, listens and responds, e.g. "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9).
  • Impersonal: the divine is more like a force or ultimate reality than a person, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism (a later mission).
  • Many believers hold that God is personal but is more than a person: not a human being.

Mission 7

God in Christianity

  • One God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit (the Trinity).
  • "God is love." (1 John 4:8)
  • God is creator, judge, all-powerful and all-good.
Mission 7

Allah in Islam

  • Tawhid: the oneness of Allah. "Say: He is Allah, the One! … He begetteth not nor was begotten." (Qur'an 112:1, 3, Pickthall)
  • So Muslims reject the Trinity and any partner with God (shirk).
  • Every surah but one opens: "In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Pickthall).
  • Transcendent: nothing is like Allah; never pictured. Immanent: Allah knows and is close to every person.
Mission 7

God in Judaism

  • One God: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
  • Creator: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • Lawgiver and judge: gave the Torah; judges with justice and mercy.
  • God has no body and cannot be pictured. Many Jews do not say God's name, and write "G-d".
Mission 7

A good God and a good creation

  • "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • Catholic teaching: God is perfect goodness, and creates out of love, not need.
  • So everything God makes is good and reflects God's goodness, as a work shows its maker.
  • The challenge: if God is all-good and all-powerful, why is there suffering? (Next missions.)
Mission 7Diversity

Contrasting beliefs about God

  • Trinity (Christian) vs tawhid (Muslim) vs Jewish oneness with no incarnation: all monotheist, but they disagree about how God is one.
  • Personal God (Abrahamic faiths; Sikh Waheguru; many Hindus' devotion to Vishnu or Shiva) vs impersonal ultimate reality (nirguna Brahman; Buddhism has no creator God).
  • Deism: God made the world but does not intervene in it.
  • Knowable through revelation vs largely unknowable: many mystics say God is best described by what God is not.
  • Atheists: there is no God to describe; Humanists judge the idea by reason and evidence.
Quick check

Mission 7Evaluate

"God cannot be both transcendent and personal."

  • Reasons for: a being beyond space and time cannot listen and respond like a person; personal language makes God too human; Buddhists and many Hindus think ultimate reality is impersonal.
  • Reasons against: scripture presents both (Isaiah 55:8–9 and Matthew 6:9; Qur'an 42:11 and 50:16); an all-powerful God can be close to all without being limited; Christians: God became personal in Jesus.
  • Another view: "personal" is an analogy: true but not complete.
  • Judge it by: Can human words describe God at all? Is a God who cannot be related to worth worshipping? Do believers' experiences count as evidence?

Most believers hold God to be beyond the world and present within it; different words name different sides of one God.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Creation accounts (Genesis 1–2, the Qur'an, Hindu scriptures) say the universe has a source and meaning; Buddhists have no creation story, and Sikh scripture describes creation without a step-by-step account.
  • The Big Bang (first proposed by Lemaître, a Catholic priest) is accepted by many believers as how creation happened; literalists reject it where it conflicts with scripture.
  • Atheists and Humanists say science explains the universe without God; believers reply that science answers how, not why.

Mission 2

  • Many believers value the world because God made it; awe and wonder point to its Creator. The commodity view treats it as a resource to use up.
  • Most Christians and Jews read dominion as stewardship; Muslims speak of khalifah; Hindus, Sikhs and Buddhists stress ahimsa, God in creation and interdependence.
  • Humanists protect the environment for present and future well-being, using scientific evidence (e.g. Humanist Climate Action).

Mission 3

  • Animal welfare allows careful use; animal rights opposes using animals as resources. UK law controls experiments (1986 Act, the 3Rs).
  • Christians, Muslims and Jews generally allow necessary experiments and eating meat, with duties of kindness; many Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs are vegetarian because of ahimsa, rebirth and compassion.
  • Peter Singer (utilitarian) argues speciesism is like racism: equal suffering counts equally.

Mission 4

  • Linear views (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life, judgement, heaven or hell; resurrection. Cyclical views (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): karma, rebirth, liberation (moksha, nibbana, mukti).
  • Catholics believe in the resurrection of the body, not just survival of the soul (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

Mission 5

  • Sin is against God's will; crime is against the law of the state; some acts are both.
  • Absolute morality: some acts always wrong; relative morality: depends on the situation.
  • Most traditions judge intentions as well as actions; conscience, scripture and religious law guide believers.

Mission 6

  • Dualists believe in a soul; physicalists say the mind is the brain. Each religion has its own picture of human nature.
  • Wrongdoing is rooted in sin, selfishness, greed or ignorance; every tradition teaches a way to overcome it.
  • Free will, determinism and predestination; for Muslims, al-Qadr and human freedom are held together.

Mission 7

  • Omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, omnibenevolent; transcendent and immanent; personal and impersonal; knowable and unknowable.
  • Christians: Trinity and love (1 John 4:8); Muslims: tawhid; Jews: one God (Deuteronomy 6:4).
