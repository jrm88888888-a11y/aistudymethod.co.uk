Mission 1

Four aims of life

  • The purusharthas: "aims of a person", four goals a full human life may pursue.
  • Dharma: right living, duty, virtue.
  • Artha: wealth, security, success, power.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, beauty, enjoyment of the senses.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara (see Mission 6).
  • All four are legitimate. The question is how they fit together.
  • Your spec: "the four aims of life; and their relative importance".
Mission 1

Artha: wealth and power

  • Earning a living, owning property, supporting a family, building a business, and for rulers, governing well.
  • Without artha, a household cannot give to the poor, support temples or pay for ritual.
  • The Arthashastra (attributed to Kautilya, compiled c. 2nd century BCE–3rd century CE): a manual of statecraft and economics; wealth as the basis of a stable kingdom.
  • Artha is good when gained and used within dharma: honestly, generously, without harm.
Mission 1

Kama: pleasure and love

  • Delight in the senses, music, art, food, friendship, romantic and sexual love within marriage.
  • The Kama Sutra (Vatsyayana, c. 3rd century CE) is a guide to the art of pleasant living, not only to sex.
  • Kama shows that Hindu tradition does not reject pleasure: it is one of life's aims.

> "These am I, free from passion and desire; / Yet am I right desire in all who yearn" (Gita 7:11, Arnold)

  • Krishna is present in desire that is not contrary to dharma (the Sanskrit of 7:11): kama, too, needs dharma's limits.
Mission 1

How the four fit together

  • Trivarga: the first three, the "group of three" for life in the world.
  • Dharma frames artha and kama, like the banks of a river: they give the water its course.
  • Moksha goes beyond the three: it is freedom from the whole cycle in which they are pursued.
  • Two directions of life: pravritti (engagement with the world: dharma, artha, kama) and nivritti (withdrawal: moksha).
  • Most Hindus live engaged lives and turn more towards moksha with age.

Mission 1

The aims across the stages of life

  • Brahmacharya (student): learn dharma; self-discipline.
  • Grihastha (householder): pursue artha and kama within dharma; raise a family; support society.
  • Vanaprastha (retirement): loosen ties; growing vairagya (detachment, freedom from craving).
  • Sannyasa (renunciation): give up everything for moksha.
  • Vairagya is the bridge: detachment turns a person from the three worldly aims towards liberation.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is moksha the most important aim?

  • Claim: Moksha is the most important of the four aims.
  • For: It alone ends samsara; the others are temporary and bind (even punya).
  • For: Upanishads and Vedanta make liberation the highest goal (parama purushartha).
  • Against: Dharma is the foundation: without it, no aim is pursued rightly, and moksha is not reached.
  • Against: For most Hindus the householder's three aims fill most of life; Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together.
  • Against: For some bhakti traditions, loving God matters more than liberation itself (see Mission 6).
  • Judgement criteria: Most important for whom, and at which stage of life? For the individual, or for society?
  • Your spec asks for "their relative importance".

Mission 2

Atman: the self that is not the body

  • Atman: in Hindu belief, the real, eternal self in every living being.
  • Hindus hold that it is not the body, the mind or the personality: these change; the atman does not.
  • The body is like clothing:

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2:22, Arnold)

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Gita 2:20, Arnold)

  • The atman wearing a body and caught in rebirth is called the jiva or jivatman (embodied self).

Mission 2

Brahman: the reality behind everything

  • Brahman: ultimate reality, the ground of all that exists.
  • Sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss. Not three parts: three ways of naming one reality.
  • Neti neti, "not this, not this" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad): nothing we can point to is Brahman; every description falls short.
Mission 2

Two selves in one heart?

  • Jivatman: the individual self, embodied, acting, reaping the results of its deeds.
  • Paramatman: the supreme self, God, dwelling in every heart as inner controller.

> "There are two who enjoy the fruits of their good deeds in the world, having entered into the cave of the heart ... The knowers of Brahman call them shadow and light." (Katha 3:1, Paramananda)

  • Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.1 (paraphrase): two birds on one tree; one eats the fruit, the other watches without eating.
  • The big question: are the two really one, or really two?
Mission 2Diversity

Three Vedanta answers

  • Advaita (non-dual), Shankara (c. 788–820 by tradition): atman is Brahman. The sense of being a separate jiva is due to avidya (ignorance) and maya. Tat tvam asi means strict identity.
  • Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual), Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137): souls are real and distinct, but are the body of Brahman, as a part to its whole. Tat tvam asi: Brahman is the inner self of "you", as a soul is of its body.
  • Dvaita (dual), Madhva (1238–1317): God (Vishnu) and souls are eternally different. Souls depend wholly on God. Madhva reads the text as atat tvam asi, "you are not that".
  • All three are Vedanta: they read the same Upanishads, Gita and Brahma Sutras.
  • "You are that" (tat tvam asi) comes from the Chandogya Upanishad (VI), where a father shows his son salt dissolved in water: unseen, yet in every sip (paraphrase).
Mission 2

Samkhya: spirit and matter

  • Samkhya, one of the six classical schools (darshanas); classic text: Ishvarakrishna's Samkhya Karika (c. 350–450 CE).
  • Dualism: two kinds of reality. Purusha: pure consciousness, many separate selves. Prakriti: matter, including body, senses, mind and intellect.
  • Classical Samkhya has no Brahman: liberation (kaivalya, "aloneness") is the self's realising it was never prakriti.
  • The chariot read two ways:
  • Samkhya: the passenger is a purusha; chariot, horses, reins and driver are all prakriti.
  • Advaita: the passenger is atman, which is Brahman; the chariot belongs to the world of maya.
  • Your spec pairs Advaita Vedanta (non-dualism) with Samkhya (dualism).

Mission 2Evaluate

Is the atman identical with Brahman?

  • Claim: Atman is Brahman: the self is God, not a separate soul.
  • For: "You are that" and "this self ... is Brahman" (Shandilya Vidya) read most naturally as identity.
  • For: If Brahman is infinite and non-dual, nothing can stand outside it, so no second self.
  • Against: Experience: I suffer and do not know everything; Brahman does not suffer. Ramanuja: identity cannot explain this.
  • Against: Bhakti needs two: love and worship require a lover and a beloved (Madhva; much popular practice).
  • Against (Samkhya): many separate conscious selves, no one cosmic self.
  • Judgement criteria: Which reading fits the most texts? Which explains experience of ignorance and suffering? Which supports the way Hindus actually live and worship?

Mission 3

Samkhya: counting what is real

  • Hindu thought has six classical darshanas ("views": schools of philosophy) that accept the authority of the Vedas.
  • Samkhya ("enumeration", counting) is one of the oldest. It lists the basic principles (tattvas) of reality: 25 in all.
  • Its legendary founder is the sage Kapila. Its classic text is the Samkhya Karika of Ishvarakrishna (c. 4th–5th century CE).
  • Samkhya ideas appear earlier in the Upanishads and the Bhagavad Gita, often in a form that includes God.
  • Yoga (Patanjali) is its sister school: Samkhya gives the theory, yoga the practice.
Mission 3

Two realities: purusha and prakriti

  • Samkhya is dualist: reality has two eternal, separate kinds.
  • Purusha: pure consciousness. It only witnesses; it does not act or change. There are many purushas, one for each living being.
  • Prakriti: nature or matter. It is one, unconscious, always active and changing.
  • Key point: the mind, ego and intellect belong to prakriti, not to purusha. Thinking is a material process; awareness of it is purusha.
  • Suffering begins when purusha mistakes the activity of prakriti for itself: "I am angry", "I am this body".
  • So in Samkhya, the atman is a purusha: one of many, distinct from body and mind and from every other self.
Mission 3

The three gunas

  • Prakriti is made of three gunas ("strands", qualities), mixed in different proportions in everything:
  • Sattva: light, purity, calm, goodness.
  • Rajas: energy, passion, restlessness, desire.
  • Tamas: heaviness, darkness, inertia, dullness.
  • Before creation the gunas are in balance; when the balance is disturbed, the world unfolds.

> "Sattwan, Rajas, and Tamas, so are named / The qualities of Nature, "Soothfastness," / "Passion," and "Ignorance." These three bind down / The changeless Spirit in the changeful flesh." (Bhagavad Gita 14:5, Arnold)

  • Hindus apply the gunas to food, character and actions: e.g. sattvic food is fresh and vegetarian; many aim to increase sattva.

Mission 3

Liberation: kaivalya

  • The goal is kaivalya: "aloneness" or isolation of purusha from prakriti.
  • It comes through viveka: discriminating knowledge that "I am not this body, mind or ego".
  • When purusha is no longer confused with prakriti, the dance of prakriti stops for it. No more rebirth.
  • Liberation is not union with God: each liberated purusha remains itself, separate and pure.
  • Classical Samkhya is non-theistic: it does not need Ishvara (a Lord) to explain the world. Many Hindus who use its ideas do believe in God.

Mission 4

One without a second

  • Advaita ("non-dual") Vedanta is the school of the philosopher Shankara (c. 8th century CE); it reads the Upanishads as teaching that atman and Brahman are one.
  • For Shankara's Advaita, Brahman alone is ultimately real: "one only, without a second" (Chandogya 6:2:1, paraphrase).
  • Nirguna Brahman: without qualities; beyond thought and words. It can only be described by negation: neti, neti ("not this, not this", Brihadaranyaka 2:3:6).
  • Positively, it is described as sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss.
  • Saguna Brahman (with qualities) or Ishvara (the Lord): Brahman as seen from within the world: creator, worshipped as Vishnu, Shiva or the Goddess. Real at the everyday level, not the final truth.
  • Shankara teaches that atman is Brahman: the true self is not a part of Brahman but Brahman, fully.
Mission 4Read the source

"You are that"

  • Chandogya Upanishad 6 (paraphrase): Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu. Salt dissolved in water cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes of it (6:13). The subtle essence pervades everything; "that is the Self, and you are that" (tat tvam asi).
  • Shankara reads "you are that" as identity: the self and Brahman are one, not two.
  • The Gita on the self (Arnold):

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never; / Never was time it was not; End and Beginning are dreams!" (Bhagavad Gita 2:20, Arnold)

  • In Katha 3 the atman is the "lord of the chariot"; for Advaita, that lord is Brahman itself, not one self among many.

Mission 4

Adhyasa: superimposition

  • Adhyasa: seeing one thing as another. Shankara opens his Brahma Sutra commentary with it.
  • Rope and snake: at dusk a rope looks like a snake. The snake is superimposed on the rope. You feel real fear; but there was only ever a rope.
  • Shell and silver: mother-of-pearl glints like silver.
  • In the same way we superimpose body, mind and individuality onto the self ("I am tall", "I am sad"), and the qualities of the self onto body and mind.
  • Advaitins call the power by which the one Brahman appears as many maya.
  • Ignorance (avidya) does not make the rope vanish; knowledge removes the snake. So, for Shankara, liberation is by jnana (knowledge).

Mission 4

Three levels of reality

  • Advaitins distinguish three levels:
  • Illusory (pratibhasika): dreams, the snake in the rope. Real only while the mistake lasts.
  • Mundane, empirical (vyavaharika): the everyday world of people, gods, karma, dharma, science and worship. Fully real for us: you must still keep your promises and avoid the bus.
  • Ultimate (paramarthika): Brahman alone.
  • Each level is cancelled only by the one above: waking cancels the dream; knowing Brahman cancels the separateness of the world.
  • Shankara himself mostly contrasts two standpoints (everyday and ultimate); later Advaitins set out three.

Mission 5

Karma: action and its results

  • Karma means action, and the results that action brings.
  • Every intentional act (in deed, word or thought) leaves an impression that will bear fruit, now or in a later life.
  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad IV.4.5 (paraphrase): as a person acts, so they become; doing good, one becomes good; doing evil, one becomes evil. What a person desires shapes what they do.
  • Karma is not fate: today's choices make tomorrow's karma.
  • Karma is not a judge's sentence: most Hindus see it as a moral law of cause and effect, like a seed growing into a plant.
Mission 5Read the source

Samsara: the cycle of lives

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and by desire and ignorance.

> "To man's frame / As there come infancy and youth and age, / So come there raisings-up and layings-down / Of other and of other life-abodes, / Which the wise know, and fear not." (Gita 2:13, Arnold)

> "Some Jivas (individual Souls) enter wombs to be embodied; others go into immovable forms, according to their deeds and knowledge." (Katha 5:7, Paramananda)

  • What travels? The atman, with a subtle body (sukshma sharira) that carries the karmic impressions.

Mission 5

Three kinds of karma

  • Sanchita (accumulated): the whole store of karma from past lives, not yet bearing fruit. Arrows in the quiver.
  • Prarabdha (fruit-bearing): the part of that store that has begun to work out in this life: body, family, lifespan. An arrow already shot: it cannot be called back.
  • Kriyamana or agami (karma in the making): karma made now by present acts. The arrow being fitted to the bow: still in your hands.
  • Liberation, on the common Vedanta account: knowledge burns sanchita, stops new kriyamana sticking; prarabdha runs its course until this body dies.

Mission 5

Realms and types of rebirth

  • Rebirth can be as a god (deva), a human, an animal, or an immovable form such as a plant (Katha 5:7). Tradition speaks of 8,400,000 forms of life.
  • Realms (lokas): heavenly worlds (svarga), the earth, and hell-worlds (naraka). Time in heaven or hell is temporary: when the karma is used up, the soul is born again.
  • Chandogya Upanishad V.10.7 (paraphrase): those of good conduct soon enter a good birth; those of bad conduct a low birth, even as an animal.
  • So neither heaven nor hell is the final goal. The goal is moksha: release from the whole cycle (see Mission 6).
Mission 5

Why a human birth matters

  • Animals and other beings mostly live out their karma; they do not choose with full moral awareness.
  • Humans can reflect, choose and act deliberately: they make new karma and can seek moksha.
  • So a human birth is precious and rare: an opportunity, not a reward to waste.
  • Gods also live out good karma in heaven, and must be reborn when it is spent.
Mission 5

All life is connected

  • An atman lives in every being, human or not; only the bodies and karma differ. (For Advaita it is one and the same self in all; for Ramanuja and Madhva each being has its own.)

> "To him who wisely sees, / The Brahman with his scrolls and sanctities, / The cow, the elephant, the unclean dog, / The Outcast gorging dog's meat, are all one." (Gita 5:18, Arnold)

  • Arnold's Victorian wording reflects the prejudice of the verse's setting against those outside the varnas; the verse's point is that the wise see the same self in all, whatever society's ranking.
  • Any being may have been, or become, a human, and the reverse: life is one family.
  • This supports ahimsa (non-violence), vegetarianism for many Hindus, and care for animals and the earth.
  • Your spec: "the interconnectedness of all life".
Mission 5

Good karma still binds

  • Good deeds bring good results, but a good result is still a result: it ties the soul to more births, even in heaven. A golden chain still binds.
  • Sakam karma: action done with desire for its fruit (reward, praise, heaven). It binds.
  • Nishkam karma: action done without desire for its fruit, as duty or as offering to God. It does not bind.
  • Karma yoga: the path of selfless action; purifies the mind and leads towards liberation.
  • Your spec: good conduct with reference to karma and karma yoga (selfless effort).

Mission 5Evaluate

Is karma fair?

  • Claim: Karma is a just way of explaining why lives turn out differently.
  • For: Everyone receives what their own acts produce: no favourites, no randomness.
  • For: It gives a reason to act well, and hope: the future is not fixed.
  • Against: No one remembers past lives; can it be just to suffer for what you cannot recall?
  • Against: It can be misused to blame the poor, the sick or the disabled for their condition; many Hindus reject that use.
  • Against: If my present is shaped by the past, how free am I? Response: prarabdha sets the situation, not the choice.
  • Judgement criteria: Does it fit experience? Does it leave room for free will? Does it lead to compassion or to blame?

Mission 6

Moksha: release from the cycle

  • Moksha means release or liberation: freedom from samsara, the cycle of birth, death and rebirth.
  • In Hindu teaching, what binds the self is karma, desire and avidya (ignorance of the true self).
  • Moksha is believed to end all three: no more rebirth, even in heaven.
  • It is the highest of the four aims of life (see Mission 1), and for many Hindus the purpose of life itself.
Mission 6

Free while still alive?

  • Jivanmukti: liberation while still in a body. Many traditions, especially Advaita, hold that the liberated live on until their fruit-bearing karma (prarabdha) runs out (see Mission 5).
  • The jivanmukta acts without selfish desire, is calm in pleasure and pain, and makes no new binding karma.
  • Videhamukti: liberation at death, when the body falls away.
  • Many theistic traditions see full liberation as coming after death, in God's presence.
Mission 6Diversity

What is moksha like?

  • Advaita (Shankara): realising that atman is Brahman. Nothing new is gained: ignorance ends. Like rivers entering the sea, losing name and form (Mundaka Upanishad 3.2.8, paraphrase).
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja): the soul keeps its identity and enjoys God's bliss for ever, serving him in his realm (Vaikuntha).
  • Dvaita (Madhva): the soul is eternally distinct and dependent; liberated souls enjoy bliss in God's presence.
  • Gaudiya Vaishnavism (Chaitanya; ISKCON today): loving service of Krishna (prema) is the highest goal, valued even above moksha.
  • Classical Samkhya and Yoga: kaivalya, the self's "aloneness", freed from matter (see Mission 3).
  • Your spec: "different understandings of the nature of moksha".

Mission 6

Many paths to one goal

  • Jnana yoga (knowledge): study, reflection and meditation that remove ignorance. Suits the reflective. Central for Advaita.
  • Karma yoga (action): duty done without desire for its fruit (Gita 2:47). Suits the active.
  • Bhakti yoga (devotion): loving worship of a personal God. Suits the emotional; open to all, whatever their birth.
  • Raja yoga (meditation, after Patanjali) is often counted as a fourth path.
  • The Gita weaves the paths together; most Hindus follow more than one (detail in a later lesson).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Four purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama, moksha; dharma frames artha and kama; moksha goes beyond all three.
  • Relative importance is debated: Manusmriti 2.224 praises the three together; Vedanta makes moksha the highest aim.

Mission 2

  • In Hindu belief, atman is the eternal self; the jivatman is that self embodied and reborn (Gita 2:22; the Katha chariot shows the self as lord, the intellect as driver).
  • Tat tvam asi (Chandogya VI) is read as identity (Shankara), part and whole (Ramanuja) or difference (Madhva).
  • Advaita: atman is Brahman. Samkhya: many purushas, separate from prakriti, with no Brahman.

Mission 3

  • Samkhya is dualist: many purushas (pure consciousness) and one prakriti (nature, made of sattva, rajas and tamas); mind and intellect belong to prakriti.
  • Liberation (kaivalya) comes through knowing the self is not prakriti; classical Samkhya needs no God, but Patanjali adds Ishvara and the eight limbs of yoga.
  • Samkhya and Advaita agree that the self is not body or mind; they disagree on whether there are many selves or one Brahman.

Mission 4

  • Vedanta interprets the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Gita; Shankara's Advaita holds that Brahman alone is real and atman is Brahman.
  • The world appears through maya and avidya, by adhyasa (superimposition, like a rope seen as a snake); there are illusory, mundane and ultimate levels of reality.

Mission 5

  • Karma: action and its fitting results, a law within the universal order; samsara: rebirth driven by karma, desire and ignorance (Gita 2:13; Katha 5:7).
  • Three kinds: accumulated (sanchita), fruit-bearing (prarabdha), karma in the making (kriyamana/agami).
  • Human birth is the chance to act freely and seek moksha; selfless action (nishkam karma, karma yoga) does not bind (Gita 2:47).

Mission 6

  • Moksha: release from samsara by ending karma, desire and ignorance; jivanmukti is liberation while alive.
  • Advaita: realising identity with Brahman; theistic traditions: eternal loving relationship with God.
