Mission 1

Mission 1: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Make a guess: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Mission 1

Genes plus surroundings

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: its observable traits.
  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment.
  • Same genotype, different surroundings → different phenotypes. That's the cuttings.
Quick check
?Identical twins have the same genotype. Two identical twins were raised apart, one in a sunny country on a good diet and the other in a cloudy country on a poor diet. Which of these traits are expected to be the same in both twins as adults: ABO blood group, adult height and skin colour?
Mission 1

Where variation comes from

  • Genetic sources: mutation (the primary source of new alleles), then meiosis and random fertilisation (new combinations).
  • Environmental sources: in animals, diet; in plants, light and minerals.
  • So a population varies widely: many allele combinations × many environments.
  • Variation from alleles is heritable; variation from the environment alone is non-heritable.
Quick check
?Seeds of one genetically uniform variety of bean were sown in two trays. Tray X was kept in darkness; tray Y in light. After two weeks, the seedlings in X had long, thin, pale stems and small yellow leaves. The seedlings in Y were short and green. Which statement is correct?

Mission 1

Many genes, one smooth curve

  • Polygenic inheritance: many genes, at different loci, affect one trait.
  • Worked example: three genes (A/a, B/b, C/c). Each capital allele adds one unit of height.
  • Cross AaBbCc × AaBbCc. Offspring carry 0–6 capital alleles.
  • Step 1: one gene alone → 3 classes, 1 : 2 : 1.
  • Step 2: two genes → 5 classes, 1 : 4 : 6 : 4 : 1.
  • Step 3: three genes → 7 classes, 1 : 6 : 15 : 20 : 15 : 6 : 1 (out of 64).
  • Step 4: add the environment. Each class spreads out; the gaps fill in → a smooth bell curve.
  • More genes → more classes → closer to continuous.
Quick check
?Human skin colour varies continuously across a population, whereas ABO blood group falls into four separate classes. Which explanation of this difference is correct?
Mission 1Practical

Investigating continuous variation

  • Specified practical: "Investigation of continuous variation in a species (including use of the Student's t test)".
  • Question: do leaves in shade differ in length from leaves in sun, on the same kind of plant?
  • Take random samples: e.g. pick plants and leaves using random numbers, not by eye.
  • Measure at least 20 leaves per sample (e.g. length, mm, with a ruler).
  • Calculate the mean and standard deviation of each sample.
  • Control: same species, leaves of the same age (e.g. the 4th leaf from the tip).
  • Null hypothesis: no significant difference between the two means.
  • t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2). Degrees of freedom = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • Illustrative data: shade x̄ = 86 mm, s = 12 mm; sun x̄ = 74 mm, s = 11 mm; n = 20 each.
  • t = 12 ÷ √(144/20 + 121/20) = 12 ÷ 3.64 = 3.30. df = 38; critical value (p = 0.05) = 2.02.
  • 3.30 > 2.02: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant.
Mission check

?Two true-breeding varieties of a crop plant differ in seed mass. Crossing them gives F1 plants with seeds of intermediate mass, all very similar. The F1 are interbred. Grown in the same field as the F1, the F2 show a wide, bell-shaped spread of seed mass, with very few at either parental extreme. Which explanation fits best?

Many genes plus the environment: that is why genetically identical cuttings still differ, and why a whole population varies even more.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 2

Chance in the gene pool

  • Gene pool: all the alleles of all the genes in a population.
  • Each generation's alleles are a sample of the last generation's.
  • Which individuals happen to breed, and which alleles go into the gametes, is partly chance.
  • Genetic drift: a random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, unrelated to whether the alleles help survival.
  • Drift can make an allele fixed (frequency 1) or lost (frequency 0), even a harmful or useful one.
  • When a river, mountains or distance split a population, no alleles move between the parts, so drift changes each part separately.
  • Say: "drift: chance change in allele frequency" — not: "drift: alleles drifting between populations".

  • Size 10: frequencies swing widely. By generation 20 every run has fixed or lost A.
  • Size 500: frequencies stay close to 0.50.
  • Why: a small sample is less representative. Toss a coin 10 times and you may get 8 heads; toss it 1,000 times and you get close to half.
  • So drift is important only in small populations.

Mission check

?A storm carries six birds of a mainland species to a distant island whose habitat is very like the mainland's. A hundred generations later, the island population is still small, and it differs greatly from the mainland in the frequencies of several alleles that have no known effect on survival. Which explanation is best supported?

Few individuals, little gene flow: chance alone can make a population's gene pool unlike any other.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 3

Three shapes of selection

  • Directional: one extreme is favoured. The mean shifts.
  • Stabilising: the intermediate (modal) phenotype is favoured; both extremes are selected against. The mean stays; the range narrows.
  • All of them change allele frequencies. Stabilising selection removes alleles for extreme phenotypes.
  • Say: "stabilising selection still changes allele frequencies" — not: "no change in the mean, so no evolution".
Mission 3

Named examples

  • Directional: antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  • Stabilising: human birth mass. In a large study of births at a London hospital, mortality was lowest at intermediate birth masses and higher for very light and very heavy babies (Karn and Penrose, 1951).
Quick check
?In a study of human births in the mid-twentieth century, babies of intermediate birth mass had the highest survival, while both very light and very heavy babies had higher mortality. Which type of selection does this show, and why?
Quick check
?Which statement about directional and stabilising selection is correct?
Mission 3

Sexual selection

  • Sexual selection: selection by success in getting mates.
  • Mate choice: usually females choose males whose traits signal overall fitness.
  • Competition: males fight or hold territories for access to females.
  • Example: male birds of paradise: elaborate plumage and complex courtship displays; females are dull.
  • The plumage costs energy and attracts predators. It spreads because the gain in mating success outweighs the survival cost.
  • Sexual selection explains the evolution of territorial and courtship behaviours.
  • Say: "females prefer the trait, so its alleles rise in frequency" — not: "it is artificial selection because the female chooses".
Quick check
?Male birds of paradise have elaborate, brightly coloured plumage and perform complex courtship displays, while females are dull. The plumage is costly to grow and makes males easier for predators to see. Which explains how this plumage evolved?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical songbird, clutch size (number of eggs) is heritable. Parents with very large clutches cannot feed all their chicks, and many starve; parents with very small clutches raise few young. Over many generations, the mean clutch size stays at 4 eggs, but nests with 2 or 6 eggs become rarer. Which statement is correct?

The mean stayed put, yet the population still evolved. Selection can hold the middle as well as push an extreme.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 4

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 4Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.
Quick check

Mission 4

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
Mission 4Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 4

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 4

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

MMMNNN
Observed178730391303

  • p(M) = (2 × 1787 + 3039) ÷ (2 × 6129) = 6613 ÷ 12 258 = 0.5395. q(N) = 0.4605.
  • Expected MM = 0.5395² × 6129 ≈ 1784. Expected MN = 2 × 0.5395 × 0.4605 × 6129 ≈ 3045.
  • Keep four decimal places in p and q, or expected counts drift.
  • Observed and expected are very close.
  • A χ² test on these numbers gives a value far below any critical value.
Quick check
?Use the MN data on the previous screen. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 4

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 4

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check
?A large population of a hypothetical mammal species is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at a gene with alleles R and r, with r at a frequency of 0.2; R is completely dominant to r. A new disease then appears, and all rr individuals die before reproducing, while RR and Rr individuals survive and reproduce equally well. Which prediction about the frequency of r over the next several generations is best supported?
Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Make a guess: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Mission 5

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then accept the null hypothesis.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 5

Which test?

  • Chi-squared (χ²): counts in categories, compared with expected counts.
  • Student's t-test: two means of continuous data (e.g. lengths, masses).
  • Correlation coefficient: is there a relationship between two variables measured on the same individuals or sites?
  • Ask: Counts or measurements? Difference or relationship?
  • t-tests and correlation coefficients are worked through in Mission 6.
Quick check
?A student measures the lengths of 20 leaves from plants growing in the sun and 20 from plants growing in the shade. She wants to know whether mean leaf length differs between the two. Which statistical test should she use?

Daisies counted in two areas of a field of equal size, one trampled and one not (illustrative data). Null hypothesis: no difference, so equal numbers are expected.

Mission 5

Correlation

  • Plot two measured variables on a scatter graph (scattergram).
  • Positive correlation: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. 0 means no correlation.
  • −0.85 is as strong as +0.85.
Mission 5

Correlation is not cause

  • A correlation, even a strong one, can arise from:
    • chance (small samples);
    • a third variable linked to both (a confounding variable);
    • cause running the other way.
  • Evidence for cause: a mechanism; controlled experiments or randomised controlled trials; the effect grows with the dose; many studies agree.
  • Say: "there is a positive correlation between X and Y" — not: "X causes Y", unless there is evidence for cause.
Quick check

Later studies found that short-sighted parents were more likely to leave a light on at night, and short sight is partly inherited. Which conclusion is best supported?

Mission 6: Leaves in the shade look bigger than leaves in the sun. How big must the gap be before you believe it?

Make a guess: Leaves in the shade look bigger than leaves in the sun. How big must the gap be before you believe it?

Mission 6

Student's t-test

  • Use it to compare two means of continuous data that are roughly normally distributed.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between the two means.
  • t gets bigger when the means are further apart, the SDs are smaller, or the samples are larger.
  • t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)
  • Degrees of freedom = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • t ≥ critical value at p = 0.05: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant.
Mission 6

Worked example: t-test

Limpet shell height on an exposed and a sheltered shore (illustrative data), 10 limpets each.

ShoreMean height, x̄ / mmSD, s / mmn
Exposed18.22.110
Sheltered15.62.410
  • Difference in means = 18.2 − 15.6 = 2.6 mm.
  • s1²/n1 = 2.1² ÷ 10 = 0.441. s2²/n2 = 2.4² ÷ 10 = 0.576.
  • √(0.441 + 0.576) = √1.017 = 1.008.
  • t = 2.6 ÷ 1.008 = 2.58.
  • Degrees of freedom = 10 + 10 − 2 = 18. Critical value at p = 0.05: 2.10.
  • 2.58 > 2.10: reject the null hypothesis. Shell height differs significantly between the shores.
  • Why it differs (wave action? predators?) needs a separate hypothesis and investigation.

Mission 6

Spearman's rank correlation

  • Tests whether two variables measured on the same individuals or sites are correlated.
  • Your board asks for "a correlation coefficient": Spearman's rank is one, and works for data that are not normally distributed.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no correlation between the two variables.
  • Rank each variable separately (smallest = 1). Tied values share the mean of their ranks.
  • d = difference between the two ranks for each pair.
  • rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1).
  • Compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs. Equal or above: significant correlation.

Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment; genetically identical organisms still differ.
  • Continuous variation comes from many genes with additive effects plus the environment; discontinuous variation from one or a few genes.

Mission 2

  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies; it matters most in small populations, where chance sampling has large effects.

Mission 3

  • Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilising selection favours the intermediate and narrows the range; both change allele frequencies.
  • Sexual selection favours traits that win mates, even at a cost to survival.

Mission 4

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.

Mission 5

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • A correlation coefficient measures direction and strength (−1 to +1); correlation alone never shows cause.

Mission 6

  • t-test: two means; t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2); df = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • Spearman's rank: rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1); compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs.
  • At or above the critical value at p = 0.05: significant. Never "proved".
