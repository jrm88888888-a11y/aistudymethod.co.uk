Mission 1

What demand means

  • Demand: the amount of a good buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a period of time.
  • Wanting is not enough: you must be able to pay.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls (other things staying the same).
  • A demand schedule is a table of prices and quantities. Sam's demand for bottles of juice. Illustrative data:

Worked example: draw the curve from the data

  • Step 1: Price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal axis. Label both, with units.
  • Step 2: choose even scales: price 0 to 3 in steps of 0.50; quantity 0 to 12 in steps of 2.
  • Step 3: plot each row as one point: (2 bottles, 2.50), (4, 2.00), (6, 1.50), (8, 1.00), (10, 0.50).
  • Step 4: join the points and label the line D.
  • The curve slopes down from left to right: a lower price, a larger quantity demanded.
  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.

Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along the demand curve.
  • Step 1: price falls → slide down the curve = extension of demand. Price rises → slide up = contraction of demand.
  • Step 2: a change in anything else → the whole curve shifts. Right (D1 → D2) = increase in demand. Left = decrease in demand.
  • Increase in demand: more is bought at every price.
  • Say: "the price cut caused an extension of demand" — not: "the price cut increased demand".
Mission 1

What shifts demand?

FactorDemand increases (shifts right) when…
Incomeincomes rise (true for most goods)
Price of a substitute (a rival good)the substitute gets dearer
Price of a complement (used together)the complement gets cheaper
Tastes and fashionthe good becomes more popular
Advertisinga successful advert campaign runs
Population (demographics)there are more buyers, or more of the age group who buy it
  • The opposite change shifts demand left.
  • Demographics: e.g. an ageing population raises demand for care homes and hearing aids.

Mission 2

What supply means

  • Supply: the amount of a good producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a period of time.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises (other things staying the same).
  • A supply schedule for a strawberry farm. Illustrative data:
Price per kgQuantity supplied (kg per week)
2100
3200
4300

  • Why more at a higher price? Each kg earns more, so producing more becomes profitable.
  • Extra output usually costs more to produce (overtime, poorer land). A higher price covers those extra costs.
Mission 2

Worked example: draw the firm's supply curve

  • Step 1: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal. Label both, with units, and use even scales: price 0 to 7 in steps of 1; quantity 0 to 600 in steps of 100.
  • Step 2: plot each row: (100 kg, 2), (200, 3), (300, 4), (400, 5), (500, 6).
  • Step 3: join the points and label the line S.
  • The curve slopes up from left to right: a higher price, a larger quantity supplied.
Quick check

Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • Step 1: price rises → slide up S = extension of supply. Price falls → slide down = contraction.
  • Step 2: a change in anything else → the whole curve shifts. Right (S1 → S2) = increase in supply; left = decrease in supply.
  • Increase in supply: more is offered at every price.
  • Say: "costs rose, so supply decreased" — not: "supply contracted" (contraction is only for a price change).

FactorSupply decreases (shifts left) when…Supply increases (shifts right) when…

Productivityoutput per worker fallsoutput per worker rises
Technology—better machines or methods arrive
Indirect taxesa tax on each unit is added or raisedthe tax is cut
Subsidiesa subsidy per unit is cut or removeda subsidy per unit is paid
Natural factorsbad weather, frost, floods or diseasegood weather
Number of firmsfirms leave the marketfirms join the market

Worked example: a tax shifts supply

  • Illustrative data: the government adds a tax of 1 per kg that the farm must pay.
  • To be just as willing to sell each amount, the farm now needs a price 1 higher.

Mission 2

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • A natural factor → supply decreases: S shifts left.
  • No change in price caused this. The price rise that followed was a result of the shift (see Mission 3), not its cause.

Mission 3

Where buyers meet sellers

  • A market is wherever buyers and sellers meet to trade: a shop, an auction, a website.
  • Put the demand and supply schedules together. Illustrative data: phone chargers.
PriceQuantity demandedQuantity supplied
5100500
4200400
3300300
2400200
1500100
  • Equilibrium price: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. Here: price 3, quantity 300.
  • On the diagram: where D crosses S. Label Pe and Qe.
  • At equilibrium the market clears: no unsold goods, no disappointed buyers.
Mission 3

Too high, too low: market forces

  • Step 1, price too high (4): supplied 400, demanded 200. Excess supply = 400 − 200 = 200 (a surplus). Unsold stock piles up → sellers cut the price.
  • Step 2, price too low (2): demanded 400, supplied 200. Excess demand = 400 − 200 = 200 (a shortage). Queues form → the price is bid up.
  • Step 3: both pressures stop only at Pe = 3. That is disequilibrium pushed back to equilibrium.
  • Excess demand or supply = the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied at that price.

When a curve shifts, the price moves

ChangePriceQuantity sold
Demand increases (D shifts right)risesrises
Demand decreases (D shifts left)fallsfalls
Supply increases (S shifts right)fallsrises
Supply decreases (S shifts left)risesfalls
  • The steps: (1) a curve shifts → (2) excess demand or supply at the old price → (3) the price moves → (4) a movement along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Say: "supply decreased, so price rose and demand contracted" — not: "price rose, so demand decreased" (a price change never shifts demand).

Mission 3

Real case: cocoa, 2022–2024

  • About 70% of the world's cocoa comes from West Africa, mainly Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana.
  • Weak harvests there meant world output fell short of the cocoa used for three seasons in a row (2021/22 to 2023/24).
  • Real data (IMF, global price of cocoa, yearly average, US dollars per tonne): 2022: 2,369 (about 2,400) · 2024: 7,391 (about 7,400).
  • The price more than tripled in two years.
  • Late 2024: hot weather with less rain threatened crops again.
  • On the diagram: supply decreased (S shifts left) → price rises, quantity sold falls.
Mission 3

Knock-on effects in related markets

  • Markets are linked. A change in one spreads to others.
  • Inputs: cocoa is used to make chocolate. Dearer cocoa → higher costs → supply of chocolate decreases → price of chocolate tends to rise.
  • Substitutes: dearer chocolate → demand for other sweets and snacks increases → their price and sales rise.
  • Complements (used together): if petrol gets much dearer, demand for large, fuel-hungry cars decreases.
Quick check
?Poor weather cuts the supply of butter, and its price rises sharply. Many shoppers use margarine instead of butter. What happens in the market for margarine?
Mission 3

The three jobs of price

  • Signal: a rising price tells producers that buyers want more.
  • Incentive: a higher price makes producing more profitable, so firms expand and new firms join.
  • Rationing: when a good is scarce, the price rises until only those willing and able to pay buy it.
  • This price mechanism answers the three questions:
  • What to produce: goods buyers pay for. How: the lowest-cost methods earn the most profit. For whom: those who can afford the price.
Quick check
?Tickets for a popular concert are scarce. The seller keeps raising the price until the number of people willing and able to pay equals the number of seats. Which job of price is this?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease (shift).

Mission 2

  • Supply slopes up: a higher price makes extra output profitable.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease in supply (shift).
  • A tax per unit shifts S left; a subsidy shifts S right.

Mission 3

  • Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied; above it a surplus, below it a shortage, and market forces push back.
  • A shift moves the equilibrium: more demand raises price and quantity; more supply lowers price and raises quantity.
  • Price signals, gives incentives and rations, which answers what, how and for whom to produce.
