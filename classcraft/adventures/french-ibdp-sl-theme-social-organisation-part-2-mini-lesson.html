Mission 1

Crime: what are we talking about?

  • French law sorts offences (les infractions) into three levels:
  • une contravention – a minor offence (e.g. a parking fine) → le tribunal de police
  • un délit – a mid-level offence (e.g. theft, assault) → le tribunal correctionnel
  • un crime – a very serious offence (e.g. murder, rape, armed robbery) → la cour d'assises (with a jury) or, since 2023 for some crimes, la cour criminelle départementale (judges only)
  • Watch out: un crime is not the everyday English "a crime". Shoplifting is un délit.
  • General word for crime as a social problem: la délinquance, la criminalité.
  • Your turn: is stealing a car un crime or un délit?
    Show answer

    un délit : le vol de voiture is judged by le tribunal correctionnel.

Mission 1

Vocabulary bank: from arrest to sentence

  • un(e) délinquant(e) – an offender
  • un(e) récidiviste / la récidive – a reoffender / reoffending
  • être arrêté(e) – to be arrested
  • le procès – the trial
  • le juge – the judge; le jury – the jury
  • être condamné(e) à … – to be sentenced to …
  • une peine – a sentence, a punishment
  • une peine de prison ferme – a prison sentence actually served
  • avec sursis – suspended
  • un(e) détenu(e) – a prisoner
  • la réinsertion – rehabilitation, return to society
  • la surpopulation carcérale – prison overcrowding
  • Watch out: une sentence is rare and formal; for "a sentence" say une peine.

Mission 1

Prisons: the numbers

  • 1 October 2026: 90,937 people held in French prisons (Ministry of Justice figures), a record.
  • One year earlier: 84,862, so a rise of about 7%.
  • There are far fewer places than prisoners: many cells hold extra people; some sleep on mattresses on the floor.
  • The Council of Europe ranks French overcrowding among the worst in Europe.
  • In 2020 the European Court of Human Rights ruled in J.M.B. c. France that conditions in several French prisons were degrading.
  • Useful phrases:
  • Les prisons sont surpeuplées. – Prisons are overcrowded.
  • Le taux d'occupation dépasse 100 %. – The occupancy rate is over 100%.
  • Les conditions de détention sont indignes. – Prison conditions are unworthy (degrading).
Mission 1

La prison : échec ou succès ? Argue both sides

  • Pour la prison (arguments its supporters make):
  • La prison protège la société contre les criminels dangereux. – Prison protects society from dangerous criminals.
  • Elle punit et dissuade : la peur de la prison empêche certains de passer à l'acte. – It punishes and deters.
  • Les victimes ont besoin que justice soit faite. – Victims need justice to be done.
  • Contre la prison (arguments its critics make):
  • La prison est l'école du crime. – Prison is a school for crime.
  • Les détenus perdent leur emploi, leur logement et leurs liens familiaux. – Prisoners lose jobs, homes and family ties.
  • La surpopulation rend la réinsertion presque impossible. – Overcrowding makes rehabilitation almost impossible.
  • Linkers for balance: d'un côté … de l'autre · certes … mais · en revanche
  • Your turn: link the two ideas with certes … mais.
    Show answer

    Certes, la prison protège la société, mais elle prépare mal la réinsertion.

Mission 1

D'autres sanctions: alternatives to prison

  • le travail d'intérêt général (TIG) – unpaid community work (created 1983).
  • le bracelet électronique – an electronic tag: the offender stays at home at set hours.
  • le sursis probatoire – a suspended sentence with conditions (treatment, training, compensating the victim).
  • une amende – a fine; le stage de citoyenneté – a citizenship course.
  • la justice restaurative – restorative justice: offender and victim (or other victims) meet, with trained staff.
  • Law of 23 March 2019 (in force March 2020): prison sentences of one month or less are banned; sentences of up to six months should in principle be served outside prison (e.g. under electronic tag).
  • Supporters: cheaper, keeps jobs and families, reduces overcrowding.
  • Critics: seen as too soft; tags and community work need staff to supervise them.
Mission 1

Two landmark choices

  • The death penalty (la peine de mort) was abolished by the law of 9 October 1981, carried by Justice Minister Robert Badinter.
  • Since 2007 the ban has been written into the Constitution.
  • Young offenders: since 30 September 2021, le Code de la justice pénale des mineurs applies.
  • Under-13s are presumed unable to understand what they did (présomption de non-discernement); the judge can decide otherwise.
  • The priority for minors is education, not punishment: l'éducatif prime sur le répressif.
  • Debate: some politicians want tougher sentences for minors; youth workers and many judges argue that education prevents reoffending better.
  • la majorité pénale – age of criminal majority (18 in France).
Mission 1

Model opinions to reuse

  • À mon avis, la prison devrait être réservée aux crimes les plus graves. – In my view, prison should be kept for the most serious offences.
  • Il est essentiel que les détenus soient préparés à leur sortie. – It is essential that prisoners are prepared for release. (il est essentiel que + subjunctive)
  • Si on investissait davantage dans la réinsertion, il y aurait moins de récidive. – If we invested more in rehabilitation, there would be less reoffending. (si + imperfect → conditional)
  • Bien que les peines alternatives coûtent moins cher, elles ne rassurent pas toujours le public. – Although alternative sentences cost less, they do not always reassure the public. (bien que + subjunctive)
  • Watch out: éventuellement = possibly, not "eventually" (finalement).

Mission 2

The right to vote in France

  • 1974: the voting age falls from 21 to 18 (law of 5 July 1974).
  • Since 1997, young French citizens have been registered automatically at 18.
  • Voting is not compulsory in France. In Belgium it is.
  • Blank votes (le vote blanc) have been counted separately since 2014, but they do not count towards the result.
  • Vocabulary:
  • le droit de vote – the right to vote
  • voter / aller aux urnes – to vote / go to the polls
  • s'inscrire sur les listes électorales – to register to vote
  • s'abstenir / l'abstention – to abstain / abstention
  • un électeur, une électrice – a voter
  • le scrutin – the ballot, the vote
  • Watch out: un homme / une femme politique = a politician (neutral). Un politicien often sounds negative.
Mission 2

Pour ou contre le vote à 16 ans ?

  • Comparisons: Austria lets 16-year-olds vote in national elections (since 2007).
  • Belgium let 16- and 17-year-olds vote in the 2024 European elections.
  • Pour (arguments supporters make):
  • À 16 ans, on peut travailler et payer des impôts : on devrait pouvoir voter. – At 16 you can work and pay tax; you should be able to vote.
  • Voter tôt crée une habitude de participation. – Voting early builds a habit.
  • Les décisions sur le climat engagent l'avenir des jeunes. – Climate decisions shape young people's future.
  • Contre (arguments opponents make):
  • À 16 ans, on manque encore de maturité et d'expérience. – At 16, people still lack maturity.
  • Les jeunes risquent d'être influencés par leurs parents ou les réseaux sociaux. – Young people may be influenced by parents or social media.
  • La majorité civile est à 18 ans : il faut rester cohérent. – Legal adulthood is 18; the rules should be consistent.

Mission 2

The numbers: who stays home?

  • 2022 presidential election, second round, abstention by age (Ipsos poll):
  • 18–24: 41% · 25–34: 38% · 60–69: 20% · 70+: 15%
  • So young people abstained almost three times as much as over-70s.
  • Among 18–24s who cast a valid vote, 61% chose Emmanuel Macron (so about 39% Marine Le Pen).
  • Reasons young non-voters give in surveys: « Ça ne changera rien », distrust of politicians, not feeling represented, being registered far from where they study.
  • Many young people vote intermittently: they skip some elections and turn out for those they see as decisive.
  • Phrase: Les jeunes s'abstiennent nettement plus que leurs aînés. – Young people abstain much more than their elders.

Mission 2

Motivés ou démotivés ? Other ways to take part

  • Many researchers say young people are not apathetic: they engage differently.
  • signer une pétition (en ligne) – sign a (online) petition
  • manifester / participer à une marche pour le climat – demonstrate / join a climate march (e.g. school strikes of 2019)
  • faire du bénévolat dans une association – volunteer
  • boycotter une marque – boycott a brand
  • faire un service civique – a paid civic service placement for 16–25s (since 2010), 6–12 months
  • être élu(e) au conseil municipal des jeunes / au conseil de la vie lycéenne – be elected to a youth council / school council
  • s'exprimer sur les réseaux sociaux – speak out on social media
  • Critics reply: a "like" or a petition is not the same as voting; protest alone does not make laws.
  • Your turn: "I prefer to sign a petition rather than vote." → Je préfère signer une pétition plutôt que de voter.
Mission 2

Quel avenir pour la politique ?

  • Ideas debated to bring young people (and others) back to the polls:
  • le vote obligatoire – compulsory voting, as in Belgium
  • la reconnaissance du vote blanc – counting blank votes in the result
  • le vote électronique – online voting (security concerns)
  • les conventions citoyennes – citizens' assemblies drawn by lot, e.g. la Convention citoyenne pour le climat (2019–2020, 150 citizens)
  • le référendum d'initiative citoyenne (RIC) – citizens' referendums, a demand of the gilets jaunes (2018–2019)
  • Supporters: more legitimacy, more voices heard.
  • Critics: compulsory voting forces uninformed votes; referendums can oversimplify complex issues.

  • Loin d'être indifférents, les jeunes s'engagent autrement. – Far from being indifferent, young people get involved in other ways.
  • Ce n'est pas la politique qui ennuie les jeunes, c'est la façon dont on la fait. – It is not politics that bores young people, it is the way it is done.
  • Watch out: la politique = politics or a policy; le politique (rare) = the political sphere.

Mission 3

The right to strike

  • 1864: going on strike stops being a crime.
  • 1884: trade unions become legal (loi Waldeck-Rousseau).
  • 1946: the right to strike is written into the Preamble of the Constitution: « Le droit de grève s'exerce dans le cadre des lois qui le réglementent. » It still applies today.
  • Limits:
  • In public services, unions must give five days' notice (un préavis de grève).
  • Since 2007, transport staff must say 48 hours ahead if they will strike.
  • Since 2008, primary schools must offer a minimum childcare service on strike days.
  • Strikers are not paid for the days they strike.
  • faire grève / être en grève – to strike / be on strike (no article) · un(e) gréviste – a striker
  • Watch out: une manifestation = a demonstration, a protest march. Manifester = to demonstrate; un(e) manifestant(e) = a protester.
Mission 3

The unions: few members, real power

  • Main union confederations: CGT, CFDT, FO, CFE-CGC, CFTC; also UNSA, Solidaires and FSU (strong in education).
  • Membership is low: about 10% of employees (DARES, 2019): about 18% in the public sector, 8% in the private sector.
  • Influence is much wider than membership:
  • unions negotiate agreements by sector that cover almost all private-sector employees;
  • workplace elections measure their weight. Since 2017 the CFDT has been ahead of the CGT in the private sector;
  • they call strikes and days of protest (journées de mobilisation).
  • L'intersyndicale: all the main unions acting together, as in 2023.
  • Your turn: "The unions called a day of action."
    Show answer

    Les syndicats ont appelé à une journée de mobilisation.

Mission 3

Do strikes work? Some victories

  • 1936: a wave of strikes and factory occupations under the Front populaire government. The accords de Matignon bring pay rises; new laws give two weeks' paid holiday and the 40-hour week.
  • May 1968: student protests, then a general strike by millions of workers. The Grenelle talks (les accords de Grenelle) end with the minimum wage raised by 35%.
  • 1995: weeks of public-sector strikes. The government drops its pension reform for public-sector special schemes.
  • 2006: students and unions protest against the CPE, a contract making it easier to dismiss under-26s. The government abandons it in April 2006, although Parliament had passed it.
  • 2018: the gilets jaunes (yellow vests), organised online without unions, occupy roundabouts. The fuel-tax rise is cancelled and about €10 billion of measures follow.
  • 2024: farmers block motorways; the government drops a planned tax rise on farm diesel.
Mission 3

... and the limits: pensions, 2023

  • The reform: legal retirement age from 62 to 64.
  • January–June 2023: 14 national days of action called by the united unions.
  • 16 March 2023: the government uses article 49.3 of the Constitution. The bill passes without a final vote in the National Assembly, unless a motion of censure brings the government down. The motion fails by 9 votes.
  • 14 April 2023: the Constitutional Council approves the core of the law. It is promulgated.
  • Afterwards: the 2024 elections left no majority in the Assembly. In October 2025 the government proposed suspending the age rise to avoid censure. In December 2025 Parliament voted it: the age is frozen at 62 years and 9 months until January 2028, after the 2027 presidential election.
  • Protest supporters call this a delayed victory. Others see it as a parliamentary deal.

Mission 3

Who holds power: the street or the ballot box?

  • Representative view: voters elect a President and a Parliament; they decide. Prime Minister Jean-Pierre Raffarin (2003): « Ce n'est pas la rue qui gouverne. »
  • Social view: between elections, strikes and protests let citizens correct decisions they never voted for. The Constitution protects both rights.
  • Other players:
  • the Constitutional Council, which can strike out a law;
  • the référendum d'initiative partagée: in 2023 opponents tried to force a referendum on pensions. The Council rejected both requests.
  • Counting the crowd is political too. 7 March 2023: 1.28 million marchers selon le ministère de l'Intérieur, 3.5 million selon la CGT.
  • selon la police / selon les organisateurs – according to the police / organisers
Mission 3

Attitudes: argue both sides

  • For strikes and protests (arguments supporters make):
  • La grève est souvent le seul moyen de se faire entendre. – Striking is often the only way to be heard.
  • Sans les grèves de 1936, les salariés n'auraient pas obtenu les congés payés. – Without the 1936 strikes, workers would not have won paid holidays.
  • Against (arguments critics make):
  • Les usagers paient le prix d'un conflit qui ne les concerne pas. – Service users pay the price of a dispute that is not theirs.
  • Une minorité ne devrait pas pouvoir bloquer tout un pays. – A minority should not be able to block a whole country.
  • Les grèves coûtent cher à l'économie. – Strikes are costly for the economy.
  • Attitudes depend on the sector and the cause. In 2023 polls repeatedly found most French people opposed to the reform, even when transport was disrupted.
  • Linkers: Les partisans de … affirment que … · Leurs détracteurs répondent que … · Il n'en reste pas moins que …
  • Your turn: "Critics say that strikes are costly for the economy."
    Show answer

Mission 4

From war to union

  • 9 May 1950: French foreign minister Robert Schuman, with Jean Monnet, proposes pooling French and German coal and steel. 9 May is now Europe Day.
  • 1951: six countries found the European Coal and Steel Community (la CECA); 1957: the Treaty of Rome creates the European Economic Community.
  • 1963: the Élysée Treaty of Franco-German friendship (de Gaulle and Adenauer).
  • 1979: first direct elections to the European Parliament (seat: Strasbourg).
  • 1992: the Maastricht Treaty creates the European Union and European citizenship; French voters approve it by referendum with 51%.
  • 2002: euro notes and coins replace the franc.
  • 2005: French voters reject the European Constitutional Treaty (55% no).
  • 2020: the United Kingdom leaves the EU; 27 member states remain.
Mission 4

What European citizenship gives

  • Every national of an EU country is automatically an EU citizen. It adds to national citizenship; it does not replace it.
  • Rights:
  • to move, live, study and work in any member state;
  • to vote and stand in municipal and European elections where you live, even if you are not a national;
  • to protection from any EU country's embassy outside the EU if your own has none there;
  • to petition the European Parliament and complain to the European Ombudsman;
  • a European Citizens' Initiative: 1 million signatures from at least 7 countries can ask the Commission to propose a law.
  • Watch out: la citoyenneté – citizenship; un citoyen / une citoyenne – a citizen. La nationalité is not the same as la citoyenneté européenne.

Mission 4

Opportunities for young people

  • Erasmus (1987; now Erasmus+): millions of students, apprentices and young workers have studied or trained in another country.
  • DiscoverEU: free rail passes for some 18-year-olds to travel around Europe.
  • No visa or work permit to study or work in another member state; no roaming charges on phones since 2017.
  • The UK after Brexit:
  • the UK left Erasmus+ and set up its own Turing scheme (2021);
  • the Irish government funded Erasmus+ places for students at Northern Ireland universities;
  • in December 2025 the UK announced it would rejoin Erasmus+ from 2027.
  • Northern Ireland: people born there are entitled to Irish citizenship, and so to EU citizenship (Good Friday Agreement, 1998).
  • un séjour à l'étranger · un échange · un stage – internship · la mobilité
Mission 4

France in Europe today

  • France is a founding member; Franco-German cooperation (le couple franco-allemand) is often seen as the EU's engine.
  • Strasbourg hosts the European Parliament and the Council of Europe (a separate organisation, which oversees the European Court of Human Rights).
  • 2024 European elections in France: turnout just over 50%; the list led by Jordan Bardella (Rassemblement National) came first.
  • French parties' positions, by policy:
  • those wanting closer integration (e.g. Renaissance and allies);
  • those wanting powers returned to nations (e.g. the Rassemblement National, which now proposes a "Europe of nations");
  • those wanting to change EU treaties on economic rules (e.g. La France insoumise).
  • l'abstention · un(e) député(e) européen(ne) – MEP · la souveraineté – sovereignty

Mission 4

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages often cited:
  • peace between former enemies;
  • the single market and the euro (no exchange costs, easier trade);
  • freedom to travel, study and work;
  • common action on climate, health (e.g. joint vaccine purchases in 2021) and trade.
  • Disadvantages often cited:
  • a democratic deficit: decisions seen as distant from voters;
  • loss of national sovereignty;
  • bureaucracy and rules seen as costly for farmers and small firms;
  • France pays more into the EU budget than it receives directly.
  • 2024–2026: French farmers protested against EU rules and the EU–Mercosur trade agreement (signed in January 2026).
Mission 4

Argue both sides

  • Is European citizenship a real advantage for young people?
  • Grâce à l'Union européenne, les jeunes peuvent étudier et travailler dans 27 pays sans visa. – Thanks to the EU, young people can study and work in 27 countries without a visa.
  • Beaucoup de jeunes ne connaissent pas leurs droits et ne votent pas aux élections européennes. – Many young people don't know their rights and don't vote in European elections.
  • More Europe or less?
  • Supporters of closer union say: Face à la Chine et aux États-Unis, les pays européens sont plus forts ensemble. – Facing China and the US, European countries are stronger together.
  • Supporters of a "Europe of nations" say: Les décisions importantes doivent être prises au niveau national, au plus près des citoyens. – Important decisions should be taken nationally, closer to citizens.
  • Linkers: Si l'on considère … · Il est vrai que …, néanmoins … · Somme toute – All things considered
