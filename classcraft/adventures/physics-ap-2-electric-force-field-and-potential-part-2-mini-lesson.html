Mission 1Topics 10.2, 10.3, 10.5

Mission 1: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Make a guess: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Mission 1

Why the field inside is zero

  • A metal has free electrons that move at the slightest force.
  • Electrostatic equilibrium: no charge is moving.
  • Any field inside the metal would push the free electrons, so they would move.
  • So in equilibrium: E = 0 everywhere inside the metal.
  • Electrons rearrange to make this happen in a tiny fraction of a second.
Quick check
?Predict first. A solid metal sphere on an insulating stand is touched at one point by a negatively charged rod, which leaves excess electrons on it. Once the sphere has reached electrostatic equilibrium, where is its excess charge?
Mission 1

Where the charge goes

  • E = 0 inside → no net charge inside. All excess charge sits on the outer surface.
  • Positive excess charge = a shortage of electrons on the surface.
  • Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface. A sideways part would push surface charges along.
  • Sphere: charge spreads evenly; field lines are radial outside, as if from a point charge at the center.
  • Hollow sphere: the same outside. No field lines in the metal or in the empty cavity.
Mission 1

Potential of a charged conductor

  • E = 0 inside → no work moving charge inside → V is the same everywhere in and on the conductor.
  • The surface is an equipotential, so the field meets it at 90°.
  • Sphere, radius R, charge Q: outside, V = kQ/r and E = kQ/r². Inside: V = kQ/R (constant), E = 0.
  • Example: R = 0.10 m, Q = +4.0 nC (k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²).
  • Inside and on the surface: V = 360 V, E = 0. Just outside: E = 3.6 × 10³ N/C.
  • At r = 0.30 m: V = 120 V, E = 400 N/C.
  • The same holds for a hollow sphere: V inside the cavity = surface value.
Quick check
?A metal sphere of radius 0.10 m, far from other charged objects, carries a charge of +4.0 × 10⁻⁸ C. Use k = 9.0 × 10⁹ N·m²/C². What is the magnitude of the electric field at a point 0.20 m from the sphere's surface?
Quick check
?A solid plastic sphere has excess positive charge spread evenly throughout its volume. It is far from other charged objects, and its charge does not move. Which statement describes the electric field at a point inside the plastic, halfway between the sphere's center and its surface?
Mission 1

Insulators are different

  • In an insulator, charge cannot move freely. It stays where it was put.
  • Excess charge can be spread through the volume, not just on the surface.
  • So the field inside an insulator can be nonzero.
  • Uniformly charged plastic sphere: inside, E points away from the center (positive charge) and grows from zero at the center.
  • Outside a uniformly charged sphere, metal or plastic: E = kQ/r².
Mission check

?A solid lump of metal with a sharp point at one end carries excess charge and has settled. Which statement is correct?

Free electrons move until the field inside the metal is zero, whatever its shape; that puts all the excess charge on the outer surface, with the field ⟂ to it.

Mission 2Topics 10.2, 10.5

Mission 2: Two metal spheres, one big and one small, are joined by a wire. Which ends up with more charge?

Make a guess: Two metal spheres, one big and one small, are joined by a wire. Which ends up with more charge?

Quick check
?Two metal spheres of different radii are far apart. One is charged and the other is uncharged. They are then connected by a long thin conducting wire. After the charges stop moving, which quantity is the same for the two spheres?
Mission 2

Conductors in contact share charge

  • Joined conductors form one conductor: in equilibrium it is one equipotential.
  • Electrons flow until the surfaces are at the same potential.
  • Two spheres far apart, joined by a thin wire: kQ1/R1 = kQ2/R2, so Q ∝ R.
  • The bigger sphere gets more charge.
  • Example: 9.0 nC shared between spheres of radius 0.050 m and 0.10 m.
  • Q1 : Q2 = 1 : 2 → 3.0 nC and 6.0 nC. Both at V = (9.0 × 10⁹)(3.0 × 10⁻⁹)/0.050 = 540 V.
  • Equal potential, not equal charge.
Quick check
?An isolated metal sphere of radius R is charged to a potential V0, taking the potential to be zero far away. It is then connected by a long, thin wire to a distant, uncharged metal sphere of radius 3R. The wire carries a negligible charge. What is the potential of the first sphere once equilibrium is reached?
Mission 2

Grounding

  • Grounding: connecting an object to the Earth with a conductor.
  • The Earth is a huge neutral conductor: it can give or take almost any amount of charge.
  • An isolated charged conductor, grounded (no other charges nearby), ends almost neutral.
  • Positive object: electrons flow up from the Earth. Negative object: electrons flow down to the Earth.
Quick check
?A metal sphere on an insulating stand has a charge of +5.0 nC. It is then connected to Earth by a metal wire, with no other charged objects nearby. Which statement describes what happens?
Mission check

?Sphere A, of radius R, is charged until its potential is V0 (zero far away). Sphere B, of radius 3R and far from A, is separately charged until it is also at V0. The two spheres are then joined by a long, thin wire. What happens?

Joined conductors end at one potential, so Q ∝ R: the big sphere takes more of the charge. Charge flows only while the potentials differ.

Mission 3Topic 10.6

Mission 3: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Make a guess: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Quick check
?Predict first. An uncharged parallel-plate capacitor is connected to a battery and left until it is fully charged. Which statement correctly describes how the capacitor becomes charged?
Mission 3

What a capacitor is

  • Two conducting plates, separated by an insulator (air, a vacuum or a plastic film).
  • Connect a cell: electrons are pulled off one plate, pass round the external circuit, and are pushed onto the other plate.
  • No charge crosses the gap.
  • One plate ends at +Q, the other at −Q. Net charge: zero.
  • "The charge on a capacitor" means Q, the charge on each plate.
Mission 3

Capacitance

  • C = Q/ΔV: the charge on each plate per unit potential difference across the plates.
  • Unit: the farad. 1 F = 1 C/V: a capacitor storing 1 C on each plate for every 1 V across it.
  • 1 F is huge. Real values: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • For one capacitor, Q ∝ ΔV. C is fixed by its construction, not by the potential difference you apply.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.
  • Q = CΔV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C on each plate.
  • Electrons moved from one plate to the other: 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹⁶.
  • Charge it to 18 V instead: Q doubles; C stays 470 μF.
Quick check
?A parallel-plate capacitor is connected to an ideal 12 V battery. When the capacitor is fully charged, its positive plate carries a charge of 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ C. What is the capacitance of the capacitor?
Mission 3

What it stores: energy

  • Separating +Q from −Q takes work. That work is stored as electric potential energy.
  • The stored energy equals the work done by an external agent to separate the charge.
  • Graph of ΔV against Q: a straight line through the origin (ΔV = Q/C).
  • Moving a small charge ΔQ when the potential difference is V takes work V·ΔQ: a thin strip under the line.
  • Add the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle.
  • UC = ½QΔV
Mission 3

Three forms, one example

  • Use Q = CΔV: UC = ½QΔV = ½C(ΔV)² = Q²/(2C).
  • The 470 μF capacitor at 9.0 V: energy = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = 1.9 × 10⁻² J.
  • At 18 V: the charge doubles and the potential difference doubles, so the energy is four times as much (7.6 × 10⁻² J).
  • Why ½? The battery moves charge Q through potential difference ΔV and transfers QΔV. The capacitor keeps ½QΔV; the other ½QΔV is dissipated as heat in the circuit's resistance.
Quick check
?A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a potential difference of 12 V. How much energy does it store?
Mission check

?The graph shows the magnitude Q of the charge on each plate of a capacitor as a function of the potential difference ΔV across it. What electric potential energy is stored in the capacitor when ΔV = 6.0 V?

The plates' charges cancel, so a capacitor stores no net charge. It stores energy: the work done separating +Q from −Q, equal to ½QV.

Mission 4Topics 10.1, 10.6

Mission 4: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Make a guess: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Mission 4

What sets C for parallel plates

  • Larger plate area A: the same charge is spread more thinly, so the field and potential difference are smaller. C ∝ A.
  • Smaller gap d → C ∝ 1/d.
  • C = κε0A/d (κ = 1 for air).
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²): the permittivity of free space.
  • Plate thickness and the charge already stored do not matter.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Air-filled plates, each 0.040 m², 1.0 mm apart.
  • C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ F (350 pF).
  • Connected to 12 V: Q = CΔV = 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ C.
  • Uniform field between the plates: E = Q/(κε0A) = 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ (1 × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040) = 1.2 × 10⁴ V/m. Same as ΔV/d.
Quick check
?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has a capacitance of 16 pF. A second air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has plates with three times the area of the first capacitor's plates, and its plates are twice as far apart. What is the capacitance of the second capacitor?
Mission 4

Dielectrics

  • Fill the gap with an insulator, a dielectric: the capacitance increases.
  • Permittivity measures how strongly a material polarizes in an electric field. Free space: the constant ε0. Every material: a different, larger value.
  • κ, the dielectric constant, is how many times larger C becomes when the material fills the gap.
  • Each multiplies C by about: air 1.0006 · polyethylene 2.3 · glass 5 to 10 · water 80.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a charged parallel-plate capacitor, disconnected from its battery, with a slab of dielectric filling the space between its plates. Which statement correctly describes the electric field produced inside the dielectric by the dielectric's own induced charges?
Mission 4

Why a dielectric helps

  • Charges in a dielectric cannot move freely through it. In a field, each molecule's charges shift slightly: the material polarizes.
  • Result: a thin layer of − charge faces the + plate, and + charge faces the − plate.
  • These induced charges make a field opposite to the plates' field. The field in the dielectric is weaker.
  • Same Q, weaker E → smaller potential difference across the gap → C = Q/ΔV is larger.
  • Still connected to the cell? The potential difference is fixed, so more charge flows on.
  • With a dielectric, E = Q/(κε0A).
Mission check

?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor is charged and then disconnected from the supply. Its plates are then pulled apart to twice their separation. What happens to the charge on each plate, the potential difference across the capacitor and the energy it stores?

Bigger plates, a smaller gap or a dielectric all raise C.

At a fixed potential difference, Q = CΔV: more C stores more charge.

Mission 5Topics 10.6, 10.7

Mission 5: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Make a guess: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Mission 5

The force on a charge in a field

  • F = qE, the same everywhere in a uniform field.
  • Positive charge: force along the field (toward the negative plate).
  • Negative charge: force against the field (toward the positive plate).
  • Between parallel plates: |E| = ΔV/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V/m.
  • Electron or proton there: |F| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N. Same size, opposite directions.
Mission 5

Same force, different acceleration

  • Constant force → constant acceleration: a = F/m.
  • Electron: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m/s².
  • Proton: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.9 × 10¹² m/s².
  • The electron's mass is about 1/1800 of the proton's, so its acceleration is about 1800 times greater.
  • Gravity is negligible: the electron's weight is about 10⁻²⁹ N, some 10¹⁴ times smaller than 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N.
Quick check
?Two horizontal parallel plates, 2.5 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 150 V between them. What is the magnitude of the acceleration of an electron between the plates? Gravity is negligible. Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission 5

Accelerating through a potential difference

  • Work done by the field = |q||ΔV| = gain in kinetic energy.
  • From rest: K = ½mv² = |q||ΔV|. The field does work; UE falls by the same amount.
  • Worked example: electron through 2000 V.
  • v = √(2e|ΔV|/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.65 × 10⁷ m/s.
  • That is about 9% of the speed of light, so ½mv² is still accurate.
Quick check
?A proton (charge +e, mass m) and an alpha particle (charge +2e, mass 4m) each start from rest and are accelerated by the electric force alone through the same potential difference. What is the ratio of the alpha particle's final kinetic energy Kα to the proton's final kinetic energy Kp?
Quick check
?A proton enters the uniform electric field between two oppositely charged, horizontal parallel plates, moving parallel to the plates. Gravitational effects are negligible. Which statement describes the proton's motion while it is between the plates?
Mission 5

Deflection: a projectile in an electric field

  • Particle enters at right angles to the field (parallel to the plates).
  • Along the plates: no force → constant velocity v.
  • Across the plates: constant force → constant acceleration a = F/m.
  • Path between the plates: a parabola, like a ball thrown horizontally.
  • Time between plates: t = L/v. Sideways shift: y = ½at².
  • After the plates: no force → straight line.
Mission 5

Worked example: deflection

  • Electron at 2.65 × 10⁷ m/s enters midway between plates 5.0 cm long, 2.0 cm apart, 400 V.
  • a = e|ΔV|/(md) = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m/s² (as for the electron on the earlier screen).
  • t = L/v = 0.050 / 2.65 × 10⁷ = 1.9 × 10⁻⁹ s.
  • y = ½at² = ½ × 3.5 × 10¹⁵ × (1.9 × 10⁻⁹)² = 6.3 mm. Less than 10 mm, so it misses the plate.
  • Sideways velocity at exit: vy = at = 6.6 × 10⁶ m/s.
  • A proton at the same speed: about 1800 times less deflection.
Quick check
?In the worked example, the electron left the plates deflected sideways by a distance y. The potential difference across the plates is now doubled, and the electron enters at twice the speed. What is its sideways deflection at exit now?
Mission check

?A proton and an alpha particle are placed, one at a time, at the same point in a uniform electric field. The alpha particle has twice the charge of the proton and about four times its mass. Gravity is negligible. What is the ratio (acceleration of the alpha particle) / (acceleration of the proton)?

Same field, acceleration = charge × E ÷ mass. The electron and proton feel equal forces, but the electron has about 1/1800 of the mass: about 1800 times the acceleration, so it swerves far more.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • In a conductor in equilibrium: E = 0 inside the metal, excess charge on the outer surface, field ⟂ to the surface; a charged sphere acts outside like a point charge.
  • The whole conductor is one equipotential: V inside = V at the surface = kQ/R for a sphere.
  • Insulators keep charge where it is put, so the field inside them can be nonzero.

Mission 2

  • Conductors in contact share charge until they are at the same potential; joined spheres have Q ∝ R.
  • Grounding lets electrons flow to or from the Earth: an isolated charged conductor, grounded, ends almost neutral.

Mission 3

  • A capacitor's plates carry +Q and −Q; C = Q/ΔV, in farads, set by construction, not by the potential difference.
  • UC = ½QΔV: the work done separating the charge.

Mission 4

  • C = κε0A/d; E = Q/(κε0A); a polarized dielectric makes an opposing field.

Mission 5

  • In a uniform field F = qE (positive charges along E, negative against it), so a = qE/m is constant; light particles accelerate most.
  • Through a potential difference ΔV, a charge gains kinetic energy |q||ΔV|.
  • Entering at right angles: constant velocity along the plates, constant acceleration across them, so a parabola.
