Media

📍 Where this sits: this is Component 01, Section B, Option 3. In Component 01 you sit the compulsory Section A (Introducing socialisation, culture and identity) and then ONE option from Section B: Families and relationships, Youth subcultures, or Media. Check which one your school teaches — you only answer on one.

This lesson covers the two OCR key questions for this option: How are different social groups represented in the media? and What effect do the media have on audiences?

The examiner's trap. Weak answers assume the media obviously have huge effects. Fifty years of audience research says the relationship is far more complicated — and OCR explicitly names active audience models. Never assert an effect you cannot defend.

Answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

Context · ownership and control

Who owns the media — and does it matter?

  • Concentration of ownership. A small number of transnational conglomerates own a very large share of global media. Horizontal integration means owning many outlets at the same level (several newspapers); vertical integration means owning the whole chain — production, distribution and exhibition.
  • The Marxist (instrumentalist) view. Ralph Miliband (The State in Capitalist Society, 1969) argued the media are an instrument of ruling-class control, transmitting ideas that legitimate inequality and manufacture false consciousness. Owners can and do intervene.
  • The neo-Marxist (hegemonic) view. More subtle, and better evidenced. The Glasgow University Media Group (Bad News, 1976, and its successors) content-analysed television news coverage of industrial disputes and argued that reporting systematically favoured management framings — strikes were reported as causing disruption, rather than management decisions being reported as causing strikes. Crucially, GUMG did not claim a conspiracy: journalists are overwhelmingly middle class, work to routines, rely on official sources, and so unconsciously reproduce a dominant worldview. Following Gramsci, this is hegemony — consent, not command.
  • The pluralist view. John Whale argued that media content reflects audience demand: a paper that loses touch with its readers loses its readers. Owners are constrained by the market, by professional norms, by regulation and by competition. On this view, the diversity of media proves that power is dispersed.

Evaluating pluralism: 'the audience chooses' is only meaningful if the audience has a real range to choose from. If ownership is concentrated and the entry costs are enormous, then consumer choice operates within limits somebody else has set. The digital age cuts both ways: it has lowered the cost of publishing dramatically — and concentrated attention into a handful of privately owned platforms.

KQ1 · gender

Representing gender: masculinity and femininity

  • Symbolic annihilation. Gaye Tuchman (1978) argued that women are symbolically annihilated by the media — they are omitted, trivialised or condemned. Where women appear, they appear disproportionately in domestic and romantic roles, and are judged by appearance in a way men are not.
  • The male gaze. Laura Mulvey ('Visual Pleasure and Narrative Cinema', 1975) argued that mainstream cinema is constructed around a male gaze: the camera looks at women as men look at women, so the woman is positioned as the object to be looked at, and the man as the bearer of the look. The audience is invited to occupy the male position regardless of their own gender.
  • The beauty myth. Naomi Wolf (The Beauty Myth, 1990) argued that as women gained legal and economic power, an intensified, unattainable standard of beauty emerged to police them — a new form of control replacing the old ones.
  • The culture of femininity. Angela McRobbie's analysis of the teenage magazine Jackie found it constructing a narrow world of romance, appearance and the search for a boyfriend, teaching girls a subordinate femininity. Importantly, McRobbie later revised her position, arguing that girls' media had become far more assertive — a reminder that representations change, and OCR asks 'how far these are changing'.
  • Masculinity. Men are over-represented in positions of authority, and represented through hegemonic masculinity (Connell) — strong, unemotional, dominant. But there is real change: the emergence of the 'new man', and of men represented as objects of desire, complicates the picture.
KQ1 · ethnicity, class, age

Representing ethnicity, class and age

  • Ethnicity. Hartmann and Husband (1974) found the British press repeatedly framing race in terms of conflict and threat, contributing to a view of minority ethnic groups as a problem. Stuart Hall analysed the recurring stereotypes through which Black people were represented, and Teun van Dijk's research on racism in the press identified persistent framing of minorities as criminals, dependants or a burden. OCR asks you to discuss a range of ethnicities — Irish, Scottish, Welsh and English identities, newer migrant groups and long-established minorities all have distinct media histories.
  • Change is real but partial. Representation has diversified — but critics note that visibility is often confined to particular genres (sport, music, crime) and that behind-the-camera power remains unevenly distributed.
  • Social class. OCR names middle, working, upper and 'under' class. The working class has often been represented through a narrow set of images — comic, criminal or feckless — while the wealthy are represented sympathetically. Owen Jones's Chavs: The Demonization of the Working Class (2011) argues that a media caricature of the 'chav' emerged that made class contempt respectable. Marxists read this as ideology: if poverty is a personality flaw, the system that produces it needs no examination.
  • Age. Youth is over-represented as a problem — folk devils, gangs, feral teenagers (see Stanley Cohen). Older people are frequently symbolically annihilated: absent, or reduced to frailty, forgetfulness and comic irrelevance — despite being a large and growing part of the audience.

KQ1 · theoretical views

Five theories of media representation

  • Marxism — representations serve ruling-class ideology. Owners and advertisers shape content; the effect is false consciousness.
  • Neo-Marxism — subtler. Hegemony (Gramsci) means dominant meanings become common sense, and journalists reproduce them unwittingly (GUMG). Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model adds that the text encodes a preferred reading, but audiences may produce a negotiated or an oppositional reading. Hegemony is therefore always contested — which is what makes neo-Marxism more defensible than the crude version.
  • Pluralism — the media reflect the diversity of society and respond to audience demand; representations change because audiences change.
  • Feminism — representations reproduce patriarchy through symbolic annihilation, the male gaze and the beauty myth.
  • Postmodernism — OCR asks you to use postmodernism as a critique of the other views. Baudrillard argues that in a media-saturated world the distinction between representation and reality collapses into hyperreality: media images no longer represent a reality behind them, they are the reality. It follows that asking whether a representation is 'accurate' — the question Marxists and feminists are asking — is the wrong question. Strinati adds that media give audiences the materials to construct fluid identities, rather than imposing one.

Turn the postmodern critique around too. Its weakness is that it can excuse everything: if there is no reality behind representations, there is no ground on which to object to a racist or misogynistic one. Structural inequality does not become fictional just because it is mediated.

KQ2 · direct effects

Direct effects: the hypodermic syringe

The hypodermic syringe model holds that media messages are injected into a passive, homogeneous audience, producing an immediate and uniform effect. The audience is a patient, not a participant.

  • The classic supporting evidence. Albert Bandura's Bobo doll experiments (1961, 1963) found that children who watched an adult behave aggressively towards an inflatable doll — including on film — were more likely to imitate that aggression. It remains the most-cited evidence for imitation effects.
  • The classic criticisms. (1) It was a laboratory experiment, so it is artificial: hitting a Bobo doll is a game, not violence, and there is no realistic context or consequence. (2) It measures short-term imitation, not lasting attitude change. (3) It treats the audience as uniform, ignoring class, gender, age and prior experience. (4) Correlation is not causation — violent people may seek out violent media rather than being made violent by it.
  • The moral-panic role. Direct-effects claims recur every time a new medium appears — penny dreadfuls, cinema, comics, television, video games, social media. That regularity is itself sociologically revealing: the effects debate is often a proxy for adult anxiety about the young.

Nuanced version worth knowing. Elizabeth Newson's 1994 report, written after the murder of James Bulger, argued that repeated exposure to media violence can desensitise viewers. It is a serious argument — but it is about a gradual, cumulative process, which pushes you towards the cultural effects model rather than the syringe.

KQ2 · indirect effects

Indirect effects: two-step flow and cultural effects

  • The two-step flow. Katz and Lazarsfeld (Personal Influence, 1955) found that media messages do not reach people directly but pass through opinion leaders — respected individuals within a social group who consume media, interpret it, and pass on their interpretation. Influence is therefore social, mediated by the group. The modern update writes itself: today's opinion leaders have follower counts.
  • The selective filter model. Joseph Klapper (1960) argued that a message must pass three filters to have an effect: selective exposure (you must encounter it), selective perception (you must accept it rather than reinterpret it) and selective retention (you must remember it). Most messages fail. This is why the media are far better at reinforcing existing views than at changing them.
  • The cultural effects model. The most sociologically powerful of the indirect models, and a neo-Marxist one. There is no injection — instead there is a drip-drip. Repeated exposure to consistent framings, over years, gradually shapes what seems normal, natural and inevitable. This is hegemony as a media process — and it explains bias without needing to claim anyone is being brainwashed.

Why the cultural effects model is the strongest answer to the exam question. It accepts everything the active-audience theorists have proved (audiences interpret, resist and select) while still explaining how media power operates — not by changing your mind in an instant, but by setting the terms in which you think.

KQ2 · active audience

The active audience

  • Uses and gratifications. Blumler and McQuail (1968) turned the question around: instead of asking what the media do to people, ask what people do with the media. They identified four uses: diversion (escape), personal relationships (companionship, and material for conversation), personal identity (comparing yourself with characters, exploring who you are) and surveillance (information about the world). The audience is a consumer with needs, not a patient with a syringe in their arm.
  • Reception analysis. Stuart Hall's encoding/decoding model: producers encode a preferred reading, but audiences may decode it in a negotiated way (broadly accepting, but adjusting it to their own situation) or an oppositional way (understanding the intended meaning perfectly and rejecting it). David Morley's The Nationwide Audience (1980) tested this empirically with different social groups and found decoding patterned by class and occupational culture — exactly as Hall predicted.
  • The new media intensify this. Henry Jenkins' participatory culture: audiences now produce, remix, comment and organise. The line between producer and audience blurs.

Evaluate the active audience too. Being active is not the same as being powerful. You may interpret a message freely — but you did not choose which messages were available, which stories were commissioned, or which voices were never funded at all. Agency in interpretation does not equal power over production. Say that in an essay and you are writing at the top of the mark scheme.

KQ2 · deviance amplification and moral panics

Folk devils, moral panics and amplification

  • Stanley Cohen (Folk Devils and Moral Panics, 1972) analysed press coverage of the 1964 seaside disturbances between mods and rockers. He found the reporting exaggerated and distorted minor events, predicted further trouble and symbolised the participants through their clothes and scooters. The result was a deviance amplification spiral: dramatic coverage → public outrage → intensified policing → more arrests → more coverage → and more young people drawn into the identity being condemned. The media reaction helped to produce the deviance it reported.
  • Jock Young (The Drugtakers, 1971) found the same spiral among Notting Hill drug users: intensified policing consolidated a marginal habit into a central deviant identity.
  • Stuart Hall and colleagues (Policing the Crisis, 1978) added the neo-Marxist turn: the 1970s 'mugging' panic was not simply media over-reaction. Arising during economic crisis and rising class conflict, it supplied a racialised folk devil onto which public anxiety could be displaced, helping to manufacture consent for a more authoritarian state. Moral panics can do ideological work.
  • Why moral panics happen (the sociology of news). News values reward the dramatic, the negative, the novel and the personalised. Deviance is cheap, vivid content. That is a structural explanation for panics that needs no conspiracy at all.

Stretch · the new media

Do the new media change the answer?

  • The optimistic (pluralist / postmodern) case. Barriers to production have collapsed: anyone can publish. Citizen journalism can document what mainstream media miss. Marginalised groups can represent themselves rather than being represented. Audiences are participants (Jenkins).
  • The pessimistic (Marxist) case. The platforms through which all this flows are privately owned and highly concentrated. Algorithms — commercial secrets — decide what is seen. Users' attention and data are the product being sold. The old concentration of media power has not been dissolved; it has been relocated, from a handful of press barons to a handful of technology companies.
  • The feminist case. Online abuse and harassment fall disproportionately on women and minority-ethnic users, functioning as a mechanism of exclusion from public digital space.
  • The digital divide. The 'democratic' new media are least accessible precisely to the groups whose representation is worst — the poor, the old, the digitally excluded.

The judgement: the new media have genuinely widened who can speak, while narrowing who owns the space in which they speak. Both halves of that sentence are true, and an essay that holds them together will beat one that picks a side.

Exam craft

Writing the media essay

  • Representation questions: describe the representation, give evidence (content analysis: GUMG, Tuchman, van Dijk), explain it theoretically (Marxist, neo-Marxist, pluralist, feminist), then use postmodernism as a critique — OCR asks for exactly this.
  • Always address change. The specification asks 'how far these are changing'. Representations of gender, ethnicity and sexuality have changed. Acknowledge it, then ask how deep the change goes and who still controls production.
  • Effects questions: run the spectrum from passive (hypodermic) through indirect (two-step, selective filter, cultural effects) to active (uses and gratifications, reception analysis) — and land on a defended judgement.
  • Use moral panics as your bridge between representation and effects — they are the clearest case of representation producing a social consequence.
