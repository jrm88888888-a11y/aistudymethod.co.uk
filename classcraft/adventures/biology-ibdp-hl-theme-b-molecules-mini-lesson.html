Mission 1

Mission 1: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Make a guess: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Mission 1

Single sugars

  • A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit: the monomer of carbohydrates.
  • Named by number of carbon atoms: triose (3 C), pentose (5 C), hexose (6 C).
  • Hexoses: glucose, fructose, galactose. Pentoses: ribose (in RNA and ATP), deoxyribose (in DNA).
  • Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6: same atoms, arranged differently. They are isomers.
Mission 1

Rings: count every carbon

  • In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mostly as rings.
  • Glucose: ring of 5 C + 1 O. Carbon 6 sits outside the ring, in a CH2OH group.
  • Ribose: ring of 4 C + 1 O. Carbon 5 sits outside.
  • Fructose (as in sucrose): ring of 4 C + 1 O, with two carbons outside.
  • So ring size does not tell you the sugar type. Count all the carbons.
  • In glucose, number the carbons from the one to the right of the ring O: C1, C2 … (In fructose, C1 is outside the ring.)

Mission 1

α-glucose and β-glucose

  • Two forms of glucose: same formula, same ring.
  • They differ only at carbon 1.
  • α-glucose: –OH on C1 points below the ring.
  • β-glucose: –OH on C1 points above the ring.
  • A tiny difference with large effects: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen; β-glucose builds cellulose (see Mission 2).
  • Sugar units join into bigger molecules by condensation (water released); hydrolysis (water used) splits them apart again.
  • Say: "the –OH on carbon 1 is inverted" — not: "β-glucose has different atoms".
Quick check
?Which statement about α-glucose and β-glucose is correct?
Mission 1

Glucose: built for transport and fuel

  • Soluble: its many –OH groups hydrogen-bond with water.
  • Transportable: dissolved in blood plasma, it reaches every cell.
  • Chemically stable: it does not break down on its own; enzymes control when it is oxidised.
  • Energy yield: oxidation in respiration releases about 16 kJ per gram.
  • But soluble means osmotically active. So cells store glucose as large polysaccharides instead (see Mission 2).
  • Say: "glucose dissolves because it is polar" — not: "because it is small".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly links a property of glucose to the way it is used in organisms?
Mission check

?Galactose and glucose both have the formula C6H12O6 and both form a ring of five carbons and one oxygen. They differ only in which way the –OH on carbon 4 points. Which statement is correct?

Same atoms, different arrangement: that is all it takes to make a new sugar, from glucose and fructose to α and β.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Make a guess: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Mission 2

One monomer, three polymers

  • Starch: energy store in plants.
  • Glycogen: energy store in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose: strong walls of plant cells.
  • All three are polymers of glucose, built by condensation.
  • Units are joined by glycosidic bonds.
  • What differs: which form of glucose, and how the units are connected.
Mission 2

Starch: amylose and amylopectin

  • Made of α-glucose.
  • Amylose: 1,4-glycosidic bonds only. Unbranched. Coils into a helix: compact.
  • Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds plus 1,6-glycosidic bonds at branch points, about every 25 units.
  • Starch grains in plant cells, e.g. potato tubers and seeds, contain both.
Mission 2

Glycogen

  • Made of α-glucose, like amylopectin.
  • 1,4 bonds, with 1,6 branches about every 10 units: more branched than amylopectin.
  • Very compact granules in liver and muscle cells.
  • Enzymes remove glucose only from chain ends. More branches = more ends = faster release.
  • Fits animals: they can need glucose for respiration very quickly.
Mission 2

What makes a good store

  • Compact: coiling and branching pack many glucose units into little space.
  • Relatively insoluble because the molecules are so large. So they do not draw water into the cell by osmosis.
  • Too large to leave the cell.
  • Easily hydrolysed to α-glucose when energy is needed; easily built up again by condensation.
  • Say: "relatively insoluble because they are so large" — not: "insoluble because they are non-polar".

Quick check

Mission 2

Cellulose

  • (1) Made of β-glucose. To form each 1,4-glycosidic bond, every other unit is inverted (rotated 180°).
  • (2) The alternating units give straight, unbranched chains.
  • (3) Straight chains lie side by side, linked by many hydrogen bonds between their –OH groups: microfibrils.
  • Microfibrils group into fibres with high tensile strength.
  • Cell walls resist stretching, so plant cells can become turgid without bursting.
  • Say: "hydrogen bonds between chains" — not: "covalent cross-links" or "coiled like starch".
Quick check
?Cellulose gives plant cell walls high tensile strength. Which feature of cellulose is responsible for this?

Mission check

Mission 3

Mission 3: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Make a guess: A cell's outer membrane is just two molecules thick. Why do its molecules form that sheet by themselves?

Mission 3

A molecule with two ends

  • A phospholipid has a head and two tails.
  • Head: a charged phosphate group. It is polar: hydrophilic ("water-loving").
  • Tails: two fatty acid chains. They are non-polar: hydrophobic.
  • A molecule with a hydrophilic and a hydrophobic region is amphipathic.
Mission 3

The bilayer builds itself

  • In water, heads face the water. Tails are kept away from it.
  • A cell has water on both sides: cytoplasm inside, tissue fluid or surroundings outside.
  • So phospholipids form two layers. Heads form both surfaces; tails meet in the middle.
  • The sheet forms spontaneously: no enzyme or ATP is needed to arrange it. (Enzymes do make the phospholipids.)
  • No covalent bonds join the phospholipids. Each stays a separate molecule.
  • Say: "water molecules bond to each other and exclude the tails" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Why do phospholipids form a bilayer when they are mixed with water?
Mission 3

The middle is a barrier

  • The core of the bilayer is the hydrophobic tails.
  • Small non-polar molecules pass between the phospholipids: O2, CO2.
  • Ions and polar molecules (such as glucose) barely enter the core. Charge matters more than size.
  • Water is polar but very small: it crosses slowly.
  • So membranes are effective barriers between two watery solutions.
  • How substances cross: a later lesson.
Mission 3

Proteins: the mosaic

  • Proteins are scattered irregularly among the phospholipids, like the pieces of a mosaic.
  • Integral proteins are embedded in one or both layers. Some span the whole membrane.
  • Peripheral proteins sit on one surface.
  • Roles vary: channels and carriers (transport), enzymes, receptors, cell recognition.
  • Say: "integral: embedded in the bilayer" — not: "integral: always spans it".
Mission 3

Sugar chains on the outside

  • Glycoprotein: a protein with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • Glycolipid: a lipid with a carbohydrate chain attached.
  • The carbohydrate is only on the outer (extracellular) face.
  • It works in cell recognition and cell adhesion.
  • Example: ABO blood groups. Red cells carry a carbohydrate chain; the A and B antigens differ in its end sugar; group O cells carry neither.
Quick check
?Which statement about glycoproteins in the plasma membrane is correct?
Mission 3

Cholesterol, and why "fluid"

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids in animal cell membranes.
  • Fluid: phospholipids and proteins move sideways within the layer. The sheet stays continuous.
  • Say: "fluid: parts move sideways within the membrane" — not: "fluid: it is leaky, with gaps".
Mission 3

Draw it

A two-dimensional drawing needs:

  • Two rows of phospholipids, heads out, tails in.
  • An integral protein spanning the bilayer; a peripheral protein on one surface.
  • A glycoprotein, carbohydrate chain on the outside.
  • Cholesterol among the tails, not on the surface.
  • Labels: hydrophilic (both surfaces), hydrophobic (the core).
  • Proteins scattered unevenly, never in a regular row.
Quick check

Mission check

?Liposomes are tiny hollow spheres made by shaking phospholipids in water; some are used to carry medicines in the blood. Each one is a single bilayer enclosing a droplet of water. A water-soluble drug is dissolved in the water before the liposomes form. Where will the drug be held, and why?

Like a cell membrane, a liposome assembles itself in water, and its hydrophobic core keeps water-soluble substances on one side.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Make a guess: How can you show that the molecules in a membrane never stop moving?

Mission 4

The cell-fusion test

  • Label the surface proteins of a mouse cell with one fluorescent dye, and a human cell's with another.
  • Fuse the two cells into one.
  • At first, each colour covers its own half.
  • At 37 °C, within about 40 minutes the colours are mixed all over.
  • The proteins moved sideways through the bilayer. The membrane is fluid.
  • This was Frye and Edidin's experiment (1970).
  • When they cooled the cells to about 15 °C or below, the colours did not mix within 40 minutes: in a cold membrane, molecules move far more slowly.

Mission 4

How a protein sits in a membrane

  • A protein's surface depends on its amino acid R groups.
  • Part touching the fatty acid tails: non-polar (hydrophobic) R groups.
  • Parts in the cytoplasm or the extracellular fluid: polar or charged (hydrophilic) R groups.
  • A channel through the protein is lined with hydrophilic R groups.
  • Compare a soluble globular protein: hydrophobic amino acids clustered in its core, hydrophilic ones on its surface.
Quick check
?A globular enzyme is dissolved in the cytoplasm, and an integral protein spans the plasma membrane. Where are the amino acids with hydrophobic R-groups located in each protein, and why?
Mission 4

Fatty acids set the fluidity

  • Saturated tails are straight and pack closely: higher melting point. Membranes are stronger at higher temperatures.
  • Unsaturated tails have cis double bonds, so they kink and pack loosely: lower melting point. Membranes stay fluid and flexible in the cold.
  • Habitat example: E. coli grown at lower temperatures builds membranes with more unsaturated fatty acids (Sinensky, 1974).
  • Cold-water fish likewise have more unsaturated fatty acids in their membranes than related warm-water fish.
  • Say: "unsaturated tails pack loosely, so the membrane has a lower melting point" — not: "cold breaks bonds in the tails".
Quick check
?In an investigation, fish of one species were kept at 5 °C or at 25 °C for several weeks. The membrane phospholipids of the fish kept at 5 °C contained a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids than those of the fish kept at 25 °C. What is the best explanation?
Mission 4

Cholesterol steadies the membrane

  • Cholesterol sits between the phospholipids: its –OH by the heads, its rings among the tails.
  • It is a modulator of fluidity:
  • Warmer: it holds the tails, reducing fluidity and stabilising the membrane.
  • Colder: it stops the tails packing closely, preventing stiffening.
Quick check
?Two artificial membranes are made from the same phospholipids, one with cholesterol added and one without. Both are cooled to a temperature at which the membrane without cholesterol becomes stiff. What is expected for the membrane with cholesterol?
Mission 4

How cells stick together

  • Cells in a tissue are held by cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs): proteins in the plasma membrane.
  • A CAM binds to CAMs on the neighbouring cell, forming a cell–cell junction.
  • Different forms of CAM are used for different types of junction.
  • The two membranes stay separate; they do not fuse.
Mission check

?A student claims that proteins in the plasma membrane can move sideways within the membrane. Which observation would best support this claim?

Mixing after fusion and a bleached patch filling in point the same way: membrane proteins drift sideways through a fluid bilayer.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Make a guess: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Mission 5

When neither allele is dominant

  • Sometimes the heterozygote differs from both homozygotes.
  • Codominance: both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. It shows both phenotypes.
  • Example: roan cattle. Red × white gives roan: a coat of separate red hairs and white hairs.
  • Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.
  • Example: four o'clock plant, Mirabilis jalapa. Red × white gives pink flowers.
  • Notation: one letter for the gene, superscripts for alleles: Cᴿ and Cᵂ. No lower case, as neither is recessive.
  • The alleles stay separate in the heterozygote. They do not blend.
  • Say: "both alleles are expressed" — not: "the alleles mix".
Mission 5

Worked cross: two heterozygotes

  • Each heterozygote (CᴿCᵂ) makes Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes in equal numbers.
  • Offspring: 1 CᴿCᴿ : 2 CᴿCᵂ : 1 CᵂCᵂ.
  • Each genotype has its own phenotype. So the phenotype ratio is also 1 : 2 : 1, not 3 : 1.
  • Roan × roan: 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white.
  • Pink × pink: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • So the card's pink plants are CᴿCᵂ. Red and white return in the next generation.
Quick check
?In the four o'clock plant Mirabilis jalapa, a cross between a red-flowered plant and a white-flowered plant gives only pink-flowered offspring. Two of these pink plants are crossed. What phenotypes are expected in their offspring?
Quick check
?In the ABO system the red blood cells of a person with genotype IᴬIᴮ carry both the A antigen and the B antigen. In Mirabilis jalapa a heterozygote for the flower-colour alleles has pink petals. What do these observations show about the two patterns of inheritance?
Mission 5

Multiple alleles: the ABO gene

  • A gene can have many alleles in a population. A diploid individual still carries only two.
  • New alleles arise by mutation. Many differ by a single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP): one base at one position.
  • ABO blood groups: one gene, three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i.
  • Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant. Both are dominant to i.
  • Six genotypes, four phenotypes:
    • IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi → group A · IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi → group B
    • IᴬIᴮ → group AB · ii → group O
  • Say: "Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant" — not: "Iᴬ is dominant to Iᴮ".
Mission 5

Worked cross: AB × O

  • Parents: group AB (IᴬIᴮ) × group O (ii).
  • Gametes: Iᴬ or Iᴮ · all i.
  • Offspring: Iᴬi (group A) and Iᴮi (group B), 1 : 1.
  • No AB and no O child: neither parent's blood group appears.
  • A child's group comes from two alleles, one from each parent, not from one parent's group.
Quick check
?A woman with blood group A whose genotype is Iᴬi has children with a man of blood group B whose genotype is Iᴮi. Which blood groups are possible in their children, and with what probabilities?
Mission 5

What the ABO alleles make

  • Iᴬ and Iᴮ code for enzymes that add different sugars to a carbohydrate chain on red cell membranes.
  • Iᴬ adds the A antigen; Iᴮ adds the B antigen. i makes no working enzyme, so neither is added.
  • These antigens sit on glycoproteins: membrane molecules used in cell–cell recognition.
  • Plasma carries antibodies against the A or B antigen a person lacks: group A has anti-B; group B has anti-A.
  • Transfuse group B cells into a group A person: anti-B binds them and they agglutinate (clump).
  • Antigens on transfused red cells can also stimulate further antibody production.
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, a cross of red-flowered plants with white-flowered plants produced only pink-flowered plants. A student claims that flower colour is controlled by one gene with two incompletely dominant alleles, so that pink-flowered plants are heterozygous. Pink plants pass on red or white unchanged, exactly as the hook's pink plants did.

Make a guess: Why is butter solid but olive oil runny, when both are made of the same kind of molecule?

Mission 6

What makes a lipid

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water.
  • Mostly non-polar C–H bonds: few δ+ or δ− regions to attract water. They are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, phospholipids and steroids.
  • Say: "insoluble because non-polar" — not: "because the molecules are large" or "because they are lighter than water".
Mission 6

Building a triglyceride

  • (1) Glycerol has three –OH groups. A fatty acid is a hydrocarbon chain (R) with a –COOH group: RCOOH.
  • (2) Condensation: the –COOH of a fatty acid reacts with an –OH of glycerol. An ester bond forms; water is released.
  • (3) Three times: one glycerol + three fatty acids → one triglyceride + 3 H2O.
  • Hydrolysis breaks the ester bonds, releasing glycerol and three fatty acids (e.g. lipase in digestion).
  • A triglyceride is not a polymer.
  • Say: "one glycerol and three fatty acids" — not: "three glycerols and one fatty acid".
Quick check
?One glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules react by condensation to form one triglyceride. How much water is produced?
Mission 6

Saturated and unsaturated

  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Every carbon holds as many H atoms as it can: saturated with hydrogen.
  • Unsaturated: at least one C=C double bond.
  • Monounsaturated: one C=C. Polyunsaturated: two or more.
  • In natural fats, each C=C puts a kink in the chain. Saturated chains are straight.
  • Only C=C in the chain counts. The C=O in –COOH does not make a fatty acid unsaturated.
  • Say: "saturated with hydrogen" — not: "full of double bonds".
Mission 6

Kinks and melting points

  • Straight saturated chains pack closely: strong attractions between molecules → higher melting point.
  • Kinked unsaturated chains cannot pack closely: weaker attractions → lower melting point.
  • Rich in saturated fatty acids: fat, solid at room temperature (butter).
  • Rich in unsaturated fatty acids: oil, liquid at room temperature (olive oil).
  • Melting breaks no covalent bonds. Molecules just move apart.
  • Plants store oils: they are at the temperature of their surroundings, so stores must stay liquid when cool.
  • Endotherms store fats: a store rich in saturated fatty acids is still fluid enough at body temperature.
Quick checkRead the data
?The graph shows the approximate melting points of four fatty acids. Each has a chain of 18 carbon atoms; they differ only in the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain. Which statement is best supported by the data?
Mission 6

A dense energy store

  • Oxidising triglycerides releases about 37 kJ g⁻¹; carbohydrate about 17 kJ g⁻¹. About twice as much per gram.
  • Why: long hydrocarbon chains, rich in C–H bonds, with little oxygen.
  • Insoluble: no osmotic effect, and stored without the water that glycogen holds.
  • So a lot of energy fits in a small, light store: adipose tissue in animals, oil droplets in seeds.
  • Say: "energy is released when fatty acids are oxidised in respiration" — not: "energy is stored in the ester bonds".
Mission 6

Insulation

  • Adipose tissue conducts heat poorly. It slows heat loss from a warm body to colder surroundings.
  • Endotherms (mammals, birds) keep their bodies warmer than their surroundings.
  • The bigger the gap between body and habitat temperature, the thicker the insulation needed: e.g. blubber in seals in polar seas.
  • Insulating fat does not make heat: it slows heat loss. It does not work by trapping air.
Quick check
?A seal living in polar seas has a layer of adipose tissue (blubber) several centimetres thick beneath its skin. A dolphin of similar size living in tropical seas has a much thinner layer, and a cod living in the same polar seas as the seal has very little adipose tissue beneath its skin. Which explanation accounts for all three observations?
Mission 6

Membranes and temperature

  • Membrane phospholipids carry two fatty acid tails.
  • Unsaturated tails: lower melting point → membranes stay fluid and flexible at the temperatures a cell meets.
  • Saturated tails: higher melting point → membranes are stronger at higher temperatures.
  • Organisms adjust: E. coli grown in the cold makes a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids.
  • Fish adapted to cold water have more unsaturated membrane fatty acids than warm-water fish.
  • Say: "keeps the membrane fluid" — not: "insulates the cell".
Quick check
?In an investigation, fish of one species were kept at 5 °C or at 25 °C for several weeks. The membrane phospholipids of the fish kept at 5 °C contained a higher proportion of unsaturated fatty acids than those of the fish kept at 25 °C. What is the best explanation?
Mission check

?To make a spread, manufacturers react a liquid vegetable oil with hydrogen, using a catalyst. Some C=C double bonds in its fatty acid chains become C–C single bonds. How does the oil change?

Same molecule type, different tails: straight tails pack into a solid; kinked tails slide past each other as a liquid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Monosaccharides are named by their carbons (triose, pentose, hexose); isomers share a formula but not an arrangement; α- and β-glucose differ only at carbon 1.

Mission 2

  • α-glucose → starch (amylose helix; branched amylopectin) and glycogen (highly branched): compact, insoluble stores. β-glucose → straight cellulose chains hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils: strength.

Mission 3

  • Phospholipids have hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, so in water they form a bilayer on their own; the hydrophobic core blocks ions and polar molecules.
  • Proteins, glycoproteins, glycolipids and cholesterol sit in or on this fluid sheet, and its parts move sideways.

Mission 4

  • Labelled proteins mix after cell fusion and refill a bleached patch: membrane components move sideways, faster when warmer.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids and cholesterol keep membranes fluid in the cold; CAMs join cells into tissues.

Mission 5

  • Codominance gives a dual phenotype, incomplete dominance an intermediate one; either way heterozygote × heterozygote gives 1 : 2 : 1.
  • ABO: three alleles, Iᴬ and Iᴮ codominant, both dominant to i; each person carries two.

Mission 6

  • A triglyceride is one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester bonds (three condensations); hydrophobic, energy-dense store.
  • Saturated chains are straight and pack closely (fats, higher melting point); C=C kinks unsaturated chains (oils, lower melting point).
