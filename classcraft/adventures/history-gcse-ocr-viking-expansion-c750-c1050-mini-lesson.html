Viking Expansion c750–c1050

This mini-lesson works through the OCR world/period study on the Vikings — the Norsemen of Scandinavia. You'll see why they expanded, how their longships made raids and settlement possible, how they built the Danelaw and trading towns like Jorvik, how they explored as far as North America, and the balanced picture of their impact — raiders, yes, but also farmers, traders and town-builders.

Who were they?

The Norsemen of Scandinavia

The people we call "Vikings" came from Scandinavia — the lands that are now Denmark, Norway and Sweden. They are more accurately called the Norsemen ("men of the north").

  • They were not only warriors: most were farmers, traders, craftsmen and explorers.
  • The word "Viking" originally described the activity of going on a raiding expedition — "to go viking" — rather than the people themselves.
  • They shared a language, gods (like Odin and Thor) and a love of the sea.

Misconception alert: the Vikings did not wear horned helmets — that is a 19th-century myth. And they were far more than "bloodthirsty raiders", as you'll see.

Causes

Why did the Vikings expand?

From around c750 the Norsemen began to travel far beyond Scandinavia. Historians point to several causes pushing and pulling them abroad:

  • Population pressure — growing numbers and a shortage of good farmland at home in mountainous, rocky Scandinavia.
  • The pull of wealth and trade — richer lands to the south and west offered silver, goods and new markets.
  • Superb ship technology — the longship let them cross open seas and sail up rivers.
  • Politics at home — power struggles pushed some leaders and their followers to seek land and fortune overseas.

Key idea: expansion was driven by need (land, wealth) made possible by technology (the longship). Both matter.

Technology

The longship: the key to it all

The Viking longship was one of the most advanced machines of its age, and it shaped everything the Vikings did.

  • Shallow-drafted — it sat high in the water, so it could sail up rivers and land straight onto a beach, not just a harbour.
  • Fast and seaworthy — it could cross the open sea yet still surprise a target inland.
  • This allowed surprise raids and deep penetration inland — and later carried settlers, traders and explorers.
Raids · 793

Lindisfarne, 793

The traditional start of the Viking Age in Britain is the raid on the monastery of Lindisfarne (Holy Island, off the Northumbrian coast) in 793.

  • The attack shocked Christian Europe because a holy site had been plundered and monks killed.
  • Monasteries were targets because they held gold, silver and treasures but had few defenders.
  • Coming from the sea, the raiders struck fast and were gone before help could arrive.

Why it matters: Lindisfarne is the famous "moment" historians use to mark when the Viking raids on Britain began — a symbol of the terror the raids caused.

Settlement · 865

From raiding to invading

At first the Vikings came for plunder and left. But from 865 a large force — later called the Great Heathen Army — arrived to conquer and settle, not just to raid.

  • The army conquered much of eastern and northern England, overrunning kingdoms like Northumbria and East Anglia.
  • They captured York, which became the great Viking town of Jorvik.
  • Only Wessex, in the south, held out against them.

Turning point: 865 marks the shift from hit-and-run raids to permanent settlement and conquest in England.

Settlement · Alfred & the Danelaw

Alfred, Edington and the Danelaw

King Alfred the Great of Wessex resisted the Vikings. After his victory at the Battle of Edington in 878, he made a treaty with the Viking leader Guthrum.

  • The treaty divided England, creating the Danelaw — the area of the north and east under Danish/Viking law and settlement.
  • Guthrum agreed to be baptised as a Christian as part of the settlement.
  • Within the Danelaw, Vikings settled as farmers and traders, not only as warriors.

Evidence of settlement: Viking place-names survive across the north and east — endings like -by (as in Derby, Grimsby) and -thorpe mark villages the Norse settled.

Trade & towns

Traders, not just raiders

The Vikings were far-reaching traders. Their towns became busy centres of craft and commerce linked to networks stretching across Europe and beyond.

  • Jorvik (York) and Dublin grew into important Viking trading towns, full of workshops making combs, leather, metalwork and jewellery.
  • Trade routes reached from the British Isles across Europe to the Middle East; Arab silver coins (dirhams) have been found at Viking sites.
  • Archaeology (for example the rich finds at Jorvik/York) shows a settled, working, trading community.

Balanced picture: alongside raiding, the Vikings built and grew towns and connected distant lands through trade — a very different image from the "raiders only" stereotype.

Exploration

To the edges of the known world

The Vikings were bold explorers who settled and traded far beyond Britain — one of the great stories of the period.

  • They settled Iceland from around c870, and reached Greenland — where Erik the Red founded a colony in the 980s.
  • Around c1000, Leif Erikson reached North America ("Vinland"); the site at L'Anse aux Meadows in Newfoundland proves the Vikings reached the Americas centuries before Columbus.
  • In the east, the Rus gave their name to Russia and traded down the rivers to Constantinople (Byzantium).
  • Vikings also settled Normandy in France — their leader Rollo was granted the land in 911.

Scale of reach: from North America in the west to Constantinople and the Middle East in the east — an astonishing spread for one seafaring people.

Later · 1016

Cnut and the North Sea empire

Viking power in England reached its height when a Danish king took the throne. In 1016, Cnut (Canute) became King of England.

  • Cnut ruled a North Sea empire — at its height including England, Denmark and Norway.
  • He governed England well, keeping many English laws and customs, and is remembered as a strong, capable king.

Context: the Viking Age in England is often said to end in 1066 (Harald Hardrada's defeat at Stamford Bridge), but this study runs to c1050, with Cnut's reign as its high point.

Impact

The impact of the Vikings

How should we judge the Vikings? The honest answer is both/and, not simply "villains" or "heroes".

  • Violence and destruction — raids on monasteries and towns, killing, and taking of slaves and treasure.
  • Settlement and farming — permanent Viking communities across the Danelaw, changing landholding and society.
  • Trade and towns — growth of centres like Jorvik and Dublin and links across a huge trading world.
  • Language, place-names, art and craft — many English words and place-names (-by, -thorpe) come from Old Norse.

The big change over time: the Vikings shifted from raiders to settlers, traders and rulers — a far more balanced picture than the old "bloodthirsty raiders" stereotype.

Exam skill

Factors driving expansion

OCR wants you to explain why the Vikings expanded and how their impact changed across the period. Look for these recurring factors:

  • Technology — the longship, which made sea and river journeys possible.
  • Economy & land — land shortage at home, and the pull of wealth and trade abroad.
  • Individuals & leaders — Alfred (resistance), Guthrum, Rollo, Erik the Red, Leif Erikson, Cnut.
  • War & politics — from the Great Heathen Army to treaties like the Danelaw and Cnut's kingship.

Turning points to argue for: the raid on Lindisfarne (793) begins the age; the Danelaw (878) marks permanent settlement; Cnut (1016) marks the height of Viking rule in England.

Exam skill

Using sources on the Vikings

You will handle sources — chronicles, archaeology, sagas and objects — about the Vikings. Two habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). Much of what we "know" came from monks whose monasteries were attacked — so written accounts often stress Viking violence and terror.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Balance a monk's account against archaeology (trade goods, homes, coins at Jorvik) that shows settlement and commerce.

Interpretations differ: historians disagree about how far the Vikings were destroyers or builders. Being aware that interpretations vary — and using both written and physical evidence — is exactly what the exam rewards.

Recap

The essentials to remember

Who & why: Norsemen from Scandinavia (Denmark, Norway, Sweden); expansion driven by land shortage, the pull of wealth/trade and the longship. "Viking" meant a raiding trip; no horned helmets.

Raids: Lindisfarne 793 shocks Christian Europe; monasteries targeted for treasure and weak defences.

Settlement: Great Heathen Army from 865; Alfred wins Edington 878; treaty with Guthrum creates the Danelaw; place-names -by / -thorpe; Jorvik (York).

Trade & exploration: Jorvik & Dublin trading towns; silver dirhams from the east; Iceland c870, Greenland (Erik the Red c985), Vinland (Leif Erikson c1000); Rus & Constantinople; Normandy (Rollo 911).

Later & impact: Cnut King of England 1016, North Sea empire; overall a balanced picture — raiders and settlers, traders and town-builders.
