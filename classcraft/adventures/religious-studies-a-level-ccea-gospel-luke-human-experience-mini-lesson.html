Mission 1: Christians agree the Bible is God's word. Why do they read the same verse in such different ways?

Make a guess: Christians agree the Bible is God's word. Why do they read the same verse in such different ways?

Mission 1

Inspired, but still to be interpreted

  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching, for reproof, for correction, and for instruction in righteousness" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Christians agree scripture is inspired; they differ on how God inspired it and what it means.
  • Why interpretation is needed: many genres (law, poetry, history, parable, letter, apocalypse); ancient languages; distance in time and culture.
  • Hermeneutics: the study of interpretation.
  • Every reading answers three questions: what did it mean then? What does it mean now? Who has authority to decide (the reader, the Church, the scholar)?
Mission 1

Literal, allegorical, moral

  • Literal sense: what the words and their human author mean, including metaphor and poetry. Literal is not the same as literalistic.
  • Allegorical sense: persons and events stand for deeper truths, often about Christ and the Church. Paul says of Hagar and Sarah, "These things contain an allegory" (Galatians 4:24).
  • Origen of Alexandria (c. 185–c. 253), On First Principles IV: scripture has a body, soul and spirit, like a human being.
  • Moral (tropological) sense: what the text teaches about how to live.
  • A fourth, the anagogical sense (about the life to come), became standard in medieval teaching. John Cassian (c. 360–435) illustrated all four with Jerusalem: a city (literal); the Church (allegorical); the soul (moral); the heavenly city (anagogical).
  • Context: the Alexandrian school favoured allegory; the Antiochene school stressed the literal-historical sense. At the Reformation, Luther and Calvin pressed for the plain sense.
Mission 1Read the source

Aquinas on the senses

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologica I, q. 1, a. 10 (English Dominican translation, 1920s):

> "That signification whereby things signified by words have themselves also a signification is called the spiritual sense, which is based on the literal, and presupposes it."

  • Aquinas divides the spiritual sense into allegorical, moral and anagogical.

Q1 Which sense does Aquinas make the foundation of the others?

Show answer

The literal sense: the spiritual senses are "based on" it.

Q2 What is the strength of this rule?

Show answer

It limits fanciful allegory: Aquinas adds that arguments can be drawn from the literal sense alone, Reply Obj. 1.

Mission 1

Barth: the Word of God

  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Swiss Reformed theologian; The Epistle to the Romans (1919; 2nd ed. 1922); Church Dogmatics I/1 (1932).
  • Context: a reaction against liberal theology after liberal leaders backed the First World War. Barth's theme: God speaks; humans do not reach God by their own reason or experience.
  • The Word of God in threefold form:
  • Revealed: Jesus Christ, the Word himself.
  • Written: scripture, the witness to that revelation.
  • Proclaimed: the Church's preaching.
  • The Bible is a human witness that becomes the Word of God when God speaks through it.
  • Strengths: keeps Christ at the centre; accepts biblical criticism without losing authority.
  • Weaknesses: How do we know when the Bible "becomes" God's Word? (subjectivity); fundamentalists say it is God's Word; liberals find it too dogmatic.
Mission 1

Bultmann: demythologising

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941).
  • The New Testament's world-picture is mythical: a three-storey universe, demons, miracles. Modern people, Bultmann argued, cannot honestly hold it (paraphrase).
  • Demythologising does not delete the myths: it interprets them to find the kerygma (proclamation) inside.
  • He used existentialist philosophy (Martin Heidegger, Being and Time, 1927): the gospel calls each person to authentic existence, in faith and openness to God's future.
  • Example: the resurrection expresses the rise of Easter faith and the meaning of the cross, not an event a historian could check.
  • Strengths: makes the message heard by modern people; takes science and history seriously.
  • Weaknesses: assumes the modern worldview is final; loses the history that faith claims (N. T. Wright); Barth thought it reduced theology to human self-understanding.

Mission 1

Four modern approaches

  • After the Enlightenment (17th–18th centuries), scholars read the Bible "like any other book":
  • Rational: reason judges what is credible. Spinoza, Theological-Political Treatise (1670); D. F. Strauss, The Life of Jesus (1835): gospel stories as myth. Strength: honest questions. Weakness: may rule out the supernatural in advance.
  • Historical: what the text meant in its original setting; Albert Schweitzer, The Quest of the Historical Jesus (1906). Strength: guards against reading our ideas into texts. Weakness: leaves the text in the past; results often uncertain.
  • Sociological: the social world of texts and communities. Gerd Theissen, Sociology of Early Palestinian Christianity (1977): wandering charismatic preachers. Also liberation and feminist readings. Strength: hears overlooked voices. Weakness: may reduce faith to social forces.
  • Literary: the text as literature: genre, story, rhetoric. Hans Frei, The Eclipse of Biblical Narrative (1974); narrative criticism. Strength: reads the text as a whole, as given. Weakness: may bracket the question of truth.
Mission 1Diversity

Fundamentalism and liberalism

  • Fundamentalism: from The Fundamentals (1910–15), a reaction to liberal theology and criticism.
  • Scripture is inerrant (without error) in all it affirms, in the original manuscripts (Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy, 1978); many read history and creation accounts literally.
  • Strength: clear authority; takes the text as God's word. Weakness: hard to square with differences between texts (e.g. the birth narratives) and with science.
  • Liberalism: Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834), On Religion (1799) and The Christian Faith (1821–22): religion rests on the feeling of absolute dependence on God; Adolf von Harnack, What Is Christianity? (1900): the kernel of Jesus' teaching within the husk of ancient forms.
  • The Bible is a human witness to God, culturally shaped; its message is reinterpreted for each age.
  • Strength: open to science and criticism. Weakness: who decides which parts are "husk"?
  • Between: Catholic teaching (Dei Verbum 11, 1965) holds that scripture teaches without error the truth God wished recorded for our salvation; many evangelicals hold infallibility in faith and morals; Barth.

Mission 1Evaluate

Which model of interpretation is best?

  • Claim: A historical-critical approach is the best model for interpreting the Bible today.
  • For: Grounds meaning in what authors intended; limits arbitrary readings; open to all, believers or not.
  • For: Accepted in principle by Catholic and mainline Protestant teaching.
  • Against: Pre-modern senses (allegorical, moral) let the Church read scripture as a whole and for life; Aquinas kept them on a literal base.
  • Against: Barth: the Bible's subject is God speaking, which history alone cannot reach; fundamentalists: criticism undermines authority.
  • Against: Literary and sociological readings show what historical study misses: story, power, the poor.
  • Judgement criteria: Best for what purpose (scholarship, worship, ethics)? Does a model respect both the text's human history and believers' claim that God speaks through it? Can models be combined?
  • Spec: "the interpretation of religious texts" (AS 3 and A2 1 synoptic theme).
Mission 1Evaluate

Sacred text in any age

  • Claim: The Bible remains a living authority for believers in every age.
  • For: Believers experience scripture as God's address: "Your word is a lamp to my feet, and a light for my path." (Psalm 119:105) It shapes worship, prayer, ethics.
  • For: Interpretation lets a fixed text speak to new questions (e.g. medical ethics, ecology).
  • Against: Ancient texts carry ancient views (e.g. on slavery, women's roles) that many believers now reject; selective reading may undermine authority.
  • Against: In a secular, multi-faith society the Bible is no longer a shared reference; many read it as literature or history.
  • Judgement criteria: Does authority depend on agreement about interpretation? Is a text "living" if it must be reinterpreted? Authority for whom: Church, individual, society?
  • "the role of the sacred text for religious believers in any age" (AS 1 and AS 2).

Mission 2: Jesus said "No one comes to the Father except through me." Does that shut everyone else out?

Make a guess: Jesus said "No one comes to the Father except through me." Does that shut everyone else out?

Mission 2

Three answers to one question

  • The question: can people of other faiths, or none, be saved (brought into a right relationship with God, now and for ever)?
  • Christian theology of religions usually gives three answers (Alan Race, Christians and Religious Pluralism, 1983):
  • Exclusivism: salvation only through explicit faith in Christ.
  • Inclusivism: salvation is only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach people who do not know him.
  • Pluralism: the great religions are different, valid paths to salvation (see Mission 3).
  • Context: the end of the colonial missionary age, the Holocaust and migration made the question urgent for twentieth-century churches.
Mission 2Read the source

Two "no other" texts

> "Jesus said to him, 'I am the way, the truth, and the life. No one comes to the Father, except through me.'" (John 14:6, WEBBE)

> "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12, WEBBE)

  • John 14: Jesus speaks to his disciples (Thomas has asked "how can we know the way?").
  • Acts 4: Peter speaks to the Jewish council after a healing done "in the name of Jesus".

Q1 What do both texts claim is unique about Jesus?

Show answer

He is the only way to God / the only name by which people are saved.

Q2 Does either text say how people who never hear of Jesus will be judged?

Show answer

No: both speak to people who have met Jesus or his apostles. That gap is what the debate is about.

Mission 2Read the source

Texts that point wider

> "Truly I perceive that God doesn't show favouritism; but in every nation he who fears him and works righteousness is acceptable to him." (Acts 10:34–35, WEBBE)

> "The true light that enlightens everyone was coming into the world." (John 1:9, WEBBE)

  • Also cited: Acts 17:23 (Paul in Athens: "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"); Romans 2:14–15 (Gentiles who do "by nature the things of the law").
  • In Luke, Simeon calls Jesus "a light for revelation to the nations" (Luke 2:32, WEBBE).
  • Q: Is Acts 10:34–35 inclusivist or pluralist? (Usually read as inclusivist: Peter goes on to preach Christ to Cornelius, so the acceptance leads to Christ, not past him.)
Mission 2

Rahner: the anonymous Christian

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), Jesuit; "Christianity and the Non-Christian Religions" (lecture, 1961; Theological Investigations vol. 5).
  • Every person is created open to God and offered grace: the supernatural existential.
  • Until the gospel truly reaches a person, their religion can be a lawful religion: a means through which Christ's grace works.
  • Someone who responds to that grace in conscience and love is an anonymous Christian: saved through Christ without knowing his name.
  • The Church is the explicit expression of what is hidden elsewhere. Mission still matters: explicit faith is a fuller, clearer form of salvation.
Mission 2Diversity

How denominations see others

  • Catholic: inclusivist towards other faiths. Other Christians are "separated brethren" joined by baptism (Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964); Dominus Iesus says Protestant communities are not churches "in the proper sense".
  • Orthodox: the Orthodox Church as the one Church; many Orthodox leave the salvation of others to God.
  • Evangelical: mostly exclusivist about other faiths; some are wary of Catholic teaching (e.g. on justification), but joint statements have grown (Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, Catholic and Lutheran, 1999).
  • Liberal Protestant: more often inclusivist or pluralist.
  • Luke–Acts holds both: "no other name" (Acts 4:12) and "God doesn't show favouritism" (Acts 10:34).
Mission 2Evaluate

Does the Bible teach exclusivism?

  • Claim: The Christian Bible teaches that only those with explicit faith in Christ can be saved.
  • For: John 14:6 and Acts 4:12 are direct and unqualified; the Great Commission assumes people need the gospel (Matthew 28:19).
  • Against: The texts say salvation is through Christ; they do not say it requires knowing him (inclusivist reading).
  • Against: Acts 10:34–35, John 1:9 and 1 Timothy 2:4 (God "desires all people to be saved") point to God's will to save all.
  • Judgement criteria: context and audience of each text; how to treat texts in tension; the character of God; the fate of those who never hear.
  • "Critically evaluate" the relevance of the Gospel's claim about salvation in a multi-religious world.

Mission 3

Diversity is a fact; pluralism is a claim

  • Religious diversity (sociological): many faiths live side by side, as in modern Britain. That is a fact.
  • Tolerance: accepting people's right to hold other beliefs, while still thinking one's own beliefs true.
  • Religious pluralism (philosophical, or theological): the claim that the great religions are equally valid routes to the same ultimate reality.
  • A Christian can welcome diversity and practise tolerance without being a pluralist.
Mission 3

Hick's Copernican revolution

  • John Hick (1922–2012), philosopher of religion; an evangelical convert as a student. Working in multi-faith Birmingham from 1967, he met devout Muslims, Sikhs, Hindus and Jews whose lives seemed as holy as Christians'.
  • God and the Universe of Faiths (1973): theology needs a "Copernican revolution".
  • Old picture (Ptolemaic): Christianity at the centre; other faiths circle it.
  • New picture (Copernican): God at the centre; all religions, Christianity included, circle God.
  • Hick calls inclusivism an epicycle: a patch that keeps the old picture going, as astronomers added epicycles to save the earth-centred model.
Mission 3

The Real

  • An Interpretation of Religion (1989; Gifford Lectures 1986–87).
  • The Real in itself (an sich) is beyond all human concepts: neither personal nor impersonal.
  • Borrowing Kant's distinction between things in themselves and things as they appear: we know the Real only as humanly experienced, through a culture's concepts.
  • Personae (the Real experienced as personal): the Holy Trinity, Allah, the LORD of Israel, Vishnu.
  • Impersonae (the Real experienced as impersonal): Brahman, Nirvana, the Tao.
  • Hick uses the old parable of blind men and an elephant: each touches one part and describes the whole wrongly.
  • The pluralist challenge to the Gospel: if the Real is beyond all religions, no Gospel claim is final.
Mission 3

Criticisms of pluralism

  • It contradicts central Christian beliefs: Incarnation, Trinity, the resurrection and John 14:6 must all be reinterpreted; critics such as Alister McGrath (Christian Theology, ch. 17) say the result is no longer recognisable Christianity.
  • It is a hidden exclusivism: Gavin D'Costa (b. 1958) argues that pluralism judges every religion by its own standard (Hick's Real), so it is one more exclusive position, not a neutral one ("The Impossibility of a Pluralist View of Religions", 1996).
  • The Real is empty: if nothing can be said of the Real, why call it the source of all religions?
  • Religions do not see themselves this way: most believers think their tradition's claims are literally true; pluralism reinterprets them without their consent.
  • Conflicting truths: a personal God and an impersonal Brahman cannot both be literally the ultimate reality.
  • Hick's reply: these are mythological truths about our response, not literal descriptions of the Real.
Mission 3Read the source

Will all be saved?

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22, WEBBE)

> "and through him to reconcile all things to himself by him" (Colossians 1:20, WEBBE)

  • Christian universalism: in the end all will be saved, through Christ. Origen (c. 185–c. 254) hoped for the restoration of all things (apokatastasis); Barth spoke of all humanity elected in Christ, though he refused to call himself a universalist.
  • Pluralistic universalism: all will be saved, through many paths, with no single saviour. Hick held this (Death and Eternal Life, 1976).
  • OCR issues: Would a loving God deny anyone salvation? Will all good people be saved?
  • Q: Which of the two kinds of universalism does 1 Corinthians 15:22 support, if either? (Christian universalism at most: it says "in Christ".)
Mission 3Evaluate

Can a committed Christian be a pluralist?

  • Claim: It is possible to be both a committed Christian and a religious pluralist.
  • For: Hick himself remained a Christian (an ordained Presbyterian minister).
  • For: God's love for all (1 Timothy 2:4) suggests many ways; pluralism respects the holiness found in other faiths.
  • Against: Pluralism reinterprets the Incarnation, the Trinity and John 14:6 as metaphor.
  • Against: D'Costa: pluralism is itself an exclusive standpoint.
  • Judgement criteria: What is essential to being Christian? Is a metaphorical incarnation enough? Does the soteriological criterion (Hick's test of changed lives) work? Can there be neutral ground?
Mission check

?A government leaflet for schools says: "All religions teach the same basic truth: be kind to others." Which criticism would Gavin D'Costa make of this claim?

Pluralism claims to stand above the religions; its critics say no one can stand there. That is the heart of the debate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians agree scripture is inspired but differ on how to read it: literal, allegorical and moral senses (Origen, Cassian, Aquinas: the literal as foundation).
  • Barth: the Bible is a human witness that becomes the Word of God; Bultmann: demythologise to hear the kerygma; modern rational, historical, sociological and literary approaches each have strengths and weaknesses.
  • Fundamentalism (inerrancy) and liberalism (human, culturally shaped witness) mark the ends of a range; the role of sacred text in any age is debated.

Mission 2

  • Exclusivism (particularism): salvation only through explicit faith in Christ (John 14:6; Acts 4:12; Barth).
  • Inclusivism: salvation only through Christ, but Christ's grace can reach those who do not know him (Rahner's anonymous Christians; Vatican II).

Mission 3

  • Diversity is a fact; tolerance is an attitude; pluralism is a claim that religions are equally valid routes to the Real.
  • Hick: a Copernican revolution; the Real beyond all concepts; salvation as transformation; the Incarnation as metaphor.
  • Critics: it rewrites central Christian beliefs; it is itself exclusive (D'Costa); the Real is empty.
