Mission 1

Two kinds of extreme environment

  • Cold and high-altitude: polar areas, glacial and periglacial areas, and high mountains in non-polar latitudes.
  • Hot, arid: hot deserts and semi-arid areas.
  • Glacial ice covers about 10% of Earth's land today.
  • Drylands cover about 46% of the global land area (IPCC, low confidence) and are home to 3 billion people.
Mission 1

Where they are: describing the pattern

  • Hot deserts lie mostly in the subtropical trade-wind belts, such as the Sahara. Coastal deserts such as the Atacama and the Namib lie on the western edges of continents near the tropics.
  • Cold environments: ice sheets cover most of Greenland and Antarctica. About 65% of Northern Hemisphere permafrost is in Eurasia and 35% in North America and Greenland.
  • High mountains carry cold environments into lower latitudes: Glacier National Park in the Rockies, La Rinconada in the Andes, and the Tibetan Plateau, where the Qinghai–Tibet Railway, 960 km of it above 4,000 m, had to be built on permafrost.
  • Exam move: describe a distribution as a pattern (latitude, continental position, altitude), with named examples and an exception.
Mission 1

Dryness, not heat, defines a desert

  • The USGS summarises Peveril Meigs's 1953 scheme by rainfall: arid land gets less than 250 mm a year, semi-arid land 250–500 mm, and extremely arid land has at least 12 consecutive months without rain.
  • "Arid and extremely arid land are deserts", while semi-arid grasslands are generally called steppes.
  • The USGS also states that about one-third of Earth's land surface is desert, and that deserts "are always dry", whether hot or cold.
Mission 1

The aridity index

  • Aridity index (AI) = mean annual precipitation ÷ potential evapotranspiration (P ÷ PET).
  • PET is the water that would evaporate and transpire if water were always available.
  • Classes: hyper-arid below 0.05; arid 0.05–0.20; semi-arid 0.20–0.50; dry sub-humid 0.50–0.65; humid above 0.65.
  • Drylands (AI 0.65 or below) cover about 46% of land; the USGS's desert area is about one-third. Different definitions give different areas.

Mission 1

Why hot deserts are dry: the global pattern

  • Subtropical high pressure: around 30° N and S, air that rose at the Equator sinks. It is dry "because its moisture has already been released near the Equator".
  • Sinking air is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate and rain rarely forms.
  • Most non-polar deserts, including the Sahara, lie in the trade-wind belts.
  • Continentality: midlatitude deserts lie in interior basins far from the oceans.
Mission 1

Why hot deserts are dry: local causes

  • Rain shadow: air loses its moisture as it rises over mountains, then sinks and warms on the far side. Four mountain ranges lie between Death Valley, California, and the Pacific.
  • Cold ocean currents: a cold current chills the air above it from below. Cool, stable air gives coastal fog but little rain.
  • In the driest parts of the Atacama, measurable rain (1 mm or more) may fall only once every 5–20 years.
Mission 1

Why polar and high places are extreme

  • Polar deserts get less than 250 mm of precipitation a year and have a warmest-month mean below 10 °C.
  • Cold: the lowest air temperature recorded at a weather station was −89.2 °C, at Vostok, Antarctica, on 21 July 1983.
  • Wind: Antarctic katabatic winds can exceed 100 km/h for days at a time.
  • Altitude: temperature drops by about 2–3 °C for every 1,000 ft (about 300 m) climbed, and the air thins: half of the atmosphere's air lies below about 5.6 km.
Mission 1

Climographs: showing climate data (skills)

  • A climograph plots monthly mean temperature (a line) and precipitation (bars) on one chart.
  • Coober Pedy Airport, South Australia (hot arid), records from 1994 to October 2026: mean annual rainfall 144.1 mm; January mean maximum 36.9 °C, July 18.7 °C.
  • Utqiaġvik, Alaska (Arctic coast), 1991–2020 normals: precipitation 5.39 inches a year (about 137 mm); the warmest month, July, averages 41.7 °F (5.4 °C).
  • Exam move: describe the annual range, the wettest and driest months and any anomaly, quoting the figures.

Mission 1

Rain in hot deserts: unreliable and intense

  • Unreliable: at Coober Pedy Airport (1994–2026), the wettest year had 308.2 mm (2016) and the driest 24.4 mm (2019), against a mean of 144.1 mm.
  • Intense: storms are short and violent. 44 mm of rain once fell within 3 hours in the Sahara, and large Saharan storms may deliver up to 1 mm a minute.
  • A long-term mean hides huge differences from year to year.

Mission 1

Flash floods

  • Bare, hard ground lets little rain soak in, so water runs off fast into dry stream beds: wadis, also called arroyos or washes. They "fill temporarily during rain storms".
  • Flash floods make these channels dangerous. The USGS states: "More people drown in deserts than die of thirst".
  • UAE and northern Oman, 14–15 April 2024: Dubai's rain on 15 April was the highest daily total in its 75 years of records. At least 20 people died in Oman and four in the UAE.
Mission 1

Why April 2024 did so much damage (AO3)

  • World Weather Attribution: urban surfaces with limited permeability, inadequate drainage and hyper-arid soils "exacerbate the risk and severity of flash floods".
  • 80% of Oman's people and 85% of the UAE's live in flood-prone, low-lying areas.
  • The rain was well forecast, but most deaths happened while people were travelling; warnings "may not have reached some people".
  • Exam move: rare rain combined with high exposure and vulnerability still makes a high risk.

Mission 2

Why extreme environments challenge people

  • Relief: steep slopes and high altitude.
  • Climate: extreme heat or cold, drought, strong winds.
  • Human discomfort: heat stress, cold stress, altitude sickness.
  • Inaccessibility and remoteness: few roads and long distances to markets and services.
  • All of these raise the cost of resource development: building, energy, water and labour.
Mission 2

Coober Pedy: hot and remote

  • An opal-mining town in South Australia, about 850 km north of Adelaide. Opals were first found there in 1915.
  • Population: 1,437 (2021 census).
  • At Coober Pedy Airport since 1994: January mean maximum 36.9 °C; on average 24.2 days a year reach 40 °C or more; the highest temperature recorded there is 48.3 °C.
Mission 2

Living in the rock

  • Many residents live in "dug-outs", homes cut into the sandstone. "Early dugouts began as mines".
  • Underground, the temperature "is said to remain constant at around 23 degrees celsius" (State Library of South Australia).
  • Water once had to be carried long distances; it now comes from an underground supply north of the town.

A resource draws people to a harsh place

  • Sources differ on Coober Pedy's share of world opal: about 70% from its minefields (State Library of South Australia), or 90% together with the Mintabie fields (District Council of Coober Pedy). Quote each figure with its source.
  • In the 2021 census, accommodation was the largest employing industry (11.8% of workers). Opal mining, counted as "other non-metallic mineral mining", employed 4.6%.
  • The town grew on opal; today it depends on tourism too.
Mission 2

High and cold: La Rinconada, Peru

  • La Rinconada lies at about 5,100 m in the Peruvian Andes. A 2020 study calls it "the highest city in the world" and describes its inhabitants as mostly involved in gold mining.
  • Half of all the air in the atmosphere lies below about 5.6 km. At La Rinconada the air is thin, so the body receives less oxygen (hypoxia).
Mission 2

The human cost of altitude

  • In a study of 1,594 adult volunteers (median age 32), seen at a medical consultation for miners' families, 44% had excessive erythrocytosis (too many red blood cells) and 14% had chronic mountain sickness.
  • Their median time living there was 3 years.
  • Human discomfort is a real cost, but a valuable resource can outweigh it.
Mission 2

Inaccessibility in the cold

  • Over permafrost, thawed summer ground is often too marshy for roads. Supplies travel in winter on ice roads at least 100 cm thick, carrying trucks of up to 64 tonnes to mines and drill sites.
  • Ice roads melt each summer and must be rebuilt every winter.
  • Inaccessibility makes fuel, food, building materials and labour expensive.

Exam move: weighing the challenges (AO3)

  • Technology removes some challenges (dugouts, ice roads) but not others: no building restores the oxygen at 5,100 m.
  • People accept extreme conditions when the reward (opal, gold, wages) is high enough.

Mission 3

A glacier is a system

  • Inputs: snowfall and avalanches, mainly in the accumulation zone high on the glacier.
  • Store: ice that flows downhill under its own weight.
  • Outputs: melting, evaporation, sublimation (ice turning straight to vapour) and calving, mainly in the ablation zone lower down.
  • Equilibrium line: the boundary between them, where a year's gain equals its loss.
Mission 3

Mass balance

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation over a year.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens and may advance. Negative: it thins and its snout may retreat. Glaciers grow "when winter snowfall exceeds summer melting".
  • It is measured in water equivalent (w.e.): the depth of water if the ice gained or lost were spread evenly over the glacier.

Mission 3

How ice moves

  • Internal deformation (creep): the weight of overlying snow, firn and ice deforms the ice.
  • Basal sliding: a glacier may slide on a thin layer of water at its base, or on soft, wet sediment.
  • Warm-based (temperate) glaciers: the base is at the pressure melting point, so meltwater lets them slide and erode strongly.
  • Cold-based (polar) glaciers are frozen to their beds; they move mainly by deformation and erode little: "the movement at the bottom can be a tiny fraction of the speed of flow at the surface".
Mission 3

Advance and retreat over time

  • At the last glacial maximum, glaciers covered about 32% of Earth's land; today they cover about 10%.
  • In the Little Ice Age, glaciers advanced in many areas from about the early 14th century to the mid-19th (NSIDC). In Glacier National Park, Montana, it began around 1400 and glaciers peaked around 1850 (USGS).
  • Glacier National Park had 80 glaciers larger than 0.1 km² at the Little Ice Age peak, and 32 in 2005, the latest count on this USGS page.

The global picture

  • Glaciers worldwide lost 273 ± 16 gigatonnes of ice a year from 2000 to 2023.

  • From 1980 to 2018 their cumulative loss was 21.7 m w.e..
  • Exam move: show glacier change as a cumulative mass-balance line or as decade-mean bars, with a zero line and units (m w.e. per year).
Mission 3

Glacial erosion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto rock at the bed, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: rock fragments held in the base scrape and polish the bedrock like sandpaper, leaving scratches called striations.
  • Freeze–thaw on the valley sides supplies more debris.
Mission 3

Transport and deposition

  • Debris travels on the ice (supraglacial), within it (englacial) and beneath it (subglacial).
  • The glacier carries debris downhill, "like a conveyor belt", and drops it at the far edge, where the ice is melting (NPS).
  • Material dropped directly by ice is till: unsorted and angular, with all sizes mixed.

Mission check

?How could Muir Glacier retreat 12 km while its ice flowed towards the sea?

Retreat describes only the position of the snout. NSIDC: "Even as it retreats, the glacier still deforms and moves down slope, like a conveyor belt... a retreating glacier does not flow uphill; it simply melts faster than it flows". A negative mass balance made the snout retreat.

Mission 4

Reading a glaciated landscape

  • Glacier National Park, Montana: "Massive U-shaped valleys, cirque lakes or tarns, horns, moraines, and arêtes".
  • Erosional landforms dominate the high ground where glaciers formed; depositional landforms lie lower down, where the ice melted.
  • Each landform is evidence of a process.
Mission 4

Cirques and tarns

  • A cirque (corrie) is a round, steep-sided hollow carved at the head of a glacier, like a scoop taken out of the mountain.
  • Ice turns in a rotational movement: plucking steepens the back wall, abrasion deepens the floor, and a rock lip is left at the front. After the ice melts, a small lake, a tarn, may fill the hollow.
  • Example: Piegan Glacier still sits in a cirque on Piegan Peak.
Mission 4

Arêtes and horns

  • Arête: a saw-toothed ridge left where two glaciers carved either side, such as the Garden Wall.
  • Pyramidal peak (horn): a peak "scraped nearly vertical by glaciers on three or more sides", such as Reynolds Mountain, Flinsch Peak and the Little Matterhorn.
  • Both form as cirques erode backwards towards each other (headward erosion).

Mission 4

Glacial troughs

  • Rivers cut V-shaped valleys; glaciers scour them into U-shaped troughs, broad at the base with steep sides.
  • Truncated spurs: the glacier cuts off the river's interlocking spurs, leaving steep cliffs along the trough.
Mission 4

Lakes and hanging valleys in troughs

  • Long, deep lakes fill some troughs (ribbon lakes): Lake McDonald and St. Mary Lake.
  • Hanging valleys: smaller tributary glaciers cut less deeply, so their valleys end high above the main trough. Bird Woman Falls pours from one.
  • Paternoster lakes: a chain of small lakes down a valley, as in the Swiftcurrent Valley.
Mission 4

Moraines along the glacier

  • Lateral moraine: debris along the valley sides, much of it from freeze–thaw on the walls above.
  • Medial moraine: where two glaciers join, their lateral moraines merge into a stripe down the middle, as on Alaska's Gilkey Glacier.
Mission 4

Moraines across the valley

  • Terminal moraine: a ridge at the snout's furthest point; the longer the snout stays in one place, the larger it grows.
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges marking pauses as the ice retreats.
  • Moraines record old ice limits: Glacier National Park's Little Ice Age maximum was reconstructed from them.

Mission 4

Erratics: Big Rock, Alberta

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on a different type of bedrock.
  • Okotoks "Big Rock": a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 9 m tall, 41 m long and 18 m wide.
  • It was part of a mountain formation in what is now Jasper National Park. A rockslide dropped debris onto a glacier, which carried it out of the mountains; it arrived between 10,000 and 30,000 years ago.
  • Exam move: landforms are evidence. Erratics trace the direction of ice movement and moraines its limits, but dating can be broad (here, a range of 20,000 years).

Mission 5

Periglacial environments and permafrost

  • Periglacial areas are cold, unglaciated areas shaped by frost; most have permafrost.
  • Permafrost: ground at or below 0 °C for at least two years. It is "defined solely by temperature".
  • Active layer: the top layer, which thaws each summer. Taliks: unfrozen ground within permafrost.
  • About 85% of Alaska has some form of permafrost.
Mission 5

How much permafrost? It depends on the definition

  • Zones: continuous (90–100% of the ground frozen), discontinuous (50–90%), sporadic (10–50%) and isolated patches (10% or less).
  • NSIDC, using the International Permafrost Association map: permafrost underlies about 23 million km², or about 25%, of the Northern Hemisphere's exposed land.
  • Obu et al. (2019), modelling ground temperatures for 2000–2016: permafrost covers 13.9 million km² (about 15%); all areas with any permafrost add up to about 21 million km² (22%).
  • The larger figures count whole zones, even where only part of the ground is frozen.

Mission 5

Frost processes

  • Freeze–thaw: water in cracks expands as it freezes and prises rock apart; repeated cycles shatter it.
  • Frost heave: water drawn towards the freezing front builds ice lenses, which lift the ground and push stones upward. Under roads, "The expanding ice pushes up the road and creates a hump".
  • Solifluction: the permafrost below acts "like a waterproof barrier", so the thawed active layer stays saturated and creeps slowly downslope, forming lobes.
Mission 5

Patterned ground and ice wedges

  • Patterned ground: circles, stripes and polygons that form "when water and ice in the ground move the rocky soil around". In polar deserts the patterns can be up to 5 m across.
  • Frost heave pushes stones up and outward, so they gather at the edges of circles and polygons (sorted patterned ground); on slopes, circles are drawn out into stripes.
  • Ice-wedge polygons: frozen ground contracts and cracks in winter; water enters, freezes and widens the crack year after year.
Mission 5

Pingos

  • Pingos are ice-cored hills 3–70 m high and 30–1,000 m across. They form only in permafrost.
  • Closed-system pingos, like those near Tuktoyaktuk, form in recently drained lakes: unfrozen ground beneath the old lake (a talik) is surrounded by permafrost.
  • As it freezes inward, water is pushed to the centre under pressure. It lifts the thin frozen layer above it and finally freezes into an ice core.
  • Ibyuk Pingo is 49 m high and 300 m across: Canada's tallest, and one of about 1,350 pingos in the region.
  • Open-system pingos, by contrast, form where groundwater under pressure rises through gaps in discontinuous permafrost and freezes.

Mission 5

Thermokarst

  • Thermokarst: thawing ground ice "can gradually cave in", leaving "irregular surfaces of marshy bowls" and lakes.
  • Permafrost can contain up to 80% ice; when it melts, the ground can collapse.
  • Signs of thaw: thaw slumps, degraded ice-wedge polygons, thermokarst lakes and "drunken forests" of tilted trees.
Mission 5

Permafrost is warming (AO3)

  • From 2007 to 2016, permafrost temperature rose by 0.29 ± 0.12 °C globally, and by 0.39 ± 0.15 °C in the continuous zone.
  • From 1979 to 2021, the Arctic warmed nearly four times as fast as the globe.
  • Degrading ice wedges, collapsing ground and new thermokarst lakes are therefore indicators of thaw.

Mission 6

What desert surfaces are made of

  • Sand covers only about 20% of Earth's deserts.
  • Nearly 50% are plains where wind has removed fine material, leaving gravel: desert pavement.
  • The rest: bare rock, desert soils, alluvial fans, playas and lakes.
  • In Death Valley, California, less than 1% of the desert is covered by dunes.
Mission 6

Weathering by heat

  • With little water, soil or plant cover, rock lies exposed to the Sun.
  • On 15 July 1972 the ground at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, reached 201 °F (about 94 °C), while the air reached 128 °F (about 53 °C).
  • Thermal (insolation) weathering: the outer layer of rock heats and cools faster than the inside. Repeated stress can open cracks and peel off sheets (exfoliation).
Mission 6

Physical weathering by salt and frost

  • Salt weathering: as water evaporates, salt crystals grow in pores and force grains apart.
  • Freeze–thaw works in cold-winter deserts too. At Arches National Park, Utah, "In winter, water trapped in cracks expands when it freezes, then contracts, prying the rock apart".
  • Arches gets only about 8–10 inches (roughly 20–25 cm) of precipitation a year, yet that is enough.
Mission 6

Chemical weathering

  • Solution: at Arches, rain "can slowly dissolve the calcite bonding the sand together" in the sandstone.
  • Oxidation: iron minerals react with oxygen and turn red and orange.
  • Desert varnish: a dark coat of manganese, iron oxides and clay minerals on long-exposed rock.
  • Chemical weathering is slow because water is scarce, but dew and rare rain still drive it.

Mission 6

Wind: erosion and transport

  • Deflation removes loose, fine particles; abrasion sand-blasts surfaces with wind-borne grains.
  • The USGS lists suspension (particles under 0.2 mm held aloft as dust), saltation (sand moving in short hops) and surface creep (larger grains pushed along, up to 25% of grain movement).
  • Wind-cut landforms: ventifacts (cut and polished rocks) and yardangs (streamlined ridges up to tens of metres high).
Mission 6

Dunes

  • Barchans (crescentic dunes) form where wind blows from one direction. Some moved more than 100 m a year in China's Ningxia Province (1954–59).
  • Linear (seif) dunes often form where winds blow from two directions, and may be more than 160 km long.
  • Star dunes form where winds blow from many directions; in China's Badain Jaran Desert they reach up to 500 m.
  • Sand climbs the gentle windward slope and avalanches down the steep slipface, so the dune moves downwind.

Mission 6

Water: wadis and flash floods

  • The USGS notes that "water is much more powerful than wind"; in arid lands, water "is an important agent of erosion" (NPS).
  • Wadis (arroyos, washes) are dry stream beds that fill during storms. Floods carry heavy loads of sediment "for a day or two".
  • Where a channel leaves the mountains, it drops its load as an alluvial fan; fans can merge into a bajada.
  • At Arches National Park, "Today, water shapes this environment more than any other force".
Mission 6

Mesas and buttes

  • Mesa: a broad, flat-topped mountain with at least one steep side. Butte: a smaller flat-topped hill, steep on all sides. Spires are erosional remnants of buttes.
  • A hard cap rock such as sandstone protects softer shale and siltstone beneath. As the soft rock erodes, the cap breaks off along joints.
  • So mesas shrink to buttes, and buttes to spires. Example: Island in the Sky mesa and Junction Butte, Canyonlands National Park, Utah.
Mission 6

Rock pedestals

  • A rock pedestal is a cap rock perched on a narrow shaft.
  • The traditional textbook explanation is sand-blasting by the wind near the ground.
  • Twidale and Campbell (1992) found that most pedestals are two-stage forms: moisture in the soil weathers the shaft below the surface, and erosion later strips the soil away to expose it.

Mission 7: From the 1960s, irrigation turned Central Asian desert into cotton fields. Later, the Aral Sea largely dried up and fields turned salty. How can watering a desert damage it?

Make a guess: From the 1960s, irrigation turned Central Asian desert into cotton fields. Later, the Aral Sea largely dried up and fields turned salty. How can watering a desert damage it?

Mission 7

Aridity is not infertility

  • Aridity is a lack of water; infertility is a lack of nutrients or poor soil structure.
  • Desert soils are mostly mineral soils with low organic content; some contain salt layers or hard calcrete.
  • Given water, desert soils can be highly productive: in Central Asia, "irrigation made the desert bloom" (NASA).
Mission 7

Access to irrigation water

  • Exotic rivers bring water from outside the desert: the Nile, the Colorado and the Yellow River.
  • Oases, fed by springs, wells or irrigation, are often the only places in deserts with crops and permanent settlement.
  • Covered underground channels carry mountain water into China's Turpan Depression, so it does not evaporate on the way.
  • Whoever controls the water controls who can farm.
Mission 7

The Aral Sea

  • In the 1960s, the Soviet Union diverted the Syr Darya and Amu Darya rivers to irrigate cotton and other crops.
  • The Aral Sea, once the world's fourth-largest lake, shrank. The eastern lobe of the South Aral disappeared completely in 2014.
  • Fisheries collapsed, and salty dust contaminated with farm chemicals blew onto fields and degraded the soil.
Mission 7

How irrigation makes soil salty

  • All irrigation water carries dissolved salts. In hot, dry air much of the water evaporates and the salt stays behind.
  • Over-watering and poor drainage raise the water table. Water rises through the soil and evaporates at the surface, leaving more salt.
  • This is secondary salinisation. Half of Central Asia's irrigated lands, especially in the Amu Darya and Syr Darya basins, were affected (Qadir et al., 2009, cited by the IPCC).

Mission 7

Salinisation worldwide

  • FAO, from 1970–80 soil maps: 45 million ha of the then 230 million ha of irrigated land were salt-affected, a share FAO gives as 19.5%.
  • FAO's Global Map of Salt-Affected Soils: more than 424 million ha of topsoil and 833 million ha of subsoil are salt-affected. More than two-thirds of these soils lie in arid and semi-arid climates.
  • FAO warns that estimates made by different methods "are difficult to compare".
Mission 7

Managing water and salt

  • Ways to reduce salinisation: drip irrigation, drainage to lower the water table, flushing salts with extra water, and salt-tolerant crops. FAO notes that farmers on salty land may be "forced to cultivate salt-tolerant crops" that do not pay as well.
  • In the Aral basin, croplands "had to be flushed with larger and larger volumes of river water": a trade-off.
  • Kazakhstan's Kok-Aral dike and dam, finished in 2005, let the North Aral and its fisheries rebound, but limited flow into the South Aral.
Mission 7

Land ownership and tenure (AO3)

  • IPCC: secure land tenure is a major factor in farmers adopting soil conservation (high confidence).
  • Privatising rangeland in Botswana and Kenya cost pastoralists their communal grazing and "actually increased rangeland degradation".
  • Resilience to climate risks is higher where tenure is flexible enough for pastoralists to move their herds (robust evidence, high agreement).

Mission 8

Case study: Alaska's North Slope

  • Oil was discovered at Prudhoe Bay, on Alaska's Arctic coast, in 1968.
  • North Slope oil fields feed the Trans-Alaska Pipeline System (TAPS): 800 miles (about 1,290 km) from Deadhorse south to the port of Valdez. It was completed in 1977, when major North Slope production began, and has carried more than 18 billion barrels.

Mission 8

Physical challenge: permafrost

Mission 11: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Make a guess: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Mission 11

What desertification is

  • IPCC: "Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas", resulting from many factors, including human activities and climatic variations.
  • It means drylands losing productivity, not simply dunes advancing. Its range and intensity have increased in some dryland areas over recent decades (high confidence).
  • Deserts have grown and shrunk naturally: about 18,000 years ago, sand seas covered almost 50% of the land between 30° N and 30° S, against about 10% today.
Mission 11

Natural desertification: the Green Sahara

  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had high rainfall, varied vegetation, permanent lakes and people. Wet conditions reached 31° N.
  • It was one of a series of wet phases paced by slow changes in Earth's orbit (precession), which raised northern-summer sunshine and strengthened the African monsoon.
  • As that forcing faded, the monsoon retreated. Tierney et al. find the wet period's high rainfall "best explained by strong vegetation and dust feedbacks". The IPCC adds that a cooling of the north Atlantic played a role.
Mission 11

Case study: the Sahel's rainfall

  • The Sahel is the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara.
  • Summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (the driest year in the index was 1984) and has partly recovered since.
  • IPCC: "a cooler north Atlantic is related to a drier Sahel"; aerosols have also been suggested as a driver of the droughts.
Mission 11

Human causes and acceleration

  • Croplands in the West African Sahel doubled after 1975, and settlement area grew by about 150%.
  • Overgrazing in the Sahel reduced the seed bank of annual plants, leaving them more vulnerable to climate change.
  • The major human drivers interacting with climate change: cropland expansion, unsustainable land management, and population and income growth.
  • Positive feedback: bare soil loses more water to runoff and more soil to wind, so plants struggle to return.
Mission 11

Is the Sahel desertifying? Weighing the evidence (AO3)

  • IPCC: despite concern since the 1970s, "wetting and greening conditions have been observed in this region over the last three decades".
  • One analysis found climate the dominant cause of greening in 1982–2003, with pockets of desertification in Nigeria and Sudan.
  • Studies disagree because they use different indicators, sites, methods and scales. The relative roles of climate and people "are location-specific and evolve over time" (high confidence).
Mission 11

Consequences

  • The Sahel droughts of the 1970s–80s brought hunger, out-migration and suffering for millions of pastoralists.
  • Desertification has reduced agricultural productivity and incomes (high confidence).
  • Dust storms were associated with about 402,000 deaths from heart and lung disease worldwide in 2005, about 1.8% of such deaths (IPCC, citing a modelling study).
Mission 11

Desertification and conflict (AO3)

  • The IPCC cites a suggestion that droughts and desertification played "a relatively minor role" in the Sahel's 1980s conflicts, the major reasons being political, especially the marginalisation of pastoralists. It also cites a study finding that greater dryness raised the likelihood of riots across Sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Insecure tenure can in turn worsen degradation where herders cannot move freely (see 7.8).
  • Exam move: avoid environmental determinism. Weigh environmental, political and economic causes.

  • Explain: "Give a detailed account including reasons or causes." Evaluate: "Make an appraisal by weighing up the strengths and limitations." Discuss: "Offer a considered and balanced review that includes a range of arguments, factors or hypotheses".
  • Plan for "Evaluate the management of desertification in one named region" (10 marks): define desertification (11.1); weigh the Great Green Wall's target against its two progress figures (11.9–11.10); add the IPCC's cost and success caveats (11.11); set zaï and land tenure against them (11.11, 7.8); conclude on scale and on who benefits.
Recap

The big ideas

  • Extreme environments are cold and high, or hot and arid. Dryness, not heat, defines a desert (Antarctica; Utqiaġvik).
  • Desert rain is rare, unreliable and intense, so flash floods are a real risk (UAE and Oman, 2024).
  • People live and work in extremes when resources reward them, and adapt (Coober Pedy; La Rinconada; ice roads).
  • Glaciers are systems: mass balance drives advance and retreat; plucking and abrasion carve cirques, arêtes, horns and troughs; ice deposits moraines and erratics.
  • Permafrost makes frost heave, solifluction, patterned ground, pingos and thermokarst. In deserts, weathering, wind and water make pavements, dunes, wadis, mesas, buttes and pedestals.
  • Irrigation can make deserts productive but salty (Aral Sea). Oil and lithium bring wealth, permafrost problems, resource nationalism and competing stakeholders (Alaska; Atacama).
  • Tourism, desertification, climate change and new technology: always ask who decides, who benefits, and what the evidence shows.
