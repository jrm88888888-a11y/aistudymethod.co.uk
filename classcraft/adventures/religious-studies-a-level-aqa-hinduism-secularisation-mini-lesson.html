Mission 1

India in the 19th century

  • Foreign rule: after centuries of Muslim rulers (the Mughals), the British East India Company controlled much of India from the late 1700s; the British Crown ruled directly from 1858.
  • Islamic influence: Persian was the language of government; Islam's strict monotheism challenged image worship.
  • Christian missions: missionaries (e.g. at Serampore, near Calcutta, from 1800) criticised Hindu worship, caste and the treatment of women.
  • Western learning: English education spread (Macaulay's Minute, 1835); ideas of reason, science and progress questioned tradition. A first wave of secularisation.
  • Orientalists (e.g. William Jones, 1746–1794) translated Sanskrit texts and praised India's ancient wisdom.
  • Hindu responses ranged from defending tradition unchanged to reforming it. Two of the best-known reformers: Ram Mohan Roy and Dayananda Saraswati.
Mission 1

Ram Mohan Roy

  • Ram Mohan Roy (1772–1833), a Bengali brahmin; worked for the East India Company.
  • Learned Persian and Arabic (and met Islamic monotheism), Sanskrit (the Upanishads) and English (Christian and Enlightenment thought).
  • Translated Upanishads into Bengali and English: he argued that they teach one formless God, and that image worship was a later corruption.
  • Admired Jesus' moral teaching (The Precepts of Jesus, 1820) but rejected the Trinity; worked with Unitarian Christians.
  • Often called the "father of the Bengal renaissance". He died in Bristol in 1833, while visiting Britain.
Mission 1

The Brahmo Samaj

  • Roy founded the Brahmo Sabha in Calcutta in 1828; it became the Brahmo Samaj ("society of Brahman").
  • Beliefs: one eternal, formless God; the Upanishads read with reason; no images, no animal sacrifice, no priestly mediators.
  • Worship: congregational: Upanishad readings, hymns, prayers and sermons, like a Protestant service.
  • Universal in spirit: its worship was meant to be open to people of any faith who sought the one God.
  • Later leaders: Debendranath Tagore (1817–1905) gave it a covenant and creed and gave up belief in the Vedas' infallibility; Keshab Chandra Sen (1838–1884) took it in a more radical, universalist direction, and the movement split (1866, 1878).
  • Influential among educated Bengalis; always a small movement.
Mission 1

Roy as social reformer

  • Sati (a widow burning on her husband's pyre): Roy argued from the shastras that it was not required, and campaigned against it. The British banned it in Bengal in 1829.
  • Argued for women's property rights and education; against polygamy.
  • Criticised caste divisions and the power of priests.
  • Founded schools teaching both Western science and Indian learning.
  • His method: reform from within, appealing to Hindu scripture and to reason at the same time.
  • Roy and the role of women.

Mission 1

Dayananda Saraswati

  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), born in Gujarat.
  • The story told of his youth: at 14, keeping a night vigil for Shiva, he saw a mouse running over the image and eating the offerings. He decided a God who could not protect his own offerings was not in the image.
  • Became a wandering sannyasi; studied Sanskrit grammar and the Vedas under the blind teacher Virajananda at Mathura, who rejected post-Vedic texts.
  • Debated orthodox pandits, Christian missionaries and Muslim scholars.
  • Founded the Arya Samaj ("society of noble ones") in Bombay in 1875; it grew strongest in the Punjab.
  • Main work: Satyarth Prakash (The Light of Truth, 1875).
Mission 1

The Arya Samaj

  • Back to the Vedas: only the four Veda samhitas are revealed and infallible; later texts are accepted only where they agree with them.
  • One formless God. Rejects image worship, avatars and the Puranic stories.
  • Worship: havan (offerings into a sacred fire) with Vedic mantras.
  • Varna by merit, not birth: a person's varna depends on qualities and actions. Opposed untouchability and child marriage; supported widow remarriage and education for girls.
  • The Vedas are believed to contain all true knowledge, including the principles of science.
  • Shuddhi (purification): a rite to (re)admit converts to Christianity or Islam, and others, into the Hindu fold.
  • Sharp criticism of Christianity and Islam, and of much popular Hinduism. Founded schools and colleges (DAV schools, from 1886).

Mission 1

In Britain: secularisation and Hindu responses

  • The challenge: science and reason replace religion as the source of truth and moral values; religion is relegated to the private sphere.
  • Preserving existing sects: Brahmo Samaj UK keeps a Brahmo community in Britain; members join the yearly remembrance (late September) at Roy's tomb in Bristol. Arya Samaj groups also meet in British cities.
  • A rational, scripture-plus-reason faith such as the Brahmo tradition may suit a secular society; but small sects struggle to pass faith to the next generation.
  • New forms of Hinduism: ISKCON (founded 1966) offers a demanding, devotional Krishna-centred life, attracting both Hindus and Western converts.
  • Other responses: mandirs as community centres; supplementary schools; festivals in public.
Mission 1Evaluate

Whose idea of Hinduism was more successful?

  • Claim: Dayananda's vision of Hinduism was more successful than Roy's.
  • For: The Arya Samaj became a mass movement, with schools and colleges across North India; the Brahmo Samaj stayed small.
  • For: Dayananda drew only on Indian sources, so he could not be accused of copying Christianity.
  • Against: Roy's ideas (reason, social reform, one God behind all faiths) passed into Neo-Vedanta and into modern Indian law and education.
  • Against: Dayananda's "Vedas alone" left out most living Hindu practice: murtis, Puranas, bhakti, festivals.
  • Against: both were selective; critics say each reshaped Hinduism to answer Western criticism.
  • Judgement criteria: numbers and lasting institutions; faithfulness to Hindu tradition; impact on social reform; effect on relations with other faiths.
Mission check

?A Hindu group in Birmingham meets weekly to make offerings into a sacred fire while reciting Vedic mantras. Its hall has no murtis, and its teachers say a person's varna depends on character, not birth. Whose movement does it most likely come from?

Roy and Dayananda both rejected images and taught one God, but they disagreed about where true Hinduism is found (the Upanishads with reason, or the Vedas alone) and about how open it should be to other faiths.

Mission 2

Ramakrishna: a priest of the Mother

  • Sri Ramakrishna (1836–1886), born Gadadhar Chattopadhyay in a Bengal village; little schooling, no English.
  • From 1856, priest at the Dakshineswar Kali temple near Calcutta.
  • He longed to see the Goddess Kali as his Divine Mother; his disciples record that he had overwhelming visions of her.
  • He then followed many disciplines, guided by different teachers: Tantric practice; Vaishnava devotional moods; and Advaita, under the monk Totapuri, reaching nirvikalpa samadhi (absorption without any sense of difference).
  • His wife Sarada Devi (1853–1920) became his disciple and is revered as the Holy Mother.
  • His sayings were recorded by a disciple, "M" (Mahendranath Gupta), in the Kathamrita (The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna).
Mission 2

As many faiths, so many paths

  • According to his disciples, Ramakrishna also practised Islam (1866) and Christianity (1874) for short periods, and reported experiences of God through each.
  • His conclusion (paraphrase): "As many faiths, so many paths": each religion, sincerely followed, leads to the same God.
  • His image (paraphrase): one water is called jal, pani and "water" by different people; so one God has many names.
  • God is both formless and with form; Brahman and Shakti are one, like fire and its power to burn.

Mission 2

Vivekananda

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta; Western-educated; a member of the Brahmo Samaj, sceptical of visions.
  • Met Ramakrishna in 1881 and became his leading disciple; after Ramakrishna's death (1886) he became a sannyasi and travelled across India, shocked by its poverty.
  • 1893: spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago; became famous overnight.
  • Lectured in the USA and Britain; founded the Vedanta Society of New York (1894).
  • Returned to India a hero; founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897). Died aged 39.

  • He greeted the audience as "Sisters and Brothers of America".
  • He said he was proud to belong to a religion that had taught the world tolerance and universal acceptance: Hindus not only tolerate other religions but accept all religions as true.
  • He quoted a Hindu hymn: as rivers from different sources all reach the sea, so the different paths people take, straight or crooked, all lead to God.
  • He hoped the Parliament would end sectarianism, bigotry and fanaticism.

Mission 2

Neo-Vedanta

  • Neo-Vedanta (term used by Indologist Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern form of Vedanta taught by Vivekananda and his successors.
  • Advaita at the centre: Brahman is the one reality; every soul is potentially divine.
  • Experience (anubhava), not only scripture, is the ground of religion: religion is "realisation".
  • Four yogas (Vivekananda's lectures, 1895–96): karma, bhakti, jnana and raja yoga, suited to different temperaments, all leading to the same goal.
  • Universal: Vedanta is presented as the eternal truth behind all religions, and as friendly to science.
  • Continued by thinkers such as Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), The Hindu View of Life (1927).
Mission 2

Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission

  • Practical Vedanta (lectures in London, 1896): if Brahman is in every being, then serving people is worshipping God.
  • Vivekananda spoke of God in the poor (daridra narayana) and attacked "don't-touch-ism" (untouchability) and neglect of the masses and of women.
  • Shankara's Advaita centred on renunciation and knowledge; Vivekananda reinterpreted it as a call to social service.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897), with Belur Math near Kolkata as headquarters. Motto: "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world".
  • Today: hospitals, schools, colleges, rural development, relief after disasters, in India and beyond.
  • A liberationist approach: working to lift the poor and oppressed, seeing service to them as service to God.

Mission 2

Vedanta as the religion behind all religions

  • The view: all faiths, and all traditions within Hinduism, are partial expressions of the one truth Vedanta states most fully.
  • Supporters: it grounds tolerance and interfaith respect; it unites Hindu diversity; it fits Ramakrishna's experience.
  • Hindu critics: theistic traditions (e.g. Vaishnavas, ISKCON) say a personal God, not impersonal Brahman, is the highest truth; the Arya Samaj puts the Vedas, not experience, first.
  • Scholarly critics: Paul Hacker called this inclusivism: other faiths are welcomed only as lower stages of Advaita. Anantanand Rambachan (b. 1951), The Limits of Scripture (1994), argues that Vivekananda moved authority from scripture (as in Shankara) to personal experience.
  • Other faiths: many Christians and Muslims reply that their own claims (e.g. Christ as unique saviour) are not respected by being called "partial".
Mission 2

Influence in India and the West

  • In the West: Vedanta Societies; yoga and meditation; writers drawn to Vedanta. Vedanta became the best-known Hindu philosophy outside India.
  • Popular image: Hinduism as tolerant, mystical and philosophical.
  • Hinduism as a world religion: a universal message, not only an Indian heritage.
  • In India: pride and confidence under colonial rule; a model of service; Vivekananda's birthday is India's National Youth Day.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Neo-Vedanta a faithful development of Hinduism?

  • Claim: Neo-Vedanta is a faithful development of Hindu tradition.
  • For: it rests on the Upanishads and Advaita; tolerance has deep Hindu roots.
  • For: Practical Vedanta draws a real ethical conclusion from "atman is Brahman".
  • Against: it centres one school (Advaita) and sidelines theistic and ritual Hinduism.
  • Against: Rambachan: it shifts authority from scripture to experience; Hacker: its inclusivism shaped Hinduism for Western audiences.
  • Judgement criteria: faithfulness to sources; fit with how most Hindus practise; fruits (service, tolerance); whether all living traditions change.
Mission check

?A volunteer at a Ramakrishna Mission hospital treats patients of every faith free of charge. She says: "When I serve a patient, I am worshipping God." Which idea from Practical Vedanta explains her view?

Ramakrishna's experiences gave the idea; Vivekananda gave it words, institutions and a world stage: a tolerant, service-centred Vedanta that became many people's picture of Hinduism.

Mission 3

Artha among the four aims

  • The purusharthas, four aims of human life: dharma (righteousness, duty), artha (wealth, prosperity), kama (pleasure, love), moksha (liberation).
  • Artha is a legitimate aim, especially for the grihastha (householder), who supports family, guests, students and renunciants.
  • The rule: artha and kama are pursued within dharma, and all point towards moksha.
  • Wealth gained by adharma (cheating, harm) or hoarded without giving brings bad karma.
  • Dana (giving) is a duty of the householder.
  • So the Hindu attitude: neither "wealth is evil" nor "wealth is everything".
Mission 3

Practical Vedanta: serving God in the poor

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) reinterpreted Advaita Vedanta for action (see Mission 2).
  • If Brahman is in every being, serving people is serving God: daridra narayana, "God in the poor".
  • From his teacher Ramakrishna: serve living beings as Shiva (shiva jnane jiva seva).
  • Ramakrishna Mission (founded 1897): hospitals, schools, disaster relief, rural development, for all faiths and castes.
  • Motto: for one's own liberation and the welfare of the world.
  • Your spec calls this a liberationist Hindu approach: supporting the oppressed and those in poverty.

Mission 4

Arriving in Britain

  • 1950s–60s: men from Gujarat and Punjab come to work in mills, factories and transport; families follow.
  • 1967–72: East African Asians, mostly Gujarati Hindus, leave Kenya and Uganda (Idi Amin's expulsion, 1972); many arrive with business and professional skills.
  • 1980s–90s: Tamil Hindus from Sri Lanka (civil war); later skilled migrants from across India, and Nepalis.
  • Main centres: London (Brent, Harrow), Leicester, Birmingham, Bradford, Coventry.
  • 2021 Census (England and Wales): just over 1 million Hindus, 1.7% of the population.
Mission 4

Crossing the black water

  • Kala pani ("black water"): some dharma texts listed sea travel among acts that caused loss of caste purity, especially for Brahmins.
  • Gandhi's caste council outcasted him for sailing to London (1888).
  • So for centuries few Brahmin teachers went West. Westerners learned "Hinduism" mostly from texts and colonial scholars, not living practice.
  • That helped build a Western image of Hinduism as Sanskrit scriptures and philosophy (see Mission 5).
  • The rule faded in the 20th century; monks such as Vivekananda (1893) and later gurus travelled freely.

Mission 4

From front rooms to mandirs

  • Worship began at home: a shrine in the front room or a kitchen shelf.
  • Then rented halls for festivals, then converted buildings (churches, chapels, schools) as mandirs.
  • Then purpose-built stone mandirs, e.g. the BAPS Shri Swaminarayan Mandir, Neasden (1995).
  • Diversity: regional (Gujarati, Punjabi, Tamil, Bengali); sampradayas (Swaminarayan, Pushtimarg, ISKCON, Sathya Sai, Shaiva temples); caste associations.
  • Many mandirs serve everyone: several deities under one roof, English used for children.
  • Your spec: "diversity within the Hindu community in Britain".
Mission 4

Daily practice in a non-Hindu society

  • Time: work and school timetables leave less time for daily puja; festivals move to the nearest weekend.
  • Priests: often trained in India; fewer can explain rituals in English.
  • Food: vegetarianism and fasting are harder at school, work and university.
  • Language: children may not speak Gujarati, Tamil or Hindi; mandirs run classes and use English.
  • Public festivals: Leicester's Diwali lights are often described as among the largest outside India; Navaratri dances (garba) fill halls.
Mission 4

Funerals and the law

  • Tradition: an open-air pyre, ideally by a river, lit by the eldest son, soon after death; ashes into a holy river.
  • UK practice: cremation in a crematorium (Cremation Act 1902); a son may press the button or view the committal.
  • Davender Ghai case (Court of Appeal, 2010): an open-air pyre is lawful if inside a structure with walls and an opening to the sky.
  • Ashes: some families fly them to the Ganges; others use river sites in Britain (e.g. on the River Soar in Leicester), following Environment Agency guidance.
  • Delays for death registration or inquests can clash with the wish for quick cremation.

Mission 4

India and sacred sites outside India

  • India stays central: pilgrimage to Varanasi, Vrindavan, Tirupati; ashes to the Ganges; family and guru links.
  • Can sacred sites grow outside India? Some say yes: a place becomes holy through deities installed by ritual (prana pratishtha), saints and devotion.
  • UK examples: Bhaktivedanta Manor (Hertfordshire), a pilgrimage centre at Janmashtami; Neasden mandir; Skanda Vale (Wales), a multi-faith Hindu-based ashram.
  • Some say no: true tirthas are tied to the sacred land of India and its rivers.
  • Your spec: "the possibility of sacred sites developing outside India".
Mission 4

Freedom of religion as a human right

  • European Convention on Human Rights, Article 9 (1950): freedom of thought, conscience and religion, including worship and practice; in UK law through the Human Rights Act 1998.
  • It may be limited by law where necessary for public safety, order, health or others' rights.
  • Skanda Vale (2007): the temple bull Shambo tested positive for bovine TB; the courts allowed the Welsh government to slaughter him despite Article 9.
  • Bhaktivedanta Manor: a long planning dispute over public worship ended when a new access road was approved (1996).
  • Effect on Hindu thought: Hindus present themselves as one faith community among others, alongside churches, mosques and gurdwaras.
Quick check
?Why did the courts allow the slaughter of Shambo, the Skanda Vale temple bull, in 2007?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Traditional Hinduism cannot be fully practised in a non-Hindu society."

  • For: no sacred rivers or tirthas; funeral rites must change; varnashrama barely fits; priests and languages from India; time pressures.
  • Against: home puja, festivals and mandir life thrive; the law protects religious freedom; sacred sites can be created; India itself has adapted constantly.
  • Another view: "traditional" was never one thing; adaptation (e.g. weekend festivals) is itself traditional, since dharma depends on time and place.
  • Judge it by: which practices are essential? Whose tradition (Gujarati, Tamil, Punjabi)? Which generation?
Mission check

British Hindus keep the heart of a rite and adapt its setting: the son's role and the river remain; the open pyre usually becomes a crematorium.

Mission 5

What makes a Hindu identity?

  • No creed, founder or single scripture to sign up to. So identity is built from several strands.
  • Belief: Brahman, deities, karma, rebirth, dharma.
  • Practice and worship: home puja, mandir, festivals, samskaras (life rites).
  • Lifestyle and conduct: vegetarianism, ahimsa, family duty, respect for elders.
  • Belonging: family, region (Gujarati, Punjabi, Tamil), caste, language, sampradaya.
  • A person may be strong in some strands and weak in others: "practising", "cultural" and "spiritual but not religious" Hindus.
Mission 5

Secularisation in Britain

  • Secularisation: religion loses social influence. In the 2021 Census, 37.2% of people in England and Wales said they had no religion.
  • Source of truth: science and reason, not scripture, become the public authority.
  • Moral values: law, human rights and personal choice, not religious teaching.
  • Personal sphere: religion becomes a private choice; schools and public life are largely secular.
  • For Hindus: pressure to treat Hinduism as private "culture"; children educated in secular values; less time for ritual.
  • Your spec: "replacement of religion as the source of truth and moral values; relegation of religion to the personal sphere".
Mission 5

Two responses: preserve or renew

  • Preserve existing sects: e.g. Brahmo Samaj UK, continuing Ram Mohan Roy's reformed, image-free worship of one God (see Mission 1). It offers a rational, ethical Hinduism suited to a secular society, but stays small.
  • New forms: ISKCON: converts, strict rules, strong community; offers what secularisation removes: authority, moral code, belonging.
  • Other growth: Swaminarayan (BAPS) and other sampradayas with youth programmes, volunteering and large mandirs.
  • Some Hindus respond by adapting: Hinduism as "a way of life", compatible with science and personal choice.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of a Hindu response to secularisation by preserving an existing sect in Britain?
Mission 5

Generations and identity

  • First generation: identity through region, caste, language and village links; religion taken for granted.
  • Second and third generations: "British Hindu"; more choice; less ritual knowledge, more questions ("why do we do this?").
  • University Hindu societies (e.g. the National Hindu Students' Forum) and youth wings build a shared Hindu identity across regions and castes.
  • Some young people stress dharma as lifestyle: vegetarianism, ethics, yoga, service.
  • Others become cultural Hindus: festivals and family, little belief or practice.
Mission 5Evaluate

"There is one clear Hindu identity in Britain."

  • For: common festivals and values; national bodies speak for "British Hindus"; the Census groups 1 million people as Hindu; young people build a shared identity.
  • Against: deep regional, linguistic, caste and sampradaya differences; ISKCON converts and Gujarati elders may share little; many are "cultural" Hindus.
  • Judge it by: which strands of identity (belief, practice, belonging) count most? Is "Hindu" a religion, a culture, or both?
Mission check

?Priya, 19, born in Leicester, is vegetarian, does yoga daily and keeps Diwali with family, but rarely visits a mandir and is unsure about rebirth. Using the strands of Hindu identity, which description fits her best?

Hindu identity in Britain is a braid of strands, not a single test: belief, practice, lifestyle, worship and belonging.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Foreign rule, Christian and Islamic challenges and Western reason pushed 19th-century Hindus to reform or defend their tradition.
  • Ram Mohan Roy (Brahmo Samaj, 1828): one formless God from the Upanishads and reason; against sati; open to other faiths. Dayananda Saraswati (Arya Samaj, 1875): the four Vedas alone; havan; varna by merit; shuddhi.
  • In Britain, Hindus respond to secularisation by preserving sects such as Brahmo Samaj UK and through new forms such as ISKCON.

Mission 2

  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): Kali devotee and mystic; experiences through many paths led him to teach "as many faiths, so many paths"; he accepted both images and the formless.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago 1893; Neo-Vedanta (Advaita, experience, four yogas, all religions true); Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897): serving people as God.
  • "Vedanta as religion" grounds tolerance but is criticised as inclusivist and as sidelining theistic Hinduism.

Mission 3

  • Artha (wealth) is one of the four aims of life: legitimate within dharma, shared through dana.
  • Practical Vedanta (Vivekananda, Ramakrishna Mission) is a liberationist approach: serving God in the poor.

Mission 4

  • Migration from the 1950s (Gujarat, Punjab, East Africa, Sri Lanka) built a diverse British Hindu community of about 1 million (2021).
  • Practice adapts to law and culture: home puja, weekend festivals, crematoria, river sites for ashes; caste survives mainly in marriage.
  • Article 9 protects religious freedom, within limits (Shambo, 2007).
  • Sacred sites may develop outside India.

Mission 5

  • Secularisation pushes religion into private life.
  • Hindus respond by preserving sects (Brahmo Samaj UK), new forms (ISKCON) and adapting.
  • Hindu identity is built from belief, practice, lifestyle, worship and belonging, so British Hindu identity is both shared and diverse.
