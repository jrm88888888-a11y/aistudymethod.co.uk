Mission 1

Mission 1: If God never changes, how can God listen to your prayers and love you back?

Make a guess: If God never changes, how can God listen to your prayers and love you back?

Mission 1

One God, maker of all

  • "In the beginning, God created the heavens and the earth." (Genesis 1:1)
  • "there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things, and we for him; and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6)
  • Creator: Christians believe everything that exists depends on God for its existence, at every moment.
  • Omnipotent creator and controller: God sustains and governs all things (providence).
  • Transcendent: God is not part of the universe and is beyond it.
Mission 1

From nothing, not from God

  • Creatio ex nihilo: God created out of nothing, without any pre-existing material.
  • Sources: 2 Maccabees 7:28 (God made them "not of things that were"); Hebrews 11:3.
  • Emanation (contrast): in Plotinus (c. 204–270), all things flow out of the One by necessity, like light from the sun.
  • Mainstream Christian theology rejects emanation: it makes the world part of God's own substance, and it makes creation necessary, not free.
  • Augustine (354–430) on creatures: "they were made of nothing, by Thee, not of Thee" (Confessions XIII.33).
  • So the world is distinct from God, and freely given.
Mission 1

Builder, artist, and "very good"

  • Construction: in Plato's Timaeus a craftsman (the demiurge) shapes matter that already exists. Christians use the image of God as builder, but deny pre-existing matter.
  • Artistic expression: creation as a free work of love and delight, expressing the maker without being the maker (e.g. Dorothy L. Sayers, The Mind of the Maker, 1941).
  • Goodness of creation: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good." (Genesis 1:31)
  • Matter, bodies and the world are good, not prisons for the soul.
  • Augustine: evil is not a created thing but a lack (privation) of good.

Mission 1

All-powerful and unchanging

  • Omnipotence: Aquinas: God "can do all things that are possible absolutely" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 25, a. 3): not the logically contradictory.
  • Immutability: God does not change. "For I, the LORD, don't change" (Malachi 3:6); "no variation nor turning shadow" (James 1:17).
  • Why it matters: change means gaining or losing something; a perfect God lacks nothing to gain.
  • Augustine: "For that truly is which remains unchangeably." (Confessions VII.11)
  • Significance for believers: God's promises and love can be trusted absolutely.
  • Challenge: the Bible also shows God "relenting" (Exodus 32:14; Jonah 3:10). Classical theists read these as human ways of speaking.
Mission 1

A personal God

  • Personal: God knows, wills, loves and can be addressed as "you".
  • God makes covenants, speaks through prophets, and is called "Abba, Father" (Mark 14:36; Galatians 4:6).
  • Humans are made "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27), able to relate to God.
  • Christians speak of a personal relationship with God in prayer, worship and the sacraments.
  • Not "a person like us": God has no body and is not one being among others.
Mission 1Read the source

Augustine's restless heart

Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions (c. 397–400), a prayer addressed to God throughout:

> "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." (I.1)

> "Thou wert more inward to me than my most inward part; and higher than my highest." (III.6)

  • God is both transcendent ("higher than my highest") and intimate ("more inward").

Show answer

Q2 Which two qualities of God does the second quotation hold together?

Show answer

Mission 1

Buber: I and Thou

  • Martin Buber (1878–1965), Jewish philosopher, I and Thou (Ich und Du, 1923).
  • Two ways of being: I–It (using, analysing, describing) and I–Thou (meeting another whole person, present, in mutual relation).
  • Every true I–Thou meeting glimpses the eternal Thou: God, who can never become an It.
  • So God cannot be fully described, only addressed: known in relation, not in theory.
  • Christian theologians use Buber to explain prayer and a "personal relationship" with God.
  • Challenge: Buber stresses mutuality; can an unchanging God be met in that way?

Mission 1

Stewards of a good creation

  • "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15)
  • Stewardship: humans look after the world on God's behalf; it is God's, not ours.
  • Follows from a creator who made a "very good" world and gave humans a share in caring for it.
  • Christians differ on how far "dominion" (Genesis 1:26) allows use of nature.
  • Experience: creation as a place to meet God (e.g. Celtic Christian prayer, Francis of Assisi's Canticle of the Creatures, 1225).
Mission 1

What follows for self, purpose, death

  • Self: made in God's image, by love, from nothing: each person is contingent, dependent and valued.
  • Purpose: to know and love God (Augustine's rest; Buber's meeting); and to care for creation.
  • Death: "the spirit returns to God who gave it" (Ecclesiastes 12:7). For Christians, death does not end a relationship God keeps.
  • Afterlife: resurrection life (John 11:25); in Catholic thought, the beatific vision: seeing God face to face.
  • For many Christians, an unchanging God means hope is secure: God's faithfulness does not end at death.
Mission 1Evaluate

"An unchanging God cannot have a personal relationship with humans."

  • For: relationship seems to need response; Buber's I–Thou is mutual.
  • For: the Bible shows God grieving and relenting (Exodus 32:14); open theists and process theologians say God truly changes.
  • Against (Augustine, Aquinas): God's love is constant; the relationship changes because we change, as the sun stays the same while we turn towards it.
  • Against: only an unchanging God can be trusted absolutely (Malachi 3:6).
  • Against: Christians believe God entered time in Jesus without ceasing to be God, so God relates through the Incarnation.
  • Judge it by: What does "change" mean for God? Must love involve being affected? Which biblical texts are literal?

Christians hold that God made the world freely, from nothing, and remains unchanging in love: the ground of a relationship the believer can trust.

Mission 2

Two words from Genesis

  • Genesis 1 gives humans dominion: rule over fish, birds, livestock and "every creeping thing".
  • Genesis 2 places the first human in the garden to cultivate and keep it (WEBBE).
  • Christians read these together. The question is what kind of rule dominion is.
  • Dominion as domination: nature exists for human use.
  • Dominion as stewardship: humans rule on God's behalf and answer to God for how they rule.
  • Link to: if God created everything and called it good, the world belongs to God; humans are its stewards.
Mission 2Read the source

Rule, keep, belong

> "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth." (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

> "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15, WEBBE)

> "The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness; the world, and those who dwell in it." (Psalm 24:1, WEBBE)

Mission 2

Stewardship

  • A steward manages property that belongs to someone else and must give an account.
  • Owner: God ("the land is mine", Leviticus 25:23).
  • Steward: humanity, made in God's image to reflect God's care, not to exploit.
  • Accountability: stewards will be judged on faithfulness (1 Corinthians 4:2; the parable of the faithful steward, Luke 12:42–48).
  • Future generations: a steward hands the estate on in good order.
  • Creation has value because God made it and called it "very good" (Genesis 1:31), not only because humans use it.
  • Your spec lists stewardship among beliefs about God as Creator: a personal Creator who entrusts the world to humans.

Mission 3

Mission 3: What is a Christian's life actually for, and which purpose matters most?

Make a guess: What is a Christian's life actually for, and which purpose matters most?

Mission 3

Three purposes

  • Christians believe God made humans on purpose: life has a goal (Greek telos).
  • Three purposes stand out:
  • 1. To glorify God and have a personal relationship with him.
  • 2. To prepare for judgement: to live now in the light of eternity.
  • 3. To bring about God's kingdom on earth: justice, peace and love in this world.
  • Most Christians hold all three. The debate is about their relative importance: which comes first, and which shapes the others.
Mission 3

To glorify God

  • Glory (Hebrew kavod, "weight"; Greek doxa, "radiance"): God's worth made visible.
  • Humans cannot add to God's glory; they reflect and acknowledge it, like a mirror reflecting light.
  • God made people "for my glory" (Isaiah 43:7).
  • "Whether therefore you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God." (1 Corinthians 10:31)
  • So glorifying God is not only worship on Sunday: it is every activity done for God.
  • "Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father who is in heaven." (Matthew 5:16)
Mission 3

A personal relationship with God

  • "This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God, and him whom you sent, Jesus Christ." (John 17:3)
  • To know God here means to be in relationship with him, not just to know facts.
  • Christians express this in prayer, worship, the sacraments and reading scripture.
  • Jesus calls God "Father" and invites followers to do the same (Matthew 6:9).
  • If God is personal and the Creator, then each self is made for relationship with God: human identity is found in being known by God (links to Buber's I–Thou).
Mission 3Read the source

Made for God

> "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." (Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. Pusey)

> "Q. What is the chief end of man? A. Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." (Westminster Shorter Catechism, 1647, Q. 1)

> "Q. Why did God make you? A. God made me to know Him, to love Him, and to serve Him in this world, and to be happy with Him forever in the next." (Baltimore Catechism, 1885, Q. 6)

Q1 Which two purposes does the Westminster answer join together?

Show answer

Glorifying God and enjoying him: worship and relationship.

Q2 What does Augustine's "restless" heart suggest about life without God?

Show answer

It is incomplete; humans are made for God and find rest only in him.

  • Augustine, a named scholar for 4B-1.1: the purpose of the self is to rest in God.
Mission 3

A personal Creator: what follows?

  • Self: made in God's image (Genesis 1:27), each person has dignity and a calling; the self is relational, not self-made.
  • Purpose: life is a gift, to be lived in relationship with its giver (Augustine's restless heart).
  • Death: not the end of the relationship; a personal God does not abandon those he loves (Romans 8:38–39).
  • Afterlife: resurrection and life with God; a Creator who made from nothing can re-create.
  • Interpretations differ: an immutable God (Augustine) secures purpose; Buber stresses meeting God in the present, here and now.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is glorifying God the most important purpose?

  • Claim: Glorifying God is the most important purpose of Christian life.
  • For: God is the source and goal of everything (Romans 11:36); Westminster Q. 1 puts it first; the first commandment is love of God (Matthew 22:37–38).
  • For: If glory is "everything done for God", it includes the other two.
  • Against: Jesus' own preaching centred on the kingdom (Mark 1:15); faith without action for others is empty (Matthew 25).
  • Against: Without judgement, choices lose their eternal seriousness; many find this the strongest motive.
  • Against: A relationship with God may be more basic: glory is the overflow of knowing him (John 17:3).
  • Judge it by: Which purpose explains the others? Which did Jesus stress? Which motive is most worthy: love, fear or justice?
  • Link: does belief in a personal Creator make relationship the deepest purpose?
Mission check

?A Christian nurse in a war zone says: "I pray every morning, I try to live so I can face God at the end, and I'm here because God wants a world without this suffering." What does her statement best illustrate?

Glory, judgement and kingdom are rarely rivals in practice. The debate is about which one explains, or should lead, the others.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Make a guess: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Mission 4

One God, three persons

  • The Trinity: one God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Christians believe each person is fully God, yet there is one God, not three.
  • "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three views the Church rejected:
  • Tritheism: three separate gods.
  • Modalism: one person appearing in three modes (associated with Sabellius, 3rd century).
  • Subordinationism: the Son and Spirit as lesser than the Father.
Mission 4Read the source

Biblical basis

The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Christians find the pattern in it:

> "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, WEBBE)

> "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all." (2 Corinthians 13:14, WEBBE)

  • At Jesus' baptism, the Spirit descends "as a dove" and the Father's voice speaks (Matthew 3:16–17).
  • "yet to us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things … and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6): Paul places Jesus inside the Jewish confession of one God.
  • "I and the Father are one." (John 10:30)

Q1 In Matthew 28:19, how many "names" are there?

Show answer

One "name", shared by three.

Mission 4

The Son and the Spirit as God

  • Son: "the Word was God" (John 1:1); "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • Spirit: lying to the Holy Spirit is lying "to God" (Acts 5:3–4); "Where could I go from your Spirit?" (Psalm 139:7).
  • Christians read the Spirit as personal: teaches and reminds (John 14:26); not just a force.
  • Early Christians worshipped and baptised in the threefold name before the doctrine was defined.
  • The doctrine explains what Christians already did in prayer.

Mission 4

From Nicaea to Constantinople

  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios (of one substance) with the Father.
  • Cappadocian Fathers: Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395).
  • Their formula: one ousia (being, substance), three hypostaseis (persons).
  • Constantinople (381) completed the creed: the Spirit is "the Lord and Giver of life", who "proceeds from the Father", and "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified".
  • This Nicene Creed is shared by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
Mission 4

Co-equal and co-eternal

  • Co-equal: no person is greater or lesser in being; all share the one divine nature.
  • Co-eternal: no person had a beginning; there was never a time when the Father was without the Son and the Spirit.
  • The Athanasian Creed (Latin, 5th–6th century): none of the three is before or after another, none greater or less (paraphrase).
  • But a relation of origin: the Father begets, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds.
  • "The Father is greater than I" (John 14:28): read as Jesus speaking in his humanity, or of the Father as source, not of a greater being.
Mission 4

The filioque

  • Latin filioque: "and the Son". The West added it to the creed: the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son.
  • First used in Spain (Council of Toledo, 589); spread in the Frankish church; sung at Mass in Rome from 1014.
  • West (Augustine, On the Trinity): the Spirit is the bond of love between Father and Son; "the Spirit of his Son" (Galatians 4:6).
  • East (Photius, 9th century): the Father is the one source (monarchia) of Son and Spirit; John 15:26 says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father".
  • East also objected that a council's creed was changed without an ecumenical council.
  • A cause of the East–West Schism (1054), with papal authority.
  • Today: the Catholic Church omits the filioque when it uses the Greek creed; Lambeth Conferences (1978, 1988) asked Anglicans to consider omitting it.

Mission 4Evaluate

"The doctrine of the Trinity is consistent with monotheism."

  • For: there is one divine being (ousia); the persons are not three beings but three relations within one God.
  • For: the New Testament writers were Jewish monotheists who placed Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
  • Against: Jewish and Muslim critics: worshipping three persons compromises God's oneness.
  • Against: popular explanations slide into tritheism or modalism; perhaps no coherent middle exists.
  • Against: "person" today means a separate centre of consciousness, making three persons sound like three gods (see Mission 5).
  • Judge it by: What does "one" mean (number, being, unity)? Does it fit the texts? Can a mystery be consistent without being explained?
Mission check

For mainstream Christian teaching, the doctrine keeps two truths together: one God, and three distinct, co-equal, co-eternal persons; losing either is seen as the error.

Mission 5

Why analogy?

  • Christians hold that God is beyond human concepts; words apply to God by analogy: partly like, partly unlike their ordinary use (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 13).
  • Every picture of the Trinity helps one truth and risks losing another.
  • Familiar classroom images and their dangers:
  • Water, ice, steam: one thing in three states at different times → modalism.
  • Three leaves of a shamrock: each leaf only part of the plant → partialism.
  • Sun, light, heat: light and heat as effects of the sun → subordinationism.
  • Theologians use two deeper approaches: relation and analogy from human persons.
Mission 5

Persons as relations

  • In classical (especially Western) theology, the persons differ only in their relations of origin: the Father begets; the Son is begotten; the Spirit proceeds.
  • Everything else (power, goodness, eternity, will) is one and shared.
  • Aquinas: "a divine person signifies a relation as subsisting" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 29, a. 4).
  • So "Father" is not a role God plays; it names a real, eternal relation to the Son.
  • Perichoresis (mutual indwelling), developed by John of Damascus (c. 675–749): each person dwells in the others: "I am in the Father, and the Father in me" (John 14:10).
  • Strength: three without division. Weakness: hard to picture what a "subsisting relation" is.
Mission 5

Augustine: the mind as an image

  • Augustine (354–430), On the Trinity (De Trinitate, c. 400–420).
  • Humans are made in the image of the triune God, so the human mind may reflect it.
  • Lover, beloved, love (Book VIII): the Spirit as the love between Father and Son.
  • Memory, understanding, will (Book X): three acts, one mind; each involves the others.
  • Earlier, in Confessions XIII.11: "To Be, to Know, and to Will … how inseparable a life there is".
  • Augustine admits the image is faint: a human mind is one person; God is three.
  • Strength: protects oneness. Weakness: may make the persons look like functions of one person (a modalist tendency).
Mission 5

The social analogy

  • Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395), On "Not Three Gods": Peter, James and John share one human nature yet are three men.
  • He adds the limit: the three divine persons act inseparably in every work, so they are not three gods.
  • Social trinitarians today (e.g. Jürgen Moltmann, The Trinity and the Kingdom, 1980) picture the Trinity as a communion of love.
  • Used to support equality, community and mutual giving in church and society.
  • Strength: makes "God is love" (1 John 4:8) relational within God. Weakness: risks tritheism, three centres of consciousness.

Mission 5

Barth: the Trinity is how God reveals himself

  • Karl Barth (1886–1968), Swiss Reformed theologian, Church Dogmatics I/1 (1932; English 1936).
  • Barth puts the Trinity at the start of theology, not after proofs of God: it is the shape of revelation itself.
  • "God reveals Himself as the Lord" (CD I/1, §8): God is the Revealer (Father), the Revelation (Son) and the Revealedness, God's being made known to us (Spirit).
  • He prefers "modes of being" (Seinsweisen) to "persons", because "person" now suggests three separate personalities.
  • Impact: Christians know God only as God gives himself, in Christ by the Spirit, so faith starts from hearing God's Word, not speculation.
Mission 5

Rahner: the Trinity we meet is the Trinity God is

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), German Jesuit theologian, The Trinity (1967; English 1970).
  • Complaint: in practice Christians are "almost mere 'monotheists'": the Trinity changes nothing in their faith (p. 10).
  • Rahner's Rule: "The 'economic' Trinity is the 'immanent' Trinity", and vice versa (p. 22).
  • Economic Trinity: God as met in the history of salvation (the Son sent, the Spirit given). Immanent Trinity: God as God is in himself.
  • So the Son and the Spirit really are God giving himself (God's self-communication), not messengers.
  • Like Barth, he is wary of "persons" and speaks of three "distinct manners of subsisting".

Mission 5Evaluate

Barth and Rahner: strengths and weaknesses

  • Strengths (both): ground the Trinity in revelation and salvation, not speculation; make the doctrine matter for faith; avoid tritheism.
  • Barth, weakness: "modes of being" sounds modalist to critics (e.g. Moltmann, 1980); his scheme starts from revelation as an event, which some find abstract.
  • Rahner, weakness: "vice versa" may tie God's inner life to history, as if God needed the world to be Trinity (critics, e.g. Paul Molnar, 2002).
  • Both: abandoning "persons" may make prayer to Father, Son and Spirit as "you" harder.
  • Social trinitarians reply: only three real persons in communion explain "God is love".
  • Judge it by: Does the view keep both oneness and threeness? Does it fit Scripture's Father, Son and Spirit addressing one another? Does it help Christians pray and live?
Mission 5

What difference does it make?

  • Prayer: to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit; Barth and Rahner both stress that this pattern is God's own life given to us.
  • Worship: baptism in the threefold name; the doxology ("Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit").
  • Experience of grace: Rahner: every experience of grace is God's self-giving in Son and Spirit.
  • Hearing the Word: Barth: Christians stand under God's Word, not over it.
  • Community: social trinitarians see the Church and society called to mirror mutual love and equality.
  • Significance: for many Christians, the Trinity is not a puzzle beside the faith but its centre: God is love in himself.
Mission check

?A Christian writer says: "We meet God the Father sending his Son and pouring out his Spirit. That is not a mask over some hidden God; this is what God truly is in himself." Whose view is this closest to?

Relation, analogy and modern views each protect part of the mystery. Christians judge them by whether they keep one God and three persons together.

Mission 6

Two questions about the Church

  • Church translates Greek ekklesia: an assembly of people called out, not first a building.
  • In Matthew's Gospel, Jesus tells Peter: "on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18).
  • Christians disagree on two linked questions:
  • Who has authority? The Bible alone, or the Bible read within the Church's teaching?
  • Where is the true Church? A visible body with bishops, or wherever the gospel is truly heard?
  • The Nicene Creed (381) confesses "one holy catholic and apostolic Church": the four marks.
Mission 6

Catholic: Scripture, Tradition, Magisterium

  • Council of Trent (1546): revealed truth comes in written books and in unwritten traditions handed down from the apostles.
  • Vatican II, Dei Verbum (1965), 9–10 (paraphrase): Scripture and Tradition flow from one source and form one sacred deposit of the word of God.
  • The Magisterium (the Pope and bishops in communion with him) interprets that word with authority.
  • But the Magisterium is not above the word: it serves it, teaching only what has been handed on (DV 10).
  • Argument used: the Church came first. It wrote the New Testament and recognised the canon.
  • Matthew 16:18–19: the keys given to Peter ground the bishops' authority to teach.
Mission 6

Protestant: Scripture alone

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): popes and councils can err (Leipzig Debate, 1519); at Worms (1521) he would retract only if shown wrong by Scripture and plain reason (paraphrase).
  • Sola scriptura: Scripture is the supreme authority that judges every church teaching.
  • "Every Scripture is God-breathed and profitable for teaching" (2 Timothy 3:16).
  • Not "no tradition": most Protestants keep the creeds, because they agree with Scripture.
  • Church of England, Article VI (1571): nothing is required for salvation that cannot be read in or proved from Scripture (paraphrase).
  • Anglican writers after Richard Hooker (1554–1600) speak of Scripture first, then reason and tradition.
  • Orthodox (contrast): the Bible lives inside Holy Tradition, with the councils and the liturgy.

Mission 6

The four marks

  • One (unity): "one body and one Spirit … one Lord, one faith, one baptism" (Ephesians 4:4–5).
  • Holy (holiness): set apart for God; "a holy nation" (1 Peter 2:9).
  • Catholic (catholicity): from Greek kath' holou, "according to the whole": the full faith, for all nations (Matthew 28:19).
  • Apostolic (apostolicity): "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets" (Ephesians 2:20).
  • All mainstream churches confess the four marks. They disagree about where the marks are found.
Mission 6Read the source

Cyprian on unity

Cyprian of Carthage (c. 200–258), bishop from c. 249, martyred 258. On the Unity of the Catholic Church (251), written after Christians lapsed under persecution and a rival bishop was set up in Rome.

> "The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole." (section 5)

> "He can no longer have God for his Father, who has not the Church for his mother." (section 6)

> "If any one could escape who was outside the ark of Noah, then he also may escape who shall be outside of the Church." (section 6)

Q1 What holds the Church together, for Cyprian?

Show answer

The one episcopate: the bishops, sharing one office.

Q2 What follows for someone who leaves the Church?

Show answer

They cannot be saved: there is no "Father" without the "mother".

Mission 6

Cyprian's Church: visible and bounded

  • One: unity is visible communion with your bishop; schism is the worst sin against the Church.
  • Holy: the Church's holiness lives in its sacraments; he held that baptisms done outside the Church are not valid (dispute with Pope Stephen, 256).
  • Catholic: the one communion spread through the world, like rays of one sun (section 5).
  • Apostolic: bishops succeed the apostles; the chair of Peter is the source of unity.
  • "Outside the Church there is no salvation" (Latin salus extra ecclesiam non est, Letter 73.21, paraphrase).
  • Impact: belonging to the visible Church is not optional; leaving it is spiritually fatal.
Mission 6

Luther's Church: the hearers of the gospel

  • The Church is the holy believers, "sheep who hear the voice of their shepherd" (Smalcald Articles, 1537, III.12, paraphrase; John 10:27).
  • The true Church is partly hidden: only God sees who truly believes.
  • It is known by marks (On the Councils and the Church, 1539): the word of God, baptism, the Lord's Supper, the keys (forgiving sins), called ministers, public prayer and praise, and suffering for Christ.
  • One: unity in the gospel, not in obedience to the Pope.
  • Holy: Christ's holiness given through faith; the members are still sinners.
  • Catholic: the universal faith, not the Roman institution.
  • Apostolic: faithful to the apostles' teaching, not a line of bishops.
  • Impact: priesthood of all believers (To the Christian Nobility, 1520; 1 Peter 2:9): every baptised Christian may read Scripture and pray without a priestly go-between.
Mission 6Diversity

Where are the marks found today?

  • Catholic: the Church of Christ "subsists in" the Catholic Church (Lumen Gentium 8, 1964); elements of truth exist in other churches.
  • Orthodox: the Orthodox Church is the one Church, kept by bishops and the councils; it rejects papal supremacy.
  • Lutheran and Reformed: the Church is where the gospel is purely preached and the sacraments rightly given (Augsburg Confession VII, 1530).
  • Free churches (e.g. Baptist): each gathered congregation of believers is fully church.
  • Challenges to both views: Christian division looks like a broken "one"; scandals look like a broken "holy". Augustine's reply (against the Donatists): the Church on earth is a mixed body of saints and sinners.
  • Ecumenism tries to make visible a unity many believe already exists in Christ.

Mission 6Evaluate

"The Church cannot be one while Christians are divided."

  • For: John 17:21 prays "that they may all be one … that the world may believe"; unity is meant to be visible (Cyprian agrees that unity must be visible).
  • For: thousands of denominations make "one" look like a slogan.
  • Against: Luther: unity lies in the one gospel and one Lord, which divided churches can share.
  • Against: Cyprian: the Church's unity cannot be severed; those who divide leave the one Church, which stays one (On the Unity, sections 12 and 23, paraphrase).
  • Against: Catholic and Orthodox views: the one Church exists in full in one communion, despite others' separation.
  • Against: ecumenical bodies (e.g. the World Council of Churches, 1948) show real unity in baptism and the creeds.
  • Judge it by: Must unity be visible? Is unity in belief enough? Whose test of the marks is used?

The four marks are shared; where they are found is the real dispute. A later mission: what the first Christian community looked like, and what churches do today.

Mission 7: Christians say love is the root of every value. Where does that love come from, and what does it ask?

Make a guess: Christians say love is the root of every value. Where does that love come from, and what does it ask?

Mission 7

Love as the principal value

  • Christians call the highest love agape: self-giving love that seeks the good of the other, even an enemy.
  • Jesus: the greatest commandments are to love God and to love "your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:29–31).
  • Paul: "faith, hope, and love remain … The greatest of these is love" (1 Corinthians 13:13, WEBBE).
  • "Above all these things, walk in love, which is the bond of perfection" (Colossians 3:14, WEBBE): love holds the other virtues together.
  • Your spec: love as the principal value and the basis of all other values and qualities.
Mission 7

Love starts with God

  • "God is love" (1 John 4:8, WEBBE).
  • "We love him, because he first loved us" (1 John 4:19, WEBBE).
  • So Christian love is a response: people love because they have first been loved by God.
  • This makes love more than a feeling: it is sharing in God's own life and way of acting.
Mission 7

Creation, incarnation, Trinity

  • Creation: God did not need to create; creation is a free gift of love. "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31, WEBBE).
  • Incarnation: "God has sent his only born Son into the world that we might live through him" (1 John 4:9, WEBBE): love that comes close and shares human life.
  • Trinity: God is love within himself: the Father loved the Son "before the foundation of the world" (John 17:24, WEBBE).
  • Augustine, On the Trinity VIII–IX: in love there are three: the lover, the beloved, and the love between them (paraphrase).
  • So love is not something God does now and then; it is what God is, eternally.
  • Christian values follow: care for creation, self-giving like the incarnation, and community like the Trinity.

> "If a man says, 'I love God,' and hates his brother, he is a liar; for he who doesn't love his brother whom he has seen, how can he love God whom he has not seen?" (1 John 4:20, WEBBE)

Q1 According to verse 10, which love comes first?

Show answer

God's love for people, shown in sending his Son.

Q2 What test does verse 20 give for whether someone loves God?

Show answer

Whether they love their brother or sister: love of God must show itself in love of others.

Mission 7

Truth, conscience, forgiveness

  • Truth: "serve him in truth with all your heart" (1 Samuel 12:24, WEBBE); "putting away falsehood, speak truth each one with his neighbour" (Ephesians 4:25, WEBBE). Love "rejoices with the truth" (1 Corinthians 13:6).
  • Conscience: Paul appeals to "the testimony of our conscience" (2 Corinthians 1:12, WEBBE); the goal is "love from a pure heart, a good conscience, and sincere faith" (1 Timothy 1:5, WEBBE).
  • Forgiveness: "if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (Matthew 6:14, WEBBE); "even as Christ forgave you, so you also do" (Colossians 3:13, WEBBE).
  • 1.4c: each value is love in a particular form: truth is love that will not deceive; conscience is love's inner guide; forgiveness is love that does not keep score.
Mission 7Evaluate

Is love of neighbour the most important principle?

  • Claim: Love of neighbour is the most important moral principle in Christianity.
  • For: "The whole law is fulfilled in one word": love your neighbour (Galatians 5:14); the Sheep and the Goats judges by care for the needy (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • For: 1 John 4:20: love of God is tested by love of others.
  • Against: Jesus calls love of God the "first commandment" (Mark 12:30); love of neighbour flows from it.
  • Against: Love needs truth and justice to guide it; without them "love" can excuse wrong.
  • Judgement criteria: What Jesus ranks first · whether the principles can be separated · how each guides real decisions.
  • The same map answers whether all values really depend on love (1.4c).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe one omnipotent, transcendent God created everything ex nihilo, freely, and called it very good; emanation and construction are rejected models.
  • God is immutable yet personal: Augustine's restless heart and Buber's eternal Thou describe a relationship with God.
  • Implications: humans are made in God's image, for God; stewards of creation; hope beyond death rests on God's faithfulness.

Mission 2

  • Dominion (Genesis 1:28) has been read as domination or as stewardship (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 24:1).

Mission 3

  • Three purposes: glorify God and know him personally; prepare for judgement; bring about God's kingdom on earth.
  • Traditions differ in emphasis (Reformed glory; Catholic beatific vision; Orthodox theosis; evangelical relationship; liberal and liberation kingdom).
  • A personal Creator implies a relational self, a purpose in God, and hope beyond death.

Mission 4

  • Christians believe in one God in three co-equal, co-eternal persons; not three gods, not three modes, not ranked.
  • Biblical basis: the threefold name (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14), the divinity of Son and Spirit, and Jewish monotheism (Deuteronomy 6:4).
  • Paul places Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
  • Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) defined it; the West's filioque helped divide East and West.

Mission 5

  • Analogies help and limp: water (modalism), shamrock (partialism), sun (subordinationism); in classical theology, relations of origin and perichoresis distinguish the persons without dividing God.
  • Augustine's psychological analogy protects oneness; the social analogy (Gregory of Nyssa, Moltmann) protects threeness.
  • Barth: God reveals himself as Revealer, Revelation, Revealedness ("modes of being"); Rahner: the economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity. Both are praised for linking the Trinity to faith and criticised for leaning towards modalism.

Mission 6

  • Catholics: Scripture and Tradition form one deposit, interpreted by the Magisterium; Protestants: Scripture alone is the final authority; Orthodox: the Bible within Holy Tradition.
  • The four marks are one, holy, catholic and apostolic. Cyprian finds them in the visible Church united with its bishops; Luther in the community where the gospel is preached and the sacraments given.
  • Division and scandal challenge every account; Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants answer differently.

Mission 7

  • Love (agape) is the principal Christian value: it holds all others together (Colossians 3:14).
  • Its basis is God's own love: in creation, incarnation and the Trinity.
