Mission 1

  • Many central banks target inflation of about 2%.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 1

More objectives

  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Development: better health, education and living standards, not just higher GDP.
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.

Mission 1

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.

Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • Growth that uses up non-renewable resources may not be sustainable.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 1

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 1

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Quick check

Mission 1Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.

Mission 2

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 2

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
Mission 2

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a negative output gap: real output and employment rise; the price level rises a little.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close a positive output gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes.

Mission 2

Two views: Keynesian vs monetarist and new classical

  • Keynesian view: AS is flat when there is lots of spare capacity.
    • In a deep recession, expansionary fiscal policy raises real output and jobs with little inflation.
    • Near full capacity, it mainly raises the price level.
  • Monetarist / new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output.
    • In the short run, more G raises output and the price level.
    • In the long run, wages and costs adjust: SRAS shifts left. Output returns to potential, at a higher price level.
    • Government borrowing may push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • So the two views disagree on how far fiscal policy can raise output for long.
Mission 2

Fiscal policy on the supply side

  • Some fiscal measures also shift LRAS right:
    • capital spending on infrastructure, broadband, transport
    • spending on education, training and health
    • tax incentives for investment or research
  • These raise productive capacity: more output with less pressure on prices.
  • But they take years, and the first effect is on AD (spending now).
Mission 2

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilisers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
  • Political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; tax rises and spending cuts are not.
  • Sustainable debt: high debt raises interest costs and may limit room for future stimulus.
Mission 2

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • UK 2008–09: VAT was cut from 17.5% to 15% from December 2008 to December 2009, to support spending during the recession. Its effect is debated: some spending was brought forward rather than added.
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary.
Mission 2

The Laffer curve

  • Plot tax revenue against the tax rate.
  • At a 0% rate, revenue is zero. At 100%, no one has a reason to earn taxable income: revenue is zero too.
  • Revenue peaks at some rate in between (t*).
  • Above t*, a cut in the rate could raise revenue: people work, invest and declare more income.
  • Below t*, a cut lowers revenue.
  • Where t* lies is uncertain and differs by tax and country. Evidence on it is debated.
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?
Mission 2Evaluate

How effective is fiscal policy?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, extra demand raises output; near capacity it mostly raises prices.
  • Size of the multiplier: high saving or imports weaken the effect.
  • Time lags: stimulus can arrive too late.
  • Confidence and debt: if people expect future tax rises to repay debt, they may save more. If markets doubt debt is sustainable, interest rates may rise.
  • Crowding out: borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment (stronger near full capacity).
  • Incentives: above the Laffer peak, cutting tax rates may raise revenue; below it, they lower revenue.
Mission check

Fiscal policy can lift a slump, but its effect depends on timing and on how much spare capacity is left.

Mission 3

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
  • Say: "capital spending" for long-lasting assets — not: "spending on capital cities" or "big spending".
Quick check

Mission 3

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
Mission 3

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 3

How deficits are financed

  • Main method: the government sells bonds (UK: gilts). A bond is a promise to pay interest each year and repay the sum at a set date.
  • Buyers: pension funds, insurers, banks, households, foreign investors, and sometimes the central bank.
  • Implications:
  • Interest must be paid every year, from future taxes.
  • If foreigners hold the debt, interest payments flow abroad.
  • Heavy borrowing can push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • Borrowing from the central bank (it creates new money to buy bonds) increases the money supply: it risks inflation if output cannot rise.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): the central bank creates money to buy government bonds from banks and other investors, raising the money supply and lowering long-term interest rates.
  • A balance of payments surplus can raise the money supply too: foreign currency flows in, and when the central bank buys it with newly created domestic currency, the money supply rises.
Mission 3

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.
  • Rough figures for gross government debt, 2023: Japan over 200% · Italy about 135% · US about 120% · UK about 100% · Germany about 62%.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, billion): debt 800 · nominal GDP 1,000 → ratio 80%.
  • Next year nominal GDP grows 5% to 1,050.

Mission 3

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Example: in April 2010 Greece's rating was cut to "junk" by S&P. Its 10-year bond yields rose above 10%, and it needed EU and IMF loans.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is a deficit a problem?

It depends on:

  • Why it exists: a cyclical deficit falls as the economy recovers; a structural deficit does not.
  • What it pays for: capital spending adds assets that can raise productive capacity; most current spending pays for today's services.
  • Interest rate vs growth: if the interest rate on the debt is below the growth rate of nominal GDP, the debt ratio is easier to keep stable.
  • Who lends: domestic lenders, long-dated bonds and borrowing in the country's own currency usually reduce the risk of a sudden crisis.
  • Starting level and confidence: the same deficit worries lenders more when debt is already high.
  • Economists disagree on how high is too high. There is no single safe debt ratio.

Mission 4

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is levied on.
    • Income tax, corporation tax (on company profits), taxes on wealth.
  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government, but can pass some or all of it on to buyers in higher prices.
    • VAT (a sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports.
  • How much of an indirect tax buyers bear depends on elasticity.
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Mission 4

Why tax, and what makes a good tax

  • Reasons to tax:
    • raise revenue for public spending
    • redistribute income and wealth
    • correct market failure (taxes on pollution, tobacco)
    • manage aggregate demand (fiscal policy)
    • protect domestic industries (tariffs)
  • Adam Smith's canons of a good tax (1776):
    • equity: in line with ability to pay
    • certainty: clear what, when and how much
    • convenience: easy to pay
    • economy: cheap to collect
  • Modern additions: efficiency (few distortions to choices) and flexibility (easy to change).
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 4

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Judge a tax by the share of income it takes, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the share of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the share stays the same at every income.
  • Regressive: the share falls as income rises.
  • A regressive tax can still take more money from richer people.
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a larger proportion of a low income" — not: "poor people pay more money".
Mission 4

Average and marginal tax rates

  • Average tax rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
  • Marginal tax rate (MRT) = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100: the tax on the next unit of income.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income.
  • Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • Regressive: ART falls as income rises.
  • Moving into a higher band raises the rate only on income inside that band.
Mission 4

  • Illustrative data.

  • With an allowance, a flat tax is mildly progressive. With no allowance, it is proportional.
  • Real case: Estonia introduced a flat income tax in 1994; several central and eastern European countries followed.

Mission 4

The Laffer curve

  • Tax revenue = tax rate × tax base (the income or spending being taxed).
  • At a 0% rate, revenue is zero. At 100%, there is little point earning declared income, so revenue is close to zero.
  • In between, revenue reaches a maximum at some rate, t*.
  • Below t*: a higher rate raises revenue.
  • Above t*: the tax base shrinks so much that a higher rate lowers revenue. A cut could raise revenue.
  • The base shrinks through less work and investment, more avoidance and evasion, and people or firms moving abroad.
  • Nobody knows exactly where t* is. Estimates differ, and most economists think most tax rates are below it.
  • Real case: in 2012 HMRC estimated that cutting the UK top income tax rate from 50% to 45% would cost only about £100 million a year, because high earners respond strongly to the top rate. HMRC stressed the estimate was very uncertain.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a tax rate is currently 70%, and the revenue-maximising rate is 55%. The government cuts the rate to 60%. According to the Laffer curve, what happens to tax revenue?
Mission 4Evaluate

Should a country make its taxes more progressive?

It depends on:

  • How people respond: if high earners cut work, move or avoid tax, revenue may rise less than planned.
  • Value judgements about equity: how much inequality is acceptable is a normative question.
  • Which taxes change: raising income tax rates at the top differs from cutting regressive taxes on spending.
  • Administration: complex systems cost more to run and leave more room for avoidance.

Mission 5

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • Tools: interest rates, the money supply and credit regulations (limits on bank lending).
  • A central bank (such as the European Central Bank or the Reserve Bank of India) sets a policy interest rate (base rate).
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 5

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • Many central banks target low, stable inflation, often about 2% a year.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 5

The central bank's tools

  • Policy rate (base rate, Bank Rate, discount rate): the rate the central bank pays or charges banks.
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds. Buying adds money to the banking system; selling drains it.
  • Minimum reserve requirements: the share of deposits banks must hold as reserves.
  • Credit regulations: limits on how much or to whom banks may lend.
Mission 5

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
Mission 5

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 5

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • Euro area: the European Central Bank raised its deposit rate from −0.5% (2022) to 4.0% (September 2023). Inflation had peaked at 10.6% (October 2022).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 5

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 5

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance.
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 5Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 6

What supply-side policies aim to do

  • Supply-side policies: government policies that aim to raise the economy's productive capacity.
  • They work on the quantity and quality of factors of production and on how efficiently markets work.
  • Goals:
    • long-term growth: LRAS shifts right
    • a lower natural rate of unemployment
    • growth with less inflationary pressure
    • better international competitiveness
Mission 6

On the AD/AS diagram

  • Capacity rises: LRAS shifts right.
  • Productive capacity rises from Yfe1 to Yfe2.
  • With AD unchanged: real output rises, the price level falls.
  • Lower costs per unit often shift SRAS right too.
  • On the Keynesian AS, the vertical section shifts right.
  • Employment can rise at a lower natural rate of unemployment.

Mission 6

Worked example: per person, what can policy change?

  • Illustrative data. Real GDP per person = output per hour × hours per worker × workers ÷ population.
  • Population 10 million; workers 5 million; 1,600 hours each a year; output per hour 50.
  • Real GDP = 50 × 1,600 × 5 million = 400 billion. Per person: 40,000.
  • Policy 1: childcare and job support bring 0.5 million more people into work. Real GDP = 440 billion; per person 44,000 (+10%).
  • Policy 2: training and better equipment raise output per hour to 55. Per person also 44,000 (+10%).
  • Both together: 48,400 per person: +21%, not +20%, because the effects multiply.
  • Labour productivity (output per worker or per hour) depends on capital per worker, skills, health, technology and management.

Mission 6

Market-based policies: how they work

  • Lower income tax raises the reward for work: some work more hours or join the labour force.
  • Lower profit tax raises the return on investment: more capital, more productivity.
  • Benefit reform: a bigger gap between income in and out of work makes taking a job more attractive. Voluntary unemployment falls: a lower natural rate.
  • Deregulation and privatisation: fewer rules and a profit motive push firms to cut costs and innovate.
  • Competition policy and trade liberalisation: rivals force firms to be efficient.
  • Labour market reform: weaker union power and more flexible contracts let wages and jobs adjust faster.
  • But: some of these may widen inequality or weaken job security.
Mission 6

Interventionist policies: how they work

  • Education and training raise human capital: more output per worker. Fewer skill gaps: lower structural unemployment.
  • Health care: fewer days lost to illness; longer working lives.
  • R&D: new ideas spill over to other firms (a positive externality), so markets under-invest. Subsidies or tax credits raise R&D.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail and broadband cut firms' costs and link workers to jobs.
  • Industrial policy: support for strategic industries.
  • But: costly, slow, and the government may "pick losers" (government failure).
Quick check
?A government sells its state-owned rail company to private investors and lets rival firms bid to run routes. How is this policy meant to raise LRAS?
Mission 6

Two effects at different speeds

  • Many supply-side policies also change spending:
    • building roads or schools is government spending: AD shifts right at once
    • a tax cut raises disposable income: AD shifts right
  • The supply-side effect comes later, once the road is built or the trained workers arrive: LRAS shifts right.
  • So fiscal policy can have supply-side effects (e.g. tax incentives), and supply-side policies can have demand-side effects.

Mission 6

Real world: cutting taxes on company profits

  • The US cut its main federal corporate tax rate from 35% to 21% from 2018.
  • Aim: more investment in capital, so higher productivity and pay.
  • One 2024 study estimated investment rose by about 11% at firms given the average tax cut, compared with firms given none, but wages and growth rose by less than supporters had claimed.
  • The UK cut its main corporation tax rate from 28% (2010) to 19% (2017), then raised it to 25% in 2023 for profits over £250,000.
  • Question to weigh: how would you judge whether a tax cut on profits raised capacity, and over how many years?
Mission 6Evaluate

How well do supply-side policies work?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education, health and infrastructure take years, even decades, to raise capacity.
  • Response to incentives. Tax cuts may change effort or investment only a little; profits may go to shareholders instead.
  • Cost and trade-offs. Interventionist policies need spending (opportunity cost); market-based ones may widen inequality.
  • The output gap. With weak AD, extra capacity sits unused; Keynesians would boost demand first.
  • Government failure. Poor information can lead to wasted subsidies or badly designed rules.
  • Strength: if they work, growth with lower inflation and a lower natural rate of unemployment.
Mission check

?A government expands free childcare and job training, paid for by cutting other spending, so AD does not change. Over several years, more parents take jobs and workers become more skilled. According to the AD/AS model, what happens in the long run?

Supply-side policies work on capacity, not spending, and capacity takes years to build.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.

Mission 2

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.
  • Crowding out may limit it too.

Mission 3

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 4

  • Direct taxes fall on income and wealth. Indirect taxes fall on spending.
  • Progressive, proportional or regressive: judged by the share of income paid (ART), not the amount.
  • ART = total tax ÷ income. MRT = tax on the next unit of income.
  • Laffer curve: above the revenue-maximising rate, a lower rate can raise revenue.

Mission 5

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 6

  • Supply-side policies aim to raise capacity: LRAS shifts right, raising real output without adding to inflation.
  • Market-based policies use incentives and competition; interventionist policies fix under-provision of skills, health, R&D and infrastructure.
  • Effects take years and are uncertain in size.
