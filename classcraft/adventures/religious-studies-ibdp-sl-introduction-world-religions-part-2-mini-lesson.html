Mission 1

Christianity in brief

  • Christianity centres on Jesus of Nazareth (c. 4 BCE – c. 30 CE), whom Christians believe to be the Son of God and the Messiah (Christ).
  • Christians believe in the Incarnation (God became human in Jesus) and the Resurrection (Jesus rose from death on the third day).
  • Most Christians believe in God as Trinity: one God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Scripture: the Bible, Old Testament and New Testament.
  • Three main families: Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant (many denominations). Answers to the three questions vary between them.
Mission 1

The condition: made good, in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)
  • For Christians, imago Dei means every person has dignity, reason, freedom and the capacity to love God.
  • Christians believe creation was good (Genesis 1) and that humans were made for relationship with God, each other and the world.
  • So for Christians the human condition begins with worth, not with sin.
Mission 1

The Fall and sin

  • Genesis 3: Adam and Eve disobey God; they are sent out of Eden. Christians call this the Fall.
  • Sin: turning away from God; it breaks relationship with God, others, creation and oneself.
  • "for all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God;" (Romans 3:23, WEBBE)
  • Paul links Adam's sin with death: "as sin entered into the world through one man, and death through sin, so death passed to all men because all sinned." (Romans 5:12, WEBBE)
  • For most Christians, the human condition: made in God's image, but damaged by sin and unable to restore the relationship alone.
Mission 1Diversity

What is "original sin"?

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430): all humans inherit from Adam both a wounded nature and guilt: original sin. Influential in Catholic and Protestant thought.
  • Catholic teaching: original sin is a lost state of holiness, not personal fault; baptism removes it, though a tendency to sin remains.
  • Orthodox Christians often speak of ancestral sin: humans inherit mortality and a tendency to sin, but not Adam's guilt.
  • Reformers such as John Calvin (1509–1564) stressed that sin affects every part of human nature (later called "total depravity").
  • Many liberal Christians read Genesis 3 as a symbolic story about every human life rather than a historical event.

Mission 1

Where are we going? Salvation and the Kingdom

  • Salvation: being rescued from sin and death and restored to relationship with God.
  • "For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 6:23, WEBBE)
  • Eternal life is not just endless time: it begins now in knowing God and is completed after death.
  • The Kingdom of God: God's reign. Jesus proclaimed it: "God's Kingdom is at hand!" (Mark 1:15, WEBBE). Christians see it as present now and still to come.
  • After death: heaven (eternal life with God); hell (separation from God; interpreted variously); purgatory for Catholics (purification before heaven).
  • Orthodox Christians describe the goal as theosis: being made like God by grace.
Mission 1Read the source

Resurrection

Jesus to Martha, after the death of her brother Lazarus:

> "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies." (John 11:25, WEBBE)

> "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22, WEBBE)

  • Christians believe in the resurrection of the body (the creeds): not just a soul escaping, but a new, transformed life, as Jesus' own resurrection shows.

Mission 1

How do we get there? Grace and faith

  • Christians believe humans cannot save themselves; salvation is God's grace: undeserved love and help.
  • Christians believe that through Jesus' death on the cross (atonement) and his resurrection, God reconciles humans to himself.
  • "for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God," (Ephesians 2:8, WEBBE)
  • Faith: trust in God through Christ, not only agreement with ideas.
  • Response: repentance (turning from sin), baptism, and a life led by the Holy Spirit.
Mission 1Diversity

Sacraments

  • Sacrament: an outward sign through which Christians believe God gives grace.
  • Catholic and Orthodox churches (Orthodox: "holy mysteries"): seven (baptism, confirmation/chrismation, Eucharist, penance/confession, anointing of the sick, marriage, holy orders).
  • Most Protestant churches: two, those Jesus commanded: baptism and the Lord's Supper (Holy Communion).
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army celebrate no outward sacraments: all of life is open to God's grace.
  • The Eucharist is central for most Christians, though they understand Christ's presence in it differently.
Mission 1Read the source

Love and works

> "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind, and with all your strength." (Mark 12:30, WEBBE)

> "The second is like this: 'You shall love your neighbour as yourself.'" (Mark 12:31, WEBBE)

> "Even so faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17, WEBBE)

  • Martin Luther (1483–1546): we are justified (made right with God) by faith alone; good works follow as fruit.
  • Catholic teaching (Council of Trent, 1545–1563): faith and works go together, both made possible by grace.
  • Q: Why might both Luther and the Catholic Church accept James 2:17? (Both say real faith shows itself in love; they differ on whether works are part of being justified.)

Mission 2

Islam in brief

  • Islam means submission (to the will of God); a Muslim is "one who submits". The word is linked to salam, peace.
  • Tawhid: the oneness of God (Allah, the Arabic word for God).
  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's word, revealed to the Prophet Muhammad (c. 570–632) through the angel Jibril (Gabriel) from 610 to 632.
  • The Sunnah (the Prophet's example, recorded in hadith) guides how to live the Qur'an.
  • Two main branches: Sunni (the majority) and Shi'a. Sufis (in both) stress the inner, mystical path.
Mission 2

The condition: fitrah and khalifah

  • Fitrah: Muslims believe every person is born with a natural disposition to know and worship God.

> "the nature (framed) of Allah, in which He hath created man. There is no altering (the laws of) Allah's creation." (Qur'an 30:30, Pickthall)

  • Khalifah: Muslims believe humans are God's vicegerent (trustee) on earth: "Lo! I am about to place a viceroy in the earth" (Qur'an 2:30, Pickthall).
  • In Islamic teaching, humans have reason and free will, and are accountable for how they use them.
  • So for Muslims the human condition starts with goodness and responsibility, not with guilt.
Mission 2Read the source

Adam forgot

> "And verily We made a covenant of old with Adam, but he forgot, and We found no constancy in him." (Qur'an 20:115, Pickthall)

> "Then Adam received from his Lord words (of revelation), and He relented toward him. Lo! He is the relenting, the Merciful." (Qur'an 2:37, Pickthall)

Q1 What word does 20:115 use for Adam's failure?

Show answer

"Forgot": forgetfulness, not a fallen nature.

Q2 What happens to Adam's sin in 2:37?

Show answer

God accepts his repentance and forgives him, so no guilt passes to his descendants.

Mission 2

Forgetfulness and the test

  • The human problem: ghaflah, heedlessness or forgetfulness of God.
  • Pulls away from God: the nafs (the lower, selfish self), the whisperings of Shaytan, pride and love of the world.
  • The worst sin is shirk: associating anything with God.
  • Life is a test: "Who hath created life and death that He may try you which of you is best in conduct;" (Qur'an 67:2, Pickthall)
  • Each person answers only for their own deeds: "nor doth any laden bear another's load." (Qur'an 6:164, Pickthall)
  • Al-Qadr: God's decree and knowledge of all things. Muslims hold both God's decree and human responsibility; Sunni and Shi'a theologians explain the balance differently.

Mission 2

Where are we going? Akhirah

  • Akhirah: the life after death; belief in it is an article of faith.
  • At death the soul enters barzakh, a waiting state, until the Day of Judgement (Yawm al-Qiyamah / Yawm ad-Din).
  • On that Day the dead are raised, and every deed is weighed:

> "And whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then, And whoso doeth ill an atom's weight will see it then." (Qur'an 99:7–8, Pickthall)

  • "Every soul will taste of death." (Qur'an 3:185, Pickthall)
  • Shi'a Muslims count the Day of Resurrection (Ma'ad) among the five roots of religion (Usul ad-Din).
Mission 2

Paradise, Hell and mercy

  • Jannah (Paradise, "the Garden"): reward for those who believed and did good; the Qur'an describes gardens, rivers and peace.
  • Jahannam (Hell): punishment for those who rejected God and did evil.
  • Mercy: Muslims believe God is al-Rahman, al-Rahim (the Most Merciful, the Most Compassionate); salvation finally rests on God's mercy, not on deeds alone.
  • Many Muslims, especially Sufis, describe the deepest goal as nearness to God and God's pleasure (ridwan), beyond the Garden itself.
  • Scholars differ on whether Jahannam is eternal for all who enter it; many hold that believing sinners are eventually brought out.

Mission 2

How do we get there? Submission

  • The path is in the name: islam, submitting one's will to God.
  • The Hadith of Jibril (Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith, paraphrase) names three levels:
  • Islam: outward practice (the Pillars).
  • Iman: faith: belief in God, angels, books, prophets, the Last Day and God's decree.
  • Ihsan: excellence: to worship God as if you see Him; for though you do not see Him, He sees you.
  • Remembering God (dhikr) is the cure for forgetfulness: "Show us the straight path," (Qur'an 1:6, Pickthall), prayed in every unit of salah.
Mission 2Diversity

Pillars and roots

  • Sunni Muslims: Five Pillars (arkan al-Islam): Shahadah (declaration of faith), Salah (prayer five times a day), Zakah (giving 2.5% of eligible wealth), Sawm (fasting in Ramadan), Hajj (pilgrimage to Makkah once in a lifetime, if able).
  • Shi'a Muslims: five Usul ad-Din (roots of religion: Tawhid, Adalah (God's justice), Nubuwwah (prophethood), Imamah, Ma'ad) and ten obligatory acts (Furu ad-Din), including salah, sawm, zakah, khums, hajj and jihad.
  • Practices remind the believer of God through the day, the year and a lifetime: an answer to forgetfulness.
Mission 2

Shari'a: the path to water

  • Shari'a: literally "the path to a watering place": God's guidance for all of life (worship, family, trade, ethics), not only criminal law.
  • Fiqh: the human work of understanding it.
  • Sunni sources: Qur'an, Sunnah, ijma (consensus), qiyas (analogy); four main schools (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, Hanbali).
  • Shi'a (Twelver): Qur'an, Sunnah (including the Imams' teaching), consensus and 'aql (reason), applied by qualified scholars (mujtahids).
  • Sufis stress the inner journey (tariqa) alongside shari'a, purifying the heart.

Mission 3

Taoism in brief

  • Tao (Dao): the Way: the source and pattern of everything; impersonal, beyond words.
  • De (Te): the virtue or power of the Tao at work in a thing or a person.
  • Two classic texts: the Tao Te Ching (Daodejing), traditionally by Laozi (Lao Tzu), probably compiled c. 4th–3rd century BCE; and the Zhuangzi, named after Zhuangzi (c. 4th century BCE).
  • Taoist religious movements: the Celestial Masters (from 142 CE, Zhang Daoling; continued today in the Zhengyi school) and Quanzhen ("Complete Perfection", 12th century) schools; temples, priests, monastics, ritual, the immortals.
  • Spelling: older Wade-Giles "Tao, Taoism"; pinyin "Dao, Daoism". Same words.
Mission 3

One tradition or two?

  • Older books split "philosophical" Taoism (daojia: the texts, the sage) from "religious" Taoism (daojiao: ritual, gods, immortality).
  • Many scholars now say the split is too sharp: religious Taoists treat Laozi and Zhuangzi as scripture, and Laozi is revered as a deified figure.
  • Taoism also mixes with Confucian and Buddhist practice in Chinese life; many people take part without calling themselves "Taoist".
  • So "what Taoists believe" varies widely: answer with care and name the strand.
Mission 3Read the source

A Way that cannot be named

> "The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name." (Tao Te Ching 1, Legge)

> "(Conceived of as) having no name, it is the Originator of heaven and earth; (conceived of as) having a name, it is the Mother of all things." (Tao Te Ching 1, Legge)

Q1 What does the first line say about words and the Tao?

Show answer

Any Tao we can describe or walk as a fixed path is not the eternal Tao: words fall short.

Q2 How is the Tao related to the world?

Show answer

It is the nameless origin of heaven and earth and the "Mother" of all things: a source, not a creator god who stands outside.

Mission 3

The condition: losing the Way

  • Humans start in harmony with the Tao, like a newborn child or an uncarved block (pu): simple and natural.
  • Harmony is lost through desire, striving, cleverness and artificial values (rules, status, ambition).

> "When the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed, benevolence and righteousness came into vogue. (Then) appeared wisdom and shrewdness, and there ensued great hypocrisy." (Tao Te Ching 18, Legge)

  • This is not sin against a god: it is being out of tune with how things naturally flow.
  • In religious Taoism, the loss also shows in the body: qi (vital energy) leaks away through excess, bringing illness and early death.
Mission 3

Yin and yang

  • All things arise through two complementary forces: yin (dark, receptive, soft, cool) and yang (bright, active, firm, warm).
  • Neither is good or evil: each needs the other and turns into the other, as night into day.
  • Disorder, in a body or a society, is imbalance: too much of one, too little of the other.

> "The Tao produced One; One produced Two; Two produced Three; Three produced All things." (Tao Te Ching 42, Legge)

  • Many commentators read "Two" here as yin and yang, harmonised by qi.

Mission 3

Where are we going? Harmony and return

  • The goal: to live in harmony with the Tao: natural, simple, spontaneous (ziran, "so of itself").

> "When things (in the vegetable world) have displayed their luxuriant growth, we see each of them return to its root." (Tao Te Ching 16, Legge)

  • The sage returns to the root: stillness, simplicity, freedom from grasping.
  • Zhuangzi (paraphrase): when his wife died he first grieved, then sang, seeing her death as part of the endless transformation of things, like the seasons. Life and death both belong to the Tao.
  • Zhuangzi's butterfly dream (paraphrase): he dreamt he was a butterfly, then woke unsure whether he was Zhuangzi who had dreamt, or a butterfly now dreaming. Fixed boundaries of self loosen.
Mission 3

Longevity and immortality

  • Many Taoist schools add a further goal: longevity and becoming an immortal (xian).
  • Tao Te Ching 33 (Legge): "he who dies and yet does not perish, has longevity."
  • Outer alchemy (waidan): elixirs (largely abandoned, partly because some were poisonous).
  • Inner alchemy (neidan): refining the body's jing (essence), qi (energy) and shen (spirit) through breathing, meditation and movement.
  • Immortality is understood in different ways: a long, healthy physical life; a transformed spiritual body; or union with the Tao itself.
  • Contrast: Zhuangzi accepts death calmly; the immortality schools seek to transcend it. Both aim at being one with the Way.

Mission 3

How do we get there? Wu wei

  • Wu wei: "non-action" or "effortless action": acting without forcing, in step with the Tao.
  • Not laziness: the sage does what the situation needs, with no grasping or show.

> "The Tao in its regular course does nothing (for the sake of doing it), and so there is nothing which it does not do." (Tao Te Ching 37, Legge)

  • The image of water (Tao Te Ching 8): it benefits all things and takes the low place without striving, yet wears away rock.
  • Zhuangzi's Cook Ding (paraphrase): a cook carves an ox by following its natural joints, so his knife stays sharp for years. Skill becomes effortless when it follows the grain.
Mission 3

Living the Way

  • Three Treasures (Tao Te Ching 67, Legge): "gentleness", "economy" and "shrinking from taking precedence of others".
  • Balance of yin and yang in diet, medicine, movement (taiji, qigong) and daily life.
  • Meditation: "sitting in forgetfulness" (zuowang), stillness, guarding the One.
  • Ritual: priests (daoshi) lead offerings and rites of renewal (jiao) for communities; temples honour deities such as the Three Pure Ones and the deified Laozi.
  • Monastic life in Quanzhen Taoism: celibacy, simple diet, discipline.
  • Ethics: simplicity, humility, non-violence, contentment.
Mission 3Worked answer

Opposites and their reconciliation

Sample question (IB subject brief): "Explain Taoist ideas about opposites and their reconciliation." A strong answer links idea → source → meaning:

  • Idea: opposites define each other. Source: Tao Te Ching 2 (Legge): "existence and non-existence give birth the one to (the idea of) the other". Meaning: good and bad, high and low are relative, not fixed.
  • Idea: yin and yang are complementary forces in everything. Source: Tao Te Ching 42 (Legge), the Tao producing One, Two, Three and all things. Meaning: harmony is balance, not victory of one side.
  • Idea: reconciliation is practical. The soft overcomes the hard (water, Tao Te Ching 78); the sage acts by wu wei.
  • Develop: in religious Taoism, health and long life come from balancing yin and yang in the body.

In Taoism the condition is losing the natural Way, the goal is harmony and return (for many, long life and immortality), and the path is wu wei, balance and cultivation.

Mission 4

Jainism in brief

  • A Jain is a follower of the Jinas ("conquerors"): souls who conquered their own passions.
  • The Jinas are also called Tirthankaras, "ford-makers": they make a crossing over the river of rebirth.
  • 24 Tirthankaras in this age of the cosmic cycle: the first is Rishabha; the 23rd Parshva; the 24th Mahavira (6th–5th century BCE; dates debated).
  • Jains do not call Mahavira the founder: they see the teaching as eternal, taught again by each Tirthankara.
  • Jains believe in no creator god: for them the universe is uncreated and eternal; it runs by its own laws, including karma.
  • Two main traditions: Svetambara ("white-clad") and Digambara ("sky-clad").
Mission 4

The condition: two kinds of reality

  • Jains teach that reality is jiva (living, conscious soul) and ajiva (everything without consciousness: matter, space, time, and the media of motion and rest).
  • Every jiva is by nature perfect: infinite knowledge, perception, bliss and energy.
  • Jiva is everywhere: in humans, animals, plants, insects, even earth, water, fire and air bodies.
  • Souls are many and individual: each one stays itself, also after liberation.
  • The problem: a soul's perfect nature is covered by karma, as a cloud covers the sun.
Mission 4

Karma is matter

  • For Jains, karma is not only "action": it is fine, invisible matter (karmic particles) filling the universe.
  • Influx (asrava): activity of body, speech and mind draws karma towards the soul.
  • Bondage (bandha): the passions (kashayas: anger, pride, deceit, greed) make it stick, like dust on an oiled cloth.
  • Bound karma clouds knowledge, causes suffering and fixes the next birth.
  • Result: samsara, rebirth in four destinies: heavenly beings, humans, animals and plants, hell-beings.
  • Only a human birth gives the chance of liberation. Even gods must be reborn as humans to be freed.

Mission 4

Where are we going? Kevala and moksha

  • Jains teach that liberation comes in stages:
  • Stopping (samvara): no new karma flows in.
  • Shedding (nirjara): old karma is worn away, above all by austerity (tapas) and meditation.
  • When the four karmas that obscure the soul are destroyed, the soul gains kevala jnana: perfect, all-embracing knowledge. Such a person is a kevalin.
  • At death the last karmas fall away: this is moksha (liberation).
  • Jains believe the freed soul, a siddha, rises to the top of the universe and dwells there for ever in knowledge and bliss. It never returns.
  • Not union with God, not absorption into one reality: each siddha remains an individual, perfect soul.
  • A Tirthankara is a kevalin who also teaches the path and founds the fourfold community (monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen).
Mission 4Read the source

The path in one sentence

The Tattvartha Sutra of Umasvati (c. 2nd–5th century CE) is accepted by both Svetambara and Digambara Jains. Its opening verse says (paraphrase):

> Right faith, right knowledge and right conduct together are the path to liberation. (Tattvartha Sutra 1.1, paraphrase)

  • These are the Three Jewels (ratnatraya).
  • Right faith (samyak darshana): trust in the Jain account of reality, the Jinas and their teaching.
  • Right knowledge (samyak jnana): accurate understanding of jiva, ajiva and karma.
  • Right conduct (samyak charitra): living by the vows.

Q1 Why does the sutra say "together"?

Show answer

No single jewel is enough: faith without conduct, or knowledge without faith, does not free the soul.

Q2 Which jewel corrects the passions that make karma stick?

Show answer

Right conduct, though it depends on right faith and knowledge.

Mission 4

How do we get there? The five vows

  • Ahimsa: non-violence, not harming any living being.
  • Satya: truthfulness.
  • Asteya: not stealing, not taking what is not given.
  • Brahmacharya: chastity (celibacy for monks and nuns; faithfulness in marriage for lay Jains).
  • Aparigraha: non-possessiveness, non-attachment to things and people.
  • Monks and nuns take them as mahavratas (great vows), in full.
  • Lay Jains take them as anuvratas (lesser vows), in a form that fits family and work.
Mission 4

Ahimsa in daily life

  • Ahimsa is the heart of the vows: harm attracts the heaviest karma, and every jiva wants to live.
  • Diet: strictly vegetarian; many Jains avoid root vegetables (pulling them kills many small lives) and do not eat after sunset.
  • Many monks and nuns carry a small brush to sweep insects from their path; many Svetambara monastics wear a mouth-cloth (muhpatti).
  • Work: lay Jains have often chosen trades that cause less harm, such as commerce.
  • Ahimsa covers thought and speech too: anger and harsh words also bind karma.
  • Anekantavada ("many-sidedness"): reality has many aspects, and any one view is partial. Jains apply ahimsa to argument: listen before you condemn.

Mission 4

Worship without a creator

  • Many Jains offer puja before images of the Tirthankaras in temples.
  • For Jains, the Tirthankaras do not answer prayers or give grace: they are models. Worship purifies the worshipper's own mind. (Many Jains also honour guardian deities, yakshas and yakshis, for worldly help; these are not liberated souls.)
  • The Namokar Mantra honours the five supreme beings: Arhats, Siddhas, spiritual leaders, teachers and all monks; it names no individual.
  • Samayika: daily meditation for equanimity.
  • Paryushana: the most important festival (8 days for Svetambaras; Digambaras keep a 10-day Das Lakshana Parva); fasting, study, and asking forgiveness of all beings ("Micchami Dukkadam").
  • Some Svetambara groups (Sthanakvasi, Terapanthi) reject image worship altogether.

Mission 5

The Bahá'í Faith in brief

  • The Báb ("the Gate"; 1819–1850), in Shiraz, Persia, declared in 1844 that he was a messenger of God and that a greater one would follow. He was executed in Tabriz in 1850.
  • Bahá'u'lláh ("the Glory of God"; 1817–1892) declared in 1863, in the Garden of Ridván in Baghdad, that he was the one the Báb foretold. He spent the rest of his life in exile, ending in Akka (in today's Israel).
  • His writings include the Kitáb-i-Aqdas (the Most Holy Book, his book of laws), the Kitáb-i-Íqán (Book of Certitude) and the Hidden Words.
  • After him: 'Abdu'l-Bahá (1844–1921), his son and interpreter; Shoghi Effendi (1897–1957), the Guardian; since 1963 the elected Universal House of Justice, in Haifa.
  • Bahá'ís believe in one God, unknowable in essence, known through his messengers.
Mission 5

The condition: a soul with two natures

  • Each human has a rational soul, which Bahá'ís believe begins at conception and lives on after death.
  • Humans have a spiritual nature (able to know and love God) and a material or animal nature.
  • No original sin: evil is not a power in its own right; it is the absence of good, as darkness is the absence of light.
  • The problem is that the spiritual nature is undeveloped: people follow selfish, material desires and prejudice.
  • Bahá'u'lláh compares a human being to "a mine rich in gems of inestimable value" (Gleanings CXXII): education brings the gems out.
  • The purpose of life: to know and worship God, and to develop spiritual qualities (love, justice, truthfulness).
Mission 5

Progressive revelation

  • Bahá'ís believe humans cannot reach God's essence, so God sends Manifestations of God, who reflect his qualities as a mirror reflects the sun.
  • Bahá'ís name Abraham, Moses, Krishna, Zoroaster, the Buddha, Jesus, Muhammad, the Báb and Bahá'u'lláh.
  • Progressive revelation: each Manifestation brings the same eternal spiritual truths, plus social laws fitted to the needs of that age.
  • For Bahá'ís, religion is one, unfolding in stages, like one school with successive classes.
  • Bahá'u'lláh is not the last: Bahá'ís believe another Manifestation will come, but not for at least a thousand years.

Mission 5

Where are we going? Nearness to God

  • For Bahá'ís, the goal of the soul is to grow nearer to God, in this life and after death.
  • 'Abdu'l-Bahá compared this life to the womb: here we develop the "limbs" (spiritual qualities) we will need in the next world.
  • Bahá'ís believe that after death the soul continues to progress through spiritual worlds.
  • Heaven and hell are not places: they are states of nearness to or remoteness from God.
  • Progress after death is possible through God's grace and the prayers and good deeds of others.
Mission 5

Where are we going? One humanity

  • The goal is also collective: the oneness of humanity.
  • Bahá'u'lláh: "The earth is but one country, and mankind its citizens" (Gleanings CXVII).
  • Bahá'ís see history as moving through stages, as a person grows from childhood to maturity. Humanity is now coming of age.
  • The Lesser Peace: nations agree to end war. The Most Great Peace: a spiritually united world civilisation.
  • So salvation is both personal (the soul's progress) and social (a unified world).

Mission 5

How do we get there? Recognition and laws

  • First duty: recognise the Manifestation for this age; second: obey his laws. Bahá'ís see the two as inseparable.
  • Obligatory prayer: each day, one of three prayers (short, medium or long), from age 15.
  • Reciting sacred verses morning and evening; meditation and reflection.
  • The Fast: 19 days (in March, before Naw-Rúz, the New Year), no food or drink from sunrise to sunset, ages 15 to 70, with exemptions (illness, pregnancy, travel, hard labour).
  • Other laws: no alcohol or drugs (unless medically prescribed), no backbiting, chastity and marriage with parents' consent, giving to the Fund.
  • Work done in a spirit of service counts as worship.
Mission 5Diversity

Principles of unity

  • Bahá'í teaching stresses three onenesses: of God, of religion, of humanity.
  • Social principles: equality of women and men; elimination of prejudice (race, class, nation, religion); harmony of science and religion; independent investigation of truth; universal education; a universal auxiliary language; economic justice.
  • Bahá'ís see these as part of the path itself: building unity draws people nearer to God.
  • The Bahá'í community has stayed largely one body: unity under the Covenant (the line of authority from Bahá'u'lláh) is itself a teaching. Very small groups that broke away are not recognised.
Mission 5

A faith without clergy

  • No priests or clergy. Each local community elects a Local Spiritual Assembly of nine members; nations elect National Spiritual Assemblies; these elect the Universal House of Justice.
  • Elections have no candidates or campaigning: members vote in prayerful secrecy for those they think best suited.
  • Decisions are made by consultation: frank, loving discussion aiming at unity.
  • Community life: the Nineteen Day Feast (worship, consultation, fellowship), the Badí' calendar (19 months of 19 days), study circles, children's classes, service projects.
  • Bahá'ís in Iran have faced severe persecution since the faith began.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Condition: made in God's image, but sin (the Fall) breaks relationship with God; original sin (Augustine) and ancestral sin (Orthodox) explain it differently.
  • Goal: salvation and eternal life; resurrection of the body; the Kingdom of God, now and to come; heaven (and purgatory for Catholics); theosis for Orthodox Christians.
  • Way: grace through faith in Christ; sacraments (seven, two or none); love of God and neighbour; Luther and the Catholic Church differ on faith and works.

Mission 2

  • Condition: fitrah and khalifah; no original sin (Adam forgot, repented, was forgiven); ghaflah, the nafs and Shaytan pull people away; life is a test.
  • Goal: Akhirah: barzakh, resurrection, the Day of Judgement, Jannah or Jahannam, all under God's mercy.
  • Way: submission (islam, iman, ihsan); the Five Pillars (Sunni) or Usul ad-Din and ten obligatory acts (Shi'a); shari'a as the path for all of life.

Mission 3

  • Condition: humans fall out of harmony with the Tao through desire, striving and artificial values; imbalance of yin and yang, and loss of qi.
  • Goal: harmony with and return to the Tao; for many religious Taoists, longevity and becoming a xian (immortal); Zhuangzi accepts death as transformation.
  • Way: wu wei, the Three Treasures, balance of yin and yang, meditation, inner alchemy and ritual; texts: Tao Te Ching and Zhuangzi.

Mission 4

  • Condition (Jain teaching): countless individual jivas, perfect by nature, are bound by karmic matter that activity attracts and the passions make stick; this causes samsara.
  • Goal (Jain teaching): stopping and shedding karma leads to kevala jnana and, at death, moksha: the siddha rests for ever at the top of the universe.
  • Way: the Three Jewels (right faith, knowledge, conduct) and five vows, kept fully by monastics and in lesser form by lay Jains; the Tirthankaras are models, not saviours.

Mission 5

  • Condition (Bahá'í teaching): a rational soul with spiritual and material natures; no original sin, but undeveloped qualities that need education and the guidance of God's Manifestations, sent by progressive revelation.
  • Goal (Bahá'í teaching): the soul's endless progress towards God (heaven and hell as nearness and distance) and the oneness of humanity, leading to world peace.
  • Way: recognise Bahá'u'lláh and keep his laws (obligatory prayer, the Fast), serve others, and live the principles of unity through consultation and elected assemblies.
