Language Change

English is always changing — in its words, meanings, grammar and spelling. This lesson covers how and why English changes and the debate over whether change is decay or evolution.

You will meet the processes and levels of change, standardisation, and theories including Aitchison's metaphors. Press Start.

Change · levels

What changes

Language change happens at every level:

  • Lexical — new words in, old words out.
  • Semantic — words shift meaning.
  • Grammatical — structures and inflections change.
  • Orthographic — spelling and punctuation standardise.
  • Phonological — pronunciation shifts.

Context: printing, empire, science and technology have all driven change.

Change · lexis

How the vocabulary grows

New words enter through several processes:

  • Borrowing (ballet, bungalow), coinage (selfie).
  • Compounding (laptop), blending (brunch), affixation (un- + follow).
  • Conversion (to text, to google), clipping (advert), acronyms (radar).

Loss: words also become archaic and obsolete over time.

Change · semantics

How meanings change

Words keep their form but shift meaning:

  • Broadening — meaning widens.
  • Narrowing — meaning contracts (meat once meant any food).
  • Amelioration — becomes more positive (nice once meant foolish).
  • Pejoration — becomes more negative (silly once meant blessed).

Keep separate: broadening/narrowing (scope) vs amelioration/pejoration (value).

✍️ Grammar / spelling

Change · standardisation

Standardisation and Johnson 1755

Early spelling was highly variable. Standardisation was driven by:

  • Caxton's printing press (from 1476) spreading fixed forms.
  • Samuel Johnson's Dictionary (1755) — a landmark codification of English.
  • Prescriptive grammars and mass schooling.

Haugen described standardisation as selection, codification, elaboration and implementation.

Remember: Johnson's Dictionary, 1755 — recording usage, not inventing it.

Change · theory

Theories and attitudes

Explain both why change happens and how people react:

  • Substratum — contact with other languages.
  • Functional — language changes to meet needs.
  • Random fluctuation (Postal) — change without a single cause.
  • Wave and S-curve models — how change spreads.

Jean Aitchison rejected decline narratives, naming three prescriptivist metaphors: the damp spoon (laziness), the crumbling castle (a mythical perfect past) and the infectious disease (usage is caught). The wider debate is prescriptivism vs descriptivism.

Attribute: the three metaphors describe attitudes Aitchison rejects.
