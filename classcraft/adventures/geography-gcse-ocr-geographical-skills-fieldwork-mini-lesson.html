Geographical Skills & Fieldwork

This mini-lesson walks you through the geographical skills OCR B expects: reading OS maps (grid references, scale, distance, direction, contours), choosing the right graph, working out statistics, running a fieldwork enquiry, and using GIS.

OS map skills

Grid references — eastings then northings

Ordnance Survey maps are covered by a numbered grid. The golden rule is along the corridor, then up the stairs — read the eastings (the lines going up, numbered left→right) first, then the northings (lines going across, numbered bottom→top).

4-figure reference = a whole 1 km square (2 digits east, 2 north). 6-figure = a point within that square (imagine each square split into tenths, 0–9, giving 3 digits east, 3 north).

OS map skills

Scale, distance and direction

  • Scale: OS maps are drawn to scale. On a 1:25 000 map, 4 cm = 1 km (each grid square is 1 km across); on a 1:50 000 map, 2 cm = 1 km. Scale tells you real distance from map distance.
  • Distance: measure a straight line with a ruler, or a winding route (road/river) with string or the edge of paper, then convert using the scale.
  • Direction: use the 8-point compass (N, NE, E, SE, S, SW, W, NW). For more precision use a bearing — the clockwise angle from north, measured in degrees (000°–360°).

Bearings: always measured clockwise from north. Due east is 090°, due south is 180°, due west is 270°.

Turning map distance into real distance

On a 1:50 000 OS map, a footpath measures 6 cm long on the map.

How long is the footpath in real life, in kilometres?

How to work it out

1:50 000 means 1 cm on the map = 50 000 cm on the ground.

6 cm × 50 000 = 300 000 cm. Then 300 000 cm ÷ 100 000 = km (100 000 cm in 1 km).

OS map skills

Relief and contour lines

Contour lines are lines on a map joining points of equal height above sea level. They show the shape of the land — its relief. Every so often a contour is labelled with its height in metres.

Graphical skills

Choosing the right graph

Part of the skill is picking the right way to present data:

  • Bar chart — compares separate categories (e.g. traffic count by street).
  • Line graph — shows change over time (e.g. rainfall through the year).
  • Pie chart — shows proportions of a whole (e.g. how land use is split).
  • Choropleth map — uses shading (darker = higher value) to show how something varies across an area (e.g. population density).
  • Proportional symbols — circles/bars whose size shows the value (e.g. bigger circle = bigger city).

Choropleth = colour/shade categories on a map; proportional symbols = symbols whose size scales with the value. Don't confuse them.

Numerical & statistical skills

Averages and spread

Four measures describe a set of data. For the pebble sizes 2, 4, 7, 8, 9 (cm):

  • Mean — add them up, divide by how many: (2+4+7+8+9) ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5 = 6.
  • Median — the middle value when in order: 2, 4, 7, 8, 9 → 7.
  • Mode — the value that appears most often (none repeats here, so there is no mode).
  • Range — highest − lowest: 9 − 2 = 7 (a measure of spread).

Median vs mean: the median is just the middle number in an ordered list — it isn't affected by one very large "anomaly", so it can be more reliable than the mean when the data has outliers.

Fieldwork enquiry

The fieldwork enquiry process

A good fieldwork investigation follows a clear route, from question to evaluation:

Fieldwork enquiry

Data: primary, secondary and sampling

  • Primary data — data you collect yourself in the field (e.g. your own traffic count or river measurements).
  • Secondary data — data collected by someone else (e.g. census figures, Environment Agency records, an OS map).
  • Quantitative — numbers (measurements, counts). Qualitative — descriptions, opinions, photos (e.g. an environmental quality survey).
  • Sampling — you can't measure everything, so you sample: random (chosen by chance), systematic (at regular intervals, e.g. every 10 m) or stratified (in proportion to sub-groups).

Primary vs secondary: if you gathered it in the field it's primary; if you looked it up from an existing source it's secondary.

How to work it out

Percentage = (part ÷ whole) × 100 = (45 ÷ 180) × 100.

GIS

Geographical Information Systems (GIS)

A GIS is computer software that stores, maps and analyses data by location. It builds a map from layers of information (e.g. roads, rivers, population, flood risk) that can be switched on and off and combined.

  • Uses: mapping fieldwork results, spotting patterns, planning (e.g. where to build), and modelling risks such as flooding.
  • Everyday examples: Google Maps, satnavs, and online OS mapping are all built on GIS.

Key idea: the power of GIS is combining layers of geographical data so you can see how different factors overlap at the same location.

Explain it

Your turn — evaluating fieldwork

✎In the evaluation stage of an enquiry, you assess how reliable your investigation was. Give two things you might comment on, and why they matter.
Model answer (any two developed points)
  • Sample size / method — a small sample or a biased sampling method may not be representative, so the data (and conclusion) could be less reliable.
  • Accuracy of measurement — equipment error or human mistakes (e.g. mis-reading a ruler) reduce accuracy; repeating readings improves reliability.
  • Timing / conditions — data collected on one day or in unusual weather may not be typical, so results could differ at another time.
  • Anomalies — you should identify any anomalous results and say how they were treated.
  • Each point should say how it affects reliability and what you would change next time.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Grid references: eastings then northings ("corridor then stairs"); 4-figure = a 1 km square, 6-figure = a point within it.

Scale & distance: use the scale (1:25 000 → 4 cm/km; 1:50 000 → 2 cm/km) to convert map distance to real distance.

Direction: 8-point compass or a bearing measured clockwise from north (E=090°, S=180°, W=270°).

Relief: contours join equal heights — close = steep, far apart = gentle.

Graphs: bar (compare), line (over time), pie (proportions), choropleth (shading), proportional symbols (size).

Statistics: mean (add ÷ count), median (middle), mode (most common), range (max − min).

Enquiry: question → collect → present → analyse → conclude → evaluate.

Data: primary (you collect) vs secondary (existing); quantitative vs qualitative; random/systematic/stratified sampling.

GIS: software that maps and analyses data by location using layers.
