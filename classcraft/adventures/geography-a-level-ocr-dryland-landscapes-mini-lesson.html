Mission 1

Dryness, not heat, makes a desert

  • The USGS summarises Meigs's scheme: arid land gets under 250 mm of rain a year; semi-arid land 250–500 mm; extremely arid land has at least 12 consecutive months without rain.
  • "Arid and extremely arid land are deserts", while semi-arid grasslands are usually called steppes.
  • About one-third of Earth's land is desert. Deserts "may be hot, they may be cold … But deserts are always dry".
Mission 1

The aridity index

  • Aridity index (AI) = mean annual precipitation ÷ potential evapotranspiration (P ÷ PET). PET is the water that would evaporate and transpire if water were always available.
  • UNEP classes, as used by the IPCC: hyper-arid below 0.05; arid 0.05–0.20; semi-arid 0.20–0.50; dry sub-humid 0.50–0.65; humid above 0.65.
  • Drylands (AI 0.65 or below) cover about 46.2% of the land and are home to 3 billion people.
  • An index beats a rainfall total: 300 mm is dry in a hot place, where PET is high, but less dry where PET is low.
Quick check
?A place receives 300 mm of precipitation a year, and its potential evapotranspiration is 1,200 mm. Which UNEP class is it in?
Mission 1

Where the hot deserts are

  • Subtropical high pressure: around 30° N and S, air that rose at the Equator sinks; it is dry "because its moisture has already been released near the Equator". Most non-polar deserts lie in the trade-wind belts, including the Sahara.
  • Cold currents: coastal deserts such as the Atacama and Namib lie "on the western edges of continents near the Tropics" and are "affected by cold ocean currents that parallel the coast". Air chilled from below is stable: fog, not rain.
  • Continentality: midlatitude deserts lie "in interior drainage basins far from oceans".
  • Rain shadow: four mountain ranges lie between Death Valley and the ocean, "each one adding to an increasingly drier rainshadow effect".
Mission 1

Arid cores and semi-arid margins

  • Deserts grade outward into semi-arid margins: steppe and savanna grassland with 250–500 mm of rain.
  • Margins have a short rainy season and very variable totals from year to year.
  • Margins hold far more people than desert cores, so they are where land degradation matters most.
  • OCR's three dryland types: polar drylands, mid- and low-latitude deserts, semi-arid environments.
Mission 1

Hot desert climate: Coober Pedy

  • Coober Pedy Airport, South Australia (1994–2026): mean annual rainfall 144.1 mm; January mean maximum 36.9 °C.
  • Rain is unreliable: the wettest year had 308.2 mm (2016), the driest 24.4 mm (2019).
  • Clear skies give a large diurnal range: strong daytime heating, rapid night-time cooling.
  • Exam move: quote the mean, the range and an extreme, then say what the variability means for plants and people.
Mission 1

Drylands as systems

  • Inputs: solar energy (intense), precipitation (low, irregular), wind energy, sediment from weathering and rivers.
  • Processes: weathering, aeolian and fluvial erosion, transport and deposition; evaporation.
  • Outputs: long-wave radiation, evaporated water, dust carried out of the system.
  • Influences: precipitation totals and patterns; geology (lithology and structure); latitude and altitude; relief and aspect (shaded slopes stay cooler and moister); sediment availability (sand seas need a supply of sand).
Mission 1

Polar drylands

  • Polar deserts have under 250 mm of precipitation a year and a warmest-month mean below 10 °C.
  • Over Antarctica's elevated plateau, annual snowfall is equivalent to less than 50 mm of water.
  • The cause differs from hot deserts: very cold air holds little water vapour, so little snow can fall. The result is the same: very low precipitation.
Quick check
?Why are most of the world's large hot deserts found around 30° N and S?
Mission check

?Coober Pedy, in the Australian outback, gets about 144 mm of rain a year. Utqiaġvik, on Alaska's frozen Arctic coast, gets about 137 mm of precipitation. Why do both count as deserts?

Deserts are defined by dryness. Antarctica's ice has built up over long periods despite low precipitation, about 150 mm of water a year on average and under 50 mm on the plateau: it is a polar desert, like Utqiaġvik, while Coober Pedy is a hot desert.

Mission 2

Energy and materials

  • Energy in: strong sunshine (insolation), wind, and rare but intense runoff.
  • With little soil or plant cover, rock lies exposed. On 15 July 1972 the ground at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, reached about 94 °C (201 °F); the air, about 53 °C (128 °F).
  • Materials: weathered rock, sand and dust, moved by gravity, water and wind.
  • Outputs: dust carried out of the desert; sediment carried by floods to basins.
Mission 2

What desert surfaces are made of

  • Sand covers only about 20% of Earth's deserts.
  • Almost half are stony deflation zones, mostly desert pavement.
  • The rest: bare rock, desert soils, alluvial fans, playas and lakes.
  • Death Valley: less than 1% of the desert is covered with dunes.
Mission 2

Weathering

  • Thermal: rock surfaces heat and cool each day. Many desert boulders show fine cracks; one study found such cracks lined up north–south and linked them to stresses from the Sun's daily heating.
  • Salt: as water evaporates, salt crystals grow in pores and force grains apart.
  • Freeze–thaw in cold-winter deserts: at Arches National Park, Utah, "water trapped in cracks expands when it freezes, then contracts, prying the rock apart".
  • Chemical: rain "can slowly dissolve the calcite bonding the sand together" in Arches' sandstone.
Quick check
?Water seeps from the foot of a sandstone cliff and evaporates, leaving white crusts. The rock there is crumbling grain by grain. Which process is most likely?
Mission 2

Mass movement

  • Weathered blocks fall from cliffs (rockfall) and pile up as slopes of debris below.
  • With little vegetation or soil to hold it, debris also slides and creeps downslope, ready for floods to carry away.
  • So cliffs retreat, and gravity delivers material to the channels.
Mission 2

Water: rare but powerful

  • "Although water is much more powerful than wind, eolian processes are important in arid environments" (USGS).
  • Wadis (arroyos, washes) are dry stream beds that "fill temporarily during rain storms. Flash floods are common following thunderstorms" (NPS).
  • Floods carry heavy loads of sediment, often "for a day or two".
  • Bare, crusted ground lets rain run off fast. One flash flood can scour a wadi floor in hours; repeated floods carve its steep sides and flat floor.
Mission 2

Canyons: the Grand Canyon

  • A canyon is an erosional valley with very steep, often near-vertical sides.
  • The Colorado River began carving the Grand Canyon 5–6 million years ago, after uplift of the Colorado Plateau.
  • It descends about 610 m over 446 km through the canyon, a steep slope that gives it speed and power in floods.
  • In the arid climate, little soil or plant cover leaves rock exposed and weathered, so the river cuts down easily; tributaries widen the canyon.
Mission 2

Alluvial fans and bajadas

  • Where a flood leaves a mountain canyon, it spreads out, slows and drops its load: an alluvial fan, coarse near the apex, finer towards the edge.
  • Bajadas are "aprons of rocky debris that form when alluvial fans coalesce" along a mountain front (NPS).
  • A single flash flood can add a new lobe of sediment.
Quick check
?A line of mountain canyons opens onto a valley. Below each is a cone of gravel, and the cones have merged into one continuous slope. What is that slope?
Mission 2

Wind: erosion and transport

  • Deflation: wind removes loose, fine particles. Abrasion: windborne grains sand-blast surfaces.
  • Suspension: typical winds hold particles under 0.2 mm aloft (USGS).
  • Saltation: sand moves in short hops, rarely more than 1 cm high, at one-half to one-third of the wind's speed.
  • Surface creep: larger grains are pushed along; up to 25% of grain movement.
Mission 2

Landforms of wind and weathering

  • Ventifacts: rocks "cut, and sometimes polished, by the abrasive action of wind".
  • Desert pavement: a surface of rock fragments left after wind and water remove the fine particles. Another view: thermal cracking of stones and dust building up beneath them create most pavements over millennia.
  • Pedestal rocks: a cap on a narrow shaft. The textbook view is wind abrasion near the ground; Twidale and Campbell found most are two-stage forms, weathered by soil moisture below ground and then exposed.
  • Exam move: where explanations compete, weigh the evidence for each.
Mission 2

Dunes

  • Sand climbs the gentle windward slope and avalanches down the steep slipface, so the dune moves downwind.
  • Barchans: crescent dunes, formed under winds from one direction; some moved more than 100 m a year in China's Ningxia Province (1954–59).
  • Linear dunes: long ridges, often formed by winds from two directions; may be over 160 km long.
  • Star dunes: formed by winds from many directions; up to 500 m tall in China's Badain Jaran Desert.
Quick check
?A dune field is made of long, straight, parallel ridges, some over 100 km long. What wind regime does it suggest?
Mission check

?A desert plain is covered by a tight mosaic of dark, varnished stones, with almost no loose sand or silt on the surface. How did this surface most likely form?

Almost half of desert surfaces are stony deflation zones. Weathering by heat, salt, frost and water breaks rock down; wind deflates and abrades; rare floods, "much more powerful than wind", cut wadis and canyons and build fans and bajadas.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Death Valley gets almost no rain. So what built its landscape?

Make a guess: Death Valley gets almost no rain. So what built its landscape?

Mission 3

A mid-latitude desert: Death Valley

  • Mid-latitude deserts lie between 30° and 50° N and S, poleward of the subtropical highs, in interior drainage basins far from oceans, with a wide annual temperature range.
  • Death Valley, eastern California, in the Mojave Desert; its weather station is at 36.5° N.
  • Its drainage never reaches the sea: floods end in the valley floor.
  • Case study scale: one basin, its mountains and their landforms.
Mission 3

Physical factor 1: a rain-shadow climate

  • Pacific storms rise over the mountains and drop rain or snow on their western sides.
  • Four mountain ranges lie between Death Valley and the ocean, "each one adding to an increasingly drier rainshadow effect".
  • Rain averages about 50 mm (two inches) a year.
  • Average high 19 °C in January, 47 °C in July: a wide annual range.
  • Late-summer thunderstorms "can cause flash floods".
Mission 3

Physical factors 2 and 3: tectonics and relief

  • About 3 million years ago the crust began pulling apart; blocks slid along faults, forming alternate valleys and ranges.
  • The valley floor subsides along the fault at the foot of the Black Mountains; "Subsidence continues today".
  • Badwater: 86 m (282 ft) below sea level, under 3,368 m (11,049 ft) Telescope Peak: about 3,450 m of relief (our calculation).
  • Steep relief gives floods energy; the sinking floor keeps a sediment trap.
Quick check
?Beneath Badwater lie more than 11,000 feet of sediment and salts. Which pair of factors best explains why so much has collected there?
Mission 3

Landforms linked down the slope

  • Canyons and washes cut the mountain fronts; they fill only in storms.
  • Where a canyon stream reaches the flat valley it slows and drops sediment: an alluvial fan.
  • Neighbouring fans merge into a bajada, an apron that partly buries the range front.
  • Fine silt and dissolved salts carry on to the lowest point: the playa and salt pan.
  • One flood links them all: erosion above, deposition below.
Mission 3

Salt pan and dunes

  • Badwater salt flats: about 520 km² (200 square miles).
  • Salt source: a drainage system of about 23,300 km² (9,000 square miles); floods dissolve minerals and leave them as water evaporates.
  • Dunes cover less than 1% of the park. Their sand comes from eroded canyons and washes; mountains trap it.
  • Mesquite Flat dunes sit on the cracked clay of an ancient lakebed: today's wind landform rests on an old lake landform.
Quick check
?A run of wet years sends far more water and sediment out of the canyons. Which knock-on change is most likely?
Mission 3

Change in hours: flash floods

  • 5 August 2022: 43 mm (1.70 inches) fell in a few hours, three-quarters of the 2.20-inch annual average; flash floods closed roads for weeks.
  • 20 August 2023: remnants of Hurricane Hilary brought 2.2 inches; "Every road in the park was damaged by flash floods".
  • 2015: a flood at Scotty's Castle left mud and debris up to 10 feet deep.
  • In hours, floods cut washes, move boulders and add a new layer to fans.
Mission 3

Change in minutes to years

  • Racetrack Playa: thin ice (3–6 mm) floating on a shallow pool, pushed by light winds, moved rocks at 2–5 m per minute; some moved 224 m in one winter (2013–14).
  • Lake Manly, 2024: after 4.9 inches of rain in six months, a lake 6 miles long and 1 foot deep formed on Badwater Basin.
  • Footprints in its salty mud "will remain visible for many years".
  • The salt crust "change[s] constantly": crystals grow and floods redissolve salt.
Mission 3

Change over millennia

  • In the last Ice Age the valley held part of a system of large lakes; they evaporated about 10,000 years ago, leaving vast salt deposits.
  • A smaller lake covered the floor about 3,000 years ago.
  • Mojave pediments: formed "prior to the establishment of full desert conditions"; today there is "no expansion" of them. They are inherited (relict).
  • Over about 3 million years, faulting has lowered the basin and raised the ranges, and it continues.
Quick check
?Which Death Valley landform mainly records conditions that no longer exist there?
Mission 3

Seconds to millennia: one system

  • Seconds to hours: flash floods cut and fill; dust storms move fine sediment.
  • Minutes to months: ice-pushed rocks; temporary lakes; salt crusts regrow.
  • Thousands of years: Ice Age lakes leave salt pans and lakebed clays; pediments inherited from a semi-arid past.
  • Millions of years: faulting builds the relief that powers every flood.
  • Exam move (OCR "Examine"): link each landform to the factor that controls it, then say over which timescale it changes.
Mission check

?A fault-bounded basin in a mid-latitude desert has bare mountains, large fans and a salt-crusted floor. Old shorelines ring the basin well above today's floor. Which account best explains the landscape?

In a mid-latitude desert such as Death Valley, water is the main shaping agent. Rare flash floods act in hours, temporary lakes and salt crusts change over months, and Ice Age lakes and inherited pediments record change over millennia, all inside a basin that tectonics keeps sinking.

Mission 4

The Namib: a low-latitude coastal desert

  • A cool coastal desert running about 1,900 km along the Atlantic coast of Africa.
  • Coastal deserts lie "on the western edges of continents near the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn".
  • Namib Sand Sea: a World Heritage Site of 3,077,700 hectares.
  • Study area: the central Namib around the Kuiseb River and Gobabeb research station.
Mission 4

Physical factor 1: a cold-current climate

  • "The cold Benguela Current flows northward along the coast, chilling the air above it and thus producing fog".
  • Cool air under hot, dry air: an inversion, so rain clouds rarely form.
  • "Fog is the primary source of water" in the sand sea.
  • Rainfall: about 13 mm a year at the coast, 51 mm at the foot of the escarpment; "in some years … no rain falls at all".
Mission 4

Physical factor 2: sand supply and wind

  • Sediment comes "from the hinterland", carried "by river, ocean current and wind" over "thousands of kilometres".
  • The main sand source is the Orange River at the desert's southern edge.
  • Dunes are shaped by "pronounced daily and seasonal changes in dominant wind directions".
  • Geology and time: the area "has been arid for at least 55 million years".
Mission 4

Aeolian landforms: the sand sea

  • The sand sea "includes most known types of dunes".
  • Dunes reach 60–240 m and "run in lines from north-northwest to south-southeast": linear (seif) dunes.
  • Near the southern coast: "rapidly moving crescent-shaped barchans".
  • Two generations: "an ancient semi-consolidated one overlain by a younger active one".
Mission 4

Fluvial landforms: ephemeral rivers

  • Ephemeral rivers flow only after heavy rain inland; "they normally flow for no more than a few days in several years".
  • The Kuiseb has water below its dry bed: its underflow supplies Walvis Bay and Swakopmund.
  • South of the Kuiseb, "every stream terminates in a vlei" (a clay pan, the Namib's playa).
  • North: gravel plains and rock platforms, with isolated mountains rising steeply above them (inselbergs).
Quick check
?Why does the Namib get fog on many days but very little rain?
Mission 4

Landforms interact: the Kuiseb edge

  • Around Gobabeb: "the Sand Sea to the south, the Gravel Plains to the north", with the Kuiseb's riparian woodland between.
  • WWF: the Kuiseb "and the vegetation within it acts as a windbreak to the southerly winds".
  • Rare floods run down the channel for days.
  • Inference from these facts: wind moves sand north; trees slow it; occasional floods carry away sand that reaches the channel. The edge is a balance of wind and water.
Quick check
?Which pair of processes best explains why the sand sea stops at the Kuiseb?
Mission 4

Process, time, landform

  • Fast, local: barchans move rapidly near the coast.
  • Rare, powerful: floods for a few days in several years cut and clear channels.
  • Slow, regional: sand stays in the sand sea for at least a million years.
  • The landscape is "continuously refreshed and maintained by wholly natural processes".
Mission 4

Change over time: from seconds to millennia

  • Seconds: single grains bounce downwind (saltation); a flood surge moves gravel.
  • Days: an ephemeral river flows, then dries.
  • Years to centuries: barchans migrate; vegetation along the Kuiseb grows or is cleared.
  • Millennia: the sand stays in the sand sea for at least a million years; the region has been arid for at least 55 million years.
Mission 4

Exam move: one landscape system

  • Inputs: sand (Orange River), fog and rare rain, wind energy.
  • Stores: the sand sea; water under the Kuiseb's bed.
  • Outputs and edges: rivers ending in vleis; the Kuiseb edge.
  • Explain inter-relationships: how one landform controls another (river limits dunes; dunes block rivers).
Mission check

?South of the Kuiseb, rivers end in clay pans among the dunes and do not reach the sea. What does this show?

At the Kuiseb, rare floods and a band of trees hold the edge against the wind. Further south, the dunes win: rivers die in vleis. One landscape, one contest between wind and water, two outcomes.

Mission 5

Relict landforms

  • A relict landform was made by processes that no longer operate in that place.
  • Drylands hold two sets:
    • Pluvial legacies, from wetter periods: inselbergs, pediments.
    • Periglacial legacies, from colder periods: frost-shattered debris, nivation hollows, solifluction deposits.
  • Today's processes (flash floods, wind, weathering) slowly modify them.
Mission 5

A pluvial period: the Green Sahara

  • Pluvial period: a long phase wetter than today in a region that is now dry.
  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago the Sahara "received high amounts of rainfall", with vegetation and permanent lakes.
  • Wet conditions reached 31°N and "likely ended abruptly".
  • More rain means more chemical weathering and more running water: the agents that shape inselbergs and pediments.
Quick check
?Which observation in a desert today would best show an earlier pluvial period?
Mission 5

Inselbergs

  • Inselberg: an isolated steep-sided hill of rock rising from a plain, e.g. Uluru, central Australia.
  • Two models explain them:
    • Two-stage: rock weathers deep below ground; later erosion strips the rotted rock (saprolite), exposing the unweathered core.
    • At the surface: slopes develop and retreat at ground level.
  • Deep chemical weathering needs water, so the two-stage model links inselbergs to wetter past climates.
Mission 5

Pediments

  • Pediment: a gently sloping rock surface at the foot of an upland, with a thin cover of debris.
  • Western Cape, South Africa: pediments are ancient; minimum age Miocene.
  • Their surfaces are now lowering at only 0.3 to 0.95 m per million years.
  • They formed first by hillslope processes, then were shaped by running water. In wetter phases more runoff means more sheetwash and stream erosion.
Quick check
?A pediment surface lowers at 0.5 m per million years. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 5

Colder periods

  • In the Pleistocene, ice fields covered plateaus in the High Atlas, Morocco, now on the edge of the Sahara.
  • These glaciers were "likely to have been associated with wetter conditions than today and colder air temperatures".
  • Beyond the ice, ground froze and thawed: periglacial conditions.
  • Cold periods have also recurred more recently: the Little Ice Age promoted slope movement in southern Spain.
Mission 5

Three periglacial legacies

  • Frost-shattered debris: angular rock broken by freeze–thaw; collects as scree below cliffs.
  • Nivation hollow: a hollow eroded by freeze–thaw beneath and around a lingering snow patch.
  • Solifluction deposit: soil that flowed slowly downslope over frozen ground in the thaw season, leaving lobes and terraces.
  • All three need repeated freezing and water.
Mission 5

Sierra Nevada, southern Spain

  • A high semi-arid mountain range.
  • Numerous solifluction lobes are "almost inactive under the current semiarid climate".
  • Water availability is the crucial control; colder and wetter periods "triggered solifluction".
  • An aridification trend since 4,200 years ago pushed solifluction to higher ground.
  • Today seasonal frost prevails above 2,500 m; inactive lobes have average soil temperatures of 3–4°C.
Quick check
?Why are most Sierra Nevada solifluction lobes inactive today?
Mission 5

Modification today

  • Inselbergs: fire spalls rock off their bases; after the 2019–20 Australian fires spalling was widespread, creating flared slopes.
  • Pediments: streams have since dissected them, cutting valleys up to 280 m deep in the Western Cape; at 0.3–0.95 m per million years, change today is slow.
  • Solifluction lobes: in warm phases vegetation and soils spread over them; they stabilise.
  • Nivation hollows and frost debris: without lasting snow and frost, they stop growing and are slowly buried or eroded.
Mission 5

Modification by future climate

  • Extreme daily rainfall is projected to intensify by about 7% per 1°C of global warming.
  • The Mediterranean and West Southern Africa are among regions with projected increases in drought.
  • Warming will bring further loss of seasonal snow cover.
  • Likely effects: more flash-flood cutting of pediments; drier, snow-poor slopes leave periglacial forms fully relict.
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): weigh inheritance from past climates against present processes, then judge which dominates for each landform.
Mission check

?A semi-arid upland has gentle rock slopes at its foot, now cut by gullies, and grassed-over lobes of soil on its upper slopes. Which explanation fits best?

Drylands hold relict landforms: inselbergs and pediments from wetter (pluvial) phases, and frost debris, nivation hollows and solifluction lobes from colder phases. Today's climate mostly modifies them; it could not have made them.

Mission 6

Why irrigate a dryland?

  • Aridity (too little water) is not infertility (too few nutrients): given water, many dryland soils grow crops well.
  • In Central Asia, NASA notes that "irrigation made the desert bloom", but it devastated the Aral Sea.
  • Irrigation brings water from rivers or aquifers to fields; the question is what else it brings, and where the water stops.
Mission 6

Case study: the Indus Basin, Pakistan

  • Pakistan's Indus Basin Irrigation System is "the largest contiguous surface water irrigation system in the world": barrages and thousands of kilometres of canals.
  • Twenty-three barrages can divert up to 69,500 m³/s; the Sukkur Barrage (1932) alone can divert 1,642 m³/s.
  • About 129 km³ of Indus water a year is abstracted for irrigation and other uses.
  • The lower basin, Sindh, is hot and arid: crops there depend on canal water.
Mission 6

Benefits

  • Socio-economic: the system's water is "central to the country's water and food security".
  • Canal leakage recharges aquifers: "fresh groundwater exists primarily due to widespread leakage" from canals.
  • Groundwater now supplies over 50% of agricultural water and 90% of domestic water in rural areas.
  • Environmental: recharged aquifers and green farmland where there would otherwise be dry plain.
Mission 6

Problem: waterlogging

  • Seepage from canals into the aquifer raised the water table.
  • Waterlogging still affects 35% of the canal command area, mostly in Sindh.
  • Roots in saturated soil lack oxygen, so yields fall.
  • The World Bank links it to "excess irrigation in areas where groundwater levels are high".
Mission 6

Problem: salinisation

  • All irrigation water carries dissolved salt. The World Bank: irrigation adds "around 16 million tons of salt" to the Indus basin each year.
  • Where the water table is near the surface, water rises through the soil and evaporates, leaving salt behind.
  • Salinity threatens soil health and production in up to 43% of the irrigated area.
  • The Aral basin shows the same process: half of Central Asia's irrigated lands were affected by secondary salinisation (cited by the IPCC).
Quick check
?Why does irrigation make soil salty more often in hot drylands than in humid regions?
Mission 6

Problems: depletion and low returns

  • Over-pumping affects about 18% of the irrigated area; wells have dried up in parts of Punjab.
  • Rice, wheat, sugarcane and cotton use 80% of the water but contribute only 5% of GDP.
  • Poor water management is conservatively estimated to cost 4% of GDP, about $12 billion a year.
  • So the same scheme can be too wet in Sindh and too dry underground in Punjab.
Mission 6

Possible solutions

  • Lower the water table: drains and pumping; the government encouraged groundwater pumping for this.
  • Manage surface and groundwater together ("conjunctively"), with an accountable agency in each province and a modern groundwater database.
  • Modernise irrigation and reform subsidies so prices reflect the real value of water.
  • Field scale: line canals, irrigate less but more precisely (drip, levelled fields), flush salts where drainage exists, grow salt-tolerant crops, which FAO notes may not be "high income cash crops".
Quick check
?A district in Sindh has waterlogged, salty fields. Which response treats the cause rather than a symptom?
Mission 6

The water-supply issue downstream

  • Reason: dams and 23 barrages divert water for irrigation; storage in Indus basin dams exceeds 23 km³.
  • Flow to the delta, 1931–54: about 107 km³ of water and 193 million tonnes (Mt) of sediment a year.
  • By 1993–2003: about 10 km³ and 13 Mt a year.
  • Reservoirs trap sediment; barrages divert water, and the silt in it, onto fields.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught, by roughly what percentage did the Indus sediment load reaching the delta fall between 1931–54 and 1993–2003?
Mission 6

Effects on landforms and landscape

  • Process change: less water and sediment reach the coast, while waves and tides keep eroding.
  • Landform change: across the delta, erosion averaged about 69 Mt a year and deposition about 22 Mt, a net loss of about 47 Mt.
  • Landscape consequence: since 1944 the delta has lost about 12.7 km² of land a year, altering 25% of it. The World Bank calls for more flow below Kotri Barrage, for the delta and for Karachi's water.
  • Channel landforms: levees confine the river to a corridor about 15 km wide; the delta channel is reduced to a single thread, and the sea now intrudes up to 225 km.
  • The same chain applies to other dryland depositional landforms: trap the floods and sediment upstream, and alluvial fans and wadi floors downstream stop growing.
  • Exam move (OCR): trace reason → flows of water and sediment → landform → landscape, with a figure at each step.
Mission check

?A new canal scheme in a hot, semi-arid valley floods fields each season. No drains are built. Ten years later, white crusts cover low-lying fields and yields are falling. What happened?

Watering a desert can damage it when water has nowhere to go: the water table rises, evaporation leaves salt, and diverted rivers starve the lakes and deltas downstream.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In Utah's desert, damaged ground can take decades to heal. How can off-road driving reshape a whole landscape?

Make a guess: In Utah's desert, damaged ground can take decades to heal. How can off-road driving reshape a whole landscape?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 1.1.3-4.b case study of a dryland landscape used for economic activity (e.g. tourism, dune buggies): crust and vegetation damage, dune erosion, loess accumulation
Mission 7

The landscape: the Colorado Plateau

  • A high, dry plateau of canyons, mesas and sandy plains in Utah, Colorado, Arizona and New Mexico; Moab, Utah, lies near its centre.
  • Rain is sparse, and plants are widely spaced. Between them, the ground is covered not by bare sand but by biological soil crusts.
  • These crusts "occur in all hot, cool, and cold arid and semi-arid regions" and "may constitute up to 70% of the living cover in some plant communities".
  • The region's soils are a major dust source: dust reaching the Upper Colorado River Basin "comes from the disturbed soils in the Colorado Plateau and Great Basin".
Mission 7

The economic activity, and why

  • Grazing: "Dramatic growth in grazing, agriculture, and resource exploration" came with settlement from the mid-1800s; livestock grazing expanded in the early twentieth century.
  • Tourism and recreation: today the area draws visitors for its scenery, with off-road driving, mountain biking and hiking on public land.
  • Researchers expect "increases in energy and mineral exploration/development and recreation" that "would further disturb soils".
  • Each activity puts tyres, feet or hooves on fragile ground.
Mission 7

The crust: a living skin

  • Crusts are "a complex mosaic of cyanobacteria, green algae, lichens, mosses, microfungi, and other bacteria".
  • Cyanobacterial filaments "weave through the top few millimeters of soil, gluing loose particles together and forming a matrix that stabilizes and protects soil surfaces from erosive forces".
  • Many crust organisms also fix nitrogen, which enriches poor dryland soils.
  • In systems terms, the crust is a store that holds sediment in place and reduces the flows of wind- and water-borne material.
Quick check
?What is the main geomorphic role of biological soil crusts in drylands?
Mission 7

Impacts on flows of energy and material

  • Vehicles exert compression and shear forces "much greater … than trampling"; they "often turn soils over and bury crustal organisms".
  • "Vehicle tracks often channel water off-site and thus slow or prevent recovery": runoff concentrates in ruts.
  • Crushed soils start to move "at lower wind speeds": well-developed crusts have "2 to 130 times greater resistance to soil erosion than less well-developed crusts or bare soil".
  • Experimental disturbance raised sediment production "up to 35 times that of adjacent undisturbed crusts".
Quick check
?Which disturbance is likely to cause the greatest damage to a crusted sandy soil?
Mission 7

Changing the landforms

  • Where crusts on sandy soils are crushed, wind lifts sand at lower speeds, so sand sheets and dunes erode faster and sand starts to move again.
  • "Nearby soil crusts are often buried by blowing sediment, resulting in death of photosynthetic organisms", so damage spreads beyond the track.
  • Tracks "channel water off-site", so water erosion concentrates along them and ruts can deepen into rills.
  • A cycle: less crust → more erosion → more burial → less crust (positive feedback).
Mission 7

Recovery: decades to centuries

  • Reported recovery times range from 2 to more than 3,800 years, depending on climate, soil and disturbance.
  • On the Colorado Plateau, scraped plots were checked after 2–5 and 10–14 years: cyanobacterial cover recovered within 14–34 years.
  • Mosses and lichens are slower: at one site an early estimate of 400 years to recovery was revised to 42 years.
  • So one day's driving can change a surface for a generation or more.
Mission 7

Consequences beyond the desert: dust and loess

  • Wind carries fine particles out of the dryland and drops them in marginal areas downwind: the process that builds loess.
  • Alpine lake sediments in the western interior USA show dust deposition rose by 500% above the late-Holocene average after nineteenth-century settlement, linked to expanding livestock grazing.
  • Dust darkens mountain snow, so it melts earlier: peak runoff of the Colorado River at Lees Ferry came about 3 weeks earlier under heavier dust, and annual runoff fell by more than 1.0 billion m³, about 5%.
  • Disturbed desert soils therefore change the landscape far away, in the mountains where the dust settles.
Quick check
?Which sequence best links off-road driving to the mountain landscape downwind?
Mission 7

Managing the impact

  • Keep vehicles to designated routes: the damage is concentrated, not spread across the landscape.
  • Stop or rest disturbance: when it ceases, soil surfaces generally stabilise "within days to years"; full crust recovery takes far longer.
  • Weigh the activity: tourism and grazing bring income; the costs (erosion, dust, earlier snowmelt) fall on others, some far away.
  • Researchers see surface stabilisation in the deserts as a possible way to restore "more persistent snow cover" and river flow.
Mission check

?A new dune-buggy route opens across a crusted sand sheet in a semi-arid basin. What is the most likely change to the landscape over the following decades?

Off-road driving breaks the biological soil crust that binds dryland soils. Wind then moves sand and dust at lower speeds, dunes and sand sheets erode, and dust settles in marginal areas downwind, as on the Colorado Plateau, where dust deposition rose by about 500% after settlement.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A desert is defined by dryness: under 250 mm a year, or an aridity index (P ÷ PET) below 0.20 for arid land.
  • Hot deserts lie under subtropical high pressure, beside cold currents, in continental interiors and in rain shadows.

Mission 2

  • Weathering supplies debris; gravity, water and wind move it.
  • Erosional landforms: wadis, canyons, ventifacts, pedestal rocks, pavements. Depositional: dunes, alluvial fans, bajadas.

Mission 3

  • Death Valley is a mid-latitude, rain-shadow desert in a subsiding, fault-bounded basin with no outlet; relief and closed drainage control where sediment and salt end up.
  • Its landforms link downslope (canyon, fan, bajada, playa, salt pan, dunes) and change on every timescale: flash floods in hours, lakes and salt crusts in months, lakes and pediments over millennia.

Mission 4

  • The Namib is a low-latitude coastal desert: a cold current gives fog, not rain (about 13 mm of rain a year at the coast); sand from the Orange River has stayed for at least a million years.
  • Landforms interact: dunes, ephemeral rivers, vleis, gravel plains and inselbergs; at the Kuiseb water and trees hold back the sand, while further south dunes block the rivers.

Mission 5

  • Pluvial periods (e.g. the Green Sahara, 11,000–5,000 years ago) supplied water for deep weathering and running water that shaped inselbergs and pediments.
  • Colder periods left frost-shattered debris, nivation hollows and solifluction deposits; in semi-arid mountains such as the Sierra Nevada these are now largely inactive, and future drying and warming will keep them so while intense storms dissect pediments.

Mission 6

  • Irrigation turns aridity into productivity, but without drainage it causes waterlogging and salinisation (Indus: 35% waterlogged command area; salt threatens up to 43% of irrigated land).
  • Abstraction and dams cut water and sediment to the Indus delta by over 90%, so erosion outpaces deposition and the delta shrinks.

Mission 7

  • On the Colorado Plateau, grazing and recreation crush biological soil crusts, lowering the wind speed needed to move soil and raising sediment output many times over.
  • The effects reach far beyond the track: dunes and sand sheets erode, crusts are buried, and dust settles in distant mountains (loess-type accumulation), darkening snow and changing river flow.
  • Recovery takes decades to centuries, so management means keeping disturbance to fixed routes.
