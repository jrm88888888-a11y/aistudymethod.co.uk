Mission 1

Mission 1: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Make a guess: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Mission 1

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 1

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
Mission 1

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a negative output gap: real output and employment rise; the price level rises a little.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close a positive output gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes.

Mission 1

Two views: Keynesian vs monetarist and new classical

  • Keynesian view: AS is flat when there is lots of spare capacity.
    • In a deep recession, expansionary fiscal policy raises real output and jobs with little inflation.
    • Near full capacity, it mainly raises the price level.
  • Monetarist / new classical view: LRAS is vertical at potential output.
    • In the short run, more G raises output and the price level.
    • In the long run, wages and costs adjust: SRAS shifts left. Output returns to potential, at a higher price level.
    • Government borrowing may push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • So the two views disagree on how far fiscal policy can raise output for long.
Mission 1

Fiscal policy on the supply side

  • Some fiscal measures also shift LRAS right:
    • capital spending on infrastructure, broadband, transport
    • spending on education, training and health
    • tax incentives for investment or research
  • These raise productive capacity: more output with less pressure on prices.
  • But they take years, and the first effect is on AD (spending now).
  • Demand-side fiscal policy aims at AD; supply-side fiscal policy aims at productive capacity and incentives.
Mission 1

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilisers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
  • Political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; tax rises and spending cuts are not.
  • Sustainable debt: high debt raises interest costs and may limit room for future stimulus.
Mission 1

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • UK 2008–09: VAT was cut from 17.5% to 15% from December 2008 to December 2009, to support spending during the recession. Its effect is debated: some spending was brought forward rather than added.
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary.
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?
Mission 1Evaluate

How effective is fiscal policy?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: in a deep recession, extra demand raises output; near capacity it mostly raises prices.
  • Size of the multiplier: high saving or imports weaken the effect.
  • Time lags: stimulus can arrive too late.
  • Confidence and debt: if people expect future tax rises to repay debt, they may save more. If markets doubt debt is sustainable, interest rates may rise.
  • Crowding out: borrowing may push up interest rates and reduce private investment (stronger near full capacity).
Mission check

?A government spots a recession and plans a large spending programme. It takes 18 months to pass and start, and by then the economy is already recovering strongly. What is the main risk?

Fiscal policy can lift a slump, but its effect depends on timing and on how much spare capacity is left.

Mission 2: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Mission 2

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
  • Say: "capital spending" for long-lasting assets — not: "spending on capital cities" or "big spending".

Mission 2

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
  • UK term: public sector net borrowing (PSNB) is the deficit.
Mission 2

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 2

Why deficits change: cyclical vs structural

  • In a recession: incomes, profits and spending fall, so tax revenue falls. Unemployment rises, so benefit spending rises. The deficit grows without any new decision.
  • In a boom, the reverse: the deficit shrinks.
  • Cyclical deficit: the part caused by the economy being below its potential output. It disappears when the economy recovers.
  • Structural deficit: the part that remains even when the economy is at potential output (no output gap). It reflects long-run choices about tax rates and spending.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical part + structural part.

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.

Mission 2

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Example: in April 2010 Greece's rating was cut to "junk" by S&P. Its 10-year bond yields rose above 10%, and it needed EU and IMF loans.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is a deficit a problem?

It depends on:

  • Why it exists: a cyclical deficit falls as the economy recovers; a structural deficit does not.
  • What it pays for: capital spending adds assets that can raise productive capacity; most current spending pays for today's services.
  • Interest rate vs growth: if the interest rate on the debt is below the growth rate of nominal GDP, the debt ratio is easier to keep stable.
  • Who lends: domestic lenders, long-dated bonds and borrowing in the country's own currency usually reduce the risk of a sudden crisis.
  • Starting level and confidence: the same deficit worries lenders more when debt is already high.
  • Economists disagree on how high is too high. There is no single safe debt ratio.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a government's national debt is 300 billion at the start of Year 1. Its budget balances are: Year 1, surplus of 20 billion · Year 2, surplus of 5 billion · Year 3, deficit of 15 billion. What is the national debt at the end of Year 3?

The deficit is the yearly flow; the debt is the stock. Every deficit adds to the debt, however small.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Make a guess: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Mission 3

Two ways the budget changes

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending. E.g. a budget that cuts income tax or funds new roads.
  • Automatic stabilisers: changes in tax revenue and transfer spending that happen on their own as the economy moves through the trade cycle (also called the business cycle). No new decision is needed.
  • Tax rates and benefit rules stay the same. What changes is how much income, spending and unemployment there is.
Mission 3

How stabilisers work

In a recession:

  • Incomes fall → income tax paid falls. Spending falls → sales taxes (VAT) collected fall.
  • With a progressive tax, tax falls proportionally more than income.
  • Unemployment rises → unemployment benefit payments rise.
  • So disposable income falls by less than income. Consumption, and AD, fall by less.

Illustrative data:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first 10,000 · 20% from 10,000 to 50,000 · 40% above 50,000.
  • Income falls from 60,000 to 40,000 (−33%). Tax falls from 12,000 to 6,000 (−50%).
  • In a boom, the reverse: tax rises faster than income and benefit spending falls. This slows the rise in AD.
  • Say: "automatic stabilisers reduce the size of fluctuations" — not: "they stop recessions".

Mission 3

Stabilisers and the deficit

  • Lower tax revenue + higher benefit spending = a bigger deficit in a recession, with no new decision.
  • In a boom the deficit shrinks (or a surplus grows) automatically.
  • This automatic part is the cyclical deficit (see Mission 2). Discretionary choices change the structural deficit.
  • 2008–09 example: UK borrowing rose to about 10% of GDP in 2009–10. Part was automatic (falling tax receipts, rising benefit claims). Part was discretionary, e.g. a temporary VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% (December 2008 to December 2009).
Mission 3

Automatic vs discretionary: strengths and limits

Automatic stabilisers:

  • Fast: no time lag for recognising the problem or passing a law.
  • Work in both directions: they cushion recessions and cool booms.
  • But partial: they reduce fluctuations; they do not remove them.

Discretionary policy:

  • Can be larger and targeted (e.g. at infrastructure or at low-income households).
  • But faces time lags: recognising the problem, deciding, then putting it into effect. It may arrive after recovery has begun.
  • Can face political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; reversing them in a boom is not.
Mission 3

Fiscal rules

  • A fiscal rule is a lasting limit on borrowing or debt, set to build lenders' trust and resist political pressure to overspend.
  • Examples:
  • EU rules: deficit no more than 3% of GDP; debt no more than 60% of GDP.
  • UK (2024): balance the current budget (borrow only to invest) and have a debt measure falling as a share of GDP, both by 2029–30.
  • Design problem: a rule on the actual deficit can force tax rises or cuts in a recession, which works against the automatic stabilisers.
  • So many rules target the structural (cyclically adjusted) balance, a forecast year, or have escape clauses. The EU suspended its deficit limits from 2020 to 2023.
  • Trade-off: flexible rules let stabilisers work; strict rules are more credible.
Quick check
?A country has a rule that its actual deficit must not exceed 3% of GDP. A recession pushes the deficit from 2% to 4% of GDP. To obey the rule at once, the government raises tax rates. What is the likely effect?
Mission 3Evaluate

How much can stabilisers do?

It depends on:

  • Progressivity and benefits: steeper tax rates and fuller unemployment benefits give a bigger cushion. Countries with larger public sectors tend to have stronger stabilisers.
  • Size of the shock: in a deep recession stabilisers cushion only part of the fall; discretionary policy may be needed too.
  • Debt and rules: high debt or strict rules on the actual deficit can force governments to work against the stabilisers.
  • Incentives: high marginal tax rates and generous benefits may reduce incentives to work, a long-run cost to weigh.
  • Confidence: if households save rather than spend, even well-designed support raises AD less.
Mission check

?Country A has a progressive income tax and pays unemployment benefits. Country B charges every adult the same fixed amount of tax and pays no unemployment benefits. Both suffer the same fall in exports. Neither government takes any new decision. Which statement is correct?

Automatic stabilisers cushion AD in a downturn, and the price is a larger cyclical deficit.

Mission 4: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Make a guess: When a government borrows more, can it leave firms with less to invest?

Mission 4

Competing for the same savings

  • To finance a deficit, the government borrows: it sells bonds to savers, banks and pension funds.
  • Firms also borrow to invest. Both compete for the same pool of saving.
  • More government borrowing can push up interest rates and so reduce private investment.
  • Crowding out: an increase in government spending or borrowing that reduces private sector spending.
  • Two kinds: financial crowding out works through higher interest rates. Resource crowding out happens near full employment: workers and materials used by the state are not available to private firms.
Mission 4

The loanable funds market

  • Supply of loanable funds: from saving. Higher real interest rates encourage more saving.
  • Demand for loanable funds: from borrowers: firms' investment plus government borrowing.
  • Illustrative data (billion): at a real interest rate of 4%, saving = private investment = 500.
  • The government borrows an extra 100. Demand shifts right by 100: D1 → D2.
  • New equilibrium: real interest rate 5%, total lending 550.
  • At 5%, private investment falls to 450. 50 is crowded out.
  • The other 50 comes from extra saving drawn out by the higher rate.
  • Say: "private investment falls" — not: "the government takes firms' money". The higher price of borrowing does the work.

Mission 4

Short run and long run

Short run:

  • A higher real interest rate cuts interest-sensitive spending: business investment, new homes, cars bought on credit.
  • This offsets part of the rise in AD from the extra government spending.

Long run:

  • Less private investment → the capital stock grows more slowly.
  • So productive capacity grows more slowly: a smaller rightward shift of LRAS (the PPC grows less).
  • Exception: if the government borrows to build public capital (roads, broadband, training), total capital may still grow.
Quick check
?At full employment, a government increases its deficit spending, financed by borrowing. Saving responds only slightly to interest rates. Which is the most likely long-run effect?
Mission 4

Debt, interest and credit ratings

  • A large debt means large interest payments every year. That money cannot pay for schools, health or lower taxes: debt interest crowds out other public spending.
  • If lenders worry about repayment, they demand a higher yield on government bonds. Agencies may downgrade the credit rating.
  • Government bond yields are a benchmark: when they rise, firms' and households' borrowing costs tend to rise too, deepening crowding out.
  • Example: Greece's rating was cut to "junk" in April 2010, and its borrowing costs soared (see Mission 2).
Mission 4

When crowding out is small, or turns to crowding in

Crowding out is smaller when:

  • The economy is in a deep recession: spare capacity, high saving, weak private demand for loans. Extra borrowing raises interest rates very little.
  • The central bank holds interest rates down (rate cuts, QE).
  • Foreign lenders supply funds at little extra cost.

Crowding in (a Keynesian view): government spending raises incomes and sales. Firms then invest more to meet demand (the accelerator).

  • Public capital (roads, research, broadband) can make private investment more profitable.
  • Example: Japan's gross government debt is over 200% of GDP, yet its 10-year bond yield was held near 0% for several years from 2016. The Bank of Japan's bond buying explains part of this.
Mission 4Evaluate

How big is crowding out?

It depends on:

  • Spare capacity: large near full employment; small in a deep recession.
  • Saving and investment responses: if saving barely responds to interest rates, or investment is very sensitive to them, crowding out is larger.
  • Monetary policy and openness: a central bank holding rates down, or inflows of foreign saving, reduce it.
  • What the money buys: public capital can crowd in private investment; spending on current items is less likely to.
  • Confidence: if high debt makes lenders nervous, yields rise and crowding out grows.
  • The evidence is contested. Monetarist and new classical economists stress crowding out; Keynesian economists stress crowding in during recessions.
Quick check

Mission 5

The bank behind the banks

  • A central bank is the bank at the centre of a country's money system. Examples:
  • Bank of England (founded 1694); US Federal Reserve (1913); European Central Bank (1998), for the countries that use the euro.
  • Its customers are the government and commercial banks, not households or firms.
  • Commercial banks hold accounts there called reserves.
  • When customers of one bank pay customers of another, the two banks settle by moving reserves between their accounts at the central bank.
  • It is usually not run for profit: its aim is a stable economy.
Mission 5

What a central bank does

  • Monetary policy: sets the policy interest rate to keep inflation low and stable.
  • Issues banknotes.
  • Banker to the government: holds its main account and helps manage its borrowing.
  • Banker to the banks: holds their reserves and runs the system they use to pay each other.
  • Lender of last resort: lends to banks that run short of cash.
  • Financial stability: watches for risks to the whole system and, in many countries, supervises banks.
Mission 5

The goal: stable prices

  • Most central banks aim for low, stable inflation, often a numerical target.
  • UK: the government sets an inflation target of 2% (CPI). The Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), nine members, sets Bank Rate to hit it.
  • The UK target is symmetric: inflation below 2% is treated as seriously as inflation above it.
  • Stable prices help firms and households plan, borrow and invest.
Mission 5

The tools

  • Policy interest rate: the rate at which the central bank lends to, or pays on reserves of, commercial banks. Other rates tend to follow.
  • UK: Bank Rate (often called the base rate), set by the MPC; it feeds through to loan and savings rates (the transmission mechanism).
  • Open market operations: buying or selling government bonds to change bank reserves and the money supply.
  • Quantitative easing (QE): buying bonds on a large scale with newly created money, to push down long-term rates.
  • The Bank of England sets no minimum reserve ratio for monetary policy.
Quick check
?Which of these is a job of a central bank, and not of a commercial bank?
Mission 5

Lender of last resort

  • A bank can be solvent (assets worth more than it owes) but illiquid: it cannot turn its loans into cash fast enough.
  • If depositors panic and withdraw at once (a bank run), even a sound bank can fail.
  • The central bank can lend it cash against good collateral: it is the lender of last resort.
  • Classic rule (Walter Bagehot, 1873): lend freely, against good collateral, at a high rate.
  • Real-world: on 14 September 2007 the Bank of England gave emergency support to Northern Rock. Queues of savers still formed outside its branches.
  • Risk: moral hazard. If banks expect rescue, they may take bigger risks.
Quick check
?A bank's loans are worth more than it owes, but depositors suddenly withdraw more cash than it holds. What can the central bank do as lender of last resort?
Mission 5

Central banks, banks and the money supply

  • Most money is bank deposits, created when commercial banks lend.
  • The central bank shapes that lending:
  • Policy rate: a higher rate makes loans dearer, so banks lend less and deposits grow more slowly.
  • QE: buying bonds creates reserves for banks and deposits for the sellers, so broad money rises directly.
  • Regulation: rules on capital and liquidity limit how far banks can expand lending.
  • So the central bank influences the money supply and the banking sector, but does not fix the quantity of money directly.
Mission 5

Keeping the system safe

  • Banks fail in ways that can spread to the whole economy. Central banks often help regulate them.
  • UK (since 2013): the Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee (FPC) looks at risks to the whole system.
  • The Prudential Regulation Authority (PRA), part of the Bank of England, supervises individual banks and insurers.
  • The Financial Conduct Authority (FCA), separate from the Bank, regulates how firms treat customers.
  • More on regulation and crises in a later lesson.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should central banks be independent?

  • UK: the Bank of England has set interest rates independently since 1997. The ECB's independence is written into EU treaties.
  • For: rate-setters cannot be pushed to cut rates before an election, so promises of low inflation are more credible. Inflation expectations may stay anchored.
  • Against: unelected officials make decisions that affect jobs, house prices and savers; accountability is weaker.
  • Against: monetary and fiscal policy may pull in opposite directions without coordination.
  • It depends on: how the target is set and reviewed, how open the central bank is, and how far it strays into areas such as QE that change who gains and loses.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 6

What monetary policy is

  • Monetary policy: the central bank's use of interest rates and the money supply to influence demand and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The Bank has had operational independence since 1997: the government sets the target; the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Expansionary policy: lower rates, more money. Contractionary: higher rates, less money.
Mission 6

Goals: an inflation target

  • Main goal: price stability, which means low and stable inflation.
  • UK: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government. The target is symmetric: undershooting is treated as seriously as overshooting.
  • If inflation is more than 1 percentage point away from 2%, the Governor writes an open letter to the Chancellor explaining why.
  • Why a target: it anchors expectations. If people expect 2%, wages and prices are set to match.
Mission 6

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the policy rate reaches inflation:
    • Market rates rise: mortgages, loans, savings. Borrowing costs more; saving pays more. Consumption (C) and investment (I) fall.
    • Asset prices fall (houses, shares, bonds). Households feel less wealthy and spend less.
    • Confidence and expectations: a signal that the central bank is serious about inflation.
    • Exchange rate rises: exports more expensive abroad, imports cheaper. Net exports fall; import prices fall.
  • Result: AD grows more slowly. Less pressure on firms' capacity, wages and prices. Inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way.
  • Say: "Bank Rate" (UK) — not: "the interest rate", when you mean the Bank of England's policy rate.
Mission 6

Monetary policy on AD/AS

  • Contractionary (rate rise): AD shifts left, AD1 → AD2.
    • Real output falls. The price level is lower than it would have been.
    • Closes an inflationary (positive) gap.
  • Expansionary (rate cut): AD shifts right.
    • Real output and the price level rise. Unemployment falls.
    • Closes a deflationary or recessionary (negative) gap.
  • Say: "inflation slows" (disinflation) — not: "prices fall", unless the price level actually falls (deflation).

Mission 6

What the committee weighs

  • The MPC forecasts inflation about two years ahead, because rate changes take time to work.
  • It looks at: spare capacity and unemployment, wage growth, inflation expectations, the exchange rate, house prices and credit, and world energy and commodity prices.
Mission 6

Real case: the rate rises after the pandemic

  • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
  • CPI inflation peaked at 11.1% (October 2022) and was back at 2.0% in May 2024 (ONS).
  • Inflation then fell, but economists debate how much was due to higher rates and how much to falling energy prices.
Mission 6

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • Flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year.
    • Reversible, and quick to decide: no new law needed.
    • An independent central bank is credible: a promise to hit the target shapes expectations.
  • Limits:
    • Time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation (Bank of England).
    • Supply shocks: rate rises can lower inflation, but at the cost of lower output and jobs.
    • Low confidence: firms and households may not borrow, however cheap credit is.
    • One rate for everyone: regions, and borrowers and savers, are hit differently.
Mission 6

The zero lower bound

  • Rates cannot be cut far below zero: people could hold cash instead.
  • Zero lower bound: once rates are near zero, conventional policy has little room left.
  • Real cases: Bank Rate 0.1% (March 2020). The European Central Bank's deposit rate reached −0.5% (2019); the Bank of Japan's short-term rate was −0.1% from 2016 to March 2024.
  • Liquidity trap (Keynes): rates are so low that more money does not raise spending; people just hold it.
  • Central banks then turn to QE and forward guidance (see Mission 7).
Quick check
?A recession has cut confidence. The central bank's policy rate is already 0.25%, and banks are unwilling to lend. Why might a further rate cut do little to raise AD?
Mission 6Evaluate

How well can interest rates steer the economy?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: demand-pull responds well; a supply shock forces a choice between inflation and output.
  • Time lags: a rate change made now acts on demand up to two years later, when conditions may have changed.
  • Confidence and debt: with many variable-rate borrowers, rates bite fast; when confidence is low, cuts may not work.
  • How low rates already are: near the zero lower bound, other tools are needed.
Mission check

?A sharp rise in world oil prices pushes a country's inflation well above target, while real output falls. The central bank raises its policy rate. What is the most likely result in the short run?

Interest rates work through demand. After a supply shock, slowing inflation comes at the cost of lower output and jobs in the short run.

Mission 7

When the usual tool runs out

  • The usual tool: cut the policy interest rate to make borrowing cheaper.
  • In the UK: the MPC cut Bank Rate from 5.75% (2007) to 0.5% (March 2009).
  • Rates cannot go far below zero: people could hold cash at 0% instead. This floor is the zero (or effective) lower bound.
  • With the economy still weak, central banks turned to unconventional tools.
  • Main one: quantitative easing (QE).
Mission 7

What QE is

  • QE: the central bank creates new central bank reserves (electronic money) and uses them to buy assets, mainly government bonds.
  • UK government bonds are called gilts.
  • It buys from investors such as pension funds and insurers, not straight from the government.
  • The seller's bank account is credited: broad money (bank deposits) rises; banks' reserves rise.
  • So QE increases the money supply while the policy rate is stuck near zero.
  • Say: "the central bank creates reserves to buy bonds" — not: "prints banknotes and gives them away".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes quantitative easing?
Mission 7

How QE reaches spending

  • 1. Bond prices up, yields down. Big purchases raise bond prices. A bond's yield (its return) moves the opposite way to its price.
  • 2. Lower long-term interest rates. Government bond yields are a benchmark, so mortgage and business loan rates tend to fall.
  • 3. Portfolio rebalancing. Sellers use their new money to buy other assets (shares, company bonds): their prices rise, firms can borrow more cheaply.
  • 4. Wealth effect. Higher asset prices make owners feel richer: more consumption.
  • 5. Exchange rate. Lower yields can make the currency less attractive: it tends to fall, exports become cheaper.
  • 6. Confidence. It signals the central bank will act.
  • Result: AD shifts right, raising real output and the price level towards the inflation target.
Mission 7

Worked example: price up, yield down

  • Illustrative data. A bond pays a fixed 3 a year, for ever.
  • Yield = annual payment ÷ price × 100.
  • Price 100: 3 ÷ 100 × 100 = 3%.
  • QE buying pushes the price to 120: 3 ÷ 120 × 100 = 2.5%.
  • Price 150: 3 ÷ 150 × 100 = 2%.
  • The payment is fixed, so paying more for it means a lower return.
  • Say: "higher bond prices mean lower yields" — not: "bond prices and interest rates rise together".

Mission 7

Funding for Lending

  • Funding for Lending Scheme (FLS): launched in July 2012 by the Bank of England and HM Treasury.
  • Banks could borrow cheaply from the Bank for several years.
  • The more a bank lent to households and firms, the more cheap funding it could get.
  • Aim: cut banks' funding costs so loans became cheaper and easier to get.
  • From 2014 it was focused on lending to small and medium-sized businesses.
  • Difference from QE: FLS targets bank lending directly; QE works through asset prices and yields.

Mission 7

Reversing QE

  • Quantitative tightening (QT): the central bank shrinks its bond holdings.
  • Two ways: stop reinvesting as bonds mature, or sell bonds back to investors.
  • Reserves and deposits shrink: the money supply contracts.
  • The chain runs in reverse: bond prices fall, yields rise, borrowing costs rise: AD tends to shift left.
  • UK: the MPC decided to begin reducing its stock of bonds in February 2022.
  • Central banks try to reverse QE gradually and predictably, so markets are not shocked.
Mission 7

Real world: QE in practice

  • The Bank of England began QE in March 2009, with Bank Rate at 0.5%.
  • In total it bought £895 billion of bonds: £875 billion of gilts and £20 billion of company bonds.
  • The last increase was announced in November 2020, in the pandemic.
  • Question to weigh: why did central banks buy on this scale, and what happens when they stop?
Mission 7Evaluate

Does QE work?

It depends on:

  • Confidence. If households and firms want to save and banks will not lend, new reserves sit idle: "pushing on a string".
  • Who gains. Higher bond, share and house prices help people who own them, which may widen wealth inequality. Supporters reply that QE protected jobs and incomes.
  • Timing and exit. Too much for too long risks inflation; unwinding risks bond-market disruption and losses for the central bank.
  • In the UK, those losses are covered by HM Treasury, so they fall on taxpayers.
  • Evidence. Most studies find early QE lowered long-term yields. How much it raised output and inflation is still debated.
Mission check

?A central bank bought large amounts of government bonds during a slump. Years later, with inflation above target, it starts selling some of the bonds back to investors. Other things equal, what is the most likely effect?

Buying bonds pushes long-term rates down; selling them pushes rates up. The same chain works in both directions.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.
  • Crowding out may limit it too.

Mission 2

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical + structural; recovery removes only the cyclical part.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 3

  • Discretionary policy = a deliberate decision. Automatic stabilisers = tax and transfers that change with the cycle, with no decision.
  • In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: AD falls by less, the deficit rises.
  • Fiscal rules must leave room for stabilisers to work, or they can deepen a recession.

Mission 4

  • Government borrowing raises the demand for loanable funds and the real interest rate; private investment falls.
  • Crowding out is usually partial: higher rates also draw out extra saving.
  • Short run: interest-sensitive spending falls. Long run: slower capital accumulation and growth.
  • It is smaller in a deep recession, and public capital can crowd in private investment.

Mission 5

  • A central bank is banker to the government and to the banks; it issues notes and runs monetary policy.
  • Its main goal is low, stable inflation; its tools include the policy rate, open market operations and QE.
  • It is lender of last resort, helps keep the financial system safe, and is often independent of government.

Mission 6

  • The central bank changes the policy rate to steer AD and keep inflation near its target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing costs more, asset prices lower, currency stronger. AD shifts left; inflation slows.
  • Lags, supply shocks and low confidence limit what interest rates can do.
  • Near the zero lower bound, rate cuts lose their power.

Mission 7

  • At the zero lower bound, QE creates reserves to buy bonds: bond prices rise, yields fall, borrowing gets cheaper, AD shifts right.
  • Funding for Lending tied cheap bank funding to lending.
  • Reversing QE (QT) contracts the money supply and pushes yields up.
