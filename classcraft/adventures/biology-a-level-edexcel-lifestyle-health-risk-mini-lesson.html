Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Make a guess: Why can an amoeba survive without lungs or a heart, but you can't?

Mission 1

Supply against demand

  • Every living cell needs oxygen in and carbon dioxide out, plus nutrients in and wastes out.
  • Heat is exchanged with the surroundings across the surface too.
  • Supply depends on surface area: the area materials cross.
  • Demand depends on volume: the amount of living, respiring tissue.
  • So the key number is the surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume. Written SA:V or SA/V.
Mission 1

Worked example: bigger means less surface per volume

  • Total surface area rises with size. SA:V falls.
  • Say: "a smaller surface area to volume ratio" — not: "a smaller surface area".

Mission 1

Small organisms: diffusion is enough

  • A single-celled organism, e.g. Amoeba, exchanges gases across its cell-surface membrane.
  • O2 diffuses in and CO2 diffuses out, each down its concentration gradient.
  • High SA:V, and no part of the cell is more than a few micrometres from the surface.
  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances. Ten times the distance takes about a hundred times as long.
  • Approximate times for O2 to diffuse through water: 0.01 mm (about one cell) 0.02 s · 1 mm 4 minutes · 1 cm 7 hours.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: change shape

Two shapes with the same volume, 8 mm³:

  • Cube, 2 × 2 × 2 mm: surface area 24 mm², SA:V = 3 mm⁻¹.
  • Flat sheet, 4 × 4 × 0.5 mm: surface area 40 mm², SA:V = 5 mm⁻¹.
  • Flattened or thin shapes add surface with little extra volume.
  • They also keep every cell close to the surface.
  • Examples: the flat body of a flatworm; thin, flat leaves; root hairs.
Mission 1

Bigger bodies: specialised systems

  • In a large, active animal the body surface is too small, and most cells are too far from it.
  • Specialised exchange surfaces pack a huge area inside the body: lungs, gills.
  • Ventilation brings fresh air or water to that surface.
  • Mass transport: a fluid flows in bulk, carrying substances long distances quickly. Diffusion covers only the last micrometres.
  • Mammals: blood pumped round by the heart (circulation); air moved in and out by ventilation.
  • Flowering plants: water and mineral ions in xylem; sugars in phloem (translocation).
  • Plants need transport despite a lower metabolic rate: they are large, and roots and leaves are far apart.

Mission check

?Two small sea animals have no gills, lungs or blood vessels, so every cell gets its O2 by diffusion from the body surface. They have the same volume. A flat shape gives a high SA:V and short distances, so diffusion alone can supply every cell. A bulky body needs exchange surfaces and transport.

Mission 2

Why diffusion is not enough

  • Diffusion is fast only over short distances.
  • Mass transport: the whole fluid flows, carrying everything in it. A pump (the heart) drives it.
  • It moves substances quickly over large distances, between exchange surfaces and cells.
Mission 2

Three layers, five vessels

  • Walls are built from up to three layers:
    • inner: endothelium (squamous: one flat cell thick, smooth);
    • middle: smooth muscle and elastic tissue;
    • outer: fibrous (collagen) tissue.

Mission 2

Arteries and arterioles

  • Each ventricular contraction forces a surge of blood into the arteries.
  • Elastic tissue stretches as the surge arrives, then recoils between beats.
  • Recoil keeps pressure up between beats and smooths the flow.
  • Thick muscle and elastic tissue let the wall withstand high pressure; fibrous tissue stops it overstretching.
  • Smooth endothelium: little friction.
  • Arterioles: smooth muscle contracts (vasoconstriction) or relaxes (vasodilation).
  • This sets how much blood enters each capillary bed.
  • Say: "the artery wall stretches and recoils" — not: "the artery wall contracts to pump blood".
Quick check

Mission 2

Veins: back to the heart at low pressure

  • By the veins, blood pressure is low and there is no pulse.
  • Wide lumen: little resistance to flow.
  • Thin, flexible wall: squeezed by surrounding skeletal muscles as they contract.
  • Valves: passive flaps that close if blood starts to flow backwards.
  • So every squeeze moves blood one way: towards the heart.
  • Say: "valves prevent backflow" — not: "valves push blood along".
Mission 2

Capillaries: built for exchange

  • Wall: endothelium only, one cell thick → short diffusion distance.
  • Narrow: red cells pass in single file, close to the wall.
  • Branching: huge numbers form capillary beds → huge total surface area.
  • Every cell lies close to a capillary.
  • Flow is slowest here: more time for exchange.
  • Say: "thin walls give a short diffusion distance" — not: "thin walls let blood flow faster".
Quick check
?A muscle receives blood through a network of thousands of capillaries rather than through a single vessel of the same total volume. What is the advantage of this arrangement for exchange?
Mission 2

From surge to trickle

  • Arteries: pressure surges with each beat; elastic recoil smooths the swings.
  • Arterioles: narrow and muscular: the biggest fall in pressure. The surges die away.
  • Capillaries: one aorta branches into billions of capillaries. Their total cross-sectional area is enormous.
  • The same volume of blood per second spread over far more area → flow hundreds of times slower.
  • Veins: pressure lowest; muscles and valves return the blood.
  • So: surges in, steady trickle past the cells.
Mission check

?With age, the walls of large arteries lose elastic tissue and become stiffer. Blood pressure in an arm artery is measured at its highest (as the ventricle contracts) and its lowest (between beats). What change in these readings is expected?

Stretch and recoil in elastic arteries turn the heart's surges into steady pressure; lose the elastic tissue and the surges come back.

Mission 3

What makes an exchange surface efficient

  • Large surface area: more molecules cross at once.
  • Thin: a short diffusion distance, often one cell or less.
  • Steep concentration gradient, kept up by supply on one side and removal on the other.
  • In short: large SA:V, thin surface, big difference in concentration.
Mission 3

Fick's law

  • Rate of diffusion ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness of surface.
  • Each feature on the last screen is one term in this relationship.
  • Area × 2 → rate × 2. Difference in concentration × 2 → rate × 2. Thickness × 2 → rate ÷ 2.
  • Area ÷ 2 and difference × 2 together → rate unchanged.
  • Use it to compare rates as ratios, not to find an absolute rate.
Quick check
?A model exchange surface is changed. Its surface area is doubled and its thickness is tripled. The difference in concentration does not change. By what factor does the rate of diffusion change?
Mission 3

Keeping the gradient steep

  • Diffusion evens out concentrations. If nothing moves, the gradient disappears and net diffusion stops.
  • Outside: ventilation replaces used air (lungs) or water (gills) with fresh.
  • Inside: continuous blood flow carries O2 away and brings CO2.
  • A dense network of capillaries puts blood next to every part of the surface.
  • Say: "blood flow carries oxygenated blood away, keeping the gradient" — not: "blood slows down to collect more oxygen".
Quick check
?Suppose that blood flow through the gill capillaries of an anaesthetised fish is stopped while water continues to flow over the gills, and that oxygen uptake across the gills falls almost to zero within a minute. Which explanation is best supported?

Mission 4

The medium for life

  • Water is the most abundant molecule in cells: about 70% of a typical cell's mass.
  • Most reactions of life happen in aqueous solution (dissolved in water).
  • Every one of those properties starts with the shape and charges of one small molecule.
Mission 4

A polar molecule

  • Each O–H bond is covalent: a pair of electrons is shared.
  • Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does.
  • Unequal sharing makes a polar covalent bond: oxygen is δ−, each hydrogen δ+.
  • The molecule is bent (about 104.5°), so the charges do not cancel. Water is a dipole: a δ− end and a δ+ end.
  • Overall, the molecule is neutral.
  • Say: "partial charges (δ+, δ−)" — not: "water is made of ions".
Mission 4

Hydrogen bonds

  • The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of another molecule: a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. The O–H bonds within a molecule are covalent.
  • Each hydrogen bond is weak, much weaker than a covalent bond. In liquid water they constantly break and re-form.
  • One molecule can hydrogen-bond to up to four neighbours. Millions of weak bonds add up to a strong effect.
  • Say: "hydrogen bond between water molecules" — not: "the hydrogen bond between H and O in the molecule".
Quick check
?Why is each covalent bond between oxygen and hydrogen in a water molecule polar?
Quick check
?The model shows three water molecules. The symbols δ+ and δ− show partial charges. Which statement correctly describes the interaction labelled 2 in the model?
Mission 4

Why some things dissolve and others don't

  • Ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻): water's δ− oxygens face cations; its δ+ hydrogens face anions. Each ion is surrounded by water and separates from the crystal.
  • Polar molecules (e.g. glucose, with many –OH groups): form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Charged and polar substances are hydrophilic: they dissolve.
  • Non-polar molecules (e.g. oils): no charges to attract water. Water molecules stay hydrogen-bonded to each other and exclude them. They are hydrophobic.
  • Dissolving is physical: molecules stay whole; no covalent bonds break.
  • Say: "ions are attracted to the partial charges of water" — not: "ions form hydrogen bonds with water".
Mission 4

The solvent at work

  • Metabolism: reactants dissolved in the cytoplasm can move and collide. Most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution.
  • Transport: dissolved substances are carried in a moving stream of water.
  • Animals: blood plasma carries glucose, amino acids, ions and urea.
  • Plants: xylem sap carries mineral ions; phloem sap carries sucrose.
Quick check
?Glucose is carried dissolved in blood plasma and in phloem sap, and it is broken down by enzymes in the aqueous cytoplasm. Which property of glucose makes this possible?
Mission check

?In blood plasma, glucose travels freely dissolved. Cholesterol does not: it travels inside lipoprotein particles whose outer surface carries polar and charged groups. Why does cholesterol need this carrier?

Like oil in the hook, cholesterol is non-polar, so water holds onto itself instead. Like salt, anything charged or polar is pulled into solution.

Make a guess: Your heart has no switches, yet its valves open and close at exactly the right moments. How?

Mission 5

Four chambers, two pumps

  • Right side: receives deoxygenated blood from the body; pumps it to the lungs.
  • Left side: receives oxygenated blood from the lungs; pumps it to the body.
  • The septum keeps the two sides apart.
  • Route: vena cava → right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary artery → lungs → pulmonary vein → left atrium → left ventricle → aorta.
  • Atrioventricular (AV) valves lie between each atrium and ventricle.
  • Semilunar valves lie at the base of the aorta and the pulmonary artery.
  • Coronary arteries supply the heart muscle itself.
  • In diagrams the heart faces you: its right side is on your left.
Mission 5

Built to deliver pressure

  • Cardiac muscle: contracts rhythmically, without tiring, for a lifetime.
  • Atria: thin walls. They pump only into the ventricles below.
  • Ventricles: thick walls. They pump out into the arteries.
  • Left ventricle wall about three times thicker than the right: it must drive blood round the whole body.
  • Both ventricles pump the same volume per beat.
  • Valves stop backflow. Septum stops mixing. Coronary vessels bring O2 to the hard-working muscle.
Quick check
?The wall of the left ventricle is about three times thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which statement correctly explains this difference?
Mission 5

Valves move with pressure

  • A valve has no muscle of its own and no nerve. Blood pressure moves it.
  • Higher pressure behind a valve pushes it open.
  • Higher pressure in front pushes it shut.
  • AV valve: open when atrial pressure > ventricular; shut when ventricular > atrial.
  • Semilunar valve: open when ventricular pressure > arterial; shut when arterial > ventricular.
  • Say: "the valve shuts because pressure in the ventricle exceeds that in the atrium" — not: "muscles close the valve".
Mission 5

The cardiac cycle

  • One cycle at 75 beats per minute lasts 60 ÷ 75 = 0.8 s.
  • (1) Atrial systole (about 0.1 s): atria contract; ventricles relaxed; AV valves open. Blood tops up the ventricles.
  • (2) Ventricular systole (about 0.3 s): ventricles contract. Their pressure rises:
    • above atrial pressure → AV valves shut;
    • above arterial pressure → semilunar valves open; blood is forced out.
  • (3) Diastole (about 0.4 s): all chambers relax. Ventricular pressure falls:
    • below arterial pressure → semilunar valves shut;
    • below atrial pressure → AV valves open; blood flows in from the veins.
  • Edexcel names: atrial systole, ventricular systole, cardiac diastole.
Quick check
?The sinoatrial node fires. Which sequence of events then follows in the left side of the heart?
Mission 5

Reading the pressure graph

  • Three lines: left atrium, left ventricle, aorta. Find where two lines cross.
  • A: ventricle rises above atrium → AV valve shuts.
  • B: ventricle rises above aorta → semilunar valve opens. Ejection starts.
  • C: ventricle falls below aorta → semilunar valve shuts. A small notch in the aortic trace.
  • D: ventricle falls below atrium → AV valve opens. Filling starts.
  • A → B and C → D: both valves shut; the ventricle's volume stays the same.
  • Aortic pressure never falls to zero: elastic recoil (see Mission 2).
  • Ventricular volume falls from B to C and rises from D onwards.

Mission 5

Stroke volume and cardiac output

  • Stroke volume: volume pumped by one ventricle per beat.
  • Left ventricle: about 120 cm³ when full, about 50 cm³ after ejection → stroke volume ≈ 70 cm³.
  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate: the volume pumped per minute.
  • Example: 70 cm³ × 72 beats min⁻¹ = 5040 cm³ min⁻¹ ≈ 5.0 dm³ min⁻¹.
  • To find stroke volume: stroke volume = cardiac output ÷ heart rate.

Mission 5Practical

Dissecting a heart

  • Investigating heart structure practically, e.g. by dissection.
  • Outside: find the ventral side (coronary artery runs diagonally); identify atria, ventricles, aorta, pulmonary artery, venae cavae.
  • Cut open each ventricle. Compare wall thicknesses: measure with a ruler.
  • Find AV valves, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles, semilunar valves (pockets at the base of the aorta), septum.
  • Push a blunt probe into each vessel to see which chamber it joins.
  • Safety: sharp scalpel cutting away from you, on a board; gloves; wash hands; dispose of tissue as instructed.
Mission check

?Pressures in the right side of a heart, early in ventricular systole: right atrium 0.4 kPa; right ventricle 1.0 kPa and rising; pulmonary artery 1.3 kPa. Which statement is correct?

No switches needed: every valve opens when the pressure behind it is higher, and shuts when the pressure in front is higher.

Mission 6

Rules for a scientific drawing

  • Sharp pencil. Clear, continuous single lines. No shading or colouring.
  • Draw only what you see, in the right proportions. Use most of the space.
  • Label lines: ruled, no arrowheads, touching the structure, never crossing.
  • Labels outside the drawing, aligned neatly.
  • Annotation = label + a short note: what the structure does, or a feature you can see.
  • Title: specimen, section (TS or LS), magnification or a scale bar.
Mission 6

Plan diagram or cell drawing?

  • Plan diagram (low power): outlines of tissue regions only. No individual cells.
  • Draw each tissue layer in the right proportion of the whole.
  • High-power drawing: a few representative cells, with the detail you see (walls, nuclei, chloroplasts).
  • Leaf TS, top to bottom: upper epidermis (cuticle) → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (air spaces) → lower epidermis (stomata).
  • In each vein: xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower.

Mission 6

Stem and root in section

  • Stem TS: epidermis → cortex → a ring of vascular bundles → pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: phloem on the outside, xylem on the inside.
  • A cap of sclerenchyma fibres sits outside the phloem: support.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 6Practical

Sections of a stem

  • Core practical 6: identify sclerenchyma fibres, phloem sieve tubes and xylem vessels, and their positions, in stems.
  • Cut thin transverse sections with a sharp blade, cutting away from you. Keep them in water.
  • Stain, e.g. toluidine blue: lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) turn blue-green; other walls purple.
  • Low power: plan diagram. High power: draw a few cells of each type.
  • Xylem vessels: wide, empty, lignified, inner side. Sclerenchyma: narrow lumens, thick walls, outside the phloem. Phloem: small, thin-walled cells between.
Mission 6

Worked example: magnification of a drawing

  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size, both in the same units.
  • A cell spans 12 eyepiece graticule divisions. At this objective, 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Step 1: actual length = 12 × 2.5 = 30 µm.
  • Step 2: the drawing is 60 mm long = 60 000 µm.
  • Step 3: magnification = 60 000 ÷ 30 = × 2000.
  • Scale bar instead: a 10 µm bar is drawn 10 × 2000 = 20 000 µm = 20 mm long.
  • Say: "draw a 10 µm scale bar at its magnified length" — not: "draw it 10 mm long".
Mission check

?A student is asked for a plan diagram of a transverse section of an artery seen at low power. The wall has three layers: a thin inner layer, a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic tissue, and a thinner outer layer. Which drawing meets the brief?

Leave out the cells, keep the proportions, and label with care: that is what turns a sketch into evidence.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • As size increases, SA:V falls; demand (volume) outgrows supply (surface).
  • Diffusion alone is enough only over short distances; large organisms need flattened shapes, specialised exchange surfaces and ventilation.
  • Mass transport (circulation, xylem, phloem) carries substances over long distances.

Mission 2

  • Arteries stretch and recoil; arterioles control flow and drop pressure; capillaries' huge total area slows flow for exchange; veins return blood with valves and muscle squeeze.

Mission 3

  • Efficient exchange surfaces: large area, thin, steep gradient kept up by ventilation and blood flow.
  • Fick's law: rate ∝ (surface area × difference in concentration) ÷ thickness.

Mission 4

  • Unequal sharing of electrons makes water polar (δ− O, δ+ H); hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
  • Charged and polar substances dissolve (hydrophilic); non-polar ones are excluded (hydrophobic). That makes water the medium for metabolism and transport.

Mission 5

  • Valves open and shut only because of pressure differences; atrial systole, then ventricular systole, then diastole.
  • Cardiac output = stroke volume × heart rate.

Mission 6

  • Scientific drawings: clear continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, true proportions; annotations add function or features.
  • Plan (block) diagrams show tissue regions, not cells: leaf, stem with a ring of bundles (xylem inside, phloem outside).
  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size; scale bars are drawn at their magnified length.
