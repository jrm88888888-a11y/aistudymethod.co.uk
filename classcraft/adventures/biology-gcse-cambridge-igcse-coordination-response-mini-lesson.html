Coordination · nervous system

The nervous system & reflex arc

The nervous system uses fast electrical impulses along neurones. It has the central nervous system (CNS) — the brain and spinal cord — and the peripheral nerves. A reflex is a fast, automatic response that protects the body. Its pathway is the reflex arc:

Synapse: where two neurones meet there is a tiny gap called a synapse. The impulse crosses it using a chemical (neurotransmitter), which then triggers a new impulse in the next neurone.

Coordination · the eye

The eye

The eye is a sense organ that detects light. Learn these key parts and jobs:

  • Cornea — transparent front; does most of the focusing (refraction).
  • Iris — controls the size of the pupil, so it controls how much light enters.
  • Lens — fine-focuses light onto the retina (changes shape for near/far — accommodation).
  • Retina — contains light receptors that make impulses.
  • Optic nerve — carries impulses to the brain.

Pupil reflex: in bright light the circular iris muscles contract and the pupil gets smaller, protecting the retina. In dim light the radial muscles contract and the pupil gets larger.

Coordination · hormones

Hormones & endocrine glands

A hormone is a chemical made by an endocrine gland, carried in the blood to target organs. Hormonal responses are usually slower but longer-lasting than nervous ones.

  • Adrenal glands → adrenaline — the "fight or flight" hormone (raises heart rate, breathing rate and blood glucose).
  • Pancreas → insulin and glucagon — control blood glucose.
  • Ovaries → oestrogen; testes → testosterone — control sexual development.

Key term · homeostasis

Homeostasis — glucose & temperature

Homeostasis is keeping a constant internal environment. It works by negative feedback: if a level rises too high, the body brings it down, and vice versa.

Blood glucose is controlled by the pancreas:

  • Glucose too high → pancreas releases insulin → liver converts glucose to glycogen → glucose falls.
  • Glucose too low → pancreas releases glucagon → liver converts glycogen back to glucose → glucose rises.

Temperature is controlled by the skin (about 37 °C):

  • Too hot → sweating (evaporation cools) and vasodilation (more blood near the skin loses heat).
  • Too cold → shivering (respiration releases heat) and vasoconstriction (less blood near the skin keeps heat in).

Watch out: in diabetes, the body cannot control blood glucose well — Type 1 is treated with insulin injections. Insulin lowers glucose; glucagon raises it.

Coordination · plant responses

Tropisms & auxin

Plants respond to stimuli by growing — this is a tropism. The response is controlled by a plant hormone called auxin.

  • Phototropism — growth in response to light. Shoots grow towards light (positive phototropism).
  • Gravitropism (geotropism) — growth in response to gravity. Roots grow downwards (positive gravitropism); shoots grow upwards.

How auxin works in a shoot: auxin gathers on the shaded side of the shoot. It makes those cells grow (elongate) more, so the shoot bends towards the light.

The big ideas to know

Nervous system: CNS (brain + spinal cord) + peripheral nerves; fast electrical impulses

Reflex arc: receptor → sensory → relay → motor → effector (synapses between)

Eye: cornea + lens focus light on the retina; iris controls the pupil (pupil reflex)

Hormones: chemicals from endocrine glands in the blood; slower, longer-lasting

Homeostasis: glucose (insulin lowers, glucagon raises); temperature (sweat/vasodilation vs shiver/vasoconstriction)

Tropisms: phototropism & gravitropism; auxin gathers on the shaded side → shoot bends to light
