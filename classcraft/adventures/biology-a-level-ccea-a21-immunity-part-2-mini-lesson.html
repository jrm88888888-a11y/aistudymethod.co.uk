Mission 1

Mission 1: If the body attacks only what is foreign, why can it turn on a new kidney, or on an unborn baby?

Make a guess: If the body attacks only what is foreign, why can it turn on a new kidney, or on an unborn baby?

Mission 1

The rhesus antigen

  • Many people's red blood cells also carry the rhesus antigen (antigen D): rhesus positive (Rh+).
  • Cells without it: rhesus negative (Rh−).
  • Anti-D antibodies do not occur naturally, unlike anti-A and anti-B.
  • An Rh− person makes anti-D only after exposure to Rh+ red cells, e.g. in a transfusion or a pregnancy.
  • First exposure: anti-D and memory cells are made. A second exposure brings a fast, large response.
Mission 1

Rhesus in pregnancy

  • (1) An Rh− mother carries an Rh+ baby (antigen D inherited from the father).
  • (2) At birth, some of the baby's red cells enter the mother's blood. She makes anti-D and memory cells. This baby is born before much antibody is made: usually unaffected.
  • (3) Her next Rh+ baby: memory cells give a fast secondary response. Anti-D crosses the placenta and agglutinates and destroys the baby's red cells.
  • For that child: haemolytic disease of the newborn: anaemia, jaundice; severe cases can be fatal.
  • Prevention: the Rh− mother is given anti-D (passive immunity). It removes the baby's Rh+ cells from her blood before she makes her own antibodies or memory cells.
Quick check
?Which baby is most at risk of having its red blood cells destroyed by its mother's anti-D antibodies?
Mission 1

Why transplants are rejected

  • Most body cells carry membrane proteins that differ between people (mainly HLA proteins).
  • A transplant exposes the recipient's immune system to foreign antigens.
  • T cells: killer T cells attack and destroy donor cells directly; helper T cells activate B cells.
  • B cells: plasma cells make antibodies against donor antigens.
  • The tissue is damaged and destroyed: rejection.
  • Tissue matching: choose a donor with antigens as similar as possible, often a close relative.
Quick check
?A kidney from an unrelated donor starts to fail some weeks after a transplant. Which statement explains this?
Mission 1

Immunosuppression

  • Aim: switch off the recipient's specific B-cell and T-cell responses.
  • X-ray irradiation, or drugs that inhibit DNA replication.
  • Lymphocytes cannot divide: no clonal expansion.
  • So foreign antigens on the transplant produce no effective immune response.
  • Cost: the person is more susceptible to infection.
  • So doctors balance the risk of rejection against the risk of infection.

Mission check

?In 1954, a kidney was transplanted from one identical twin to the other. No immunosuppressive drugs were used, and the kidney worked for years. Why was it not rejected?

The body attacks what it reads as foreign. A twin's kidney is not foreign; an Rh+ baby's cells are, to a sensitised Rh− mother.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Make a guess: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Mission 2

When neither allele is dominant

  • Sometimes the heterozygote differs from both homozygotes.
  • Codominance: both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. It shows both phenotypes.
  • Example: roan cattle. Red × white gives roan: a coat of separate red hairs and white hairs.
  • Notation: one letter for the gene, superscripts for alleles: Cᴿ and Cᵂ. No lower case, as neither is recessive.
  • The alleles stay separate in the heterozygote. They do not blend.
  • Say: "both alleles are expressed" — not: "the alleles mix".
Mission 2

Worked cross: two heterozygotes

  • Each heterozygote (CᴿCᵂ) makes Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes in equal numbers.
  • Offspring: 1 CᴿCᴿ : 2 CᴿCᵂ : 1 CᵂCᵂ.
  • Each genotype has its own phenotype. So the phenotype ratio is also 1 : 2 : 1, not 3 : 1.
  • Roan × roan: 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white.
  • Flowers can follow the same pattern: red × white gives pink heterozygotes, which pass on Cᴿ or Cᵂ unchanged, so pink × pink gives 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • So the card's pink plants are CᴿCᵂ. Red and white return in the next generation.

Mission 2

Multiple alleles: the ABO gene

  • A gene can have many alleles in a population. A diploid individual still carries only two.
  • ABO blood groups: one gene, three alleles: Iᴬ, Iᴮ and i.
  • Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant. Both are dominant to i.
  • Six genotypes, four phenotypes:
    • IᴬIᴬ or Iᴬi → group A · IᴮIᴮ or Iᴮi → group B
    • IᴬIᴮ → group AB · ii → group O
  • Say: "Iᴬ and Iᴮ are codominant" — not: "Iᴬ is dominant to Iᴮ".
Mission 2

Worked cross: AB × O

  • Parents: group AB (IᴬIᴮ) × group O (ii).
  • Gametes: Iᴬ or Iᴮ · all i.
  • Offspring: Iᴬi (group A) and Iᴮi (group B), 1 : 1.
  • No AB and no O child: neither parent's blood group appears.
  • A child's group comes from two alleles, one from each parent, not from one parent's group.
Quick check
?A woman with blood group A whose genotype is Iᴬi has children with a man of blood group B whose genotype is Iᴮi. Which blood groups are possible in their children, and with what probabilities?
Mission 2

What the ABO alleles make

  • Iᴬ and Iᴮ code for enzymes that add different sugars to a carbohydrate chain on red cell membranes.
  • Iᴬ adds the A antigen; Iᴮ adds the B antigen. i makes no working enzyme, so neither is added.
  • Plasma carries antibodies against the A or B antigen a person lacks: group A has anti-B; group B has anti-A.
  • Transfuse group B cells into a group A person: anti-B binds them and they agglutinate (clump).
Mission 2

Lethal allele combinations

  • Some genotypes kill the embryo. The offspring ratio then shifts.
  • Yellow mice: allele Aʸ gives yellow fur and is dominant to a (non-yellow).
  • AʸAʸ embryos die before birth.
  • Yellow × yellow (Aʸa × Aʸa): expected 1 AʸAʸ : 2 Aʸa : 1 aa.
  • AʸAʸ never appear, so the litters are 2 yellow : 1 non-yellow.
  • Clue: a 2 : 1 ratio from heterozygote × heterozygote, and every yellow mouse is heterozygous.
Quick check
?In Manx cats, the tailless allele Mᴸ is dominant to the allele M for a normal tail. MᴸMᴸ embryos die early in development. Two tailless Manx cats are mated many times. What ratio is expected among their kittens?
Mission check

?In Shorthorn cattle, the alleles Cᴿ (red coat) and Cᵂ (white coat) are codominant; CᴿCᵂ cattle are roan. A farmer mates roan cows with a white bull. What ratio of calves is expected?

The red and white alleles pass through roan animals unchanged; that is why red and white reappear after a generation of mixed coats.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 3

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 3

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 3

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check
?In a weed population of a hypothetical plant species, an allele gives resistance to herbicide H. Where the herbicide is not used, resistant plants produce fewer seeds than susceptible plants. After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission 3

Finding new antibiotics

  • Few new classes of antibiotic since the late 1980s. Resistance keeps rising.
  • Natural environments: soil microbes make antibiotics to compete.
  • E.g. teixobactin: from a soil bacterium that could not be grown until a new culture device was used.
  • Value to society and the economy: resistant infections mean longer hospital stays, costlier care and riskier surgery.
  • Careful use is still needed: a new antibiotic will select for resistance too.
Quick check
?Researchers screened a chemical library of thousands of compounds using a computer model trained to predict antibacterial activity. They identified a compound, structurally unlike any existing antibiotic, that killed several multiresistant strains of bacteria in the laboratory. Which statement is the best evaluation of this work?
Mission 3Practical

Antibiotics on a bacterial lawn

  • Practical: different antibiotics or e-strips on bacteria; a streak plate; antimicrobial properties of plants.
  • Spread a bacterial culture evenly over agar: a lawn.
  • Add paper discs soaked in different antibiotics. Control: a disc soaked in sterile water.
  • Plant extracts: discs soaked in an extract (e.g. garlic); control disc soaked in the solvent only.
  • Tape the lid (not sealed all round). Incubate upside down at 25 °C for 24–48 h.
  • Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition). Larger zone, more effective antibiotic, if concentration and disc size are the same (and the drugs diffuse through agar alike).
  • E-strip: a gradient of concentration along a strip. The minimum inhibitory concentration is read where the clear zone meets the strip.
Mission 3

Aseptic technique

  • Aim: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture, and the culture away from people.
  • Disinfect the bench. Work beside a lit Bunsen burner: the updraft carries microbes away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; let it cool.
  • Flame the neck of the culture bottle when opening and closing it.
  • Lift the Petri dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • Sterile pipettes, spreaders and forceps; dispose of cultures by autoclaving.
  • Streak plate: streak the sample in sections, flaming the loop between: cells are diluted to single colonies.

Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Make a guess: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Mission 4

What bacteria need

  • Nutrients: a carbon source (e.g. glucose) for respiration and for building molecules.
  • A nitrogen source (e.g. amino acids, nitrate) for proteins and nucleic acids; plus minerals.
  • Water: for reactions and transport into the cell.
  • A suitable temperature: growth depends on enzymes, each with an optimum.
  • A suitable pH: most bacteria grow best near neutral.
Mission 4

Agar or broth

  • Nutrient broth: liquid medium. Growth shows as cloudiness (turbidity).
  • Nutrient agar: broth set with agar, a seaweed polysaccharide most bacteria cannot digest.
  • Agar melts near 85 °C and sets below about 40 °C: poured hot, solid at incubation temperature.
  • On agar, one cell (or a small clump) grows into a visible colony.
  • Spread culture evenly over the agar: colonies merge into a lawn.
  • Streak plate: streak in sections, flaming the loop between them. Cells thin out to single colonies: a pure culture.
Mission 4

Aseptic technique

  • Two aims: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture; keep the culture away from people.
  • Before: wash hands; disinfect the bench; sterilise media and glassware in an autoclave (121 °C, 15 min).
  • During: work beside a lit Bunsen burner: its updraught carries airborne microbes up and away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; cool it before touching the culture.
  • Flame the neck of each bottle as you open and close it. Lift the dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • After: secure the lid with two short strips of tape, not all round: air can still enter, which discourages anaerobic bacteria (including many pathogens).
  • Label the base. Incubate upside down (condensation cannot drip onto the agar) at 25 °C. School labs avoid 30–42 °C: temperatures near body heat (37 °C) favour human pathogens.
  • Never open a plate after incubation. Autoclave cultures before disposal.
Mission 4

Serial dilution

  • Transfer 1.0 cm³ into 9.0 cm³ of sterile water; mix. Total 10 cm³: a 1 in 10 dilution (10⁻¹).
  • Repeat from the new tube: 10⁻², 10⁻³, 10⁻⁴ …
  • Dilution factor = total volume ÷ volume transferred = 10 ÷ 1 = 10.
  • After k tenfold steps, concentration = original × 10⁻ᵏ. Factors multiply; they never add.
  • A fresh sterile pipette for each transfer: no carry-over between tubes.
  • Uses here: a range of antimicrobial concentrations.
Quick check
?A student adds 1.0 cm³ of a bacterial culture to 9.0 cm³ of sterile water and mixes. She transfers 1.0 cm³ of this into a second 9.0 cm³ of sterile water, and repeats until she has made four dilutions. What fraction of the original concentration of bacteria is in the fourth dilution?
Mission 4Practical

Testing antimicrobials

  • Assessed practical tasks: antibiotics or e-strips on bacteria; antimicrobial properties of plants.
  • Spread a known volume of broth culture over agar with a sterile spreader: a lawn.
  • Add sterile paper discs soaked in each antibiotic or plant extract, or fill wells cut with a sterile cork borer.
  • Extract in ethanol? Let the solvent evaporate first: ethanol itself kills bacteria.
  • Control: a disc or well with solvent only.
  • Incubate. Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition) through the base: two diameters at right angles, take the mean.
  • E-strip: an antibiotic concentration gradient along a strip. The minimum inhibitory concentration is read where the edge of the clear zone meets the strip.
Quick check
?A student soaks paper discs in garlic, ginger and mint extracts, all made in ethanol, and places them on a lawn of bacteria. Another disc is soaked in ethanol only. Which of these is a controlled variable in her investigation?
Mission 4

Worked example: clear area around a well

  • Clear zone diameter 21 mm; well diameter 6 mm (illustrative data).
  • Step 1: radii. Zone 21 ÷ 2 = 10.5 mm. Well 6 ÷ 2 = 3.0 mm.
  • Step 2: zone area = π × 10.5² = 346 mm².
  • Step 3: well area = π × 3.0² = 28.3 mm².
  • Step 4: clear agar = 346 − 28.3 = 318 mm².
  • Subtract areas, never diameters. Halve before squaring.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Make a guess: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Mission 5

Counting what you cannot see

  • Too many cells to count. So count a tiny, known sample, then scale up.
  • Viable count: counts living cells only. Each colony is taken to come from one living cell.
  • Total count (haemocytometer): counts every cell in a known volume, living or dead.
Mission 5Practical

Yeast growth with a haemocytometer

  • Practical / assessed task: investigate yeast population growth using a haemocytometer.
  • Culture medium: water, a sugar (e.g. glucose) as respiratory substrate, a nitrogen source (e.g. yeast extract), minerals.
  • Haemocytometer: a slide with an etched grid. With the coverslip on, each square holds a known volume.
  • Common grid: squares 0.2 mm × 0.2 mm, liquid 0.1 mm deep. Always use the dimensions given.
  • Count cells touching the top and left lines; ignore those touching the bottom and right.
  • Count several squares; take the mean.
  • Add methylene blue: dead cells stain blue; living cells decolourise it. Count unstained cells for living yeast.
Mission 5

Worked example: cell density

  • Counts in five 0.2 mm squares: 23, 19, 26, 21, 24 (illustrative data). Mean = 22.6.
  • Step 1: volume of one square = 0.2 × 0.2 × 0.1 = 0.004 mm³.
  • Step 2: density = 22.6 ÷ 0.004 = 5650 cells per mm³.
  • Step 3: 1 cm³ = 1000 mm³, so × 1000 = 5.65 × 10⁶ cells per cm³.
  • Step 4: the sample was mixed 1 : 1 with stain, so × 2 = 1.13 × 10⁷ cells per cm³.
Quick check
?On a haemocytometer, the smallest squares are 0.05 mm × 0.05 mm and the liquid is 0.1 mm deep. A student counts a mean of 4.0 yeast cells per smallest square in an undiluted sample. What is the cell density?
Mission 5

Growth on a log scale

  • Microbe numbers span many orders of magnitude: 10² to 10⁹ per cm³.
  • On an ordinary axis, the early points squash flat against zero.
  • On a log10 scale, each equal step is × 10. log10(10⁶) = 6.
  • Closed culture phases: lag, log (exponential), stationary, death (decline).
  • Exponential growth plots as a straight line on a log scale.
  • Say: "exponential: multiplied by the same factor each interval" — not: "the same number added each interval".
Mission 5

Worked example: doublings and logs

  • N = N0 × 2ⁿ. N0 = starting number; n = number of generations.
  • N0 = 2.0 × 10³ cells per cm³; one generation every 25 min; 5 h of log phase.
  • Step 1: n = 300 min ÷ 25 min = 12.
  • Step 2: 2¹² = 4096 (calculator: 2 xʸ 12).
  • Step 3: N = 2.0 × 10³ × 4096 = 8.2 × 10⁶ cells per cm³.
  • On a log10 axis: 3.30 → 6.91. Each generation adds log102 = 0.301; 12 × 0.301 = 3.61. ✓
Quick check
?A student records the size of a duckweed population every two days and plots it on a logarithmic vertical axis against time on a non-logarithmic horizontal axis. Which observation would show that growth was exponential?
Mission check

?A bacterial culture is in its log phase. Plotted on a log10 scale, its count rises in a straight line from 10³ to 10⁶ cells per cm³ over 3 hours. Approximately how long does one generation take?

Huge numbers become manageable twice over: count a tiny sample and scale it up, then plot the logs so that doublings line up.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Anti-D is made only after exposure to Rh+ cells; in a later Rh+ pregnancy it crosses the placenta and destroys the baby's red cells.
  • Transplants are rejected by T cells and B cells responding to foreign antigens; immunosuppression (X-rays, drugs that stop DNA replication) prevents this but raises infection risk.

Mission 2

  • Codominant alleles are both expressed in the heterozygote; heterozygote × heterozygote gives 1 : 2 : 1.
  • ABO: three alleles, Iᴬ and Iᴮ codominant, both dominant to i; each person carries two.

Mission 3

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.

Mission 4

  • Bacteria need nutrients, water, a suitable temperature and pH; aseptic technique keeps contaminants out and the culture contained.
  • Serial dilution multiplies the dilution factor at each step: four tenfold steps give 10⁻⁴.
  • Antimicrobial tests compare clear zones against a solvent-only control; clear area = zone area − well (or disc) area.

Mission 5

  • Haemocytometer density = mean count per square ÷ volume of one square; × 1000 for per cm³; × any dilution.
  • On a log10 scale, exponential growth is a straight line; each generation adds 0.301 (N = N0 × 2ⁿ).
