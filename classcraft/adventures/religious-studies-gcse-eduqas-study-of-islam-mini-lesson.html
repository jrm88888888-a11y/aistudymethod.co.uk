Mission 1

Mission 1: Sunni Muslims list six core beliefs and Shi'a Muslims five roots. Do they believe different things?

Make a guess: Sunni Muslims list six core beliefs and Shi'a Muslims five roots. Do they believe different things?

Mission 1

Iman: faith in what?

  • Iman means faith: the beliefs that make a Muslim a believer.
  • Islam (submission) is lived out in practice, such as the Five Pillars; iman is what a Muslim holds in the heart and mind.
  • Sunni Muslims (around 87–90% of Muslims) summarise iman as the six articles of faith.
  • Shi'a Muslims summarise the foundations as the five Usul ad-Din: "roots" or "principles of religion".
  • Both lists come from the Qur'an and the teaching of the Prophet Muhammad.
Mission 1Read the source

Believe in Allah, His angels, His books…

"Any who denieth Allah, His angels, His Books, His Messengers, and the Day of Judgment, hath gone far, far astray." (Qur'an 4:136, Yusuf Ali)

  • A fuller report in Sahih Muslim's Book of Faith, the Hadith of Jibril (1:1), adds faith in the divine decree, good and bad (paraphrase).

Q1 Which five beliefs does Qur'an 4:136 name?

Show answer

Allah, angels, books, messengers, the Day of Judgement.

Q2 Which sixth article comes from the Hadith of Jibril?

Show answer

Al-Qadr: God's decree, or predestination.

Mission 1

The six articles of faith (Sunni)

1. Tawhid: Allah is one; nothing is like Him (see Mission 2).

2. Mala'ikah (CIE: Malaʾikah): angels, Allah's messengers and servants (see Mission 6).

3. Kutub: the holy books: Scrolls, Torah, Psalms, Gospel and, finally, the Qur'an.

4. Risalah: prophethood; Allah sends messengers, ending with Muhammad (see Mission 3).

5. Akhirah: the Day of Judgement and life after death.

6. Al-Qadr (CIE: Qadar): predestination; Allah knows and decrees all things.

Mission 1

The five roots of Usul ad-Din (Shi'a)

1. Tawhid (OCR: Al-Tawhid): the oneness of Allah.

2. 'Adl (OCR: Al-Adl; also Adalat): divine justice.

3. Nubuwwah (OCR: Al-Nubuwwah; CIE: Risalah): prophethood.

4. Imamah (OCR: Al-Imamah): the Imams, the Prophet's chosen successors.

5. Mi'ad (OCR: Al-Ma'ad): the Day of Judgement and the Resurrection.

  • They are roots: the rest of religious practice (the "branches", furu' ad-Din, such as prayer and fasting) grows from them.
  • Many Shi'a scholars teach that each believer should accept the roots through their own understanding, not just by copying others.
Mission 1

What makes the roots Shi'a: justice and Imamah

  • 'Adl / Adalat: Allah is perfectly just and never does wrong; good and evil are real, and reason can recognise them. "Whoever works righteousness benefits his own soul; whoever works evil, it is against his own soul: nor is thy Lord ever unjust (in the least) to His Servants." (Qur'an 41:46, Yusuf Ali)
  • So humans are free and responsible, and Allah's judgement is fair.
  • Imamah: after the Prophet, Allah appointed Imams from his family (the Ahl al-Bayt), beginning with Ali, to guide the community without error.
  • Sunni view: leaders (caliphs) were chosen by the community; leadership is important, but not an article of faith.
  • More on the Imams.
Mission 1Diversity

Side by side

  • Shared: Tawhid; prophethood; the Day of Judgement and resurrection.
  • Shi'a also believe in angels, the holy books and Allah's decree: these sit inside the roots (e.g. books and angels as part of prophethood and revelation), not as separate roots.
  • Sunni also believe Allah is just (al-'Adl is one of His names), but justice is not a separate article.
  • Distinctive to Sunni lists: al-Qadr as a named article.
  • Distinctive to Shi'a roots: 'Adl and Imamah.
  • So the two lists are mostly shared beliefs, arranged differently; the real division is over Imamah (who leads after the Prophet) and how far stress falls on justice and human freedom.

Mission 1

Living by the articles and roots

  • Tawhid: only Allah is worshipped; the Shahadah begins with it.
  • Angels and Akhirah: every word and deed is recorded and will be judged, so Muslims try to act well even when unseen.
  • Books and prophets: the Qur'an is recited daily; the Prophet's Sunnah is a model for life.
  • Al-Qadr: trust in Allah; saying insha'Allah ("if Allah wills"); patience in hardship.
  • 'Adl: a duty to work for justice, since Allah is just.
  • Imamah: Twelvers follow a senior scholar's guidance and remember the Imams (e.g. Husayn at Ashura); Isma'ilis follow the Imam of the time.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The differences between Sunni and Shi'a beliefs matter more than the similarities."

  • For: Imamah is a root of faith for Shi'a Muslims, and it shapes leadership, law and devotion. Historically, the division over leadership has run deep. 'Adl leads to different ideas of human freedom.
  • Against: Tawhid, prophethood, the Qur'an and the Day of Judgement are shared. Both believe in angels, books and Allah's decree, just listed differently. Both pray, fast, give zakah and make Hajj (Sunni Muslims count these among the Five Pillars, Shi'a Muslims among the branches, furu' ad-Din), and both belong to the ummah (the worldwide Muslim community).
  • Another view: "more important" depends on the question: for daily worship the similarities dominate; for authority and leadership the differences do.
  • Judge it by: Which beliefs shape daily life most? Are the differences about what is believed or how it is listed? How do Muslims themselves describe the relationship?
Mission check

?A classmate says: "Shi'a Muslims don't believe in angels or holy books, because those are not in the five roots." Which reply is most accurate?

The six articles and five roots are two ways of summarising one faith. The deepest difference is the Imamah.

Mission 2

Tawhid: Allah is One

  • Tawhid means "oneness". It is the first and most important Muslim belief.
  • Allah is one: no partners, no family, no equal.
  • Allah is unique: nothing in creation is like Him. "There is nothing whatever like unto Him." (Qur'an 42:11, Yusuf Ali)
  • So only Allah is to be worshipped: this is the first part of the Shahadah, "There is no god but Allah".
  • For this reason Muslims never make pictures or statues of Allah.
Mission 2Read the source

Surah 112, al-Ikhlas

"Say: He is Allah, the One and Only; Allah, the Eternal, Absolute; He begetteth not, nor is He begotten; And there is none like unto Him." (Qur'an 112:1–4, Yusuf Ali)

  • "There is no god but He: That is the witness of Allah, His angels, and those endued with knowledge, standing firm on justice." (Qur'an 3:18, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Which line rules out Allah having a son or parents?

Show answer

Verse 3: "He begetteth not, nor is He begotten".

Q2 Why do many Muslims call this surah "a third of the Qur'an"?

Show answer

It sums up Tawhid, the heart of the whole message.

Mission 2

Shirk: the opposite of Tawhid

  • Shirk means associating partners with Allah: treating anything as equal to Him or worshipping it beside Him.
  • The Qur'an calls it the worst wrong: "false worship is indeed the highest wrong-doing" (31:13, Yusuf Ali). Allah does not forgive shirk if a person dies without turning away from it (4:48).
  • Obvious shirk: worshipping idols.
  • Hidden shirk: letting money, power or showing off take Allah's place.

Mission 2

Transcendent and immanent

  • Transcendent: beyond the universe, time and human understanding. "No vision can grasp Him, but His grasp is over all vision." (6:103, Yusuf Ali)
  • Immanent: close to and active in the world and in each life. "We are nearer to him than (his) jugular vein." (50:16, Yusuf Ali)
  • "He is with you wheresoever ye may be." (57:4) "I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant." (2:186)
  • Muslims hold both: Allah is not part of creation, but is near it through His knowledge, power and care.
Mission 2

All-powerful, beneficent, merciful

  • Omnipotent: "When He decreeth a matter, He saith to it: 'Be,' and it is." (2:117, Yusuf Ali)
  • Allah, who created the heavens and the earth without tiring, "is able to give life to the dead … He has power over all things" (46:33, Yusuf Ali).
  • Beneficent (al-Rahman): loving and generous to all creation, believer or not: life, food, rain, guidance.
  • Merciful (al-Rahim): forgiving those who turn back to Him. "My mercy extendeth to all things." (7:156)
  • 113 of the 114 surahs begin: "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful."
Mission 2

Fair and just; Adalat

  • Just (al-'Adl): Allah treats every person fairly. "Allah is never unjust in the least degree." (4:40)
  • On the Day of Judgement even an atom's weight of good or evil counts (21:47, 99:7–8).
  • Adalat (Shi'a Islam): divine justice is one of the five roots (see Mission 1). Allah always does what is truly just; good and evil are real and reason can recognise them; so humans have free will and are rightly judged.
  • Many Sunni (Ash'ari) scholars put it differently: whatever Allah wills is just, because He wills it; humans cannot judge Allah by their standards.
  • Both agree: Allah is never unjust, and Muslims must act justly too.
Quick check
?"We are nearer to him than (his) jugular vein." (Qur'an 50:16) Which quality of Allah does this verse show most clearly?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Allah cannot be both transcendent and immanent."

  • For: something beyond the universe seems unable to be inside it. If Allah is beyond understanding (6:103), how can people feel close to Him? Some Muslims stress one side: Sufis Allah's closeness, others His otherness.
  • Against: the Qur'an affirms both (42:11; 50:16). Allah is close through knowledge, power and care, not by being part of creation.
  • Another view: many Sunni scholars accept what the Qur'an says "without asking how" (bila kayf): human words cannot fully describe Allah.
  • Judge it by: Does "near" mean near in space? Must a believer understand how both are true? What would prayer be like if Allah were only one of these?
Mission check

?Amira, a Muslim nurse on a night shift, says she feels Allah is with her on the ward. Yet she would never draw or picture Allah in any way. Which pair of beliefs best explains both?

Tawhid holds Allah's nearness and Allah's otherness together: close enough to hear a whisper, beyond any picture.

Mission 3

Risalah: why Allah sends messengers

  • Risalah means "message": Allah's way of communicating with humans through chosen people.
  • A nabi is a prophet; a rasul is a messenger, often given a book or law. Every rasul is also a nabi.
  • Why? Humans have free will and need guidance: prophets bring "good news as well as warning" (4:165, Yusuf Ali).
  • Allah sent a messenger to every nation (16:36). The Qur'an names 25; a well-known tradition speaks of many thousands.
  • Muslims believe all brought the same core message: Tawhid, worship of the one God.
  • Prophets are human, chosen and protected by Allah; Muslims honour them with "peace be upon him", but never worship them.
Mission 3Read the source

"We make no difference between one and another"

"Say ye: 'We believe in Allah, and the revelation given to us, and to Abraham, Isma'il, Isaac, Jacob, and the Tribes, and that given to Moses and Jesus, and that given to (all) prophets from their Lord: We make no difference between one and another of them …'" (Qur'an 2:136, Yusuf Ali)

  • Eduqas: Qur'an 2:136 on the nature and importance of prophethood.

Q2 What does "we make no difference between one and another" mean for Muslims?

Mission 3

Adam, the first prophet

  • The Qur'an teaches that Allah created Adam, the first human, from clay and breathed into him "of My spirit" (15:29, Yusuf Ali).
  • He made Adam His khalifah (steward) on earth (2:30) and "taught Adam the names of all things" (2:31): the gift of reason and choice.
  • The angels bowed to Adam; Iblis refused (see Mission 6).
  • Adam and his wife disobeyed, repented, and were forgiven (2:37). Muslims do not believe in original sin: each person is born pure.
  • Adam is the first prophet: he taught his children to worship Allah alone.
  • Importance: every human shares Adam's dignity and responsibility as steward.
Mission 3

Ibrahim, father of the prophets

  • In the Qur'an, Ibrahim (Abraham) rejected the idols of his father and people (6:74); he was thrown into a fire, which Allah made "cool" (21:69).
  • He was a hanif: "true in Faith, and he joined not gods with Allah" (16:120, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muslims believe Allah tested him (2:124), including the command to sacrifice his son (37:102), which he obeyed until Allah provided a ram. Muslims remember this at Id-ul-Adha.
  • With his son Isma'il he built the Ka'bah in Makkah (2:127).
  • Ibrahim is father of Isaac and Ishma'il: through Isaac came the prophets of Israel; Muslim tradition traces many Arabs, and Muhammad's own family, to Ishma'il.
  • Allah called him "friend" (4:125). He received the Scrolls (Sahifah, 87:19).

Mission 3

Isa: a prophet, not divine

  • Muslims believe Isa (Jesus) was born of the virgin Maryam by Allah's command: "The similitude of Jesus before Allah is as that of Adam" (3:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • "We gave Jesus the son of Mary Clear (Signs) and strengthened him with the holy spirit." (2:87, Yusuf Ali)
  • He worked miracles "by Allah's leave" (3:49) and received the Injil (Gospel). The Qur'an calls him al-Masih (the Messiah) and Allah's "Word" (4:171).
  • He is not divine and not Allah's son: "Christ the son of Mary was no more than a messenger" (5:75, Yusuf Ali).
  • Most Muslims believe he was not crucified, but raised up to Allah (4:157–158), and will return before the Day of Judgement (43:61), when he will be a witness (4:159).

Mission 3

Muhammad, the Seal of the Prophets

  • "Muhammad is not the father of any of your men, but (he is) the Messenger of Allah, and the Seal of the Prophets" (33:40, Yusuf Ali).
  • Seal means last: prophethood is complete, so mainstream Muslims accept no prophet after him.
  • Muslims believe earlier books were changed over time, while the Qur'an, revealed through Muhammad, is complete and unchanged: guidance for all people until the end of time.
  • He is a mercy to all (21:107) and the model to follow: "If ye do love Allah, Follow me" (3:31, Yusuf Ali). His Sunnah guides daily life.
  • Yet he is human: "Muhammad is no more than a messenger" (3:144, Yusuf Ali).
  • Known before prophethood as al-Amin, "the trustworthy".

Mission 3Evaluate

"Muhammad is the most important prophet for Muslims."

  • For: he is the Seal; the Qur'an came through him; his Sunnah shapes daily worship and law; he is named in the Shahadah.
  • Against: "We make no difference between one and another" (2:136); all brought the same message. Ibrahim is the father of the prophets and is remembered in every Hajj and Id-ul-Adha; Adam is the first; Muhammad himself is "no more than a messenger" (3:144).
  • Another view: the Qur'an says some messengers were raised "above others" (2:253) but every one must be believed; Muhammad may be most important today because his message is the final one.
  • Judge it by: Does "important" mean rank, influence on daily life, or role in history? Does 2:136 forbid ranking, or only rejecting? Do Muslims' practices (Hajj, salah) point to more than one prophet?

For Muslims, Muhammad is the last link in one long chain: every prophet brought the same message, and through him it was sealed.

Mission 4

Adam: first human, first prophet, first khalifah

  • Muslims believe, from the Qur'an's account, that Allah formed Adam from clay and breathed into him of His spirit (15:29).
  • He taught Adam "all the names" (2:31): knowledge and reason, which set humans apart.
  • The angels bowed to Adam; Iblis, a jinn, refused out of pride (2:34, 18:50).
  • Adam and his wife ate from the forbidden tree, repented and were forgiven (7:23, 2:37). Muslims believe there is no original sin: every child is born sinless.
  • First prophet: Allah "chose him, and relented toward him, and guided him" (Qur'an 20:122, Pickthall). Adam received guidance and taught his children to worship Allah alone.
  • First khalifah: placed on Earth as Allah's khalifah: a deputy or steward who looks after it on Allah's behalf.
  • Significance: Adam shows that prophethood began with the first human, that Islam is humanity's original religion, and that repentance is always open.

Mission 5

The Seal of the Prophets

> "Muhammad is not the father of any man among you, but he is the messenger of Allah and the Seal of the Prophets; and Allah is ever Aware of all things." (Surah 33:40, Pickthall)

  • A seal closes and completes a document. Muslims understand the Seal of the Prophets (Khatam an-Nabiyyin) as:
  • Final: no prophet will come after him.
  • Complete: the Qur'an completes God's guidance; it does not need adding to.
  • Confirming: he brings the same message as all earlier prophets, especially Tawhid (one God), and so fulfils their teaching.
  • Universal: earlier prophets came to their own peoples; he was sent "unto all mankind" (Surah 34:28, Pickthall).
  • Implications: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims agree that any later claim to be a prophet is false. The Shi'a Imams are guides, not prophets: they bring no new revelation.
Mission 5Read the source

A messenger, not more

> "O Prophet! Lo! We have sent thee as a witness and a bringer of good tidings and a warner. And as a summoner unto Allah by His permission, and as a lamp that giveth light." (Surah 33:45–46, Pickthall)

  • He is fully human. Muslims honour him, but never worship him; they add "peace be upon him" after his name.

Mission 5

A role model: the Sunnah

> "Verily in the messenger of Allah ye have a good example for him who looketh unto Allah and the Last Day, and remembereth Allah much." (Surah 33:21, Pickthall)

  • His way of life is the Sunnah; reports of it are the Hadith.
  • Examples Muslims learn: he mended his own clothes and shoes, helped at home, ate simply, was gentle with children and animals, and forgave enemies at Makkah.
  • Today: Muslims copy his prayer and fasting, his greetings and manners, and his care for the poor.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Muhammad is the most important prophet in Islam."

  • For: he is the Seal: the final, complete message, sent to all people. The Qur'an is the only revealed book Muslims believe is unchanged. His Sunnah guides daily life and is named in the Shahadah.
  • Against: "We make no distinction between any of them" (2:136). All prophets taught the same Tawhid. Ibrahim is father of the prophets and is blessed in every salah; Adam was the first.
  • Another view: no distinction in faith (all are true messengers), but a difference in role: "some of whom We have caused to excel others" (2:253).
  • Judge it by: Important in what way: truth, role or example? Does "final" mean "greatest"? What does the Shahadah suggest?
Mission check

?A new group claims its founder received a fresh revelation from God in 2020, adding to the Qur'an. How would most Sunni and Shi'a Muslims respond?

For Muslims, the Seal means the message is complete. That is why Muslims look to the Qur'an and the Prophet's example, not to new prophets.

Mission 6

What angels are

  • Belief in angels (Mala'ikah; Edexcel: Malaikah) is an article of faith (see Mission 1). The Arabic word means "messengers".
  • A hadith teaches that angels were created from light (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase); humans from clay; the jinn from smokeless fire.
  • They are usually unseen, but can take human form: an angel "appeared before her as a man in all respects" to Maryam (19:17, Yusuf Ali).
  • They are not divine: they are servants who worship Allah and are never worshipped themselves.
Mission 6

Free will: angels, jinn and humans

  • Muslims believe angels have no free will: they always obey. "They act (in all things) by His Command." (21:27, Yusuf Ali)
  • Angels over the Fire "flinch not (from executing) the Commands they receive from Allah, but do (precisely) what they are commanded" (66:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • So angels are sinless.
  • Humans and jinn have free will: they can obey or disobey, and will be judged.
  • Iblis (Satan) refused to bow to Adam. The Qur'an says "He was one of the Jinns" (18:50), which is why he could choose to disobey.
  • Some Muslims ask whether angels such as Harut and Marut (2:102) show limited choice; most scholars hold that they acted only by Allah's permission, as a test.

Mission 6

Jibril, the angel of revelation

  • Muslims believe Jibril (Gabriel) is the chief angel and Allah's messenger to the prophets.
  • He brought the Qur'an to Muhammad over about 23 years, beginning with "Read!" in the cave of Hira (96:1).
  • He told Maryam she would have a son, Isa (19:17–21).
  • In the Hadith of Jibril he appears as a man and asks the Prophet about Islam, iman and ihsan, to teach the companions.
  • Significance: because an obedient angel delivered it, Muslims trust the Qur'an as Allah's exact words, not human ideas.

Allah is an enemy to those who reject Faith." (Qur'an 2:97–98, Yusuf Ali)

  • "Each one (of them) believeth in Allah, His angels, His books, and His messengers." (2:285, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 What does 2:97 say Jibril brings, and to where?

Show answer

The revelation, to the Prophet's heart, by Allah's will.

Q2 Why does 2:98 list angels alongside Allah and the messengers?

Show answer

Mission 6

Mika'il, the angel of provision

  • Mika'il (Michael) is named in the Qur'an once, beside Jibril (2:98).
  • Muslim tradition teaches that Allah placed him in charge of rain and plants: the provision (rizq) that keeps creation alive.
  • Significance: every harvest and rainfall reminds Muslims that provision comes from Allah, which encourages gratitude and sharing (e.g. zakah).
  • He shows Allah's beneficence (see Mission 2) at work in the world.
Mission 6

Izra'il and Israfil

  • Izra'il, the Angel of Death: "The Angel of Death, put in charge of you, will (duly) take your souls: then shall ye be brought back to your Lord." (32:11, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims believe he takes the soul at the time Allah has set: gently for the righteous, harshly for wrongdoers (hadith, paraphrase).
  • The name Izra'il comes from tradition; the Qur'an says "the Angel of Death".
  • Israfil will blow the trumpet (sur) to end the world and then to raise the dead: "a second one be sounded, when, behold, they will be standing and looking on!" (39:68, Yusuf Ali)
  • His trumpet announces the Day of Resurrection.
  • Significance: death is not chance but Allah's decree; Muslims prepare for it by living well.

Mission 6

Recording and guardian angels

  • Muslims believe two recording angels (Kiraman Katibin, "honourable scribes") note every deed: "Kind and honourable,- Writing down (your deeds): They know (and understand) all that ye do." (82:11–12, Yusuf Ali)
  • "…one sitting on the right and one on the left. Not a word does he utter but there is a sentinel by him." (50:17–18): good deeds on the right, bad on the left.
  • The record is shown on the Day of Judgement.
  • Guardian angels protect each person "by command of Allah" (13:11).
  • Angels also pray for the believers' forgiveness (40:7).
Mission 6

Why angels matter today

  • Accountability: if every word is recorded, honesty matters even when no one is watching.
  • Trust in revelation: an obedient angel brought the Qur'an, so it can be trusted.
  • Comfort: guardian angels and angels' prayers mean believers are not alone.
  • Prayer: at the end of salah, Muslims turn to the right and left saying "Peace be upon you"; many teachers say this also greets the angels.
  • Preparation for death: Muslims believe the Angel of Death comes at the time Allah has set.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Belief in angels makes little difference to how Muslims live today."

  • For: angels are unseen and rarely mentioned in daily life; the Five Pillars and the Qur'an seem to matter more. Belief in Allah's knowledge alone would make people accountable.
  • Against: belief in recording angels shapes behaviour (honesty, kindness); belief in Jibril underpins trust in the Qur'an; angels are an article of faith, named with Allah in 2:285 and 4:136.
  • Another view: angels matter mainly as reminders: they make Allah's knowledge, care and judgement feel close.
  • Judge it by: Would a Muslim's practice change if belief in angels were removed? Is the effect on behaviour or on belief? Does it differ between Muslims?

Muslims believe angels never disobey, so they can be trusted to carry Allah's word and to record every deed: two reasons belief in angels shapes Muslim life.

Mission 7

What the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": it is meant to be heard and recited aloud.
  • Muslims believe it is the word of God (Allah), in Arabic, revealed through the angel Jibril to the Prophet Muhammad from 610 to 632 CE.
  • It is the last of the revealed books (Kutub), and Muslims believe it is the only one kept exactly as revealed.
  • 114 surahs (chapters) made of ayat (verses; the word also means "signs").
  • After the short opening surah, al-Fatihah, surahs run roughly from longest to shortest, not in the order they were revealed.
  • Makkan surahs stress belief: one God, prophets, judgement. Madinan surahs add guidance for the community: worship, family, law.
Mission 7Read the source

Not his own words

> "Nor doth he speak of (his own) desire. It is naught save an inspiration that is inspired" (Surah 53:3–4, Pickthall)

  • Jibril "hath revealed (this Scripture) to thy heart by Allah's leave, confirming that which was (revealed) before it" (Surah 2:97, Pickthall); 2:98 warns that enmity to God's angels is enmity to God.

Mission 7

The Night of Power and a gradual revelation

  • Muslims believe the revelation began on Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, in Ramadan: "Lo! We revealed it on the Night of Predestination." (Surah 97:1, Pickthall)
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Surah 97:3, Pickthall)
  • After that night, it came in parts over about 23 years, often in answer to events, to strengthen the Prophet's heart (Surah 25:32).
  • The Prophet recited each revelation; companions memorised it and scribes wrote it down on parchment, palm leaves and bone.
  • How it is viewed today: Muslims spend the last ten nights of Ramadan in extra prayer and recitation, seeking the Night of Power; many aim to recite the whole Qur'an during the month.
  • Significance of Jibril: the message came through a trusted angel, so Muslims believe it reached the Prophet complete and unaltered.

Mission 7

Why the Qur'an has supreme authority

  • God's own words: for Muslims, not a book about God, but God speaking. It is the highest source of guidance and of Shari'ah law.
  • Unchanged: Muslims believe it is preserved exactly (15:9): the final, undistorted message.
  • Beyond imitation: "though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall). Muslims see its language as a miracle (i'jaz).
  • In Arabic: "a Lecture in Arabic, that ye may understand" (Surah 12:2, Pickthall). A translation is treated as an interpretation of the meaning, not the Qur'an itself.
  • Complete: it confirms the earlier books and needs no addition.

Mission 7

The Qur'an in daily life

  • Formal worship: al-Fatihah is recited in every unit (rak'ah) of salah; the Friday sermon is built on the Qur'an.
  • Informal worship: personal reading and recitation; verses used in du'a (personal prayer) and for comfort; many learn it by heart and become a hafiz.
  • Home: read daily by many families; recited at a birth, a marriage and a death; verses in calligraphy on walls.
  • Mosque: children learn to read and recite it at madrasah; in Ramadan many Sunni mosques recite the whole Qur'an over the nights of tarawih prayer.
  • Guidance: for decisions about family, money, food and conduct; with the Sunnah, the basis of Shari'ah.
Mission 7

Treated with respect

  • Many Muslims make wudu (washing) before touching the Arabic Qur'an. They point to "Which none toucheth save the purified" (Surah 56:79, Pickthall). Some scholars read this verse as about angels and the heavenly book; most still see wudu as the respectful way.
  • It is kept on a high shelf, often wrapped in cloth, with nothing placed on top.
  • It is read on a stand (rahl), not on the floor; it is not taken into a bathroom.
  • Recitation begins by seeking God's protection from Satan (Surah 16:98).
  • Worn-out copies are not thrown away: they are buried or respectfully disposed of.
  • Many scholars apply these rules to the Arabic text more strictly than to translations.

Mission 7Evaluate

"Reciting the Qur'an in Arabic matters more than understanding it."

  • For: the Arabic words are God's own; translation loses meaning and beauty. Recitation is itself worship (Surah 73:4: "chant the Qur'an in measure", Pickthall). One Arabic Qur'an unites Muslims everywhere.
  • Against: it was revealed "that ye may understand" (12:2). "Will they then not meditate on the Qur'an" (47:24). Guidance only guides if it is understood; it is meant to change how people live.
  • Another view: most Muslim scholars say both matter: recite in Arabic and learn its meaning, through study, tafsir and translation.
  • Judge it by: What is the Qur'an for: worship, guidance, or both? Can a translation carry God's words? What do Muslims in different communities actually do?

Because Muslims believe the Arabic words are God's own, the Qur'an is recited, kept and obeyed in ways no other book is.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sunni Muslims summarise iman as six articles: Tawhid, angels, books, prophets, Akhirah, al-Qadr.
  • Shi'a Muslims hold five roots of Usul ad-Din: Tawhid, 'Adl (Adalat), Nubuwwah, Imamah, Mi'ad; they also believe in angels, books and Allah's decree.

Mission 2

  • Tawhid: Allah is one and unique (Surah 112); shirk, associating partners with Allah, is the gravest sin.
  • Allah is transcendent and immanent, omnipotent, beneficent, merciful and just.

Mission 3

  • Risalah: Muslims believe Allah guides humans through prophets, from Adam to Muhammad, all teaching Tawhid; Muslims believe in every one (2:136).
  • Adam (first prophet, khalifah, forgiven) and Ibrahim (hanif, tested, builder of the Ka'bah) are key examples.
  • Muslims believe Isa is a great prophet, born of Maryam, but not divine; Christians believe he is the Son of God.
  • Muslims believe Muhammad is the Seal of the Prophets: the last messenger, bringing the final revelation.

Mission 4

  • Muslims believe Allah created everything with purpose; humans are honoured, and placed on Earth as khalifah (2:30), holding it in trust (amanah).
  • In Muslim belief, Adam was the first human, first prophet and first khalifah; he repented and was forgiven, so there is no original sin.

Mission 5

  • Muslims believe Muhammad is the Seal of the Prophets (33:40): the final prophet, confirming all earlier prophets and sent to all people.
  • His Sunnah makes him the model for Muslim life (33:21); traditions differ on how his teaching was passed on.

Mission 6

  • Muslims believe angels are created from light, unseen, sinless servants of Allah with no free will; humans and jinn (including Iblis) have free will.
  • In Muslim belief, Jibril brought the Qur'an; Mika'il is linked with rain and provision; recording angels note every deed.
  • Muslims believe Izra'il takes souls at death (32:11); Israfil will blow the trumpet for the Resurrection.

Mission 7

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's word in Arabic, revealed through Jibril to Muhammad from 610 to 632, beginning on the Night of Power.
  • For Muslims it has supreme authority; it shapes worship, home life and moral choices; it is handled with special respect.
