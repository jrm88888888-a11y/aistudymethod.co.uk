How Unit 2 works

Choose ONE focus — Catholic or Protestant

Unit 2 studies the Christian Church with a focus on EITHER the Catholic Church OR the Protestant Church. Your school chooses one option and you answer on that one. This mini-lesson teaches the shared foundation, then takes each option in turn — so use the half your class is sitting, and read the other half for context.

The shared foundation (identical in both options)

Pentecost (Acts 2: 1–8) — wind, fire and speech in many languages; the Spirit is given and the church is born.

St Paul on the church (1 Cor. 12: 1–11, 14–22, 25–27) — many gifts, one Spirit; many parts, one body; the weaker parts are indispensable; if one suffers, all suffer.

Characteristics of the Early Church — preaching, teaching, prayer, miracles, hymn-singing and sharing (Matt. 10: 1, 5–14; Acts 2: 42, 44–47; 1 Cor. 11: 23–25; James 2: 14–17; Col. 3: 12–17).

The Apostles' Creed — a three-part summary of belief: Father, Son, Holy Spirit (and then the church, forgiveness, resurrection and life everlasting).

Christianity in Ireland — the life and work of St Patrick: enslaved in Ireland as a boy, escaped, and returned as a missionary bishop. His own Confessio is the evidence; the shamrock and the snakes are later legend.

Protestant option note: it adds one extra foundation bullet — how Protestantism emerged as a result of the Reformation (Luther's Ninety-Five Theses, 1517; sola scriptura, sola fide, sola gratia; the printing press; Calvin and the Reformed churches).

Catholic option · church government

Hierarchy of authority, ordination and the priest

Hierarchy of authority. The Catholic Church is governed as a hierarchy, understood as apostolic succession — authority handed on from the apostles:

  • The Pope — Bishop of Rome, successor of St Peter, universal pastor.
  • Cardinals — senior bishops who advise the Pope and elect his successor.
  • Archbishops and bishops — each bishop leads a diocese, teaching, governing and ordaining.
  • Priests — lead parishes and celebrate the sacraments; deacons assist, preach, baptise and marry.
  • The laity — all the baptised, who share in the mission of the church.

Ordination is the sacrament of Holy Orders. The bishop lays hands on the candidate and prays; the new priest is anointed and given the chalice and paten. Catholic teaching is that a priest acts in persona Christi — in the person of Christ — when he celebrates the Eucharist and gives absolution. In the Latin Church priests are celibate men.

The role of the priest: celebrating Mass; hearing confessions and giving absolution; anointing the sick; baptising, marrying and burying; preaching and teaching; leading the parish and its schools and charities.

Protestant option · church government

Two denominations, ordination and the minister

CCEA asks for church organisation in two denominations. Protestant churches use three main patterns of government — and the contrast between them is the answer:

Presbyterian (rule by elders)

The congregation elects elders. Minister and elders form the Kirk Session, which runs the congregation. Sessions send representatives to a Presbytery (a regional court), and on to the General Assembly, which elects a Moderator for one year. Authority is shared and representative, not held for life by one person.

Baptist (congregational rule)

Each local church is self-governing. The church meeting of all members decides matters and calls its own pastor; deacons assist. Churches co-operate voluntarily in a union or association, which cannot overrule a local congregation.

Other patterns exist: the Church of Ireland is episcopal (bishops, in a synodical structure), while Methodism is connexional — ministers belong to the Conference and serve in circuits.

Ordination in Protestant churches is a solemn setting apart for the ministry of Word and sacrament, by prayer and the laying on of hands. It is generally not counted as a sacrament; ministers may marry, and many Protestant churches ordain women. The minister or pastor preaches, leads worship and Communion, teaches, provides pastoral care and shares oversight with elders or deacons.

The priesthood of all believers: the Reformation conviction that every Christian has direct access to God through Christ, needs no human mediator, and shares responsibility for ministry. The minister is set apart for a function, not raised to a different spiritual rank.

Catholic option · private worship

The Bible, prayer, the Rosary, icons and statues

  • Using the Bible — Catholics read Scripture privately and in groups; lectio divina is a slow, prayerful reading. The daily readings follow the same lectionary used at Mass.
  • Prayer — set prayers (the Our Father, the Hail Mary, the Angelus), and personal prayer of adoration, confession, thanksgiving and intercession.
  • The Rosary — a string of beads used to pray five decades. Each decade is one Our Father, ten Hail Marys and a Glory Be, while the believer meditates on a mystery from the life of Christ. The sets are the Joyful, Sorrowful and Glorious mysteries, with the Luminous mysteries added in 2002 by Pope John Paul II. The repetition is designed to quieten the mind so it can dwell on the gospel events.
  • Icons and statues — aids to prayer, reminding the believer of Christ, Mary and the saints. Catholic teaching draws a firm line: worship (latria) belongs to God alone; saints are venerated (dulia) and asked to pray for us, as you might ask a friend to pray for you.

Handle the criticism accurately. The Protestant objection is that such practices risk obscuring the one mediator, Christ, and lack clear biblical warrant. The Catholic reply is that asking a saint to pray for you no more replaces Christ than asking your friend to pray for you does. State both fairly — that is what AO2 rewards.

Catholic option · public worship

The order of the Mass and the meaning of the Eucharist

The Mass has a fixed shape, and the shape carries the meaning: God speaks, then God gives.

  • Introductory Rites — greeting, Penitential Act ("I confess..."), Gloria, Collect.
  • Liturgy of the Word — first reading (usually Old Testament), Responsorial Psalm, second reading, Gospel (all stand), homily, Creed, Prayers of the Faithful.
  • Liturgy of the Eucharist — preparation of the gifts; the Eucharistic Prayer with the words of institution and the epiclesis (calling on the Spirit); the Our Father; the Sign of Peace; the Agnus Dei; Holy Communion.
  • Concluding Rite — blessing and dismissal: the congregation is sent out ("Go in peace").

The meaning of the Eucharist. Catholic teaching is transubstantiation: the substance of the bread and wine truly becomes the body and blood of Christ, although the appearances remain unchanged. The Mass is not a new sacrifice but a re-presentation of Christ's one sacrifice on the cross, and Communion is real nourishment for the Christian life. This is why the consecrated hosts are reserved in the tabernacle and treated with reverence.

Catholic option · Mary and the saints

Mother of God, guide and role model

CCEA names Mary twice: as Mother of God, and as guide and role model.

  • Mother of God — the ancient title Theotokos, "God-bearer", affirmed by the Council of Ephesus in 431. It is first of all a claim about Jesus: the child Mary bore is truly God, not merely a man later adopted by God.
  • Role model — at the annunciation Mary answers, "Let it be to me according to your word" (Luke 1: 38). Catholics hold her up as the model disciple: faith, obedience, humility and endurance at the cross.
  • Guide in prayer — the Hail Mary asks her to "pray for us sinners". Marian devotions include the Rosary, the Angelus, May processions and pilgrimage (for example to Knock or Lourdes).
  • The saints — Christians whose holiness the church recognises; they are examples to imitate and companions who pray with and for the church. Many parishes, schools and individuals take a saint's name.
Catholic option · architecture and sacraments

Altar, font, tabernacle, ambo — and the sacraments

The four items CCEA names in a Catholic church:

  • Altar — the focal point; the table of sacrifice where the Eucharist is celebrated.
  • Baptismal font — usually near the entrance, showing that baptism is the way in to the church.
  • Tabernacle — the locked box where consecrated hosts are reserved; a sanctuary lamp burns beside it, and Catholics genuflect towards it.
  • Ambo — the raised stand from which the Scriptures are proclaimed: the "table of the Word", balancing the altar, the "table of the Sacrament".
Baptism and Confirmation

Baptism: water poured three times in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Symbols: water (cleansing, new life), white garment (purity), candle lit from the paschal candle (the light of Christ), oil (strength). Infants are baptised on the promises of parents and godparents.

Confirmation: the bishop lays hands on the candidate and anoints the forehead with chrism, saying, "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit." A sponsor stands with the candidate. It completes Christian initiation: the young person now claims for themselves the faith professed at their baptism.

Reconciliation and the Anointing of the Sick

Reconciliation (Confession): examination of conscience → contrition → confession of sins to a priest → a penance → absolution. Its importance: assurance of forgiveness, an honest look at one's life, and a fresh start. The seal of the confessional is absolute.

Anointing of the Sick: for those who are seriously ill, frail or dying. The priest lays on hands in silence and anoints the forehead and hands with the oil of the sick, praying for healing, forgiveness and strength. It is no longer called "the last rites" because it may be received more than once, and not only at the point of death.

Protestant option · private worship

The Bible, prayer, reading aids and fellowship

  • Using the Bible — daily personal reading (often called a "quiet time") is strongly encouraged, on the conviction that God speaks through Scripture to every believer, not only to the clergy.
  • Prayer — set prayers are used, but extempore prayer, spoken freely in one's own words, is highly valued; families may pray together at home.
  • Bible reading aids — daily notes and devotional guides, study Bibles with footnotes, concordances, commentaries, reading plans and apps. They exist because Protestants expect ordinary believers to interpret Scripture for themselves — and want them to do it well.
  • Fellowship meetings — midweek house groups, Bible studies, prayer meetings and youth fellowships: smaller gatherings where members study, pray, support one another and hold one another accountable.
Protestant option · public worship

Order of service, music, preaching and tongues

  • The order of service (a typical Presbyterian or Methodist Sunday): call to worship → hymn → prayer of approach and confession → Bible readings → hymn → sermon → intercessions → offering → hymn → benediction.
  • Singing and music — from unaccompanied metrical psalms, through the great hymn traditions of Watts, Wesley and the revivals, to contemporary worship bands. Hymns teach doctrine as well as express praise.
  • Liturgical and non-liturgical — liturgical worship follows a set written order (for example the Book of Common Prayer); non-liturgical worship follows a flexible pattern chosen by the leader, on the argument that the Spirit should not be constrained by a script. Both are found within Protestantism.
  • Preaching — usually the climax of the service, which is why the pulpit is often central and raised. The sermon expounds and applies the Bible, and its authority is understood to come from the text, not from the preacher.
  • Speaking in tongues — a gift Paul lists in 1 Cor. 12. In Pentecostal and charismatic churches it is practised in worship, usually with an interpretation, as a sign of the Spirit's presence. Other Protestants are cautious or believe such gifts belonged to the apostolic age; Paul himself insists worship must be orderly and build up the church.
Protestant option · the Bible and the building

Authority, inspiration, truth — and church furniture

Beliefs about the Bible. The Reformation principle is sola scriptura: Scripture is the final authority for belief and practice, above church tradition and above the claims of any office-holder.

  • Inspiration — 2 Tim. 3: 16 calls Scripture "God-breathed". Christians differ over what that means: literalists hold every statement is factually accurate; conservatives hold the Bible is wholly trustworthy in what it teaches; liberals hold it is a human record of encounter with God, inspired but conditioned by its age.
  • Meaning and purpose — to reveal God, to make Christ known and to guide the believer's life. Protestants insist Scripture should be available in the language of the people — a direct fruit of the Reformation and of printing.
  • Truth — the debate over Genesis and science is the classic testing ground. Is the Bible making scientific claims, or theological ones? A good AO2 answer shows it knows the difference between those questions.

The building: the pulpit (raised and often central — the Word proclaimed), the communion table (a table, not an altar: a shared meal rather than a sacrifice), and the baptismal font (pouring, for infants) or baptismal pool (full immersion, for believers). Plain interiors in some traditions are a deliberate statement: nothing should distract from the Word.

Protestant option · sacraments, ordinances and membership

Baptism and Communion in two churches

Baptism in two Protestant churches

Presbyterian: infants are baptised, water poured or sprinkled, on the promises of believing parents; the congregation promises to support the child. The argument: baptism is a sign of God's covenant grace, which comes before our response.

Baptist: only believers are baptised, by full immersion in a pool, on personal profession of faith. The argument: the New Testament shows only those who have believed being baptised, and going under the water pictures dying and rising with Christ.

Communion in two Protestant churches

Presbyterian / Reformed: the elements remain bread and wine, but Christ is truly received spiritually by faith. Communion is often celebrated quarterly, with careful preparation, and the table is fenced with an invitation to those who trust Christ.

Baptist: the bread and cup are a memorial — "do this in remembrance of me" (1 Cor. 11: 23–25). It is an ordinance, an act of obedience, rather than a channel of grace. Most Baptist churches use non-alcoholic wine and practise an open table.

Confirmation and church membership. In Presbyterian and Methodist churches, young people baptised as infants later go through a communicants' class and publicly profess faith, becoming full members entitled to receive Communion and vote in church meetings. In Baptist churches, believers' baptism itself is the point of entry into membership. Either way, the logic is the same: at some point the faith must become personal.

Shared · festivals and the church in society

The church year, community and challenge

Festivals (both options): Advent (preparation), Christmas (incarnation), Epiphany (Christ revealed to the nations), Lent (self-examination and fasting), Holy Week and Easter (death and resurrection), Ascension, Pentecost (the gift of the Spirit) and Saints' Days. The Catholic option keeps Saints' Days prominently; the Protestant option adds Harvest and gives less emphasis to Saints' Days.

The church in contemporary society. Contribution: food banks, debt and addiction support, youth work, night shelters, schools, hospital and prison chaplaincy, and — in Northern Ireland especially — cross-community work that builds cohesion across historic division.

AO2 — the challenge of changing moral and social trends

The pressures: falling attendance and a more secular culture; internal disagreement over sexuality, marriage and the role of women; the damage done by abuse scandals and their cover-up; and the difficulty of being heard in a society that no longer shares Christian assumptions.

Adapt: a church that will not change loses the next generation — and it has changed before, on slavery and on the vernacular Bible.

Hold firm: a church that simply follows public opinion has nothing distinctive left to say; its authority is Scripture and tradition, not the opinion poll.

Weigh both, then judge. The marks are in the weighing, not in the shouting.
