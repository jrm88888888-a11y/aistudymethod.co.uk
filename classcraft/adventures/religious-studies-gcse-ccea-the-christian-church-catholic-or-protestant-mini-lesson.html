Mission 1

What popular piety is

  • Popular piety: forms of prayer and devotion that grow from ordinary believers' faith and culture.
  • Examples: the Rosary, the Stations of the Cross, Eucharistic adoration, pilgrimages, processions, candles, medals.
  • Stations of the Cross: fourteen images around a church wall showing Jesus' journey to his death; people walk from one to the next and pray at each.
  • They sit alongside the liturgy (the Mass and sacraments), not instead of it.
  • They engage the whole person: hands (beads), feet (walking), eyes (images), voice (repeated prayer).
  • They can be prayed anywhere: at home, in hospital, on a bus, in a group.
  • Much of it is private worship: prayer at home that feeds a Catholic's public worship at Mass.
Mission 1

The Rosary

  • A string of beads with a crucifix, used to count prayers.
  • Start: Apostles' Creed, Our Father, three Hail Marys, Glory Be.
  • Then five decades. Each: one Our Father, ten Hail Marys, one Glory Be.
  • The Hail Mary begins with Gabriel's and Elizabeth's words: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28); "Blessed are you amongst women, and blessed is the fruit of your womb!" (Luke 1:42)
  • During each decade the person thinks about one mystery: an event in the life of Jesus and Mary.
  • Four sets: Joyful, Luminous (added by Pope John Paul II in 2002), Sorrowful, Glorious.
  • Christ-centred: Pope John Paul II called it a summary of the whole Gospel, prayed "with Mary". The repetition calms the mind for meditation.
Mission 1

The Sorrowful Mysteries

  • Prayed especially on Tuesdays and Fridays, and in Lent.
  • 1. The Agony in the Garden: "My soul is exceedingly sorrowful, even to death." (Matthew 26:38)
  • 2. The Scourging at the Pillar (John 19:1). 3. The Crowning with Thorns (Matthew 27:29).
  • 4. The Carrying of the Cross (John 19:17). 5. The Crucifixion and Death (Luke 23:46).

Mission 1

Private worship: Bible, icons and statues

  • Using the Bible: reading slowly and prayerfully (lectio divina): read, reflect, pray, rest in God.
  • Prayer: morning and night prayers, grace before meals, the Rosary; types of prayer in an earlier lesson.
  • Icons: painted images of Christ, Mary and saints, especially from Eastern tradition; called "windows" onto heaven.
  • Statues of Mary and the saints (e.g. St Patrick, St Brigid) at home and in church, with candles.
  • Catholics venerate (honour) the people images show; they worship God alone. The Second Council of Nicaea (787) taught that honour given to an image passes to the person it shows.
  • Many homes have a small prayer corner with a crucifix, a statue or icon, and a Bible.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Popular piety is as important for Catholics as the Mass."

  • Claim: devotions like the Rosary matter as much as the Mass.
  • For: they can be prayed daily, anywhere, by anyone, including the sick. The Rosary summarises the Gospel. They make faith personal and part of everyday life. The Church encourages them (CCC 1674).
  • Against: they extend the liturgy but do not replace it (CCC 1675). The Eucharist is the source and summit of Christian life. Devotions can drift into superstition and need guiding (CCC 1676). Many Protestants see some of them as unbiblical.
  • Judge it by: Does "as important" mean "the same kind of thing"? Which leads to which? What does the person praying actually gain?

Mission 2

Why Catholics make statues

  • Catholic churches and homes have statues: of Jesus, Mary, the saints and angels.
  • The Incarnation: God became human in Jesus, so God has been seen, touched and heard. Catholics believe this makes images of Christ possible.
  • A statue is three-dimensional: it stands in the same space as the believer, as if present.
  • Purposes: to remember, to teach, to inspire, and to focus prayer.
Mission 2

Veneration, not worship

  • Catholics worship God alone (adoration).
  • They venerate (honour) Mary and the saints: a statue is a reminder, not a god.
  • The Second Council of Nicaea (787) and the Catechism (CCC 2132) teach that honour given to an image passes to the person it shows.
  • So lighting a candle before a statue of St Anthony is asking him to pray with you, not praying to stone.
  • The same is true of icons: flat sacred paintings, central to Orthodox prayer and used by many Catholics, called windows into heaven.

Mission 2

Statues in a Catholic church

  • Crucifix: Christ on the cross, above or near the altar: his sacrifice, renewed in the Mass.
  • Mary: often a side altar or shrine, with candles and flowers.
  • Saints, especially the church's patron; Sacred Heart of Jesus: his love for all people.
  • Stations of the Cross: 14 carved or painted scenes around the walls.
  • Christmas crib: figures of the Nativity scene.
  • How they are used: kneeling, lighting candles, prayer, processions (e.g. crowning a statue of Mary in May), funerals and feasts.
Mission 2

Statues in private prayer

  • Many Catholic homes have a small shrine: a crucifix or statue of Mary or a saint, a candle, perhaps flowers.
  • Families may pray the rosary together in front of a statue of Mary.
  • A statue or holy picture beside a bed, or a small figure carried in a bag, reminds the believer to pray during the day.
  • Statues help children learn the stories of Jesus and the saints.
  • Private prayer before a statue is personal and quiet: thanks, sorrow, asking for help.
Mission 2

Michelangelo's Pietà

  • Pietà (Italian: "pity", "compassion"); Michelangelo, carved 1498–1499 from one block of Carrara marble, aged about 24.
  • Now in St Peter's Basilica, Rome; the only work he signed (on the sash across Mary's chest).
  • Mary holds the dead body of Jesus across her lap after the crucifixion.
  • Details: a pyramid shape: stable and calm; Mary looks young and serene; her eyes are lowered; her left hand is open, as if offering or accepting; Christ's wounds are small; his body is at peace.
  • The scene is not described in the Gospels: it is a devotional image of Mary's sorrow.
  • Catholics read her calm as trust in God and her open hand as offering her son to God, in hope of the resurrection: so the statue gives hope in suffering.

For Catholics a statue is a reminder that God became visible in Christ: it is honoured for the person it shows, and worship is kept for God alone.

Mission 3

Who Mary is

  • Mary of Nazareth: the mother of Jesus. Catholics and Orthodox honour her above all other saints.
  • Annunciation: the angel Gabriel tells her she will bear God's Son; she agrees (Luke 1:26–38).
  • Cana: she tells the servants, "Whatever he says to you, do it." (John 2:5)
  • The cross: she stands by Jesus as he dies (John 19:25); he entrusts her to the beloved disciple: "Behold, your mother!" (John 19:27)
  • Pentecost: she prays with the apostles, waiting for the Spirit (Acts 1:14).
  • She "kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19): a woman of faith and prayer.
Mission 3

Mother of God

  • Mother of God (Greek Theotokos, "God-bearer"): defined at the Council of Ephesus (431).
  • It is first a claim about Jesus: the child Mary bore is one person, truly God and truly human. So his mother is the Mother of God.
  • It does not mean Mary is divine, existed before God, or is mother of the Father or the Trinity. She is a human being, a creature.
  • Catholics and Orthodox use the title; many Anglicans and Lutherans accept it too.
  • Other Catholic teachings about Mary:
  • Immaculate Conception (defined 1854): Mary was kept free from original sin from the first moment of her own life.
  • Assumption (defined 1950): at the end of her life Mary was taken body and soul into heaven.

The Annunciation (Luke 1:26–39)

  • Gabriel: "Rejoice, you highly favoured one! The Lord is with you." (Luke 1:28)
  • "You will conceive in your womb and give birth to a son, and shall name him 'Jesus.'" (Luke 1:31)
  • Mary asks how this can be. "The Holy Spirit will come on you, and the power of the Most High will overshadow you." (Luke 1:35)
  • "For nothing spoken by God is impossible." (Luke 1:37)
  • Mary: "Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word." (Luke 1:38)
  • Then she sets out "with haste" to help her relative Elizabeth (Luke 1:39).

Mission 3

Mary as guide and role model

  • Model of faith: she says "yes" to God without knowing what it will cost (Luke 1:38).
  • Model of discipleship: "blessed are those who hear the word of God, and keep it" (Luke 11:28).
  • Model of charity: she hurries to help Elizabeth; at Cana she notices others' needs.
  • Model of courage in suffering: she stays at the cross.
  • In prayer: the Hail Mary, the Rosary (see Mission 1), the Angelus (prayed at 6 a.m., noon and 6 p.m.), May devotions, Marian feasts.
  • Pilgrimage: shrines such as Lourdes (France) and Knock (Ireland).
  • Catholics ask Mary to pray for them ("pray for us sinners"); they do not worship her.
Mission 3

The saints

  • Saints: holy people whom the Church believes are now with God in heaven.
  • Communion of saints (Apostles' Creed): the Church on earth, in purgatory and in heaven is one family.
  • Canonisation: after a long investigation, the pope declares someone a saint; usually two miracles are required, after death, through their prayers (except for martyrs, for whom one miracle is required).
  • Role models: e.g. St Patrick and St Brigid, patrons of Ireland; St Oliver Plunkett, martyr.
  • Intercessors: Catholics ask saints to pray for them, as they ask friends: "pray for one another" (James 5:16); "we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses" (Hebrews 12:1).
  • Patron saints, feast days, saints' names at baptism and confirmation, statues and relics.

Mission 3Diversity

Different views of Mary

  • Orthodox: deep love for the Theotokos; icons of her in every church; celebrate her Dormition ("falling asleep") and taking up into heaven, but do not accept the Immaculate Conception as defined by Rome.
  • Catholics: Mother of God, Mother of the Church, model and intercessor; Immaculate Conception and Assumption are dogmas.
  • Most Protestants: honour Mary as the faithful mother of Jesus and a model of faith; do not pray to her or to the saints: "there is one God and one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5).
  • They reject the Immaculate Conception and Assumption as not taught in the Bible.
  • Anglicans vary: some keep Marian feasts and shrines (e.g. Walsingham); others share the Protestant view.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Mary is the most important example for Catholics today."

  • Claim: Mary is the best model of Christian life.
  • For: she says "yes" to God (Luke 1:38); she is the first disciple, faithful at the cross; the Catechism calls her the model of faith and charity (CCC 967); the Magnificat shows care for justice.
  • Against: Jesus is the perfect example: "I have given you an example" (John 13:15). Some find Mary's sinlessness makes her hard to imitate; modern saints may feel closer to everyday lives. Protestants note the Bible says little about her.
  • Judge it by: example of what? for whom? Is a model someone you copy, or someone who points you to Christ?
Mission check

"Mother of God" is above all a claim about Jesus: Catholics honour Mary as the human mother of the one who is God.

Mission 4Read the source

Worship in spirit and truth

"God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth." (John 4:24, WEBBE)

  • The Catechism starts here: worship is not tied to one place; the whole earth is holy, and Christians are the "living stones" of God's house (CCC 1179, 1 Peter 2:5; paraphrase).
  • Yet where they are free to, Catholics build churches. A church is more than a meeting hall: it is a visible sign of the Church living in that place, God dwelling with his people (CCC 1180, paraphrase).
  • It is a house of prayer where the Eucharist is celebrated and kept, and it should be beautiful and fit for prayer (CCC 1181, paraphrase).

Q1 If God can be worshipped anywhere, why build a church at all?

Show answer

It gathers the community, houses the Eucharist, and is a sign of God among his people.

Mission 4

A building shaped like the faith

  • Many churches have a cruciform (cross-shaped) plan: the building itself recalls Christ's death.
  • The nave (from the Latin for "ship") holds the people: the Church as a ship carrying believers to God.
  • The sanctuary, raised and at the head of the church, holds the altar and ambo.
  • Walking from the door to the altar pictures the Christian life: entering by baptism, fed by the Eucharist, travelling towards heaven.
Mission 4

Common and divergent styles

  • Common to all: an altar, an ambo, a tabernacle, a font, a crucifix, a chair for the priest.
  • Gothic and Gothic Revival (e.g. St Chad's Cathedral, Birmingham, by A. W. N. Pugin, 1841): height, pointed arches and stained glass lift the eye and mind to heaven: God's transcendence.
  • Byzantine style (e.g. Westminster Cathedral, opened 1903): domes and mosaics: heaven brought close.
  • Modern, "in the round" (e.g. Liverpool Metropolitan Cathedral, 1967): the altar in the centre with people gathered around it.
  • After the Second Vatican Council (1962–65), Catholic teaching asks that churches help the people take a full and active part in the liturgy (Sacrosanctum Concilium 14, 124, paraphrase).

Mission 4

The altar

  • The altar is the centre of the church. The church is built around it.
  • It is both an altar of sacrifice and the table of the Lord (CCC 1182, paraphrase).
  • Sacrifice: at Mass, Catholics believe Christ's one sacrifice on the cross is made present, so the altar stands for the cross itself.
  • Meal: Jesus "took bread … saying, 'This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me.'" (Luke 22:19)
  • So the altar expresses redemption: Christ's death and resurrection bring salvation, and believers share in it.
  • A fixed altar is usually of stone (Christ the "cornerstone", Ephesians 2:20), often with relics of saints placed beneath it.
Mission 4

The lectern (ambo)

  • The ambo (CCEA's term; AQA and Edexcel say lectern) is the fixed reading desk from which the Word of God is proclaimed.
  • The Catechism says the dignity of God's Word needs a fitting place, where everyone's attention is easily drawn (CCC 1184, paraphrase).
  • Used for the readings, the psalm and usually the homily.
  • Redemption: the readings tell the story of salvation, from creation to Christ. "So faith comes by hearing, and hearing by the word of God." (Romans 10:17)
  • Catholics speak of two tables: the table of the Word (ambo) and the table of the Eucharist (altar). Both feed the people.
Mission 4

The tabernacle

  • The tabernacle is a locked, richly made box where the consecrated hosts are reserved (kept) after Mass.
  • Catholics believe Christ is really present in the consecrated bread (the Real Presence).
  • A sanctuary lamp (often red) burns beside it: Christ is here.
  • Catholics genuflect (bend the knee) towards it, and come to pray before it.
  • The Catechism asks that it stand in a most worthy place, so that its dignity and position help people adore the Lord present there (CCC 1183, paraphrase).
  • Redemption: the risen Christ stays with his people; Holy Communion is taken from it to the sick and dying.
Mission 4

The crucifix

  • A crucifix is a cross bearing the figure of the crucified Christ.
  • Catholic liturgical rules ask for one on or near the altar at Mass.
  • It shows the cost of redemption: "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23).
  • It joins the altar and the cross: what happened on Calvary is what the Mass makes present.
  • It invites personal reflection on Christ's love and on carrying one's own cross.
Mission 4

The font, by the door

  • The font holds the water for baptism. In many churches it stands near the entrance; some have a baptistery (CCC 1185).
  • Its place says: you enter the Church through baptism.
  • Baptism unites a person with Christ's death and resurrection: "buried … with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised … so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4)
  • Many fonts are eight-sided: Christ rose on the "eighth day", the start of the new creation.
  • Holy water stoups at the door let Catholics bless themselves and remember their baptism as they come in.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Catholics do not need a special building to worship God."

  • For: God is worshipped "in spirit and truth" (John 4:24); the whole earth is holy (CCC 1179). Mass can be said in a field, hospital or prison. The Church is the people, "living stones" (1 Peter 2:5).
  • Against: the sacraments need an altar and a font; the tabernacle keeps Christ present for prayer and for the sick. A church is a visible sign of God among his people (CCC 1180). Beauty, orientation and layout teach redemption and lift the mind to God.
  • Another view: the building is not essential, but it is valuable: worship is possible anywhere, and still richer in a place made for it.
  • Judge it by: Is worship mainly personal or communal? What does a building do that a field cannot? Does persecution (Christians without churches) show what is essential?

Mission 5

What a sacrament is

  • Sacrament: an outward, visible sign, given by Christ to the Church, that gives the inward grace it signifies (CCC 1131, paraphrase).
  • A long-standing summary: "a visible sign of invisible grace".
  • Each has matter (the sign: water, oil, bread) and form (the words that go with it).
  • Catholics believe Christ himself acts in every sacrament. It works by Christ's power, not the minister's holiness.
  • The fruit depends on the openness of the person receiving it.
Mission 5Read the source

CCC 1210–1211

Catechism of the Catholic Church 1210–1211 (paraphrase):

  • Christ gave the Church seven sacraments: baptism, confirmation, Eucharist, penance (reconciliation), anointing of the sick, holy orders and matrimony.
  • They touch every stage and key moment of life: they give birth and growth, healing and mission to the life of faith.
  • This mirrors natural life: being born, growing up, eating, being healed, serving others.
  • So there are three groups: initiation, healing, and sacraments at the service of communion (holy orders, matrimony).
  • The Eucharist has a unique place: the "sacrament of sacraments"; all the others lead to it.

Mission 5

Seven sacraments, seven effects

  • Baptism: new birth; original sin and all sins washed away; member of the Church.
  • Confirmation: strengthened by the Holy Spirit to live and witness to the faith.
  • Eucharist: receiving Christ himself; food for the journey; unity with the Church.
  • Reconciliation: forgiveness of sins committed after baptism.
  • Anointing of the sick: strength, peace and healing in serious illness.
  • Matrimony: grace for a faithful, lifelong love; holy orders: a deacon, priest or bishop given to serve the Church.

Mission 5

Baptism: the ceremony

  • Usually for babies, at the font; parents and godparents present the child.
  • Sign of the cross traced on the forehead: the child belongs to Christ.
  • Anointing with oil of catechumens: strength against evil.
  • Parents and godparents reject sin and profess the faith for the child.
  • Water poured three times (or immersion): "N., I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit" (from Matthew 28:19).
  • Anointing with chrism on the head: sharing Christ's role as priest, prophet and king.
  • White garment: a new life, "clothed in Christ". Candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle: the light of the risen Christ.
Mission 5

Baptism: what it does

  • Catholics believe that baptism:
  • Washes away original sin and all sin.
  • New birth as a child of God; a member of the Church, the Body of Christ: "in one Spirit we were all baptised into one body" (1 Corinthians 12:13).
  • Gives sanctifying grace; leaves a permanent spiritual mark, so it is never repeated.
  • Water means both death and life: "buried… with him through baptism into death… so we also might walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4).
Mission 5

Confirmation

  • Completes the grace of baptism. Usually received in the teenage years; in Ireland, often near the end of primary school.
  • The bishop is the usual minister. Candidates renew their baptismal promises; a sponsor stands with each one.
  • The bishop extends his hands over them and prays for the seven gifts of the Holy Spirit: wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord (from Isaiah 11:2).
  • Anointing with chrism on the forehead: "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit."
  • Roots: "Then they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit." (Acts 8:17)
  • Meaning: a closer bond with Christ and the Church; strength to witness; a permanent mark, received once. Many take a saint's name.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Catholics need the sacraments to receive God's grace."

  • For: Christ gave the sacraments himself: "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19). The Church teaches that for believers they are necessary for salvation (CCC 1129, paraphrase). Humans are bodily: visible signs meet them where they are.
  • Against: the sacramental view of reality says God's grace is found everywhere: in creation, prayer, Scripture and other people. The Catechism says God has bound salvation to baptism but is not himself bound by his sacraments (CCC 1257, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians: most Protestants stress grace received through faith and the Word; Quakers through inward experience.
  • Judge it by: Is "need" the same as "only"? What did Christ command? Can grace be found outside the sacraments and still lead to them?

For Catholics, ordinary things (water, oil, bread) become signs through which Christ really gives his grace.

Mission 6

Why a second step?

  • In churches that baptise babies, parents and godparents make the promises.
  • Later, the person makes those promises for themselves: a rite of passage from childhood faith to adult faith.
  • In Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran and Methodist churches this is confirmation.
  • "Confirm" has two senses: the person affirms their faith, and, Christians believe, God strengthens them with the Holy Spirit.
  • Afterwards the person is a full member of the church.
Mission 6Read the source

Laying on of hands

  • In Acts 8:14–17, Philip has baptised people in Samaria. The apostles Peter and John come, pray for them, and lay their hands on them, and they receive the Holy Spirit (paraphrase).
  • In Acts 19:5–6, Paul lays hands on newly baptised believers in Ephesus and the Spirit comes on them (paraphrase).
  • Confirmation follows this pattern: baptism first, then laying on of hands and the gift of the Spirit.

Mission 6

Catholic confirmation

  • A sacrament of initiation (with baptism and the Eucharist). Usually given by the bishop, often to young people aged about 11–16.
  • Renewal of baptismal promises: candidates reject sin and profess the faith themselves.
  • Laying on of hands: the bishop extends his hands over the candidates and prays for the Holy Spirit.
  • Anointing with chrism: the bishop traces a cross on each forehead with chrism (perfumed oil) and says, "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit."
  • Sponsor: stands behind with a hand on the candidate's shoulder; often a godparent; must be a confirmed Catholic.
  • Many candidates take a confirmation name, a saint as a model.
  • Catholics believe it gives the gifts of the Holy Spirit (wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord) and is given only once.
Mission 6

First Holy Communion

  • Catholic children usually make their First Holy Communion at about 7 or 8, the "age of reason".
  • Before it they make their first confession (sacrament of Reconciliation).
  • Months of preparation in parish and school; the families take part.
  • On the day, often in white clothes, children receive the host for the first time at Mass, then celebrate.
  • Meaning: they now share fully in the Eucharist, "the source and summit" of Catholic life.
  • In the Orthodox Church there is no separate First Communion: babies receive from their baptism.

Anglican and Methodist confirmation

  • Church of England / Church of Ireland: the bishop confirms. Candidates answer questions about their faith in front of the congregation, then the bishop lays hands on each one.
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1662) prays: "Defend, O Lord, this thy Child with thy heavenly grace, that he may continue thine for ever".
  • In some Anglican parishes children now receive Communion before confirmation; confirmation is still the adult commitment.
  • Methodist Church: the minister lays hands on candidates, who are then received into membership of the Methodist Church.
  • Officially these churches do not count confirmation among the two sacraments of the Gospel (Article XXV); it is a rite of commitment and blessing (many Anglo-Catholics still see it as sacramental).
Mission 6Diversity

Different routes into full membership

  • Catholic: baptism → First Communion → confirmation (in some dioceses confirmation now comes before First Communion).
  • Orthodox: baptism, chrismation (anointing with holy oil called myron) and Communion together, in infancy.
  • Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist: infant baptism → confirmation by bishop or minister.
  • Presbyterian: infant baptism → profession of faith before the elders (Kirk Session) → communicant member.
  • Baptist, Pentecostal: dedication → believers' baptism → received into membership, often welcomed with the "right hand of fellowship".
  • Quakers: no ceremony; people apply for membership and the meeting discerns together.

Mission 6

Why full membership matters

  • Belonging: the person is a full part of the Body of Christ in that local church.
  • Rights: e.g. receiving Communion (Presbyterian), voting in church meetings (Baptist), being a godparent or sponsor (Catholic).
  • Responsibilities: regular worship, prayer, giving, serving others, sharing the faith.
  • Support: the community promises to support members; members support each other.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Confirmation should only happen when people are adults."

  • Reasons for: a promise for life needs adult maturity. Teenagers may be confirmed because of family pressure or the party. Adults who choose freely may keep their promises better.
  • Reasons against: Catholics and Orthodox see confirmation as God's gift of the Spirit, not a reward for maturity. Young people need the Spirit's strength while they grow. Orthodox churches give chrismation to babies.
  • Judge it by: Is confirmation mainly God's action or the person's promise? What does Acts 8 show? Can a teenager make a genuine commitment?
Mission 6Worked answer

Why confirmation matters

Question: Explain why confirmation is important for many Christians.

  • Point: it is the moment people make the baptismal promises for themselves. Develop: their faith becomes their own choice, not only their parents'.
  • Point: Catholics believe it gives the Holy Spirit's strength. Source: Acts 8:14–17, where the apostles lay hands on the baptised and they receive the Spirit. Develop: so the bishop lays hands on candidates and anoints them with chrism.
  • Point: it brings full membership. Develop: the person can play a full part in church life.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Catholic teaching is that popular piety (Rosary, Stations, adoration) extends the liturgy but never replaces it (CCC 1674–1676).
  • The Rosary: five decades, each a meditation on one mystery.
  • The Stations retrace Jesus' way to the cross.

Mission 2

  • Catholics use statues because of the Incarnation (God made visible in Christ): images remind, teach and focus prayer; they are venerated, not worshipped.

Mission 3

  • "Mother of God" (Theotokos, Ephesus 431) means the child Mary bore is truly God; Mary herself is human.
  • Catholics see Mary as model of the Church: of faith ("let it be done to me", Luke 1:38), discipleship and charity, and pray with her and to the saints for their prayers.

Mission 4

  • A church is a sign of God dwelling with his people and a house of prayer; its layout traces the journey from baptism (font) to Eucharist (altar).
  • Altar (sacrifice and meal), ambo/lectern (Word), tabernacle (Real Presence), crucifix (cost of redemption) and font (new birth) each express redemption.

Mission 5

  • Catholics believe all creation can show God (sacramental reality), and the seven sacraments are visible signs that really give grace.
  • CCC 1210–1211: seven sacraments in three groups: initiation, healing, service of communion; the Eucharist at the centre.
  • Baptism gives new birth and membership of the Church; confirmation seals with the Spirit; the Eucharist completes initiation.

Mission 6

  • Confirmation lets people baptised as babies make the promises themselves and, Catholics believe, seals them with the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:14–17).
  • Catholic rite: bishop, renewal of promises, laying on of hands, chrism, sponsor; Anglicans and Methodists confirm by laying on of hands.
  • Other routes to full membership: Orthodox chrismation in infancy; Presbyterian profession of faith; Baptist believers' baptism.
