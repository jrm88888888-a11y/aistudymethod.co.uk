Mission 1

Mission 1: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Make a guess: How do your lungs pull air in when they have no muscle of their own?

Mission 1

The airway tree

  • Air path: trachea → two bronchi → many bronchioles → alveoli (air sacs).
  • The smallest bronchioles open into alveolar ducts, which lead to the alveoli.
  • Repeated branching ends in hundreds of millions of alveoli: a surface of tens of square metres inside the chest.
  • Gas exchange happens only across the alveoli. The airways conduct air to them.
  • The lungs sit in the thorax, enclosed by the ribs and intercostal muscles, with the diaphragm below.
Mission 1

The alveolus: built for diffusion

  • Alveolar wall: one layer of flattened (squamous) epithelial cells.
  • Capillary wall: one layer of endothelial cells. Over much of the surface, air to blood is under 1 µm.
  • Both are simple squamous layers: squamous epithelium and squamous endothelium.
  • A dense capillary network wraps each alveolus. Continuous blood flow keeps the gradient.
  • Ventilation keeps alveolar air high in O2 and low in CO2.
  • A moist lining lets gases dissolve. Surfactant in it lowers surface tension, so alveoli do not collapse.
  • Say: "thin alveolar epithelium, one cell thick" — not: "thin cell walls". Animal cells have no cell walls.
Quick check
?Mammalian lungs contain a branched network of bronchioles ending in millions of alveoli. How does this branching contribute to gas exchange?
Mission 1

Breathing in

  • (1) The diaphragm contracts and flattens, moving down.
  • (1) The external intercostal muscles contract, moving the ribs up and out. The internal intercostals relax.
  • (2) Thorax volume rises, so pressure in the lungs falls below atmospheric.
  • (3) Air flows in, down the pressure gradient. The lungs are pulled open; they do no work themselves.
Mission 1

Breathing out

  • At rest, breathing out is mostly passive. The diaphragm and external intercostals relax.
  • The diaphragm domes upward; the ribs move down and in; stretched elastic fibres in the lungs recoil.
  • Thorax volume falls, pressure rises above atmospheric, and air flows out.
  • Forced breathing out: the internal intercostal muscles contract, pulling the ribs down and in.
  • External and internal intercostals are antagonistic: they move the ribs in opposite directions.
  • Say: "the diaphragm relaxes and domes upward" — not: "the diaphragm contracts and pushes up".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events draws air into the lungs during inspiration?
Mission check

?During a rugby match, a player is tackled hard. A small tear in the surface of one lung lets air leak into the space around that lung. The diaphragm and intercostal muscles on that side still contract normally, but that lung does not inflate. Which explanation is best?

The muscles change the volume of the thorax; the lungs simply follow. Break that link and the lung stays collapsed.

Mission 2

What smoking does to the airways

  • Tobacco smoke contains tar (with carcinogens) and irritants.
  • Cilia are damaged and stop beating; goblet cells make more mucus. Mucus builds up.
  • Chronic bronchitis: inflamed bronchi; mucus narrows the airways; persistent cough and infections. Less air reaches the alveoli.
  • Emphysema: inflammation breaks down elastic fibres and alveolar walls. Alveoli merge into fewer, larger spaces: less surface area. Loss of elastic recoil makes breathing out hard.
  • Lung cancer: carcinogens cause mutations → uncontrolled cell division → a tumour that can block airways and replace exchange surface.

Mission 2

Correlation or cause?

  • A correlation: two variables change together. On its own it does not show that one causes the other.
  • A confounding variable (something else smokers share) could explain a correlation.
  • Evidence that smoking causes lung cancer:
  • The dose–response pattern; risk falls after stopping; smoking came before the cancer (people followed forward in time).
  • A mechanism: painting cigarette tar on mice caused skin tumours (Wynder, Graham and Croninger, 1953); carcinogens in tar cause mutations.
  • The same result in many studies in many countries.
  • Randomly assigning people to smoke would be unethical, so human evidence is observational.
Quick check

Mission check

?In pneumonia, many alveoli fill with fluid and white blood cells. The patient's blood oxygen concentration falls. Which explanation is best?

Lung volumes, breathing rate and disease data all answer one question: how much air reaches a large, thin, well-supplied surface.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Make a guess: How can a bead of liquid creeping along a thin tube show how fast seeds respire, and which fuel they use?

Mission 3

Other fuels

  • Glucose and fatty acids are the main respiratory substrates. Many other carbon compounds can be respired.
  • Fatty acids are cut into many 2-carbon acetyl groups → acetyl coenzyme A → Krebs cycle.
  • Lipids are first hydrolysed to glycerol + fatty acids. Glycerol → triose phosphate → glycolysis.
  • Amino acids (from protein) are deaminated: the amino group is removed (→ urea, in the liver). The rest enters as pyruvate, acetyl coenzyme A or a Krebs cycle intermediate.
Mission 3

The respiratory quotient

  • RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed (same time; volumes or moles).
  • Glucose: C6H12O6 + 6O2 → 6CO2 + 6H2O. RQ = 6 ÷ 6 = 1.0.
  • A lipid (tripalmitin): C51H98O6 + 72.5O2 → 51CO2 + 49H2O. RQ = 51 ÷ 72.5 = 0.70.
  • Lipids need extra O2 to oxidise their extra hydrogen, so their RQ is lower.
  • Typical RQ: carbohydrate 1.0; protein about 0.9; lipid about 0.7.
  • A mixed fuel gives a value in between (resting humans: about 0.8).
  • RQ above 1: CO2 is released with no matching O2 uptake, so some anaerobic respiration (e.g. yeast making ethanol).
Mission 3Practical

The respirometer

  • CCEA practical: a respirometer to find oxygen uptake, carbon dioxide production and RQ.
  • Organisms (germinating seeds, woodlice or yeast) sit in a sealed tube, above potassium hydroxide solution (or soda lime), which absorbs CO2.
  • O2 is used → gas volume falls → the liquid bead in the capillary moves towards the organisms.
  • O2 volume = distance moved × πr² (r = capillary radius).
  • Control tube: glass beads of the same volume. It corrects for temperature and pressure changes.
  • Constant-temperature water bath; let the tubes equilibrate first; reset the bead with the syringe.
  • Without the alkali, the bead shows O2 used − CO2 released (the net change).
  • Carbohydrate: CO2 released = O2 used, so no net change. Lipid: less CO2 than O2, so the bead still moves towards the organisms. The net change hints at the fuel.
Mission 3

Worked example: rate of oxygen uptake

Illustrative data: 2.0 g germinating seeds, 10 min, 25 °C, capillary diameter 1.0 mm. Bead moved 24 mm towards the seeds; the control's bead moved 2 mm the same way.

  • Step 1, correct with the control: 24 − 2 = 22 mm.
  • Step 2, volume: r = 0.50 mm, so π × 0.50² × 22 = 17.3 mm³ O2.
  • Step 3, rate: 17.3 ÷ 2.0 g ÷ 10 min = 0.86 mm³ g⁻¹ min⁻¹.
  • Always divide by time and by mass, so different samples can be compared.
Quick check
?In a respirometer, carbon dioxide is absorbed by potassium hydroxide. With 5.0 g of germinating seeds, the gas volume in the apparatus fell by 1.2 cm³ in 10 minutes. What is the rate of respiration per gram?
Mission 3

Worked example: RQ from a respirometer

Illustrative data: the same seeds, two 10-minute runs.

  • Run 1, with KOH: bead moves towards the seeds; gas volume falls by 30 mm³ → O2 used = 30 mm³.
  • Run 2, without KOH: bead moves towards the seeds; gas volume falls by 6 mm³ → O2 used − CO2 released = 6 mm³.
  • CO2 released = 30 − 6 = 24 mm³.
  • RQ = 24 ÷ 30 = 0.80: a mixture of substrates, including some lipid or protein.
  • If the bead moves away in run 2, CO2 released > O2 used: add the movement, and RQ > 1.
Quick check
?Yeast in a respirometer was studied for 10 minutes. With potassium hydroxide present, the bead moved towards the yeast by a volume of 45 mm³. Without potassium hydroxide, the bead moved away from the yeast by 9 mm³. What is the RQ?
Quick checkRead the data

Mission check

?A student set up a respirometer with blowfly larvae above potassium hydroxide solution, and the bead moved steadily towards the larvae. In a second run, identical except that the potassium hydroxide was replaced with water, the bead hardly moved. Which explanation is best?

With the alkali, the bead measures oxygen uptake: the rate. Without it, it measures the balance of the two gases, and that balance points to the fuel.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Make a guess: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Mission 4

Sources and sinks

  • Phloem carries organic substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids: translocation.
  • Source: where sugars enter the phloem. A photosynthesising leaf; a storage root in spring.
  • Sink: where sugars leave the phloem to be used or stored. Roots, meristems, fruits, growing storage organs.
  • Sap flows from source to sink: up or down.
  • Different sieve tubes can carry sap in opposite directions at the same time.
  • Translocation requires energy.
  • Say: "from source to sink" — not: "always down from the leaves".
Mission 4

Sieve tubes and companion cells

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells, end to end, forming sieve tubes.
  • End walls are sieve plates, full of pores: sap flows from cell to cell.
  • No nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm: little blocks the flow.
  • Companion cells: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria: they make the ATP for loading.
  • Many plasmodesmata link each companion cell to its sieve tube element.
  • Walls of cellulose, not lignified.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".
Quick check
?Which feature of a sieve tube element helps sap to flow along the phloem?
Mission 4

Loading at the source

  • (1) The companion cell uses ATP to pump H⁺ out: active transport.
  • (2) H⁺ diffuses back in through a co-transporter protein, carrying sucrose in with it.
  • (3) Sucrose passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata.
  • At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used in respiration or converted to starch.
  • So translocation needs energy: block respiration in the companion cells and it stops.
Quick check
?In spring, sugars stored in the roots of a deciduous tree are converted to sucrose and carried in the phloem to the opening buds. Which statement about this transport is correct?
Mission 4

The evidence

  • Ringing: remove a ring of bark, and with it the phloem, from a woody stem. The xylem is left. Sugars pile up above the ring; the bark swells. Leaves stay turgid.
  • Tracers: give a leaf ¹⁴CO2. It makes ¹⁴C-sucrose. An autoradiograph (photographic film laid on a stem section) shows ¹⁴C in the phloem, then in the sinks.
  • Aphids: the stylet enters one sieve tube. Cut the aphid away: sap keeps oozing from the stylet, under pressure. It is rich in sucrose.
  • Energy: respiratory inhibitors, lack of oxygen or cold slow translocation. Companion cells are packed with mitochondria.
  • Two-way flow: ¹⁴C given to one leaf moves both up and down the stem.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Make a guess: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Mission 5

Inside a root

  • Epidermis with root hairs: a huge surface for uptake.
  • Cortex: loosely packed cells; storage.
  • Endodermis: one ring of cells round the centre.
  • Central vascular cylinder (stele): xylem forms a star or X; phloem lies between its arms.
  • Root: xylem in the centre. Stem: xylem in a ring of bundles.
Quick check
?In a transverse section of a dicotyledonous root, where are the xylem and the phloem found?
Mission 5

Into the root hair

  • Root hair cells take in mineral ions by active transport: carrier proteins, ATP.
  • This lowers the water potential of the cell.
  • Soil water has a higher water potential. Water enters by osmosis.
  • Root hairs: long and thin, a large surface; many mitochondria for ATP.
  • Say: "water moves down a water potential gradient" — not: "water is actively pumped in".
Mission 5

Across the cortex

  • Apoplast: through cell walls and the spaces between cells. No membranes crossed. Fast; cohesion pulls water along.
  • Symplast: through the cytoplasm, from cell to cell via plasmodesmata.
  • The same pathways carry water across the leaf, from xylem to mesophyll cell walls.
Mission 5

The endodermis: a gatekeeper

  • Endodermis cell walls carry the Casparian strip: a waterproof band of suberin.
  • It blocks the apoplast. Water and ions must cross a membrane into the symplast.
  • So the root controls which ions reach the xylem.
  • Endodermal cells actively transport ions into the xylem. The water potential of the xylem sap falls; water follows by osmosis.
  • Say: "the Casparian strip blocks the apoplast pathway" — not: "it blocks all water".
Quick check
?A mutant plant forms no Casparian strip in its endodermis. Which effect is most likely?
Mission 5

Root pressure: a push from below

  • Cells around the xylem actively transport mineral ions into it.
  • Water follows by osmosis. A positive pressure builds and pushes water up: root pressure.
  • Evidence: sap oozes from cut stems; guttation (drops at leaf tips); both stop with respiratory inhibitors.
  • It matters when transpiration is low: humid air, or spring before deciduous leaves open.
  • Too small to lift water up a tall tree. Cohesion–tension does that.
  • Say: "ions are pumped; water follows by osmosis" — not: "root cells pump water".
Quick check
?A cut stem of a well-watered plant exudes sap from the xylem for several hours. When the roots are treated with a respiratory inhibitor, the exudation stops. What does this show about how the sap was being pushed out?
Mission 5

Xerophytes: the compromise

  • Xerophytes: plants adapted to dry habitats.
  • They must open stomata for carbon dioxide, so they cannot stop water loss. They reduce it.
  • Smaller gradient: sunken stomata, hairs, rolled or curved leaves trap humid air.
  • Less surface: reduced leaf area, spines, fewer stomata, a thick waxy cuticle.
  • More water in: deep or widespread roots; succulent tissue stores it.
  • Flowering plants succeed on land through wood (lignified xylem for support), xerophytic adaptations and desiccation-resistant seeds.
Mission 5

Marram grass and cacti

  • Marram grass (sand dunes): in dry wind, hinge cells lose water and the leaf rolls up.
  • Its stomata lie on the inner surface, in pits between ridges, among hairs. Thick cuticle outside.
  • Cacti: leaves reduced to spines; a green, fleshy stem photosynthesises.
  • Thick cuticle; succulent tissue stores water; widespread shallow roots catch brief rain.
Mission 5

Hydrophytes: the water lily

  • Hydrophytes: plants adapted to living in water.
  • Floating leaves: stomata on the upper surface, open to the air.
  • Aerenchyma: large air spaces keep leaves afloat and carry oxygen to submerged parts.
  • Thin cuticle and little lignified tissue: water loss is no threat, and water supports the plant.
Quick check
?A cactus grows where rain falls only a few times a year. Which explanation of one of its features is correct?
Mission check

?A gardener scatters a large amount of fertiliser on dry soil around a plant. Within a day the plant wilts, although the soil has not dried further. Which explanation is correct?

Ions in, water follows: roots take up water only while the soil has a higher water potential than the root.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Make a guess: A desert fox has enormous ears and almost never drinks. How did it end up so well suited to the desert?

Mission 6

What is an adaptation?

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature that increases an organism's chance of surviving and reproducing in its environment.
  • It arises by natural selection: random variation exists; individuals with a useful allele survive and breed more; the allele becomes more common over generations.
  • Individuals do not adapt in their lifetime. Populations change over generations.
  • An adaptation helps in one environment; the same trait may be useless or harmful in another.
  • Say: "individuals with the allele survived and bred more" — not: "the fox adapted because it needed to".
Mission 6

Three kinds of adaptation

  • Morphological (structural): body form. Fennec fox: huge ears with blood vessels near the surface radiate heat.
  • Physiological: body processes. Fennec fox: kidneys conserve water; it gets most of its water from food.
  • Behavioural: what it does. Fennec fox: active at night; shelters in a burrow by day.
  • Most organisms show all three, each meeting the same challenge.
  • Adaptation is the combination of behavioural, physiological and morphological ways an organism meets an environmental challenge.
Quick check
?Which adaptation is classified correctly?
Quick check
?A hypothetical grass species grows both on soil contaminated with zinc around an old mine and in nearby uncontaminated fields. Mission 6

Plants built for their habitat

  • Xerophytes live where water is scarce.
  • Leaf curvature (rolled leaves); sunken stomata; hairs: trap humid air near the stomata.
  • Thick cuticle; reduced surface area (e.g. spines instead of leaves).
  • Succulent tissue stores water; deep roots reach water far down.
  • Hydrophytes live in water. Example: water lily.
  • Stomata mainly on the upper surface of floating leaves, in contact with air.
  • Aerenchyma: large air spaces; keeps leaves afloat and carries oxygen to submerged parts.
  • Say: "trapped humid air lowers the water vapour gradient" — not: "rolled leaves collect water".
Quick check
?In dry, windy weather the leaves of marram grass roll inwards, so that the stomata, which lie in grooves on the inner surface, open into an enclosed space lined with hairs. How does this help marram grass to survive on a sand dune, where water drains away quickly?
Mission check

?Arctic foxes have much smaller ears than fennec foxes. Which statement best explains how small ears became typical of Arctic foxes?

The fennec's big ears and the Arctic fox's small ones arose the same way: selection acting on inherited variation, in different environments.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Make a guess: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Mission 7

The heart feeds itself

  • Heart muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose.
  • It gets them from the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.
  • Blood inside the chambers does not supply the heart wall.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): coronary arteries narrowed or blocked.
  • Say: "occlusion starves the heart muscle of oxygen" — not: "blood can no longer pass through the chambers".
Mission 7

Atherosclerosis, step by step

  • (1) Endothelial dysfunction: the inner lining is damaged, e.g. by high blood pressure, smoking or high LDL cholesterol.
  • (2) Inflammatory response: white blood cells enter the wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
  • (3) Plaque (atheroma) builds inside the wall: cholesterol, white cells, fibrous tissue, calcium salts. The lumen narrows; the wall stiffens.
  • (4) Narrower, stiffer arteries raise blood pressure, which damages more endothelium.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening and narrowing of arteries by atheroma.
  • Say: "atheroma forms within the artery wall" — not: "fat sticks to the inside like grease in a drainpipe".
Mission 7

From plaque to blockage

  • A plaque can rupture, exposing the tissue beneath.
  • A clot (thrombus) forms in the artery: thrombosis.
  • Plaque plus clot can occlude (block) the artery. Or the clot breaks off and blocks a smaller artery downstream.
Quick check
?A blood clot completely occludes a branch of a coronary artery. What is the direct consequence?
Mission 7

What blockage does

  • Narrowed coronary artery: too little oxygen during exercise → chest pain (angina).
  • Blocked coronary artery: muscle beyond dies → myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Dead muscle is replaced by scar tissue, which does not contract.
  • A clot in an artery to the brain → stroke.
  • Atheroma can weaken an artery wall, which balloons out: an aneurysm. It may burst.
Mission 7

Seeing inside arteries: angiography

  • A thin tube (catheter) is passed into the artery.
  • A contrast dye that X-rays cannot pass through is injected.
  • X-ray images show the lumen.
  • Atherosclerosis / CHD: a narrowed or blocked section.
  • Aneurysm: a bulge in the vessel.
Mission check

?A man has atheroma in a carotid artery, which carries blood to the brain. A plaque ruptures. What is most likely to happen next?

Damage, plaque, rupture, clot, blockage: the same chain causes heart attacks and strokes; only the artery differs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Trachea → bronchi → bronchioles → alveoli; thin, moist, ventilated alveoli wrapped in capillaries give a huge, short diffusion path.
  • Breathing in: diaphragm and external intercostals contract → thorax volume up → pressure down → air in. Breathing out reverses this.

Mission 2

  • Lung diseases cut surface area, lengthen the diffusion distance or reduce ventilation; smoking is a cause of lung cancer, emphysema and chronic bronchitis.

Mission 3

  • A respirometer with an alkali measures O2 uptake: rate = corrected volume ÷ mass ÷ time; without the alkali it shows O2 used − CO2 released.
  • RQ = CO2 produced ÷ O2 consumed: about 1.0 carbohydrate, 0.9 protein, 0.7 lipid; above 1 means some anaerobic respiration.
  • Lipids and amino acids enter respiration mostly via acetyl coenzyme A and the Krebs cycle; lipids give about twice the energy per gram.

Mission 4

  • Phloem sieve tubes (living, no nucleus, sieve plates) and companion cells (many mitochondria) translocate sucrose from sources to sinks, up or down.
  • Ringing, tracers and aphid stylets show sugars move in the phloem, need energy, and flow both ways.

Mission 5

  • Roots pump in mineral ions; water follows by osmosis.
  • The Casparian strip forces water and ions through the symplast at the endodermis.
  • Xerophytes cannot stop water loss while gas exchange goes on; they reduce the gradient, the area and the route.

Mission 6

  • An adaptation is an inherited feature, favoured by natural selection, that improves survival and reproduction in a particular environment.
  • Adaptations are anatomical (or morphological), physiological or behavioural.

Mission 7

  • Atheroma builds inside a damaged artery wall; it narrows the lumen and raises blood pressure.
  • A ruptured plaque triggers a clot; occlusion of a coronary artery starves heart muscle of oxygen.
