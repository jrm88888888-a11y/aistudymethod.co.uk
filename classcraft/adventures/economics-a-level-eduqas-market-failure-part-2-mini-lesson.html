Mission 1

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.

Mission 1

Equality or equity?

  • Equality: everyone has the same: equal incomes, wealth or opportunities.
  • Equity: fairness. What is fair is a value judgement: a normative question.
  • Equality of outcome: the same result for all. Equality of opportunity: the same chances, results may differ.
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same.
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently, e.g. higher incomes pay a higher share in tax.
  • Say: "equity means fairness" — not: "equity means equality". An equitable distribution need not be equal.
Mission 1

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
Mission 1

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • UK disposable household income: Gini 32.9 on the 0–100 scale (financial year ending 2024, ONS).
  • Great Britain household wealth: Gini about 0.6, or 60 on the 0–100 scale (2018–2020, ONS). The richest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth; the poorest half held 9%.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.

Mission 1

Why is income unequal?

  • Human capital: education and skills raise productivity and pay.
  • Ownership of wealth: land, property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends; inheritance passes them on.
  • Inequality of opportunity: family background affects schooling, contacts and health.
  • Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and others.
  • Power: bargaining power of unions, employers with monopsony power, top executives.
  • Tax and benefit policy: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; regressive taxes widen them.
  • Globalisation and technological change: raise the pay of scarce skills; some routine jobs move abroad or are automated.
Mission 1

Inequality as a market failure, and its effects

  • Markets allocate goods to those willing and able to pay. A market can be efficient and still leave some with very little.
  • So an unequal distribution can be seen as a market failure: the market fails to produce an equitable outcome. Whether it does is a value judgement.
  • Effects:
    • individuals: worse health, education and housing for those on low incomes
    • growth: big rewards can create incentives to work and invest; but very unequal societies may waste talent, and an OECD study (2014) found rising inequality lowered growth in OECD countries
    • living standards and social stability: high inequality is linked to lower trust and more political tension
  • The evidence on growth points both ways, so economists disagree on how much inequality harms it.
Mission 1Evaluate

How much inequality is a problem?

It depends on:

  • Values. Fairness is a normative judgement; people disagree on how equal outcomes should be.
  • Causes. Inequality from effort and skill is viewed differently from inequality from inheritance, luck or discrimination.
  • Mobility. Wide gaps matter more if children of poor families stay poor.
  • Living standards of the poorest. Rising inequality with rising absolute incomes differs from falling incomes at the bottom.
  • Measurement. Income or wealth? Before or after taxes and benefits? Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income.

Mission 2

Why crop prices jump

  • Primary commodities: raw materials and crops, e.g. cocoa, coffee, wheat, copper, oil.
  • Their supply shifts often and without warning: weather, pests, disease, strikes at mines.
  • Once a crop is harvested, supply is almost fixed for the season: a near-vertical short-run supply curve.
  • Demand is price inelastic: buyers cut back little when price rises, so the demand curve is drawn steep.
  • Inelastic demand + supply shifts = large price swings for small changes in quantity.
  • Say: "a bumper harvest shifts supply to the right; price falls and there is an extension of demand" — not: "demand increases".
Mission 2

Why both curves are so steep

  • Example: a new cocoa tree takes several years to bear its first pods, so a high cocoa price cannot quickly raise output.
  • Both PED and PES are usually less than 1 in the short run (ignoring the minus sign for PED).
  • In the long run both become more elastic: farmers replant, buyers find substitutes.
Mission 2

Worked example: a bigger harvest, less money

  • Illustrative data: a world market for a crop. Quantity in thousand tonnes; price in $ per tonne.
  • Normal harvest: 90 sold at $40. Revenue = 40 × 90 = $3,600 thousand.
  • Bumper harvest: 100. Buyers take the extra only if the price falls to $20.
  • Revenue = 20 × 100 = $2,000 thousand.
  • %ΔQ = (100 − 90) ÷ 90 × 100 = +11.1%. %ΔP = (20 − 40) ÷ 40 × 100 = −50%.
  • PED = 11.1 ÷ −50 = −0.22: price inelastic.
  • Poor harvest: 80, price rises to $60. Revenue = 60 × 80 = $4,800 thousand.
  • Inelastic demand: price changes by a bigger percentage than quantity, so revenue moves the same way as price and the opposite way to the harvest. A bigger crop earns less; a smaller crop earns more.
  • Shortcut: %ΔP ≈ %ΔQ ÷ PED. With PED −0.22, an 11% rise in quantity needs about a 50% price fall.

Mission 2

Why volatile prices matter

  • Producers: farm incomes rise and crash from year to year; planning and borrowing become risky, so investment falls.
  • Signals: planting decisions are made on last year's price, so a high price can lead to over-planting and a later crash.
  • So volatile prices are a market failure: price signals give a poor guide to future scarcity, and resources are misallocated.
  • Countries: many low-income countries earn most export revenue from one or two commodities: primary product dependency.
  • UNCTAD calls a country commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its merchandise exports.
  • Export earnings, tax revenue and the exchange rate all swing with the commodity price.
  • Case: in 2024 cocoa futures passed $10,000 per tonne for the first time, after poor harvests in West Africa. Many cocoa farmers in Ghana and Côte d'Ivoire sell at prices set in advance by their governments, so they gained much less than the world price rise.

Mission 2

The buffer stock scheme

  • Buffer stock scheme: an agency holds a stock of a storable commodity and buys or sells it to keep the price inside a band.
  • Floor: the minimum price. Ceiling: the maximum price.
  • Step 1: normal harvest. Price is inside the band. The agency does nothing.
  • Step 2: bumper harvest. The free-market price would fall below the floor. The agency buys the excess supply at the floor price and adds it to the stock.
  • Step 3: poor harvest. The free-market price would rise above the ceiling. The agency sells from its stock at the ceiling price.
  • Needs: a commodity that can be stored, money to buy in good years, and a stock to sell in bad years.

Mission 2

Worked example: three harvests

  • Illustrative data: demand from an earlier screen; band $30–$50.
  • Normal (90): free price $40, inside the band. No action.
  • Bumper (100): free price $20. At the floor of $30, buyers take 95. Agency buys 100 − 95 = 5, costing 5 × 30 = $150 thousand.
  • Poor (80): free price $60. At the ceiling of $50, buyers want 85. Agency sells 85 − 80 = 5, earning 5 × 50 = $250 thousand.
  • Farm revenue without the scheme: $2,000–$4,800 thousand.
  • With the scheme: bumper 30 × 100 = $3,000 thousand; poor 50 × 80 = $4,000 thousand.
  • Incomes are more stable. Here the stock is back where it started, because good and bad years balanced out.

Mission 2

Guaranteed minimum prices for farmers

  • Price stabilisation: reducing swings in price and farm income, e.g. by a buffer stock.
  • Guaranteed minimum price: the government promises to buy a crop at a set price, so the market price cannot fall below it.
  • It works like a buffer stock floor, but with no ceiling and often no plan to sell the stock again.
  • If the guaranteed price is above equilibrium, the state buys a surplus every year and must store, sell abroad or destroy it.
  • Case: India sets minimum support prices for crops such as wheat and rice; a state agency, the Food Corporation of India, buys at these prices and has held large grain stocks.
  • Case: the European Community's price support in the 1970s–80s built up large stores of butter and other farm products.
  • For: secure farm incomes; stable food supply. Against: cost to taxpayers, storage and waste, and incentives to overproduce.
Mission 2Evaluate

Why many schemes collapsed

  • It depends on where the band is set. Nobody knows the long-run equilibrium price. A band set too high means the agency buys year after year: the stock and the bill keep growing.
  • It depends on whether a change is temporary or permanent. A scheme can smooth good and bad years, but not a lasting fall in demand or rise in supply.
  • It depends on storage. Coffee and metals store; fresh fruit does not. Storage costs money every year.
  • It depends on funding. The agency must have money to buy in a run of bumper years.
  • Case: the International Tin Council ran out of funds defending the tin price in October 1985; tin trading on the London Metal Exchange was suspended and the price collapsed.
  • Case: Australia's wool reserve price scheme was suspended in February 1991 with a stockpile of 4.7 million bales, which took until 2001 to sell.
  • Supporters reply: with a band near the long-run average and enough funds, a scheme can reduce income swings for poor producers.

Mission 3: What happens when a government makes something cheaper by law?

Make a guess: A law holds the price of bread below the market price. What is most likely?

Mission 3

A legal top price

  • A maximum price (price ceiling): the highest price that can legally be charged.
  • To have any effect it must be set below the equilibrium price. Then it is binding.
  • Set above equilibrium, it changes nothing: the market price is already lower.
  • Why do governments use them? To make essentials affordable (rents, basic food, fuel) and to protect consumers from sharp price rises.
Mission 3

Why a shortage appears

  • Step 1: market clears at Pe, Qe.
  • Step 2: a ceiling is drawn below Pe.
  • At the lower price, quantity demanded extends to Qd.
  • Quantity supplied contracts to Qs.
  • Step 3: the gap Qd − Qs is excess demand: a shortage.
  • Only Qs is bought and sold. Trade falls from Qe to Qs.
  • Say: "an extension of demand and a contraction of supply" — not: "demand rises and supply falls" (neither curve shifts).
Quick check
?In a market, the equilibrium price is $20. The government sets a maximum price of $25. What happens?
Mission 3

Worked example: how big is the shortage?

  • Illustrative data. Demand: Qd = 100 − 2P. Supply: Qs = 4P − 20 (P in $, Q in thousands).
  • Equilibrium: 100 − 2P = 4P − 20, so 6P = 120, Pe = $20, Qe = 60.
  • Ceiling at $15:
  • Qd = 100 − 2 × 15 = 70.
  • Qs = 4 × 15 − 20 = 40.
  • Shortage = 70 − 40 = 30 thousand.
  • Quantity traded falls from 60 to 40.

Mission 3

Who gets the goods?

  • Price no longer rations, so something else does:
  • Queues (first come, first served), waiting lists, ballots.
  • Ration coupons from the government.
  • Sellers' preference: friends, regular customers, or discrimination.
  • Black (shadow) markets: illegal resale above the ceiling.
  • In an earlier screen, only 40 thousand units are supplied. Buyers would pay up to $30 for the 40-thousandth unit (100 − 2P = 40 gives P = 30), so black-market prices can rise above the old equilibrium of $20.
  • Berlin froze most rents at 2019 levels in 2020. Reports followed of landlords demanding large payments for furniture as a condition of renting. Germany's Constitutional Court struck the law down in 2021.
Mission 3

Winners and losers

  • Assume the scarce goods go to the buyers who value them most.
  • Area B (between Pe and the ceiling, up to Qs): moves from producers to consumers who still buy.
  • Areas C + E (between Qs and Qe): trades that no longer happen. This is the deadweight (welfare) loss.
  • Consumers: gain B, lose C. Those who buy gain; those shut out lose.
  • Producers: lose B and E. Always worse off.
  • If goods go to the wrong buyers (waiting in line, luck), consumers gain less, and the time spent waiting is a further loss.
  • Say: "welfare loss" or "deadweight loss" — not: "the shortage is the loss" (the shortage is a quantity, the loss is an area).

Mission 3

Case: rent control

  • In 1994, San Francisco extended rent control to small, older rental buildings.
  • Covered tenants became nearly 20% more likely to stay at their address.
  • But landlords of covered buildings cut their supply of rental housing by 15% (selling to owner-occupiers, converting to condominiums).
  • Rents across the city rose by an estimated 5.1%: new renters paid more.
  • Over time supply becomes more elastic, so the shortage grows.
  • Landlords may cut maintenance, so quality falls.
  • Tenants stay in homes that no longer suit them: housing is misallocated and workers move less.
  • Supporters point to security: tenants avoid sudden rent rises and can stay in their communities.
Mission 3

Evaluate: does a maximum price help?

It depends on…

  • Elasticity and time: inelastic demand and supply keep the shortage small in the short run. Supply becomes more elastic over the years, so the shortage grows.
  • How far below equilibrium: a small gap may protect buyers at little cost. A large gap creates queues and black markets.
  • Enforcement and rationing: who actually gets the goods, and at what price?
  • Alternatives: subsidies, income support or building more homes raise supply instead of cutting it.
Mission check

A maximum price fixes the gap in price; time decides how big the gap in quantity becomes.

Mission 4

A legal bottom price

  • A minimum price (price floor): the lowest price that can legally be charged.
  • To have an effect it must be set above the equilibrium price (binding).
  • Why do governments use them?
  • Raise producers' incomes, especially farmers, whose prices swing with the weather.
  • Cut consumption of harmful (demerit) goods, such as cheap strong alcohol.
  • A guaranteed minimum price for farm goods: the state promises to buy any output not sold at that price.
  • The minimum wage is a floor in the labour market.
Mission 4

Why a surplus appears

  • Step 1: market clears at Pe, Qe.
  • Step 2: a floor is drawn above Pe.
  • At the higher price, quantity demanded contracts to Qd.
  • Quantity supplied extends to Qs.
  • Step 3: the gap Qs − Qd is excess supply: a surplus.
  • Without other action, only Qd is sold. Trade falls from Qe to Qd.
  • Say: "a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls" (the demand curve has not moved).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Demand: Qd = 100 − 2P. Supply: Qs = 4P − 20 (P in $, Q in thousands). Equilibrium is $20 and 60 thousand. A minimum price of $25 is set. What is the surplus?
Mission 4

What happens to the surplus?

  • Left unsold: producers carry the cost of goods they cannot sell. Some sell illegally below the floor.
  • Bought by the government (a guaranteed price): taxpayers pay. The stock must be stored, destroyed, given away or sold abroad cheaply.
  • Production limits: output is capped so less surplus is made.
  • Real case: the US dairy price support programme bought dairy products that did not sell. By December 1981, more than 560 million pounds of cheese were in government warehouses. The President ordered 30 million pounds given free to people in need.
  • Pounds here are a weight (lb): 560 million lb is about 254,000 tonnes.
  • Say: "excess supply" or "surplus" — not: "oversupply means supply has shifted".
Mission 4

Worked example: a guaranteed price

  • Illustrative data: same market as an earlier screen. Floor $25, and the government buys the surplus.
  • Qd = 50, Qs = 80, surplus = 30 thousand.
  • Government spending = $25 × 30 = $750 thousand.
  • Consumers' spending: 20 × 60 = $1200 → 25 × 50 = $1250 thousand (they pay more for less).
  • Producers' revenue: $1200 → 25 × 80 = $2000 thousand.
  • Of that $2000, consumers pay 1250 and taxpayers 750.

Mission 4

Case: minimum unit pricing for alcohol

  • Scotland set a minimum price per unit of alcohol: 50p from 2018, raised to 65p on 30 September 2024.
  • A unit is 10 ml of pure alcohol. Units = volume (litres) × % alcohol.
  • 70 cl bottle of 40% spirit: 0.7 × 40 = 28 units. Minimum price = 28 × 65p = £18.20 (it was £14.00 at 50p).
  • It mainly raises the price of cheap, strong drinks.
  • Estimates for the first years (Public Health Scotland, 2023), compared with no policy: alcohol sales down about 3%, deaths wholly caused by alcohol down 13.4%, hospital admissions down 4.1%.
  • Unlike a tax, the extra money goes to retailers and producers, not the government.
  • Debate: supporters say it targets harmful drinking; critics say it hits low-income drinkers hardest and rewards retailers.
Quick check

Mission 4

Winners and losers

  • Consumers lose: they pay more and buy less.
  • Producers who sell gain: the higher price on Qd (a transfer from consumers).
  • If the state buys the surplus: producers gain much more, taxpayers pay, and resources are used to make goods nobody buys at that price. Large deadweight loss.
  • If nobody buys it: producers lose on the unsold output, and a deadweight loss appears between Qd and Qe.
  • Allocative inefficiency: at Qs, the cost of the last unit is above what buyers value it at.
  • But for a demerit good with external costs, the market quantity may be too high. A floor that cuts consumption towards the social optimum can raise welfare.

Mission 4

Production quotas and stabilisation

  • A production quota limits how much each producer may make. Supply cannot go past the quota, so price stays higher without a stockpile.
  • Example: the EU capped milk output with quotas from 1984 until 2015, after years of dairy surpluses.
  • Drawbacks: quotas must be shared out and policed; efficient farms cannot grow; output may still be too high or too low.
  • Price stabilisation: buying in good harvests and selling in bad ones keeps prices in a band (buffer stocks, Mission 2).
Mission 4

Evaluate: does a minimum price help?

It depends on…

  • Elasticity: inelastic demand means a small surplus and higher spending by consumers. Elastic demand and supply mean a big surplus.
  • What happens to the surplus: storage and dumping abroad cost money and can hurt farmers in other countries.
  • The aim: to support farm incomes, or cut harmful consumption? A tax raises the price too, and the money goes to the government.
  • Time: guaranteed prices encourage more output each year, so surpluses can grow.
  • Who gains: large producers sell most, so they gain most.

Mission 5

Equity is not equality

  • Equality: everyone gets the same (income, wealth or opportunity).
  • Equity: a fair distribution. What is fair is a value judgement (normative).
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same (same income → same tax).
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently (higher income → pay more).
  • Say: "an equitable distribution" (fair) — not: "an equal distribution", unless you mean identical shares.
Mission 5

Why redistribute?

  • In a free market, income depends on the factors you own and what they earn: wages, rent, interest, profit.
  • Some own little: low skills, no property, unable to work. Their market income may be very low.
  • Many economists call a very unequal outcome a market failure: markets can be efficient yet produce an inequitable result.
  • Redistribution: government moves income or wealth from richer to poorer households, using:
    • taxes, transfers and benefits, state-provided services, and laws on pay.
Mission 5

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Compare the average tax rate (tax ÷ income) at different incomes:
    • Progressive: average rate rises as income rises.
    • Proportional: average rate stays the same.
    • Regressive: average rate falls as income rises.
  • Marginal tax rate: the rate on the next unit of income.
  • Illustrative data. Tax bands: 0% on the first 12,000; 20% from 12,000 to 50,000; 40% above 50,000.
    • Income 30,000: tax = 20% × 18,000 = 3,600. Average rate = 3,600 ÷ 30,000 = 12%. Marginal rate 20%.
    • Income 80,000: tax = 20% × 38,000 + 40% × 30,000 = 7,600 + 12,000 = 19,600. Average rate = 24.5%. Marginal rate 40%.
  • Average rate rises with income: progressive.
  • Taxes on spending (e.g. VAT) are usually regressive relative to income: poorer households spend a bigger share of income.

Mission 5

Transfers: universal or means-tested?

  • Transfer payments: money paid by government with nothing produced in return: pensions, unemployment and disability benefits, child benefit.
  • Universal benefit: paid to everyone in a group, whatever their income (e.g. a state pension for all over an age).
  • Means-tested benefit: paid only to those with low income or wealth (e.g. the UK's Universal Credit).

Mission 5

The poverty trap

  • Poverty trap: as a low earner earns more, benefits are withdrawn and taxes start, so they keep little of the rise.
  • Effective marginal tax rate (EMTR): share of an extra unit of earnings lost to tax and benefit withdrawal.
  • Case: UK, Universal Credit. For each extra £1 of net earnings, benefit falls by 55p (the taper rate, cut from 63% in November 2021).
  • Worked example: a basic-rate taxpayer on Universal Credit earns £100 more.
    • Income tax 20% + National Insurance 8% = £28. Net earnings rise by £72.
    • Benefit withdrawn: 55% × £72 = £39.60.
    • Kept: £72 − £39.60 = £32.40.
    • EMTR = 1 − 0.72 × 0.45 = 67.6%.
  • Say: combine the rates by multiplying what is kept (0.72 × 0.45) — not: by adding them (28% + 55% = 83%), since the taper applies to net earnings.

  • Takeaway: a higher taper flattens the line (stronger trap) but ends benefit sooner (cheaper). A lower taper helps work incentives but stretches benefit to higher earners.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker pays income tax at 20% on extra earnings. A means-tested benefit is withdrawn at 60% of each extra unit of net (after-tax) earnings. What is the worker's effective marginal tax rate?
Mission check

?A government wants to weaken the poverty trap for low-income working families on a means-tested benefit. Which change would do this most directly?

The poverty trap is about the share of each extra unit that is lost, not the level of support. Lower the tax and withdrawal rates on the next unit, and work pays more, though usually at a higher cost to government.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Can a tax take more money from richer people and still hit poorer people harder?

Make a guess: Can a tax take more money from richer people and still hit poorer people harder?

Mission 6

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is levied on.
    • Income tax, corporation tax (on company profits), taxes on wealth.
  • UK direct taxes also include National Insurance contributions, capital gains tax and inheritance tax.
  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government, but can pass some or all of it on to buyers in higher prices.
    • VAT (a sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports.
  • How much of an indirect tax buyers bear depends on elasticity.
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Mission 6

Why tax, and what makes a good tax

  • Reasons to tax:
    • raise revenue for public spending
    • redistribute income and wealth
    • correct market failure (taxes on pollution, tobacco)
    • manage aggregate demand (fiscal policy)
  • Adam Smith's canons of a good tax (1776):
    • equity: in line with ability to pay
    • certainty: clear what, when and how much
    • convenience: easy to pay
    • economy: cheap to collect
  • Modern additions: efficiency (few distortions to choices) and flexibility (easy to change).
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 6

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Judge a tax by the share of income it takes, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the share of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the share stays the same at every income.
  • Regressive: the share falls as income rises.
  • A regressive tax can still take more money from richer people.
  • Progressive taxes plus benefits for low-income households narrow the gap in incomes (redistribution).
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a larger proportion of a low income" — not: "poor people pay more money".
Mission 6

Average and marginal tax rates

  • Average tax rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
  • Marginal tax rate (MRT) = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100: the tax on the next unit of income.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income.
  • Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • Regressive: ART falls as income rises.
  • Moving into a higher band raises the rate only on income inside that band.
Mission 6

Worked example: income tax bands

  • Illustrative data.
Income bandTax rate
0 – 10,0000% (allowance)
10,001 – 40,00020%
Above 40,00040%
  • Income 60,000:
    • first 10,000: 0
    • next 30,000 × 20% = 6,000
    • last 20,000 × 40% = 8,000
    • total tax = 14,000
  • ART = 14,000 ÷ 60,000 × 100 = 23.3%.
  • Earn 1,000 more: tax rises by 400. MRT = 400 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 40%.
  • Income 30,000: tax 4,000, ART 13.3%, MRT 20%.
  • ART rises from 13.3% to 23.3% as income rises: progressive.

  • A flat tax: one rate on all income above a tax-free allowance.
  • Illustrative data: 20% on income above 12,000.

  • With an allowance, a flat tax is mildly progressive. With no allowance, it is proportional.
  • Real case: Estonia introduced a flat income tax in 1994; several central and eastern European countries followed.
  • Supporters: simple, cheap to collect, keeps the reward for earning more. Critics: less redistribution than several rising bands.
Mission 6

The Laffer curve

  • Tax revenue = tax rate × tax base (the income or spending being taxed).
  • At a 0% rate, revenue is zero. At 100%, there is little point earning declared income, so revenue is close to zero.
  • In between, revenue reaches a maximum at some rate, t*.
  • Below t*: a higher rate raises revenue.
  • Above t*: the tax base shrinks so much that a higher rate lowers revenue. A cut could raise revenue.
  • The base shrinks through less work and investment, more avoidance and evasion, and people or firms moving abroad.
  • Nobody knows exactly where t* is. Estimates differ, and most economists think most tax rates are below it.
  • Real case: in 2012 HMRC estimated that cutting the UK top income tax rate from 50% to 45% would cost only about £100 million a year, because high earners respond strongly to the top rate. HMRC stressed the estimate was very uncertain.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a tax rate is currently 70%, and the revenue-maximising rate is 55%. The government cuts the rate to 60%. According to the Laffer curve, what happens to tax revenue?
Mission 6Evaluate

Should a country make its taxes more progressive?

It depends on:

  • How people respond: if high earners cut work, move or avoid tax, revenue may rise less than planned.
  • Value judgements about equity: how much inequality is acceptable is a normative question.
  • Which taxes change: raising income tax rates at the top differs from cutting regressive taxes on spending.
  • Administration: complex systems cost more to run and leave more room for avoidance.
Mission check

?A local council charges every adult the same fixed amount each year, whatever their income. How is this tax best described?

Judge a tax by the share of income it takes. A tax can take more money from richer people and still be regressive.

Mission 7

When the cure is worse

  • Markets can fail (earlier missions). Governments step in with taxes, subsidies, rules and provision.
  • But intervention can also go wrong.
  • Government failure: intervention that causes a net welfare loss compared with leaving the market alone. Resources end up misallocated.
  • A policy that has costs, or does not fix the problem fully, is not automatically a failure.
  • The test: is society better or worse off overall than with no intervention?
  • Say: "government failure = intervention leads to a net welfare loss" — not: "any policy that costs money".
Mission 7

Inadequate information

  • To set a Pigouvian tax, the government needs the MEC. It rarely knows it.
  • Also called information gaps.
  • Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q; true MEC = 20.
  • No tax: Qm = 40; welfare loss = 100.
  • The government overestimates the harm and sets a tax of 50.
  • New supply: 70 + Q. 100 − Q = 70 + Q, so Q = 15. Q* is 30.
  • Now underproduction. At Q = 15: MSB = 85, MSC = 55, a gap of 30.
  • New welfare loss = ½ × (30 − 15) × 30 = 225.
  • 225 > 100: society is worse off than with no tax. Government failure.

Illustrative data: as the previous screen. MEC = 20; the market alone gives a welfare loss of 100.

  • Takeaway: a tax a little wrong still helps. A tax far too high can do more harm than the externality.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 60 − Q; MPC = Q. The MEC is 10 at every output, so the market output is 30, the social optimum is 25 and the welfare loss with no tax is 25. The government sets a tax of 30 per unit. What is the new welfare loss, and is this government failure?
Mission 7

Distortions of price signals

  • Prices tell producers what to make and consumers what to buy.
  • Taxes, subsidies and price controls change prices. Set badly, they send the wrong signals.
  • Also called price distortion or market distortion.
  • Real world: the European Community's farm policy (CAP) guaranteed farmers high prices and bought up what did not sell.
    • Farmers produced more than consumers wanted.
    • Surpluses filled stores: "butter mountains" and "wine lakes", paid for by taxpayers.
    • From 1984, milk quotas were brought in to limit dairy output.
  • One intervention led to another to fix its side-effects.
Mission 7

Unintended consequences

  • People and firms respond to a policy, sometimes in ways nobody planned.
  • Real world: Mexico City, 1989, "Hoy No Circula" (No Driving Today).
    • Most cars banned from driving one weekday a week, by the last digit of the number plate.
    • Aim: cleaner air.
    • A 2008 study found no evidence that air quality improved.
    • Many households bought an extra car, often an older, more polluting one, to drive on their banned day.
  • Other examples: high taxes on cigarettes can encourage smuggling; rent controls can shrink the supply of rented homes.
Mission 7

Administrative costs

  • Every policy must be designed, run, monitored and enforced.
  • These administrative costs use scarce resources: staff, inspections, IT, legal cases.
  • Firms bear compliance costs too: paperwork, reporting, meeting standards.
  • Illustrative data: a tax on a pollutant removes a welfare loss of 100 a year.
    • Collecting the tax and checking emissions costs 120 a year.
    • Net effect: 100 − 120 = −20. Society is worse off: government failure.
  • Costs can exceed gains when the externality is small, or polluters are many and small.
Mission 7

Conflicting objectives

  • Governments pursue many goals at once: cleaner air, low prices, jobs, tax revenue, re-election.
  • A policy that serves one goal can harm another, so it may be set at the wrong level, or dropped.
  • Real world: the UK planned yearly inflation-linked rises in fuel duty. They were cancelled every year from 2011 to 2025, and duty was cut by 5p a litre in 2022, after fuel prices jumped.
  • Tobacco taxes aim to cut smoking, yet they also raise revenue that depends on people still smoking.
  • Say: "conflicting objectives" — not: "the government did not care". Both goals may be valid.
Mission 7

Capture and political incentives

  • Regulatory capture: a regulator comes to serve the industry it regulates, not the public.
    • Close contacts; staff moving between regulator and industry; lobbying.
  • Asymmetric information in regulation: the firm knows its true costs and harm; the regulator does not. Firms can overstate the cost of meeting rules.
  • Political incentives: politicians face elections. They may favour quick, visible gains over long-term benefits (short-termism), or respond to strong lobby groups.
  • These are why some economists argue that intervention should be limited to clear, large market failures; others argue for better-designed institutions instead.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a city charges households for rubbish by weight. Households respond as planned and throw away less, a gain to society worth 2 million a year. Weighing every bin, sending bills and chasing unpaid ones costs 3 million a year. Which cause of government failure does this show?
Mission 7Evaluate

Market failure or government failure?

It depends on:

  • The size of the market failure. A large externality can justify intervention even if the policy is imperfect.
  • Information. Where the MEC can be measured, policy can be set closer to the optimum.
  • Flexibility. A tax can be adjusted as evidence comes in; a large project or a ban is harder to undo.
  • Effectiveness. Does the policy change behaviour? Inelastic demand can make a tax raise revenue but cut little harm.
  • Consequences for stakeholders. Who gains and who loses: consumers, producers, workers, taxpayers, the government, third parties.
  • Government failure is a reason to design policy carefully, not proof that intervention never works.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a market for private bus services gives too few journeys. The welfare loss is 300 a year. The government brings in a subsidy. Afterwards, the welfare loss is 80 a year and the subsidy scheme costs 150 a year to run. Has government failure occurred?

Government failure is judged against the market without intervention, counting every cost of the policy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 2

  • Primary commodity prices are volatile: supply shifts often, and both PED and PES are low.
  • With inelastic demand, a bigger harvest can mean lower revenue.
  • A buffer stock buys at the floor in good years and sells at the ceiling in bad years; it fails if the band is set above the long-run price or funds run out.

Mission 3

  • A binding maximum price sits below equilibrium. Quantity demanded extends, quantity supplied contracts: a shortage.
  • Non-price rationing and black markets follow. Producers lose, some consumers gain, and a deadweight loss appears.

Mission 4

  • A binding minimum price sits above equilibrium. Quantity demanded contracts, quantity supplied extends: a surplus.
  • Someone pays for the surplus: producers (unsold goods) or taxpayers (guaranteed prices).
  • Consumers pay more; sellers keep the higher price.

Mission 5

  • Equity is fairness, a value judgement; equality means identical shares.
  • Progressive taxes: the average tax rate rises with income.
  • Means-tested benefits target the poor but create a poverty trap: a high effective marginal tax rate.

Mission 6

  • Direct taxes fall on income and wealth. Indirect taxes fall on spending.
  • Progressive, proportional or regressive: judged by the share of income paid (ART), not the amount.
  • ART = total tax ÷ income. MRT = tax on the next unit of income.
  • Laffer curve: above the revenue-maximising rate, a lower rate can raise revenue.

Mission 7

  • Government failure: intervention that causes a net welfare loss compared with the market outcome.
  • Causes: inadequate information, price distortion, unintended consequences, administrative costs, conflicting objectives, regulatory capture and political incentives.
