Aspects of tragedy

This mini-lesson covers Paper 1: Literary genres — Option 1A, Aspects of tragedy. It is a genre paper, not a book paper: you are examined on how well you can read any text through the lens of tragedy.

  • Paper 1 structure: Section A — passage-based question on the Shakespeare text (25). Section B — essay on the Shakespeare text (25). Section C — essay linking two texts (25).
  • Your texts: one Shakespeare text, a second drama text, and one further text — at least one written pre-1900.
  • Remember the either/or: Paper 1 is Aspects of tragedy OR Aspects of comedy. You sit one of them, never both.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Named texts appear only as swappable exemplars — the skills are what the exam rewards, and set-text lists change. Press Start when you are ready.

Genre · the classical model

Aristotle and the shape of tragedy

Tragedy is the oldest sustained genre in Western literature, and the vocabulary the exam expects still comes from Aristotle's Poetics (4th century BC), written about Greek tragic drama.

  • Hamartia — the error, mistake or fatal flaw that sets the fall in motion. Note the ambiguity: is it a moral defect, or a mistake of judgement? Good essays argue this, rather than assuming.
  • Hubris — overreaching pride that ignores limits (human, divine, social).
  • Peripeteia — the reversal, the point at which the action swings against the protagonist.
  • Anagnorisis — recognition: the protagonist finally sees what the audience has seen for some time.
  • Catharsis — the purging or clarification of pity and fear in the audience.
  • Nemesis — the retribution that completes the pattern.

Aristotle also expects a protagonist of elevated status, so that the fall has public consequence: when a king falls, a kingdom falls with him. That assumption is exactly what later writers attack.

Use the terms as tools, not decoration. Naming peripeteia earns nothing. Showing where the reversal falls in the structure, and what the dramatist gains by placing it there, is AO2.

Genre · classical vs domestic

Classical tragedy and domestic tragedy

AQA wants you to consider what kind of tragedy you are reading. Two broad types:

  • Classical / heroic tragedy — an elevated protagonist (king, general, prince), a public world, a fall that shakes the state. Fate, gods or a cosmic order press on the action.
  • Domestic tragedy — an ordinary protagonist in a private, familial, economic world. The pressures are social: money, class, work, marriage, respectability.

The pivot between them is a genuine argument in criticism. In his 1949 essay Tragedy and the Common Man, Arthur Miller argued that the common person is as apt a subject for tragedy as kings — that the tragic feeling is provoked whenever a character is ready to lay down life to secure a sense of personal dignity.

Against that, critics in the mid-twentieth century (George Steiner's The Death of Tragedy is the standard reference) argued that true tragedy requires a metaphysical order to offend against — and that a secular, therapeutic modern world can only produce pathos, not tragedy.

This is AO5 in its purest form. Two defensible readings of the same text. Do not report the debate — enter it, and let your text arbitrate.

Genre · the tragic ensemble

Hero, villain, victim — and the fatal cost

Tragedy is a system of roles, not a solo. AQA asks you to look at:

  • The tragic hero/heroine — status, virtues, and the flaw or error. Watch for a protagonist who is both agent and victim of the plot.
  • The tragic villain or opponent — the figure who accelerates the fall. Ask whether the villain causes the tragedy or merely exposes a weakness already there. The strongest answers argue the second.
  • The tragic victim — the innocent who suffers. The suffering of the guiltless is what makes tragedy feel unjust rather than merely deserved, and it is where readers most often locate the genre's moral protest.
  • Fate, chance and free will — is the outcome authored by the character, or by a universe indifferent or hostile to them?

Exemplar, not syllabus: the texts named below are only illustrations of the method. AQA rotates and revises its set-text lists, so swap in whatever your class is studying. The examiner rewards your handling of the genre and the AOs, never your loyalty to a particular book.

Swappable exemplars. A Shakespearean general destroyed by a manipulator; a mid-century salesman destroyed by an economic dream; a Victorian heroine destroyed by a sexual double standard. Different worlds, same architecture — that is exactly the connection Section C rewards.

AO2 · structure

The tragic structure: order, disorder, catastrophe

AO2 is not just imagery. Structure is a method. The classic tragic arc:

  1. Order — an established world, often deceptively secure, with the flaw already visible.
  2. Disruption — a temptation, a mistake, an intruder, a prophecy.
  3. Rising action / entanglement — the protagonist commits, and each attempt to escape tightens the trap. This produces the tragic sense of inevitability.
  4. Peripeteia — the reversal.
  5. Catastrophe — suffering, usually death.
  6. Restoration — a diminished order returns; someone survives to speak the epilogue.

Ask structural questions in the exam: where does the passage fall in that arc? What does it foreshadow? Is the pace accelerating? Has the protagonist's freedom of action narrowed since the last act?

Passage-based questions (Section A) are structural questions in disguise. Always place the extract in the arc before you analyse a single word. The phrase to have ready is: at this point in the play, the protagonist can still turn back — but the language has already stopped letting him.

AO2 · language

The language of tragedy

Tragedy has a recognisable verbal texture. Look for:

  • Elevated register and blank verse — and, crucially, its collapse. When a verse-speaking hero drops into broken prose, the form is enacting the disintegration.
  • Dramatic irony — the audience knows; the character does not. This is the engine of pity.
  • Soliloquy — privileged access to a mind in the act of self-deceiving.
  • Imagery patterns — disease, poison, animals, darkness, the natural order disordered. Track a chain of images across the text, not within one line.
  • Prolepsis / foreshadowing — prophecy, omen, a casual line that later becomes unbearable.
  • Bathos and the anticlimactic — in domestic tragedy, catastrophe often arrives in flat, ordinary speech, and the ordinariness is the point.

Do not import quotations you are unsure of. A precisely described method with no quotation beats a misremembered quotation every time. Examiners mark what the text does, not how much you can recite.

AO3 · context

Contexts of production and reception

AO3 has two halves, and most candidates only write one.

  • Production — the world the text came out of: the Jacobean anxiety about succession and damnation; the post-war American economy; Victorian sexual double standards.
  • Reception — the world the text is read into. A tragedy of a betrayed outsider reads differently in 1604, in 1930s Britain, and after the civil-rights movement. Meaning is not fixed at the moment of writing.

The examiner's test: does the context change the reading? If you could delete the context sentence and the analysis would be unaffected, it was decoration. Weld it: because contemporary audiences believed X, this line would have landed as Y — whereas a modern audience is more likely to hear Z.

Never bolt a paragraph of history on the end. Context is a lens you look through, not a hat you put on.

AO5 · interpretations

Reading tragedy through theory

AO5 is worth serious marks and is where good students separate from safe ones. The productive lenses for tragedy:

  • Feminist — who is permitted to be a tragic subject, and who is only ever the tragic object? Female suffering in tragedy is often a spectacle staged for a male protagonist's development. Whose death is mourned in the closing speech, and whose is not mentioned?
  • Marxist — tragedy as the collision of an individual with an economic order. Raymond Williams argued in Modern Tragedy that we should treat suffering caused by social systems as genuinely tragic, not merely political.
  • Post-colonial — the outsider, the racial other, the empire's edge. Is the protagonist a tragic hero, or an exotic figure whose destruction reassures a metropolitan audience?
  • Psychoanalytic — the flaw relocated inward: repression, desire, the return of what was denied.

Discipline for AO5: a critical lens must produce a textual claim. Not a Marxist critic would say the play is about class, but a Marxist reading notices that every one of the hero's decisions is priced — and that the play never lets him make a choice that money has not already narrowed.

AO4 · Section C

Comparison: the linked essay

Section C links two texts. AO4 is not a list of similarities. The examiner is looking for an argument in which the two texts illuminate each other.

A workable comparative method:

  1. Lead with the genre proposition, not with a text: Tragedy makes suffering intelligible by giving it a cause — and both texts finally refuse that comfort.
  2. Interleave. Argue in genre-paragraphs (the flaw; the villain; the ending), each drawing on both texts, rather than doing text A for two pages then text B.
  3. Prize the productive difference. Similarity is cheap; the marks are in the divergence — where one text has a cosmic order to offend and the other has only a mortgage.
  4. Let the older text be strange. The pre-1900 text is not a rough draft of the modern one.

Sentence stem worth stealing: Where [text A] locates the catastrophe in a flaw of character, [text B] relocates it in a flaw of the society — and in doing so asks whether the tragic hero was ever anything more than a way of not blaming the world.

Exam craft

Section A: the passage-based question

Section A gives you an extract from the Shakespeare text and asks how it presents an aspect of tragedy. A method that works under time pressure:

  1. Place it (one sentence): where in the tragic arc does this fall, and what has just become impossible?
  2. Argue a line: the passage is doing one main thing to the audience.
  3. Three or four analytical paragraphs, each: method → effect → aspect of tragedy → (where it earns it) context or interpretation.
  4. Move around the passage, including its end — weak answers run out of time at line 20 of 40.
  5. Look outward once: how does this moment set up, or pay off, the whole tragic structure?

Time discipline. Three sections, 25 marks each — they are worth the same. The single commonest way to lose a grade is a magnificent Section A and a rushed Section C.

Recap

The big ideas to take into the exam

Paper 1 (Option 1A): Section A passage on Shakespeare (25) · Section B essay on Shakespeare (25) · Section C linked essay on two texts (25). Either/or with Aspects of comedy.

Classical vocabulary: hamartia · hubris · peripeteia · anagnorisis · catharsis · nemesis — used as tools, not labels

Types: classical/heroic (elevated, public, fated) vs domestic (ordinary, social, economic) — and the Miller vs Steiner argument between them

Roles: tragic hero (agent and victim) · villain/opponent (exposes rather than causes) · innocent victim · fate and free will

AO2: structure = order, disruption, entanglement, peripeteia, catastrophe, diminished restoration; form as meaning (verse collapsing into prose)

AO3: production AND reception — meanings change as readers change

AO5: feminist, Marxist, post-colonial, psychoanalytic readings — each must produce a textual claim

AO4: interleaved genre-paragraphs; hunt the productive difference, not the easy similarity
