Mission 1

Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Make a guess: Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Tragedy · Aristotle and the tragic heroScreen 1

Aristotle's definition

In the Poetics (4th century BCE), Aristotle built a theory of tragedy from the Greek plays he knew. In S. H. Butcher's 1895 translation:

  • Tragedy is "an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude;"
  • It works "through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions." This purgation is catharsis.
  • Plot matters most: it is "the soul of a tragedy".
  • We pity suffering that is undeserved, and fear for someone like ourselves.

Tragedy · Aristotle and the tragic heroScreen 3

Four terms from Aristotle

The hero is "not eminently good and just", and falls "not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty". Aristotle's own example is Sophocles' Oedipus.

Tragedy · Aristotle and the tragic heroScreen 6

Bradley's tragic hero

A. C. Bradley, Shakespearean Tragedy (1904):

  • Tragedy is "essentially a tale of suffering and calamity conducting to death", centred on "a man in high estate".
  • Its centre is "action issuing from character, or in character issuing in action."
  • The hero's one-sidedness is "the fundamental tragic trait": "It is a fatal gift, but it carries with it a touch of greatness."
  • The central feeling is "the impression of waste".
  • Later critics objected that he treats characters as real people.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why do audiences pay to watch a good person destroyed?

Aristotle's answer is catharsis: watching a good but flawed person fall through an error arouses pity and fear, and the play shapes those feelings into release and understanding. Bradley adds that we feel the waste of something great. Critics still argue about what catharsis means, so treat it as a debate, not a fact.

Shakespearean tragedy · hero and worldScreen 7

The critics divide

  • The hero: Bradley sees tragedy as "action issuing from character". He rejects the old view that Hamlet is "the tragedy of reflection"; Hamlet's delay comes from "a state of profound melancholy".
  • The world: Coleridge saw in Iago "the motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity". Gloucester's "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods," blames the gods.
  • The audience: Johnson (1765) was "so shocked by Cordelia's death" he could hardly reread the ending.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Othello, Lear, Hamlet: is the flaw in the hero or the world?

Both. Shakespeare gives each hero a weakness (Lear's rashness, Othello's credulity, Hamlet's delay) and sets it in a world that exploits it (Iago, Goneril and Regan, Claudius's crime). The debate is about the balance, and critics from Coleridge to Bradley disagree, so argue it play by play.

Mission 3

Can a novel or a poem be a tragedy?

Make a guess: Can a novel or a poem be a tragedy?

Tragedy · novels and narrative poemsScreen 1

Tragedy off the stage

  • AQA contrasts classical tragedy, about public figures like Lear, with domestic tragedy, about ordinary people like Tess.
  • In a novel or narrative poem, a narrator shapes our pity and fear, much as a chorus might.
  • In his 1892 preface Hardy quotes Gloucester: "As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods;".
  • The aspects survive the change of form: a flawed protagonist, suffering, fate, catastrophe.
Tragedy · novels and narrative poemsScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Paper 1 Section C (closed book): one essay question linking two texts, 25 marks. You usually write on your two non-Shakespeare texts: one must be a drama (Richard II or Death of a Salesman), and one text must be pre-1900.
  • Questions name an aspect of tragedy, such as fate, and ask how far it applies. The absence of an aspect can be as significant as its presence.
  • Essay practice is for class; this mission prepares the connections (AO4).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Can a novel or a poem be a tragedy?

Yes. Tragedy began on the stage, but its aspects (a flawed protagonist, suffering, fate, catastrophe, pity and fear) can be carried by a narrator in a novel or a poem. Hardy makes a farm girl a tragic heroine and names Greek tragedy; Fitzgerald's Nick makes Gatsby's fall seem fated; Keats bends romances towards tragedy, or holds it off.

Mission 4

Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

Make a guess: Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 1

What AO5 asks

  • Every board's AO5: explore literary texts informed by different interpretations.
  • An interpretation is an argued reading of the text: a critic's essay, a director's staging, a theory (feminist, Marxist, post-colonial), or another reader's view.
  • Readings change over time: a speech admired in 1904 can be distrusted in 1937.
  • AO5 rewards debate: testing a reading against the text. A critic's name on its own earns nothing.
A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature B: AO5 counts in every question (about 12%), and questions usually set a view for you to weigh. Theory readings from the AQA Critical Anthology count as interpretations, and drive the NEA.
Extract A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 3

Othello's last speech

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 4

Two readings of the speech

  • A. C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) took Othello at his word: "not easily jealous" is "perfectly just", and at the close "the Othello of the Council-chamber and the quay of Cyprus has returned".
  • T. S. Eliot (1927) disagreed: Othello is consoling himself, thinking less of what he did than of how he'll be remembered.
  • F. R. Leavis (1937) went further: Iago succeeds fast because Othello's self-ideal makes him ready to believe.
  • The speech supports both sides: that makes it a debate.

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 6

Using a critic, not dropping one

  1. State the view briefly in your own words, and say whose it is.
  2. Test it against precise evidence from the text.
  3. Qualify it: what does it explain, and what does it miss?
  4. Decide your own position. "Yes, but…" is a position.
  • You don't have to pick a side. You do have to weigh the sides.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Aristotle's Poetics defines tragedy as a serious action that works "through pity and fear" towards catharsis; plot is "the soul of a tragedy".
  • Four terms: hamartia (error), peripeteia (reversal), anagnorisis (recognition), catharsis (purgation). The hero is neither saint nor villain.
  • Bradley (1904): "action issuing from character", a "fatal gift" with "a touch of greatness", and "the impression of waste". Use critics as views to test, not verdicts to repeat.
  • AQA reads tragedy as "aspects", so you can also argue about which aspects a text lacks.
  • Shakespeare's tragedies share a pattern: a hero of high status, a flaw or error, a hostile world or villain, a fall that harms many, and order restored by survivors.
  • Hamlet's "The time is out of joint" puts the fault in the world and the burden on the hero; "the pale cast of thought" lets readers find a flaw in him too.
  • Critics divide: Bradley (character, melancholy), Coleridge (Iago's "motiveless malignity"), Johnson (shock at Cordelia's death). Treat each as a view to test.
  • AQA Sections A and B are both on your Shakespeare play: the hero-or-world question works for passage and essay alike.
  • Domestic tragedy puts ordinary people where classical tragedy put kings: Tess is AQA's example.
  • A narrator can do a chorus's work: Hardy's "President of the Immortals" and Nick's "already behind him" both make the fall seem fated.
  • Keats's narrative poems are romances bent towards tragedy; "The Eve of St. Agnes" shows how an absent aspect (the lovers' deaths) can be discussed.
  • Section C links two texts: compare how each keeps or changes a named aspect of tragedy.
  • AO5 asks you to explore a text through different interpretations: critics, productions, theories and other readers, including how readings change over time.
  • Othello's last speech is read as nobility restored (Bradley, 1904) and as self-dramatising (Eliot, 1927; Leavis, 1937): the lines support both, so you weigh them.
  • Use a critic in four moves: state, test, qualify, decide. A name alone earns nothing.
  • AQA marks AO5 in every question; the NEA also needs readings over time.

Key quotations to learn

  • through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions. (Aristotle, Poetics VI, trans. Butcher (1895)) The definition of catharsis.
  • not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty (Aristotle, Poetics XIII, trans. Butcher) The basis of hamartia: the hero is not wicked.
  • Of one that loved not wisely, but too well; (Othello, Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Recognition and self-judgement at the end.
  • I stumbled when I saw. (King Lear, Act 4 Scene 1 (Gloucester)) Anagnorisis through blindness.
  • This central feeling is the impression of waste. (Bradley, Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), Lecture I) Why tragedy moves us: loss of greatness.
  • The time is out of joint. O cursed spite, (Hamlet, Act 1 Scene 5 (Hamlet)) The world is broken, and the task falls on him.
  • Is sicklied o’er with the pale cast of thought, (Hamlet, Act 3 Scene 1 (Hamlet)) Thought weakening action: a flaw in the hero?
  • More sinn’d against than sinning. (King Lear, Act 3 Scene 2 (Lear)) Lear's claim that others are more to blame.
  • As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods, / They kill us for their sport. (King Lear, Act 4 Scene 1 (Gloucester)) A world ruled by cruel gods.
  • Of one not easily jealous, but being wrought, (Othello, Act 5 Scene 2 (Othello)) Othello's own case: worked on from outside.
  • the motive-hunting of a motiveless malignity (Coleridge, note on Othello 1.3 (c.1818–19; Literary Remains, pg8533)) Evil located outside the hero.
  • “Justice” was done, and the President of the Immortals, in Æschylean phrase, had ended his sport with Tess. (Tess, Chapter LIX) Ironic narrator; Greek tragedy claimed for a farm girl.
  • Once victim, always victim—that’s the law! (Tess, Chapter XLVII (Tess)) Tess's own sense of fate.
  • it is what preyed on Gatsby, what foul dust floated in the wake of his dreams (The Great Gatsby, Chapter I (Nick)) Blame placed on the world, not the hero.
  • So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past. (The Great Gatsby, Chapter IX (Nick)) Fate and the past as an undertow.
  • And she forgot the stars, the moon, and sun, (Keats, "Isabella", stanza LIII) Grief that shrinks a world to one pot of basil.
  • At the mere touch of cold philosophy? (Keats, "Lamia", Part II) The force that destroys the romance.
