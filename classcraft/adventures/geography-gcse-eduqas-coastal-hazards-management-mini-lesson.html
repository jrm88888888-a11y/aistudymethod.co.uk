Mission 1: What can push the sea far enough inland to flood a whole coastline?

Make a guess: What can push the sea far enough inland to flood a whole coastline?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 4.1.1 physical and human factors increasing vulnerability to coastal erosion and coastal flooding
  • 4.1.1 how severe weather events and climatic change create vulnerability to coastal flooding
  • 4.1.1 social and economic factors increasing coastal vulnerability in countries at different levels of development
Mission 1

Coastal flooding

  • Coastal flooding: the sea covers land that is normally dry.
  • Three causes in this mission: storm surges, tsunamis and climate change (rising sea level).
  • Floods are worst when a surge or wave arrives at high tide.
Mission 1

Storm surges

  • Storm surge: "an abnormal water level rise generated by a storm, over and above the predicted astronomical tide".
  • Strong onshore winds pile water against the coast. A "wide, gently sloping continental shelf will allow for a higher storm surge".
  • UK example: in December 2013, "a huge tidal surge" breached Spurn, a spit in East Yorkshire.
Mission 1

Tsunamis

  • A large undersea earthquake moves the seabed, shifting the whole depth of water above it. A series of waves spreads out.
  • Near the source, waves arrive in minutes: after New Zealand's 2016 Kaikōura earthquake, "the first wave arrived within 10 minutes".
  • The 2004 Indian Ocean earthquake "generated a tsunami that killed over 230,000 people".
Quick check
?Which conditions would give the highest storm surge?
Mission 1

Climate change: a higher sea

  • Global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The rate is rising: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 3.7 mm a year (2006–2018).
  • Projection for 2100: a likely rise of 0.28–0.55 m with very low emissions, and 0.63–1.01 m with very high emissions.
  • A higher sea lets every surge and wave reach further inland.
Mission 1

Why some coasts erode fast

  • Physical factors: soft, weak rock and exposure to large waves.
  • Holderness, East Yorkshire: cliffs of soft glacial till. "At high tides the lower and, in some cases, middle sections of the cliff are subject to considerable erosion by wave action".
  • At Aldbrough, the cliff top retreated 1.16 m a year (1852–1951) and 2.16 m a year (1951–2004).
  • Human factors: homes and roads built close to the cliff top; defences in one place can starve beaches further along of sediment.
Mission 1

Why some coasts flood

  • Physical factors: low, flat land; a gently sloping seabed; low barrier islands; storms that drive surges.
  • Severe weather: a 2014 winter storm surge removed up to 10 m of sand dunes at Studland, Dorset, "in just one storm".
  • Human factors: dense building on low coasts. In Lee County, Florida, Hurricane Ian impacted at least 52,514 structures in 2022; 5,369 were destroyed.
Quick check
?Which is a human factor that increases coastal vulnerability?
Mission 1

One storm, two countries: Hurricane Ian, 2022

  • Cuba (developing): landfall on 27 September, category 3 (110 kt). 5 deaths reported; more than 100,000 homes damaged or destroyed; the whole island reportedly lost power for a few days.
  • Florida, USA (developed): landfall on 28 September, category 4 (130 kt). At least 156 deaths in the USA, 150 of them in Florida; all 66 direct deaths were in Florida. Storm surge killed 41.
  • US damage: about $112.9 billion. No monetary estimate is available for Cuba.
Mission 1

Wealth and vulnerability

  • Economic factors: wealth pays for insurance and recovery. By September 2024, US federal support topped $10.2 billion, including $4.67 billion of flood-insurance claims paid to about 47,000 policyholders.
  • Social factors: housing and power networks shape how far damage spreads: in Cuba, more than 100,000 homes were damaged or destroyed and the whole island lost power.
  • The hazard differed too: Ian was stronger at its Florida landfall (130 kt) than in Cuba (110 kt).
  • Wealth does not remove exposure: 36 of Florida's 41 surge deaths were in Lee County, where many people lived on low coasts.
  • Missing data matter too: Cuba's losses cannot be compared in dollars.
Quick check
?Ian killed more people in Florida than in Cuba. Which conclusion is best supported by the evidence?
Mission check

?On a low coast, the sea floods two towns overnight. There is no rain and no earthquake, but strong onshore winds blow over a shallow sea at high tide. What pushed the sea inland?

The sea is pushed inland by storm surges (wind-driven), tsunamis (earthquake-driven) and, slowly, by rising sea level, which makes both reach further. Vulnerability depends on physical factors, on where people build, and on the wealth to warn, protect and recover.

Mission 2

Mission 2: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Make a guess: By 2014 the Environment Agency had built new banks inland at Medmerry, West Sussex, and let the sea flood the land between. Why give land to the sea?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 4.2.1 hard and soft engineering to reduce erosion and flood risk at the local scale
  • 4.2.1 contrasting opinions on hold the line vs managed retreat for one low-lying coastline at risk from sea-level rise
Mission 2

Four policies for each stretch of coast

  • Hold the line: keep the defences where they are.
  • Advance the line: build new defences further out to sea.
  • Managed retreat (managed realignment): let the sea in, behind a new line of defence inland.
  • No active intervention: "Let nature take its course – no work will be carried out to repair defences".
Mission 2

Hard engineering

  • Sea walls: concrete walls that reflect waves. Strong, but very costly, and waves can scour the beach in front.
  • Rock armour (rip rap): big boulders that break up waves. Cheaper than a sea wall, but can look out of place and make the beach harder to reach.
  • Gabions: wire cages filled with rocks; cheaper than walls, but the wire rusts, so they do not last as long.
  • Groynes: barriers across the beach that trap longshore drift and keep the beach wide. Down-drift, the beach can be starved: groynes at one point on the Sussex coast "have contributed to erosion" of the spit beyond them.
Mission 2

Soft engineering

  • Beach nourishment (replenishment): add sand or shingle to the beach, which absorbs wave energy. It looks natural, but must be repeated.
  • Managed retreat: give low land to the sea; it becomes saltmarsh, which absorbs wave energy.
Quick check
?Groynes keep one town's beach wide. What is their most likely cost?
Mission 2

Medmerry: the problem

  • Medmerry lies west of Selsey. A shingle bank there protected 300 properties, large caravan sites and 650 hectares of land.
  • The flood risk was 1 in 1 in any given year, and the bank had about 1 year of life left.
  • The B2145 and the utilities that serve Selsey cross the low land behind the bank.
Mission 2

The options and their costs (2007)

  • No active intervention: £8–10 million. Without winter repairs, the bank was likely to breach within a year.
  • Hold the line: £80 million; judged "not practical".
  • Managed realignment: £10 million, for benefits valued at £40 million.
  • Costs are present values: today's value of the spending over the strategy's time span.
Quick check
?Managed realignment at Medmerry was estimated to cost £10 million and to bring benefits worth £40 million. How many pounds of benefit for each £1 spent?
Mission 2

Hold the line or let the sea in?

  • For holding the line: all property and land keep some protection, and losses are delayed.
  • Against: rising flood risk, more future funding, and "increasingly out of step with natural processes".
  • For managed realignment: it works with natural processes, cuts long-term spending, manages flood risk and may create a large area of wildlife habitat.
  • Against: flooding of land and property, possible loss of beach access, and a possible cut in shingle supply to the Witterings, along the coast.
  • Interests differ by stakeholder, so opinions differ. The Environment Agency weighed cost and risk; the RSPB supported the scheme. Owners of the low land stood to lose it to flooding, and Selsey relied on the B2145 crossing it.
Mission 2

What happened: effects and conflicts

  • Completed in 2014, with 7 km of new embankments inland, protecting key infrastructure for Selsey.
  • The £28 million scheme was then "the largest open-coast scheme in Europe"; it created a new wetland, with the RSPB's support.
  • The government also had a duty to create new intertidal habitat, to replace losses elsewhere.
  • Conflict: realignment meant "the flooding of a relatively large area of land".
  • Evaluate: £28 million spent is not directly comparable with the 2007 present-value estimate of £10 million.
Mission check

?On a low farmland coast, an old sea wall is failing. Rebuilding it would cost far more than the land behind it is worth. A town sits on higher ground nearby, but its only road crosses the low land. Which policy fits best?

At Medmerry, holding a failing bank was costed at £80 million against £10 million to realign (2007 estimates). New banks inland protect Selsey's road and services, and the flooded land became wetland. The price was a "relatively large area of land" given to the sea.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does England defend some stretches of coast and let the sea take others?

Make a guess: Why does England defend some stretches of coast and let the sea take others?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 4.2.1 cost-benefit; social and economic reasons why some coastlines are protected and others not
  • 4.2.1 role of Shoreline Management Plans in co-ordinating coastal management regionally/nationally
  • 4.2.1 monitoring (e.g. Met Office forecasting), hazard mapping and emergency planning to reduce coastal flood risk
Mission 3

Defences cost money

  • Sea walls, rock armour and barriers cost millions to build and maintain.
  • There is not enough money to defend every metre of coast.
  • So decision-makers ask: is it worth it here?
Mission 3

Cost-benefit analysis

  • Cost-benefit analysis: compare the cost of a scheme with its benefits: the damage it will prevent.
  • If benefits are much bigger than costs, the scheme is worth funding.
  • Flooding costs a home about £30,000 and a business about £82,000 on average.
  • The National Audit Office says every £1 spent protecting communities avoids about £9 in damage.
Mission 3

A real example: Medmerry

  • In 2007 planners valued the benefit of a scheme at Medmerry, West Sussex, at about £40 million.
  • The work would cost about £10 million.
  • Benefit ÷ cost = 40 ÷ 10 = 4: each £1 spent would prevent about £4 of damage.
Quick check
?A council compares two schemes (made-up figures). Which gives best value?
Mission 3

Why some coasts are protected

  • Economic: towns, ports, roads, railways, power and sewage works, and tourism are worth a lot.
  • Social: many homes and people at risk; loss of a community.
  • Example: Great Yarmouth's £42.9 million wall works protect 2,115 properties.
  • Example: the Boston Barrier, Lincolnshire, protects 13,000+ homes and businesses.
Mission 3

Why others are not

  • Few homes or low-value farmland: costs would be bigger than benefits.
  • A natural coast may be left to change (for habitats, or to supply sediment elsewhere).
  • At Happisburgh, Norfolk, the council bought and removed homes at risk of erosion.
  • Councils can get £6,000 per home to demolish houses about to be lost to erosion.
Quick check
?Which stretch of coast is most likely to be protected?
Mission 3

Why plan a whole coastline?

  • Sand and shingle move along the coast. Defending one place can starve the beach next to it.
  • Example: groynes at the Hinge "contributed to erosion" of East Head, West Sussex.
  • Shoreline Management Plans (SMPs) plan a whole stretch together, to 2105.
  • England has 20 SMPs, made by coastal groups (Environment Agency, councils and others) between 2006 and 2012.
Mission 3

The four SMP policies

  • Hold the line: keep the shoreline where it is.
  • No active intervention: let natural processes act.
  • Managed realignment: move the shoreline in a controlled way, e.g. creating habitat.
  • Advance the line: build defences further out to sea.
  • All depend on funding being available. Councils use SMPs to decide where new building is allowed.
Quick check
?At Medmerry, new banks were built inland and the sea allowed to flood the land in front. Which SMP policy is this?
Mission 3

Monitoring and forecasting

  • The Flood Forecasting Centre is a partnership of the Environment Agency and the Met Office.
  • It combines weather forecasts with flood models, and briefs emergency services before floods.
  • Warnings come in three levels: flood alert, flood warning, severe flood warning (danger to life).
  • A flood alert is usually issued 2–12 hours before flooding, including from high tides and surges.
Mission 3

Hazard mapping

  • The Environment Agency maps flood risk from rivers, the sea and surface water.
  • The National Coastal Erosion Risk Map shows erosion risk to 2055 and 2105, including sea-level rise.
  • Anyone can check the long-term flood risk for an address online.
  • Maps guide where to build, what to defend, and who should get warnings.
Mission 3

Emergency planning: the 2013 surge

  • On 5 December 2013, a storm and high tides caused a surge along England's east coast; some tides were higher than in 1953.
  • Over 30 severe flood warnings were issued; thousands were evacuated to rest centres.
  • In Lincolnshire, 44 people were rescued.
  • Warnings and defences meant no loss of life.
Mission check

?Two neighbouring coasts (made-up figures). Coast X: a resort with 2,000 homes; a new sea wall would cost £20 million and prevent £100 million of damage. Coast Y: farmland and 5 houses; a wall would cost £15 million and prevent £3 million of damage. What would an SMP most likely say?

Cost-benefit analysis, set within a Shoreline Management Plan, decides where to hold the line: high-value towns get defences; low-value coasts are left to change, sometimes with help to move, as at Happisburgh. Forecasting, hazard maps and warnings then reduce the danger where people remain, as in December 2013.

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 4.2.2 reasons for increased future vulnerability of some coastal communities; why some coastlines face greater risk
  • 4.2.2 specific challenges faced by Small Island States as sea levels rise

  • 4.2.2 how governments in countries at different levels of development are facing sea-level rise
Mission 4

Hazard, exposure, vulnerability

  • Hazard: the physical threat, such as sea-level rise or a storm surge.
  • Exposure: people and property in places that could be harmed.
  • Vulnerability: how likely people are to be harmed, and how able they are to cope and adapt.
  • Risk comes from the three together. The same hazard can bring very different risk.
Mission 4

Why some coasts face greater risk

  • Low land: little height above the sea, and no higher ground to move to.
  • Sinking land (subsidence) and soft coasts that erode easily.
  • People and money: dense populations and big cities on the coast; poor communities with little to spend on defences.
  • By 2050, many low-lying megacities and small islands are projected to face, at least once a year, extreme sea levels that used to come once a century.
Mission 4

The Netherlands: highly exposed

  • 26% of the Netherlands lies below sea level; with land at risk from rivers, 55% is at risk of flooding.
  • It is a high-income country: income (GNI) per person US$68,530 in 2025.
Mission 4

The Netherlands: the government responds

  • Since 2017, flood-protection standards have been written into the Water Act.
  • Target: by 2050, a yearly chance of dying in a flood of no more than 1 in 100,000 for everyone behind the main defences.
  • In the first check (2021), about 67% of dike sections met the standard.
  • The Delta Programme has a budget of about €29 billion for 2015–2050; costs are estimated at about €38 billion: a funding gap.
Quick check
?Which best explains why the Netherlands is less vulnerable than its height suggests?
Mission 4

Tuvalu: highly exposed

  • Tuvalu is a Pacific atoll nation; its land averages 2 m above sea level.
  • 9,492 people (2025); more than half live on the largest islet, in Funafuti atoll.
  • Sea level there was nearly 15 cm higher in 2023 than 30 years earlier.
  • By 2050, much of its land and key infrastructure is projected to sit below the average high-tide level.
  • The UN lists Tuvalu as a Small Island Developing State.
Mission 4

Tuvalu: responses

  • Raising land: a project funded by the Green Climate Fund created 8 hectares of raised land on Fogafale, Funafuti's main islet (2025).
  • Mobility: the Falepili Union treaty with Australia (in force since 28 August 2024) lets Tuvaluans choose to live, work and study in Australia; the 2025 ballot offered up to 280 visa places.
  • Australia recognises that Tuvalu stays a state "notwithstanding the impact of climate-change related sea-level rise".
Quick check
?In 2025, income per person was US$68,530 in the Netherlands and US$9,780 in Tuvalu. How many times Tuvalu's is the Dutch figure, to 1 decimal place?
Mission 4

Small Island States: special challenges

  • Little land and no high ground: there is nowhere inland to retreat to.
  • Fresh water: above 1.5 °C of warming, limited fresh water may set hard limits to adapting on small islands.
  • Cost: defences for a whole nation of islands can cost more than a small economy can pay alone.
  • Fairness: small island developing states produced 0.5% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
Mission 4

Environmental refugees?

  • Rising seas, salt in water supplies and bigger floods may force people to leave low islands and coasts.
  • The IPCC: at higher warming, people will be forced to move from places with high exposure and low capacity to adapt.
  • In law, a refugee flees persecution, not floods or rising seas. "Environmental refugee" describes the cause of the move, not a legal status.
  • Tuvalu's treaty with Australia is a planned route for people to move before they are forced to.
Mission 4

Reducing risk from rising seas

  • The IPCC's responses to sea-level rise include: protect (dikes, sea walls), accommodate (raise homes, flood-proof buildings), advance (build land out or up) and planned relocation.
  • Sustainable options: coastal wetlands protect against erosion and flooding.
  • Sea walls protect people in the short term but can "lock in" risk by encouraging building behind them.
  • The Netherlands mainly protects; Tuvalu advances (raised land) and prepares for relocation (Falepili).
Mission 4

Governments at different levels of development

  • Netherlands (high income): pays for its own defences, sets legal standards, plans to 2050.
  • Tuvalu (small island developing state): relies on international funds for raised land and a partner country for migration.
  • Trade-off: under the treaty, Tuvalu must agree with Australia any security or defence deal with another state.
Mission check

?Two coastal towns face the same rise in sea level. Town P is 1 m above sea level, with high incomes, sea walls built to a legal standard and a funded 30-year plan. Town Q is 3 m above sea level on a small island, with low incomes, no defences and no higher ground. Which is more vulnerable, and why?

Both the Netherlands and Tuvalu are exposed. The Netherlands has about seven times Tuvalu's income per person, a €29 billion programme and legal flood standards. Tuvalu has little land and no high ground, so it relies on raised land and mobility to Australia. Tuvalu is more vulnerable.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Causes: storm surge, tsunami, sea-level rise; worst at high tide.
  • Vulnerability: physical factors (soft till at Holderness, low shelving coasts) and human factors (building on low coasts); Ian showed wealth helps recovery but not exposure.

Mission 2

  • Hard engineering holds the line but is costly and can harm the coast down-drift; soft engineering works with natural processes.
  • Medmerry chose managed retreat: cheaper and lower-risk than holding a failing bank, with habitat gained and land lost.
  • Different stakeholders favour different options: say whose view each argument represents.

Mission 3

  • Cost-benefit: defend where the damage prevented is much bigger than the cost (Medmerry: about £40m benefit for £10m cost). Towns and infrastructure are protected; farmland and natural coasts often are not.
  • SMPs co-ordinate whole coastlines (20 in England, to 2105; hold the line, no active intervention, managed realignment, advance the line). Forecasting (Flood Forecasting Centre: Environment Agency + Met Office), hazard maps and emergency plans reduce flood risk.

Mission 4

  • Risk = hazard + exposure + vulnerability. Money, land, plans and partners change vulnerability.
  • Netherlands: protect (dikes, standards). Tuvalu: raise land and plan for migration.
  • Small Island States: little land, limited fresh water, few resources; "environmental refugees" is not a legal category.
