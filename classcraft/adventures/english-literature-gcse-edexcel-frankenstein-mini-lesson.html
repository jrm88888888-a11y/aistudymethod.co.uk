Why does a novel about a monster open with letters from a ship?

How your exam asks this

Frankenstein · the frame narrativeScreen 5

Why wrap the story in a frame?

  • Doubles: Walton would trade "One man's life or death" for knowledge, as Victor once did.
  • A warning: Victor breaks off in Chapter IV to tell Walton, "Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example".
  • Layers of trust: we hear the Creature through Victor, and Victor through Walton. Victor even "corrected and augmented" Walton's notes.
  • Evidence: Walton sees the Creature twice himself, in Letter IV and on the last pages.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why does a novel about a monster open with letters from a ship?

The letters make Walton Victor's double: an explorer ready to risk lives for knowledge. Victor tells his story to stop him, so we read the whole novel as a warning passed through two narrators. At the end we watch whether Walton learns from it.

Frankenstein · VictorScreen 4

Fate or choice?

  • Victor often blames fate: "Destiny was too potent" (Ch II); Waldman's lecture was "the words of fate, enounced to destroy me" (Ch III). Both lines were added in 1831.
  • Yet the plot shows choices: he flees the newly living being (Ch V) and stays silent at Justine's trial (Ch VIII).
  • He calls himself "the true murderer" (Ch VIII, and again in Ch IX) but tells no one.
  • Readers disagree: a tragic hero with a fatal flaw, or an unreliable narrator excusing himself?

?Victor says destiny ruined him. Should we believe him?

Victor's narration is full of destiny and evil powers, much of it added in 1831. But the plot shows him choosing: he flees his creation, keeps silent at Justine's trial and leaves Elizabeth alone. The best answers weigh his words against his actions and treat him as a narrator who may be excusing himself.

?Born good, made evil? What turns the Creature into a killer?

The Creature begins kind: he gathers the De Laceys' firewood and saves a drowning girl. Each time he reaches out, he is attacked, and only then does he turn to revenge. Shelley makes rejection the cause, but he still chooses to kill, so the best answers weigh both.

Mission 4

Is Frankenstein a warning against science, or something else?

Make a guess: Is Frankenstein a warning against science, or something else?

Frankenstein · ambition and scienceScreen 1

From alchemy to chemistry

  • At thirteen Victor reads the alchemists Agrippa, Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus, who promised an "elixir of life" (Ch II).
  • His father calls Agrippa "sad trash" but never explains why, so Victor reads on.
  • At Ingolstadt, Krempe scorns these "exploded systems"; Waldman praises modern chemists who "penetrate into the recesses of nature" (Ch III).
  • Victor wants both: modern methods and the old dream of "immortality and power".

Frankenstein · ambition and scienceScreen 5

The Modern Prometheus

  • The novel's subtitle is The Modern Prometheus. In Greek myth, Prometheus stole fire from the gods for humans, and Zeus had him chained to a rock as punishment.
  • Victor's punishment comes through what he makes: he loses William, Justine, Clerval, Elizabeth and his father.
  • He states the moral: a study that weakens your affections is "certainly unlawful" (Ch IV).
  • Many readers note the target: Waldman's chemistry harms no one; Victor's secrecy and isolation do.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Is Frankenstein a warning against science, or something else?

The novel doesn't condemn knowledge: Waldman's chemistry harms no one. It warns against ambition that cuts a person off from others and ignores the duty to care for what they make. Even Victor's last words stay divided between warning and hope.

?Why does Victor climb a glacier to feel better?

The Alps are sublime: vast, dangerous and powerful, "mighty as Omnipotence". Facing them, Victor's own sorrow feels small, so they lift and calm him. The relief doesn't last: on the glacier he meets the being he made.

Mission 6

Who is the real monster: the maker or the made?

Make a guess: Who is the real monster: the maker or the made?

Frankenstein · responsibility and isolationScreen 1

What a creator owes

  • On the glacier the Creature demands: "Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine" (Ch X).
  • Victor admits that "For the first time" he felt "what the duties of a creator towards his creature were" (Ch X).
  • Yet he flees at the creation (Ch V), stays silent at Justine's trial (Ch VIII) and destroys the half-made female (Ch XX).
  • Dying, he insists: "I did right in refusing" (Ch XXIV).

Frankenstein · responsibility and isolationScreen 4

Isolation makes monsters

  • Victor works in "a solitary chamber, or rather cell" (Ch IV), and after Justine's death "shunned the face of man" (Ch IX).
  • The Creature: "My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I abhor" (Ch XVII).
  • Walton: "I have no friend, Margaret" (Letter II).
  • Against them Shelley sets warm bonds: the De Laceys, Clerval, Elizabeth. The Preface (written by Percy Shelley) praises "the amiableness of domestic affection".

Mission checkScreen 7

?Who is the real monster: the maker or the made?

Shelley doesn't hand you a verdict. The Creature commits the murders, but Victor abandons him, lets Justine die and refuses him any companion. Both end up isolated, and isolation is what turns each of them monstrous, so the best answers weigh both.

Mission 7

What did a reader in 1818 know that you don't?

Make a guess: What did a reader in 1818 know that you don't?

Frankenstein · contextsScreen 1

A ghost story from 1816

WhenWhat happened
June 1816At the Villa Diodati near Geneva, Lord Byron proposes: "We will each write a ghost story"
Summer 1816Cold rain keeps the group indoors: a volcanic eruption in 1815 had cooled the climate
1 January 1818Frankenstein is published anonymously, in three volumes
1831A revised one-volume edition adds Mary Shelley's Introduction
  • Mary Shelley was eighteen in 1816. Her parents were the radical writers Mary Wollstonecraft (A Vindication of the Rights of Woman, 1792), who died soon after her birth, and William Godwin (Political Justice, 1793), to whom the 1818 edition is dedicated.
Frankenstein · contextsScreen 2

Galvanism: a spark of life?

  • Galvanism: in 1791 Luigi Galvani reported that electricity made dead frogs' legs twitch.
  • In London in January 1803, his nephew Giovanni Aldini passed current through the body of George Forster, a hanged criminal: the jaw quivered and an eye opened.
  • In 1831 Mary Shelley recalled talk that "galvanism had given token of such things".
  • Victor never reveals his method (Ch IV), so readers could imagine it was nearly possible.

Frankenstein · contextsScreen 5

Romantic and Miltonic voices

  • Romanticism valued feeling, imagination, nature and the individual.
  • The novel quotes Romantic poems: Coleridge's Ancient Mariner (Ch V), Wordsworth's "Tintern Abbey" (Ch XVIII), Percy Shelley's "Mutability" (Ch X).
  • The 1818 title page quotes Milton's Paradise Lost (1667): "Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay / To mould me man?"
  • The Creature reads it "as a true history" (Ch XV).
  • Context isn't marked in your Section A: use it to sharpen a point.

Mission checkScreen 7

?What did a reader in 1818 know that you don't?

An 1818 reader didn't know who wrote the book, but did know about galvanism: electricity had made dead frogs and even a hanged man's face move. Many also knew Paradise Lost, quoted on the title page. So Victor's experiment felt frighteningly close to real, and his role as a careless "Maker" was easy to see.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Walton's letters frame Victor's story, which frames the Creature's own story (Chapters XI–XVI).
  • Walton is Victor's double, so Victor's tale works as a warning; in September Walton turns back.
  • Part (a) is about language in the extract; part (b) is about the whole novel.
  • Victor narrates most of the novel: a loved child, an ambitious student, then a guilty, grieving pursuer.
  • He blames destiny, but the plot shows choices: fleeing the Creature, silence at Justine's trial, breaking his promise.
  • For part (b), a character question needs evidence from across the novel, not just one scene.
  • The Creature learns language, history and feeling by watching the De Laceys and reading (Ch XI–XV).
  • He tells his own story eloquently, and links his violence to rejection: "misery made me a fiend".
  • For part (b), trace how the Creature changes across Chapters XI–XVII, not just one moment.
  • Victor moves from the alchemists' dreams to modern chemistry, but keeps the dream of "immortality and power".
  • Shelley's warning falls on ambition pursued in secret and without care, not on knowledge itself.
  • For a theme question in part (b), contrast Victor with Waldman, Walton and Clerval.
  • The sublime is vast, powerful nature that brings awe mixed with terror; Victor seeks it in the Alps (Ch IX–X).
  • Nature heals, is ignored, mirrors mood (pathetic fallacy) and teaches the Creature.
  • Part (a) may print a nature passage: name the method (e.g. pathetic fallacy) and its effect.
  • A creator owes his creation care: the Creature demands it, and Victor briefly admits it (Ch X), but keeps failing it.
  • Victor, the Creature and Walton are all isolated; Shelley sets them against the warm bonds of family and friendship.
  • In part (b), a theme question on responsibility needs moments from across the novel.
  • The novel began as a ghost story at the Villa Diodati in June 1816; it was published anonymously in 1818 and revised in 1831.
  • Galvanism made reanimation seem close; Romantic poems and Paradise Lost shape its ideas of nature, feeling and creation.
  • Context isn't marked in Section A: use it only where it sharpens analysis.

Key quotations to learn

  • "I have no friend, Margaret" (Letter II) Walton's loneliness links him to Victor and the Creature.
  • "Do you share my madness?" (Letter IV (added in 1831)) Victor sees himself in Walton.
  • "You seek for knowledge and wisdom, as I once did" (Letter IV) The tale is told as a warning.
  • "Learn from me, if not by my precepts, at least by my example" (Ch IV) Victor turns to Walton mid-story.
  • "Seek happiness in tranquillity, and avoid ambition" (Ch XXIV (Walton's last letters)) Victor's dying advice.
  • "lost in darkness and distance" (Ch XXIV, last words) The Creature's end is left uncertain.
  • "Destiny was too potent" (Ch II (added in 1831)) Victor blames fate.
  • "more, far more, will I achieve" (Ch III (added in 1831)) His ambition after Waldman's lecture.
  • "breathless horror and disgust filled my heart" (Ch V) Horror replaces the dream at once.
  • "I, not in deed, but in effect, was the true murderer" (Ch IX) He admits guilt only to himself.
  • "I am a blasted tree; the bolt has entered my soul" (Ch XIX) He sees himself as destroyed, like the oak in Ch II.
  • "like the archangel who aspired to omnipotence, I am chained in an eternal hell" (Ch XXIV (Walton's letters)) He compares himself to Milton's Satan.
  • "I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend" (Ch X) He blames suffering, not nature.
  • "I ought to be thy Adam; but I am rather the fallen angel" (Ch X) The Paradise Lost parallel.
  • "Was I then a monster, a blot upon the earth" (Ch XIII) He learns to see himself as others do.
  • "from that moment I declared everlasting war against the species" (Ch XVI) The turning point after Felix's attack.
  • "I am malicious because I am miserable" (Ch XVII) His argument for his violence.
  • "the fallen angel becomes a malignant devil" (Ch XXIV (Walton's letters)) His own final judgement on himself.
  • "It was the secrets of heaven and earth that I desired to learn" (Ch II (added in 1831)) His ambition from childhood.
  • "more, far more, will I achieve" (Ch III (added in 1831)) Waldman's lecture fires him up.
  • "Life and death appeared to me ideal bounds" (Ch IV) He wants to break nature's limits.
  • "how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge" (Ch IV) Victor's warning to Walton.
  • "then that study is certainly unlawful" (Ch IV) The cost: lost affections.
  • "Seek happiness in tranquillity, and avoid ambition" (Ch XXIV (Walton's letters)) His dying advice, at once undercut.
  • "my eyes were insensible to the charms of nature" (Ch IV) Ambition cuts Victor off from nature.
  • "This noble war in the sky elevated my spirits" (Ch VII) A storm as sublime spectacle.
  • "spoke of a power mighty as Omnipotence" (Ch IX (added in 1831)) Nature's power is like God's.
  • "maternal nature bade me weep no more" (Ch IX (added in 1831)) Nature as comforting mother.
  • "These sublime and magnificent scenes afforded me the greatest consolation" (Ch X) The sublime soothes his grief.
  • "The desert mountains and dreary glaciers are my refuge" (Ch X) The Creature lives where humans can't.
  • "How dare you sport thus with life?" (Ch X) The Creature accuses his maker.
  • "Do your duty towards me" (Ch X) Creation brings duties.
  • "I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were" (Ch X) Victor briefly accepts it.
  • "My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I abhor" (Ch XVII) Isolation as the cause of evil.
  • "I shall be with you on your wedding-night" (Ch XX) Victor's refusal brings revenge.
  • "Am I to be thought the only criminal, when all human kind sinned against me?" (Ch XXIV (Walton's letters)) The Creature's last defence.
  • "We will each write a ghost story" (1831 Introduction) Byron's challenge, 1816.
  • "galvanism had given token of such things" (1831 Introduction) The science behind the idea.
  • "the pale student of unhallowed arts" (1831 Introduction) The first image of Victor.
  • "I bid my hideous progeny go forth and prosper" (1831 Introduction) The novel as her "child".
  • "Did I request thee, Maker, from my clay" (1818 title page (Paradise Lost)) The Creature's complaint, in Milton's words.
  • "Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition" (Ch XV) The Creature reads Milton.
