Mission 1

Mission 1: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Make a guess: Cross a red flower with a white one and every offspring is pink. So why do red and white come back?

Mission 1

When neither allele is dominant

  • Sometimes the heterozygote differs from both homozygotes.
  • Codominance: both alleles are fully expressed in the heterozygote. It shows both phenotypes.
  • Example: roan cattle. Red × white gives roan: a coat of separate red hairs and white hairs.
  • Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote has an intermediate phenotype.
  • Example: four o'clock plant, Mirabilis jalapa. Red × white gives pink flowers.
  • Notation: one letter for the gene, superscripts for alleles: Cᴿ and Cᵂ. No lower case, as neither is recessive.
  • The alleles stay separate in the heterozygote. They do not blend.
  • Say: "both alleles are expressed" — not: "the alleles mix".
Mission 1

Worked cross: two heterozygotes

  • Each heterozygote (CᴿCᵂ) makes Cᴿ and Cᵂ gametes in equal numbers.
  • Offspring: 1 CᴿCᴿ : 2 CᴿCᵂ : 1 CᵂCᵂ.
  • Each genotype has its own phenotype. So the phenotype ratio is also 1 : 2 : 1, not 3 : 1.
  • Roan × roan: 1 red : 2 roan : 1 white.
  • Pink × pink: 1 red : 2 pink : 1 white.
  • So the card's pink plants are CᴿCᵂ. Red and white return in the next generation.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Make a guess: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Mission 2

Chance or a real difference?

  • Which gametes fuse is random. So observed counts scatter around the expected ones.
  • The χ² test asks: is the difference small enough to be due to chance?
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed and expected results.
  • The test uses counts in categories (phenotype classes). Never percentages or ratios.
  • The counted offspring are a sample of all the offspring the cross could produce.
Mission 2

The formula

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. O = observed, E = expected, Σ = sum over all classes.
  • On the equation sheet: χ² = Σ (o − e)² ÷ e.
  • E = total counted × expected fraction for that class.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of classes − 1.
  • Big differences → big χ². A perfect fit → χ² = 0.

Mission 2

When the null hypothesis is rejected

  • A significant difference means the expected ratio is probably wrong for this cross.
  • Then look for a biological reason:
    • two genes linked (too many parental types);
    • a sex-linked gene;
    • unequal survival of one phenotype.
  • χ² tells you whether the difference is significant, not why.
Mission 2

Common slips

  • Use raw counts, not percentages.
  • Scale E to your total. Never use 9, 3, 3, 1 as E.
  • Degrees of freedom = classes − 1.
  • Big χ² = big difference. Above the critical value → significant.
  • Not significant ≠ proved.
Mission check

?In tomatoes, purple stems (A) are dominant to green stems (a). An Aa plant is crossed with an aa plant. Of 200 seedlings, 115 are purple and 85 are green (illustrative data). Critical values at p = 0.05: 3.84 for 1 degree of freedom, 5.99 for 2. Which conclusion is correct?

Unlike Mendel's seed counts, this gap is too large to be chance. Next step: look for a reason, such as fewer green seedlings surviving.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Some traits pass from mothers to all their children, but not from fathers. How can that happen?

Make a guess: Some traits pass from mothers to all their children, but not from fathers. How can that happen?

Mission 3

Mendel's model, and two exceptions

  • Mendel's model, in modern terms: genes on chromosomes in the nucleus, in pairs; one allele of each pair into each gamete.
  • Predicts ratios such as 3 : 1 and 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.
  • Many traits break the pattern. Two here:
  • Pleiotropy: one gene, many traits.
  • Non-nuclear inheritance: genes in mitochondria and chloroplasts.
Mission 3

Pleiotropy: one gene, many effects

  • Pleiotropy: the expression of one gene produces several traits or effects.
  • Why: one protein is used in many tissues.
  • Example: one human gene codes for fibrillin, a protein in connective tissue. Faulty alleles can affect the skeleton, the eyes and the heart.
  • All the effects come from one gene, so they are inherited together.
  • So these traits do not segregate independently.
  • Say: "pleiotropy: one gene → many traits" — not: "many genes → one trait" (that is polygenic inheritance).

Mission 3

Genes outside the nucleus

  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own small, circular DNA.
  • A cell holds many of them, not a pair.
  • At cell division they are shared out at random.
  • So daughter cells, and gametes, can get different mixes of normal and mutant organelles.
  • So organelle traits do not follow Mendel's ratios.
Mission 3

Passed on by the mother

  • Animals: the egg passes on its mitochondria. The sperm usually passes on none.
  • Plants: the ovule passes on mitochondria and chloroplasts; the pollen usually does not.
  • So mitochondrial and chloroplast traits are usually maternally inherited.
  • Affected mother → children of either sex can be affected.
  • Affected father → none of his children inherit it.
  • Compare X-linked: an affected father passes the allele to all his daughters.
  • Say: "maternal inheritance via the egg's mitochondria" — not: "X-linked".
Mission 3

Read the data: leaf patches in four-o'clocks

Real study: Carl Correns (1909) crossed flowers from different branches of four-o'clock plants (Mirabilis jalapa). Some branches had green leaves, some white, some variegated (green and white).

  • The pollen's branch makes no difference.
  • Variegated ovules carry a random mix of normal and mutant chloroplasts → mixed seedlings.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Make a guess: Cuttings from one plant share every gene. So why don't they all grow to the same height?

Mission 4

Genes plus surroundings

  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has.
  • Phenotype: its observable traits.
  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment.
  • Same genotype, different surroundings → different phenotypes. That's the cuttings.
Mission 4

One genotype, many phenotypes

  • Phenotypic plasticity: one genotype can produce different phenotypes in different environments.
  • It works by changing gene expression, not the genotype.
  • The base sequences stay as inherited. Many such changes can reverse in the organism's lifetime; some are fixed during development.
  • Hydrangea flower color: blue in acidic soil, pink in alkaline soil.
  • Arctic foxes: brown fur in summer, white fur in winter.
  • In many turtles, egg incubation temperature sets the sex: warmer eggs hatch mostly as females. Once set, sex does not change.
  • Say: "the environment changed gene expression" — not: "the environment changed the genes".
Quick check
?Arctic foxes grow brown fur in summer and white fur in winter. Which statement best explains how one fox can produce both fur colors?
Mission check

?In Himalayan rabbits, the enzyme that makes dark fur pigment is active at the cooler temperatures of the ears, nose, feet and tail but inactive at the warmer temperature of the rest of the body, where fur grows white. A patch of white fur is shaved from a rabbit's back, and an ice pack is kept on the patch while the fur regrows. The rabbit is later mated with another Himalayan rabbit. Which outcome is predicted?

Same genotype, different temperature, different fur: the environment changes how genes are expressed, not the genes that are passed on.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Codominance gives a dual phenotype, incomplete dominance an intermediate one; either way heterozygote × heterozygote gives 1 : 2 : 1.

Mission 2

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E with degrees of freedom = classes − 1; compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Below it: fail to reject the null hypothesis. Above it: reject it.

Mission 3

  • Pleiotropy: one gene affects several traits, which are therefore inherited together.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts are shared out at random and passed on in the egg or ovule, so their traits are non-Mendelian and usually maternally inherited.

Mission 4

  • Phenotype = genotype interacting with the environment; genetically identical organisms still differ.
  • Phenotypic plasticity: the environment changes gene expression, not the genotype; many such changes can reverse, and none alters the alleles passed on.
