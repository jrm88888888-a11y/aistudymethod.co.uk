Paper 2: Directed Writing and Composition

Paper 2 is the writing paper: 2 hours, 80 marks. You answer one question from each section — Section A Directed Writing (40 marks, based on one or two texts) and Section B Composition (40 marks, a descriptive or narrative piece). The paper is 80% writing, 20% reading.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

The paper

How Paper 2 is built

2 hours, 80 marks, two answers — one from each section. That is roughly an hour each, including planning and checking.

  • Section A — Directed Writing (40 marks). One question in two parts, based on one or two texts totalling 550–650 words, printed on the insert. Part 1(a) is a structured question that asks you to analyse and evaluate the text(s) (5 reading marks). Part 1(b) asks you to use, develop and evaluate the information to write a discursive, argumentative or persuasive speech, letter, article or report of about 250–350 words.
  • Section B — Composition (40 marks). A choice of four titles: two descriptive and two narrative. You write about 350–450 words, marked for writing skills only.

Marks split, Section A 1(b): 10 reading marks + 25 writing marks (plus the 5 reading marks from 1(a)). So the text is not decoration — it is a third of the section.

Directed writing

Form, audience, purpose

Before you write a word of Section A, answer three questions. Cambridge calls this audience, purpose and form.

  • Form — speech, letter, article or report? Each has its own conventions and layout expectations.
  • Audience — a head teacher, a council, readers of a magazine, an assembly of students? This fixes your register: how formal, how warm, how technical.
  • Purpose — to discuss (weigh both sides), to argue (drive one case with reasons), or to persuade (drive one case with feeling as well as reasons)?

The most common self-inflicted wound: writing a good general essay when the question asked for a speech to your year group. Form and audience are worth marks (W4), and they cost you nothing but attention.

Directed writing

Using the text — not copying it

The reading marks in Section A come from what you do with the text. Cambridge asks you to use, develop and evaluate the ideas.

  • Use — take a point from the text and put it into your own words.
  • Develop — carry it one step further: what follows from it, who is affected, what would change.
  • Evaluate — judge it: is it convincing, one-sided, exaggerated? Whose interests does it serve? Answering 1(a) well trains exactly this.
From lifting to developing (invented example)

Text says: a late bus service would cost the council £40,000 a year.

Lifted: The late bus would cost £40,000 a year.

Developed and evaluated: Forty thousand pounds sounds alarming until you set it beside the cost of the empty minibuses the council already runs at midday — the figure is real, but presented without any comparison it is designed to frighten rather than to inform.

Directed writing

Structuring the argument

A 250–350 word response has room for about four to five paragraphs. Organise them deliberately — W2 rewards structure for effect.

  • Open by establishing your voice and your position in one or two sentences.
  • Build two or three developed points, each anchored in an idea from the text and pushed further.
  • Concede and rebut — acknowledge the strongest objection, then answer it. This is what separates a real argument from a rant.
  • Close with the point you most want remembered — a call to action for a speech, a firm recommendation for a report.

Discursive vs persuasive: a discursive piece genuinely weighs both sides before reaching a view. A persuasive piece has already decided and is bringing you with it. Check which the question asked for.

Composition

Section B — descriptive writing

Two of the four Section B titles are descriptive. A description is a picture, not a plot. Cambridge wants ideas and images that build a convincing, well-defined scene with varieties of focus.

  • Vary the focus. Move deliberately: wide shot (the whole market), mid shot (one stall), close-up (a cracked thumbnail pressing a coin into a palm) — then back out again.
  • Use more than sight. Sound, smell, texture and temperature are what make a place feel inhabited.
  • Choose precise nouns and verbs before you reach for adjectives. The crowd surged beats the very big crowd moved quickly.
  • Control the mood. Every image should pull in the same emotional direction — or change direction on purpose.

The trap: a description that turns into a story. If a robbery breaks out in paragraph three, you have written a narrative under a descriptive title — and the examiner cannot reward what was not asked for.

Composition

Section B — narrative writing

The other two titles are narrative. Cambridge asks for a plot that is well defined and developed, with description, characterisation and convincing details. You have about 350–450 words — that is one incident, not an epic.

  • One crisis. A single moment of change, properly built and properly landed, beats five events sprinted through.
  • Characterise through action. We learn who someone is from what they do when it costs them something.
  • Shape the time. Slow down at the crisis; summarise the travel. Uniform pace is what makes a story feel flat.
  • Earn the ending. Twists that cancel the story (it was all a dream) throw away everything you built.

Show, do not tell: instead of she was nervous, write she read the same line of the timetable four times. The reader does the feeling — which is the whole point of W1.

Accuracy

Sentence variety, vocabulary and accuracy

Two of the five writing skills are about the texture of your sentences (W3) and about accuracy (W5). They are the easiest marks to lose and the easiest to protect.

  • Vary sentence length on purpose. Several long, layered sentences, then a short one. The short one lands.
  • Vary sentence openings. If four paragraphs in a row begin with I or The, rewrite one to start with a verb, an adverbial or a subordinate clause.
  • Choose vocabulary precisely, not extravagantly. A thesaurus word used slightly wrongly does more damage than a plain word used exactly.
  • Punctuate speech and clauses properly. Comma splices (two sentences joined by a comma) are one of the most common accuracy errors at GCSE.

Five-minute check: spend the last five minutes of each hour re-reading. Look for missing full stops, agreement slips (they was), and paragraphs that never got a break.

Planning

Five minutes that are worth ten marks

Both sections reward organisation for deliberate effect (W2). That is a planning mark, and it is won before you write.

  • Section A plan (3 minutes): jot your position, then three points, each with the text idea it grows from and the objection you will concede. Number them in the order that builds best.
  • Section B plan (3 minutes): for description, list five focal points and the order you will move through them. For narrative, write the crisis first, then decide where to start so you reach it by the middle.
  • Decide the ending before you begin. Writers who discover their ending at the bottom of the page usually run out of both words and time.

Timing: roughly one hour per section — say 5 minutes planning, 45–50 writing, 5 checking. The two sections carry equal marks, so never let Section A eat Section B.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Section A (40): 1(a) a structured question on the text(s) (5 reading marks); 1(b) a discursive / argumentative / persuasive speech, letter, article or report of about 250–350 words

Section A texts: one or two texts totalling 550–650 words, printed on the insert

Section B (40): one of four titles — two descriptive, two narrative — written in about 350–450 words

The writing skills: express what is thought, felt and imagined · organise for effect · varied vocabulary and sentence structures · language suited to purpose and audience · accurate spelling, punctuation and grammar

Description: a picture, not a plot: varieties of focus, the five senses, precise nouns and verbs

Narrative: a shaped plot with characterisation and convincing detail — one crisis, not five
