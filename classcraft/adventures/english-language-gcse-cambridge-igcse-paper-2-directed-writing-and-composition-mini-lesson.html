Mission 1

Forty marks, forty-five minutes. Why spend five of them not writing?

Make a guess: Forty marks, forty-five minutes. Why spend five of them not writing?

Writing · planning and structureScreen 1

Five minutes that shape forty

  • A plan is not a draft. It is five or six lines of notes, written in about five minutes.
  • Choose your angle first: one viewpoint, one character, one place.
  • Decide your ending before you start, so every paragraph travels towards it.
  • Give each paragraph one job: a new reason, a shift in time, a closer look.
  • Keep the last few minutes to check spelling, punctuation and sentences.
Writing · planning and structureScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Q1(b): about 250–350 words, 35 marks (25 writing, 10 reading). Composition: one of four titles, about 350–450 words, 40 marks. Coursework: three assignments of 500–800 words.

  • Cohesion: each paragraph's first sentence links back to the last, so the reader never gets lost.
  • A cyclical ending returns to the opening and shows what has changed.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Forty marks, forty-five minutes. Why spend five of them not writing?

The five minutes buy control. A short plan fixes your angle, your ending and a job for each paragraph, so the writing builds towards a point instead of running out of time halfway.

Mission 2

Two articles, one speech to write. How much can you borrow?

Make a guess: Two articles, one speech to write. How much can you borrow?

Writing · directed writingScreen 1

What directed writing asks

  • You read one or two texts, then write in a set form (speech, letter, article, report) for a set audience, often in a role (a student, a resident).
  • Your answer is marked for reading (how well you use the texts' ideas) and writing (how well you write for the task).
  • Three verbs: use the ideas, develop them (consequences, examples) and evaluate them (weigh them, question them, spot bias).
  • Copied sentences show only that you found an idea, not that you can use it.
Extract Writing · directed writingScreen 2

Two texts on a car-free Saturday

Writing · directed writingScreen 3

Select, sort, question

  • Read the task first, then the texts: mark only ideas that help this form, audience and purpose.
  • Sort ideas into columns (for and against, or problems and solutions) and note whose idea each is.
  • Question every claim: is it a fact, an opinion or a generalisation? Who gains? What's missing?
  • Look for links: Text B's shuttle bus answers Text A's worry about village customers. Linking the texts shows close reading of both.
Tap the evidenceScreen 4
?Tap the phrase in Text A that makes a sweeping claim you could challenge. (Use the extract on screen 2.)

Tap a word in the extract.

Writing · directed writingScreen 5

Make it yours

  • Own words: keep the idea, change the wording. Short facts (a number, a name) can be reused.
  • Develop: who is affected, and what happens next?
  • Evaluate: weigh the texts' ideas and say which is stronger, and why.
  • Source: ‘Half my customers drive in from the villages.’ Lifted: ‘Half the café's customers drive in from the villages.’ Made yours: ‘Ban cars without a better bus, and the villagers who fill our cafés will drive to the retail park instead.’
Pick the strongestScreen 6
?Task: as a local student, write a speech to the town council on whether the high street should be car-free on Saturdays. Which sentence uses the texts best?
Write itScreen 7
?Open the speech.

Write the opening of the speech to the council (3–4 sentences), as a local student. Use at least one idea from each text in your own words, and question one of them.

Model answer

Good evening, councillors. I walk past Ms Price's café every Saturday, so I care about both our shops and the air on our high street. The cycle group says a car-free day would fill our streets, but it gives no examples, and the villagers who keep her café busy can hardly walk in. A car-free Saturday could work, but only with a shuttle bus that runs often enough to replace their cars.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two articles, one speech to write. How much can you borrow?

Borrow the ideas, not the sentences. Select what fits the task, rework it in your own words and voice, then develop and evaluate it, linking the texts, so your answer shows how well you read as well as how well you write.

Mission 3

Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Make a guess: Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 1

An argument is a line, not a list

  • Make your viewpoint clear early, in one sentence.
  • Each paragraph adds a reason that builds on the last, not a random new point.
  • Fit tone and register to the reader and form: a letter to a council is not a speech to Year 9.
  • End by turning to the reader: what should they now think or do?
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Q1(b): a discursive, argumentative or persuasive speech, letter, article or report that uses the text(s); about 250–350 words; 35 marks (25 writing, 10 reading). Coursework Assignment 1: 30 marks.
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 3

Concede, counter, return

  • Concede: state the strongest objection fairly, in its best form.
  • Counter (rebut): answer it with a reason or an example.
  • Return: bring the reader back to your view.
  • Why it works: a reader who disagrees feels heard and keeps reading; your view looks tested, not naive.
  • Pick the objection your reader really holds. Knocking down a silly version (a straw man) convinces nobody.
Pick the strongestScreen 4
?Task: write an article arguing that the school day should start at 10am. Which counter-argument is strongest?
Writing · argue and persuadeScreen 5

Evidence that convinces

  • A specific, believable example beats an invented statistic: a made-up "99% of experts" makes readers doubt you.
  • Use the reader's world: their street, their school, their Saturday.
  • Techniques such as a rhetorical question or a pattern of three only work when they serve a point.
  • Save your strongest reason for last, or for just after the counter-argument.

Write itScreen 8
?Concede and counter.

Statement: 'Homework should be banned for students under 14.' You disagree. Write a counter-argument paragraph in three to five sentences: concede the strongest point for a ban, answer it, then return to your view.

Model answer

Those who want a ban have a point: a ten-year-old who spends every evening on worksheets is losing time to play, read and rest. Too much homework is a real problem. But the answer to too much is not none. Twenty minutes of reading, or practising the times tables that confused them in class, is the difference between a skill that fades by Friday and one that sticks. Cut the pile, by all means, but keep the one habit that teaches children to learn on their own.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does admitting the other side has a point make you stronger?

Because you then answer it. Conceding the strongest objection, fairly, and then countering it shows your view survives the best case against it, so a doubtful reader feels heard and is more willing to be persuaded.

Mission 4

A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Make a guess: A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Writing · descriptionScreen 1

Point the camera

  • Good description works like a camera, not a list. You choose where it points.
  • Wide shot: the whole scene in a sentence or two. Mid shot: one group or corner. Close-up: one small detail.
  • Choose three or four shots, not twenty. Each new paragraph can be a new shot.
  • The close-up usually carries the feeling: one exact detail the reader remembers.
Writing · descriptionScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Composition: two of the four titles are descriptive; about 350–450 words, 40 marks. Coursework Assignment 2: writing to describe, 500–800 words, 25 marks.
Writing · descriptionScreen 3

Show it, don't label it

Label (tells)Detail (shows)
The café was crowded.Two queues for the counter have merged into one argument.
The kitchen was freezing.Her breath clouds over the cereal bowl.
He was nervous.He reads the same line of the menu four times.
  • Pick details only this scene would have.
  • One exact noun ("crate", "awning") beats three adjectives.
  • Use more than sight: one sound, one smell, one texture.
Pick the strongestScreen 4
?Task: describe a busy market. Which opening sentence is strongest?
Writing · descriptionScreen 5

Shape without a plot

  • A description needs structure, even without events. Three reliable shapes:
  • Zoom: wide, then closer, then one detail.
  • Time shift: the same place at different times (dawn and dusk, summer and winter).
  • One moment stretched: a few seconds, described slowly.
  • End on purpose: a quiet close-up, or an image that echoes your first line.

Describe a bus station late at night in three to five sentences. Start wide, then close in on one detail that carries the mood. Don't use the words 'empty' or 'lonely'.

Model answer

The last bus has gone, but the departures board keeps listing towns nobody is travelling to. Under it, the plastic seats are bolted together in fours, all facing the same dark road. One seat has a coat folded on it, neatly, as if its owner has only stepped away. The sleeve hangs down and swings a little each time the automatic doors open on nothing.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Point it at a few chosen shots and finish close. A wide shot sets the scene, a mid shot finds a corner, and one exact detail (a man turning sideways with a crate and still not getting through) shows the crowd better than any label.

Mission 5

Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Make a guess: Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Writing · narrativeScreen 1

A short story is small

  • In 350–450 words, write a small story: one main character, one place, a short stretch of time.
  • Something must change: a decision, a discovery, a realisation.
  • A simple shape: situation → problem → turning point → aftermath.
  • Choose a point of view (first or third person) and keep it consistent.
Writing · narrativeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Composition: two of the four titles are narrative; about 350–450 words, 40 marks. Coursework Assignment 3: writing to narrate, 500–800 words, 25 marks.
Writing · narrativeScreen 3

Where stories go wrong

  • Too many events: a crash, a rescue, a hospital, a funeral. Each gets one line, and none is felt.
  • A rushed ending: the last event is squeezed in as time runs out.
  • A slow start: the alarm clock, breakfast, the walk to school.
  • The fix: plan the ending first, cut events, and spend your words on the turning point, slowing it down.
Rank themScreen 4
?Title: The Last Train. Rank these story plans from strongest to weakest.

Tap the strongest answer first, then the next one. Tap a numbered answer again to undo.

Writing · narrativeScreen 5

Openings and endings

  • Open close to the change: start in the moment, or with one telling detail, not with the alarm clock.
  • End with an action or image, not an explanation. Let the reader work out what has changed.
  • An ending can be open: a character pauses, decides or notices, and the story stops.
  • Avoid endings that cancel the story (it was all a dream).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Usually at the end, though the problem starts in the plan. Too many events mean every moment is summarised and the ending is squeezed in; a small story with one change, a planned ending and a slowed-down turning point has time to land.

Mission 6

‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

Make a guess: ‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 1

Verbs and nouns that do the work

  • A precise verb carries meaning a plain one leaves out. Compare ‘walked’ with ‘trudged’ (tired), ‘strode’ (confident) and ‘crept’ (secretive).
  • A precise noun can replace a stack of adjectives: ‘a big, loud, angry crowd’ → ‘a mob’.
  • Precision, not length: one exact word beats three loose ones.
  • Cut empty words such as ‘very’, ‘really’, ‘nice’ and ‘lots of’: find the word that says it.
Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 2

Connotation, register and over-writing

  • Connotations are a word's associations: ‘slender’, ‘skinny’ and ‘scrawny’ share a meaning but pass different judgements.
  • Match register to the task: ‘kids’ suits a chatty blog; ‘young people’ suits a letter to a council.
  • Avoid over-writing: showy thesaurus words that don't fit, or an adjective on every noun, distract the reader.
  • Ask of each choice: what does this word make my reader see, feel or think?
Pick the strongestScreen 3
?Task: write one sentence describing a market at closing time. Which is strongest?
Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 4

Four sentence types

TypeBuilt fromExample
Simpleone main clauseThe lights failed.
Compoundtwo main clauses joined by and, but, or, so or a semicolonThe lights failed, and the hall fell silent.
Complexa main clause plus a subordinate clause (because, when, although, if…)When the lights failed, the hall fell silent.
Minorno main verb; used for effectSilence.
  • Simple doesn't mean short: one main clause can carry plenty of detail.
Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 5

Shaping sentences for effect

  • Vary length: a short sentence after long ones lands with force.
  • Vary openings: start with a subordinate clause (‘Although it was late, …’), an adverb (‘Slowly, …’), a prepositional phrase (‘Beneath the bridge, …’) or an -ing verb (‘Shivering, …’).
  • Save the key word for the end: ‘At the bottom of the drawer, under the old letters, lay the key.’
  • Patterns: a list of three or a repeated structure builds rhythm. Use them on purpose, not by habit.

Writing · vocabulary and sentencesScreen 8

How your exam asks this

  • W3, a range of vocabulary and sentence structures appropriate to context, is assessed in Paper 1 Question 4, Paper 2 Question 1(b) and the Composition (or Component 3).
Mission checkScreen 9

?‘The door opened’ is flat. Why does ‘the door gave way’ tell a story?

The difference is the verb. ‘Opened’ reports a result; ‘gave way’ implies pressure, resistance and collapse, so the reader starts imagining what pushed against the door and what lies behind it. Precise words, chosen for their connotations and shaped into varied sentences, let a few words do a lot of work.

Mission 7

‘Let's eat, Grandma’ or ‘Let's eat Grandma’? What can one comma do?

Make a guess: ‘Let's eat, Grandma’ or ‘Let's eat Grandma’? What can one comma do?

Writing · technical accuracyScreen 1

Where does a sentence end?

  • A full sentence needs a main clause: a subject and verb that make sense alone (‘The bell rang.’). Minor sentences (‘Silence.’) are a deliberate exception.
  • Sentence demarcation: a capital to start; a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark to end.
  • A comma splice joins two main clauses with only a comma: ‘The bell rang, everyone ran.’ ✗
  • Four fixes: a full stop, a semicolon, a conjunction (‘The bell rang, and everyone ran.’) or a subordinate clause (‘When the bell rang, everyone ran.’).
Sort itScreen 2
?Is each sentence correct, or a comma splice?

Tap a statement, then tap the group it belongs in. Then press Check.

Writing · technical accuracyScreen 3

Commas that change meaning

  • Direct address: a comma separates the person spoken to. ‘Let's eat, Grandma.’ invites her to dinner; without the comma, she is dinner.
  • After a fronted clause or phrase: ‘After the match, the fans went home.’
  • Lists: ‘We packed water, maps and a torch.’
  • A pair of commas marks extra information. ‘My sister, who lives in Leeds, is visiting.’ (I have one sister.) ‘My sister who lives in Leeds is visiting.’ (I have more than one.)
Writing · technical accuracyScreen 4

Apostrophes

  • Omission shows missing letters: do not → don't; it is → it's; they are → they're.
  • Possession, one owner: add 's (‘the dog's lead’). A plural ending in s: add ' (‘the dogs' leads’). A plural not ending in s: add 's (‘the children's toys’).
  • Its (belonging to it) has no apostrophe: ‘The town lost its bus.’ It's always means ‘it is’ or ‘it has’.
  • Never add an apostrophe just to make a plural: ‘two coffees’, ‘the 1990s’.
Pick the strongestScreen 5
?Three students correct the apostrophes in this sentence about one team and its whole squad: ‘The teams coach said its the players best season.’ Whose correction is right?
Writing · technical accuracyScreen 6

Semicolons, colons and punctuation for effect

  • Semicolon: joins two closely linked main clauses (‘The hall was silent; nobody moved.’) or separates list items that already contain commas.
  • Colon: follows a main clause to introduce a list, an explanation or a reveal: ‘I knew one thing: we were lost.’
  • Before a colon, and on both sides of a semicolon joining clauses, you need a full sentence.
  • For effect: a dash adds a sharp aside; an ellipsis builds suspense; a rhetorical question involves the reader. One well-placed mark beats ten.

?Fix this comma splice by changing only the comma, keeping it one sentence: ‘The rain stopped, the match began.’ Which punctuation mark do you need in formal writing?
Writing · technical accuracyScreen 8

How your exam asks this

  • W5 (spelling, punctuation and grammar) is assessed in Paper 2 Question 1(b) and the Composition, or across the Component 3 portfolio. Paper 1 Question 4's writing marks cover W1–W4 only.
Mission checkScreen 9

?‘Let's eat, Grandma’ or ‘Let's eat Grandma’? What can one comma do?

One comma can change what a sentence means: in ‘Let's eat, Grandma’ it marks Grandma as the person spoken to. But a comma can't carry two sentences on its own: joining two main clauses needs a full stop, a semicolon, a conjunction or a subordinate clause.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A plan is five or six lines of notes: angle, ending, then one job per paragraph.
  • Argument moves point by point to a final turn to the reader; description and narrative move by shifts in time, place or focus to an ending that echoes the opening.
  • Link each paragraph back to the last (cohesion), and save the last few minutes for checking accuracy.
  • Read the task for form, audience, purpose and role; then select only the ideas that serve it.
  • Use, develop and evaluate: reword ideas, push them further, question claims and link the texts.
  • Paper 2 Question 1(b) gives 10 marks for reading and 25 for writing; Paper 1 Question 4 gives 10 and 10.
  • An argument is a line: a clear viewpoint, reasons that build, and an ending that turns to the reader.
  • Concede the strongest objection fairly, counter it with a reason or example, and return to your view; avoid straw men.
  • Specific, believable examples from the reader's world persuade better than invented statistics or piled-up techniques.
  • Description works like a camera: choose wide, mid and close-up shots, and move closer on purpose.
  • Show qualities through exact details and nouns, not labels or piled adjectives.
  • Give a description a shape (zoom, time shift, or one moment stretched) and end on a deliberate close-up or echo.
  • Write a small story: one character, one place, a short time, and one change.
  • Shape it (situation, problem, turning point, aftermath), plan the ending first, and slow down at the turning point.
  • Open close to the change and end with an action or image, not an explanation.
  • Choose precise verbs and nouns over plain words and adjective stacks; check connotation and register, and don't over-write.
  • Know the sentence types (simple, compound, complex, minor) and vary length, openings and word order for effect.
  • Your board's writing objective (AO4 i and iii, W3 or AO5) rewards a range of vocabulary and sentence structures.
  • Every sentence needs a main clause. Two main clauses joined only by a comma make a comma splice: fix it with a full stop, a semicolon, a conjunction or a subordinate clause.
  • Commas mark direct address, fronted clauses, lists and extra information; apostrophes mark omission and possession (its = belonging to it; it's = it is).
  • Semicolons join linked main clauses; colons introduce a list, explanation or reveal after a full sentence. Use dashes, ellipses and rhetorical questions sparingly, for effect.
