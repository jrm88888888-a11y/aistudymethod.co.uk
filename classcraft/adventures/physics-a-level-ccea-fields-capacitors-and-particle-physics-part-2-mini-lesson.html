Mission 1

Mission 1: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Make a guess: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Mission 1

Capacitors in parallel and in series

  • Parallel: same potential difference across each; the charges add, Q = Q1 + Q2.
  • C = C1 + C2 + …
  • Series: the same charge Q on every capacitor. The two joined inner plates form an isolated conductor: they hold −Q and +Q, and charge is conserved.
  • The p.d.s add, V = V1 + V2.
  • 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …
  • Series total is less than the smallest; parallel total is more than the largest.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • 3.0 μF and 6.0 μF in series across 12 V.
  • 1/C = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 = 1/2.0, so C = 2.0 μF.
  • Q = 2.0 μF × 12 V = 24 μC on each capacitor.
  • Potential differences: 24 ÷ 3.0 = 8.0 V and 24 ÷ 6.0 = 4.0 V. The smaller capacitor takes the larger potential difference.
  • The same pair in parallel: C = 9.0 μF, total charge 9.0 × 12 = 108 μC (1.1 × 10² μC).
Quick check
?Two capacitors, of capacitance C and 3C, are connected in series with a battery of emf E and negligible internal resistance. After they have been charging for a long time, what is the potential difference V across the capacitor C?
Quick check
?Four identical capacitors, each of capacitance C, are first connected all in parallel, giving an equivalent capacitance CX. They are then reconnected all in series, giving an equivalent capacitance CY. How does CX compare with CY?
Quick check
?A 4.7 μF capacitor is connected to a 9.0 V battery. Without changing the battery, which change doubles the total charge taken from the battery?
Mission check

?The circuit shown contains a battery and three capacitors, initially uncharged. After the capacitors have been charging for a long time, which capacitor has the largest potential difference across it?

The plates joined between two series capacitors form an isolated conductor that starts neutral: if one plate holds −Q, the other holds +Q. So every capacitor in a series chain carries the same charge, whatever its capacitance.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Make a guess: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Mission 2

Charging, step by step

  • Cell (emf E), resistor R and uncharged capacitor C in series. Close the switch.
  • At the start: VC = 0, so the whole emf is across R. The current is largest: I0 = E/R.
  • Electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other. VC rises.
  • p.d. across R = E − VC falls, so the current falls, so charging slows.
  • After a long time: VC = E, current zero. Fully charged.
Mission 2

Discharging

  • Now connect the charged capacitor across R alone.
  • The capacitor's p.d. drives the current: I = VC/R. Electrons flow back to the plate they left. The current is reversed compared with charging.
  • Charge leaves → VC falls → current falls → charge leaves more slowly.
  • The rate of loss is proportional to the charge left. That is exponential decay.
  • It gets ever closer to zero but, in this model, never reaches it.
Mission 2

The graphs

  • Charging: Q and VC rise steeply, then level off at the final value. Current starts at I0 and decays to zero.
  • Discharging: Q, VC and I all decay with the same shape.
  • Q and VC always have the same shape, because Q = CVC.
  • Gradient of a Q–t graph = current.
  • Area under an I–t graph = charge that has flowed.
Quick check
?A capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. At time t1 the p.d. across the capacitor is one quarter of the battery's emf. At a later time t2 it is three quarters of the emf. What is the current at t2 divided by the current at t1?
Mission 2

The time constant

  • Time constant τ = CR. Unit: Ω × F = s.
  • Discharging: after one τ, 37% of the starting charge is left.
  • Charging: after one τ, the charge has reached 63% of its final value.
  • After about 5τ: over 99% of the way. Treat as finished.
  • Bigger R → smaller current → slower. Bigger C → more charge to move → slower.
  • The starting p.d. does not change τ.
Mission 2

The equations

  • Discharging: Q = Q0e−t/CR, V = V0e−t/CR, I = I0e−t/CR.
  • At t = RC: e⁻¹ = 0.37, so 37% left; 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.63 reached.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor, charged to 9.0 V, discharges through 10 kΩ.
  • τ = CR = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 × 10³ = 4.7 s.
  • Initial current: I0 = 9.0 ÷ 10 × 10³ = 0.90 mA.
  • After 5.0 s: V = 9.0 × e−5.0/4.7 = 3.1 V.
  • Time to fall to 1.0 V: e−t/4.7 = 1.0/9.0, so t = 4.7 × ln 9.0 = 10 s.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a 3.0 kΩ resistor by an ideal battery of emf 9.0 V, all connected in series. What is the current in the resistor at the instant when the potential difference across the capacitor is 6.0 V?
Quick check
?A 50 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V and then discharged through a 200 kΩ resistor. What is the potential difference across the capacitor 15 s after the discharge starts?
Mission check

?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. Its charge takes a time t to reach 63% of its final value. The capacitor is emptied. The battery is replaced by one with twice the emf, and a second, identical resistor is added in series with the first. How long does the charge now take to reach 63% of its new final value?

The pace is set by RC alone: the time constant. Every RC, the charge falls to 37% of what it was, so it gets close to empty but never quite there.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Mission 3

Equal times, equal fractions

  • Exponential decay: in equal time intervals, the charge falls by the same fraction.
  • Every CR: × 0.37. Every 0.69CR: × ½.
  • Test for exponential: read V at equal intervals. Equal ratios V1/V0 = V2/V1 = … → exponential.
  • Example: 12.0 V, 8.0 V, 5.4 V, 3.6 V every 2.0 s: each ratio ≈ 0.67.
Mission 3

A straight line from a curve

  • Take natural logs of V = V0e−t/CR: ln V = ln V0 − t/CR.
  • Compare with y = c + mx. Plot ln V against t.
  • Straight line → the decay is exponential.
  • Gradient = −1/CR. Intercept = ln V0.
  • Logs need a pure number: plot ln V with V in volts (the log of the number of volts).
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A capacitor discharges through 100 kΩ. A graph of ln V (V in volts) against t is a straight line.
  • Gradient = −0.20 s⁻¹. Intercept = 2.48.
  • Time constant = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 s.
  • C = time constant ÷ R = 5.0 ÷ (100 × 10³) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ F (50 μF).
  • V0 = e2.48 = 12 V.
Quick check
?A student plots ln(I/A) against t for a capacitor discharging through a 20 kΩ resistor. The graph is a straight line of gradient −0.025 s⁻¹. What is the capacitance?
Mission 3

The practical

  • Circuit: d.c. supply, two-way switch, capacitor, resistor. Voltmeter (or data logger) across the capacitor, or an ammeter in series with R.
  • Charge fully. Switch to discharge and start timing.
  • Slow decay (time constant about a minute): read V every 10 s by hand.
  • Fast decay: use a data logger or an oscilloscope.
  • Plot ln V against t; time constant = −1/gradient.
  • A voltmeter across the capacitor is a second discharge path: use a very high-resistance meter.
  • Capacitor values have wide tolerances (often ±20%): expect the time constant to differ from the value worked out from the labels.
Quick check
?A student measures the time constant of a 10 μF capacitor discharging through a 1.0 MΩ resistor, so the time constant should be 10 s. A voltmeter of resistance 10 MΩ stays connected across the capacitor throughout. How does the measured time constant compare with 10 s?
Mission 3

Flash guns and defibrillators

  • Both need a large energy in a very short time: a very high power. A battery cannot supply a current that large.
  • Charging: the battery charges the capacitor through a large resistance. Long time constant: a few seconds. That is why there is a wait between shots.
  • Discharging: through a small resistance (the flash tube, or the patient's chest). Short time constant: about a millisecond for a flash, about 10 ms for a defibrillator.
  • Energy stored = ½CV², so the capacitor is charged to a high p.d.: hundreds of volts in a flash gun, a few thousand in a defibrillator.
Mission 3

Worked example: a defibrillator

  • C = 150 μF, charged to 1.6 kV: energy = ½ × 150 × 10⁻⁶ × 1600² = 190 J.
  • Chest resistance about 75 Ω: τ = CR = 150 × 10⁻⁶ × 75 = 11 ms.
  • Energy ∝ V², so after one CR only (0.37)² = 0.14 of it is left: 86% delivered in 11 ms.
  • Mean power over that time ≈ 0.86 × 192 ÷ 0.011 ≈ 15 kW.
Quick check
?A flash gun makes you wait several seconds between shots while its capacitor recharges. Which change would shorten the wait?
Mission check

?A student plots ln I (I in amperes) against t for a discharging capacitor. The best-fit straight line passes through ln I = −6.20 at t = 0 and ln I = −7.70 at t = 30 s. How long does the current take to fall to half of any value?

ln V (or ln I, ln Q) against t is a straight line of gradient −1/(time constant). The curve never reaches zero, but the line gives the time constant directly.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Mission 4

Where magnetic fields come from

  • A magnetic field is produced by moving charges (an electric current) and by permanent magnets.
  • It exerts forces on moving charges, currents and magnetic materials (iron, steel).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Mission 4

Field lines

  • Direction of a field line: the way the north end of a compass needle points.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Field lines are closed loops: outside a magnet they run N → S; inside, S → N.
Mission 4

Field of a long straight wire

  • Field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current; curled fingers give the field direction.
  • Lines spread out with distance: the field gets weaker away from the wire.
  • End-on views: ⊙ current out of the page → field anticlockwise; ⊗ into the page → clockwise.
Quick check
?A long, straight, vertical wire carries a steady current upwards. Point P is 5.0 cm due east of the wire, level with it. What is the direction of the magnetic field of the wire at P?
Mission 4

Flat coil and solenoid

  • Flat coil: the circles around each part of the wire combine. At the centre the field is along the axis.
  • Solenoid (long coil): inside, straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines (uniform field). Outside, the pattern of a bar magnet.
  • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows around the turns; the thumb points along the field inside, towards the coil's N end.
Quick check
?You look at the left end of a long solenoid. Seen from there, the current in its turns flows clockwise. Which end of the solenoid is its north end?
Mission check

?A flat horizontal circular coil carries a steady current that flows clockwise when viewed from above. What is the direction of the magnetic field at the centre of the coil?

Same rule, new shape. Around a straight wire the field circles the current, so a compass needle just below a wire laid along it swings towards lying across the wire, tangent to a circle centred on it: fully across if the wire's field is much stronger than Earth's. Reverse the current and it swings the other way.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Make a guess: A wire carrying a current lies between the poles of a magnet. Which way is it pushed?

Mission 5

The motor effect

  • A current-carrying wire in a magnetic field feels a force.
  • The force is at right angles to both the current and the field.
  • No force when the current is parallel to the field.
  • Reverse the current or the field: the force reverses.
Mission 5

Which way?

  • Fleming's left-hand rule: First finger = Field (N → S); seCond finger = Current (+ → −); thuMb = Motion (force).
Quick check
?A straight wire lies in the plane of the page and carries a current towards the right of the page. A uniform magnetic field in the plane of the page is directed towards the top of the page. What is the direction of the force on the wire?
Mission 5

How big?

  • F = BIl, with the field perpendicular to the current.
  • The length is the length inside the field.
  • B is in tesla (T).
Mission 5

Magnetic flux density

  • B is the magnetic flux density.
  • Rearranged (current ⟂ field): B = F/(Il): force per unit current per unit length.
  • 1 tesla: the field in which a wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field feels 1 N on each metre.
  • 1 T = 1 N A⁻¹ m⁻¹.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 4.0 cm length of wire carries 2.5 A at right angles to a 0.15 T field.
  • Force = 0.15 × 2.5 × 0.040 = 0.015 N.
  • Backwards: 0.012 N on a 5.0 cm wire carrying 3.0 A at right angles. B = force ÷ (current × length) = 0.012 ÷ (3.0 × 0.050) = 0.080 T.
Quick check
?A 6.0 cm length of wire carrying a current of 4.0 A lies at right angles to a uniform magnetic field. The force on it is 0.018 N. What is the magnetic flux density?
Mission 5

The motor principle

  • A rectangular coil carries a current in a magnetic field.
  • The two sides across the field carry current in opposite directions, so they feel equal, opposite forces: a couple. The coil turns.
  • A split-ring commutator reverses the current every half turn, so the coil keeps turning the same way.
  • More turns, larger current, stronger field → larger turning effect.
Mission check

?A long straight wire carries a current vertically upwards. It is in a uniform horizontal magnetic field that points due north. In which direction is the magnetic force on the wire?

The force is at right angles to both the current and the field, and its size is B × current × length when they are perpendicular: so a wire between the poles is pushed sideways, out of the gap or into it, never towards a pole.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Make a guess: Drop a strong magnet down a copper pipe and it falls in slow motion. Why?

Mission 6

Magnetic flux

  • Magnetic flux: how much field passes through an area. Think: number of field lines through it.
  • φ = BA, with B normal (⟂) to the area A.
  • Unit: weber. 1 Wb = 1 T m².
  • Plane of the loop parallel to the field: zero flux.
Mission 6

Flux linkage

  • A coil of N turns: the same flux passes through every turn.
  • Flux linkage = Nφ.
  • With B ⟂ to the coil: Nφ = NBA.
Quick check
?A flat coil of 150 turns, each of area 20 cm², is placed with its plane at right angles to a uniform magnetic field of 0.40 T. What is the flux linkage of the coil?
Mission 6

Faraday's law

  • An emf is induced whenever the flux linkage changes: move a magnet, move the coil, change B, change the area, or turn the coil.
  • Steady flux, however large: no emf.
  • Size: emf = rate of change of flux linkage.
  • Average emf E = Δ(Nφ)/Δt (magnitude).
  • On a flux linkage–time graph, size of the emf = gradient. On a graph of the flux through one turn, size of the emf = N × gradient.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A coil of 200 turns, area 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ m², is ⟂ to a 0.50 T field. The field falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s.
  • Change in flux per turn = 0.50 × 4.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ Wb. Change in flux linkage = 200 × 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.040 Wb.
  • emf = 0.040 ÷ 0.10 = 0.40 V (while the field is falling).
  • Same change in 0.050 s: twice the emf. Once the field stops changing: zero.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the magnetic flux through each turn of a coil of 200 turns varies with time t. The coil is held at rest. What is the magnitude of the emf induced in the coil between t = 0.50 s and t = 0.70 s?
  • (The graph labels the flux Φ; your data sheet writes it φ.)
Mission 6

Lenz's law

  • The induced current flows in the direction that opposes the change that produces it.
  • N pole pushed in: the near end of the coil becomes an N pole, repelling it.
  • N pole pulled out: the near end becomes an S pole, attracting it back.
  • Faraday's law gives the size of the emf; Lenz's law gives its direction.
Mission 6

Lenz's law is energy conservation

  • Suppose the induced current helped the change: the magnet would be pulled in faster, the current would grow, and so on. Energy from nothing.
  • So the induced current must oppose the motion. You must do work against that force.
  • That work becomes electrical energy, then thermal energy in the circuit.
  • Open circuit: an emf, but no current, so no opposing force.
  • A solid metal sheet or pipe acts like many loops: changing flux drives eddy currents around inside it, and they oppose the motion too.
Quick check
?The south pole of a bar magnet is pulled away from one end of a coil whose ends are joined. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A thick aluminium plate swings as a pendulum through the gap between the poles of a strong magnet, and stops after a few swings. An identical plate with many deep slots cut into it swings for much longer. Which explains the difference?

The falling magnet changes the flux through each ring of the pipe, so currents flow around the pipe. By Lenz's law their field opposes the magnet's motion, slowing it; its lost energy heats the copper.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in one coil. Why does a meter on a separate coil flick, then fall back to zero?

Mission 7

Seeing induction: magnet and coil

  • Coil joined to a centre-zero galvanometer (or data logger). Push a magnet in: the needle flicks.
  • Magnet at rest, even deep inside: no reading.
  • Pull it out, or use the other pole: the needle flicks the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster motion, stronger magnet, more turns.
Mission 7

Linked coils

  • Two coils on one iron core. Coil 1 has a switch and a cell; coil 2 has a meter.
  • Switch on: the current in coil 1 grows, its flux grows, and coil 2's flux linkage changes: a flick.
  • Steady current: steady flux, zero emf. Switch off: a flick the other way.
  • Bigger emf with: faster change of current, more turns on coil 2, an iron core (more of coil 1's flux links coil 2).
  • An alternating current in coil 1 gives a continuous alternating emf in coil 2: the principle of the transformer.
Quick check
?According to Faraday's law of induction, the magnitude of the emf induced in a coil with a fixed number of turns is proportional to which quantity?
Mission 7

Demonstrating Lenz's law

  • Hanging aluminium ring. Push a magnet towards it: the ring moves away. Pull the magnet back: the ring follows.
  • Ring with a slit: no complete circuit, no current, no movement. The emf alone exerts no force.
  • Galvanometer and coil: find which way current makes an N pole at the coil's end (test with a cell). Push an N pole in: the coil's near end becomes N.
  • Each time, the induced current opposes the change. To keep the magnet moving you must do work: that is where the electrical energy comes from.
Quick check
?Some trains brake with strong magnets held just above the steel rail. The magnets move along the rail with the train and never touch it. Where does the train's kinetic energy go, and how does the braking force change as the train slows down?
Mission 7

Reading flux-linkage graphs

  • Size of emf = gradient of the flux linkage–time graph. The induced emf opposes the change, so the graph draws it as minus the gradient.
  • Example: flux linkage rises steadily from 0 to 0.060 Wb in 0.20 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.20 = 0.30 V.
  • Flux linkage then stays at 0.060 Wb: emf = 0.
  • Flux linkage falls to 0 in 0.10 s: emf = 0.060 ÷ 0.10 = 0.60 V, in the opposite direction.
  • Steeper gradient → bigger emf. Flat → none, whatever the value.
Mission check

?Two coils share an iron core. The current in coil 1 rises steadily from 0 to 2.0 A in 0.40 s, stays at 2.0 A for a while, then falls steadily to zero in 0.10 s. How does the emf induced in coil 2 compare in the three intervals?

An emf appears only while flux linkage is changing. Switching on coil 1 changes coil 2's flux linkage for an instant: a flick. Once the current is steady, nothing changes, and the reading falls back to zero.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Parallel: C = C1 + C2; series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2, same charge on each.

Mission 2

  • Charging: Q and V rise to a limit while I decays; discharging: Q, V and I all decay exponentially.
  • τ = CR: 37% left after one τ (discharging), 63% reached (charging).
  • Discharge: Q = Q0e−t/CR, V = V0e−t/CR, I = I0e−t/CR.

Mission 3

  • ln V = ln V0 − t/CR: plot ln V against t; gradient = −1/CR.
  • Flash guns and defibrillators: slow charge (large R), fast discharge (small R), high power.

Mission 4

  • Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets; field lines are closed loops, closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: circles, direction by the right-hand grip rule. Solenoid: uniform inside, bar-magnet pattern outside.

Mission 5

  • Force on a current in a field: at right angles to both; zero when parallel.
  • Direction: Fleming's left-hand rule.
  • F = BIl (perpendicular).
  • 1 T: 1 N on 1 m of wire carrying 1 A at right angles to the field.

Mission 6

  • Flux φ = BA with B ⟂ A; flux linkage Nφ. Unit: Wb = T m².
  • Faraday: emf = rate of change of flux linkage; no change, no emf.
  • Lenz: the induced current opposes the change; otherwise energy would be created.

Mission 7

  • Induced emf only while flux linkage changes: faster change, stronger field or more turns give more emf.
  • Lenz's law in practice: the induced current opposes the change, so work must be done; a cut ring has emf but no current and no force.
