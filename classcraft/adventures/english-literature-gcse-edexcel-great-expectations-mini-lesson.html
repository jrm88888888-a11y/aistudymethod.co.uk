Mission 1

Why does Dickens split Pip's life into three "stages"?

Make a guess: Why does Dickens split Pip's life into three "stages"?

Great Expectations · structureScreen 1

A novel of growing up

  • A bildungsroman is a novel about a character growing from childhood to maturity, morally as well as in age.
  • Pip tells his own story as an older man looking back: a retrospective first-person narrator.
  • Dickens published it weekly in his magazine All the Year Round, 1 December 1860 to 3 August 1861: 36 instalments. Then came three volumes, one per "stage".
  • It is set decades before 1860: Pip's parents lived "long before the days of photographs" (Ch I).

How your exam asks this

  • Edexcel Paper 2 Section A: (a) language in an extract of about 400 words, 20 marks (AO2); (b) an essay on the whole novel, 20 marks (AO1). Closed book. Context isn't marked in this section.
  • Essay practice is for class; these missions build the knowledge.

Great Expectations · structureScreen 7

Two endings

  • First ending: Pip meets a remarried Estella briefly in London; they part. The novelist Bulwer-Lytton found it too sad, so Dickens rewrote it. Forster's Life of Charles Dickens later printed it.
  • Published ending (Ch LIX): Pip meets the widowed Estella in the ruins of Satis House. It ends: "I saw no shadow of another parting from her."
  • Editions word that line slightly differently: check yours.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does Dickens split Pip's life into three "stages"?

Each stage ends at a turning point. Stage 1 ends with Pip leaving for London full of hope, Stage 2 with the shock that his money comes from Magwitch, and Stage 3 with Pip, older and humbler, meeting Estella again. The stages trace how he grows up.

Mission 2

Pip is a snob for half the novel. Should we still like him?

Make a guess: Pip is a snob for half the novel. Should we still like him?

Great Expectations · PipScreen 1

Two Pips in one voice

  • Young Pip is the character who acts; older Pip is the narrator who looks back and judges.
  • Dickens presents Pip's growth in an arc: innocent child → ashamed apprentice → snobbish young gentleman → humbled, grateful man.
  • The older narrator often confesses: "Let me confess exactly with what feelings I looked forward to Joe’s coming" (Ch XXVII).
  • Write 'Dickens presents Pip as…': Pip is a construct, not a real person.

Great Expectations · PipScreen 4

The gentleman in London

  • In Stage 2 Pip spends freely, runs up debts and joins a club of idle young men, the Finches of the Grove (Ch XXXIV).
  • He dreads Joe's visit: "If I could have kept him away by paying money, I certainly would have paid money." (Ch XXVII)
  • The older narrator judges this: "our worst weaknesses and meannesses are usually committed for the sake of the people whom we most despise."
  • Yet he secretly pays for Herbert's start in business (Ch XXXVII): he can still be generous.

Great Expectations · PipScreen 6

Pip's change

  • Magwitch's return (Ch XXXIX) destroys Pip's dream: "I had deserted Joe."
  • He turns from disgust to loyalty, holding Magwitch's hand in court (Ch LVI).
  • Ill and in debt, he is nursed by Joe (Ch LVII): "O God bless this gentle Christian man!"
  • He works abroad and pays his debts (Ch LVIII).
  • Interpretations: many readers see a hard-earned redemption; others argue he is left alone, paying for his snobbery.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Pip is a snob for half the novel. Should we still like him?

There's no single verdict, but the best answers weigh both sides. Dickens shows Pip's snobbery clearly, through Joe's visit and his debts, yet the older Pip judges himself honestly, and his loyalty to Magwitch and gratitude to Joe show he has grown. Many readers like him because he learns.

Mission 3

Miss Havisham stopped every clock. Who is she really punishing?

Make a guess: Miss Havisham stopped every clock. Who is she really punishing?

Great Expectations · Miss Havisham and EstellaScreen 1

The jilted bride of Satis House

  • Herbert tells Pip the story (Ch XXII): on her wedding day Miss Havisham received a letter breaking off the marriage, at twenty to nine.
  • She stopped every clock at that minute, left the wedding feast to rot and still wears the dress.
  • The man had taken "great sums of money from her". Much later Pip learns he was Compeyson, Magwitch's enemy (Ch XLII).
  • She adopted the girl Estella.

Great Expectations · Miss Havisham and EstellaScreen 4

Estella, raised as a weapon

  • Miss Havisham whispers to the young Estella: "break their hearts and have no mercy!" (Ch XII).
  • Her idea of love is "blind devotion, unquestioning self-humiliation, utter submission" (Ch XXIX).
  • Estella warns Pip, "I have no heart" (Ch XXIX).
  • When Miss Havisham complains of her coldness, Estella replies, "I am what you have made me." (Ch XXXVIII)
  • Gothic: a dark, decaying house and a corpse-like bride make Satis House a place of living death.

Great Expectations · Miss Havisham and EstellaScreen 6

Consequences

  • Estella chooses to marry the brutal Drummle (Ch XLIV). Miss Havisham kneels to Pip: "What have I done! What have I done!" (Ch XLIX).
  • Her dress catches fire; Pip burns his hands saving her. She later dies (Ch LVII).
  • Years later Estella says she has "been bent and broken, but—I hope—into a better shape" (Ch LIX).
  • Interpretations: some readers see Miss Havisham as a villain, others as a victim whose grief turned to revenge; many see Estella as her victim too.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Miss Havisham stopped every clock. Who is she really punishing?

She sets out to punish men, using Estella to break their hearts, starting with Pip. But the stopped clocks trap her too, and she makes Estella unable to love. Her cry "What have I done!" shows she finally sees that her revenge has damaged Estella and herself.

Mission 4

A convict and a blacksmith. Which one is Pip's real father?

Make a guess: A convict and a blacksmith. Which one is Pip's real father?

Great Expectations · Magwitch and JoeScreen 1

Two men in a father's place

  • Pip's parents are dead (Ch I). His harsh sister brings him up "by hand", with her husband Joe Gargery, the blacksmith.
  • Joe is Pip's friend and protector: "Ever the best of friends" (Ch VII).
  • Magwitch is the convict Pip feeds on the marshes (Ch I–III). Transported to New South Wales, he grows rich as a "sheep-farmer, stock-breeder" (Ch XXXIX).
  • Magwitch is Pip's secret benefactor.

Great Expectations · Magwitch and JoeScreen 4

Joe: gentle strength

  • Older Pip compares Joe to "the steam-hammer that can crush a man or pat an egg-shell" (Ch XVIII).
  • Joe's dialect ("Diwisions", "meshes") marks him as working-class; Dickens gives that voice wisdom.
  • Joe nurses Pip through fever and pays his debt: "the receipt was in his name" (Ch LVII).
  • Interpretations: most readers see Joe as the novel's moral centre. Some argue he is too passive: he can't protect Pip from Mrs Joe.

Great Expectations · Magwitch and JoeScreen 7

From horror to love

  • At first Pip recoils "as if he had been a snake" (Ch XXXIX).
  • Hiding him, Pip begins to pity him. After the failed escape he stands by him, holding his hand in court (Ch LVI).
  • On his deathbed Magwitch tells Pip: "You’ve never deserted me, dear boy." Pip prays, "O Lord, be merciful to him a sinner!"
  • Interpretations: some readers see Magwitch's gift as possessive ("I’ve made a gentleman"); others see a love as real as Joe's.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A convict and a blacksmith. Which one is Pip's real father?

The strongest answer is both, in different ways. Joe gives steady, selfless love; Magwitch gives his fortune and risks his life. Pip is ashamed of both, and his growing up is learning to value them: he stands by Magwitch to the end and finally sees Joe as a "gentle Christian man".

Mission 5

Pip gets a gentleman's money and clothes. Is he a gentleman yet?

Make a guess: Pip gets a gentleman's money and clothes. Is he a gentleman yet?

Great Expectations · class and gentilityScreen 1

What makes a "gentleman"?

  • Jaggers says Pip will be "removed from his present sphere of life" and "brought up as a gentleman" (Ch XVIII).
  • Traditionally a gentleman had birth, land or money and didn't work with his hands. Magwitch's aim is for Pip to "be above work" (Ch XXXIX).
  • In the 19th century, men from the middle classes increasingly claimed the title through education, manners and money.
  • Samuel Smiles's Self-Help (1859) praised rising by hard work.
  • Context isn't marked in Section A, but it helps you see the theme.

Great Expectations · class and gentilityScreen 4

Gentlemen in the novel

  • Compeyson: "set up fur a gentleman", school-educated, a swindler. At their trial he gets seven years, Magwitch fourteen (Ch XLII).
  • Drummle: "next heir but one to a baronetcy" (Ch XXIII), sulky and later cruel to Estella.
  • Herbert: poor, kind and honest.
  • Joe: a working man whom Pip finally calls a "gentle Christian man" (Ch LVII).
  • Pip learns a gentleman's manners in London but loses his gentleness.

Great Expectations · class and gentilityScreen 6

Where a gentleman's money comes from

  • Pip's money comes from Magwitch, a convict's earnings in Australia: "I’ve made a gentleman on you!" (Ch XXXIX). The shock shows how fragile his status is.
  • By the end Pip has no fortune but says, "I work pretty hard for a sufficient living, and therefore—yes, I do well." (Ch LIX)
  • Interpretations: many readers see the novel attacking snobbery; some argue it still admires refinement, since Pip never returns to the forge.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Pip gets a gentleman's money and clothes. Is he a gentleman yet?

Not yet. Money, clothes and lessons give Pip a gentleman's manner, but Dickens shows, through Compeyson, Drummle, Herbert and Joe, that a true gentleman is made "at heart". Pip becomes one only when he loses his fortune and learns gratitude and honest work.

Mission 6

Pip never commits a real crime. Why does he feel so guilty?

Make a guess: Pip never commits a real crime. Why does he feel so guilty?

Great Expectations · crime and justiceScreen 1

Crime and punishment in Pip's world

  • Hulks: old ships used as floating prisons, 1776–1857. Mrs Joe: "Hulks are prison-ships, right ’cross th’ meshes." (Ch II)
  • Transportation: convicts shipped to Australia, to New South Wales 1788–1840; it ended entirely in 1868. Magwitch was "sent for life. It’s death to come back." (Ch XXXIX)
  • Newgate: London's main prison; public hangings outside it 1783–1868.
  • Returning had stopped being a capital crime by the mid-1830s, so 1860s readers saw an older, harsher world.
Great Expectations · crime and justiceScreen 2

Pip's guilt, from the first chapter

  • He steals food and a file for the convict (Ch II), then keeps it secret from Joe: "I was too cowardly to do what I knew to be right" (Ch VI).
  • Mrs Joe is struck down, and a convict's leg-iron lies beside her (Ch XV–XVI). Pip half-believes he "must have had some hand in the attack".
  • After visiting Newgate he feels "contaminated" by "this taint of prison and crime" (Ch XXXII).

Great Expectations · crime and justiceScreen 5

Is the law fair?

  • Compeyson and Magwitch commit the same crimes. Compeyson, who "looked" a gentleman, gets seven years; Magwitch gets fourteen (Ch XLII).
  • Jaggers "washed his clients off, as if he were a surgeon or a dentist" (Ch XXVI).
  • The narrator notes that "At that time jails were much neglected" (Ch XXXII).
  • Magwitch is sentenced to death with "two-and-thirty men and women" at once (Ch LVI).
  • Context isn't marked in Section A, but it explains Dickens's criticism.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Pip never commits a real crime. Why does he feel so guilty?

Pip's guilt is moral, not legal. A small theft and a secret from Joe make the marshes shout "young thief!", and his later "taint of prison" is mixed with shame at deserting Joe. Meanwhile Dickens shows the real courts judging by class and looks, so the novel asks who the true criminals are.

Mission 7

Two Pips tell this story. Which one should you listen to?

Make a guess: Two Pips tell this story. Which one should you listen to?

Great Expectations · narration and imageryScreen 1

The older Pip looks back

  • A retrospective first-person narrator: the older Pip tells the story of the younger one.
  • He can see himself from outside: "the small bundle of shivers growing afraid of it all and beginning to cry, was Pip" (Ch I).
  • Hindsight lets him judge and hint at what's coming: "the turning point of my life, now opens on my view" (Ch XXXVII).
  • The narrator isn't the author: write 'Dickens has the older Pip…', not 'Dickens says'.

Great Expectations · narration and imageryScreen 4

Patterns of imagery

  • Mist: rises as Pip leaves home, "and the world lay spread before me" (Ch XIX); his expectations "dissolved, like our own marsh mists before the sun" (Ch LVII).
  • Chains and iron: the convict's leg-iron; the hulk "ironed like the prisoners" (Ch V); Magwitch's money as "gold and silver chains" (Ch XXXIX).
  • Stopped time: Miss Havisham's stopped clocks and decaying rooms (Ch VIII).
  • Hands: "coarse hands" (Ch VIII), Jaggers's hand-washing (Ch XXVI), Pip's burned hands (Ch XLIX).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Two Pips tell this story. Which one should you listen to?

Listen to both. Young Pip lets us feel his shame, hopes and fears as they happened; older Pip looks back with hindsight, judging and regretting, as in his cry "O dear good Joe". The gap between them is where much of the novel's meaning lies, and images like mist and chains carry it across the book.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Great Expectations is a bildungsroman told by the older Pip, serialised weekly in All the Year Round (1860–61) and then published in three volumes.
  • The three stages end at turning points: leaving for London (Ch XIX), Magwitch's return (Ch XXXIX), the meeting with Estella in the ruins (Ch LIX).
  • Dickens replaced a sadder first ending; the published ending is open to different readings.
  • Edexcel: (a) extract language 20 + (b) whole novel 20.
  • Dickens presents Pip through two viewpoints: young Pip, who acts, and older Pip, who narrates and judges.
  • Pip's shame begins at Satis House (Ch VIII) and grows into snobbery in London (Ch XXVII, XXXIV).
  • Magwitch's return, Magwitch's death and Joe's care change him; readers debate how complete his redemption is.
  • Jilted at twenty to nine on her wedding day, Miss Havisham stopped the clocks and let Satis House decay: a Gothic image of time frozen.
  • She raised Estella to "break their hearts"; Estella's coldness is something she was taught.
  • Miss Havisham repents (Ch XLIX) and Estella is changed by suffering (Ch LIX); readers debate how far each is villain or victim.
  • Pip's parents are dead; Joe, the blacksmith, and Magwitch, the convict, both act as fathers.
  • Joe's forge language ("partings welded together") gives him dignity; he nurses Pip and pays his debt.
  • Magwitch "lived rough" so Pip could "live smooth"; Pip moves from disgust to loyalty, and readers debate whether Magwitch's love is generous or possessive.
  • Pip is to be "brought up as a gentleman"; the novel tests whether money and manners are enough.
  • "No varnish can hide the grain of the wood": Compeyson and Drummle look the part; Herbert and Joe are gentlemen at heart.
  • Pip's status rests on a convict's money; he ends working "pretty hard for a sufficient living".
  • Context isn't marked in Section A, but understanding it sharpens your reading.
  • Hulks (to 1857), transportation (ended 1868) and Newgate set Pip's story in a harsher, earlier world than Dickens's readers lived in.
  • Pip's guilt begins with the pie and the file (Ch II–VI) and grows into a "taint of prison and crime" and shame at deserting Joe.
  • Dickens questions human justice: Compeyson's lighter sentence, Jaggers's hand-washing, and the light linking judge and condemned "with absolute equality".
  • The older Pip narrates the younger Pip's life: a retrospective first-person narrator whose hindsight judges and foreshadows.
  • Shifts into apostrophe and the present tense ("O dear good Joe… I see you again") show the narrator's regret.
  • Patterns of imagery (mist, chains and iron, stopped time, hands) link moments across the novel.

Key quotations to learn

  • "So, I called myself Pip, and came to be called Pip." (Ch I) The first sentence's end: the older narrator names his younger self.
  • "he has great expectations" (Ch XVIII) Jaggers's announcement gives the novel its title and turns Pip's life.
  • "the world lay spread before me" (Ch XIX) The end of Stage 1: a new life opens.
  • "THIS IS THE END OF THE SECOND STAGE OF PIP’S EXPECTATIONS." (Ch XXXIX) The narrator marks the structure after Magwitch's return.
  • "I saw no shadow of another parting from her." (Ch LIX) The published last line: hopeful, but open to interpretation.
  • "my coarse hands and my common boots" (Ch VIII) Estella's contempt becomes Pip's own shame.
  • "I wished Joe had been rather more genteelly brought up" (Ch VIII) Pip begins to blame Joe for his own shame.
  • "If I could have kept him away by paying money, I certainly would have paid money." (Ch XXVII) Pip's snobbery at its lowest point.
  • "that I had deserted Joe" (Ch XXXIX) The sharpest pain when his expectations collapse.
  • "O God bless this gentle Christian man!" (Ch LVII) Pip finally sees Joe's true worth.
  • "the bride within the bridal dress had withered like the dress" (Ch VIII) She has decayed with her relics.
  • "Break their hearts my pride and hope, break their hearts and have no mercy!" (Ch XII) Her revenge is taught to Estella.
  • "I have no heart" (Ch XXIX) Estella's own warning to Pip.
  • "I am what you have made me." (Ch XXXVIII) Estella blames her upbringing.
  • "What have I done! What have I done!" (Ch XLIX) Miss Havisham's repentance.
  • "I have been bent and broken, but—I hope—into a better shape." (Ch LIX) Estella changed by suffering.
  • "Ever the best of friends" (Ch VII (repeated Ch XVIII, LVII)) Joe's loyalty, repeated across the novel.
  • "life is made of ever so many partings welded together" (Ch XXVII) Joe's wisdom in blacksmith's language.
  • "I lived rough, that you should live smooth" (Ch XXXIX) Magwitch's sacrifice for Pip.
  • "I’m your second father. You’re my son" (Ch XXXIX) Magwitch claims Pip as his own.
  • "You’ve never deserted me, dear boy." (Ch LVI) Magwitch's trust, and Pip's guilt.
  • "be brought up as a gentleman" (Ch XVIII) Jaggers's plan for Pip.
  • "no varnish can hide the grain of the wood" (Ch XXII) Manners can't hide character.
  • "He set up fur a gentleman, this Compeyson" (Ch XLII) A gentleman in appearance, a criminal in fact.
  • "I’ve made a gentleman on you!" (Ch XXXIX) The shameful source of Pip's status.
  • "O God bless this gentle Christian man!" (Ch LVII) Joe as the true gentleman.
  • "Hulks are prison-ships, right ’cross th’ meshes." (Ch II) Pip's first idea of punishment.
  • "A boy with somebody else’s pork pie! Stop him!" (Ch III) Guilt makes the landscape accuse him.
  • "I was too cowardly to do what I knew to be right" (Ch VI) Pip's guilt about his secret from Joe.
  • "this taint of prison and crime" (Ch XXXII) Crime follows Pip into his new life.
  • "I was sent for life. It’s death to come back." (Ch XXXIX) The danger of transportation's rules.
  • "My Lord, I have received my sentence of Death from the Almighty, but I bow to yours" (Ch LVI) Magwitch's dignity before human judgement.
  • "the small bundle of shivers growing afraid of it all and beginning to cry, was Pip" (Ch I) The narrator sees his younger self from outside.
  • "the long chain of iron or gold, of thorns or flowers" (Ch IX) One day shapes a whole life.
  • "O dear good Joe, whom I was so ready to leave and so unthankful to" (Ch XVIII) The older Pip's regret breaks in.
  • "the world lay spread before me" (Ch XIX) Mist rises on new hopes.
  • "It was wretched weather; stormy and wet, stormy and wet" (Ch XXXIX) Pathetic fallacy before Magwitch's return.
  • "dissolved, like our own marsh mists before the sun" (Ch LVII) The mist image turned to loss.
