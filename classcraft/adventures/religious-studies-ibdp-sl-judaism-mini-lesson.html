Mission 1

Shabbat: creation and freedom

  • Shabbat (rest): from just before sunset on Friday until nightfall on Saturday, about 25 hours.
  • Creation: God "rested on the seventh day from all His work which He had made" (Genesis 2:2, JPS 1917). Resting imitates God.
  • Freedom: Deuteronomy's version of the commandment links rest to the Exodus: "thou was a servant in the land of Egypt" (Deuteronomy 5:15, JPS 1917). Slaves cannot rest; free people can.
  • Covenant: Shabbat is "a perpetual covenant" between God and Israel (Exodus 31:16, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis read "Remember" (Exodus 20:8) and "Observe" (Deuteronomy 5:12) as two sides: positive celebration and refraining from work.

Mission 1

Shabbat at home

  • Friday evening: candles lit before sunset, traditionally by the woman of the house, with a blessing.
  • Kiddush ("sanctification") over wine: recalls creation and the Exodus.
  • Two challah loaves: a reminder of the double portion of manna before the Sabbath in the wilderness (Exodus 16:22).
  • Family meals, songs, guests; synagogue services Friday night and Saturday morning, with the weekly Torah portion.
  • Havdalah ("separation") on Saturday night: wine, sweet spices, a plaited candle; Shabbat is divided from the week.
  • In Ninian Smart's terms, this ritual dimension carries the social dimension: family and community are built around the table.

Mission 1Diversity

Keeping Shabbat differently

  • Orthodox (shomer Shabbat, "Shabbat-keeping"): the 39 melachot are binding halakhah; no driving, phones, cooking or writing; walk to synagogue.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic in principle; in 1950 American Conservative rabbis allowed driving to synagogue and using electricity on Shabbat. Practice among members varies.
  • Reform and Liberal: Shabbat as rest and renewal; individuals decide which practices make it holy (e.g. candles and family meal, but driving to synagogue).
  • Secular Jews: many keep a Friday-night family dinner as a cultural tradition.
  • All see Shabbat as a sign of Jewish identity; they differ on whether the rabbinic definitions of work are binding.
Mission 1

The daily rhythm

  • On waking, Modeh Ani ("I thank you"); blessings (brachot) before and after food and for many daily events.
  • Three daily services: Shacharit (morning), Minchah (afternoon), Ma'ariv (evening); the Shema morning and evening.
  • Mezuzah on the doorposts (Deuteronomy 6:9): touched on entering and leaving.
  • Daily Torah study, alone or with a partner.
  • The Temple: the rabbis linked the three prayer times to the daily Temple offerings (Talmud Berakhot 26b); after 70 CE prayer replaced sacrifice, "the offering of our lips" (Hosea 14:3, paraphrase).

Mission 2

The story: Exodus 12–15

  • Pesach (Passover): spring festival, from 15 Nisan, for 7 days (8 for many Jews outside Israel).
  • Exodus 12: before the tenth plague, each family kills a lamb and marks the doorposts with its blood; God "will pass over" those houses (Exodus 12:13). Israel leaves in haste: the dough has no time to rise (Exodus 12:39).
  • Exodus 13: keep the festival; eat unleavened bread; tell your children.
  • Exodus 14: the sea divides; Israel crosses on dry land; the Egyptian army is drowned.
  • Exodus 15: the Song of the Sea: "I will sing unto the LORD, for He is highly exalted" (Exodus 15:1, JPS 1917).
  • The core theme: redemption, God freeing a people from slavery to serve God.
Mission 2Read the source

"Because of what the LORD did for me"

> "And this day shall be unto you for a memorial, and ye shall keep it a feast to the LORD; throughout your generations ye shall keep it a feast by an ordinance for ever." (Exodus 12:14, JPS 1917)

> "And thou shalt tell thy son in that day, saying: It is because of that which the LORD did for me when I came forth out of Egypt." (Exodus 13:8, JPS 1917)

  • The Mishnah draws the lesson: in every generation, each person must see themselves as if they personally came out of Egypt (Pesachim 10:5, paraphrase).

Q1 Which word in Exodus 13:8 makes the Exodus personal for every later generation?

Show answer

"me": "what the LORD did for me".

Q2 What does "memorial" mean here?

Show answer

Not only remembering facts: re-living the event so that it shapes who the people are.

Mission 2

Preparing: removing chametz

  • Chametz: leavened food from wheat, barley, rye, oats or spelt (bread, pasta, beer).
  • "the first day ye shall put away leaven out of your houses" (Exodus 12:15, JPS 1917).
  • Houses are cleaned; the night before, a search (bedikat chametz) by candlelight; remaining chametz burned or sold.
  • Many families use separate Pesach dishes.
  • Matzah (unleavened bread) is eaten: the "bread of affliction" and the bread of haste.
  • Meaning often drawn: removing "puffed-up" pride along with leaven.
Mission 2

The seder plate

  • Seder means "order": a home meal with a fixed order of 15 steps.
  • On the plate:
  • Zeroa (roasted shank bone): the Pesach lamb offered in the Temple. Vegetarians often use a roasted beetroot, an option the Talmud already mentions (Pesachim 114b).
  • Beitzah (roasted egg): the festival offering; also read as mourning for the Temple.
  • Maror (bitter herbs, often horseradish): "they made their lives bitter with hard service" (Exodus 1:14, JPS 1917).
  • Charoset (apple, nuts, wine): the mortar the slaves used for bricks.
  • Karpas (green vegetable) dipped in salt water: spring and new life; tears.
  • Chazeret (a second bitter herb, often lettuce).
  • Beside it: three matzot and wine for four cups.

Mission 2

Elijah and "next year in Jerusalem"

  • A fifth cup is poured: the cup of Elijah; the door is opened for him.
  • The prophet Malachi says God will send Elijah "before the coming of the great and terrible day of the LORD" (Malachi 3:23, 4:5 in many English Bibles; paraphrase). Jewish tradition sees Elijah as the herald of the Messiah.
  • One explanation of the extra cup: a fifth promise, "I will bring you in unto the land" (Exodus 6:8), still to be fulfilled.
  • The seder ends: "Next year in Jerusalem" (L'shanah haba'ah birushalayim): hope for the messianic redemption and the rebuilding of Jerusalem. In Israel, some say "next year in Jerusalem rebuilt".
  • So the seder moves from past redemption to future hope.
Mission 2Diversity

Community, family and identity

  • Pesach is one of the most widely kept Jewish practices, including among Jews who rarely go to synagogue.
  • It is a home festival: parents teach children; the whole family takes part; guests are invited.
  • Orthodox: full traditional Haggadah (the book that guides the seder) in Hebrew; two seders outside Israel; strict chametz (leavened food) rules.
  • Progressive: one seder (Reform follows the Israeli 7 days); new Haggadot with English, discussion, and additions such as Miriam's cup (water, honouring Miriam and women in the story).
  • Secular and communal seders: freedom as a cultural and ethical theme.

Mission 3

Origins in the Torah

  • Rosh Hashanah ("head of the year"): 1–2 Tishri (autumn). The Torah calls it "a memorial proclaimed with the blast of horns" (Leviticus 23:24, JPS 1917) and "a day of blowing the horn" (Numbers 29:1, JPS 1917).
  • The rabbis name it the New Year, the anniversary of creation, and the Day of Judgement (Yom ha-Din).
  • Yom Kippur ("Day of Atonement"): 10 Tishri. "ye shall afflict your souls" (Leviticus 23:27, JPS 1917).
  • In Temple times, the High Priest made atonement in the Holy of Holies (Leviticus 16). Since 70 CE, prayer, repentance and fasting take its place.
  • From Rosh Hashanah to Yom Kippur: the Ten Days of Repentance (Aseret Yemei Teshuvah, 1–10 Tishri), the Days of Awe.
Mission 3Read the source

Leviticus 16 and 23

> "And it shall be a statute for ever unto you: in the seventh month, on the tenth day of the month, ye shall afflict your souls, and shall do no manner of work" (Leviticus 16:29, JPS 1917)

> "For on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you; from all your sins shall ye be clean before the LORD." (Leviticus 16:30, JPS 1917)

> "from even unto even, shall ye keep your sabbath." (Leviticus 23:32, JPS 1917)

  • The rabbis read "afflict your souls" as fasting and other self-denial.

Q1 What two things does Leviticus 16:30 say the day does?

Show answer

Atonement and cleansing: being made clean from sins before God.

Q2 Which phrase gives the day its evening-to-evening length?

Show answer

"from even unto even": a full day, extended to about 25 hours.

Mission 3

The shofar in the Hebrew scriptures

  • A shofar is a ram's horn (or another kosher animal's horn, not a cow's).
  • Sinai: "the voice of a horn exceeding loud" as God gives the Torah (Exodus 19:16, JPS 1917).
  • Abraham and Isaac: a ram caught by its horns is offered instead of Isaac (Genesis 22:13). The Talmud links the ram's horn to God's remembering the binding of Isaac (Rosh Hashanah 16a).
  • Kings: Solomon is anointed: "blow ye with the horn, and say: Long live king Solomon" (1 Kings 1:34, JPS 1917). On Rosh Hashanah God is proclaimed King.
  • Alarm and repentance: "Blow the horn in Zion, sanctify a fast" (Joel 2:15, JPS 1917).
  • Freedom and future: the Jubilee is proclaimed with the horn on Yom Kippur (Leviticus 25:9); a "great horn" will gather the exiles (Isaiah 27:13).
  • Maimonides (Laws of Repentance 3:4, paraphrase): its call says "wake up, sleepers; examine your deeds and return".
Mission 3

Rosh Hashanah: rituals

  • Shofar sounded in the synagogue: tekiah (long), shevarim (three broken), teruah (rapid short), tekiah gedolah (very long). Customarily 100 blasts. Not blown when the day falls on Shabbat.
  • At home: apples dipped in honey for "a sweet year"; round challah (the cycle of the year, or a crown).
  • Tashlikh ("you will cast"): on the first afternoon, Jews say prayers by flowing water, often shaking out pockets or throwing crumbs. It acts out "Thou wilt cast all their sins into the depths of the sea" (Micah 7:19, JPS 1917).
  • Tashlikh is a custom first recorded in medieval Germany; some rabbis criticised it as open to superstition, and many stress the prayers, not the crumbs.
  • Teshuvah (returning), tefillah (prayer) and tzedakah (charity) mark the Ten Days.

Mission 3

The 25-hour fast

  • Five afflictions (Mishnah Yoma 8:1): no eating or drinking, no washing, no anointing (oils, creams), no leather shoes, no marital relations.
  • About 25 hours: the fast begins before sunset and ends after nightfall, adding time to the holy day.
  • Exempt: children under bar/bat mitzvah age; anyone whose health would be endangered (pikuach nephesh overrides).
  • Many wear white (some men a kittel): purity and simplicity.
  • The evening service opens with Kol Nidre ("all vows"), which annuls rash personal vows made to God, not promises to people.
  • Five services, ending with Ne'ilah ("closing of the gates") and a final shofar blast; the book of Jonah is read in the afternoon.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person it does not atone until you have made peace with that person.
Mission 3Diversity

How far are they kept?

  • Yom Kippur has the highest synagogue attendance of the year; many Jews who keep little else fast.
  • Orthodox: the full day in synagogue, every service, the full fast; two days of Rosh Hashanah, including in Israel.
  • Progressive: shorter services, much English, egalitarian participation; most keep the fast; Kol Nidre kept; Reform often keeps one day of Rosh Hashanah.
  • Secular Jews: some fast as a mark of identity; in Israel the roads fall quiet on Yom Kippur.
  • Value of the fast: focus on the soul instead of the body; empathy with the hungry (Isaiah 58:6–7 is read in the morning: the fast God wants is freeing the oppressed). Challenge: hunger and tiredness may distract from prayer; health exemptions apply.

Mission 4

Three parts: TaNaKh

  • Tenakh is an acronym: Torah + Nevi'im + Ketuvim. 24 books in the Jewish count, mostly in Hebrew.
  • Torah (teaching, instruction): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The heart of the Tenakh.
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): Joshua to Kings (history); Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
  • Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot (scrolls, e.g. Esther, Ruth), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
  • "Torah" can also mean all Jewish teaching, written and oral.
Mission 4

Given at Sinai

  • The Torah's account: after the Exodus, Israel camps at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19). God makes a covenant with the people.
  • God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); Moses receives the rest of the teaching.
  • The people promise to do all God has said (Exodus 24:7); the rabbis read the Hebrew order, "we will do" before "we will hear", as accepting before fully understanding.
  • Moses writes the Torah and gives it to the priests and elders (Deuteronomy 31:9).
  • Revelation is to a whole people, not one individual: the root of Jewish identity.
  • 613 mitzvot (commandments) are counted in the Torah.

Mission 4Diversity

Word of God or inspired word?

  • Orthodox: Torah min ha-shamayim ("Torah from heaven"). God gave the whole Torah to Moses; every letter is divine, complete, unchangeable. Maimonides' 8th Principle.
  • "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans over centuries; it records Israel's encounter with God. Ethical teaching binds; ritual laws may change.
  • Modern scholarship (e.g. the theory of several sources behind the Torah) shaped this view.
  • Masorti (Conservative): divine in origin, passed on and shaped by humans; law still binding but developing.
  • All treat the Torah as holy and central.
Mission 4

The Tenakh in worship and life

  • Synagogue: the Torah is read on Shabbat, Mondays, Thursdays and festivals; the yearly cycle ends and restarts at Simchat Torah. Public reading needs a minyan.
  • A haftarah from the Nevi'im follows the Shabbat reading; the Megillot are read at festivals (Esther at Purim, Ruth at Shavuot).
  • Psalms fill daily prayer; the Shema (Deuteronomy 6:4–9) is in the mezuzah and tefillin.
  • Home and life: study, Shabbat table discussion of the week's portion; guidance for ethics and law.
  • Beliefs shape use: Orthodox Jews follow the laws as divine command; some Reform and Masorti communities read a third of each portion a week (a three-year cycle) and choose which practices to keep.
Mission 4

The Sefer Torah: care and study

  • Sefer Torah: handwritten in Hebrew by a trained scribe (sofer) on parchment, with a quill; one error makes it unfit until corrected.
  • Kept in the Aron Hakodesh (ark), facing Jerusalem; dressed in a mantle; read with a yad (pointer) so hands do not touch the text.
  • People stand when the ark is opened; it is lifted and shown to the congregation. On return: "She is a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her" (Proverbs 3:18, JPS 1917).
  • A worn-out scroll is not thrown away but buried in a Jewish cemetery.
  • Yeshiva: a place of study of Tenakh and Talmud, often in pairs (chavruta), where the oral Torah opens the written one.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is the Torah still the main authority?

  • Claim: The Torah remains the main authority in Judaism today.
  • For: it is read in every synagogue, holds the 613 mitzvot and is treated with unique honour.
  • For: both Orthodox and Reform Jews call it holy and base their identity on Sinai.
  • Against: in practice the Talmud and later codes decide how laws apply (see Mission 5); rabbis interpret.
  • Against: many secular and Progressive Jews give individual conscience and modern ethics the final say.
  • Judge it by: Does "authority" mean honour or decision-making? Can the written Torah be separated from its interpretation? Which Jews?

Mission 5

A text that needs explaining

  • The written Torah gives commands without details:
  • "thou shalt not do any manner of work" on the Sabbath (Exodus 20:10, JPS 1917): what counts as work? (The Mishnah later lists 39 categories of work, such as kindling a fire, writing and building: Shabbat 7:2.)
  • "thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thy hand" (Deuteronomy 6:8, JPS 1917): how? (the answer: tefillin).
  • Slaughter "as I have commanded thee" (Deuteronomy 12:21, JPS 1917): where was that commanded? Nowhere in writing.
  • Rabbinic Judaism teaches that God gave Moses an oral Torah with the written one: explanations, methods and laws passed on by word of mouth.
  • Together: Torah she-bikhtav (written) and Torah she-be'al peh (oral).
Mission 5Read the source

The chain of transmission

> "Write thou these words, for after the tenor of these words I have made a covenant with thee and with Israel." (Exodus 34:27, JPS 1917)

  • The Hebrew for "after the tenor of" is literally "by the mouth of": the Talmud reads this as a hint of an oral covenant too (Gittin 60b).
  • Pirkei Avot 1:1 (paraphrase): Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Then pairs of sages, down to Hillel and Shammai (1st century BCE): order gives authority.

Q1 According to Avot 1:1, who received the Torah from the prophets?

Show answer

The Men of the Great Assembly.

Q2 Why does the chain matter for the oral Torah's authority?

Show answer

It claims an unbroken line back to Moses at Sinai.

Mission 5

The Mishnah

  • Why written down: after the Temple's destruction (70 CE) and the failed Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135), sages feared the oral teaching would be lost.
  • Edited c. 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi in the land of Israel, in Hebrew.
  • Content: six orders (Shas): Zera'im (seeds, farming), Mo'ed (festivals), Nashim (women, marriage), Nezikin (damages, civil law), Kodashim (holy things), Tohorot (purity); 63 tractates.
  • Style: short, arranged by topic not by verse; records disputes and minority opinions by name.
  • The teachers of the Mishnah are the Tannaim.
  • Importance: the first authoritative code of the oral Torah; studied as the base text of the Talmud.
Mission 5

The Gemara

  • Gemara: the discussion of the Mishnah by later sages, the Amoraim (c. 200–500 CE), mostly in Aramaic.
  • Style: a debate on paper: question, answer, objection, proof from scripture, digressions. Often no final ruling.
  • Content: halakhah (law) and aggadah (stories, ethics, theology, history).
  • Talmud = Mishnah + Gemara.
  • A printed page: Mishnah and Gemara in the centre; Rashi's commentary on the inner side; Tosafot on the outer side.
  • Importance: it teaches how to reason about law, not only what the law is.

Mission 5Diversity

Whose authority?

  • Orthodox: the oral Torah is divine revelation given at Sinai with the written Torah; the Talmud is binding as rabbis apply it. Many study a page a day (daf yomi), finishing the Talmud every seven and a half years.
  • Masorti (Conservative): authoritative, but developed over history; law can change by rabbinic process.
  • Reform and Liberal: human wisdom from inspired teachers; valuable, not binding; individual informed choice.
  • Karaites (from the 8th century): reject the oral Torah altogether, following the written text only.
  • Challenges: historians trace the Talmud's growth over centuries; the Talmud itself records disagreement. Orthodox reply: disagreement belongs inside one revealed tradition.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is the oral Torah divine revelation?

  • Claim: The oral Torah has the same divine authority as the written Torah.
  • For: the written Torah cannot be kept without it (tefillin, Shabbat); the chain of transmission (Avot 1:1); Exodus 34:27.
  • For: Orthodox Jews hold both were given at Sinai.
  • Against: historians see it as developing over centuries; Karaites reject it; the Talmud records many human disagreements.
  • Against: Reform Jews treat it as human wisdom, not binding.
  • Judge it by: Does "divine" require dictation, or can inspired interpretation count (Bava Metzia 59b)? Which community's view?

Mission 6

One God, no other

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one God, with no partners, rivals or parts.
  • Jews say it twice a day in the Shema: God is one (Hebrew echad).
  • God alone is worthy of worship: no intermediaries, no idols (Exodus 20:3–5).
  • Ethical monotheism: the one God is also the source of justice and goodness, and demands them from people. Belief and conduct cannot be separated.
  • The phrase became central in 19th-century Jewish thought (e.g. Hermann Cohen, 1842–1918), but the idea runs through the prophets.
Mission 6Read the source

"The LORD is One"

> "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)

> "I am the first, and I am the last, and beside Me there is no God." (Isaiah 44:6, JPS 1917)

> "I form the light, and create darkness; I make peace, and create evil; I am the LORD, that doeth all these things." (Isaiah 45:7, JPS 1917)

Q1 What does Isaiah 44:6 add to "one"?

Show answer

God is eternal, first and last, and there is no other god at all.

Q2 How does Isaiah 45:7 rule out a second, evil power?

Show answer

One God is the source of light and darkness alike: no rival god of evil.

Mission 6

Creator, sustainer, judge

  • Jewish teaching describes God in these roles:
  • Creator: "IN THE beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917) Most Jewish thinkers: creation from nothing.
  • Sustainer / controller: creation is not a one-off. The morning prayer praises God who "in his goodness renews every day, continually, the work of creation" (paraphrase).
  • Eternal: "from everlasting to everlasting, Thou art God" (Psalm 90:2). God does not change (Malachi 3:6).
  • Judge: God rewards and punishes: "shall not the judge of all the earth do justly?" (Genesis 18:25, JPS 1917).
  • Judgement is just and merciful; Rosh Hashanah and Yom Kippur are the "days of judgement".
Mission 6

No body, no likeness

  • Jews believe God is incorporeal: no body, no shape, no physical comparison.
  • At Sinai (Horeb) "ye saw no manner of form" (Deuteronomy 4:15, JPS 1917): so no image of God may be made.
  • Bible phrases like God's "hand" or "voice" are metaphors: "the Torah speaks in human language" (Talmud, Berakhot 31b, paraphrase), a principle Maimonides uses.

Mission 6

What God is like: attributes

  • Jewish teaching describes God as:
  • Omnipotent: all-powerful; nothing is beyond God.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing, including human thoughts and the future.
  • Omnibenevolent / merciful: "merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth" (Exodus 34:6, JPS 1917): the start of the Thirteen Attributes of Mercy, recited on fast days and festivals.
  • Holy: "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of hosts" (Isaiah 6:3), set apart and pure.
  • Just and perfect: God's acts are right and complete.
  • Many Jewish thinkers (e.g. Maimonides) say such words describe what God does, not what God is in essence.
Mission 6

Maimonides' Thirteen Principles

  • Moses Maimonides (Rambam, 1138–1204), Commentary on the Mishnah, Sanhedrin chapter 10 (c. 1168).
  • About God (1–5): (1) God exists and is the cause of all; (2) God is one; (3) God has no body; (4) God is eternal (first and last); (5) only God may be worshipped.
  • Revelation (6–9): prophecy; Moses is the greatest prophet; the Torah is from God; it will not be changed.
  • Reward and the end (10–13): God knows human deeds; rewards and punishes; the Messiah will come; the dead will be raised.
  • Sung in Yigdal and recited as Ani Ma'amin ("I believe") in many prayer books.
  • Critics: Hasdai Crescas (c. 1340–1410/11) and Joseph Albo (Sefer ha-Ikkarim, c. 1425) counted fewer principles (Albo: three). Some argue Judaism stresses practice more than creed.
Mission 6Diversity

One God, many understandings

  • Orthodox: God as in Maimonides' principles: personal, revealing the Torah, rewarding and punishing.
  • Kabbalah and Hasidism: God is Ein Sof ("without end"), beyond all thought, revealed through ten sefirot; some Hasidic teachers say nothing exists apart from God.
  • Reform: ethical monotheism is the essence of Judaism; God known through conscience and history more than through law.
  • Reconstructionist: Mordecai Kaplan (1881–1983) described God as "the power that makes for salvation": a force in nature and people, not a supernatural person.
  • Humanistic Judaism: Jewish identity without belief in God.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Shabbat recalls creation (Genesis 2:2) and freedom from slavery (Deuteronomy 5:15): from Friday sunset to Saturday nightfall.
  • Candles, Kiddush, challah and Havdalah frame the day; Orthodox Jews keep the 39 melachot, Progressive Jews adapt.

Mission 2

  • Pesach recalls the Exodus (Exodus 12–15): redemption from slavery; "because of that which the LORD did for me" (Exodus 13:8).
  • The seder plate and the Haggadah teach the story through taste, questions and song; the family and community pass on identity.
  • Elijah's cup and "next year in Jerusalem" turn memory into hope for the Messiah and the redemption of the world.

Mission 3

  • Rosh Hashanah (1–2 Tishri): New Year and Day of Judgement; the shofar wakes people to repentance; tashlikh casts sins away symbolically.
  • Yom Kippur (10 Tishri): the 25-hour fast, Kol Nidre, plural confession and Ne'ilah; it atones for sins against God, and against people only once peace is made.

Mission 4

  • The Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah tells of its own giving at Sinai through Moses, within a covenant.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the Torah is the complete, unchangeable Word of God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Reform and Liberal Jews see it as inspired but humanly written.
  • The Sefer Torah is read publicly, studied, and treated with great care: ark, mantle, yad, burial.

Mission 5

  • The oral Torah explains the written Torah; Pirkei Avot 1:1 traces its chain from Moses to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Mishnah (Hebrew, c. 200 CE, six orders) + Gemara (Aramaic discussion) = Talmud; the Babylonian Talmud became more authoritative than the Jerusalem Talmud.

Mission 6

  • Judaism is absolute monotheism: one, eternal, incorporeal God, creator, sustainer and judge, who alone is worshipped and who demands justice (ethical monotheism).
  • Maimonides' Thirteen Principles summarise belief; Jewish traditions interpret the one God differently.
