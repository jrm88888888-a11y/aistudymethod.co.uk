Component 2: Prose, Drama & Unseen Poetry

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of Eduqas GCSE English Literature Component 2 (C720QS/02): how the source-based "exploding extract" questions work on your post-1914 text and 19th-century novel, and how to analyse and compare unseen poems — all judged against the Assessment Objectives AO1, AO2 and AO3.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Three sections, three tasks

Component 2 is a 2-hour-30-minute exam worth 120 marks (60% of the GCSE) and it is closed book. You answer three questions — one from each section, each worth 40 marks:

  • Section A — Post-1914 Prose/Drama (40 marks): a source-based question on your studied post-1914 text (e.g. An Inspector Calls, Lord of the Flies, Blood Brothers, Anita and Me, Never Let Me Go). Rewards AO1 and AO2.
  • Section B — 19th Century Prose (40 marks): a source-based question on your studied 19th-century novel (e.g. A Christmas Carol, Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde, Pride and Prejudice, Silas Marner, The War of the Worlds). Rewards AO1, AO2 and AO3.
  • Section C — Unseen Poetry (40 marks): two poems you've never seen, printed on the paper. Analyse one, then compare both. Rewards AO1 and AO2.

Set texts differ by school: your class studies one post-1914 text and one 19th-century novel from Eduqas's lists. The unseen poems are the same for everyone and need no prior knowledge — pure analytical skill on poems you meet in the exam.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark is tied to an Assessment Objective (AO). Know what each one asks for — it tells you what to put in your answer:

Which AO where? Post-1914 extract → AO1 + AO2. 19th-century extract → AO1 + AO2 + AO3 (context matters here). Unseen poetry → AO1 + AO2 (part a analyses one poem; part b compares both — no context or AO3).

Sections A & B skill

The "exploding extract" question

In both Section A and Section B, a short extract is printed on the paper. The question is source-based: you dig into that extract closely, then reach out to the wider text.

explode the extract → link to the whole textclose analysis of the printed lines + evidence from elsewhere in the novel/play

So a strong answer does two jobs: it "explodes" the extract (analysing its language, form and structure in detail), and it zooms out to show how this moment fits the play or novel as a whole.

Golden rule: don't just paraphrase the extract. Anchor your points in the printed lines, analyse the methods and effects, then broaden to the wider text.

Worked model · annotation

Annotating an unseen stanza

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not a set text) annotated the way you'd mark up an unseen poem in Section C:

The iron sea gnawed the shore,
grey, grey as an old regret,
while gulls screamed, screamed for more —
and still the tide would not forget.
  • "iron sea" — metaphor: the sea feels hard and unforgiving, hinting at a cold mood.
  • "grey, grey" — repetition of colour: drains warmth and mirrors the speaker's regret.
  • "screamed, screamed" — repetition + harsh verb: an anxious, relentless soundscape.
  • "gnawed" — violent verb (personification): the sea attacks the land, raising tension.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your unseen-poetry paragraphs need.

Section C · AO1 + AO2

Two poems, two tasks

Section C prints two unseen poems. It splits into two parts worth 40 marks together:

  • Part (a) — 15 marks: analyse one unseen poem on its own. Explore its meaning, methods and effects and your response (AO1 + AO2).
  • Part (b) — 25 marks: compare the two poems. Weigh them against each other on theme, language, form and tone — the comparison is where most marks live.

To compare, you need comparative connectives:

  • Similarity: similarly, likewise, both poems, in the same way, equally.
  • Difference: whereas, however, by contrast, on the other hand, unlike ___, while.

Misconception: for part (b), writing everything about Poem 1, then everything about Poem 2, is not comparing. Link the two throughout — in almost every paragraph.

Sections A & B skill

Answer the exact question with a clear line

For the extract questions, open with a clear response — a line that answers the exact question set. Everything after it is proof from the extract and wider text.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "How does the writer present injustice in this extract and the wider text?"

Response: "The writer presents injustice as something ordinary people accept too easily, using a naïve young narrator so that readers notice cruelty the adults ignore."

Why it works: it takes a position (injustice is normalised), names a method (naïve narrator) and points to an effect (readers see what characters miss). Now every paragraph can prove one part of it, anchored in the extract.

Paragraph shape

Build paragraphs with PEA / PEEL

Each body paragraph follows a reliable shape so it always hits AO1 and AO2 (and AO3 in Section B):

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Weave short quotations into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line. Short + embedded = fluent and precise:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The narrator is scared. The quote is: 'a cold fear crept up my spine and would not leave.'"

✔ "The narrator's 'cold fear' that 'would not leave' shows how the injustice haunts even the young."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3 · Section B

Context serves the analysis

In Section B (19th-century prose), context (the ideas and conditions the novel was written in) is assessed as AO3 — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture.

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "This was written in the Victorian era. Many people were poor. Now back to the novel."

✔ "Set against Victorian anxieties about poverty and charity, the miser's cold refusal feels all the more damning, which is why his change later hits so hard."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

You have 2h 30 for the whole paper — roughly 50 minutes per section. Spend the first few minutes of each planning — it saves time and lifts your mark:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word).
  • Jot 3 points — for the extract questions, anchor each in the printed lines then reach to the wider text.
  • Tag a short quote beside each point.
  • Write a one-line response/thesis, then write. Leave time to check.

Closed book: the extracts and the unseen poems are printed on the paper, but your set texts are not — so learn a bank of short, flexible quotations in advance.

Worked model · Section C part (b)

An unseen comparison in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two (made-up) unseen poems throughout, with method and effect:

Model paragraph

"Both poems present the sea as a threat, yet they do so differently. In the first, the metaphor 'iron sea' makes the water feel hard and merciless, and the violent verb 'gnawed' personifies it as a predator, unsettling the reader. By contrast, the second poem's gentle sibilance in 'soft, sighing swell' lulls us, so its later turn to danger feels more sudden and shocking. Whereas the first builds dread steadily, the second ambushes us — but both leave the reader wary of nature's power."

Spot the moves: shared idea stated first, connectives ("by contrast", "whereas"), method → effect in both, and a comparative closing line.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Ignoring the extract: writing only about the wider text — the extract questions are source-based.
  • Block comparison: Poem 1 then Poem 2 in part (b) — compare throughout.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't serve a point (Section B, AO3).
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should either make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — anchored in the extract or poem in front of you.
