Mission 1

Mission 1: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Make a guess: Hormones reach almost every cell in the body. So why does each one affect only a few?

Mission 1

Two ways to send a message

  • Nervous system: electrical impulses along neurones to specific cells. Fast (milliseconds), short-lived, localised.
  • Endocrine system: hormones secreted into the blood. Slower to start, longer-lasting, often widespread.
  • Neurotransmitters act locally: they diffuse about 20 nm across a synapse. Hormones act at a distance, carried in the blood.
  • The two systems work together, e.g. in the "fight or flight" response.
  • The blood also integrates organs by transporting materials: glucose from the gut to muscles, CO2 from muscles to lungs.
Mission 1

Glands, blood, target cells

  • Endocrine glands are ductless: they secrete hormones directly into the blood.
  • The blood carries each hormone to almost every cell.
  • Only target cells respond: they have receptors with a shape complementary to that hormone.
  • Cells without the receptor are unaffected, however much hormone reaches them.
  • Say: "the hormone binds to complementary receptors on target cells" — not: "the blood takes the hormone to its target".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes the roles of the nervous system and the endocrine system in sending messages between organs?
Mission 1

Hypothalamus and pituitary

  • The hypothalamus (in the brain) links the nervous and endocrine systems.
  • It receives nerve impulses and controls the pituitary gland below it.
  • Some pituitary hormones act on tissues directly, e.g. ADH on the kidney, growth hormone on many tissues.
  • Others control other glands, e.g. TSH → thyroid; FSH and LH → ovaries and testes.
Quick check
?Which statement describes how the hypothalamus and the pituitary gland control the endocrine system?
Mission 1

The adrenal glands

  • Two adrenal glands, one on top of each kidney.
  • Medulla (inner): secretes adrenaline (and noradrenaline) when stimulated by sympathetic nerves.
  • IB name: epinephrine (adrenaline).
Mission 1

Adrenaline: ready for action

  • Released in seconds when danger or effort is sensed: fight or flight.
  • Heart rate and stroke volume rise.
  • Bronchioles widen: easier ventilation.
  • Arterioles to skeletal muscle dilate; arterioles to gut and skin constrict.
  • Liver: glycogen → glucose, so blood glucose rises.
  • Net effect: more oxygen and glucose delivered to muscles for intense contraction.
  • Say: "adrenaline prepares the muscles" — not: "adrenaline gives muscles energy".
Quick check
?A sprinter waits for the starting signal and adrenaline (epinephrine) is secreted into the blood. Which set of effects of adrenaline helps to facilitate intense contraction of the leg muscles during the race?
Mission 1

Melatonin and the daily clock

  • Melatonin is secreted by the pineal gland in the brain.
  • Light detected by the eyes suppresses its secretion.
  • Low all day; rises in the evening dark; peaks in the middle of the night; falls by morning.
  • High melatonin promotes sleep.
  • This sets a roughly 24-hour circadian rhythm of sleeping and waking.
  • Evening light (screens, jet lag) delays the rise, so the whole cycle shifts later.
Quick check
?A student who uses a bright screen until midnight finds it hard to fall asleep and wakes late the next morning. Using the role of melatonin in circadian rhythms, which explanation is best supported?
Mission check

?In humans, cells of the pituitary gland at the base of the brain release human growth hormone, and target cells in many tissues respond, leading to growth. Which flowchart best represents how this signal reaches and affects its target cells?

Every hormone is broadcast to the whole body; the receptor decides who listens.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Make a guess: A few hormone molecules can make a cell release thousands of sugar molecules. How?

Mission 2

Where is the receptor?

  • A hormone acts only by binding a receptor with a complementary shape.
  • Polar hormones (proteins, peptides, adrenaline) cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer.
  • So their receptors are on the cell-surface membrane. The hormone stays outside.
  • Steroid hormones (e.g. oestradiol, testosterone) are non-polar.
  • They diffuse through the bilayer and bind receptors inside: in the cytoplasm or nucleus.
Mission 2

Spotting a steroid

  • Steroids are lipids built on four fused rings of carbon.
  • Three rings have six atoms; one has five.
  • Few polar groups, so the molecule is non-polar.
  • It dissolves in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer and crosses by simple diffusion: no channel, no carrier.
  • Examples: oestradiol, testosterone.
  • Say: "non-polar, so it dissolves through the bilayer" — not: "small enough to slip between phospholipids".
Quick check
?Testosterone binds to receptors inside its target cells. How does it get through the plasma membrane to reach them?
Mission 2

Many signals, many chemicals

  • Four functional categories of signalling chemical:
  • Hormones: secreted into the blood; act at a distance.
  • Neurotransmitters: released at synapses; act on one adjacent cell.
  • Cytokines: small proteins released by cells, often immune cells, acting on nearby cells.
  • Calcium ions: messengers inside cells, e.g. Ca²⁺ entry triggers neurotransmitter release.
  • Hormone groups: amines (epinephrine, thyroxin), proteins (insulin), steroids (oestradiol, testosterone).
  • Neurotransmitter groups: amino acids, peptides, amines and the gas nitric oxide.
  • Your guide words this as "nitrous oxide". The gas is nitric oxide (NO); nitrous oxide (N2O) is an anaesthetic.
  • Why so many? Each signal needs its own distinct shape, and chemistry decides solubility and so where its receptor sits.
Quick check
?To which chemical groups do the hormones epinephrine, insulin and testosterone belong?
Mission 2

The second messenger model

  • Adrenaline is the first messenger. It binds a receptor on the surface of a liver cell.
  • The receptor is a G protein-coupled receptor: binding changes its shape and activates a G protein inside the cell.
  • This activates the enzyme adenylyl cyclase.
  • The enzyme converts ATP → cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • cAMP activates protein kinase, which phosphorylates enzymes, switching on glycogen → glucose.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next: the signal is amplified.
Quick check
?A few epinephrine (adrenaline) molecules binding to receptors on a liver cell can lead to the release of millions of glucose molecules. Which statement best explains this amplification?
Mission 2

Receptors in the membrane or inside

  • Transmembrane receptor: a protein that spans the bilayer.
  • Its middle section has hydrophobic amino acids, in contact with the fatty acid tails.
  • Its outer and inner ends have hydrophilic amino acids, in contact with water.
  • The signal (e.g. insulin, epinephrine) stays outside; the receptor passes the message in.
  • Intracellular receptor: in the cytoplasm or nucleus, with a hydrophilic surface.
  • Its signal (a steroid) must cross the membrane to reach it.
Mission 2

Steroids switch genes on and off

  • The steroid diffuses into the cell.
  • It binds its receptor in the cytoplasm or nucleus: a hormone–receptor complex.
  • The complex acts as a transcription factor: it binds a specific DNA sequence near the target gene.
  • RNA polymerase transcribes the gene → more mRNA → more of the protein.
  • IB examples: oestradiol, progesterone, testosterone.
  • New mRNA and protein take time: steroid responses take minutes to hours, not seconds.
Quick check
?Cells respond to testosterone only after about 30 minutes, and the response is abolished by a drug that blocks transcription. The same cells respond to epinephrine (adrenaline) within seconds, and this response is unaffected by the drug. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?Inside liver cells, the enzyme phosphodiesterase breaks cAMP down to an inactive product. Caffeine inhibits phosphodiesterase. Liver cells are given caffeine and then a low dose of adrenaline. Compared with cells given adrenaline alone, what is predicted?

The second messenger is the amplifier: anything that keeps cAMP around turns the volume up.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Make a guess: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Mission 3

One plan, twenty versions

  • Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  • The alpha carbon carries four groups:
  • Amine group (–NH2), carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • The twenty amino acids in proteins differ only in their R group.
  • The simplest R group is a single hydrogen atom.
  • Say: "amine group, –NH2" — not: "–NH3". Nitrogen's third bond is to the central carbon.
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all 20 of the amino acids that are coded for in the genetic code?
Mission 3

The peptide bond

  • (1) Line up two amino acids: the carboxyl group of one beside the amine group of the next.
  • (2) –OH from the carboxyl and –H from the amine leave as one water molecule.
  • (3) C joins N: a peptide bond, –CO–NH–. A covalent bond. Two amino acids make a dipeptide.
  • This is a condensation reaction.
  • Word equation: amino acid + amino acid → dipeptide + water.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it: adding water breaks the peptide bond.
  • The R groups take no part.
Quick check
?Two amino acids react to form a dipeptide. Which statement describes this reaction?
Mission 3

Worked example: counting bonds and water

  • Many amino acids joined in a chain: a polypeptide.
  • A chain has two ends: a free amine group at one, a free carboxyl group at the other.
  • n amino acids → n − 1 peptide bonds → n − 1 water molecules released.
  • 3 amino acids: 2 bonds, 2 H2O.
  • Masses: glycine (Mr 75) + alanine (Mr 89) → dipeptide.
  • Mr = 75 + 89 − 18 = 146. Subtract one water per bond.
  • Hydrolysis of the chain uses the same number of water molecules.
Quick check
?A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is assembled by condensation reactions. How does the number of water molecules change as the whole chain is formed?
Mission 3

Endless variety

  • 20 amino acids are coded for in the genetic code.
  • At each position: any of the 20. Repeats allowed. Any order.
  • Chains can be any length: from a few amino acids to thousands.
  • Number of possible sequences = 20ⁿ for a chain of n.
  • Dipeptides: 20² = 400. Tripeptides: 20³ = 8000.
  • A chain of 100: 20¹⁰⁰ ≈ 1.3 × 10¹³⁰. Effectively infinite.
  • Real examples: insulin's two chains have 21 and 30 amino acids; titin, in muscle, has over 30 000.
  • Say: "20 to the power n" — not: "n to the power 20".
Quick check
?How many different tripeptides could be formed from four different types of amino acid?
Mission 3

Amino acids in the diet

  • Essential amino acids: cannot be synthesised in the body. They must come from food.
  • Non-essential amino acids: made in the body from other amino acids.
  • Plant proteins contain essential amino acids, but one plant protein may be low in one or more.
  • So a vegan diet needs a range of plant protein sources.
  • Say: "essential = cannot be made by the body" — not: "essential = needed most".
Quick check
?A dietitian advises someone following a vegan diet to eat a range of plant protein sources rather than rely on one. Which statement best explains the advice?
Mission 3

Three families of R group

  • Hydrophobic R groups: non-polar.
  • Hydrophilic R groups: either polar (uncharged) or charged.
  • Charged ones are acidic (e.g. –COOH loses H⁺ → –COO⁻) or basic (e.g. –NH2 gains H⁺ → –NH3⁺).
  • The backbone is the same in every chain. The R groups give a polypeptide its properties.
  • R groups interact with water and with each other. That decides how the chain folds (see Mission 4).
Quick check
?Which statement about the R-groups of amino acids is correct?
Mission check

?Two short polypeptides, P and Q, are each made of the same five amino acids, but the amino acids are joined in a different order in P and in Q. Which statement about P and Q is correct?

Twenty kinds, joined in any order, any number of times, to any length. That is how so few make so many.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Make a guess: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Mission 4

Sequence, shape, job

  • A polypeptide folds into a precise three-dimensional shape.
  • Bonds between parts of the chain hold that shape. Most are weak; together they are strong.
  • The shape decides the function: enzymes, hormones, antibodies, transport, support.
  • The same sequence folds the same way, every time.
  • Different sequences fold differently. So endless sequences give endless proteins.
  • Four levels describe the shape: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
Mission 4

Primary structure

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Held by peptide bonds. Set by the gene.
  • The sequence, with the exact position of each amino acid, determines every later level.
  • Change one amino acid, anywhere, and the shape can change.
  • So proteins have precise, predictable and repeatable structures.
  • Say: "primary structure = the order of amino acids" — not: "which amino acids are present".
Mission 4

Secondary structure

  • The backbone carries polar C=O and N–H groups.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another, at regular positions.
  • α-helix: the chain coils; each turn is held to the next.
  • β-pleated sheet: lengths of chain lie side by side, folded like pleats, held to each other.
  • R groups point away from the backbone and take no part in these bonds, so helices and sheets form in many sequences.
  • Say: "backbone hydrogen bonds" — not: "bonds between R groups".
Quick check
?What stabilises the alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets of a protein's secondary structure?
Mission 4

Tertiary structure: R groups lock the fold

  • The chain, with its helices and sheets, folds further into its overall 3D shape: tertiary structure.
  • Held by bonds between R groups:
  • Hydrogen bonds: between polar R groups. Weak, but many.
  • Ionic bonds: between a –COO⁻ and an –NH3⁺ R group. pH-sensitive.
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between two cysteines. Strong.
  • Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar R groups cluster together, away from water.
  • R-group charges come from amine and carboxyl groups binding or losing H⁺.
Mission 4

Oily inside, watery outside

  • In a water-soluble protein: non-polar R groups cluster in the core.
  • Polar and charged R groups sit on the surface, in contact with water. So the protein dissolves.
  • Why: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude non-polar groups.
  • A membrane protein has a band of non-polar R groups where it crosses the bilayer, and polar R groups in the water on each side.
  • Say: "non-polar R groups are excluded from water" — not: "they repel water".
Quick check
?In one region of a polypeptide there are two cysteines, an amino acid whose R-group contains a carboxyl group and an amino acid whose R-group contains an amine group. Which statement about the bonds these could form in the tertiary structure is correct?
Mission 4

Quaternary structure

  • Some proteins are built from more than one polypeptide: quaternary structure.
  • The polypeptides are held together by the same kinds of bond as tertiary structure.
  • Example: haemoglobin, four polypeptides (see Mission 5).
  • All four levels together decide what the protein does.
Mission 4

Denaturation

  • (1) A folded protein: its shape held by many weak bonds.
  • (2) High temperature: more vibration breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. The chain unfolds.
  • (3) pH change: gaining or losing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break.
  • This loss of shape is denaturation. The protein can no longer do its job.
  • Peptide bonds stay intact: the sequence is unchanged.
  • Usually permanent: the chains do not refold correctly. A cooked egg white stays white.
  • Cold does not denature: it slows molecules down, and activity returns on warming.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?When egg white is heated, its soluble proteins turn into a white solid. What has happened to the protein molecules?
Mission 4

Enzymes: globular proteins with an active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: compact, folded, usually soluble.
  • The active site is a small pocket made of only a few amino acids.
  • But the rest of the folded chain holds those few in exactly the right places.
  • So the active site's shape and properties come from the enzyme's tertiary structure.
  • The substrate binds to the active site, forming an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Denature the enzyme: the active site changes and the substrate no longer binds.
Quick checkRead the data
?(Illustrative data.) An enzyme's activity at its optimum pH of 7 was 100 units. One sample was placed at pH 2 for 10 minutes, then returned to pH 7: its activity was 4 units after 1 hour and 4 units after 24 hours, and its polypeptide chains were found to be intact. A second sample was cooled to 5 °C for 10 minutes, then returned to 37 °C: its activity was 100 units. What do these results show?
Mission check

?A mutation replaces one amino acid in an enzyme at a position on the surface far from the active site in the folded protein. The enzyme loses most of its activity. What is the best explanation?

Sequence sets shape, and shape sets function. One amino acid in the wrong place can be enough.

Mission 5

Two body plans: globular and fibrous

GlobularFibrous
Shapecompact, roughly sphericallong, parallel chains
In watersoluble: polar R groups on the outsideinsoluble
Jobsenzymes, hormones, transportstrength and support
Exampleshaemoglobin, insulin, enzymescollagen, keratin, elastin
  • Both have a precise, repeatable 3D structure.
  • Say: "fibrous proteins have an elongated structure" — not: "fibrous proteins are unfolded".
Mission 5

Haemoglobin

  • A globular protein of four polypeptides: two α and two β. So it has quaternary structure.
  • Each polypeptide holds one haem group: a ring with an Fe²⁺ ion at its centre.
  • Each haem binds one O2. One haemoglobin carries up to four O2.
  • Haem is not made of amino acids. It is a prosthetic group.
  • A protein with a prosthetic group is a conjugated protein.
  • Polar R groups on the surface keep it soluble inside red blood cells.
Mission 5

Insulin and a named enzyme

  • Insulin: a small globular hormone.
  • Two polypeptides (21 and 30 amino acids), linked by disulfide bridges.
  • Soluble, so it travels in blood plasma.
  • Its precise shape fits its receptor on target cells.
  • Polypeptides only, no prosthetic group: non-conjugated.
Mission 5

Conjugated and modified proteins

  • Conjugated protein: protein + prosthetic group (a non-protein part).
  • Haemoglobin: prosthetic group haem.
  • Say: "conjugated = has a prosthetic group" — not: "conjugated = has more than one polypeptide".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares insulin, collagen and haemoglobin?
Mission 5

Collagen: built to take a pull

  • Three polypeptides wound round each other: a triple helix.
  • Hydrogen bonds hold the three chains together. Covalent cross-links join neighbouring molecules into fibrils, then fibres.
  • Long, insoluble, with very high tensile strength.
  • Found in tendons, ligaments, skin, bone, cartilage and artery walls.
Quick check
?Protein X is compact and roughly spherical, dissolves in blood plasma and binds to a specific receptor on target cells. Protein Y forms long insoluble fibres with high tensile strength in tendons. Which statement correctly relates the form of each protein to its function?
Mission check

?Spider dragline silk is a protein. Its long polypeptide chains lie parallel to one another, with many stretches folded into β-pleated sheets that are stacked and held together by hydrogen bonds. It does not dissolve in water. Which statement best explains why it suits the frame of a web?

Long and cross-linked for strength, compact and soluble for work in solution. Shape fits job.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hormones are secreted into the blood and reach almost every cell; only target cells with complementary receptors respond.
  • The hypothalamus links nerves to hormones by controlling the pituitary.
  • The adrenal medulla's adrenaline prepares the body for vigorous activity.

Mission 2

  • Polar hormones bind cell-surface receptors and stay outside the cell.
  • Adrenaline's message is relayed and amplified inside by cAMP and protein kinase.
  • Non-polar steroids diffuse through the bilayer to intracellular receptors.

Mission 3

  • Every amino acid: central carbon + amine group + carboxyl group + H + R group; only R differs.
  • Condensation joins carboxyl to amine: a peptide bond and one water per bond; hydrolysis reverses it.
  • 20ⁿ possible sequences; essential amino acids must come from food.

Mission 4

  • The amino acid sequence decides how a chain folds; bonds between parts of the chain hold the shape; the shape decides the job.
  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H bonds: α-helix, β-pleated sheet) → tertiary (R-group bonds) → quaternary (more than one polypeptide).
  • Heat and pH changes break the weak bonds: the protein is denatured, though its peptide bonds survive.

Mission 5

  • Globular proteins (haemoglobin, insulin, enzymes) are compact and soluble; fibrous proteins (collagen) are long, insoluble and strong.
  • Haemoglobin: four polypeptides, each with a haem prosthetic group holding Fe²⁺; one O2 per haem.
