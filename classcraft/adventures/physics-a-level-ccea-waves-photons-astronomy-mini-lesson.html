Mission 1

Mission 1: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Make a guess: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Quick check
?A loudspeaker plays a steady note to a listener 5 m away in a room with still air. Which of the following travels from the loudspeaker to the listener?
Mission 1

What a wave carries

  • A progressive (travelling) wave transfers energy from place to place.
  • It does not transfer matter. Each particle oscillates about a fixed position.
Mission 1

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Waves on a string; all EM waves.
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound; a slinky pushed along its length.
  • Longitudinal waves have compressions (particles close, pressure higher) and rarefactions (spread out, pressure lower).
Mission 1

Describing a wave

  • Displacement: distance and direction of a particle from its rest position.
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: distance between neighbouring points that move in step, e.g. crest to crest.
  • Period T: time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f: oscillations per second, in Hz.
  • f = 1/T.
Mission 1

The wave equation

  • In one period the wave moves on one wavelength: v = λ/T, so v = fλ.
  • For sound in air and waves on a string, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Change f in the same medium: λ changes, v stays the same.
  • Example: sound at 340 m s⁻¹, f = 680 Hz → λ = 340/680 = 0.50 m.
Mission 1

Two graphs, two meanings

  • Displacement–position graph: a snapshot of the whole wave. Read λ and A.
  • Displacement–time graph: one particle over time. Read T and A.
  • Same shape, different axis: always check the x-axis label.
  • Particle direction: for a wave moving right, a point copies what the point just to its left did a moment ago.
Quick check
?A transverse wave travels along a stretched string. Adjacent crests are 1.2 m apart. A graph of displacement y against time t for one point on the string is a sine curve: y = 0 at t = 0, y is a maximum at t = 5 ms, y = 0 at t = 10 ms, y is a minimum at t = 15 ms and y = 0 at t = 20 ms, after which the pattern repeats. What is the wave speed?
Mission check

?A vibrator sends waves along a stretched string at constant tension. At 25 Hz the wavelength is 0.80 m. The frequency is raised to 100 Hz. What is the new wavelength, and how long does a wave now take to travel 4.0 m along the string?

The medium sets the speed. A higher note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength, but the same speed: it arrives no sooner.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Make a guess: Two corks bob on the same ripple, half a wavelength apart. How do their movements compare?

Mission 2

Phase

  • Phase: how far through its cycle a particle is.
  • One cycle = 360° = 2π rad.
  • Points one wavelength apart move exactly together: in phase.
  • Points half a wavelength apart always move opposite ways: antiphase (180°, π rad).
Mission 2

Phase difference from distance

  • Fraction of a cycle = Δx/λ.
  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ (rad) = 360° × Δx/λ.
  • Example: λ = 0.80 m, points 0.20 m apart. Δx/λ = ¼ cycle = 90° = π/2 rad.
Quick check
?Two points on a progressive wave of wavelength 1.2 m are 0.40 m apart along the direction of travel. What is the phase difference between their oscillations?
Mission 2

Phase difference from graphs

  • Two displacement–time traces, same period T. Find the time shift Δt between matching points (crest to crest).
  • φ = 2πΔt/T = 360° × Δt/T.
  • Example: T = 8.0 ms; crests 2.0 ms apart → ¼ cycle = 90°.
  • On a displacement–position graph, use the separation Δx and λ instead.
  • The trace that reaches its crest first leads.
Quick check
?Two displacement–time graphs, P and Q, show the oscillations of two points on a wave. Both have a period of 6.0 ms. Each crest of Q comes 1.0 ms after the matching crest of P. What is the phase difference between P and Q?
Mission check

?A loudspeaker emits a steady 500 Hz note. Two microphones are placed on a straight line from the loudspeaker, 0.17 m apart. The speed of sound is 340 m s⁻¹. How do the oscillations detected by the two microphones compare?

Phase difference = 2πΔx/λ. Half a wavelength apart means antiphase: the two corks always move in opposite directions.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Make a guess: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement describes the nature of electromagnetic waves?
Mission 3

What an EM wave is

  • An electromagnetic (EM) wave is an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, travelling together.
  • The two fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel: EM waves are transverse.
  • Energy travels along the direction of travel; the fields oscillate across it.
  • EM waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
  • In a vacuum all EM waves travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, whatever their wavelength.
  • Examples: the radio waves from an FM radio station, visible light, the X-rays used in medical imaging.
Quick check
?Predict first. The diagram shows the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum on a logarithmic scale of wavelength λ (wavelength increases to the right). A wave has a wavelength of 2 × 10⁻⁵ m. In which region of the spectrum shown does it lie?
Mission 3

The spectrum, in order

  • Seven regions, by decreasing wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Same order: increasing frequency (f = c/λ) and increasing photon energy.
  • Visible light, longest to shortest: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet.
  • Visible runs from about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet).
  • The regions differ only in wavelength (and so frequency). Same nature, same c.
  • Boundaries are not sharp. X-rays and gamma rays overlap: they are named by origin (X-rays from electrons, gamma rays from nuclei).
Mission 3

Orders of magnitude

  • Typical wavelengths (learn the power of ten):
  • Radio: 1 m (from about 0.1 m up to kilometres)
  • Microwave: 10⁻² m (1 mm to 0.1 m)
  • Infrared: 10⁻⁵ m (700 nm to 1 mm)
  • Visible: 5 × 10⁻⁷ m (400–700 nm)
  • Ultraviolet: 10⁻⁷ m (10–400 nm)
  • X-ray: 10⁻¹⁰ m (about 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹² m)
  • Gamma: 10⁻¹² m (below about 10⁻¹¹ m)
Mission check

?In a vacuum, the microwaves in a speed camera have a wavelength of 3.0 cm, and the light from a yellow lamp has a wavelength of 600 nm. How do their frequencies compare?

Microwaves and light, like radio waves and X-rays, are the same kind of wave, with the same speed c in a vacuum. Only the wavelength, and so the frequency, tells them apart.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Make a guess: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Mission 4

Plane polarised

  • Transverse wave: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel. They can point in many directions across it.
  • Plane polarised: the oscillations stay in one plane that contains the direction of travel.
  • For light (EM waves), the plane of oscillation is the plane of the electric field.
  • Unpolarised light (Sun, lamps): the oscillation direction changes rapidly and randomly.
  • Longitudinal wave: oscillations along the direction of travel. Only one direction is possible, so it cannot be polarised.
  • So: if a wave can be polarised, it must be transverse.
Quick check
?Predict first. A long rope passes through a narrow slot in a board. The figure shows the view along the rope towards the board, with the slot and the direction in which a student moves the rope back and forth to send a transverse wave towards the board. Which describes the wave on the far side of the board?
Mission 4

A rope through a slot

  • Shake a rope that passes through a vertical slot.
  • Shake it up and down: the wave passes through.
  • Shake it side to side: the slot stops the rope moving. No wave beyond the slot.
  • Shake it in every direction: only the up-and-down part gets through. The wave beyond is plane polarised.
Quick check
?Predict first. Unpolarised light from a lamp passes through two ideal polarising filters, P1 and P2. The lines drawn on the filters in the figure show their transmission axes. I1, I2 and I3 are the intensities of the light at points 1, 2 and 3. Which ranking of the intensities is correct?
Mission 4

Polarising filters

  • A polarising filter (Polaroid) lets through only oscillations along its transmission axis.
  • Unpolarised light in → plane polarised light out. An ideal filter passes half the intensity.
  • Add a second filter, the analyser. Axes parallel: the light passes on. Axes crossed (at 90°): no light gets through.
  • Rotate the analyser through a full turn: brightest at 0° and 180°, dark at 90° and 270°.
Mission check

?A student looks at a calculator's display through a polarising filter and slowly rotates the filter. The display is bright, goes dark after 90° of rotation, and is bright again after 180°. What does this show?

Only transverse waves can be polarised. Light can be polarised and sound cannot: light is transverse, sound is longitudinal.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does light bend as it enters water, but not when it hits the surface head-on?

Make a guess: Why does light bend as it enters water, but not when it hits the surface head-on?

Quick check
?Circular ripples spread out across a ripple tank from a vibrating dipper. A student sketches a wavefront–ray diagram of the waves. Which statement correctly describes the rays on the diagram?
Mission 5

Wavefronts and rays

  • Wavefront: a line (or surface) joining points that are in phase, e.g. every crest.
  • Neighbouring wavefronts are one wavelength apart.
  • Ray: a line perpendicular to the wavefronts, showing the direction the wave's energy travels.
  • Point source: circular wavefronts, rays spreading out.
  • Distant source or laser: straight (plane) wavefronts, parallel rays.
Mission 5

Reflection

  • The normal: a line at 90° to the surface where the ray hits it.
  • Measure every angle from the normal, not from the surface.
  • Law of reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection.
  • Incident ray, reflected ray and normal lie in the same plane.
  • Reflection changes direction only: speed, frequency and wavelength stay the same.
Mission 5

Why refraction happens

  • At a boundary, part of a wave is reflected and part is transmitted.
  • The transmitted wave changes speed. Its frequency stays the same (set by the source), so its wavelength changes too: wavelength = speed ÷ frequency.
  • Hitting at an angle, one end of each wavefront reaches the boundary first and slows first. The wavefront turns, so the ray changes direction: refraction.
  • Into a slower medium: wavefronts closer together, ray bends towards the normal.
  • Into a faster medium: ray bends away from the normal.
  • Along the normal: the whole wavefront slows at once. Speed and wavelength change; the direction does not.
  • Water waves travel more slowly in shallower water. In a ripple tank, a shallow region refracts waves as glass refracts light.
Mission 5

Refractive index

  • n = c/v: speed of light in a vacuum ÷ speed in the material.
  • No unit. Vacuum: n = 1 exactly. Air: n ≈ 1.00 (1.0003).
  • Water 1.33 · typical glass about 1.5 · diamond 2.42.
  • Larger n, slower light.
Quick check
?The refractive index of a certain plastic is 1.5. Which statement correctly describes what this value means?
Mission 5

Snell's law

  • n = sin i/sin r = c1/c2: i in the first medium (speed c1), r in the second (speed c2). From air (vacuum), n is the material's refractive index.
  • Into larger n: smaller angle (towards the normal). Into smaller n: larger angle (away).
  • θ1 = 0 gives θ2 = 0: no bending along the normal.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Light of wavelength 589 nm in air enters water (n = 1.33) at 60.0° to the normal.
  • n = sin i/sin r → sin r = sin 60.0°/1.33 = 0.651 → r = 40.6°.
  • Speed in water = c/n = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 1.33 = 2.26 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • Same frequency, so the wavelength falls by the same factor: 589 ÷ 1.33 = 443 nm.
  • Check: 40.6° < 60.0°, so the ray bent towards the normal, as it should entering a slower medium.
Quick check
?A ray of light passes from air into a transparent liquid. The incident ray makes an angle of 40.0° with the surface of the liquid and the refracted ray makes an angle of 55.0° with the surface. The speed of light in air is 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the speed of light in the liquid?
Mission 5

Measuring n with a ray box

  • Rectangular glass block on paper; draw round it. Narrow ray from a ray box into one face.
  • Mark the ray in and out with crosses; join them through the block. Draw the normal.
  • Measure i (in air) and r (in glass) with a protractor for 5–6 angles from about 10° to 70°.
  • Plot sin i (y-axis) against sin r (x-axis): a straight line through the origin confirms Snell's law. Gradient = n.
  • Dim room, narrow ray, sharp pencil: they reduce the uncertainty in each angle.
  • A semicircular block, with the ray aimed at the centre of its flat face, is used to measure the critical angle (see the total internal reflection mission).
Quick check
?Another student does the same experiment but plots her axes the other way round: sin r on the y-axis against sin i on the x-axis. Her points lie on a straight line through the origin with a gradient of 0.66. What is the refractive index of the glass?
Mission check

?The diagram shows straight water waves in a ripple tank crossing the boundary between region X and region Y. The wavefronts, a ray and the normal at the point where the ray crosses the boundary are drawn. Which statement is correct?

Refraction happens because the speed changes at the boundary. Head-on, the whole wavefront slows at once, so the direction does not change.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can light stay trapped inside a thin glass strand, even when the strand bends?

Make a guess: How can light stay trapped inside a thin glass strand, even when the strand bends?

Mission 6

Heading into a lower n

  • Light going from glass into air (larger n to smaller n) bends away from the normal.
  • Increase the angle of incidence: the refracted ray swings towards the surface.
  • At the critical angle, the refracted ray runs along the boundary (angle of refraction 90°).
  • Beyond the critical angle no light leaves: all of it reflects back. This is total internal reflection (TIR).
  • Below the critical angle there is also a partial reflection, growing stronger as the angle nears the critical angle.
  • TIR obeys the law of reflection.
Mission 6

The critical angle

  • Put θ2 = 90° (sin 90° = 1) into Snell's law.
  • Boundary with air (n ≈ 1): sin c = 1/n, c the critical angle.
  • Glass (1.50) to air: 41.8°. Water (1.33) to air: 48.8°.
  • TIR needs both: light heading into a medium of lower n, and angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.
Quick check
?Light can cross a flat boundary between water (n = 1.33) and glass (n = 1.50) in either direction. Which statement about total internal reflection at this boundary is correct?
Mission 6

Worked example: will it reflect?

  • A ray in water (n = 1.33) meets the water surface. Critical angle: sin⁻¹(1/1.33) = 48.8°.
  • At 50.0° to the normal: 50.0° > 48.8°, so TIR. No light leaves.
  • At 45.0°: 45.0° < 48.8°, so it refracts out. 1.33 × sin 45.0° = 1.00 × sin θ2 → sin θ2 = 0.940 → θ2 = 70.1°.
  • Always compare the angle from the normal with the critical angle.
Quick check
?A glass block of refractive index 1.52 is under water of refractive index 1.33. A ray inside the glass meets the glass–water boundary at an angle of incidence of 55.0°. What happens to the ray?
Mission 6

Measuring the critical angle

  • Semicircular glass block. Aim a narrow ray through the curved face at the centre of the flat face. It enters along a radius, so it is not bent there.
  • Slowly increase the angle of incidence at the flat face until the emerging ray just grazes the face.
  • Mark the ray, draw the normal, measure that angle: the critical angle c.
  • Then n = 1/sin c.
  • Repeat and average; approach the critical angle from both sides.
Mission 6

Step-index optical fibre

  • A thin glass core (higher n) inside a glass cladding (slightly lower n).
  • Light meets the core–cladding boundary at more than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected, again and again, along the core.
  • Step index: n drops suddenly at the core–cladding boundary.
  • Cladding: keeps TIR at a boundary that cannot be scratched or dirtied, and stops light crossing between fibres that touch (signal security).
  • Speed in the core: v = c/ncore. Core n = 1.52 gives 1.97 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
  • The fibre can bend gently. A sharp bend makes the angle of incidence fall below the critical angle, and light leaks out.
Quick check
?An optical fibre has a core of refractive index 1.52 and a cladding of refractive index 1.46. What is the critical angle at the core–cladding boundary?
Mission 6

The flexible endoscope

  • A flexible tube with two fibre bundles, each of thousands of thin clad fibres.
  • Non-coherent bundle: carries light in from an external lamp. The fibres can be in any order.
  • Coherent bundle: carries the image out. Each fibre keeps the same position at both ends, so each carries one dot of the image, in the right place.
  • Objective lens at the far end focuses an image onto the end of the coherent bundle. An eyepiece (or camera) at the near end views it.
  • Medicine: view inside the body, take biopsies, keyhole surgery; no ionising radiation.
  • Industry: inspect inside engines and pipes without taking them apart.
Quick check
?By mistake, an endoscope is made with the fibres jumbled in both of its bundles. The lamp and lenses work normally. What does the doctor see at the eyepiece?
Mission check

?An optical fibre has a glass core of refractive index 1.50, surrounded by glass of refractive index 1.40. On a straight stretch, a ray meets the core's wall at 75° to the normal. Where the fibre bends, the same ray meets the wall at 66° to the normal. What happens at each point?

In a fibre, light heads towards lower n and meets the wall at more than the critical angle, so it is totally internally reflected at every bounce, round gentle bends too.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A magnifying glass can show a scene upside down or upright. What decides which?

Make a guess: A magnifying glass can show a scene upside down or upright. What decides which?

Quick check
?This lens is thinner in the middle than at its edges. Predict before the next screen. The diagram shows a thin lens, its two focal points F, and two rays of a beam of light travelling parallel to the principal axis towards the lens from the left. Which statement describes the light after it passes through the lens?
Mission 7

Two kinds of lens

  • Converging lens (thicker in the middle): rays parallel to the principal axis meet at the principal focus F.
  • Diverging lens (thinner in the middle): parallel rays spread out as if from F on the side they came from.
  • Focal length f: lens centre to F.
  • A thin lens has a focal point on each side, the same distance from the lens.
Mission 7

Ray diagrams

  • Draw two (or three) rays from the top of the object:
  • 1. Parallel to the axis → refracted through F (converging) or as if from F (diverging).
  • 2. Through the centre of the lens → undeviated.
  • 3. Through F (or heading for F) → leaves parallel to the axis.
  • Rays actually meet: a real image. It can be caught on a screen.
  • Rays only appear to come from a point: a virtual image. No screen can show it.
Mission 7

Where the object is decides

  • Converging lens, object beyond 2F: real, inverted, smaller.
  • At 2F: real, inverted, same size (image at 2F).
  • Between F and 2F: real, inverted, larger.
  • At F: rays leave parallel: no image forms.
  • Inside F: virtual, upright, larger. A magnifying glass.
  • Diverging lens, anywhere: virtual, upright, smaller.
Quick check
?The ray diagram shows an object O near a thin converging lens with focal points F, two rays from the tip of O, and the image I that an observer on the right sees by looking through the lens. Which claim about the image I is supported by the diagram?
Mission 7

The lens equation

  • 1/u + 1/v = 1/f: u = object distance, v = image distance.
  • Real is positive: real distances +, virtual distances −. Converging f +; diverging f −.
  • Magnification m = hᵢ/h0 = v/u (hᵢ image height, h0 object height).
  • A negative image distance means a virtual image, on the same side as the object.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • Converging lens, f = 10.0 cm.
  • Object at u = 14.0 cm: 1/v = 1/10.0 − 1/14.0 → v = +35.0 cm. Real, inverted. m = 35.0/14.0 = 2.5.
  • Move it to u = 6.0 cm (inside F): 1/v = 1/10.0 − 1/6.0 → v = −15.0 cm. Virtual, upright. m = 15.0/6.0 = 2.5.
  • Same lens, both images 2.5 times larger, but different object distances: the image flips from inverted to upright.
Quick check
?An object is placed 24.0 cm from a thin diverging lens whose focal length has a magnitude of 12.0 cm. Using the real-is-positive convention, what is the image distance v?
Quick check
?An object 3.0 cm tall is placed 30.0 cm from a thin converging lens of focal length 20.0 cm. What is the height of the image?
Mission check

?A projector has a thin converging lens of focal length 8.0 cm. A slide is placed 9.0 cm from the lens, close to it. Which describes the image?

Object outside F → rays really meet → real, inverted image. Object inside F → rays only appear to meet → virtual, upright image.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A progressive wave transfers energy, not matter. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ travel; longitudinal: oscillation ∥ travel.
  • f = 1/T and v = fλ. For sound and waves on strings, the medium sets v and the source sets f.

Mission 2

  • Phase difference φ = 2πΔx/λ = 2πΔt/T, in radians; × 360°/2π for degrees. λ apart: in phase. λ/2 apart: antiphase.

Mission 3

  • EM waves: oscillating E and B fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse); no medium; all travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum.
  • Radio → microwave → infrared → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma: wavelength falls, frequency rises.

Mission 4

  • Plane polarised: oscillations in one plane. Only transverse waves can be polarised, so polarisation is evidence that light (and all EM waves) are transverse.
  • Crossed polarising filters block all the light; rotating one varies the intensity from maximum to zero every 90°.

Mission 5

  • Rays are perpendicular to wavefronts. Reflection: angle of incidence = angle of reflection, measured from the normal.
  • n = c/v. Refraction is caused by a change of speed; frequency is unchanged, wavelength changes.
  • n = sin i/sin r = c1/c2: towards the normal into the slower medium.

Mission 6

  • TIR: light heading into lower n at more than the critical angle is all reflected.
  • sin c = 1/n (boundary with air).
  • Step-index fibre: TIR at the core–cladding boundary; the cladding protects the core and stops cross-talk.

Mission 7

  • Converging lens: object outside F → real, inverted image; inside F → virtual, upright, magnified. Diverging lens → always virtual, upright, smaller.
  • 1/u + 1/v = 1/f (real is positive); m = hᵢ/h0 = v/u.
