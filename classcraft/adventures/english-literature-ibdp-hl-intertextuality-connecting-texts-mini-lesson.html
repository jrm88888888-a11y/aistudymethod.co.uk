Intertextuality: Connecting Texts

Intertextuality: connecting texts is the third area of exploration. Its question is how texts speak to one another — through comparison, shared genre conventions, literary traditions, allusion and rewriting. No text stands alone.

  • Foregrounds the concepts of creativity, transformation, representation and perspective.
  • Builds the comparative skill assessed in Paper 2 and in the two-work Individual Oral.
  • Across your thirteen works, you learn to read each one in dialogue with others.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Any named work appears only as a swappable, illustrative example — the IB reading lists are chosen by your school and rotate, so this lesson teaches the skills and ideas, never a set text. Press Start when you are ready.

First principles

What intertextuality means

Intertextuality — a term coined by the theorist Julia Kristeva in the 1960s — names the web of relationships every text has with other texts. A work is woven from, and answers to, what came before it.

  • Explicit links: allusion, quotation, epigraph, echo.
  • Structural links: shared genre, form and convention.
  • Cultural links: participation in a tradition or movement.

The point for you: recognising a text’s conversations lets you read its choices as deliberate positions — to affirm, revise or reject what it inherits.

Allusion

Allusion and echo

Allusion is a brief, indirect reference to another text, figure or event, relying on the reader to catch it.

  • An allusion imports meaning compactly: one phrase can summon a whole story.
  • It can dignify (aligning a modern scene with an epic) or deflate (ironic contrast between the grand source and the shabby present).

Illustrative only: a novel might name a doomed lover after a figure from myth, so the reader feels the ending arriving before it does. Use whatever allusions appear in your actual works — and never invent a source you cannot verify.

Genre

Genre as a contract

Genre is a set of conventions and the expectations they create. Texts fulfil, subvert or transform them.

  • Every genre carries a contract: a tragedy promises a fall, a detective story promises a solution.
  • The interesting question is always what a text does with the shape it inherits — satisfying the contract, breaking it, or turning it to new ends.

Subversion signifies: a detective story that withholds its solution is making an argument about knowledge itself. The broken convention is the meaning.

⚖️ Comparison method

Comparison

Comparison is a discipline

Comparison is a discipline, not a list. Strong comparison runs through a shared frame.

  • Choose a common axis: a theme, a technique, a form, a global issue.
  • Track both convergence (where the works agree or resemble) and divergence (where they part).
  • Integrate: each point holds both texts at once, rather than describing one and then the other.

Weak comparison is two mini-essays side by side. Strong comparison is a single argument that needs both texts to make its point.

Tradition

Traditions and the canon

Texts belong to traditions — lineages of form and theme that stretch across time and place.

  • A movement (Romanticism, Modernism, magical realism, and so on) supplies shared assumptions and techniques.
  • The canon is the contested set of works a culture treats as central — and asking who decides is itself a literary question.
  • Writers position themselves within or against a tradition; both are meaningful choices.

Transformation

Transformation and rewriting

One of the most powerful intertextual moves is transformation — rewriting an earlier work.

  • Adaptation — carrying a story into a new form, medium, time or place.
  • Writing back — retelling a canonical text from the silenced figure’s point of view, changing whose story it is.
  • Parody mocks a source; pastiche imitates or assembles a style, often in homage.

Transformation as a concept: a rewriting is a reading. What a later text changes about an earlier one is an argument about the original.

Concepts

Creativity and representation

Two concepts anchor this area.

  • Creativity — originality is rarely creation from nothing; it is inventive recombination of what already exists.
  • Representation — comparing texts exposes how differently the same subject can be constructed, denaturalising each version.

Reading intertextually trains a habit of mind: never ask only what does this text say? but what is it answering, echoing, or refusing?
