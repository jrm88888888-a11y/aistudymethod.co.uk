Mission 1

One God or many?

  • Theism: belief in God or gods.
  • Monotheism: one God (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism).
  • Polytheism: many gods, each with their own powers (e.g. ancient Greek and Norse religion).
Mission 1

The "omni" words

  • Omnipotent: all-powerful.
  • Omniscient: all-knowing: past, present, future, and every thought.
  • Omnipresent: present everywhere at once. "If I ascend up into heaven, you are there." (Psalm 139:8)
  • Omnibenevolent: all-loving and all-good.
  • These are ways of saying God has no limits.
  • Believers find these qualities through revelation: scripture, prophets and experience.
Mission 1

Beyond the world, and within it

  • Transcendent: beyond and outside the universe, space and time; greater than anything humans can imagine. "My thoughts are not your thoughts" (Isaiah 55:8).
  • Immanent: present and active within the world and in people's lives.
  • A transcendent God inspires awe and worship; an immanent God is close enough to pray to.
  • Most Jews, Christians and Muslims hold both together: God is not part of the world, yet is never absent from it.
  • The Qur'an: "We are nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall), yet "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11, Pickthall).
Mission 1

Personal or impersonal? Knowable or not?

  • Personal: God can be known as someone: who loves, speaks, listens and responds, e.g. "Our Father" (Matthew 6:9).
  • Impersonal: the divine is more like a force or ultimate reality than a person, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism (a later mission).
  • Many believers hold that God is personal but is more than a person: not a human being.
Quick check

Mission 1

God in Christianity

  • One God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit (the Trinity).
  • "God is love." (1 John 4:8)
  • God is creator, judge, all-powerful and all-good.
  • Christians see God as both transcendent (Father, Creator) and immanent (the Holy Spirit; God made flesh in Jesus).
Mission 1

Allah in Islam

  • Tawhid: the oneness of Allah. "Say: He is Allah, the One! … He begetteth not nor was begotten." (Qur'an 112:1, 3, Pickthall)
  • So Muslims reject the Trinity and any partner with God (shirk).
  • Every surah but one opens: "In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful" (Qur'an 1:1, Pickthall).
  • Transcendent: nothing is like Allah; never pictured. Immanent: Allah knows and is close to every person.
Mission 1

God in Judaism

  • One God: "HEAR, O ISRAEL: THE LORD OUR GOD, THE LORD IS ONE." (Deuteronomy 6:4, JPS 1917)
  • Creator: "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof" (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917).
  • Lawgiver and judge: gave the Torah; judges with justice and mercy.
  • God has no body and cannot be pictured. Many Jews do not say God's name, and write "G-d".
Mission 1Diversity

Contrasting beliefs about God

  • Trinity (Christian) vs tawhid (Muslim) vs Jewish oneness with no incarnation: all monotheist, but they disagree about how God is one.
  • Personal God (Abrahamic faiths; Sikh Waheguru; many Hindus' devotion to Vishnu or Shiva) vs impersonal ultimate reality (nirguna Brahman; Buddhism has no creator God).
  • Deism: God made the world but does not intervene in it.
Mission 1Evaluate

"God cannot be both transcendent and personal."

  • Reasons for: a being beyond space and time cannot listen and respond like a person; personal language makes God too human; Buddhists and many Hindus think ultimate reality is impersonal.
  • Reasons against: scripture presents both (Isaiah 55:8–9 and Matthew 6:9; Qur'an 42:11 and 50:16); an all-powerful God can be close to all without being limited; Christians: God became personal in Jesus.
  • Another view: "personal" is an analogy: true but not complete.
  • Judge it by: Can human words describe God at all? Is a God who cannot be related to worth worshipping? Do believers' experiences count as evidence?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two different ideas

Question: Explain two different ideas about the nature of God.

  • Idea 1: God is transcendent: beyond the universe. Development: so Muslims never picture Allah: "Naught is as His likeness" (Qur'an 42:11).
  • Idea 2: God is immanent: present in the world. Development: so Muslims pray to Allah directly, anywhere, believing Allah is "nearer to him than his jugular vein" (Qur'an 50:16).
  • 4-mark tip: two different beliefs (AQA now says "different", not "contrasting"), each explained with a development.

Most believers hold God to be beyond the world and present within it; different words name different sides of one God.

Mission 2

What revelation means

  • Revelation: God (or ultimate reality) making known something humans could not find out on their own.
  • Believers say God is transcendent (beyond the world), so humans need God to take the first step.
  • Two kinds:
  • Special revelation: God revealed to particular people at particular times.
  • General revelation: God revealed to everyone, everywhere, through ordinary things.
Mission 2

Special revelation

  • Direct, to particular people or groups, often life-changing.
  • Prophets receiving God's word (Moses at Sinai; the Prophet Muhammad in the cave of Hira).
  • Visions and dreams: seeing or hearing something sacred (see Mission 3).
  • Miracles: events showing God acting in the world.
  • In a person: Christians believe God is revealed most fully in Jesus.
  • Special revelation includes visions; your spec asks for one example (see Mission 3). Enlightenment as a source of knowledge is in Mission 5.
Mission 2

General revelation

  • Open to anyone, at any time: no special event is needed.
  • Nature: the beauty, order and size of the universe point to its Creator.
  • Conscience and reason: an inner sense of right and wrong; the ability to think about God.
  • History and inspirational people: seeing God at work in events and in holy lives.
  • Your spec lists scripture with nature as general revelation: anyone can read it. Many Christians also call scripture the record of special revelation.
  • Most Christians say nature shows that God exists and is powerful, but God's love and purposes are known through special revelation.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of special revelation?
Mission 2Diversity

Nature as general revelation: contrasting beliefs

  • Many Christians: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE). Nature shows God's power (Romans 1:20). Catholics teach God can be known through creation by reason (CCC 36).
  • Some Protestants: Karl Barth (1886–1968) taught that nature on its own gives no true knowledge of God; only God's revelation in Christ does.
  • Atheists and Humanists: nature shows natural processes, explained by science (evolution, physics).
  • Nature also contains cruelty and disaster; to many non-religious people it does not look like the work of a loving God.
  • Humanists still feel awe at nature, without seeing it as a message from God.
Mission 2

Value and problems

  • Value: revelation can give knowledge of God that reason cannot; it guides life; it can lead people to faith and change their lives.
  • Conflicting revelations: religions report different ideas of the divine (personal or impersonal; one God or many forms). They cannot all be literally true.
  • Alternative explanations: illness, drugs, tiredness, wishful thinking or upbringing.
  • Lying or mistaken: a person might invent a revelation, or honestly misunderstand an experience.
  • Responses: believers test claims against scripture, tradition and the person's life ("by their fruits"). Some, like John Hick (1922–2012), suggest different revelations show different sides of one ultimate reality.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Revelation is a reliable way of knowing about God."

  • Reasons for: if God exists and is personal, God could make himself known; many lives have been transformed; scripture and tradition test claims; general revelation is open to everyone.
  • Reasons against: revelations conflict; they cannot be tested by others; natural explanations are possible; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: revelation may be reliable for the person who receives it, but not proof for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Who can check a revelation? Do differences between religions undermine all revelation, or only some? Do changed lives count as evidence?
Quick check
?A man says God spoke to him in a vision telling him to start a new religion. Doctors find he had a very high fever at the time. Which challenge to revelation does this illustrate?
Mission 2Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs in contemporary British society about nature as general revelation.

  • Belief 1 (Christian): nature reveals God. Source: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1). Development: the order and beauty of the universe show God's power and wisdom, so many Christians feel led to worship.
  • Belief 2 (Humanist): nature reveals nothing about God. Development: science explains the universe through natural processes, and suffering in nature does not fit a loving designer.
  • 4-mark tip: two different beliefs, each developed.
Mission check

?An astronomer becomes convinced, from studying the stars, that the universe has a Creator. She says: "But I still don't know whether this Creator cares about me." Which response would most Christians give?

General revelation can point to God; special revelation says who God is. Traditions differ on how far each one reaches.

Mission 3

What is a vision?

  • A vision: seeing or hearing something sacred that others normally cannot: a holy figure, a light, a voice, a scene.
  • Some are seen with the eyes, some in the mind, some in dreams.
  • Visions are a form of special revelation (see Mission 2): given to particular people, often with a message.
  • Why might they lead to belief in God? They feel real and personal, can carry knowledge the person did not have, and often change lives.
  • Your spec asks for one example of a vision: learn one of the later screen–the later screen in detail.
Mission 3Read the source

Abram's vision

"After these things the LORD's word came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'" (Genesis 15:1, WEBBE)

  • God takes Abram outside: "Look now towards the sky, and count the stars" (Genesis 15:5, WEBBE). His descendants will be as many.
  • "He believed in the LORD, who credited it to him for righteousness." (Genesis 15:6, WEBBE)
  • God then makes a covenant with Abram, promising the land (Genesis 15:18).

Mission 3

The Transfiguration

  • Matthew 17:1–13: Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain.
  • "His face shone like the sun, and his garments became as white as the light." (Matthew 17:2, WEBBE)
  • Moses and Elijah appear, talking with Jesus.
  • A voice from a bright cloud: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him." (Matthew 17:5, WEBBE)
  • The disciples fall down in fear; Jesus tells them not to tell anyone until he is raised from the dead.
  • Significance for Christians: it shows Jesus as the Son of God; Moses (the Law) and Elijah (the Prophets) are seen as pointing to him. Three people saw it together.
Mission 3

Joan of Arc

  • Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431), a French peasant girl.
  • From about age 13 she heard voices and saw visions of St Michael, St Catherine and St Margaret.
  • They told her to help Charles VII drive the English from France. In 1429 she led French forces to lift the siege of Orléans.
  • Captured, she was tried by a pro-English Church court and burned in Rouen in 1431.
  • A retrial declared her innocent (1456); the Catholic Church canonised her (made her a saint) in 1920.
  • For Catholics, her courage and the results of her mission are signs that the visions were genuine.
Quick check
?Why do many Christians see the Transfiguration (Matthew 17:1–13) as an important vision?
Mission 3

"Just hallucinations"?

  • Atheists and Humanists argue visions give no proof of God.
  • Hallucinations: the brain can produce sights and voices through illness, fever, drugs, lack of sleep or fasting. Some writers suggest Joan of Arc had a neurological illness (a guess; no medical records exist).
  • Wish-fulfilment: people may see what they long for (Sigmund Freud, The Future of an Illusion, 1927).
  • Expectation: visions usually fit the person's own religion (Catholics see Mary; Hindus see Krishna).
  • Lack of evidence: no one else can check a private vision. The person might also be lying or mistaken.
Mission 3

Religious responses

  • Shared visions: at the Transfiguration three disciples saw the same thing; hallucinations are usually private.
  • Fruits: a genuine vision leads to good, lasting change (Joan's courage; Abram's faith).
  • Tested by tradition: the Catholic Church investigates claims carefully and approves few. A vision must agree with scripture and Church teaching.
  • God can use natural means: even if the brain is involved, God may be at work through it.
Quick check
?Which point would a Christian most likely use to argue that the Transfiguration was not a hallucination?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Visions prove that God exists."

  • Reasons for: visions feel real and personal; some are shared (Matthew 17); they change lives (Joan of Arc); some bring messages the person could not have known.
  • Reasons against: hallucination, wish-fulfilment and expectation explain them; they cannot be checked; visions conflict between religions; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: a vision may be strong evidence for the person who has it, but weak evidence for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Does a natural cause rule out God? Do shared visions matter? Should the fruits of a vision count as evidence?
Mission check

?A woman in a small town says the Virgin Mary has appeared to her with a message. Crowds begin to gather. Which response best matches the Catholic Church's usual approach?

A vision is hard to test from outside. Believers look at its message, the person and its fruits; critics look for natural causes.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can a religion have no creator God at all, and if so, what is ultimately real?

Make a guess: Can a religion have no creator God at all, and if so, what is ultimately real?

Mission 4

Personal God or ultimate reality?

  • Personal God: a being who creates, knows, loves, judges and answers prayer (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism, many Hindus).
  • Ultimate reality: whatever is most real and final, the ground of everything. It need not be a person.
  • Non-theistic: a religion without a creator God at its centre, e.g. Buddhism.
  • Impersonal: ultimate reality is beyond personal qualities, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism.
  • Non-theistic does not mean non-spiritual: these traditions still have worship, ethics, and a final goal.
Mission 4

The Buddha and the creator question

  • The Buddha did not teach a creator God. He taught a path to end suffering (dukkha).
  • Asking who made the world does not help that goal.
  • The Poisoned Arrow (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta): a man shot by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it, the bow, the feathers… He would die first. Pull out the arrow.
Mission 4

Brahman: with and without qualities

  • Brahman: Hindu ultimate reality: the one source, sustainer and goal of all.
  • Nirguna Brahman: "without qualities": beyond every description; impersonal. Upanishads describe it only as "not this, not this" (neti neti).
  • Saguna Brahman: "with qualities": Brahman known in personal forms, such as Vishnu, Shiva or Devi, who can be loved and worshipped.
  • Atman: the true self in each being. Many Hindus teach that atman and Brahman are one: "Tat twam asi" (That thou art), Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7.
  • So many Hindus hold both: one impersonal reality, known through personal deities.
Quick check
?A Hindu worships Krishna every day, yet says ultimate reality is beyond any form. Which idea makes sense of this?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Religion does not need a personal God."

  • Reasons for: Buddhism offers ethics, worship and a goal without a creator; some Hindus see personal deities as steps to an impersonal reality; avoids the problem of evil (no all-good creator to blame).
  • Reasons against: most religious people worship a personal God; love and prayer need someone to relate to; many Christians, Muslims and Jews: without God, morality and meaning have no final ground.
  • Another view: "personal" and "impersonal" may describe two sides of one reality.
  • Judge it by: What makes something a religion? Does practice depend on belief in a creator? Which view better explains religious experience?
Mission check

?At a Buddhist temple open day, a visitor asks a monk: "So who created the universe?" The monk smiles and says that question will not help anyone end suffering. Which teaching is he following?

A religion can centre on liberation, Dhamma or Brahman rather than a personal creator.

Mission 5

Revelation and realisation

  • Revelation: God makes something known to people (scripture, prophets, visions).
  • Enlightenment: a person sees for themselves the truth about reality, through practice and insight.
  • "Buddha" means "awakened one": someone who has woken up to things as they really are.
  • Enlightenment ends ignorance; it is called knowing, not just believing.
  • In Hinduism and Sikhism the goal is liberation (moksha, mukti): freedom from rebirth, often with direct knowledge of the divine.
  • Your spec asks: is enlightenment a source of knowledge about the divine (God, gods or ultimate reality)?
Mission 5

The Buddha's night of awakening

  • Siddhattha Gotama spent six years in extreme austerities: fasting until he was close to death. They brought no insight.
  • He remembered sitting as a child under a rose-apple tree, calm and joyful in the first jhana (meditative absorption). Could this be the way?
  • He ate food again (the Middle Way), sat under the Bodhi tree and passed through the four jhanas.
  • In the three watches of the night he knew: (1) his own past lives; (2) how beings die and are reborn according to their kamma; (3) the Four Noble Truths and the ending of the taints (craving, ignorance).
  • This account is in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
Mission 5Read the source

Knowledge arose

The Buddha's words, in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (MN 36, paraphrase): when my mind was concentrated, pure and steady, I directed it to knowing the ending of the taints. I knew as it really is: "This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ending; this is the way." Ignorance was destroyed; knowledge arose; darkness was destroyed; light arose.

> "Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Q1 What did the Buddha come to know in the third watch?

Show answer

The Four Noble Truths and the ending of the taints.

Q2 Why do Buddhists call this knowledge rather than faith?

Show answer

It is seen directly, "as it really is", not accepted from someone else.

Mission 5

Theravada and Mahayana

  • Theravada: the goal is to become an arhat ("worthy one"), who has ended greed, hatred and delusion and will not be reborn.
  • The path: ethics, meditation and wisdom: seeing the three marks of existence: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • Mahayana: the ideal is the bodhisattva, who seeks enlightenment in order to free all beings.
  • Many Mahayana Buddhists teach Buddha-nature: every being already has the potential to be a Buddha; enlightenment uncovers it.
  • Mahayana stresses sunyata (emptiness): nothing exists independently.
  • Both agree: enlightenment is nibbana (nirvana), the end of craving and suffering, not union with a creator God.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly contrasts Theravada and Mahayana ideas of enlightenment?
Mission 5

Hindus: liberation and knowing the divine

  • Moksha: liberation from samsara (rebirth). Many Hindus say it brings direct knowledge of the divine.
  • Advaita Vedanta (Shankara, 8th century): the true self (atman) is Brahman. Liberation is realising this through knowledge (jnana) and meditation.
  • Bhakti traditions: liberation through loving devotion to a personal God (Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva); the soul knows and loves God, but is not identical with God.
  • Brahman in the Gita: "He is within all beings--and without--" (Bhagavad Gita 13.15, Arnold).
Mission 5

Sikhs: mukti by the Guru's grace

  • Sikh enlightenment is mukti: liberation, union with Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord"). A jivan mukt is liberated while still alive.
  • The barrier is haumai (ego); the way is naam simran (remembering God's name), seva and living as a gurmukh (God-centred person).
  • It comes by God's nadar (grace), not by human effort alone.
  • Vismad: wonder at God's creation, a numinous experience.
  • Guru Granth Sahib 448 (Guru Ram Das, paraphrase): the arrow of the Lord's love has pierced my mind and body; through the Guru I have met the Lord.
  • Sikh tradition: Guru Nanak vanished into the River Vein for three days, met God and returned saying, "There is no Hindu, there is no Muslim" (janamsakhi tradition).
Mission 5

Non-religious explanations

  • Brain states: brain scans show meditation changes attention and emotion areas. Intense experiences may be brain events, nothing more.
  • Altered states: fasting, lack of sleep and long isolation can produce vivid experiences.
  • Illogical: atheists may say "knowing" by meditation cannot be tested; experiences of different faiths contradict each other.
  • Lying or mistaken: people may exaggerate, or misread a strong feeling as insight.
  • Humanists: value calm and mindfulness for their real benefits, without a supernatural or "ultimate" claim.
Mission 5

Religious responses

  • Many believers reply that brain activity during insight does not show the insight is false: seeing a tree also involves the brain.
  • Sikhs: mukti is shown in a transformed life of seva and humility, consistent with Gurbani.
  • Many traditions test claims by fruits: compassion, wisdom, freedom from greed.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Enlightenment gives real knowledge of ultimate reality."

  • Reasons for: direct seeing, not hearsay; practice can be repeated and taught; enlightened people show lasting compassion and calm; the Buddha invited testing.
  • Reasons against: cannot be checked from outside; brain-state explanations; experiences may be mistaken or exaggerated.
  • Problem of different ideas: Buddhists find no creator; Advaita Hindus find the self is Brahman; Sikhs find a personal, loving Waheguru. Can all be knowledge?
  • Another view: traditions may describe one reality in different ways; or each tradition's own account may be the true one.
  • Judge it by: Is inner seeing a kind of knowledge? Do changed lives count as evidence? Do the differences weaken every claim?
Mission check

?Harpreet, a Sikh, says that through naam simran she felt the ego fall away and knew Waheguru's grace. Arjun, an Advaita Hindu, says that in deep meditation he realised his self is Brahman. Sam, an atheist, says both were having unusual brain states. Which statement is accurate?

Enlightenment is claimed as knowledge seen from the inside. Whether it shows ultimate reality, and which reality, is where traditions and critics disagree.

Mission 6

What is a religious experience?

  • A religious experience: an experience in which a person believes they have encountered God or ultimate reality.
  • It may be sudden or gradual, alone or with others, ordinary or overwhelming.
  • William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), said mystical states are hard to put into words (ineffable), feel like knowledge (noetic), do not last long (transient), and happen to the person (passive).
  • For many believers it is the most direct evidence of God they have.
  • Your spec asks how valuable such experiences are as knowledge of the divine, and how else they might be explained.
Mission 6

Kinds of religious experience

  • Conversion: a change of heart, belief or religion.
  • Mystical: a sense of union with God or ultimate reality, beyond words.
  • Numinous: awe and fear before a holy power. Rudolf Otto (1869–1937), The Idea of the Holy (1917): the holy is a mystery that makes us tremble yet draws us in.
  • Charismatic: gifts of the Holy Spirit, such as glossolalia (speaking in tongues: Acts 2:4), prophecy and healing.
  • Worship and sacraments: Catholics believe Christ is truly present in the Mass; many feel God's presence in prayer and liturgy.
Quick check
?Which of these is the clearest example of a numinous experience?
Mission 6

Challenges from atheists and Humanists

  • Hallucination: illness, drugs, tiredness or fasting can produce powerful experiences.
  • Wish-fulfilment: Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Future of an Illusion (1927): belief in God answers our longing for a protective father.
  • Lack of evidence: private experiences cannot be checked by others.
  • Culture shapes experience: Christians meet Jesus, Hindus meet Krishna, Buddhists find no God. If the source were one God, why so different?
  • Lying or mistaken: some claims are invented; honest people can misread a feeling.
  • Humanists: the experiences are real, but have natural, psychological causes.
Mission 6

Religious responses

  • Fruits: "By their fruits you will know them." (Matthew 7:16, WEBBE) A lasting change for good (Paul) is hard to explain as illusion.
  • Shared experiences: at Pentecost a whole group was filled with the Spirit (Acts 2).
  • Testing: traditions check claims against scripture and teaching; most are not approved.
  • God may use natural means: brain activity during an experience does not show God is absent.
  • Different traditions, one reality? Some believers see differences as different responses to the same divine reality; others think their own tradition's account is the true one.
Quick check
?After her husband dies, a lonely woman feels God's comforting presence every evening. A critic says: "She feels this because she badly longs for comfort." Which challenge is the critic using?
Mission 6Evaluate

"Religious experience proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: experiences are widespread; changed lives; some are shared.
  • Reasons against: hallucination, wish-fulfilment (Freud); cannot be checked; experiences differ between religions; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: religious experience may justify belief for the one who has it, without proving God to anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Is a natural cause the whole story? Should we trust what seems real to us? Do differences between religions weaken every claim?
Mission check

?Amir, a Muslim, says that during dhikr (chanting in remembrance of Allah) he felt Allah very close. His atheist friend says it was the rhythm and the breathing. His Christian friend asks whether Amir's life has changed for the better since. Which statement is accurate?

Religious experiences are powerful for those who have them. Others can explain them naturally, or test them by their fit with tradition and their fruits.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Omnipotent, omniscient, omnipresent, omnibenevolent; transcendent and immanent; personal and impersonal; knowable and unknowable.
  • Christians: Trinity and love (1 John 4:8); Muslims: tawhid; Jews: one God (Deuteronomy 6:4).

Mission 2

  • Revelation is God making himself known: special (prophets, visions, miracles, Jesus, scripture received) and general (nature, conscience, reason, scripture read by all).
  • Problems: conflicting revelations, alternative explanations, lying or mistake; believers reply with tests of scripture, tradition and fruits.

Mission 3

  • A vision is a form of special revelation: seeing or hearing something sacred. Examples: Abram (Genesis 15), the Transfiguration (Matthew 17), Joan of Arc.
  • Atheists and Humanists explain visions as hallucinations, wish-fulfilment or expectation; believers point to shared visions, fruits and testing by tradition.

Mission 4

  • Buddhism: the Buddha taught no creator God; the Poisoned Arrow: such questions do not help end suffering.
  • Hinduism: Brahman is nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (personal forms).

Mission 5

  • Enlightenment is seeing reality for oneself; the Buddha's awakening (Maha-Saccaka Sutta) came through the jhanas and insight into the Four Noble Truths. Theravada stresses the arhat; Mahayana the bodhisattva and Buddha-nature. Critics give brain-state and mistake explanations.
  • Hindus seek moksha (Advaita: atman is Brahman; bhakti: loving union).
  • Sikhs seek mukti, union with Waheguru by the Guru's grace.

Mission 6

  • Religious experience: conversion, mystical, numinous, charismatic, worship and sacraments.
  • Challenges: hallucination, wish-fulfilment, lack of evidence, cultural differences; responses: fruits, shared experiences, testing.
