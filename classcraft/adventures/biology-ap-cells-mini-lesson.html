Mission 1

What is an organelle?

  • An organelle is a distinct part of a cell with a specific function.
  • Eukaryotic cells have membrane-bound organelles; ribosomes are non-membrane structures.
  • The cell surface (plasma) membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • Internal membranes divide the cell into compartments, each with its own enzymes and reactions.
Mission 1

The nucleus

  • Nuclear envelope: a double membrane with nuclear pores. mRNA leaves through the pores; proteins enter.
  • Chromatin: DNA wound around histone proteins. It condenses into visible chromosomes when the cell divides.
  • Nucleolus: a dense region that makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes.
  • The nucleus holds the cell's genes and controls which proteins are made.
  • The nuclear envelope is part of the endomembrane system.
Mission 1

Ribosomes

  • Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein; no membrane.
  • Two subunits. They read mRNA and join amino acids: protein synthesis.
  • Free in the cytoplasm, or bound to the RER.
  • Ribosomes are found in cells of all forms of life: they reflect the common ancestry of all known life.
  • Say: "ribosomes translate mRNA into protein" — not: "ribosomes copy DNA".
Mission 1

The endoplasmic reticulum

  • Rough ER (RER): flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes. Makes and transports proteins, e.g. proteins for secretion.
  • Smooth ER (SER): a network of tubes with no ribosomes. Makes lipids.
  • Smooth ER also detoxifies the cell.
  • The ER gives mechanical support, helping the cell keep its shape, and helps transport within the cell.
Mission 1

Golgi apparatus, vesicles and lysosomes

  • Golgi apparatus: a stack of curved, flattened membrane sacs; no ribosomes.
  • It modifies proteins (e.g. adds carbohydrate) and packages them into vesicles.
  • AP calls it the Golgi complex: it folds and chemically modifies new products and packages proteins for trafficking.
  • Vesicles: small membrane sacs that carry substances around the cell.
  • Lysosome: a vesicle with a single membrane, full of hydrolytic enzymes. It digests engulfed material and worn-out organelles.
  • Lysosomes also play a role in apoptosis (programmed cell death).
  • The lysosome membrane keeps the enzymes apart from the cytoplasm they would digest.
  • ER, Golgi complex, lysosomes, vacuoles, transport vesicles, nuclear envelope and plasma membrane form the endomembrane system.
Mission 1

Mitochondria

  • Double membrane. The inner membrane folds into cristae; the fluid inside is the matrix.
  • Site of aerobic respiration, which makes ATP.
  • Cristae give a large surface area for the membrane-bound steps that make ATP.
  • The outer membrane is smooth; the inner membrane is highly convoluted, so ATP is synthesized more efficiently.
  • Say: "mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".

Mission 2

The cell wall

  • A cell wall lies outside the plasma membrane.
  • Plant walls are made of cellulose. Fungal walls are made of chitin.
  • Cells of Bacteria, Archaea, Fungi and plants all have cell walls.
  • The wall gives strength and shape, and stops the cell bursting when water enters by osmosis.
  • Water and most small solutes pass through the wall. The plasma membrane controls what enters the cell.
  • The wall is a permeability barrier for some substances, and protects against osmotic lysis.
  • Say: "the membrane controls entry" — not: "the wall controls entry".
Quick check
?Which statement about cell walls is correct?
Mission 2

Chloroplasts

  • Found in plants and algae: the site of photosynthesis.
  • Double membrane (envelope).
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs holding chlorophyll, stacked into grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, with the enzymes that make sugars.
  • Not every plant cell has them: root cells, for example, do not.
  • Say: "plant cells have chloroplasts and mitochondria" — not: "chloroplasts instead of mitochondria".
Mission 2

The vacuole

  • Plant cells have a large, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.
  • Water enters the vacuole; the contents press against the wall: the cell is turgid (firm).
  • In plant cells a large central vacuole maintains turgor pressure by storing water and nutrients.
  • Animal cells have vacuoles too: smaller, more plentiful and often temporary; they store materials.

Mission 3

The secretory pathway

  • Ribosomes on the rough ER make the protein. It passes into the space inside the RER.
  • The protein is folded, then carried in transport vesicles that bud off the RER.
  • The vesicles fuse with the Golgi apparatus, which modifies and packages the protein.
  • Secretory vesicles bud off the Golgi and move to the cell surface.
  • They fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing the protein outside: exocytosis.
  • The ER, Golgi complex, vesicles and plasma membrane are parts of one endomembrane system.
Mission 3

Read the data: following a labeled protein

  • Pulse-chase: cells get radioactive amino acids for a few minutes (pulse), then unlabelled ones (chase).
  • Then: where is the labeled protein at each time? George Palade's group used this method on pancreas cells in the 1960s.

Mission 3

Finishing the protein

  • Many polypeptides do not work until they are modified.
  • Carbohydrate chains are added to many proteins, making glycoproteins. This starts in the RER and is finished in the Golgi.
  • The Golgi complex helps proteins fold correctly and chemically modifies them.
  • The Golgi sorts proteins into vesicles by destination, e.g. secretion or the plasma membrane.
  • Say: "the Golgi modifies and packages proteins" — not: "the Golgi makes proteins".
Mission 3

Vesicles: bud, travel, fuse

  • A vesicle buds from one membrane and fuses with another. Membranes are fluid, so they can bend, pinch off and fuse.
  • Exocytosis: a vesicle fuses with the plasma membrane. Its contents leave; its membrane becomes part of the plasma membrane.
  • Endocytosis: the plasma membrane folds in around material and pinches off as a vesicle.
  • Both use energy from ATP.
  • Both move large substances, or large amounts of substances, into or out of cells.
Quick check
?Cells of a hypothetical gland make a digestive enzyme, a large protein, and package it into vesicles inside the cell. A chemical is added that blocks exocytosis in these cells but does not affect how the enzyme is made or packaged. Which prediction about the cells is best supported?
Mission 3

Why build compartments?

  • Membranes keep enzymes and their substrates together in a small volume, so reactions run faster.
  • Membranes keep incompatible processes apart: hydrolytic enzymes stay inside lysosomes.
  • Each compartment can keep its own conditions: the inside of a lysosome is acidic (about pH 5).
  • Proteins made for export stay inside membranes until they leave the cell.
  • Internal membranes minimize competing interactions and increase the surface area where reactions happen (e.g. the folded inner membrane of mitochondria).
  • Eukaryotic internal membranes partition the cell into specialized regions; prokaryotes typically lack them.
Quick check
?Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes that can break down proteins, lipids, nucleic acids and polysaccharides. Which statement best describes the role of the lysosome's membrane in the cell?
Mission check

?Plasma cells make antibodies, which are proteins they secrete into the blood. At which stage, if any, is a newly made antibody molecule free in the cell's cytoplasm?

Like the pancreas's enzymes, an exported protein is never loose in the cytoplasm. Membranes surround it from the RER until exocytosis.

Mission 4

Supply and demand

  • Materials enter and leave across the surface. Supply depends on surface area.
  • Every part of the cytoplasm uses materials. Demand depends on volume.
  • The surface area to volume ratio (SA:V) compares the two.
  • SA/V = surface area ÷ volume (written SA:V on some screens here).
  • High SA:V: plenty of surface for each unit of volume.
  • Say: "SA:V is surface area ÷ volume" — not: "volume ÷ surface area".
Mission 4

Worked example: growing cubes

Model cells as cubes of side s. Surface area = 6s². Volume = s³.

Side (µm)1234
Surface area (µm²)6245496
Volume (µm³)182764
SA:V (µm⁻¹)6.03.02.01.5
  • Side × 2: area × 4, volume × 8. SA:V halves.
  • Volume grows faster than surface. Big cells run short of surface.
  • So cells stay small, and large organisms are made of many small cells.
Quick check
?A hypothetical spherical cell grows until its radius has doubled, keeping the same shape. What happens to its SA/V?
Mission 4

Worked example: other shapes

  • Sphere: SA = 4πr²; V = (4/3)πr³.
  • Cylinder: SA = 2πrh + 2πr²; V = πr²h.
  • Cuboid (rectangular solid): SA = 2lh + 2lw + 2wh; V = lwh.
  • These formulae, and the cube's, are on the AP equation sheet.
  • Sphere, r = 5 µm:
  • SA = 4 × π × 5² = 314 µm².
  • V = (4/3) × π × 5³ = 524 µm³.
  • SA:V = 314 ÷ 524 = 0.60 µm⁻¹.
  • Count every face: hidden faces too.

Mission 4

Large organisms

  • As an organism grows, SA:V falls. Many cells are far from the surface.
  • Diffusion is too slow over long distances.
  • So large organisms have specialized exchange surfaces (lungs, gills, gut, leaves) and mass transport systems (blood).
  • Small animals: high SA:V, so they lose heat faster per gram, and have a higher metabolic rate per unit mass.
  • As mass increases, SA/V and the rate of heat exchange with the surroundings both decrease.
Quick check
?Shrews are among the smallest mammals. Like larger mammals, they keep their body temperature nearly constant. A shrew must eat much more food per gram of its body mass each day than a deer does. Which statement best explains this difference?
Mission 4

Cells that beat the limit

  • Shape, not just size, sets SA:V. Cells add surface without adding much volume.
  • Microvilli: finger-like folds of the cell-surface membrane, e.g. ileum epithelial cells.
  • Other examples: root hairs, guard cells, gut epithelial cells, membrane folds.
  • Say: "microvilli are folds of membrane that add surface" — not: "microvilli beat like cilia".
Quick check
?Root hairs are thin, microscopic extensions of the epidermal cells of plant roots, and a single root can bear many thousands of them. When a seedling is moved to a new pot, many of its root hairs are torn off. Which prediction about the seedling in the following days is best supported?
Mission check

Bigger always means less surface per unit of volume. That is why cells stay small and large organisms are built from many of them.

Mission 5

Supply against demand

  • Every living cell needs oxygen in and carbon dioxide out, plus nutrients in and wastes out.
  • Heat is exchanged with the surroundings across the surface too.
  • Supply depends on surface area: the area materials cross.
  • Demand depends on volume: the amount of living, respiring tissue.
  • So the key number is the surface area to volume ratio = surface area ÷ volume. Written SA:V or SA/V.
Mission 5

Worked example: bigger means less surface per volume

Cube sideSurface area = 6 × side²Volume = side³SA:V
1 mm6 mm²1 mm³6 mm⁻¹ (6 : 1)
3 mm54 mm²27 mm³2 mm⁻¹ (2 : 1)
10 mm600 mm²1000 mm³0.6 mm⁻¹ (0.6 : 1)
  • Side × 3 → area × 9, but volume × 27. SA:V ÷ 3.
  • Total surface area rises with size. SA:V falls.
  • Say: "a smaller surface area to volume ratio" — not: "a smaller surface area".

Mission 5

Bigger bodies: change shape

Two shapes with the same volume, 8 mm³:

  • Cube, 2 × 2 × 2 mm: surface area 24 mm², SA:V = 3 mm⁻¹.
  • Flat sheet, 4 × 4 × 0.5 mm: surface area 40 mm², SA:V = 5 mm⁻¹.
  • Flattened or thin shapes add surface with little extra volume.
  • They also keep every cell close to the surface.
  • Examples: the flat body of a flatworm; thin, flat leaves; root hairs.
  • A single cell such as an amoeba has a high SA:V, so diffusion across its membrane can supply it. A large body has a low SA:V: it needs specialized exchange surfaces (lungs) and a transport system (heart and blood vessels).
Quick check
?African elephants are among the largest land animals and live in hot climates. Their ears are very large, thin flaps of skin with many blood vessels close to the surface. Which statement best explains how ears of this shape affect the elephant's exchange of thermal energy with air that is cooler than its body?
Mission 5

Size, heat loss and metabolic rate

  • Mammals and birds keep a nearly constant body temperature. They lose heat across their surface.
  • Small body → high SA:V → loses heat faster per gram.
  • To replace it, its cells respire faster: a higher metabolic rate per unit body mass.
  • So small mammals and birds need more O2 per gram, delivered quickly.
  • Smaller masses exchange proportionally more heat with the surroundings. As mass increases, SA:V and the rate of heat exchange decrease.
  • Say: "higher metabolic rate per unit body mass" — not: "higher metabolic rate". A large animal still uses more energy in total.

Mission check

?An amoeba gets all its oxygen by diffusion across its cell-surface membrane. Why could a mouse not get enough oxygen by diffusion across its body surface alone?

Small size helps the amoeba: its high SA:V lets diffusion supply every part. A larger body has too little surface for its volume, so it needs lungs to exchange gases and a heart to carry them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ribosomes (rRNA + protein) are in all cells; the endomembrane system (ER, Golgi complex, lysosomes, vesicles) and mitochondria divide the work of a eukaryotic cell.
  • Many of one organelle means a lot of its job: lots of RER and Golgi means protein export.

Mission 2

  • Plant cells add a cellulose wall, chloroplasts (in cells that get light) and a large permanent vacuole; fungal walls are chitin; animal cells have no wall.

Mission 3

  • Secreted proteins: made on RER → transport vesicles → Golgi (modified, packaged) → secretory vesicles → exocytosis.
  • Compartments concentrate enzymes and keep incompatible processes apart; exported or digestive enzymes stay behind membranes.

Mission 4

  • Supply depends on surface area, demand on volume; as size increases, volume grows faster, so SA:V falls and limits cell size.
  • Large organisms need exchange surfaces and transport systems; small animals lose heat faster per gram.
  • Cells raise SA:V by shape: microvilli, flattening, folds.

Mission 5

  • As size increases, SA:V falls; demand (volume) outgrows supply (surface).
  • Small endotherms: high SA:V → fast heat loss → high metabolic rate per unit body mass.
