Paper 1: Poetry & Modern Prose

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of Edexcel International GCSE English Literature Paper 1 (4ET1/01): how to compare two anthology poems, how to analyse poetic methods and their effects, and how to write a strong modern-prose essay — all judged against the Assessment Objectives AO1–AO4.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Three sections, three tasks

Paper 1 is a 2-hour exam worth 90 marks. You answer three questions — one from each section:

  • Section A — Unseen Poetry (20 marks): a poem you've never seen, printed on the paper. Explore its meaning and effects. Rewards AO2.
  • Section B — Anthology Poetry (30 marks): compare two poems from the Pearson Edexcel International GCSE English Anthology — one is named, you choose or are given the second. Rewards AO2 and AO3.
  • Section C — Modern Prose (40 marks): an essay on the set novel your school has studied. Rewards AO1 and context AO4.

Set texts differ by school: your class picks one modern-prose novel from Edexcel's list — e.g. Of Mice and Men, To Kill a Mockingbird, The Whale Rider, Things Fall Apart or The Joy Luck Club. The anthology poems are fixed for everyone, so the comparison skills here apply to whatever pairing comes up.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark is tied to an Assessment Objective (AO). Know what each one asks for — it tells you what to put in your answer:

Which AO where? Unseen poetry → AO2 only. Anthology comparison → AO2 + AO3 (so you must compare). Modern prose → AO1 + AO4 (argue with quotes and weave in context).

Name the Assessment Objective

Tap the AO that each sentence in an essay is mainly serving.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader:

method + evidence → effect on the readere.g. "the harsh plosive 'b' sounds (method) make the line feel violent (effect)"

Useful poetic methods to spot: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, alliteration, sibilance, enjambment, caesura, rhyme, rhythm, repetition, tone and structure (stanza shape, volta / turn).

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a stanza

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not anthology text) annotated the way you'd mark up a real poem in the exam:

The iron sea gnawed the shore,
grey, grey as an old regret,
while gulls screamed, screamed for more —
and still the tide would not forget.
  • "iron sea" — metaphor: the sea feels hard and unforgiving, hinting at a cold mood.
  • "grey, grey" — repetition of colour: drains warmth and mirrors the speaker's regret.
  • "screamed, screamed" — repetition + harsh verb: an anxious, relentless soundscape.
  • "gnawed" — violent verb (personification): the sea attacks the land, raising tension.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your essay paragraphs need.

Section B skill · AO3

How to compare poems

A comparison isn't two separate essays glued together. You must weigh the poems against each other using comparative connectives:

  • Similarity: similarly, likewise, both poems, in the same way, equally.
  • Difference: whereas, however, by contrast, on the other hand, unlike ___, while.

Compare across the same dimensions in both poems: theme, language, form & structure, and tone/mood.

Misconception: writing everything about Poem A, then everything about Poem B, is not comparing. You must compare throughout — link the two in almost every paragraph.

Section C skill

The modern-prose essay starts with a thesis

For the prose question, open with a thesis — a clear line of argument that answers the exact question. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "How does the writer present the theme of injustice?"

Thesis: "The writer presents injustice as something ordinary people accept too easily, using a naïve young narrator so that readers notice cruelty the adults ignore."

Why it works: it takes a position (injustice is normalised), names a method (naïve narrator) and points to an effect (readers see what characters miss). Now every paragraph can prove one part of it.

Paragraph shape

Build paragraphs with PEA / PEEL

Each body paragraph follows a reliable shape so it always hits AO1 and AO2:

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Weave short quotations into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line. Short + embedded = fluent and precise:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The narrator is scared. The quote is: 'a cold fear crept up my spine and would not leave.'"

✔ "The narrator's 'cold fear' that 'would not leave' shows how the injustice haunts even the young."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO4

Context serves the analysis

Context (the world the text was written in) can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture.

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "This was written during the Great Depression. Many people were poor. Now back to the novel."

✔ "Set against Depression-era hardship, the characters' impossible dream of land feels all the more fragile, which is why its collapse hits so hard."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

You have ~40 minutes for each essay (a little more for the 40-mark prose question). Spend the first 5 minutes planning — it saves time and lifts your mark:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word).
  • Jot 3 points — for Section B, each should compare both poems.
  • Tag a short quote beside each point.
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. Leave 2 minutes to check.

Closed book: the anthology poems are printed on the paper, but your modern-prose novel is not — so learn a bank of short, flexible quotations in advance.

Worked model · Section B

A comparison paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two (made-up) poems throughout, with method and effect:

Model paragraph

"Both poems present the sea as a threat, yet they do so differently. In the first, the metaphor 'iron sea' makes the water feel hard and merciless, and the violent verb 'gnawed' personifies it as a predator, unsettling the reader. By contrast, the second poem's gentle sibilance in 'soft, sighing swell' lulls us, so its later turn to danger feels more sudden and shocking. Whereas the first builds dread steadily, the second ambushes us — but both leave the reader wary of nature's power."

Spot the moves: shared theme stated first, connectives ("by contrast", "whereas"), method → effect in both, and a comparative closing line.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Block comparison: Poem A then Poem B — you must compare throughout.
  • Bolted-on context: a history block that doesn't serve a point.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • No thesis: retelling the story instead of arguing an answer.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should either make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and in Section B, keep comparing.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Paper 1: A Unseen Poetry (AO2) · B Anthology comparison (AO2+AO3) · C Modern Prose (AO1+AO4)

AO1: argue a thesis, support with short quotes

AO2: method + evidence → effect on the reader

AO3: compare the two poems throughout with connectives · AO4: weave context into the prose essay

Compare: integrated structure, same dimensions in both poems

Prose: thesis → PEEL paragraphs → embedded quotes

Timing: ~40 min each; plan 3 points in the first 5
