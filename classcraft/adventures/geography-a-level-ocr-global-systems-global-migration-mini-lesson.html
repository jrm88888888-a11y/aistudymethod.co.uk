Mission 1

Who is a migrant?

  • International migrant: "any person who changes his or her country of usual residence" (UN DESA).
  • Voluntary: chooses to move (work, family, study). Forced: compelled by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or income.
  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted".
  • Asylum seeker: has claimed protection; claim not yet decided. Undocumented (irregular): no legal permission to enter or stay.
Mission 1

Global numbers

  • 304 million international migrants in 2024, up from 275 million in 2020.
  • 3.7% of the world's population.
  • 48% were women or girls.
  • One in six was forcibly displaced across borders.
  • End of 2025: 41.6 million refugees; 9 million asylum seekers awaiting decisions.
Quick check
?International migrants rose from 275 million in 2020 to 304 million in 2024. What was the percentage increase?
Mission 1

Patterns: inter- and intra-regional

  • Intra-regional: 45% of migrants live in their region of origin.
  • Share from the same region: Africa 86%; Asia 81%; Europe 48%.
  • Inter-regional: the largest corridor is Latin America and the Caribbean → Northern America (27 million); then Central and Southern Asia → Northern Africa and Western Asia (20m); Northern Africa and Western Asia → Europe (13m).
  • Destinations: USA 52.4 million; Germany 16.8 million. Origins: India 18.5m; China 11.7m; Mexico 11.6m.
Mission 1

New sources, new hosts, South–South

  • New hosts: in the UAE, migrants are 74% of the population.
  • Bilateral corridor: 4.33 million non-resident Indians in the UAE; 2.75 million in Saudi Arabia (Jan 2026).
  • Money flows back: India received US$150.7 billion in remittances in 2025.
  • Towards South–South: less developed regions now host about 136 million migrants, against 168 million in more developed regions (2024).
  • This counts where migrants live, not where they came from: a strict South–South v South–North comparison needs UN DESA's origin–destination data.
  • Globalised oil, construction and service economies created demand that pulls workers from South Asia.
Mission 1

Young workers and women

  • Qatar: 248 males per 100 females; UAE 177 (2024).
  • In Qatar, 48.6% of residents are aged 20–39, against 29.6% worldwide (2024, calculated).
  • Women: 48% of migrants worldwide; lowest share in Asia (42%), highest in Europe (52%).
  • Labour corridors to the Gulf skew young and male; family and care migration to Europe and North America is more female.
Quick check

Mission check

?Nepal's 2024 net migration rate was −13.5 per 1,000 people, and it had 92 males for every 100 females. Gulf destinations such as Qatar have 248 males per 100 females. What best explains Nepal's figures?

Most people stay: only 3.7% are international migrants. Those who move are selected: mostly voluntary, often young, working-age and, on labour corridors, male, pulled by jobs and helped or blocked by barriers and policy. Conflict and disaster add forced flows on top.

Mission 2

Remittances as a development index

  • Remittances: money that migrants send to families in their home country.
  • OCR uses the value of migrant remittances as an index linking migration to development.
  • Remittances to low- and middle-income countries: $608 billion (2023), $665 billion (2024), $704 billion (2025).
Mission 2

Who depends on remittances?

  • Largest totals, 2024: India $137.7 billion, Mexico $67.9 billion, Philippines $40.3 billion.
  • Largest as a share of GDP, 2024: Tajikistan 47.2%, Tonga 39.4%, Nicaragua 26.6%, Nepal 26.0%.
  • The biggest totals go to large economies; the biggest dependence is in small ones.
Mission 2

Remittances and HDI

  • Human Development Index (HDI): combines health, education and income.
  • Remittance-dependent countries, HDI 2023: Tonga high (92nd), Nicaragua high (123rd), Tajikistan medium (128th), Nepal medium (145th).
  • Two of the largest payers of remittances rank very high: UAE 15th, USA 17th.
  • Remittances paid out, 2024: USA $105.3 billion, UAE $58.5 billion, Saudi Arabia $46.6 billion.
Quick check
?Which conclusion do this mission's 2024 remittance figures and 2023 HDI ranks best support?
Mission 2

Money: stability and growth

  • In 2023, remittances to low- and middle-income countries ($608 billion) were larger than FDI ($408 billion) and aid ($252 billion).
  • Remittances go straight to households: food, school fees, health care, housing.
  • They can steady incomes when harvests fail or local jobs are lost.
Quick check
?In 2023, remittances to low- and middle-income countries were $608 billion and FDI $408 billion. How many times as large as FDI were remittances?
Mission 2

People, ideas and technology

  • People: migrants fill labour shortages in host economies, from care work to engineering.
  • Ideas: "social remittances": new norms and practices sent home, such as the value of girls' schooling.
  • Technology: returning migrants bring skills, contacts and know-how; diasporas invest in home businesses.
  • Together these can promote development, not only income.
Mission 2

Inequalities

  • Cost: sending money cost an average of 6.36% of the amount sent in Q3 2025; the UN target is "less than 3 per cent" by 2030.
  • Within source countries: households with a migrant gain; those without fall behind.
  • Brain drain: source places lose trained workers, such as nurses and engineers.
  • Dependence: a country relying on remittances is exposed to job losses in a few host countries.
Mission 2

Conflicts and injustices

  • Exploitation: UNODC recorded 74,785 detected trafficking victims in 2022; detections of trafficking for forced labour rose by 47%.
  • Irregular routes: in the year to June 2025, the UK recorded 49,341 detected irregular arrivals, 43,309 of them by small boat.
  • Political conflict: UK net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023; immigration became a central political issue.
  • These flows of people raise questions of rights and justice for migrants, hosts and sources.
Quick check
?Which is an example of an injustice caused through flows of money?
Mission check

?A government whose remittances equal a quarter of its GDP plans for the next decade. Which assessment is best supported by this mission?

The inflow was remittances: 47.2% of Tajikistan's GDP in 2024. For medium- and high-HDI countries such as Tajikistan, Tonga and Nepal, migrant money is a very large inflow relative to the economy. The same flows of people, money and ideas promote stability and growth, but also inequality, conflict and injustice.

Mission 3

Voluntary and forced migration

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work, study or family.
  • Forced migration: people are compelled to move by threats to life or livelihood: conflict, persecution, disaster.
  • In practice it is a continuum: many moves mix choice and compulsion.
Mission 3

Who is who

  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted for reasons of race, religion, nationality, membership of a particular social group or political opinion" (1951 Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked for protection; the claim is not yet decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee but "not crossed an internationally recognized State border".
  • Economic migrant: moves by choice, mainly for work and higher income.
Quick check
?A man flees threats from an armed group, crosses a border and applies for protection. His claim has not yet been decided. What is he now?
Mission 3

Global numbers

  • End of 2025: 117.8 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide.
  • They include 41.6 million refugees, 9 million asylum seekers and 68.7 million people displaced inside their own country by conflict or violence.
  • Most displaced people never cross a border.
Mission 3

Conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers

  • Refugees under UNHCR's mandate (this series leaves out Palestine refugees under UNRWA and others in need of international protection, so it is lower than the 41.6 million total): 16.1 million at the end of 2015; 31.6 million at the end of 2023, almost double (our calculation).
  • The largest one-year rise came in 2022: up 8.1 million (our calculation). Refugees from Ukraine rose from about 28,000 to 5.7 million that year, as war began.
  • Largest origins at the end of 2025: Ukraine 5.2 million, Syria 4.9 million, Sudan 2.8 million, Afghanistan 2.7 million.
  • Numbers fell to 28.5 million by the end of 2025: trends can reverse when people return.
Quick check
?Refugees under UNHCR's mandate: 16.1 million (2015), 31.6 million (2023), 28.5 million (2025); largest origins in 2025: Ukraine, Syria, Sudan, Afghanistan. Which statement is best supported?
Mission 3

Political push: the Rohingya, 2017

  • On 25 August 2017 the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army (ARSA) attacked a military base and up to 30 security-force outposts in northern Rakhine State.
  • The authorities called their response "clearance operations".
  • A UN fact-finding mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
Mission 3

Root causes and the trigger

  • The mission found most Rohingya "de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • At least 392 villages, 40% of all settlements in northern Rakhine, were partly or totally destroyed.
  • Long-term causes (statelessness, restrictions) made the group vulnerable; the 2017 operations were the trigger, so people fled at once.
  • On 31 October 2026, Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees.
Mission 3

Environmental push

  • Sudden onset: Pakistan's 2022 floods affected 33 million people, killed more than 1,730 and displaced more than 8 million. Those displaced within Pakistan are IDPs, not refugees.
  • Slow onset: drought, salinity and sea-level rise push people gradually; such moves often look like economic migration.
Mission check

?In 2022 the number of refugees under UNHCR's mandate rose by about 8 million in one year. Which explanation and consequence fit best?

People flee at once when a trigger (here, the 2017 "clearance operations") hits a group already made vulnerable (statelessness). Where they arrive, camps grow fast: Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in 2026, with long-term pressure on land, jobs, services and politics.

Mission 4: From 1 January 2021, EU citizens needed points to get a UK work visa. By the year to March 2023, UK net migration had peaked at 944,000. How far can governments control global interactions?

Make a guess: From 1 January 2021, EU citizens needed points to get a UK work visa. By the year to March 2023, UK net migration had peaked at 944,000. How far can governments control global interactions?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

Mission 4

Immigration and emigration policies

  • Immigration policy: rules on who may enter, work, study and settle.
  • Emigration policy: rules on citizens leaving: exit controls, recruitment rules, protection of workers abroad, links with the diaspora.
  • Policies can be open, selective (by skill, salary, nationality) or restrictive, and they change with labour demand and politics.
Mission 4

UK change 1: a points-based system, 2021

  • After Brexit, free movement with the EU ended on 1 January 2021, and a points-based system began.
  • From then, "EU and non-EU citizens will be treated equally".
  • Workers needed 70 points: a job offer from an approved sponsor at the required skill level, English, and a salary threshold (generally £25,600).
  • The skills threshold fell "from RQF6 to RQF3" (degree level to A-level equivalent); there was no general low-skilled route.
  • The stated aim: "reduce overall migration numbers".
Quick check
?Who gained easier access to UK work visas under the 2021 rules?
Mission 4

UK change 2: tightening, 2023–24

  • In December 2023 the government announced it would raise the earnings threshold for overseas workers from £26,200 to £38,700 from spring 2024.
  • It also stopped overseas care workers bringing dependants.
Quick check
?The threshold rose from £26,200 to £38,700. What was the percentage rise, to 1 decimal place?
Mission 4

What happened to the flows

  • UK net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023, after the 2021 change.
  • It fell to 171,000 in the year to December 2025 (provisional).
  • In that year 77% of immigrants were non-EU+ nationals. ONS defines the term: "EU+ is all current EU countries, plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Switzerland".
  • Of non-EU+ immigrants, 47% came to study, 23% to work and 14% were asylum seekers; non-EU+ work arrivals fell by 47% in 2025.
  • The fall followed the tighter rules; the figures are provisional and other causes may contribute.
Mission 4

An emigration policy: the Philippines

  • Republic Act 11641, approved on 30 December 2021, created the Department of Migrant Workers, merging the Philippine Overseas Employment Administration with other agencies.
  • The department protects the rights and welfare of Overseas Filipino Workers and regulates their recruitment, deployment and reintegration.
  • The Act says the State "does not promote overseas employment as a means to economic growth and national development" and wants it to be a choice, not a necessity.
  • Yet remittances to the Philippines were US$40.3 billion in 2024.
Mission 4

Why policies change, and how far they work

  • Labour demand: shortages (care, health, farming) push governments to open routes.
  • Politics: pledges to cut numbers push them to close routes.
  • Development: source countries depend on remittances, so they manage and protect emigration rather than ban it.
  • Exam move: separate a policy's stated aim from its measured outcome, and give dates.
Quick check
?What kind of policy change is the Philippines' 2021 Act?
Mission check

?Country X raises the salary threshold for work visas by half. The next year its net migration falls sharply. Which conclusion is best supported?

Governments control global interactions partly. The UK's 2021 rules changed who could come, yet net migration still peaked at 944,000; tighter rules followed and it fell to 171,000 (2025, provisional), with non-EU+ work arrivals down 47% in 2025. The Philippines manages and protects emigration rather than stopping it, because remittances matter.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.2-2.b case study of one EDC (India): immigration and emigration patterns, change over time, interdependence, impacts on development
Mission 5

What is a diaspora?

  • A diaspora is a population living outside its country or region of origin that keeps links to it, including descendants born abroad.
  • Diasporas are networks: people, money, ideas and goods flow both ways between origin and destinations.
  • India is an EDC (emerging and developing country): it both sends and receives migrants.
Mission 5

The world's largest diaspora

  • UN estimate, 2024: 18.5 million people born in India lived in other countries, the most of any country of origin. Next: China 11.7 million, Mexico 11.6 million.
  • India's emigrant stock was 6.6 million in 1990.
  • These are migrant stocks: people counted where they live, by country of birth.
  • India's Ministry of External Affairs counts 37.3 million "Overseas Indians" (January 2026), because it adds Persons of Indian Origin born abroad.
Mission 5

Where the India-born live

  • UN, 2024: United Arab Emirates 3.25 million · USA 3.17 million · Saudi Arabia 1.95 million · Kuwait 1.23 million · UK 1.04 million · Canada 1.02 million.
  • Western Asia (including the Gulf states): 8.33 million.
  • Two streams: contract work in the Gulf, and longer-term settlement in North America, Europe and Australia.
Quick check
?In 2024, 8,327,502 of the 18,533,845 India-born emigrants lived in Western Asia. What share is that?
Mission 5

Changing the origin: money home

  • Personal remittances to India were US$150.7 billion in 2025: 3.8% of GDP.
  • Kerala, in south-west India, had about 2.2 million emigrants in 2023 and received ₹2.16 lakh crore (₹2.16 trillion) in remittances; the UAE has the most emigrants from Kerala.
  • Remittances build houses and pay for schooling, but tie household incomes to jobs abroad.
Mission 5

Changing a destination: Leicester

  • Leicester has the highest share of India-born residents of any local authority in England and Wales: 16.2% (59,842 people) in 2021.
  • Gujarati was the main language of 12.6% of residents aged 3 and over.
  • Some arrived via East Africa: after Uganda ordered the expulsion of all Asians on 4 August 1972, just over 28,000 people came to the UK.
  • Leicester's Diwali is described by the University of Leicester as "one of the largest Diwali celebrations outside of India".
Mission 5

Southall: places of worship

  • In three Southall wards (Ealing), 31.7% of residents were Sikh in 2021; in England as a whole, 0.9% (our calculation).
  • Southall Green: Sikh 36.8%, Muslim 19.6%, Christian 24.2%, Hindu 11.6%. A mixed, not single-faith, place.
  • Sri Guru Singh Sabha Southall says it "welcomes over 50,000 people each week"; its langar (free vegetarian meal) is open to everyone "whatever their faith or background".
Mission 5

India also receives migrants

  • UN, 2024: 4.80 million international migrants lived in India, down from 7.21 million in 1990.
  • Main origins in 2024: Bangladesh 2.29 million, Pakistan 0.77 million, Nepal 0.66 million, Sri Lanka 0.25 million.
  • So India is a net sender: 18.5 million India-born abroad against 4.8 million migrants in India.
  • India's emigrant stock grew 2.8 times between 1990 and 2024 (6.58 million to 18.53 million) while its immigrant stock fell by a third (our calculation).
Mission 5

Corridors and interdependence

  • Economic: the Gulf needs contract labour; Indian households need wages (remittances, above).
  • Social: Indian was the most common non-EU+ nationality to immigrate long-term to the UK in 2025, most commonly for study.
  • Political: on India's proposal the UN proclaimed 21 June the International Day of Yoga (resolution 69/131, 2014).
  • Regional: most migrants in India come from neighbouring South Asian states: a South–South corridor.
Quick check
?India's immigrant stock fell from 7,212,791 (1990) to 4,796,255 (2024). What was the percentage change?
Mission 5

Impacts on India's development

  • Economic: remittances of US$150.7 billion (3.8% of GDP, 2025); in Kerala, ₹2.16 lakh crore in 2023.
  • Social equality: gains are concentrated in emigrant households and states such as Kerala, so migration can widen gaps between regions and households.
  • Costs: families separated by contract work; skilled emigrants leaving (brain drain), offset in part by money, skills and links that return.
  • Political: the state courts its diaspora; the Ministry of External Affairs publishes its own count of 37.3 million Overseas Indians.
Mission 5

Identity and hybridity

  • Many cultures, not one: Gujarati in Leicester; Panjabi and Sikh institutions in Southall; Malayali migrants from Kerala in the Gulf.
  • Hybrid identities: children of migrants often hold several identities, such as British and Indian, and use two languages.
  • Exam move: name the scale (household, street, city, nation, world) and the direction of each influence (origin ↔ destination).
Quick check
?Which is an example of a diaspora changing culture at a local scale in a destination?
Mission check

A diaspora is a two-way network. Indian flows reshaped Leicester and Southall and sent US$150.7 billion home in 2025.

Mission 6

The UK in the global migration system

  • The UK is an AC (advanced country): a major destination and also a source of emigrants.
  • UN estimates: 11.85 million international migrants lived in the UK in 2024, up from 3.66 million in 1990.
  • 4.80 million people born in the UK lived abroad in 2024; the largest groups were in Australia (1.11 million), the USA (0.89 million) and Canada (0.43 million).
  • Net migration = immigration − emigration.
Mission 6

Patterns: in and out, 2025

  • Year ending December 2025 (provisional): immigration 813,000, emigration 642,000.
  • Immigration peaked at 1,469,000 in the year ending March 2023.
  • Net migration fell from a peak of 944,000 (YE March 2023) to 331,000 (YE December 2024) and 171,000 (YE December 2025).
Quick check
?In YE December 2025, immigration was 813,000 and emigration 642,000. What was net migration?
Mission 6

Who comes, who goes, and why

  • ONS sorts migrants into British, EU+ and non-EU+ nationals: "EU+ is all current EU countries, plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein, and Switzerland".
  • Net migration by nationality, 2025: non-EU+ +350,000; EU+ −42,000; British −136,000.
  • Reasons for non-EU+ immigration: 47% study, 23% work, 14% asylum, 7% family, 6% humanitarian.
  • Indian was the most common non-EU+ nationality to immigrate long-term, most commonly for study.
  • 246,000 British nationals left in 2025: the UK is a source as well as a destination.
Mission 6

Policies: the government as gatekeeper

  • 1962: the Commonwealth Immigrants Act first controlled Commonwealth immigration; work entry needed a voucher.
  • To 2021: EU free movement; it ended in January 2021.
  • 2021: a points-based system treated EU and non-EU citizens equally and aimed to "reduce overall levels of migration".
  • 2024–25: rules stopped most students and care workers bringing family members, raised the Skilled Worker salary requirement, and closed the Health and Care route to overseas care workers.
Mission 6

Did the policies work?

  • Net migration rose after 2021, to 944,000 in YE March 2023, before falling.
  • In 2025, work-related arrivals of non-EU+ nationals fell 47%, "the main reason for the continued fall in net migration".
  • Policy is not the only driver: conflict, demand from employers and universities, and conditions in origin countries also matter.
Quick check
?What does ONS give as the main reason for the fall in net migration in 2025?
Mission 6

Opportunities: labour supply

  • Around 146,000 people arrived for work-related reasons in 2025 (74,000 dependants, 71,000 main applicants), down 47% from 272,000 in 2024.
  • Migrants fill gaps in sectors such as health and social care; the Health and Care route existed to recruit care workers until it closed.
  • Study migration brings fee income to universities and graduates to the labour market.
  • Opportunity for the AC: labour and skills it has not paid to train.
Mission 6

Interdependence

  • Money: personal remittances sent from the UK were US$13.2 billion in 2025.
  • People: 1.04 million India-born people lived in the UK in 2024, and Indian nationals led non-EU+ immigration in 2025.
  • Two-way: UK-born emigrants (4.80 million) link the UK to Australia, the USA, Canada, Spain and Ireland.
  • An AC's rules change flows in other countries: a UK rule on students' dependants alters decisions in families in India or Nigeria.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of interdependence between the UK and countries linked to it by migration?
Mission 6

Challenges: border issues

  • People detected crossing the English Channel in small boats: 299 in 2018, 45,755 in 2022, 41,472 in 2025 (Home Office operational data).
  • 88,000 people immigrated to claim asylum in 2025, including people who arrived by illegal entry routes such as small boats.
  • The Illegal Migration Act 2023 states its purpose as "to prevent and deter unlawful migration, and in particular migration by unsafe and illegal routes".
  • Controlling a sea border depends on cooperation with other states: an AC's power has limits.
Mission 6

Exam move: how an AC drives the system

  • Power: visa rules set who may come, from where and with which family members.
  • Limits: emigration, EU+ departures and irregular arrivals are not set by visa rules.
  • Judgement: an AC drives the system mainly through regular routes; its control of irregular flows and of its own citizens' departures is weaker.
Mission check

?In YE December 2025, net migration of British nationals was −136,000 and of EU+ nationals −42,000. Who decides these parts of the total?

The government decides a lot: its 2024–25 rules cut work-related arrivals by 47%. But it does not decide everything: British and EU+ departures, asylum and irregular arrivals depend on people's choices and on other states. An AC drives the system through its rules, within limits.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.2-3.a case study of one LIDC (Nepal) with limited influence over, and restricted response to, the global migration system: patterns of emigration and immigration, migration policies, interdependence with linked countries; opportunities (remittances); challenges (loss of skilled workers)
Mission 7

Nepal: an LIDC in the migration system

  • OCR defines an LIDC as a country eligible for the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust.
  • Nepal: medium human development, HDI rank 145 (2023).
  • Landlocked between India and China; a mainly source country of migrants.
  • Key idea: an LIDC responds to the system; it does not drive it.
Mission 7

Patterns: more leave than arrive

  • Net migration rate, 2024: −13.5 per 1,000 people (UN estimate).
  • Negative = emigration exceeds immigration.
  • Sex ratio, 2024: 92 males per 100 females: men are missing from the home population.
Mission 7

Patterns: where the work is

  • Gulf labour markets are male-dominated: Qatar 248 and UAE 177 males per 100 females (2024).
  • Newer, more skilled routes: Nepal-born people in Australia rose from 50,150 (2015) to 213,580 (2025, preliminary).
  • Nepal ranked 8th among Australia's countries of birth in 2025.
  • Two streams: low-wage contract work (Gulf) and study and skilled work (Australia, UK).
Quick check
?Nepal had 92 males per 100 females in 2024; Qatar had 248. What do these figures best suggest?
Mission 7

Opportunity: remittances

  • Remittances: money migrants send home.
  • Nepal received US$6.73 billion in 2015 and US$14.70 billion in 2025: 2.2 times as much (our calculation).
  • As a share of GDP: 27.6% (2015), 22.3% (2021), 26.0% (2024).
  • Uses: food, school fees, health care, housing, small businesses.
Quick check
?Remittances to Nepal were US$6.73 billion in 2015 and US$14.70 billion in 2025. By about how much did they grow?
Mission 7

Policies: rules set elsewhere

  • Destination states decide who may enter, for which jobs, and for how long.
  • Example, UK health and care recruitment code: "There must be no active international recruitment from countries on the red list".
  • Countries on the WHO health workforce support and safeguards list are graded red.
  • Nepal is graded amber: recruitment "can happen only on the terms of the agreement" between the two governments.
Mission 7

Why Nepal's influence is limited

  • Economic: households depend on wages earned abroad (26.0% of GDP); a sudden stop would hurt.
  • Political: Nepal can negotiate government-to-government agreements, but the destination writes the visa rules.
  • Social: the migrant's choice of route depends on fees, contacts and skills, not on Nepal's wishes.
  • Nepal responds (agreements, protection of workers abroad); the destination decides.
Mission 7

Challenge: loss of skilled workers

  • Brain drain: emigration of trained people, such as nurses and graduates.
  • The WHO list names countries that "face the most pressing health workforce challenges"; the UK code bars active recruitment from them.
  • Australia's Nepal-born population grew by 163,000 from 2015 to 2025; its recent arrivals had a median age of 30: young adults, often students and skilled workers.
  • The cost of training stays in Nepal; the skills are used abroad.
Quick check
?Why does the UK code allow recruitment of health workers from Nepal only under a government-to-government agreement?
Mission 7

Exam move: judging an LIDC's influence

  • Opportunity: remittances are large and rising (US$6.73 billion in 2015 → US$14.70 billion in 2025) and go straight to households.
  • Challenge: loss of young, skilled people; men absent from families and farms.
  • Influence: limited. Rules on entry, recruitment and skills are written by destination governments; Nepal negotiates within them.
  • Judgement: migration brings Nepal money but not control.
Mission check

?A small LIDC receives remittances worth a quarter of its GDP. Most of its migrants work in two countries. One of them cuts work visas by half. Which outcome is most likely?

For Nepal, money arrives from people who left, but the rules are set by destination governments, as the UK's amber grading shows. That is what OCR means by limited influence and restricted response.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • 304 million international migrants (3.7%, 2024); 48% female; one in six forcibly displaced; most move voluntarily for work, family or study.
  • 45% stay in their region; corridors such as India–UAE; labour migration to the Gulf is young and male; less developed regions host 136 million migrants.

Mission 2

  • Remittances flow mainly from very high HDI hosts to medium- and high-HDI source countries; small economies are the most dependent.
  • Migration promotes stability and development through money, people, ideas and technology, but brings costs, dependence, brain drain, exploitation and political conflict.

Mission 3

  • Forced migration (refugees, asylum seekers, IDPs) and voluntary economic migration have political, environmental and socio-economic causes; conflict and persecution have raised refugee numbers since 2015.

Mission 4

  • Immigration and emigration policies change with labour demand, politics and development needs: the UK opened, then tightened; the Philippines moved to regulate and protect its workers abroad.
  • Policies shape who moves more than whether people move.

Mission 5

  • Diasporas change places at both ends: remittances and returning ideas at home; language, faith, food and festivals in destinations.
  • India is a net sender: emigrants up 2.8 times since 1990, immigrants (mainly from neighbouring states) down by a third.

Mission 6

  • The UK is both a destination (11.85 million migrants, 2024) and a source (4.80 million UK-born abroad); net migration fell from 944,000 (2023) to 171,000 (2025).
  • Policy is the main lever (1962 vouchers → free movement → points-based system → 2024–25 rules), bringing opportunities (labour supply, fees) and challenges (border issues), with interdependence through people and remittances.

Mission 7

  • Nepal (LIDC) loses more people than it gains (−13.5 per 1,000, 2024) and receives remittances worth about a quarter of GDP (26.0%, 2024; US$14.70 billion, 2025).
  • Destination states set the rules (e.g. the UK amber grading); Nepal responds with agreements while losing young and skilled workers.
