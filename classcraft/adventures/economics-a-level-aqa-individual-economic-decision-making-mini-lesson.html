Mission 1

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
  • Governments aim to maximise social welfare (the well-being of society as a whole).
  • Incentives steer choices: a change in price or reward changes the costs and benefits agents weigh.
Mission 1

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.
  • Slices of pizza at one meal (illustrative data, in "utils"):

  • Law of diminishing marginal utility: as more units are consumed, each extra unit adds less utility.
  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

Mission 1

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".
Quick check

Decide at the margin: MB = MC

  • Marginal benefit (MB): extra benefit from one more unit of an activity. Marginal cost (MC): extra cost of that unit.
  • Rule: do one more unit while MB ≥ MC. Stop where MB = MC.

Mission 1

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • People also act from altruism and a sense of fairness, not only self-interest.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: Mission 3.
Mission check

What we pay depends on how much one more unit adds, not on how much the good matters in total.

Mission 2

What markets need to know

  • Markets allocate resources well when buyers and sellers know the prices, the quality and the consequences of what they trade.
  • Imperfect information: people lack some of this knowledge, so they make choices they would not make if fully informed.
  • Information failure: missing or uneven information leads to a misallocation of resources: a market failure.
Mission 2

Asymmetric information

  • Asymmetric information: one party to a deal knows more than the other.
  • The seller knows more:
    • a used car's history
    • whether a repair was needed
    • the risks of a financial product
  • The buyer knows more:
    • an insurance customer knows their own health or driving
    • a borrower knows how likely they are to repay
  • The better-informed side can gain at the other's expense, so the other side trades less, or not at all.
  • Say: "asymmetric information" — not: "imperfect information", when one side knows more than the other.

Mission 2

Hidden quality: the used-car problem

  • Used cars are either good or poor ("lemons"). The seller knows which. The buyer cannot tell.
  • So buyers will pay only what an average car is worth to them.
  • Owners of good cars get less than their car is worth, so many keep them.
  • Fewer good cars on sale → the average falls → buyers offer even less.
  • The market can shrink until mostly poor cars are traded. Good cars that both sides would gladly trade are not sold.
  • Idea from George Akerlof, "The Market for Lemons" (1970).
Mission 2

Worked example: what will a buyer pay?

  • Illustrative data.
Worth to a buyerLowest price its owner accepts
Good car10,0008,000
Poor car4,0003,000
  • Half the cars are good, half are poor. A buyer cannot tell which is which.
  • Expected value to a buyer = 0.5 × 10,000 + 0.5 × 4,000 = 7,000.
  • So buyers offer at most 7,000.
  • Good-car owners want at least 8,000. 7,000 < 8,000 → they withdraw.
  • Now only poor cars are left, so buyers will pay at most 4,000.
  • Every good car is worth more to a buyer (10,000) than to its owner (8,000), yet none is sold.

Mission 2

Government responses

  • Provide information: make it public, free and easy to use.
    • Food hygiene ratings: in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, restaurants are inspected and rated from 0 to 5.
    • Nutrition labels; health campaigns on smoking.
  • Legislation and regulation: minimum standards, compulsory disclosure, consumer rights (e.g. a refund for faulty goods), bans on misleading adverts.
  • Private fixes also exist: warranties, independent inspections, online reviews.
  • Limits: information costs money to collect; people may ignore it; government may not know better than the market (government failure).
Quick check
?In England, Wales and Northern Ireland, food outlets are inspected and given a hygiene rating from 0 to 5. In England, displaying the rating at the door is voluntary. Which is the strongest reason this may not fully close the information gap in England?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should government close information gaps?

It depends on:

  • The cost of information: inspections and labels cost money; is the gain worth it?
  • Whether people use it: information is ignored if it is complex or people are biased (smokers already see warnings).
  • Whether government knows more: regulators may lack information too (government failure).
  • Private fixes: warranties, reviews and inspections may already close much of the gap.
Mission check

?Online, buyers of second-hand laptops cannot check whether a laptop's battery is healthy. Sellers know. Nothing else changes. Which outcome is most likely?

When only sellers know quality, the best products are the first to leave the market.

Mission 3

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 3

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 3

Weak at sums

  • Many shoppers are weak at computation: working out unit prices, interest or the value of an offer.
  • Example: "Buy one, get one half price" on two items costing 4 each.
    • Pay 4 + 2 = 6 for goods worth 8.
    • Discount = 2 ÷ 8 = 25%, not 50%.
  • Sellers can frame offers so they look bigger than they are.
Mission 3

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
  • Habits: repeated choices made with little thought, e.g. the same coffee every morning. A price rise may barely change behaviour in the short run.
Mission 3

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Mission 3

Availability

  • Availability bias: people judge how likely an event is by how easily examples come to mind.
  • Vivid, recent or heavily reported events seem more likely than they are.
  • Example: after a widely reported plane crash, some travellers switch to driving, which is riskier per kilometre travelled.
  • Insurance demand often rises just after a disaster nearby, then fades.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 3

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 3

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 3

Bounded selfishness: altruism and fairness

  • Bounded selfishness: people care about others, not only themselves.
  • Altruism: giving to charity, donating blood, tipping in a café you will never revisit.
  • Fairness: the ultimatum game.
    • Player 1 splits a sum of money; Player 2 accepts (both get paid) or rejects (both get nothing).
    • A purely self-interested Player 2 accepts any amount above zero.
    • In experiments, offers are typically 40–50% of the sum, and offers under 20% are often rejected.
  • Rejecting money to punish unfairness is not self-interested in the narrow sense.
Quick check
?In an ultimatum game, Player 1 splits 100 and offers Player 2 just 5. Player 2 rejects, so both get nothing. What does the standard rational, self-interested model predict, and what does the rejection suggest?
Mission 3Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Policy uses of these biases: Mission 4.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?
Mission check

?Many workers say they should save more for retirement but do not start. In one scheme, workers sign up now to put part of each future pay rise into their pension; nothing leaves today's pay. Many more workers saved more under the scheme. Which bias does the design best get round?

People who mean well today often fail tomorrow; designs that commit the future self can close the gap.

Mission 4

Choice architecture

  • Choice architecture: the way choices are presented to people.
  • Order, layout, wording and the option already selected all affect what people pick.
  • There is no neutral design: a canteen must put something at eye level.
  • Firms use it to sell. Governments can use it to improve choices people say they want to make.
  • Builds on: bounded rationality, inertia, present bias.
Mission 4

Framing

  • Framing: presenting the same information in different ways to influence a choice.
  • "Surgery has a 90% survival rate" vs "a 10% death rate": same fact, but more people choose surgery under the first frame.
  • Loss frames often bite harder than gain frames: "You are losing 300 a year by not insulating" vs "You could save 300 a year".
Mission 4

Nudges

  • Nudge (Thaler and Sunstein, 2008): a change in choice architecture that alters behaviour predictably without banning any option or significantly changing financial incentives.
  • A nudge must be easy and cheap to avoid.
  • Examples: fruit at eye level in a canteen; a letter saying "most people in your area pay their tax on time"; a text reminder before a doctor's appointment.
  • Say: "a nudge keeps every option open" — not: "a nudge is a small tax or a ban".
Mission 4

Default choice

  • Default choice: the option that applies if a person does nothing.
  • People tend to stick with defaults: inertia, effort, and a sense that the default is recommended.
  • Organ donation (Johnson and Goldstein, 2003): effective consent rates.
    • Germany (opt in: you must sign up): 12%.
    • Austria (opt out: you are a donor unless you refuse): 99.98%.
  • UK pensions: since October 2012, employers must automatically enrol eligible workers; they can opt out.
    • Eligible private-sector employees in a workplace pension: 42% in 2012, 85% in 2018.
Mission 4

Worked example: percentage points or per cent?

  • Pension participation rose from 42% to 85%.
  • Change in percentage points: 85 − 42 = 43 percentage points.
  • Percentage change: (85 − 42) ÷ 42 × 100 = 102% (participation roughly doubled).
  • Say: "rose by 43 percentage points" — not: "rose by 43%".
Quick check
?A school canteen moves fruit from the back counter to beside the till. The share of pupils buying fruit rises from 20% to 30% (illustrative data). How large is the rise?
Mission 4

Restricted and mandated choice

  • Restricted choice: the number of options is limited, so people are not overwhelmed and poor options are removed.
    • Example: a pension scheme that offers a handful of well-run funds rather than hundreds.
  • Mandated choice: people must make an active decision; there is no default.
    • Example: an application for a driving licence that cannot be completed until you answer "Do you want to be an organ donor? Yes / No".
  • Mandated choice avoids presuming what people want, but still beats inertia.
Quick check

  • Taxes and bans are stronger tools where harm to others is large or behaviour is hard to shift.
Mission 4Evaluate

How much should we rely on nudges?

  • It depends on the size of effects at scale: in trials by government nudge units, the average effect was about 1.4 percentage points, against about 8.7 in published academic studies (DellaVigna and Linos, 2022).
  • It depends on the default chosen: auto-enrolled workers often stay at the minimum contribution, which may be too low for a comfortable retirement.
  • It depends on who designs the choice: governments can misjudge what is good for people (government failure), and firms use the same tools for profit.
  • Ethics: supporters call it "libertarian paternalism" (guiding while keeping choice). Critics call some nudges manipulation, especially if hidden.
  • Nudges often work best alongside taxes, regulation and information.
Quick check
?A government wants to cut childhood obesity and is choosing between a nudge (healthy food placed at eye level in shops) and a tax on high-sugar foods. Which is the strongest argument for preferring the nudge?
Mission check

?An energy company makes its renewable-electricity tariff the pre-selected option on its sign-up page. Customers can switch to the standard tariff with one click, and neither price changes. Far more new customers now end up on the renewable tariff. Which explanation fits best?

Choices are never presented neutrally; the default often decides what busy people end up with.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.

Mission 2

  • Information failure: missing or uneven information leads to a misallocation of resources.
  • Asymmetric information: one side knows more. Hidden quality can drive good products out of a market.
  • Governments respond with information provision and regulation.

Mission 3

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.
  • Weakness at computation and habits shape real spending.

Mission 4

  • Choice architecture shapes decisions; framing, defaults, restricted and mandated choices are its tools.
  • A nudge changes behaviour without banning options or significantly changing incentives.
  • Nudges are cheap and preserve choice, but effects at scale can be small and the designer can get it wrong.
