Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do whole economies swing between good years and bad years?

Make a guess: Why do whole economies swing between good years and bad years?

Mission 1

Trend growth and the cycle

  • Over many years, most economies grow: real GDP rises.
  • The trend rate of growth: the average rate of growth over a long period.
  • But real GDP does not rise smoothly. Some years it grows fast; some years it falls.
  • The economic cycle (business cycle): the ups and downs of real GDP around its trend.
  • Say: "real GDP growth slowed" when output still rose, more slowly — not: "output fell".
Mission 1

The economic cycle diagram

  • Real GDP on the vertical axis; time on the horizontal axis.
  • A straight line shows the trend. A wavy line shows actual real GDP.
  • Boom: output rises fast, above trend, to a peak.
  • Downturn: growth slows after the peak; output may start to fall.
  • Recession: real GDP falls, down to the lowest point (the trough).
  • Recovery: output starts to rise again, back towards trend.
  • Then the cycle repeats. No two cycles are the same length or size.

Mission 1

What counts as a recession

  • A recession: real GDP falls for two quarters in a row (a quarter = 3 months).
  • A slowdown is different: growth falls, but stays above zero. Output still rises.
  • A long, deep recession is sometimes called a depression.
  • Say: "real GDP fell for two quarters in a row" — not: "growth slowed", for a recession.

Mission 1

Why economies swing

  • Spending changes: households and firms spend more when they feel confident, less when they are worried.
  • Interest rates: higher rates make borrowing dearer, so spending falls; lower rates do the opposite.
  • Investment swings: firms buy machines and buildings in good times and stop in bad times.
  • Shocks from outside: a jump in oil prices, a pandemic, a financial crisis, or a recession in a country that buys our exports.
  • One change spreads: lower spending → firms sell less → they cut jobs → workers have less to spend → spending falls again.
  • The same chain works upwards in a recovery.
Mission 1

Real recessions

  • UK, 2008–09: after a banking crisis, the economy shrank by more than 6% between early 2008 and mid-2009. It shrank for five quarters in a row, and took five years to get back to its pre-recession size (ONS).
  • United States, 2020: the COVID-19 pandemic caused a recession that lasted just two months, from February to April 2020: the shortest US recession on record (NBER).
    • US real GDP did fall for two quarters in a row. The US committee that dates recessions counts in months, from the peak to the lowest point, so the two tests give different lengths.
  • Recessions differ in cause, depth and length.
Mission 1

Impacts of the cycle

  • In a recession:
    • households: more unemployment, lower incomes, worry about jobs
    • firms: lower sales and profits; some close; less investment
    • government: less tax revenue, more spent on unemployment benefits, so a bigger budget deficit
    • prices: inflation usually falls
  • In a boom:
    • more jobs and higher incomes; more tax revenue
    • but shortages of workers and materials, so inflation rises
    • more imports, as people buy more goods from abroad
  • Some firms do well in recessions: e.g. shops selling cheaper goods.

Mission 2

What inflation is

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level: prices on average, not one price.
  • Rate of inflation: the % rise in the general price level over a year.
  • Inflation means each unit of money buys less: its purchasing power falls.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level (inflation below 0%).
  • Disinflation: inflation falls, but stays above 0%. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 6% to 3%, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 2

Measuring inflation: the CPI

  • CPI (Consumer Prices Index): measures the average change in the prices of a basket of goods and services bought by households.
  • The basket holds hundreds of items: food, clothes, rent, energy, transport, phones, haircuts.
  • Each item is weighted by how much households spend on it. Fuel matters more than matches.
  • Prices are collected every month. The index starts at 100 in a base year.
  • Inflation rate = % change in the CPI over 12 months.

Mission 2

Worked example: inflation and prices

  • Illustrative data. CPI last year = 120; CPI this year = 126.
  • Inflation rate = (126 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = 5%.
  • Effect on a price: a meal costing 40 last year costs about 40 × 1.05 = 42 this year, if its price rose in line with inflation.
  • Say: "the CPI rose by 6 points, which is 5%" — not: "inflation was 6%".

?Illustrative data. A country's CPI rises from 150 to 156 over a year. What is the rate of inflation?
Mission 2

Real and nominal

  • Nominal value: measured in money, not adjusted for inflation.
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation; what the money can actually buy.
  • Real change ≈ nominal change − inflation.
  • Illustrative data:
    • Pay rises 3%; inflation is 5%. Real pay falls by about 2%: the worker can buy less.
    • Savings of 1,000 earn 2% interest: 1,020 next year. Inflation is 5%, so the 1,020 buys about 3% less than the 1,000 did.
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when the money amount rose.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 520, a rise of 4%. Inflation over the same year is 3%. What has happened to her real pay?
Mission 2

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull inflation: total spending grows faster than the economy can produce, so prices are pulled up.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • Causes:
    • consumers spend more (rising confidence, cheap borrowing, low interest rates)
    • government spends more or cuts taxes
    • foreign buyers want more of our exports
  • Most likely when firms are already near full capacity: they cannot produce much more, so they raise prices.
Mission 2

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push inflation: firms' costs rise, and they push prices up to protect their profits.
  • Causes:
    • dearer raw materials and energy (e.g. world oil and gas prices)
    • higher wages, if they rise faster than productivity
    • dearer imports, e.g. when the exchange rate falls
    • higher indirect taxes, such as VAT
  • Real case: UK CPI inflation reached 11.1% in October 2022, with energy and food prices the biggest contributors (ONS).
Quick check
?Which of these is most likely to cause demand-pull inflation?
Mission 2

Who gains and who loses?

  • Consumers: if prices rise faster than incomes, they can buy less.
  • People on fixed incomes (e.g. some pensions, benefits that are not raised): lose purchasing power.
  • Savers: lose if the interest rate is below inflation; their savings buy less.
  • Borrowers: gain if the interest rate they pay is below inflation, because the real value of what they owe falls.
  • Lenders: lose for the same reason: they are repaid with money that buys less.
  • Producers: costs rise; if inflation is higher than in other countries, exports become less competitive.
  • Government: tax revenue rises as prices and pay rise, but so does the cost of pensions, benefits and public-sector pay.
Mission 2

Costs for the whole economy

  • Menu costs: firms must keep changing prices: price lists, menus, websites.
  • Shoe-leather costs: people and firms spend time and effort managing money to protect its value (e.g. moving savings around).
  • Uncertainty: when inflation is high or unpredictable, firms are less confident about future costs and prices, so they may invest less.
  • Low and stable inflation (around 2%) is seen as healthy: it is easy to plan around.
Mission 2

Too much, or too little

  • Hyperinflation: extremely high inflation. Zimbabwe's official annual inflation rate reached 231,000,000% in July 2008. In 2009, people stopped using the local currency.
  • Deflation, prices falling, can also be harmful:
    • people delay spending, hoping prices will fall further, so firms sell less and cut jobs
    • the real value of debts rises, so borrowers lose and lenders gain
    • savers gain: their money buys more
  • Example: in Japan, consumer prices fell in 9 of the 14 years from 1999 to 2012.
Mission 2

Controlling inflation

  • Interest rates (monetary policy, set by the central bank):
    • higher rates make borrowing dearer and saving more rewarding
    • so households and firms spend less, and demand-pull inflation falls
    • but spending, output and jobs may fall too
  • Fiscal policy: higher taxes or lower government spending reduce total spending.
  • Supply-side policies: training, competition and new technology raise productivity, which keeps costs down in the long run.
  • Cost-push inflation is harder to control: raising interest rates can cut output without lowering firms' costs.
Quick check
?Inflation turns out much higher than anyone expected. Who is most likely to gain?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Last year, Amira's pay rose by 4%. Her savings account paid 1% interest. Inflation was 6%. Which statement is correct?

A pay rise smaller than inflation is a cut in real pay. Money only matters for what it can buy.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Make a guess: How can one interest rate, set by a handful of people, slow down inflation across a whole country?

Mission 3

Who sets the rate?

  • Monetary policy: action by the central bank to change interest rates and the money supply, to influence spending and inflation.
  • UK: the Bank of England. Its Monetary Policy Committee (MPC), 9 members, sets Bank Rate.
  • The MPC meets about eight times a year and votes. A handful of people decide one rate for the whole country.
  • Since 1997 the Bank has been independent: the government sets the target, the MPC chooses the rate.
  • Other countries: a central bank such as the European Central Bank or the Reserve Bank of India sets its own policy interest rate.
  • Bank Rate feeds through to the rates that banks charge on loans and mortgages and pay on savings.
Mission 3

What it aims for

  • Main aim: price stability: low and stable inflation.
  • UK target: 2% CPI inflation, set by the government.
  • Many central banks aim for about 2%.
  • Other objectives: support growth and jobs, as long as inflation stays near target.
  • Why it matters: high inflation brings menu costs (changing prices), shoe-leather costs (time spent managing money), and damages confidence and investment.
Mission 3

The transmission mechanism

  • How a rise in the interest rate reaches prices:
    • Borrowing costs more: mortgages, loans, credit cards. People and firms borrow less.
    • Saving pays more: people save more and spend less.
    • Households with variable-rate mortgages have less to spend.
    • Firms invest less in new machines and buildings.
    • The exchange rate tends to rise: exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper.
  • Result: total spending grows more slowly. Firms face weaker demand, so they raise prices less: inflation slows.
  • A cut works the other way: more borrowing and spending.
  • This chain is called the transmission mechanism.
  • Say: "inflation slows" — not: "prices fall". Prices usually still rise, just more slowly.
Mission 3

Worked example: what a rate rise does to a family

  • Illustrative data. The interest rate on loans and savings rises by 3 percentage points.
  • A loan of 10,000:
    • at 2% a year: interest = 10,000 × 0.02 = 200
    • at 5% a year: interest = 10,000 × 0.05 = 500
    • 300 more a year to the lender, so 300 less to spend elsewhere
  • Savings of 5,000:
    • at 1%: interest = 50
    • at 4%: interest = 200
    • saving is more rewarding, so spending now is less attractive
  • Borrowers spend less; savers have a reason to save more. Total spending slows.

Mission 3

Other tools

  • Asset purchases: the central bank creates new money to buy government bonds and other assets. This lowers longer-term interest rates and adds money to the economy.
  • The Bank of England bought £895 billion of bonds in this way between 2009 and 2020.
Mission 3

Real case: the rate rises of 2021–23

  • Inflation rose sharply around the world. It peaked in October 2022:
    • UK CPI inflation: 11.1%
    • euro area: 10.6%
  • Central banks raised interest rates again and again.
    • UK: Bank Rate was 0.1% (March 2020). The MPC raised it from December 2021 to 5.25% (August 2023).
    • euro area: the European Central Bank raised its deposit rate from −0.5% (2022) to 4.0% (September 2023).
  • UK inflation was back at 2.0% in May 2024.
  • Economists debate how much of the fall came from higher rates and how much from falling energy prices.
Mission 3

Strengths and limits

  • Strengths:
    • flexible: rates can change in small steps, several times a year
    • quick to decide: no new law needed
    • an independent central bank is trusted to keep inflation low
  • Limits:
    • time lags: up to about two years for the full effect on inflation
    • if inflation comes from rising costs (e.g. oil prices), higher rates cut inflation only by cutting spending, output and jobs
    • when confidence is low, people may not borrow even if rates are low
    • one rate for everyone: borrowers lose, savers gain
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A household has a variable-rate mortgage of 200,000 and savings of 10,000. The interest rate on both rises by 2 percentage points. By how much does its yearly interest position change?
Mission 3

Weigh it up: how well do interest rates work?

It depends on:

  • The cause of inflation: too much spending responds well; a jump in costs forces a choice between inflation and jobs.
  • Time: a change made now works over the next two years, when things may have changed.
  • Debt: if many people have loans at variable rates, a rise bites quickly.
  • Confidence: when people are worried, cheap loans may not get them spending.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Growth in a country has slowed and unemployment is rising. Inflation is 1.5%, below the 2% target. The central bank cuts its interest rate from 4% to 2%. What is most likely over the next year or two?

Interest rates work by changing how much people and firms borrow and spend, and that takes time.

Mission 4: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 4

Who counts as unemployed?

  • Unemployed (the ILO measure): without a job, have looked for work in the last 4 weeks, and can start within 2 weeks.
  • Employed: did at least one hour of paid work in the week.
  • Labour force (economically active) = employed + unemployed.
  • Economically inactive: not working and not looking, e.g. students, retired people, full-time carers. They are not unemployed.
  • In the UK the survey is the Labour Force Survey (ONS).
Mission 4

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Unemployment rate = number unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 32.4 m, unemployed 1.6 m, inactive 16 m.
  • Labour force = 32.4 + 1.6 = 34 m.
  • Unemployment rate = 1.6 ÷ 34 × 100 = 4.7%.
  • Divide by the labour force, not the whole population: 1.6 ÷ 50 = 3.2% would be wrong.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country has 48 million people of working age. 28.8 million are employed, 1.2 million are unemployed, and the rest are economically inactive. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 4

Types of unemployment

  • Frictional: people between jobs, searching for the right one. Usually short.
  • Seasonal: jobs that disappear at certain times of year: farm work, beach and ski resorts, building in winter.
  • Structural: a mismatch between people's skills or location and the jobs on offer, because some industries decline while others grow. Often long-lasting and concentrated in particular regions.
    • Technological unemployment, where machines or software replace a skill, is a type of structural unemployment.
  • Cyclical: too little spending in the economy, in a downturn or recession. Firms sell less, so they need fewer workers.
  • Voluntary: people who choose not to take the jobs on offer at the going wage, e.g. while waiting for a better offer.
Mission 4

Never zero

  • Even in a boom, some people are always between jobs (frictional) and some have the wrong skills or live in the wrong place (structural).
  • So full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • In a recession, cyclical unemployment is added on top, and the rate rises fast:
    • US unemployment rose from 5.0% in December 2007 to 10.0% in October 2009 (BLS).
    • UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS).
  • When spending recovers, cyclical unemployment falls. Frictional and structural unemployment remain.
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 4

Causes of unemployment

  • Too little spending (recession): cyclical.
  • Changes in what is produced: industries such as coal mining or traditional manufacturing decline: structural.
  • New technology replaces some jobs: structural.
  • Workers can't easily retrain or move: lack of skills, high housing costs, family ties: structural.
  • Time to find a match: frictional.
  • Time of year: seasonal.
  • Unemployment is often regional: when a big employer closes, a whole town can be hit.
Mission 4

The costs of unemployment

  • For people (individuals and families): lost income; skills fade the longer they are out of work; stress and worse health.
  • For communities (regions): shops and services close when local incomes fall; young people move away; crime and social problems can rise.
  • For the whole economy:
    • lost output: goods and services that could have been produced are lost for ever; the economy is inside its PPC
    • for the government: less income tax and sales taxes such as VAT, more spent on benefits, so more borrowing
    • for firms: fewer customers, though it is easier to recruit workers
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?Unemployment in a country rises from 4% to 7%. Which is the most likely effect on the government's budget?
Mission 4Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: a short search between jobs does little harm; long spells cut skills and income.
  • Who and where: job losses packed into one town or group hit harder than the same rise spread thinly.
  • The response: benefits, retraining and quick action to raise spending reduce the lasting damage.
Quick check
?Who is counted as unemployed on the ILO measure?
Mission check

?During a boom, a region has thousands of job vacancies for software developers and nurses. Yet 6% of its labour force is unemployed, most of them former factory workers whose factories closed. What kind of unemployment is this mainly, and what would help most?

Extra spending cures cyclical unemployment. Mismatches of skills or place, and time spent between jobs, remain even in a boom.

Mission 5: Nobody sets the price of a dollar or a euro, so what decides it from one day to the next?

Make a guess: Nobody sets the price of a dollar or a euro, so what decides it from one day to the next?

Mission 5

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example (illustrative): €1 = $1.25.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency. It is stronger.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency. It is weaker.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system the government sets the rate. Raising it is a revaluation; cutting it is a devaluation.
Mission 5

Worked example: converting currencies

Illustrative data.

  • Rule (rate quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency): home → foreign, multiply; foreign → home, divide.
  • Rate €1 = $1.30.
  • A €500 phone made in Europe sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans made in the US cost a European buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = €50.
  • The euro appreciates to $1.50. Phone: $750. Jeans: €43.33.
  • A stronger currency: exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper at home.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The exchange rate is 1 pound = 1.20 US dollars. A US-made guitar costs 600 dollars. What does it cost a buyer in the UK, ignoring other costs?
Mission 5

Real data: the euro, 2021 to 2022

  • The euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • No government announced it, and no committee voted for it: the euro floats.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate, but only $105.34 at the 2022 average.
Mission 5

Who buys and sells a currency?

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Traders, firms, tourists, investors and central banks all buy and sell.
  • Demand for a currency (e.g. the pound) comes from people abroad who need it:
    • to buy the country's exports of goods and services, including tourist trips
    • to invest in the country: build factories, buy shares, save in its banks.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own people selling it:
    • to buy imports
    • to invest abroad.
Mission 5

The currency diagram

  • Price of the currency on the vertical axis; quantity of the currency on the horizontal.
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods cheaper abroad, so more is wanted.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper at home, so people sell more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply: the exchange rate.

Mission 5

Worked example: an exporter and a stronger currency

Illustrative data. A UK firm sells a pair of boots for £100.

  • At £1 = $1.25: price in the US = 100 × 1.25 = $125.
  • The pound appreciates to £1 = $1.50: price in the US = $150.
  • US buyers face a 20% higher price: (150 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 20%. They buy fewer.
  • The firm can keep its £100 price and sell less, or cut its pound price to stay competitive and earn less per pair.
  • Its leather comes from the US: $50 cost £40 at $1.25, but only £33.33 at $1.50. So a stronger pound also cuts some costs.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates sharply. Which group is most likely to gain?
Mission 5

Weigh it up: is a strong currency good news?

It depends on:

  • Who you are: importers and travellers gain from a strong currency; exporters gain from a weak one.
  • How much buyers react: if foreign buyers barely change what they buy, exporters lose little when prices rise.
  • Imported inputs: firms that import materials gain some of their loss back.
  • Time: contracts are often fixed for months, so effects build up slowly.
  • Inflation: a weak currency raises import prices, which can push up inflation.
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is written.
Mission check

?Foreign demand for Country Z's wine rises sharply. Nothing else changes. What happens to Z's currency, and to the price of imported goods in Z?

Every change in a floating rate starts with a shift: who wants more of the currency, and who wants to sell more of it?

Mission 6

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all money flows between a country and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in, e.g. a foreign buyer pays for our exports.
  • Debit (−): money flows out, e.g. we pay for imports.
  • Two main parts:
    • current account: trade in goods and services, and income flows
    • financial account: buying and selling assets (firms, shares, bonds), lending and borrowing, and the central bank's reserves of foreign currency.
  • (A small capital account also exists.)

Trade in goodsA car sold abroadOil bought from abroad
Trade in servicesA foreign tourist's hotel billInsurance bought from abroad
Primary incomeProfits and interest earned on assets abroadProfits paid to foreign owners of firms here
Secondary incomeMoney sent home by people working abroadForeign aid given
  • Balance of trade = exports − imports of goods and services.
  • Current account balance = balance of trade + primary income + secondary income.
  • Credits > debits: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Goods are visibles; services are invisibles. The goods balance on its own is the visible balance; some books call that the balance of trade, so check which is meant.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "the country made a loss".

Mission 6

Paying for a deficit

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit.
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 billion.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and keeps it in our banks or buys our assets: money flowing in on the financial account, +10.
  • So a current account deficit is paid for by money flowing in on the financial account:
    • borrowing from abroad
    • selling assets to foreigners: firms, shares, property, government bonds
    • running down the central bank's reserves of foreign currency.
  • Overall the balance of payments balances: current + capital + financial account = 0 (apart from errors).
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit".
Mission 6

Why deficits and surpluses happen

Causes of a current account deficit:

  • A boom at home: rising incomes mean more spending on imports.
  • A strong currency: exports dearer abroad, imports cheaper (see Mission 5).
  • Low competitiveness: home firms' costs or quality fall behind rivals'.
  • Higher inflation than trading partners: home goods become relatively dearer.
  • A recession abroad: foreign buyers buy fewer of our exports.

Causes of a surplus: the reverse, e.g. a weak currency, strong demand abroad, highly competitive exporters.

Mission 6

Does a deficit matter?

A current account deficit can mean:

  • fewer jobs and less output in industries that compete with imports
  • a growing debt to the rest of the world, with interest and profits paid abroad
  • downward pressure on the exchange rate (see Mission 5)
  • reserves running down, if the central bank pays for it.

But it can be fine when:

  • it is small and short-lived
  • it is paid for by long-term investment (foreign firms building factories) rather than short-term borrowing
  • the imports are machinery that will raise future output.

A surplus brings export jobs and income, but may mean low spending at home, and pushes the currency up.

Policies to reduce a deficit

PolicyHow it worksDrawback
Raise interest rates or taxesLess spending at home, so fewer importsSlower growth; more unemployment. Higher interest rates can also push the currency up (see Mission 5)
Let the currency depreciateExports cheaper, imports dearerHigher import prices: inflation
ProtectionismTariffs and quotas cut importsHigher prices; other countries may retaliate
Supply-side policiesBetter skills, technology and productivity make firms more competitiveSlow: takes years
  • Policies may conflict: cutting the deficit by cutting spending also cuts growth and jobs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The economic cycle: boom, downturn, recession, recovery, around a rising trend.
  • A recession: real GDP falls for two quarters in a row. A slowdown is not a recession.
  • Cycles come from changes in spending and from shocks; each change spreads through incomes.
  • Recessions raise unemployment and government borrowing; booms raise inflation.

Mission 2

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level, measured by the CPI.
  • Inflation rate = % change in the CPI over a year.
  • Real change ≈ nominal change − inflation.
  • Demand-pull: spending outruns what firms can produce. Cost-push: firms' costs rise.
  • Inflation hurts savers, lenders and people on fixed incomes; it helps borrowers. Deflation does the opposite.
  • Higher interest rates reduce spending and so reduce inflation.

Mission 3

  • The central bank (UK: the Bank of England's MPC) sets the interest rate to keep inflation near target.
  • Higher rates: borrowing dearer, saving more rewarding, spending slows, inflation slows; output and jobs may suffer.
  • Other tools: money supply, asset purchases (QE), the exchange rate.
  • Rates work with time lags and work less well against rising costs or low confidence.

Mission 4

  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Frictional: between jobs. Seasonal: time of year. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little spending.
  • Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Unemployment costs people, communities and the whole economy.

Mission 5

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in the country; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • Higher interest rates, more exports or more investment inflows raise demand: the currency appreciates.
  • A stronger currency makes imports cheaper and exports dearer; a weaker one does the reverse.

Mission 6

  • The current account records trade in goods and services plus primary and secondary income; balance = credits − debits.
  • Balance of trade is goods and services only; the current account adds income flows.
  • A current account deficit is paid for by money flowing in: borrowing from abroad or selling assets to foreigners.
  • Or by running down reserves.
  • Causes include booms, a strong currency and low competitiveness; policies include higher interest rates, depreciation, protection and supply-side reform.
