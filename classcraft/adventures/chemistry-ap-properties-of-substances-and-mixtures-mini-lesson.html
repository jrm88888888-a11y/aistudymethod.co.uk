Mission 1

Elements, compounds and mixtures

  • Element: one kind of atom. O2 is an element: two atoms, same element.
  • Compound: atoms of different elements bonded in a fixed ratio, e.g. H2O.
  • Mixture: substances not bonded together; proportions can vary, e.g. air, salt water.
  • A pure substance (element or compound) has one type of particle and a fixed composition.
Quick check
?Which of the numbered diagrams represents a pure substance?
Mission 1

Particles in the three states

  • Solid: particles touching, vibrate about fixed positions. Fixed shape and volume.
  • Liquid: particles still in close contact, but constantly moving, colliding and sliding past each other. Fixed volume, takes the container's shape.
  • Gas: particles far apart, in constant, fast, random motion. No definite shape or volume.
  • The arrangement depends on the forces between particles and on temperature.
Mission 1

Solids, liquids and molar volume

  • Crystalline solids: particles in a regular, repeating pattern (NaCl, ice).
  • Amorphous solids: no long-range order (glass, many polymers).
  • Solid and liquid have similar molar volumes: particles touch in both.
  • Water: ice 19.7 mL/mol at 0 °C; liquid 18.0 mL/mol at 0 °C and 18.8 mL/mol at 100 °C.
  • Gas is very different: H2O(g) at 100 °C and 1 atm is about 30.6 L/mol, roughly 1600 times the liquid at 100 °C (30.6 L ÷ 18.8 mL).
Quick check
?For a typical substance at 1 atm, how does the molar volume of the solid at its melting point compare with the molar volume of the liquid at the same temperature?
Mission 1

Naming the changes of state

  • Melting (s → l), freezing (l → s).
  • Vaporization (l → g), condensation (g → l).
  • Sublimation (s → g), deposition (g → s).
  • Evaporation: at the surface, at any temperature. Boiling: bubbles of vapor form throughout the liquid, at the boiling point.
  • These are physical changes: properties change, composition doesn't. H2O(l) → H2O(g).
  • So the big bubbles in boiling water are water vapor: whole H2O molecules, not air, not H2 and O2.
  • (The tiny bubbles seen early on, while warming, are dissolved air coming out.)
Mission 1

Why the temperature stops rising

  • Heating curve: temperature rises, then stays flat during melting, rises, then flat again during boiling.
  • Temperature measures the average kinetic energy of the particles.
  • On a flat section, energy is still absorbed. It overcomes the attractions between particles, so the average kinetic energy doesn't rise.
  • The flat for boiling is longer than for melting: boiling separates the particles completely.
Quick check
?A pot of water is boiling steadily over a burner, and a thermometer in the water reads 100 °C throughout. A student claims that the boiling water is not absorbing energy from the burner. Which statement best evaluates the claim?
Mission 1

Energy in, energy out

  • Melting, vaporization, sublimation: energy absorbed (endothermic, ΔH positive).
  • Freezing, condensation, deposition: energy released (exothermic, ΔH negative).
  • Water: ΔHfus = +6.01 kJ/mol; ΔHvap = +40.7 kJ/mol (at 100 °C).
  • Vaporization needs far more: it separates molecules completely; melting only loosens them.
  • ΔHcond = −ΔHvap. Freezing releases the same energy per mole that melting absorbs: −ΔHfus.
Mission 1

Worked example: energy to boil water

  • How much energy is needed to vaporize 9.00 g of water at 100 °C? ΔHvap = 40.7 kJ/mol.
  • Step 1, moles: n = 9.00 g ÷ 18.02 g/mol = 0.499 mol.
  • Step 2, energy: q = n × ΔHvap = 0.499 mol × 40.7 kJ/mol = 20.3 kJ.
  • Condensing the same 9.00 g of steam releases 20.3 kJ.
  • No ΔT here: q = mcΔT is for heating within one phase, not for the phase change.
Quick check
?The molar enthalpy of vaporization of water at 100 °C is 40.7 kJ/mol. How much energy is released when 1.60 g of steam at 100 °C condenses to liquid water at 100 °C?
Mission check

?A drop of steam at 100 °C on the skin causes a far worse burn than a drop of the same mass of water at 100 °C. Which statement explains this?

The bubbles in boiling water are water vapor. Making them takes in energy without raising the temperature; turning them back into liquid gives that energy out.

Mission 2

Polar bonds

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the bonding pair of electrons in a covalent bond. Pauling scale.
  • Rises across a period (more protons, same shielding, smaller atom) and falls down a group (bigger atom, more shielding).
  • Different electronegativities: the pair is shared unequally. A polar bond: δ+ and δ−.
  • H–Cl: H 2.2, Cl 3.2. δ− on Cl. C–H (2.6, 2.2) is treated as nonpolar.
  • Bigger difference, more polar. Very large difference: ionic. Bonding is a continuum.
Quick check
?The electronegativities of H, C and F are 2.20, 2.55 and 3.98, respectively. In a molecule of fluoromethane, CH3F, the C atom is bonded to three H atoms and to the F atom. Which statement best describes the bonds in the molecule?
Mission 2

Polar molecules

  • A molecule is polar if its bond dipoles leave a permanent dipole overall.
  • Equal dipoles arranged symmetrically cancel: CO2, BF3 and CCl4 are nonpolar.
  • In H2O, NH3 and CH2Cl2 they don't cancel: polar.
  • Check both: are the bonds polar, and does the shape cancel them?
Quick check
?Which molecule has no dipole moment?
Mission 2

Forces between molecules

  • London dispersion forces act between all molecules and atoms.
  • Electrons move: a temporary dipole forms and induces a dipole in a neighbor. They attract.
  • More electrons and more surface contact: stronger London forces.
  • Permanent dipole–dipole: δ+ of one polar molecule attracts δ− of another (two HCl molecules).
  • Dipole–induced dipole: a polar molecule distorts a nonpolar one (H2O and Ar). Ion–dipole: an ion attracts the oppositely charged end of polar molecules (Na⁺ in water).
  • All are much weaker than covalent or ionic bonds.
Mission 2

Hydrogen bonding

  • Hydrogen bond: an H atom bonded to N, O or F (δ+) attracts a lone pair on N, O or F of another molecule.
  • Drawn as a dotted line, O–H⋯O roughly straight.
  • The O–H bond inside water is covalent; the hydrogen bond is between molecules.
  • Water forms up to four per molecule. Ice: an open lattice, so ice is less dense than water.
  • Anomalously high boiling points: H2O, NH3 and HF boil far above the trend of their groups.
  • Noncovalent attractions hold large biomolecules in shape: protein folding, DNA's paired strands.
Quick check
?In which of the following pure liquids do hydrogen bonds form between the molecules?
Mission 2

Ranking the forces

  • At similar molar mass: London < permanent dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonding.
  • O2, H2S and CH3OH: molar masses 32.0, 34.1 and 32.0 g/mol.
  • O2, London only: boils at −183 °C.
  • H2S, polar, adds dipole–dipole: −60 °C.
  • CH3OH adds hydrogen bonds: 65 °C.
  • Very different masses? London forces can win: octane, C8H18, nonpolar but far heavier, boils at 126 °C.
  • Stronger attractions → fewer molecules escape → lower vapor pressure, higher boiling point.
  • Alkanes: longer chain, higher bp. Branched isomers: less contact, lower bp.
Quick check
?Diethyl ether, CH3CH2OCH2CH3, and 1-butanol, CH3CH2CH2CH2OH, are liquids with the same molecular formula, C4H10O. Which statement correctly compares their vapor pressures at 20 °C?
Mission 2

Simple molecular substances

  • Melting and boiling overcome forces between molecules. Covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Weak forces: low melting and boiling points. No ions or free electrons: no conduction.
  • Draw them as particles: covalent bonds inside molecules, dashed attractions between them.
  • Changes of state alter only intermolecular attractions: physical changes.
Mission 2

Solubility: like dissolves like

  • A solute dissolves if new solute–solvent attractions make up for those broken.
  • Ionic compounds in water: polar water molecules surround the ions (hydration).
  • These are ion–dipole attractions. Dissolving a salt breaks ionic bonds, so it can be argued as physical or chemical.
  • Alcohols: the O–H hydrogen bonds to water. Short chains mix; longer chains dissolve less.
  • Polar molecules that can't hydrogen bond (alkyl halides) barely dissolve in water.
  • Nonpolar solutes dissolve in nonpolar solvents: I2 in hexane.
Quick check
?A few crystals of I2 are added to each of two test tubes, one containing water and the other containing hexane, C6H14, and both tubes are shaken. Which claim about the result, with its reasoning, is correct?
Mission 2

Separating by attractions: chromatography

  • A spot of mixture sits on a stationary phase (paper, or a TLC plate). A solvent, the mobile phase, moves up through it.
  • Each component is attracted to both phases. More attraction to the stationary phase: it moves less.
  • Rf = distance moved by spot ÷ distance moved by solvent, both from the baseline.
  • Spot 2.4 cm, solvent 8.0 cm: Rf = 0.30. Same conditions, same substance, same Rf.
  • On polar paper or silica, the more polar component usually has the lower Rf.
Mission check

?Ammonia, NH3 (molar mass 17.0 g/mol), boils at −33 °C. Phosphine, PH3 (34.0 g/mol), boils at −88 °C. Electronegativity: H 2.2, N 3.0, P 2.2. Why does the lighter molecule boil higher?

Water does the same, only more: each H2O can make up to four hydrogen bonds. Methane, with only weak London forces, boils at −162 °C; water, hydrogen bonded, at 100 °C.

Mission 3

Amino acids

  • An amino acid: an –NH2 group and a –COOH group on the same carbon: RCH(NH2)COOH.
  • Both groups sit on one carbon, the α-carbon. R is the side chain.
  • Glycine: R = H, H2NCH2COOH. Alanine: R = CH3, H2NCH(CH3)COOH.
  • About 20 amino acids build proteins. They differ only in R.
Mission 3

Two groups, two jobs

  • –COOH is an acid group. –NH2 is a base group, like ammonia.
  • With a strong base: H2NCH2COOH + OH⁻ → H2NCH2COO⁻ + H2O.
  • With a strong acid: H2NCH2COOH + H3O⁺ → ⁺H3NCH2COOH + H2O.
  • Each group reacts with its opposite: the molecule is amphoteric.
Quick check
?Glycine, H2NCH2COOH, is added to excess dilute hydrochloric acid at room temperature. Which species of glycine is present?
Mission 3

What holds a protein's shape

  • A protein is a long chain of amino acids joined covalently.
  • The chain folds into one precise shape. Noncovalent interactions hold it:
  • Hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O in different regions of the same chain.
  • Ion–ion attractions between –COO⁻ and –NH3⁺ side chains. London dispersion forces between nonpolar side chains.
  • These act within one molecule as well as between molecules.
  • The shape decides the function. Disrupt the interactions and the protein stops working.
Quick check
?The diagram represents part of one large biomolecule that has folded back on itself. Which statement about the interaction represented by the dashed line is correct?
Mission check

?In a folded protein, a side chain ending in –COOH and a side chain ending in –NH2 sit next to each other. At the pH inside a cell, the first is –COO⁻ and the second is –NH3⁺. Which statement best describes the interaction between them?

The same acid and base groups that let an amino acid react with acids and bases give proteins charged side chains that attract.

Mission 4

Enzymes: protein catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a catalyst: it speeds up a reaction and is not used up.
  • Its folded chain leaves a small pocket: the active site.
  • The reacting molecule, the substrate, binds in the active site: an enzyme–substrate complex.
  • E + S ⇌ ES → E + P. ES is a new reaction intermediate, with S bound to the catalyst.
  • Bound, the substrate is held in a good orientation and reacts by a path with a lower activation energy.
  • The products leave; the enzyme is free to bind another substrate.
Quick check
?An enzyme, E, catalyzes the conversion of a substrate, S, into a product, P. In the mechanism, S binds to the active site of E to form an enzyme–substrate complex, ES, which then forms P and releases E. Which statement best explains how the enzyme increases the rate of the reaction?
Mission 4

Why a mirror image doesn't fit

  • Background: some molecules exist as two mirror-image forms (enantiomers), like a left and a right hand.
  • The active site is 3-D. It has several binding points, made by groups on the amino acids.
  • One enantiomer can line up with all the binding points at once.
  • Its mirror image can match at most two at a time: it binds weakly or not at all.
Mission 4

Too hot

  • The fold is held by noncovalent interactions. Heating breaks them and the active site loses its shape: the enzyme is denatured.
  • The covalent chain stays intact.
Quick check
?An enzyme is a protein: a long chain molecule that is folded into a specific shape. When a solution of an enzyme is warmed from 37 °C to 70 °C, the enzyme stops working. A student claims that the enzyme molecules have changed shape. Which reasoning best supports the claim?
Mission 4

The double helix

  • DNA has two strands, each a long covalent chain, coiled round each other.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds: A with T (2 hydrogen bonds), G with C (3).
  • The N–H and C=O / N groups only line up in those pairs: shape decides the partner.
  • The strands are held together by noncovalent interactions, not covalent bonds.
Quick check
?The two strands of a DNA molecule are held together by attractions between bases on opposite strands. When a solution of DNA is heated, the two strands separate from each other. Which particulate-level change best accounts for the separation of the strands?
Mission check

?An enzyme from the small intestine works best near pH 7. At pH 2 it stops working, although no covalent bonds in it break. Which explanation is best?
  • (Hint: some side chains end in –COO⁻ at pH 7.)

Like a mirror image, a misshapen site can't line up with the substrate. Binding needs an exact 3-D fit.

Mission 5: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Make a guess: Diamond and graphite are both pure carbon. Why does only graphite conduct electricity?

Mission 5

Diamond: a giant covalent lattice

  • Covalent bonds run through the whole solid. There are no separate molecules.
  • Name: covalent network solid. Formed only from nonmetals and metalloids.
  • Diamond: each C bonded to four others, tetrahedrally (109.5°), in 3D.
  • Very hard, very high melting point: melting breaks strong covalent bonds.
  • No conduction: all four outer electrons of each C are in localized bonds.
Mission 5

Graphite

  • Flat layers of hexagons. Each C bonded to three others (120°).
  • Each C's fourth outer electron is delocalized across its layer.
  • Delocalized electrons move when a voltage is applied: graphite conducts.
  • Weak forces between the layers. Layers slide, so graphite is soft.
  • Still a very high melting point: strong covalent bonds within layers must break.
Quick check
?The diagram represents parts of the structures of diamond and graphite, two forms of carbon. Which statement best explains why graphite is soft and diamond is hard?
Mission 5

Graphene and fullerenes

  • Background from the video: graphene and fullerenes are not examined on your course.
  • Graphene: a single layer of graphite, one atom thick.
  • Each C bonded to three others, with delocalized electrons: it conducts.
  • Very strong for its mass: strong covalent bonds run across the whole sheet.
  • Fullerenes, e.g. C60: closed cages of carbon atoms. These are separate molecules.
  • Only weak forces act between C60 molecules: far lower sublimation temperature than diamond.
  • Diamond, graphite, graphene and fullerenes are allotropes: one element, different structures.
Mission 5

Silicon and silicon dioxide

  • Silicon: the diamond structure. Each Si bonded to four Si, tetrahedrally.
  • Si–Si bonds are longer and weaker than C–C: silicon is less hard than diamond and melts lower (1414 °C).
  • Silicon conducts only slightly: it is a semiconductor, not a metal.
  • Silicon dioxide, SiO2, and silicon carbide, SiC, are binary covalent network solids. In SiO2 each Si bonds to four O; each O to two Si.
  • SiO2 is a giant lattice, so its formula is just the ratio Si : O = 1 : 2.
  • Melts near 1700 °C. Hard, insoluble, non-conducting.
Mission 5

Predicting properties from structure

Giant covalent (e.g. diamond, SiO2)Simple molecular (e.g. CO2, I2)
Melting pointvery high: covalent bonds breaklow: only weak forces between molecules
Conductivitynone (except graphite, graphene; Si slightly)none
Solubilityinsoluble in any solventdepends on the solvent
  • Decide the structure first. Then explain each property from it.
  • High melting point: many strong covalent bonds. Hard 3D networks: bond angles are fixed.
Quick check
?Substance X melts at 1710 °C. It does not conduct electricity, either as a solid or when molten. It does not dissolve in water or in hexane. Which structure does X have?
Mission check

?Silicon carbide, SiC, is a covalent network solid. Every Si atom is bonded to four C atoms, and every C atom to four Si atoms, tetrahedrally, in three dimensions. Which pair of properties does this structure predict?

Properties follow from structure. A 3D covalent network (diamond, SiC, SiO2) is hard with a very high melting point; graphite's 2D layers make it soft. Graphite conducts because each C's fourth electron is delocalized across its layer; diamond has no free electrons.

Mission 6

Pressure and volume

  • Fixed amount of gas, constant temperature: P ∝ 1/V.
  • Halve the volume: particles hit each bit of wall twice as often, so p doubles.
  • p against V: a falling curve. p against 1/V: a straight line through the origin.
  • Constant T and P: V ∝ n. Twice the gas needs twice the room.
Mission 6

Why kelvin?

  • Constant pressure: V ∝ T. Constant volume: P ∝ T. Only with T in kelvin.
  • T(K) = T(°C) + 273.15 (273 is fine for most data).
  • Hotter particles move faster: they hit the walls harder and more often. They don't expand.
  • Against T in K: straight lines through the origin. Against °C: they reach zero at −273 °C (0 K).
  • So 10 °C → 20 °C does not double p: 283 K → 293 K is only 3.5% more.
Quick check
?A student will measure the pressure of a fixed amount of gas in a rigid container at several temperatures to test the claim that the pressure is directly proportional to the absolute temperature. Which graph of the data, if it is a straight line through the origin, would support the claim?
Mission 6

The ideal gas law

  • PV = nRT: P in atm, V in L, n in mol, T in K.
  • R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K).
  • 1 atm = 760 torr = 101.3 kPa. 1 L = 1000 mL.
  • With P in kPa, use R = 8.314 L·kPa/(mol·K) (the same number as 8.314 J/(mol·K)).
  • Match R to your units, convert, then substitute.
Mission 6

Worked example: amount of gas

  • 250. mL of gas at 735 torr and 22 °C. What amount is present?
  • P = 735 ÷ 760 = 0.967 atm · V = 0.250 L · T = 295 K.
  • n = PV/RT = (0.967 × 0.250) ÷ (0.08206 × 295)
  • = 9.99 × 10⁻³ mol.
  • Units check: atm·L ÷ (L·atm/(mol·K) × K) = mol.
Quick check
?A rigid 2.50 L flask contains O2(g) at a pressure of 1.20 atm and a temperature of 27 °C. What is the mass of O2 in the flask?
Mission 6

The combined gas law

  • Fixed amount of gas: n and R are constant, so PV/T is constant.
  • P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2. T in kelvin; P and V in the same units on both sides.
  • Leave out anything that stays constant.
  • 15.0 L at 1.00 atm and 290 K goes to 0.500 atm and 250 K.
  • V2 = V1 × (P1/P2) × (T2/T1) = 15.0 × 2 × (250/290) = 25.9 L.
  • Sense-check: lower pressure → bigger; cooler → smaller.
Quick check
?A weather balloon holds 5.00 L of helium at 1.00 atm and 25 °C. It rises to where the pressure is 0.500 atm and the temperature is −23 °C. Assuming no gas escapes, what is its new volume?
Mission 6

Partial pressures

  • In a mixture of ideal gases, each gas exerts its own partial pressure, as if it were alone.
  • Ptotal = PA + PB + …
  • Mole fraction: XA = moles of A ÷ total moles. PA = XA × Ptotal.
  • 1.00 mol N2 + 0.250 mol O2 at 2.00 atm: X(O2) = 0.250 ÷ 1.25 = 0.200, so P(O2) = 0.400 atm.
  • Add another gas at constant V and T: Ptotal rises, but P(O2) doesn't change.
Quick check
?A rigid 2.50 L container holds a mixture of 0.200 mol of He(g) and 0.300 mol of Ne(g) at 127 °C. What is the partial pressure of Ne in the container?
Mission 6

Gas stoichiometry

  • Moles link everything: mass (n = m/M), solution (n = M × V), gas (n = PV/RT).
  • 2 KClO3(s) → 2 KCl(s) + 3 O2(g). What volume of O2 comes from 2.45 g KClO3, at 25 °C and 1.00 atm?
  • n(KClO3) = 2.45 ÷ 122.55 = 0.0200 mol.
  • n(O2) = 0.0200 × 3/2 = 0.0300 mol.
  • V = nRT/P = 0.0300 × 0.08206 × 298 ÷ 1.00 = 0.734 L.
Quick check
?A 2.70 g sample of Al(s) reacts completely with excess HCl(aq) according to the equation 2 Al(s) + 6 HCl(aq) → 2 AlCl3(aq) + 3 H2(g). What volume of H2(g) is produced, measured at 25 °C and 1.00 atm?

What happens to the pressure inside the can?

Mission 7

Gas particles in motion

  • Kinetic molecular theory (KMT): a gas is particles in constant, random motion.
  • They move in straight lines until they collide, with each other or the walls.
  • Wall collisions cause pressure.
  • Forces between gas particles are tiny, so a gas has no definite shape or volume: it fills its container.
  • How often particles collide, and how far apart they are, depends on T, P and V.
Mission 7

The ideal gas model

  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • No attractions or repulsions between particles.
  • Collisions are elastic: total kinetic energy is unchanged.
  • Average kinetic energy is proportional to the Kelvin temperature.
  • A gas that fits every assumption obeys PV = nRT exactly.
Quick check
?A sealed, rigid steel container holds N2(g) at 300 K. When the container is heated to 450 K, the pressure of the gas increases. Which statement gives correct particulate-level reasoning for the claim that heating the gas raises its pressure?
Mission 7

Speed, mass and temperature

  • Kinetic energy of a particle: KE = ½mv².
  • Kelvin temperature is proportional to the average KE of the particles.
  • Same temperature → same average KE, whatever the gas.
  • So lighter particles move faster on average.
  • He (4.00 g/mol) vs N2 (28.02 g/mol) at the same T: He atoms are about 2.6 times faster on average.
  • Double the Kelvin temperature → double the average KE (not double the speed).
Quick check
?A rigid container holds a mixture of He(g) and Ar(g) at 300 K. A student claims that the He atoms and the Ar atoms have the same average speed, because the two gases are at the same temperature. Which evaluation of the student's claim is best?
Mission 7

The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution

  • At any temperature, particles have a spread of speeds and energies.
  • Graph: number of particles against kinetic energy (or speed). Starts at zero, rises to a peak, long tail.
  • Higher T: peak moves right and gets lower; curve gets broader. Area (number of particles) is unchanged.
  • Same T, lighter gas: the speed curve is broader and further right.
  • Broader energy spread = energy more dispersed, so entropy increases as T rises.
Quick check
?A sample of N2(g) in a rigid container is heated from 300 K to 600 K. Which statement correctly describes the change?
Mission 7

Why real gases deviate

  • Real particles attract each other and take up space.
  • Attractions matter most near condensation: low T (slow particles) or high P (close particles).
  • Particle volume matters at extremely high P, when particles fill much of the space.
  • Stronger interparticle forces → bigger deviation.
  • He: tiny atoms, only weak London dispersion forces. Almost ideal.
  • NH3: polar, with hydrogen bonding, so stronger attractions. Less ideal.
Mission 7

Reading the PV/nRT graph

  • For an ideal gas, PV/nRT = 1 at every pressure.
  • Below 1: attractions pull particles away from the walls, so P is lower than ideal.
  • Above 1: particle volume dominates; the gas can't be squeezed as much, so V is bigger than ideal.
  • The further from 1, the less ideal the gas.
  • A strongly attracting gas (like NH3) dips well below 1; He stays close to 1.
Quick check

At each temperature the student will calculate the ratio Pmeasured/Pideal, where Pideal = nRT/V. Which result should the student predict?
Quick check
?A sample of N2 gas at 25 °C is compressed to an extremely high pressure. The measured volume of the sample is compared with the volume calculated from the ideal gas law for the same amount of gas at the same temperature and pressure. Which claim, with its reasoning, is correct?
Mission check

?Four gases are each at 1.00 atm and 25 °C. Which one has PV/nRT furthest below 1?

Same for ammonia. Its hydrogen-bonded molecules attract each other, and it is near condensing at room temperature. Helium's atoms barely attract, so it stays close to ideal.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Bubbles in boiling water: H2O(g). Changes of state are physical: molecules unchanged.
  • Melting and boiling absorb energy at constant temperature (attractions overcome); freezing and condensing release it.
  • q = n × ΔH for a phase change; ΔHcond = −ΔHvap.

Mission 2

  • Unequal sharing gives polar bonds; a molecule is polar only if its shape stops the dipoles canceling.
  • Similar mass: London < permanent dipole–dipole < hydrogen bonds. Boiling overcomes these, not covalent bonds.
  • Stronger attractions: lower vapor pressure, higher boiling point.

Mission 3

  • An amino acid has an acid group (–COOH) and a base group (–NH2), so it reacts with both acids and bases.
  • Protein shape is held by noncovalent interactions within the chain; shape decides function.

Mission 4

  • Binding needs an exact 3-D fit: an active site is stereospecific, so the mirror image doesn't fit.
  • Heat or the wrong pH changes the shape that holds the active site.
  • DNA strands pair A–T and G–C by hydrogen bonds.

Mission 5

  • Giant covalent: strong covalent bonds throughout, so very high melting point and insoluble.
  • Diamond: 4 bonds per C, all electrons localized, hard, no conduction. Graphite: 3 bonds per C, one delocalized electron per C, conducts along layers, layers slide.

Mission 6

  • P ∝ 1/V (T fixed); V ∝ T and P ∝ T, with T in kelvin. Fixed amount: P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2.
  • PV = nRT in atm, L, K with R = 0.08206 L·atm/(mol·K); PA = XA × Ptotal.

Mission 7

  • KMT: particles in constant random motion; Kelvin T ∝ average KE = ½mv²; heating broadens the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, so entropy rises.
  • Real gases deviate most near condensation (attractions: PV/nRT < 1) and at extremely high P (particle volume: PV/nRT > 1).
