Mission 1

Mission 1: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Make a guess: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Mission 1

A tube through the body

  • Animals are heterotrophs: they get carbon compounds from other organisms.
  • Holozoic nutrition: food is taken in and digested inside the body.
  • Four stages: ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation. Undigested waste is egested.
  • The gut is a tube open at both ends. Its lumen is still outside the body's cells.
  • Digestion: enzymes hydrolyse large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones.
  • Absorption: those small molecules cross the gut lining into the blood or lymph. Only now are they inside.
  • Assimilation: cells build the absorbed molecules into the animal's own molecules and tissues.
Quick check
?A dog eats meat. At which stage are the amino acids from the meat first inside the dog's body?
Mission 1

Moving food along

  • Peristalsis: a wave of contraction of the circular muscle behind the food, relaxation in front. It pushes food one way.
  • It works without gravity: you can swallow upside down.
  • Starting a swallow is voluntary: controlled by the CNS (brain and spinal cord).
  • Egestion of faeces is also under voluntary CNS control.
  • Between these points, peristalsis is involuntary: the enteric nervous system (ENS) in the gut wall coordinates it.
Quick check
?A person swallows a mouthful of food. Which statement correctly describes how the movement of this food through the digestive system is controlled?
Mission check

?A person eats celery. Humans make no enzyme that digests cellulose. One cellulose fibre from the celery passes along the whole gut and leaves in the faeces. Which statement about this fibre is correct?

A meal gets inside you only when its molecules cross the gut lining. Before that, it is still in a tube that runs through you.

Mission 2

Mission 2: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Make a guess: To study one part of a cell, you first have to smash the cell open. How do you get that part out undamaged?

Mission 2

Why take cells apart?

  • A microscope shows what an organelle looks like. To find out what it does, you need it on its own, in quantity.
  • Cell fractionation: break cells open, then separate their organelles into fractions.
  • Isolated organelles keep working: e.g. isolated mitochondria use oxygen and make ATP.
  • An organelle is a discrete subunit of a cell, adapted to a function. Nuclei, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane count; the cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm do not.
  • Science often advances when a new technique appears: organelle function could be studied only once ultracentrifuges and fractionation methods existed.
Mission 2

Step 2: ultracentrifugation

  • First, cells are broken open in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution: enzymes are slowed so they cannot digest the organelles, organelles do not burst or shrink by osmosis, and pH stays constant so proteins are not denatured. The homogenate is filtered.
  • Spin the filtered homogenate in an ultracentrifuge.
  • The largest, densest organelles settle first, into a pellet at the bottom.
  • The liquid above, the supernatant, is poured into a fresh tube and spun faster and longer.
  • Repeat. Each spin brings down smaller, less dense organelles.
  • This is differential centrifugation.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly applies the term organelle as it is used in IB Biology?
Mission check

?Liver cells were broken open in a cold, isotonic, buffered solution and the homogenate was spun in an ultracentrifuge at a series of increasing speeds. Only the pellet formed at the second speed consumed oxygen and produced ATP when supplied with substrates. What does this investigation illustrate about how progress in science is made?

Keep the organelles cold, isotonic and buffered, then spin at rising speeds: each fraction can then be tested on its own.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Make a guess: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Mission 3

Transcription comes first

  • Transcription: making an RNA copy of a gene, using one DNA strand as a template.
  • It is the first stage of gene expression. Translation comes next.
  • In eukaryotes, the mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore. The DNA stays behind.
  • Not all genes are expressed at once. Transcription is a key stage at which a gene is switched on or off.
Mission 3

Transcription, step by step

  • RNA polymerase binds at the start of a gene. The DNA unwinds; hydrogen bonds between bases break.
  • Only one strand, the template strand, is read.
  • Free RNA nucleotides pair with exposed template bases: A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds, building the sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • The RNA separates from the template. The DNA rewinds.
  • Say: "RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides" — not: "RNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form by hydrogen bonding.
Quick check
?A section of the DNA template strand of a gene has the base sequence TACGCAT. What is the base sequence of the RNA transcribed from this section?
Mission 3

Worked example: three sequences

  • Template strand: T A C G G A T C
  • mRNA (complementary to the template): A U G C C U A G
  • Coding strand (paired with the template): A T G C C T A G
  • So mRNA = the coding strand, with U in place of T.
  • mRNA is a single strand. Its sugar is ribose. It is a copy of one gene, so it is short.
  • Its bases are read in triplets: codons.
Quick check
?During transcription, what holds each RNA nucleotide in position against the DNA template strand while it is being added to the growing RNA?
Mission 3

The template is not used up

  • Transcription reads the template. It does not change its base sequence.
  • One gene can be transcribed again and again.
  • Cells that never divide again (most neurons) keep the same DNA for their whole life.
  • So their DNA sequences must be conserved for decades.
Quick check
?A neuron formed before birth does not divide again, yet it transcribes the same genes throughout a lifetime of many decades. Which statement explains how this is possible?
Mission 3

Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

  • Eukaryotes: transcription in the nucleus; translation on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • The nuclear membrane keeps them apart, so mRNA can be modified before it meets a ribosome.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus. mRNA can meet ribosomes at once, even while it is still being made.

Mission check

?α-Amanitin, a toxin from the death cap mushroom, blocks the RNA polymerase that makes mRNA in eukaryotic cells. It has no effect on DNA polymerase or on ribosomes. Which prediction about liver cells exposed to it is correct?

The DNA stays put. RNA polymerase makes a temporary, complementary copy of one gene, and that copy carries the message out. Stop RNA polymerase and the message stops.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 4

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • This one transformation supplies most of the chemical energy used by life in ecosystems.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 4

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 4

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission check

?Algae are given carbon dioxide labelled with the heavy oxygen isotope ¹⁸O, and ordinary water. They photosynthesise in the light. Where do the ¹⁸O atoms end up?

The O2 released comes from water; the oxygen of CO2 ends up in the sugar.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Make a guess: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Mission 5

One leaf, several pigments

  • Chloroplasts hold several photosynthetic pigments:
  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green.
  • Carotenoids: carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment. The others are accessory pigments.
  • Each pigment absorbs a different set of wavelengths, so together they use more of the spectrum.
Mission 5

Absorbing light

  • A pigment absorbs a photon only if its energy matches the gap to an excited state.
  • Then an electron is raised to a higher energy level. This energy is later turned into chemical energy.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue (about 430–450 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm) strongly.
  • It absorbs little green (about 500–600 nm): green is reflected or transmitted, so leaves look green.
  • Absorption spectrum: absorbance against wavelength (nm), for one pigment.
  • Label the x-axis with wavelengths and colours.
  • Say: "leaves look green because green light is reflected" — not: "chlorophyll absorbs green light".
Quick check
?The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a has peaks at about 430 nm (blue) and 660 nm (red) but a trough at about 550 nm (green). Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 5

Action spectrum

  • Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis against wavelength.
  • Rate = O2 produced, or CO2 taken in, per unit time.
  • Similar to the absorption spectra: peaks in blue and red, trough in green.
  • Different: the action spectrum is not zero in green. Accessory pigments absorb some green and pass the energy on.
  • Absorption spectrum: one pigment, light absorbed. Action spectrum: whole leaf, photosynthesis done.
  • Engelmann (1882) lit a filament of alga with a spectrum: oxygen-seeking bacteria crowded in the blue and red zones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A leaf sealed in a chamber was illuminated with light of one wavelength at a time and the fall in carbon dioxide concentration in the chamber was recorded: at 450 nm the concentration fell by 80 ppm in 4 minutes; at 550 nm by 30 ppm in 5 minutes; at 650 nm by 90 ppm in 6 minutes; at 700 nm by 20 ppm in 2 minutes. Which statement about these results is correct?
Mission 5

Practical: separating the pigments

  • Paper or thin-layer chromatography; calculate Rf and identify pigments.
  • Grind leaf with sand and solvent (e.g. propanone). Spot the extract on a pencil origin line; dry; repeat to concentrate.
  • Stand in solvent below the origin, in a sealed container. Remove before the front reaches the top; mark the front at once.
  • Pigments separate by solubility in the solvent versus attraction to the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance from origin to spot centre ÷ distance from origin to solvent front.
  • Identify by Rf and colour against reference values for the same solvent and stationary phase.
Mission 5

Worked example: Rf

Illustrative data. Distances from the origin line: solvent front 82 mm.

1. Orange spot: 77 mm. Rf = 77 ÷ 82 = 0.94.

2. Blue-green spot: 49 mm. Rf = 49 ÷ 82 = 0.60.

3. Yellow-green spot: 33 mm. Rf = 33 ÷ 82 = 0.40.

  • Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
  • Measure from the origin, not the paper edge; to the centre of the spot.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Chromatography reveals the hidden pigments; each one widens the range of light the leaf can use.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Make a guess: Your body makes about four million new cells a second. How does each one get a full set of chromosomes?

Mission 6

One cell becomes two

  • A parent cell (often called the mother cell) divides into two daughter cells.
  • The parent does not survive as a third cell: its contents are shared out.
  • First the nucleus divides: mitosis.
  • Then the cytoplasm divides: cytokinesis.
  • Nuclear division must come first, or one cell would be anucleate (no nucleus).
  • Not every cell keeps dividing: most mature nerve cells never do.
Mission 6

What mitosis is for

  • Growth: more cells.
  • Repair and replacement: cells lost by wounds or wear.
  • Each daughter cell gets the same chromosome number and the same genes: genetic constancy.
  • Say: "growth needs cell division to add cells" — not: "organs grow just by their cells getting bigger".
Mission 6

Copy first: two chromatids

  • Before a cell divides, every DNA molecule is replicated.
  • Each chromosome is then two identical sister chromatids, joined at the centromere.
  • They stay joined until anaphase.
  • One chromatid = one DNA molecule. Two joined chromatids = one chromosome.
  • Say: "replication gives each chromosome two sister chromatids" — not: "replication doubles the chromosome number".
Quick check
?A cell of an organism has 8 chromosomes before its DNA is replicated. How many chromosomes and how many DNA molecules are in its nucleus at metaphase of mitosis?
Mission 6

Mitosis in four stages

  • Prophase: chromosomes condense (shorter, thicker, visible). The nuclear envelope breaks down. The spindle forms.
  • Condensation: DNA wraps around histones and is supercoiled.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes line up on the equator. Spindle fibres from both poles attach at each centromere.
  • Anaphase: centromeres divide. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles; the spindle fibres shorten.
  • The pull: microtubule motors move chromatids along microtubules, which shorten.
  • Telophase: chromatids reach the poles and uncoil. A nuclear envelope forms round each set. The spindle breaks down.
  • Say: "sister chromatids separate" — not: "homologous chromosomes separate".
Quick check
?A body cell has 4 chromosomes. It is in anaphase of mitosis. What is moving towards each pole of the cell?
Mission 6

Cytokinesis

  • Mitosis divides the nucleus. Cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm.
  • It usually begins during telophase.
  • Animal cell: the membrane is pulled in at the equator, forming a cleavage furrow that pinches the cell in two.
  • The pull comes from a ring of contractile actin and myosin proteins.
  • Plant cell: the rigid wall cannot be pinched. Vesicles gather at the equator and fuse into a cell plate.
  • The cell plate becomes new membranes and a new wall. It grows outwards until it joins the old wall.
  • Say: "spindle microtubules move chromosomes" — not: "the spindle pinches the cell in two".
Quick check
?A dividing cell is examined after its two new nuclei have formed. Many small vesicles are seen gathering and fusing in a line across the equator of the cell, midway between the nuclei. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission check

?A population of a hypothetical freshwater plant reproduces only by fragmentation: pieces break off a parent plant and grow into new plants by mitosis. Which claim about the new plants is best supported?

Copy every chromosome, then pull the copies apart: each new cell gets a full, identical set. Four million times a second, or once in a broken-off fragment.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Make a guess: A microscope slide freezes a root tip in one instant. How can counting its cells reveal time?

Mission 7

Read the stage from the chromosomes

  • Interphase: intact nucleus; no separate chromosomes (DNA uncoiled). Most cells look like this.
  • Cells divide out of step, so the commonest stage is the one that lasts longest.
  • Prophase: thread-like chromosomes, scattered; nuclear envelope disappearing.
  • Metaphase: chromosomes in a single line across the equator.
  • Anaphase: two groups of V-shaped chromatids moving apart.
  • Telophase: two clusters at the poles; nuclear envelopes re-forming. In plants, a cell plate between them.
  • Why visible? Condensed chromosomes are short and thick, so they take up stain densely.
  • Say: "intact nucleus, no visible chromosomes = interphase" — not: "= prophase".
Quick check
?Two cells are seen in a stained micrograph of a root tip. Cell 1 has an intact nucleus in which no separate chromosomes can be distinguished. Cell 2 has no nuclear envelope, and its condensed chromosomes lie in a single line across the middle of the cell. Which identification is correct?
Mission 7Practical

Root-tip squash

  • Skill (D2.1.8): identify phases in cells under the microscope.
  • Cut the last 5 mm of a growing root (onion or garlic). Dividing cells are in the meristem, near the tip.
  • Warm in hydrochloric acid (e.g. 1 mol dm⁻³, about 60 °C, a few minutes). This breaks the middle lamella, so cells separate, and stops division.
  • Rinse; add a stain that binds DNA (e.g. toluidine blue or acetic orcein).
  • Cover with a coverslip and squash with firm, vertical pressure: no sideways slide. Aim for one layer of cells.
  • Find the meristem at low power, then identify stages at high power (×400).
  • Key controls: use only the tip; same acid time and temperature for every root; count every cell in a field, not just the dividing ones.
Quick check
?In a stained root-tip squash, one cell shows two groups of V-shaped chromatids, their points facing opposite ends of the cell. Which explains this appearance?
Mission 7

Equal and unequal cytokinesis

  • Cytokinesis is usually equal: two daughter cells of similar size.
  • Each daughter must get at least one mitochondrion (and, in plants, a plastid, e.g. a chloroplast). These form only by division of existing ones.
  • Unequal cytokinesis, example 1: human oogenesis. The egg cell keeps almost all the cytoplasm; a tiny polar body gets almost none.
  • Example 2: budding in yeast. A small bud receives a nucleus after nuclear division, then separates.
  • Chromosomes are still shared equally. Only the cytoplasm is unequal.
Quick check
?Which statement about the division of the cytoplasm between two daughter cells is correct?
Mission check

?A student counted 100 cells in a micrograph of a growing root tip. 85 cells had an intact nucleus with no visible chromosomes; 6 had condensed chromosomes scattered through the cell; 3 had chromosomes aligned across the equator; 4 had two groups of chromatids moving apart; 2 had two groups of chromosomes at the poles with nuclear envelopes forming. Which conclusion is correct?

More cells in a stage means more time spent in it. One frozen moment, counted carefully, is a clock.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Holozoic nutrition: ingest, digest (extracellular hydrolysis in the lumen), absorb across the gut lining, assimilate.

Mission 2

  • Isolating one type of organelle lets its function be tested directly: a new technique opened a new field.

Mission 3

  • RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand (the template) and joins complementary RNA nucleotides (A–U, C–G) to make mRNA.
  • mRNA has the coding strand's sequence with U for T; the DNA template is unchanged.
  • The nucleus separates transcription from translation, so mRNA can be modified first.

Mission 4

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 5

  • Chromatography separates leaf pigments; identify each by Rf (origin to spot ÷ origin to front) and colour, against references for the same solvent.
  • Pigments absorb only wavelengths whose energy matches an electron jump: mainly blue and red. Accessory pigments widen the range used.
  • Absorption spectrum: light absorbed by a pigment. Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength.

Mission 6

  • Every chromosome is replicated first (two sister chromatids); mitosis then sends one chromatid of each chromosome to each pole, so both daughter cells get an identical, complete set. Cytokinesis then divides the cytoplasm (cleavage furrow in animals, cell plate in plants).

Mission 7

  • Stages are identified from the chromosomes: none visible (interphase), scattered (prophase), in a line (metaphase), V-shaped groups moving apart (anaphase), clusters at the poles (telophase).
  • Cytokinesis is usually equal; oogenesis and yeast budding are unequal, but every daughter gets at least one mitochondrion.
