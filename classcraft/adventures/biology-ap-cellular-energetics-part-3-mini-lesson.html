Mission 1

Mission 1: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Make a guess: Switch off the light and a leaf stops making sugar within seconds, yet that stage needs no light. Why?

Mission 1

The Calvin cycle

  • Takes place in the stroma.
  • ATP and NADPH from the light reactions power the building of carbohydrate from CO2.
  • Using them returns ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺ to the light reactions, to be recharged.
  • The cycle does not use light directly. It runs in the light, while ATP and NADPH are being made.
  • You do not need the cycle's steps, molecules or enzymes.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a model of how the two stages of photosynthesis in a chloroplast are connected. Which set of arrows between the light reactions and the Calvin cycle should the model show?
Mission 1

Two stages, one system

  • Light off: no ATP or NADPH. The Calvin cycle soon stops.
  • CO2 removed (light on): the Calvin cycle stops using NADPH and ATP.
  • So NADP⁺ and ADP are not returned. The light reactions slow, and O2 release falls.
  • Each stage needs the other's products.
Mission check

All CO2 is then removed from the solution while the light stays on. Which prediction about the release of O2 by the chloroplasts over the next few minutes is best supported?

The two stages run as one system: stop the Calvin cycle, and the light reactions soon slow too.

Mission 2

Inside the mitochondrion

  • Outer membrane: smooth; encloses the organelle.
  • Inner membrane: folded into cristae. Holds the electron carriers and ATP synthase (see Mission 3).
  • Intermembrane space: the narrow gap between the two membranes.
  • Matrix: the fluid inside the inner membrane.
  • The matrix holds the enzymes of pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs (citric acid) cycle.
Mission 2

Pyruvate oxidation

  • Pyruvate is transported from the cytosol into the mitochondrion.
  • There it is oxidized: electrons go to NAD⁺, forming NADH.
  • One carbon leaves as CO2.
  • The remaining 2-carbon fragment feeds into the Krebs cycle.
Quick check
?A mutant strain of a hypothetical yeast species grows normally when O2 is absent, but when O2 is present it grows much more slowly than the wild type. A student hypothesizes that the mutant's mitochondria cannot take up pyruvate. Which investigation would best test this hypothesis?
Mission 2

The Krebs cycle

  • The Krebs cycle takes place in the mitochondrial matrix.
  • Each turn:
  • CO2 is released from organic intermediates.
  • ATP is made from ADP + Pᵢ.
  • Electrons are transferred to NAD⁺ and FAD, forming NADH and FADH2.
  • The cycle regenerates its starting compound, so it can turn again.
  • No O2 is used in the cycle itself.
Mission 2

Where the carbon goes

  • Glucose has 6 carbon atoms.
  • Glycolysis splits it into 2 pyruvate (3 C each). No CO2 yet.
  • Pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle release all 6 carbons as CO2.
  • The energy is now carried mostly by NADH and FADH2, on its way to the electron transport chain.
Mission 2

Why the cycle needs oxygen without using it

  • NAD⁺ and FAD are regenerated only when NADH and FADH2 pass their electrons to the ETC, which needs O2.
  • No O2 → no NAD⁺ → pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle stop.
  • Say: "the Krebs cycle depends on oxygen" — not: "the Krebs cycle uses oxygen".

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Make a guess: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Mission 3

Handing on electrons

  • NADH and FADH2 carry electrons from glycolysis and the Krebs cycle.
  • They deliver electrons (and protons) to a chain of carriers in the inner membrane, on the cristae.
  • NADH passes electrons to the first carrier and becomes NAD⁺ again.
  • Electrons pass from carrier to carrier in redox reactions: each carrier is reduced, then oxidized.
  • Each carrier is at a lower energy level than the last. Energy is released at each transfer.
Mission 3

Pumping protons

  • The energy released by electron flow pumps protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • The inner membrane is impermeable to H⁺, so they build up.
  • Result: a proton gradient across the inner membrane. It is electrochemical: a concentration difference and a charge difference.
  • More H⁺ = lower pH. The intermembrane space has a lower pH than the matrix.
  • Say: "protons are pumped across the membrane" — not: "electrons are pumped across".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares the mitochondrial matrix with the intermembrane space while electron transport is taking place?
Mission 3

ATP synthase: chemiosmosis

  • H⁺ return to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase, a channel protein in the inner membrane.
  • H⁺ flow down their gradient. The flow turns part of the enzyme.
  • That energy joins ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, on the matrix side.
  • Making ATP using energy from a proton gradient = chemiosmosis.
  • The whole process, electron flow + chemiosmosis = oxidative phosphorylation. Most of the cell's ATP is made this way.
  • Say: "protons flow through ATP synthase" — not: "water moves by osmosis".
Quick check
?In aerobic cellular respiration, which process is called oxidative phosphorylation?
Mission 3

Oxygen: the last acceptor

  • Oxygen accepts electrons from the last carrier, and protons from the matrix.
  • Product: water (metabolic water).
  • This keeps the last carrier oxidized, so electrons keep flowing.
  • No oxygen → the last carrier stays reduced → every carrier backs up → flow and proton pumping stop.
  • Then NADH builds up and no NAD⁺ is regenerated, so pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle stop too.
  • Say: "oxygen becomes water" — not: "oxygen becomes carbon dioxide".
Quick check

Mission 4

Mission 4: Mitochondria, chloroplasts and bacteria all make ATP with the same machine. What does that tell us?

Mission 4

Built for the job: the mitochondrion

  • Cristae: the folded inner membrane gives a large surface area for electron carriers and ATP synthase. More cristae → more ATP per second.
  • Very active cells (e.g. heart and flight muscle) have mitochondria packed with cristae.
  • Say: "cristae increase the area for ATP synthase and carriers" — not: "for absorbing oxygen or glucose".
Quick check
?The diagram represents typical mitochondria, drawn to the same scale, from two cell types of a hypothetical animal. Which prediction is best supported by the model?
Mission 4

The same machine in the chloroplast

  • In the thylakoid membrane, light excites electrons in chlorophyll (photosystems).
  • Excited electrons pass along a chain of carriers. The energy released pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space (lumen).
  • H⁺ flow back out through ATP synthase → ATP made in the stroma.
  • ATP made this way in light = photophosphorylation.
  • Electrons end on NADP⁺, reducing it to NADPH at photosystem I.

Which conclusion do the data support?

  • In 1966, André Jagendorf and Ernest Uribe tested the chemiosmotic theory with thylakoids from spinach chloroplasts.
  • They soaked thylakoids in an acid buffer (pH 4) in the dark, so H⁺ filled the thylakoid space.
  • Then they moved them, still in the dark, into an alkaline buffer (pH 8) with ADP + Pᵢ.
  • Their finding: ATP was made in the dark, with no light and no electron flow.
Mission 4

Prokaryotes: the original owners

  • Prokaryotes have no mitochondria. Their ETC and ATP synthase sit in the plasma membrane.
  • They pump H⁺ out of the cytoplasm; H⁺ flows back in through ATP synthase.
  • Aerobic prokaryotes use O2 as the terminal electron acceptor.
  • Anaerobic prokaryotes use other molecules, e.g. nitrate (NO3⁻) or sulfate (SO4²⁻). This is still respiration: an ETC with a terminal acceptor.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts descend from prokaryotes taken into an ancestral cell (endosymbiosis).
  • So the shared machine is best explained by inheritance from prokaryotes. Oxidative phosphorylation is conserved across all three domains.
Mission 4

Uncoupling: ATP or heat?

  • Normally H⁺ returns to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase: electron transport is coupled to ATP synthesis.
  • An uncoupling protein in the inner membrane lets H⁺ leak back without passing through ATP synthase.
  • Electron transport and O2 use continue; less ATP is made.
  • The gradient's energy is released as heat.
  • Brown fat cells are rich in this protein. Endotherms (e.g. newborn mammals, hibernators) use the heat to regulate body temperature.

Mission 5

Mission 5: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Make a guess: When oxygen runs out, muscles make lactate and yeast makes alcohol. What do they gain from it?

Mission 5

Fermentation

  • Without O2, cells still run glycolysis in the cytosol, making a little ATP.
  • Fermentation follows glycolysis. It lets glycolysis continue in the absence of O2.
  • Products: lactic acid (e.g. muscle cells, some bacteria) or alcohol (ethanol) and CO2 (e.g. yeast).
  • Fermentation, like respiration, is found in all forms of life.
Mission 5

Recycling NAD⁺

  • Glycolysis passes electrons to NAD⁺, forming NADH.
  • Without O2, the ETC cannot re-oxidize NADH. NAD⁺ would run out and glycolysis would stop.
  • Fermentation: NADH passes its electrons to pyruvate (or a product of it), forming lactic acid or alcohol.
  • This regenerates NAD⁺, so glycolysis keeps making ATP.
  • No ETC is used, and the fermentation step itself makes no ATP.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Calvin cycle in the stroma uses ATP and NADPH from the light reactions to make carbohydrate from CO2, returning ADP, Pᵢ and NADP⁺.

Mission 2

  • In the matrix, pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle release CO2, make some ATP and load electrons onto NAD⁺ and FAD as NADH and FADH2.

Mission 3

  • Electrons from reduced NAD and FAD flow along carriers in the inner membrane; the energy released pumps H⁺ into the intermembrane space; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase, making ATP (chemiosmosis); oxygen accepts the electrons and forms water.

Mission 4

  • Chemiosmosis works the same way in mitochondria and chloroplasts: carriers pump H⁺ across a membrane and ATP synthase uses the flow back to make ATP; only the energy source, the compartments and the last acceptor differ.
  • Prokaryotes run the same machine across their plasma membrane, some with acceptors other than O2; uncoupling releases the gradient's energy as heat.

Mission 5

  • Without oxygen only glycolysis makes ATP (net 2 per glucose); lactate (animals) or ethanol and carbon dioxide (yeast, plants) are the products.
  • Fermentation regenerates NAD⁺ so glycolysis can continue without O2, producing lactic acid or alcohol.
