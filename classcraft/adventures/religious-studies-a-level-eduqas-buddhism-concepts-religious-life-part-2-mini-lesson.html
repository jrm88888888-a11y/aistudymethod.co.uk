Mission 1: Why would anyone vow to stay in a world of suffering until every being is free?

Make a guess: Why would anyone vow to stay in a world of suffering until every being is free?

Mission 1

Bodhisattva: a being set on awakening

  • Bodhisattva: bodhi (awakening) + sattva (being): a being bound for full Buddhahood.
  • In the Pali texts the word names Gautama before his awakening, through many lives (the Jataka stories).
  • Tradition tells how, ages ago, the ascetic Sumedha met the Buddha Dipankara and resolved to become a Buddha himself.
  • In Mahayana Buddhism the bodhisattva becomes the ideal for everyone: not only one future Buddha, but all who make the vow.
Mission 1

The arhat: the worthy one

  • Arhat ("worthy one"): one who has fully ended greed, hatred and delusion, and so ends rebirth at death.
  • Four stages of awakening:
  • Stream-enterer: has seen the Dhamma; at most seven more lives.
  • Your spec says stream-winner.
  • Once-returner: one more human birth.
  • Non-returner: no return to the human world.
  • Arhat: free.
Mission 1

Bodhicitta and the vow

  • Bodhicitta, "the awakening mind": the wish to become a Buddha for the sake of all beings.
  • It is made formal in the bodhisattva vow, often taken in a ceremony before a teacher.
  • A common East Asian form, the Four Great Vows (paraphrase): beings are countless, I vow to free them; delusions are endless, I vow to end them; teachings are boundless, I vow to learn them; the Buddha way is unsurpassed, I vow to reach it.
  • Not "I postpone nirvana forever". Many Mahayana texts teach that a Buddha reaches a non-abiding nirvana: free, but never withdrawn from beings.
Mission 1Read the source

Shantideva's aspiration

Shantideva (8th century, India), Bodhicaryavatara ("Entering the Way of Awakening"), chapter 10, verse 55 (paraphrase):

  • For as long as space remains, and as long as there are living beings, may I too remain, to drive away the suffering of the world.
  • The 14th Dalai Lama often names this verse as a lifelong inspiration.

Q1 What two things set the length of the bodhisattva's commitment?

Show answer

The existence of space and of suffering beings.

Q2 Does the verse say the bodhisattva never reaches awakening?

Show answer

No: it is about staying to help; on the non-abiding view, a Buddha does both.

Mission 1

The six perfections

  • Paramita, "perfection" or "gone to the far shore": a quality practised to its fullest.
  • Dana: generosity, giving even one's life.
  • Sila: ethical conduct.
  • Ksanti: patience, enduring harm without anger.
  • Virya: energy, joyful effort.
  • Dhyana: meditation, a stable mind.
  • Prajna: wisdom, seeing the emptiness of all things.
  • A list of ten adds skilful means, vow, power and knowledge, one for each of the ten stages.
Mission 1

Wisdom and compassion: two wings

  • Compassion (karuna) without wisdom becomes attachment: "I am the helper; you are the helped."
  • Wisdom (prajna) without compassion becomes cold: seeing emptiness, but doing nothing.
  • So the perfection of wisdom guides the other five, like a sighted person leading others on a road (an image from the Perfection of Wisdom texts, paraphrase).
  • True giving sees no fixed giver, gift or receiver: all are empty. It gives freely, without pride.
Mission 1

The ten bhumis

  • Bhumi, "ground" or stage. The classic list is in the Dasabhumika Sutra.
  • 1 Joyful · 2 Stainless · 3 Luminous · 4 Radiant · 5 Hard to conquer · 6 Facing (wisdom) · 7 Far-going · 8 Immovable · 9 Good intelligence · 10 Cloud of Dharma.
  • Stage 1 begins with the first direct insight into emptiness; each stage perfects one paramita (1 generosity … 6 wisdom … 10 knowledge).
  • From stage 8 the bodhisattva cannot fall back.
  • Traditionally the whole path takes three countless ages.

Mission 1Diversity

Arhat and bodhisattva compared

  • Goal: arhat — nirvana, the end of rebirth; bodhisattva — full Buddhahood.
  • Motive: arhat — freedom from suffering, then teaching others; bodhisattva — freedom of all beings from the start.
  • Length: arhat — possible in this life; bodhisattva — countless lives.
  • Who: Theravada — the arhat is the goal for most; a few aim at Buddhahood. Mahayana — the bodhisattva path is for all.
  • Fairness check: calling arhats "selfish" is a Mahayana polemic. In the Pali texts the Buddha sent his first arhat disciples out to teach "for the welfare of the many" (Vinaya, Mahavagga I.11, paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the bodhisattva ideal higher than the arhat ideal?

  • Claim: The bodhisattva ideal is a higher ideal than that of the arhat.
  • For: It joins wisdom to universal compassion; no one is left behind.
  • For: It follows the Buddha's own example: he chose Buddhahood and taught for 45 years.
  • Against: Arhats also teach and serve; the contrast is overdrawn.
  • Against: A goal that is reachable in this life may motivate more than one that is countless lives away.
  • Judgement criteria: Which ideal fits the Buddha's teaching as recorded? Which is reachable? Does a higher aim make a better path?
  • Your spec's issues: bodhisattva and arhat as an essential feature of Buddhism; their relative legitimacy.

Mission 2: Why did the Buddha's first sermon sound like a doctor's diagnosis?

Make a guess: Why did the Buddha's first sermon sound like a doctor's diagnosis?

Mission 2

The first sermon

  • After his awakening, the Buddha walked to the Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath), near Varanasi (Benares), to his five former companions in asceticism.
  • The sermon is the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion" (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).
  • It opens with the Middle Way: avoid both indulgence in sense pleasure and harsh self-torment.
  • Then it sets out the Four Noble Truths.
  • One listener, Kondanna, understood: whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing. He became the first disciple to "see the Dhamma".
Mission 2

Four truths, four tasks

  • In the sermon, the Buddha teaches:
  • Dukkha: there is suffering, unsatisfactoriness. Task: fully understand it.
  • Samudaya (origin): dukkha arises from craving (tanha). Task: abandon it.
  • Nirodha (cessation): dukkha ends when craving ends. Task: realise it.
  • Magga (path): the Noble Eightfold Path leads there. Task: develop it.
  • In the sutta the Buddha claims full awakening only after knowing each truth, its task, and that the task is done: twelve aspects in all.
  • They are "noble" (ariya) truths: truths as seen by the noble ones, who have realised them.
Mission 2

The doctor analogy

  • A traditional comparison treats the Buddha as a physician:
  • Illness = dukkha · Cause = tanha · Cure is possible = nirodha · Prescription = magga.
  • Buddhaghosa uses it in the Visuddhimagga (XVI): the truths are like a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine.
  • What it teaches: the Buddha diagnoses in order to heal; the first truth is not the last word.
  • Its limits: the patient must take the medicine personally. No one can be cured by someone else's practice.
  • Your spec: "the medical metaphor (the Buddha as a physician, diagnosing the symptoms, identifying cause and providing a prescription)".
Mission 2Read the source

The second and third truths

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (paraphrase):

  • Origin: it is craving that leads to renewed existence, bound up with delight and lust, seeking pleasure now here, now there: craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.
  • Cessation: the complete fading away and ending of that same craving: giving it up, letting it go, being free of it.

Q1 Does the sutta say pleasure itself causes dukkha?

Show answer

No: craving, the thirst that chases pleasure "here and there", is the origin.

Q2 How is the third truth defined?

Show answer

As the ending of the very craving named in the second truth: the cure removes the cause.

  • Your spec translates tanha as thirst.

Mission 2

Three kinds of craving

  • Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: food, comfort, sex, entertainment, possessions.
  • Bhava-tanha: craving for existence or becoming: to be someone, to be admired, to live on for ever.
  • Vibhava-tanha: craving for non-existence: to get rid of what we dislike, even to stop existing.
  • All three grasp at what is impermanent, so all three bring dukkha: the pleasure fades, the identity cracks, the escape never arrives.
  • Vibhava-tanha shows that pushing away is craving too, not freedom from it.
Mission 2

Tanha in the chain of causes

  • In dependent origination, tanha is link 8 of the twelve nidanas: conditioned by feeling, it conditions grasping.
  • So the second truth is a short form of the whole chain: craving is the most practical point to work on, while ignorance is its root.
  • Ending craving needs wisdom, not just willpower: craving thrives on not seeing anicca and anatta.

Mission 2

Nirodha and magga

  • Nirodha: the "cutting off" of craving through detachment: no longer gripping what changes. This is nibbana.
  • Magga: the Noble Eightfold Path, in three trainings: wisdom (right view, right intention), ethics (right speech, action, livelihood), meditation (right effort, mindfulness, concentration). Full study.

Mission 3: The Eightfold Path has eight parts. Is it a ladder you climb, or something else?

Make a guess: The Eightfold Path has eight parts. Is it a ladder you climb, or something else?

Mission 3

The Middle Way

  • In his first sermon (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11) the Buddha names two extremes to avoid (paraphrase):
  • Indulgence in sense pleasures: low, worldly, unprofitable.
  • Self-mortification (harsh asceticism): painful, unprofitable.
  • Between them, the Middle Way leads to calm, insight, awakening and nirvana. The Buddha says that Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • It mirrors his life: palace luxury, then six years of harsh ascetic practice, including extreme fasting, then awakening after accepting food.
  • The path is the fourth Noble Truth: magga, the way to the end of dukkha (see Mission 2).
Mission 3

Eight factors, three trainings

  • Each factor is samma: "right" in the sense of complete, fitting, skilful; not "right" as opposed to a rule broken.
  • Wisdom (panna): 1 right view (understanding the Four Noble Truths) · 2 right intention (renunciation, goodwill, harmlessness).
  • Ethics / morality (sila): 3 right speech · 4 right action · 5 right livelihood.
  • Meditation (samadhi): 6 right effort · 7 right mindfulness · 8 right concentration.
  • The nun Dhammadinna teaches that the path is included in the three trainings, not the reverse: each factor sorts under one training (Culavedalla Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 44).
  • Practice usually begins with ethics, which steadies the mind for meditation, which ripens into wisdom; but the list opens with view.
  • Your spec: the threefold trainings (wisdom, morality, meditation).
Mission 3Read the source

The way, in the Dhammapada

> "The best of ways is the eightfold; the best of truths the four words; the best of virtues passionlessness; the best of men he who has eyes to see." (Dhammapada 273, Müller 1881)

> "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller 1881)

  • "The four words" = the Four Noble Truths.

Show answer

Mission 3

Inter-reliant, not linear

  • "Path" does not mean eight steps in a row. In the Mahacattarisaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 117) the Buddha says (paraphrase):
  • Right view comes first: it lets you tell right intention, speech, action and livelihood from wrong.
  • Right view, right effort and right mindfulness "run around" each of the other factors: you see what is skilful, strive for it, and stay aware of it.
  • Bhikkhu Bodhi (b. 1944), The Noble Eightfold Path (1984): the factors are better called components than steps; with maturity all eight are practised together.
  • A common image: a spiral. Ethics, meditation and wisdom feed each other, and each turn goes deeper.
Mission 3

Speech, action, livelihood

  • Defined in the Magga-vibhanga Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 45.8) (paraphrase):
  • Right speech: no lying, no divisive speech, no harsh speech, no idle chatter.
  • Right action: no killing, no taking what is not given, no sexual misconduct.
  • Right livelihood: giving up wrong ways of earning a living. Lay followers should not trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poison (Vanijja Sutta, Anguttara Nikaya 5.177).
  • These overlap with the five precepts (see Mission 4). Practice today: a Buddhist may refuse work in an arms factory, avoid gossip at work, or choose a caring profession.
Mission 3Diversity

One path, different settings

  • Theravada: the path leads to arahantship. Ethics, meditation and wisdom are often taught in that order (Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga, 5th century CE, is structured as sila, samadhi, panna).
  • Mahayana: the path sits inside the bodhisattva way; the six perfections (giving, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom) map onto the three trainings. Karuna stands beside wisdom as one of the two wings of awakening.
  • Zen: the path is lived in daily tasks and zazen rather than taught as a list; Pure Land: many rely on Amida's vow more than on their own effort.
  • Contemporary: the path is taught in the West as a practical ethic for lay people; Engaged Buddhists apply right livelihood and right action to social issues.
  • Your spec: the Eightfold Path "within the context of the diversity of Buddhism".
Mission 3Evaluate

Is any part of the path more important?

  • Claim: Some factors of the Eightfold Path matter more than others.
  • For: Right view comes first and guides the rest (Majjhima Nikaya 117).
  • For: Meditation (samadhi) is where insight happens; without it ethics may stay on the surface.
  • Against: The factors are inter-reliant components (Bodhi); remove one and the others weaken.
  • Against: Ethics is the foundation most lay Buddhists actually practise; and on Keown's view it is part of the goal itself.
  • Judgement criteria: What does the text say about order? What do lay and monastic Buddhists practise? Is the path a moral guide for every Buddhist, including Pure Land and Zen?
  • Your spec issue: "the usefulness of the Eightfold Path as a moral guide for all Buddhists".

Mission 4: Buddhists keep precepts that no god commands. So what happens when one is broken?

Make a guess: Buddhists keep precepts that no god commands. So what happens when one is broken?

Mission 4

Training rules, not commandments

  • Each precept is a sikkhapada: a training rule (literally a "step of training").
  • The formula is spoken in the first person: "I undertake the training rule to abstain from …" (sikkhapadam samadiyami).
  • No god gives them and no one enforces them on lay people: they are undertaken voluntarily, usually after going for refuge.
  • Breaking one is not a sin against God. It is akusala action with karmic results; the response is to recognise it, resolve again and retake the precept.
  • They describe how an awakened person naturally behaves; for everyone else they are aspirations to train towards.
  • Your spec: precepts as training aspirations rather than commandments.
Mission 4

The five precepts

  • I undertake the training rule to abstain from:
  • 1 Killing or harming living beings (panatipata).
  • 2 Taking what is not given (adinnadana).
  • 3 Sexual misconduct (kamesu micchacara).
  • 4 False speech (musavada).
  • 5 Intoxicating drinks and drugs that cause heedlessness (sura-meraya-majja-pamadatthana).
  • Lay Buddhists in Theravada countries often chant them in Pali at the temple, after the three refuges.
  • The fifth protects the other four: an intoxicated mind breaks precepts more easily.
Mission 4

The positive side

  • Each restraint has a positive quality to grow:
  • 1 not harming → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion.
  • 2 not stealing → generosity (dana).
  • 3 no sexual misconduct → contentment and faithfulness.
  • 4 no false speech → truthfulness.
  • 5 no intoxicants → mindfulness and clarity.
  • This pairing is widely taught today (e.g. the Triratna "positive precepts"; Thich Nhat Hanh's Five Mindfulness Trainings, which reword the precepts as commitments).
  • So the precepts are about building character, not only avoiding acts (kusala roots).

Mission 4

Eight and ten precepts

  • Eight precepts: kept by many lay Buddhists on uposatha (observance) days and on retreat. The third becomes celibacy, and three more are added: no food after midday; no entertainment or adornment; no high or luxurious beds.
  • Ten precepts (dasa sila): kept by novices (samaneras and samaneris), and by some lay women renunciants (e.g. dasa sil matas in Sri Lanka).
  • Ten = the eight, with entertainment and adornment as separate rules (7 and 8), plus 10: not accepting gold and silver (money).
  • Fully ordained monks (bhikkhus) keep the Patimokkha: 227 rules in the Theravada Vinaya.
  • Your spec: the dasa sila (ten precepts).
Mission 4

Lay and monastic sanghas

  • The monastic sangha keeps the fuller precepts and lives the ideal of renunciation; the lay sangha keeps five (or eight on observance days).
  • The roles are complementary: lay people give food, robes and support (dana) and gain merit (punya); monastics give the Dhamma: teaching, ritual and example.
  • By keeping the precepts fully, the monastic sangha keeps them on behalf of the wider sangha: it preserves the Vinaya and shows that the path can be lived.
  • A lay person may keep the ten precepts for a time, e.g. a short ordination as a novice, common for young men in Thailand and Myanmar (see Mission 5).
  • Your spec: "the role of the monastic sangha in keeping the precepts on behalf of the wider sangha".

Mission 4Evaluate

Are the precepts still relevant today?

  • Claim: The traditional precepts are still a sufficient guide for Buddhists today.
  • For: They are short, shared by all schools and easy to apply; they cover the main harms in any society.
  • For: As training rules they are flexible: intention and circumstance are taken into account.
  • Against: Modern questions (online speech, factory farming, recreational drugs, digital piracy) need interpretation the wording does not give; hence reworded trainings (Thich Nhat Hanh).
  • Against: Some monastic rules (e.g. not handling money) are hard to keep in modern economies; some monasteries use lay stewards, others are criticised for laxity.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the spirit or the letter more important? Who interprets them: the individual, the sangha, or tradition? Do the dasa sila still regulate the monastic sangha effectively?
  • Spec issues: "the relative importance of the dasa sila as a means of regulating the sangha"; "the contemporary relevance of precepts and monastic life".

Mission 5

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community"; the third of the Three Refuges (Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha).
  • Monastic Sangha: ordained monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), living by the Vinaya.
  • Noble Sangha (ariya-sangha): all who have reached at least stream-entry, monastic or lay. For Theravada, the refuge in its fullest sense.
  • Fourfold assembly: monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen together.
Mission 5

The exchange

  • Monks may not farm, cook stored food or (by the Vinaya) handle money. They depend on lay people.
  • Lay people give material support: food on the dawn alms round, robes at Kathina (after the rains retreat), medicine, lodging.
  • Monks give the Dhamma: teaching, example, chanting, counsel.
  • Lay people gain merit: the Sangha is called the best field of merit, where good deeds grow most.
  • On the alms round the monk does not ask and does not thank: the giver is grateful for the chance to give.
  • On observance days (uposatha), lay people visit the wat and take extra precepts.
Mission 5Read the source

Mutual help

The Itivuttaka (107), paraphrase: the Buddha tells his monks that householders are very helpful to them, providing robes, food, lodging and medicine; and the monks are very helpful to householders, teaching them the Dhamma. So, supporting each other, both live the holy life.

"As the bee collects nectar and departs without injuring the flower, or its colour or scent, so let a sage dwell in his village." (Dhammapada 49, Müller)

Q1 What does each side give the other in Itivuttaka 107?

Show answer

Q2 What does the image of the bee say about how a monk should receive alms?

Show answer

Mission 5

The dasa sila: ten precepts

  • Lay people keep the five precepts: no harming life, taking what is not given, sexual misconduct, false speech, intoxicants.
  • On observance days many keep eight.
  • Novices keep the dasa sila (ten precepts): the five (with full celibacy), plus no eating after midday; no dancing, music or shows; no garlands, perfumes or adornments; no high or luxurious beds; no accepting gold and silver (money).
  • Each is a training rule (sikkhapada): "I undertake the training rule to abstain from …". An aspiration, not a commandment from a god.
  • The monastic sangha keeps the precepts fully on behalf of the wider sangha: an example to follow and a worthy field of merit.
  • So the roles are complementary: different precepts, one path.

Mission 5Evaluate

"The relationship between monks and lay people is an equal exchange."

  • For: Itivuttaka 107: each side is "very helpful" to the other; neither could live the path alone.
  • For: lay people gain merit and teaching; monks gain the support to practise.
  • Against: the gift of the Dhamma "exceeds all gifts" (Dhammapada 354), so the exchange is not equal in value.
  • Against: monks are materially dependent; lay donors can hold power over a temple.
  • Against: in Theravada, the monastic life is usually seen as the surer path to Nibbana, though lay people can also awaken.
  • Judgement criteria: Equal in what: need, value or status? Is a field of merit "receiving" or "giving"? Do modern changes (see Mission 6) alter the balance?
  • Spec issue: "the contemporary relevance of precepts and monastic life".

Mission 6

Changing roles

  • Over the 20th century, the Thai state took over many monastic roles: schools (from the 1920s), hospitals, social welfare.
  • Cities grew; many people now meet monks mainly at ceremonies, funerals and on the alms round.
  • Fewer men make the monastic life lifelong; temporary ordination continues but often for shorter periods.
  • Money scandals and a trade in blessed amulets have damaged trust in some monks.
  • New roles have grown:
  • Development monks: village co-operatives, rice banks.
  • Ecology monks: "ordaining" trees with saffron cloth to protect forests.
  • Care: hospices, e.g. for people with HIV/AIDS (from the 1990s).
  • Media: Dhamma talks on radio, TV and social media.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Monastic life and the precepts are still relevant today."

  • For: monks keep the Dhamma alive and teach it, now online as well as in person.
  • For: a life of few possessions is a living challenge to consumerism.
  • For: new roles (hospices, ecology, media teaching) show the Sangha serving modern needs.
  • Against: the state now does what monks once did (schools, health care).
  • Against: scandals and commercialisation suggest the Vinaya is hard to keep in a money economy.
  • Against: lay meditation movements and Western groups such as Triratna show the path without traditional monasticism.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevant to whom: Thai villagers, city workers, Western converts? Is relevance measured by numbers, by service, or by faithfulness to the Dhamma?
  • Spec issue: "the contemporary relevance of precepts and monastic life".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A bodhisattva vows to reach Buddhahood for the sake of all beings (bodhicitta); the arhat reaches nirvana through four stages.
  • Six perfections: generosity, ethics, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom; wisdom guides the rest, and compassion moves them.
  • The ten bhumis map the path stage by stage, from Joyful to Cloud of Dharma.

Mission 2

  • The Deer Park sermon (SN 56.11): dukkha (understand), samudaya: craving (abandon), nirodha (realise), magga: the Eightfold Path (develop).
  • The doctor analogy: illness, cause, cure, prescription.
  • Three cravings: for sense pleasures, for existence, for non-existence.
  • Tanha is link 8 of the twelve nidanas.

Mission 3

  • The Middle Way avoids indulgence and self-mortification; the Buddha identifies it with the Noble Eightfold Path, the fourth Noble Truth.
  • Eight factors in three trainings: wisdom (view, intention), ethics (speech, action, livelihood), meditation (effort, mindfulness, concentration); practised together, not one by one.

Mission 4

  • The precepts are training rules (sikkhapada), undertaken voluntarily, not commandments; breaking one is akusala and is met by renewed resolve.
  • Five for lay people, eight on observance days, ten (dasa sila) for novices; each restraint has a positive quality to grow.
  • Lay and monastic sanghas have complementary roles; monastics keep the fuller precepts on behalf of the wider sangha.

Mission 5

  • "Sangha" means the monastic community, the noble ones (stream-entry and beyond), or the whole community of Buddhists.
  • Lay people give material support and make merit; monks give the Dhamma.
  • Novices keep the dasa sila (ten precepts) as training rules.

Mission 6

  • The Thai state has taken over schooling and health care; monks have found new roles (development, ecology, hospices, media); scandals have damaged trust in some monks.
  • Whether monastic life and the precepts are still relevant is debated: service and simplicity versus changed roles and scandal.
