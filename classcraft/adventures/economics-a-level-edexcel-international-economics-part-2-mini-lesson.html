Mission 1

From no trade to free trade

  • Start with domestic demand and domestic supply only (no trade). Price settles at 7, quantity 50 (illustrative data).
  • Now open the border. A small country is a price taker: it can buy or sell any amount at the world price (Pw).
  • World price 4, below the domestic price:
    • consumers buy more: 80
    • domestic firms produce less: 20
    • the gap, 80 − 20 = 60, is imports.
  • Say: "domestic supply" — not just "supply": with trade, total supply at Pw is domestic output plus imports.
Quick check
?A country that did not trade in rice opens its market. The world price of rice is below its domestic no-trade price. Which outcome follows?
Mission 1

The protectionist toolbox

Protectionism: government measures that shield domestic producers from foreign competition.

  • Tariff: a tax on imports (per unit, or a percentage of value).
  • Quota: a legal limit on the quantity (or value) of a good that may be imported.
  • Subsidy: a payment to domestic producers, so they can compete with imports or sell more abroad (export subsidy).
  • Embargo: a ban on trade in a good, or with a country.
  • Administrative barriers (non-tariff barriers, "red tape"): standards, regulations, licences and slow customs checks that make importing harder.
Mission 1

The tariff diagram

Follow the diagram step by step (illustrative data):

1. Free trade at Pw = 4: domestic output 20, consumption 80, imports 60.

2. A tariff of 2 per unit: the price in the home market rises to Pw + tariff = 6.

3. Domestic output extends to 40; consumption contracts to 60; imports fall to 20.

4. Government revenue = tariff × imports after the tariff: the rectangle.

5. Two triangles: the deadweight (welfare) loss.

  • Producer side: high-cost domestic firms replace cheaper imports (wasted resources).
  • Consumer side: buyers who valued the good above the world price no longer buy it.
  • Say: "domestic supply extends" — not: "supply increases": the curve does not shift.
Mission 1

Worked example: counting a tariff's effects

Tariff of 2 per unit, Pw 4 → 6 (illustrative data):

Mission 1

Quotas

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, free-trade imports 60.

  • A quota of 20 units: once 20 units have come in, no more imports.
  • Supply in the home market = domestic supply + 20: draw S + quota, 20 units right of domestic supply, starting at Pw.
  • Price rises until demand meets S + quota: price 6, consumption 60, domestic output 40.
  • Same price and quantities as the tariff of 2, with one key difference:
    • the gap (6 − 4) × 20 = 40 is not government revenue.
    • It goes to whoever holds the import licences (domestic importers or foreign exporters): the quota rent.
    • If foreign exporters hold the licences, the 40 leaves the country: an extra loss to the home economy.
  • If demand grows, a quota lets the price rise further; a tariff lets imports grow at the same price.
Quick check
?A government replaces a tariff on imported shoes with an import quota. The quota is set so that the domestic price of shoes stays exactly the same. What changes?
Mission 1

Subsidies to domestic producers

Same market (illustrative data): Pw 4, imports 60. Subsidy of 2 per unit to domestic producers.

  • Domestic supply shifts right (down by 2): S → S + subsidy.
  • The price in the home market stays at Pw: consumers are unaffected (still buy 80).
  • Domestic output rises 20 → 40; imports fall 60 → 40.
  • Government pays 2 × 40 = 80: a cost to taxpayers (opportunity cost of other spending).
  • Harder to spot than a tariff, and it does not raise prices, but it must be paid for.
Mission 1

Embargoes, red tape and other barriers

  • Embargo: a total ban. Real case: in August 2014 Russia banned many food imports from the EU, USA and others.
  • Administrative barriers: product standards (safety, labelling, health) and regulations that imports must meet.
    • Real case: the EU bans beef from cattle given growth hormones, which shuts out much US and Canadian beef. The WTO ruled against the ban in 1998; the EU kept it.
  • Standards can protect health, safety and the environment. They become protectionism when they are designed to block imports rather than reduce real risks.
Quick check
?Country X requires all imported toys to be tested in its own laboratories, which takes six months. Domestic toys are tested within a week. There is no limit on numbers and no tax. Which tool of protection is this?
Mission 1

Real case: washing machines, 2018

  • In 2018 the USA put safeguard tariffs on imported large washing machines.
  • The tariff rate was higher once imports passed a set number of units each year.
  • Researchers (Flaaen, Hortaçsu and Tintelnot, 2020) found:
    • washer prices rose by nearly 12%
    • dryers, which were not taxed, rose by about the same amount: dryers are complements, often bought as a pair, and sellers raised both prices
  • Domestic brands raised their prices too. Counting washers and dryers together, buyers in the USA paid more than the tariff itself.
  • Lesson: a tariff's cost can spread into related markets.
Mission 1

Evaluate: how much harm does a tariff do?

It depends on…

  • Elasticities. The more price-elastic domestic demand and supply, the more imports fall and the larger the welfare-loss triangles.
  • Country size. A large importer can push the world price down, so foreign producers bear part of the tariff; a small country cannot.
  • Retaliation. Partners may answer with tariffs on this country's exports.
  • Time. Protected firms may invest and catch up, or grow complacent.
  • The tool. Quotas fix quantity, so rising demand pushes prices up; tariffs raise revenue; subsidies avoid higher prices but cost taxpayers.
  • Administration and evasion. Tariffs invite smuggling and lobbying for exemptions.
Mission check

?A small country puts a tariff on imported steel. It cannot affect the world price of steel. Which statement is correct?

In a small country, home buyers bear a tariff through higher prices; home firms and the government gain less than buyers lose.

Mission 2

Free trade: winners and losers

Free trade raises total output and consumption (comparative advantage). But:

  • Winners: consumers (lower prices, more choice), exporters, firms buying cheaper imported inputs.
  • Losers: firms and workers in import-competing industries.
  • Gains are spread thinly over millions of buyers; losses are concentrated in particular towns and industries.
  • Losers are easier to organise, so they lobby for protection.
Mission 2

For protection: new and strategic industries

  • Infant (sunrise) industry: a new domestic industry has high average costs, because it is small and inexperienced.
    • Established foreign firms produce at scale, at lower average cost.
    • Temporary protection lets the infant grow, gain economies of scale and learn, until it can compete at the world price.
    • Then the protection should be removed.
  • National security / strategic industries: food, energy, steel, defence equipment. Relying on imports is risky if trade is cut off by war or sanctions.
  • Say: "infant industry" — not "small firm": the argument is about costs falling as the industry grows.
Mission 2

For protection: unfair trade and standards

  • Dumping: selling exports below cost, or below the price charged at home, often supported by state subsidies.
    • Aim: win market share abroad and drive out rivals.
    • Anti-dumping duties are allowed under WTO rules, if dumping and injury are shown.
  • Unfair competition: rivals backed by state subsidies, or by very weak labour or environmental rules.
  • Health and safety: block unsafe food, medicines or toys.
  • Environmental standards: stop goods made in ways that cause serious pollution or harm wildlife.
  • These arguments are strongest when the risk is real; they are weakest when standards are a disguise for protecting home firms.
Mission 2

For protection: jobs, the balance of payments and revenue

  • Protecting jobs: slow the decline of sunset (declining) industries, giving workers time to retrain and limiting structural unemployment.
  • Balance of payments: tariffs switch spending from imports to home goods, reducing a current account deficit (at least for a time).
  • Government revenue: tariffs are easy to collect at the border. They matter most in low-income countries with weak income-tax systems.
Quick check
?A government notices that a foreign producer is selling tyres in its market at a price lower than the producer charges in its own country, and below its cost of production. Domestic tyre makers are losing sales. Which argument for protection fits best?
Mission 2

Against protection

  • Misallocation of resources: resources stay in industries where the country has no comparative advantage.
  • Higher prices and less choice for consumers. These hit poorer households hard, as they spend more of their income on goods.
  • Increased costs for firms that use imported inputs (steel for car makers, parts for electronics).
  • Inefficiency: protected firms lack the incentive to cut costs or innovate.
  • Retaliation: other countries answer with their own tariffs: a trade war that hurts exporters.
  • Reduced export competitiveness: dearer inputs and retaliation make exports harder to sell.
Mission 2

Real case: steel tariffs and retaliation, 2018

  • In March 2018 the USA put tariffs of 25% on steel and 10% on aluminium imports, using a national security argument.
  • The EU, Canada, Mexico, China and others retaliated.
    • The EU's duties (June 2018) hit US whiskey, motorcycles and jeans: goods from industries unrelated to steel.
  • US steelmakers gained protection; US firms that use steel faced higher input costs.
  • The case shows three arguments at once: security (for), retaliation (against) and higher costs for users (against).
Quick check
?Country Z puts a tariff on imported steel to protect its steelworks. Z's car makers, which export most of their cars, buy steel as a key input. What is the most likely effect on Z's car industry?
Mission 2

Evaluate: free trade or protection?

It depends on…

  • The real problem. For security, health or pollution, a targeted tool (regulation, a subsidy, a tax on pollution) usually does less harm than a tariff on all imports.
  • Whether protection is temporary. Infant industries need a clear end date; many never "grow up", and firms lobby to keep protection.
  • Retaliation and size. A small country gains little leverage; a trade war can cost exporters more than protection saves.
  • Time. Protection can save jobs in the short run; the long-run costs of inefficiency build up.
  • Stage of development. Arguments for diversification and revenue are stronger for low-income economies.
Quick check
?A country's textile industry is declining because of cheaper imports, and its workers face long-term unemployment. The government's aim is to help those workers. Which policy targets this aim most directly?
Mission check

?A developing country puts a tariff on imported solar panels to help its new solar-panel industry grow. Which condition makes this infant-industry argument strongest?

The infant-industry case holds only if protection is temporary and the industry's costs really fall as it grows.

Mission 3

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: £1 = $1.25. So $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system, the government or central bank sets the rate; a change is a devaluation or revaluation (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 3

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate £1 = $1.30.
  • A £500 phone sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans cost a UK buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = £50.
  • The pound appreciates to $1.50. Phone: 500 × 1.50 = $750. Jeans: 65 ÷ 1.50 = £43.33.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 3

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The exchange rate changes from 1 euro = 160 yen to 1 euro = 176 yen. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Much of the trading is for financial investment and speculation, not to pay for goods.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 3

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • US central bank (the Federal Reserve): top of its target range for interest rates 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission 3Evaluate

How far can you predict a currency?

  • It depends on:
    • several factors at once: interest rates may rise while growth slows; the effects can offset
    • expectations: speculators act on what they expect, so a rate can move before anything else changes
    • relative, not absolute: what matters is a country's interest rates, inflation and growth compared with other countries'
    • time horizon: day to day, financial flows dominate; over years, trade and competitiveness matter more
  • A central bank may also intervene (see Mission 4).

Every exchange rate change starts with a shift: who now wants more of the currency, and who wants to sell more of it?

Mission 4

Three exchange rate systems

  • Floating: demand and supply in the currency market set the rate (see Mission 3). A rise is an appreciation, a fall a depreciation.
  • Fixed (a peg): the central bank commits to one rate against another currency or a basket. A deliberate cut in the official rate is a devaluation; a deliberate rise, a revaluation.
  • Managed float: the rate floats, but the central bank steps in to limit or steer its movements.
  • Real examples: the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at about 7.80 since 1983; the euro floats; Singapore manages its currency against a basket of its trading partners' currencies.
  • Say: "the pound depreciated" for a floating currency — not: "the pound was devalued". Devaluation is a policy decision under a fixed rate.
Quick check
?A country pegs its currency to the US dollar. Its government announces that the official rate will be cut from 2.0 to 1.6 US dollars per unit of its currency. Which term describes this?
Mission 4

Holding a fixed rate

  • Step 1: the market for the currency clears at the peg.
  • Step 2: demand for the currency falls (D1 → D2). At the peg, supply now exceeds demand: the market rate would fall below the peg.
  • Step 3: the central bank closes the gap. It buys its own currency, paying with foreign currency reserves. This adds to demand: D2 → D3, back to the peg.
  • Other tools: raise interest rates (attracts financial inflows, so more demand for the currency); exchange controls (limits on buying foreign currency); borrowing reserves from abroad.
  • Pressure the other way (demand rises): the central bank sells its own currency and builds up reserves, or cuts interest rates.

Mission 4

Overvalued and undervalued currencies

  • Overvalued: the rate is held above the market-clearing rate.
    • Excess supply of the currency: the central bank must keep buying it, so reserves drain.
    • Exports are dearer abroad and imports cheaper: a current account deficit is likely; imported inflation is low.
  • Undervalued: the rate is held below the market-clearing rate.
    • Excess demand for the currency: the central bank sells it, so reserves pile up.
    • Exports are cheap: export-led growth, but imported inflation and complaints from trading partners.
  • Real example: from September 2011 to January 2015, the Swiss National Bank set a floor of 1.20 Swiss francs per euro, selling francs to stop the franc rising further.
  • Real example: China held the yuan at about 8.28 per US dollar until July 2005, then let it rise gradually in a managed system.
  • Competitive devaluation: lowering the rate on purpose to win exports. If others do the same, the gains cancel out.
Quick check
?A central bank holds its currency at a rate above the market-clearing rate. The diagram shows demand for and supply of the currency at the managed rate. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Why have an exchange rate policy?

  • Lower inflation: a strong, stable currency keeps import prices down and can anchor expectations.
  • Exports and jobs: a weaker currency makes exports cheaper abroad.
  • Certainty: stable rates help firms that trade and invest across borders.
  • Fix a current account deficit: a devaluation can switch spending toward home goods.
  • Tools: buying and selling currency (reserves), interest rates, exchange controls, or a formal peg.
  • The targets can clash: a weak currency helps exports but adds to inflation.

It depends on:

  • Credibility and reserves: on 16 September 1992 the UK raised interest rates from 10% to 12% in one day to hold the pound in Europe's Exchange Rate Mechanism (ERM), then left the system that evening.
  • Where shocks come from: a floating rate absorbs shocks from abroad; a peg imports the anchor country's interest rates, which may not suit.
  • Openness and trade links: a small, open economy trading mainly with one partner (Hong Kong, with the US dollar) gains more from a peg.
  • Inflation history: a peg can import credibility, but if inflation stays higher than the anchor's, the currency becomes overvalued.
Mission check

?A country pegs its currency to the US dollar. A boom in world demand for its exports raises demand for its currency. What must its central bank do to keep the peg?

To hold a peg, the central bank must supply or absorb whatever gap opens at the fixed rate, in either direction.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Free trade at a world price below the domestic price: consumption rises, domestic output falls, imports fill the gap.
  • A tariff or quota raises the domestic price: home producers gain, consumers lose more, and there is a deadweight loss. Tariff revenue goes to the government; quota rent goes to licence holders.
  • Subsidies, embargoes, administrative barriers and (for some) currency manipulation are other tools, each with its own winners and losers.

Mission 2

  • Free trade raises total income, but its losses are concentrated, so pressure for protection is strong.
  • Arguments for protection: infant industries, security, dumping and unfair competition, health, safety and environmental standards, jobs, the balance of payments, revenue and (for low-income economies) diversification.
  • Arguments against: misallocation, higher prices, less choice, higher costs, inefficiency, retaliation, weaker exports. The case for protection depends on the problem, the tool, time and retaliation.

Mission 3

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 4

  • Floating: market sets the rate (appreciation/depreciation). Fixed: the central bank holds it (devaluation/revaluation). Managed: it floats, with intervention.
  • A peg is held by buying or selling the currency with reserves, and by interest rates.
  • A rate held above market clearing is overvalued (reserves drain); below it, undervalued (reserves pile up).
