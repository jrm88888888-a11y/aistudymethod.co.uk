Mission 1

Mission 1: Monitored wildlife populations have shrunk by almost three-quarters since 1970. Why should people care?

Make a guess: Monitored wildlife populations have shrunk by almost three-quarters since 1970. Why should people care?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.1 biodiversity: concept; local and global trends; causes, rates and potential impacts of decline
  • 3.1.6.1 importance of ecosystems to people amid population growth and economic development; human populations in ecosystem development and sustainability
Mission 1

What is biodiversity?

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life at three levels: genetic (within species), species, and ecosystem.
  • About 8 million animal and plant species are estimated to exist, including 5.5 million insect species.
  • All three levels matter: genetic variety helps crops and wild species adapt; varied ecosystems support different services.
Mission 1

Global trends: the Living Planet Index

  • Monitored vertebrate populations fell by 73% on average between 1970 and 2022.
  • Based on 35,803 populations of 5,790 species of amphibians, birds, fish, mammals and reptiles.
  • Freshwater: −85%. By region: Latin America and the Caribbean −95%, Africa −80%, Asia and the Pacific −49%.
  • But about half of the populations in the index are stable or increasing.
Quick check
?What does the Living Planet Index's 73% decline mean?
Mission 1

Rates of decline

  • The current global extinction rate is tens to hundreds of times higher than the average over the last 10 million years, and accelerating.
  • Up to 1 million species are threatened with extinction, many within decades.
  • On average 25% of species in well-studied groups are threatened; for amphibians, over 40%.
  • At least 680 vertebrate species have been driven extinct by human actions since the 16th century.
Mission 1

Local trends: the UK

  • UK species studied have declined by 19% on average since 1970.
  • Nearly one in six species (16%) is threatened with extinction from Great Britain; in Northern Ireland, 12% of assessed species are at risk.
  • 151 of 10,008 species assessed have become extinct since 1500.
  • Pollinators fell by 18% on average and predatory insects by 34%; 13 seabird species fell by an average of 24% since 1986.
Mission 1

Causes of decline

  • IPBES ranked five direct drivers, largest first: (1) changes in land and sea use; (2) direct exploitation; (3) climate change; (4) pollution; (5) invasive alien species.
  • Indirect drivers: population growth, consumption per person, technology, and governance.
  • UK, on land and in fresh water: changes in farm management and climate change are the biggest causes.
  • UK seas: unsustainable fishing, climate change and marine development.
Quick check
?Which direct driver has had the largest global impact on nature so far, according to IPBES?
Mission 1

Why ecosystems matter: ecosystem services

  • Ecosystem services: "the benefits people obtain from ecosystems".
  • Provisioning: food, water, timber, fibre. Regulating: climate, floods, disease, wastes, water quality. Cultural: recreation, beauty, spiritual value. Supporting: soil formation, photosynthesis, nutrient cycling.
  • Over 75% of global food crop types rely on animal pollination; US$235–577 billion of annual crop output is at risk from pollinator loss.
  • Land and ocean ecosystems take up carbon equal to about 60% of fossil-fuel emissions each year.
Mission 1

More people, more pressure

  • World population: 8.2 billion in 2024, projected to peak at about 10.3 billion in the mid-2080s.
  • Already, more than a third of the land surface and about 75% of freshwater resources go to crops or livestock.
  • Three-quarters of the land environment and about 66% of the marine environment have been significantly altered by people.
  • Development raises demand for food, land and materials, and so does dependence on the services that biodiversity provides.
Quick check
?A peat bog stores carbon and slows floodwater from reaching a town downstream. Which type of ecosystem service is this?
Mission 1

People can also rebuild biodiversity

  • Potential impacts of decline: lost pollination, fisheries, flood and climate regulation, and the genetic variety that crops and medicines draw on.
  • Recovery is possible: in Lyme Bay Marine Protected Area, species numbers rose after trawling was banned in 2008.
  • Decline has been less severe, or avoided, in areas held or managed by Indigenous Peoples and Local Communities.
  • Global goal: the Kunming-Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (December 2022) aims to conserve at least 30% of land, inland waters and seas by 2030 (Target 3).
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): weigh scale and rate of decline against evidence of recovery, and judge whether management can keep pace with demand.
Mission check

?A council plans to drain a wetland to build homes for a growing town. Which argument best uses ecosystem services to evaluate the plan?

Biodiversity matters because ecosystems supply services people depend on: food, pollination, clean water, flood and climate regulation. As population and development grow, so do the pressures on these services and our dependence on them, and people can choose to degrade ecosystems or to restore them.

Mission 2

What is an ecosystem?

  • An ecosystem: living things and their non-living surroundings, linked by flows of energy and matter.
  • Biotic components: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
  • Abiotic components: climate, water, rock, soil, relief.
  • Change one component and the others respond.
Mission 2

The ecosystem as a system

  • Inputs: solar energy; water; nutrients from rainfall and weathered rock.
  • Stores: living biomass, dead litter, soil.
  • Transfers: photosynthesis, feeding, litter fall, decomposition, uptake by roots.
  • Outputs: heat; water lost by evapotranspiration and runoff; nutrients leached out of the soil.
  • Energy passes through the system; nutrients cycle within it.
Mission 2

Trophic levels

  • Trophic level: a feeding level in an ecosystem.
  • Level 1: producers (autotrophs): green plants fixing the Sun's energy by photosynthesis.
  • Level 2 and above: consumers (heterotrophs): herbivores (primary consumers), then carnivores (secondary, tertiary).
  • Decomposers (fungi, bacteria) feed on dead matter at every level and release nutrients.
  • Food chain: one feeding path. Food web: the interlocking chains of a real ecosystem.
  • At each level much energy is lost as heat in respiration, so energy flows one way and chains are short. The trophic-level idea comes from Lindeman (1942).
Quick check
?In a woodland, fungi break down fallen oak leaves. What is their role?
Mission 2

Biomass and net primary production

  • Biomass: the mass of living material in an area (e.g. kg per m²). A store.
  • Gross primary production (GPP): total energy fixed by plants by photosynthesis.
  • Net primary production (NPP) = GPP − plant respiration. It is the energy available to consumers.
  • NPP is highest where light, warmth and water are all plentiful year-round.
Quick check
?Model food chain: producers fix 10,000 kJ. Assume 10% passes to each next level (a model value). How much reaches the secondary consumers?
Mission 2

Nutrient cycling

  • Nutrients (e.g. nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium) cycle between three stores: biomass, litter, soil.
  • Transfers: litter fall (biomass → litter); decomposition (litter → soil); uptake by roots (soil → biomass).
  • Inputs: dissolved in rainfall; released by weathering of rock.
  • Outputs: leaching in drainage water; runoff; removal of plants (harvest, fire).
Mission 2

The Gersmehl model

  • Gersmehl's nutrient cycling model (1976): three circles (biomass, litter, soil) sized by the store; arrows sized by the flow.
  • Tropical rainforest: biomass is the largest store; litter is small because warm, wet conditions make decay fast; heavy rain leaches the soil.
  • Taiga: litter is large because cold slows decay; flows are small.
  • Temperate grassland: soil is the largest store; grasses die back each year into thick, fertile soil.
Quick check
?A Gersmehl diagram shows a large litter circle, small biomass and soil circles, and thin arrows. Which ecosystem fits?
Mission 2

What makes a terrestrial ecosystem?

  • Climate: temperature and rainfall set NPP and decay rates.
  • Soil: depth, drainage and nutrients limit what can grow.
  • Vegetation: adds litter, shades and protects the soil, holds nutrients.
  • Topography: altitude, slope and aspect change temperature and drainage (e.g. waterlogged hollows).
  • They are interconnected: change one control and the rest respond.
Quick check
?A slope is cleared of trees and heavy rain follows. Which chain of responses is most likely?
Mission check

?A temperate grassland is ploughed and cropped every year, with the crop taken away. After decades, yields fall unless fertiliser is added. Which explanation is best?

Where nutrients are stored decides how an ecosystem responds to use. Grassland's large soil store lasts decades; a rainforest's riches are in its biomass, so clearing removes them quickly and heavy rain leaches the rest.

Mission 3

Ecosystems are products of their controls

  • Climate (temperature, rainfall, season length) sets the broad biome.
  • Soil, vegetation and topography (altitude, slope, aspect, drainage) shape it locally.
  • The parts are linked: vegetation builds soil; soil water and nutrients limit vegetation; animals depend on both.
  • Change one control (e.g. warmer summers) and the components respond: new species arrive, soils thaw or dry, the system reorganises.
Mission 3

Climate zones are moving

  • IPCC: warming has shifted climate zones in many regions: arid zones expanding, polar zones contracting.
  • Many species have changed their ranges, numbers and timing of seasonal activity.
  • About half of species assessed have shifted towards the poles or, on land, uphill.
Mission 3

In the ocean

  • Upper-ocean species (top 200 m): shifted poleward 52 ± 33 km per decade since the 1950s; seafloor species 29 ± 16 km.
  • Species track the temperatures they are adapted to: the habitat moves, so they move.
  • Timing shifts too: when predators and prey shift at different rates, food chains can fall out of step.
Quick check
?Why are many species shifting towards the poles or uphill?
Mission 3

Tundra case study: impacts now

  • Region: the Arctic tundra of North America, Fennoscandia and Siberia.
  • Greening overall across the tundra; some areas browning (lower productivity).
  • Arctic June snow cover on land shrank 13.4 ± 5.4% per decade (1967–2018); permafrost is at record temperatures.
  • More wildfire and abrupt thaw have changed disturbance, with positive and negative impacts on wildlife such as reindeer and salmon.
  • Swedish Saami reindeer herders report thawing ground and "rotten spring vegetation" in summer grazing areas since the mid-1990s.
Mission 3

Tundra case study: potential impacts

  • Shrubs and trees projected to cover 24–52% of Arctic tundra by 2050.
  • High-Arctic species with few refuges risk being outcompeted by temperate species: a loss of globally unique biodiversity.
  • The boreal forest is projected to spread north at its northern edge and shrink at its southern edge.
  • About 20% of Arctic land permafrost is vulnerable to abrupt thaw and ground subsidence; wildfire projected to increase.
Quick check
?Which statement about the tundra is best supported by the IPCC?
Mission 3

Coral reefs: a habitat that cannot move fast

  • The ocean has taken up more than 90% of the excess heat since 1970.
  • Early 2023 to mid-2025: bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area.
  • Bleaching: heat-stressed corals expel the algae that feed them; repeated bleaching can kill them.
Mission 3

Human exploitation

  • Forest loss, 1990–2023 (FAO data via World Bank): Latin America and the Caribbean 10.70 → 9.25 million km² (−14%); sub-Saharan Africa 6.83 → 6.15 million km² (−10%).
  • Soil: farmland erodes 10–20 times faster than soil forms under no-tillage, over 100 times under conventional tillage.
  • Climate change can worsen land degradation through heavier rain, drought, heat and wind.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): ecosystems under stress usually face several factors at once; weigh climate change against exploitation for a named place.
Mission 3

Limits to adapting

  • On land, 3–14% of species assessed face very high extinction risk at 1.5 °C; 3–18% at 2 °C.
  • "Hard limits to adaptation have been reached in some ecosystems".
  • Most at risk: species that cannot move (corals) or have nowhere left to go (mountain tops, polar edges).
Mission 3

Farms: winners and losers

  • IPCC: climate change has slowed the growth of farm productivity over 50 years globally.
  • Studies that separate out climate change find it has lowered yields of some crops (maize, wheat) in many lower-latitude regions and raised yields of some (maize, wheat, sugar beet) in many higher-latitude regions.
  • Africa's pastoral systems show lower animal growth and productivity.
  • Higher CO₂ can lower crops' nutritional quality.
Quick check
?Which conclusion about climate change and farming is best supported?
Mission check

?A snow-dependent alpine plant grows near a mountain summit. With continued warming, what is most likely, and why?

Living things follow their climate zone: upper-ocean species have moved poleward about 52 km a decade; on land, biomes shift (tundra greening, shrubs spreading, boreal forest moving north). Where they cannot move (corals, summits), they decline. Farming has winners and losers: higher-latitude gains, lower-latitude losses.

Mission 4: Tundra and hot desert both get very little rain, yet they look nothing alike. What sorts the world's biomes?

Make a guess: Tundra and hot desert both get very little rain, yet they look nothing alike. What sorts the world's biomes?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.3 concept of the biome; global distribution of major terrestrial biomes
Mission 4

What is a biome?

  • A biome is a global-scale ecosystem: a large region with a typical climax vegetation and the animals that go with it.
  • Climate sets it; soils and wildlife follow.
  • One widely used map divides land into 14 biomes made of 846 ecoregions.
Mission 4

Climate sorts biomes

Annual figures, 1991–2020 normals:

  • Utqiaġvik, Alaska (tundra): mean −10.2 °C; 137 mm.
  • Phoenix, Arizona (hot desert): mean 24.2 °C; 183 mm.
  • Dodge City, Kansas (temperate grassland): mean 13.3 °C; 559 mm.
  • Hilo, Hawaiʻi (tropical wet forest): mean 23.3 °C; 3,058 mm.
  • Temperature sets the energy and growing season; precipitation sets the water.
Quick check
?Utqiaġvik averages −10.2 °C and 137 mm a year; Phoenix averages 24.2 °C and 183 mm. Why are they in different biomes?
Mission 4

Latitude and the global circulation

  • Equator: strong sun; rising air cools and gives heavy rain all year → tropical rainforest.
  • About 30° N and S: "the poleward flowing air begins to descend"; the sinking air is dry. "Most of the nonpolar deserts lie within the two trade winds belts" → hot deserts.
  • Polar edges: weak sun, long winters, cold dry air → tundra.
  • So biomes form rough belts by latitude.
Mission 4

What breaks the belts

  • Continentality: inland areas are far from ocean moisture and swing between hot summers and cold winters. Dodge City's monthly means run from 0.6 °C (Jan) to 26.7 °C (Jul) → temperate grassland.
  • Altitude: high mountains carry alpine tundra even near the equator.
  • Relief: mountains cast rain shadows on their lee side.
  • Ocean currents: cold currents along some west coasts help keep coastal deserts dry.
Mission 4

Tropical rainforest: where

  • A belt close to the equator, mostly within about 10° N and S.
  • Main blocks: the Amazon basin, the Congo basin, South-East Asia and New Guinea.
  • Tropical and subtropical moist forests cover about 14.4% of Earth's land.
Mission 4

Hot desert: where

  • Mainly between about 15° and 30° N and S, often on the west sides of continents.
  • Examples: Sahara, Arabian, Kalahari and Namib, Australian interior, Sonoran, Atacama.
  • Deserts and xeric shrublands cover about 19.3% of land. That includes cold deserts too: deserts "may be hot, they may be cold".
Mission 4

Temperate grassland: where

  • Mid-latitude continental interiors, roughly 35°–55°, far from the sea.
  • Examples: North American prairies, Eurasian steppes, Argentina's pampas, South Africa's veld.
  • Temperate grasslands, savannas and shrublands cover about 7.8% of land.
Mission 4

Tundra: where

  • North of the Arctic treeline: northern Alaska and Canada, coastal Greenland, northern Scandinavia and Russia.
  • Almost none in the Southern Hemisphere: little land lies at those latitudes outside Antarctica.
  • Alpine tundra sits above the treeline on high mountains.
  • Tundra covers about 8.7% of land.
Quick check
?A place lies at 48° N in the middle of a large continent, more than 1,500 km from any ocean. Which biome is most likely?
Mission 4

The other major biomes

Share of Earth's land, 14-biome map:

  • Tropical and subtropical grasslands and savannas 15.8%; boreal forest (taiga) 11.4%; temperate broadleaf and mixed forest 9.3%.
  • Montane grasslands 3.6%; tropical dry forest 2.9%; temperate conifer forest 2.8%; Mediterranean woodland and scrub 2.4%.
  • Real landscapes are often farmland or cities: the same study treats these "anthropogenic biomes" separately.
Quick check
?Tropical moist forest covers 14.4% of land, desert 19.3%, tundra 8.7% and temperate grassland 7.8%. What share do these four cover together?
Mission check

?Two places each get about 500 mm of precipitation a year. One lies at 40° N in a continental interior with hot summers; the other lies at 70° N on an Arctic coast. Why do they carry different biomes?

Biomes are sorted by temperature and precipitation together, and those follow latitude, the global circulation and continentality. Tundra and hot desert are both dry, but one is cold and one is hot.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Darwin and Manaus are both hot all year. Why is one surrounded by savanna and the other by rainforest?

Make a guess: Darwin and Manaus are both hot all year. Why is one surrounded by savanna and the other by rainforest?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.3 main characteristics of tropical rainforest and savanna grassland biomes
  • 3.1.6.3 ecological responses to climate, soil and soil moisture budget: flora and fauna adaptations in each biome
Mission 5

Two tropical biomes

  • Tropical and subtropical moist broadleaf forest covers 14.4% of Earth's land; tropical and subtropical grasslands, savannas and shrublands cover 15.8%.
  • Rainforest: close to the equator, where the ITCZ brings rising air and rain for most of the year.
  • Savanna: between the rainforest and the hot deserts. The ITCZ arrives for only part of the year, so there is a wet season and a dry season.
Mission 5

Rainforest climate: Manaus, Brazil

  • Monthly mean temperature 27–28 °C all year (1970–2000).
  • Annual rainfall 2,323 mm.
  • Every month gets rain: wettest April (310 mm), driest August (68 mm).
  • Leaf litter "decomposes rapidly in the wet, warm conditions".
Mission 5

Savanna climate: Darwin, Australia

  • Mean maximum 30.7–33.4 °C every month.
  • Annual rainfall 1,732 mm: not far below Manaus.
  • But January brings 432 mm, while June, July and August bring 1.8, 1.0 and 4.4 mm.
  • 88% of the rain falls from November to March (our calculation).
  • Days with at least 1 mm of rain: 19 in January, 0.2 in July.
Mission 5

The soil moisture budget

  • Compare precipitation (P) with potential evapotranspiration (PET): the water that would evaporate and transpire if it were available.
  • P > PET: soil fills (recharge), then surplus drains away and leaches the soil.
  • P < PET: plants draw on stored water (utilisation), then the soil dries out (deficit).
  • Estimated PET is about 1,750 mm a year at both stations (our calculation, Thornthwaite method):
    • Manaus: surplus about Nov–May; short, mild deficit about Jun–Oct.
    • Darwin: large surplus Dec–Mar; deficit about 8 months, Apr–Nov.
Quick check
?In August, Manaus gets 68 mm of rain; estimated PET is about 153 mm. What is the balance, and what happens in the soil?
Mission 5

Soils: latosols

  • Tropical latosols (oxisols in US soil taxonomy) are "very highly weathered soils" found mainly in the tropics.
  • They are rich in iron and aluminium oxides; the iron oxides give their red colour.
  • "Extremely low native fertility": few nutrients in reserve.
  • "Most nutrients in Oxisol ecosystems are contained in the standing vegetation and decomposing plant material".
  • Rainforest: year-round surplus leaches nutrients down and out; fast decay and fast uptake keep nutrients in the biomass.
  • Savanna: leaching in the wet season; in the dry season water is drawn upward and iron can harden into a crust (laterite).
Mission 5

Rainforest plants: responses to constant heat and rain

  • Layers: the dense canopy "prevents much of the sunlight from reaching the ground"; woody vines (lianas) climb to reach light.
  • Drip tips shed water fast, which limits fungi and algae on wet leaves.
  • Buttress roots steady tall trees in shallow, nutrient-poor soil, where roots spread near the surface.
  • Epiphytes grow on branches high in the light and catch water and nutrients from rain and litter.
  • Evergreen: no dry season or winter, so trees can photosynthesise all year.
Mission 5

Rainforest animals

  • Most life is in the canopy, where light, food and shelter are.
  • Two-toed sloth: hooked claws 8–10 cm long for hanging in the canopy; algae grow in its hair, a green tint that camouflages it.
  • Its metabolic rate is only 40–45% of what would be expected for its body weight: it saves energy on a low-energy diet of leaves.
Quick check
?Which rainforest adaptation is a response mainly to nutrient-poor soil?
Mission 5

Savanna plants: responses to drought and fire

  • Grasses grow fast in the wet season; in the dry season they die back above ground but survive as roots, and regrow quickly after fire.
  • Trees are scattered: the long deficit cannot support a closed canopy.
  • Deep roots reach water stored lower in the soil through the dry months.
  • Deciduous trees drop leaves in the dry season to cut transpiration.
  • Thick, fire-resistant bark (e.g. many eucalypts around Darwin) and water-storing trunks (e.g. the baobab in Africa).
  • Small leaves, thorns (e.g. acacias): less water loss and less browsing.
Mission 5

Savanna animals: following the rain

  • The Serengeti's 1.5 million ha of savanna supports about 2 million wildebeest, 900,000 Thomson's gazelles and 300,000 zebras.
  • They make a "1,000 km long annual circular trek spanning the two adjacent countries of Kenya and Tanzania", "in search of pasture and water".
  • Predators follow: about 4,000 lions, 3,500 spotted hyenas and 1,000 leopards.
  • Grazers and browsers share food by height: grass, shrubs or tree leaves.
Quick check
?Why do the Serengeti's wildebeest migrate each year?
Mission check

?Two hypothetical lowland stations are hot all year. Station X gets 2,400 mm of rain a year, but P is well below PET for seven months. Station Y gets 2,000 mm, and P falls slightly below PET in only one month. Which is more likely to be surrounded by rainforest?

Darwin and Manaus are both hot, and both wet overall. The difference is the seasonality of rain: Manaus has a soil moisture surplus most of the year, Darwin a deficit for about eight months. Plants and animals respond to that budget (and to fire and poor latosols), not to the annual total alone.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Clearing tropical forest and savanna brings farms, jobs and food. Who gains, and what is lost?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.6.3 human activity and its impact on each biome (tropical rainforest, savanna)
  • 3.1.6.3 development issues in each biome: population change, economic development, agricultural extension and intensification, biodiversity, sustainability
Mission 6

Why tropical land is cleared

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–2015 was driven by agriculture.
  • Amazon: cattle pasture first; soy often follows. "Most soybean expansion occurred on pastures originally converted from natural vegetation for cattle production".
  • Brazil's satellite survey (PRODES): 3,335 km² cleared in the Legal Amazon, Aug 2025–Jul 2026 (preliminary estimate), the lowest since 1988; the previous low was 4,571 km² (2012).
  • Pará cleared the most: 1,130 km².
Mission 6

What the Amazon loses

  • Biodiversity: about 16,000 tree species.
  • Rain: air that has passed over extensive forest produces "at least twice as much rain" as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Carbon: "Southeastern Amazonia, in particular, acts as a net carbon source".
  • Medicines: quinine from the Andean Cinchona tree was for centuries "the primary treatment of malaria".
Mission 6

Nutrient cycle and soil after clearance

  • The zonal soil is the latosol (oxisol): "very highly weathered", with "extremely low native fertility".
  • "Most nutrients … are contained in the standing vegetation and decomposing plant material": the Gersmehl biomass store.
  • Clear and burn: the biomass store is removed; ash gives a brief flush of nutrients.
  • Heavy rain then leaches them; bare soil erodes; no litter returns.
  • Oxisols "can be quite productive with inputs of lime and fertilizers": without them, yields fall and new land is cleared.
Quick check
?Why do crop yields on newly cleared Amazon land fall unless fertiliser and lime are added?
Mission 6

Human activity in the savanna

  • Cerrado (Brazil's savanna): 5,517 km² of native vegetation cleared Aug 2025–Jul 2026, the lowest since 2001 but more than in the Amazon.
  • 74.3% of it was in Matopiba (Maranhão, Tocantins, Piauí, Bahia), an agricultural frontier.
  • In South America, soy area more than doubled, 26.4 → 55.1 million ha, 2000–2019.
  • East Africa: the Serengeti faces "tourism pressure, wildfires, and lack of adequate capacity in resource monitoring".
  • Tanzania's population grew from 26.1 million (1990) to 66.6 million (2023): more demand for farmland and grazing beside protected savanna.

Mission 6

Protection and expansion

  • Brazil combines satellite monitoring, enforcement, land-use planning and support for sustainable production.
  • Since January 2023: 18 new and 8 enlarged federal conservation units (~25,000 km² in all) and 20 indigenous territories (~32,000 km²).
  • Bolsa Verde: since 2023 it has paid 86,635 low-income families to conserve forest.
  • Forest expansion, share of land under forest, 1990 → 2025:
    • Vietnam 29.9% → 47.2%; China 16.7% → 24.2%; Costa Rica 54.0% → 58.6%.
    • Brazil 74.0% → 58.2%; world 33.5% → 31.9%.
Mission check

?In a tropical lowland, a company plans to clear 10,000 ha of rainforest on oxisols for plantations, creating 3,000 jobs. Which evaluation is best supported?

Clearing brings income, jobs and food to some, mostly locally and soon. The losses (nutrients locked in the biomass, complex food webs, rainfall recycling, carbon storage and biodiversity) are wider, longer-lasting and shared. Protection and forest expansion decide whether the balance shifts.

Mission 7

What makes a savanna?

  • Trees and grasses together: savannas are "transitional between grasslands and forests".
  • Climate: "alternating wet and dry seasons that result in 'boom–bust' cycles of growth followed by dry, fire-prone periods".
  • Savannas cover about 22% of the world's land and 25% of Australia (about 1.9 million km²).
  • Examples: northern Australia, East Africa (Serengeti), Brazil (Cerrado).
Mission 7

Climate: a wet–dry tropical regime

Mean rainfall43231320117147
  • Annual mean 1,732 mm; Nov–Apr brings 94% of it (our calculation).
  • Mean maximum 30.7–33.4 °C in every month: hot all year.
  • Savanna at Howard Springs, 35 km south-east of Darwin.
Mission 7

Soils: old and poor

  • "Highly weathered" sands of "low nutrient, red and grey Kandosol soils".
  • Freely draining sandy loam; a hard ferricrete layer (duricrust) at 1.5–2 m.
  • Heavy rain over long periods has leached nutrients; plants rely on nutrients held in biomass and recycled through litter and fire.
Mission 7

The soil moisture budget

  • Wet season: rain exceeds evapotranspiration → soil recharge and surplus; heavy rains drain to deep subsoil (1–5 m).
  • Dry season: evapotranspiration exceeds rain → utilisation, then deficit.
  • Soil water content "dominated dry season fluxes" of carbon and water.
  • Growing season (wet topsoil) ≈ mid-October to early May.
Quick check
?In Darwin, July averages 1 mm of rain but mean maxima stay above 30 °C. What is the soil moisture state in July?
Mission 7

Flora adaptations

  • Deep water: subsoil moisture (1–5 m) "maintains the gas exchange of the evergreen Eucalyptus/Corymbia tree species through the dry season".
  • Leaf drop: a mid-storey of "semi-deciduous to fully deciduous" trees and shrubs.
  • Annual grasses (tall C4 Sorghum): green leaf area near zero in the late dry season, peak in March, then seed and die back.
  • Fire: savannas "have coevolved with fire"; low-intensity early dry-season fires vs 5,000–7,000 kW/m late fires.
Mission 7

Fauna adaptations: move with the rain

  • Serengeti (Tanzania): 1.5 million ha of savanna.
  • About 2 million wildebeest, 900,000 Thomson's gazelles and 300,000 zebras.
  • The herds migrate "in search of pasture and water": they follow the green grass of the wet season.
  • Plentiful grazers support predators: about 4,000 lions, 3,500 spotted hyenas.
Quick check
?Which adaptation is a direct response to the dry-season soil moisture deficit?
Mission 7

Human activity: fire and its management

  • On average 18% of Australia's tropical savannas burn each year (1997–2011); over two-thirds of fires are in the late dry season (Aug–Nov).
  • Near Darwin, burning is "largely caused by human ignition".
  • Late fires scorch crowns and reduce tree cover.
  • Grazing: cattle grazing is "a major land use" in Australian savannas; cattle "reduce fuel for fire and create ecological space for shrub and tree invasion".
  • Savanna fire management: planned burning in the early dry season "reduces the frequency and severity of fires in the late dry season"; projects earn carbon credits.
Mission 7

Development issues: population and tourism

  • Tanzania's population doubled from 34.3 million (2000) to 68.6 million (2024) (our calculation).
  • A growing population needs land, food and fuel, so pressure on land around protected savanna is likely to rise.
  • The Serengeti's management concerns include poaching, tourism pressure and wildfires; a planned transport route through the park is a potential threat.
  • Economic development through tourism brings income, but also pressure.
Mission 7

Development issues: farming spreads

  • Brazil's Cerrado "is a tropical savanna and an important global biodiversity hot spot".
  • "Only a fraction of its original area remains undisturbed"; much is at risk of conversion to soybeans.
  • Nearly half of South America's soy-driven deforestation was in the Cerrado.
  • Agricultural extension: 5,517 km² of Cerrado cleared, Aug 2025–Jul 2026 (down 24%); 74% of it in the Matopiba states.
  • Intensification: more output from land already cleared, rather than new clearance.
Quick check
?Which policy would best combine agricultural development with biodiversity in the Cerrado?
Mission check

?A West African savanna has rain from June to September and almost none from November to April. Which set of observations is best explained by its soil moisture budget?

Savanna life times itself to water: grasses grow and seed in the wet season, trees use deep stores or drop leaves, herds migrate; people add fire, grazing and farming, so development must balance land for food with biodiversity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Biodiversity (genetic, species, ecosystem) is declining: monitored populations −73% on average since 1970; up to 1 million species threatened; the UK −19% since 1970. The main driver is land and sea use change.
  • Ecosystems provide provisioning, regulating, cultural and supporting services. With 8.2 billion people heading for about 10.3 billion, demand rises; protection and restoration (e.g. Lyme Bay, the 30% target) show people's role in sustainability.

Mission 2

  • Ecosystems are systems: energy flows one way through trophic levels (autotrophs → heterotrophs, with decomposers), while nutrients cycle between biomass, litter and soil.
  • NPP (GPP − respiration) sets the energy available; climate, soil, vegetation and topography interact, so changing one changes the rest.

Mission 3

  • Ecosystems reflect climate, soil, vegetation and topography; when warming changes a control, species ranges, timing and biomes shift.
  • Climate change acts alongside human exploitation (forest loss, soil erosion).

Mission 4

  • A biome is a global-scale ecosystem defined by its climax vegetation, set mainly by climate.
  • Tropical rainforest near the equator; hot deserts under sinking air near 15°–30°; temperate grasslands in mid-latitude continental interiors; tundra beyond the Arctic treeline and on high mountains.
  • Fourteen biomes cover the land; deserts, savannas and tropical moist forest are the largest.

Mission 5

  • Characteristics: rainforest (Manaus 27–28 °C, 2,323 mm, rain in every month); savanna (Darwin 30.7–33.4 °C maxima, 1,732 mm, 88% in Nov–Mar). Rainforest on deep, red, nutrient-poor latosols, with nutrients held in the vegetation; savanna soils leached in the wet season, with laterite crusts in places.
  • Responses: rainforest: layers, drip tips, buttresses, epiphytes, canopy animals (sloth). Savanna: grasses that regrow, deep roots, deciduous and fire-resistant trees, migrating herds following pasture and water.

Mission 6

  • Impacts: agriculture drives 90–99% of tropical deforestation; clearance removes the biomass nutrient store, so latosols are leached and food webs simplified; the Cerrado now loses more native vegetation each year than the Amazon.
  • Responses: protection (conservation units, indigenous lands, payments), forest expansion (Vietnam, China, Costa Rica).

Mission 7

  • Characteristics: trees + grasses, hot all year, wet–dry seasons (Darwin: 94% of rain Nov–Apr), old leached soils; fire-prone.
  • Adaptations: deep roots and evergreen eucalypts, deciduous shrubs, annual C4 grasses, migrating herds (2 million wildebeest).
  • Human activity and development: late dry-season fires vs early-season burning (carbon credits); population growth (Tanzania doubled 2000–24); Cerrado soy extension (5,517 km² cleared 2025–26) and moratorium-style sustainability.
