Mission 1

Reincarnation: the soul moves on

  • Reincarnation (transmigration of souls): an eternal soul passes from one body to another, life after life.
  • In Hinduism the soul is the atman, the true, unchanging self.
  • The cycle of birth, death and rebirth is samsara.
  • Karma: actions have consequences that shape future lives; good actions lead to better births.
  • The goal is moksha: release from samsara. For Advaita Vedanta, realising that atman is one with Brahman; for devotional traditions, eternal union with God.
  • Sikhs and Jains also hold beliefs in rebirth and karma, with their own goals.
Mission 1Read the source

The Bhagavad Gita

Bhagavad Gita 2.22 (Edwin Arnold translation, The Song Celestial, 1885):

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh, / And passeth to inherit / A residence afresh."

Q1 In the image, what stays the same and what changes?

Show answer

The spirit, the atman, stays; the body, like clothing, is changed.

Q2 What does this suggest carries personal identity in reincarnation?

Show answer

The atman, not the body or memory.

Mission 1

Rebirth: no unchanging self

  • Buddhism teaches anatta (Sanskrit anatman): there is no unchanging soul or self.
  • A person is five changing khandhas (aggregates): body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness.
  • "'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain" (Dhammapada 279, Müller translation, 1881). Here "unreal" renders anatta, "not-self".
  • So what is reborn? A causal stream: the energy of karma (kamma, intentional action) shapes a new life.
  • The Questions of King Milinda (paraphrase): rebirth is like lighting one lamp from another. The new flame is neither the same nor another.
  • The goal is nibbana (nirvana): the end of craving and of rebirth.
  • Traditions differ: many Theravada teachers hold rebirth is immediate; Tibetan Buddhism teaches an intermediate state (bardo).

Mission 1

Who is reborn? The identity problem

  • Most people remember no past lives. If memory makes identity (Locke), the past person was not me.
  • Hindu reply: identity rests on the atman, not memory. The same self lives each life, as one actor plays many roles.
  • Buddhist reply: there is no fixed self to be "the same". Lives are linked by cause, like flames passed on. "Neither the same nor another."
  • Critique: if nothing carries over except karmic effects, is it me who is reborn, or someone affected by me?
  • Critique: why should I care about, or deserve, the results of actions I cannot remember?
Mission 1

Near-death experiences

  • Near-death experience (NDE): an experience reported by some people who come close to death, often in cardiac arrest.
  • Term popularised by Raymond Moody, Life After Life (1975).
  • Common features: feeling of peace; leaving the body and seeing it from above; a tunnel; a bright light or loving presence; meeting dead relatives; a life review; a border and a return.
  • Some report verifiable details seen while unconscious (e.g. the Pam Reynolds case, 1991, often cited).
  • Believers read NDEs as glimpses of the soul leaving the body; reports vary across cultures and faiths.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are NDEs evidence of life after death?

  • Claim: Near-death experiences show that the self can exist apart from the body.
  • For: Many, similar reports from people of different ages and cultures.
  • For: Some report accurate details from while they were unconscious.
  • For: Life-changing effects: many lose the fear of death.
  • Against: "Near" death is not death: the brain may still be active. Not one has returned from lasting death.
  • Against: Brain-based explanations: lack of oxygen, REM intrusion, drugs such as ketamine, temporal lobe activity (Susan Blackmore, Dying to Live, 1993: the "dying brain" hypothesis).
  • Against: Content varies with culture and religion; verifiable cases are few and hard to check.
  • Judgement criteria: What would count as evidence for life after death? Do brain explanations account for all features? Is testimony reliable here?

Mission 1

Evidence for reincarnation?

  • Ian Stevenson (1918–2007), Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966): children who seem to recall details of a dead person's life, sometimes with matching birthmarks.
  • Critiques: the child may have heard the details (cryptomnesia); families may shape the story; cases cluster where reincarnation is already believed.
  • Most believers do not rest belief on such cases, but on scripture, tradition and the justice of karma.

Mission 2

Right because God commands

  • Divine command theory (DCT): an act is right because God commands it and wrong because God forbids it.
  • God is the origin of morality (God makes moral truth) and its regulator (God's will settles what is binding).
  • Moral goodness = complying with God's commands.
  • Moral truths are objective: true whatever any human thinks, because they rest on God's will.
  • A meta-ethical theory: it says what "right" and "wrong" mean or what makes them true, not only which acts are right.
Mission 2

Why believers hold it

  • Omnipotence: if anything were good apart from God, God would be bound by it. DCT keeps God sovereign over everything, morality included.
  • Objectivity: morality is not a matter of opinion or culture; it has a fixed source.
  • Metaphysical foundation: DCT answers "why is anything really wrong?" with a reason outside human minds.
  • Motivation and certainty: believers know what to do (revealed commands) and why it matters (obedience to God).
  • Scripture: moral law is presented as given by God, not invented by people.
Mission 2Read the source

Commands and a hard case

> "I am the LORD your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage. You shall have no other gods before me." (Exodus 20:2–3, WEBBE)

> "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE)

> "Now take your son, your only son, Isaac, whom you love, … Offer him there as a burnt offering …" (Genesis 22:2, WEBBE)

  • The Ten Commandments open with who commands, not with reasons: the authority is God's.
  • In Genesis 22 the angel of the LORD stops Abraham at the last moment (22:11–12). Jewish and Christian readers praise Abraham's faith and read the story in different ways (e.g. a test of trust; a rejection of child sacrifice).

Mission 2Read the source

Plato's Euthyphro

  • Plato (c. 427–347 BCE), Euthyphro, a dialogue set in 399 BCE outside the court where Socrates' trial is about to begin.
  • Euthyphro is prosecuting his own father, sure that the gods approve. Socrates asks what piety (holiness) is.

> "what all the gods love is pious and holy, and the opposite which they all hate, impious." (Euthyphro, 9e, tr. Jowett)

> "The point which I should first wish to understand is whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods." (10a, tr. Jowett)

Q1 Which half of Socrates' question matches DCT?

Show answer

Holy because beloved of the gods: the gods' attitude makes it so.

Q2 Euthyphro's gods are many and quarrel. Why do monotheists still face the question?

Show answer

Swap "the gods love" for "God commands": the same two options remain.

Mission 2

Two horns

The Euthyphro dilemma, restated for one God: is X good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good?

  • Horn 1: God commands it because it is good.
    • Goodness exists independently of God; God recognises it.
    • Implications: God is not the source of morality; God seems bound by a standard (a problem for omnipotence); we could know right and wrong without God.
  • Horn 2: It is good because God commands it.
    • This is DCT.
    • Implications: if God had commanded cruelty, cruelty would be right (arbitrariness). "God is good" would mean only "God does what God commands" (emptiness).
  • Spec: the implications of the dilemma. Neither horn suits a believer who holds that God is both sovereign and good.

Mission 2

Arbitrariness and pluralism

  • Arbitrariness problem: if God's will alone makes right, God's commands have no reason behind them. Morality becomes purely arbitrary, and cruelty could have been a duty.
  • William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347) is often cited as accepting this: God could command even hatred of God, and it would then be right.
  • Pluralism objection: different religions (and different groups within one religion) claim different divine commands: on diet, the holy day, marriage, war. Which commands are God's?
  • Believers' replies to pluralism: a shared core (e.g. love of neighbour, the Golden Rule, Luke 6:31); revelation is true in one tradition; some commands fit a time and place.
  • Critics' reply: we then need human reasoning to choose between claimed commands, so morality does not rest on command alone.
Mission 2

Adams: a modified theory

  • Robert Merrihew Adams (1937–2024), "A Modified Divine Command Theory of Ethical Wrongness" (1973), developed in Finite and Infinite Goods (1999).
  • Wrong = contrary to the commands of a loving God (paraphrase).
  • God's commands come from God's nature, which is perfectly good: omnibenevolence is part of what God is.
  • So God would not command cruelty for its own sake. If God did, Adams argued, the believer's idea of wrongness would break down; he would not conclude that cruelty is a duty.
  • Later Adams: goodness is grounded in God's nature (God is the standard of the good); obligation is grounded in God's commands.
  • Answers arbitrariness (commands have a reason: God's love) while keeping morality rooted in God.
  • Critics: is God's nature good by some standard? If so, horn 1 returns. If "good" just names God's nature, the emptiness worry returns.
Mission 2Diversity

Will or wisdom?

  • Voluntarists stress God's will: Ockham (Christian); many Ash'ari theologians in Sunni Islam (al-Ash'ari, 874–936): acts are good or bad because God commands or forbids them.
  • Mu'tazila (early Islamic school): reason can know good and bad before revelation; God commands what is just.
  • Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274): God commands in line with God's wisdom; natural law is reason's share in God's eternal law. Most Catholic and many Protestant thinkers follow this path.
  • Hebrew Bible: Abraham argues with God over Sodom: "Shouldn't the Judge of all the earth do right?" (Genesis 18:25, WEBBE). God is expected to act justly.
  • Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855), Fear and Trembling (1843): Abraham's faith involves a "teleological suspension of the ethical": duty to God can stand above the ethical.

Mission 2Evaluate

Is morality what God commands?

  • Claim: Morality is what God commands.
  • For: gives objective, universal morality; keeps God sovereign (omnipotence); explains why morality feels like a command and not just advice; fits scripture's presentation of law.
  • For: Adams' version answers arbitrariness: commands flow from God's loving nature.
  • Against: the Euthyphro dilemma: either goodness is independent of God, or it is arbitrary.
  • Against: pluralism: claimed commands conflict; we must judge between them using something other than command.
  • Against: many non-religious people live morally; morality seems knowable without God.
  • Judgement criteria: Does a reply to Euthyphro avoid both horns? Is "God is good" still meaningful? Can we know God's commands? Does the view fit believers' own sense that God is just?

Mission 3

What a moral argument claims

  • Starts from morality: real moral obligations, or our sense of being bound by duty.
  • Concludes that God exists, or that belief in God is required.
  • Explanation type: objective goodness or obligation needs a source; God is the best one (Aquinas' Fourth Way is related).
  • Practical type: the moral life only makes sense if God exists (Kant).
  • Neither type claims to prove God from the definition of God (contrast the ontological argument).
Mission 3

Aquinas: the Fourth Way

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, question 2, article 3 (c. 1265–74).
  • We judge things to be more or less good, true and noble.
  • "More" and "less" are measured against a maximum: something best, truest, noblest.
  • The maximum in any kind is the cause of everything of that kind (his example: fire, hottest, causes heat).
  • So something is the cause of all goodness and every perfection: "this we call God".
  • A moral argument from the degrees of goodness we observe.
Mission 3Read the source

The Fourth Way

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, q. 2, a. 3 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "The fourth way is taken from the gradation to be found in things. Among beings there are some more and some less good, true, noble and the like."

> "Therefore there must also be something which is to all beings the cause of their being, goodness, and every other perfection; and this we call God."

Q1 What does Aquinas observe first?

Show answer

That things are more or less good, true and noble.

Q2 Critics ask: does "more good" need a real maximum, or only a standard in our minds? Which step does that question target?

Show answer

The step from degrees to a real maximum.

Mission 3

Kant: duty and the highest good

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Practical Reason (1788).
  • Morality comes from reason, not from God's commands: the categorical imperative. Kant is not arguing that we need God to know what is right.
  • Reason commands us to aim at the highest good (summum bonum): perfect virtue, rewarded with happiness in proportion to it.
  • Ought implies can: if we must aim at it, it must be possible.
  • But in this life virtue and happiness often do not match. The good suffer; the wicked prosper.
Mission 3

The three postulates

A postulate is something reason must assume for the moral law to make sense, though it cannot be proved:

  • Freedom: to be bound by duty, we must be able to choose to obey it.
  • Immortality: perfect virtue (holiness) needs endless progress, so the soul must continue beyond death.
  • God: only a being who is all-good and all-powerful can match happiness to virtue.
  • Kant, Abbott translation (1873): "it is morally necessary to assume the existence of God."
  • Postulates are practical faith, not knowledge: Kant rejected the theoretical proofs of God.

Mission 3

Freud: conscience is the internal parent

  • Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Ego and the Id (1923); The Future of an Illusion (1927).
  • The superego forms in childhood as we take in the commands and prohibitions of our parents and society.
  • Conscience and guilt are the superego at work: they feel absolute, like a voice from beyond, because they carry a parent's authority.
  • Belief in God projects that parent onto the universe.
  • So the moral sense can be explained without God. The moral argument mistakes a psychological origin for a divine one.
  • Responses: Freud's evidence base is narrow and hard to test; a psychological origin would not show a belief false.
Mission 3

Russell: a dilemma and an upbringing

  • Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), Why I Am Not a Christian (lecture, 1927).
  • The dilemma (paraphrase): if right and wrong exist only because God decrees them, then for God there is no difference between them, and "God is good" says nothing. If they exist independently, they do not come from God (compare the Euthyphro dilemma).
  • On Kant (paraphrase): Kant's moral convictions were those he had learned in childhood; the argument dresses up an upbringing.
  • So moral experience is better explained by human sources.
  • Responses: many theists say goodness is God's nature, so neither arbitrary nor independent; and where a belief came from does not settle whether it is true.
Mission 3

Morality as social conditioning

  • Moral codes are learned: from family, religion, school, law and culture.
  • They vary across societies and change over time, which fits a human origin.
  • Evolutionary accounts: cooperation, care for kin and fairness helped groups survive; Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion (2006), explains our moral sense this way (paraphrase).
  • So the sense of obligation needs no divine source.
  • Responses: a shared moral core (e.g. against cruelty for its own sake) appears across cultures; reformers have resisted their own conditioning (e.g. opponents of slavery); explaining how we learned a belief does not show it false (genetic fallacy).

Does moral experience point to God?

  • For: Aquinas: degrees of goodness need a standard and source.
  • For: Kant: duty commands the highest good; only God makes it possible.
  • Against: Freud and conditioning: obligation has human sources; Russell's dilemma.
  • Against: the highest good may be a hope, not something duty requires to be achieved: we must only aim at it.

Mission 4

Faith and freedom: the question

  • Existentialism: a 19th–20th-century philosophy that starts from the individual who exists, chooses and must take responsibility.
  • Its big question: what does it mean to live freely and authentically?
  • Theistic existentialists: freedom is fulfilled in a personal commitment of faith in God.
  • Atheistic existentialists: freedom is complete only if there is no God to fix what we are.
  • Both reject faith as mere acceptance of doctrine or custom.
Mission 4

Kierkegaard: the leap

  • Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855), Danish Lutheran thinker; the "father of existentialism".
  • Three stages of life: aesthetic (pleasure), ethical (duty and social rules), religious (a personal relationship with God). Either/Or (1843); Stages on Life's Way (1845).
  • Faith cannot be reached by proof. It is a passionate, personal commitment in the face of uncertainty: commonly called the leap of faith.
  • "Subjectivity is truth": the truth that matters is the one a person lives by (Concluding Unscientific Postscript, 1846).
  • He attacked the comfortable, state Church of Denmark: being a Christian by birth and habit is not faith.
  • So faith is the highest act of freedom: chosen, risky, one's own.
Mission 4Read the source

Abraham and Isaac

> "After these things, God tested Abraham, and said to him, 'Abraham!' He said, 'Here I am.'" (Genesis 22:1, World English Bible, British Edition)

  • God tells Abraham to offer his son Isaac (Genesis 22:2); at the last moment an angel stops him (22:11–12).
  • Kierkegaard, Fear and Trembling (1843, under the name Johannes de silentio), paraphrased: Abraham is the knight of faith. He cannot justify himself by ethics; he trusts God beyond reason. Kierkegaard calls this a teleological suspension of the ethical.
  • Jewish readings (the Akedah, "binding") stress Abraham's obedience and God's rejection of child sacrifice.

Q1 Why can't Abraham explain his act to others?

Show answer

No ethical rule supports it; only his personal relation to God does.

Q2 What does this suggest about faith and freedom for Kierkegaard?

Show answer

Faith is a lonely, free decision that no one else can make for you.

Mission 4

Sartre: condemned to be free

  • Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980): Being and Nothingness (1943); lecture Existentialism Is a Humanism (1945, published 1946).
  • "Existence precedes essence": there is no God to design human nature, so we exist first and then make ourselves by our choices.
  • We are "condemned to be free": we did not choose to exist, but we cannot escape choosing.
  • Anguish: we choose for all humanity. Bad faith: pretending we have no choice ("I had to; it's my role").
  • In the lecture he cites the saying (from Dostoevsky) that if God does not exist, everything is permitted, and accepts it as the existentialist starting point.
  • On Sartre's view, using God or religion as an excuse for our choices would be bad faith: handing our responsibility to someone else.

Mission 4

Feuerbach: God as projection

  • Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872), The Essence of Christianity (1841; English translation by Marian Evans, later known as George Eliot, 1854).
  • Humans take the best qualities of human nature (love, wisdom, goodness), project them outward and call them "God".
  • Theology is really anthropology: the study of humanity.
  • Result: alienation. People see their own best qualities as belonging to someone else and see themselves as sinful and weak.
  • Cure: take the qualities back; love humanity rather than a projected God.
Mission 4

Marx: opium and oppression

  • Karl Marx (1818–1883), introduction to A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right (1844).
  • Religion is "the opium of the people": it soothes the pain of poverty and injustice but does not cure it.
  • It is also the sigh of the oppressed: a real protest against suffering (paraphrase).
  • World-evading: it points to happiness in heaven, so people accept injustice now.
  • Oppressive: ruling classes use it to justify their power (e.g. "the rich man in his castle, the poor man at his gate", from the hymn "All Things Bright and Beautiful", 1848, verse now usually omitted).
  • Alienating: workers are alienated from their labour and from each other; religion masks this.
  • Also: Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900): Christianity is "slave morality", hostile to life (On the Genealogy of Morals, 1887).

Mission 4Diversity

Religious responses

  • Liberation theology: Gustavo Gutiérrez (1928–2024), A Theology of Liberation (1971). Takes Marx's critique seriously: God's preferential option for the poor; faith must change unjust structures.

> "The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent me … to proclaim release to the captives" (Luke 4:18, World English Bible, British Edition)

  • Paul Tillich (1886–1965), The Courage to Be (1952): faith is ultimate concern and the courage to face anxiety; theistic existentialism.
  • Martin Buber (1878–1965), I and Thou (1923): Jewish; we become fully human in I–Thou relationships, with others and with God.
  • Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945): faith means sharing in the world's suffering, not escaping it.
  • Many traditions also accept part of the critique: religion has sometimes served power, and prophets (e.g. Amos 5:24) attacked it too.
Mission 4

Humanism outside and inside religion

  • Outside religion (secular humanism): reason and evidence; morality from empathy and human welfare; one life, so make it good; freedom of belief. Statement: the Amsterdam Declaration (1952; updated 2002 and 2022, Humanists International).
  • Inside religion (religious and Christian humanism): human dignity and creativity as a gift of God.
  • Renaissance: Desiderius Erasmus (c. 1466–1536); Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, Oration on the Dignity of Man (1486).
  • Basis: humans made "in God's image" (Genesis 1:27); love of neighbour (Luke 10:27).
  • Shared ideas: dignity of each person, education, compassion, human rights.
  • Difference: the source and the goal. Secular humanism: human welfare in this life. Christian humanism: human flourishing as part of a relationship with God.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is religion world-evading?

  • Claim: Religion is world-evading, keeping humanity oppressed and alienated.
  • For: Promises of heaven have been used to quieten the poor (Marx); churches have supported unjust rulers and slavery.
  • For: Projection explains why God has human qualities (Feuerbach); faith can be bad faith, escaping responsibility (Sartre).
  • Against: Religious movements have fought oppression: abolition (e.g. William Wilberforce), civil rights (Martin Luther King Jr), liberation theology.
  • Against: Kierkegaard, Tillich and Buber show faith as the most demanding use of freedom, not an escape.
  • Judgement criteria: Judge religion by its ideals or its history? Does a psychological or social origin show a belief is false? Who is more free: the believer or the atheist?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Reincarnation: the atman passes from body to body (Gita 2.22); karma shapes each birth; goal moksha.
  • Rebirth: no unchanging self (anatta); a causal stream shaped by karma continues, "neither the same nor another"; goal nibbana.
  • Near-death experiences: features, verifiable cases and the dying-brain explanation; evidence is disputed.

Mission 2

  • DCT: right is what God commands, wrong is what God forbids; morality is objective and rooted in God's will.
  • The Euthyphro dilemma: if God commands it because it is good, goodness is independent of God; if it is good because God commands it, morality looks arbitrary. Pluralism adds: which commands are God's?
  • Adams' modified DCT grounds commands in God's loving nature; critics ask whether this escapes the dilemma.

Mission 3

  • Aquinas' Fourth Way: degrees of goodness need a maximum that causes all goodness: God.
  • Kant: the moral law comes from reason, but the highest good (virtue with matching happiness) is possible only if we postulate freedom, immortality and God.
  • Critics: Freud (conscience as superego), Russell (dilemma; upbringing), social conditioning.

Mission 4

  • Theistic existentialists (Kierkegaard, Tillich, Buber) see faith as freedom's highest act; atheistic existentialists (Sartre) see freedom as complete only without God.
  • Feuerbach and Marx charge religion with projection, alienation and oppression; liberation theology accepts part of the critique and turns faith towards justice.
  • Humanist principles (dignity, reason, compassion) are found outside religion and inside it; they differ in source and goal.
