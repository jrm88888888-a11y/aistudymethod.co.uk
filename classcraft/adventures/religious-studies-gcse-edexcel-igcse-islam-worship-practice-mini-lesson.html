Mission 1

What salah is

  • Salah (OCR: Salat): the second pillar; set prayer at fixed times, five times a day.
  • A duty (fard) for every Muslim from puberty who is of sound mind; exemptions include women during menstruation.
  • Different from du'a (personal prayer in one's own words;): salah follows set words and movements in Arabic.

Prostrate and remember

Q1 According to 29:45, what does prayer do for the person who prays?

Q2 According to 15:99, how long should a Muslim keep serving Allah?

Show answer

Mission 1

Wudu: washing before prayer

  • Niyyah (intention) and "Bismillah" (in the name of Allah).
  • Sunni practice: wash hands, rinse mouth, nose, wash face, arms to the elbows (right then left), wipe the head and ears with wet hands, wash feet to the ankles; most parts three times.
  • Based on Qur'an 5:6: wash face, hands to the elbows, rub the head, feet to the ankles.
  • If no water: tayammum (clean earth or dust). After sex or menstruation: a full bath (ghusl).
  • Wudu is broken by e.g. using the toilet or deep sleep.
  • Significance: to come before Allah physically and spiritually clean; a time to leave behind distractions and sins.
Mission 1

Five times a day

  • Fajr: dawn to sunrise: 2 rak'ahs.
  • Zuhr (Dhuhr): just after midday: 4.
  • Asr: afternoon: 4.
  • Maghrib: just after sunset: 3.
  • Isha: night: 4.
  • Times follow the sun, so they change through the year; mosques publish timetables, and the adhan announces each one.
  • Rhythm: the day is framed by Allah, from waking to sleeping.

Mission 1

Direction, intention, place

  • Qiblah (OCR: qibla): all Muslims face the Ka'bah in Makkah. In Britain that is roughly south-east.
  • At first Muslims faced Jerusalem; the qiblah changed to Makkah (2:144), about 16 months after the Hijrah.
  • Facing one point unites the ummah worldwide: like rings round the Ka'bah.
  • Niyyah: the intention, made in the heart, to pray this particular prayer for Allah alone.
  • Clean place and clothes: often a prayer mat; shoes removed; body covered modestly (for men at least navel to knee; for women all but face, hands and, in many schools, feet).
Mission 1

One rak'ah, step by step

  • Rak'ah (OCR: raka't; CIE: rakʿah): one cycle of set words and movements.
  • Standing (qiyam): hands raised, "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is greatest). Recite al-Fatihah, then (in the first two rak'ahs) another passage.
  • Bowing (ruku'): "Glory to my Lord, the Most Great."
  • Standing again: "Allah hears the one who praises Him."
  • Prostration (sujud), twice, with a short sitting between: forehead, nose, palms, knees and toes on the ground: "Glory to my Lord, the Most High."
  • After every two rak'ahs and at the end: sitting, reciting the tashahhud (including the Shahadah) and blessings on the Prophet.
  • End: "As-salamu 'alaykum wa rahmatullah" (peace be upon you and the mercy of Allah), turning the head right and left.
  • Significance: standing in respect, bowing in humility, prostrating in total submission: the moment Muslims say they are closest to Allah.
Mission 1Read the source

Al-Fatihah: the Opening

"Praise be to Allah, Lord of the Worlds, The Beneficent, the Merciful. Master of the Day of Judgment, Thee (alone) we worship; Thee (alone) we ask for help. Show us the straight path" (Qur'an 1:2–6, Pickthall)

  • Recited in every rak'ah: at least seventeen times a day.

Mission 1

Why pray?

  • Obedience: Allah commands it (4:103); it is a pillar of Islam.
  • Remembrance: "establish worship for My remembrance" (Qur'an 20:14, Pickthall).
  • Protection: it "restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (29:45).
  • Cleansing: the Prophet compared five daily prayers to bathing in a river five times a day: no dirt remains (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Discipline and unity: the day is shaped by Allah; Muslims everywhere pray the same way towards one point.

Mission 2

Masjid: a place of prostration

  • Mosque comes from Arabic masjid: "place of prostration" (sujud, bowing the forehead to the ground in salah).
  • Muslims may pray anywhere clean: the Prophet said the whole earth has been made a place of prayer for him (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • So a mosque is not needed for every prayer. It is where the community prays together.
  • The first mosques: Quba' near Madinah, built when the Prophet arrived in 622 CE; then the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah: a simple courtyard, walls of mud brick, a roof of palm leaves on palm trunks.
  • A large mosque used for Friday prayer is a jami' masjid ("gathering mosque").
Mission 2

Outside: minaret and dome

  • Minaret: the tower from which the adhan (call to prayer) was traditionally called.
  • Dome: a symbol of the vault of heaven, of Allah's creation over all; it also carries the voice of the imam round the hall.
  • Neither is required. Mosques take the shape of their culture:
  • e.g. pagoda-style roofs at the Great Mosque of Xi'an (China); mud-brick walls at Djenné (Mali); terraced houses and former churches, chapels and shops in Britain.
  • What every mosque needs: a clean prayer space, a way to show the direction of Makkah, and somewhere for wudu (washing).
Mission 2

Inside: everything faces Makkah

  • Qiblah: the direction of the Ka'bah in Makkah. The wall facing it is the qiblah wall.
  • Mihrab: a niche in the qiblah wall. It shows the direction and marks where the imam leads prayer.
  • Minbar: stepped platform beside the mihrab, from which the imam gives the khutbah (sermon) at Friday prayer. The Prophet preached from a minbar of three steps.
  • Musalla (prayer hall): an open carpeted space, no seats or images; lines on the carpet keep worshippers in straight rows shoulder to shoulder.
  • Wudu area (ablution area): taps, seats and drains for washing hands, mouth, nose, face, arms, head and feet before salah (see Mission 1).
  • Shoes are left at the entrance: the prayer space is kept clean.

Mission 2

Calligraphy, pattern, no images

  • Mosques contain no pictures of people or animals: nothing that could be worshipped or distract from Allah. Hadith also warn against making images of living beings (paraphrase).
  • Shirk (associating anything with Allah) is the gravest sin; Ibrahim's breaking of the idols (21:58) is its model rejection.
  • Instead: calligraphy (beautiful writing) of Qur'an verses, the name Allah, the name Muhammad, and in Shi'a mosques often Ali, Hasan and Husayn.
  • Styles include angular Kufic and flowing Naskh and Thuluth.
  • Geometric patterns and arabesques (plant-like scrolls) repeat without end: many Muslims see them as pointing to Allah's infinity and the order of creation.
  • Purpose: to express belief (Tawhid), honour Allah's words, aid remembrance, and beautify worship without images.
Mission 2

What happens in a mosque

  • Salah five times a day; Jumu'ah (Friday midday prayer) with the khutbah from the minbar; Eid prayers.
  • Madrasah: classes, often after school, where children learn to read the Qur'an in Arabic and learn about Islam.
  • Life events: marriages (nikah), funeral prayers (janazah).
  • Ramadan: shared iftar (meal to break the fast) and night prayers.
  • Community: food banks, advice, youth groups, libraries, and open days for visitors of other faiths and none.
Mission 2Diversity

How important is the mosque?

  • Very important: a well-known hadith says prayer in congregation is worth twenty-seven times prayer alone (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). Jumu'ah must be prayed together.
  • Many say home matters as much: salah is valid anywhere clean; for women, many Sunni scholars teach that prayer at home is as rewarded as in the mosque.
  • Different communities, different uses: in Britain many mosques serve one ethnic or language group; others are deliberately mixed. Some Shi'a communities also use an imambargah or husayniyya for gatherings (e.g. Muharram).
  • For many young British Muslims, the mosque's role as community and youth centre matters as much as prayer.
Mission 2Evaluate

"A mosque is essential for a Muslim's worship."

  • Reasons for: Jumu'ah must be prayed in congregation; congregational prayer brings greater reward (hadith); the mosque teaches the Qur'an, unites the ummah and supports family life.
  • Reasons against: "the earth has been made a mosque": salah is valid anywhere clean; many Muslims pray most prayers at home, work or school; women are not required to attend Jumu'ah.
  • Another view: the mosque is essential for the community, while each person's worship depends on intention and remembrance wherever they are.
  • Judge it by: Which kinds of worship need a group? Does "essential" mean required by the Shari'ah or very important in practice? Does the answer differ for men and women, or for Muslims far from any mosque?

Mission 3

Jumu'ah: Friday prayer

  • Jumu'ah (AQA, OCR, EDXA, EDXB, EDQ: Jummah; CIE: Jumʿah; EDXI: Jum'a): the Friday congregational prayer, held in place of Zuhr.
  • Form: a sermon (khutbah), then two rak'ahs led by the imam.
  • Who must attend (Sunni teaching): adult Muslim men who are healthy and not travelling. Women, children, travellers and the sick need not attend, but may; if they do, it replaces their Zuhr.
  • Preparation: a full wash (ghusl), clean clothes, arriving early.
  • Shi'a practice: during the Imam's occultation, many Twelver scholars teach that Jumu'ah may be prayed instead of Zuhr, not that it is required; large Friday prayers are held in Iran and Iraq, and in some British Shi'a centres.
Mission 3Read the source

Leave your trading

"O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday … hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business … That is best for you if ye but knew!" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

"And when the prayer is ended, then disperse in the land and seek of Allah's bounty, and remember Allah much, that ye may be successful." (Qur'an 62:10, Pickthall)

  • Friday is not a day of rest like a Sabbath: work stops for the prayer, then continues.

Q1 What must believers do when the Friday call is heard?

Show answer

Hurry to remember Allah and leave business.

Q2 What may they do after the prayer?

Show answer

Go back to work and seek Allah's bounty, still remembering Allah.

Mission 3

The khutbah

  • Khutbah (OCR: khutba): a sermon in two parts, given by the imam from the minbar (pulpit); he sits briefly between them.
  • Contains praise of Allah, blessings on the Prophet, verses of the Qur'an and a du'a for the ummah.
  • Language: traditionally Arabic; in Britain usually partly in English or another community language, so all can understand.
  • Topics: faith, character, family life, current events, how to live as Muslims in society.
  • Worshippers sit in silence and listen: the khutbah is part of the worship.
Mission 3

Praying together

  • Rows: shoulder to shoulder, feet together, facing the qiblah behind one imam.
  • Equality: rich and poor, of every race and nation, stand side by side: the ummah made visible.
  • Reward: the Prophet taught that prayer in congregation is twenty-seven times better than prayer alone (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Mosque practice: men usually pray in front; women behind or in their own area or balcony; some mosques have no women's space, which many Muslims criticise.
  • Purpose of congregational prayer: unity, discipline, learning from others, support for the weak in faith.
  • Many Muslims pray all five prayers in the mosque; others go mainly for Jumu'ah, Ramadan and Eid.

Mission 3

Prayer at home and elsewhere

  • The Prophet taught that the whole earth has been made a place of prayer: any clean place will do (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Home: many Muslims pray most daily prayers at home, often on a prayer mat in a quiet room; families may pray together, with a parent leading.
  • The Prophet urged believers to pray some prayers (especially voluntary ones) at home, so that homes are not like graves without worship (hadith, paraphrase).
  • The home as a place of worship: children learn the movements and words by copying; the Qur'an is recited; du'a is part of daily life.
  • Elsewhere: prayer rooms at school, work, airports, hospitals; outdoors on a coat or mat. Travellers may shorten four-rak'ah prayers to two.
  • Women: prayer at home is fully valid; many women also attend mosques.
Mission 3

Du'a: calling on Allah

  • Du'a ("call"): personal prayer or supplication: asking Allah for help, forgiveness and guidance, or thanking Him.
  • Any time, any place, any language, in a person's own words or in prayers from the Qur'an and the Prophet; often with hands raised, palms up.
  • Preparation (CIE): being clean, facing the qiblah, praising Allah and blessing the Prophet first, being sincere and patient.
  • Special times: after salah, the last part of the night, while fasting, on Friday.
  • Content: e.g. "Our Lord! Give unto us in the world that which is good and in the Hereafter that which is good" (Qur'an 2:201, Pickthall).
Mission 3Read the source

"I am indeed close"

"When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" (Qur'an 2:186, Yusuf Ali)

Show answer

Q2 Why might this verse encourage private worship?

Show answer

Mission 3

Dhikr: remembering Allah

  • Dhikr (remembrance): repeating praises of Allah, aloud or silently.
  • After salah: "SubhanAllah" (glory be to Allah) 33 times, "Alhamdulillah" (praise be to Allah) 33 times, "Allahu Akbar" (Allah is greatest) 33 or 34 times, often counted on prayer beads (misbaha or tasbih).
  • Significance: "in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Qur'an 13:28, Pickthall); it keeps Allah in mind between prayers.
  • Sufis make dhikr a central practice.

Mission 3Diversity

How important is prayer? Different views

  • All Muslims agree salah is a duty; many teach it is the first deed judged on the Day of Judgement.
  • Abandoning prayer: some scholars (e.g. many Hanbalis) teach that deliberately abandoning salah takes a person out of Islam; most teach it is a very grave sin, but not unbelief.
  • Inner and outer: some stress correct performance; others, especially Sufis, stress khushu' (humble attention): a prayer without the heart is empty.
  • Mosque and home: some Muslims see the mosque as the heart of the community and attend every prayer; others pray mostly at home and go to the mosque on Fridays.
  • Individual and congregational: congregational prayer builds unity and gains reward; individual prayer and du'a allow personal, heartfelt conversation with Allah. Most Muslims value both.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Praying in the mosque is more important than praying at home."

  • For: congregational prayer is "twenty-seven times" better (hadith). Jumu'ah must be prayed in congregation. The khutbah teaches and guides. The ummah is strengthened when Muslims pray side by side.
  • Against: the whole earth is a place of prayer, and Allah is "close" to every caller (2:186). The Prophet encouraged prayer at home. Sincerity matters more than place. For many women, carers and people far from a mosque, home is where they can pray.
  • Another view: they do different jobs: the mosque builds the community; the home builds personal faith and passes it to children.
  • Judge it by: What is prayer for? Does "more important" mean more reward, more duty or more effect on a person's life? Does it differ for men and women?

Mission 4

First words: adhan and iqamah

  • Soon after birth, the father or an elder whispers the adhan (call to prayer) into the baby's right ear.
  • Many also say the iqamah (the call that begins prayer) into the left ear.
  • So the first words the child hears are "Allahu akbar" and the Shahadah: there is no god but Allah, and Muhammad is His messenger.
  • Meaning: the child is born into the ummah; life begins with Tawhid and the call to worship.
  • Tahnik: a tiny piece of softened date (or honey) is rubbed on the baby's gums, following the Prophet's practice (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). It expresses hope for a sweet, good life.
  • Muslims believe every child is born in a natural state of submission to Allah (fitrah).
Mission 4

The seventh day: name, hair and aqiqah

  • Tasmiyah (naming): often on the 7th day. A name with a good meaning: a prophet's name, or 'Abd ("servant") with one of Allah's names, e.g. Abdullah, Abdur-Rahman.
  • Head-shaving: the baby's hair is shaved; its weight in silver (or its value) is given to the poor, following the example of Fatimah for her son Hasan.
  • Aqiqah: an animal is sacrificed in thanks for the child. In Sunni practice, often two sheep or goats for a boy and one for a girl; in Shi'a practice one for either. The meat is shared with family, friends and the poor.
  • Khitan (circumcision) of boys: a sunnah from Ibrahim. Some do it in the first week; others later in childhood.
  • Importance: thanks to Allah, a good start in the faith, and the community sharing the family's joy.
Mission 4Diversity

One ummah, different welcomes

  • Shared by all: adhan, a good name, thanks to Allah, gifts to the poor. These mark the child as part of the ummah.
  • Who whispers the adhan: father, grandfather, or a respected imam or scholar.
  • Aqiqah: two animals for a boy and one for a girl (most Sunni scholars); one for either (Shi'a scholars, and the Maliki school). Some hold it later, or pay a charity to sacrifice abroad and give the meat to the poor.
  • Status: most scholars call aqiqah a strong sunnah; some Shi'a scholars stress it more strongly.
  • Culture: in some families a large party follows; others keep it simple, worried that spending can hide the religious purpose.
  • Britain: circumcision is done by trained practitioners or doctors; aqiqah meat comes from licensed butchers.

Mission 4

Marriage: the nikah

  • Nikah: marriage, a contract (aqd nikah) and a sacred bond. Marriage is strongly encouraged as the Prophet's sunnah.
  • Consent: both bride and groom must freely agree; a forced marriage is not valid.
  • Offer and acceptance (ijab and qabul) are spoken before witnesses: in Sunni law two adult Muslim witnesses are required; Shi'a law recommends witnesses but does not require them.
  • Wali: a guardian, usually the bride's father, represents her in most Sunni schools; the Hanafi school allows an adult woman to contract her own marriage.
  • Mahr: a gift from the groom to the bride, agreed in the contract. It belongs to her alone.
  • The contract is signed; a short sermon (khutbah) and du'a follow, often in a mosque or at home.

Mission 4

Customs and the walimah

  • Before: families meet; in South Asian families, a mehndi (henna) evening for the bride.
  • During: the nikah itself is short and simple: contract, mahr, witnesses, khutbah, du'a for the couple.
  • After: the walimah, a wedding feast given by the groom's family. The Prophet told a newly married companion to give a walimah "even with one sheep" (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Much of the rest (dress colours, music, gold) is culture, varying between countries.
  • Britain: a nikah alone is not a legal marriage. Many couples also have a civil ceremony, or marry in a mosque registered for marriages.

Mission 4

Divorce, khul' and remarriage

  • Divorce is allowed but a last resort; many Muslims cite a hadith calling it the most disliked of permitted things (Sunan Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • First, reconciliation: "appoint an arbiter from his folk and an arbiter from her folk" (Qur'an 4:35, Pickthall).
  • Talaq: divorce pronounced by the husband, followed by a waiting period (iddah, about three months, 2:228). Reconciliation is possible during it, up to twice (2:229).
  • Khul': the wife may end the marriage, usually returning the mahr ("if the woman ransom herself", Qur'an 2:229, Pickthall).
  • A qadi or, in Britain, a Shari'ah council can also dissolve a marriage at the wife's request.
  • Differences: Shi'a law needs two witnesses for talaq and rejects "triple talaq" said at once; many Muslim countries have restricted it too.
  • Remarriage is allowed for both after the iddah.
Mission 4

Death and the funeral

  • Beliefs: life is a test; death comes at the time Allah decrees (sovereignty of Allah); then judgement and akhirah (life after death).

> "Every soul will taste of death. And ye will be paid on the Day of Resurrection only that which ye have fairly earned." (Qur'an 3:185, Pickthall)

  • At death: the dying person hears or says the Shahadah; eyes are closed; mourners say "we are Allah's and unto Him we are returning" (2:156).
  • Ghusl: the body is washed, by people of the same sex, usually family.
  • Kafan: wrapped in simple white cloths: rich and poor alike.
  • Salat al-janazah: funeral prayer said standing, with no bowing or prostration.
  • Burial: as soon as possible, usually within a day; never cremation. The body lies on its right side, facing Makkah.
  • Graves are simple. Mourners pray for the dead.
Mission 4Diversity

How long do Muslims mourn?

  • Weeping is allowed: the Prophet wept when his infant son Ibrahim died (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). Loud wailing and tearing clothes are discouraged.
  • Three days of mourning for relatives (hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari); visitors bring food so the family need not cook.
  • Widows: a waiting period of "four months and ten days" (Qur'an 2:234, Pickthall), with no remarriage and simple dress.
  • Shi'a Muslims often hold majalis for the dead on the 3rd, 7th and 40th days, and yearly.
  • Some Sunni cultures, e.g. in South Asia, also mark the 40th day; other Sunni scholars reject this as bid'ah (an added practice).
  • Why the differences: different hadith collections and schools, and local culture.
  • Britain: Muslim sections in cemeteries; families ask for quick release of the body; some fly the body home for burial.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Salah: set prayer five times a day (Fajr, Zuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha) after wudu, facing the qiblah, with niyyah and set rak'ahs reciting al-Fatihah.
  • Muslims pray to obey and remember Allah, to be cleansed and kept from wrong (29:45), and to unite the ummah.

Mission 2

  • A mosque (masjid, "place of prostration") is the centre of communal worship and of the local ummah; its key features face Makkah: qiblah wall, mihrab, minbar, musalla, wudu area.
  • No images of living beings; calligraphy and geometric patterns express Tawhid and reject idolatry (Ibrahim breaking the idols, Qur'an 21:58).

Mission 3

  • Jumu'ah (Friday prayer) replaces Zuhr: a two-part khutbah then two rak'ahs in congregation (62:9–10); in Sunni teaching it is required of adult men.
  • Salah may be prayed at home or anywhere clean; congregational prayer brings unity and greater reward.
  • Du'a is personal prayer in any words or language (2:186); dhikr is remembrance of Allah, especially after salah.

Mission 4

  • Birth: adhan and iqamah, tahnik, tasmiyah on the 7th day, head-shaving with charity, aqiqah, khitan for boys.
  • Marriage: nikah with consent, offer and acceptance, witnesses, contract and mahr (4:4), then walimah; love and mercy (30:21).
  • Divorce is a last resort: talaq, khul', iddah, remarriage.
  • Death: ghusl, white kafan, salat al-janazah, quick burial facing Makkah; mourning periods differ (three days; widows four months and ten days; Shi'a 3rd, 7th, 40th days).
