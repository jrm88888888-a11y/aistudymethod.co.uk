Mission 1

Mission 1: If someone is dying and in pain, is helping them die an act of mercy or of killing?

Make a guess: If someone is dying and in pain, is helping them die an act of mercy or of killing?

Mission 1

Kinds of euthanasia

  • Euthanasia (Greek: "good death"): ending a life to relieve suffering, sometimes called mercy killing.
  • Voluntary: the person asks for it. Non-voluntary: the person cannot ask (e.g. in a long-term coma). Involuntary: against, or without asking, the wishes of a person who could decide.
  • Active: something is done to end life (e.g. a lethal injection). Passive: treatment that keeps someone alive is withdrawn or withheld, and they die of their illness.
  • Assisted suicide (assisted dying): the person ends their own life with help, e.g. with drugs prescribed by a doctor.
  • Not euthanasia: refusing treatment that is too burdensome, or giving pain relief that may shorten life when the aim is to ease pain.
Mission 1

The law

  • UK: active euthanasia is treated as murder or manslaughter. In England and Wales, assisting suicide is a crime (Suicide Act 1961).
  • Withdrawing treatment that is no longer in a patient's best interests can be lawful (e.g. the Tony Bland case, 1993). Patients may refuse treatment.
  • In 2025 MPs voted for a bill to allow assisted dying for some terminally ill adults in England and Wales, but it ran out of time in the House of Lords in 2026 and did not become law. The Scottish Parliament rejected a similar bill in March 2026.
  • Elsewhere: euthanasia is legal in the Netherlands and Belgium (2002) and Canada (2016); assisted suicide is allowed in Switzerland and some US states (Oregon, 1997).

Mission 1

Hospice and palliative care

  • Palliative care: care that relieves pain and other symptoms when a cure is no longer possible. It neither hastens nor delays death.
  • Hospices care for dying people and their families: physical, emotional, social and spiritual needs.
  • Dame Cicely Saunders (1918–2005), a Christian nurse, social worker and doctor, founded St Christopher's Hospice, London (1967), the start of the modern hospice movement.
  • Many religious believers see hospices as the alternative to euthanasia: dying well, with dignity and without pain.
  • Dying well: Catholic teaching sees death as a passage to eternal life; it should be faced with prayer, the sacraments (e.g. anointing of the sick) and loving care.

Mission 1Diversity

Christian views

  • Catholic teaching (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2276–2279, paraphrase): direct euthanasia, whatever the motive, is murder and gravely wrong. Stopping treatment that is burdensome, extraordinary or disproportionate can be right. Painkillers may be given even if they may shorten life, if death is not intended. Palliative care is a special form of charity.
  • Pope John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae (1995), 65: euthanasia is a grave violation of God's law; refusing over-aggressive treatment is not euthanasia (paraphrase).
  • Church of England and most other churches oppose changing the law: life is God's gift; vulnerable people could feel pressure to die.
  • Some Christians support assisted dying out of compassion; e.g. a former Archbishop of Canterbury, Lord Carey (2014), and Archbishop Desmond Tutu.
Quick check
?A Catholic woman with terminal cancer asks to stop chemotherapy, which no longer helps and makes her very ill, and to receive strong pain relief. What does Catholic teaching say?
Mission 1

Non-religious views

  • Dignity in Dying (UK campaign, founded in 1935 as the Voluntary Euthanasia Legalisation Society; renamed in 2006): campaigns for assisted dying for terminally ill, mentally competent adults, with safeguards.
  • Humanists: people should have autonomy over their own lives and deaths; quality of life matters; there is no God whose will must be obeyed.
  • Utilitarianism: assisted dying may reduce suffering for the person and family.
  • Some non-religious people oppose a change: disabled people's groups (e.g. Not Dead Yet UK) fear pressure on the vulnerable; the slippery slope argument says safeguards may weaken over time.
Quick check
?Which argument would most Humanists use to support assisted dying?
Mission 1Evaluate

"Euthanasia should be legal in the UK."

  • Reasons for: autonomy (Humanists; Dignity in Dying, a UK campaign group); compassion (Christian agape, Buddhist karuna); quality of life; strict safeguards work in other countries.
  • Reasons against: sanctity of life (Job 1:21); vulnerable people may feel a burden; the slippery slope; good palliative care (hospices) relieves most suffering; doctors are meant to heal.
  • Middle positions: allow refusing treatment and strong pain relief, but not active euthanasia (Catholic and many other teachings).
  • Judge it by: Whose life is it? Can safeguards protect the vulnerable? Is relieving suffering the same as ending life?
Mission check

?A hospice nurse, a practising Catholic, cares for a dying patient who says: "I just want the pain to stop." Which response best fits her faith and her work?

Almost everyone agrees that suffering should be relieved; the disagreement is whether this may ever include deliberately ending a life.

Mission 2

Two shapes, and the question of the soul

  • Afterlife: existence of some kind after death.
  • Linear view (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life → death → judgement → eternal destiny.
  • Cyclical view (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): birth → death → rebirth, again and again (samsara), shaped by karma (actions and their results), until liberation.
  • Soul: the non-physical, spiritual part of a person; for many believers, the real self that survives death.
Mission 2

Christian beliefs: judgement, heaven and hell

  • Death is not the end: Christians believe the risen Jesus has defeated death.
  • Judgement: God judges each person, through Christ; faith and how people lived both matter (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Heaven: eternal life with God. Many Christians, including Catholic teaching, see it as a state of being with God more than a place in the sky.
  • Hell: eternal separation from God. Catholic teaching describes it as a person's own final rejection of God.
  • Purgatory (Catholic): a state of cleansing after death for those who die in God's friendship but are not yet fully pure; the living pray for them. Most Protestants reject it: they find no clear basis for it in the Bible.
  • At the end of time Christ will return, the dead will be raised, and there will be a Last Judgement.

Show answer

Mission 2

Resurrection of the body or survival of the soul?

  • Survival of the soul (popular belief, and the Greek idea of immortality): the soul floats free of the body for ever; the body was only a shell.
  • Resurrection of the body (Catholic and mainstream Christian teaching): the whole person is raised, body and soul, by God.
  • Catholic teaching: at death the soul is separated from the body and meets God; at the end of time it is reunited with a transformed, glorified body.
  • Why the body matters for Catholics: God made the body good (Genesis 1:31); Jesus rose bodily; the Creed says "I believe in the resurrection of the body".
  • So Catholics treat the dead body with respect: burial is preferred, and ashes after cremation are buried or placed in a sacred place such as a cemetery, not scattered or kept at home.
  • Not resuscitation: Lazarus (John 11) came back to ordinary life and later died again; resurrection means a new, deathless life.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the Catholic belief in the resurrection of the body?
Mission 2

Judaism: this world and the world to come

  • The Tenakh says little about the afterlife; Judaism focuses on living this life by the mitzvot.
  • Later books and the rabbis teach resurrection: "many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to reproaches and everlasting abhorrence." (Daniel 12:2, JPS 1917)
  • Olam ha-ba: "the world to come": the age after the Messiah, or the life of the soul after death.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Belief in life after death makes this life more valuable."

  • Reasons for: life is a test or preparation; how we live is judged (Matthew 25; Qur'an 99:7–8: "whoso doeth good an atom's weight will see it then"). Karma means every act counts. Human life is rare and precious (Sikh, Buddhist, Hindu). Hope gives comfort and courage to care for the dying.
  • Reasons against: focus on the next life may lessen effort in this one. Humanists: because this is our only life, it is all the more precious; people live on through memory and good done.
  • Judge it by: Does hope of reward make good acts less generous? Which view better motivates care for others? Can value come without an afterlife?
Mission check

?Two hospital volunteers, one Catholic and one Hindu, each say their belief about death is part of why they help. Which pair of explanations fits their traditions?

Linear or cyclical, most religions link what happens after death to how people live now.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can anyone show there is life after death, or is it only a comforting hope?

Make a guess: Can anyone show there is life after death, or is it only a comforting hope?

Mission 3

What could count as evidence?

  • Nobody can return from a full death and be tested, so people argue from other kinds of evidence:
  • Experience: near-death experiences; remembered lives; the paranormal (contact with the dead through mediums, ghosts).
  • Logic: reasoning that a soul or a just God makes an afterlife likely.
  • Reward: justice seems to need a life where good and evil are finally put right.
  • Comfort and meeting loved ones: hope that death is not the end of love.
  • Revelation: what scripture and religious teachers say.
  • Each one can be questioned. That is this mission.
Mission 3

Near-death experiences

  • Near-death experience (NDE): what some people report after coming close to death, e.g. in cardiac arrest.
  • Common features: leaving the body and seeing it from above; a tunnel; a bright, loving light; meeting dead relatives; a review of one's life; a sense of being sent back.
  • Made widely known by Raymond Moody, Life After Life (1975).
  • Real? Reports are similar across cultures; some people describe events in the room they could not have seen.
  • Illusory? The dying brain short of oxygen, drugs, or dream-like states (REM intrusion) can produce similar effects; the person did not actually die; reports are made afterwards and memory changes.
  • Believers usually treat NDEs as support for faith, not the basis of it.
Mission 3

Remembered lives and the paranormal

  • Remembered lives: some young children describe a previous life: names, places, the way they died.
  • Ian Stevenson (1918–2007), psychiatrist, Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation (1966), collected hundreds of such cases, many in India, Sri Lanka, Lebanon and Turkey.
  • Critics: cases come mainly where rebirth is already believed; families may shape the child's story; checks were made after families had met.
  • Paranormal: mediums claim to pass messages from the dead; some people report ghosts.
  • Critics: many mediums have been exposed as frauds; "cold reading" (clever guessing from clues) explains many messages.
  • Christians, Muslims and Jews mostly reject contacting the dead: the Torah forbids anyone "who consults with a familiar spirit, or a wizard, or a necromancer" (Deuteronomy 18:11).
Quick check
?A newspaper reports that a four-year-old in Sri Lanka names a village she has never visited and says she drowned there in a past life. Which response is a critic most likely to make?
Mission 3

The case against

  • Lack of evidence: no claim has been confirmed under controlled tests.
  • Mind depends on the brain: brain damage changes memory and personality; with no working brain, there is nothing left to experience (physicalism).
  • Fraud: fake mediums and invented stories damage trust in all such reports.
  • Comfort: belief may be wishful thinking: people believe because they fear death or loss.
  • Social control: promises of heaven and threats of hell can keep people obedient. Karl Marx (1818–1883) called religion "the opium of the people" (1844): it soothes the poor instead of changing their situation.
  • Non-religious views of the soul: Humanists and most atheists see the "self" as the activity of the brain; it ends at death. People live on only in memories, children and the good they did.
Mission 3

Is death the end of the person?

  • Yes: Epicurus (341–270 BCE): death is nothing to us, because when death is, we are not (paraphrase). So there is nothing to fear.
  • Humanists: this is our one life; make it good for ourselves and others.
  • No (Catholic): death ends earthly life, but not the person. The soul lives on and meets God; the whole person will be raised (1 Corinthians 15).
  • Catholics reply to Epicurus: love and the longing for God point beyond death; Christ's resurrection is the "first fruit" of those who have died (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Some liberal Christians are agnostic about details, holding only that people rest in God.

Mission 3Diversity

Replying to the objections

  • Lack of evidence: religious believers say the afterlife is known by revelation and faith, not lab tests; absence of proof is not proof of absence.
  • Christians point to the resurrection of Jesus and its witnesses (1 Corinthians 15:6).
  • Mind and brain: dualists say the brain may be how the soul acts in the body, as a radio receives a signal; damage the radio and the signal seems to change.
  • Comfort: something can be comforting and true. Many teachings (judgement, hell, karma) are not comforting at all.
  • Fraud: fake mediums do not show that every claim is false; most religions reject mediums anyway.
  • Social control: early Christians, the first Muslims in Makkah and early Sikhs were persecuted minorities, not rulers; believers point to prophets and Gurus who challenged the powerful.
Quick check
?A Humanist says: "People believe in heaven because it comforts them, not because it is true." Which reply would most Christians give?
Mission 3Evaluate

"There is no good evidence for life after death."

  • Reasons for: no claim survives controlled testing; NDEs have brain-based explanations; remembered-life cases may be shaped by culture; fraud is common; mind depends on brain.
  • Reasons against: NDE reports are consistent and some are hard to explain; Stevenson's best cases remain puzzling; billions find revelation and religious experience convincing; Christians point to eyewitnesses of the risen Jesus.
  • Another view: evidence may not be the right test: belief in an afterlife is a matter of faith or trust, like trusting a person.
  • Judge it by: What should count as "good" evidence for something beyond death? Is personal testimony enough? Does lack of proof show there is nothing?
Mission 3Worked answer

Evaluate with reasons on both sides

Statement: "Belief in life after death is just a comfort for people who are afraid of dying." Evaluate this statement.

  • For: a Humanist argues there is no evidence, and fear of death explains why the belief is so widespread.
  • Against + source: Catholics reply that faith rests on the risen Christ, not on fear: "If Christ has not been raised … your faith also is in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14).
  • Development: belief in judgement can be frightening, not comforting, so comfort cannot be the whole explanation.
  • Judgement: weigh which reasons are stronger and say why, rather than repeating one side.
Mission check

?A TV show claims a medium has passed on messages from a famous singer who has died. A sceptic watching says this tells us nothing about life after death. Which reason fits the sceptic's case best?

Every argument for life after death meets an objection, and every objection meets a reply; the key question is what counts as evidence.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Euthanasia: voluntary, non-voluntary, involuntary; active or passive; assisted suicide. In the UK active euthanasia and assisting suicide are crimes; bills to allow assisted dying failed in 2026.
  • Most religions oppose euthanasia (sanctity of life, God decides, karma, First Precept) and support hospices and palliative care (Cicely Saunders, 1967); Catholic teaching allows refusing burdensome treatment and strong pain relief.
  • Humanists and Dignity in Dying support assisted dying for autonomy and quality of life; utilitarianism may support it; some non-religious groups oppose it.

Mission 2

  • Linear views (Christianity, Islam, Judaism): one life, judgement, heaven or hell; resurrection. Cyclical views (Hinduism, Buddhism, Sikhism): karma, rebirth, liberation (moksha, nibbana, mukti).
  • Catholics believe in the resurrection of the body, not just survival of the soul (1 Corinthians 15:42–44).

Mission 3

  • Arguments for: NDEs, remembered lives, the paranormal, logic, reward, comfort, meeting loved ones, revelation.
  • Objections: lack of evidence, mind depends on brain, fraud, wishful thinking, social control (Marx).
  • Religious replies: revelation and faith; Jesus' resurrection; comfort can still be true; the Gita: the unenlightened do not see the soul.
