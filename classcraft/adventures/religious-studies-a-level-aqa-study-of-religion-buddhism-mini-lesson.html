Mission 1

The Going Forth

  • At 29, after the Four Sights, Siddhattha leaves the palace: the Great Going Forth.
  • Later biographies: he leaves at night on his horse Kanthaka with Channa, glances at his sleeping wife Yasodhara and newborn son Rahula, then cuts off his hair and puts on an ascetic's robe.
  • The Buddha's own account is plainer (Majjhima Nikaya 26, paraphrase): while still young and black-haired, he shaved his head and went forth, though his mother and father wept.
  • He becomes a samana (wandering seeker), seeking what is "beyond ageing and death".
  • For Theravada Buddhists this renunciation is a model: monks and nuns repeat it when they go forth.
Mission 1

Teachers and austerities

  • He studies with two meditation teachers (Majjhima Nikaya 26):
  • Alara Kalama: the meditative state of "nothingness".
  • Uddaka Ramaputta: the state of "neither perception nor non-perception".
  • He masters both, but they do not end suffering: when the meditation ends, the problem returns.
  • Then about six years of extreme austerity with five companions: holding the breath, eating almost nothing.
  • His own memory (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase): his spine stood out like a string of beads; when he touched his belly he felt his backbone.
  • Austerity did not bring awakening either.
Mission 1

The Middle Way

  • He remembers a childhood moment: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he entered calm, joyful meditation (jhana) without effort (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Insight: that joy is not to be feared; it may be the path. But a starved body cannot reach it.
  • He eats again. Later lives name the giver: Sujata, who offers milk-rice.
  • His five companions leave him, thinking he has given up.
  • The Middle Way: neither sensual indulgence (the palace) nor self-torment (the austerities). Taught in his first sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).

Mission 1

Mara: the night of the Awakening

  • At Bodh Gaya, he sits under a tree (later called the Bodhi tree) and resolves not to rise until he is awake.
  • Mara ("death", "the killer"), lord of desire and death, tries to stop him: storms and armies, his daughters as temptation, then a challenge to his right to the seat.
  • Readings of Mara:
  • A real being: in traditional cosmology, a powerful deity of the realm of desire.
  • Inner forces: the Sutta Nipata lists Mara's ten "armies" as sensual desire, discontent, hunger and thirst, craving, sloth, fear, doubt, hypocrisy, gain and fame, and pride (Sutta Nipata 3.2, paraphrase).
  • Both readings agree: awakening means overcoming craving, fear and doubt.
Mission 1

Touching the earth

  • Mara demands: who will witness your right to sit here?
  • Siddhattha says nothing. He touches the earth with the fingers of his right hand.
  • The earth itself witnesses the good deeds of his past lives (the perfections he built over many births).
  • In South-East Asian tradition the earth goddess (Thai: Phra Mae Thorani) wrings water from her hair and floods Mara's army away.
  • Bhumisparsha mudra (earth-touching gesture): seated, left hand in the lap, right hand over the right knee, fingers to the ground. One of the most common Buddha images, especially in Thailand and Myanmar.
  • Meaning: his authority comes from his own effort across lives, and the earth, not a god, is witness.
Mission 1Read the source

Three knowledges

The Buddha describes the three watches of that night (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase):

  • First watch: he recalls his past lives: names, families, joys and sufferings, over many ages.
  • Second watch: with the "divine eye" he sees beings dying and being reborn according to their karma.
  • Third watch: he knows dukkha, its cause, its ending and the path to its ending. The "taints" are destroyed: he is awake (Buddha).

The Dhammapada gives words for that moment, addressed to craving as the builder of the house of rebirth:

> "But now, maker of the tabernacle, thou hast been seen; thou shalt not make up this tabernacle again." (Dhammapada 153–154, Müller 1881)

Q1 Who or what is the "maker of the tabernacle"?

Show answer

Craving: the cause of rebirth, now seen clearly.

Q2 Which watch is this verse about?

Show answer

Mission 1

Teaching career and parinibbana

  • At first he hesitates: the truth is subtle. The god Brahma Sahampati asks him to teach (Majjhima Nikaya 26).
  • Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath): first sermon to the five companions, the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11). Kondañña understands first.
  • The sangha grows: monks, then nuns (Mahapajapati Gotami), and lay followers of every class.
  • About 45 years of teaching across the Ganges plain.
  • At 80, at Kusinara, he dies: the parinibbana (final passing away, with no further rebirth).
  • Last words (Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrase): all conditioned things decay; strive on with diligence.
Mission 1

Role model or skilful means?

  • Theravada: the Buddha was a human being who found the path by his own effort. He is "the enlightened one": his authority comes from his awakening, not from being a god.
  • He is a role model: his renunciation, effort and Middle Way show what anyone can do.

> "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller 1881)

  • Mahayana (Lotus Sutra): the Buddha was awakened countless ages ago. His birth, going forth, awakening and death were skilful means (upaya): a life shown to lead beings to the Dharma.
  • Burning house (Lotus Sutra, chapter 3, paraphrase): a father lures his children from a burning house by promising three kinds of carts; outside, he gives each the one best carriage. Teachings are adapted to hearers. Full study in Mission 2.

Mission 2

The Lotus Sutra

  • Full title: Saddharmapundarika Sutra, "the Lotus of the True Dharma". A Mahayana text.
  • Composed in stages, roughly 1st century BCE to 2nd century CE, in India.
  • Kumarajiva's Chinese translation (406 CE) made it one of the most loved texts in East Asia: Tiantai (China), Tendai and Nichiren Buddhism (Japan).
  • Its big claims: there is really one vehicle (ekayana) to Buddhahood; all beings can become Buddhas; the Buddha is far greater than his one human life.
  • It teaches mainly through parables: stories that carry the doctrine.
Mission 2

Upaya: skilful means

  • Upaya (full form upaya-kausalya): skilful means, skill in adapting the teaching to the hearer.
  • A good teacher meets people where they are: a child, a scholar and a grieving parent need different words.
  • Lotus Sutra chapter 2 ("Skilful Means"): the Buddha's wisdom is too deep to be grasped at once, so he taught in many ways, to lead beings step by step (paraphrase).
  • So some teachings are provisional: true enough to help, but not the final word.
Mission 2Read the source

The burning house

Lotus Sutra, chapter 3 (paraphrase):

  • A rich old man owns a huge, decaying house with one narrow door. Fire breaks out.
  • His children are inside, absorbed in their games. They do not know what fire is, or fear it.
  • He calls them; they ignore him. So he says: the goat-carts, deer-carts and ox-carts you wanted are waiting outside the gate!
  • They rush out and are safe. Then he gives every child the same great carriage, drawn by white oxen: better than anything promised.
  • The Buddha asks Sariputra: did the father lie? Sariputra: no; he saved their lives, and that was his aim from the start.

Q1 Why did the father not simply tell the truth about the fire?

Show answer

The children did not understand fire, so the warning meant nothing to them; the promise of carts did.

Q2 Why does Sariputra say no falsehood was told?

Show answer

The father's purpose was their rescue, and he gave them more than he promised.

Mission 2

Decoding the parable

  • House on fire = samsara, burning with birth, old age, sickness, death and suffering.
  • Children = all beings, absorbed in pleasures, unaware of danger.
  • Father = the Buddha, who cares for all beings as his children.
  • Three carts = the three vehicles taught earlier: the way of disciples (sravaka), of solitary Buddhas (pratyekabuddha), and of bodhisattvas.
  • Great carriage = the one Buddha vehicle: all are finally led to full Buddhahood.
  • So Mahayana reads the Buddha's own teaching, even his life, as skilful means.

Mission 2

The Buddha's life as skilful means

  • Lotus Sutra "Lifespan" chapter (ch. 16 in Kumarajiva's version; ch. 15 in Kern's): the Buddha says he became awakened countless ages ago (paraphrase).
  • So his birth, renunciation, awakening under the tree and death were a display for beings' sake.
  • The physician (paraphrase): a doctor's sons have taken poison; some refuse the cure. He goes away and sends word he has died. Shocked, they take the medicine. He returns.
  • Meaning: if the Buddha always seemed present, people would grow careless. His parinirvana was skilful means.
  • This is the Mahayana view your spec names: Gautama's life and teaching as skilful means.
Mission 2Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana readings

  • Mahayana: the Lotus Sutra is the Buddha's word (buddhavacana), revealing what earlier teaching held back. Nichiren Buddhists chant its title, Namu Myoho Renge Kyo.
  • Theravada: does not accept the Mahayana sutras as the Buddha's word. Arahantship is the real goal, not a resting place.
  • Common ground: Pali texts already show the Buddha adapting his teaching to hearers, and the raft simile (Majjhima Nikaya 22) says the Dhamma is for crossing over, not for clinging to.
  • The difference: adapting how truth is taught (shared) vs treating earlier doctrines as provisional (Mahayana).
  • Scholar: Michael Pye, Skilful Means: A Concept in Mahayana Buddhism (1978), traces how upaya became a key Mahayana idea.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is skilful means the key to Buddhism's diversity?

  • Claim: Skilful means explains why Buddhism has so many forms.
  • For: Different schools can be seen as teachings fitted to different people and cultures, all leading to one goal.
  • For: It lets Buddhists respect other traditions without calling them simply false.
  • Against: It is a Mahayana idea: Theravadins reject the claim that their goal is provisional, so it cannot unite all Buddhists on its own terms.
  • Against: If any teaching may be provisional, how does anyone tell which is final? The risk is that "skilful" excuses anything.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the idea fit what the Pali texts already say? Does it respect each school's own view of itself? Does it keep a test for what counts as helpful?

Mission 3

Three marks of existence

  • The Buddha taught three marks (Pali tilakkhana) of conditioned existence:
  • anicca: impermanence; dukkha: suffering, unsatisfactoriness; anatta: not-self.
  • The classic summary (Dhammapada 277–279): all conditioned things (sankhara) are impermanent; all conditioned things are dukkha; all dhammas are not-self.
  • Note the shift: dhammas is wider than sankhara. For Theravada, nibbana is unconditioned: not impermanent, not dukkha, but still not-self.
  • Your spec expects Dukkha, Anicca and Nirvana untranslated.
Mission 3

Translating dukkha

  • No English word fits. Common translations:
  • suffering (the usual one); unsatisfactoriness; pain, ill; stress (Thanissaro Bhikkhu).
  • "Suffering" is too strong for a mild let-down; "unsatisfactoriness" is too weak for grief or torture. Dukkha covers both.
  • A traditional explanation (folk etymology, not certain): du- (bad) + kha (axle-hole): a wheel that fits badly, so the ride always jars.
  • The Buddhist point is not that every moment hurts, but that nothing conditioned can give lasting, secure satisfaction.
Mission 3Read the source

The first truth at the Deer Park

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta ("Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion"), Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (paraphrase): the noble truth of dukkha is this: birth, ageing, sickness and death are dukkha; sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha; being joined with what is unpleasant, being parted from what is pleasant, and not getting what one wants are dukkha. In brief, the five clinging-aggregates are dukkha.

And Dhammapada 278 (Müller, 1881):

> "'All created things are grief and pain,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity."

  • "Passive in pain" renders a Pali word meaning turns away from it, grows disenchanted: seeing dukkha is a step to freedom, not despair.

Q1 Which items on the list can no one avoid, however lucky?

Show answer

Birth, ageing, death; parting from what is pleasant. Sickness for almost everyone.

Q2 Why does the list end with the five aggregates?

Show answer

Dukkha is not just bad events: it is built into the conditioned, grasping personality itself.

  • At the end of the sermon Kondañña, one of the five listeners, sees that whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing (paraphrase): anicca in one line.
Mission 3

Three kinds of dukkha

  • Dukkha-dukkha: the dukkha of pain itself: physical and mental pain, illness, grief.
  • Viparinama-dukkha: the dukkha of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • Sankhara-dukkha: the dukkha of conditioned existence: everything depends on causes that shift, so nothing gives a secure footing. The subtlest kind; often seen only through insight meditation.
  • Source: e.g. Dukkhata Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 38.14); explained by Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga XVI.

Mission 3

Anicca: gross and subtle

  • Gross (obvious) impermanence: seasons turn, bodies age, buildings crumble, kingdoms fall, everyone dies.
  • Subtle or momentary impermanence: every bodily and mental event arises and ceases moment by moment. What looks solid is a fast series, like the frames of a film or a flame renewed each instant.
  • The Abhidhamma and its commentators, including Buddhaghosa (5th century CE, Visuddhimagga, "The Path of Purification"), analyse experience into momentary events.
  • In Buddhaghosa's account of insight, the meditator comes to see formations breaking up moment by moment (Visuddhimagga XXI, paraphrase).
  • Anicca is not bad news in itself: change also makes growth, healing and awakening possible.
Mission 3

How the marks link

  • The Buddha's own chain (e.g. Samyutta Nikaya 22.15, paraphrase): what is impermanent is dukkha; what is dukkha is not-self.
  • Anicca → dukkha: change is not painful by itself; dukkha comes from craving and clinging to what changes.
  • Dukkha → anatta: what is unstable and beyond our control cannot be a lasting self ("this is mine, this I am, this is my self").
  • So the three marks are one insight from three angles, not three separate facts.
Quick check

Mission 3

From anicca to emptiness

  • Early Abhidharma schools analysed experience into momentary dharmas; some (e.g. the Sarvastivada) held that each dharma has its own own-nature (svabhava).
  • Mahayana wisdom texts (Prajnaparamita) and Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE) pushed anicca further: even dharmas are empty (sunya) of own-nature.
  • Emptiness (sunyata) is not nothingness: things exist dependently, arising from conditions, with no fixed core.
  • The Heart Sutra: "form is emptiness, emptiness is form" (short phrase in common use).
  • So emptiness can be read as anicca and dependent origination taken all the way down. Full study.
Mission 3

Is Buddhism pessimistic?

  • The charge: the first teaching is suffering; even pleasure is counted as dukkha; the goal is to end rebirth. Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860, The World as Will and Representation, 1818/1844) admired Buddhism as agreeing with his pessimism. Many 19th-century Western readers took nibbana to mean annihilation.
  • The reply: Walpola Rahula (1907–1997), What the Buddha Taught (1959): Buddhism is "neither pessimistic nor optimistic" but realistic (short phrase).
  • Like a doctor, the Buddha names an illness in order to cure it: the third truth says dukkha can end (see Mission 4).
  • Joy has a place: calm and happiness in meditation; "Nirvana the highest happiness" (Dhammapada 204, Müller).
Mission 3Evaluate

Is the teaching on dukkha pessimistic?

  • Claim: The Buddha's teaching on dukkha is pessimistic.
  • For: It defines all conditioned life, even happiness, as dukkha; it values ending rebirth over enjoying it.
  • For: For most beings, release lies many lives away; for them, the teaching mainly diagnoses.
  • Against: A diagnosis is not a mood. The same teaching says dukkha has a cause and can end, and gives a path.
  • Against: Buddhists report contentment, generosity and joy on the path; nibbana is called the highest happiness.
  • Judgement criteria: Does "pessimistic" mean "sees problems" or "denies a cure"? Does the teaching match how things are? How do Buddhists actually live it?

Mission 4

The first sermon

  • After his awakening, the Buddha walked to the Deer Park at Isipatana (Sarnath), near Varanasi (Benares), to his five former companions in asceticism.
  • The sermon is the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, "Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion" (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11).
  • It opens with the Middle Way: avoid both indulgence in sense pleasure and harsh self-torment.
  • Then it sets out the Four Noble Truths.
  • One listener, Kondanna, understood: whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing. He became the first disciple to "see the Dhamma".
Mission 4

Four truths, four tasks

  • In the sermon, the Buddha teaches:
  • Dukkha: there is suffering, unsatisfactoriness. Task: fully understand it.
  • Samudaya (origin): dukkha arises from craving (tanha). Task: abandon it.
  • Nirodha (cessation): dukkha ends when craving ends. Task: realise it.
  • Magga (path): the Noble Eightfold Path leads there. Task: develop it.
  • In the sutta the Buddha claims full awakening only after knowing each truth, its task, and that the task is done: twelve aspects in all.
  • They are "noble" (ariya) truths: truths as seen by the noble ones, who have realised them.
Mission 4

The doctor analogy

  • A traditional comparison treats the Buddha as a physician:
  • Illness = dukkha · Cause = tanha · Cure is possible = nirodha · Prescription = magga.
  • Buddhaghosa uses it in the Visuddhimagga (XVI): the truths are like a disease, its cause, its cure and the medicine.
  • What it teaches: the Buddha diagnoses in order to heal; the first truth is not the last word.
  • Its limits: the patient must take the medicine personally. No one can be cured by someone else's practice.
  • This is the usual reply to the charge of pessimism (see Mission 3).

Mission 5

The Tipitaka: three baskets

  • Pali Canon = Tipitaka ("three baskets"), the scripture of Theravada Buddhism.
  • Vinaya Pitaka: monastic discipline.
  • Sutta Pitaka: the Buddha's discourses (and some of his disciples').
  • Abhidhamma Pitaka: systematic analysis of the teaching ("higher Dhamma").
  • Pali: the language of these texts. Theravada tradition identifies it with the Buddha's own speech (Magadhi).
  • Status: for Theravada, buddhavacana, the word of the Buddha, and the final authority.
  • Mahayana Buddhists have other canons (Chinese, Tibetan) that include parallel early texts and Mahayana sutras.
  • Your spec: "the nature and authority of the Pali Canon".
Mission 5

From memory to palm leaf

  • First Council (Rajagaha), soon after the parinibbana: 500 arahants. Ananda recited the discourses ("Thus have I heard…"); Upali recited the discipline.
  • Oral transmission for about four centuries: groups of reciters (bhanakas) memorised whole collections, chanting together to check each other.
  • Memory aids still visible in the texts: repetition, numbered lists, set phrases, verse.
  • Second Council (Vesali, about a century later): disputes over monastic rules.
  • Third Council (Pataliputta, c. 250 BCE, under Emperor Ashoka), in the Theravada account; then the mission of Mahinda to Sri Lanka.
  • Written down at Aluvihara, Sri Lanka, in the 1st century BCE, when war and famine threatened the reciters.
  • Other early schools kept parallel collections (e.g. the Agamas preserved in Chinese). Much of their content matches the Pali discourses.
Mission 5

The Vinaya Pitaka

  • Rules for monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis), each with the story of why it was made.
  • Includes the Patimokkha (227 rules for Theravada monks), recited every fortnight.
  • Procedures: ordination, the rains retreat, settling disputes.
  • Aim: a cohesive community that lives in harmony and earns the trust of lay supporters.
Mission 5Read the source

The Sutta Pitaka

  • Five collections (nikayas): Digha (long), Majjhima (middle-length), Samyutta (connected), Anguttara (numbered), Khuddaka (minor: includes the Dhammapada, Sutta Nipata, Jataka tales, the poems of the first monks and nuns).
  • Teachings adapted to each hearer: kings, farmers, brahmins, monks.
  • The search for Enlightenment: the Buddha's own quest (Majjhima Nikaya 26, 36), the Four Noble Truths, meditation guidance (Majjhima Nikaya 10).

> "Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened." (Dhammapada 183, Müller 1881)

Show answer

Show answer

Mission 5

The Abhidhamma Pitaka

  • Seven books analysing experience into its ultimate elements (dhammas): consciousness, mental factors, matter, and nibbana.
  • Ultimate truth (described in dhammas) vs conventional truth (persons, things).
  • Is it the Buddha's own work?
  • Theravada tradition: the Buddha taught it to the gods, his mother among them, in the Tavatimsa heaven, and gave its outline to Sariputta.
  • Most historians: developed by monks over centuries after the Buddha. One book, the Kathavatthu, is itself credited to Moggaliputta Tissa at the Third Council.
  • Studied intensively in Myanmar; chanted at funerals in Thailand.
Quick check
?Why do many historians doubt that the Abhidhamma Pitaka records the Buddha's own words?
Mission 5Diversity

How accurate a record?

  • Traditional Theravada: a faithful record, fixed by arahants at the First Council and guarded by disciplined recitation.
  • Shared early core: parallels between the Pali Nikayas and the Chinese Agamas of other schools suggest a common body of teaching from before the schools split. Richard Gombrich (b. 1937), What the Buddha Thought (2009), argues the core shows the mind of one original thinker.
  • Sceptical: Gregory Schopen (b. 1947), Bones, Stones, and Buddhist Monks (1997): texts give monks' ideals; inscriptions and archaeology show different practice. Pali was probably not the Buddha's own dialect.
  • Relevance of the debate: for some, authority depends on accuracy. Others point to the Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65): test teachings in your own experience. In one sutta a monk says, with approval, that whatever is well spoken is the word of the Buddha (Anguttara Nikaya 8.8, paraphrase).
  • Mahayana: accept later sutras as buddhavacana too, so the Pali record is not their only standard.
Mission 5

In worship and daily life

  • Paritta ("protection") chanting: monks chant suttas such as the Mangala Sutta (blessings), Karaniya Metta Sutta (loving-kindness) and Ratana Sutta at house blessings, illness, new ventures and festivals. A thread held during the chant is tied on wrists.
  • Funerals: monks chant passages on impermanence; in Thailand, from the Abhidhamma.
  • Uposatha days: lay people visit the temple, take precepts, hear a sermon based on a sutta.
  • Study and memory: novices learn chants; many lay people know Dhammapada verses; Myanmar holds national Tipitaka exams.
  • Meditation: guidance from suttas such as Majjhima Nikaya 10 (foundations of mindfulness).
  • Respect: texts are treated as precious objects, placed high, often on the shrine.
Mission 5Evaluate

The most important source of wisdom?

  • Claim: The Pali Canon is the most important source of wisdom in Buddhism.
  • For: the earliest full collection; core teachings (Four Noble Truths, Eightfold Path) shared by all schools.
  • For: the Buddha named the Dhamma and the discipline as the teacher after his death (Digha Nikaya 16).
  • Against: Mahayana relies on its own sutras (Lotus, Heart); Zen speaks of a transmission "outside the scriptures"; Tibetan Buddhism stresses the living teacher.
  • Against: the raft simile (Majjhima Nikaya 22) and ehipassiko: experience, not text, is the final test.
  • Judgement criteria: Most important for which Buddhists? Is a text more reliable than a teacher? Can experience be a "source" without texts to guide it?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Siddhattha went forth, tried extreme austerity, then took the Middle Way; he taught for about 45 years and reached parinibbana at Kusinara.
  • Under the Bodhi tree he faced Mara, touched the earth as witness, and gained three knowledges: past lives, karma and rebirth, the ending of dukkha.
  • Theravada sees him as a human role model; the Lotus Sutra sees his life as skilful means.

Mission 2

  • Upaya (skilful means): the Buddha adapts his teaching to his hearers, so some teachings are provisional.
  • The burning house: the father lures his children out with promised carts, then gives all one great carriage; the three vehicles lead to the one Buddha vehicle.

Mission 3

  • Three marks: anicca, dukkha, anatta. Dukkha has three kinds: pain, the dukkha of change, and the dukkha of conditioned existence.
  • Anicca works at a gross level (ageing, death) and a subtle, momentary level; Buddhists teach that craving for what changes is what turns anicca into dukkha.
  • Anicca develops into Mahayana emptiness; the pessimism debate turns on whether diagnosing dukkha is gloomy or realistic.

Mission 4

  • The Deer Park sermon (SN 56.11): dukkha (understand), samudaya: craving (abandon), nirodha (realise), magga: the Eightfold Path (develop).
  • The doctor analogy: illness, cause, cure, prescription.

Mission 5

  • The Tipitaka has three baskets: Vinaya (discipline), Sutta (discourses), Abhidhamma (analysis); for Theravada it is the word of the Buddha.
  • Oral for about four centuries, written down in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE; scholars debate how accurate a record it is.
  • It is used above all through chanting, sermons, study and meditation guidance.
