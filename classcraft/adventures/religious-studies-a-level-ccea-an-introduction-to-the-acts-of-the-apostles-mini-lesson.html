Mission 1

The hinge of Luke–Acts

  • Luke wrote two volumes for Theophilus: "The first book I wrote… concerned all that Jesus began both to do and to teach" (Acts 1:1).
  • "Began": Acts tells what Jesus continues to do, through the Spirit and the apostles.
  • Both books meet at the Ascension: the end of Luke (24:50–53), the start of Acts (1:9–11).
  • Forty days of appearances, "speaking about God's Kingdom" (1:3).
  • The command: "Don't depart from Jerusalem, but wait for the promise of the Father" (1:4); "you will be baptised in the Holy Spirit not many days from now" (1:5).
Mission 1Read the source

The programme of Acts

> "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth." (Acts 1:8, WEBBE)

  • The disciples had asked: "Lord, are you now restoring the kingdom to Israel?" (1:6). Jesus turns them from dates (1:7) to mission.
  • Acts follows this map: Jerusalem (chapters 1–7), Judea and Samaria (8–12), towards the ends of the earth, reaching Rome (13–28).

Q1 What three things does Acts 1:8 promise or command?

Show answer

Power, the Spirit, worldwide witness.

Q2 How does Jesus answer the question about the kingdom?

Show answer

Not with a date: with a task.

Mission 1

The Ascension

  • "…he was taken up, and a cloud received him out of their sight." (Acts 1:9, WEBBE)
  • Cloud: in scripture, a sign of God's presence (Sinai; the Transfiguration).
  • Two men in white: "This Jesus… will come back in the same way as you saw him going into the sky." (Acts 1:11, WEBBE)
  • Then the disciples return to Jerusalem and pray together, with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus and his brothers (1:14).
  • An echo: Elijah is taken up and his successor Elisha receives his spirit (2 Kings 2:9–15). Jesus goes; his followers receive the Spirit.
Mission 1

Why the Ascension matters

  • End of the appearances: Jesus is no longer met in the same way; a new period begins.
  • Exaltation: Jesus is "exalted by the right hand of God" (Acts 2:33): his authority as Lord.
  • The Spirit can come: the exalted Jesus pours out the promised Spirit (2:33).
  • Mission handed on: the apostles become his witnesses.
  • Hope: he will come again (1:11), so the Church lives between his going and his return.
  • Many churches keep Ascension Day forty days after Easter, a Thursday.

Mission 1

The Day of Pentecost

  • Pentecost (Greek "fiftieth") = Shavuot, the Jewish Feast of Weeks, fifty days after Passover (Leviticus 23:16): a harvest festival bringing pilgrims to Jerusalem.
  • "Suddenly there came from the sky a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind…" (Acts 2:2, WEBBE)
  • "Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them." (Acts 2:3, WEBBE)
  • All are filled with the Holy Spirit and speak "with other languages" (2:4).
  • Wind and breath: one word (pneuma) for Spirit in Greek; fire: God's presence and purifying power.
Mission 1Read the source

"Each in our own language"

> "How do we hear, everyone in our own native language?" (Acts 2:8, WEBBE)

> "Others, mocking, said, 'They are filled with new wine.'" (Acts 2:13, WEBBE)

  • Pilgrims "from every nation under the sky" (2:5): Parthians, Medes, Elamites… Cretans and Arabians (2:9–11).
  • Acts 2 describes known languages (xenolalia). Paul's "tongues" in 1 Corinthians 14:2 are prayer "no one understands" (glossolalia), needing interpretation.

Show answer

Mission 1

What the symbols say

  • Babel reversed: at Babel God "confused the language of all the earth" and scattered people (Genesis 11:9); at Pentecost many languages carry one message, and people are drawn together.
  • Sinai: God came down on the mountain "in fire" (Exodus 19:18). Jewish tradition came to link Shavuot with the giving of the Law; Christians see a new gift: the Spirit written on hearts.
  • Prophecy fulfilled: Peter quotes Joel: "I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" (Acts 2:17; Joel 2:28).
  • John the Baptist's promise: "He will baptise you in the Holy Spirit and fire" (Luke 3:16).
  • First fruits: the harvest festival becomes the first harvest of believers: about three thousand (2:41).
Mission 1

The Spirit in the emerging church

  • Some call Acts "the Acts of the Holy Spirit". The Spirit:
  • Empowers witness and boldness: after prayer "they spoke the word of God with boldness" (4:31).
  • Guides mission: "The Spirit said to Philip, 'Go near…'" (8:29); "Separate Barnabas and Saul for me" (13:2).
  • Includes outsiders: the Spirit falls on Gentiles at Cornelius' house (10:44–45), settling a dispute.
  • Builds community: one heart and soul, sharing (4:32); the Spirit can be lied to (5:3).
  • Works signs: healings and wonders through the apostles (5:12).

Mission 1Evaluate

How historical is Luke's account?

  • Claim: Acts 1–2 records events as they happened.
  • For: Pentecost was a real pilgrim feast; a sudden public movement in Jerusalem needs a cause; Paul also knows ecstatic gifts in the early churches (1 Corinthians 12–14).
  • For: Luke claims careful research (Luke 1:1–4), and the 'we' passages suggest some first-hand material.
  • Against: Luke 24 reads as placing the Ascension on Easter day; Acts 1 puts it after forty days: the timing may be theological (forty: a time of preparation).
  • Against: John places the gift of the Spirit on Easter evening: "Receive the Holy Spirit!" (John 20:22).
  • Against: the scene is rich in symbols (Babel, Sinai, Joel), suggesting theological shaping.
  • Judgement criteria: Is theological shaping the same as invention? Can two accounts both be true in different ways? What do sceptical and believing readers each assume?
Mission 1

Significance for the Early Church

  • Birthday of the Church: the Spirit forms a new community; "the Lord added to the assembly day by day" (2:47).
  • Continuity: the Spirit who empowered Jesus (Luke 4:18) now empowers his followers.
  • Universality: the Spirit "on all flesh" anticipates the Gentile mission (10:45).
  • Authority: the apostles, as witnesses, lead; later churches look back to them.
  • But: is Pentecost a one-off founding event, or a pattern every generation should expect? Christians answer differently (next screen).
Mission 1

Pentecostal and charismatic movements today

  • Pentecostal churches (from Azusa Street, Los Angeles, 1906) and the charismatic renewal in older churches (from 1960) expect the gifts of Acts 2 and 1 Corinthians 12 today: tongues, prophecy, healing.
  • Characteristics: baptism in the Spirit; lively, participatory worship; strong evangelism; expectant prayer for healing.
  • Relevance: among the fastest-growing forms of Christianity, especially in Africa, Latin America and Asia; personal experience of God; often strong among the poor.
  • Criticisms: cessationists hold that the sign gifts ceased with the apostles (e.g. B. B. Warfield, Counterfeit Miracles, 1918); risk of a "two-tier" faith; emotionalism; some prosperity teaching.
  • Catholic, Orthodox and many Protestant churches teach that every Christian receives the Spirit in baptism (and, for Catholics and Orthodox, confirmation or chrismation), with or without visible gifts.
  • Judgement criteria: Is Acts 2 a pattern for every generation or a unique founding event? Do the gifts' fruits (growth, devotion) or the risks (division, excess) weigh more?

Mission 2

Luke's summary portraits

  • Acts pauses three times to sum up the Jerusalem church: 2:42–47, 4:32–35, 5:12–16.
  • Each gives a picture of an ideal community: shared life, prayer, generosity, signs, growth.
  • Each follows a crisis or turning point: Pentecost; the first arrest; Ananias and Sapphira.
  • Luke writes decades later, for readers in other cities: the portraits teach as well as record.
Mission 2Read the source

Four marks of the church

> "They continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer." (Acts 2:42, WEBBE)

  • Apostles' teaching (didachē): instruction in the faith, from the witnesses.
  • Fellowship (koinōnia): a shared life, including possessions.
  • Breaking of bread: shared meals and, for most interpreters, the Eucharist.
  • Prayer: they still worship daily "in the temple" (2:46): these are Jews who believe Jesus is Messiah.

Q1 Which mark shows the believers still belonged to Jewish worship?

Show answer

Daily attendance at the Temple, 2:46.

Q2 What result does Luke report?

Show answer

Favour with the people; "The Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved" (2:47).

Mission 2

Sharing everything?

  • "All who believed were together, and had all things in common." (Acts 2:44, WEBBE)
  • They sold property "according as anyone had need" (Acts 2:45). "Neither was there amongst them any who lacked" (Acts 4:34).
  • That echoes God's promise to Israel: "there will be no poor with you" (Deuteronomy 15:4).
  • Barnabas (Joseph, a Levite from Cyprus, "Son of Encouragement") sells a field and lays the money at the apostles' feet (4:36–37).
  • Voluntary: Peter tells Ananias the land "remained your own" (5:4). This was generosity, not compulsory communism.
  • Some think the sharing left the Jerusalem church poor later (Paul's collection, Romans 15:26); others see it as a model never meant to be permanent.
Mission 2

Prayer under pressure (4:23–31)

  • Peter and John, released with threats, return to "their own company" (4:23).
  • The church prays together, quoting Psalm 2: rulers rage against God's anointed.
  • They ask not for safety but for boldness: "grant to your servants to speak your word with all boldness" (4:29), and for healing and signs "through the name of your holy Servant Jesus" (4:30).
  • "The place was shaken… They were all filled with the Holy Spirit, and they spoke the word of God with boldness." (Acts 4:31, WEBBE)
  • Pattern: opposition → common prayer → Spirit → bold witness.

Mission 2

Ananias and Sapphira (5:1–11)

  • Straight after Barnabas' gift, a couple sell land and keep back part of the price, while presenting the rest as the whole (5:2).
  • "Ananias, why has Satan filled your heart to lie to the Holy Spirit…?" (Acts 5:3, WEBBE)
  • "You haven't lied to men, but to God." (Acts 5:4, WEBBE)
  • Ananias falls dead; three hours later Sapphira repeats the lie and dies too (5:7–10).
  • "Great fear came on the whole assembly, and on all who heard these things." (Acts 5:11, WEBBE): in the oldest manuscripts, the first time Acts calls the believers the "church" (ekklēsia; WEBBE "assembly"). WEBBE, following later manuscripts, already has "assembly" at 2:47.
Mission 2

Why such severity?

  • The sin is the lie, not the keeping: they were free to keep the money (5:4). Pretended generosity deceives the community and God.
  • Holiness of a Spirit-filled community: lying to it is lying to the Holy Spirit (5:3, 9).
  • Achan echo: Achan "took some of the devoted things" and Israel suffered (Joshua 7:1). Luke's word for "kept back" (5:2) is the Greek Old Testament's word for Achan's sin: a new people, a new beginning, tested the same way.
  • Who acts? Peter announces, but does not pronounce a curse on Ananias; Luke presents the death as God's judgement.
  • Effect: fear on outsiders (5:13), yet growth (5:14).
  • Some scholars read it as a warning story Luke uses for later churches; others as historical memory.
Mission 2Evaluate

Does a church need discipline?

  • Claim: Strict discipline is necessary for a faithful church.
  • For: a community built on trust and shared goods cannot survive deceit; holiness matters (5:11).
  • For: churches still practise discipline: Matthew 18:15–17 steps; excommunication; safeguarding and financial honesty.
  • Against: the deaths seem harsh: Ananias gets no chance to repent, and Sapphira is not told of her husband's death; Jesus' welcome of sinners (Luke 15) seems different.
  • Against: fear-based discipline can be abused by leaders.
  • Perennial issue: how to hold grace and integrity together in a religious community.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the story descriptive or a pattern to copy? Who has authority to discipline? What is the aim: punishment, protection or restoration?

Mission 2

Miracles and the spread of the Gospel

  • 3:1–10: a man lame from birth at the Beautiful Gate: "In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!" (3:6). The crowd gathers; Peter preaches (3:12–26).
  • 4:23–31: the church prays for signs "through the name of your holy Servant Jesus"; the place is shaken.
  • 5:12–16: "many signs and wonders"; the sick laid in the streets for Peter's shadow to fall on them; "they were all healed" (5:16); "more believers were added to the Lord" (5:14).
  • 16:16–37 (Philippi): Paul drives a spirit of divination out of a slave girl; her owners lose income, so Paul and Silas are beaten and jailed; at midnight an earthquake opens the doors; the jailer asks, "what must I do to be saved?" (16:30) and is baptised with his household.
  • Pattern: miracle → attention → preaching → faith and opposition.
  • Miracles are done "in the name" of Jesus (3:16): the apostles deny their own power (3:12).
Mission 2Evaluate

Do miracles strengthen faith?

  • Claim: Miracles are essential for strengthening religious faith.
  • For: in Acts they draw crowds and lead to belief (5:14; 16:30–34); Jesus' signs did the same.
  • For: many Pentecostal Christians today report healing as part of conversion (see Mission 1).
  • Against: in Acts miracles also provoke opposition (4:1–3; 16:19–24): they do not compel faith.
  • Against: faith resting on wonders may be shallow (Luke 16:31: not even one rising from the dead would persuade some).
  • Against: sceptics (Hume) doubt the reports themselves; liberal Christians read the stories as signs of the gospel's power, not proofs.
  • Judgement criteria: Are miracles the cause of faith or a confirmation of it? What happens to faith when no miracle comes?
Mission 2Diversity

A model for churches today?

  • Many churches take the four marks of 2:42 as a checklist: teaching, fellowship, sacraments, prayer.
  • Catholic and Orthodox: the breaking of bread is the Eucharist, centre of church life; religious communities (e.g. monks and nuns) hold goods in common.
  • Some Protestant and Anabaptist groups (e.g. the Bruderhof, Hutterites) share all property; house churches copy "breaking bread at home" (2:46).
  • Others: Acts describes one moment in Jerusalem, not a law for every age.
  • Issue: should churches copy the structures of Acts 2, or its values (generosity, unity, prayer)?

Mission 3

From denial to boldness

  • At Jesus' trial Peter said three times he did not know him: "Woman, I don't know him." (Luke 22:57, WEBBE)
  • Jesus had prayed that his faith would not fail: "when once you have turned again, establish your brothers" (Luke 22:32).
  • The risen Jesus appeared to him (Luke 24:34); at Pentecost he is filled with the Spirit.
  • In Acts 1–5 Peter leads: he proposes the replacement for Judas (1:15), preaches, heals, and answers the Council.
  • John stands with him, mostly silent, as a second witness (Jewish law required two).
  • The Council notices: they were "unlearned and ignorant men… They recognised that they had been with Jesus." (Acts 4:13, WEBBE)
Mission 3

The Pentecost speech: its shape

  • Answer to the mockers (2:14–15): not drunk; "it is only the third hour of the day" (9 a.m.).
  • Scripture fulfilled (2:16–21): Joel's promise of the Spirit "on all flesh"; "whoever will call on the name of the Lord will be saved".
  • Jesus' story (2:22–24): approved by God by "mighty works and wonders and signs"; handed over "by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God"; crucified; raised.
  • Scripture proof (2:25–31): David's Psalm 16, "neither will you allow your Holy One to see decay" (Acts 2:27): not true of David, so it points to the Messiah.
  • Witness and exaltation (2:32–35): "we all are witnesses"; Jesus exalted, pours out the Spirit; Psalm 110, "Sit by my right hand".
  • Conclusion (2:36).
Mission 3Read the source

Lord and Christ

> "Let all the house of Israel therefore know certainly that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified." (Acts 2:36, WEBBE)

> "Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit." (Acts 2:38, WEBBE)

  • Lord (Kyrios): the word the Greek Old Testament uses for God's name. Christ: Messiah, the anointed king.
  • Peter, a Jew, speaks to fellow Jews as "brothers" (2:29); later he says they acted "in ignorance" (3:17). The speeches give no warrant for blaming Jews as a people.

Mission 3

The reaction (2:37–41)

  • "They were cut to the heart, and said to Peter and the rest of the apostles, 'Brothers, what shall we do?'" (Acts 2:37, WEBBE)
  • The answer is 2:38: repent, be baptised, receive.
  • The promise is wide: "to you and to your children, and to all who are far off" (2:39): a hint of the Gentile mission.
  • "There were added that day about three thousand souls." (2:41)
  • Significance: the first mass response; the pattern preaching → conviction → repentance → baptism → Spirit → community (2:42).

Mission 3

The lame man at the Beautiful Gate (3:1–10)

  • Peter and John go to the Temple "at the hour of prayer, the ninth hour" (3 p.m.): still observant Jews.
  • A man "lame from his mother's womb", over forty years old (4:22), begs at the gate.
  • "I have no silver or gold, but what I have, that I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!" (Acts 3:6, WEBBE)
  • He stands, enters the Temple "walking, leaping, and praising God" (3:8): an echo of Isaiah 35:6, the lame leaping in God's new age.
  • A man once kept at the gate now enters the place of worship: restoration as well as healing.
Mission 3

The speech in Solomon's Porch (3:11–26)

  • Not our power: "why do you fasten your eyes on us, as though by our own power or godliness we had made him walk?" (3:12). It is faith in Jesus' name (3:16).
  • Titles: God's Servant (3:13, 26), the Holy and Righteous One (3:14), the Prince (author) of life (3:15).
  • Ignorance: "I know that you did this in ignorance, as did also your rulers." (3:17)
  • Call: "Repent therefore, and turn again, that your sins may be blotted out" (3:19); "times of refreshing"; Jesus' return at "the restoration of all things" (3:21).
  • Prophet like Moses (3:22; Deuteronomy 18:15) and the blessing promised to Abraham for "all the families of the earth" (3:25).
  • Same pattern as Pentecost: sign → question → speech → call to repent.
Mission 3

First opposition (4:1–22)

  • Who: priests, the captain of the Temple and the Sadducees, "upset because they… proclaimed in Jesus the resurrection from the dead" (4:2). Sadducees denied resurrection.
  • Arrested overnight; before Annas, Caiaphas and the high-priestly family (4:6). "By what power, or in what name, have you done this?" (4:7)
  • Peter speaks "filled with the Holy Spirit" (Acts 4:8). The healed man stands there "whole" through Jesus' name; Jesus is the rejected stone become "head of the corner" (Acts 4:11, quoting Psalm 118:22).
  • "There is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven that is given amongst men, by which we must be saved!" (Acts 4:12, WEBBE)
  • The Council forbids them to speak in Jesus' name (4:18). Reply: "we can't help telling the things which we saw and heard." (Acts 4:20, WEBBE)
  • They are released: the people glorify God for the healing (4:21).

Mission 3

Signs, prison and Gamaliel (5:12–42)

  • Many signs; the sick laid out for Peter's shadow (5:15); multitudes join (5:14).
  • The high priest and the Sadducees, "filled with jealousy", jail all the apostles (5:17–18). "An angel of the Lord opened the prison doors by night" (5:19); they return to teach in the Temple.
  • Before the Council: "We must obey God rather than men." (Acts 5:29, WEBBE)
  • The Council wants to kill them (5:33). Gamaliel, a respected Pharisee, advises: leave them; if this is from men it will fail, like Theudas and Judas the Galilean (5:36–37); "if it is of God, you will not be able to overthrow it" (5:39).
  • Flogged and released, they leave "rejoicing that they were counted worthy to suffer dishonour for Jesus' name" (5:41) and keep teaching daily (5:42).
Mission 3

The apostles' attitude to opposition

  • Boldness (parrēsia: frank, fearless speech), asked for in prayer (4:29), given by the Spirit (4:31).
  • Obedience to God first: 4:19–20; 5:29. Civil authority is respected, but not above God's command.
  • No violence or revenge: they argue, pray and accept punishment.
  • Joy in suffering: dishonour for Jesus' name is an honour (5:41), as Jesus had taught (Luke 6:22–23).
  • Persistence: "they never stopped teaching" (5:42).
  • Trust in God's control: the Psalm 2 prayer (4:25–28); Gamaliel's test (5:39).
Mission 3Evaluate

Is their response to opposition a model today?

  • Claim: The apostles' response to opposition in Acts 3–5 is a model for religious believers today.
  • For: non-violent, principled witness; later examples include Christians under persecution and in civil-rights movements who cited "obey God rather than men".
  • For: prayer and community support (4:23–31) sustain people under pressure.
  • Against: "obey God rather than men" can justify defying any law a group dislikes; who decides what God commands?
  • Against: Luke may idealise: the apostles are always bold and the growth always steady; tensions surface soon after (6:1).
  • Historical caution: Gamaliel's speech names Theudas before Judas the Galilean (AD 6), but the historian Josephus dates a Theudas to c. AD 44–46, after this scene. Luke may be compressing or mistaken, or refer to another Theudas (see Mission 4).
  • Judgement criteria: What makes resistance legitimate? Is Acts describing or prescribing? How reliable is Luke's portrait?

Mission 4

Kerygma and didache

  • Kerygma (Greek kērygma): the proclamation a herald (kēryx) cries aloud: the public announcement of what God has done in Jesus.
  • Didache (didachē): teaching for those already believers: ethics, instruction, worship (Acts 2:42).
  • The kerygma is aimed at outsiders and calls for a response: repent, believe, be baptised.
Mission 4

Dodd's six points

  • C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Apostolic Preaching and its Developments (1936), reconstructed the "Jerusalem kerygma" from Peter's speeches in Acts 2–4 and 10 (paraphrase):
  • 1. Fulfilment: the age foretold by the prophets has dawned.
  • 2. Jesus: it has come through the ministry, death and resurrection of Jesus, descended from David.
  • 3. Exaltation: by the resurrection, Jesus is exalted at God's right hand as messianic head of the new Israel.
  • 4. Spirit: the Holy Spirit in the Church is the sign of Christ's present power and glory.
  • 5. Return: the messianic age will soon be completed when Christ returns.
  • 6. Appeal: repent; receive forgiveness and the Holy Spirit; the promise of salvation.
Mission 4Read the source

Finding the points in Acts 2 and 3

> "But the things which God announced by the mouth of all his prophets, that Christ should suffer, he thus fulfilled." (Acts 3:18, WEBBE)

> "Repent therefore, and turn again, that your sins may be blotted out, so that there may come times of refreshing from the presence of the Lord" (Acts 3:19, WEBBE)

  • 3:13–15: God glorified his Servant; you killed the Prince of life; God raised him.
  • 3:21: heaven receives Jesus "until the times of restoration of all things".
  • The Pentecost speech (Acts 2:14–39) follows the same pattern: Joel's prophecy fulfilled (2:16–21: point 1); Jesus' mighty works, death and resurrection (2:22–24: point 2); the appeal to repent and be baptised for forgiveness, with the promise of the Spirit (2:38–39: point 6). Placing 2:32–36 is the next check.

Mission 4

Dodd's wider claims

  • One message: the same pattern underlies Paul's earliest tradition: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures… he was buried… he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures" (1 Corinthians 15:3–4).
  • The Gospels grew from it: Mark's outline (John the Baptist, Galilee ministry, journey, Jerusalem, death, resurrection) follows the kerygma (Dodd, 1932).
  • Early material in Acts: some phrases sound primitive, not like Luke's later theology:
  • Jesus as God's Servant (3:13, 26); "God has made him both Lord and Christ" (2:36), as if at the resurrection; Semitic turns of phrase.
  • So, for Dodd, the speeches preserve the preaching of the Jerusalem church, not just Luke's ideas.
Mission 4

Do the speeches record what was said?

  • Ancient practice: the Greek historian Thucydides (c. 460–c. 400 BC), History of the Peloponnesian War 1.22, says he could not recall speeches word for word, so he had speakers say what the occasion required, keeping as close as possible to the general sense (paraphrase).
  • Martin Dibelius (1883–1947; essays on Acts, 1949): the speeches are Luke's compositions, written as a historian of his day would; Peter and Paul preach in the same pattern and style.
  • F. F. Bruce (1910–1990), The Speeches in the Acts of the Apostles (1942): Luke gives faithful summaries that keep early tradition, as their primitive features show.
  • Length: Peter's Pentecost speech takes only a few minutes to read aloud: clearly a summary.
  • Accuracy test: in Acts 5:36–37 the Pharisee Gamaliel names a rebel Theudas before Judas the Galilean (AD 6), but the historian Josephus dates a Theudas to c. AD 44–46, after this scene: a possible slip by Luke, or another Theudas.
Mission 4

Bultmann's challenge

  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), Theology of the New Testament (1948–53).
  • The kerygma is not mainly a summary of past facts: it is God's word addressing the hearer now and calling for a decision of faith (paraphrase).
  • Faith needs only that Jesus came and was crucified (German Dass), not a detailed story of what he did: against Dodd's link between the kerygma and a historical outline.
  • The kerygma comes wrapped in myth: exaltation to a heaven above, a quick return on the clouds. It must be demythologised to speak to modern people.
  • Dodd's point 5 (an imminent return) did not happen as expected: for Bultmann, a sign that the framework is mythical, while the call to decision remains.
  • Bultmann also doubted how much the Gospels and Acts tell us about the historical Jesus and the earliest preaching.

Mission 4

Adapting the message

  • To Jews in Jerusalem (Acts 2; 3): Scripture fulfilled; David, Moses, Abraham; Jesus as Messiah; "you… we".
  • To country people at Lystra (14:15–17): turn "to the living God, who made the sky, the earth, the sea"; he gave "rains from the sky and fruitful seasons". No Scripture quoted.
  • To philosophers at Athens (17:22–31): an altar "TO AN UNKNOWN GOD"; Greek poets: "For in him we live, move, and have our being" (17:28); then judgement and resurrection.
  • What stays constant: turn to the living God who acts; in the fuller speeches (Jerusalem, Athens) this leads to Jesus raised and a call to repent. At Lystra the speech, as Luke gives it, ends before Jesus is named (the crowd has to be stopped from offering sacrifice, 14:18).
  • Paul: "I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some." (1 Corinthians 9:22, WEBBE)
Mission 4Evaluate

Do the speeches have historical value?

  • Claim: The speeches in Acts are reliable evidence of the earliest Christian preaching.
  • For (Dodd, Bruce): primitive titles and Christology (Servant; "made him Lord and Christ"); agreement with Paul's early tradition (1 Corinthians 15:3–5).
  • For: Luke claims careful research (Luke 1:1–4); some first-hand sources likely.
  • Against (Dibelius): ancient convention let historians compose speeches; Peter and Paul sound alike; Luke's own vocabulary and themes run through them.
  • Against: written perhaps 50 years later; possible slips (Theudas).
  • Middle view: Luke's words, shaped for his book, carrying early content.
  • Judgement criteria: What did "history" mean to an ancient writer? Can early material survive in a later style? How much would summaries change?
Mission 4Evaluate

Is the kerygma of value for Christians today?

  • Claim: The kerygmata in Acts are of little value for Christians today.
  • For: the expected quick return did not come; the language of exaltation "above" reflects an ancient picture of the universe (Bultmann).
  • For: they were tailored to 1st-century Jews; modern hearers do not share those scriptures or hopes.
  • Against: they give the core message many churches still preach (creeds, evangelism): Jesus died, rose, is Lord; repent and receive forgiveness.
  • Against: Bultmann himself kept the kerygma central, only reinterpreting it; the call to decision still speaks.
  • Against: Acts itself models adapting the message, which churches still follow.
  • Judgement criteria: Is value in the wording, the structure or the core claims? Who decides what is myth?

Mission 5

The complaint and the Seven (6:1–7)

  • The church grows; "a complaint arose from the Hellenists against the Hebrews, because their widows were neglected in the daily service." (Acts 6:1, WEBBE)
  • Hellenists: Greek-speaking Jewish believers, many from the diaspora. Hebrews: Aramaic-speaking Jewish believers from the land. Both groups are Jewish.
  • The Twelve will not "forsake the word of God and serve tables" (6:2). They ask for "seven men of good report, full of the Holy Spirit and of wisdom" (6:3).
  • All seven have Greek names; Nicolaus is "a proselyte of Antioch" (6:5): a Gentile convert to Judaism.
  • Prayer and laying on of hands commission them (6:6).
  • Result: "The word of God increased… A great company of the priests were obedient to the faith." (6:7)
Mission 5

Servers who preach

  • Acts uses diakonia (service, 6:1, 4) for both the food and "the ministry of the word". It never calls the Seven deacons; later tradition (Irenaeus, c. 180) does.
  • Yet the two we hear of act like apostles: Stephen, "full of faith and power, performed great wonders and signs" (6:8); Philip preaches, heals and baptises (ch. 8).
  • Luke's point: the Spirit, not the job title, drives the mission.
  • The Seven also give the Hellenist believers leaders of their own: the church solves a division by sharing authority.
Mission 5

Stephen accused (6:8–15)

  • Opponents from the synagogue "called 'The Libertines'" (freedmen) and diaspora Jews (Cyrene, Alexandria, Cilicia, Asia) debate him: "They weren't able to withstand the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spoke." (6:10)
  • They stir up people, elders and scribes; he is brought before the Council (Sanhedrin).
  • False witnesses: "This man never stops speaking blasphemous words against this holy place and the law." (Acts 6:13, WEBBE)
  • Compare the charge at Jesus' trial (Mark 14:58). Here the charge in full: Jesus "will destroy this place, and will change the customs which Moses delivered to us" (6:14).
  • The Council "saw his face like it was the face of an angel" (6:15).
Mission 5

The longest speech in Acts (7:2–53)

  • Abraham (7:2–8): "The God of glory appeared to our father Abraham when he was in Mesopotamia" (Acts 7:2, WEBBE): God met him outside the land.
  • Joseph (7:9–16): rejected by his brothers, but God was with him in Egypt.
  • Moses (7:17–43): "This Moses whom they refused… God has sent him as both a ruler and a deliverer" (Acts 7:35, WEBBE). God speaks at the bush, on holy ground in Sinai; Israel turns to the golden calf.
  • Tent and Temple (7:44–50): the tabernacle was God's design; Solomon built a house, but God does not live in it.
  • Charge reversed (7:51–53): you resist the Spirit, as your fathers killed the prophets.
  • The pattern: God's chosen deliverers are rejected by their own people; God is not confined to one place.

Mission 5

A "Hellenist defence"?

  • Hellenist view: Stephen voices a Greek-speaking Jewish-Christian outlook more critical of the Temple than the apostles were (they still prayed there, 3:1). Marcel Simon (1907–1986), St Stephen and the Hellenists in the Primitive Church (1958), argued this at length.
  • Support: the persecution in 8:1 scatters believers "except for the apostles": perhaps the Hellenists were targeted, not the Twelve.
  • Is it a defence at all? Martin Dibelius (1883–1947), Studies in the Acts of the Apostles (essays, English 1956), judged much of the speech irrelevant to the charges: a Lukan composition (paraphrase).
  • Counter-view: F. F. Bruce (1910–1990), The Book of the Acts (1954, rev. 1988), read it as a deliberate, relevant answer: history itself shows the charges are upside down.
  • Either way, the speech prepares for a faith that can live without the Temple and beyond Judea.
Mission 5

The first martyr (7:54–8:1)

  • The Council is "cut to the heart" with rage (7:54). Stephen, "full of the Holy Spirit", sees "the Son of Man standing at the right hand of God" (Acts 7:56, WEBBE): Jesus standing, as if a witness or welcoming him.
  • They rush him, throw him out of the city and stone him (7:57–58), the penalty for blasphemy (Leviticus 24:16). Whether the Council could lawfully execute under Rome is debated.
  • His death echoes Jesus' in Luke: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit!" (7:59; Luke 23:46); "Lord, don't hold this sin against them!" (7:60; Luke 23:34).
  • "The witnesses placed their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul." (7:58) "Saul was consenting to his death." (8:1)
  • Martyr comes from Greek martys, "witness": Paul later calls him "Stephen, your witness" (22:20).
Mission 5

Philip in Samaria (8:4–25)

  • Persecution scatters the church "throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria" (8:1); "those who were scattered abroad went around preaching the word." (8:4)
  • "Philip went down to the city of Samaria and proclaimed to them the Christ." (8:5) Samaritans: worshipped on Mount Gerizim, accepted only the Torah; long hostile to Jews.
  • Signs, healings, "great joy in that city" (8:8). Simon, a magician, believes and is baptised (8:13).
  • Peter and John come; "they laid their hands on them, and they received the Holy Spirit." (8:17) The Spirit links the new group to the Jerusalem church.
  • Simon offers money for this power: "May your silver perish with you" (8:20). Hence simony: buying church office.
Mission 5

Philip and the Ethiopian (8:26–40)

  • An angel sends Philip to the desert road to Gaza (8:26). A eunuch, treasurer of "Candace, queen of the Ethiopians", has been to Jerusalem to worship (8:27).
  • He reads Isaiah 53: "He was led as a sheep to the slaughter" (8:32). "Do you understand what you are reading?" "How can I, unless someone explains it to me?" (8:30–31)
  • "Beginning from this Scripture, [Philip] preached to him about Jesus." (8:35) Then: "Behold, here is water. What is keeping me from being baptised?" (8:36)
  • Deuteronomy 23:1 barred eunuchs from the assembly; Isaiah 56:3–5 promised them a place. Philip's baptism enacts that promise.
  • Philip is caught away; he preaches from Azotus to Caesarea (8:40), where we meet him later as "Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven" (21:8).
  • Verse 8:37 (a confession of faith) is missing from the oldest manuscripts, so WEBBE leaves it blank.

Mission 5Evaluate

Was Stephen's way the right way to defend belief?

  • Claim: Stephen's speech is a good model for defending religious belief in a hostile world.
  • For: he answers from the hearers' own scriptures, not from outside them; truthful, not evasive.
  • For: he refuses violence and forgives his killers (7:60); his integrity outlasts the trial.
  • Against: his final attack (7:51–53) ends the hearing; Paul at Athens starts from common ground instead (17:22–23).
  • Against: 1 Peter 3:15 asks for an answer given "with humility and fear"; confrontation can harden opponents.
  • Today: responses to hostility range from apologetics and legal defence of religious freedom to quiet perseverance. Read Stephen's words today against a long history of Christians blaming Jews: Stephen is a Jew speaking to fellow Jews, as the prophets did.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the aim to persuade, to stay faithful, or both? Does the setting (a trial) change what is wise? Is Acts describing or prescribing?
  • In Section B, part (b) (AO2) is on other aspects of human experience, such as this: weigh Acts and today's world together.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is witness harder now than then?

  • Claim: Religious witness is more challenging today than in the time of Stephen and Philip.
  • Harder now: secular and plural societies; religion seen as private; claims to unique truth (4:12) can sound intolerant; online witness is easily mocked.
  • Harder then: witness could cost life, as for Stephen; in some countries believers of many faiths still face prison or death for their faith.
  • Same in any age: Philip's method still works: start with the other's question, listen, explain ("Do you understand…?", 8:30).
  • Different kinds of witness: spoken (Stephen), service (the Seven at tables), sacrifice (martyrdom), presence and example.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "challenge" danger, indifference, or both? Is witness measured by results or by faithfulness?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Acts 1:8 is the programme: power, the Spirit, witness from Jerusalem to the ends of the earth.
  • The Ascension ends the appearances, marks Jesus' exaltation and hands on the mission; Pentecost brings the promised Spirit, read through Babel, Sinai, Joel and the harvest.
  • The Spirit empowers, guides, includes and builds the community; Pentecostal and charismatic Christians expect the same gifts today, while others see Pentecost as a unique founding event.

Mission 2

  • The Jerusalem church: apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, prayer (2:42); voluntary sharing by need (2:45; 4:34; 5:4).
  • Ananias and Sapphira die for lying to the Holy Spirit; miracles in 3:1–10, 4:23–31, 5:12–16 and 16:16–37 draw crowds to the preaching, and opposition too.
  • Churches today copy Acts' structures or its values, and differ on which.

Mission 3

  • Peter's Pentecost speech: Joel, Jesus' works, death by God's plan, resurrection (Psalm 16), exaltation (Psalm 110), "Lord and Christ", call to repent and be baptised; about three thousand respond.
  • The healing at the Beautiful Gate leads to a second speech (Servant, Prince of life, ignorance, repent) and to arrest by the Sadducees: "no other name"; "we can't help telling".
  • Facing opposition the apostles show boldness, obedience to God, non-violence, joy in suffering and persistence; Gamaliel advises waiting to see if it is from God.

Mission 4

  • Kerygma is the public proclamation; Dodd's six points: fulfilment, Jesus' death and resurrection, exaltation, Spirit, return, appeal (Acts 2:14–39; 3:12–26).
  • Historical value: Dibelius sees Luke's compositions on ancient models; Dodd and Bruce see summaries with early content.
  • Bultmann: the kerygma is a present call to decision needing only "that" Jesus came, and its mythical framework must be demythologised; Acts shows the message adapted to each audience.

Mission 5

  • The Seven (6:1–7) were chosen to end neglect of Hellenist widows; Stephen and Philip went on to preach, work signs and widen the mission.
  • Stephen's speech (7:2–53) shows God acting outside the land and Temple and Israel rejecting its deliverers; read as a Hellenist critique of the Temple (Simon), a Lukan composition (Dibelius) or a relevant defence (Bruce). His death mirrors Jesus' and introduces Saul.
  • Philip takes the Gospel to Samaria and to an Ethiopian eunuch: the Acts 1:8 pattern begins to unfold.
