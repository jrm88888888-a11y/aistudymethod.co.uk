Mission 1

Mission 1: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Make a guess: Why is helium in group 18 when its atoms have only two electrons?

Mission 1

Periods, groups and blocks

  • Elements are arranged in order of atomic (proton) number.
  • A period is a row. Its number is the outer occupied shell (energy level).
  • A group is a column, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in a group have similar chemical properties.
  • Blocks are named after the sub-shell being filled: s-block groups 1–2; p-block groups 13–18; d-block groups 3–12.
Mission 1

Metals, metalloids, non-metals

  • Background: useful for reading the table.
  • Metals fill the left and centre. Non-metals sit at the upper right, plus hydrogen.
  • Metalloids lie along the stepped line: B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te.
  • Metalloids have in-between properties: silicon is shiny but only a semiconductor.
  • Aluminium touches the line but is a metal: shiny, malleable, a good conductor.
Mission 1

Valence electrons and the group

  • Valence electrons: the electrons in the outer shell.
  • Groups 1–2: valence electrons = group number.
  • Groups 13–18: valence electrons = group number − 10.
  • Group 16 atoms have 6 valence electrons, not 16. Group 13 atoms have 3.
  • Helium is the one exception. A later screen explains it.
Mission 1

From position to configuration

  • Period gives the outer shell, n. Block gives the sub-shell being filled.
  • Bromine: period 4, p-block, group 17 → 7 valence electrons, 4s² 4p⁵.
  • Bromine: [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵. The 3d¹⁰ is inner: not valence.
  • Manganese: period 4, d-block, group 7 → [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s².
Mission 1

From configuration to position

  • Highest n → period. Sub-shell being filled → block.
  • [Ne] 3s² 3p¹: period 3, p-block, 3 outer electrons → group 13 (aluminium).
  • [Ar] 3d⁸ 4s²: period 4, d-block, 8 + 2 → group 10 (nickel).
  • Count the whole outer shell, not just the last sub-shell: 3s² 3p¹ is three.
Quick check
?Germanium is in period 4, group 14. Which is the full electron configuration of a germanium atom?
Quick check
?An atom has the configuration [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵. Where is the element in the periodic table?
Mission 1

Outer configuration decides the chemistry

  • Chemical reactions involve the outer electrons.
  • Same group → same outer configuration → similar chemical properties.
  • Group 2: outer ns². The highest-energy electrons are in an s sub-shell, so Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • Each Group 2 metal loses its two s electrons: Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Sr²⁺, Ba²⁺.
  • Same pattern of formulae: MgO, CaO, SrO, BaO; H2O, H2S, H2Se.
Mission 1

So where does helium go?

  • Helium is 1s². By configuration alone, its last electron is in an s sub-shell.
  • But shell n = 1 holds at most 2 electrons (2n² = 2). So 1s² is a full outer shell.
  • A full outer shell → very hard to remove or add electrons → unreactive, like Ne, Ar, Kr.
  • It does not behave like group 2: those metals readily lose their ns² electrons.
  • So helium is placed with the chemistry it shares: group 18.
  • By configuration helium is s² (s-block); by position and chemistry it sits in group 18.
Mission check

?Beryllium, 1s² 2s², and helium, 1s², both have two electrons in their outer s sub-shell. Why is beryllium placed in group 2, but helium in group 18?

Position in the table follows the outer shell and the chemistry it gives, not a simple count of electrons.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Make a guess: Why is a chlorine atom smaller than a sodium atom, even though it has six more electrons?

Mission 2

Three factors explain every trend

  • Nuclear charge: more protons, a stronger pull on the outer electrons.
  • Distance: outer electrons in a higher shell are further away, so pulled less.
  • Shielding: inner electrons repel outer ones, reducing the pull they feel.
  • Across a period: nuclear charge rises, same shell, similar shielding → stronger pull.
  • Down a group: a new shell adds distance and shielding → weaker pull, despite more protons.
Mission 2

Atomic radius

  • Across a period, atomic radius decreases. Down a group, it increases.
  • Na: 11 protons. Cl: 17 protons. Both have 10 inner electrons and outer electrons in shell 3.
  • Chlorine's six extra electrons join the same shell. Electrons in one shell shield each other poorly.
  • So the outer shell feels a much larger net pull and is drawn closer: Cl is smaller.
Mission 2

Ionic radius

  • Background: ionic size returns in lattice energy and bonding.
  • Positive ions are smaller than their atoms: Na → Na⁺ loses the whole outer shell.
  • Negative ions are larger than their atoms: Cl → Cl⁻ has the same 17 protons but more electron repulsion.
  • Down a group, ions with the same charge get larger: Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺; F⁻ < Cl⁻ < Br⁻.
  • Isoelectronic ions share a configuration; more protons → smaller ion.
  • N³⁻ > O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺: all 1s² 2s² 2p⁶, with 7 to 13 protons.
Mission 2

First ionisation energy

  • First ionisation energy: the energy needed to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1+ ions.
  • Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻.
  • Across a period it generally increases: more protons, same shell, similar shielding.
  • Down a group it decreases: the outer electron is further away and more shielded.
  • Small dips (Mg → Al, P → S) are explained in an earlier lesson.
Quick check
?The first ionisation energy of magnesium is lower than that of beryllium. Which explains this?
Mission 2

Electron affinity

  • Background here; you'll use it in Born–Haber cycles.
  • First electron affinity: the enthalpy change when each atom in 1 mol of gaseous atoms gains one electron, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g).
  • Energy is usually released: the nucleus attracts the incoming electron.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → more energy released.
  • Down a group: further away, more shielded → less energy released.
  • Many exceptions. Cl (−349 kJ mol⁻¹) releases more than F (−328 kJ mol⁻¹): fluorine's small, crowded atom repels the newcomer.
Mission 2

Electronegativity

  • Electronegativity: the ability of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Pauling scale, no units. Fluorine is highest, 4.0.
  • Increases across a period and decreases down a group: the same three factors.
  • Period 3: Na 0.9 → Cl 3.0.
  • Not electron affinity: that is measured on isolated gaseous atoms.
Quick check
?Fluorine is more electronegative than chlorine. Which explains this?
Mission 2

Melting points across Period 3

  • Period 3: m.p. rises Na → Mg → Al, peaks at Si, then drops sharply.
  • Na, Mg, Al: giant metallic. More delocalised electrons and smaller, more highly charged ions → stronger metallic bonding → m.p. rises.
  • Si: giant covalent. Melting breaks many strong covalent bonds → highest m.p.
  • P4, S8, Cl2, Ar: simple molecular (Ar: atoms). Melting overcomes only weak London forces.
  • S8 > P4 > Cl2 > Ar: more electrons per molecule → stronger London forces.
Quick check
?Why does silicon have the highest melting point of the Period 3 elements?
Mission 2

All the trends on one map

  • Across a period → radius decreases; IE and electronegativity increase.
  • Down a group → radius increases; IE and electronegativity decrease.
  • The pattern repeats in each period: this is periodicity.
  • Metallic character increases down a group and towards the left.
Mission check

?An argon atom has seven more electrons than a sodium atom, yet argon's first ionisation energy is about three times sodium's. Which explains this?

Same rule as chlorine vs sodium. Across a period, more protons pull the same shell in tighter.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Make a guess: Why does barium fizz in cold water when magnesium, in the same group, barely reacts?

Mission 3

Group 2 atoms

  • Outer configuration ns²: Mg [Ne] 3s², Ca [Ar] 4s², Sr [Kr] 5s², Ba [Xe] 6s².
  • The outer electrons are in an s sub-shell: Group 2 is in the s-block.
  • The metals react by losing both s electrons: M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Losing electrons is oxidation: oxidation state 0 → +2. The metals are reducing agents.
Mission 3

Trends down the group

  • Atomic radius increases: one extra shell per period.
  • First ionisation energy decreases: the outer electrons are further from the nucleus and more shielded. This outweighs the rising nuclear charge.
  • Second ionisation energy: M⁺(g) → M²⁺(g) + e⁻. It also decreases down the group, for the same reasons.
Mission 3

The metals react

  • Water: Ca, Sr, Ba fizz, faster down the group: M(s) + 2H2O(l) → M(OH)2(aq) + H2(g).
  • Mg with cold water: very slow. Mg(s) + 2H2O(l) → Mg(OH)2(s) + H2(g).
  • Mg with steam: burns with a bright white light. Mg(s) + H2O(g) → MgO(s) + H2(g).
  • Oxygen: 2M(s) + O2(g) → 2MO(s), white solids. Mg burns with a brilliant white flame.
  • Group 1 shows the same trend, and each Group 1 metal reacts faster than the Group 2 metal next to it.
Mission 3

Why reactivity increases down the group

  • To react, each atom must lose two electrons: energy needed ≈ first IE + second IE.
  • This total falls down the group: Mg 2189 → Ca 1735 → Sr 1614 → Ba 1468 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Ba's outer electrons are in shell 6: far from the nucleus and heavily shielded. Mg's are in shell 3.
  • So barium loses its electrons far more easily: it fizzes, while Mg barely reacts with cold water.
  • Group 1 atoms lose only one electron, with a lower IE, so they are more reactive than Group 2.
Quick check
?Which equation shows the reaction of strontium with cold water?
Mission 3

Oxides and hydroxides are bases

  • Oxides with water: MO(s) + H2O(l) → M(OH)2. CaO(s) + H2O(l) → Ca(OH)2(aq).
  • Approximate pH: Mg(OH)2 ≈ 10 · Ca(OH)2 ≈ 12 · Sr(OH)2, Ba(OH)2 ≈ 13.
  • Alkalinity increases down the group: the hydroxides get more soluble, so more OH⁻ is released.
  • With dilute acids: MgO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2O(l).
  • Ca(OH)2(aq) + 2HCl(aq) → CaCl2(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • Group 1 oxides and hydroxides are basic too: Na2O(s) + H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq).
Quick check
?Equal amounts of magnesium oxide and barium oxide are each shaken with water containing universal indicator. Which results are expected?
Mission 3

Solubility of hydroxides and sulfates

  • Hydroxides: solubility increases down the group. Mg(OH)2 is sparingly soluble.
  • Sulfates: solubility decreases down the group. MgSO4 is soluble; BaSO4 is insoluble.
  • Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s): white precipitate.
  • Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s): white precipitate.
  • With CO3²⁻(aq), Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺ and Ba²⁺ all give white precipitates. Ca²⁺ gives only a slight precipitate with OH⁻ or SO4²⁻.
Quick check
?Dilute sodium hydroxide is added to separate solutions of magnesium chloride and barium chloride. Dilute sulfuric acid is then added to fresh samples of each. Which observations are expected?
Mission 3

Thermal stability of carbonates and hydroxides

  • Carbonates: MCO3 → MO + CO2. Hydroxides: M(OH)2 → MO + H2O.
  • Stability increases down the group: higher temperatures are needed.
  • Cause: the cation's charge and size. All are 2+, but Mg²⁺ is smallest → highest charge density.
  • A small cation polarises the large CO3²⁻ or OH⁻ ion, distorting its electron cloud and weakening its bonds.
  • Larger Ba²⁺ polarises least, so BaCO3 is the most stable.
Mission 3

Worked example: gravimetric analysis

  • 25.0 cm³ of CaCl2(aq) + excess Na2CO3(aq) → CaCO3 precipitate.
  • Filter, wash with distilled water, dry, weigh to constant mass: 0.425 g.
  • n(CaCO3) = 0.425 ÷ 100.1 = 4.25 × 10⁻³ mol = n(CaCl2).
  • c = 4.25 × 10⁻³ ÷ 0.0250 = 0.170 mol dm⁻³.
  • Common errors: dividing by 25.0 (not 0.0250), or using M(CaCl2) instead of M(CaCO3).
Quick check
?Which Group 2 carbonate decomposes at the lowest temperature, and why?
Mission check

?Strontium reacts more vigorously than calcium with dilute hydrochloric acid. The sum of the first and second ionisation energies is 1735 kJ mol⁻¹ for calcium and 1614 kJ mol⁻¹ for strontium. Which explains the difference?

Same reason barium fizzes and magnesium barely reacts: down Group 2 the outer electrons are lost more easily.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A jar of white crystals has lost its label. How can a few test tubes reveal exactly which salt it is?

Make a guess: A jar of white crystals has lost its label. How can a few test tubes reveal exactly which salt it is?

Mission 4

One salt, two ions

  • A salt has a positive ion (cation) and a negative ion (anion). Identify each one.
  • Dissolve a little in water. Test separate small portions.
  • A good test gives one clear observation: a coloured precipitate, a gas, a flame colour.
  • Halide ions: acidified silver nitrate, then ammonia.
  • Record observations precisely: "white precipitate", not "it went cloudy".
Mission 4

Carbonate ions

  • Add a dilute acid (e.g. hydrochloric or nitric acid). Carbonates fizz.
  • CO3²⁻(s or aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO2(g) + H2O(l).
  • Bubble the gas into limewater: it turns milky (cloudy). The gas is CO2.
Mission 4

Sulfate ions

  • Add dilute hydrochloric acid, then barium chloride solution.
  • Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s): white precipitate.
  • Why barium? BaSO4 is extremely insoluble.
  • Why acidify? Carbonate ions would also give a white precipitate, BaCO3. Acid removes them as CO2 first.
  • Not sulfuric acid: it would add sulfate ions.
Quick check
?A student adds barium chloride solution to a solution of an unknown sodium salt, without adding acid first. A white precipitate forms. Why can the student not yet conclude that sulfate ions are present?
Mission 4

Group 2 cations

  • Hydroxide (NaOH(aq)): Mg²⁺ white precipitate · Ca²⁺ slight white precipitate · Ba²⁺ no precipitate.
  • Sulfate (dilute H2SO4 or Na2SO4(aq)): Mg²⁺ none · Ca²⁺ slight white · Ba²⁺ white precipitate.
  • Carbonate (Na2CO3(aq)): white precipitate with all three.
  • Hydroxide solubility increases down the group; sulfate solubility decreases.
  • e.g. Mg²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → Mg(OH)2(s). Ba²⁺(aq) + SO4²⁻(aq) → BaSO4(s).
Mission 4

The flame test

  • Clean a nichrome (or platinum) wire: dip it in concentrated hydrochloric acid, then hold it in a hot flame until it gives no colour.
  • Dip the clean wire in the acid, then into the solid sample.
  • Hold it in the edge of a blue (non-luminous) Bunsen flame. Note the colour.
  • Clean the wire between samples, or traces of sodium hide other colours.
Mission 4

Flame colours

  • Li⁺ red (crimson) · Na⁺ yellow-orange · K⁺ lilac.
  • Ca²⁺ brick-red (orange-red) · Ba²⁺ apple green (pale green).
  • Sr²⁺ crimson (red).
  • Mg²⁺: no colour.
Quick check
?A white solid gives a brick-red flame. Its solution fizzes with dilute hydrochloric acid, giving a gas that turns limewater milky. What is the solid?
Mission check

?A jar of white crystals has lost its label. It could be magnesium sulfate, calcium chloride, barium chloride or sodium sulfate. Which pair of results shows it is barium chloride?

Test the cation and the anion separately, each with a test that only one ion passes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why does sodium fizz on water while caesium explodes?

Make a guess: Why does sodium fizz on water while caesium explodes?

Mission 5

Group 1 metals with water

  • Recap from GCSE: alkali metals react with water to form a hydroxide and hydrogen.
  • 2Na(s) + 2H2O(l) → 2NaOH(aq) + H2(g). In general: 2M + 2H2O → 2MOH + H2.
  • The metal is oxidised: Na 0 → +1. OH⁻ ions make the solution alkaline.
  • Lithium fizzes steadily. Sodium melts into a ball and whizzes about. Potassium ignites the hydrogen (lilac flame).
  • Rubidium and caesium react explosively: reactivity increases down the group.
Quick check
?A small piece of sodium is added to a large volume of water containing universal indicator. Which statement describes what happens?
Mission 5

Why reactivity rises down Group 1

  • Group 1 metals react by losing their one outer electron: M → M⁺ + e⁻.
  • Down the group, that electron is further from the nucleus and more shielded by inner shells.
  • Nuclear charge rises, but distance and shielding win: the electron is held less strongly.
  • First ionisation energy falls: Na 496 → Cs 376 kJ mol⁻¹. The electron is lost more easily.
  • So caesium (outer electron in n = 6) reacts far faster than sodium (n = 3).
Mission 5

Group 2 follows the same trend

  • Group 2 metals lose two electrons: M → M²⁺ + 2e⁻. With water: M + 2H2O → M(OH)2 + H2.
  • Magnesium reacts very slowly with cold water; calcium fizzes steadily; barium reacts vigorously.
  • Reactivity increases down Group 2, for the same reason: the outer electrons are further out and more shielded.
  • A Group 2 metal is less reactive than the Group 1 metal beside it: it must lose two electrons, and needs more energy in total.
Quick check
?How does the first ionisation energy change from lithium to sodium to potassium in Group 1, and why?
Mission 5

Halogens displace halide ions

  • Halogens react by gaining electrons: X2 + 2e⁻ → 2X⁻. They are oxidising agents.
  • A halogen displaces a halide below it in the group from solution.
  • Cl2(aq) + 2Br⁻(aq) → 2Cl⁻(aq) + Br2(aq): the solution turns yellow-orange.
  • Colours in water: Cl2 pale green, Br2 yellow-orange, I2 brown.
  • Iodine cannot displace chloride or bromide: no change.
Mission 5

The opposite trend in Group 7

  • Down the group, atoms are larger: an incoming electron is further from the nucleus.
  • It is also more shielded, so it is attracted less.
  • So reactivity, as oxidising power, decreases down Group 7: Cl2 > Br2 > I2.
  • Same cause as Group 1 (distance and shielding), opposite result: metals react by losing electrons, halogens by gaining them.
Quick check
?Which mixture shows a colour change caused by a displacement reaction?
Mission check

?Calcium fizzes steadily in cold water, but barium reacts much more vigorously. Which explains the difference?

The same idea explains the hook. Caesium's outer electron is in n = 6, far from the nucleus and well shielded, so caesium loses it far more easily than sodium does.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Period = outer shell; group = similar outer configuration and chemistry; block = sub-shell being filled.
  • Classify an element as s, p or d (and f) block from its position.
  • Helium's 1s² is a full shell, so it sits with the noble gases.

Mission 2

  • Nuclear charge, distance and shielding explain radius, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.
  • Across a period: more protons, same shell → smaller atoms that hold electrons more tightly.
  • Melting points across a period follow structure: giant metallic → giant covalent → simple molecular.

Mission 3

  • Down Group 2: radius up, IE down, so the metals lose their two s electrons more easily and reactivity rises.
  • Hydroxides get more soluble down the group; sulfates get less soluble.
  • Thermal stability increases down the group: smaller cations polarise anions more.

Mission 4

  • NH4⁺: warm with NaOH(aq) → NH3 turns damp red litmus blue. CO3²⁻: dilute acid → CO2 turns limewater milky. SO4²⁻: dilute HCl then BaCl2(aq) → white BaSO4.
  • Flame tests identify many metal ions: Li red, Na yellow-orange, K lilac, Ca brick-red, Ba green.

Mission 5

  • Down Groups 1 and 2: outer electrons further out and more shielded → lost more easily → reactivity increases.
  • Down Group 7: incoming electron further out and more shielded → gained less easily → oxidising power decreases; a halogen displaces halides below it.
