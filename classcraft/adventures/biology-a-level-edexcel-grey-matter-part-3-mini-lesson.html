Mission 1

Mission 1: Cover one eye for a few weeks early in life and it may never see properly. Why does timing matter so much?

Make a guess: Cover one eye for a few weeks early in life and it may never see properly. Why does timing matter so much?

Mission 1

From eye to visual cortex

  • Retina → optic nerve → relay in the thalamus → visual cortex (occipital lobe).
  • In the visual cortex, neurones are grouped in columns.
  • Neighbouring columns respond mainly to the left eye or the right eye: ocular dominance columns.
  • At birth, the connections are present but rough.
  • Visual experience refines them: synapses that are used are strengthened and kept; unused ones are lost.
Mission 1

The critical period

  • A critical period: a window early in life when certain experience must happen for normal development.
  • During it, inputs compete for synapses in the cortex. Active inputs win; inactive ones lose their connections.
  • Missing the experience in this window causes lasting change. The same deprivation in an adult has little effect.
  • Human example: a child born with a cloudy lens (cataract) in one eye needs it removed early. If not, that eye stays poor-sighted even after surgery.
Mission 1

Hubel and Wiesel's experiments

  • Frame 1 — Kittens: one eyelid stitched closed from birth for the first months. Electrodes recorded from neurones in the visual cortex.
  • Frame 2 — Result: almost all cortical neurones responded only to the open eye. The deprived eye was effectively blind, although its retina worked.
  • Frame 3 — Adult cats deprived in the same way for months: no lasting effect. So the effect depends on age: a critical period.
  • Monkeys: closing one eye early made that eye's ocular dominance columns shrink; the open eye's columns expanded.
  • Conclusion: the damage is in the cortex, not the eye. Inactive synapses are lost to active competitors.

Mission 1

Animals in brain research: two standpoints

  • Hubel and Wiesel's work helped explain why children's cataracts and squints must be treated early. It also caused animals to suffer.
  • Rights and duties standpoint: some actions are right or wrong in themselves.
    • If animals have a right not to be harmed, harmful experiments are wrong, whatever the benefit.
    • Others argue that we have a duty to relieve human disease, or that rights depend on capacities animals may lack.
  • Utilitarian standpoint (maximise the good): weigh benefits against harms.
    • Acceptable if the benefits (knowledge, treatments) outweigh the suffering, and suffering is minimised.
    • Critics: suffering and benefit are hard to measure and compare; benefits are uncertain when the experiment is planned.
  • Animals cannot give informed consent.
  • UK practice: the 3Rs — Replace, Reduce, Refine — and a licence with a harm–benefit assessment (Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act 1986).
Quick check
?A scientist argues that experiments on kittens were acceptable because the knowledge gained has helped many children avoid permanent sight loss, and the animals' suffering was kept as low as possible. Which ethical standpoint is this?
Mission check

?Some children born deaf receive a cochlear implant, which stimulates the auditory nerve directly. Children implanted in the first few years of life usually develop spoken language far better than children implanted at age seven or older. Which explanation fits?

Timing matters because the brain wires itself with what it receives, and some wiring happens only once.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Make a guess: Why does an animal stop reacting to a harmless touch that keeps coming back?

Mission 2

Innate or learned?

  • Innate behaviour: inherited; much the same in every member of the species; done correctly the first time.
  • Learned behaviour: changes with experience.
  • Learning changes the individual, not its alleles. Offspring must learn the behaviour again.
Mission 2

Habituation

  • Habituation: a reduced response to a repeated stimulus that brings neither harm nor reward.
  • A snail touched between its tentacles pulls them in. Touch it again and again: it pulls them in less, and comes out sooner.
  • It is stimulus-specific: a different stimulus still gives a full response. So it is not tired muscles.
  • Advantage: no time or energy wasted on harmless stimuli; more time to feed, while staying alert to new ones.
  • Humans habituate too: you stop noticing a ticking clock.
  • At the synapse: with repeated stimulation, fewer Ca²⁺ ions enter the sensory neurone's terminal. Less neurotransmitter is released, so the motor neurone fires less.
  • Say: "a reduced response to a repeated stimulus" — not: "the animal is too tired to respond".
Mission 2Practical

Habituation in snails

  • Core practical 18: investigate habituation to a stimulus.
  • Let a snail settle on a clean, damp surface until its tentacles are fully out.
  • Touch it between the tentacles with a moist cotton bud. Time how long until the tentacles are fully out again.
  • Repeat the touch each time it re-emerges, 10 or more times. Use several snails.
  • Independent variable: number of times touched. Dependent variable: time to re-emerge.
  • Keep constant: force and place of touch, interval, temperature, light, surface.
  • Handle gently; keep snails damp; return them afterwards.
  • Analysis: plot mean time against touch number; Spearman's rank correlation tests whether the fall is significant.

Mission check

?In one field, birds feeding on newly sown seed fly up whenever a gas-powered bird scarer gives a loud bang. It bangs every 10 minutes and never harms them. After ten days, most of the birds keep feeding when it bangs. On day eleven, a recording of a hawk's call is played; every bird flies up at once. Which explanation is best supported?

The snail and the birds both stop reacting to a stimulus that keeps coming and never hurts. Ignoring it saves time and energy; responding to anything new keeps them safe.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Make a guess: The first human genome took years and a fortune to read. What can we do now that it is quick and cheap?

Mission 3

Reading whole genomes

  • Genome sequencing finds the order of nucleotides in all of an organism's DNA.
  • Human Genome Project (1990–2003): about 3 billion base pairs; about 20 000–21 000 protein-coding genes.
  • Thousands of genomes are now sequenced: bacteria, crops, the malaria mosquito, and extinct Neanderthals, from bone.
  • Modern machines cut DNA into millions of short fragments, read them all at the same time, and computers assemble the overlapping reads.
Mission 3

Comparing genomes: bioinformatics

  • Bioinformatics: storing and analysing sequence data in databases, using computer software.
  • Between individuals: single-base differences (SNPs) are linked to traits and disease risk.
  • Between species: more similar sequences mean a more recent common ancestor. This tests evolutionary relationships.
Mission 3

Genomes in medicine

  • Personalised medicine: choose a treatment or dose to suit a person's genotype.
  • Cancer: sequence a tumour; choose drugs that target its particular mutations.
  • Rare diseases: a genome can reveal the cause, ending years of uncertain diagnosis.
  • A genome shows risks and likely responses, not a fixed future.
Mission 3

Issues raised

  • Privacy: who can see your genome? Could insurers or employers use it?
  • Consent: tests may find things nobody looked for, e.g. a risk allele for an untreatable disease.
  • Relatives: your genome carries information about your family too.
  • Fairness: expensive targeted drugs; unequal access.
  • Psychological: knowing a risk can cause anxiety, or false reassurance.
  • Answer from several viewpoints: weigh benefits to patients against these costs.
Quick check
?A hospital proposes to sequence the whole genome of every patient diagnosed with cancer. Which is the most accurate description of what this could achieve?

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Make a guess: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Mission 4

One gene, a new host

  • Recombinant DNA: DNA joined from two sources, e.g. a human gene inside a bacterial plasmid.
  • An organism carrying a gene from another species is transgenic: a genetically modified organism (GMO).
  • The genetic code is universal. Transcription and translation work the same way in all cells.
  • So the host reads the human gene and makes the same polypeptide.
  • Five stages: get the gene → put it in a vector → transform host cells → find the transformed cells → grow them.
Mission 4

Into a vector, into a host

  • A vector carries the gene into the host: usually a plasmid.
  • DNA ligase joins the gene into the cut plasmid: phosphodiester bonds in the backbone. Result: a recombinant plasmid.
  • Transformation: host cells take up the plasmid.
    • Calcium ions plus a brief heat shock make bacterial membranes more permeable.
  • Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid.
Mission 4

Grow them: one cell becomes a factory

  • Transformed cells divide. Every new cell copies the plasmid and the gene.
  • Human insulin: DNA coding for human insulin (no introns) put into E. coli; grown in large fermenters; insulin extracted and purified. Approved for use in 1982.
  • Microorganisms grow fast in fermenters, so many drugs are made this way. Plants and animals: Mission 5.
Mission 4

Why the gene still works

  • The bacterium's RNA polymerase transcribes the human gene. Its ribosomes translate the mRNA.
  • Each codon codes for the same amino acid as in a human cell.
  • So the amino acid sequence, and the protein, are the same.
  • Say: "the genetic code is universal" — not: "all organisms have the same genes".
Mission check

?In 1986, researchers inserted the firefly luciferase gene, with a promoter that works in plant cells, into tobacco cells and grew whole plants from them. When supplied with luciferin, the plants glowed. Why could tobacco cells make active firefly luciferase?

Bacterium, plant or animal: any cell can read a gene from any species. That is what makes recombinant DNA possible.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If a goat can be made to produce a human medicine in its milk, what else could we change, and should we?

Make a guess: If a goat can be made to produce a human medicine in its milk, what else could we change, and should we?

Mission 5

GM microorganisms

  • Bacteria and yeasts grow fast in fermenters. Many medicines are made this way:
    • insulin and human growth hormone from GM bacteria
    • the hepatitis B vaccine protein from GM yeast (since 1986)
Mission 5

GM plants

  • Insect resistance: a gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis makes a Bt toxin that kills certain caterpillars.
  • Herbicide tolerance: e.g. GM soya survives a herbicide that kills weeds.
  • Nutrition: Golden Rice makes β-carotene (the body converts it to vitamin A) in its grain. Aimed at vitamin A deficiency.
  • Drugs from plants: e.g. an enzyme for Gaucher disease made in GM carrot cells (approved 2012).
Mission 5

Transgenic animals

  • Pharming: animals that make a human protein in their milk.
    • Example: human antithrombin (stops blood clotting) from GM goats' milk; approved 2006 (EU) and 2009 (US).
    • The gene is joined to the promoter of a milk-protein gene and put into an early embryo.
    • So every cell carries the gene, but only mammary gland cells express it.
  • Why not bacteria? Some human proteins need changes after translation, such as added sugar groups. Bacteria cannot make them.
Quick check
?Why are some human proteins for medicines made in transgenic animals or plants rather than in GM bacteria?
Mission 5

Risks, and how they are reduced

  • Documented risks:
    • Resistance: pests exposed to Bt toxin year after year can evolve resistance. Pink bollworm in India survives some Bt cotton.
    • Gene flow: a GM herbicide-tolerant grass escaped from field trials and spread in the wild (USA, 2000s).
    • Escape of GM microorganisms from a laboratory or factory.
  • Safety precautions:
    • Use host strains that survive poorly in the human body (e.g. lab strains of E. coli).
    • "Suicide genes": cells die outside the conditions they are given.
    • Containment: sealed fermenters; restricted labs; separation distances for field trials.
  • Products are tested before approval; rules differ between countries.
Quick check
?Bt cotton made a toxin that killed pink bollworm caterpillars. After several years of growing it in parts of India, many caterpillars survived it. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 5

Weighing the arguments

  • Arguments for:
    • Humanitarian: more food; fewer deficiency diseases; safer medicines in larger supply.
    • Less insecticide sprayed on insect-resistant crops.
  • Arguments against or concerns:
    • Environmental: resistance in pests and weeds; gene flow; effects on other species.
    • Ownership: GM seeds and genes are patented. Farmers may have to buy new seed each season. Large companies control supply.
    • Animal welfare: in pharming and in disease-model animals.
  • Exam answers: give both sides, then weigh them using the evidence in the question.
Mission check

?A company plans to make a human enzyme in the egg white of GM hens. The human gene is joined to the promoter of a gene for an egg-white protein and inserted into early embryos. The enzyme is found in egg white but not in the hens' blood or muscle. Why?

One inserted gene, one chosen tissue: that control is what makes pharming work. Whether to use it is a separate question, answered by weighing evidence and values.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • During a critical period, active inputs keep and strengthen their synapses in the cortex and inactive ones lose them; the same deprivation later has little effect.
  • Hubel and Wiesel: closing one eye early, not in adulthood, left the cortex driven by the open eye. Nature–nurture evidence: newborns, animals, brain damage, twins, cross-cultural studies; ethics from rights-based and utilitarian standpoints.

Mission 2

  • Habituation: a learned, stimulus-specific drop in response to a repeated, harmless stimulus; it saves time and energy.

Mission 3

  • Sequencing reads nucleotide order; it has become automated, faster and vastly cheaper, so whole genomes are compared routinely.
  • Uses: relationships between species, outbreaks, personalised medicine; a genome gives risks, not a fixed future.

Mission 4

  • A gene from one species is expressed in another because the genetic code, transcription and translation are universal.
  • Get the gene, insert it into a vector, transform host cells, select them with marker genes, then grow them.

Mission 5

  • GM microorganisms, plants and animals make medicines, food traits and research models; tissue-specific promoters target where a gene is expressed.
  • Risks (resistance, gene flow, escape) are documented and reduced by safety precautions; decisions weigh benefits against environmental, ownership and welfare concerns.
