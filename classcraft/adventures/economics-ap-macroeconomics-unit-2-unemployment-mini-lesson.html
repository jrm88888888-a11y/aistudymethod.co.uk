Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Make a guess: Why does an economy grow in bursts and slumps instead of at a steady pace?

Mission 1

The trend and the cycle

  • Plot real GDP against time.
  • The trend line shows potential output: it rises steadily as productive capacity grows (long-term growth).
  • Actual real GDP wobbles above and below the trend: short-term fluctuations.
  • These ups and downs are the business cycle.
  • Business cycle fluctuations come from changes in AD and short-run AS.
  • Say: "actual output fell below potential" — not: "potential output fell", when the economy has spare capacity.
Mission 1

The phases of the cycle

  • Two phases: expansion (real GDP rising) and recession (contraction: real GDP falling).
  • Two turning points: peak (end of an expansion) and trough (end of a recession).
  • In the US, the NBER dates recessions as a significant decline in economic activity, spread across the economy, lasting more than a few months.
  • Say: "a slowdown: growth falls but stays positive" — not: "a recession".
Mission 1

Mission 1

Output gaps

  • Output gap = actual output − potential output.
  • Recessionary gap: actual below potential.
  • Spare capacity, idle machines, cyclical unemployment. Downward pressure on inflation.
  • Inflationary gap: actual above potential.
  • Overtime, shortages of workers, capacity stretched. Upward pressure on wages and prices. It cannot last.
  • At potential output, unemployment is at its natural rate (frictional + structural). Cyclical unemployment = actual unemployment rate − natural rate.
Mission 1

Output gaps on AD/AS and the PPC

  • AD/AS: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf).
  • AD1 meets SRAS left of Yf: output Y1 < Yf.
  • That is a recessionary gap.
  • AD2 meets SRAS right of Yf: output Y2 > Yf.
  • That is an inflationary gap.
Mission 1

Worked example: the size of the gap

  • Illustrative data.
  • Potential output = 2,000 bn. Actual real GDP = 1,950 bn.
  • Gap = 1,950 − 2,000 = −50 bn.
  • As % of potential: −50 / 2,000 × 100 = −2.5%.
  • A recessionary gap of 50 bn.
  • Two years later, actual = 2,030 bn and potential = 2,000 bn: gap = +30 bn = +1.5% of potential.
Mission 1Graph lab

Opening a gap

Illustrative data.

  • Takeaway: shifts in AD move actual output away from potential. Potential output does not move.
Mission 1

Why output swings: shocks and instability

  • Demand shocks shift AD: a collapse in confidence, a financial crisis, falling export demand, a big change in government spending.
  • Supply shocks shift SRAS: a sharp rise in oil prices, a pandemic, a harvest failure.
  • Real case: the NBER dates a US recession from December 2007 (peak) to June 2009 (trough): 18 months. The 2020 recession lasted 2 months (February to April 2020), the shortest US recession on record.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: potential output is estimated at 800 bn. Actual real GDP is 776 bn. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A surge in confidence and borrowing has kept a country's actual output above its potential output for two years. Unemployment is below its natural rate. Which is most likely to happen next?

When demand pushes output above what the economy can sustain, inflation tends to rise. When it falls below, workers and machines sit idle.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Make a guess: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Mission 2

Three groups, not two

  • Start with the working-age population.
  • US: the adult population: civilians aged 16 and over, not in prisons or other institutions.
  • Split it three ways:
    • Employed: did any paid work in the survey week (even 1 hour), or has a job but is temporarily away.
    • Unemployed: no job, available to start, and actively looking for work.
    • Not in the labor force: everyone else. Economists also call this group economically inactive.
  • BLS definition of unemployed: no job, looked for work in the last 4 weeks, available to work (or on temporary layoff, waiting to be recalled).
  • Labor force = employed + unemployed.
Mission 2

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 28.5 m, unemployed 1.5 m, inactive 20 m.
  • Labor force = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100.
  • = 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.
  • Not 1.5 ÷ 50 (= 3%): the inactive are not in the labor force.
  • Labor force participation rate = labor force ÷ adult population × 100 = 30 ÷ 50 × 100 = 60%.
  • Say: "5% of the labor force" — not: "5% of the population".
Mission 2

Inactive is not unemployed

  • Not in the labor force: not working and not both looking and available.
  • Who? Full-time students, retired people, people looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled.
  • And discouraged workers: they want a job but have stopped looking, because they think none is available.
  • Discouraged workers are not counted as unemployed. Joblessness is understated.
  • Say: "economically inactive" — not: "unemployed", for someone who is not looking for work.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for a country: working-age population 8.0 m, employed 4.6 m, unemployed 0.4 m, inactive 3.0 m. What is the unemployment rate?
Mission 2

What the rate misses

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers count as inactive, not unemployed.
  • Part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed.
  • So the unemployment rate understates how many people want work, or more work.
Mission 2

US data in practice

  • The BLS runs the Current Population Survey: about 60,000 households each month.
  • The official unemployment rate is called U-3.
  • A wider BLS measure, U-6, also counts discouraged workers, other people who want work but have stopped looking, and part-time workers who want full-time work. U-6 is always higher than U-3.
  • Real data (BLS): the labor force participation rate peaked at 67.3% in early 2000. In 2023 it averaged 62.6%; the unemployment rate averaged 3.6%.
  • An aging population is one reason: more adults are retired, so they are not in the labor force.
  • Say: "the labor force participation rate fell" — not: "unemployment rose", when people leave the labor force.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: the adult population is 200 million. 114 million are employed and 6 million are unemployed. What are the unemployment rate and the labor force participation rate?

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 3

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
Mission 3

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
Mission 3

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labor is a derived demand).
  • Real GDP falls below full-employment output: a recessionary gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: US unemployment rose from 5.0% in December 2007 to 10.0% in October 2009 (BLS).
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 3

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • Natural rate = frictional + structural unemployment.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
Mission 3

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 = 5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5 = 2 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "2 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "2% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Make a guess: How can one number tell you how much prices have risen for a whole country of shoppers?

Mission 4

Inflation, deflation, disinflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level. The inflation rate is its % change, usually over 12 months.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the general price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, just more slowly.
  • Real data: US CPI inflation was 9.1% in the 12 months to June 2022, the largest 12-month rise since 1981.
  • Say: "inflation fell, so prices rose more slowly" — not: "inflation fell, so prices fell".
Mission 4

Index numbers and the base year

  • An index number shows a value relative to a base year, which is set to 100.
  • Index = (value ÷ value in base year) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: a price is 2.00 in the base year, 2.50 a year later, 2.60 the year after.
  • Index: 100 → 125 → 130.
  • Change from the base year: read it straight off: 125 means 25% higher.
  • Change between two later years: % change, not points. 125 → 130 is a 5-point rise but (130 − 125) ÷ 125 × 100 = 4%.
  • Index numbers have no units, so series in different units or currencies can be compared.
  • Say: "the index rose by 5 points, a 4% rise" — not: "the index rose by 5%".
Mission 4

Measuring inflation with a basket

  • Statisticians pick a basket of goods and services that typical households buy.
  • They record the prices of the basket's items every month, in many shops and places.
  • A household spending survey shows how much households spend on each item. These shares become the weights.
  • Big spending shares (e.g. housing, food) get big weights: their price changes move the index more.
  • The consumer price index (CPI) tracks the cost of this basket over time.
  • In the US, the BLS publishes the CPI. Its base period is 1982–84 = 100.
  • CPI = (cost of basket in current year ÷ cost of basket in base year) × 100.
Mission 4

Worked example: the cost of a fixed basket

  • Illustrative data. A basket of three items; the quantities stay fixed. Year 1 is the base year.
ItemQuantityPrice Y1Price Y2Price Y3
Bread (loaves)501.001.201.27
Bus rides402.002.102.20
Phone plan (months)1210.0010.5011.00
  • Basket cost: Y1 = 50 + 80 + 120 = 250. Y2 = 60 + 84 + 126 = 270. Y3 = 63.5 + 88 + 132 = 283.5.
  • CPI: Y1 = 100; Y2 = 270 ÷ 250 × 100 = 108; Y3 = 283.5 ÷ 250 × 100 = 113.4.
  • Inflation Y2: (108 − 100) ÷ 100 × 100 = 8%.
  • Inflation Y3: (113.4 − 108) ÷ 108 × 100 = 5%.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A fixed basket of goods costs 400 in the base year, 440 in year 2 and 462 in year 3. What is the inflation rate in year 3?
Mission 4

Real versus nominal

  • Nominal (money) value: measured in the prices of the time (current prices).
  • Real value: adjusted for inflation, measured in the prices of one year (constant prices).
  • Real value = nominal value × (price index in base year ÷ price index in current year).
  • Illustrative data: weekly pay rises from 400 to 520 while the CPI rises from 100 to 135.
  • Real pay now, in base-year prices: 520 × 100 ÷ 135 = 385.19.
  • Nominal pay is up 30%. Real pay is down 3.7%: prices rose faster than pay.
  • Shortcut: real growth ≈ nominal growth − inflation. It works well only for small changes (here: 30 − 35 = −5, against an exact −3.7).
  • Real wage = nominal wage ÷ (CPI ÷ 100).
  • Say: "real pay fell" — not: "pay fell", when nominal pay rose.
Mission 4

Real GDP and the GDP deflator

  • The GDP deflator is a price index for all goods and services in GDP, not just a consumer basket.
  • Real GDP = nominal GDP ÷ GDP deflator × 100.
  • Illustrative data: nominal GDP = 2,200 billion; deflator = 110 → real GDP = 2,200 ÷ 110 × 100 = 2,000 billion.
  • Real GDP can also be calculated with base-year prices. Illustrative data: year 1 (base): 100 pizzas at 10 and 50 books at 20 → nominal GDP = 2,000.
  • Year 2: 120 pizzas at 12 and 60 books at 22 → nominal GDP = 2,760; real GDP at year 1 prices = 1,200 + 1,200 = 2,400.
  • GDP deflator = nominal ÷ real × 100 = 2,760 ÷ 2,400 × 100 = 115. Nominal GDP grew 38%; real GDP grew 20%.
  • The inflation rate can be measured as the % change in the CPI or in the GDP deflator.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A worker's weekly pay rises from 500 to 540. Over the same period, the consumer price index rises from 120 to 132. What happened to the worker's real pay?
Mission 4

Limits of the CPI

  • Substitution bias: when one item's price jumps, people switch to cheaper alternatives. A fixed basket misses this, so it overstates the rise in the cost of living.
  • New products: new goods enter the basket only after a delay.
  • Quality changes: a more expensive phone may also be a much better phone. Not all of the price rise is inflation.
  • Not your basket: the CPI tracks an average household. Students, pensioners and renters spend differently, so face different inflation rates.
  • Weights lag behind: they come from past spending surveys and are updated only from time to time (often yearly).
  • Real data: in 1996 the US Boskin Commission estimated that the CPI overstated the rise in the cost of living by about 1.1 percentage points a year; about 0.4 points of this was substitution bias.
  • Say: "the CPI measures changes in the price of a basket" — not: "the CPI measures each person's cost of living".
Quick check
?The price of beef rises by 30% while the price of chicken does not change. Many households buy less beef and more chicken. The consumer price index uses a fixed basket bought in the base year. What is the most likely effect on the measured inflation rate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. In one year, food prices rise by 10% and all other prices stay the same. Household X spends 40% of its budget on food. Household Y spends 10% on food. In the country's consumer price index, food has a weight of 20%. Which set of inflation rates is correct?

A price index is only as good as its weights. Each household has its own inflation rate, and the CPI is the average one.

Mission 5

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 5

Worked example: reading the inflation rate

  • Illustrative data. Consumer price index (CPI) at the end of each year:
Year1234
CPI250260265.2262.5
  • Inflation rate = (new CPI − old CPI) ÷ old CPI × 100.
  • Year 2: (260 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = 4.0%. Inflation.
  • Year 3: (265.2 − 260) ÷ 260 × 100 = 2.0%. The rate fell but prices still rose: disinflation.
  • Year 4: (262.5 − 265.2) ÷ 265.2 × 100 = −1.0%. Prices fell: deflation.
  • The same formula works with the GDP deflator in place of the CPI.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A country's annual inflation rate was 3% in 2020, 5% in 2021, 2% in 2022 and −1% in 2023. In which year did the country have disinflation?
Mission 5

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 5

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.
Quick check
?Inflation turns out much higher than anyone expected when contracts were signed. Who gains?
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm owes 100,000 on a loan with no interest. Over a year, the price level falls by 10%, and so do the prices the firm charges. What happens to the real burden of the debt?
Mission 5Explain

Show who gains and who loses

  • State the contract: a nominal interest rate fixed in advance, based on expected inflation.
  • Compute: real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − actual inflation.
  • If actual inflation > expected: real rate is lower than planned → borrowers gain, lenders lose.
  • If actual inflation < expected (or deflation): real rate is higher than planned → lenders gain, borrowers lose.
  • Name others hurt by unexpected inflation: savers and people on fixed incomes.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A family takes a one-year loan from a bank at a fixed 4% interest rate. Both sides expect 3% inflation. Instead, prices fall by 1% over the year. Who gains from the surprise?

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Actual real GDP fluctuates around a rising trend (potential output): the business cycle.
  • Phases: expansion and recession; turning points: peak and trough.
  • Output gap = actual − potential. Below potential: spare capacity. Above potential: inflationary pressure.
  • Demand and supply shocks cause the swings.

Mission 2

  • Working-age population = employed + unemployed + inactive. Labor force = employed + unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labor force × 100.
  • Labor force participation rate = labor force ÷ adult population × 100.
  • Discouraged and underemployed workers are not counted as unemployed, so the rate understates joblessness.

Mission 3

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Natural rate = frictional + structural. Cyclical = actual − natural.

Mission 4

  • Inflation is a sustained rise in the general price level; disinflation is a fall in the inflation rate while prices still rise; deflation is a falling price level.
  • A price index compares the cost of a weighted basket with its cost in a base year (= 100). Inflation is the % change in the index, not the change in points.
  • Real values remove inflation: real = nominal × (base-year index ÷ current index). The CPI has limits, including substitution bias, quality change and its average-household basket.

Mission 5

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
