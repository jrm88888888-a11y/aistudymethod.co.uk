Mission 1

Mission 1: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Make a guess: Why might a business that brings in money every year still be making no real profit?

Mission 1

What profit is

  • Profit = total revenue − total costs.
  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Total costs means the opportunity cost of every resource the firm uses.
  • Some costs are paid out in money. Some are not. Both count.
Mission 1

Explicit and implicit costs

  • Explicit costs: money actually paid out. Wages, rent, materials, energy, interest on loans.
  • Implicit costs: the value of the owner's own resources used in the business. No payment is made.
  • Examples: the salary the owner gives up by not working elsewhere; the interest her own savings could have earned; the rent her own building could have earned.
  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs.
  • Economic profit = TR − explicit costs − implicit costs.
  • Economists treat normal profit as a cost: the reward the owner must get to keep her resources in this business.
  • Say: "accounting profit ignores implicit costs" — not: "accounting profit is wrong". It answers a different question.
Mission 1

Worked example: the café

  • Illustrative data. A café, one year. Figures per year.
  • Total revenue: 120,000.
  • Explicit costs (food, staff, rent, energy): 85,000.
  • Accounting profit = 120,000 − 85,000 = 35,000.
  • Implicit costs: the owner gives up a salary of 30,000 as a manager elsewhere. She put 100,000 of her savings in; at 5% interest they would earn 100,000 × 0.05 = 5,000.
  • Implicit costs = 30,000 + 5,000 = 35,000.
  • Economic profit = 35,000 − 35,000 = 0.
  • The accounts show a profit. Yet she would do just as well elsewhere. That is normal profit.
Mission 1

Normal, supernormal and subnormal profit

  • Normal profit: the minimum profit needed to keep the firm in its current use. TR = TC, so AR = AC.
  • Supernormal profit: profit above normal profit. TR > TC, so AR > AC.
  • Subnormal profit (a loss): profit below normal profit. TR < TC, so AR < AC.
  • Your course calls supernormal profit abnormal profit. Same idea.
  • Here costs include normal profit. So TR = TC is not "no money": it is exactly enough.
  • Say: "the firm earns normal profit" — not: "the firm makes no profit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A designer runs her own studio. Last year her revenue was £200,000 and her explicit costs were £150,000. She gave up a salary of £45,000 to run it, and she put £80,000 of her savings into the business, which could have earned 5% interest. What kind of profit did the studio earn?
Mission 1

Profit on a cost diagram

  • Profit per unit = AR − AC.
  • Total profit = (AR − AC) × Q: a rectangle.
  • Illustrative data, Q = 100, AC = 9:
  • AR = 12: profit = (12 − 9) × 100 = 300. Supernormal.
  • AR = 9: profit = 0. Normal profit.
  • AR = 8: profit = (8 − 9) × 100 = −100. A loss.
  • (Which output a firm picks comes in Mission 2.)

Quick check
?Illustrative data. A diagram shows a firm producing 50 units. At this output its average revenue is 20 and its average cost is 16. What is the area of the profit rectangle?
Mission 1

Why profit matters in a market economy

  • Signal: supernormal profit shows buyers want more. Resources move in. Losses show they want less. Resources move out.
  • Firms enter in search of supernormal profit and leave when they make subnormal profit.
  • Incentive: profit rewards risk-taking, effort and new ideas.
  • Source of finance: retained profit pays for investment and research.
  • Normal profit is the minimum that keeps resources in their current use.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is supernormal profit good or bad?

It depends on …

  • Barriers to entry: with free entry, new firms compete it away. Behind high barriers it can last, with higher prices for buyers.
  • Where it comes from: a better product or lower costs, or market power?
  • How it is used: reinvested in research and new products (dynamic efficiency), or paid out while prices stay high.
  • Time: a short-run reward for risk is different from a profit that lasts for decades.
  • Judge: supernormal profit is a useful signal and reward, but it also needs watching when it persists.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A software engineer leaves a job paying 60,000 a year to build her own app business. In its first year the app earns revenue of 90,000. She pays 50,000 for servers, design and marketing. She has no other costs. Which statement is correct?

Revenue has to cover the owner's best alternative too. Until it does, the business is not earning a real profit.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Make a guess: Why would a business stop making more when each extra sale still brings in money?

Mission 2

Thinking at the margin

  • Profit = TR − TC.
  • Marginal revenue (MR): the extra revenue from selling one more unit.
  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of making one more unit.
  • MR > MC: one more unit adds to profit. Make more.
  • MR < MC: one more unit cuts profit. Make less.
  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR.
  • Say: "profit is maximised where MC = MR" — not: "where revenue is highest" or "where cost per unit is lowest".

Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bakery sells batches of bread at a fixed price of 5 per batch, however many it sells. Its fixed costs are 4. The marginal cost of the 1st to 5th batches is 2, 3, 4, 6 and 8. How many batches maximise its profit?
Mission 2

MC = MR on the diagram

  • Find Q where MC cuts MR from below. That is the profit-maximising output.
  • Go up from that Q to the AR (demand) curve to read the price.
  • Go up to AC at the same Q to read cost per unit.
  • Profit = (AR − AC) × Q: the rectangle between price and AC.
  • If AR < AC at that output, MC = MR gives the smallest loss the firm can make while producing.
  • Say: "read the price off the demand (AR) curve" — not: "off the point where MC = MR".
Mission 2Graph lab

Find the best output

  • Takeaway: profit climbs while MR > MC and falls once MC > MR, even though revenue is still rising. The peak is the MC = MR output.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a diagram, MC cuts MR from below at an output of 40, where MC = MR = 12. At an output of 40, average revenue is 25 and average cost is 18. What price does the profit-maximising firm charge, and what is its profit?
Mission 2Evaluate

Do real firms set MC = MR?

It depends on …

  • Information: firms rarely know their exact MC and MR. Many use rules of thumb, such as adding a mark-up to cost.
  • Objectives: owners and managers may aim for growth, sales or a satisfactory profit instead.
  • Time: a firm may hold prices below the short-run profit-maximising level to keep rivals out or win loyal buyers, aiming at long-run profit.
  • Competition: in fierce competition, firms that stray far from maximum profit may not survive.
  • Judge: MC = MR is a model of how profit-seeking firms behave, more accurate in competitive markets than as a description of how managers decide.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A water park makes a large profit each year. It is thinking of opening for one extra hour every evening. The extra hour would bring in 900 of ticket and food sales. It would add 1,100 to costs (staff, lighting, heating the pools). What should a profit-maximising water park do?

Profit rises only while each extra unit adds more to revenue than to cost. Past that point, more sales mean less profit.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some firms grow by buying up other firms, while most firms stay small?

Make a guess: Most businesses never grow beyond a handful of people. What is the main reason?

Mission 3

Why grow?

  • Economies of scale: a bigger firm can cut its average cost (bulk buying, specialist machines, cheaper finance).
  • Market power: a larger market share gives more control over price and less threat from rivals.
  • Spreading risk: selling more products or in more countries means one bad market hurts less.
  • Survival: a large firm is harder to take over and better able to ride out a downturn.
  • Managers' goals: pay, status and power often rise with size (the principal–agent problem, another lesson).
Mission 3

Two routes: organic or external

  • Organic (internal) growth: the firm expands by itself: new outlets, new products, more capacity, often paid for from retained profit.
  • Slower and lower risk; the firm keeps its culture and control.
  • External growth joins firms together:
  • Merger: two firms agree to combine into one.
  • Takeover (acquisition): one firm buys a controlling share of another, with or without its agreement.
  • Faster: it buys market share, brands and skills at once. But it is costly, risky, and the two cultures may clash.
Mission 3

Three directions of integration

  • Think of a chain of production: raw materials → manufacturing → wholesale → retail.
  • Horizontal integration: firms at the same stage of the same industry join (two car makers).
  • Vertical integration: firms at different stages of the same chain join.
  • Backward: towards the supplier (a car maker buys a steel works).
  • Forward: towards the customer (a car maker buys a chain of car showrooms).
  • Conglomerate integration: firms in unrelated industries join (a car maker buys a hotel chain).
  • Say: "backward vertical integration" for buying a supplier — not: "forward", which means moving towards the final customer.
Quick check
?A coffee-shop chain buys a company that roasts coffee beans and already supplies the chain. What type of integration is this?
Mission 3

Mission 3

Real cases

  • Horizontal, blocked: in April 2019 the UK's competition authority (the CMA) blocked a merger of Sainsbury's and Asda, two of the country's largest supermarkets. It expected higher prices, less choice or lower quality for shoppers.
  • Vertical: in March 2018 Tesco, a supermarket chain, completed its takeover of Booker, a food wholesaler that supplies shops and restaurants.
  • Demerger (break-up): in July 2022 the drugs company GSK split off its consumer health products (toothpaste, painkillers) as a separate company, Haleon.
  • The same patterns appear in every economy.
Mission 3

What holds growth back

  • Constraints before growth:
  • Market size: there may not be enough customers to sell more.
  • Finance: banks or investors may refuse to fund expansion.
  • Owner objectives: owners may not want to dilute control.
  • Regulation: competition authorities can block mergers that reduce competition.
  • Problems of growth (costs once the firm is big):
  • Diseconomies of scale: average cost rises as the firm becomes too big to manage well.
  • Communication: more layers of management slow decisions and distort messages.
  • Skills: the firm may lack managers or workers with the skills a bigger operation needs.
  • Corporate culture: after a merger, two ways of working may clash, lowering motivation and productivity.
Quick check
?Two engineering firms merge. Within two years, average cost per unit has risen. Staff complain that decisions now pass through three extra layers of managers, and that the two halves of the firm still work in very different ways. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 3Evaluate

Does growth pay?

  • Whether growth benefits a firm and its customers depends on:
  • Type: horizontal and vertical growth can give real cost savings; conglomerate growth gives few economies of scale.
  • Route: organic growth is slower but less risky; a takeover is fast but the buyer may overpay.
  • Culture and management: savings promised before a merger may be lost to clashing ways of working.
  • Size reached: past a point, diseconomies of scale outweigh economies.
  • Who you ask: shareholders may gain from market power while consumers pay higher prices; competition authorities weigh both.
  • Workers may gain promotion and job security, or lose jobs where the merged firm cuts duplicate roles.
  • So growth is not right for every firm. Most firms stay small: their market is small, finance for expansion is hard to get, or the owner prefers to keep control.
Mission check

?A chocolate maker's profits swing wildly because the price and supply of cocoa beans are unreliable. Its managers want growth that most directly reduces this problem. Which route fits best?

Match the direction of growth to the problem: supply worries point backward, selling worries point forward.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Make a guess: If buyers and sellers both gain from a trade, how can a free market still produce the wrong amount?

Mission 4

What a free market gets right

  • Each point on the demand curve shows the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit to buyers.
  • Each point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost (MC) of one more unit to sellers.
  • At competitive equilibrium, P = MB = MC.
  • Consumer surplus: what buyers would pay minus what they do pay.
  • Producer surplus: what sellers receive minus the least they would accept.
  • Consumer + producer surplus = community surplus.
  • If there is no market failure, equilibrium makes this total as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
Mission 4

Market equilibrium vs the social optimum

  • Buyers and sellers weigh only their private benefits and costs: market equilibrium is where MPB = MPC.
  • Society counts all benefits and costs: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods society values most; the value of the last unit equals its full cost, MSB = MSC (for a single firm: P = MC).
  • No outside effects: MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the market reaches the social optimum.
  • Outside effects: the market equilibrium and the social optimum are at different quantities.
  • Say: "the market equilibrium is not the socially optimum position" — not: "the market is not in equilibrium". A market can be in equilibrium and still be inefficient.
Mission 4

Other kinds of efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output made at the lowest possible average cost (the bottom of the AC curve); no resources wasted.
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC; society gets the mix of goods it values most.
  • Dynamic efficiency: efficiency over time, from investment and innovation that cut costs or create better products.
  • Pareto optimality: no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • A Pareto optimal economy is allocatively and productively efficient. Market failure means it is not Pareto optimal: some change could help one person and harm no one.
Quick check
?Which condition shows that a market produces the allocatively efficient quantity?
Mission 4Graph lab

Too much, too little

Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. The social optimum is Q = 50.

  • Takeaway: any quantity other than MSB = MSC creates a welfare loss, whether output is too high or too low.
Mission 4

Worked example: measuring the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q; MSC = 20 + Q (price in $, quantity in units per week).
  • Optimum: 120 − Q = 20 + Q, so Q = 50, value 70.
  • Output is 65 instead. At Q = 65: MSC = 20 + 65 = 85; MSB = 120 − 65 = 55.
  • Each extra unit from 50 to 65 costs society more than it is worth.
  • Welfare loss = triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q = 50 to 65.
  • Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (65 − 50) × (85 − 55) = ½ × 15 × 30 = 225.
  • Total surplus at the optimum = ½ × 50 × (120 − 20) = 2,500. At 65 it falls to 2,500 − 225 = 2,275.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. A single seller restricts output to Q = 40. What is the welfare loss?
Mission 4

What market failure means

  • Market failure: the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, so the market outcome is not the social optimum.
  • The result is too much or too little of a good, and a welfare loss.
  • Complete market failure: no market exists at all: a missing market (e.g. street lighting).
  • Partial market failure: a market exists, but at the wrong price or quantity (e.g. too much tobacco, too little vaccination).
Mission 4

Sources of market failure

SourceWhat goes wrongMission
Negative externalitiesCost to third parties: overproduction
Positive externalitiesBenefit to third parties: underproduction
Public goodsFree riders: often no market at all
  • Information gaps (asymmetric information): buyers or sellers misjudge value.
  • Market power (monopoly): output restricted, price above MC.
  • Volatile prices: covered in a later lesson.
Mission 4

Private, external, social

  • Externality: a cost or benefit to a third party, not paid for or received through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. At the margin: MSB = MPB + MEB.
  • External cost from production (e.g. pollution): MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces.
  • External benefit from consumption (e.g. vaccination): MSB above MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • Either way: a welfare (deadweight) loss triangle that points to the social optimum.
Mission 4

Market power

  • A monopoly maximises profit where MR = MC.
  • It charges the price on the demand curve at that output: P > MC.
  • So buyers value the next unit more than it costs to make, but it is not produced: underproduction.
  • Result: a welfare (deadweight) loss, even with no externalities.
Quick check
?In a region, a factory's smoke harms nearby farms' crops, and there is no way to charge the factory for this. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a competitive market for bread has no externalities and settles at 1,000 loaves a day. The government pays bakers to raise output to 1,200 loaves a day. What happens to total surplus from bread?

With no market failure, equilibrium is already the efficient quantity. Moving away from it in either direction loses surplus.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a firm make goods at the lowest possible cost and still waste resources?

Make a guess: Can a firm make goods at the lowest possible cost and still waste resources?

Mission 5

Two questions about waste

  • Efficiency: getting the most out of scarce resources.
  • Ask two separate questions:
    • How is it made? At the lowest possible cost per unit? → productive efficiency
    • What is made, and how much? The goods society values most? → allocative efficiency
  • One can hold without the other.
  • Both are static efficiency: efficiency at a point in time, with today's technology.
  • Dynamic efficiency asks a third question: are costs and products improving over time?
Mission 5

Productive efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output where average cost (AC) is at its minimum.
  • At that output, MC = AC: the MC curve cuts the AC curve at its lowest point.
  • Why? If the next unit costs less than the average (MC < AC), it pulls AC down. If it costs more (MC > AC), it pushes AC up.
  • Say: "productively efficient at minimum AC" — not: "at the lowest total cost" (that is zero output).

Mission 5

Allocative efficiency

  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods and services society values most, in the right amounts.
  • Condition: P = MC.
    • Price shows what buyers will pay for the last unit: its marginal benefit (MB) to them.
    • MC shows the cost of the resources used for it: the value of what else they could have made.
  • P > MC: buyers value one more unit above its cost → too little is made.
  • P < MC: the last unit costs more than it is worth to buyers → too much is made.
  • Say: "allocatively efficient where P = MC" — not: "where P = AC" (that is normal profit).

  • Takeaway: P = MC holds at every price; minimum AC only when the price equals minimum AC (here 26).
Quick check
?A firm sells its product for 30. At its current output, the marginal cost of the last unit is 18. What does this show?
Mission 5

Pareto efficiency

  • Pareto efficiency (Pareto optimality): no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • It needs both: productive efficiency (no waste in how goods are made) and allocative efficiency (the right goods are made).
  • A Pareto improvement: someone gains and no one loses.
  • Pareto efficiency says nothing about fairness: a very unequal outcome can still be Pareto efficient.
Mission 5

Dynamic efficiency

  • Dynamic efficiency: efficiency over time, through innovation.
    • Process innovation: new methods lower costs → the whole AC curve shifts down.
    • Product innovation: new and better products.
  • It needs investment, R&D and new technology.
  • Funding: retained supernormal profit is a major source.
  • Incentive: firms innovate to win or protect profit.
Mission 5

Comparing market structures

  • Long-run outcomes, as usually taught:
ProductiveAllocativeDynamic
Perfect competitionYes (min AC)Yes (P = MC)Limited: only normal profit; no secrets to protect
Monopolistic competitionNo (excess capacity)No (P > MC)Some: product variety
OligopolyUnlikelyNo (P > MC)Possible: profits and rivalry fund R&D

  • Firms with market power face a downward-sloping demand curve, so they can set P above MC.
Mission 5

Case: patents

  • A patent gives an inventor the sole right to sell an invention for a set time.
  • WTO members must offer patent protection for at least 20 years from the filing date.
  • Static cost: a monopoly → P > MC → allocatively inefficient.
  • Dynamic gain: the expected profit pays for costly R&D (e.g. new medicines).
  • A deliberate trade: less static efficiency now for more dynamic efficiency later.
Mission 5Evaluate

Static or dynamic: which matters more?

  • It depends on whether profit is reinvested: supernormal profit may fund R&D, or be paid out to owners.
  • It depends on competitive pressure: a protected monopoly may stop innovating and let costs drift up.
  • It depends on economies of scale: one large firm's minimum AC may be far below that of many small firms.
  • It depends on time: static losses happen now; dynamic gains, if any, come later and are uncertain.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A firm produces exactly at the lowest point of its AC curve. Its price is 12. At this output, its marginal cost is 7. Which statement is correct?

Low cost and the right amount are two different tests. A firm can pass the first and fail the second.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Accounting profit = TR − explicit costs; economic profit also subtracts implicit costs (opportunity costs of the owner's own resources).
  • Normal profit: AR = AC. Supernormal (abnormal) profit: AR > AC. Subnormal profit (loss): AR < AC.

Mission 2

  • Profit is maximised where MC = MR (MC cutting MR from below); the price is read off the AR (demand) curve.

Mission 3

  • Firms grow for economies of scale, market power, lower risk and survival; organically (by themselves) or externally (merger or takeover).
  • Horizontal joins the same stage, vertical joins different stages (backward to suppliers, forward to customers), conglomerate joins unrelated industries.
  • Growth can bring diseconomies of scale: poor communication, skills gaps and culture clashes.
  • Most firms stay small because of small markets, limited finance or the owner's own goals.

Mission 4

  • At the social optimum MSB = MSC and total surplus is as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
  • Market failure (for example externalities and public goods) leaves output above or below that point, causing a welfare loss.

Mission 5

  • Productive efficiency: minimum AC (MC = AC).
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC.
  • Dynamic efficiency: costs and products improve over time through R&D and investment.
