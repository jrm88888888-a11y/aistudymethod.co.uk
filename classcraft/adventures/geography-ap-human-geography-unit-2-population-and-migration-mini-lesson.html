Mission 1

Distribution and density

  • Population distribution: where people live, and how they are spread across an area.
  • Population density: how many people per unit of area (per km² or per sq mi).
  • Two places can have the same density but very different distributions.
Mission 1

Physical factors

  • Climate: reliable rain and mild temperatures support farming; deserts and ice caps repel settlement.
  • Landforms: flat lowlands and river valleys are easy to farm and build on; high mountains and plateaus are not.
  • Water bodies: rivers, lakes and coasts supply water, food and transportation.
Mission 1

Human factors

  • Economics: jobs, industry, ports and trade routes draw people.
  • History: long-settled farming regions keep large populations; cities grow where cities already are.
  • Politics: capital cities, government investment, migration controls.
  • Culture: ties to homeland, religion or family keep people in place or draw them together.
Quick check
?Which of these is a human factor drawing people to an area?
Mission 1

Scale changes the explanation

  • Global scale: Asia holds about 59% of the world's people (2024); physical limits such as deserts, ice and high mountains explain the empty areas.
  • National scale: within one country, climate and landforms divide crowded and empty regions.
  • Local scale: within a city, jobs, transportation, housing costs and planning decide where people live.
  • Exam move: name the scale before you name the factor.
Mission 1

China's Hu Line

  • Geographer Hu Huanyong drew the line in 1935, from Heihe in the northeast to Tengchong in the southwest.
  • Southeast of it: about 43% of the land but about 94% of the people.
  • Physical: fertile plains and river valleys and adequate rainfall in the east; mountains, plateaus, deserts and an arid climate in the west.
  • Human: ports, economic hubs and early special economic zones in the east reinforced the pattern.
  • Researchers find the split has stayed at "roughly 94:6".
Mission 1

Egypt: one river

  • About 95% of Egypt's people live in the Nile Valley and Delta.
  • Egypt's national density was about 117 people per km² in 2024, but almost all the land is desert.
  • One physical factor, water, explains the pattern; irrigation, cities and history have built on it.
Quick check

Mission 1

How distribution affects society

  • Political: seats in the US House of Representatives are divided among the states by population after each census; states that grow faster than others can gain seats.
  • Economic: dense areas support more firms, markets and specialized services.
  • Social, including medical care: rural and remote Australians "face barriers to accessing health care", including geographic spread and low population density.
  • In 2020–21, non-referred visits, such as visits to a general practitioner (GP), per person: 6.8 in major cities; 4.7 in remote and 3.4 in very remote areas.
Quick check
?Why do very remote areas tend to have fewer doctors' visits per person than big cities?
Mission check

?In 2001, more than 8 in 10 Australians (85%) lived within 50 km of the coast, and Australia's overall density was only about 3.5 people per km² in 2024. What best explains this pattern?

China's Hu Line marks a physical divide (plains, river valleys and rainfall in the southeast; mountains, plateaus and deserts in the northwest) that human factors have reinforced, so about 94% of people live on 43% of the land.

Mission 2

Arithmetic density

  • Arithmetic density = total population ÷ total land area.
  • 2024, people per km² (calculated from World Bank data):
    • Egypt: 117
    • Netherlands: 534
  • By this measure, the Netherlands is about 4.6 times as crowded.
Mission 2

Physiological density

  • Physiological density = total population ÷ arable land (land that can grow crops).
  • Arable land, 2024: Egypt 3.3% of its land; Netherlands 29.8%.
  • People per km² of arable land (our calculation):
    • Egypt: 3,560
    • Netherlands: 1,794
  • Now Egypt is about twice as crowded.
Quick check
?Why does Egypt's density rise so much more than the Netherlands' when we switch from arithmetic to physiological density?
Mission 2

Agricultural density

  • Agricultural density = number of farmers ÷ arable land.
  • Farmers per km² of arable land, 2024 (estimated from World Bank and ILO data):
    • Egypt: about 181
    • Netherlands: about 17
  • Egypt has about 10 times as many farmers working each km² of farmland.
Mission 2

What each measure reveals

  • Arithmetic: a simple average; hides where people actually live.
  • Physiological: pressure on farmland: how many people each km² of cropland must feed. High = more strain on food-growing land.
  • Agricultural: how farming is done. High = many farmers on little land (less mechanized). Low = few farmers with machines and technology.
  • Read them together.

Mission 2

Carrying capacity

  • Carrying capacity: the largest population an area can support without damaging its environment and resources.
  • It depends on resources (soil, water) and on technology and trade (irrigation, fertilizer, imports).
  • Where density pushes past carrying capacity: soil is overused, water runs short, forests are cleared, or food must be imported.
Mission 2

Density and resources

  • Arable land per person, 2024 (our calculation):
    • Egypt: about 281 m²
    • Netherlands: about 557 m²
    • United States: about 4,457 m²
  • Less farmland per person = more pressure on soil and water to feed everyone.
  • Technology and trade can raise what land supports; overuse can lower it.

Each measure answers a different question. Egypt is less crowded than the Netherlands by arithmetic density, but about twice as crowded on its farmland.

Population composition

  • Age structure: how a population is split between age groups, such as children (0–14), working age (15–64) and older people (65+).
  • Sex ratio: the number of males for every 100 females.
  • At birth it is naturally above 100: worldwide, 105.4 boys were born per 100 girls in 2024.

Mission 3

Reading a population pyramid

  • Bars show the % of the population in each 5-year age group: males on the left, females on the right.
  • The base shows young children; the top shows the oldest people.
  • The shape records past births, deaths and migration: a dent can mark a time of few births, or many people leaving.

Mission 4

What changes a population

  • Three demographic factors: fertility (births), mortality (deaths) and migration.
  • Natural change = births − deaths.
  • Net migration = immigrants − emigrants.
  • Population change = natural change + net migration.
Mission 4

Rates geographers use

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Rate of natural increase (RNI), % per year = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10.
  • Dividing by 10 turns "per 1,000" into "per 100" (%).

Mission 4

Fertility and replacement level

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's rates.
  • Replacement level is 2.1 births per woman: the level at which a population keeps a constant size in the long run without migration.
  • World TFR, 2024: 2.25. Niger 5.94; Japan 1.22.
  • Niger is one of the countries with four or more births per woman.
Mission 4

Doubling time

  • Doubling time: years for a population to double at a constant growth rate.
  • Rule of 70: doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate (% per year).
  • It is an approximation: it assumes the rate stays the same.
Quick check

Mission 4

Where migration fits

  • Japan, 2024: about 751,000 births and 1,540,000 deaths, a natural decrease of about 790,000.
  • Net migration added about 153,000, so the population still fell, by 0.51%.
  • Niger, 2024: net migration was small (about −4,000), so growth comes almost entirely from natural increase.
  • The UN notes that in countries whose populations have peaked, immigration "in some cases" slows the decline.
Mission 4

Why rates differ: four kinds of factor

  • Social: girls' education, access to health care and contraception lower fertility; clean water and vaccination lower mortality.
  • Cultural: norms about family size, marriage age and the role of women.
  • Political: pronatalist or antinatalist policies, immigration rules, conflict that drives people out.
  • Economic: where children work on family farms, large families pay; where children are costly (housing, schooling), families shrink; jobs pull migrants.
Quick check
?A government pays families a cash bonus for each new baby. Which kind of factor is this?
Mission 4

The demographic transition model (DTM)

  • Stage 1: high, fluctuating CBR and CDR; little growth.
  • Stage 2: CDR falls (food supply, clean water, sanitation, vaccination); CBR stays high, so growth is rapid.
  • Stage 3: CBR falls (fewer child deaths, urbanization, education, contraception); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low CBR and CDR; slow or no growth.
  • Stage 5: CBR below CDR; the population declines.
Mission 4

Using and criticizing the DTM

  • Niger: late stage 2, entering stage 3: CDR already low (8.7); CBR still very high (41.4) but falling (55.1 in 1990).
  • Japan: stage 5: deaths exceed births.
  • Limits: based on Western Europe and North America; in many countries death rates fell faster, with imported medicine; it leaves out migration and policy; it does not predict when a country moves on.
Quick check
?Over 40 years, a country's CDR has fallen from 30 to 15, while its CBR has stayed at about 45. Which DTM stage fits best?
Mission check

?A country has a CBR of 9 and a CDR of 11, yet its population is growing. What explains this?

Niger's births far exceed deaths (RNI 3.27%), so at that rate it doubles in about 21 years. In Japan deaths exceed births, and net migration (about 153,000 in 2024) is too small to stop the fall. Fertility, mortality and migration together decide growth or decline.

Mission 5

Why death rates change

  • The demographic transition model shows death rates falling before birth rates.
  • The epidemiological transition explains why: the main causes of death change as societies develop.
  • Infectious (communicable) diseases spread from person to person, or through water, food or insects: cholera, tuberculosis, measles.
  • Non-communicable diseases (NCDs) do not: heart disease, cancers, diabetes, chronic lung disease.
Mission 5

Omran's stages (1971)

  • Abdel Omran described three stages:
    • 1. "The Age of Pestilence and Famine": high, unstable death rates from infection and hunger.
    • 2. "The Age of Receding Pandemics": epidemics fade and death rates fall.
    • 3. "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases": NCDs become the main killers.
  • Over time, "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
Mission 5

A fourth stage

  • Olshansky and Ault (1986) added "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • People still die mostly of NCDs, but at older ages, as treatment for heart disease and other conditions improves.
  • New and re-emerging infections, such as HIV and COVID-19, show the shift is not always one-way.
Quick check
?In which stage do epidemics fade and death rates fall quickly, as in stage 2 of the demographic transition model?
Mission 5

The evidence

  • WHO: NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • Dementia rose from the 19th to the 5th leading cause of death between 2000 and 2023: a disease of old age.
Quick check
?In a country, the chance of dying from an NCD at each age has not changed, yet NCDs' share of all deaths rises. What best explains this?
Mission 5

Why the causes change

  • Clean water, sanitation and vaccines cut deaths from infection, especially among children.
  • More people survive to old age, when NCDs are most common.
  • Diets, tobacco, alcohol and physical inactivity are the main behavioral risks for NCDs.
Mission 5

An uneven transition

  • In 2023, for the first time, infections caused less than half of deaths in low-income countries.
  • 82% of the 18 million NCD deaths before age 70 in 2021 were in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Many poorer countries face infections and NCDs at once: a double burden of disease.
  • Limit: Omran's main ("classical") model follows England and Wales and Sweden; he added a faster model (Japan) and a delayed one (Chile, Ceylon, now Sri Lanka). Real countries rarely fit one path neatly.
  • Exam move: to explain a change in death rates, name the stage, the cause of death that changed, and a reason (e.g., vaccines, aging).
Mission check

?In a country, child deaths from diarrhea fall sharply over 30 years, life expectancy rises, and heart disease becomes the leading cause of death. Is this good news or bad news?

The rise from 58% to 74% is the epidemiological transition: infections recede, people live longer, and NCDs (and dementia) take over. It is good news for survival, and a growing burden of chronic disease.

Mission 6

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Thomas Malthus, An Essay on the Principle of Population (1798).
  • "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Geometric (exponential) growth: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16… Arithmetic (linear) growth: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5…
  • So, he argued, population would outrun food.
Mission 6

What holds population back

  • Malthus said growth is held back by checks:
    • preventive checks lower births (for example, marrying later);
    • positive checks raise deaths (for example, famine and disease).
  • In his view, if people did not limit births, hunger and disease would.
Quick check
?A famine kills many people and population growth stops. In Malthus's terms, this is a…
Mission 6

Neo-Malthusians: The Limits to Growth

  • Neo-Malthusians apply Malthus's logic to all resources and to pollution, not just food.
  • The Limits to Growth (Meadows et al., 1972) warned that if trends in "population, industrialization, pollution, food production, and resource depletion continue unchanged, the limits to growth on this planet will be reached sometime within the next one hundred years".
  • But it also said "It is possible to alter these growth trends".
Mission 6

Boserup: a critique

  • Ester Boserup (The Conditions of Agricultural Growth, 1965) argued the reverse of Malthus.
  • Summarized by Turner and Ali: "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • Pressure on land drives new methods, such as irrigation that allows "double and triple cropping".
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.

Mission 6

The other side of the ledger

  • An estimated 645 million people were still chronically undernourished in 2025.
  • In 2023, scientists found "six of the nine" planetary boundaries crossed; a 2025 update reported seven.
  • Higher yields came partly from fertilizer, irrigation and clearing land.
  • A study of Bangladeshi villages found farmers "kept pace with the demands on production", but more households became landless.
Mission 6

Critiques of both models

  • Malthus did not foresee new technology (the Green Revolution), trade in food, or birth rates falling as incomes rise.
  • Boserup assumed slow growth with time to adapt; rapid growth or worn-out soils can outpace new methods.
  • Neo-Malthusians reply that food is not the only limit: pollution and resource depletion matter too.
  • Turner and Ali: the answer "depends in a large part on temporal and spatial scale".

Mission check

Worldwide, food per person rose, from 2,196 to 2,884 calories a day (1961–2013), because yields roughly tripled. That fits Boserup better than Malthus. But 645 million people were still undernourished in 2025, and planetary limits are being crossed, so neo-Malthusian warnings still matter.

Mission 7

Three kinds of population policy

  • Antinatalist policy: tries to lower the birth rate (limits on births, free family planning).
  • Pronatalist policy: tries to raise the birth rate (cash for children, childcare, parental leave).

Mission 7

An antinatalist policy: China

  • From 1980, China limited most couples to one child.
  • Intent: slow the growth of China's population.
  • The policy was ended in early 2016.
Mission 7

Did the policy cause the fall in births?

  • China's total fertility rate (TFR) was 6.1 children per woman in 1970 and 2.7 in 1979, before the one-child policy.
  • By 2000 it was 1.6.
  • So the policy added to a fall that was already under way, alongside economic change and more education.

The U-turn to pronatalist

  • China revised its family planning law in 2015 to allow two children for all couples; the one-child policy ended in early 2016.
  • On August 20, 2021, the law was amended to let all couples have three children.
  • Why? The 2020 fertility rate had "dropped to 1.3 children per woman", and the government pointed to "a graying populace and declining fertility rates".

  • Short-term baby bonuses have "a limited effect, mostly affecting the timing of childbearing": people have children sooner, not more children.
  • South Korea's TFR was 0.72 in 2023 and 0.80 in 2025: a small rise, still far below replacement level.

Mission 8

Fertility is falling almost everywhere

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have in her lifetime.
  • Today "the global fertility rate stands at 2.25 live births per woman".
  • Replacement level is 2.1, "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
Mission 8

Four levers on fertility

  • Changing social values: smaller families become the norm; later marriage.
  • Education for girls and women.
  • Employment: paid work outside the home.
  • Health care and contraception: women can choose when and how often to have children.
  • The UN links later childbearing to "improved education, greater gender equality and rising workforce participation".
Mission 8

Bangladesh: the levers moved

  • TFR: 6.3 (1980) → 2.1 (2024).
  • Contraceptive use (married women 15–49): 12.1% (1980) → 64.0% (2022).
  • Girls completing lower secondary school: 55.0% (2000) → 84.7% (2024).

Niger: the levers barely moved

  • School delays first birth: the UN says later childbearing is "driven by factors like improved education".

Women's roles and mortality

  • From 2000 to 2023 the maternal mortality ratio "dropped by about 40% worldwide".

  • Lifetime risk: "1 in 7933" in high-income countries, "1 in 66 in low-income countries".

Ravenstein's laws of migration

  • E. G. Ravenstein studied the 1871 and 1881 censuses of the British Isles.
  • In 1885 he wrote: "Females are more migratory than males."

Women's migration today

  • In 2024, "just under half (48 per cent)" of international migrants were women or girls.

  • Today the global share of women among international migrants is a little under half: patterns depend on jobs, laws and women's roles in each place.

Mission 9: Japan has more people over 80 than children under 10. How does a whole country grow that old?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • SPS-2.C.1 population aging is determined by birth and death rates and life expectancy
  • SPS-2.C.2 aging has political, social and economic consequences, including the dependency ratio
Mission 9

What population aging means

  • Population aging: older people make up a growing share of a population, and the median age rises.
  • Worldwide, people aged 65+ were 10.2% of the population in 2024, and are projected to be 16.3% by 2050.
  • WHO: "By 2030, 1 in 6 people in the world will be aged 60 years or over".
Mission 9

Cause 1: fewer births

  • When birth rates fall, each new generation of children is smaller than the one before.
  • Japan's total fertility rate (average children per woman) fell from 3.62 in 1950 to 1.22 in 2024 (UN estimates).
  • Small young age groups make the base of the pyramid narrow, so the older share grows.
Mission 9

Cause 2: longer lives

  • When death rates fall, especially at older ages, more people survive into their 80s and 90s.
  • Japan's life expectancy at birth rose from 59.3 years in 1950 to 84.9 in 2024.
  • Migration can age places too: the UN notes rural "population ageing and out-migration, as young people move to cities and towns".
Quick check
?Which combination would make a population age fastest?
Mission 9

The result in Japan

  • People aged 65+ rose from 4.9% of Japan's population (1950) to 29.8% (2024).
  • In 2024 Japan had about 13.1 million people aged 80+ and 8.8 million children under 10.
  • Deaths exceeded births by about 790,000 in 2024.
Mission 9

The dependency ratio

  • Dependency ratio = (people aged 0–14 + people aged 65+) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100: dependents for every 100 people of working age.
  • Old-age dependency ratio = people aged 65+ ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100.
  • Japan 2024: dependency ratio 70.1; old-age dependency ratio 50.7, or about 2 people of working age for each person aged 65+.
  • Caution: many 15–19-year-olds are still in school, and many people over 65 still work.

?In Italy in 2024, 11.9% of people were aged 0–14, 63.5% were 15–64 and 24.6% were 65 or over. What was Italy's old-age dependency ratio?
Mission 9

Economic consequences

  • Pensions and health care for more older people are paid for by fewer workers per retiree.
  • South Korea's old-age dependency ratio is projected to rise from 27.5 (2024) to 75.6 (2050): from about 3.6 to about 1.3 people of working age for each person aged 65+.
  • Labor shortages can appear as the working-age population shrinks.
  • Opportunities: older people spend, work, volunteer and care for grandchildren.
Mission 9

Social and political consequences

  • Social: the number of people aged 80+ is expected to triple between 2020 and 2050, to 426 million. Cities face more demand for "housing, care facilities and social support".
  • WHO: "All countries face major challenges to ensure that their health and social systems are ready".
  • Political: as the older share of a population grows, so does the older share of voters, which can shift debates on pensions, health care and retirement age.
Quick check
?A country's old-age dependency ratio is rising fast. Which challenge follows most directly?
Mission 9

Aging is spreading

  • WHO: aging "started in high-income countries", but "it is now low- and middle-income countries that are experiencing the greatest change".
  • China: 65+ projected to rise from 14.7% (2024) to 30.9% (2050).
  • Where birth rates fall quickly, aging comes quickly too.
Mission check

?In 2024 South Korea's total fertility rate was 0.73 and its life expectancy was 84.4 years. People aged 65+ are projected to rise from 19.3% to 39.7% of its population by 2050. What is causing this, and what is the most likely consequence?

Population aging comes from low birth rates and long life expectancy (and, locally, young people moving away). In Japan, people over 80 now outnumber children under 10. Consequences include a rising dependency ratio, pressure on pensions and care, labor shortages, and older voters' growing political weight.

Mission 10: What makes people leave home, and what decides where they end up?

Make a guess: What makes people leave home, and what decides where they end up?

Course framework: what this mission covers

AP Human Geography

  • SPS-2.B.2 changing social, economic and political roles for females have influenced patterns of fertility, mortality and migration, as illustrated by Ravenstein's laws of migration (migration part)
  • IMP-2.C explain how different causal factors encourage migration
  • IMP-2.C.1 migration is commonly divided into push and pull factors
  • IMP-2.C.2 push/pull factors and intervening opportunities/obstacles can be cultural, demographic, economic, environmental or political
  • IMP-2.D describe types of forced and voluntary migration (voluntary part)
  • IMP-2.D.2 voluntary migration types: transnational, transhumance, internal, chain, step, guest worker, rural-to-urban
Mission 10

Push and pull

  • Migration: a permanent or long-term move to a new place.
  • Push factors: things at home that make people want to leave.
  • Pull factors: things elsewhere that attract them.
  • In 2024, 304 million people lived outside their country of birth, 3.7% of the world's population, up from 2.9% in 1990.
Mission 10

Five kinds of factor

  • Economic: jobs and wages (push: unemployment; pull: better-paid work).
  • Political: rights, safety, laws (push: persecution; pull: freedom, open immigration rules).
  • Environmental: climate and hazards (push: drought, floods; pull: fertile land, a pleasant climate).
  • Cultural: religion, language, family (push: discrimination; pull: a community that shares your language).
  • Demographic: population size and age (push: too many young workers for too few jobs; pull: aging places short of workers).
Quick check
?Years of failed rains ruin a farming family's harvest, so they move to a city. What kind of factor is this?
Mission 10

What lies in between

  • Intervening obstacles (Everett Lee, 1966): barriers on the way, such as distance, cost, borders, visas, mountains or seas.
  • Intervening opportunities (Samuel Stouffer, 1940): a good opportunity closer to home that stops the move short.
  • Obstacles and opportunities can also be cultural, demographic, economic, environmental or political.
Mission 10

Ravenstein's laws

  • E. G. Ravenstein studied British census data and published "laws of migration" in 1885 and 1889.
  • He thought women were more migratory than men, and tended to move shorter distances.
  • Rural people moved more than urban people.
  • Each migration flow creates a weaker counter-flow back.
  • Most migrants moved for economic reasons: better-paid work.
  • Distance and population size matter: big, nearby places attract most migrants.
Mission 10

Women and migration today

  • In 2024, 48% of all international migrants were women or girls.
  • In Northern Africa and Western Asia the share was only 35.8%: large numbers of male migrant workers moved there.
  • In Qatar, migrants were 76.7% of the population, but only 22.7% of them were female.
  • Changing roles for women (more education, paid work, independent travel) change who migrates, and why.
Quick check
?Qatar had about 2,337,000 international migrants in 2024, and 22.7% were female. About how many female migrants is that?
Mission 10

Moving within a country

  • Internal migration: moving within one country.
  • Rural-to-urban migration: from countryside to city. In China in 2025, 180.06 million migrant workers worked away from their home towns.
  • Step migration: moving in stages, for example village → town → city.
  • Chain migration: following relatives or neighbors who moved earlier; they offer housing, jobs and contacts.
Mission 10

Moving between countries

  • Guest workers: people allowed into a country to work, usually on a temporary contract, and expected to return home. Qatar's large male migrant workforce is a modern example.
  • Transnational migrants: people who keep strong ties, such as family, money and votes, in both their home and new countries.
  • Transhumance: the seasonal movement of herders with their livestock between pastures. UNESCO listed transhumance in ten European countries as cultural heritage in 2023.
Quick check
?A nurse from the Philippines works in Canada, sends money home every month, votes in Philippine elections and video-calls family daily. Which term fits best?
Mission 10

One move, many labels

  • A single move can fit several types: a rural-to-urban move can also be step and chain migration.
  • Push, pull, obstacles and opportunities explain why; the types describe how and where.
  • AP move: name the type, then explain the factor that caused it.
Mission check

?Invented case: Ana grows up in a farming village with few jobs. She moves to a nearby town for school, then to the capital, where wages are higher. Later she takes a two-year contract in another country, where her cousin already works, and returns home when it ends. Which description fits best?

People move because of push and pull factors (economic, political, environmental, cultural, demographic), shaped by intervening obstacles and opportunities. Voluntary moves include internal, rural-to-urban, step, chain, guest-worker, transnational and transhumance; Ravenstein's laws, and today's data on women migrants, show patterns in who moves and how far.

Mission 11

Forced or voluntary?

  • Voluntary migration: people choose to move, usually for work, family or a better life.
  • Forced migration: people are made to move by threats to their life or freedom, such as violence, persecution, enslavement or disaster.
  • Many real moves mix choice and pressure. Ask: could they safely have stayed?
Mission 11

Slavery: forced migration across an ocean

  • Slavery is forced migration: enslaved people had no choice about leaving, where they went or what work they did.
  • The Trans-Atlantic Slave Trade Database estimates that about 12.5 million captives left Africa for the Americas.
  • About 10.7 million enslaved Africans were landed, mainly in the Americas.
  • So about 1.8 million fewer people arrived than left (12.5 − 10.7).
Quick check

Mission 11

Refugee, asylum seeker, IDP

  • Refugee: outside their own country because of a "well-founded fear of being persecuted" for race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion (1951 Refugee Convention).
  • Asylum seeker: has asked another country for protection, and the claim has not yet been decided.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP): forced to flee home but has not crossed an international border.
  • People fleeing disasters are not refugees under the Convention, even if the media calls them "climate refugees".

How many people are forcibly displaced?

  • End of 2025: 117.8 million people were forcibly displaced worldwide.

  • 68.7 million were internally displaced by conflict or violence.
  • So most forcibly displaced people never leave their own country.

A political push: the Rohingya

  • In August 2017, Myanmar's security forces launched military operations in Rakhine State after an armed Rohingya group attacked security posts.

  • Extreme monsoon rains flooded huge areas of Pakistan in 2022.

Three kinds of effect

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Physical factors (climate, landforms, water) set broad limits; human factors (economics, history, politics, culture) decide where within them.
  • The explanation depends on scale, and uneven distribution shapes political power and access to services such as medical care.

Mission 2

  • Arithmetic = people ÷ land; physiological = people ÷ arable land (pressure on farmland); agricultural = farmers ÷ arable land (how farming is done).
  • Carrying capacity: the population an area can support; high density on little farmland pushes toward it.

Mission 3

  • Age structure and sex ratio vary by region and by scale (world region, country, rural and urban areas); migration and life expectancy can skew sex ratios.
  • Population pyramids show composition, signal growth or decline, and predict demand for goods and services.

Mission 4

  • RNI = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10; doubling time ≈ 70 ÷ growth rate; population change = natural change + net migration.
  • The DTM links falling death and then birth rates to growth; Niger is late stage 2 entering stage 3, Japan stage 5.

Mission 5

  • The epidemiological transition explains changing death rates: from infections and famine (Omran stage 1), through receding pandemics (stage 2), to degenerative and human-made diseases (stage 3), and delayed degenerative diseases (stage 4).
  • It is uneven: many poorer countries carry a double burden of infections and NCDs.

Mission 6

  • Malthus: population grows geometrically, food arithmetically, so checks follow. Neo-Malthusians widen the limits to resources and pollution.
  • Boserup and the data: population pressure drove higher yields, but hunger and environmental limits remain.

Mission 7

  • Antinatalist, pronatalist and immigration policies try to change population size and composition.
  • Their intent and their effects often differ: compare the stated aim with dated data.

Mission 8

  • Education, employment, health care and contraception, plus changing values, have cut fertility in most places (Bangladesh: 6.3 → 2.1).
  • Changing female roles also cut maternal mortality and reshape migration: Ravenstein, "Females are more migratory than males"; women are now about 48% of international migrants.

Mission 9

  • Aging is caused by falling birth rates and rising life expectancy; out-migration of young people ages places locally.
  • Consequences are economic (pensions, labor), social (care, housing) and political (older voters), measured partly by the dependency ratio.

Mission 10

  • Push and pull factors can be economic, political, environmental, cultural or demographic; intervening obstacles and opportunities shape the move.
  • Ravenstein (1885, 1889): women more migratory but over shorter distances; counter-flows; economic motives; distance matters. Women are 48% of international migrants today, less where male guest workers dominate.

Mission 11

  • Forced migration includes slavery and moves that create refugees, asylum seekers and IDPs.
  • Ask two questions: could they safely stay? and did they cross a border?

Mission 12

  • Migration has economic, political and cultural effects, at the origin and the destination.
  • Remittances matter most to small economies with many workers abroad; brain drain is their cost.
