Role of HRM

What human resource management does

HRM manages the most valuable resource — people — through the employment cycle:

  • Workforce (human resource) planning — forecasting how many staff, with what skills, are needed.
  • Recruitment and selection — job description, person specification, internal vs external recruitment.
  • Training — induction, on-the-job and off-the-job.
  • Appraisal — formative, summative, 360-degree and self-appraisal.
  • Dismissal (ending a contract for cause) vs redundancy (the job itself no longer exists).

HL: firms also flex the workforce through outsourcing, offshoring and reshoring, and manage the effects of migration and remote/flexible working.

Organisational structure

Hierarchy, span and chain of command

Structure shows how roles and authority are arranged:

  • Levels of hierarchy and chain of command — the line of authority top to bottom.
  • Span of control — how many subordinates one manager oversees (wide = flat, narrow = tall).
  • Delegation — passing authority (not responsibility) down.
  • Centralised (decisions at the top) vs decentralised (authority pushed down).
  • Delayering — removing levels to cut costs and speed decisions.

HL structures: matrix (staff report to both function and project), project-based, and Handy's flexible shamrock organisation.

Leadership and management

Styles of leadership

Management is planning, organising and controlling to hit objectives; leadership is inspiring people to follow a direction. Common styles:

  • Autocratic — leader decides, expects obedience.
  • Paternalistic — leader decides but consults and cares for staff.
  • Democratic — decisions shared through consultation.
  • Laissez-faire — staff given freedom to decide.
  • Situational — style flexes to the task and team.

Motivation theory

Taylor, Maslow and Herzberg

Motivation is the drive to act. Key theories:

  • F. W. Taylor (scientific management) — workers are motivated mainly by money; use time-and-motion study and piece-rate pay.
  • Maslow — a hierarchy of five needs (physiological, safety, social, esteem, self-actualisation); lower needs must be met first.
  • Herzberg (two-factor) — motivators (achievement, recognition, responsibility) drive satisfaction; hygiene factors (pay, conditions, policy) only prevent dissatisfaction.

HL — Adams' equity theory: people compare their input/reward ratio with others; perceived unfairness demotivates.

Organisational culture

Handy's cultures (HL only)

Organisational (corporate) culture is the shared values, norms and 'way we do things here'. Charles Handy identified four types:

  • Power culture — control radiates from one central figure; fast decisions, few rules.
  • Role culture — bureaucratic; clear rules, job titles and hierarchy.
  • Task culture — teams formed around projects; expertise beats status.
  • Person culture — the organisation exists to serve its individual experts.

Culture clash: mergers and acquisitions often fail when two strong, incompatible cultures collide — a key change-management challenge.

Industrial/employee relations

Unions, action and resolution (HL only)

Industrial (employee) relations concern the relationship between employers and employees, often via trade unions and collective bargaining.

  • Employee methods: strikes, work-to-rule, go-slows, overtime bans.
  • Employer methods: changing contracts, threats of redundancy, closure, or a lock-out.
  • Resolution: conciliation, arbitration (a neutral third party decides), employee participation and no-strike agreements.

Good relations reduce disputes, absenteeism and turnover, protecting productivity.
