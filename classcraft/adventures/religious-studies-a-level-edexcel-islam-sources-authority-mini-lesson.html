Mission 1: What was Makkah like before Islam, and how did an orphan become its most trusted man?

Make a guess: What was Makkah like before Islam, and how did an orphan become its most trusted man?

Mission 1

Jahiliyya: the "time of ignorance"

  • Muslims call Arabia before Islam the jahiliyya: the age of ignorance.
  • The Qur'an uses the word for the old ways, e.g. "a judgment of (the days of) ignorance" (Qur'an 5:50, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Ignorance" means ignorance of God's guidance: in worship and in morals.
  • It does not mean no culture: Arabia had trade, law-like customs and brilliant poetry.
  • Muslims see the jahiliyya as the need for revelation: people had lost the religion of Ibrahim.
Mission 1

Religion: gods, jinn and the Ka'bah

  • Polytheism: many tribes kept their own gods. Three goddesses were widely honoured: "Have ye seen Lat. and 'Uzza, And another, the third (goddess), Manat?" (Qur'an 53:19–20, Yusuf Ali)
  • The Ka'bah in Makkah held idols of many tribes; Hubal stood inside. Muslim tradition counts 360 idols around it.
  • Allah was known as a high creator god, but worshipped alongside others (compare Qur'an 29:61).
  • Animism and jinn: spirits were thought to live in stones, trees and desert places; a kahin (soothsayer) claimed contact with them.
  • Many expected no life after death: "nothing but time can destroy us" (Qur'an 45:24, Yusuf Ali).
  • Your spec lists: polytheism, animism and jinns.
Mission 1

Other faiths, festivals and sacrifices

  • Jews lived in Yathrib (later Madinah) and Khaybar.
  • Christians lived in Najran (south), among Arab tribes on the Byzantine border, and across the Red Sea in Abyssinia.
  • Zoroastrian Persia ruled Yemen and influenced eastern Arabia.
  • Hanifs: a few Arabs, such as Zayd ibn Amr, rejected idols and sought the one God of Ibrahim.
  • Festivals: yearly pilgrimage to the Ka'bah, joined to trade fairs such as Ukaz.
  • Sacred months: four months when fighting stopped so pilgrims and traders could travel.
  • Sacrifices: animals were slaughtered at sacred stones in honour of the gods.

Mission 1

Economy: a trading city round a shrine

  • Makkah had little farming. Its wealth came from trade and pilgrimage.
  • The ruling tribe, Quraysh, guarded the Ka'bah and ran caravans between Yemen and Syria.
  • The Qur'an recalls "Their covenants (covering) journeys by winter and summer" (Qur'an 106:2, Yusuf Ali).
  • Pilgrims brought business; idols of many tribes in the Ka'bah brought pilgrims. Religion and money were bound together.
  • New wealth grew unequally: rich merchant clans prospered; orphans, widows and the poor fell behind.
  • Status of Makkah: a haram (sanctuary) where violence was forbidden. Muslims believe Ibrahim and Isma'il built the Ka'bah (Qur'an 2:127).
Mission 1

Society and morals

  • No king or state: tribe (qabila) and clan were everything. Muhammad's clan, Banu Hashim, was part of Quraysh.
  • Your clan protected you; a killing was answered by blood revenge, so feuds could last for generations.
  • Muruwwa (manly honour): courage, loyalty to the tribe, generosity and hospitality.
  • Wrongs the Qur'an condemns: burying baby girls alive, "For what crime she was killed" (Qur'an 81:8–9, Yusuf Ali); hoarding wealth; neglect of orphans.
  • Women's position varied: most had little legal protection, yet Khadijah ran her own trading business.
  • Slavery was common.
  • Your spec: "political and economic factors, social groupings and changes".
Mission 1

Poetry: the voice of the tribe

  • Poetry was Arabia's highest art: it recorded history, praised one's tribe and mocked enemies.
  • Poets competed at fairs such as Ukaz. The finest odes became known as the Mu'allaqat ("the hung poems"; the story that they hung on the Ka'bah is a later legend).
  • Kahins spoke in rhymed prose; poets were thought to be inspired by jinn.
  • So Makkans had words for an inspired speaker: some called Muhammad a poet or soothsayer.
  • The Qur'an replies: "It is not the word of a poet" (Qur'an 69:41, Yusuf Ali).
  • Muslims see the Qur'an's language as beyond any poet's: it challenges hearers to "produce a Sura like thereunto" (Qur'an 2:23, Yusuf Ali).
  • Your spec: "literature, including poetry".

Mission 1

Birth and childhood

  • Birth: c. 570 CE, the "Year of the Elephant", when an army from Yemen with an elephant failed to attack the Ka'bah (Surah 105).
  • Orphan: his father Abdullah died before his birth; his mother Aminah when he was about six; his grandfather Abd al-Muttalib two years later.
  • Desert childhood: as was usual for Makkan families, he was nursed among the Bedouin by Halimah.
  • Uncle Abu Talib raised him and later protected him as clan leader.
  • Bahira: the sources tell of a Christian monk in Syria who saw signs of prophethood in the boy on a caravan journey.
Mission 1

Al-Amin and Khadijah

  • As a young man he was known as al-Amin, "the trustworthy".
  • Hilf al-Fudul (Pact of the Virtuous): he joined clans who swore to defend anyone wronged in Makkah.
  • Khadijah, a wealthy widow merchant, hired him to lead her caravan to Syria, then proposed marriage. He was about 25; tradition gives her age as 40.
  • A loving marriage of about 25 years: he took no other wife while she lived. Fatimah was among their children.
  • The Black Stone (c. 605): when the clans quarrelled over who should set it back in the rebuilt Ka'bah, he placed it on a cloak and had every clan lift it together.
  • In his late 30s he began retreats in the cave of Hira, outside Makkah (see Mission 2).

Mission 1Diversity

How two scholars read the background

  • Karen Armstrong (b. 1944), Muhammad: A Biography of the Prophet (1991). A sympathetic historian, not a Muslim.
  • Armstrong (paraphrase): Makkah's new market wealth broke the old tribal duty to care for the weak, leaving a social and spiritual crisis; Muhammad's message answered it.
  • Martin Lings (1909–2005), Muhammad: His Life Based on the Earliest Sources (1983). A Muslim (Abu Bakr Siraj ad-Din) writing from Sufi tradition.
  • Lings (paraphrase): retells the early Arabic sources (Ibn Ishaq and others) closely, from Ibrahim and the Ka'bah onwards; signs such as Bahira are told as the sources tell them. Makkah is the place God prepared for the final prophet.
  • Contrast: Armstrong explains why the message fitted its time; Lings presents God's providence at work through that time.
  • Your spec names both: Armstrong and Lings.
Mission 1Evaluate

How far did the context shape Muhammad's message?

  • Claim: The pre-Islamic context largely explains Muhammad's life and message.
  • For: The message targets Makkah's real problems: idols, greed, neglect of orphans, infanticide (Armstrong).
  • For: Hanifs, Jews and Christians gave Arabia ideas of one God and of prophets.
  • Against: Muslims believe the message is wahy (revelation) from God, not a product of its setting.
  • Against: Muhammad rejected many features of the context: idols, blood feud, tribal loyalty above faith. And the Makkans who shaped him fought him.
  • Judgement criteria: Can context explain content and also explain rejection? What do the sources allow a historian to say? Is "explaining" the same as "explaining away"?
  • Spec 2.1g: Muslims see Muhammad as final messenger whose message transcends any one context.

Mission 2

The cave of Hira, 610 CE

  • Around the age of 40, Muhammad spent nights in a cave on Mount Hira near Makkah, in prayer and reflection.
  • The sources say this was in the month of Ramadan.
  • Muslims believe the angel Jibril (Gabriel) came to him with the command "Read!" or "Recite!" (Arabic iqra').
  • Muhammad answered that he could not read. Muslims call him ummi, usually understood as "unlettered".
  • The angel pressed him three times, then gave him the first verses of the Qur'an: Surah 96.
  • This is the start of wahy (revelation) and of his prophethood (nubuwwa).
Mission 2Read the source

Surahs 96 and 97

> "Proclaim! (or read!) in the name of thy Lord and Cherisher, Who created- Created man, out of a (mere) clot of congealed blood: Proclaim! And thy Lord is Most Bountiful,- He Who taught (the use of) the pen,- Taught man that which he knew not." (Qur'an 96:1–5, Yusuf Ali)

> "We have indeed revealed this (Message) in the Night of Power: … The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:1–3, Yusuf Ali)

  • Surah 97 describes the night itself: angels and the Spirit come down; it is "Peace!... This until the rise of morn!" (Qur'an 97:5, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims keep it in the last ten nights of Ramadan (often the 27th).

Q1 What do 96:1–5 say about God?

Show answer

Creator of humans; most bountiful; the teacher of knowledge through the pen.

Q2 Why might a Muslim find it significant that an unlettered man is told to "read"?

Show answer

Mission 2

Khadijah and Waraqah

  • Shaken, Muhammad went home and asked Khadijah to cover him with a cloak.
  • Khadijah reassured him (paraphrase): God would never disgrace him, for he kept ties with family, helped the poor, honoured guests and stood by people in trouble.
  • She is honoured as the first person to believe in his message.
  • She took him to her cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal, an old Christian who knew the earlier scriptures.
  • Waraqah said (paraphrase): this was the same angelic messenger (the Namus) who came to Musa; his people would one day drive him out.
  • Significance: a believer outside Makkah's idol worship confirmed the call fitted the pattern of earlier prophets.

Mission 2

Hadith and Sira: two kinds of source

  • Hadith: a short report of what the Prophet said or did. Each has an isnad (chain of named narrators) and a matn (the text).
  • Sahih al-Bukhari: collected by al-Bukhari (810–870), who kept only reports whose chains he judged sound. Arranged by topic; Book 1 is on how revelation began.
  • Sira: a biography, a continuous story in time order, mixing reports, background and poetry.
  • Ibn Ishaq (c. 704–767) wrote the first great Sira; it survives mainly in the edited version of Ibn Hisham (d. c. 833), who cut and annotated it.
  • Sira chains are often combined or incomplete, so Muslim scholars apply looser standards to it than to Sahih hadith.
  • Value: hadith are relied on for belief and law (religious wisdom); Sira supplies order and context (historical knowledge).

Mission 2

A pause, then three years of quiet preaching

  • After the first revelation came a pause (fatra). Then: "O thou wrapped up (in the mantle)! Arise and deliver thy warning!" (Qur'an 74:1–2, Yusuf Ali)
  • For about three years he preached privately to family and friends.
  • First believers: Khadijah; his young cousin Ali; his freed slave Zayd ibn Harithah; his friend Abu Bakr.
  • Who was "first"? Sunni tradition stresses Abu Bakr as the first free adult man; Shi'a tradition stresses Ali as the first male believer.
  • Early converts included poor people and slaves. They met in secret, e.g. at the house of al-Arqam.
Mission 2

Open preaching and the message

  • Command to go public: "And admonish thy nearest kinsmen," (Qur'an 26:214, Yusuf Ali)
  • On Mount Safa he called Quraysh together and warned them. His uncle Abu Lahab cursed him; Surah 111 answers Abu Lahab.
  • The message of the Makkan surahs:
  • Tawhid: one God; idols are powerless.
  • Judgement: resurrection and accountability before God.
  • Justice: care for orphans and the poor; condemnation of greed and infanticide.
  • Prophethood: Muhammad is a messenger and warner, like the prophets before him.
Mission 2

Why Quraysh turned against him

  • Religion: tawhid attacked the gods of the ancestors and the idols in the Ka'bah.
  • Money: no idols, fewer pilgrims, less trade for Makkah.
  • Power: accepting a prophet meant clan leaders accepting the authority of a man from Banu Hashim.
  • Society: slaves and nobles as equal believers threatened the tribal order.
  • Belief: resurrection seemed absurd to many.
  • Leaders such as Abu Jahl led the opposition. First: mockery (poet, soothsayer, madman); then bribes (wealth, kingship), which he refused.
Mission 2

Persecution, c. 613–622

  • Torture of believers without clan protection: Bilal, an Abyssinian slave, was left under a rock in the sun, repeating "One, One" (Ahad).
  • Sumayyah was killed: honoured as the first martyr of Islam.
  • Abyssinia (c. 615): some Muslims fled to the Christian king (the Negus), who protected them.
  • Boycott (c. 616–619): Quraysh refused to trade with or marry into Banu Hashim, who were pressed into a valley in hunger.
  • Year of Sorrow (c. 619): Khadijah and Abu Talib died. The new clan head, Abu Lahab, withdrew protection.
  • Ta'if: he sought support there and was driven out with stones.
  • Then pilgrims from Yathrib accepted Islam and invited him: the road to the Hijrah (see Mission 3).

Mission 2Evaluate

Could Islam have failed in Makkah?

  • Claim: By 619 the Muslim movement in Makkah was close to failure.
  • For: Few converts in ten years; the weakest were tortured; emigration, boycott, and the loss of Khadijah and Abu Talib.
  • For: Without clan protection the Prophet was in real danger; Ta'if refused him.
  • Against: Persecution made the community stronger and more committed; the faith of Bilal and Sumayyah became a model.
  • Against: Muslims believe the mission was guided by God: the Qur'an promised help, and Yathrib's invitation came at the low point.
  • Against: Armstrong stresses the message's appeal to the young and the weak; Lings narrates the sources' signs of God's support.
  • Judgement criteria: Count followers or count commitment? Is "failure" a historian's or a believer's question? What would Makkah have been without the Hijrah?

Mission 3

The Hijrah, 622 CE

  • Quraysh planned to kill him: "They plot and plan, and Allah too plans; but the best of planners is Allah." (Qur'an 8:30, Yusuf Ali)
  • Ali slept in the Prophet's bed to mislead the attackers.
  • With Abu Bakr, he hid three days in the cave of Thawr. When Abu Bakr was afraid, he told him: "Have no fear, for Allah is with us" (Qur'an 9:40, Yusuf Ali).
  • They reached Quba, outside Yathrib, then the city, now called Madinah ("the city" of the Prophet).
  • Escape and guidance: Muslims see it both as flight from persecution and as a move guided by God.
  • Significance: the Islamic calendar counts years from the Hijrah (AH, anno Hegirae), because it began the Muslim community as a society.
  • Before this, pilgrims from Yathrib had met him at Aqabah (621, 622) and pledged to accept Islam and protect him: the Pledges of Aqabah.
  • Your spec: "the significance of the hijrah".
Mission 3

One ummah: the Constitution of Madinah

  • Muhajirun (emigrants from Makkah) arrived with almost nothing; Ansar (helpers: Muslims of Madinah) shared homes and land.
  • The Prophet paired emigrants and helpers as brothers: faith before tribe.
  • The Constitution (Charter) of Madinah: an agreement among the groups of the city:
  • the believers form one ummah;
  • the Jewish tribes belong to the community, keeping their own religion;
  • all defend the city together;
  • disputes go to God and Muhammad.
  • Here the "growth of theocracy" begins: a community ordered by God's revelation through His prophet.
  • Wars and the Jewish tribes: war with Makkah followed (Badr 624, Uhud 625, the Trench 627); relations with the Jewish tribes broke down: the Qaynuqa and Nadir were expelled, and the Qurayzah's men were executed (sources and their reliability are debated).
Mission 3

Leader of the whole community

  • Religious: received revelation, led prayer, taught the Qur'an, set the pattern of worship.
  • Moral: an example of justice, mercy and simplicity (he mended his own clothes; he lived poorly).
  • Political: arbiter, law-giver, treaty-maker; sent envoys and collected zakah.
  • Military: commanded the believers in war. Permission to fight came in Madinah: "To those against whom war is made, permission is given (to fight), because they are wronged" (Qur'an 22:39, Yusuf Ali).
  • W. Montgomery Watt (1909–2006) called him "prophet and statesman": Muhammad: Prophet and Statesman (1961).
  • Lings narrates these roles from the early sources; Armstrong stresses his efforts to end the cycle of violence.
Mission 3

Death and significance

  • Before this: a truce at Hudaybiyyah (628); in 630 Makkah surrendered almost without fighting, former enemies were forgiven and the Ka'bah's idols destroyed; in 632 he led the Farewell Pilgrimage and preached the Last Sermon at Arafat: lives and property are sacred, blood feuds and usury are abolished, and no Arab is above a non-Arab except by piety.
  • He died in Madinah in 632, in the room of his wife Aishah, and was buried there; the site lies within his mosque.
  • Some refused to believe it. Abu Bakr said (paraphrase): whoever worshipped Muhammad, Muhammad is dead; whoever worships God, God is alive. Then he recited:

> "Muhammad is no more than a messenger: many Were the messenger that passed away before him." (Qur'an 3:144, Yusuf Ali)

  • Significance for Muslims today: prophet (final messenger), preacher, leader, statesman, example: "a beautiful pattern (of conduct)" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali).
  • Spec 2.1g: Muslims see him as the final messenger, whose example transcends his 7th-century context.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is Madinah the ideal model?

  • Claim: The Prophet's Madinah is the ideal model for an Islamic society today.
  • For: Led by the Prophet himself; a charter uniting different groups; justice, care for the poor, worship at the centre.
  • For: Many Muslims, Sunni and Shi'a, look to it as the pattern for any just community.
  • Against: A small 7th-century city-state under a living prophet: its conditions cannot be repeated (no prophet now).
  • Against: Wars and the fate of the Jewish tribes make some ask whether every policy is a model, or only its principles.
  • Against: Some modern Muslims take principles (consultation, justice, protection of minorities) rather than the structure.
  • Judgement criteria: Model in structure or in values? Who interprets it? Does "ideal" mean "unrepeatable"?

Mission 4

Three words: Sunnah, Hadith, Sira

  • Sunnah ("trodden path", custom): the Prophet's example: what he said, did, and silently approved.
  • Hadith ("report", plural ahadith): a single report of one saying or action. Hadiths are how the Sunnah is known.
  • Sira ("way of going", biography): the story of the Prophet's life, told in order.
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as God's word; the Sunnah as the Prophet's lived explanation of it.
Mission 4Read the source

Why follow the Prophet's example?

> "Ye have indeed in the Messenger of Allah a beautiful pattern (of conduct) for any one whose hope is in Allah and the Final Day" (Qur'an 33:21, Yusuf Ali)

> "He who obeys the Messenger, obeys Allah" (Qur'an 4:80, Yusuf Ali)

  • The Qur'an tells the Prophet to "explain clearly to men" what was revealed (16:44, paraphrase): the Sunnah is that explanation.

Mission 4

Anatomy of a hadith

  • Every hadith has two parts.
  • Isnad (chain, "support"): the names of those who passed the report on, back to the Prophet: "A told me that B told him that C heard the Prophet say…".
  • Matn (text): the words or action reported.
  • Scholars test both: is every link in the chain known, trustworthy and able to have met the next? Does the text fit the Qur'an and well-established Sunnah?
  • The study of narrators ('ilm al-rijal, "knowledge of the men") produced huge biographical dictionaries.
  • Hadith qudsi ("sacred report"): God's words in the Prophet's phrasing. It is not Qur'an: it is not recited in salah as Qur'an.

Mission 4

Grading a hadith

  • Sahih (sound): unbroken chain of trustworthy, accurate narrators; no hidden defect; not contradicting stronger reports.
  • Hasan (good): as sahih, but a narrator's memory is slightly less exact. Still used in law.
  • Da'if (weak): a break in the chain or an unreliable narrator. Many allow it only for encouraging good deeds, not for law.
  • Mawdu' (fabricated): invented. Rejected.
  • By number of chains: mutawatir (mass-transmitted, by so many that error is ruled out) and ahad (single or few chains: most hadiths).
Mission 4

From memory to collections

  • 632 CE: the Prophet dies. Companions remember and teach his words; some write notes.
  • 8th century: reports are gathered; fabrication (for politics, sects, piety) makes scholars insist on chains.
  • 9th century: the great Sunni collections. Muhammad al-Bukhari (810–870) chose only reports he judged sound: Sahih al-Bukhari. With Sahih Muslim (Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj, d. 875) it is the most trusted Sunni book after the Qur'an.
  • Sunni Six Books add Abu Dawud, al-Tirmidhi, al-Nasa'i and Ibn Majah.
  • Shi'a Islam accepts reports from the Prophet and from the Imams of his family, mainly through narrators loyal to them. Its Four Books begin with al-Kafi of al-Kulayni (d. 941).
  • Compilation gives authority: Muslims trust a hadith because of how it was tested, not because it is written down.

Mission 4

Sira: the life told as a story

  • Ibn Ishaq (c. 704–767) wrote the first full life of the Prophet. It survives mainly in the edition of Ibn Hisham (d. c. 833), who shortened it and cut material he judged unsuitable.
  • Composition: one continuous narrative, in order of events, with poetry, speeches and accounts of the campaigns (maghazi).
  • Transmission: chains are often given, but looser: several reports may be blended into one account; some passages have no chain or say "it is alleged".
  • Hadith, by contrast: separate short reports, each with its own chain, arranged by topic (prayer, fasting, trade) for law and practice.
  • Value: Sira gives the historical context of the revelation; Hadith gives tested guidance for religious practice. Muslims use both.
Mission 4

The Sunnah in daily life and law

  • The Qur'an commands salah; the Sunnah shows how: times, number of units, words, movements. Likewise zakah rates and Hajj rites.
  • So most Muslims treat the Qur'an and Sunnah as one guidance: the Qur'an supreme, the Sunnah explaining and applying it.
  • Everyday Sunnah: eating with the right hand, greeting with "salam", the call to prayer at a baby's birth, kindness to neighbours.
  • Al-Shafi'i (767–820), al-Risala: the "wisdom" taught alongside the Book (2:151) is the Sunnah; a sound hadith binds, even if it has a single chain. This made the Sunnah the second source of law.
  • Implications for living: Muslims imitate the Prophet in small acts as well as large, as a way of loving and following him (3:31).
Mission 4Diversity

How much can hadiths be trusted?

  • Mainstream Sunni: hadiths graded sahih are authoritative; the Sunnah is revelation in meaning (53:3–4 is often cited).
  • Shi'a: hadiths are trusted when they come through reliable narrators, especially from the Imams; many Sunni-transmitted reports are treated with caution.
  • Qur'an-only Muslims (Qur'aniyyun), e.g. Ghulam Ahmad Parwez (1903–1985) in Pakistan: hadiths are human records and not binding; the Qur'an is enough. A small minority.
  • Western sceptics: Ignaz Goldziher (Muslim Studies, vol. 2, 1890) saw many hadiths as products of later debates; Joseph Schacht (The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence, 1950) argued that chains "grew backwards" to earlier authorities.
  • Replies: Harald Motzki (1948–2019) dated some legal material to the 7th century through study of early collections; Jonathan A. C. Brown (Hadith, 2009) shows that Muslim critics examined texts, not just chains.
  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (Ideals and Realities of Islam, 1966) argues that rejecting the Hadith would cut the Qur'an off from how it was first lived (paraphrase).
Mission 4Evaluate

"Without the Hadith, Muslims could not live by the Qur'an."

  • Reasons for: the Qur'an gives commands without detail (salah, zakah); 16:44 and 4:80 give the Prophet's explanation authority; 14 centuries of law and worship depend on it.
  • Reasons against: the Qur'an calls itself clear and complete (Qur'an-only Muslims cite 6:38 and 16:89); some practice is known by continuous community practice (the Maliki stress on the practice of Madinah), not single reports; weak and fabricated hadiths have misled people.
  • Middle view: the Qur'an needs the Sunnah, but the Sunnah can be known through practice and mass transmission, so not every single hadith carries equal weight.
  • Judge it by: Could salah be performed from the Qur'an alone? Is the Sunnah the same as the written Hadith? How reliable is the grading system? Who has authority to decide?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jahiliyya, "ignorance" of God's guidance: polytheism round the Ka'bah, jinn and kahins, tribal feuds, infanticide; but also trade, sacred months and great poetry.
  • Muhammad: born c. 570, orphaned, raised by Abu Talib, known as al-Amin; married Khadijah; retreated to Hira.
  • Armstrong reads the message as answering a social crisis; Lings retells the early sources as providence.

Mission 2

  • 610 CE, Mount Hira, Ramadan: Jibril, "Read!", Qur'an 96:1–5; Surah 97 names the Night of Power. Khadijah believed; Waraqah linked the call to Musa.
  • Quiet preaching (Khadijah, Ali, Zayd, Abu Bakr), then open preaching of tawhid, judgement and justice; Quraysh opposed it for religious, economic and political reasons; persecution, boycott, the Year of Sorrow.
  • Bukhari's hadith (topic-arranged, tested chains) and Ibn Hisham's Sira (continuous life story) tell the first revelation differently.

Mission 3

  • 622: the Hijrah from persecution to Madinah (8:30, 9:40), guided by God; year 1 of the Islamic calendar.
  • In Madinah: the first masjid, the brotherhood of Muhajirun and Ansar, the Constitution; Muhammad as religious, moral, political and military leader; Badr, Uhud, the Trench.
  • 628 Hudaybiyyah; 630 return to Makkah with amnesty; 632 Last Sermon and death (3:144); his example (33:21).

Mission 4

  • Sunnah is the Prophet's example; Hadith are the reports that carry it, each with an isnad (chain) and matn (text); Sira is his biography.
  • Scholars grade hadiths (sahih, hasan, da'if, mawdu'); al-Bukhari and Muslim compiled the most trusted Sunni collections; Shi'a Muslims have their own Four Books.
