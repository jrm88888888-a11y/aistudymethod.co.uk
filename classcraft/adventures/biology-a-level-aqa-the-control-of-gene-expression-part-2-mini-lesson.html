Mission 1: How can a speck of DNA from one hair be copied into billions and then sorted by size?

Make a guess: How can a speck of DNA from one hair be copied into billions and then sorted by size?

Mission 1

PCR: copy one stretch of DNA

  • PCR (polymerase chain reaction) copies one chosen stretch of DNA, in a tube.
  • It needs:
    • the template DNA sample
    • two primers: short single strands of DNA
    • free DNA nucleotides
    • a heat-stable DNA polymerase
  • The usual enzyme is Taq polymerase.
  • The cycle repeats, about 25–35 times. Each cycle doubles the target.
  • PCR is an in vitro method of amplifying DNA. Cloning DNA inside host cells (in vivo) is in Mission 2.
Mission 1

One cycle, three steps

  • 1 Denature, about 95 °C. Hydrogen bonds between bases break. The strands separate.
  • 2 Anneal, about 55 °C. Primers bind by complementary base pairing to each end of the target, one on each strand.
  • 3 Extend, about 72 °C. Taq polymerase adds nucleotides to each primer, building new complementary strands.
  • The new strands become templates in the next cycle.
  • Say: "heat breaks the hydrogen bonds between strands" — not: "heat breaks the DNA into nucleotides".
Mission 1

Why primers? Why a heat-stable enzyme?

  • DNA polymerase can only add to an existing strand. Primers give it a start.
  • The two primers sit either side of the target. Only the DNA between them is amplified.
  • Primers also stop the separated strands re-pairing at once.
  • Taq polymerase comes from Thermus aquaticus, a bacterium of hot springs.
  • It is not denatured at 95 °C, so it works through every cycle. A human DNA polymerase would be denatured in the first cycle.
  • 72 °C is close to Taq's optimum temperature.
  • Say: "primers anneal to complementary sequences" — not: "primers copy the DNA" or "primers separate the strands".
Quick check
?Why is Taq polymerase, rather than a DNA polymerase from human cells, used in the polymerase chain reaction?
Mission 1

Worked example: how many copies?

  • Every molecule is copied in every cycle, so the number doubles each cycle.
  • Copies after n cycles = starting number × 2ⁿ.
  • Example: 1 molecule, 30 cycles.
    • 1 × 2³⁰ = 1 073 741 824
    • About 1.1 × 10⁹ copies: over a billion.
  • Example: 5 molecules, 3 cycles: 5 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 5 × 2³ = 40.
  • This assumes every strand is copied each cycle. In a real tube, copying slows in later cycles as primers and nucleotides are used up.
  • Say: "multiply by 2ⁿ" — not: "multiply by 2 × n".
Quick check
?A PCR reaction starts with 3 double-stranded DNA molecules of the target section. How many double-stranded molecules of the target are present after 4 complete cycles?
Mission 1

Gel electrophoresis: sorting by length

  • DNA samples go into wells at one end of a gel. A voltage is applied across the gel.
  • DNA is negatively charged (its phosphate groups). Every fragment moves towards the positive electrode.
  • The gel is a mesh. Shorter fragments slip through more easily and travel further.
  • Fragments of the same length travel the same distance: they form one band.
  • A ladder of fragments of known length runs in one lane, for comparison.
  • Say: "shorter fragments travel further" — not: "longer fragments travel further because they carry more charge".
Quick check
?Three DNA fragments of 200, 500 and 1200 base pairs are loaded into the same well of a gel and a voltage is applied. Which statement describes the result?
Mission 1Practical

Running a DNA gel

  • AT g: for example, test the specificity of restriction enzymes on extracted DNA, then run a gel.
  • Method:
    • Cast an agarose gel with a comb; remove the comb to leave wells.
    • Cover the gel with buffer. Wells at the negative end.
    • Load each sample with a dense loading dye. Load a ladder in one lane.
    • Run at a set voltage until the dye nears the far end. Stain to show the bands.
  • Keep the same for every lane: gel, voltage, run time. The ladder is the reference.
  • Measure each band's distance from its well. Compare with the ladder to estimate length.

Mission 1

What PCR and gels are used for

  • Forensics: amplify DNA from a hair, a drop of blood or skin cells, then compare it (see Mission 4).
  • Paternity and relationships: compare amplified regions between people, animals or plants.
  • Diagnosing infections: detect a pathogen's DNA or RNA, even at low levels.
  • Genetic testing: amplify a gene, then test it for a particular allele.
  • Ancient and degraded DNA: amplify the little that survives.
  • Before sequencing: make enough copies of a gene to read its base sequence.
Mission check

?The human ACE gene has two common alleles. Allele I contains an extra stretch of about 300 base pairs that allele D lacks. One pair of primers on either side of this stretch gives a 490 base pair product from allele I and a 190 base pair product from allele D. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, one person's lane shows two bands. Which interpretation is correct?

Primers pick the stretch to copy; the gel sorts the copies by length. Together they turn a trace of DNA into a genotype.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Make a guess: How can a bacterium be made to produce a human hormone it has never made before?

Mission 2

One gene, a new host

  • Recombinant DNA: DNA joined from two sources, e.g. a human gene inside a bacterial plasmid.
  • An organism carrying a gene from another species is transgenic: a genetically modified organism (GMO).
  • The genetic code is universal. Transcription and translation work the same way in all cells.
  • So the host reads the human gene and makes the same polypeptide.
  • Five stages: get the gene → put it in a vector → transform host cells → find the transformed cells → grow them.
Mission 2

Get the gene

  • Reverse transcriptase: make DNA from the gene's mRNA. This DNA is cDNA (complementary DNA).
    • Cells making the protein are full of its mRNA.
    • mRNA has had its introns removed, so cDNA has no introns. Bacteria cannot remove introns.
  • Restriction enzymes cut the gene out of a cell's DNA (next screen).
  • Gene machine: build the gene nucleotide by nucleotide from a known sequence. No introns.
  • Add a promoter (where RNA polymerase binds) and a terminator (where transcription stops), so the gene is expressed.
Mission 2

Cutting: restriction enzymes and sticky ends

  • Restriction enzymes (restriction endonucleases) cut DNA at one specific base sequence: the recognition site.
  • Each enzyme's active site is complementary to its own sequence. EcoRI cuts at GAATTC.
  • Many sites read the same on both strands, 5′ to 3′: they are palindromic.
  • A staggered cut leaves sticky ends: short single-stranded overhangs, here AATT.
  • Cut the gene and the plasmid with the same enzyme: their sticky ends are complementary.
  • Say: "complementary sticky ends" — not: "matching" or "identical sticky ends".
Quick check
?A human gene and a plasmid are both cut with EcoRI, then mixed. Which statement describes how the recombinant plasmid forms?
Mission 2

Into a vector, into a host

  • A vector carries the gene into the host: usually a plasmid.
  • DNA ligase joins the gene into the cut plasmid: phosphodiester bonds in the backbone. Result: a recombinant plasmid.
  • Transformation: host cells take up the plasmid.
    • Calcium ions plus a brief heat shock make bacterial membranes more permeable.
  • Only a small fraction of cells take up a plasmid.
Mission 2

Marker genes: which cells took it up?

  • The plasmid also carries a marker gene.
  • Antibiotic resistance marker: spread the cells on agar with the antibiotic. Only cells with the plasmid grow.
  • Fluorescent marker (e.g. the jellyfish GFP gene): transformed cells glow under UV or blue light.
  • If the gene is inserted inside a second marker gene, that marker stops working. Cells with the recombinant plasmid lose that marker: an easy test.
  • You will not need to recall named marker genes.

Mission 2

Grow them: one cell becomes a factory

  • Transformed cells divide. Every new cell copies the plasmid and the gene.
  • This is in vivo amplification. PCR (see Mission 1) is in vitro.
  • PCR: fast, needs no cells, makes DNA only. In vivo: slower, but the cells also express the gene and make the protein.
  • Human insulin: DNA coding for human insulin (no introns) put into E. coli; grown in large fermenters; insulin extracted and purified. Approved for use in 1982.
Mission 2

Why the gene still works

  • The bacterium's RNA polymerase transcribes the human gene. Its ribosomes translate the mRNA.
  • Each codon codes for the same amino acid as in a human cell.
  • So the amino acid sequence, and the protein, are the same.
  • This is why cDNA is used: bacteria cannot remove introns, so a gene with introns would give the wrong polypeptide.
  • Say: "the genetic code is universal" — not: "all organisms have the same genes".
Mission check

?In 1986, researchers inserted the firefly luciferase gene, with a promoter that works in plant cells, into tobacco cells and grew whole plants from them. When supplied with luciferin, the plants glowed. Why could tobacco cells make active firefly luciferase?

Bacterium, plant or animal: any cell can read a gene from any species. That is what makes recombinant DNA possible.

Mission 3

Mission 3

GM microorganisms

  • Bacteria and yeasts grow fast in fermenters. Many medicines are made this way:
    • insulin and human growth hormone from GM bacteria
    • the hepatitis B vaccine protein from GM yeast (since 1986)
  • Industry and food: e.g. chymosin for cheese-making from GM microorganisms.
Mission 3

GM plants

  • Insect resistance: a gene from the bacterium Bacillus thuringiensis makes a Bt toxin that kills certain caterpillars.
  • Herbicide tolerance: e.g. GM soya survives a herbicide that kills weeds.
  • Nutrition: Golden Rice makes β-carotene (the body converts it to vitamin A) in its grain. Aimed at vitamin A deficiency.
Mission 3

Transgenic animals

  • Pharming: animals that make a human protein in their milk.
    • Example: human antithrombin (stops blood clotting) from GM goats' milk; approved 2006 (EU) and 2009 (US).
    • The gene is joined to the promoter of a milk-protein gene and put into an early embryo.
    • So every cell carries the gene, but only mammary gland cells express it.
  • Why not bacteria? Some human proteins need changes after translation, such as added sugar groups. Bacteria cannot make them.
Quick check
?Why are some human proteins for medicines made in transgenic animals or plants rather than in GM bacteria?
Mission 3

Risks, and how they are reduced

  • Documented risks:
    • Resistance: pests exposed to Bt toxin year after year can evolve resistance. Pink bollworm in India survives some Bt cotton.
    • Gene flow: a GM herbicide-tolerant grass escaped from field trials and spread in the wild (USA, 2000s).
    • Escape of GM microorganisms from a laboratory or factory.
  • Safety precautions:
    • Use host strains that survive poorly in the human body (e.g. lab strains of E. coli).
    • "Suicide genes": cells die outside the conditions they are given.
    • Containment: sealed fermenters; restricted labs; separation distances for field trials.
  • Products are tested before approval; rules differ between countries.
Quick check
?Bt cotton made a toxin that killed pink bollworm caterpillars. After several years of growing it in parts of India, many caterpillars survived it. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 3

Weighing the arguments

  • Arguments for:
    • Humanitarian: more food; fewer deficiency diseases; safer medicines in larger supply.
    • Less insecticide sprayed on insect-resistant crops.
  • Arguments against or concerns:
    • Environmental: resistance in pests and weeds; gene flow; effects on other species.
    • Ownership: GM seeds and genes are patented. Farmers may have to buy new seed each season. Large companies control supply.
    • Anti-globalisation concern: power over farming moves to a few multinational companies.
    • Animal welfare: in pharming and in disease-model animals.
  • The same tools (vectors carrying a working gene) are used in gene therapy in humans: Mission 5.
  • Exam answers: give both sides, then weigh them using the evidence in the question.
Mission check

?A company plans to make a human enzyme in the egg white of GM hens. The human gene is joined to the promoter of a gene for an egg-white protein and inserted into early embryos. The enzyme is found in egg white but not in the hens' blood or muscle. Why?

One inserted gene, one chosen tissue: that control is what makes pharming work. Whether to use it is a separate question, answered by weighing evidence and values.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If almost all human DNA is shared, how can a trace of it point to just one person?

Make a guess: If almost all human DNA is shared, how can a trace of it point to just one person?

Mission 4

Repeats that vary between people

  • Much of the genome does not code for proteins. Some of it is tandem repeats: a short sequence repeated end to end, e.g. GATA GATA GATA.
  • These are VNTRs: variable number tandem repeats.
  • The number of repeats at a locus varies between people. More repeats: a longer fragment.
  • You inherit one allele at each locus from each parent: one or two bands per locus.
  • Many loci, many alleles at each: the chance that two people share all of them is very low. Identical twins are the exception.
Mission 4

Making a profile

  • The method is called genetic fingerprinting.
  • 1 Extract DNA from the sample.
  • 2 Amplify several repeat loci by PCR. Primers bind either side of each repeat.
  • 3 Separate the fragments by length: electrophoresis.
  • 4 Detect the bands: labelled primers or labelled probes make them visible. Compare samples.
  • Classic route: restriction endonucleases cut the DNA either side of the repeats; the fragments are separated; labelled DNA probes bind the repeats by complementary base pairing and show where they are.
Quick check
?Two people have different alleles at one short tandem repeat locus. After PCR and gel electrophoresis, their bands for this locus are in different places. What difference between the alleles does the gel detect?
Mission 4

Worked example: why test many loci?

  • Illustrative: suppose 1 in 10 unrelated people share a profile at one locus by chance.
  • Loci on different chromosomes are inherited independently. Multiply the chances.
  • 2 loci: 1/10 × 1/10 = 1 in 100.
  • 10 loci: (1/10)¹⁰ = 1 in 10¹⁰: about one person in 10 000 000 000.
  • Real casework: the UK system tests 16 repeat loci; the US core set has 20.
  • Relatives share more alleles, so their chance of matching is higher.
  • Say: "the chance of a coincidental match is very low" — not: "the profile proves the suspect did it".
Quick check
?A forensic laboratory compares a sample from a crime scene with a suspect's DNA using 20 different markers rather than one. Why does this make the result more reliable?
Mission 4

Reading a profile

  • Forensic match: the sample could come from a person only if every band matches.
  • One band different: that person is excluded.
  • Paternity: each band in a child came from the mother or the father.
    • Cross out the child's bands that match the mother.
    • Every band left must be in the father's profile.
    • The father has other bands the child did not inherit: that is expected.
  • Matching bands are consistent with a relationship. Different bands exclude one.
Quick check
?A DNA profile was prepared for a mother, her child and a man who may be the father. The fragment lengths (in base pairs) in each profile were: mother 100, 220, 260, 340; child 100, 150, 220, 300; man 150, 180, 300, 330. What can be concluded?
Mission 4

Uses: identity and relationships

  • Forensics: link a sample from a scene to a person, or exclude a suspect.
  • Paternity and family relationships; identifying human remains.
  • Animals and plants: confirm the parents of a pedigree animal; identify a crop variety; trace the source of illegally traded wildlife.
  • Breeding: choose unrelated parents to avoid inbreeding; check claimed pedigrees.
Mission 4

Uses: health and populations

  • Some repeats cause disease when they are too long. In Huntington's disease, a CAG repeat in one gene is measured by PCR and electrophoresis:
    • 26 or fewer repeats: normal; 40 or more: the disease develops.
  • Markers close to a disease allele are inherited with it: they help estimate disease risk in a family.
  • Genetic variability of a population: more different bands across individuals means more alleles: higher genetic diversity.
  • Conservation and breeding programmes use this to keep diversity high.
Mission check

Mission 5

Mission 5: If a gene is faulty, can we find it before birth, or even fix it?

Make a guess: If a gene is faulty, can we find it before birth, or even fix it?

Mission 5

DNA probes find one allele

  • A DNA probe: a short single strand of DNA with a known base sequence.
  • It is complementary to the allele being looked for.
  • It carries a label: fluorescent or radioactive.
  • Steps:
    • Extract the patient's DNA; heat it so the strands separate.
    • Add the probe. It base-pairs only with its complementary sequence: DNA hybridisation.
    • Wash off unbound probe. Detect the label: label present means allele present.
  • The DNA can come from an adult, an embryo or a foetus, so an allele can be found before birth.
  • Say: "the probe is complementary to the allele" — not: "the probe is the same as the allele".
Quick check
?Before a labelled DNA probe is added, the patient's DNA is heated. Why?
Mission 5

Why screen?

  • Genetic screening tests people for particular alleles.
  • Heritable conditions: e.g. alleles for cystic fibrosis.
  • Carriers: people with one recessive allele and no symptoms, who could pass it on.
  • Health risks: e.g. BRCA1 and BRCA2 alleles raise the risk of breast and ovarian cancer.
  • Drug responses: alleles that change how a drug works, so the dose or drug can be chosen (personalised medicine).
  • Genetic counselling: a trained counsellor explains results, risks for future children, and the options. The decisions stay with the person.
Quick check
?Two carriers of a cystic fibrosis allele already have one child with cystic fibrosis. At genetic counselling they ask about a second pregnancy. What is the chance that the next child has cystic fibrosis?
Mission 5

Weighing screening

  • Possible benefits:
    • Early diagnosis and treatment; time to prepare.
    • Informed choices about having children.
    • Safer prescribing when drug responses are known.
  • Concerns:
    • Miscarriage risk from CVS and amniocentesis; false positive and false negative results.
    • Decisions about ending a pregnancy, or discarding embryos after PGD.
    • Knowing about an untreatable, late-onset condition such as Huntington's disease may cause distress.
    • Privacy: could insurers or employers use the results?
    • Fear that selection could extend to non-medical traits.
  • Present several viewpoints; weigh them with the evidence given.
Mission 5

Gene therapy: somatic or germ line?

  • Gene therapy: put a functional copy of a gene into cells to make up for a faulty allele.
  • It uses recombinant DNA tools: a vector carries the functional allele into cells (see Mission 2).
  • Vectors: harmless modified viruses, or liposomes (lipid spheres).
  • Somatic cell gene therapy: body cells only, e.g. liver, airway or blood stem cells.
    • Not inherited. May need repeating as treated cells die.
  • Germ line gene therapy: an egg, sperm or early embryo.
    • Every cell has the allele, and so do the person's descendants.
    • Not allowed for treatment in the UK and many other countries.
Mission 5

Has it worked?

  • Haemophilia B: a viral vector carries the clotting factor IX gene into liver cells. One dose raised factor IX for years. Approved 2022.
  • Cystic fibrosis: the gene was inhaled in liposomes. Airway lining cells are replaced, so doses had to be repeated: monthly for a year gave a modest benefit (2015).
  • Advantages: treats the cause, not just symptoms; one dose may last years.
  • Disadvantages: immune reactions to the vector; effects may fade; a vector inserting next to another gene has caused cancer (2002); very high cost.
Quick check
?A patient's blood stem cells receive a working allele by somatic gene therapy. Will the patient's children inherit the working allele?
Mission check

?One form of inherited blindness is caused by two recessive faulty alleles of a gene needed by cells at the back of the eye. A single injection into each eye of a viral vector carrying a working allele has restored some sight, with effects lasting years (approved 2017). Which statement about this treatment is correct?

Screening finds faulty alleles; somatic gene therapy can add a working copy to body cells. Neither changes what the next generation inherits.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • PCR repeats denature, anneal and extend; primers mark the target and each cycle doubles it (starting number × 2ⁿ).
  • Taq polymerase survives 95 °C; annealing about 55 °C; extension about 72 °C.
  • Gel electrophoresis: DNA moves towards the positive electrode; shorter fragments travel further.

Mission 2

  • A gene from one species is expressed in another because the genetic code, transcription and translation are universal.
  • Get the gene, insert it into a vector, transform host cells, select them with marker genes, then grow them.

Mission 3

  • GM microorganisms, plants and animals make medicines, food traits and research models; tissue-specific promoters target where a gene is expressed.
  • Risks (resistance, gene flow, escape) are documented and reduced by safety precautions; decisions weigh benefits against environmental, ownership and welfare concerns.

Mission 4

  • Repeat loci vary in length between individuals; PCR copies them and a gel sorts them by length.
  • Chances at independent loci multiply, so more loci make a chance match less likely.
  • One band that cannot be explained excludes; matching bands are consistent with, not proof of, identity or relationship.

Mission 5

  • Labelled DNA probes hybridise only with complementary sequences, so they reveal specific alleles; microarrays use thousands at once.
  • Screening identifies carriers, affected embryos or foetuses and drug responses; it informs choices and raises ethical questions.
  • Somatic gene therapy treats body cells and is not inherited; germ line therapy would change every cell and future generations.
