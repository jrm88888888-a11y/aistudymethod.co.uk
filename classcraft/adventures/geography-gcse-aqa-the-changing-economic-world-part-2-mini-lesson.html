Mission 1

Mission 1: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Make a guess: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.2 causes of UK economic change: deindustrialisation, globalisation, government policies
Mission 1

The Motor City empties

  • Detroit, USA, the home of the American car industry, had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020.
  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963.
  • Factories closed or moved out; people who could, often the better-off, moved to the suburbs and beyond.
  • Jobs left the city, and people followed.
Mission 1

Deindustrialisation: two big causes

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs in a city or country.
  • Globalisation: countries trade more freely, so firms can make goods where wages and costs are lower (the global shift). UK factories faced cheaper imports.
  • Technology: machines and robots (automation) do work people once did, so factories need fewer workers.
Mission 1

More causes

  • Decentralisation: firms leave crowded, costly inner-city sites for cheaper, bigger sites at the edge or in other regions.
  • Transport: bigger ships, containers and lorries favour new ports and motorway sites over old inner-city docks and rail yards.
  • Government policies can speed decline (ending support for loss-making industries, opening markets to imports) or slow it (grants to firms, regeneration schemes).
Mission 1

The UK picture

  • Manufacturing's share of UK jobs fell from an average of 25% between the world wars to an average of 9.5% in 2000–2016.
  • ONS: manufacturing employment "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • By 2016, services (the tertiary sector) gave 83.6% of UK jobs.
Quick check
?A car firm closes its UK factory and builds the same model in a country where wages are lower. Which cause of deindustrialisation is this?
Mission 1

Four stages of urban change

  • Urbanisation: people move into the city; it grows.
  • Suburbanisation: people move from the inner city to the suburbs at its edge.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the city to small towns and villages.
  • Re-urbanisation: people move back into the inner city.
Mission 1

Deindustrialisation's impact on London

  • Jobs: manufacturing jobs fell nationally from the 1960s, including in London's factories and docks.
  • People: as factory and dock jobs went, more people left; Greater London's population kept falling until 1981. (The fall began in 1939, so war and planned moves to new towns played a part too.)
  • Deprivation: England's 2025 deprivation index still finds concentrations in areas with a history of heavy industry and in "parts of east London".
  • Land: closed docks and factories left derelict land, later reused for offices and housing (re-urbanisation).
Quick check
?Which is an impact of deindustrialisation on a city, rather than a cause?
Mission check

?Greater London lost about 2 million residents between 1939 and 1981, then gained about 2.2 million by 2021. What best explains the loss?

In Detroit, as in London, jobs left the city (deindustrialisation) and people followed them outwards (suburbanisation and beyond). Detroit lost about 65% of its 1950 population by 2020; London lost about 23% by 1981, then grew again as it re-urbanised.

Mission 2

Mission 2: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Make a guess: In 1978 a quarter of UK jobs were in manufacturing. Today it is about one in fifteen. What replaced them?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.2 moving towards a post-industrial economy: IT, services, finance, research, science and business parks
  • 3.2.2 impacts of industry on the physical environment; an example of more environmentally sustainable modern industrial development
Mission 2

From making things to services

  • UK workforce jobs in manufacturing (June each year):
  • 1978: 6.73 million of 26.99 million = 24.9% (our calculation)
  • 2001: 3.83 million of 30.14 million = 12.7%
  • 2026: 2.46 million of 36.73 million = 6.7%
  • Services rose to 84.8% of all jobs in 2026 (31.16 million).
  • This is deindustrialisation: the decline of the traditional industrial base.
Mission 2

A post-industrial economy

  • A post-industrial economy earns most of its income from services and knowledge, not factories.
  • Tertiary jobs provide a service (shops, health, transport); quaternary jobs are based on knowledge: research, IT, consultancy.
  • Professional, scientific and technical jobs: 1.85 million (2001) → 3.50 million (2026), up 90% (our calculation).
  • Information and communication (IT, media, telecoms): 1.21 million → 1.61 million.
  • Finance: UK financial services exports were worth £98.7 billion in 2025.
Mission 2

Research, science and business parks

  • Science park: offices and labs for research firms, often next to a university.
  • Cambridge Science Park: in 1970 Trinity College "turned farmland outside the city into the UK's very first science park"; it covers 152 acres with over 7,000 members.
  • Business park: offices on the edge of a town, near motorways and airports, with parking and space to expand.
Quick check
?Which job is in the quaternary sector?
Mission 2

Why the shift happened

  • Manufacturing employment "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • Globalisation: many goods are now made more cheaply abroad and imported.
  • Technology: machines do more of the work in the factories that remain.
  • Rising incomes: people spend more on services such as health, travel and finance; tertiary jobs reached 83.6% of employment by 2016.
Mission 2

Industry and the physical environment

  • Air: industry produced 11% of UK greenhouse gas emissions in 2025.
  • Since 1990, UK emissions fell 54%, helped by a "decline in the relative importance of energy intensive industries".
  • Land and water: quarries and mines leave scars; old works can leave contaminated land and polluted water.
  • Resources: factories use large amounts of energy, water and raw materials.
Mission 2

A more sustainable modern industry: Hull

  • Siemens Gamesa opened an offshore wind-blade factory in Hull in 2016.
  • In 2021 it planned a £186 million expansion, creating and safeguarding up to 1,080 direct jobs, to make blades longer than 100 m.
  • Its products supply offshore wind farms "providing clean power across the UK".
  • More sustainable: it makes low-carbon energy equipment, brings skilled jobs to a former port-industry region, and its wind farms cut the need for fossil fuel power.
Quick check
?Why is the Hull wind-blade factory described as more environmentally sustainable than older heavy industry?
Mission check

?A northern town loses its steelworks. Ten years later most new jobs are in a hospital, a call centre and a software firm. Which description fits best?

In the UK, manufacturing fell from 24.9% of jobs in 1978 to 6.7% in 2026, and services rose to 84.8%. The shift brought high-value research and finance jobs, lower industrial emissions, but also job losses and regional inequality.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Make a guess: Why are some UK villages growing fast while others are losing people?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.2 social and economic rural change in one UK area of population growth and one of decline
Mission 3

Rural England in numbers

  • In 2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements; 27% of them were aged 65+.
  • Farming makes up 14% of rural businesses but only 8% of rural employment.
  • Rural people travel further, mostly by car, and have poorer access to services.
Mission 3

Two kinds of rural change

  • Growth near cities: people move out of towns into villages (counter-urbanisation); towns spread outwards (suburbanisation).
  • Decline in remote areas: more people leave or die than arrive or are born (rural depopulation).
  • In England, people on average move into rural areas, except 15–19-year-olds, who move out.
Mission 3

Growth: South Cambridgeshire

  • A rural district around the city of Cambridge.
  • Population: 148,800 (2011) → 162,000 (2021), up 8.9%; England grew 6.6%.
  • People aged 65+ rose by 28.7%; children under 15 by 8.5%.
Mission 3

Northstowe: a new town in the fields

  • Northstowe, South Cambridgeshire, is planned to have 10,000 homes and about 25,000 people.
  • New schools, roads, public transport routes, cycleways and green space are part of the plan.
  • Social change: new families and commuters; pressure on services until new ones are built.
  • Economic change: building jobs; more customers for local shops.
Quick check
?A family moves from Cambridge to a nearby village and the parents still work in the city. What process is this?
Mission 3

Decline: the Outer Hebrides

  • Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides): islands off north-west Scotland.
  • Population fell 5.5% between the 2011 and 2022 censuses: the biggest fall of any Scottish council area.
  • It has Scotland's lowest population density.
  • In Scotland's shrinking areas, mostly in the west and south-west, more people died than were born.
Mission 3

What changes when people leave?

  • 13.7% of people are aged 55+ and living alone: the highest share in Scotland.
  • Only 19.7% of households have dependent children, among the lowest in Scotland.
  • Social change: an ageing population; fewer children for schools; more need for care.
  • Economic change: fewer workers and customers, so shops, buses and services are harder to keep open.
Quick check
?South Cambridgeshire grew from about 148,800 to 162,000 people (2011–2021). How many extra people is that?
Mission check

?Village X is 10 km from a growing city and has a railway station. Village Y is on a remote peninsula, two hours from any city. What is most likely?

Accessibility to city jobs decides it. South Cambridgeshire, around Cambridge, grew 8.9% (2011–21); the remote Outer Hebrides fell 5.5% (2011–22).

Mission 4

Mission 4: A baby boy in Hart, Hampshire can expect to live ten years longer than one in Blackpool. Why?

Make a guess: A baby boy in Hart, Hampshire can expect to live ten years longer than one in Blackpool. Why?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.2 improvements and new developments in UK road, rail, port and airport infrastructure
  • 3.2.2 the north–south divide and strategies to resolve UK regional differences
Mission 4

What is the north–south divide?

  • The north–south divide is the gap in wealth, jobs and health between the south of England (especially London and the South East) and the north (and much of Wales, Scotland and Northern Ireland).
  • GDP per head in 2024: London £72,201; South East £43,178; UK £41,627; North East £29,957.
  • London's figure is 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
Mission 4

The divide in health and deprivation

  • The 10 local areas with the highest life expectancy were all in the south of England; the 10 lowest were concentrated in the north of England and Scotland (2022–2024).
  • Male life expectancy at birth was highest in Hart, Hampshire (83.7 years), and lowest in Blackpool (73.7 years): a gap of 10 years.
  • Male life expectancy was 3.0 years higher in the South East than in the North East.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in big conurbations, former heavy-industry and mining areas, coastal towns and parts of east London.
  • So the divide is a broad pattern, not a neat line: rich and poor places exist in both north and south.
Quick check
?Which statement about the north–south divide is the most accurate?
Mission 4

Economic causes

  • Deindustrialisation: coal, steel, shipbuilding and textiles were concentrated in the north, Wales and Scotland; manufacturing jobs "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • Closures hit whole towns: after the Redcar steelworks closed in 2015, 2,150 workers and supply-chain staff claimed benefits within a year.
  • Growth sectors (finance, business services, tech) cluster in London and the south-east: London alone produced 22.8% of UK GDP in 2024 (our calculation).
Mission 4

Political and social causes

  • Political: where government invests matters. HS2's chairman wrote in 2014 of "historic underinvestment in infrastructure and our economic over-reliance on London".
  • Political: London is the seat of government and of most national institutions and company headquarters.
  • Social: poorer health and lower life expectancy reduce people's ability to work.
  • Social: young, qualified people often move to where the jobs are, which widens the gap.
Mission 4

New rail links

  • HS2: first services Old Oak Common (London) to Birmingham are now expected between May 2036 and October 2039; cost £87.7–102.7 billion (2025 prices).
  • The phase north of Birmingham was cancelled.
  • Northern Powerhouse Rail: up to a further £45 billion for a "turn-up-and-go railway" linking Liverpool, Manchester, Bradford, Leeds, Sheffield and York.
Mission 4

Roads, ports and airports

  • Road: the Lower Thames Crossing, a 23 km road with two tunnels under the Thames linking Kent and Essex, got development consent in March 2025.
  • Port: a fourth berth at London Gateway helped Port of London container traffic rise 38% in 2025.
  • Airport: a third runway at Heathrow was chosen as the basis for expansion in November 2025, aiming for flights by 2035.
  • Freeports: 12 special port zones in England, Scotland and Wales to boost investment in places that "historically missed out".
Quick check
?Which scheme is designed most directly to improve links between northern cities?
Mission 4

Strategies to reduce regional differences

  • Enterprise Zones (from 2012): up to 100% off business rates for firms, to help "the parts of Britain that had missed out".
  • Investment Zones: the North East zone was "expected to create more than 4,000 new jobs over the first five years".
  • Freeports: tax and customs incentives around ports.
  • Devolution: elected mayors with local funds, e.g. Tees Valley (first mayor elected 2017) with £15 million a year for 30 years.
  • Transport: Northern Powerhouse Rail.
Mission 4

Are the strategies working?

  • Some local success: Nissan's 2023 Sunderland investment, announced alongside a North East Investment Zone, supports a 7,000-strong workforce and 30,000 supply-chain jobs. It shows investment the region wants, not proof that one strategy caused it.
  • But the gap remains: London's lead in GDP per head grew from 2.2× to 2.4× between 2001 and 2024 (our calculation).
  • Big projects shrink or slip: HS2's northern phase was cancelled.
  • Where money goes: several major schemes (Lower Thames Crossing, Heathrow, London Gateway) are in the south-east.
Mission check

?A northern city gets an Enterprise Zone and a faster rail link to Leeds. Ten years later, which result would best show the north–south divide is narrowing?

The north–south divide shows in wealth (£72,201 v. £29,957 GDP per head), health (10 years of life expectancy) and housing. Its causes are economic (deindustrialisation), political (where investment goes) and social, and the gap has widened since 2001.

Mission 5

Mission 5: The UK left the European Union in 2020. So why does it still sell the EU more than anywhere else?

Make a guess: The UK left the European Union in 2020. So why does it still sell the EU more than anywhere else?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA GCSE Geography (8035)

  • 3.2.2 the place of the UK in the wider world: links through trade, culture, transport and electronic communication; economic and political links: the EU and the Commonwealth
Mission 5

Trade: who buys from the UK?

  • In 2025 the UK exported £913.2 billion of goods and services.
  • The EU as a group took 40.7%.
  • The largest single country market was the United States: 21.8%.
  • Next came Germany (6.7%), Ireland (6.1%) and the Netherlands (5.7%).
Mission 5

Trade: what does the UK sell?

  • Goods were 42.7% of exports in 2025, so services were about 57% (our calculation).
  • Top service exports: business services (£179.2 bn), finance (£98.7 bn), travel (£67.0 bn), telecoms and computer services (£53.6 bn).
  • The UK is a service economy that sells expertise, money management and digital work to the world.
Quick check
?Which statement about UK exports in 2025 is supported by the data?
Mission 5

Transport links

  • Air: Heathrow is "the UK's only hub airport"; a record 23.4 million passengers used it in summer 2025.
  • The government chose a third runway scheme in November 2025, aiming for flights "by 2035".
  • Sea: UK ports handled 428.3 million tonnes of freight in 2025.
  • A fourth berth at London Gateway helped container traffic at the Port of London rise 38%.
Mission 5

Electronic communication

  • Subsea cables carry 99% of the UK's international data traffic.
  • Video calls, online banking and streaming to and from other countries all depend on them.
  • In 2026 the government consulted on new laws to protect critical undersea infrastructure.
Quick check
?A London bank's staff hold a live video meeting with colleagues in New York. How does most of that data cross the Atlantic?
Mission 5

Cultural links

  • People: in 2021, 10.0 million people in England and Wales (16.8%) were born outside the UK.
  • India was the most common country of birth outside the UK (920,000 people).
  • Media: the BBC World Service broadcasts news through 42 language services.
  • Family, food, music, sport and language travel along these links in both directions.
Mission 5

The European Union (EU)

  • Political link ended: the UK left the EU at 11.00 p.m. on 31 January 2020 ("exit day").
  • Economic link remains: the UK–EU Trade and Cooperation Agreement covers all UK trade with the EU.
  • The EU took 40.7% of UK exports in 2025 (42.2% in 2015) and supplied 49.1% of imports.
  • Why so strong? EU countries are close, together form a large market, and supply chains cross the Channel.
Mission 5

The Commonwealth

  • 56 independent countries in Africa, Asia, the Americas, Europe and the Pacific.
  • About 2.7 billion people live in Commonwealth countries.
  • Political link: leaders meet every two years at the Commonwealth Heads of Government Meeting (CHOGM); every member has "an equal say".
  • Economic link: the UK's trade deal with India, a Commonwealth member, came into force on 15 July 2026.
Quick check
?Which is the best description of the Commonwealth?
Mission check

?A software firm in Manchester sells online services to customers in Ireland and in India. Its staff work with both through video calls. Which links does this firm show?

The UK's links are layered: it trades most with the EU (40.7% of exports) and the US (21.8%), moves people through Heathrow and goods through ports, sends 99% of its international data by subsea cable, and keeps political ties through the Commonwealth.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Deindustrialisation came from globalisation, technology, decentralisation, transport change and government policy. UK manufacturing fell from about a quarter of jobs to under a tenth.
  • London shows the whole sequence: urbanisation to 1939, suburbanisation, decline to 1981, then re-urbanisation.

Mission 2

  • Manufacturing jobs: 24.9% → 6.7% (1978–2026); services 84.8%.
  • Post-industrial growth in IT, finance, research, science parks (Cambridge Science Park, 1970); Hull wind-blade factory as more sustainable industry.

Mission 3

  • Growth area (South Cambridgeshire, +8.9%): counter-urbanisation and new towns such as Northstowe; more homes, schools and traffic. Decline area (Outer Hebrides, −5.5%): an ageing population, more deaths than births, fewer families.

Mission 4

  • The divide: the south (especially London) is richer and healthier on average, with exceptions on both sides.
  • Infrastructure: HS2 (cut back), Northern Powerhouse Rail, Lower Thames Crossing, London Gateway, Heathrow third runway, Freeports. Strategies: Enterprise and Investment Zones, devolution.
  • Exam move: for "Evaluate" questions, set one strategy's success (Nissan jobs) against the overall trend (gap widening 2.2× → 2.4×).

Mission 5

  • Trade: services are the majority of UK exports; the EU (as a bloc) and the US (as a country) are the biggest markets.
  • Transport, electronic and cultural links: Heathrow and ports; subsea cables; migration, media and language.
  • EU: the political link ended in 2020; a trade agreement keeps the economic link. Commonwealth: 56 countries, equal say, and new trade deals such as UK–India.
