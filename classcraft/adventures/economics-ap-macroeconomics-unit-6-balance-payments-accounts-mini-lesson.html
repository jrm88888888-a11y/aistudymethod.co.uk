Mission 1

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • AP groups the last two as the capital and financial account (CFA).
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
Mission 1

Inside the current account

PartCredit (+) exampleDebit (−) example
Net exports (goods and services)Wheat sold to Japan; a foreign student's tuitionCars imported from Germany; a vacation abroad

Net unilateral transfersGifts received from abroadMoney sent to family abroad; foreign aid
  • Current account balance = net exports + net income from abroad + net unilateral transfers.
  • Credits > debits: current account surplus. Debits > credits: deficit.

Mission 1

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • The CFA = capital account + financial account. Changes in the central bank's official reserves are counted in it here.
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 1

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • CA + CFA = 0 (with any statistical discrepancy added).
  • Statistical discrepancy: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.

Mission 2

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: $1 = €0.80. So €1 = 1 ÷ 0.80 = $1.25.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 2

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate $1 = €0.80.
  • A $500 phone sells in Europe for 500 × 0.80 = €400.
  • A €60 jacket costs a US buyer 60 ÷ 0.80 = $75.
  • The dollar appreciates to €1.00. Phone: €500. Jacket: $60.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 2

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. The exchange rate changes from 1 euro = 160 yen to 1 euro = 176 yen. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a more expensive currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 2

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • The Fed: upper limit of its federal funds rate target range 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission check

?Foreign tourists' spending in Country K doubles in one year. Nothing else changes. What happens in the market for K's currency, the kip?

Every exchange rate change starts with a shift: who now wants more of the currency, and who wants to sell more of it?

Mission 3

Prices across the border

  • A firm sets its price in its own currency. The exchange rate decides what that price is abroad.
  • Illustrative data: the dollar falls from 1.00 to 0.80 euros: a 20% depreciation.
  • US export priced $50,000: in Europe, it was €50,000; now 50,000 × 0.80 = €40,000. Cheaper abroad.
  • European import priced €40,000: in the US, it was $40,000; now 40,000 ÷ 0.80 = $50,000. Pricier at home.
  • So US exporters gain sales, while US buyers of imported goods pay more.
Quick check
?A country's currency depreciates from 2.00 to 1.50 units of foreign currency. A firm keeps the price of its export at 50 in its own currency. What does a foreign buyer now pay?
Mission 3

Net exports and AD

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad → export volumes rise. Imports cost more at home → import volumes fall.
  • So net exports (X − M) tend to rise, and AD shifts right (AD1 → AD2).
  • Step 1: AD1 meets SRAS below full capacity.
  • Step 2: AD1 → AD2: real GDP rises, the price level rises, unemployment tends to fall.
  • An appreciation does the reverse: net exports fall, AD shifts left.
  • Say: "a depreciation shifts AD to the right" — not: "a movement along AD".
  • Say: "the dollar depreciates, so net exports increase and AD shifts right."

?Investors abroad move funds into a country, and its currency appreciates sharply. Other things equal, which effect of the appreciation is most likely in the short run?

An appreciation is the mirror image of a depreciation: net exports and AD fall, and imported inflation eases.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current account: net exports, net income from abroad, net unilateral transfers.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • CA + CFA = 0.

Mission 2

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • Demand for a currency slopes down and supply slopes up in the exchange rate; a tariff or quota on imports reduces supply of the dollar.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 3

  • Depreciation: exports cheaper abroad, imports more expensive at home, net exports and AD rise. Appreciation: the reverse.
