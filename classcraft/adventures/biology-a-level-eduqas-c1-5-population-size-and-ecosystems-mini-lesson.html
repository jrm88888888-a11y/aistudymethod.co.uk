Mission 1

Mission 1: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Make a guess: A rock pool and a rainforest are both called ecosystems. What do they have in common?

Mission 1

Levels of ecology

  • Population: all the organisms of one species in one area at one time.
  • Community: all the populations of every species in an area: plants, animals, fungi and bacteria.
  • Ecosystem: a community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment.
Mission 1

Habitats and ecosystems of every size

  • Habitat: the place where an organism, population or community lives.
  • Ecosystems range in size from very small to very large: a rock pool, a playing field, a large tree, a lake, an ocean.
  • The answer to the hook: each is a community plus its non-living surroundings. Size does not matter.
Quick check
?Two groups of great crested newts breed in ponds 5 km apart, and no newt ever moves between the ponds. How should the two groups be described?
Mission 1

Abiotic and biotic factors

  • Abiotic factors: non-living. Temperature, light, water, pH, salinity, oxygen, wind.
  • Biotic factors: living. Food, predators, competitors, parasites, pathogens.
  • Both control where a species is found (distribution) and how many (abundance).
Quick check
?A student finds a woodland plant only on the shaded side of a hedge. Which pair names one abiotic factor and one biotic factor that could explain this?
Mission check

?A fallen, rotting log in a wood holds beetle larvae, woodlice, fungi and bacteria. Inside it are damp, decaying wood and still air. Which statement is correct?

Like a rock pool and a rainforest, the log is living things plus the non-living world they depend on. That is all an ecosystem needs.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Make a guess: Why does a population that doubles again and again eventually stop growing?

Mission 2

Four ways numbers change

  • Births and immigration add individuals. Deaths and emigration remove them.
  • Change in population = (births + immigrants) − (deaths + emigrants).
Quick check
?A newt population starts the year at 500. During the year there are 120 births, 80 deaths, 15 immigrants and 35 emigrants. What is the population at the end of the year?
Mission 2

Exponential growth

  • Few individuals, plentiful resources, weak limiting factors (little competition, predation or disease).
  • Births exceed deaths by a constant proportion of the population.
  • So the number added grows each time: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 … A J-shaped curve.
  • Each individual does not breed faster. There are simply more individuals breeding.
Mission 2

Sigmoid growth and carrying capacity

  • Real populations slow down and level off: an S-shaped (sigmoid) curve.
  • Carrying capacity: the largest population the environment's resources can support sustainably.
  • Limiting resources: food, water, light, space, nest sites, mineral ions.
  • As numbers rise, resources per individual fall. Births fall; deaths rise. At the plateau, births ≈ deaths.
  • Births and deaths do not stop at the plateau: they balance.
  • Carrying capacity varies with abiotic factors, competition and predation.
Quick check
?Yeast was grown in a sealed flask of glucose solution and cell counts were made every four hours. After 24 hours the count stopped rising. Which explanation for the plateau is correct?
Mission 2

Density-dependent and density-independent factors

  • Density-dependent: affects a larger proportion of the population as it gets denser.
    • Competition for limited resources, predation, disease and parasites spread faster when crowded.
  • Density-independent: affects a similar proportion whatever the density.
    • Frost, drought, flood, fire, storms.
  • Density-dependent factors act as negative feedback: above K they push numbers down; below K they relax, so numbers recover.
  • Say: "a storm kills the same proportion" — not: "density-dependent because it killed so many".
Quick check
?A storm kills 30% of a seabird colony. Over the following years, the colony returns to its former size. Which explanation is correct?

Mission check

?In 1911, 25 reindeer were introduced to St Paul Island, Alaska, where they had almost no predators. By 1938 there were about 2000; by 1950 only 8 remained, after the lichens they ate in winter had been destroyed. How should this be interpreted in relation to the sigmoid growth model?

Growth stops because each individual's share of the resources shrinks. Destroy the resources and the carrying capacity itself falls.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Make a guess: Why do members of the same species often compete harder with each other than with any other species?

Mission 3

Two kinds of competition

  • Competition: individuals use the same resource, and it is in limited supply.
  • Intraspecific: between members of the same species. They need exactly the same food, light, water, space, nest sites and mates.
  • Interspecific: between different species whose needs overlap.
  • No fight is needed. Seedlings shading each other are competing.
  • Both kinds reduce breeding success and survival of the individuals that lose out.
  • Say: "competition for a limited resource" — not: "competition means fighting".
Quick check
?Why does competition occur between members of the same population?
Mission 3

What competition does to growth curves

  • Intraspecific competition rises with density. It is what bends the curve into a plateau at carrying capacity.
  • Interspecific, grown together: each species may level off lower than when alone.
  • Or one keeps rising while the other falls to zero: competitive exclusion (Gause's Paramecium).
  • Remove a competitor: the other population rises to a new, higher plateau. It does not grow for ever.

Mission check

?A forester plants beech seedlings very close together in a cleared plot with no other plant species. Over ten years, most of the seedlings die and the survivors are tall and spaced out. Which explanation is best?

Same species, same needs, same limited light: that is why competition within a species is so fierce.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can you know how many snails live in a whole wood without finding every single one?

Make a guess: How can you know how many snails live in a whole wood without finding every single one?

Mission 4

Why sample, and why at random

  • Counting every individual is usually impossible: too slow, organisms hidden or moving, the habitat trampled.
  • So count a sample and scale up.
  • Random sampling: every point has an equal chance of being chosen. This avoids bias.
  • The estimate still differs from the true size. That difference is sampling error. More samples reduce it; only a full count removes it.
  • Say: "random placement avoids bias" — not: "choose typical spots".
Mission 4

Random quadrats

  • For sessile or slow-moving organisms: plants, limpets, barnacles.
  • Lay two tape measures at right angles. Use random numbers as coordinates for each quadrat.
  • Measures of abundance:
    • Density: number of individuals per m².
    • Frequency: % of quadrats in which the species occurs.
    • Percentage cover: % of the quadrat area it covers. Use where individuals can't be counted, e.g. grass.
Mission 4

Worked example: from quadrats to a population

  • Plantain plants in a 1200 m² field. Ten random 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats.
  • Counts: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 2, 4. Total 30.
  • Mean per quadrat = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0.
  • Quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m². Density = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12 per m².
  • Estimate = 12 × 1200 = 14 400 plants (14 000 to 2 significant figures).
Quick check
?A student places ten 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats at random in a 250 m² meadow and counts 20 orchids in total. What is the estimated population of orchids in the meadow?
Mission 4

Transects: sampling along a change

  • Use a transect where conditions change across the site: shore, woodland edge, sand dune, path.
  • Line transect: record every species touching a line at regular intervals.
  • Belt transect: place quadrats along the line, continuously or at regular intervals (interrupted).
  • At each quadrat, measure the abiotic factor: light intensity, soil moisture, soil pH, salinity, temperature. Sensors and data loggers help.
  • Pair each abundance value with its abiotic value. Then look for a correlation.
  • A correlation is not proof of cause. Up a shore, several factors change together.
Mission 4Practical

Distribution and an abiotic factor

  • Specified practical: abundance and distribution of organisms in a habitat.
  • Method: lay a transect across the gradient; quadrat at fixed intervals; record % cover or density; measure the factor at each quadrat.
  • Control: same quadrat size, same observer, same time of day; repeat transects.
  • Analysis: plot abundance against the factor; test the correlation (e.g. Spearman's rank; choosing tests in another lesson).

?A student estimates the number of dandelions on a school field. She places ten quadrats in the spots where she can see dandelions growing, counts carefully, and scales up by the area of the field. Which statement about her estimate is correct?

Count a random sample and scale up: the snails in a wood, or dandelions on a field. Choose the spots yourself and the estimate is biased.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Make a guess: Energy flows along every food chain but never comes back. So where does it start, and where does it go?

Mission 5

Energy in, energy out

  • Sunlight is the source of energy for almost every ecosystem.
  • Producers (plants, algae) capture light and fix carbon dioxide from the air or water into organic compounds.
  • That energy is now chemical energy in carbon compounds.
  • It passes along food chains when one organism eats another.
  • At every step, respiration releases energy that leaves as heat. Heat cannot be captured again.
  • Decomposers feed on dead matter and faeces, and respire too.
  • So energy flows through an ecosystem, one way. It must be resupplied all the time.
  • Say: "energy is transferred, and lost as heat" — not: "energy is used up".
Mission 5

Food chains and food webs

  • Food chain: one feeding pathway, e.g. grass → rabbit → fox.
  • Each arrow points from the food to the feeder: the direction energy and biomass move.
  • Food web: many linked chains in one community.
  • Say: "the arrow means 'is eaten by'" — not: "the arrow means 'eats'".
Quick check
?What do the arrows in a food web represent?
Mission 5

Trophic levels

  • A trophic level is a feeding position in a food chain.
  • Level 1: producers. Level 2: primary consumers (herbivores). Level 3: secondary consumers. Level 4: tertiary consumers.
  • An animal with a varied diet sits at different levels in different chains.
  • Count from the producer: the producer is not a consumer.
Quick check
?In a garden, blackbirds eat berries from shrubs and also eat snails, which feed on the leaves of the same shrubs. Which trophic level or levels does the blackbird occupy?
Mission check

?In autumn, an oak tree drops its leaves. Over the next year, fungi and bacteria break the leaves down. What happens to the carbon and to the energy that were in the leaves?

Matter goes round; energy goes through. Sunlight in, heat out: that is why every food chain needs the sun, or another outside source, all the time.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Population (one species) → community (all species) → ecosystem (community + abiotic environment); ecosystems can be any size.

Mission 2

  • Plentiful resources: exponential growth. As resources per individual shrink, births fall and deaths rise until they balance at the carrying capacity.
  • Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease) push numbers back towards carrying capacity; density-independent factors (weather) knock them about.

Mission 3

  • Intraspecific competition: same species, identical needs; it levels a population off at carrying capacity and cuts each individual's breeding success.
  • Interspecific competition lowers both species' numbers or excludes one; removing a competitor raises the other to a new, higher plateau.

Mission 4

  • Sample at random and scale up: quadrats for sessile organisms, transects where conditions change.

Mission 5

  • Energy enters as light (or, for chemoautotrophs, from oxidising inorganic substances), passes along food chains in carbon compounds, and leaves as heat; it is never recycled.
  • Trophic level is a feeding position; one species can occupy several. Decomposers feed on dead matter from every level.
