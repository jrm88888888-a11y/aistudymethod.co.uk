Mission 1

Mission 1: One dead bulb can put out a whole string of lights, yet home lamps work on their own. Why?

Make a guess: One dead bulb can put out a whole string of lights, yet home lamps work on their own. Why?

Mission 1

Closed, open, short

  • Charge flows only round a closed loop.
  • A gap anywhere in a loop (open circuit): no current in that loop.
  • Short circuit: a wire straight across a component. No potential difference across it, so no current through it.
Mission 1

Circuit diagrams

  • A circuit diagram shows what connects to what, not where things sit.
  • Conventional current flows from + to − round the external circuit.
  • A diagonal arrow through a symbol = variable component.
  • Ideal wires have no resistance. An ideal cell has no internal resistance.
Quick check
?Three circuits are each made from an identical cell of negligible internal resistance, a resistor and a lamp. In circuit 1 the three form a single closed loop. Circuit 2 is the same loop with an open switch added. Circuit 3 is the same loop as circuit 1 with a wire of negligible resistance connected directly across the lamp's terminals. Which ranks the currents I1, I2 and I3 in the lamps of circuits 1, 2 and 3?
Mission 1

Two conservation laws

  • Junction: total current in = total current out. Charge is conserved: none piles up.
  • Closed loop: sum of emfs = sum of potential differences. Energy is conserved: each coulomb gains in the cell what it loses in the components.
  • Current is not used up. Energy is transferred.
Quick check
?A 9.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance is connected to three resistors in series. The potential differences across two of them are 2.0 V and 4.5 V. What is the potential difference across the third resistor?
Mission 1

Series and parallel

  • Series: one path. Same current everywhere. pds add: V = V1 + V2.
  • Parallel: separate branches. Same pd across each. Currents add: I = I1 + I2.
  • Series: R = R1 + R2 + … Parallel: 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2 + …
  • Parallel: more paths, so the total is less than the smallest branch resistance.
  • Home lamps are in parallel: each has the full supply potential difference, whatever the others do.
Mission 1

Where the formulas come from

  • Series, same I. Energy: V = V1 + V2.
  • So IR = IR1 + IR2 → R = R1 + R2.
  • Parallel, same V. Charge: I = I1 + I2.
  • So V/R = V/R1 + V/R2 → 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
Quick check
?Resistors of 3.0 Ω, 6.0 Ω and 8.0 Ω are connected in parallel. What is the equivalent resistance of the combination?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • 6.0 V supply, negligible internal resistance. A 1.0 Ω resistor in series with 3.0 Ω and 6.0 Ω in parallel.
  • Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 → 2.0 Ω.
  • Total: 1.0 + 2.0 = 3.0 Ω. Supply current: 6.0 ÷ 3.0 = 2.0 A.
  • Potential difference across the pair: 2.0 × 2.0 = 4.0 V. Across the 1.0 Ω: 2.0 V. Sum: 6.0 V.
  • Branch currents: 4.0 ÷ 3.0 = 1.3 A; 4.0 ÷ 6.0 = 0.67 A. Sum: 2.0 A.
Mission 1

Meters

  • Ammeter: in series. Ideal: zero resistance, so it does not change the current.
  • Voltmeter: in parallel, across the component. Ideal: infinite resistance, so it draws no current.
  • Treat meters as ideal unless told otherwise.
  • Real meters change the circuit a little: a real ammeter adds resistance; a real voltmeter draws some current.
Quick check
?A student wants to measure the current in a lamp and the pd across it, but connects the meters the wrong way round. The circuit is: a cell, a voltmeter and the lamp connected in series in a single loop, with an ammeter connected in parallel with the lamp. Both meters are ideal. What does the student observe?
Mission check

?Three identical lamps are connected to a supply of negligible internal resistance: lamp A is in series with lamps B and C, which are in parallel with each other. The filament of lamp C breaks. What happens to the brightness of lamps A and B? (Assume each lamp's resistance stays the same.)

Parallel branches share the same potential difference, so each lamp works on its own. In series, one break stops the current in the whole loop.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Put one cell in backwards in a pack of three. What happens to the total voltage?

Make a guess: Put one cell in backwards in a pack of three. What happens to the total voltage?

Mission 2

Applying the two laws

  • Junctions: current in = current out (charge). Loops: Σ emf = Σ pd (energy).
  • Pick a direction round the loop. Crossing a cell from − to + in that direction: +emf. From + to −: −emf.
  • A current you guessed the wrong way comes out negative. That is fine.
Mission 2

Cells together

  • Series, all the same way: emfs add. Three 1.5 V cells → 4.5 V.
  • One cell reversed: its emf subtracts. 1.5 + 1.5 − 1.5 = 1.5 V.
Quick check
?Three cells are connected in series in a single line: two of emf 1.5 V face one way, and one of emf 2.0 V faces the other way. What is the total emf of the line?
Quick check
?A 12 V dc supply of negligible internal resistance is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor in series with a parallel combination of a 6.0 Ω resistor and a 12 Ω resistor. What is the current in the 12 Ω resistor?
Mission check

?A 15 V supply charges a 12 V battery. They are connected in a single loop, positive terminal to positive terminal, and the total resistance in the loop is 2.0 Ω. What is the current, and which way does it pass through the battery?

Opposing emfs subtract, whether in a battery pack or a charger. Three identical cells with one reversed: the total is one cell's emf, a third of the full pack.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A street lamp switches itself on at dusk. How does its circuit sense that it is getting dark?

Make a guess: A street lamp switches itself on at dusk. How does its circuit sense that it is getting dark?

Mission 3

Sharing a pd

  • Two resistors in series across a supply Vin carry the same current.
  • So the pd splits in proportion to resistance. Bigger R, bigger share.
  • V/Vtotal = R/Rtotal (or VOUT/VIN = R/Rtotal): R is the resistor the output is taken across; Rtotal = R1 + R2.
  • V1/V2 = R1/R2.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • 12 V supply, negligible internal resistance. R1 = 2.2 kΩ, R2 = 3.3 kΩ. Find the pd across R2.
  • VOUT/12 = 3.3/(2.2 + 3.3), so VOUT = 7.2 V.
  • The pd across R1 is 12 − 7.2 = 4.8 V. Ratio 4.8 : 7.2 = 2.2 : 3.3.
Quick check
?An LDR and a 300 Ω resistor are in series across a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. In the room's light the LDR's resistance is 900 Ω. What does an ideal voltmeter across the 300 Ω resistor read?
Mission 3

Potentiometer

  • A resistance track across the supply, with a sliding contact.
  • Output between one end and the slider: any pd from 0 to Vin.
  • Uniform track: pd ∝ length from that end.
Quick check
?A potentiometer has a uniform resistance track connected across a 9.0 V dc supply of negligible internal resistance. An ideal voltmeter is connected between one end of the track and the sliding contact, which is one third of the way along the track from that end. What does the voltmeter read?
Mission 3

Sensors in a divider

  • NTC thermistor: R falls as temperature rises.
  • LDR: R falls as light gets brighter.
  • Put one in a divider: when its R changes, its share of the pd changes.
  • A component's pd rises when its share of the total R rises.
Mission 3

Control circuits

  • Dusk lamp: Vout across the LDR. Darker → LDR's R up → Vout up → switches the lamp on.
  • Heating control: Vout across the thermistor. Colder → R up → Vout up → switches the heater on.
  • Make the fixed resistor variable to set the switching point.
  • Vout goes to a switching circuit, not straight to the lamp or heater.
Quick check
?An LDR and a 10 kΩ fixed resistor are in series across a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. An ideal voltmeter is connected across the fixed resistor. The LDR's resistance is 50 kΩ in the dark and 1.0 kΩ in bright light. What does the voltmeter read in each case?
Mission check

?An NTC thermistor and a fixed resistor are in series across a supply of constant pd. An ideal voltmeter is connected across the thermistor. The thermistor is warmed. What happens to the voltmeter reading?

Same idea at dusk: the LDR's resistance rises in the dark, so the pd across it rises, and that switches the street lamp on.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Make a guess: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Mission 4

emf and internal resistance

  • emf: energy transferred to each coulomb of charge by the source.
  • A real cell has internal resistance r. Model: an ideal cell in series with r.
  • With a current, some energy per coulomb is dissipated inside the cell. That pd is the lost volts, Ir.
  • Terminal pd = emf − lost volts.
Mission 4

Terminal potential difference

  • V = E − Ir, with V the terminal pd.
  • No current: terminal pd = emf.
Quick check
?A battery with an internal resistance of 0.50 Ω drives a current of 0.60 A through a lamp, and an ideal voltmeter across the battery's terminals reads 4.2 V. The lamp is then disconnected, so that only the voltmeter remains connected across the battery. What does the voltmeter now read?
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Cell: emf 1.5 V, internal resistance 0.50 Ω. External resistor: 2.5 Ω.
  • Current = emf ÷ total resistance = 1.5 ÷ (2.5 + 0.50) = 0.50 A.
  • Lost volts = Ir = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 V.
  • Terminal pd = 1.5 − 0.25 = 1.25 V.
  • Check: IR = 0.50 × 2.5 = 1.25 V.
Quick check
?A battery of emf 9.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor. What is the terminal potential difference of the battery?
Mission 4

Consequences

  • More current → more lost volts → lower terminal pd.
  • A starter motor draws a very large current. The battery's terminal pd drops, so the headlights dim.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): current = emf ÷ r. Small r → a very large current; the cell heats up.
  • Car batteries need a small r to deliver large currents. High-voltage lab supplies have a large r so the current stays safe.
Quick check
?Cells P and Q have the same emf. P has a smaller internal resistance than Q. Each cell is connected in turn to the same lamp. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Measuring emf and r

  • Cell, variable resistor and ammeter in series. Voltmeter across the cell's terminals.
  • Change the resistance: record terminal pd V and current I each time.
  • V = −rI + emf: plot V against I. Straight line.
  • y-intercept = emf. Gradient = −r.
  • Switch off between readings: a warm cell's r changes.
  • This is a specified practical.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the terminal pd V of a cell varies with the current I that it delivers. What is the internal resistance of the cell?
Mission check

?A battery of internal resistance r is connected to an external resistor whose resistance equals r. The external resistor is then replaced by one of resistance 2r. How does the potential difference across the terminals of the battery change?

Larger external resistance → smaller current → smaller Ir inside the battery → higher terminal potential difference. The starter motor is the reverse: tiny resistance, huge current, so the terminal potential difference drops and the headlights dim.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Make a guess: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Quick check
?Predict first. An uncharged parallel-plate capacitor is connected to a battery and left until it is fully charged. Which statement correctly describes how the capacitor becomes charged?
Mission 5

What a capacitor is

  • Two conducting plates, separated by an insulator (air, a vacuum or a plastic film).
  • Connect a cell: electrons are pulled off one plate, pass round the external circuit, and are pushed onto the other plate.
  • No charge crosses the gap.
  • One plate ends at +Q, the other at −Q. Net charge: zero.
  • "The charge on a capacitor" means Q, the charge on each plate.
Mission 5

Capacitance

  • C = Q/V: capacitance is the charge stored per unit p.d.
  • Unit: the farad. 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹: a capacitor storing 1 C on each plate for every 1 V across it.
  • 1 F is huge. Real values: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • For one capacitor, Q ∝ V. C is fixed by its construction, not by the p.d. you apply.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.
  • Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C on each plate.
  • Electrons moved from one plate to the other: 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹⁶.
  • Charge it to 18 V instead: Q doubles; C stays 470 μF.
Quick check
?A parallel-plate capacitor is connected to an ideal 12 V battery. When the capacitor is fully charged, its positive plate carries a charge of 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ C. What is the capacitance of the capacitor?
Mission 5

What it stores: energy

  • Separating +Q from −Q takes work. That work is stored as electric potential energy.
  • Graph of p.d. against charge: a straight line through the origin (V = Q/C).
  • Moving a small charge ΔQ across a p.d. V takes work VΔQ: a thin strip under the line.
  • Add the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle.
  • U = ½QV
Mission 5

Three forms, one example

  • Use Q = CV: U = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C.
  • The 470 μF capacitor at 9.0 V: energy = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = 1.9 × 10⁻² J.
  • At 18 V: the charge doubles and the potential difference doubles, so the energy is four times as much (7.6 × 10⁻² J).
  • Why ½? The cell moves charge Q through p.d. V and transfers QV. The capacitor keeps ½QV; the other ½QV is dissipated as heat in the circuit's resistance.
Quick check
?A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a potential difference of 12 V. How much energy does it store?
Mission check

?The graph shows the magnitude Q of the charge on each plate of a capacitor as a function of the potential difference V across it. What energy is stored in the capacitor when V = 6.0 V?

The plates' charges cancel, so a capacitor stores no net charge. It stores energy: the work done separating +Q from −Q, equal to ½QV.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Make a guess: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Mission 6

What sets C for parallel plates

  • Larger plate area A: the same charge is spread more thinly, so the field and potential difference are smaller. C ∝ A.
  • Smaller gap d → C ∝ 1/d.
  • Vacuum or air between the plates: C = ε0A/d.
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹: the permittivity of free space.
  • Plate thickness and the charge already stored do not matter.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • Air-filled plates, each 0.040 m², 1.0 mm apart.
  • C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ F (350 pF).
  • Connected to 12 V: Q = CV = 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ C.
  • The field between the plates is uniform: E = V/d = 12 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.2 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
Quick check
?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has a capacitance of 16 pF. A second air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has plates with three times the area of the first capacitor's plates, and its plates are twice as far apart. What is the capacitance of the second capacitor?
Mission 6

Dielectrics

  • Fill the gap with an insulator, a dielectric: the capacitance increases.
  • Each multiplies C by about: air 1.0006 · polythene 2.3 · glass 5 to 10 · water 80.
Mission check

?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor is charged and then disconnected from the supply. Its plates are then pulled apart to twice their separation. What happens to the charge on each plate, the potential difference across the capacitor and the energy it stores?

Bigger plates, a smaller gap or a dielectric all raise C.

At a fixed p.d., Q = CV: more C stores more charge.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Make a guess: Join two different capacitors in series to a battery. Which one ends up holding more charge?

Mission 7

Capacitors in parallel and in series

  • Parallel: same potential difference across each; the charges add, Q = Q1 + Q2.
  • C = C1 + C2 + …
  • Series: the same charge Q on every capacitor. The two joined inner plates form an isolated conductor: they hold −Q and +Q, and charge is conserved.
  • The p.d.s add, V = V1 + V2.
  • 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2 + …
  • Series total is less than the smallest; parallel total is more than the largest.
Mission 7

Worked example

  • 3.0 μF and 6.0 μF in series across 12 V.
  • 1/C = 1/3.0 + 1/6.0 = 1/2.0, so C = 2.0 μF.
  • Q = 2.0 μF × 12 V = 24 μC on each capacitor.
  • Potential differences: 24 ÷ 3.0 = 8.0 V and 24 ÷ 6.0 = 4.0 V. The smaller capacitor takes the larger potential difference.
  • The same pair in parallel: C = 9.0 μF, total charge 9.0 × 12 = 108 μC (1.1 × 10² μC).
Quick check
?Two capacitors, of capacitance C and 3C, are connected in series with a battery of emf E and negligible internal resistance. After they have been charging for a long time, what is the potential difference V across the capacitor C?
Quick check
?Four identical capacitors, each of capacitance C, are first connected all in parallel, giving an equivalent capacitance CX. They are then reconnected all in series, giving an equivalent capacitance CY. How does CX compare with CY?
Quick check
?A 4.7 μF capacitor is connected to a 9.0 V battery. Without changing the battery, which change doubles the total charge taken from the battery?
Mission check

?The circuit shown contains a battery and three capacitors, initially uncharged. After the capacitors have been charging for a long time, which capacitor has the largest potential difference across it?

The plates joined between two series capacitors form an isolated conductor that starts neutral: if one plate holds −Q, the other holds +Q. So every capacitor in a series chain carries the same charge, whatever its capacitance.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Junction: current in = current out (charge). Loop: emfs = sum of potential differences (energy).
  • Series: same I, pds add, R = R1 + R2. Parallel: same V, currents add, 1/R = 1/R1 + 1/R2.
  • Ideal ammeter: zero resistance, in series. Ideal voltmeter: infinite resistance, in parallel.

Mission 2

  • Emfs in series add; a reversed emf subtracts.

Mission 3

  • VOUT/VIN = R/Rtotal: series resistors share the pd in proportion to resistance.
  • Thermistor or LDR in a divider: its share of the pd changes with temperature or light.

Mission 4

  • V = E − Ir.
  • Graph of V against I: intercept = emf, gradient = −r.

Mission 5

  • A capacitor's plates carry +Q and −Q; C = Q/V, in farads, set by construction, not by the p.d.
  • Energy stored U = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C: the area under the V–Q graph.

Mission 6

  • C = ε0A/d for vacuum or air; a dielectric increases C.

Mission 7

  • Parallel: C = C1 + C2; series: 1/C = 1/C1 + 1/C2, same charge on each.
