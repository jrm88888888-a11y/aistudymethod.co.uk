Mission 1

Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Make a guess: Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 1

What makes a comedy

  • Aristotle: comedy is "an imitation of characters of a lower type"; their fault "is not painful or destructive".
  • AQA: stories that mainly amuse, where no great disaster occurs, with a love interest and marriage as a focal point.
  • Satire aims the laughter at a society's follies and vices, to expose them. AQA says its comedies ridicule society itself.
  • Tragedy moves from order to catastrophe; comedy moves from disorder to order.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Paper 1, Aspects of comedy (closed book, 75 marks): Section A, a passage from The Taming of the Shrew or Twelfth Night; Section B, an essay on that play; Section C, an essay linking two texts (usually your other two). 25 marks each.
  • Essay practice is for class; this mission gives you the conventions to argue about.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 3

The New Comedy plot

  • Greek New Comedy (Menander) and the Roman comedies of Plautus and Terence gave Europe a stock plot.
  • Young lovers are blocked by a senex (an old father) or a rival.
  • Trickery, disguise and mistaken identity follow; a recognition or discovery clears the way.
  • Northrop Frye (1948) argued that the blockers are overcome or absorbed into a new society round the couple, sealed by a festive ending: a feast, a dance, a wedding.
Comedy · conventions and festivityScreen 4

Festive comedy

  • C. L. Barber's Shakespeare's Festive Comedy (1959) links the comedies to holidays such as May Day and Twelfth Night.
  • On holiday, everyday rules are suspended: misrule, mockery and licence, as under a Lord of Misrule.
  • Barber traces a movement from release to clarification: misrule lets characters see their everyday world more clearly.
  • Festivity has enemies. Many readers see Twelfth Night's steward, Malvolio, as its killjoy.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why is a wedding ending a convention, not just a happy ending?

Marriage is a convention because it does a job in the plot. From Roman New Comedy onwards it marks the moment when blockers are overcome and disorder becomes a new social order, often with a feast or dance. Because audiences expect it, writers can use it to satisfy, to question (who is left out?) or to unsettle.

Shakespearean comedy · problem playsScreen 5

Problem plays

  • F. S. Boas (1896) called All's Well That Ends Well, Measure for Measure and Troilus and Cressida problem plays.
  • Measure for Measure has comic machinery (disguise, a bed-trick, last-minute pardons, marriages) round dark material: Claudio sentenced to death for getting Juliet pregnant; Angelo demanding Isabella's body for her brother's life.
  • Coleridge called it "the most painful—say rather, the only painful—part of his genuine works".

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why do some Shakespeare comedies leave audiences uneasy?

The unease comes from the comedy itself. Shakespeare completes the festive pattern (disguises dropped, couples paired, pardons given) but lets someone resist it: Malvolio walks out, Isabella says nothing, Lucio is married as a punishment, Sly never returns. The strongest answers ask who the ending leaves out, and what that does to the laughter.

Mission 3

What is the joke in a comedy of manners really aimed at?

Make a guess: What is the joke in a comedy of manners really aimed at?

Comedy · comedy of mannersScreen 1

Laughing at a class's rules

  • A comedy of manners laughs at the codes of a social class: how its members court, marry, talk, dress and rank each other. AQA names it as one type of comedy.
  • Its tools are wit, the epigram (a short, polished paradox), mistaken identity and the marriage market.
  • She Stoops to Conquer (1773) mocks country and town gentry; The Importance of Being Earnest (1895) mocks late-Victorian high society.
Comedy · comedy of mannersScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Paper 1, Aspects of comedy (closed book): alongside your Shakespeare play you study one pre-1900 drama and one further text. She Stoops to Conquer and The Importance of Being Earnest are the two pre-1900 dramas on the list.
  • Section C: one essay linking two texts, 25 marks. Questions name an aspect of comedy, such as folly, disguise or the marriage plot, and ask how far it works in your texts.
  • Essay practice is for class; this mission prepares the connections (AO4).

Comedy · comedy of mannersScreen 5

Two playwrights, two targets

  • Goldsmith's essay on laughing and sentimental comedy (1773) attacked plays that praised private virtue and asked for tears; comedy, he argued, should laugh at folly.
  • In She Stoops, a prank makes Marlow take the Hardcastles' house for an inn, so a gentleman's rudeness to his "landlord" is exposed.
  • Reviewing Earnest in 1895, William Archer enjoyed it but found it raised no principle of art or morals; Shaw felt a comedy should move as well as amuse, and later called it heartless.

Mission checkScreen 9

?What is the joke in a comedy of manners really aimed at?

The joke is aimed at the rules of a social class: who may marry whom, how rank, money and family are judged, and how people perform politeness. In She Stoops, a gentleman's manners depend on a woman's rank; in Earnest, the epigrams make high society's values sound reasonable and absurd at once. Because the audience often belonged to that class, the laughter turned back on them.

Mission 4

Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

Make a guess: Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 1

What AO5 asks

  • Every board's AO5: explore literary texts informed by different interpretations.
  • An interpretation is an argued reading of the text: a critic's essay, a director's staging, a theory (feminist, Marxist, post-colonial), or another reader's view.
  • Readings change over time: a speech admired in 1904 can be distrusted in 1937.
  • AO5 rewards debate: testing a reading against the text. A critic's name on its own earns nothing.
A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature B: AO5 counts in every question (about 12%), and questions usually set a view for you to weigh. Theory readings from the AQA Critical Anthology count as interpretations, and drive the NEA.
Extract A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 3

Othello's last speech

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 4

Two readings of the speech

  • A. C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) took Othello at his word: "not easily jealous" is "perfectly just", and at the close "the Othello of the Council-chamber and the quay of Cyprus has returned".
  • T. S. Eliot (1927) disagreed: Othello is consoling himself, thinking less of what he did than of how he'll be remembered.
  • F. R. Leavis (1937) went further: Iago succeeds fast because Othello's self-ideal makes him ready to believe.
  • The speech supports both sides: that makes it a debate.

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 6

Using a critic, not dropping one

  1. State the view briefly in your own words, and say whose it is.
  2. Test it against precise evidence from the text.
  3. Qualify it: what does it explain, and what does it miss?
  4. Decide your own position. "Yes, but…" is a position.
  • You don't have to pick a side. You do have to weigh the sides.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Comedy (Aristotle) shows faults that are "not painful or destructive"; AQA's comedies amuse, avoid disaster, centre on love and marriage, and ridicule society.
  • The New Comedy plot: lovers blocked by a senex or rival, trickery and disguise, a discovery, then a new society sealed by a wedding (Frye).
  • Festive comedy (Barber): holiday misrule moves from release to clarification; festivity has its killjoy, and the ending may leave someone out.
  • AQA's "aspects" include rule and misrule and the comic villain whose power is curtailed: use these terms in Sections A–C.
  • Shakespeare's comedies use festive conventions (disguise, twins, misrule, discovery, marriages) and then test them.
  • Uneasy notes: Malvolio's exit (Lamb's "tragic interest"), Katherina's final speech (Shaw's objection), Isabella's silence.
  • "Problem plays" (Boas, 1896): comic form round dark material; Coleridge found Measure for Measure "painful".
  • AQA's aspects include "moments of unhappiness" and the comic villain whose power is curtailed: ask whether Malvolio's is.
  • A comedy of manners mocks the codes of a class: courtship, marriage, rank, money and appearances.
  • Goldsmith (1773): laughing comedy against sentimental comedy; Marlow's manners change with a woman's rank, and a country house mistaken for an inn exposes a gentleman's rudeness.
  • Wilde (1895): epigrams such as Lady Bracknell's "carelessness" make absurd values sound reasonable. Archer found no principle in it and Shaw found it heartless; you can test those views.
  • Both plays are AQA's pre-1900 comedy dramas: in Section C, link one to your further text through an aspect such as folly, disguise or the marriage plot.
  • AO5 asks you to explore a text through different interpretations: critics, productions, theories and other readers, including how readings change over time.
  • Othello's last speech is read as nobility restored (Bradley, 1904) and as self-dramatising (Eliot, 1927; Leavis, 1937): the lines support both, so you weigh them.
  • Use a critic in four moves: state, test, qualify, decide. A name alone earns nothing.
  • AQA marks AO5 in every question; the NEA also needs readings over time.

Key quotations to learn

  • Comedy is, as we have said, an imitation of characters of a lower type (Aristotle, Poetics V, trans. Butcher (1895)) The classical starting point for comedy.
  • It consists in some defect or ugliness which is not painful or destructive. (Aristotle, Poetics V, trans. Butcher) Why comic suffering doesn't hurt.
  • Dost thou think, because thou art virtuous, there shall be no more cakes and ale? (Twelfth Night, Act 2 Scene 3 (Sir Toby)) Festivity against the killjoy.
  • Is there no respect of place, persons, nor time, in you? (Twelfth Night, Act 2 Scene 3 (Malvolio)) Everyday order versus holiday misrule.
  • He hath been most notoriously abus’d. (Twelfth Night, Act 5 Scene 1 (Olivia)) The cost of the festive joke.
  • And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges. (Twelfth Night, Act 5 Scene 1 (Feste)) The joke turned back on its victim.
  • I’ll be revenged on the whole pack of you. (Twelfth Night, Act 5 Scene 1 (Malvolio)) The outsider who refuses the ending.
  • Thy husband is thy lord, thy life, thy keeper, (The Taming of the Shrew, Act 5 Scene 2 (Katherina)) The speech whose tone is debated.
  • Give me your hand and say you will be mine. (Measure for Measure, Act 5 Scene 1 (Duke)) A proposal the text leaves unanswered.
  • Marrying a punk, my lord, is pressing to death, whipping, and hanging. (Measure for Measure, Act 5 Scene 1 (Lucio)) Marriage as punishment.
  • Malvolio is not essentially ludicrous. He becomes comic but by accident. (Charles Lamb, "On Some of the Old Actors" (1822)) A PD critic who felt the cruelty of the joke.
  • I love everything that’s old: old friends, old times, old manners, old books, old wine; (She Stoops to Conquer, Act I (Hardcastle)) Country against town fashion.
  • Among women of reputation and virtue he is the modestest man alive; (She Stoops to Conquer, Act I (Miss Neville)) Marlow's manners depend on rank.
  • To lose one parent, Mr. Worthing, may be regarded as a misfortune; to lose both looks like carelessness. (Earnest, Act I (Lady Bracknell)) Grief judged as bad form.
  • I do not approve of anything that tampers with natural ignorance. (Earnest, Act I (Lady Bracknell)) Paradox that exposes a class's values.
  • The good ended happily, and the bad unhappily. That is what Fiction means. (Earnest, Act II (Miss Prism)) The play mocking its own comic conventions.
