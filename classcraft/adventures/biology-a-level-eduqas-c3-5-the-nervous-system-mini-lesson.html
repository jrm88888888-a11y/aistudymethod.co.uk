Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Make a guess: Why does a resting nerve cell keep spending energy when it is not sending any signal?

Mission 1

A motor neurone

  • Cell body: nucleus and most of the cytoplasm.
  • Dendrites: many short fibres; carry impulses towards the cell body.
  • Axon: one long fibre; carries impulses away from the cell body.
  • Axon terminals end at an effector (a muscle or gland).
  • Drawing: label cell body, nucleus, dendrites, axon, myelin sheath, Schwann cell, node of Ranvier, motor end plates. Single clear lines; no shading.
Mission 1

Myelin

  • Many axons are wrapped in a myelin sheath.
  • Made by Schwann cells, which wrap their membrane round and round the axon.
  • Myelin is lipid-rich: an electrical insulator.
  • Gaps between sheath sections: nodes of Ranvier.
  • Non-myelinated axons have no sheath and no nodes.
Quick check
?Which description of the structure of a neurone is correct?
Mission 1

The resting potential

  • At rest, the inside of the axon is negative relative to the outside.
  • Typical value: about −70 mV. This is the resting potential.
  • A membrane with a charge difference across it is polarised.
  • Membrane potential = voltage inside relative to outside.
  • The neurone is not "off": it is held ready to fire.
Mission 1

How the resting potential is made

  • Sodium–potassium pump: uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out for every 2 K⁺ in.
  • Result: Na⁺ high outside; K⁺ high inside. Two concentration gradients.
  • The membrane is more permeable to K⁺ than to Na⁺: many K⁺ channels are open; most Na⁺ channels are closed.
  • So K⁺ diffuses out faster than Na⁺ diffuses in → more positive charge outside.
  • Large negatively charged proteins stay inside.
  • Say: "Na⁺ is actively transported out" — not: "Na⁺ diffuses out".
Quick check
?Measurements on a resting neurone show that potassium ions are about 30 times more concentrated inside than outside and sodium ions about 10 times more concentrated outside than inside, yet the inside is at −70 mV relative to the outside. Which explanation of the negative resting potential is consistent with these measurements?
Mission check

?A neurone is treated with a chemical that stops it producing ATP. Which prediction about its resting potential is correct?

Leaks never stop, so the pump never can: that is the energy cost of being ready.

Mission 2

From stimulus to response

  • A stimulus is a change in the environment, inside or outside the body.
  • Receptor detects it → sensory neurone → CNS → motor neurone → effector responds.
  • CNS (central nervous system) = brain + spinal cord. It coordinates: it links input to output.
  • Effectors: muscles contract; glands secrete.
Mission 2

The reflex arc

  • A reflex: a rapid, automatic response to a stimulus, with no conscious decision.
  • Frame 1 — Receptor detects the stimulus; sensory neurone carries impulses into the spinal cord.
  • Frame 2 — In the grey matter of the spinal cord, one relay neurone (interneuron) passes impulses on, across synapses.
  • Frame 3 — Motor neurone carries impulses out to the effector: a skeletal muscle, which contracts.
  • Three neurones, two synapses: a three-neurone simple reflex.
Quick check
?A person touches a hot pan and withdraws their hand before feeling any pain. Which sequence describes the reflex arc that produced the withdrawal?
Mission 2

Fast, automatic, protective

  • Fast: a short pathway with only two synapses.
  • Involuntary: the response is produced in the spinal cord. The brain does not decide.
  • Protective: the body part is removed before much damage is done.
  • The brain still finds out: other neurones carry impulses up the cord. Pain is felt after the movement.
  • Say: "the reflex is coordinated in the spinal cord" — not: "the brain decides very quickly".
Quick check
?Which statement about the spinal cord and unconscious processes is correct?
Mission 2

Inside the spinal cord

  • T.S. spinal cord, from the centre out:
    • Central canal: contains cerebrospinal fluid.
    • Grey matter: butterfly shape. Cell bodies, relay neurones, synapses.
    • Dorsal horns (towards the back) and ventral horns (towards the front) of grey matter.
    • White matter: myelinated axons running up and down the cord.
  • Each spinal nerve joins the cord by two roots:
    • Dorsal root: sensory neurones in. Swelling = dorsal root ganglion (sensory cell bodies).
    • Ventral root: motor neurones out. Their cell bodies sit in the ventral horn.
  • Beyond the roots, the spinal nerve is mixed: sensory and motor fibres together.
  • Under the microscope: find the central canal, the grey "butterfly", its horns, and the white matter around it.
Quick check
?In a classic animal experiment, the dorsal roots of the spinal nerves supplying one hind leg are cut. The ventral roots are left intact. Which impulses can no longer pass between that leg and the spinal cord?
Mission 2

A nervous system with no brain

  • Cnidarians (e.g. Hydra) have a nerve net: neurones spread through the body wall.
  • No brain, no spinal cord, no CNS.
  • Many synapses pass impulses both ways, so impulses spread in all directions.
  • Neurones are unmyelinated and of few types.
  • So responses are slow and whole-body: e.g. a firm touch makes the whole animal contract.
  • Mammals, by contrast:
    • A CNS that coordinates; sensory, relay and motor neurones.
    • Synapses pass impulses one way, along fixed pathways.
    • Many neurones myelinated: fast conduction.
    • Responses can be precise and localised.
Quick check
?A firm touch on one tentacle of Hydra makes its whole body contract. Which feature of its nervous system best explains this whole-body response?
Mission check

?Months after an injury that cut through the spinal cord in the neck, a doctor pricks the sole of a patient's foot. The leg pulls the foot away, but the patient says they felt nothing. Which explanation is correct?

The movement and the feeling travel separate routes. The spinal cord moves you first; the brain finds out later.

Mission 3

The autonomic nervous system

  • Autonomic = involuntary. Motor neurones to cardiac muscle, smooth muscle and glands.
  • Two branches, usually acting on the same organs with opposite effects (antagonistic):

Pupildilatesconstricts
Gut activitydecreasesincreases
  • The balance between the two sets the level of activity.
  • Centres in the medulla and hypothalamus control both branches.
Mission 3

Worked example: heart rate

  • The SAN starts every beat by itself: heart muscle is myogenic.
  • Nerves only change the rate. A centre in the medulla oblongata controls them.
  • Sympathetic neurones → SAN fires faster → heart rate up.
  • Parasympathetic neurones (in the vagus nerve) → SAN fires slower → heart rate down.
  • Say: "the medulla changes the rate" — not: "the medulla makes the heart beat".
Quick check
?In animal experiments, cutting both vagus nerves makes the resting heart rate rise. What does this show?
Quick check
?Atropine is a drug that blocks the effects of parasympathetic neurones on their effectors. What does it do to heart rate and pupil size?
Mission check

?A patient takes a drug that blocks the effect of sympathetic neurones on the heart. What happens to their heart rate when they exercise?

The heart beats on its own; the autonomic system only turns the rate up or down.

Mission 4

An action potential

  • A nerve impulse is an action potential: a brief reversal of the membrane potential, lasting about 1–2 ms.
  • Resting: −70 mV.
  • Depolarisation: a stimulus raises the potential to threshold (about −55 mV), then it shoots up to about +30 to +40 mV.
  • Repolarisation: it falls back towards −70 mV.
  • Hyperpolarisation: a brief dip below −70 mV, then back to rest.
Mission 4

Ions and gates

  • A stimulus opens some Na⁺ channels: Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation.
  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open. More Na⁺ in → more depolarisation → more channels open: positive feedback.
  • At the peak, Na⁺ channels close. Voltage-gated K⁺ channels open: K⁺ diffuses out → repolarisation.
  • K⁺ channels are slow to close → too much K⁺ leaves → hyperpolarisation.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump and leak channels then hold the resting potential.
  • Say: "K⁺ diffusing out repolarises the membrane" — not: "the pump repolarises it".
Quick check
?Which sequence of events produces depolarisation and then repolarisation during an action potential?
Mission 4

All or nothing

  • Below threshold: no action potential.
  • At or above threshold: a full-size action potential, the same size every time.
  • This is the all-or-nothing principle.
  • So a stronger stimulus cannot make a bigger action potential.
  • It makes more action potentials per second: a higher frequency.
Quick check
?Two oscilloscope traces (illustrative data) were recorded from the same sensory neurone. With a weak stimulus there were 5 action potentials in 100 ms; with a strong stimulus there were 20 action potentials in 100 ms. In both traces every action potential peaked at +30 mV. What do the traces show?
Mission 4

The refractory period

  • Straight after an action potential, that region cannot fire again: the refractory period.
  • Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels are closed and cannot reopen yet.
  • Effects: impulses are discrete (separate); they travel one way; their frequency is limited.
  • Worked example: refractory period = 2 ms.
  • Step 1: 2 ms = 0.002 s.
  • Step 2: maximum frequency = 1 ÷ 0.002 = 500 impulses per second.
Mission 4

Local currents carry it along

  • Na⁺ that entered at the active region diffuses sideways inside the axon.
  • Outside, Na⁺ moves towards the active region.
  • The next region is depolarised to threshold → its own voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open → a new action potential.
  • The region behind is refractory (it cannot fire again yet), so the impulse moves forwards only.
  • The ions only move a short way: the impulse travels, not the ions.
Mission 4

Myelin speeds things up

  • Myelinated fibres conduct much faster than non-myelinated fibres of similar diameter.
  • Myelin insulates: no ions cross the membrane under it.
  • So action potentials occur only at the nodes. Local currents spread to the next node.
  • The impulse jumps node to node: saltatory conduction. Fewer regions to depolarise → faster.
  • Say: "myelin insulates" — not: "myelin conducts the impulse".
Mission 4

What sets the speed?

  • Myelination: the biggest effect.
  • Axon diameter: wider axon → less resistance to current flow inside it → faster.
  • Temperature: warmer → ions diffuse faster → faster. Too hot → channel and pump proteins denature.
  • Squid giant axon: non-myelinated but about 0.5 mm wide. Fast enough for its escape response.
  • Small non-myelinated fibres are slow: often about 1 m/s or less.
  • Many invertebrates lack myelin; large diameter is their route to speed. Ectotherms conduct faster when warm.

Mission 4

Reading an oscilloscope trace

  • x-axis: time (ms). y-axis: membrane potential (mV).
  • Flat at −70 mV: resting. Steep rise: depolarisation. Fall: repolarisation. Dip: hyperpolarisation.
  • Worked example: 6 action potentials in 30 ms. Frequency?
  • Step 1: 30 ms = 0.030 s.
  • Step 2: 6 ÷ 0.030 = 200 impulses per second.
  • Count spikes; divide by time in seconds.
Quick check
?An oscilloscope trace covering 60 ms shows 15 action potentials. What is the frequency of impulses?
Mission check

?In multiple sclerosis, patches of myelin are lost from some myelinated axons in the brain and spinal cord. What happens to impulses along a damaged stretch of axon?

Speed is built into the fibre: insulation and width set how fast every impulse travels, however strong the stimulus.

Mission 5

Receptors are transducers

  • A receptor responds to one kind of stimulus only: it is specific.
  • It is a transducer: it converts stimulus energy (pressure, light, heat) into an electrical change in a membrane.
  • That change is the generator potential. It is graded: a bigger stimulus gives a bigger generator potential.
  • If the change reaches threshold, action potentials start in a sensory neurone.
  • Below threshold: no impulses at all.
  • Say: "the receptor converts the stimulus into an electrical signal" — not: "the receptor sends the stimulus to the brain".
Mission 5

The Pacinian corpuscle

  • A pressure receptor deep in the skin.
  • Centre: the ending of one sensory neurone.
  • Around it: many lamellae (layers of connective tissue) with gel between them.
  • Pressure deforms the lamellae → membrane stretches → channels open → Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation = generator potential.
  • At threshold → action potential in the sensory neurone.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Make a guess: A nerve signal reaches a gap it cannot jump. So how does the message get across?

Mission 6

Inside a synapse

  • A synapse is a junction between a neurone and another cell.
  • Presynaptic side: the axon terminal, the synaptic knob.
  • It holds vesicles of neurotransmitter and many mitochondria.
  • The synaptic cleft: a gap of about 20 nm.
  • Postsynaptic membrane: carries specific receptor proteins.
  • At a cholinergic synapse the neurotransmitter is acetylcholine (ACh).
Mission 6

Transmission, step by step

  • (1) An action potential depolarises the presynaptic membrane.
  • (2) Calcium ion channels open; Ca²⁺ diffuses in.
  • (3) Ca²⁺ is a signal inside the neurone: vesicles move to and fuse with the presynaptic membrane. ACh is released by exocytosis.
  • (4) ACh diffuses across the cleft and binds to its complementary receptors.
  • (5) The receptors open Na⁺ channels; Na⁺ diffuses in → depolarisation: an excitatory postsynaptic potential (EPSP).
  • If the depolarisation reaches threshold, an action potential starts in the postsynaptic neurone.
  • Say: "ACh binds to receptors with a complementary shape" — not: "ACh enters the next neurone".
Quick check
?What is the role of calcium ions in the release of neurotransmitter from a presynaptic neurone?
Mission 6

Clearing the cleft

  • The signal must stop, or the postsynaptic cell would keep firing.
  • Neurotransmitter is removed: broken down, or taken back into the presynaptic neurone (reuptake).
  • Acetylcholinesterase (in the cleft) hydrolyses ACh into choline and ethanoic acid.
  • These diffuse back into the synaptic knob and are recombined into ACh, using ATP from the mitochondria.
  • ACh is then repackaged into vesicles.
Mission 6

One-way traffic

  • Signals cross a synapse in one direction only: presynaptic → postsynaptic.
  • Why: vesicles of neurotransmitter are only on the presynaptic side.
  • And receptors are only on the postsynaptic membrane.
Quick check
?Why can a signal pass in only one direction across a synapse?
Mission 6

Neurone to muscle

  • A synapse can link a neurone to an effector cell, such as a muscle fibre.
  • Motor neurone → skeletal muscle fibre: the neuromuscular junction.
  • Its neurotransmitter is acetylcholine: the same one used at many synapses between neurones.
  • ACh binds to receptors on the muscle fibre membrane → depolarisation → the fibre contracts.
Quick check
?A motor neurone forms a neuromuscular junction with a skeletal muscle fibre. Which statement about acetylcholine at this junction is correct?
Mission check

?Calcium ions were removed from the fluid around a synapse. When the presynaptic neurone was stimulated, action potentials still reached the presynaptic terminal, but no response was recorded in the postsynaptic neurone. What does this result show?

The gap is crossed by a chemical, and the chemical is released only when calcium tells the vesicles to go.

Mission 7

Mission 7: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Make a guess: One nerve cell can receive thousands of messages at once. How does it decide whether to fire?

Mission 7

Adding up: summation

  • One EPSP is usually too small to reach threshold.
  • Spatial summation: several presynaptic neurones release neurotransmitter at the same time → their EPSPs add.
  • Temporal summation: one presynaptic neurone fires several times in quick succession → its EPSPs add before they fade.
  • Total reaches threshold → action potential. Below threshold → none (all-or-nothing).
Mission 7

Brakes: inhibitory synapses

  • An inhibitory neurotransmitter (e.g. GABA) opens channels that let Cl⁻ in or K⁺ out.
  • The inside becomes more negative than at rest: hyperpolarised.
  • This is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential (IPSP).
  • The membrane is now further from threshold: an action potential is much less likely.
  • EPSPs and IPSPs act on the same membrane, so they sum: excitation minus inhibition.
  • Say: "an IPSP hyperpolarises the membrane" — not: "an inhibitory synapse is just a weaker excitatory one".
Quick check
?A postsynaptic neurone has a resting potential of −70 mV and a threshold of −55 mV. At the same moment it receives two excitatory inputs, each producing a +8 mV change, and one inhibitory input producing a −6 mV change. What is the result?
Quick check
?One presynaptic neurone synapses with a postsynaptic neurone. A single impulse gives an EPSP below threshold. When the presynaptic neurone fires several impulses in rapid succession, the postsynaptic neurone fires an action potential. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 7

Drugs at synapses: find the step

  • Ask: which step does the drug change? Release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
  • Same shape as the neurotransmitter → binds and activates receptors → more stimulation.
  • Binds and blocks receptors → less stimulation.
  • Inhibits the breakdown enzyme or blocks reuptake → neurotransmitter stays in the cleft → stimulation lasts longer.
  • Organophosphates (insecticides) inhibit acetylcholinesterase.
  • Psychoactive example: nicotine activates nicotinic ACh receptors in the brain.
  • Cocaine blocks reuptake of dopamine.
Quick check
?Organophosphate insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase. Predict the effect at the neuromuscular junctions of a poisoned animal.
Mission check

?A drug binds to GABA receptors on a postsynaptic neurone. When GABA is also bound, the drug makes the receptor's chloride ion channel open more often. What is the effect on the postsynaptic neurone?

A neurone fires only when its accelerators outweigh its brakes; a drug that strengthens the brakes quietens it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A neurone: cell body, many short dendrites, one long axon; myelin insulates the axon except at nodes of Ranvier.
  • Myelin is made by Schwann cells.
  • At rest the inside is about −70 mV: the membrane is polarised.
  • The Na⁺/K⁺ pump (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, ATP) builds gradients; K⁺ leaks out faster than Na⁺ leaks in, so the inside stays negative.

Mission 2

  • Receptor → sensory neurone → relay neurone in the spinal cord → motor neurone → effector: a fast, involuntary, protective reflex, with no decision by the brain.
  • Dorsal root = sensory in (ganglion of cell bodies); ventral root = motor out. A nerve net spreads impulses in all directions, with no CNS.

Mission 3

  • The autonomic system is involuntary: sympathetic and parasympathetic branches act antagonistically on the same organs, e.g. heart rate via the SAN.

Mission 4

  • At threshold, voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open (depolarisation); then K⁺ channels open (repolarisation). Action potentials are all-or-nothing; strength is coded by frequency.
  • Myelinated and wider fibres conduct faster.
  • Myelin restricts action potentials to the nodes (saltatory conduction).

Mission 5

  • Receptors are specific transducers: a stimulus opens (or closes) ion channels, changing the membrane potential; impulses start only at threshold.
  • Pacinian corpuscle: pressure deforms lamellae, stretch-mediated Na⁺ channels open, graded generator potential.

Mission 6

  • Action potential → Ca²⁺ enters the presynaptic terminal → exocytosis of neurotransmitter → diffusion across the cleft → binding to complementary receptors → Na⁺ in → EPSP → action potential if threshold is reached.
  • One-way, because vesicles are presynaptic and receptors postsynaptic.
  • Acetylcholinesterase breaks ACh down so the signal stops.

Mission 7

  • EPSPs and IPSPs sum on one membrane; an action potential fires only if the total reaches threshold. IPSPs hyperpolarise.
  • Predict a drug's effect from the step it changes: release, receptor, breakdown or reuptake.
