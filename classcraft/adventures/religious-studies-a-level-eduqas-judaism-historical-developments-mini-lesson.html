Mission 1

Zion in Jewish memory

  • Zion: a hill in Jerusalem; then a name for Jerusalem and the whole Land of Israel.
  • After exile, Jews kept Zion at the centre of prayer:
  • the Amidah, three times daily, asks God to gather the exiles and rebuild Jerusalem;
  • synagogues face Jerusalem; the Pesach Seder ends "Next year in Jerusalem";
  • at weddings a glass is broken to remember the destroyed Temple.
  • Traditional belief: the return would come through God, in the days of the Messiah, not by human politics.
  • "Zion theology": the Hebrew Bible's hope that God will restore the people to Zion.

They "shall no more be plucked up out of their land".

  • Religious Zionists read verses like these as a promise now being fulfilled; most Haredi Jews read them as for the Messiah's time (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Why the 19th century changed things

  • Haskalah and emancipation: Jews became citizens in much of western Europe; many hoped to be accepted as Germans or Frenchmen "of the Mosaic faith".
  • Assimilation: some left Jewish practice or converted; yet hostility did not end.
  • Nationalism: Italians, Germans, Poles and Greeks were claiming nations. Were Jews a nation too?
  • Modern antisemitism: hatred of Jews as a "race", not only as a religion (the word was popularised in Germany c. 1879).
  • Persecutions: pogroms in the Russian Empire (1881–82) and the restrictive May Laws (1882).
  • Response: Hovevei Zion ("Lovers of Zion") societies; Leon Pinsker's Autoemancipation (1882): Jews must liberate themselves.
Mission 1

Rabbis who said: start now

  • Rabbi Yehuda Alkalai (1798–1878, Sarajevo) and Rabbi Zvi Hirsch Kalischer (1795–1874, Prussia), Derishat Tziyon ("Seeking Zion", 1862).
  • Their argument: redemption can begin naturally, through human effort: buying land, farming, settling. The miraculous stage follows.
  • Settling the Land is itself a mitzvah.
  • Roots of later Religious Zionism (see Mission 2).
  • Most rabbis of their time disagreed: forcing the end is forbidden; wait for God.

Mission 1

The Dreyfus affair

  • 1894, France: Captain Alfred Dreyfus, a Jewish army officer, is convicted of spying for Germany. He was innocent; the real spy was another officer, Esterhazy.
  • January 1895: Dreyfus is publicly stripped of rank in Paris; crowds shout against the Jews.
  • France was the first European country to emancipate its Jews (1791). If antisemitism could flare there, could assimilation ever protect Jews?
  • Theodor Herzl, reporting from Paris for a Vienna newspaper, saw the case unfold. He later said it made him a Zionist.
  • Émile Zola's open letter J'accuse…! (1898) defended Dreyfus; he was fully cleared in 1906.
Mission 1

Herzl's The Jewish State (1896)

  • Theodor Herzl (1860–1904): Budapest-born, Vienna-based, secular journalist and playwright.
  • Der Judenstaat (The Jewish State, 1896): the Jewish question "is a national question" (tr. Sylvie d'Avigdor, 1896).
  • "We are a people—one people." (same translation)
  • Antisemitism will not disappear through assimilation; the answer is sovereignty over a territory, secured by international agreement.
  • Method: diplomacy with the great powers, not small-scale settlement first.
  • Territory: Palestine ("our ever-memorable historic home") or Argentina; he did not yet insist on one.
  • This is Political Zionism: national, mostly secular, aiming at a recognised state.
Mission 1

Basel, 1897

  • First Zionist Congress, Basel, Switzerland, 29–31 August 1897: about 200 delegates.
  • Founds the Zionist Organisation (later World Zionist Organisation); Herzl its president.
  • Basel Programme (paraphrase): Zionism aims to create for the Jewish people a home in Palestine secured by public law.
  • The German word was Heimstätte ("home"), not "state": a cautious word for the Ottoman Empire's sake.
  • Means: settlement of farmers and workers; organising Jews worldwide; strengthening national feeling; seeking governments' consent.
  • The political and legal claims: Zionism now sought recognition in international law, not just charity for settlers.
  • Herzl in his diary (paraphrase): at Basel he had founded the Jewish State; in fifty years everyone would see it. Israel was declared in 1948.
Mission 1

Reviving Hebrew

  • For centuries Hebrew was leshon ha-kodesh ("the holy tongue"): for prayer and study, not everyday speech.
  • Eliezer Ben-Yehuda (1858–1922) settled in Jerusalem (1881), raised his son speaking only Hebrew, coined new words and began a great dictionary.
  • Hebrew schools and the Second Aliyah settlers (1904–14) made it the daily language of the new Jewish community.
  • Some Orthodox Jews objected: using the holy tongue for daily, secular life profanes it.
  • Modern Hebrew became an official language of Israel (1948): the only ancient spoken language revived on this scale.

Mission 1Evaluate

Is Zionism a "solution" to antisemitism?

  • Claim: Zionism is the solution to antisemitism.
  • For: Herzl: assimilation did not stop hatred (Dreyfus); only a state can protect Jews.
  • For: since 1948, Jews fleeing persecution (e.g. from the Soviet Union, Arab states, Ethiopia) have had a state guaranteed to take them in.
  • Against: antisemitism has continued, in the diaspora and in hostility towards Israel; a state has not ended it.
  • Against: Ahad Ha'am: a state cannot solve the "problem of Judaism", the loss of identity (see Mission 2).
  • Against: Haredi critics: the true remedy for exile is repentance and the Messiah, not politics.
  • Judgement criteria: "Solution" as refuge, or as an end to hatred? Safety for whom: Jews in Israel, or everywhere?

Mission 2

Many Zionisms

  • All Zionists wanted a Jewish national home in the Land of Israel. They disagreed on why, how and what kind.
  • Political (Herzl): a recognised state, through diplomacy (see Mission 1).
  • Practical: settle and build now, without waiting for a charter.
  • Cultural: a centre for Hebrew culture and Jewish values.
  • Labour: a socialist society built by Jewish workers.
  • Revisionist: a state on both sides of the Jordan, defended by strength.
  • Religious: return to the Land as part of God's redemption, under the Torah.
  • Against them all, for different reasons: most Haredi Jews and, until the 1930s, most Reform Jews.
Mission 2

Labour Zionism and the kibbutz

  • Practical Zionism: build facts on the ground: land, villages, schools. Chaim Weizmann (1874–1952) joined practical and political work ("synthetic Zionism").
  • Labour Zionism: Jewish national rebirth through Jewish work on the land and a socialist society.
  • A. D. Gordon (1856–1922): a "religion of labour"; manual work heals the people and binds it to the land.
  • Kibbutz ("gathering"): first at Degania (1910), by the Jordan near the Sea of Galilee. Shared property, equal work, decisions by assembly; children often raised together.
  • Labour leaders, above all David Ben-Gurion (1886–1973), led the Jewish community and then Israel until 1977.
  • Most were secular: the pioneer, not the rabbi, would redeem the people. There were also religious kibbutzim (from the 1930s).
Mission 2

Religious Zionism

  • Mizrachi (founded 1902) by Rabbi Yitzchak Yaakov Reines (1839–1915): Orthodox Jews should work inside the Zionist movement.
  • Its slogan (paraphrase): the Land of Israel for the people of Israel according to the Torah of Israel.
  • Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of the Land of Israel (1921):
  • even secular pioneers are unknowingly part of God's plan; their work is the beginning of redemption (atchalta de-geulah).
  • Settling the Land is a mitzvah; the state is a religious event, not only a political one.
  • After 1967 his son's followers founded Gush Emunim.
Mission 2Diversity

Political and Religious Zionism

  • Why a state? Political: Jews are a nation that needs safety. Religious: God promised the Land; return is part of redemption.
  • Who acts? Political: Jews, through diplomacy and settlement. Religious: Jews act, but God works through them.
  • What kind of state? Political: modern, mainly secular, for all Jews. Religious: shaped by Torah, Shabbat and halakhah in public life.
  • The Messiah? Political: not needed. Religious: the state is a step towards the Messianic age.
  • Shared ground: both support the State of Israel, Hebrew, immigration (aliyah) and defence.
Mission 2Read the source

"That ye awaken not"

> "I adjure you, O daughters of Jerusalem, by the gazelles, and by the hinds of the field, that ye awaken not, nor stir up love, until it please." (Song of Songs 2:7, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Ketubot 110b–111a, paraphrase) reads the repeated oath as three oaths made at the start of exile:
  • Israel would not go up to the Land en masse ("as a wall");
  • Israel would not rebel against the nations;
  • the nations would not oppress Israel too much.

Q1 According to this reading, what was forbidden?

Show answer

A mass, forced return before God's time: "awakening love" too early.

Q2 How do religious Zionists answer?

Show answer

Many say the oaths are aggadah, not binding law, or lapsed when the nations broke their oath through persecution.

Mission 2

Haredi opposition

  • Haredi ("trembling" before God; ultra-Orthodox) leaders mostly opposed Zionism:
  • it "forces the end": only the Messiah may restore Jewish rule;
  • it replaced Torah with secular nationalism: "Jewish" became a nationality, not a covenant;
  • secular pioneers broke Shabbat and kashrut in the Holy Land.
  • Agudat Israel (1912): anti-Zionist at first, later non-Zionist: lives in the state and sits in parliament, without giving it religious meaning.
  • Satmar Hasidim (Rabbi Joel Teitelbaum, Vayoel Moshe, 1961): the state is a grave sin.
  • The Haredi response to a secular state: separate schools and yeshivot, Haredi parties to protect Shabbat, kashrut and marriage law, and (long-disputed) exemption from army service for yeshiva students.
Mission 2

Neturei Karta

  • Neturei Karta: Aramaic for "guardians of the city", founded in Jerusalem in 1938, leaving Agudat Israel.
  • The name comes from a Talmudic story: the true guardians of a city are its teachers of Torah, not its soldiers.
  • Beliefs: a Jewish state before the Messiah is rebellion against God; exile ends only by divine redemption.
  • Practice: refuse to vote, take state money or recognise Israel; some join anti-Israel protests abroad.
  • A small group: most Haredi Jews reject its political actions, even while sharing its non-Zionist theology.
Mission 2Evaluate

How strong are the links between Zionism and Judaism?

  • Claim: Zionism is a Jewish religious movement.
  • For: the Land is central to the covenant (Genesis 12; 15); prayer for Zion is daily; religious Zionists see the State as the beginning of redemption.
  • For: cultural Zionism revived Hebrew and Jewish learning.
  • Against: its founders (Herzl, Labour) were mainly secular; it was a modern nationalism, like others in Europe.
  • Against: many religious Jews (Haredim, early Reform) rejected it on religious grounds; some non-Jews (Christian Zionists) support it.
  • Judgement criteria: the founders' motives, or the movement's meaning for Jews today? Is "Jewish" a religion, a people, or both?

Mission 3

What secularisation is

  • Secularisation: religion loses influence over society, institutions and individuals.
  • Three features:
  • Replacement: science, law and reason, not scripture, become the accepted sources of truth and moral values.
  • Relegation: religion is pushed into the personal sphere: fine at home, kept out of public life.
  • Militant atheism: the view that religion is irrational and a threat to social stability.
  • For Jews, the challenge began with emancipation: citizenship came with the expectation that religion was private.
Mission 3

Ghettoism

  • Ghettoism: preserving Jewish identity by choosing to live separately from secular society.
  • Not the forced ghettos of history (the word comes from Venice, 1516), but a voluntary separation.
  • Today: close Haredi communities (e.g. Stamford Hill, Gateshead, Bnei Brak): own schools and yeshivot, Yiddish or Hebrew, distinctive dress, limits on television and internet, marriage within the community.
  • Aim: to protect Torah life from secular values, as Nehemiah's covenant protected Shabbat and family.
  • Strengths: high observance and strong community; large families mean rapid growth.
  • Criticisms: isolation, poverty from limited secular education, little dialogue with others.
Mission 3

Assimilation

  • Assimilation: becoming like the majority so fully that a distinct Jewish identity fades.
  • Routes: dropping Shabbat and kashrut; intermarriage without raising children as Jews; in the 19th century, conversion (the poet Heinrich Heine called baptism his entry ticket to European culture: paraphrase).
  • Some see it as a free personal choice in an open society.
  • Most Jewish leaders see it as the greatest threat in free societies: Jews disappearing not by persecution but by choice.
Mission 3

Integration

  • Integration: full participation in society while keeping Jewish belief and practice.
  • Samson Raphael Hirsch (1808–1888): Torah im Derekh Eretz: Torah together with the culture and work of the wider world.
  • Modern Orthodoxy: university, professions, civic life, with halakhah kept in full.
  • Progressive Judaism: adapts practice to modern life and values, while keeping a distinct religious identity (see Mission 5).
  • Jonathan Sacks: integration, not assimilation: every group helps build the shared "home".
  • Jeremiah 29:7: "seek the peace of the city": the scriptural root of integration.
Mission 3

The Haredi response

  • Haredi Jews see secular culture as a challenge to be resisted, not adopted.
  • Torah study at the centre: many men study in yeshiva or kollel for years.
  • Own institutions: schools, courts, charities (e.g. free-loan societies and volunteer ambulances).
  • In Israel: Haredi parties defend Shabbat, kashrut and marriage law in public life; long dispute over exemption of yeshiva students from army service (the Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that there was no legal basis for a blanket exemption).
  • Variety: some reject the secular State outright (Satmar, Neturei Karta); most are non-Zionist and take part pragmatically (see Mission 2).
  • Chabad takes another path: outreach to secular Jews, meeting secularisation by engagement.
Mission 3Diversity

Four responses compared

  • Haredi: secular society is a threat; separate to protect Torah. Source: Nehemiah 10. Risk: isolation.
  • Modern Orthodox: secular knowledge is valuable; integrate with halakhah intact. Source: Hirsch. Risk: compromise under pressure.
  • Progressive: adapt practice to modern values; Judaism is evolving. Source: Reform principles (see Mission 5). Risk: losing distinctiveness.
  • Secular: identity by culture and peoplehood, not belief. Source: Haskalah, Zionism. Risk: weaker transmission to children.
  • All four are Jewish answers to the same question.

Mission 4

How science challenges religion

  • Modern science asks for evidence and reason: claims are tested, not accepted on authority.
  • Some discoveries seem to clash with a plain reading of Genesis:
  • The Big Bang (from the 1920s–60s): the universe began about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • Evolution (Darwin, On the Origin of Species, 1859): life developed over billions of years by natural selection.
  • Genetics: humans can now read and edit DNA, raising new ethical questions.
  • Jewish responses range from rejection of some findings to full acceptance with a re-reading of Torah.
Mission 4Read the source

Bereshit

> "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth." (Genesis 1:1, JPS 1917)

  • Bereshit: "in the beginning", the first word of the Torah and the Hebrew name of Genesis.
  • Rashi (1040–1105), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): the plain sense is "At the beginning of God's creating the heaven and the earth…"; the verse does not set out to teach the order of creation.
  • Nachmanides (Ramban, 1194–1270), on Genesis 1:1 (paraphrase): God created from absolute nothing a tiny substance, no bigger than a mustard seed, from which everything else came.
  • Creatio ex nihilo (creation from nothing): God alone is eternal; the world had a beginning (Saadia Gaon; Maimonides).
  • Maimonides also taught that where reason proves something, a verse that seems to contradict it should be read figuratively (Guide for the Perplexed II.25, paraphrase); for him, studying nature leads to love of God.

Q1 According to Rashi, what is Genesis 1 not trying to teach?

Show answer

The scientific order in which things were made.

Q2 Why do many Jews welcome the Big Bang?

Show answer

Mission 4Diversity

Responses to evolution

  • Rejection (many Haredi Jews): six literal days; species created separately. Rabbi Menachem Mendel Schneerson (1902–1994), the Lubavitcher Rebbe, argued in letters that science offers theories about the distant past, not proof.
  • Embrace through mysticism: Rabbi Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935), first Ashkenazi Chief Rabbi of Mandatory Palestine: evolution, as gradual ascent towards perfection, fits kabbalistic teaching (paraphrase).
  • Modern Orthodox: Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Great Partnership (2011): science explains how things work; religion asks what they mean (paraphrase). Rabbi Natan Slifkin (b. 1975), The Challenge of Creation (2006): Torah and evolution are compatible; some Haredi rabbis banned his books in 2004–05.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): accept evolution fully; Genesis teaches values (one Creator, a good world, human dignity), not biology.
  • Most Jewish movements found evolution less of a crisis than many Christian groups did: the Oral Torah had long allowed non-literal readings.
Mission 4

How old is the universe?

  • The Jewish calendar counts years from creation: Rosh Hashanah 2026 began the year 5787.
  • Science: the universe is about 13.8 billion years old; the Earth about 4.5 billion.
  • Jewish responses:
  • Literal: the world is under 6,000 years old; it was made mature (with apparent age), so evidence of age does not show real age (Schneerson's view).
  • Earlier worlds: a midrash says God "built worlds and destroyed them" before this one (Bereshit Rabbah 3:7, paraphrase); Rabbi Israel Lipschutz (1782–1860) linked it to fossils.
  • Day = age: yom (day) can mean a long period; the calendar counts from Adam, not from the first moment.
  • Relativity: Gerald Schroeder (next screen).
Mission 4

Gerald Schroeder: six days and 15 billion years

  • Gerald Schroeder (b. 1938), MIT-trained physicist living in Jerusalem; Genesis and the Big Bang (1990), The Science of God (1997).
  • Einstein's relativity: time passes at different rates in different frames of reference.
  • The six days are told from the perspective of creation's beginning; the universe has since stretched by a factor of about a million million (10¹²).
  • So six days "then" equal about 15 billion years "now". Each day is half the one before: about 8, 4, 2, 1, ½ and ¼ billion years.
  • From Adam, time is counted in human terms: hence about 5,800 years.
  • Critics: scientists say the calculation uses relativity loosely; some religious critics say it reads modern science into Torah (when science changes, so must the reading).

Mission 4Evaluate

Is Judaism compatible with science?

  • Claim: Jewish belief is fully compatible with scientific thinking.
  • For: Maimonides made studying nature a path to loving God; reason can reshape how a verse is read.
  • For: Rashi and the Oral Torah never required a literal, scientific Genesis; Kook and Sacks welcome evolution.
  • Against: many Haredi Jews reject evolution and an old universe; the Slifkin ban shows real conflict.
  • Against: Schroeder-style harmonising may change Torah to fit each new theory (critics' view).
  • Judgement criteria: Which Jews? Compatible with scientific findings, or with science's method (evidence over authority)? Does science answer the questions Judaism asks?

Mission 5

Why Reform began

  • 19th-century Germany: emancipation was opening citizenship; many young Jews were drifting away or converting.
  • Reformers wanted a Judaism modern Jews could stay in.
  • Israel Jacobson opens a temple in Seesen (1810); the Hamburg Temple follows (1818).
  • Changes: sermons in German, an organ and choir, shorter services, decorum, some prayers in German.
  • The Hamburg prayer book altered prayers for a return to Zion and the restoration of sacrifice.
  • Reformers drew on the Haskalah: reason and the historical study of Judaism.
Mission 5

Progressive revelation

  • Abraham Geiger (1810–1874): Judaism has always developed; each age expresses its eternal core in new forms.
  • Progressive revelation: God's revelation continues through history; the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans in their time.
  • The ethical heart (the prophets' justice and compassion) is lasting; many ritual laws belonged to an earlier age.
  • Today Reform speaks of informed choice: Jews study the tradition and decide which mitzvot to keep.
  • Contrast: Orthodox Torah from heaven, binding as given.
Mission 5Diversity

Germany, America, Jerusalem

  • Germany: moderate. Reform worked inside united communities beside traditional Jews. Much Hebrew kept; kashrut and Shabbat largely kept. Rabbinical conferences (Brunswick 1844, Frankfurt 1845, Breslau 1846). A radical exception: Samuel Holdheim's Berlin congregation (Sunday services).
  • America: radical. Free of state-recognised communities. Isaac Mayer Wise (1819–1900) builds institutions (Union of American Hebrew Congregations 1873; Hebrew Union College 1875). The Pittsburgh Platform (1885) makes only moral laws binding; many pray bareheaded; some hold Sunday services.
  • Jerusalem (Israel): radical in another way. Hebrew Union College opens a Jerusalem campus (1963); the World Union for Progressive Judaism moves its headquarters there (1973); the Israel Movement for Progressive Judaism grows. Worship is in Hebrew and fairly traditional, but it challenges the Orthodox monopoly over marriage, conversion and prayer at the Western Wall.

Mission 5Read the source

The Pittsburgh Platform (1885)

American Reform rabbis, Pittsburgh, 1885 (drafted by Kaufmann Kohler; public domain):

> "We recognize in every religion an attempt to grasp the Infinite" (§1)

> "today we accept as binding only its moral laws" (§3, on the Mosaic legislation)

> "We consider ourselves no longer a nation, but a religious community" (§5)

> "to solve, on the basis of justice and righteousness, the problems presented by the contrasts and evils of the present organization of society" (§8)

Q1 Which section shows Reform's attitude to ritual law?

Show answer

§3: only the moral laws bind.

Q2 Which section grounds Reform social action?

Show answer

§8: justice for rich and poor as a religious duty.

  • Legacy: later platforms revised it: Columbus (1937) supported a Jewish homeland and more ritual; Pittsburgh again (1999) encouraged mitzvot and Hebrew.
Mission 5

Concern for the poor and tikkun olam

  • §8 of the Platform makes social justice a religious duty, rooted in the prophets:

> "Is not this the fast that I have chosen? to loose the fetters of wickedness, to undo the bands of the yoke, and to let the oppressed go free" (Isaiah 58:6, JPS 1917)

  • Tikkun olam ("repair of the world"): in the Mishnah, rulings made "for the repair of the world" (Gittin 4); in the Aleinu prayer, to perfect the world under God's kingship; in Kabbalah, cosmic repair.
  • Modern Reform uses it for social action: the Religious Action Center in Washington (1961), civil rights, poverty, refugees.
  • Liberationist thought: Marc H. Ellis, Toward a Jewish Theology of Liberation (1987), paraphrase: Jews should read the Exodus as a call to stand with the oppressed today.
  • Critics: some say "tikkun olam" has become a political slogan cut off from mitzvot and Torah.
Mission 5

Other religions and interfaith dialogue

  • Pittsburgh §1: every religion is an attempt to grasp the Infinite; the Platform saw Christianity and Islam as daughter religions helping spread monotheism (paraphrase).
  • Reform: Israel's mission to be "a light of the nations" (Isaiah 42:6) through ethics, not by converting others; strong support for interfaith dialogue and joint action.
  • Orthodox diversity:
  • Rabbi Joseph B. Soloveitchik (1903–1993), "Confrontation" (1964), paraphrase: no dialogue about the content of faith; cooperation on shared social and moral concerns welcome.
  • Rabbi Jonathan Sacks (1948–2020), The Dignity of Difference (2002): difference between faiths is to be respected, not just tolerated (paraphrase).
  • Many Haredi leaders avoid interfaith dialogue altogether.

Mission 5Evaluate

Has the Pittsburgh Platform helped the poor?

  • Claim: The Pittsburgh Platform has been effective in relation to the plight of the poor.
  • For: §8 made social justice a religious duty, not an optional extra; this shaped Reform's social action for over a century.
  • For: its legacy: the Religious Action Center, Reform involvement in civil rights, labour and poverty campaigns; tikkun olam as a core Reform value.
  • Against: a statement of principle feeds no one; the Platform gives no programme for the poor.
  • Against: its authors were mostly comfortable, acculturated Jews; poorer immigrant Jews arriving from Eastern Europe in the 1880s found classical Reform distant.
  • Against: later platforms (1937, 1999) changed emphases; the legacy is shared with other Jewish movements and secular campaigns.
  • Judgement criteria: effective in words or in action? Then or now? For Jews only or for society? How far is any change due to the Platform itself?

Mission 6

Tzedakah: justice, not charity

  • Jewish teaching sees wealth as a gift held in trust from God ("The earth is the LORD'S", Psalm 24:1), so it carries limits: ma'aser (the custom of giving a tenth), Shabbat rest from trade, bal tashchit (do not waste).
  • Tzedakah comes from tzedek, "justice": giving is a duty, not an optional kindness.
  • Even a person who receives tzedakah should give some.
  • Maimonides' eight levels (Mishneh Torah, Gifts to the Poor 10:7–14): the highest is to help someone become self-supporting (a loan, partnership or job).
  • Gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness): personal help that money cannot replace: visiting the sick, comforting mourners.
  • Communities run free-loan societies (gemachs), food banks and care homes.
Mission 6

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberation theology began among Latin American Catholics (Gustavo Gutiérrez, 1971): God sides with the poor and oppressed.
  • Jews point out that the Exodus is the original liberation story: God frees slaves from Pharaoh.
  • The prophets demand justice for the widow, orphan, stranger and poor (Isaiah 1:17; Amos 5:24).
  • Abraham Joshua Heschel (1907–1972) marched with Martin Luther King at Selma (1965); he said afterwards that he felt as if his legs were praying (paraphrase).
  • Marc Ellis, Toward a Jewish Theology of Liberation (1987): Jews, as a people who suffered, must stand with the oppressed today (paraphrase); his application to Palestinians is controversial among Jews.
  • Liberationist Judaism: social action for the poor as a religious duty, not only charity.

Mission 6

Tikkun olam

  • Tikkun olam: "repair of the world".
  • Mishnah (Gittin 4): rabbinic rulings made "for the repair of the world": to keep society fair and orderly.
  • Aleinu prayer: to perfect the world under God's kingship.
  • Kabbalah (Luria): every mitzvah repairs a broken cosmos.
  • Modern use: social justice and social action, especially in Reform Judaism.
  • Your spec links tikkun olam to the Pittsburgh Platform's "concern for the poor" (see Mission 5).
  • Critics: some Orthodox thinkers say "tikkun olam" has become a political slogan detached from Torah and mitzvot.
Mission 6Diversity

Different Jewish ways of social action

  • Orthodox: strong chesed (kindness) within and beyond the community: welfare charities, volunteer ambulances, free-loan societies; tzedakah as halakhah; often wary of party politics.
  • Haredi: dense networks of mutual aid within the community.
  • Progressive: social action and campaigning central to identity (refugees, poverty, climate); tikkun olam as a core value.
  • Secular Jews: many work for justice as part of a Jewish ethical and historical identity.
  • Shared basis: tzedakah, the prophets, the stranger, and pikuach nefesh (saving life).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Jews prayed daily for a return to Zion, traditionally expected through God and the Messiah; Amos 9:14–15 promises a permanent replanting in the land.
  • 19th-century nationalism, racial antisemitism, pogroms and the Dreyfus affair convinced Herzl that Jews were a nation needing a homeland secured by public law (The Jewish State, 1896; Basel, 1897).

Mission 2

  • Zionism took many forms: political (Herzl), practical and Labour (settlement, kibbutz), cultural (Ahad Ha'am's spiritual centre), Revisionist (Jabotinsky) and Religious (Mizrachi, Rav Kook).
  • Most Haredim reject any religious meaning in the State; Neturei Karta reject the State entirely; Reform moved from opposition (1885) to support (1937).

Mission 3

  • Secularisation: religion replaced as a source of truth and morals, relegated to private life, and attacked by militant atheism.
  • Haredi Judaism meets secularisation with separate institutions, Torah study and political defence of religious life; Chabad reaches out instead.

Mission 4

  • Science's evidence-based method and discoveries (Big Bang, evolution, genetics) challenge literal readings of Genesis; Jewish responses run from rejection (many Haredim) to full acceptance (Progressive), with Kook, Sacks and Slifkin in between.
  • Bereshit: Rashi says Genesis 1 does not teach the order of creation; Ramban's tiny first substance and creatio ex nihilo fit a universe with a beginning; Schroeder uses relativity to make six days equal about 15 billion years.

Mission 5

  • Reform began in early 19th-century Germany (Seesen 1810, Hamburg 1818), shaped by emancipation and the Haskalah; Geiger's progressive revelation sees Judaism developing with an ethical core.
  • The Pittsburgh Platform made social justice a religious duty (§8), saw other religions as attempts to grasp the Infinite (§1) and shaped Reform's tikkun olam and interfaith work; Orthodox views on dialogue range from Soloveitchik's limits to Haredi refusal.

Mission 6

  • Liberationist approaches read the Exodus and the prophets (Isaiah 58:6–7) as a call to free the oppressed.
  • Tikkun olam moved from Mishnaic law and Kabbalah to modern social justice; Jews differ on how political it should be.
