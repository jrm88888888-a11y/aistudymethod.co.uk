Mission 1

Mission 1: Is bringing a child up in your faith a gift to them, or a choice made for them?

Make a guess: Is bringing a child up in your faith a gift to them, or a choice made for them?

Mission 1

What a religious upbringing involves

  • Rites at birth: baptism (Christian), the adhan whispered and aqiqah (Muslim), brit milah or a baby-naming (Jewish), naming in the gurdwara (Sikh), namakarana (Hindu).
  • At home: prayer, scripture stories, food rules, festivals.
  • In the community: worship, classes (Sunday school, madrassah, cheder), faith schools.
  • Coming of age: confirmation, bar/bat mitzvah; later, for Sikhs, taking Amrit.
  • Values: honesty, compassion, service.

Mission 1

A Christian upbringing and belief in God

  • At baptism, parents and godparents promise to bring the child up in the faith.
  • Children learn to pray, hear Bible stories, join worship, attend Sunday school or a church school.
  • First Communion and confirmation: the young person affirms the faith for themselves.
Mission 1

How the parish supports families

  • Family worship: family Masses or services, children's liturgy (Matthew 19:13–14).
  • Sacraments and rites of passage: baptism, First Communion, confirmation, weddings, funerals.
  • Classes for parents: baptism preparation, marriage preparation, parenting courses.
  • Groups for children: Sunday school, youth groups, holiday clubs.
  • Counselling: e.g. marriage and relationship support, bereavement visits.
Mission 1Diversity

The ummah supports families

  • Worship: Jumu'ah prayer, Ramadan iftars, Eid.
  • Rites of passage: adhan at birth, aqiqah, nikah (marriage), funeral prayers.
  • Classes and groups: madrassah (Qur'an and Arabic), youth groups, parenting and marriage courses.
  • Counselling: imams and community elders help couples reconcile (Qur'an 4:35).
  • "And hold fast, all of you together, to the cable of Allah, and do not separate." (Qur'an 3:103, Pickthall)
Mission 1Diversity

Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish and Sikh communities

  • Buddhists: the sangha is one of the Three Refuges: Buddhists go for refuge to the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha (Khuddakapatha 1, paraphrase). Support: vihara worship, Wesak, blessing ceremonies, dhamma school, counselling.
  • Hindus: samskaras (namakarana, upanayana), mandir worship, festivals (Diwali), Bal Vihar classes, help from elders.
  • Jews: brit milah or naming, cheder, bar/bat mitzvah, Jewish schools, Shabbat hospitality, Jewish care charities.
  • Sikhs: naming in the gurdwara, Punjabi and kirtan classes, langar, the sangat (congregation) supporting families.

Mission 1

Communities and family breakdown

  • When families struggle, communities offer: food and money help, childcare, mediation, bereavement and marriage counselling.
  • Society helps too: schools, social services, family courts, charities.
  • Non-religious groups (e.g. Humanist pastoral carers, secular charities) offer similar support without faith.
  • Religious believers add: shared worship, prayer for families, a sense of belonging to God's people.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Equal but different, or equal and the same: who should do what in a family?

Make a guess: Equal but different, or equal and the same: who should do what in a family?

Mission 2

Roles then and now

  • Traditional roles: man as breadwinner and head; woman as homemaker and main carer.
  • Today: most couples both work; more fathers share childcare or stay at home; roles are negotiated.
  • Gender equality: men and women have equal worth, rights and opportunities.
  • Two religious approaches:
  • Complementarian: equal in worth, different and complementary roles.
  • Egalitarian: equal in worth, roles shared or interchangeable, decided by gifts and circumstances.
Mission 2

Genesis: equal in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Both share God's image and the same blessing and task (Genesis 1:26–31).
  • Genesis 2:18: the woman is "a helper comparable to him". The Hebrew word (ezer) is also used of God helping Israel, so it does not mean inferior.
  • Genesis 3:16: after the Fall, "he will rule over you". Many Christians and Jews read this as the result of sin, not God's original plan.
Mission 2Diversity

Christian views on roles

  • Complementarian (many evangelicals, some traditional Christians): the husband is a loving, serving head; the wife supports and respects him (Ephesians 5:22–25).
  • Egalitarian (many Anglicans, Methodists, liberal Christians): 5:21 means mutual submission; "neither male nor female" (Galatians 3:28); roles by gifts.
  • Catholic: man and woman are equal in dignity and complementary. John Paul II (Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988) read Ephesians 5 as mutual submission.
Mission 2Diversity

Islam

  • Equal before Allah: "Whosoever doeth right, whether male or female, and is a believer..." (Qur'an 16:97, Pickthall); both created "from a single soul" (Qur'an 4:1).
  • Different responsibilities: the husband must provide for the family (Qur'an 4:34); a wife's own earnings and property remain hers; mahr (dowry) is paid to her.
  • "they (women) have rights similar to those (of men) over them in kindness, and men are a degree above them." (Qur'an 2:228, Pickthall) Scholars differ on what the "degree" means (e.g. extra responsibility).
  • Diversity: traditional Muslims stress distinct roles; many British Muslims share work and childcare.
Mission 2Diversity

Judaism

  • "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917) This poem (31:10–31) is sung in many Jewish homes on Friday night, often by the husband; the woman runs a home, trades and buys a field (31:16).
  • Orthodox Jews: equal worth, different roles. Women are exempt from many time-bound commandments, partly to allow for family duties; the home is a woman's special sphere of holiness (e.g. Shabbat candles).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews: egalitarian: roles in home and synagogue are shared.
Mission 2Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: Manusmriti 3.55–60 (paraphrase): women must be honoured by fathers, brothers and husbands; where women are honoured the gods rejoice, and where they are not, rites bring no reward.
  • The wife is ardhangini ("half of the body") of her husband; stridharma (women's duty) traditionally centred on home and family. Many Hindus today share roles; others keep traditional ones.
  • Buddhists: Sigalovada Sutta: the husband honours her, does not look down on her, is faithful, gives her authority in the home and gives her gifts (ornaments); the wife manages the home well, is hospitable to relatives, faithful, protects the family's wealth and is skilful.
  • Sikhs: Guru Granth Sahib 788 (paraphrase): those who merely sit together are not husband and wife; husband and wife are those who have one light in two bodies. Equal partners in grihasth.

Mission 2

Non-religious views

  • Humanists: equal rights and opportunities; roles decided by the couple, not by sex or scripture.
  • Feminist critiques: "different roles" can hide inequality (e.g. women doing most unpaid housework).
  • Many atheists and Humanists support shared parental leave and equal pay.
  • Religious responses: complementarity values women's distinct gifts; Catholic teaching says work in the home deserves recognition; many believers already share roles.
Mission check

?In a family, the mother is a surgeon and the father works part-time so he can do the school run and cook. Which statement best describes how the main religious traditions would see this?

The real disagreement is not about whether women and men are equal in worth, but whether equal means the same roles.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If a couple cannot have a baby, how far should science help, and who should decide?

Make a guess: If a couple cannot have a baby, how far should science help, and who should decide?

Mission 3

Infertility and childlessness

  • Infertility: being unable to conceive after a year or more of regular sex without contraception.
  • Causes in women: eggs not released (ovulation problems), blocked fallopian tubes, endometriosis.
  • Causes in men: few sperm, or sperm that do not move well.
  • Either partner: age, illness, smoking, weight. Sometimes no cause is found.
  • Childlessness may also be a choice: career, health, the state of the world, or a religious calling such as monastic life.
  • For many couples who long for a child, infertility brings deep grief.
Mission 3

Ways to overcome infertility

  • Drugs to help ovulation; surgery to clear blocked tubes.
  • Artificial insemination: sperm placed in the womb; from the husband or partner (AIH) or a donor (AID).
  • IVF (in vitro fertilisation, "in glass"): eggs collected, fertilised with sperm in a lab; an embryo is placed in the womb. First IVF baby: Louise Brown, 1978, Oldham.
  • Egg, sperm or embryo donation: a third person's genetic material is used.
  • Surrogacy: another woman carries the baby.
  • Adoption (a child becomes legally and permanently part of a new family) and fostering (care for a child for a time).
Mission 3

IVF: the issues

  • Spare embryos: more are made than used. They may be frozen, donated, given to research, or allowed to perish.
  • Multiple births: placing more than one embryo raises the risk of twins or triplets, which is riskier for mother and babies.
  • Success falls with age; many cycles fail; the process is costly and stressful. NHS funding varies by area.
  • Donors: in the UK, children conceived from donations since 2005 can learn the donor's identity at 18.
  • Meaning: conception happens in a lab, apart from the couple's act of love; a third party may be involved.
Mission 3

Surrogacy

  • Partial (traditional): the surrogate's own egg is used; she is the genetic mother.
  • Full (gestational): an IVF embryo from the intended parents or donors; the surrogate carries it.
  • UK law: surrogacy is legal, but commercial surrogacy is not (Surrogacy Arrangements Act 1985). Only reasonable expenses may be paid.
  • The surrogate is the legal mother at birth; the intended parents apply for a parental order. Agreements cannot be enforced in court.
  • Issues: the surrogate may not want to give up the baby; risk of exploiting poorer women, especially abroad; the child's questions about identity; "renting a womb" treats pregnancy as a service.
  • Supporters: an act of great generosity that gives a family a child it could not otherwise have.

"She was in bitterness of soul, and prayed to the LORD, weeping bitterly." (1 Samuel 1:10)

  • She vows that if God gives her a son, she will give him to the LORD all his life (1:11). The priest Eli at first thinks she is drunk, then blesses her (1:12–17); her "facial expression wasn't sad any more" (1:18). Her son is Samuel.

Mission 3

Catholic teaching

  • Every human life is sacred from conception; a child should come from the loving sexual union of a married couple.
  • So IVF is rejected, even with the couple's own egg and sperm: it separates conception from the act of love, and usually destroys spare embryos.
  • Donors and surrogates are rejected more strongly: a third person enters the marriage.
  • "A child is not something owed to one, but is a gift" (Catechism of the Catholic Church 2378).
  • Allowed: drugs and surgery that help a couple conceive naturally.
  • The Church urges compassion for childless couples and encourages adoption and fostering.
Mission 3Diversity

A range of responses

  • Most Protestant and Anglican churches: IVF with the couple's own egg and sperm is a good use of God-given skill; many have concerns about spare embryos and donors.
  • Islam: IVF allowed for a married couple using their own egg and sperm. Most Sunni scholars forbid donors and surrogacy (they confuse lineage); some Shi'a scholars allow donor eggs.
  • Judaism: "Be fruitful, multiply" (Genesis 1:28) is a key mitzvah; IVF widely accepted, with rabbinic guidance among the Orthodox; donor sperm is debated.
  • Hinduism and Sikhism: IVF within marriage generally accepted; many prefer the couple's own genetic material; adoption is respected.
  • Buddhism: no single view; motives of compassion matter; concern for destroyed embryos (First Precept).
  • Humanists: support regulated treatment, guided by reason, compassion and the welfare of the child.
Mission 3

Is there a right to a child?

  • UK law: single people and same-sex couples can have fertility treatment and adopt; female same-sex couples can both be legal parents (HFE Act 2008).
  • Catholic teaching: a child has a right to be born to, and raised by, a married mother and father; so the Church opposes IVF for single people and same-sex couples.
  • Other Christians: views differ: some agree with Catholic teaching; many liberal Protestants and Quakers say loving, stable care matters more than the parents' sex.
  • Humanists: equal access; what matters is the child's wellbeing.
  • Adoption is widely praised: Christians see it as like God adopting believers (Galatians 4:5); in Islam, care for orphans is a duty, and fostered children keep their birth family name (Qur'an 33:5).
Quick check
?A married Muslim couple ask about treatment. The wife can produce eggs; the husband has a low sperm count. Which option would most Sunni scholars accept?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Everyone has the right to have a child."

  • Reasons for: having a family is a deep human good; IVF and surrogacy relieve real suffering; Humanists: equal treatment for all; God gave humans skill to heal.
  • Reasons against: Catholic: a child is a gift, not a right; the child's own rights come first; IVF often destroys embryos; surrogacy can exploit women.
  • Another view: a right to try for a child with support, but not a right to any child by any method.
  • Judge it by: Whose rights come first: adults' or the child's? Is a "right" something others must provide? Does the method matter, or only the love?
Mission check

?A married Catholic couple are told that IVF using only their own egg and sperm is their best chance of a baby. What does Catholic teaching say?

Most traditions welcome some fertility help; they disagree about embryos, donors and surrogates.

Mission 4: Why can one married couple use the pill with a clear conscience while another won't?

Make a guess: Why can one married couple use the pill with a clear conscience while another won't?

Mission 4

Methods and reasons

  • Contraception: deliberately preventing pregnancy. Family planning: deciding whether, when and how many children to have.
  • Artificial methods: barrier (condom), hormonal (pill, implant), IUD (coil).
  • Natural methods (natural family planning, NFP): avoiding sex on the woman's fertile days, tracked by body signs.
  • Permanent: sterilisation (vasectomy for men, tubal ligation for women).
  • Some methods may work after conception (some emergency pills, the IUD in some cases). Many believers who accept contraception reject these, seeing them as close to abortion.
  • Reasons: spacing children, health, money, the couple's relationship; condoms also protect against STIs.
Mission 4

Catholic teaching: Humanae Vitae

  • Humanae Vitae (Pope Paul VI, 1968), paraphrase: sex in marriage has two meanings, unitive (joining the couple) and procreative (open to new life). They must never be separated (HV 12).
  • So each act of intercourse must stay open to life (HV 11): artificial contraception and sterilisation are wrong (HV 14).
  • Responsible parenthood (HV 10): couples may space births for serious reasons using NFP, which works with the body's natural rhythm (HV 16).
Quick check
?A Catholic married couple have serious health reasons to avoid a pregnancy for two years. What does Catholic teaching allow?
Mission 4Diversity

Other Christians

  • Church of England: the Lambeth Conference of 1930 first accepted contraception within marriage for good reasons; by 1958 it called family planning a matter of the couple's conscience.
  • Methodists and most Protestants: responsible use of contraception within marriage is good stewardship; it can strengthen the couple's love.
  • Orthodox: many allow non-abortive methods within marriage after pastoral guidance.
  • Some conservative evangelicals: leave family size to God and avoid contraception.
  • All these Christians reject methods that act after conception if they see them as ending a life.
  • Many Catholics use contraception in practice, though the teaching has not changed.
Mission 4

Situation ethics and non-religious views

  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics, 1966): the only absolute is agape (selfless love). Do the most loving thing in each situation.
  • E.g. a couple where another pregnancy could endanger the mother's life: the loving act may be reliable contraception.
  • Humanists: decide by reason and consequences: health, the child's welfare, the couple's choice, the planet's resources.
  • Atheists: no divine command against it; most support easy access.
  • Catholic response: love is not measured only by results; Humanae Vitae warned that contraception could lead to less respect for women and more infidelity (HV 17).
Mission 4Diversity

Islam

  • Muslims believe children are a gift from Allah; having a family is a strong Sunnah.
  • Hadith report that Companions practised 'azl (withdrawal) and the Prophet did not forbid it.
  • So most scholars allow reversible methods within marriage, with both spouses' consent, for reasons such as health or spacing.
  • Permanent sterilisation: generally only for serious health reasons. Methods acting after conception: widely rejected.
  • Some Muslims oppose contraception from fear of poverty, citing: "Slay not your children, fearing a fall to poverty, We shall provide for them and for you." (Qur'an 17:31, Pickthall)
Mission 4Diversity

Judaism

  • "Be fruitful and multiply" (Genesis 1:28) is counted the first mitzvah (commandment).
  • Orthodox Jews: contraception allowed with a rabbi's guidance, especially for the wife's health. Methods used by the woman (the pill) are preferred to the condom, which wastes seed (compare Onan, Genesis 38:9).
  • Reform and Liberal Jews: a responsible choice for the couple; health and family welfare come first.
  • Both put the mother's life and health first.
Mission 4Diversity

Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh views

  • Hindus: the householder stage (grihastha) includes the duty to have children. Many Hindus accept contraception once that duty is met; ahimsa (non-harm) rules out methods that destroy an embryo.
  • Buddhists: no ban. Preventing conception harms no being, so most accept it. Methods that act after conception may break the First Precept (not harming life). Intention (cetana) matters.
  • Sikhs: no Guru forbids it; a matter for the couple. Kaam (lust) is one of the five thieves, so sex should be disciplined within marriage. Methods that act after conception are opposed.
Quick check
?Which statement about religious attitudes to contraception is accurate?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Family planning should be left to the couple, not religious teaching."

  • Reasons for: the couple knows their health, money and relationship; situation ethics: love decides case by case; Humanists: personal freedom; most Protestants and many Jews, Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs leave it to conscience.
  • Reasons against: Catholics: the meaning of sex comes from God, not from choice (Humanae Vitae); a conscience needs forming by teaching; children are a gift, not a lifestyle decision; some methods may end a life.
  • Another view: the couple decides, but within religious limits (e.g. no methods that act after conception).
  • Judge it by: Who has authority over moral choices? Is every act or the whole marriage what must be open to life? How much do consequences matter?
Mission check

?A doctor in a busy clinic is asked by two married patients from different faiths whether their religion allows them to use an implant to space their children. Which reply would be most accurate for both a Church of England patient and a Sunni Muslim patient?

Faiths differ less on "whether" than on "which method" and "why": openness to life, consent, and not harming life are the key tests.

Mission 5: Is a five-day-old embryo a person, a future person, or just a cluster of cells?

Make a guess: Is a five-day-old embryo a person, a future person, or just a cluster of cells?

Mission 5

From fertilisation to fourteen days

  • Fertilisation: egg and sperm join to form a single cell, the zygote, with a full set of human DNA.
  • Day 5–6: a hollow ball of about 100–200 cells, the blastocyst. This is when IVF embryos are usually transferred or frozen.
  • Days 6–12: implantation in the lining of the womb.
  • About day 14: the primitive streak appears, the first sign of the body's head-to-tail axis; the nervous system starts to form soon after. After this the embryo can no longer split into twins.
  • From about 8 weeks it is called a foetus.
  • Embryo: the early stage of development, from fertilisation to about 8 weeks.
Mission 5

What is the status of an embryo?

  • A person from conception: a new human life with its own DNA; it must be protected like any person (Catholic teaching; many evangelicals; many Buddhists, who hold that consciousness begins at conception).
  • A potential person: it deserves special respect, but not the full rights of a born child (e.g. the Warnock Report, 1984; many Anglicans).
  • Gradual: its value grows as it develops, e.g. after the primitive streak, or when the heart or brain forms.
  • A cluster of cells: with no brain and no feelings, it is not yet "someone" (some Humanists and philosophers).
  • Ensoulment: many Muslims hold that the soul is breathed in at 120 days (some say 40); many Jews follow the Talmud that before 40 days the embryo is "like water"; many Hindus believe the atman is present from conception (the Garbha Upanishad traces life from conception).

Mission 5

Genetic manipulation and "creating life"

  • Gene therapy: repairing faulty genes. Somatic (body cells): affects only the patient. Germline (eggs, sperm, embryos): passed to future generations; illegal for reproduction in the UK.
  • Embryo screening (PGD): testing IVF embryos for serious inherited conditions and choosing a healthy one; "saviour siblings" chosen to match a sick brother or sister.
  • Designer babies: choosing traits like looks or intelligence: not allowed, but feared.
  • Mitochondrial donation: replaces faulty mitochondria with a donor's; legal in the UK since 2015.
  • Cloning: Dolly the sheep (1996). Reproductive human cloning is banned in the UK.
  • Creating life: making new life in the lab (IVF, cloning, synthetic cells). Critics call it "playing God"; supporters call it using God-given skill.
Mission 5

Christian responses

  • Catholic: the embryo is to be treated as a person from conception, so no research that destroys embryos, no embryo selection, no cloning. Somatic gene therapy and adult stem-cell research are welcomed.
  • Many Anglicans and other Protestants: research up to 14 days can be right if it may relieve great suffering, with the embryo treated with special respect.
  • "Playing God": only God gives life; changing the human germline crosses a line.
  • "Co-creators": humans are made in God's image and given skill and stewardship (Genesis 1:28); healing is Christ-like.
Mission 5Diversity

Other religions and Humanists

  • Islam: "whoso saveth the life of one, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall): used to support research that may cure. Many scholars allow research on early spare embryos (before ensoulment), but not altering God's creation for trivial reasons.
  • Judaism: saving life (pikuach nefesh) is a great duty; many authorities support embryo research and screening.
  • Buddhism: the First Precept (not harming living beings) leads many to oppose destroying embryos; compassion leads others to accept research.
  • Hinduism: ahimsa (non-violence) and the belief that the atman is present from conception lead many to oppose destroying embryos; others stress relieving suffering.
  • Humanists: decide by evidence and outcomes: support regulated research and screening to reduce suffering; oppose designer babies.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Scientists who change human embryos are playing God."

  • Reasons for: only God gives life; germline changes affect people not yet born; risk of designer babies; destroyed embryos may be human lives.
  • Also for: the First Precept; ahimsa.
  • Reasons against: curing disease is a duty; humans are stewards and co-creators; Humanists: the phrase assumes a God; outcomes and consent matter.
  • Sources against: Qur'an 5:32; pikuach nefesh.
  • Another view: some changes (preventing a deadly disease) may be right while others (choosing looks) are not.
  • Judge it by: Where does healing end and "enhancement" begin? Whose consent counts? Does the embryo's status decide everything?
Mission check

?A scientist agrees to research on embryos that are 10 days old, but refuses to use embryos that are 20 days old. Which belief best explains this line?

Where you draw the line on embryos depends on when you believe a person, or a soul, begins.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A religious upbringing: rites at birth, prayer at home, classes, worship, coming-of-age rites (Proverbs 22:6; Matthew 19:14).
  • Parish, ummah, sangha, mandir, synagogue and sangat support families through worship, rites, classes, groups and counselling.

Mission 2

  • Equal in God's image (Genesis 1:27); Ephesians 5 read as headship (complementarian) or mutual submission (egalitarian).
  • Other faiths: Qur'an 2:228 and 4:34; Proverbs 31; Manusmriti 3.55–60; Sigalovada Sutta.
  • Sikhs: GGS 788, one light in two bodies.

Mission 3

  • Infertility can be treated by drugs, surgery, insemination, IVF, donation and surrogacy; adoption and fostering are alternatives.
  • Catholic teaching rejects IVF and surrogacy; most other Christians and many other faiths accept IVF within marriage; Humanists support regulated access.

Mission 4

  • Catholics (Humanae Vitae): only natural family planning, for serious reasons; most other Christians accept contraception within marriage.
  • Islam ('azl hadith) and Judaism (first mitzvah, pill preferred) allow it with conditions; Hindus, Buddhists and Sikhs mostly accept it but reject methods that act after conception.
  • Situation ethics and Humanists judge by love and consequences.

Mission 5

  • Views on the embryo: a person from conception, a potential person, gradual value, or a cluster of cells.
  • Genetic manipulation (gene therapy, screening, mitochondrial donation, cloning) divides religious believers between "playing God" and using God-given skill to heal.
