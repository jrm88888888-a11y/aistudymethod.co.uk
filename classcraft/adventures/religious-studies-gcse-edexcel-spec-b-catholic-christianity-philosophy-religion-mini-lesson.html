Mission 1

Mission 1: If God exists, how could anyone ever know what God is like?

Make a guess: If God exists, how could anyone ever know what God is like?

Mission 1

What revelation means

  • Revelation: God (or ultimate reality) making known something humans could not find out on their own.
  • Believers say God is transcendent (beyond the world), so humans need God to take the first step.
  • Two kinds:
  • Special revelation: God revealed to particular people at particular times.
  • General revelation: God revealed to everyone, everywhere, through ordinary things.
Mission 1

Special revelation

  • Direct, to particular people or groups, often life-changing.
  • Prophets receiving God's word (Moses at Sinai; the Prophet Muhammad in the cave of Hira).
  • Visions and dreams: seeing or hearing something sacred (see Mission 2).
  • Miracles: events showing God acting in the world (see Mission 3).
  • In a person: Christians believe God is revealed most fully in Jesus.
Mission 1

General revelation

  • Open to anyone, at any time: no special event is needed.
  • Nature: the beauty, order and size of the universe point to its Creator.
  • Conscience and reason: an inner sense of right and wrong; the ability to think about God.
  • History and inspirational people: seeing God at work in events and in holy lives.
  • Most Christians say nature shows that God exists and is powerful, but God's love and purposes are known through special revelation.

Mission 1Read the source

God has spoken by his Son

"God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways, has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2, WEBBE)

  • The Son is "the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance" (Hebrews 1:3, WEBBE), greater than the angels (Hebrews 1:4).
  • For Catholics, Jesus is the culmination (high point and completion) of revelation: in him God says everything (CCC 65, paraphrase).

Q1 How did God speak before Jesus, according to Hebrews?

Show answer

Through the prophets, at many times and in various ways.

Q2 What does Hebrews 1:3 say Jesus shows of God?

Show answer

God's glory and very being: "the very image of his substance".

Mission 1

Revelation in the Bible

  • Covenant: a binding agreement in which God reveals himself and makes promises.
  • Noah: never again a flood; the rainbow is "a sign of a covenant between me and the earth" (Genesis 9:13, WEBBE).
  • Abraham: God calls him to a new land and promises descendants (Genesis 12:1–3; 15).
  • Jesus: the "new covenant" (Luke 22:20).
  • What it shows: God is personal, faithful, keeps promises, acts in history.
  • Divergent views: Catholics receive revelation through Scripture and Tradition, taught by the Magisterium (Dei Verbum, 1965). Many Protestants hold sola scriptura (scripture alone). Some Christians read the Bible as God's exact words; others as inspired writing by humans.
Mission 1

The Qur'an as revelation

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is Allah's own word, revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibril over about 23 years, from 610 CE.
  • The first words: "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth" (Surah 96:1, Pickthall).
  • Allah speaks to humans only "by revelation or from behind a veil, or (that) He sendeth a messenger" (Surah 42:51, Pickthall).
  • Direct: in Surah 34:46–50 the Prophet tells people to reflect for themselves; his guidance is "because of that which my Lord hath revealed unto me" (Surah 34:50, Pickthall).
  • Divergent views: Sunni Muslims take guidance from the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah; Shi'a Muslims also follow the Imams' teaching as the authoritative interpretation; Sufis seek inner knowledge of Allah through the Qur'an.
Mission 1

The Tenakh and Gurbani

  • Jews: God revealed the Torah to Moses at Sinai; the Tenakh (Torah, Prophets, Writings) records God's revelation.
  • Direct: a man of God tells Eli, "Thus saith the LORD: Did I reveal Myself unto the house of thy father" (1 Samuel 2:27, JPS 1917).
  • Indirect or veiled: God warns that when Israel turns away, "I will surely hide My face in that day" (Deuteronomy 31:18, JPS 1917). God may be hidden (hester panim) yet still present.
  • Sikhs: Gurbani ("the Guru's word") is God's word revealed through the Gurus, collected in the Guru Granth Sahib.
  • Guru Nanak disappeared into the River Bein for three days (c. 1499, told in the Janamsakhis) and returned with God's message. The Mool Mantar (Guru Granth Sahib 1) begins: there is one God, whose name is Truth, the Creator (paraphrase).
Mission 1

Hindu and Buddhist views

  • Hindus: shruti ("what is heard"): the Vedas and Upanishads, revealed to ancient sages; smriti ("what is remembered"): epics such as the Ramayana and the Bhagavad Gita.
  • The Agamas guide temple worship. In Bhagavad Gita 13.26–33, Krishna teaches that the one who sees the Supreme Lord dwelling equally in all beings truly sees (paraphrase).
  • Some Hindus value revelation less: they seek direct experience of Brahman in meditation.
Mission 1Diversity

Nature as general revelation: contrasting beliefs

  • Many Christians: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE). Nature shows God's power (Romans 1:20). Catholics teach God can be known through creation by reason (CCC 36).
  • Some Protestants: Karl Barth (1886–1968) taught that nature on its own gives no true knowledge of God; only God's revelation in Christ does.
  • Atheists and Humanists: nature shows natural processes, explained by science (evolution, physics).
  • Nature also contains cruelty and disaster; to many non-religious people it does not look like the work of a loving God.
  • Humanists still feel awe at nature, without seeing it as a message from God.
Mission 1

Value and problems

  • Value: revelation can give knowledge of God that reason cannot; it guides life; it can lead people to faith and change their lives.
  • Conflicting revelations: religions report different ideas of the divine (personal or impersonal; one God or many forms). They cannot all be literally true.
  • Alternative explanations: illness, drugs, tiredness, wishful thinking or upbringing.
  • Lying or mistaken: a person might invent a revelation, or honestly misunderstand an experience.
  • Responses: believers test claims against scripture, tradition and the person's life ("by their fruits"). Some, like John Hick (1922–2012), suggest different revelations show different sides of one ultimate reality.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Revelation is a reliable way of knowing about God."

  • Reasons for: if God exists and is personal, God could make himself known; many lives have been transformed; scripture and tradition test claims; general revelation is open to everyone.
  • Reasons against: revelations conflict; they cannot be tested by others; natural explanations are possible; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: revelation may be reliable for the person who receives it, but not proof for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Who can check a revelation? Do differences between religions undermine all revelation, or only some? Do changed lives count as evidence?

Mission 2

What is a vision?

  • A vision: seeing or hearing something sacred that others normally cannot: a holy figure, a light, a voice, a scene.
  • Some are seen with the eyes, some in the mind, some in dreams.
  • Visions are a form of special revelation (see Mission 1): given to particular people, often with a message.
  • Why might they lead to belief in God? They feel real and personal, can carry knowledge the person did not have, and often change lives.
Mission 2Read the source

Abram's vision

"After these things the LORD's word came to Abram in a vision, saying, 'Don't be afraid, Abram. I am your shield, your exceedingly great reward.'" (Genesis 15:1, WEBBE)

  • God takes Abram outside: "Look now towards the sky, and count the stars" (Genesis 15:5, WEBBE). His descendants will be as many.
  • "He believed in the LORD, who credited it to him for righteousness." (Genesis 15:6, WEBBE)
  • God then makes a covenant with Abram, promising the land (Genesis 15:18).
  • For Jews, this is the founding promise to Abraham, father of the Jewish people.

Mission 2

The Transfiguration

  • Matthew 17:1–13: Jesus takes Peter, James and John up a high mountain.
  • "His face shone like the sun, and his garments became as white as the light." (Matthew 17:2, WEBBE)
  • Moses and Elijah appear, talking with Jesus.
  • A voice from a bright cloud: "This is my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased. Listen to him." (Matthew 17:5, WEBBE)
  • The disciples fall down in fear; Jesus tells them not to tell anyone until he is raised from the dead.
  • Significance for Christians: it shows Jesus as the Son of God; Moses (the Law) and Elijah (the Prophets) are seen as pointing to him. Three people saw it together.
Mission 2

Joan of Arc

  • Joan of Arc (c. 1412–1431), a French peasant girl.
  • From about age 13 she heard voices and saw visions of St Michael, St Catherine and St Margaret.
  • They told her to help Charles VII drive the English from France. In 1429 she led French forces to lift the siege of Orléans.
  • Captured, she was tried by a pro-English Church court and burned in Rouen in 1431.
  • A retrial declared her innocent (1456); the Catholic Church canonised her (made her a saint) in 1920.
  • For Catholics, her courage and the results of her mission are signs that the visions were genuine.

Maryam and the angel

Surah 19:16–22 tells how Maryam (Mary) withdrew to a place apart:

  • "Then We sent unto her Our Spirit and it assumed for her the likeness of a perfect man." (Surah 19:17, Pickthall)
  • The angel says: "I am only a messenger of thy Lord, that I may bestow on thee a faultless son." (Surah 19:19, Pickthall)
  • Maryam asks how, since no man has touched her. The reply: "Thy Lord saith: It is easy for Me." (Surah 19:21, Pickthall)
  • Muslims identify "Our Spirit" as the angel Jibril. The son is the prophet Isa (Jesus).

Mission 2Diversity

Sunni, Shi'a and Sufi views of visions

  • All Muslims accept the visions in the Qur'an, such as Jibril appearing to Maryam and to the Prophet Muhammad.
  • Sunni: prophecy ended with Muhammad, the Seal of the Prophets (Surah 33:40). True dreams can still come from Allah: a hadith calls a good dream one of the 46 parts of prophethood (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase). They give no new law.
  • Shi'a: some report dreams or visions of the Imams, including the hidden Twelfth Imam; scholars treat such claims with great caution.
  • Sufi: visions and kashf ("unveiling") can come on the path of closeness to Allah; a Sufi teacher (shaykh) guides how to understand them.
  • Some Muslims, especially Salafi Muslims, reject most claimed visions outside the Qur'an and Sunnah.
  • Test for all: no vision may contradict the Qur'an.
Mission 2Diversity

Visions in Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh traditions

  • Hindus: in Bhagavad Gita 11, Krishna shows Arjuna his universal form: "Sunburst of a thousand suns" (Bhagavad Gita 11.12, Arnold 1885). Arjuna is terrified and asks to see Krishna's gentle form again. It shows God as all-powerful and all-containing.
  • Buddhists: in the Vajira Sutta, Mara appears to the nun Vajira to frighten and confuse her. She sees through him: there is no "being" there, only a heap of parts (paraphrase). Visions can be illusions to see through.
  • Asanga (4th century CE) meditated for twelve years for a vision of the future Buddha Maitreya. Only when he tended a wounded dog with compassion did Maitreya appear, and Asanga received the Five Treatises of Maitreya.
  • Buddhists differ: visions may be empty like dreams, message dreams, or signs of a person's mental health; they matter only if they lead to wisdom and compassion.
  • Sikhs: Guru Granth Sahib 263 (Sukhmani Sahib) describes the peace and fearlessness of one who remembers God in simran (paraphrase). Mystical union matters more than seeing forms; Guru Nanak's River experience is the key example.

Mission 2

"Just hallucinations"?

  • Atheists and Humanists argue visions give no proof of God.
  • Hallucinations: the brain can produce sights and voices through illness, fever, drugs, lack of sleep or fasting. Some writers suggest Joan of Arc had a neurological illness (a guess; no medical records exist).
  • Wish-fulfilment: people may see what they long for (Sigmund Freud, The Future of an Illusion, 1927).
  • Expectation: visions usually fit the person's own religion (Catholics see Mary; Hindus see Krishna).
  • Lack of evidence: no one else can check a private vision. The person might also be lying or mistaken.
Mission 2

Religious responses

  • Shared visions: at the Transfiguration three disciples saw the same thing; hallucinations are usually private.
  • Fruits: a genuine vision leads to good, lasting change (Joan's courage; Abram's faith).
  • Tested by tradition: the Catholic Church investigates claims carefully and approves few. A vision must agree with scripture and Church teaching.
  • God can use natural means: even if the brain is involved, God may be at work through it.
  • Muslims: visions are judged by the Qur'an; the Qur'an itself is the clearest sign.
  • Buddhists agree some visions are illusions; what counts is whether they lead to insight.
  • Jews test a claimed vision by the Torah: a prophet or dreamer who leads people to other gods must not be followed, even if a sign comes true (Deuteronomy 13, paraphrase), and a prophecy that does not come to pass is not from God (Deuteronomy 18:21–22, paraphrase). Abram's vision is trusted because the covenant it promised is lived out in Jewish history.
  • Hindus generally judge a vision by whether it agrees with scripture and a trusted guru's teaching, and whether it deepens devotion; Arjuna's vision is itself in scripture (Gita 11).
  • Sikhs generally value inner union with God through simran above seeing forms, and test any experience against Gurbani.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Visions prove that God exists."

  • Reasons for: visions feel real and personal; some are shared (Matthew 17); they change lives (Joan of Arc); some bring messages the person could not have known.
  • Reasons against: hallucination, wish-fulfilment and expectation explain them; they cannot be checked; visions conflict between religions; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: a vision may be strong evidence for the person who has it, but weak evidence for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Does a natural cause rule out God? Do shared visions matter? Should the fruits of a vision count as evidence?

Mission 3

What is a miracle?

  • A miracle: an extraordinary event that seems to break the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God.
  • David Hume (1711–1776): "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature" (An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, 1748, Section X).
  • Believers add that a miracle has meaning: it shows something about God or answers prayer.
  • Some believers also call a striking, well-timed coincidence a miracle, if they see God at work in it.
  • Miracles are a form of special revelation (see Mission 1): God acting in the world.
Mission 3Read the source

The official's son

John 4:43–54: at Cana, a royal official begs Jesus to come and heal his dying son in Capernaum.

  • "Jesus said to him, 'Go your way. Your son lives.' The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him, and he went his way." (John 4:50, WEBBE)
  • On the way his servants meet him: the fever left the boy at the very hour Jesus spoke.
  • "He believed, as did his whole house." (John 4:53, WEBBE)
  • John calls this "the second sign" (John 4:54): miracles are signs pointing to who Jesus is.

Mission 3

Lourdes

  • In 1858, Bernadette Soubirous (1844–1879), aged 14, reported 18 appearances of the Virgin Mary at a grotto in Lourdes, France. A spring appeared there.
  • Millions of pilgrims now visit each year; some report healings.
  • The Lourdes Medical Bureau (from 1883) examines claimed cures. A cure must be of a serious illness, sudden, complete and lasting, with no medical explanation.
  • Only then can the local bishop declare it a miracle. Very few cures pass.
  • One example: Sister Bernadette Moriau, a French nun disabled for decades, recovered suddenly in 2008 after a pilgrimage; her bishop declared it a miracle in 2018.
Mission 3

The Qur'an: the greatest miracle

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an itself is the greatest miracle: the Prophet could not read or write, yet brought words no one could match.
  • "Say: Verily, though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like thereof" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall).
  • In Surah 17:89–93 unbelievers demand other signs; the Prophet replies: "Am I aught save a mortal messenger?" (Surah 17:93, Pickthall).
  • Other miracles: the Night Journey (Surah 17:1); the splitting of the moon (Surah 54:1); miracles of earlier prophets, such as Isa healing the sick by Allah's leave.
  • Divergent views: many Sufis accept karamat (wonders granted to saints); Salafi Muslims reject most such claims; some modern Muslims read some miracle accounts symbolically.

Mission 3

Miracles in Judaism

  • Hebrew nes: a miracle, also meaning a sign or banner: something lifted up to show God's presence.
  • In the desert God sends manna: "It is the bread which the LORD hath given you to eat." (Exodus 16:15, JPS 1917)
  • The manna was also a test: whether Israel "will walk in My law, or not" (Exodus 16:4, JPS 1917).
  • Hanukkah celebrates the miracle of the oil: "a great miracle happened there".
  • Daily prayer thanks God for miracles "which are with us every day": hidden miracles in ordinary life.
  • Divergent views: many Orthodox Jews accept the biblical miracles literally; the Mishnah says some, like the manna, were built into creation from the start (Pirkei Avot 5:6). Many Reform Jews read them as stories of faith.
Mission 3Diversity

Buddhist, Hindu and Sikh views

  • Buddhists: in the Kevatta Sutta (DN 11) the Buddha names three wonders: psychic power, mind-reading and instruction. He rejects the first two as a way to win followers; the true wonder is teaching that frees people from suffering.
  • Theravada Buddhists see powers as by-products of deep meditation, not to be shown off. Many Mahayana texts celebrate the wondrous powers of Buddhas and Bodhisattvas.
  • Hindus: on 21 September 1995, murtis of Ganesha around the world appeared to drink milk offered on spoons. Scientists said capillary action; many Hindus saw a sign of the divine.
  • In the Puranas, Parvati forms Ganesha; Shiva beheads him, then restores him to life with an elephant's head: a story of divine power over life and death.
  • Sikhs: Guru Nanak says miraculous powers and wealth are worthless if they make him forget God's Name (Guru Granth Sahib 14, paraphrase). The Gurus refused to work miracles on demand: the true wonder is God's grace.
Mission 3

The argument from miracles

  • 1. Miracles happen. 2. Only God could cause them. 3. So God exists.
  • Strengths: eyewitness accounts across centuries; cures examined by doctors (Lourdes); lives changed; miracles fit a God who cares and acts.
  • Christians also see miracles as signs of who Jesus is (John 4:54) and of God's love.
  • Muslims: anyone can still examine the Qur'an, the miracle that remains.
  • Weaknesses: every step can be challenged.
Mission 3

Scientific and Humanist objections

  • Hume (Enquiry, 1748, Section X): our experience that the laws of nature hold is very strong; a report of a miracle is weaker. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."
  • Hume adds: few reliable witnesses; people love wonder stories; and miracles from rival religions cancel each other out.
  • Science: cures can come from spontaneous remission, the placebo effect, wrong diagnosis or coincidence.
  • Unexplained is not the same as explained by God: science may explain it later.
  • Unfairness: why would God heal one person and not millions of others? (Maurice Wiles, 1923–2005, God's Action in the World, 1986.)
  • Humanists: wonder at nature, but trust evidence; no miracle has passed a controlled test.
Mission 3

Religious responses

  • Careful testing: Lourdes doctors (believers and non-believers) reject most claims. The Church does not call every recovery a miracle.
  • Laws describe, God acts: laws of nature describe what normally happens; the God who made them can act in new ways.
  • Signs, not shows: miracles are rare by purpose, pointing to God, not solving every problem.
  • Muslims: the Qur'an's challenge (Surah 17:88) still stands for anyone to test.
  • Jews: hidden miracles in daily life matter more than spectacular ones.
  • Buddhists and Sikhs: belief does not rest on miracles anyway.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Miracles prove that God exists."

  • Reasons for: examined cures with no medical explanation (Lourdes); eyewitness accounts (John 4); changed lives.
  • Also for: the Qur'an as a lasting miracle anyone can examine.
  • Reasons against: Hume: testimony is weaker than experience of nature's laws; spontaneous remission and placebo; rival miracle claims; why heal some and not others?
  • Another view: a miracle may strengthen faith for those who already believe, without proving God to sceptics.
  • Judge it by: Is "no explanation yet" good evidence? Can testimony ever outweigh experience? Why are miracles rare?

Mission 4

What is a religious experience?

  • A religious experience: an experience in which a person believes they have encountered God or ultimate reality.
  • It may be sudden or gradual, alone or with others, ordinary or overwhelming.
  • William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), said mystical states are hard to put into words (ineffable), feel like knowledge (noetic), do not last long (transient), and happen to the person (passive).
  • For many believers it is the most direct evidence of God they have.
Mission 4

Kinds of religious experience

  • Conversion: a change of heart, belief or religion.
  • Mystical: a sense of union with God or ultimate reality, beyond words.
  • Numinous: awe and fear before a holy power. Rudolf Otto (1869–1937), The Idea of the Holy (1917): the holy is a mystery that makes us tremble yet draws us in.
  • Charismatic: gifts of the Holy Spirit, such as glossolalia (speaking in tongues: Acts 2:4), prophecy and healing.
  • Worship and sacraments: Catholics believe Christ is truly present in the Mass; many feel God's presence in prayer and liturgy.

The burning bush

"The LORD's angel appeared to him in a flame of fire out of the middle of a bush. He looked, and behold, the bush burnt with fire, and the bush was not consumed." (Exodus 3:2, WEBBE)

  • "Moses hid his face because he was afraid to look at God." (Exodus 3:6, WEBBE)
  • God reveals his name, "I AM WHO I AM" (Exodus 3:14), and sends Moses to free Israel.
  • For Jews, this is the call of Moses, greatest of the prophets; it leads to the Exodus and Sinai.

Mission 4

Christian attitudes: welcome and caution

  • CCC 66–67 (paraphrase): God's public revelation is complete in Christ; no new public revelation will come before his return.
  • Private revelations, even those the Church recognises, add nothing to faith; they can help people live it more fully. None can correct or go beyond Christ.
  • So the Church does not approve every experience: it tests whether one agrees with scripture and teaching, and looks at the person and the fruits.
  • Many Protestants test experiences by scripture: "test the spirits, whether they are of God" (1 John 4:1, WEBBE).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic Christians (including Catholic charismatics) expect experiences of the Spirit, such as glossolalia, today.
  • Quakers wait in silence for the inner light. Experience as revelation: God still makes himself known personally (Exodus 3 as the model).
Mission 4

Muslim attitudes

  • "Those who have no knowledge say: Why doth not Allah speak unto us, or some sign come unto us? … We have made clear the revelations for people who are sure." (Surah 2:118, Pickthall)
  • Muslims take from this: Allah's signs are already clear in the Qur'an and creation. Demanding a private experience is not needed for faith.
  • Yet believers find closeness to Allah in prayer: "Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!" (Surah 13:28, Pickthall)
  • Sunni: value prayer, Qur'an recitation and true dreams, but are cautious about claimed experiences.
  • Shi'a: deep experience in mourning for Imam Husayn (Muharram) and visiting shrines of the Imams.
  • Sufi: seek direct experience of Allah through dhikr (remembrance) and fana (passing away of the self); some Sunni scholars warn against claims that go beyond the Qur'an.
Mission 4Diversity

Jewish, Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh attitudes

  • Jews: Exodus 3 and Sinai are the great experiences of God. Many Jews meet God mainly through community, prayer and keeping the mitzvot; Kabbalah and Hasidism value mystical experience.
  • Hindus: Bhagavad Gita 12 praises bhakti: loving devotion to the personal Lord, who quickly lifts devotees out of rebirth (paraphrase). Gita 13.23–27: some see the Self within through meditation, some through knowledge, some through selfless action (paraphrase). Murti puja and darshan (seeing the deity) bring experience of the divine.
  • Buddhists: in the Kevatta Sutta, a monk asks the gods where the elements cease; even Great Brahma cannot answer and sends him to the Buddha (paraphrase). Experiences of gods are not final knowledge. The jhanas (meditative absorptions) and Zen kensho/satori (flashes of insight) matter only as steps to awakening.
  • Sikhs: vismad, wonder before God's creation, is a numinous experience. Guru Granth Sahib 448 describes love for God piercing mind and body (paraphrase); Guru Granth Sahib 1402 praises God as Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord") (paraphrase). Enlightenment (mukti) comes by God's grace through the Guru's word.
Mission 4

The argument from religious experience

  • 1. Millions of people, in every age and culture, report experiences of God. 2. The best explanation is that God exists. 3. So God probably exists.
  • Richard Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979), principle of credulity: if it seems to you that something is present, it probably is, unless there is a special reason to doubt (paraphrase).
  • Principle of testimony: we should believe what others report, unless there is reason to doubt them (paraphrase).
  • Lives changed by experience (Paul) add weight.
  • Divergent: many believers say an experience is strong evidence for the one who has it, but not proof for others.
Mission 4

Challenges from atheists and Humanists

  • Hallucination: illness, drugs, tiredness or fasting can produce powerful experiences.
  • Wish-fulfilment: Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Future of an Illusion (1927): belief in God answers our longing for a protective father.
  • Lack of evidence: private experiences cannot be checked by others.
  • Culture shapes experience: Christians meet Jesus, Hindus meet Krishna, Buddhists find no God. If the source were one God, why so different?
  • Lying or mistaken: some claims are invented; honest people can misread a feeling.
  • Humanists: the experiences are real, but have natural, psychological causes.
Mission 4

Religious responses

  • Fruits: "By their fruits you will know them." (Matthew 7:16, WEBBE) A lasting change for good (Paul) is hard to explain as illusion.
  • Shared experiences: at Pentecost a whole group was filled with the Spirit (Acts 2).
  • Testing: traditions check claims against scripture and teaching; most are not approved.
  • God may use natural means: brain activity during an experience does not show God is absent.
  • Different traditions, one reality? Some believers see differences as different responses to the same divine reality; others think their own tradition's account is the true one.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Religious experience proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: experiences are widespread; changed lives; some are shared.
  • Also for: principles of credulity and testimony (Swinburne).
  • Reasons against: hallucination, wish-fulfilment (Freud); cannot be checked; experiences differ between religions; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: religious experience may justify belief for the one who has it, without proving God to anyone else.
  • Church view: experiences must be tested; even approved ones add nothing to revelation in Christ (CCC 67).
  • Judge it by: Is a natural cause the whole story? Should we trust what seems real to us? Do differences between religions weaken every claim?

Mission 5

Mission 5: Everything that happens has a cause. Does the universe itself need one?

Make a guess: Everything that happens has a cause. Does the universe itself need one?

Mission 5

A chain of causes

  • Everything we see has a cause: you were caused by your parents, a tree by a seed, rain by clouds.
  • Each cause was itself caused by something earlier.
  • The First Cause argument asks: what started the chain?
  • Its answer: there must be a first cause that was not itself caused. Believers say this is God.
  • It is also called the cosmological argument (from kosmos, Greek for "ordered world"): it argues from the existence of the universe to God.
Mission 5

Aquinas' First Three Ways

  • Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Catholic priest and philosopher, Summa Theologiae (1265–1274), Part I, Question 2, Article 3: "five ways" to show God exists.
  • 1. Motion (change): things change, and each change is caused by something else. There must be a first mover, unmoved by anything.
  • 2. Cause: nothing causes itself, and the chain of causes cannot go back forever. There must be a first cause.
  • 3. Contingency: everything in nature could have not existed (it is contingent). If everything were like that, at some time there would have been nothing, and nothing would exist now. So there must be a necessary being: one that cannot not exist.
  • Aquinas ends each Way: this is what everyone calls God.
Mission 5Read the source

The Second Way

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, Q. 2, Art. 3 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "There is no case known (neither is it, indeed, possible) in which a thing is found to be the efficient cause of itself; for so it would be prior to itself, which is impossible."

> "Therefore it is necessary to admit a first efficient cause, to which everyone gives the name of God."

  • An efficient cause is whatever makes something happen or exist.

Q1 Why can nothing cause itself, according to Aquinas?

Show answer

It would have to exist before itself, which is impossible.

Q2 What does Aquinas say "everyone" calls the first cause?

Show answer

God.

Mission 5

Kalam: al-Ghazali

  • Kalam (Arabic: "speech", then "theology by reasoned argument").
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), Muslim theologian, Kitab al-Iqtisad fi'l-I'tiqad (The Moderation in Belief), c. 1095.
  • His argument (paraphrase):
  • 1. Everything that begins to exist has a cause.
  • 2. The universe began to exist: it cannot be infinitely old.
  • 3. So the universe has a cause, and that cause is God, who did not begin.
  • Note the wording: not "everything has a cause", but "everything that begins".
  • Many Muslims see the argument as reason confirming what the Qur'an teaches: Allah alone is the eternal Creator.

Mission 5Diversity

What the argument shows about God

  • Catholic teaching: human reason can know God exists from created things (CCC 36, paraphrase; First Vatican Council, 1870). The Ways show God as Creator, eternal, necessary and omnipotent.
  • Other Christians: many Protestants value the argument as support for faith, but say it cannot show God is loving or the God of the Bible; for that, revelation is needed.
  • Muslims: the heaven Allah built shows his power: "Are ye the harder to create, or is the heaven that He built?" (Surah 79:27, Pickthall). Kalam fits tawhid: one Creator, uncaused.
  • Jews: "By the word of the LORD were the heavens made" (Psalm 33:6, JPS 1917). In Isaiah 45, God alone forms light and darkness and made the earth (Isaiah 45:7–12, paraphrase).
  • Sikhs: the Mool Mantar (Guru Granth Sahib 1) calls God the one Creator, timeless and self-existent (paraphrase): uncaused, as the argument requires.
  • Most believers say the argument supports faith; few say it alone creates it.
Mission 5

Objections from atheists and Humanists

  • What caused God? If everything needs a cause, so does God. If God needs none, perhaps the universe needs none either.
  • The universe might just be there. Bertrand Russell (1872–1970), BBC radio debate with Frederick Copleston (1948): the universe is a brute fact that needs no explanation (paraphrase).
  • Parts and whole. Russell: every human has a mother, but the human race does not have a mother (paraphrase). A cause for each part does not prove a cause for the whole. A character in David Hume's (1711–1776) Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part IX, makes the same point.
  • The chain might go back forever. Why can't there be an infinite chain of causes?
  • Science: the Big Bang (about 13.8 billion years ago) may have a natural explanation. Humanists say "we don't yet know" is more honest than "God did it".
  • Which God? Even a first cause would not show it is loving, personal, or the God of any one religion.
Quick check

Mission 5

Religious replies

  • God is not caused. The argument never says "everything has a cause". Aquinas: contingent things need a cause, but God is necessary. Kalam: what begins needs a cause, but God is eternal.
  • An infinite chain explains nothing. A train of carriages stretching back forever still needs an engine to move at all.
  • The Big Bang fits a beginning. It was first proposed (1927, 1931) by Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), a Catholic priest and physicist. Many believers say science explains how; the argument asks why there is anything at all.
  • Which God? Believers agree the argument is limited. It shows a Creator; revelation and experience fill in the rest (see Mission 1).
  • Muslims add: the Qur'an invites people to reflect on creation, so reasoning to the Creator is a duty, not a rival to faith.
Mission 5Evaluate

"The First Cause argument proves that God exists."

  • Reasons for: everything we observe has a cause; nothing causes itself (Aquinas); the universe had a beginning (Big Bang), as kalam claims; a necessary being explains why anything exists.
  • Reasons against: "what caused God?"; the universe may be a brute fact (Russell); parts-and-whole (Hume); an infinite chain may be possible; the cause need not be the God of religion.
  • Another view: many believers say it shows God is reasonable to believe in, not proved; faith completes it.
  • Judge it by: Is "uncaused God" special pleading? Does "proof" mean certainty or good reason? Does the Big Bang help or hurt?
Mission 5Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why some religious believers think the cosmological (First Cause) argument shows God exists.

  • Reason 1: Nothing can cause itself, so the chain of causes needs a first cause. Source: Aquinas' Second Way. Development: an infinite chain would leave nothing to start it, so Catholics see God as the uncaused Creator.
  • Reason 2: Whatever begins to exist has a cause, and the universe began. Source: al-Ghazali's kalam argument; "Are ye the harder to create, or is the heaven that He built?" (Surah 79:27, Pickthall). Development: the eternal Creator explains the beginning; the Big Bang is taken as support.
  • (c) 5-mark tip: two reasons, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.

Mission 6

Order that looks planned

  • The design argument says the world shows signs of being planned, so it must have a designer: God.
  • It is also called the teleological argument (from Greek telos, "end" or "purpose").
  • It points to two things:
  • Order: nature follows regular laws (planets orbit, seasons repeat).
  • Purpose: parts work together for a goal (the eye for seeing, wings for flying).
  • Believers say order and purpose like this do not happen by chance.
Mission 6

Paley's watch

  • William Paley (1743–1805), Church of England priest, Natural Theology (1802).
  • Crossing a heath, you kick a stone: you might think it had always been there.
  • You find a watch: its springs and wheels are shaped and fitted together to tell the time. You conclude it had a maker (paraphrase).
  • You would think this even if you had never seen a watch made, or if it sometimes went wrong.
  • Living things, such as the eye, are far more complex than any watch, with parts fitted for a purpose.
  • So nature, Paley argues, needs an even greater designer: God.
Mission 6Read the source

Aquinas' Fifth Way

Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274), Summa Theologiae I, Q. 2, Art. 3 (English Dominican translation, 1920):

> "Whatever lacks intelligence cannot move towards an end, unless it be directed by some being endowed with knowledge and intelligence; as the arrow is shot to its mark by the archer."

  • Aquinas' design argument is from regularity: things without minds (planets, plants) act in regular ways towards an end.

Show answer

God, the intelligent being who directs nature.

Q2 Why can't the arrow reach its mark on its own?

Show answer

Mission 6

A universe tuned for life

  • Physicists find that some basic numbers of nature (such as the strength of gravity) fall in a narrow range that allows stars, planets and life.
  • Change them slightly, and no life could exist. This is called fine-tuning.
  • The anthropic principle (from Greek anthropos, "human"; named by physicist Brandon Carter in 1973): the universe has the features that allow observers like us.
  • Many believers read this as design: the universe was formed for humanity.
  • Others read it differently: we could only exist to notice it in a universe that allows life.

What design shows about God

  • Catholics: God is a wise, powerful and good designer. Evolution can be the way God works: Pope Pius XII (Humani Generis, 1950) and Pope John Paul II (1996) accepted evolution of the body as compatible with faith.
  • Many Protestants agree. Some evangelical creationists read Genesis 1 literally and reject evolution; some support intelligent design, which says some features are too complex to have evolved.
  • Muslims: nature is full of ayat (signs) of Allah; the Qur'an repeatedly asks people to reflect on them.
  • Jews: Genesis 1 shows God's ordered, good creation. Some Orthodox Jews read it literally; many Jews read the six days non-literally.
  • Sikhs: God is the Creator and is immanent in creation, so the order of nature reveals God.
Mission 6

Objections from atheists and Humanists

  • Weak comparison. David Hume (1711–1776), Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779): the universe is not much like a machine, so we cannot be sure it had a maker like a watch's.
  • Which designer? Hume's character Philo says the world could be "the first rude essay of some infant deity" (Dialogues, Part V): perhaps a team of gods, or a careless one.
  • Evolution. Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859): natural selection produces living things fitted for their surroundings, step by small step, without a designer.
  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The Blind Watchmaker (1986): nature's only "watchmaker" is blind natural selection (paraphrase).
  • Evil and suffering. Diseases, parasites and earthquakes look like poor design for a loving God.
  • Fine-tuning by chance. If there are many universes, or we could only exist in one that allows life, fine-tuning needs no designer. Humanists say science explains order better than a designer does.

Mission 6

Religious replies

  • Evolution is how God designs. Many Christians, Jews and Muslims accept evolution and see the laws that make it possible as designed. F. R. Tennant (1866–1957), Philosophical Theology (1930), argued that the whole process, not single organs, shows design.
  • Fine-tuning comes before evolution. Natural selection cannot explain why the universe allows life at all.
  • One designer is simpler. One God explains order more simply than many gods (a rule of thumb called Ockham's razor).
  • Suffering may have purposes: free will, or the growth of souls (see Mission 7).
  • Muslims: the signs are clear "for people who have sense" (Surah 2:164); the argument invites reflection, not blind belief.
  • Creationists reply differently: they reject evolution as an account of origins.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The design argument shows that God exists."

  • Reasons for: order and purpose look planned (Paley, Aquinas); fine-tuning allows life; scripture says creation reveals God (Romans 1:20; for Muslims, Surah 2:164 calls nature signs of Allah).
  • Reasons against: evolution explains apparent design (Darwin); the analogy is weak (Hume); suffering suggests poor design; chance or many universes could explain fine-tuning.
  • Another view: the argument supports faith for those who already believe, but does not convince on its own.
  • Judge it by: Does evolution remove the need for a designer, or push design back to the laws of nature? Is the watch analogy strong? Can a designer's goodness be read from a world with suffering?

Mission 7

Mission 7: Believers have lived through floods, plagues and genocide and kept faith. What answers do they give?

Make a guess: Believers have lived through floods, plagues and genocide and kept faith. What answers do they give?

Mission 7

Three kinds of answer

  • A theodicy ("justifying God") tries to show why a good, all-powerful God allows evil. The word was coined by Gottfried Leibniz (1710).
  • Philosophical answers: free will, soul-making.
  • Scriptural answers: Job, the Psalms, life as a test, the suffering of Christ.
  • Practical answers: prayer, charity, care for those who suffer.
  • Most believers use all three. Many say: we may not fully explain suffering, but we can trust God and act.
Mission 7

Augustine: free will and privation

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions, City of God, Enchiridion.
  • Augustine teaches that God made a good world. Evil is not a thing God made: "evil has no positive nature; but the loss of good has received the name 'evil.'" (Augustine, City of God XI.9, Dods)
  • Angels and humans misused free will: the fall of the angels and of Adam and Eve.
  • Original Sin: all humans inherit a damaged nature; natural evil follows the disorder sin brought into creation.
  • So evil comes from creatures, not from God. God is just to allow its consequences, and merciful to save through Christ.
Mission 7

Irenaeus and Hick: soul-making

  • St Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies: humans are made in God's image, but must grow into his likeness (Genesis 1:26).
  • Humans are made immature on purpose, so they can grow freely into goodness.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966): the world is a "vale of soul-making" (a phrase from the poet Keats).
  • Courage, compassion and forgiveness can only grow where there is real danger and pain.
  • Epistemic distance: God keeps a distance, so that people are free to believe or not.
  • Hick: in the end all people will reach the likeness of God (universal salvation), so all suffering will prove worthwhile.

Mission 7

Do these answers work?

  • Against Augustine: science shows death and disease long before humans existed, so natural evil cannot all come from the Fall. How could perfect beings choose evil? Why punish all humans for Adam's sin?
  • Against soul-making: the amount of suffering is too great: the Holocaust destroyed people, it did not make souls. Babies and animals suffer without growing. Is it loving to use pain as a teacher?
  • For both: free will is real only if evil is possible; virtues need challenges; God's goodness may reach beyond this life (Romans 8:18).
  • Atheists and Humanists: the answers explain away real pain; a world with less suffering would still allow free will.
  • Divergent Christian views: many Catholics hold both together; some Protestants stress mystery and trust over explanation; liberal Christians question an all-controlling God.

Mission 7

Judaism: Job, Psalms and the Shoah

  • Life as a test: "God did prove Abraham" (Genesis 22:1, JPS 1917).
  • Practical responses: prayer (psalms of lament and trust) and tzedakah (charity, a duty of justice).

  • Richard Rubenstein (1924–2021), After Auschwitz (1966): belief in a God who controls history is no longer possible (paraphrase).
  • Elie Wiesel (1928–2016): faith that argues with God, in the tradition of Job and Abraham.

  • "By his wounds we are healed." (Isaiah 53:5, WEBBE) Christians read this as pointing to Christ's death.

  • Humanists: use reason, science and compassion to reduce suffering now; this life is the only one, so it matters.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Revelation is God making himself known: special (prophets, visions, miracles, Jesus, scripture received) and general (nature, conscience, reason, scripture read by all).
  • Christians see Jesus as the culmination of revelation (Hebrews 1:1–4); Muslims see the Qur'an as Allah's word revealed through Jibril.
  • Problems: conflicting revelations, alternative explanations, lying or mistake; believers reply with tests of scripture, tradition and fruits.

Mission 2

  • A vision is a form of special revelation: seeing or hearing something sacred. Examples: Abram (Genesis 15), the Transfiguration (Matthew 17), Joan of Arc.
  • Maryam and Jibril (Surah 19:16–22); Sunni, Shi'a and Sufi Muslims judge visions by the Qur'an.
  • Atheists and Humanists explain visions as hallucinations, wish-fulfilment or expectation; believers point to shared visions, fruits and testing by tradition.
  • Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh traditions value visions differently: Arjuna's vision of Krishna, Asanga and Maitreya, mystical union through simran.

Mission 3

  • A miracle is an extraordinary event, beyond the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God and to carry meaning: John 4:43–54; Lourdes (Bernadette Moriau).
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as the greatest miracle (Surah 17:88).
  • The argument from miracles faces Hume's challenge, scientific explanations and the "why some?" problem; believers reply with careful testing and the meaning of signs.

Mission 4

  • Religious experience: conversion, mystical, numinous, charismatic, worship and sacraments.
  • Exodus 3 (the burning bush) and Acts 9 (Saul on the Damascus road) are key examples.
  • Catholics welcome but test experiences: private revelations add nothing to Christ (CCC 66–67); Muslims see Allah's signs as already clear (Surah 2:118).
  • Challenges: hallucination, wish-fulfilment, lack of evidence, cultural differences; responses: fruits, shared experiences, testing.

Mission 5

  • The First Cause (cosmological) argument reasons from the existence of the universe to an uncaused cause: God.
  • Aquinas' First Three Ways argue from motion, cause and contingency; al-Ghazali's kalam argues from the universe's beginning.
  • Objections: what caused God? the universe as brute fact; parts and whole; infinite chains; science; which God? Believers reply that God is necessary and uncaused.

Mission 6

  • The design (teleological) argument reasons from order and purpose in nature to a designer: Paley's watch, Aquinas' Fifth Way, fine-tuning and the anthropic principle.
  • Romans 1:20 teaches that creation shows God's power, and for Muslims Surah 2:164 calls nature signs of Allah; traditions differ on evolution and on how to read Genesis 1.
  • Objections: Hume (weak analogy, which designer?), Darwin's natural selection, evil and suffering, chance; replies: evolution as God's method, fine-tuning, simplicity.

Mission 7

  • Augustine: evil is a privation of good, caused by misused free will (the Fall). Irenaeus and Hick: the world is a vale of soul-making. Both face objections (evolution; the amount of suffering).
  • Job: trust God beyond understanding; practical responses: prayer and charity; Humanists act to reduce suffering.
  • Psalm 119:71: affliction can bring a believer closer to God's laws.
  • Muslims see life as a test (Surah 2:155–156, 67:2) met with sabr, prayer and charity.
  • Jews respond through Job, the Psalms, tzedakah and, after the Shoah, very different theologies.
