Islam: Worship

This mini-lesson covers the whole of Section I1 — Worship from the Cambridge IGCSE Religious Studies (0490) Islam content: the mosque and its features, the artefacts used in worship, public and private worship (adhan, wudu, salah, Jumu'ah, du'a, dhikr) and the leaders of the mosque.

Cambridge rewards two things: knowledge (what happens) and understanding (what it means and why it matters). Every screen here gives you both. Answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

A note on the diagrams: the images in this lesson use shapes, words and patterns only. Most Muslim traditions avoid pictures of humans and animals in a place of worship, and this lesson respects that.

I1.1 · the mosque

The mosque: a centre for worship

The Arabic word masjid means place of prostration — the place where Muslims bow down (sujud) before God. That single word tells you what a mosque is fundamentally for: salah, formal prayer.

  • A centre for worship. Muslims may pray anywhere that is clean, so a mosque is not strictly necessary. What it adds is congregation: praying shoulder to shoulder, in rows, behind one leader.
  • A centre of the ummah. The ummah is the worldwide community of Muslims. A local mosque is where that global idea becomes a real, visible community: it hosts madrasah classes for children, marriages and funerals, Ramadan iftars, zakah and sadaqah collection, advice, and welfare for those in need.

Exam-grade point: the very first thing Prophet Muhammad (pbuh) did on arriving in Madinah was build a mosque. It was not only a prayer hall — it was the meeting place, the court, the school and the welfare centre of the new community. That is why Cambridge asks about the mosque as the centre of the ummah, not just as a building.

I1.2 · key features

Key features of a mosque

Mosques look very different around the world — a domed mosque in Istanbul, a mud-brick mosque in Mali, a converted terraced house in Bradford. But certain features recur, each with a meaning:

  • Minaret ("site of light") — the tower from which the adhan is called. In many countries it is now a loudspeaker; in Britain, some councils restrict the call, so mosques use apps or a caller inside.
  • Dome — a symbolic representation of the vault of heaven. It also carries sound across the hall.
  • Qiblah — the direction of Makkah. Every worshipper faces it, so the whole ummah faces one point: a physical expression of tawhid, the oneness of God.
  • Mihrab — the niche in the qiblah wall that marks the direction. The imam stands at it; its curved shape helps carry his voice.
  • Minbar — the stepped platform from which the khutbah (sermon) is delivered, so the speaker can be seen and heard.
  • Musalla — the prayer hall: open, carpeted, no chairs or pews, so worshippers can form straight rows and prostrate.

I1.2 · art, calligraphy & space

Calligraphy, geometry — and no images

Walk into a mosque and you will see no statues and no pictures of people or animals. Instead you will see calligraphy (beautifully written verses of the Qur'an and the names of God) and geometric and floral patterns, often repeating without end.

Why? Two reasons Cambridge wants you to give:

  • Tawhid. God is one and cannot be pictured. An image risks shirk — associating something with God — and could become an object of worship in itself.
  • Creation belongs to God. Making living images can be seen as imitating God's act of creating life.

The positive side matters too: calligraphy turns the word of God itself into the decoration, and endless geometric patterns suggest a God who is infinite. The art directs attention to God rather than to a human artist or a human face.

Separate areas for men and women. Most mosques provide separate rows, a screened area or a separate room. The usual reasons given are modesty and concentration — prostration is a physically close, exposed posture, and the point is undistracted focus on God. Muslims themselves disagree about how this should work in practice: some mosques have women praying in the same hall in rows behind the men (as in the Prophet's mosque in Madinah), others provide a separate hall with a video link, and some Muslim women argue that poor facilities effectively exclude them. A strong answer notes the reason and the range of practice.

I1.3 · artefacts

Artefacts used in worship

Islam has no sacraments and no relics to venerate, but Muslims do use practical objects. Each one serves worship rather than being worshipped:

  • Copies of the Qur'an (mushaf) — the words of God. Often placed on a wooden stand (kursi) and kept on the highest shelf.
  • Compass — used to find the qiblah, essential when travelling, in a hotel room, or at sea. Today most Muslims use a phone app, which does exactly the same job.
  • Prayer mat (sajjadah) — guarantees a clean place to pray anywhere. It is usually decorated with an arch, echoing the mihrab, to point towards Makkah.
  • Turbah — a small tablet of clay, used by many Shi'a Muslims, who place the forehead on it during prostration so that the head touches the natural earth. The clay often comes from Karbala. Most Sunni Muslims do not use one.
  • Misbahah — prayer beads, usually 33 or 99, used to count phrases of dhikr such as "Subhan Allah" (Glory be to God). Some Muslims prefer to count on the fingers, following the Prophet's practice.

Different Muslim communities: notice that the turbah and the misbahah are places where practice genuinely differs. Cambridge expects you to know that Islam is not uniform — and to explain a difference without ranking one group above another.

I1.4 · public worship

Adhan, wudu and salah

Adhan — the call to prayer. The mu'azzin calls out that God is greatest, that there is no god but God and Muhammad is His messenger, and that people should come to prayer and to success. It is a public announcement that turns ordinary time into prayer time; it is also whispered into a newborn's ear, so a Muslim's first and daily words are the same.

Wudu — ablution. Before salah a Muslim washes in a set order: hands, mouth, nose, face, arms to the elbows, wiping the head, then the feet. It is done with running water at the mosque's washing facilities.

Its significance is not hygiene. Wudu is a ritual purification: it marks the boundary between the everyday and standing before God. Physically washing away dust becomes a symbol of washing away distraction and sin, and the deliberate, unhurried routine settles the mind into the right state (niyyah, intention) for prayer.

Salah — the five daily prayers. Fixed times, fixed words, fixed movements, facing the qiblah:

Different Muslim communities: all Muslims perform the five prayers, but many Shi'a Muslims combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at three times of day. Many Shi'a also pray with the arms at the sides, while most Sunni Muslims fold them. Same salah, different practice.

I1.4 · the rak'ah

The rak'ah: what actually happens

A rak'ah is one complete cycle of the prayer. Each prayer is a set number of them — Fajr has 2, Maghrib has 3, and Zuhr, Asr and Isha have 4 (the obligatory, fard, part).

stand → bow → stand → prostrate → sit → prostrateqiyam · ruku · qawmah · sujud · jalsah · sujud — then the next rak'ah begins
  • Qiyam (standing) — recite Surah al-Fatihah, the opening chapter, and another passage of the Qur'an. The body is upright and attentive.
  • Ruku (bowing) — hands on the knees, back level. A bow of respect, as before a king.
  • Sujud (prostration) — forehead, nose, palms, knees and toes on the ground. The lowest possible position of the body: total submission. The word islam itself means submission, and muslim means one who submits.
  • Tashahhud — sitting to declare faith, ending with taslim: turning the head right and left with the greeting of peace.

Why the movements matter: salah trains the whole person. The body enacts what the words say. Doing it five times a day, in rows with strangers, rich beside poor, is a repeated, physical rehearsal of both tawhid and equality. That is the "significance" mark Cambridge is looking for.

I1.4 · Jumu'ah, du'a, dhikr, the Qur'an

Friday prayer, private prayer and the Qur'an

  • Jumu'ah — the Friday midday congregational prayer. It replaces Zuhr and is obligatory for adult men to attend in congregation (women may attend but are generally not obliged). It includes a khutbah, a sermon from the minbar, which may address community issues and current affairs — so Jumu'ah teaches and unites as well as worships. Its significance: the ummah made visible, once a week, in one place.
  • Du'a — private, personal prayer. Unlike salah it has no fixed words, times or postures: a Muslim raises the hands, praises God and the Prophet (pbuh), then asks in their own language for whatever they need — an exam, a sick relative, forgiveness. It is often made straight after salah, and Muslims are encouraged to prepare by being in a state of wudu, facing the qiblah, and asking with sincerity and confidence.
  • Dhikr — remembrance of God: repeating phrases such as "Subhan Allah", "Alhamdulillah" and "Allahu Akbar", or the 99 Beautiful Names, often counted on a misbahah. It can be done while walking or working, keeping God constantly in mind. Sufi groups may hold communal dhikr with rhythm and movement; other Muslims prefer it silent and individual, and some are critical of communal forms.

The Qur'an in worship — different views. In public worship the Qur'an is recited aloud from memory in Arabic, often by a hafiz (someone who has memorised it all); recitation (tajwid) is treated as an art. In private, Muslims read and study it, and many use a translation alongside the Arabic. Muslims differ on how much weight to give translations: all agree the Qur'an is the Arabic text and a translation is only an interpretation of its meaning, but some argue strongly for study in translation so that the meaning shapes daily life, while others stress learning the Arabic itself.

Respectful treatment. Muslims usually perform wudu before touching a copy of the Qur'an; keep it above other books, never on the floor; wrap it in a clean cloth; place it on a stand (kursi) when reading; do not eat, drink or talk while it is open; and dispose of a worn copy respectfully rather than throwing it away. All of this expresses one belief: this is the literal word of God.

I1.5 · leadership

The imam and the mu'azzin

The imam literally means "the one in front" — the person who stands in front and leads the salah. Any adult male Muslim of good character who knows the prayer can do it; in practice a mosque employs someone learned, often a hafiz, to lead the five prayers, deliver the Friday khutbah, teach the children, conduct marriages and funerals, and advise the community.

  • There is no priesthood. An imam is not ordained, cannot forgive sins, and does not mediate between a Muslim and God — every Muslim approaches God directly. His authority comes from knowledge and trust, not from a sacred office.
  • A crucial difference in vocabulary. For Shi'a Muslims the word Imam also carries a much larger meaning: the divinely appointed, rightful leaders of the ummah descended from the Prophet (pbuh) — beginning with Imam 'Ali. So "the imam" at a mosque and "the Imams" of Shi'a belief are not the same thing. Watch for this in exam questions.

The mu'azzin is the official who calls the adhan — traditionally from the minaret, today usually over a microphone. He must have a clear, strong voice and know the prayer times. Muslims remember Bilal ibn Rabah, a freed Black African enslaved man, as the first mu'azzin chosen by the Prophet (pbuh) — a powerful sign that in Islam status comes from piety, not race or wealth.
