Language diversity · language and powerScreen 2

Face, politeness and indirectness

  • Face (Goffman, 1967): the public self-image we want to protect. Brown and Levinson (1978; 1987): positive face = the wish to be liked; negative face = the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Orders, requests and criticism are face-threatening acts (FTAs). Choice of strategy depends on power, social distance and size of imposition.
StrategyInvented example
Bald on record'Sit down.'
Positive politeness (stress closeness)'Shall we all grab a seat, guys?'
Negative politeness (avoid imposing)'Could you possibly sit down?'
Off record (hint)'Plenty of chairs free.'
  • Grice (1975): hearers assume we cooperate, so a hint creates an implicature they must work out.
Language diversity · language and powerScreen 3

Powerful and powerless speech

  • In unequal encounters the powerful speaker opens and closes the talk, sets topics, asks the questions, can phrase orders as questions (indirect directives), may interrupt, and chooses terms of address (first name downwards, title upwards).
  • O'Barr and Atkins (1980): low-status witnesses in US trials, of both sexes, used more hedges and intensifiers: powerless language.
  • Synthetic personalisation (Fairclough, 1989): mass texts address everyone as one friendly 'you'.
  • Edexcel also lists political discourse, persuasion and prestige: ask who gains from each choice.

Mission checkScreen 9

?How does a headteacher's 'Shall we sit down?' differ from a friend's?

From a friend, 'Shall we sit down?' is a real suggestion between equals that can be turned down. From a headteacher it is a directive: the question form and inclusive 'we' are politeness strategies that soften the face threat, but the head's instrumental power means the student can't really say no.

Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 1

Saying and doing

  • Pragmatics studies meaning in context: what a speaker means, not only what the words say.
  • Austin (1962) showed that utterances are speech acts: the locution (the words said), the illocution (what the speaker is doing: requesting, warning, promising) and the perlocution (the effect on the listener).
  • A direct speech act matches form and function: Shut the window is an imperative used as a command.
  • An indirect speech act doesn't: It's cold in here is a declarative that can work as a request (Searle 1975).
Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 2

Grice: reading between the lines

  • Grice (1975): conversation works because we assume speakers are cooperating: the Cooperative Principle.
  • Four maxims: Quantity (as much information as needed), Quality (say what's true), Relation (be relevant), Manner (be clear).
  • We assume It's cold in here is relevant, so we ask why it's said now, and infer a request: an implicature.
  • A speaker who openly flouts a maxim creates an implicature; one who covertly violates it misleads (a lie violates Quality).

Language levels · pragmatics and discourseScreen 4

Politeness and face

  • Face is the public self-image people want to keep (from Goffman). Brown and Levinson (1987): positive face is the wish to be liked; negative face, the wish not to be imposed on.
  • Requests, orders and criticism are face-threatening acts. Speakers choose a strategy:
  • Positive politeness: friendliness, compliments, in-group terms (mate), inclusive we.
  • Negative politeness: hedges, apologies, modal verbs, indirectness (Could I possibly…?).
  • Off record: a deniable hint (It's cold in here). Bald on record: no softening (Shut the window).

Discourse and cohesion

  • Discourse is how a whole text or conversation is organised: openings, sequences, closings. Discourse markers (so, anyway, right) signal shifts.
  • Cohesion is the grammatical and lexical ties that hold a text together (Halliday and Hasan 1976):
TypeInvented example
Reference: anaphoric (back) or cataphoric (forward)The window's open. Can you shut it?
SubstitutionI need a jumper. Have you got one?
EllipsisWho opened the window? Sam. (opened the window is left out)
Conjunctionso, however, because, then
Lexical cohesionrepetition, synonyms, a field: cold, freezing, chilly

Mission checkScreen 9

?‘It’s cold in here.’ How does the listener know to shut the window?

The listener infers it. The words are a statement, but in context (an open window, a cold speaker) the listener assumes the remark is relevant and works out the implicature. As an off-record request, it lets the speaker ask without imposing, protecting both people's face.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 1

From topic to question

  • Start with an area of interest, then narrow it to a research question your own data can answer.
  • A good question is specific, answerable from the data and often comparative (two groups, two settings).
  • You may add a hypothesis, a prediction to test, but your conclusion can reject it.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 3

Collecting data

  • Choose the type: spoken, written, multimodal, word lists, or attitudes (surveys, interviews).
  • Collect enough to see patterns, and keep the conditions comparable.
  • Observer's paradox (Labov, 1972): we want to study how people speak when not being observed, but we can only find out by observing them. Reduce it by recording for longer and using later sections.
  • Transcribe spoken data with a key (pauses, overlaps); give speakers labels.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 4

Choosing methods

  • Quantitative: count features and compare (e.g. number of questions per speaker per hour), shown in tables or charts.
  • Qualitative: close analysis of examples in context, using the language levels and frameworks (e.g. politeness, turn-taking).
  • Strong investigations combine both: counts show the pattern, examples explain it.
  • Evaluate your method: sample size, what you couldn't control, what you'd do next.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 5

Ethics

  • Informed consent: tell people what you're studying and get their permission, spoken or written.
  • Under 16: you also need a parent's or carer's permission.
  • Right to withdraw: anyone can pull out, even after agreeing.
  • Anonymity: no real names or identifying details; use labels (Speaker A, Parent 1). Keep recordings secure.
  • No secret recording: BAAL guidance calls deception unacceptable. If knowing the aim would change how people speak, give the general purpose, or explain straight after and delete the data if anyone refuses.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Power can be instrumental (from a role) or influential (from persuasion) (Wareing); Fairclough separates power in discourse from power behind it.
  • Brown and Levinson's face and politeness strategies, and Grice's implicature, explain how indirect forms can carry commands.
  • In unequal encounters the powerful control topics, turns and address; O'Barr and Atkins linked 'powerless' features to status.
  • Speech acts (Austin 1962; Searle 1975): locution, illocution, perlocution; direct and indirect speech acts.
  • Grice (1975): the Cooperative Principle and four maxims; flouting creates implicature, violating misleads.
  • Brown and Levinson (1987): positive and negative face; positive, negative, off-record and bald-on-record strategies.
  • Discourse structure and cohesion (Halliday and Hasan 1976): reference, substitution, ellipsis, conjunction, lexical cohesion.
  • Pragmatics and discourse are Edexcel's two key language frameworks.
  • Narrow a topic into a specific, answerable, often comparative research question; a hypothesis is optional and can be rejected.
  • Match data and method to the question: combine quantitative counts with qualitative analysis, and evaluate your method's limits. Reduce the observer's paradox.
  • Ethics: informed consent (parents too for under-16s), right to withdraw, anonymity, no secret recording.
