Mission 1

Mission 1: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Make a guess: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Mission 1

Coulomb's law

  • The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and to 1/r²: an inverse-square law.
  • It acts along the line joining them: like charges repel, unlike attract. Each exerts an equal and opposite force on the other.
  • F = Q1Q2/(4πε0r²)
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹, the permittivity of free space.
  • Treat air as a vacuum.
Mission 1

Spheres act as points

  • A uniformly charged sphere acts, outside itself, like a point charge at its centre.
  • So r is measured centre to centre.
  • Two spheres 2.0 cm across with a 3.0 cm gap: r = 1.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 5.0 cm.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • +3.0 nC and +5.0 nC, centres 4.0 cm apart, in air.
  • F = (3.0 × 10⁻⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ (4π × 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040²) = 8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N.
  • Both positive: repulsion.
  • Halve r: force × 2² = 4 times: 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • Double one charge: force × 2.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres whose centres are 0.60 m apart exert electric forces of magnitude 1.0 N on each other. Their charges do not change. At what centre-to-centre separation would the forces have a magnitude of 9.0 N?
Mission 1

More than two charges

  • Find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • A (+2.0 nC) at x = 0; B (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m; C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Force on B:
  • From A: repulsion, pushes B in +x: 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ N.
  • From C (0.20 m away): attraction, pulls B towards C, also +x: 6.7 × 10⁻⁷ N.
  • Resultant: 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ N in +x.
  • Not in a line? Resolve into components, add, then use Pythagoras.
Quick check
?Three point charges are fixed on the x-axis: P (+4.0 nC) at x = 0, Q (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m and R (+2.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric force on Q?
Mission check

?Two small charged spheres exert electric forces of magnitude F on each other. The charge on each sphere is halved, and the distance between them is reduced to one third of its value. What is the magnitude of the force now?

Force ∝ 1/r². Halving the distance makes the force 4 times bigger; a third of the distance makes it 9 times bigger.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Make a guess: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Mission 2

The electric field

  • Electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Charges create it; it is there even when nothing is placed in it.
  • E = F/Q: the force per unit positive charge.
  • A vector. Unit: N C⁻¹.
  • Direction: the push on a positive charge: away from +, towards −.
  • A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.
Quick check
?At point P, a charge of +2.0 µC experiences an electric force of 5.0 × 10⁻³ N directed east. This charge is removed and a charge of −6.0 µC is placed at P; the charges that produce the field do not move. Taking east as positive, what is the electric force on the −6.0 µC charge?
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sketches the electric field lines around two equal positive point charges a short distance apart. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 2

Field lines

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines start on + charges and end on − charges (or run to or from far away).
  • Lines never cross: the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines are drawn so their density matches E.
  • Patterns: single + (radially out) · single − (radially in) · + and − pair · two + (a neutral point between) · parallel plates.
  • A field line is not the path a charge follows.
Mission 2

The field of a point charge

  • E = Q/(4πε0r²)
  • Inverse square: double r, E falls to ¼.
  • Field lines agree: the same lines pass through every sphere round the charge, area 4πr². Line density ∝ 1/r².
  • A charged sphere: outside, its field is that of a point charge at its centre.
  • +4.0 nC, at 0.20 m: E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.20² = 9.0 × 10² N C⁻¹, away from the charge.
Mission 2

Adding fields

  • Several charges: add their fields as vectors.
  • +4.0 nC and +1.0 nC, 0.40 m apart. Where between them is E = 0?
  • There the two fields point opposite ways. Equal when 4.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)², x measured from the 4.0 nC charge.
  • Square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 2(0.40 − x) = x and x = 0.27 m.
  • The neutral point is nearer the smaller charge.
Quick check
?The diagram shows two point charges, Q1 = +4.0 nC and Q2 = −2.0 nC, fixed on a line, and a point P on the same line. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric field strength at P?
Mission 2

The uniform field between plates

  • Two parallel plates, p.d. V, separation d.
  • Between them, away from the edges: a uniform field, the same strength and direction everywhere. Lines parallel, evenly spaced, + to −.
  • E = V/d. Unit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
  • 200 V across plates 4.0 cm apart: E = 200 ÷ 0.040 = 5.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹.
  • Force on a proton: eE = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, the same anywhere between the plates.
Mission check

?The field lines of a long, straight charged wire spread out radially, in planes at right angles to the wire: they spread in two dimensions, not three. Point P is 2.0 cm from the wire and point Q is 6.0 cm from it, both far from the wire's ends. How does the electric field strength at P compare with that at Q?

Lines are drawn so that their density matches E. Closer lines, stronger field. A point charge's lines spread over spheres (area ∝ r²), so E ∝ 1/r²; a long wire's spread over cylinders (area ∝ r), so E ∝ 1/r.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Make a guess: If the electric field at a point is zero, must the electric potential there be zero too?

Mission 3

Electric potential

  • Electric potential at a point: the work done per unit charge to bring a small positive test charge from infinity to that point.
  • Zero at infinity, by definition.
  • A scalar: it has a sign but no direction.
  • Unit: J C⁻¹ = volt (V).
  • Set by the source charges, not by the test charge: any test charge at P, of any size or sign, sees the same V.
Mission 3

Potential of a point charge

  • V = Q/4πε0r
  • Falls as 1/r, not 1/r² (field strength falls as 1/r²).
  • Positive charge: V positive, highest near the charge. Negative charge: V negative.
  • Example: +4.0 nC. At 0.20 m, V = 180 V.
Mission 3

Potentials add as numbers

  • Several charges: add the potentials, with their signs. No angles, no components.
  • Two charges of +4.0 nC, 0.30 m apart. Midpoint M is 0.15 m from each.
  • Field at M: equal and opposite → E = 0.
  • Potential at M: 240 V + 240 V = 480 V. Not zero.
  • E = 0 does not mean V = 0. The field depends on how V changes, not on its value.
Mission 3

Work and potential difference

  • Work done moving charge q: W = qΔV
  • Worked example: move +2.0 nC from 0.20 m to 0.10 m from the +4.0 nC charge.
  • ΔV = 360 − 180 = 180 V. Work done on it by an external force (moved slowly) = 2.0 × 10⁻⁹ × 180 = 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J.
  • Released at 0.10 m, it is pushed outwards. By 0.20 m it has gained 3.6 × 10⁻⁷ J of kinetic energy.
  • A negative charge does the opposite: it speeds up moving towards higher V.
  • Only the start and end potentials matter, not the path.
Quick check
?A point charge of +5.0 nC is fixed in place. A second charge of +2.0 nC is moved slowly from a point 0.30 m from the fixed charge to a point 0.10 m from it. Take 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the work done on the +2.0 nC charge by the external force?
Mission 3

Equipotentials

  • Equipotential: a surface (or line on a 2D map) where V is the same everywhere.
  • Moving along one: ΔV = 0, so no work is done.
  • Always perpendicular to field lines.
  • The field points towards lower potential.
  • Point charge: concentric spheres. Equal steps of V get closer together near the charge.
  • Uniform field between plates: parallel planes, evenly spaced.
Mission 3

How the field follows from the potential

  • E = −ΔV/Δr
  • The minus sign: the field points down the potential gradient, towards lower V.
  • The field depends on how V changes, not on its value.
  • Uniform field: E = V/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
  • Point charge: E at r = −(gradient of the tangent) to the V–r curve. Its size is how steep the curve is.
  • Steep curve → strong field. Flat (maximum or minimum) → E = 0, whatever V is.
Quick check
?In a region of uniform electric field of strength 4.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹ directed in the +x direction, point A at x = 0 has an electric potential of +500 V. What is the electric potential at point B, at x = 0.050 m?
Mission check

?Point charges +Q and −Q are fixed a short distance apart, and M is the midpoint between them. A student claims: 'The potential at M is zero, so no work is needed to bring a small test charge from infinity to M, and the test charge feels no electric force at M.' Which evaluation of the claim is correct?

Potential and field strength are different things. Between two equal positive charges, E = 0 but V is not zero; between +Q and −Q, V = 0 but E is not zero.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Make a guess: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Quick check
?Predict first. An uncharged parallel-plate capacitor is connected to a battery and left until it is fully charged. Which statement correctly describes how the capacitor becomes charged?
Mission 4

What a capacitor is

  • Two conducting plates, separated by an insulator (air, a vacuum or a plastic film).
  • Connect a cell: electrons are pulled off one plate, pass round the external circuit, and are pushed onto the other plate.
  • No charge crosses the gap.
  • One plate ends at +Q, the other at −Q. Net charge: zero.
  • "The charge on a capacitor" means Q, the charge on each plate.
Mission 4

Capacitance

  • C = Q/V: capacitance is the charge stored per unit p.d.
  • Unit: the farad. 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹: a capacitor storing 1 C on each plate for every 1 V across it.
  • 1 F is huge. Real values: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • For one capacitor, Q ∝ V. C is fixed by its construction, not by the p.d. you apply.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.
  • Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C on each plate.
  • Electrons moved from one plate to the other: 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹⁶.
  • Charge it to 18 V instead: Q doubles; C stays 470 μF.
Quick check
?A parallel-plate capacitor is connected to an ideal 12 V battery. When the capacitor is fully charged, its positive plate carries a charge of 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ C. What is the capacitance of the capacitor?
Mission 4

What it stores: energy

  • Separating +Q from −Q takes work. That work is stored as electric potential energy.
  • Graph of p.d. against charge: a straight line through the origin (V = Q/C).
  • Moving a small charge ΔQ across a p.d. V takes work VΔQ: a thin strip under the line.
  • Add the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle.
  • W = ½QV
Mission 4

Three forms, one example

  • Use Q = CV: W = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C.
  • The 470 μF capacitor at 9.0 V: energy = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = 1.9 × 10⁻² J.
  • At 18 V: the charge doubles and the potential difference doubles, so the energy is four times as much (7.6 × 10⁻² J).
  • Why ½? The cell moves charge Q through p.d. V and transfers QV. The capacitor keeps ½QV; the other ½QV is dissipated as heat in the circuit's resistance.
Quick check
?A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a potential difference of 12 V. How much energy does it store?
Mission check

?The graph shows the magnitude Q of the charge on each plate of a capacitor as a function of the potential difference V across it. What energy is stored in the capacitor when V = 6.0 V?

The plates' charges cancel, so a capacitor stores no net charge. It stores energy: the work done separating +Q from −Q, equal to ½QV.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Make a guess: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Mission 5

Charging, step by step

  • Cell (emf ε), resistor R and uncharged capacitor C in series. Close the switch.
  • At the start: VC = 0, so the whole emf is across R. The current is largest: I0 = ε/R.
  • Electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other. VC rises.
  • p.d. across R = ε − VC falls, so the current falls, so charging slows.
  • After a long time: VC = ε, current zero. Fully charged.
Mission 5

Discharging

  • Now connect the charged capacitor across R alone.
  • The capacitor's p.d. drives the current: I = VC/R. Electrons flow back to the plate they left. The current is reversed compared with charging.
  • Charge leaves → VC falls → current falls → charge leaves more slowly.
  • The rate of loss is proportional to the charge left. That is exponential decay.
  • It gets ever closer to zero but, in this model, never reaches it.
Mission 5

The graphs

  • Charging: Q and VC rise steeply, then level off at the final value. Current starts at I0 and decays to zero.
  • Discharging: Q, VC and I all decay with the same shape.
  • Q and VC always have the same shape, because Q = CVC.
  • Gradient of a Q–t graph = current.
  • Area under an I–t graph = charge that has flowed.
Quick check
?A capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. At time t1 the p.d. across the capacitor is one quarter of the battery's emf. At a later time t2 it is three quarters of the emf. What is the current at t2 divided by the current at t1?
Mission 5

The time constant

  • Time constant τ = RC. Unit: Ω × F = s.
  • Discharging: after one τ, 37% of the starting charge is left.
  • Charging: after one τ, the charge has reached 63% of its final value.
  • After about 5τ: over 99% of the way. Treat as finished.
  • Bigger R → smaller current → slower. Bigger C → more charge to move → slower.
  • The starting p.d. does not change τ.
Mission 5

The equations

  • Discharging: Q = Q0e−t/RC. The same form gives V = V0e−t/RC and I = I0e−t/RC.
  • At t = RC: e⁻¹ = 0.37, so 37% left; 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.63 reached.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor, charged to 9.0 V, discharges through 10 kΩ.
  • τ = RC = 10 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.7 s.
  • Initial current: I0 = 9.0 ÷ 10 × 10³ = 0.90 mA.
  • After 5.0 s: V = 9.0 × e−5.0/4.7 = 3.1 V.
  • Time to fall to 1.0 V: e−t/4.7 = 1.0/9.0, so t = 4.7 × ln 9.0 = 10 s.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a 3.0 kΩ resistor by an ideal battery of emf 9.0 V, all connected in series. What is the current in the resistor at the instant when the potential difference across the capacitor is 6.0 V?
Quick check
?A 50 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V and then discharged through a 200 kΩ resistor. What is the potential difference across the capacitor 15 s after the discharge starts?
Mission check

?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. Its charge takes a time t to reach 63% of its final value. The capacitor is emptied. The battery is replaced by one with twice the emf, and a second, identical resistor is added in series with the first. How long does the charge now take to reach 63% of its new final value?

The pace is set by RC alone: the time constant. Every RC, the charge falls to 37% of what it was, so it gets close to empty but never quite there.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Mission 6

Equal times, equal fractions

  • Exponential decay: in equal time intervals, the charge falls by the same fraction.
  • Every RC: × 0.37. Every 0.69RC: × ½.
  • Test for exponential: read V at equal intervals. Equal ratios V1/V0 = V2/V1 = … → exponential.
  • Example: 12.0 V, 8.0 V, 5.4 V, 3.6 V every 2.0 s: each ratio ≈ 0.67.
Mission 6

A straight line from a curve

  • Take natural logs of V = V0e−t/RC: ln V = ln V0 − t/RC.
  • Compare with y = c + mx. Plot ln V against t.
  • Straight line → the decay is exponential.
  • Gradient = −1/RC. Intercept = ln V0.
  • Same for charge and current: ln Q = ln Q0 − t/RC; ln I = ln I0 − t/RC.
  • Logs need a pure number: plot ln V with V in volts (the log of the number of volts).
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A capacitor discharges through 100 kΩ. A graph of ln V (V in volts) against t is a straight line.
  • Gradient = −0.20 s⁻¹. Intercept = 2.48.
  • Time constant = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 s.
  • C = time constant ÷ R = 5.0 ÷ (100 × 10³) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ F (50 μF).
  • V0 = e2.48 = 12 V.
Quick check
?A student plots ln(I/A) against t for a capacitor discharging through a 20 kΩ resistor. The graph is a straight line of gradient −0.025 s⁻¹. What is the capacitance?
Mission 6

The practical

  • Circuit: d.c. supply, two-way switch, capacitor, resistor. Voltmeter (or data logger) across the capacitor, or an ammeter in series with R.
  • Charge fully. Switch to discharge and start timing.
  • Slow decay (time constant about a minute): read V every 10 s by hand.
  • Fast decay: use a data logger or an oscilloscope.
  • Plot ln V against t; time constant = −1/gradient.
  • A voltmeter across the capacitor is a second discharge path: use a very high-resistance meter.
  • Capacitor values have wide tolerances (often ±20%): expect the time constant to differ from the value worked out from the labels.
  • Core practical 11: oscilloscope or data logger.
Quick check
?A student measures the time constant of a 10 μF capacitor discharging through a 1.0 MΩ resistor, so the time constant should be 10 s. A voltmeter of resistance 10 MΩ stays connected across the capacitor throughout. How does the measured time constant compare with 10 s?
Mission check

?A student plots ln I (I in amperes) against t for a discharging capacitor. The best-fit straight line passes through ln I = −6.20 at t = 0 and ln I = −7.70 at t = 30 s. How long does the current take to fall to half of any value?

ln V (or ln I, ln Q) against t is a straight line of gradient −1/(time constant). The curve never reaches zero, but the line gives the time constant directly.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Coulomb's law F = Q1Q2/(4πε0r²): inverse-square, along the line of centres; like repel, unlike attract.

Mission 2

  • E = F/Q: force per unit positive charge; field lines show its direction, and their density its strength.
  • Point charge: E = Q/(4πε0r²); parallel plates: uniform, E = V/d.

Mission 3

  • Potential = work done per unit positive charge from infinity; V = kQ/r = Q/4πε0r; a scalar, so potentials simply add.
  • The field is the potential gradient: equipotentials are ⟂ to field lines, and E = 0 does not mean V = 0.

Mission 4

  • A capacitor's plates carry +Q and −Q; C = Q/V, in farads, set by construction, not by the p.d.
  • Energy stored W = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C: the area under the V–Q graph.

Mission 5

  • Charging: Q and V rise to a limit while I decays; discharging: Q, V and I all decay exponentially.
  • τ = RC: 37% left after one τ (discharging), 63% reached (charging).
  • Discharge: Q = Q0e−t/RC (same form for V and I).

Mission 6

  • ln V = ln V0 − t/RC: plot ln V against t; gradient = −1/RC.
