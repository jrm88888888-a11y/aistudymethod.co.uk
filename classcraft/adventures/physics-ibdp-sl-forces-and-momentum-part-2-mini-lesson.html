Mission 1

Mission 1: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Make a guess: How could you test whether a crown is pure gold without damaging it?

Mission 1

Density

  • Density = mass per unit volume: ρ = m/V.
  • SI unit: kg m⁻³. Also common: g cm⁻³.
  • 1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³. Water: about 1.0 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³.
  • Density belongs to the material, not the object. Cut a block in half: half the mass, half the volume, same ρ.
  • Rearranged: m = ρV and V = m/ρ.
Quick check
?The density of aluminium is 2.7 g cm⁻³. What is the volume, in m³, of an aluminium block of mass 0.081 kg?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A brass cylinder: radius 1.0 cm, height 5.0 cm, mass 133 g.
  • V = πr²h = π × 1.0² × 5.0 = 15.7 cm³.
  • ρ = m/V = 133 ÷ 15.7 = 8.5 g cm⁻³.
  • In SI: 8.5 g cm⁻³ × 1000 = 8.5 × 10³ kg m⁻³.
  • Convert at the end, or work in kg and m³ throughout. Never mix g with m³.
Mission check

?A coin that looks like silver has a mass of 26.9 g. Lowered into a measuring cylinder of water, it raises the reading by 3.0 cm³. The table gives densities in kg m⁻³: aluminium 2.70 × 10³; copper 8.96 × 10³; silver 10.5 × 10³; lead 11.3 × 10³. Which metal is the coin most likely made of?

Same idea for the crown: find its mass, find its volume by displacement, and compare ρ = m/V with gold's 19.3 × 10³ kg m⁻³. No damage needed.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Make a guess: A steel ship floats, but a steel bolt sinks. What decides which?

Mission 2

Archimedes' principle

  • A fluid pushes up on an object in it: buoyancy, Fb.
  • Archimedes' principle: it equals the weight of the fluid displaced.
  • Fb = ρVg: ρ = density of the fluid; V = volume of fluid displaced (the submerged volume).
Quick check
?A solid steel sphere and a solid aluminium sphere have the same volume. Both hang from threads, completely under water: the steel sphere 0.20 m below the surface and the aluminium sphere 0.50 m below it. Steel is about three times as dense as aluminium. How do the buoyancy forces on the two spheres compare?
Mission 2

Float or sink?

  • Fully submerged object, volume V: upward force ρfluid Vg; weight ρobject Vg.
  • ρobject > ρfluid: weight wins. It sinks.
  • ρobject < ρfluid: it rises until only part is submerged, then floats.
  • Floating: upward force = weight. It displaces its own weight of fluid.
  • Fraction submerged = ρobject / ρfluid.
  • A steel ship is mostly air inside: its average density is less than water's.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A 2.0 kg stone, volume 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ m³, hangs from a spring balance, fully under water (ρ = 1000 kg m⁻³).
  • Fb = ρVg = 1000 × 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 9.8 = 7.8 N. Weight = 2.0 × 9.8 = 19.6 N.
  • Balance reading = 19.6 − 7.8 = 11.8 N.
  • The stone feels lighter in water, but its weight has not changed.
Quick check
?A foam ball of volume V would float, but it is held at rest completely under water by a light string tied to the bottom of a tank. The density of the foam is a quarter of the density ρ of water. Which expression gives the tension in the string?
Mission check

?A wooden block floats in water (density 1000 kg m⁻³) with 60% of its volume below the surface. It is then placed in oil of density 800 kg m⁻³. What fraction of its volume is below the surface of the oil?

Floating or sinking is set by density: the ship's steel hull encloses so much air that its average density is below water's, so it floats once it has displaced its own weight of water. A solid bolt cannot.

Make a guess: Why does fine silt keep water cloudy for hours, when sand settles in seconds?

Mission 3

Viscous drag and Stokes' law

  • Viscosity η: a fluid's resistance to flow; internal friction between layers. Unit: Pa s (N s m⁻²).
  • Honey: high η. Water: low η (about 1 × 10⁻³ Pa s at room temperature).
  • A sphere moving through a fluid feels a viscous drag force opposing its motion.
  • Stokes' law: Fd = 6πηrv (r = radius of the sphere, v = its speed).
  • Drag ∝ r and ∝ v.
Quick check
?Sphere X moves through an oil at speed v and feels a viscous drag F. Sphere Y, with twice the radius of X, moves through the same oil at half the speed. Stokes' law applies. What is the viscous drag on Y?
Mission 3

Terminal velocity of a sphere

  • Three forces on a falling sphere: weight (down), buoyancy (up), viscous drag (up).
  • As it speeds up, drag grows. At terminal velocity: weight = buoyancy + drag. Resultant force = 0.
  • (4/3)πr³ρs g = (4/3)πr³ρf g + 6πηrv
  • Rearranged: v = 2r²g(ρs − ρf) / 9η.
  • v ∝ r²: halve the radius, and the terminal velocity is a quarter.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Steel ball bearing, radius 1.5 mm, ρs = 7800 kg m⁻³, falling in oil: ρf = 920 kg m⁻³, η = 0.85 Pa s.
  • Weight = (4/3)π(1.5 × 10⁻³)³ × 7800 × 9.8 = 1.08 × 10⁻³ N. Buoyancy = 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • At terminal velocity, drag = 1.08 × 10⁻³ − 1.3 × 10⁻⁴ = 9.5 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • v = F/6πηr = 9.5 × 10⁻⁴ ÷ (6π × 0.85 × 1.5 × 10⁻³) = 0.040 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A small aluminium sphere of radius 0.50 mm falls at terminal velocity through glycerol. The density of aluminium is 2.7 × 10³ kg m⁻³. The density of glycerol is 1.26 × 10³ kg m⁻³ and its viscosity is 1.4 Pa s. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the terminal velocity of the sphere?
Mission check

?Two water droplets in fog fall at terminal velocity through still air. Droplet P has twice the radius of droplet Q. Stokes' law applies to both. What is the terminal velocity of P divided by the terminal velocity of Q?

v ∝ r². A silt grain 1/100 the radius of a sand grain settles 10 000 times more slowly: seconds for sand become hours for silt.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A cannon fires a ball forwards. Why does the cannon roll backwards, and more slowly than the ball?

Make a guess: A cannon fires a ball forwards. Why does the cannon roll backwards, and more slowly than the ball?

Mission 4

Momentum

  • Momentum p = mv: mass × velocity.
  • Unit: kg m s⁻¹ (the same as N s).
  • A vector: same direction as the velocity. Choose a positive direction; the other way is negative.
  • A 0.20 kg ball hits a wall at 5.0 m s⁻¹ and bounces straight back at 5.0 m s⁻¹. Its momentum changes, from +1.0 to −1.0 kg m s⁻¹, though its speed does not.
Quick check
?A cricket ball of mass m moving horizontally with speed v is struck by a bat. It leaves the bat moving horizontally with speed 3v/2 in the opposite direction. Taking the ball's initial direction of motion as positive, what is the change in the ball's momentum?
Mission 4

Conservation of momentum

  • The total momentum of a system stays constant unless a resultant external force acts on it.
  • Why: in a collision, A pushes B and B pushes A with equal and opposite forces (Newton's third law), for the same time.
  • So A's change in momentum is equal and opposite to B's. The total does not change.
  • Total momentum before = total momentum after. Add the momenta with signs.
Mission 4

Worked example: a collision

  • Glider A, 1.5 kg, moves at 4.0 m s⁻¹ along an air track into glider B, 0.50 kg, at rest. They stick together.
  • Before: p = 1.5 × 4.0 + 0.50 × 0 = 6.0 kg m s⁻¹.
  • After: (1.5 + 0.50) × v = 2.0v.
  • 2.0v = 6.0, so v = 3.0 m s⁻¹, in A's original direction.
Mission 4

Explosions

  • Everything starts at rest: total momentum = 0. So it is still 0 afterwards.
  • Two pieces: they move off in opposite directions, with equal and opposite momenta.
  • 2.0 kg and 3.0 kg blocks pushed apart by a spring. The 3.0 kg block moves at 1.2 m s⁻¹.
  • 2.0 × v = 3.0 × 1.2 = 3.6, so v = 1.8 m s⁻¹ the other way. Lighter piece: faster.
  • Kinetic energy increases (0 → 5.4 J): it comes from energy stored in the compressed spring (or in the explosive, for a cannon).
Quick check
?Two blocks, of masses 1.0 kg and 4.0 kg, are held at rest on a horizontal surface with negligible friction, with a compressed spring between them. When they are released, the 1.0 kg block moves off at 2.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about the 4.0 kg block is correct?
Mission 4

Elastic or inelastic?

  • Kinetic energy Eₖ = ½mv².
  • Momentum is conserved in every collision (no resultant external force). Kinetic energy is not always.
  • Elastic: total kinetic energy unchanged.
  • Inelastic: total kinetic energy decreases. It becomes internal (thermal) energy, sound, and energy of permanent deformation.
  • Perfectly inelastic: the bodies stick together and share one velocity. Biggest loss of kinetic energy.
  • Total energy is always conserved; only the kinetic part changes.
  • Test it with the glider collision: kinetic energy before = ½ × 1.5 × 4.0² = 12 J; after = ½ × 2.0 × 3.0² = 9.0 J. 3.0 J transformed into other forms: inelastic.
Mission 4

Kinetic energy from momentum

  • Eₖ = ½mv². Multiply top and bottom by m: Eₖ = m²v² / 2m.
  • Eₖ = p² / 2m.
  • Check with glider A: p = 6.0 kg m s⁻¹, m = 1.5 kg → Eₖ = 36 ÷ 3.0 = 12 J. ✓
  • Same momentum, smaller mass: more kinetic energy. In an explosion from rest, the lighter piece carries more of the energy.
Quick check
?A 0.50 kg trolley moving at 5.0 m s⁻¹ collides with a 3.0 kg trolley at rest on a level track with negligible friction. After the collision, the 0.50 kg trolley moves backwards at 1.0 m s⁻¹ and the 3.0 kg trolley moves forwards at 1.0 m s⁻¹. How much of the system's kinetic energy is transformed into other forms of energy?
Mission check

?A person of mass 60 kg stands in a rowing boat of mass 120 kg. Both are at rest, and water resistance is negligible. The person jumps horizontally from the boat onto a jetty, leaving at 1.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about the boat immediately afterwards is correct?

Total momentum starts at zero, so it stays zero: equal and opposite momenta. The cannon recoils for the same reason: same mv as the ball, much bigger m, so a much smaller v.

Make a guess: Why can a stunt performer walk away from a fall onto an airbag that would kill on concrete?

Mission 5

Impulse

  • Impulse J = FΔt, F = the average resultant force, Δt = contact time.
  • J = Δp: the impulse equals the change in momentum.
  • Unit: N s, the same as kg m s⁻¹.
  • A vector: in the direction of the resultant force, which need not be the direction of motion.
Mission 5

Worked example: a rebound

  • A 0.060 kg ball hits a wall at 25 m s⁻¹ and rebounds at 15 m s⁻¹. Contact time 0.020 s.
  • Take away from the wall as positive.
  • u = −25 m s⁻¹, v = +15 m s⁻¹.
  • Δp = m(v − u) = 0.060 × (15 − (−25)) = 2.4 N s, away from the wall.
  • Average force = 2.4 ÷ 0.020 = 120 N, away from the wall.
  • Trap: 0.060 × (15 − 25) = −0.60 N s. Velocity is a vector; the direction reverses.
Quick check
?A sledge on level snow is pulled horizontally with a constant force of 60.0 N for 4.00 s. Throughout this time it slides forwards and the frictional force on it is 22.0 N. What is the magnitude of the impulse delivered to the sledge by the resultant force during the 4.00 s?
Mission 5

Area under a force–time graph

  • Real impacts: the force rises and falls. It is not constant.
  • Impulse = area under the F–t graph = change in momentum.
  • Average force = area ÷ contact time.
  • Area below the time axis = impulse in the negative direction.
Quick check
?Each graph shows the resultant force F exerted on a different trolley, P, Q or R, as a function of time t. Which ranking of the magnitudes of the impulses delivered to the trolleys is correct?
Mission 5

Longer contact, smaller force

  • Stopping: the change in momentum is fixed by the mass and the speed.
  • Longer stopping time → smaller average force.
  • A 70 kg occupant stops from 14 m s⁻¹: Δp = 980 N s.
  • Seat belt and crumple zone, 0.12 s: F ≈ 8.2 kN. Hard dashboard, 0.012 s: F ≈ 82 kN.
  • Same idea: airbags, bicycle helmets, packaging foam, bending your knees on landing.
Quick check
?Sand falls vertically onto a horizontal conveyor belt at a steady rate of 0.80 kg s⁻¹. The belt moves at a constant 2.5 m s⁻¹, and the sand quickly reaches the speed of the belt. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the horizontal force that the belt exerts on the sand?
Mission check

?Two identical 0.50 kg gliders on an air track each move at 2.0 m s⁻¹ towards an obstacle. Glider 1 hits a steel buffer and bounces straight back at 2.0 m s⁻¹; the contact lasts 0.010 s. Glider 2 hits a foam pad and stops; the contact lasts 0.040 s. How many times larger is the average force on glider 1 than the average force on glider 2?

Same change in momentum, longer time, smaller force. The airbag stops the performer over a much longer time than concrete, so the average force is many times smaller.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Make a guess: A car rounds a bend at a steady speed. Is it accelerating, and if so, how much?

Mission 6

The radian

  • Angle in radians: θ = arc length ÷ radius = s/r.
  • 1 radian: the angle at the centre when the arc length equals the radius.
  • Full circle: s = 2πr, so 2π rad = 360°. 1 rad ≈ 57.3°.
  • Convert: degrees × π/180 → radians.
Mission 6

Period, frequency, speed

  • Period T: time for one full revolution (s).
  • Frequency f: revolutions per second (Hz). f = 1/T.
  • One revolution = one circumference, 2πr. Constant speed: v = 2πr/T.
Mission 6

Angular velocity

  • Angular velocity ω: angle swept per second, in rad s⁻¹.
  • One revolution sweeps 2π rad in time T: ω = 2π/T = 2πf.
  • v = ωr.
  • Every point on a rigid turning object has the same ω. Points further out have larger v.
Quick check
?A bicycle wheel of diameter 0.700 m rotates at a steady rate about a fixed axle, making one complete revolution every 0.250 s. What is the linear speed of a point on the rim of the wheel?
Mission 6

Steady speed, changing velocity

  • Velocity is a vector. Going around a circle, its direction keeps changing.
  • Changing velocity = acceleration, even at constant speed.
  • Draw v1 and v2 at two nearby points. The change, Δv = v2 − v1, points towards the centre.
  • So the acceleration points to the centre: centripetal acceleration.
  • It needs a resultant force towards the centre.
Mission 6

Centripetal acceleration

  • a = v²/r = ω²r = 4π²r/T², towards the centre.
  • Example: a child on a spinning playground ride, 1.5 m from the centre, one revolution every 4.0 s.
  • v = 2πr/T = 2π × 1.5 ÷ 4.0 = 2.4 m s⁻¹.
  • ω = 2π/T = 2π ÷ 4.0 = 1.6 rad s⁻¹.
  • a = v²/r = 2.36² ÷ 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻², towards the centre.
  • Check: ω²r = 1.57² × 1.5 = 3.7 m s⁻².
Quick check
?Car X rounds a curve of radius 50 m and car Y rounds a curve of radius 100 m. Both cars move at the same constant speed. How does the magnitude of car X's centripetal acceleration compare with that of car Y?
Mission 6

Speeding up on a circle

  • Speed changing → an extra component along the path: tangential acceleration, at = rate of change of speed.
  • Direction changing → centripetal acceleration, ac = v²/r, towards the centre.
  • The two are perpendicular. Net acceleration = their vector sum: a = √(ac² + at²).
  • Speeding up: a points ahead of the centre.
  • Only at constant speed does the acceleration point exactly at the centre.
Quick check
?A car moves around a horizontal circular track and its speed is decreasing. Which statement about the resultant force on the car is correct?
Mission 6

Recap: three ways to write it

  • Given v and r: a = v²/r.
  • Given ω (or f) and r: a = ω²r, with ω = 2πf.
  • Given T and r: ω = 2π/T, so a = 4π²r/T².
  • Always use the radius, not the diameter. ω in rad s⁻¹, not degrees per second.
Mission check

?The drum of a washing machine has a diameter of 0.50 m. It rotates about a horizontal axis at constant speed, with a time period of 0.050 s. What is the centripetal acceleration of a sock pressed against the inside of the drum?

Constant speed is not constant velocity. The sock's direction keeps changing, so it accelerates towards the centre of the drum, with a = v²/r.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outwards?

Make a guess: Whirl a ball on a string in a circle. Is anything pulling it outwards?

Mission 7

Centripetal force is the resultant

  • Circular motion at constant speed: acceleration v²/r towards the centre.
  • Newton's second law: it needs a resultant force towards the centre: the centripetal force.
  • F = mv²/r = mω²r, towards the centre.
  • It is perpendicular to the velocity: it changes the direction, not the speed.
  • It is not an extra force. It is the resultant of real forces: tension, friction, gravity, normal force.
Mission 7

No outward force

  • Nothing pulls the ball outwards. The string pulls it inwards.
  • Cut the string: the ball flies off along the tangent, in a straight line (first law). Not outwards along the radius.
  • The "flung outwards" feeling in a turning car: your body keeps going straight while the car turns under you.
  • On a free-body diagram, never draw a "centripetal force" arrow or a "centrifugal force" arrow. Draw only real forces.
Quick check
?A cyclist rides around a flat circular track at a constant speed of 8.0 m s⁻¹. Which statement about her motion is correct?
Quick check
?A car travels at constant speed around a bend on a flat horizontal road; its path is part of a horizontal circle. Air resistance is negligible. Which statement about the horizontal forces on the car is correct?
Mission 7

Worked example: a car on a flat bend

  • A 1200 kg car rounds a flat bend of radius 50 m at 15 m s⁻¹.
  • Only friction from the road acts sideways, so friction is the centripetal force.
  • F = mv²/r = 1200 × 15² ÷ 50 = 5400 N, towards the centre.
  • If the greatest friction the tyres can give is 7000 N: vmax = √(Fr/m) = √(7000 × 50 ÷ 1200) = 17 m s⁻¹.
  • Faster than that: friction cannot supply mv²/r, and the car skids towards the outside of the bend.
Mission 7

Vertical circles: top and bottom

  • Take towards the centre as positive.
  • (T here is tension, not period.)
  • Bottom (centre above): T − mg = mv²/r. Tension is more than the weight.
  • Top (centre below): T + mg = mv²/r. Tension is less than mv²/r.
  • Loop-the-loop, at the top: the track pushes down, N + mg = mv²/r.
  • Minimum speed at the top: N = 0, gravity alone gives the centripetal force: mg = mv²/r → v = √(gr).
Quick check
?A ball of mass 0.20 kg on a string of length 0.50 m is whirled in a vertical circle. At the top of the circle its speed is 3.0 m s⁻¹. Take g = 9.8 m s⁻². What is the tension in the string at the top?
Mission check

?A car drives at constant speed over the top of a rounded hill in the road. Near the top, the road's vertical cross-section is a circular arc. The car stays in contact with the road. At the top, how does the normal force from the road compare with the car's weight?

Nothing pulls the ball outwards. The string's inward pull is the centripetal force; cut it and the ball flies off along the tangent.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ρ = m/V, in kg m⁻³ (1 g cm⁻³ = 1000 kg m⁻³); density belongs to the material, not the object.

Mission 2

  • Fb = ρVg: the weight of fluid displaced (ρ of the fluid, V displaced).
  • An object floats if its average density is less than the fluid's; a floating object displaces its own weight.

Mission 3

  • Stokes' law Fd = 6πηrv for a small sphere in a fluid.
  • At terminal velocity weight = buoyancy + drag, so v = 2r²g(ρs − ρf)/9η ∝ r².

Mission 4

  • p = mv, a vector. With no resultant external force, total momentum is conserved in every collision and explosion.
  • Elastic: total Eₖ unchanged. Inelastic: total Eₖ decreases. Explosions: Eₖ increases from a stored energy.

Mission 5

  • Impulse = FΔt = Δp: the area under a force–time graph; a longer contact time means a smaller average force.
  • F = ma is for constant mass; F = Δp/Δt also handles changing mass (sand on a belt: F = (Δm/Δt)v).

Mission 6

  • ω = 2π/T = 2πf (rad s⁻¹); v = ωr.
  • Constant speed in a circle means acceleration towards the centre: a = v²/r.
  • Also a = ω²r.
  • If the speed is changing too, add a tangential component; the net acceleration no longer points at the centre.

Mission 7

  • Centripetal force is the resultant of the real forces, towards the centre: F = mv²/r. There is no outward force.
  • Vertical circle: bottom T − mg = mv²/r; top T + mg = mv²/r; minimum speed at the top of a loop v = √(gr).
