Mission 1: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Make a guess: Guyana's income per person is more than three and a half times Sri Lanka's. Why do they have exactly the same HDI?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

Mission 1

What is development?

  • Development: improvement in people's lives and in a country's economy.
  • Economic view: how much wealth and income a country has.
  • Social view: health, education, housing, food and water.
  • Political view: freedom, rights and how honestly a country is governed.
Mission 1

Classifying countries

  • The World Bank groups countries by GNI per head (2025 data, used from July 2026):
    • low income: $1,175 or less · lower-middle: $1,176–$4,635 · upper-middle: $4,636–$14,375 · high: more than $14,375
  • The UK is high income, India lower-middle income and Rwanda low income.
  • OCR uses the IMF's groups: ACs (advanced countries), EDCs (emerging and developing countries) and LIDCs (low-income developing countries, eligible for the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust).
  • Rwanda borrows through the IMF's Extended Credit Facility, a facility of the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust, so it is an LIDC. The UK is an AC and India an EDC.
Mission 1

Economic measures

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the value of everything produced inside a country in a year.
  • GNI (gross national income): GDP plus income earned abroad by its people and firms, minus income sent out by foreign firms.
  • Divide by population to get per head (per capita) figures.
  • GNI per head, 2025: UK $54,550, India $2,760, Rwanda $1,150.

Mission 1

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • The HDI combines three dimensions into one score from 0 to 1:
    • a long and healthy life (life expectancy)
    • knowledge (years of schooling)
    • a decent standard of living (GNI per head)
  • 2023: UK 0.946 (13th), India 0.685 (130th), Rwanda 0.578 (159th) of 193 countries.

Mission 1

Why income cannot buy a high HDI

  • In the HDI, each extra dollar counts for less and less: income is scored on a logarithmic scale, and any income above $75,000 counts the same as $75,000.
  • The three dimension scores are multiplied together (then the cube root is taken), not simply averaged. A weak score pulls the total down, and a strong one cannot fully make up for it.
  • So Guyana's high income is held back by its lower health and education scores.
Mission 1

Limitations of measures

  • Averages hide inequality: GNI per head says nothing about how income is shared.
  • Money is not well-being: Guyana's real GDP grew 43.8% in 2024 after oil and gas production began; health and schooling change much more slowly.
  • Data quality: the World Bank lists India's adult literacy as 81.7% for 2023 but 78.2% for 2024; different surveys can give different answers.
  • Missing parts: no single measure covers rights, safety or the environment; even the HDI leaves them out.
  • Single measures (GNI) are simple; composite ones (HDI) are broader but still incomplete.
Mission 1

Exam move: evaluating a measure

  • Say what it measures, give a strength and a limitation, and use data from named countries.
  • Finish with a judgement: use more than one measure, because each shows only part of development.
Mission check

?Country A's income per head is four times Country B's, yet Country A has a lower HDI. What is the most likely reason?

Same HDI, different lives. Sri Lanka: life expectancy 77.5, mean schooling 10.8 years. Guyana: 70.2 and 8.7, but income of $46,959 against $12,616. Because extra income counts for less and less and the three scores are multiplied, one strong dimension cannot buy a high HDI.

Mission 2

Income groups

  • The World Bank sorts countries by GNI per capita. For 2026–27:
    • low income: $1,175 or less
    • lower-middle income: $1,176–4,635
    • upper-middle income: $4,636–14,375
    • high income: more than $14,375
  • 25 low, 47 lower-middle, 59 upper-middle and 87 high-income economies.
  • GNI per capita is a national average: the country's income divided by its population. One label covers the whole country, so its regions can be far richer or poorer than the label suggests.
Mission 2

The global pattern

  • Low income: 21 of the 25 are in sub-Saharan Africa (e.g. Niger, Rwanda).
  • High income: Europe and Central Asia has the most (40); others are in the Americas, East Asia and the Pacific, and the Gulf (e.g. the UK, Japan, Qatar).
  • Middle income covers most of Asia and Latin America: India is lower-middle, China upper-middle.
  • Pattern: a rich "North" and poorer "South", with many exceptions (Australia, Singapore).

Mission 2

OCR's labels: AC, EDC, LIDC

  • Advanced countries (AC): "well-developed financial markets" and "diversified economic structures with rapidly growing service sectors".
  • Low-income developing countries (LIDC): eligible for the IMF's Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust.
  • Emerging and developing countries (EDC): neither advanced nor eligible for that trust.
  • All three are "as classified by the IMF". ACs cluster in North America, Western Europe, East Asia and Australasia; LIDCs are mostly in sub-Saharan Africa and parts of Asia.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Make a guess: Why does a baby born in Norway expect to live about 25 years longer than one born in Somalia?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 6.1b human and physical factors influencing global uneven development
  • 6.1b factors that trap countries in poverty: debt, trade, political unrest
Mission 3

The development gap

  • The development gap is the difference in wealth and wellbeing between the richest and poorest countries.
  • UNDP's Human Development Index (2023): Iceland is top at 0.972; South Sudan is bottom of 193 countries at 0.388.
  • Life expectancy at birth: Norway 83.3 years, Somalia 58.8, a gap of about 24.5 years (our calculation).
  • OCR uses AC, EDC and LIDC.
Mission 3

Physical factors

  • Landlocked: no coast, so trade goes through neighbours. Landlocked developing countries paid $3,204 to export a container against $1,268 for transit countries (World Bank data, 2014).
  • The UN estimates their development is "about 20 percent lower than it would be were they not landlocked".
  • Disease: in 2024 the WHO African Region had 95% of the world's malaria cases and deaths. Illness keeps children out of school and adults out of work.
  • Hazards and climate: drought, floods and tropical storms destroy crops, homes and roads again and again.
Mission 3

Historical factors

  • Colonialism: from the 1500s to the mid-1900s, European powers ruled much of Africa, Asia and the Americas.
  • Colonies were organised to export raw materials (cotton, rubber, minerals) to the ruling country, which made the profitable goods.
  • Railways and ports often ran from mines and plantations to the coast, not between towns.
  • Many countries became independent with few factories, few trained officials and borders drawn by others.
Quick check
?Which is a physical (environmental) factor that holds back development?
Mission 3

Economic: relying on primary products

  • Primary products: raw materials such as crops, minerals and oil, sold unprocessed.
  • UNCTAD counts a country as commodity-dependent when commodities make up more than 60% of its goods exports: 95 of 142 developing countries were, in 2019–2021. In Africa it was 45 of 53.
  • Raw materials earn less than processed goods, and their prices rise and fall sharply, so income is hard to plan.
Mission 3

Debt

  • Poor countries borrow from banks, governments and international lenders to build roads, dams and schools.
  • If export earnings fall, repayments and interest take money that could pay for health and education.
  • The HIPC debt-relief scheme: of 39 eligible countries, 36 had completed it by February 2023; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • Debt can trap a country in poverty (a poverty trap), because each year's repayments leave less to invest.
Mission 3

Trade and geo-political relationships

  • Trade is buying (imports) and selling (exports) goods and services between countries.
  • Powerful countries and companies set many of the rules and prices of world trade.
  • Geo-political relationships: alliances, former colonial ties and aid agreements decide who can sell where, and on what terms.
  • OCR: unfair trade can trap a country, because it earns too little from exports to pay debts or invest.
Mission 3

Trade blocs

  • A trade bloc is a group of countries that agree to trade with fewer barriers between them.
  • The EU single market allows "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons" between its members.
  • Members gain easy access to each other's markets. Countries outside may face tariffs (taxes on imports) or rules that make selling harder.
Mission 3

Political unrest

  • Conflict, coups and corruption scare away investors and tourists, close schools and clinics, and destroy roads and crops.
  • The World Bank estimates that severe conflicts "lower GDP per capita by about 15% after five years".
  • OCR: political unrest traps countries, because rebuilding uses money that could fund development.
Quick check
?Which sequence best describes a poverty trap?
Mission check

?A landlocked country earns most of its export income from one crop, coffee. The world coffee price halves, and the country owes large debts in US dollars. What is the most likely result?

Uneven development has many linked causes: physical (landlocked, disease, hazards), historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade). Political conflict adds to them. They add up, which is why a baby in Somalia expects to live about 24.5 years less than one in Norway.

Mission 4

Rwanda: where and what

  • A small landlocked country in central Africa, bordered by Uganda, Tanzania, Burundi and DR Congo.
  • Known as "the Land of a Thousand Hills".
  • 14.6 million people (2025); about 600 people per km² (2024).
  • The World Bank classes Rwanda as low income: GNI per person US$1,150 (2025).
  • Rwanda borrows through the IMF's Extended Credit Facility, a facility of the Poverty Reduction and Growth Trust (PRGT). PRGT-eligible = LIDC in OCR's terms.

Mission 4

Politics: colony, independence, 1994

  • German colony from 1899; ruled by Belgium under a League of Nations mandate from 1919.
  • Joined the UN on 18 September 1962 as an independent state.
  • 1994: the genocide against the Tutsi. More than one million people were "systematically killed in less than three months".
  • That year GDP fell by 50%; in 1995 it grew by 35% as recovery began.
  • Since then: recovery and fast growth: 7–9% a year in 2023–25.

Mission 4

Society: smaller families, healthier children

  • Births per woman: 8.1 (1975) → 3.6 (2024).
  • Children dying before age 5: 245 per 1,000 (1975) → 38 (2024).
  • Adults who can read: 58% (1991) → 79% (2022).
  • Fewer deaths and fewer births mean more adults of working age for each child: a chance for faster growth.
Mission 4

Technology: electricity and drones

  • People with electricity: 10% (2010) → 72% (2024).
  • Rural homes far from the grid use off-grid solar, a government policy choice.
  • Medical drone deliveries: Zipline's "first flight in Rwanda" was in 2016.
  • Internet users: 32% of people (2024): most Rwandans are still offline.
Mission 4

A changing economy

Agriculture49%20%

  • Services (tourism, conferences, finance, transport) now produce over half of output.
  • Manufacturing: 12% of GDP (1975), 8% (2025).
  • Farming still employed about half of workers in 2020.
Mission 4

Rostow's model

  • W. W. Rostow, The Stages of Economic Growth (1960). Five stages: 1. Traditional society: mostly farming. 2. Preconditions for take-off: investment in transport, education, trade. 3. Take-off: a few manufacturing industries grow fast. 4. Drive to maturity: industry spreads; technology improves. 5. High mass consumption: most people buy consumer goods; services grow.
  • It assumes every country climbs the same steps, in order.
Mission 4

Does Rostow fit Rwanda?

  • Fits: farming's share fell; roads, power and schools grew: like preconditions for take-off.
  • Does not fit: no manufacturing take-off. Manufacturing's share fell; services grew instead.
  • Does not fit: the path was not a steady climb. 1994 threw Rwanda back.
  • Missing: outside help. Aid was worth 95% of national income in 1994 and 10% in 2023; Rostow says little about aid.
  • Exam move (OCR "To what extent"): judge it. Rostow describes parts of Rwanda's path but not its route.
Quick check
?Which evidence shows most clearly that Rwanda has not followed Rostow's path?
Mission 4

Environment: hills, land and power

  • Landlocked: imports and exports cross other countries to reach the sea, which adds cost.
  • Hilly and crowded: about 600 people per km² (2024); farmland is scarce.
  • Natural assets: volcanoes, 23 lakes and rivers that feed the Nile; mountain gorillas draw tourists.
  • Energy from the landscape: Lake Kivu holds methane gas; since 2016 KivuWatt has used it to generate 26 MW.
  • Context shapes the economy: landlocked and short of land, Rwanda has grown services (tourism, conferences) that do not need a port.
Mission check

?Country Q is a landlocked low-income country. Its economy halved in a civil war 30 years ago. Since then, peace has held, aid has fallen from half to a tenth of national income, growth has continued and services now earn more than farming. Which conclusion is best supported?

Rwanda shows the same mix. The 1994 genocide halved the economy; stability and aid helped rebuild it, while being landlocked and short of land shaped what it could grow.

Mission 5

The Sustainable Development Goals

  • 17 goals adopted by all UN member states in 2015, to reach by 2030.
  • Each goal has targets with measurable indicators.
  • Three that matter most for Rwanda, a low-income, mostly rural country:
    • SDG 1 No poverty
    • SDG 3 Good health
    • SDG 7 Affordable and clean energy
Mission 5

SDG 1: poverty

  • Target 1.1: "By 2030, eradicate extreme poverty for all people everywhere".
  • Share of Rwandans below the World Bank's US$3.00-a-day line: 82% (2000) → 39% (2023).
  • Progress: 44 percentage points in 23 years (our calculation).
  • Gap: about 4 in 10 people are still below the line, seven years before 2030.
Mission 5

SDG 3: children's and mothers' health

  • Target 3.2: cut under-5 deaths "to at least as low as 25 per 1,000 live births" by 2030.
  • Rwanda: 184 per 1,000 (2000) → 38 (2024): a fall of about 80% (our calculation).
  • Mothers dying from pregnancy or childbirth: 885 per 100,000 live births (2000) → 229 (2023).
  • Close, but not yet at the target.
Quick check
?Rwanda's under-5 death rate was 37.7 per 1,000 in 2024. The SDG target is 25 or lower. By about what percentage must the rate still fall?
Mission 5

SDG 7: electricity

  • Target 7.1: "By 2030, ensure universal access to affordable, reliable and modern energy services".
  • People with electricity: 10% (2010) → 72% (2024).
  • Rwanda's energy company reports a national access rate of 88.3% (grid + off-grid, June 2026).
  • Uneven: by district, from 63.4% (Gisagara) to 98.0% (Muhanga) in July 2025.

Mission 5

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: planned and run by governments or large organisations; usually national, large and expensive.
  • Bottom-up: works directly with local people, who help decide and run it; usually small, local and cheaper.
  • Exam move (OCR "Compare"): give advantages and disadvantages of both, then say which suits which job.
Mission 5

Top-down: national electrification

  • The government chose two routes: extend the national grid, and use off-grid solar for homes far from it.
  • July 2025: 59.6% of households on the grid; 25.0% off-grid, mainly solar.
  • Grid connections have grown "more than 12 times" in 20 years.
  • The state energy body signed agreements with 30 private companies to sell solar home systems.
  • For: fast, national, planned; links to SDG 7.
  • Against: costly (off-grid was chosen partly to ease "historical government subsidies"); districts left unevenly served.
Mission 5

Bottom-up: One Acre Fund and farmers

  • A non-profit that began in Rwanda in 2007 with 38 farm families.
  • Farmers choose to join; it supplies seed, fertiliser and training on their own small plots.
  • Now reports serving over 2.7 million farmers, directly and with partners.
  • It reports that the farmers it serves directly raise farm income by 40%+ a year (the charity's own estimate).
  • For: reaches families one by one; farmers keep control of their land.
  • Against: slow to build up; relies on donors and partners; works alongside the government, so it is not wholly local.

Mission 6

What Rwanda trades

  • April–June 2026: domestic exports US$549 million; re-exports US$180 million; imports US$1,606 million.
  • Trade deficit (all exports minus imports): US$878 million in three months: Rwanda buys far more than it sells.
  • Top export buyers: China (26%), the UAE (20%), DR Congo (16%).
  • Top import: machinery and transport equipment.
  • A deficit must be paid for with aid, loans, investment or money sent home by migrants.
Mission 6

Relying on a few commodities

  • A commodity is a raw material sold in bulk: ores, coffee, tea.
  • April–June 2026: crude materials (incl. metal ores) earned 43.7% of domestic exports; food (incl. coffee and tea) 19.6% (our calculation).
  • Together: 63.3% of exports from raw materials (our calculation).
  • Coffee earns foreign currency and cash for rural farmers; tea earned US$90 million in 2020–21.
Mission 6

Why commodity reliance is risky

  • Prices swing: world prices for ores and crops rise and fall, so export income rises and falls too. The government names "global commodity price volatility" as a risk to growth.
  • Rwanda's export earnings do swing: domestic exports US$346m (Apr–Jun 2025) → US$549m (Apr–Jun 2026): +59%.
  • One export group ("other commodities") earned US$438m in Oct–Dec 2024 but US$28m in Jan–Mar 2026.
  • The statistics office does not say how much of each swing came from prices and how much from amounts sold.
  • Effect on development: hard to plan budgets, wages and investment when income jumps around.
Quick check
?Why does reliance on a few commodities hold back development?
Mission 6

TNCs and foreign investment in Rwanda

  • TNC: a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Volkswagen (Germany): set up a company in Rwanda in 2018 with a planned investment of about US$20 million; planned "up to 1,000 jobs" with partners and car assembly in Kigali.
  • ContourGlobal (an international power company): KivuWatt turns Lake Kivu methane into 26 MW of electricity.
  • Zipline (US): drone deliveries began with its "first flight in Rwanda" (2016).
  • Foreign direct investment into Rwanda: US$873 million in 2024.
Mission 6

Trade and TNCs: benefits and problems

  • Benefits
    • Jobs and skills: VW aimed at "know-how transfer".
    • Technology: ride-hailing app built by a local start-up; electric car pilot.
    • Infrastructure: power from KivuWatt.
    • Exports bring in foreign currency to pay for imports.
  • Problems
    • Profits can be sent back to the TNC's home country.
    • Plans are not promises: VW's 2018 job and output figures were targets.
    • Big companies can leave if markets change.
    • Raw exports earn less than processed goods; the trade deficit remains.
Quick check
?Which is a problem of TNC investment for Rwanda's development?
Mission 6

Aid to Rwanda

  • Aid (official development assistance) was worth 95% of national income in 1994, after the genocide.
  • 2023: US$1.39 billion, about 10% of national income.
  • Bilateral aid: from one government (e.g. the UK, the US). Multilateral: through bodies such as the World Bank or Global Fund.
  • Aid paid for health care: the US PEPFAR programme and the Global Fund funded HIV treatment from rural districts first.
Mission 6

Debt relief

  • The IMF and World Bank began the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996, so "no poor country faces an unmanageable debt burden".
  • Rwanda is one of 36 countries to reach the final stage and receive full debt relief.
  • Rwanda's foreign debt: 71% of national income (2004) → 13% (2006).
  • Since then it has borrowed again: 91% (2025).
  • Advantage: money once used to repay debt can go to schools and health. Disadvantage: relief is one-off; new loans can rebuild debt.
Mission 6

Aid: advantages and disadvantages

  • Advantages: pays for health, schools and power the government cannot yet afford; emergency aid saves lives after disasters.
  • Disadvantage: dependence: a budget that leans on aid is hit when donors cut. In 2026 the finance minister spoke of "declining budget support".
  • Disadvantage: conditions: donors can pause aid for political reasons. In February 2025 the UK government paused "direct bilateral financial aid to the Government of Rwanda", citing the conflict in eastern DR Congo; support to "the poorest and most vulnerable" continued.
  • Disadvantage: debt: loans, unlike grants, must be repaid.
Quick check
?Rwanda's foreign debt fell from 71% of national income in 2004 to 13% in 2006, then rose to 91% by 2025. What best explains this pattern?
Mission check

?Country M earns 70% of its exports from one metal ore. The ore's world price rose this year and export earnings jumped by half, so M took out large foreign loans for a new railway, to be repaid from future ore sales. What is M's biggest development risk?

Most of Rwanda's exports are raw materials, so earnings can swing; Rwanda's debt fell with HIPC relief, then rose again with new borrowing. Commodity income swings, TNCs bring skills but can take profits home, and aid and debt relief help but can be cut or rebuilt as new debt.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Make a guess: Apple employs about 166,000 people. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan. Why do these two giants make their products in such different ways?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 6.2b benefits and problems of trade and TNC investment for development
Mission 7

Transnational corporations

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) is a company that operates in more than one country.
  • Its headquarters is in its home country; a country where it sets up is a host country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a company builds or buys a business abroad and runs it.
  • Outsourcing: a company pays another firm to do part of its work, such as making its products.
Mission 7

How TNCs organise themselves

  • Headquarters, design and research usually stay in the home country: Apple is based in Cupertino, California.
  • Making the product can happen anywhere. Apple's manufacturing is mostly done by outsourcing partners in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
Mission 7

Why locate in many countries?

  • Lower costs: Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs".
  • Near customers: in the year to March 2026 Toyota made 73.9% of the vehicles it sold outside Japan in factories outside Japan. Cars are big and heavy to ship.
  • Local supplies and money: buying parts locally matches local costs to local sales.
  • Avoiding trade barriers: Toyota notes a "significant increase" in US tariffs, including on cars, announced in 2025; making cars inside a market avoids them.
  • Government incentives such as tax breaks also attract TNCs.
Quick check
?Which is an example of FDI rather than outsourcing?
Mission 7

Apple in India: outsourcing

  • India is one of the main countries where Apple's outsourcing partners make its products.
  • The partner firms own and run the factories and employ the workers; Apple designs the products and sells them worldwide.
  • Apple says outsourcing lowers costs but "reduce[s] the Company's direct control over production".
  • Because partners work in six countries, production can shift between them.
Mission 7

Toyota in India: FDI

  • Toyota Kirloskar Motor has made cars in India since December 1999.
  • Models include the Innova, Fortuner and Urban Cruiser Hyryder, some as hybrids.
  • A Toyota group company, Toyota Kirloskar Auto Parts, has made axles and transmissions in India since 2002.
  • Toyota invests in its own factories and in nearby suppliers: its Just in Time system needs parts to arrive as they are needed.
Mission 7

FDI into India

  • Foreign direct investment into India (World Bank):
    • 2000: $3.6 billion · 2020: $64.4 billion (peak) · 2024: $27.1 billion · 2025: $40.8 billion
  • FDI grew hugely after 2000, but it rises and falls from year to year.
Mission 7

Advantages for the host country

  • Jobs and wages in factories, and in partner and supplier firms.
  • Skills and technology: workers learn new methods (for example Toyota's production system).
  • Multiplier effect: suppliers (such as parts makers) and local services grow too.
  • Exports and taxes: goods made in India are sold abroad, and firms pay tax.
  • In OCR's terms India is an EDC. Apply the same benefits and problems to your LIDC, Rwanda, in its case-study missions.
Mission 7

Disadvantages for the host country

  • Profits flow back to the home country.
  • Top jobs (design, research, management) often stay at headquarters.
  • Footloose: contracts and investment can move. FDI into India fell by about 58% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Competition: local firms may struggle against large TNCs.
  • Working conditions and the environment depend on how well laws are enforced.
Quick check
?Why might outsourcing jobs be less secure for a host country than FDI factories?
Mission check

?Company P makes heavy washing machines. Company Q designs small wireless earbuds. Which strategies are they most likely to use?

Apple keeps design and services and outsources assembly to partners in Asia, including India: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, with its own factories in India since 1999, building cars near customers. Both bring jobs and skills to host countries, but profits and top jobs mostly stay at home, and contracts and investment can move.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Make a guess: In 2022 Sri Lanka defaulted on its foreign debt and turned to the IMF. Who sets the terms of rescue loans, and is there an alternative?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 6.2b advantages and disadvantages of international aid, e.g. debt relief, for the LIDC
Mission 8

Closing the development gap

  • The development gap: the difference in wealth and well-being between richer and poorer countries.
  • Strategies to narrow it:
    • aid and debt relief
    • trade (including fairtrade) and investment
    • industrial development and tourism
    • small-scale tools: intermediate technology, microfinance.
Mission 8

Types of aid

  • Bilateral: from one government to another. Multilateral: through bodies such as the World Bank or UN.
  • Government aid (official development assistance) and NGO aid (charities such as Oxfam or Partners In Health).
  • Emergency aid: short-term help after a disaster: food, tents, medicine.
  • Long-term development aid: projects that build schools, clinics, water supplies and skills.
  • Grants are gifts; loans must be repaid.
Quick check
?Which is long-term development aid?
Mission 8

Development targets, and falling aid

  • Since a 1970 UN resolution, rich countries have been asked to give 0.7% of their national income (GNI) as aid.
  • The Sustainable Development Goals (2015) set development targets for 2030, such as ending hunger.
  • Aid from rich (OECD DAC) countries: $215.1 billion (2024) → $174.3 billion (2025, preliminary): 0.34% → 0.26% of GNI.
  • In 2025 only Norway, Luxembourg, Sweden and Denmark reached 0.7%.
Quick check
?Removing inflation, DAC aid fell from $215.14 billion (2024) to $165.51 billion (2025). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 8

Aid in Rwanda, a low-income country

  • Rwanda is a low-income country (World Bank).
  • Aid received: $1.39 billion in 2023, 9.5% of national income, down from 19.7% in 2005.
  • Government aid: the UK reports that its aid in Rwanda helped 364,000 children gain a decent education and reached 327,000 children and women with nutrition support (2015–2020).
  • NGO aid: Partners In Health has worked in Rwanda since 2005, helping the government bring health care to more than 860,000 people in three districts; it supports Butaro District Hospital, opened in 2011.
  • Rwanda is our LIDC case study.
Mission 8

Aid: for and against

  • For: saves lives in emergencies; pays for schools, clinics and clean water; NGOs work with communities.
  • Against: can create dependency; may be tied to buying the donor's goods; may not reach the poorest; donor priorities can change.
  • Unreliable: DAC aid fell by about 23% in one year, 2025.
  • Best when it builds local skills and services, as when NGOs work through the national health system.
Quick check
?Which is the strongest disadvantage of relying on aid?
Mission 8

Debt relief

  • Many poor countries owed more than they could repay. Repayments took money from schools and hospitals.
  • The IMF and World Bank started the Heavily Indebted Poor Countries (HIPC) Initiative in 1996; in 2005 another scheme added 100% relief on some debts.
  • To qualify, countries need a track record of reform and a poverty reduction strategy carried out for at least a year.
  • Rwanda is one of the countries that completed HIPC; Somalia became the 37th in December 2023.
  • + frees money for services. − strict conditions; countries can fall into debt again.
Mission 8

Rescue loans: Sri Lanka

  • In 2022 Sri Lanka stopped paying its foreign debts: "the external default in 2022" (IMF).
  • Its economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022. Inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
  • March 2023: the IMF approved a four-year loan of about US$3 billion.
  • Conditions (conditionality) included higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy really costs).
  • Results: inflation fell "to around 4 percent in 2023", and the economy grew 5.0% in 2024.
Mission 8

Who sets the terms?

  • The government negotiates conditions with IMF staff; the IMF's Executive Board approves the loan.
  • Votes follow each country's quota, roughly its economic weight.
  • Big decisions, such as changing quotas, need 85% of the votes. The USA holds 16.49%, enough to block them.
  • Creditors also had to accept less: governments, Chinese state banks and private bondholders.
  • Alternatives: grants from donors, or loans from newer banks such as the BRICS countries' New Development Bank, which funds projects rather than rescues.
Quick check
?What does conditionality mean for an IMF loan?
Mission check

?A low-income country asks to have its debts to the IMF and World Bank cancelled. Who sets the conditions it must meet?

Sri Lanka's government agreed conditions with the IMF; the IMF's Executive Board, where votes follow quotas, approved them, and creditors had to accept less. Alternatives include grants, debt relief and newer lenders such as the New Development Bank.

Mission 9

Mission 9: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Make a guess: In 2006 a bank for poor villagers won the Nobel Peace Prize. Do small loans and fairer prices really lift people out of poverty?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 6.2c compare one top-down and one bottom-up development strategy in the LIDC
Mission 9

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: planned and run by governments, large companies or international bodies (such as the World Bank). Often large-scale.
    • Advantages: big scale, fast, can build national infrastructure.
    • Limitations: costly, may ignore local needs, local people have little say.
  • Bottom-up: run by and with local communities, often helped by charities. Usually small-scale.
    • Advantages: meets local needs, cheap, local people keep control.
    • Limitations: small reach, slow to spread, depends on funding and volunteers.
Mission 9

Microfinance

  • In 2006 Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) won the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
  • Microfinance: small loans, savings and insurance for people that ordinary banks will not serve.
  • In 2018, microfinance lenders had 139.9 million borrowers: 80% women, 65% in rural areas.
Mission 9

Does microfinance work?

  • Six randomised trials in six countries on four continents found "a consistent pattern of modestly positive, but not transformative, effects".
  • In Andhra Pradesh, India, rapid lending left many households over-indebted: by 2010, 83% had loans from more than one source.
  • So small loans help some families run businesses and manage money, but do not on their own lift large numbers out of poverty.
Quick check
?What do the six randomised trials suggest about microfinance?
Mission 9

Fair trade: how it works

  • Fair trade: a system that gives producers in poorer countries a fairer, more stable deal.
  • Minimum price: a floor paid when world prices fall. For washed Arabica coffee it rose from $1.40 to $1.80 per pound for contracts from 1 August 2023.
  • Fairtrade Premium: an extra sum that producer groups decide together how to spend.
  • In 2024 Fairtrade covered 1.87 million farmers and workers and paid €201.6 million in Premium.
Mission 9

The benefits for producers

  • A safety net: the minimum price protects income when world prices crash.
  • Investment: in 2024 small-scale producer groups spent 35% of their Premium on improving production and farming, and 19% on payments to farmers.
  • Communities: workers on certified plantations put 67% of their Premium into social investments.
  • Reach: 75% of Fairtrade farmers and workers were in Africa and the Middle East.
Mission 9

The limits

  • In 2024 only about 27% of reported certified coffee production was sold on Fairtrade terms; the rest was sold at ordinary prices.
  • Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of world coffee exports in 2009: a small share of trade.
  • A study in Ethiopia and Uganda found that, in some cases, hired workers in areas with Fairtrade groups were "significantly worse paid" than elsewhere.
  • Fairtrade itself says its Premium data do not show "outcomes or impacts".
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of fair trade for producers?
Mission 9

Top-down in Rwanda

  • Rwanda's Ministry of Education built more than 22,000 classrooms under a national project supported by the World Bank.
  • To staff them quickly, it relaxed hiring rules: about 25,000 graduates who had not been to a teacher training college became teachers.
  • From April 2024 a national programme trained and certified 24,000 of them in less than a year, in all 30 districts.
  • Advantages: national scale and speed. Limitations: thousands of pupils were taught at first by uncertified teachers; the project depends on outside funding.
Mission 9

Bottom-up compared

  • Microfinance (e.g. Grameen Bank, Bangladesh) and fair trade co-operatives are bottom-up: local people borrow, save, decide and run them.
  • Advantages: reach women and small farmers directly (80% of microfinance borrowers are women); local control.
  • Limitations: modest effects; small share of trade; risk of over-borrowing.
  • Compared: top-down reaches a whole country fast; bottom-up fits local needs but spreads slowly.
Quick check
?Which project is bottom-up?
Mission 9

Exam move: comparing strategies

  • Compare on scale, cost, speed, local control and evidence of success.
  • Use named places: Rwanda's classrooms and teachers (top-down) against microfinance and fair trade (bottom-up).
  • Finish with a judgement: the two often work best together.
Mission check

?A charity starts offering small loans and a guaranteed minimum price to cocoa farmers in a new region. What is the most realistic expectation?

Modestly. Microcredit's effects are "modestly positive, but not transformative", and too much lending caused a crisis in Andhra Pradesh. Fairtrade sets a floor price and pays a Premium, but only about 27% of certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024. Both are tools, not cures.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Development is economic, social and political; measures include GNI per head, birth and death rates, infant mortality, life expectancy, doctors, literacy, water and the HDI.
  • Every measure has limits (averages, data quality, missing dimensions), so use several.

Mission 2

  • Global pattern: low income mostly in sub-Saharan Africa; high income mostly in Europe, the Americas, East Asia and the Gulf.
  • Within countries: a national label is an average that hides richer and poorer regions.

Mission 3

  • Causes: physical/environmental, historical (colonialism) and economic (primary products, debt, trade and trade blocs).
  • Political unrest also holds development back.

Mission 4

  • Rwanda (landlocked, crowded, low income) grew from US$90 to US$1,150 GNI per person (1975–2025); 1994 was a catastrophic break.
  • Rostow fits partly: farming shrank, but services, not manufacturing, led, and aid and conflict shaped the path.

Mission 5

  • SDG progress: poverty 82% → 39%; under-5 deaths 184 → 38 per 1,000; electricity 10% → 72% of people (88% access by June 2026): closest to SDG 7.
  • Top-down (national electrification) is fast and national but costly and uneven; bottom-up (One Acre Fund) reaches farmers directly but builds slowly and depends on donors.

Mission 6

  • Trade: a large deficit, and 63% of domestic exports from raw materials (Apr–Jun 2026); prices and earnings swing.
  • TNCs (Volkswagen, ContourGlobal, Zipline) bring jobs, skills, power and technology; profits can leave and plans are not guarantees.
  • Aid fell from 95% (1994) to 10% (2023) of national income; HIPC cut debt from 71% to 13% of GNI, but debt has risen again; aid can be paused for political reasons.

Mission 7

  • TNCs keep headquarters and design at home and spread production abroad, by outsourcing (Apple) or FDI (Toyota).
  • For India: jobs, skills, exports and suppliers; but profits leave, top jobs stay away, and FDI rises and falls.

Mission 8

  • Strategies to reduce the gap: aid, debt relief, trade, fairtrade, investment, industry, tourism, intermediate technology and microfinance, each with gains and limits.
  • Lenders (the IMF and World Bank) set the conditions for rescue loans and debt relief; voting follows quotas.
  • In Rwanda, government and NGO aid support education, nutrition and health; aid fell from 19.7% to 9.5% of national income (2005–2023).

Mission 9

  • Microfinance and fair trade bring modest, real benefits (stable prices, Premium, small loans for women), but reach only part of trade and need careful lending.
  • Top-down (Rwanda's classrooms and teachers) is big and fast; bottom-up (savings groups, co-operatives) is local and slower.
