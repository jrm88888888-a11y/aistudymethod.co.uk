Mission 1

Mission 1: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Make a guess: Every time your cells build a big molecule, they make water. Why?

Mission 1

One chemistry for all life

  • Life is hugely varied, but its biochemical basis is similar in all living things.
  • Four groups of biological molecules: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • C, H and O are in all four, and are the most common elements in them.
MoleculesElements
CarbohydratesC, H, O
LipidsC, H, O (phospholipids also P)
ProteinsC, H, O, N, S
Nucleic acidsC, H, O, N, P
  • Cells cannot make atoms. Every N, P and S atom must be taken in from the environment.
Mission 1

Monomers and polymers

  • Monomer: a small unit from which larger molecules are made.
  • Polymer: a large molecule made of many monomers joined together.
MonomerPolymer
Monosaccharide (e.g. glucose)Polysaccharide (starch, glycogen, cellulose)
Amino acidPolypeptide (protein)
NucleotidePolynucleotide (DNA, RNA)
  • Triglycerides are built the same way, but are not polymers: one glycerol + three fatty acids, not a repeating chain.
Mission 1

Condensation: building

  • Condensation: joins two molecules with a new covalent bond and eliminates a molecule of water.
  • One monomer loses an –OH, the other an –H. Together: H2O.
  • One water per bond formed.
  • Repeat: monomer → dimer → polymer.
  • Cells use enzymes for each joining reaction.
Mission 1

Hydrolysis: breaking

  • Hydrolysis: breaks a covalent bond between two molecules and uses a water molecule.
  • The water is split: –H goes to one product, –OH to the other.
  • One water per bond broken.
  • Digestion is hydrolysis. Enzymes make it fast; polymers in plain water break down extremely slowly.
  • So water is a metabolite: a product of condensation, a reactant in hydrolysis.
  • Say: "condensation releases water; hydrolysis uses water" — not: the other way round.
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides are joined by a condensation reaction to form a disaccharide. What happens in this reaction?
Mission 1

Worked example: counting the water

  • Bonds sit between monomers. An unbranched chain of n monomers has n − 1 bonds.
  • So building it releases n − 1 water molecules; hydrolysing it uses n − 1.
  • Example: 5 glucose molecules joined into one unbranched chain.
  • Glucose Mr = 180; water Mr = 18.
  • Bonds: 5 − 1 = 4. Water released: 4.
  • Mr of chain = 5 × 180 − 4 × 18 = 900 − 72 = 828.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it exactly: 828 + 4 × 18 = 900.
Quick check
?A polypeptide made of 100 amino acids is completely hydrolysed into free amino acids. How many water molecules are used in the process?

Mission 2

Mission 2: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Make a guess: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Mission 2

Single sugars

  • A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit: the monomer of carbohydrates.
  • Named by number of carbon atoms: triose (3 C), pentose (5 C), hexose (6 C).
  • Hexoses: glucose, fructose, galactose. Pentoses: ribose (in RNA and ATP), deoxyribose (in DNA).
  • Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6: same atoms, arranged differently. They are isomers.
Mission 2

Rings: count every carbon

  • In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mostly as rings.
  • Glucose: ring of 5 C + 1 O. Carbon 6 sits outside the ring, in a CH2OH group.
  • Ribose: ring of 4 C + 1 O. Carbon 5 sits outside.
  • Fructose (as in sucrose): ring of 4 C + 1 O, with two carbons outside.
  • So ring size does not tell you the sugar type. Count all the carbons.
  • In glucose, number the carbons from the one to the right of the ring O: C1, C2 … (In fructose, C1 is outside the ring.)
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides, P and Q, are drawn in their ring forms. In P the ring is made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, with one further carbon atom (in a CH2OH group) attached outside the ring. In Q the ring is also made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, but two further carbon atoms (each in a CH2OH group) are attached outside the ring. How should P and Q be classified?
Mission 2

α-glucose and β-glucose

  • Two forms of glucose: same formula, same ring.
  • They differ only at carbon 1.
  • α-glucose: –OH on C1 points below the ring.
  • β-glucose: –OH on C1 points above the ring.
  • A tiny difference with large effects: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen; β-glucose builds cellulose (see Mission 3).
  • Say: "the –OH on carbon 1 is inverted" — not: "β-glucose has different atoms".
Quick check
?Which statement about α-glucose and β-glucose is correct?
Mission 2

Two sugars make a disaccharide

  • Two monosaccharides join by condensation: a glycosidic bond forms and water is released.
  • Maltose = glucose + glucose. Two α-glucose, joined C1 to C4: a 1,4-glycosidic bond.
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose.
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose.
  • Hydrolysis splits a disaccharide back into its monosaccharides, using water.
Quick check
?The enzyme maltase, in the small intestine, acts on maltose. Which statement describes the reaction?
Mission check

?Galactose and glucose both have the formula C6H12O6 and both form a ring of five carbons and one oxygen. They differ only in which way the –OH on carbon 4 points. Which statement is correct?

Same atoms, different arrangement: that is all it takes to make a new sugar, from glucose and fructose to α and β.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Make a guess: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Mission 3

One monomer, three polymers

  • Starch: energy store in plants.
  • Glycogen: energy store in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose: strong walls of plant cells.
  • All three are polymers of glucose, built by condensation.
  • Units are joined by glycosidic bonds.
  • What differs: which form of glucose, and how the units are connected.
Mission 3

Starch: amylose and amylopectin

  • Made of α-glucose.
  • Amylose: 1,4-glycosidic bonds only. Unbranched. Coils into a helix: compact.
  • Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds plus 1,6-glycosidic bonds at branch points, about every 25 units.
  • Starch grains in plant cells, e.g. potato tubers and seeds, contain both.
Mission 3

Glycogen

  • Made of α-glucose, like amylopectin.
  • 1,4 bonds, with 1,6 branches about every 10 units: more branched than amylopectin.
  • Very compact granules in liver and muscle cells.
  • Enzymes remove glucose only from chain ends. More branches = more ends = faster release.
  • Fits animals: they can need glucose for respiration very quickly.
Mission 3

What makes a good store

  • Compact: coiling and branching pack many glucose units into little space.
  • Relatively insoluble because the molecules are so large. So they do not draw water into the cell by osmosis.
  • Too large to leave the cell.
  • Easily hydrolysed to α-glucose when energy is needed; easily built up again by condensation.
  • Say: "relatively insoluble because they are so large" — not: "insoluble because they are non-polar".

The graph shows the mass of monosaccharide released over 30 minutes (illustrative data). Which statement correctly describes the data?
Quick check
?Liver cells store glucose in the form of glycogen. Why is the glucose not simply stored as free glucose?
Mission 3

Cellulose

  • (1) Made of β-glucose. To form each 1,4-glycosidic bond, every other unit is inverted (rotated 180°).
  • (2) The alternating units give straight, unbranched chains.
  • (3) Straight chains lie side by side, linked by many hydrogen bonds between their –OH groups: microfibrils.
  • Microfibrils group into fibres with high tensile strength.
  • Cell walls resist stretching, so plant cells can become turgid without bursting.
  • Say: "hydrogen bonds between chains" — not: "covalent cross-links" or "coiled like starch".
Quick check
?Cellulose gives plant cell walls high tensile strength. Which feature of cellulose is responsible for this?

Mission check

?Humans make amylase, which hydrolyses the 1,4-glycosidic bonds between α-glucose units in starch. Humans make no enzyme that hydrolyses cellulose, which also has 1,4-glycosidic bonds between glucose units. Which explanation is best?

One small flip at carbon 1 decides whether glucose ends up as fuel or as wood.

Mission 4

Mission 4: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Make a guess: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Mission 4

How chromatography separates

  • (1) Stationary phase: the paper, or a thin layer of silica gel on a plate (TLC). It stays put.
  • (2) Mobile phase: the solvent. It creeps up the stationary phase by capillary action, carrying the mixture.
  • (3) Each molecule spends time dissolved in the solvent and time attached to the stationary phase.
  • More soluble in the solvent, less attracted to the stationary phase: it travels further.
  • So molecules in a mixture separate into spots.
  • TLC runs faster and gives sharper spots than paper.
  • Say: "distance depends on solubility and attraction" — not: "on how much of it there is".
Mission 4

Running a chromatogram

  • Draw an origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate), about 1.5 cm from the bottom.
  • Put a small, concentrated spot of the sample on the origin. Dry it; add more; dry again.
  • Stand the paper in solvent with the origin above the solvent level, so the sample does not wash off into it.
  • Cover the container, so the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Take it out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front in pencil at once.
  • Coloured spots show themselves. Colourless ones need a locating agent.
Mission 4

Worked example: Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
  • Measure both from the origin; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Illustrative data: origin 20 mm from the paper's bottom edge; spot centre 56 mm; solvent front 80 mm.
  • Solute: 56 − 20 = 36 mm. Solvent: 80 − 20 = 60 mm.
  • Rf = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60. Always between 0 and 1; no units.
  • Same solute, solvent and stationary phase: same Rf. Compare with reference values, or run standards beside the sample.
Quick check
?On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission 4

Leaf pigments

  • Grind a leaf with a little sand and propanone to extract its pigments. Spot on the origin.
  • A green leaf gives several spots, not one. Typical order, top to bottom:
  • carotene (orange) · xanthophyll (yellow) · chlorophyll a (blue-green) · chlorophyll b (yellow-green).
  • Each pigment absorbs different wavelengths of light.
  • Identify each by colour and Rf, against reference values for the same solvent.
  • Required practical 7: compare the pigments in leaves of different plants, e.g. shade-tolerant and shade-intolerant plants, or leaves of different colours.

Mission check

?Sugars in a fruit juice are separated by thin-layer chromatography, with glucose, fructose and sucrose standards run on the same plate. The juice gives two spots, level with the glucose and fructose standards; nothing is level with sucrose. On a second plate, with a different solvent, every spot (juice and standards) has a different Rf from the first plate. Which conclusion is correct?

A spot is named by where it lands compared with a known, under the same conditions. Not by its size, and not by guesswork.

Mission 5

What makes a lipid

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water.
  • Mostly non-polar C–H bonds: few δ+ or δ− regions to attract water. They are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, phospholipids and steroids.
  • Two groups to know: triglycerides (here) and phospholipids.
  • Say: "insoluble because non-polar" — not: "because the molecules are large" or "because they are lighter than water".
Mission 5

Building a triglyceride

  • (1) Glycerol has three –OH groups. A fatty acid is a hydrocarbon chain (R) with a –COOH group: RCOOH.
  • (2) Condensation: the –COOH of a fatty acid reacts with an –OH of glycerol. An ester bond forms; water is released.
  • (3) Three times: one glycerol + three fatty acids → one triglyceride + 3 H2O.
  • Hydrolysis breaks the ester bonds, releasing glycerol and three fatty acids (e.g. lipase in digestion).
  • A triglyceride is not a polymer.
  • Say: "one glycerol and three fatty acids" — not: "three glycerols and one fatty acid".
Quick check
?One glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules react by condensation to form one triglyceride. How much water is produced?
Mission 5

Saturated and unsaturated

  • The fatty acid's R-group is the hydrocarbon chain.
  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Every carbon holds as many H atoms as it can: saturated with hydrogen.
  • Unsaturated: at least one C=C double bond.
  • In natural fats, each C=C puts a kink in the chain. Saturated chains are straight.
  • Only C=C in the chain counts. The C=O in –COOH does not make a fatty acid unsaturated.
  • Say: "saturated with hydrogen" — not: "full of double bonds".
Mission 5

Kinks and melting points

  • Straight saturated chains pack closely: strong attractions between molecules → higher melting point.
  • Kinked unsaturated chains cannot pack closely: weaker attractions → lower melting point.
  • Rich in saturated fatty acids: fat, solid at room temperature (butter).
  • Rich in unsaturated fatty acids: oil, liquid at room temperature (olive oil).
  • Melting breaks no covalent bonds. Molecules just move apart.
Quick checkRead the data
?The graph shows the approximate melting points of four fatty acids. Each has a chain of 18 carbon atoms; they differ only in the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain. Which statement is best supported by the data?
Mission 5

A dense energy store

  • Oxidising triglycerides releases about 37 kJ g⁻¹; carbohydrate about 17 kJ g⁻¹. About twice as much per gram.
  • Why: long hydrocarbon chains, rich in C–H bonds, with little oxygen.
  • Insoluble: no osmotic effect, and stored without the water that glycogen holds.
  • So a lot of energy fits in a small, light store: adipose tissue in animals, oil droplets in seeds.
  • Say: "energy is released when fatty acids are oxidised in respiration" — not: "energy is stored in the ester bonds".
Mission check

?To make a spread, manufacturers react a liquid vegetable oil with hydrogen, using a catalyst. Some C=C double bonds in its fatty acid chains become C–C single bonds. How does the oil change?

Same molecule type, different tails: straight tails pack into a solid; kinked tails slide past each other as a liquid.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Make a guess: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Mission 6

Starch: the iodine test

  • Add a few drops of iodine in potassium iodide solution.
  • Starch present: orange-brown → blue-black.
  • Works on solids or solutions.
  • Iodine slots inside the coiled amylose chain (see Mission 3): that is what gives the colour.
Mission 6

Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

  • Reducing sugars: all monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose.
  • (1) Add an equal volume of Benedict's reagent (blue: Cu²⁺ ions).
  • (2) Heat in a boiling water bath for about 5 minutes.
  • (3) Reducing sugar reduces Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺: a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide forms.
  • The more reducing sugar, the further along: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • So the colour is semi-quantitative. For a number, use a colorimeter.
Mission 6

Non-reducing sugars

  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar: Benedict's stays blue.
  • (1) Take a fresh sample. Boil with dilute hydrochloric acid: this hydrolyses the glycosidic bond.
  • (2) Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's works only in alkaline conditions). Check with pH paper.
  • (3) Benedict's test again. Brick red now: a non-reducing sugar was present.
  • This only works if the first Benedict's test was negative, or gave less precipitate.
Quick check
?A solution gives a negative result with iodine. With Benedict's reagent, heated, it stays blue. A fresh sample is boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised, and tested with Benedict's again: a brick-red precipitate forms. What does the solution contain?
Mission 6

Proteins and lipids

  • Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulphate).
  • Protein present: blue → lilac (purple). The reagent detects peptide bonds.
  • Free amino acids give no lilac: they have no peptide bonds.
  • Emulsion test: shake the sample with ethanol; any lipid dissolves.
  • Pour the ethanol into water. Lipid present: a milky-white emulsion.
  • The lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.
Quick check
?In the emulsion test, a sample is shaken with ethanol and the ethanol is then poured into water. A milky-white emulsion forms. Why?
Mission 6

From colour to number: colorimetry

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample and measures absorbance.
  • After Benedict's: more glucose → more Cu²⁺ used up → less blue left in solution.
  • Remove the precipitate (filter or centrifuge). Use a red filter: the blue solution absorbs red light.
  • Zero the colorimeter with distilled water first.
  • Make standards of known concentration, treat them identically, and plot a calibration curve.
  • Required practical 11: a glucose dilution series and calibration curve to find the glucose concentration of an unknown 'urine' sample.
Mission 6

Worked example: read a calibration curve

Illustrative data. Glucose standards (mmol dm⁻³) tested with Benedict's; absorbance of the remaining blue solution:

Glucose concentration / mmol dm⁻³0.02.04.06.08.010.0
Absorbance0.920.780.630.480.340.20
  • Unknown sample: absorbance 0.55.
  • On the graph: across from 0.55 to the line, then down. Or between the two nearest standards:
  • 0.55 lies between 0.63 (4.0) and 0.48 (6.0).
  • 4.0 + (0.63 − 0.55) ÷ (0.63 − 0.48) × 2.0 = 4.0 + 1.07 = 5.1 mmol dm⁻³.
  • Only read inside the range of your standards.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Monomers join by condensation (one water released per bond) and polymers are split by hydrolysis (one water used per bond).

Mission 2

  • Monosaccharides are named by their carbons (triose, pentose, hexose); isomers share a formula but not an arrangement; α- and β-glucose differ only at carbon 1.
  • Condensation of two monosaccharides forms a glycosidic bond: maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose).

Mission 3

  • α-glucose → starch (amylose helix; branched amylopectin) and glycogen (highly branched): compact, insoluble stores. β-glucose → straight cellulose chains hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils: strength.

Mission 4

  • Molecules separate by how they share time between the solvent and the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin; compare with standards or reference values for the same solvent.
  • Leaf extracts give carotene, xanthophyll, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b.

Mission 5

  • A triglyceride is one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester bonds (three condensations); hydrophobic, energy-dense store.
  • Saturated chains are straight and pack closely (fats, higher melting point); C=C kinks unsaturated chains (oils, lower melting point).

Mission 6

  • Iodine: blue-black = starch. Benedict's, heated: brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. Biuret: lilac = protein.
  • Non-reducing sugar: acid hydrolysis, neutralise, Benedict's again. Emulsion test: milky = lipid.
  • Colorimeter + calibration curve turns a colour into a concentration.
