Make a guess: Can a belief be reasonable and make sense, yet rest on trust rather than proof?

Mission 1

What a "dialogue" question asks

  • Component 2, Section B: the dialogue between philosophy of religion and your religion.
  • A dialogue runs both ways: philosophy challenges and reshapes religious belief; religious belief shapes philosophy.
  • Topics: God or ultimate reality; self, death and afterlife; sources of authority; religious experience; science; other religions' truth claims; miracles.
  • You bring together Component 1 (arguments, evil, experience, language, miracles, self) and your Component 2 religion.
  • Four issues run through every answer: is the belief reasonable, meaningful, coherent, and what does philosophy matter to faith (belief in or belief that)?
Mission 1

Reasonable

  • AQA's definition: a belief is reasonable if it is based on reason and/or consistent with reason.
  • Based on reason: supported by argument or evidence, e.g. the cosmological or design argument; testing a teaching in experience.
  • Consistent with reason: not proved, but not contrary to reason either; e.g. believing on trustworthy testimony or revelation.
  • Some philosophers hold that belief in God can be reasonable without argument: Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932), Warranted Christian Belief (2000), calls it properly basic, like trusting memory.
  • Others demand evidence: W. K. Clifford (1845–1879), "The Ethics of Belief" (1877): it is wrong to believe anything on insufficient evidence (paraphrase).
  • Fideism: faith does not need to answer to reason at all. Critics: then any belief would be acceptable.
Mission 1

Meaningful, and for whom?

  • Verification (A. J. Ayer, 1910–1989, Language, Truth and Logic, 1936): a statement is factually meaningful only if experience could in principle verify it. "God exists" fails, on his view.
  • Falsification (Antony Flew, 1923–2010, "Theology and Falsification", 1950): if believers allow nothing to count against a claim, it asserts nothing.
  • Language games (Ludwig Wittgenstein, 1889–1951, Philosophical Investigations, 1953): words get their meaning from their use in a form of life. Religious language is meaningful to those who share the practice.
  • So "for whom?" matters: a statement may be meaningful to a believer, puzzling to an outsider, and empty to a verificationist.
  • Responses: Hick's eschatological verification; Hare's bliks; analogy and symbol.

Mission 1

Coherent

  • Coherent beliefs fit together without contradiction.
  • Two tests in the spec: is each belief internally coherent, and is it consistent with other beliefs in the same system?
  • Examples: Can God be omnipotent and unable to sin? (God's attributes.) Can an all-good, all-powerful God allow evil? Can there be rebirth without a self? (Buddhism.) Can God know my future free choices? (Free will.)
  • A coherent system can still be false; an incoherent one has a problem whatever the evidence.
  • Religions often respond by refining the belief (e.g. omnipotence as doing what is logically possible): a clear case of philosophy shaping religion.
Mission 1

"Belief that" and "belief in"

  • H. H. Price (1899–1984), "Belief 'In' and Belief 'That'" (Religious Studies, 1965).
  • Belief that: holding a statement true (e.g. that God exists; that rebirth happens). It can be argued for or against.
  • Belief in: trust, commitment, loyalty (e.g. believing in a friend, in God, in the Buddha's teaching). It is personal and evaluative.
  • Price asks whether belief-in reduces to belief-that. Belief in God seems to presuppose belief that God exists, but adds trust and commitment.
  • Relevance of philosophy for faith: if faith is mainly belief-that, arguments matter a great deal. If faith is mainly belief-in, arguments matter less, though they may still test what the trust presupposes.
Quick check

Mission 1

The dialogue runs both ways

  • Philosophy shaping religion: Aristotle's philosophy shaped Aquinas (Christianity), Maimonides (Judaism) and Ibn Rushd (Islam); Hume's critique of miracles and design pushed some theologians to stress experience and meaning instead.
  • Religion shaping philosophy: Anselm's ontological argument grew out of prayer; the kalam cosmological argument comes from Islamic theology (al-Ghazali); Buddhist teaching on no-self is discussed by Western philosophers of personal identity (e.g. Derek Parfit).
  • Impact past and present: the spec asks how far each debate has changed belief, then and now.
  • Strong answers name who was influenced, how, and how far.
Mission 1Worked answer

A 25-mark dialogue paragraph

Example question: "Religious beliefs about life after death are meaningless." Evaluate this view with reference to your religion and philosophy.

  • Belief: state the belief precisely (e.g. resurrection; rebirth; the soul's survival).
  • Philosophical challenge: e.g. Ayer: no possible experience could verify a claim about life after death.
  • Religious response: e.g. Hick: such claims are verifiable after death (eschatological verification); or the language-game reply: meaningful within the practice of faith.
  • Impact: has the challenge changed the belief, or how it is expressed (e.g. more symbolic readings in some groups)?
  • Judgement: "meaningful for whom?" Reach a reasoned conclusion and keep it consistent to the end.
  • Skill tip: Section B questions test AO1 and AO2 together in one unstructured 25-mark answer: knowledge must serve the argument.

Mission 2: Arguments for God assume a creator. What happens when a religion has none?

Mission 2

A non-theistic partner in the dialogue

  • Most of Component 1 assumes a theistic God: one creator, all-powerful, all-good.
  • Buddhism has no creator God. Gods (devas) exist in many Buddhist cosmologies, but they are beings within samsara, not its source.
  • So Buddhism tests the scope of philosophy of religion: which arguments and objections still apply?
  • And Buddhist ideas (no-self, beginningless samsara, emptiness) feed back into philosophy.
  • Throughout: ask whether each belief is reasonable, meaningful (for whom?) and coherent (see Mission 1).
Mission 2

Ultimate reality without a creator

  • Dependent origination (paticcasamuppada): everything arises from conditions; nothing is self-caused or permanent.
  • Nibbana: the unconditioned, the end of craving (Udana 8.3). It is not a being and does not create.
  • Samsara has no discernible beginning: no first point can be found (Samyutta Nikaya 15.1, paraphrase).
  • Mahayana: emptiness (sunyata: nothing has fixed own-being), the Dharmakaya (truth-body of the Buddha), Buddha-nature in all beings.
  • Diversity: Pure Land Buddhists rely on Amitabha's vow; some philosophers see theistic features here, but Amitabha is not a creator.
Mission 2

Buddhist arguments against a creator

  • Brahma's mistake (Brahmajala Sutta, Digha Nikaya 1, paraphrase): the first being reborn in a new world-age thinks "I made these beings", because they appeared after him. Buddhists read this as a causal error.
  • A problem of evil (Bhuridatta Jataka, Jataka 543, paraphrase): if a creator shapes all lives, why does he make the world unjust and full of suffering? He would share in the wrong.
  • Santideva (8th c.), Bodhicaryavatara ch. 9: a permanent, unchanging creator could not produce changing effects one after another.
  • Dialogue with the cosmological argument: Aquinas needs a first cause; many Buddhists accept an infinite regress of conditions, as Hume and Russell do ("brute fact").
  • Dialogue with the problem of evil: Epicurus, Hume and Mackie use evil against God; Buddhist texts made a similar move long before Hume and Mackie.

Mission 2

Can nibbana be spoken of meaningfully?

  • Nibbana is described mostly negatively (unborn, unmade, unconditioned), like the via negativa for God.
  • The Buddha left some questions unanswered (avyakata), e.g. whether an awakened one exists after death.
  • Ayer's challenge: if no experience could verify it, "nibbana exists" is meaningless.
  • Buddhist responses: it is verifiable in this life by practice (the arahant knows it); meaningful within the form of life of practice (Wittgenstein); two truths (Nagarjuna, c. 2nd century): conventional language is useful, but ultimate truth is beyond concepts.
  • Meaningful for whom? The practitioner on the path, says the tradition; an outsider may find it empty.
Mission 2

No self: in dialogue with Western philosophy

  • Anatta: no permanent self; a person is five changing khandhas (form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness).
  • Descartes (1596–1650), Meditations (1641): "I think", so I am a thinking substance. Buddhists reply: there are thoughts, but no extra thinker behind them.
  • Hume (1711–1776), A Treatise of Human Nature (1739): looking within, he never catches a self, only a bundle of perceptions (paraphrase). A striking parallel.
  • Derek Parfit (1942–2017), Reasons and Persons (1984): personal identity is not what matters; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness. Parfit himself noted the closeness of his view to the Buddha's.
  • So Buddhism gains philosophical allies, and gives philosophy an ancient worked-out alternative to the soul.
Mission 2

Rebirth without a self: coherent?

  • The challenge: if there is no self, who is reborn, and who reaps the karma?
  • Buddhist answer: causal continuity, not identity. A flame lit from another flame is neither the same nor different (Milindapanha, paraphrase).
  • Dialogue with personal identity: Hick's replica theory needs God to create a replica; Buddhism needs no God, only karma as cause.
  • Critics: without memory or a continuing subject, is it fair to call the next life "mine"? Is karma just?
  • Responses: conventionally there is a person; ultimately there are processes. Many say this is coherent; others (e.g. Stephen Batchelor) bracket rebirth as unverifiable.

Mission 2

Sources of wisdom and authority

  • Buddhavacana (the Buddha's word): the Pali Canon; Mahayana sutras; teachers and lineages.
  • But authority is tested: the Kalama Sutta (Anguttara Nikaya 3.65, paraphrase): do not go by tradition, scripture, logic or a teacher's status alone; when you know for yourselves that something leads to harm, abandon it.
  • The raft (Majjhima Nikaya 22, paraphrase): the teaching is for crossing over, not for holding on to.
  • Saddha (faith, confidence): provisional trust in the Three Jewels, to be confirmed in practice.

> "You yourself must make an effort. The Tathagatas (Buddhas) are only preachers." (Dhammapada 276, Müller 1881)

  • Dialogue: this looks like evidentialism (Clifford). Critics: rebirth and karma are not tested by ordinary evidence; much is still accepted on the Buddha's authority.

Mission 2Evaluate

Are Buddhist beliefs about ultimate reality and the self reasonable and coherent?

  • Claim: Without a creator, Buddhist beliefs about ultimate reality and the self are more reasonable and coherent than theism's.
  • For: no problem of evil for a creator; no need for a first cause; anatta has support from Hume and Parfit; authority is to be tested.
  • Against: rebirth and karma are hard to verify and to square with anatta; nibbana is described mainly by negation; much rests on the Buddha's authority.
  • Against: theists reply that a beginningless regress explains nothing, and that "no self" undermines moral responsibility.
  • Judgement criteria: reasonable by which standard: evidence, consistency, or practice? Coherent within the Buddhist system, or by outside standards? Which Buddhism: Theravada, Mahayana, secular?
Mission 2Worked answer

Linking the issues

Question: "Buddhist beliefs about the self are incoherent." Evaluate this view with reference to Buddhism and philosophy.

  • Belief: anatta: no permanent self; five khandhas; rebirth by causal continuity.
  • Challenge: no self + rebirth seems contradictory: who is reborn? (coherence).
  • Response: the flame image; two levels of truth: a conventional person, ultimate processes.
  • Philosophical support: Hume's bundle; Parfit's view that identity is not what matters.
  • Impact: secular Buddhists bracket rebirth; traditional Buddhists keep it as coherent with anatta.
  • Model sentence: "Anatta is incoherent only if rebirth requires a continuing self; Buddhists deny that it does, and Parfit's account of continuity without identity shows that this denial is at least philosophically respectable."
Mission check

?An AI system is shut down and its patterns of activity are restarted on a new machine, with no stored memories. A Buddhist philosopher is asked whether "the same" system has come back. Which answer best fits Buddhist teaching?

Buddhism treats identity as continuity of causes, not a hidden self. That idea has found allies in Western philosophy.

Mission 3

Experience at the heart of Buddhism

  • The tradition begins with an experience: the Buddha's awakening under the Bodhi tree.
  • Meditation: calm states (jhana) and insight (vipassana) into impermanence, suffering and no-self.
  • Zen: kensho or satori, seeing one's own nature. Pure Land: shinjin, entrusting to Amitabha.
  • These experiences are of insight or awakening, not of meeting a creator God.
  • Buddhists value them as knowing for oneself (Kalama Sutta), not as proofs of a being.
Mission 3Read the source

William James on Buddhist meditation

William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), Lecture XVI–XVII "Mysticism":

> "The Buddhists use the word “samâdhi” as well as the Hindus; but “dhyâna” is their special word for higher states of contemplation." (James, Varieties, 1902)

  • James counts these as mystical: his first two marks are ineffability (cannot be put into words) and a noetic quality (they feel like knowledge).
  • Elsewhere he notes that "in strictness the Buddhistic system is atheistic".

Mission 3

Experience as evidence: the dialogue

  • Swinburne (b. 1934), The Existence of God (1979): principles of credulity and testimony: trust how things seem unless there is reason not to. So religious experience is evidence for God.
  • Buddhist challenge: deep meditators report no God; so either experiences conflict, or "God" is an interpretation added afterwards.
  • W. T. Stace (1886–1967), Mysticism and Philosophy (1960): an introvertive mystical core of "unitary consciousness" shared across traditions; Buddhist experience fits this core.
  • Steven Katz (b. 1944), "Language, Epistemology, and Mysticism" (1978): no unmediated experience; a Buddhist and a Christian mystic construct different experiences from their traditions.
  • So Buddhism is evidence in the debate: for a common core (Stace), or for conflicting, tradition-shaped experiences (Katz).

Mission 3

Miracles and powers in Buddhist texts

  • Texts describe iddhi (supernormal powers: passing through walls, walking on water, mind-reading) as by-products of deep meditation (Samaññaphala Sutta, Digha Nikaya 2).
  • Kevaddha Sutta (Digha Nikaya 11, paraphrase): three wonders: of power, of mind-reading, and of instruction. The Buddha dislikes the first two being displayed, since a sceptic could explain them by a charm. The wonder of instruction (teaching that transforms people) is the one he praises.
  • Vinaya (Cullavagga V.8): after Pindola flies up to take a sandalwood bowl, the Buddha forbids monks to display such powers to lay people.
  • Later accounts (e.g. the Twin Miracle at Savatthi) and Mahayana sutras are rich in wonders, often read as skilful means.
Mission 3

Hume and the Buddhist miracle

  • Hume (1748): miracles break laws of nature; testimony is weak; and rival religions' miracles cancel each other out:

> "in matters of religion, whatever is different is contrary; and that it is impossible the religions of ancient Rome, of Turkey, of Siam, and of China should, all of them, be established on any solid foundation" (Hume, Enquiry, Section X, 1748)

  • Siam (Thailand) is a Buddhist country: Hume includes Buddhist miracles in his argument.
  • Buddhist position: miracles are not the foundation of faith (Kevaddha); powers are natural results of a trained mind, not violations by a God. So Hume's "contrary miracles" argument has less bite.
  • But science still questions iddhi; many modern Buddhists read the stories symbolically, others keep them as real.
Quick check

Mission 3

Scientific and religious discourses

  • Kalama spirit: test teachings in experience. Many see Buddhism as uniquely compatible with science.
  • The Dalai Lama (b. 1935), The Universe in a Single Atom (2005): where science clearly disproves a Buddhist claim (e.g. Mount Meru cosmology), Buddhists should give that claim up.
  • Limits: karma and rebirth are not tested by science; the Dalai Lama says science has not disproved rebirth.
  • Critique: Donald Lopez, Buddhism and Science: A Guide for the Perplexed (2008): "Buddhism is scientific" is a modern story that edits out ritual, cosmology and devotion.
  • Three models: conflict, independence (separate questions, e.g. Gould's "non-overlapping magisteria", 1997), dialogue (Mind and Life meetings with scientists, from 1987).
Mission 3

Other religions' truth claims

  • John Hick (1922–2012), An Interpretation of Religion (1989): religions are different human responses to one Real, experienced as personal (God) or non-personal (e.g. nibbana, sunyata).
  • Buddhist inclusivism: truth and goodness in other faiths, but the Dhamma is the fullest path. The Dalai Lama: for one person, one truth and one religion; for society, many (paraphrase).
  • Theravada sources: the eightfold path is found only in the Buddha's teaching (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrase).
  • Nichiren (1222–1282): only the Lotus Sutra is true for this age; other schools mislead.
  • Critique of Hick from Buddhists: nibbana is not "a Real" behind religions; Hick fits Buddhism into a theistic frame.

Mission 3Evaluate

Does philosophical enquiry matter less for Buddhists than for theists?

  • Claim: Philosophical enquiry matters less to Buddhist faith than to theistic faith.
  • For: Buddhist faith is mainly belief-in (saddha in the Three Jewels) confirmed by practice; it does not rest on proofs of God or on miracles.
  • For: the Buddha set aside speculative questions (the poisoned arrow, Majjhima Nikaya 63).
  • Against: Buddhism makes strong belief-that claims (rebirth, karma, nibbana) open to challenge; Buddhist philosophers (Nagarjuna, Santideva, Dharmakirti) argued rigorously.
  • Against: the Kalama Sutta invites testing: enquiry is built into the path.
  • Judgement criteria: Which Buddhism (Theravada, Mahayana, secular)? Which kind of enquiry: proofs of God, or analysis of experience and language? Does practice-based confirmation count as reasonable?
Mission 3Worked answer

A dialogue paragraph on experience

Question: "Religious experience gives Buddhists good reason for their beliefs." Evaluate this view.

  • Belief: insight in meditation confirms impermanence, dukkha and anatta; awakening confirms nibbana.
  • Philosophy for: Swinburne's credulity; James's noetic quality; Buddhist experience is first-person and repeatable by training.
  • Philosophy against: Katz: experiences are shaped by prior teaching, so they cannot independently confirm it; experiences may not confirm rebirth.
  • Impact: modern Buddhists (and secular mindfulness) stress experience over doctrine; traditionalists keep both.
  • Judgement: e.g. "Experience gives good reason for what can be checked in practice (impermanence), less for what cannot (rebirth)."

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Every dialogue essay asks how reasonable (based on or consistent with reason), meaningful (and for whom) and coherent a belief is.
  • Price: belief-that holds a claim true; belief-in adds trust and commitment, and usually presupposes a belief-that.
  • The dialogue runs both ways: philosophy reshapes belief, and religious ideas shape philosophy.

Mission 2

  • Buddhism has no creator; it rejects a first cause (beginningless samsara) and used a problem of evil against creator gods.
  • Anatta and rebirth meet Descartes, Hume and Parfit: continuity without a permanent self.
  • Authority is tested in experience (Kalama Sutta, raft); saddha is trust (belief-in) confirmed in practice.

Mission 3

  • Buddhist meditative experience is mystical but not of God: evidence for Stace's common core or Katz's constructivism, and a challenge to arguments from experience to God.
  • The Buddha downplayed miracles (Kevaddha Sutta; Vinaya rule), so Hume's contrary-miracles argument bites less; science and Buddhism meet in dialogue, with critics such as Lopez.
  • On other religions Buddhists range from inclusivism to Nichiren's exclusivism; many reject Hick's "one Real".
