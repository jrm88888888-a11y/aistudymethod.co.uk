Dialogues · what is this unit?

Connecting philosophy and religion

This dialogue unit asks you to assess how far the philosophy of religion (Component 1) and your chosen religion (Component 2) influence, support and challenge one another.

  • Where does philosophy reinforce religious belief?
  • Where does it challenge it — and how does the tradition respond?
  • Do the two share common ground or stand in tension?

Apply this to your religion (Christianity, Buddhism, Hinduism, Islam or Judaism); the examples below are illustrative.

Dialogues · arguments for God

Do the proofs support faith?

The arguments for God (ontological, cosmological, teleological) connect directly with theistic religions:

  • Support: a tradition may welcome them as rational backing for belief in a creator (cosmological) or designer (teleological).
  • Tension: some believers (e.g. following Pascal or a fideist line) say faith rests on revelation and experience, not proofs, and that the "God of the philosophers" is not the living God of worship.
  • Non-theistic Buddhism has no creator God, so these arguments are largely beside the point — itself a striking dialogue result.

Dialogues · problem of evil

Does evil challenge the tradition?

The problem of evil is philosophy's sharpest challenge to religions that affirm an all-powerful, all-loving God.

  • Challenge: Mackie's inconsistent triad and Rowe's evidential argument press on the tradition's concept of God.
  • Response: traditions draw on their own theodicies and scriptures — free will, soul-making, the redemptive value of suffering, or trust in a wisdom beyond ours.
  • How a religion answers evil is a test of the coherence of its doctrine of God.
Dialogues · religious language

Speaking of the transcendent

Debates about religious language connect deeply with how traditions actually talk about God:

  • Via negativa — strong in Judaism (Maimonides) and Islam, guarding God's transcendence.
  • Analogy — Aquinas' Christian account of God-talk.
  • Symbol — Tillich's reading of doctrine and scripture.
  • Challenge: Ayer's verificationism calls all such talk meaningless — met by Wittgenstein's point that religion is its own "language game".

Dialogues · experience & miracles

Experience, miracles and revelation

  • Religious experience is foundational for many traditions (revelation to prophets, mystical union, enlightenment). Philosophy (James, Otto, Swinburne) can support its evidential value; Freud and neuroscience challenge it.
  • Miracles feature in founding claims (e.g. resurrection, revelation of scripture). Hume challenges belief on evidential grounds; Wiles on theological ones — pressing the tradition to explain divine action.
Dialogues · self & afterlife

The self, death and salvation

Philosophical debates about the self and afterlife engage each religion's teaching:

  • Dualism vs materialism: does the tradition teach an immortal soul (Plato-like), bodily resurrection (Hick's replica), or — in Buddhism — rebirth without a self (anatta)?
  • Challenge: Ryle and Dawkins' materialism questions the very idea of a soul that survives death.

Dialogues · evaluation

Reaching a judgement

  • Does philosophy strengthen faith by making it reasonable, or threaten it by demanding proof it cannot give?
  • Are there questions religion answers that philosophy cannot, and vice versa?
  • Is the relationship best described as conflict, independence or dialogue (cf. Barbour on science)?
  • A strong answer reaches a reasoned overall judgement, supported by named thinkers on both sides.

The big ideas to hold together

The task: Assess how far philosophy of religion supports and challenges the chosen religion

Support: Theistic proofs, and defences of religious experience (James, Swinburne) and language (Wittgenstein)

Challenge: The problem of evil (Mackie), verificationism (Ayer), miracles (Hume), materialism (Ryle, Dawkins)

Tradition's response: Theodicies, revelation, the via negativa/analogy/symbol, and its own account of the self

Judgement: Weigh conflict, independence and dialogue; reach a reasoned conclusion
