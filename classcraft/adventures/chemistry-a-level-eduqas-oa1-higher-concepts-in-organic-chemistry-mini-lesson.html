Mission 1

Stereoisomers and restricted rotation

  • Stereoisomers: same structural formula, different arrangement of atoms in space.
  • A C=C is a σ bond plus a π bond. The π bond is a sideways overlap of p orbitals, above and below the plane.
  • Twisting about the C=C would break the π bond: a large energy barrier. So there is no rotation.
  • Groups are locked on the same side or on opposite sides of the C=C.
  • This needs two different groups on each C=C carbon. Propene, CH2=CHCH3, has none: one carbon carries two H.
Mission 1

Naming E and Z: CIP priority rules

  • On each C=C carbon, rank its two groups by the atomic number of the atom attached. Higher = priority.
  • A tie? Compare the atoms attached next: –CH2CH3 (C, H, H) beats –CH3 (H, H, H).
  • Z (zusammen, together): the two priority groups on the same side.
  • E (entgegen, opposite): on opposite sides.
  • ClCH=CHCl: Cl > H on each carbon. Both Cl on the same side: (Z)-1,2-dichloroethene.
  • BrClC=CHCH3: Br > Cl on C1; CH3 > H on C2. Br and CH3 on opposite sides: E.
  • The video said cis and trans. Your exam names these isomers E and Z.

Mission 1

Chiral centres and enantiomers

  • A chiral centre (an asymmetric carbon) is bonded to four different groups.
  • It gives two optical isomers, or enantiomers: non-superimposable mirror images.
  • Example: 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH3CH(OH)COOH. C2 carries H, OH, CH3 and COOH.
  • Draw it in 3D: two bonds in the plane, a wedge towards you, a dash away (tetrahedral, 109.5°).
  • Draw the other enantiomer as the mirror image. No rotation makes the two match.
Mission 1

Spotting a chiral centre

  • Only a carbon with four single bonds can be chiral: CH3, CH2 and C=C carbons never are.
  • Compare whole groups, not just the atom attached.
  • 1-Chloro-2-methylbutane, CH2ClCH(CH3)CH2CH3: C2 carries H, CH3, C2H5 and CH2Cl: chiral.
  • Pentan-3-ol, CH3CH2CH(OH)CH2CH3: C3 carries two C2H5 groups: not chiral.
  • Mark a chiral centre with an asterisk: CH3C*H(OH)COOH.
Quick check
?Which carbon atom in 3-methylhexane, CH3CH2CH(CH3)CH2CH2CH3, is a chiral centre?
Mission 1

Optical activity and racemic mixtures

  • Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light: by equal angles, in opposite directions. It is optically active.
  • A racemic mixture (racemate): equal amounts of both enantiomers. The rotations cancel: optically inactive.
  • Enantiomers share melting point, boiling point, density and most reactions.
  • They behave differently only in a chiral environment: an enzyme, a receptor.
  • Your smell receptors are chiral. Each carvone enantiomer fits them differently: spearmint or caraway.
Mission 1

Where chirality matters

  • Optical isomers turn up in many natural compounds: amino acids, sugars, drugs.
  • Complexes show stereoisomerism too: cis–trans (cisplatin, Pt(NH3)2Cl2, is the cis isomer) and optical isomers with three bidentate ligands, e.g. [Ni(NH2CH2CH2NH2)3]²⁺. More in the complexes mission.

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • E/Z: restricted rotation about C=C plus two different groups on each carbon; name by CIP priorities.
  • A chiral centre (four different groups) gives enantiomers: equal and opposite rotation of plane-polarised light; a racemate is inactive; they differ only in chiral environments.
