Industrialisation & the People: Britain c1783-1885

This is an optional route. AQA A-level History is option-based. 1F Industrialisation and the people is one of eleven Component 1 breadth studies (1A–1L). It is a British option, so pair it with a non-British Component 2 depth study, plus the Component 3 Historical Investigation.

This mini-lesson covers both halves of 1F: industrialisation and reform, 1783–1832 and reform and challenge, 1832–1885. The organising question is how an industrialising society changed its politics — and whether change came from pressure below or calculation above.

The option · what 1F actually asks

A century of transformation, six key questions

AQA's key questions for 1F run across the whole period:

  • How did industrialisation and economic change affect Britain?
  • How and why did the political system change?
  • How and why did protest and radicalism develop, and how far did they succeed?
  • How did the condition of the people — work, poverty, health, education — change?
  • How important were ideas and ideology (laissez-faire, utilitarianism, evangelicalism)?
  • How important were key individuals and groups?

The 1F trap: writing economic history and political history as two separate essays inside one answer. The marks are in the join — how urbanisation created constituencies that the 1832 system could not represent; how the railway created the national market that made free trade irresistible.

1783–1815 · Pitt, war and repression

The state closes ranks

William Pitt the Younger became Prime Minister in 1783, aged 24, and rebuilt public finance after the American war: consolidation of customs duties, the sinking fund (1786), and — as war costs mounted — the first income tax (1799).

  • The French Revolution changed everything. Radical societies — the London Corresponding Society (1792) — and Thomas Paine's Rights of Man (1791–92) alarmed the governing class.
  • Repression: suspension of habeas corpus (from 1794), the Treasonable Practices and Seditious Meetings Acts (1795) — the 'Two Acts' — and the Combination Acts (1799 and 1800) banning workers' associations.
  • Industrial protest: Luddism (1811–1813) in the textile districts was not blind machine-breaking but a targeted response to the destruction of customary trades. Frame-breaking was made a capital offence in 1812.

E.P. Thompson, The Making of the English Working Class (1963), argued that these decades made a working class — not as an automatic product of factories, but through shared experience of repression, dispossession and radical politics. The title is deliberate: class was made, not simply generated by machinery.

1815–1832 · protest and the Great Reform Act

Peterloo, Liberal Toryism, and 1832

Peace in 1815 brought demobilisation, depression and the Corn Law of 1815 — which barred cheap foreign grain until the domestic price reached 80 shillings a quarter. Radicalism revived.

  • Peterloo, 16 August 1819: the yeomanry charged a peaceful mass reform meeting at St Peter's Field, Manchester, killing and injuring demonstrators. The government's response was the Six Acts (1819), restricting meetings and the radical press.
  • The Cato Street Conspiracy (1820) — a plot to murder the Cabinet — was used to justify continued repression.
  • Liberal Toryism (1822–1827): Robert Peel reformed the criminal code and (in 1829) founded the Metropolitan Police; William Huskisson cut tariffs; the Combination Acts were repealed (1824–25). Catholic Emancipation (1829) split the Tory party — and proved that mass extra-parliamentary pressure could force Westminster's hand.
  • The Great Reform Act (1832): it disenfranchised the worst rotten boroughs and gave seats to the new industrial towns, but the electorate remained a small propertied minority and it explicitly excluded women. Its purpose, in the Whigs' own understanding, was to preserve the constitution by conceding to the middle class.

The examinable paradox: 1832 was both a revolution in principle (Parliament could be reformed by statute) and a conservative measure in practice. Working-class radicals who had helped force it through were left out — which is precisely where Chartism comes from.

1833–1848 · the reforming state and Chartism

The condition of England

The reformed Parliament legislated on an unprecedented scale — and provoked an unprecedented movement.

  • 1833: the Factory Act (limiting child labour in textile mills and — crucially — creating factory inspectors) and the Abolition of Slavery Act across most of the British Empire.
  • 1834: the Poor Law Amendment Act — the New Poor Law — abolished outdoor relief in principle, grouped parishes into unions, and made the workhouse, governed by 'less eligibility', the test of destitution. It was the most hated statute of the century. In the same year the Tolpuddle Martyrs were transported for administering unlawful oaths.
  • Chartism (1838–1848): the People's Charter demanded universal manhood suffrage, the secret ballot, equal constituencies, annual parliaments, payment of MPs and the abolition of the property qualification. Three great petitions — 1839, 1842 and 1848 — were rejected by the Commons.
  • Why did Chartism fail? Divisions over 'moral' and 'physical' force; regional and trade fragmentation; a state that combined firmness with concession; and — after 1848 — improving conditions. Note that five of the six points were eventually achieved.

Interpretation: Gareth Stedman Jones, in Languages of Class (1983), argued that Chartism should be read through its own political language — an attack on 'Old Corruption' and an unrepresentative state — rather than as a reflex of economic distress. That challenges the older reading (associated with Hobsbawm and the Marxist tradition) of Chartism as the political expression of class hardship.

1846–1885 · free trade and a widening franchise

From Corn Law repeal to the Third Reform Act

After 1846 the political question shifts from whether to reform to how far.

  • Repeal of the Corn Laws (1846): the Anti-Corn Law League (Richard Cobden and John Bright) built the model modern pressure group. Peel's repeal, forced on by the Irish famine, split the Conservative party for a generation and made free trade the national orthodoxy.
  • The mid-Victorian settlement: the Ten Hours Act (1847); the Great Exhibition (1851); the growth of the railways after the Liverpool and Manchester (1830) made a genuinely national economy.
  • The Second Reform Act (1867): passed by Disraeli's minority Conservative government, it enfranchised most male urban householders — roughly doubling the electorate. Motive is disputed: principle, party advantage, or an educated guess about which way new voters would go.
  • Consolidation: the Elementary Education Act (1870); the Trade Union Act (1871); the secret ballot (1872); and the Third Reform Act (1884) with the Redistribution Act (1885), extending the householder franchise to the counties and creating single-member constituencies. Roughly 60% of adult men could now vote — and no women.

The breadth judgement 1F wants: Britain moved from an oligarchy in 1783 to a mass — but still male and partial — electorate by 1885, without revolution. Explaining that absence, without lazily calling the British 'moderate', is the heart of the option.

Interpretations · two great debates

Standard of living, and the meaning of Chartism

1F has two set-piece historiographical quarrels. Learn both.

1. The standard of living debate. Did industrialisation make working people better or worse off between roughly 1780 and 1850? The optimists — classically T.S. Ashton — pointed to rising real wages and falling prices for manufactured goods. The pessimists — E.J. Hobsbawm, and in a different register E.P. Thompson — pointed to urban squalor, mortality, loss of independence and the destruction of customary work. Thompson's key move was to insist that the question is not only about wages but about what was done to people: quality of life, not just quantity of goods.
2. What was Chartism? The older answer: the political voice of an immiserated working class — 'a knife and fork question'. Gareth Stedman Jones (Languages of Class, 1983) replied that Chartists' own words attacked political exclusion and 'Old Corruption', not capitalism as such — so Chartism was a political, radical-constitutional movement, and it faded when the state ceased to look predatory.

Evaluate, don't referee from the stands. The optimist case rests on national wage series; the pessimist case on local mortality, housing and testimony. Different evidence bases give different answers — and saying so is exactly the analytical move that earns Level 5.

Exam technique · Component 1

Sources for a documentary century

AQA Component 1: 80 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes, 40%. One compulsory three-primary-source question (30 marks) plus two essays from three (25 marks each).

  • 1F's sources are rich and treacherous: parliamentary Blue Books and select committee evidence, the radical press (Northern Star), Chartist speeches, factory inspectors' reports, and hostile middle-class commentary.
  • Provenance does the work. A select committee's evidence was solicited by reformers with a bill to pass; the Northern Star was written to build a movement; a mill-owner's testimony had a business to defend. None of that makes them worthless — it makes them valuable for different things.
  • The essays are always judgement questions. Practise: 'How far was popular protest responsible for political reform in the years 1783 to 1885?'

Key question · ideas and the state

Laissez-faire — and the state that kept growing

1F's fifth key question is about ideas, and here the received story is wrong in an interesting way.

  • The ideology of the age was laissez-faire: free trade, minimal interference, the self-regulating market. Utilitarianism (Bentham) supplied the language of efficiency; evangelicalism supplied the language of duty.
  • Yet the reality was an inexorably growing state. The 1833 Factory Act created inspectors. The 1834 New Poor Law created a national bureaucracy. Public health legislation, mines inspection, education grants and the Home Office's expanding remit all followed.
  • The paradox is only apparent: it was precisely the evidence gathered by state inspection — the Blue Books, the commissions — that made further intervention politically irresistible. Investigating a problem creates the constituency for solving it.

The essay line: Victorian Britain professed laissez-faire and practised a steadily expanding administrative state. Argue that with the 1833 inspectorate and the 1834 Commission, and you are making a genuinely historiographical point about the gap between ideology and practice.
